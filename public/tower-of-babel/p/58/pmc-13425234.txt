A neuroanatomical basis for co-occurring low attention and low reading ability
Authors: Michael F Romano, Pierre Nedelec, Maria Luisa Mandelli, Jason D Yeatman, Luke W Bonham, Pedro Pinheiro-Chagas, Leo P Sugrue, Marilu Gorno-Tempini, Andreas M Rauschecker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAttention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and dyslexia are two common disorders that can have devastating impacts on child development and occur together more often than one would expect by chance. Identifying imaging markers characteristic of a combined ADHD and dyslexia phenotype could improve our ability to diagnose these entities early and inform our understanding of the underlying disease processes. In this study, we set out to determine whether there are unique cortical, white matter, or subcortical characteristics in adolescents with combined low attention (LA) and low reading ability (LR), which we utilized as behavioral surrogate markers of ADHD and dyslexia. We conducted a retrospective, cross-sectional analysis using imaging, demographic, genetic, and neuropsychiatric data from 8779 individuals in the Adolescent Behavioral Cognitive Development study. We modeled cortical area, subcortical volume, and white matter integrity (fractional anisotropy) across brain regions and white matter tracts as functions of LA and LR. Cortical area was reduced in both LA and LR separately, with overlapping reductions in orbitofrontal cortex that persist in vertex-wise maps after adjustment for total cortical area. Global white matter integrity uniquely differed in individuals with combined LA/LR. These findings are most supportive of a shared underlying susceptibility for ADHD and dyslexia.

Introduction

Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) and dyslexia are both common developmental disorders occurring at rates of ~11% (Danielson et al. 2024) and 7% (Peterson and Pennington 2015), respectively. Though the characteristics of these disorders are drastically different, they occur together far more often than expected by chance, with a bidirectional rate of ~25% to 40% in children and adolescents (August and Garfinkel 1990; Willcutt et al. 2010). ADHD is characterized by inattention, hyperactivity, and/or impulsivity (American Psychiatric Association 2022), while developmental dyslexia, or a specific learning disorder with impairment in reading, is characterized by deficits in word recognition, decoding, and spelling, below what is expected for age, and which can’t be explained by another disorder such as an intellectual disability (Vellutino et al. 2004; Willcutt et al. 2010; Peterson and Pennington 2012; Kibby et al. 2021; American Psychiatric Association 2022). Early treatment of both disorders is crucial. While dyslexia can have a significant impact on school performance, for example, reading remediation has been shown to be most beneficial when implemented prior to the initial acquisition of reading (Peterson and Pennington 2015). Additionally, while ADHD is associated with several negative outcomes including poor school performance, poor social function, and drug use, treatment can ameliorate them (Shaw et al. 2012; Rajaprakash and Leppert 2022). Given that those with both ADHD and dyslexia have been shown to have even worse educational outcomes than those with either disorder alone (Willcutt et al. 2007a), identifying an underlying explanation for their high rate of co-occurrence and elucidating related biomarkers could have significant clinical impacts by providing physicians with tools to help them know when to screen an individual for both disorders.

Multiple theories have been proposed to explain the strong link between these two conditions, with investigations spanning the fields of genetics, neuropsychiatry, and neuroimaging (Germanò et al. 2010; Willcutt et al. 2010; DuPaul et al. 2013; Moura et al. 2017; Hendren et al. 2018). One theory is that co-occurring dyslexia and ADHD constitute a unique subtype with some features distinct from “pure” dyslexia or ADHD. Another theory is the shared susceptibility hypothesis. Under this hypothesis, ADHD and dyslexia both arise from a common genetic influence (Gayán et al. 2005; Willcutt et al. 2005; Willcutt et al. 2007b; Germanò et al. 2010; DuPaul et al. 2013; Hongyao et al. 2023), potentially leading to common deficits in inattention (Willcutt et al. 2005) and processing speed (Willcutt et al. 2010; McGrath et al. 2011; Peterson et al. 2017).

In addition to being reflected in neuropsychological differences, shared genetic influences may manifest as common neurostructural changes associated with these conditions. For example, several studies investigating gray matter differences in those with ADHD or dyslexia have elucidated differences in overlapping cortical regions in temporal and frontal areas compared with normal controls (Owens et al. 2021; Bernanke et al. 2022; Ligges et al. 2022). Purported white matter changes include decreased integrity of the left arcuate fasciculus (AF), which has been observed in both individuals with ADHD and those with a reading disability (Chiang et al. 2016; Christodoulou et al. 2017; Vanderauwera et al. 2017; Tsai et al. 2021).

Despite such extensive research regarding differences in gray and white matter characteristics in individuals with dyslexia or with ADHD, the neurostructural underpinnings of the co-occurring condition remain underexplored. In the few studies where the co-occurring condition has been examined specifically in relation to ADHD and dyslexia separately, decreases in right thalamus and left medial frontal volume (Jagger-Rickels et al. 2018), left middle frontal volume (Kibby et al. 2020), and middle temporal gyrus cortical thickness (Langer et al. 2019) have been shown. However, these analyses were either limited in scope to frontal regions (Kibby et al. 2020) or did not investigate non-additive, synergistic changes in gray matter associated with co-occurring ADHD and dyslexia (Jagger-Rickels et al. 2018; Langer et al. 2019). Regarding white matter, one recent study found sequential decreases in fractional anisotropy (FA) in the SLF in those with combined ADHD and dyslexia, those with dyslexia alone, and controls (Slaby et al. 2023). However, it is unclear whether their findings are in support of a unique subtype or a shared susceptibility underlying high ADHD and dyslexia co-occurrence, given the lack of a comparison with an ADHD subgroup.

In summary, despite substantial genetic and cognitive evidence for the shared susceptibility hypothesis, the question of whether it is supported by neural signatures remains unanswered. Identifying volumetric or white matter features unique to co-occurring ADHD and dyslexia could help generate an imaging fingerprint for this combined deficit and serve to improve targeting of early interventions. Alternatively, finding that volumetric and white matter features in those with co-occurring ADHD and dyslexia reflect the summation of the features of the two independent conditions would offer further support for the shared susceptibility hypothesis and harmonize prior scientific investigations spanning the genetic and cognitive levels. Given that studies involving brain imaging in ADHD and dyslexia have been largely limited by low numbers of individuals and limited inclusion of covariates, we sought to leverage data from the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development study (ABCD) to conduct a cross-sectional, exploratory analysis to address the outstanding question of why ADHD and dyslexia frequently co-occur. The ABCD study is a longitudinal study of child health and development that includes multimodal imaging, demographic, genetic, and cognitive metrics from over 11,000 early adolescent participants. While this dataset does not contain clinical diagnoses of ADHD and dyslexia, it contains measures such as the Child Behavior Checklist (CBCL) questionnaire and the NIH Toolbox Oral Reading Recognition Task that could serve as surrogate behavioral markers for these respective conditions. By leveraging these behavioral measures, we were able to conduct a comprehensive structural analysis and probe for subtle global and regional changes in gray and white matter that could offer insight into the underlying neural etiologies of the high comorbidity between ADHD and dyslexia.

Materials and methods

Dataset

ABCD is a national longitudinal study of child health and development across 21 different data collection sites within the US, tracking 11,880 children who were aged 9 to 10 years at the time of enrollment. Parents were consented and children were assented (Clark et al. 2018; Dick et al. 2021). ABCD collection sites mostly utilize a central Institutional Review Board (IRB) at the University of California, San Diego for ethical approval, though some sites obtain approval from their local IRB (Auchter et al. 2018). The present study uses only de-identified data obtained from the central ABCD data repository. Therefore, the study does not qualify as human subjects research per the University of California, San Francisco (UCSF), and does not require approval by the UCSF IRB.

The goals of ABCD include studying both psychological and neurobiological development of a socio-demographically representative group of children in the United States considering race, ethnicity, age, gender, socio-economic status, and rural versus urban location (Garavan et al. 2018). Our study primarily utilized the baseline time point data from the NIMH Data Archive ABCD Collection Release 4.0 (DOI: 10.15154/1523041, with release-specific updates described at https://nda.nih.gov/study.html?id=1299), with surface area vertex maps derived from ABCD Collection release 6.1. At the baseline timepoint, a number of neurocognitive batteries were performed to assess different components of cognition. Relevant to the present study are the Matrix Reasoning subtest of the Wechsler Intelligence Test for Children-V (MR), which correlates with general ability and fluid reasoning (Luciana et al. 2018), in addition to the NIH Toolbox Oral Reading Recognition Task (NIH-TORRT), which has examinees pronounce letters and words and the results of which correlate with reading ability (Gershon et al. 2014; Luciana et al. 2018). Mental health questionnaires included the CBCL, which is performed by a parent and contains an attention scale that has a high association with ADHD (Biederman et al. 1993; Chen et al. 1994; Biederman et al. 2021), and a specific DSM-Oriented Attention Problem Subscale with an even better ability to classify individuals with ADHD versus those without (Aebi et al. 2010). Finally, both structural and functional imaging were performed for each child, including diffusion weighted MRI, using a scanning protocol harmonized across sites (Casey et al. 2018).

Initial variable processing

We analyzed baseline data from an initial sample of 11,877 study participants. Participants missing values for at least one of the variables of interest (genetic principal components 1 through 5, parent income, CBCL ADHD t-score, NIH-TORRT theta value, MR total scaled score, sex, education level, MRI serial number, and anxiety, all to be described later) were excluded. Only individuals with English speaking abilities rated as “excellent” or “good” were utilized to prevent a lack of fluency in English language being a potential contributor to LR. Participants were restricted to those with at least 3 through 5 years of education given that (i) we sought to use education level to normalize relative reading ability and (ii) the majority of individuals fell into these categories, with only 3 individuals having less than 2 years of education at baseline, 69 individuals having 2 years of education at baseline, 358 individuals having 6 years of education at baseline, and 5 individuals having more than 6 years of education at baseline. Following these steps, a total of 8,779 participants remained in our dataset. Definitions of low attention and low reading

To the greatest extent possible, we wanted to align our statistical models with the clinical and translational framing of our central research questions: are there neuroanatomical signatures characteristic of co-occurring dyslexia and ADHD? Because clinicians and educators typically encounter dyslexia and ADHD as categorical presentations, we chose to dichotomize reading ability and attention, our respective behavioral proxies for these conditions, into categorical variables. This approach has the dual benefit of permitting direct estimation of the interaction contrast (ie the co-occurring group relative to what would be predicted from the additive combination of each condition alone), which is the quantity of central interest.

LA (low attention) was defined as a CBCL ADHD T-score above a value of 65, in keeping with Bernanke et al. (Bernanke et al. 2022), who utilized the ABCD dataset CBCL ADHD t-score to classify individuals as having ADHD. This assessment is considered more specific than the CBCL attention-only subscale, particularly in community settings (Aebi et al. 2010; Spencer et al. 2018).

NIH-TORRT theta scores were z-scored within each education level (years of education of 3, 4, or 5), and LR (low reading ability) was denoted as a z-score less than 1.5 standard deviations below the mean. Theta scores are not corrected for age or demographics, and so are suitable measures of overall reading performance (NIH Toolbox 2006-2020). Definitions of dyslexia have included both IQ-achievement discrepancies and reading achievement 1.5 standard deviations below the mean for age (Peterson and Pennington 2012). Here, we attempted to balance these definitions by using an education level-adjusted threshold and controlling for general ability in our mixed-effects models, as detailed below, by using MR as a covariate. The applied thresholds yielded 515 participants with LR and 541 with LA.

Justification for additional variables

We included “anxiety” as a categorical variable in our statistical models due to its relatively high prevalence and known comorbidity with ADHD, again in keeping with Bernanke et al. (Bernanke et al. 2022). MR (the WISC-V matrix reasoning total scaled score), which correlates with fluid intelligence and general ability, was included given that the NIH Toolbox TORRT theta score does not control for overall intelligence. We further incorporated MRI serial number numbers as categorical variables to account for technical differences between scanners and their potential impacts on gray and white matter metrics. Parent income was included as a categorical variable to account for potential socioeconomic effects on brain metrics. For example, individuals with a lower socioeconomic status may show lower subcortical volumes and cortical thickness (Rakesh et al. 2023). Income was binned into those making <$50,000 per year, those making between $50,000 and $100,000 per year, and those making ≥$100,000 per year. Finally, genetic principal components were included to control for population structure differences attributable to ancestry rather than the factors of interest (Fan et al. 2023).

MRI acquisition and processing

Structural and diffusion MRI were used in the current study as provided by ABCD wherein acquisition protocols were harmonized across the 21 ABCD sites. Imaging data protocols (Casey et al. 2018; Hagler et al. 2019) and image processing (Hagler et al. 2019) for the ABCD Study are described elsewhere. Briefly, for structural MRI, FreeSurfer v7.1.1 was used to pre-process T1-weighted images. Using an icosahedron order number of 5 and 16 smoothing iterations, cortical surface area maps consisting of 18,742 vertices were derived. These cortical surface area maps were utilized for our vertex-wise confirmation of results obtained via tabulated cortical areas.

For diffusion MRI, using ABCD publicly available “minimally processed” imaging data in each subject’s space, we computed tractography maps and tractometry using pyAFQ with standard parameters (constrained spherical deconvolution fiber orientation functions and probabilistic tracking across the brain) (Kruper et al. 2021). We computed diffusion kurtosis imaging FA maps and projected them to a set of available white matter tracts for the subsequent analyses. Overall, we obtained FA values for 30 white matter tracts across 100 different points (nodes), and the 50 middle nodes were averaged to obtain an average FA value for each tract. The mean of these 30 average FA values was utilized as a global measure of white matter FA for each participant in the study.

Whole brain metric models

Linear mixed-effects models were utilized to investigate the contributions of the variables of interest to structural MRI metrics (Fig. 1). First, we addressed whether LA and/or LR were predictive of global brain metrics. Gray matter metrics included mean cortical thickness, mean sulcal depth, total cortical area, and total subcortical volume, all measured in millimeters, square millimeters, or cubic millimeters as appropriate. White matter microstructure was measured by mean white matter FA. Numeric independent variables were all z-scored before modeling to account for innate differences in the magnitudes of each measurement. If a given subject had missing data for at least one independent variable or the respective dependent variable for a given model, this subject was excluded from the analysis. Then, outliers with z-scored outcome variable values exceeding a magnitude of 3 were excluded from the analysis. Ultimately, 8310 participants were included in the model corresponding to mean white matter FA; 8751 participants were included in each other model.

Vertex-wise confirmation of results

We utilized three-dimensional representations of cortical area across ~25,000 independent vertices as response variables for a descriptive confirmation of our regional findings. Due to the number of vertices in these representations, FEMA (fast and efficient mixed-effects algorithm) was used to compute model coefficients, statistical significance, and surface plots (Parekh et al. 2024). Briefly, FEMA leverages certain features of brain imaging data to make mixed-effects model estimation fast and memory efficient, for example, by using a method-of-moments approach to approximate the random effects covariance structure. Z-scores were computed analytically. Model coefficients corresponding to the “LR” and “LA” terms were plotted as surface plots in Fig. 3A. Subsequently, model coefficients for each vertex were thresholded at a magnitude of 1.96.

Sensitivity to global cortical area

To address whether regional cortical area associations reflected region-specific effects or global differences in brain size, we conducted a series of supplementary analyses at both the regional and vertex-wise levels. At the regional level, we repeated the LME models with total cortical area included as an additional covariate. We extracted the LA and LR coefficients to evaluate whether regional effects survived adjustment, and we extracted the standardized β of the total cortical area covariate itself to characterize the magnitude of global scaling effects across regions (Supplementary Fig. S1A to C). At the vertex-wise level, we repeated the FEMA analysis with total cortical area as a covariate (Supplementary Fig. S1D).

Results

Demographics

We first validated our LA and LR labels using demographic data (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1, Supplementary Table S2, and Supplementary Table S3). Across the 8779 participants selected for our demographic analysis, participants with LR were more likely to have co-occurring LA than participants without LR, as expected based on prior literature (P < 0.001). Age did not differ in terms of LR (W = 2095430.5, P = 0.559, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) nor in terms of LA (W = 2161883.0, P = 0.244).

WISC-V matrix reasoning total scaled scores (MR), a surrogate measure of intelligence, were higher in participants without LA (W = 1873686.0, P < 0.001) and in participants without LR (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 1182264.0, P < 0.001). Extrapolating from our surrogate behavioral measures of LA and LR, the latter result is in keeping with prior findings showing decreases in scaled MR in those with a combined reading disorder and ADHD in comparison to ADHD only (Parke et al. 2020). We found that those with LR had spent fewer years reading than those with normal reading ability (Wilcoxon rank sum test, W = 364859.5, P < 0.001). As expected, LA was more common in males (7.0% vs 5.0%, Fisher’s exact test P < 0.001), consistent with prior literature noting that males have a higher predilection for ADHD (Willcutt 2012). LR did not differ significantly between sexes (6.3% vs 5.4%, Fisher’s exact test, P = 0.084).

In terms of parent income, a surrogate measure of socioeconomic status, the proportions of participants with ADHD (X2(2) = 57.0, P < 0.001) and with LR (X2(2) = 399, P < 0.001) differed across parent income brackets. Those in the lowest parent income group (<$50,000 per year) had the highest proportions of participants with LA (Fisher’s exact test: 9.0% vs 6.3% for ≥$50,000 per year and <$100,000 per year, P < 0.001; and 9.0% vs 4.3% for >$100,000, P < 0.001, FDR corrected 6 comparisons) and LR (Fisher’s exact test: 14% vs. 4.6% for >$50,000 and < $100,000 per year, P < 0.001; and 14% vs. 1.7% for >$100,000 per year, P < 0.001, FDR correction for 6 comparisons). That those of lower socioeconomic status are more likely to exhibit LA and LR underscores that these measures are coarse behavioral proxies for ADHD and dyslexia, both of which also reflect contextual factors. The number of individuals with LA did not differ significantly between different education levels (X2(2) = 4.74, P = 0.094), though slightly fewer participants with grade 5 education levels had LR than participants with grade 3 education levels (5.1% vs 7.1%, X2(2) = 7.47; Fisher’s exact test: 3 years vs 5 years of education: P = 0.021, FDR correction for 6 comparisons).

Finally, participants with anxiety were much more likely to have LA (Fisher’s exact test, P < 0.001), consistent with a known relationship between anxiety and ADHD (Janiczak et al. 2020).

Global models

Having addressed the validity of our LR and LA metrics, we asked whether global white matter or gray matter metrics differed in those with LR, those with LA, or those with combined LR and LA.

Mean white matter FA demonstrated a significant interaction between LR and LA (β = −.027, t(7871) = −2.80, P = 0.005, 95% CI [−.045, −.008], r = −.032, Fig. 1A), with individuals with LR and LA together demonstrating significantly lower mean FA when compared to the no LR/no LA group (t(7833) = 2.94, P = 0.017, d = 0.556, 95% CI [0.185, 0.926]), when compared to the typical reading/LA group (t(7864) = 2.63, P = 0.042, d = 0.524, 95% CI [0.133, 0.914]), and when compared to the LR/typical attention group (t(7858) = 3.12, P = 0.010, d = 0.623, 95% CI [0.231, 1.015]). To investigate whether this difference could be attributed to variability in the time spent reading, we subsequently performed a correlation analysis between mean white matter FA and the number of years each individual spent reading for pleasure, which was not statistically significant (Spearman r2 = 0.0031, P = 0.81, n = 5941 samples with mean FA and time spent reading available). Further, to ensure that this result did not depend on our specific aggregation strategy (ie computing the mean FA across 30 white matter tracts), we recomputed this interaction term utilizing the median FA across the 30 white matter tracts, the 10% trimmed mean across 30 tracts, and the 1st principal component across all of these tracts (Supplementary Table S4). Across each of these strategies, this interaction term maintained statistical significance (P = 0.005 – 0.033), directionality, and a similar standardized β (−0.027- −0.022).

Given the relatively small number of individuals in the co-occurring LR/LA group (n = 63), we also performed leave-one-out cross-validation and group-stratified bootstrap resampling to confirm that our result was not dependent on a single or a few observations in this group (Supplementary Table S5). Standardized β coefficients for the interaction coefficient across leave-one-out resamples ranged from [−0.0210, −0.0148] with a mean of −0.0189, in comparison to LME estimate of −0.0266, with only 3/63 resamples resulted in the loss of significance of this p-value. 10,000 bootstrap resamples resulted in a standardized β coefficient estimate of −0.0244 with a 95% CI of [−0.0454, −0.0033].

There were no significant interactions between LR and LA with cortical or subcortical metrics (cortical thickness: β = −.001, t(8303) = −0.056, P = 0.955, 95% CI [−.022, 0.021], r = −.001; sulcal depth: β = −.006, t(8516) = −0.579, P = 0.563, 95% CI [−.026, 0.014], r = −.006; cortical area: β = −.005, t(8058) = −0.517, P = 0.605, 95% CI [−.023, 0.013], r = −.006; subcortical volume: β = −.003, t(7946) = −0.357, P = 0.721, 95% CI [−.021, 0.015], r = −.004). These findings suggest that global cortical and subcortical measurements are each related to LR and LA independently of the other diagnosis. Those with LR demonstrated lower overall cortical thickness, cortical area, and subcortical volume (Fig. 1B) (cortical area: β = −.039, t(7771) = −4.696, P < 0.001, 95% CI [−.057, −.021], r = −.053; cortical thickness: β = −.022, t(8239) = −2.165, P = 0.030, 95% CI [−.043, −.000], r = −.024; subcortical volume: β = −.051, t(7678) = −6.074, P < 0.001, 95% CI [−.069, −.033], r = −.069). Additionally, those with LA demonstrated lower sulcal depth, cortical area, and subcortical volume (sulcal depth: β = −.017, t(8647) = −1.975, P = 0.048, 95% CI [−.036, 0.003], r = −.021; cortical area: β = −.038, t(8482) = −4.657, P < 0.001, 95% CI [−.056, −.021], r = −.051; subcortical volume: β = −.037, t(8452) = −4.442, P < 0.001, 95% CI [−.054, −.019], r = −.048).

Finally, because thresholding variables can decrease statistical power and influence estimates of interaction, we performed sensitivity analysis for each of these metrics, with results included in Supplementary Table S5. Mean white matter FA demonstrated a statistically significant interaction between LR and LA across all investigated thresholds for LA (60, 65, and 70) when the LR threshold was held stable at −1.5, and the interaction between LR and LA approached significance when the LR threshold was varied from −1.25 to −1.75 while the LA threshold was held stable at −65 (P = 0.052 and P = 0.078 for LR thresholds of −1.25 and −1.75, respectively).

Regional models

No regional models for white matter, cortical, or subcortical metrics demonstrated a significant LA/LR interaction term. In terms of white matter, FA did not differ in any regions in terms of LR or LA (Supplementary Table S6). Given that cortical area was the cortical metric that demonstrated the largest number of statistically different regions between the LR and no LR conditions (Supplementary Tables S7 to S9), this metric was analyzed further, and spatial representations were generated (Fig. 2A) with areas separated as relating to LR, LA, and/or both conditions shown in Fig. 2B. Noteworthy relationships included lower bilateral entorhinal and parahippocampal cortical areas uniquely relating to LR (left entorhinal: β = −.035, t(8636) = −3.25, P = 0.001, pFDR = 0.003, 95% CI [−.056, −.014], r = −.035; right entorhinal: β = −.031, t(8616) = −2.90, P = 0.004, pFDR = 0.007, 95% CI [−.051, −.010], r = −.031; left parahippocampal: β = −.049, t(8503) = −4.54, P < 0.001, pFDR < 0.001, 95% CI [−.070, −.028], r = −.049; right parahippocampal: β = −.057, t(8491) = −5.44, P < 0.001, pFDR < 0.001, 95% CI [−.078, −.037], r = −.059). In LA, the bilateral cuneus, precuneus, and pericalcarine cortices appeared uniquely related (left cuneus: β = −.032, t(8606) = −3.16, P = 0.002, pFDR = 0.006, 95% CI [−.052, −.012], r = −.034; right cuneus: β = −.022, t(8634) = −2.21, P = 0.027, pFDR = 0.040, 95% CI [−.042, −.003], r = −.024; left precuneus: β = −.035, t(8646) = −3.59, P < 0.001, pFDR = 0.002, 95% CI [−.054, −.016], r = −.039; right precuneus: β = −.030, t(8636) = −3.12, P = 0.002, pFDR = 0.006, 95% CI [−.048, −.011], r = −.034; left pericalcarine: β = −.030, t(8565) = −2.86, P = 0.004, pFDR = 0.011, 95% CI [−.050, −.009], r = −.031, right pericalcarine: β = −.029, t(8554) = −2.77, P = 0.006, pFDR = 0.012, 95% CI [−.049, −.009], r = −.030). Overall, of the 68 Desikan-Killiany regions assessed, 50% (34) were significantly related to both ADHD and LR, with as many as 70% of the significant regions shared in the frontal lobes (14) (Fig. 2B). Finally, subcortical volumes, including subcortical white matter volume, were lower in both LR and ADHD in nearly all regions assessed (Supplementary Table S10).

Sensitivity to global cortical area

When total cortical area was included as a covariate in regional models, no regions remained significantly associated with LA after FDR correction, while only two regions, the right parahippocampal gyrus (β = −.034, t(8524) = −3.53, P < 0.001, pFDR = 0.029, 95% CI [−.052, −.015], r = −.038) and the right precuneus (β = 0.023, t(8583) = 3.24, P = 0.001, pFDR = 0.041, 95% CI [.009, 0.036], r = 0.035), retained significant associations with LR.

Examination of the total-area covariate itself revealed that the standardized β varied substantially across regions (range: 0.29–0.84; median: 0.65), indicating non-uniform scaling (Supplementary Figs. S1B to C; Supplementary Table S11). Regions with the strongest scaling relationships were predominantly frontal and temporal (eg superior frontal: β = 0.84; superior temporal: β = 0.83), whereas medial temporal regions showed the weakest relationships (eg entorhinal: β = 0.29 – 0.31). The proportion of total cortical area accounted for by each region was positively correlated with the magnitude of the total-area covariate β (r2 = 0.41; Supplementary Fig. S1C), indicating that larger regions, such as many regions within the frontal cortex, showed disproportionately stronger scaling with total cortical area.

Vertex-wise whole-brain confirmation of results

To ensure that findings in Fig. 2 were not related to parcellation averaging, LMEs were recomputed using the FEMA software for a vertex-based analysis of associations between LA and LR with cortical area (Fig. 3A) (Parekh et al. 2024). Overall, the unadjusted vertex-wise pattern of results appeared similar to that of our regional models in Fig. 2, with both LA and LR showing widespread relationships with cortical area, particularly in lateral temporal and frontal regions (Fig. 3B, left panel; ADHD: 7,521 of 20,484 vertices at |z| > 1.96; LR: 9,222 vertices; both conditions: 4,363 vertices. Participants with LA demonstrated lower cortical areas in the bilateral lateral orbitofrontal cortices and right rostral middle frontal gyrus, for example, as well as in superior temporal gyri. Participants with LR had lower cortical areas in the bilateral ventral temporal lobes, including the fusiform gyri, the inferior parietal lobes, and, like LA, in the bilateral lateral orbitofrontal cortices (Fig. 3B, right panel).

When total cortical area was included as a covariate in the vertex-based models (Supplementary Fig. S1D), the number of vertices with |z| > 1.96 decreased (ADHD: 835; LR: 1581), and the shared overlap was reduced (from 4,363 to 65 vertices). Qualitative inspection of the adjusted vertex maps (Supplementary Fig. S1D) revealed that vertices with |z| > 1.96 persisted for both predictors in bilateral lateral orbitofrontal cortex, overlapping spatially with the frontal predominant pattern observed in the unadjusted analysis.

In addition, surviving LR-associated effects after adjusting for total cortical area were concentrated in the ventral temporal lobes including the left greater than right fusiform gyrus (Supplementary Fig. S1D). These observations suggest that while the global surface area adjustment substantially attenuates the overall signal, the spatial distribution of residual effects in a priori regions of interest is consistent with the unadjusted findings.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the relationships among white matter tractometry, cortical parcellations, and subcortical volumes with LA and LR, the former a behavioral surrogate for ADHD and the latter a behavioral surrogate for dyslexia. In a large, demographically diverse sample of almost 9,000 children aged 9 to 11 with a substantial number of control variables, we demonstrate that (i) average global white matter FA is lower in those with co-occurring LA and LR, and (ii) a number of overlapping brain regions demonstrate lower cortical area in LA and LR independently, which we discover are driven by whole-brain differences in total cortical area. As we will discuss, these findings primarily lend support, on a neurostructural level, for the shared susceptibility hypotheses, though further investigation is needed to account for the global decrease in average white matter FA.

Having found an association between co-occurring LA and LR with average FA, it seems surprising that we do not observe differences in tract-specific white matter microstructure (as measured by FA) in LR, LA, or co-occurring LA and LR. Extrapolating from these behavioral surrogates, it is even more surprising given that there are purported associations between white matter integrity in multiple tracts and ADHD (Onnink et al. 2015; Parlatini et al. 2023; Slaby et al. 2023), dyslexia (Vanderauwera et al. 2017; Slaby et al. 2023), and possibly in a distinct co-occurring ADHD and dyslexia condition (Slaby et al. 2023). It is possible that the dynamic relationship between reading and white matter integrity may obscure subtle tract-wise differences in those with LR. For example, Yeatman et al. (2012) note that in individuals with developmental dyslexia, FA is higher in the left AF and inferior longitudinal fasciculus at baseline compared with typically developing individuals, although this trend reverses over time (Yeatman et al. 2012). Further, Roy et al. (2024) find that changes in reading ability can predict changes in white matter integrity of the left AF (Roy et al. 2024). Therefore, differences in FA within specific white matter tracts in individuals with LR may be difficult to detect at a single time point, and a longitudinal analysis may be necessary to identify them. While numerous white matter tracts have been implicated in ADHD, a meta-analysis of studies examining tract-based spatial statistics in those with and without ADHD found no relationships between white matter integrity and ADHD when considering only “high quality” studies that utilize a large number of directions, a high b-value, and control for motion, among other factors (Parlatini et al. 2023). Our findings are consistent with this result, given that the ABCD dataset utilizes high b-value imaging with many directions (96 directions) (Casey et al. 2018) and that our FA linear models control for patient motion.

That we found global FA to be uniquely lower in combined LA and LR is consistent with there being a unique co-occurring ADHD and dyslexia phenotype. While global FA is known to relate to age in early development (Lebel et al. 2019), we account for age in our linear model, making age differences an unlikely exclusive explanation for this result. In a cohort of individuals ranging from ages 8-30, both performance IQ, which correlates with executive function in this age group (Riccio et al. 1994), and verbal IQ have been shown to correlate with FA across ~1.6% and 4.6% of the white matter skeleton, respectively (Tamnes et al. 2010). Elevated white matter integrity has also been shown to correlate with improved executive function in those with innate propensities for ADHD or reading difficulty (Ferrer et al. 2013). Given that executive function has been found to be impaired in co-occurring ADHD and dyslexia and reading disorder (Bental and Tirosh 2007; Lonergan et al. 2019), it is possible that decreased global FA in LA and LR reflects a non-additive decrease in executive function in this group.

Numerous overlapping temporal and frontal regions were associated with lower cortical areas in both LA and LR. While much of this pattern was driven by a shared global reduction in total cortical area, vertex-wise analyses controlling for total cortical area confirmed persistent high associations between cortical area and both LA and LR in the bilateral orbitofrontal cortices. Meanwhile, ventral temporal cortex (eg fusiform gyri) demonstrated unique associations with LR and not with LA. This latter finding is consistent with extensive prior research showing structural and functional changes in dyslexic compared with typical readers (Salmelin et al. 1996; Paulesu et al. 2001; Frye et al. 2010; Yeatman et al. 2011; Brem et al. 2020).

The persistence of reduced cortical area in bilateral orbitofrontal cortices is consistent with prior reports of decreased surface area and volumes in orbitofrontal regions in those with ADHD (Ambrosino et al. 2017; Jagger-Rickels et al. 2018), though notably Jagger-Rickels et al. found that individuals with a comorbid reading disability did not demonstrate an additional decrease in orbitofrontal volume. In contrast, others have found structural differences in orbitofrontal regions in those with dyslexia, with one group finding these individuals to have decreased cortical thickness in the right orbitofrontal cortex (Williams et al. 2018). Cognitively, the orbitofrontal cortex has a role in executive function and has been implicated in encoding differences from expectation. With reading, this might include increased activation when presented with unpredictable low frequency words (Hofmann et al. 2014) and with ADHD, this might include an inability to assign correct values to outcomes of actions (Tegelbeckers et al. 2018). Thus, it is possible that this region contributes to the known shared executive deficit and an underlying shared susceptibility to these two conditions.

Taken together, these supplementary analyses suggest that the overlapping cortical area reductions reported for LA and LR in the bilateral orbitofrontal cortices reflect a combination of globally distributed scaling and genuine region-specific contributions. The vertex-wise adjusted maps provided the most direct evidence that the shared orbitofrontal pattern was spatially coherent and reproducible across predictors. These findings, extrapolated from LR and LA to clinical diagnoses of dyslexia and ADHD, respectively, lend support to the shared susceptibility hypothesis. Also consistent with this hypothesis is the fact that there are numerous cognitive (Kibby et al. 2019; Lonergan et al. 2019; Kibby et al. 2020; Dahhan et al. 2022; Horowitz-Kraus et al. 2023) and genetic (Ciulkinyte et al. 2025) factors shared by these disorders. However, our finding that global white matter integrity is uniquely lower in co-occurring LA and LR is more consistent with the notion of a unique phenotype. While speculative due to the cross-sectional nature of this study, it is possible that a shared genetic susceptibility is the primary driver of initial cognitive and cortical differences in individuals with ADHD and dyslexia. Subsequently, a lower reading propensity in those with both LA and LR may produce a non-linear alteration in white matter microstructure. Individuals with ADHD and dyslexia read more slowly (Stagg and Kiss 2021; Stern et al. 2024). Therefore, although we do not observe an additive decrease in the amount of time spent reading in individuals with co-occurring LR and LA (Table 1), “time spent reading” likely does not accurately capture the quantity of material that individuals with LR, LA, or both have read. Investigating the dynamic changes in white matter that occur following the development of reading habits could be an area for further investigation with appropriate longitudinal data.

Limitations

In addition to the cross-sectional design, several important limitations apply to this study. First, LR, as denoted by education-level-adjusted TORRT scores 1.5 standard deviations below the mean, is not synonymous with dyslexia, and instead serves as a behavioral proxy. We attempt to mitigate this limitation by including in our models a surrogate for intelligence, the matrix reasoning score. Likewise, LA serves as a behavioral surrogate for ADHD, a diagnosis that requires a clinical diagnosis. Though more granular, continuous cognitive data pertaining to word recognition, phonology, attention, and different domains of executive function and processing speed, in conjunction with clinical diagnoses of ADHD and dyslexia, could yield more precise inferences, such data are not all widely available in the ABCD dataset.

Furthermore, we use thresholds to distinguish between LA and LR, rather than relying on continuous variables. Although conducted in a principled manner to enhance the interpretability of our results, this approach may limit the power of our analysis. Additionally, utilizing these thresholds results in a relatively small number of individuals with co-occurring ADHD and LR (n = 63) relative to the overall population of our study (~0.7%). However, the selected thresholds are supported by the existing literature, and we aimed to prioritize the specificity of our diagnostic categories over sensitivity. Further, we support our findings with a sensitivity analysis to assess the impact of altering the thresholds we use and with bootstrap and leave-one-out analyses to assess the robustness of our findings.

Conclusions

In this study, we use cross-sectional imaging, neurocognitive, and demographic data from nearly 9,000 early adolescents in the ABCD cohort to conduct the largest investigation to date of the neurostructural underpinnings of co-occurring ADHD and dyslexia by using the respective behavioral correlates of LA and LR. We find that both behavioral correlates are independently associated with globally reduced cortical area, and that overlapping reductions in bilateral orbitofrontal cortices persist in vertex-wise maps even after adjustment for total cortical area. Additionally, we find evidence that white matter microstructure is uniquely reduced in co-occurring LA and LR. While our results most favor the common susceptibility hypothesis, future longitudinal investigations are needed to explore dynamic changes of white matter with respect to reading in co-occurring ADHD and LR.
