Multimorbidity in India: an analysis of prevalence and patterns of chronicity of diseases among older adults using Study on global AGEing and adult health (SAGE) Wave 2
Authors: Ananya Anurakta Pattanaik, Rikat ul ain Hashmi, Rutuparna Sibani Dandsena, Smiteerekha Sahoo, Priyanka Verma, Shishirendu Ghosal, Sushmita Kerketta, Srikanta Kanungo, Sanghamitra Pati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMultimorbidity, the coexistence of two or more chronic non-communicable diseases, poses a significant health challenge in India, particularly among older adults. Factors such as population ageing, lifestyle changes and urbanisation have led to an increasing burden of chronic diseases.MethodsData from the Study on Global Ageing and Adult Health Wave 2, which surveyed 11 818 individuals in India, were utilised, focusing on those aged 50 and above. The analysis included 7118 participants after excluding those with missing or incorrect data. The prevalence and patterns of multimorbidity were assessed by examining the co-occurrence of eight chronic conditions: arthritis, stroke, angina, diabetes, chronic lung disease, asthma, depression and hypertension. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA software to determine the frequency and chronicity of these conditions.ResultsThe prevalence of multimorbidity was 45.14%, with hypertension (22%) and arthritis (19%) being the most common conditions. Hypertension also showed the highest chronicity, often being the first diagnosed condition. Significant patterns of dyads and triads were identified, with hypertension frequently co-occurring with other chronic conditions.DiscussionThis study provides a comprehensive analysis of multimorbidity among older adults in India, revealing a high prevalence of conditions like hypertension and arthritis. The findings highlight the substantial burden of multimorbidity, with significant implications for healthcare management. The study underscores the need for integrated, patient-centred care models to manage multimorbidity effectively. These insights are crucial for developing targeted interventions and health policies aimed at improving care coordination and outcomes for individuals with multiple chronic conditions in India.Keywords: Comorbidity, Cardiovascular Diseases, Public Health

Introduction

Multimorbidity, the coexistence of two or more chronic non-communicable diseases (NCDs), has become a significant health challenge globally. In recent decades, developed nations with ageing populations have seen an increase in chronic illnesses. About 37.2% of adults globally suffer from multimorbidity, with South America having the highest prevalence (45.7%).1 The prevalence of multimorbidity rises significantly with age, from 48.31% in those aged 45 and up to 73.86% in those over 75 years old.2 India is following suit, with a rapid rise in the burden of multimorbidity, owing mostly to greater life expectancy and increased exposure to chronic disease risk factors. Chronicity of diseases, which refers to the duration or persistence of a disease or health condition over time, plays a crucial role in understanding the burden of multimorbidity. In India, chronicity—the prolonged presence of chronic disease—is attributed to various interconnected factors such as lifestyle changes, urbanisation, sedentary habits, dietary modifications, tobacco and alcohol consumption, air pollution and an ageing population. These factors have contributed to a rise in risk factors like obesity, hypertension, high cholesterol levels and stress, thereby increasing the burden of NCDs.3

According to the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India 2017–2018, more than 75 million people over the age of 60 have at least one chronic disease, with 27% having multimorbidity.4 In another study, the common combinations of multimorbidities among older persons are hypertension with obesity (2.9%), hypertension with anaemia (2.2%) and obesity with anaemia (1.2%).5 Heart disease is also more common in people who have hypertension, diabetes, high cholesterol or have had a stroke.6 Among older persons, gender differences are also obvious, with male older persons having greater rates of chronic heart disease, chronic lung disease and diabetes, whereas females had higher rates of hypertension.7

Multimorbidity does not have a universally agreed-upon definition, unlike comorbidity. At its simplest, multimorbidity is characterised by the presence of at least two chronic conditions in an individual.8 9 It leads to increased healthcare utilisation, decreased quality of life, higher mortality rates and increased healthcare costs.3 10 11 Hospitalisation data show that communicable diseases account for 38% of hospitalisations among older individuals, while NCDs account for 52%.12 Regional discrepancies also occur; non-Empowered Action Group states have approximately double the risk of chronic illness as Empowered Action Group states.13 There is a 16.3% greater prevalence of multimorbidity among urban older individuals compared with those in rural areas.14 Demographic and social factors also influence multimorbidity. Older persons who are middle-aged, elderly, economically dependent, unemployed, physically immobile, chronically ill, Muslim or from the Eastern region are more likely to indicate poor self-reported health.15 Nearly 30% of older persons report at least one chronic disease, 16% have two, 6% have three and 2.4% have four or more chronic disorders.16

Managing multimorbidity requires a multidisciplinary approach. WHO recommends a person-centred care that considers an individual’s preferences, needs and values.17 For this, it is essential that there is collaboration between healthcare providers from different specialties for coordinated care.18 Also, management in primary care settings is complex due to the need to address multiple conditions simultaneously. This is complicated by healthcare systems often being organised around a ‘single disease’ model, making comprehensive care difficult.19,21 India has shifted from infectious to NCDs as the leading cause of morbidity and mortality,22 23 yet gaps remain in understanding chronicity among the ageing population. This study aims to address this gap by analysing dyad and triad combinations of chronic conditions, providing a complete description of chronicity and multimorbidity patterns. Such insight will help in designing targeted interventions and health promotion programmes.

Methods

Overview of data

This study utilised data from the WHO’s Study on Global AGEing and Adult Health (SAGE) Wave 2 (2014–2015), a multicountry longitudinal survey on ageing, health and well-being in low-income and middle-income countries, including India.24 SAGE focuses on individuals aged 50 and older, with a smaller comparison group aged 18 to 49.

The survey employed a stratified, multistage cluster sampling design for national representation and collected data on demographics, health status, chronic conditions, functional limitations and social determinants of health. The chronic conditions reported in this survey are arthritis, diabetes, hypertension, asthma, stroke, chronic lung disease, angina and depression. Thus, we have considered these eight chronic illnesses in our study. Ethical approval was obtained from the WHO Ethical Review Committee and national ethics bodies, with informed consent from all participants. Further details on the survey are available at WHO-SAGE India—Wave 2 report.25

As shown in figure 1, the final sample included 7118 individuals aged 50 and above, after excluding those under 50 and participants with incomplete or invalid data.

Variables

Sociodemographic characteristics

The analysis considered various sociodemographic characteristics: gender (male, female), residence (rural, urban), ethnic group (scheduled tribe, scheduled caste, other backward class, general, others), education level (from ‘no formal education’ to ‘postgraduate’), occupation (never worked, ever worked), partner status (currently married/live-in, separated/divorced/widowed/never married), wealth index quintiles (‘poorest’ to ‘richest’) and age groups in years (50–59, 60–69, 70–79, 80 and above), smoking (yes/no), vigorous physical activity (yes/no), alcohol consumption (yes/no).

Outcome

The primary outcome variable of our study is multimorbidity, defined as the concurrent presence of two or more chronic conditions within an individual, where one is not necessarily more central than others.26 We assessed eight self-reported chronic conditions: arthritis, stroke, angina, diabetes mellitus, chronic lung disease, asthma, depression and hypertension as per the SAGE Wave 2 dataset. But it should be noted that the definition of multimorbidity encompasses all other chronic conditions apart from these eight as well. Pattern analysis was performed to identify dyads (pairs of diseases) and triads (sets of three diseases), helping us explore multimorbidity patterns within the sample.

Another outcome variable is chronicity of a disease, which refers to the characteristic of that disease lasting for an extended period, often persisting for months or years. To assess this, we used data from the SAGE Wave 2 India dataset, asking participants, ‘How long ago were you diagnosed with this disease?’ Their responses, provided in years, were used to determine the onset of each chronic disease. We calculated the age at which each participant was diagnosed by subtracting the number of years since diagnosis from their current age. This process was repeated separately for each of the eight chronic diseases included in the study.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was performed using STATA V.17. Descriptive statistics (frequency and percentage) described the study population’s characteristics. To explore dyad and triad multimorbidity patterns, we calculated the frequency of each combination and 95% CIs to measure uncertainty for weighted proportions. We employed a matrix approach to analyse all possible combinations of chronic conditions with a frequency above 0.5%, allowing us to identify the most common dyad and triad patterns among participants.

To assess disease chronicity, we calculated the onset of each chronic disease by subtracting the years since diagnosis from the participant’s current age. Lower values indicated earlier diagnoses and greater chronicity, while higher values indicated later diagnoses and lower chronicity, helping identify more persistent diseases among older adults.

Online supplemental table 1 shows the prevalence of self-reported chronic illnesses by age, starting at 50 years. Overall, 45% report having at least one of eight chronic conditions, with prevalence increasing with age, peaking at 52% in those 80 and older. Hypertension is the most common condition (22%), most prevalent in the 70–79 age group (25%), followed by arthritis (19%). Chronic lung disease is the least common, at 2%.

Online supplemental table 2 shows the prevalence of the sequence of diagnosis of the most prevalent dyads. For the most prevalent dyad, which consists of arthritis and hypertension, 1.29% of the population was diagnosed with arthritis first, followed by hypertension, while 1.09% of the study population were diagnosed with hypertension first, followed by arthritis. The next most prevalent dyad is diabetes and hypertension, in which 0.54% of the study population is diagnosed with diabetes first, followed by hypertension.

Online supplemental table 3 shows the prevalence of dyads that have two chronic conditions diagnosed in the same year or at the same time. 0.86% of the study population was reportedly diagnosed with arthritis and hypertension in the same year, with none of them succeeding the other in occurrence. Hypertension and diabetes were diagnosed in the same year in 1.09% of the study population. Online supplemental table 4 presents the statistical distribution of age of diagnosis for chronic diseases across different age groups. Arthritis is diagnosed at a mean age of 58 years, with a median of 59 years and an IQR of 51–66 years. Chronic lung disease has the lowest mean age of diagnosis at 56 years, and diabetes also has a median age of 56 years. Angina has the highest mean age of diagnosis at 60 years, while stroke is diagnosed at a median age of 61 years.

Online supplemental table 5 depicts the median age difference between diagnoses of two chronic diseases among the most prevalent dyads. For the most prevalent dyad, which is of arthritis and hypertension, if a person is diagnosed with arthritis first, then the median time duration after which hypertension is diagnosed is 4 years (IQR 2–10) while it is 3 years (IQR 1–7) if the sequence of their diagnoses is the other way around. The median age gap between the dyad diabetes and hypertension is 3 years no matter which of the two is diagnosed before or after.

Online supplemental table 6 shows the most prevalent dyads (two chronic conditions occurring together) and triads (three conditions occurring together) in people aged 50 years or above. The most prevalent dyad is arthritis and hypertension (3.19%, 95%CI 2.79% to 3.62%). There are five more such dyads with a prevalence of or more than 0.5%. The most prevalent triad is of arthritis, diabetes and hypertension (1.18%, 95% CI 0.94% to 1.45%), and it is the only triad with a prevalence of more than 1%, followed by a triad of arthritis, angina and hypertension (0.29%, 95% CI 0.18% to 0.45%).

Discussion

The results of our study indicate a significant burden of chronic diseases, with approximately 45% of the population reporting the presence of at least one chronic condition. This burden increases with age, reaching its peak among individuals aged 80 years (52%) and older. Hypertension emerged as the most prevalent chronic disease, affecting 22% of the population across all age groups, followed by arthritis at 19%. The findings of this study align with the Seventh Report of the Joint National Committee on Prevention, Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Pressure, which states that hypertension occurs in more than two-thirds of individuals after the age of 65.27 The chronicity of hypertension is noteworthy in our study, as it is often the first-diagnosed condition in the majority (16%) of individuals. Age-related physiological changes, such as arterial stiffening and neurohormonal dysregulation, likely contribute to the early onset, progression and chronicity of hypertension in older adults.28 Conversely, chronic lung disease presents a lower prevalence and chronicity, potentially due to several factors. First, underdiagnosis due to atypical clinical presentations, limited use of spirometry and lack of clinical suspicion in primary care settings can lead to the condition being overlooked in this population.29 30 Chronic respiratory diseases in low-resource settings in India are often poorly diagnosed, leading to missed opportunities for early initiation of treatment and suboptimal patient pathways.31 This observation aligns with insights from supplementary articles, which emphasise the diagnostic challenges and disparities in respiratory healthcare among the elderly in India.32 The underestimation of chronic lung disease underscores the necessity for improved awareness, diagnostic tools and clinical suspicion in primary care settings to accurately identify and manage respiratory conditions. The concept of chronicity provides valuable insights into the longitudinal burden of chronic diseases. Particularly, hypertension, often regarded as a silent killer, exhibited a notable chronicity, with a median time duration of 4 years from the diagnosis of arthritis to the diagnosis of hypertension, and 3 years in the reverse sequence. Hypertension is relatively straightforward to diagnose and detect due to the simplicity of measuring blood pressure and the availability of effective treatment options. This may account for its status as the first disease to be diagnosed in the majority of the population. The inclusion of Blood Pressure measurement in the vital measurements during any medical examination also contributes to early diagnosis and hence chronicity. The co-occurrence of chronic conditions, particularly hypertension, arthritis and diabetes, underscores the complex interplay of shared pathophysiological mechanisms. Hypertension is commonly associated with other diseases due to its high prevalence, which can lead to its frequent co-occurrence with various conditions in multimorbidity patterns. In our study, the most prevalent dyad was arthritis and hypertension, affecting 3.19% of the population, followed by hypertension and diabetes, affecting 2.21%. These findings are consistent with the existing literature, highlighting the clustering of chronic diseases in multimorbid individuals.33 34 Hypertension and arthritis frequently occur together, with one potential explanation being the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs for managing arthritis pain, which can elevate blood pressure.35 Research conducted by Liang36 reinforces this association, demonstrating a notable correlation between hypertension and types of arthritis such as rheumatoid arthritis and osteoarthritis.36 This underscores the necessity of regular hypertension screening and effective management in individuals with arthritis. Shared risk factors between hypertension, arthritis and diabetes, such as obesity, inflammation and oxidative stress, contribute to the clustering of these conditions, emphasising the need for comprehensive management approaches that address multiple health concerns simultaneously. Regarding healthcare delivery and policy implications, there is a need for integrated and patient-centred care models to address the complex needs of multimorbid individuals. Furthermore, digital health and telemedicine can enhance access to care and self-management for multimorbid individuals. Developing evidence-based guidelines and evaluating interventions targeting risk factors and patient preferences are crucial to addressing the burden of multimorbidity. Future research should focus on understanding the mechanisms of multimorbidity and identifying effective prevention and management strategies across diverse populations. This includes evaluating various strategies such as medicine management interventions, interventions targeting health behaviours like exercise and diet and identifying and treating depression.37 Patient adherence is often compromised when taking multiple medications, for which pillbox maintenance could be of significant utility and could greatly enhance medication adherence and management, especially for elderly individuals with multimorbidity.38 Our study sheds light on a few important steps that need to be taken for better management of NCDs in India. Focusing more on the primordial and primary modes of prevention for hypertension is essential, as it is the most prevalent condition. After diagnosis of hypertension, the next 3–4 years are extremely crucial for the patient, as there is a risk of developing arthritis and vice versa. So, in those 3–4 years, all the patients diagnosed with hypertension or arthritis should be regularly monitored, and additional preventive measures should be applied for them. Also, as arthritis, hypertension and diabetes were found to frequently co-occur, special programmes should be conducted targeting these three conditions specifically. The programmes should include joint screening and management practices and point-of-care facilities for the benefit of the patients. By addressing these challenges and implementing these proposed solutions, policymakers can work towards improving the health outcomes of individuals with multimorbidity and ensuring a continuum of care across the lifespan.

Strengths

This study is novel as it is the first to systematically investigate the chronicity (persistence over time) of the most prevalent NCDs in India and to determine the sequence of diagnosis within common disease dyads. Such evidence is critical for preventing or delaying the onset of multimorbidity through timely behavioural modification and early clinical interventions.

We deliberately utilised data from SAGE Wave 2 to address specific research questions on chronicity, diagnosis sequence and multimorbidity patterns in the Indian context. These findings have direct relevance for both clinical practice and public health policy, as understanding disease sequences can help predict disease progression and inform preventive strategies for populations at risk.

Importantly, the observed 3–4-year interval between these diagnoses (online supplemental table 5) represents a critical window for early detection and targeted intervention. For instance, individuals with arthritis may benefit from more frequent blood pressure monitoring, whereas those with hypertension should undergo early diabetes risk assessment and receive guidance on lifestyle modifications.

While a prior multicountry SAGE Wave 1 analysis identified hypertension–arthritis and hypertension–angina as the most prevalent dyads in middle-income countries,38 our India-specific Wave 2 analysis confirmed the prominence of hypertension–arthritis but identified hypertension–diabetes, rather than angina, as the second most common dyad. This finding underscores India’s unique and increasing diabetes burden.39

Furthermore, our study highlights the importance of determining the age of onset of chronic conditions to inform early detection strategies. By examining dyads and triads of frequently co-occurring conditions, we provide valuable insights into multimorbidity patterns that can inform healthcare planning. Recognising these disease clusters can enable national health programmes to target interventions more effectively.

Limitations

The NCDs and chronic conditions are limited to eight diseases, namely arthritis, diabetes, hypertension, asthma, stroke, chronic lung disease, angina and depression since SAGE Wave 2 dataset does not provide data on other chronic illnesses like cervical cancer or gastrointestinal disorders. Since SAGE Wave 1 dataset is available, a longitudinal analysis would be advantageous, but it has not been performed in this study. We focused on SAGE Wave 2 data only to address specific research questions and objectives. The reliance on self-reported information might lead to recall bias, as participants might not accurately remember the onset or duration of their conditions. Also, due to study’s cross-sectional nature, its ability to establish causal relationships between diseases and their progression over time is limited, which could have provided deeper insights into the dynamics of multimorbidity.

Conclusion

This study sheds light on the complex landscape of chronic diseases among India’s ageing population, with a shift from infectious to NCDs necessitating healthcare system adaptation. Diagnoses often occur in the early 60s, providing insights for targeted interventions. Arthritis and hypertension are particularly prevalent, emphasising the need to focus on musculoskeletal health and blood pressure management. Understanding the sequence of chronic disease diagnoses and common multimorbidity patterns calls for a holistic healthcare approach. These findings enable the development of focused, resource-efficient healthcare strategies that promote early diagnosis, integrated disease management and patient-centred care. While a longitudinal study considering SAGE Wave 1 data and SAGE Wave 2 data together could be performed in the future, which would offer added depth, our cross-sectional insights provide crucial evidence to inform interventions tailored to India’s distinct NCD burden, particularly the central role of diabetes in multimorbidity.

Thus, a multidisciplinary approach that considers age-related patterns and the complexities of multimorbidity is crucial to improving the quality of life for older adults facing the challenges of chronic diseases in India.
