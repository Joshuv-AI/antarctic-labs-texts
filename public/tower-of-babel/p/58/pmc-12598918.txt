Tolvaptan versus other interventions for hyponatraemia in people with cirrhosis
Authors: K N Anila, Nair S Saraswathy, Zubair U Mohamed, Christi Titus Varghese, S T Binoj, Unnikrishnan Gopalakrishnan, Dinesh Balakrishnan, Arun Valsan, Surendran Sudhindran
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis is a protocol for a Cochrane Review (intervention). The objectives are as follows:To evaluate the benefits and harms of tolvaptan versus standard medical management, placebo, or other vaptans for the treatment of hyponatraemia in people with cirrhosis.

Background

Solute‐free water excess in people with advanced cirrhosis can cause ascites or oedema and lead to electrolyte abnormalities [1]. Using diuretics to treat ascites or oedema might make hyponatraemia (a low serum sodium level) worse, and hyponatraemia is the most common electrolyte problem in chronic liver disease (CLD). Hyponatraemia is an independent risk factor for hepatic encephalopathy and other adverse outcomes, including mortality [2]. Literature has reported that the one‐year mortality rate in hyponatraemic people with cirrhosis can range between 30% and 60%, depending on the severity of hyponatraemia and associated complications [3, 4]. Reduction or discontinuation of diuretics and laxatives, along with the administration of hyperoncotic albumin, are considered key management strategies for the management of hyponatraemia in people with cirrhosis [5]. Hypertonic saline is not recommended, as it may worsen the ascites, aggravate circulatory load, and even cause osmotic demyelination syndrome [6, 7]. A pharmacological strategy facilitates free‐water loss using vasopressin receptor antagonists, such as vaptans [2].

Description of the condition

Cirrhosis is the end stage of chronic liver disease, which is defined as a diffuse hepatic process characterised by fibrosis and the conversion of normal liver architecture into structurally abnormal nodules. The clinical course is variable, but people typically progress from a compensated phase to a decompensated phase. Decompensation is defined by the development of complications such as ascites, variceal haemorrhage, hepatic encephalopathy, or jaundice [2]. Cirrhosis commonly results from chronic viral hepatitis (B and C), excessive alcohol use, metabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and autoimmune hepatitis. Progressive scarring impairs liver function and leads to serious complications, including portal hypertension, ascites, hepatic encephalopathy, and increased infection risk [8]. Notably, hyponatraemia, defined as a serum sodium concentration of less than 130 mEq/L, is a frequent and serious complication in people with cirrhosis, particularly those with ascites or oedema [2]. While both hypovolaemic and hypervolaemic forms can be seen, euvolaemic or hypervolaemic dilutional hyponatraemia is most common in advanced cirrhosis, driven by a complex pathophysiological process. This involves portal hypertension triggering systemic and splanchnic vasodilatation due to mediators like nitric oxide and prostaglandins [9]. This reduces the ‘effective arterial blood volume’, thereby activating the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system (RAAS) and the release of arginine vasopressin (AVP), which leads to sodium and water retention [2, 10, 11]. The resulting intravascular volume expansion and hypoalbuminaemia cause fluid to shift into the peritoneal cavity (ascites) and interstitial spaces (oedema). As cirrhosis progresses, resistance to blood flow increases within the liver, leading to portal hypertension, which causes impaired reticuloendothelial function and increased intestinal permeability [12]. The impaired defence barrier within the intestinal lumen results in bacterial translocation, endotoxaemia, and the release of vasodilators, such as nitric oxide and prostaglandins, within the splanchnic circulation [9]. Ascites is not just a marker of fluid overload but is associated with increased mortality, with complications such as spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) and hepatorenal syndrome (HRS), which significantly worsen prognosis [13, 14].

The treatment of ascites and oedema in cirrhosis includes the use of diuretics such as spironolactone and furosemide. These drugs promote natriuresis and fluid loss by inhibiting sodium reabsorption in the renal tubules, thereby reducing extracellular fluid volume. Not all patients tolerate high doses of diuretics because of the adverse effects, including hyponatraemia, hypotension, hyperkalaemia (from spironolactone), and hypokalaemia (from furosemide) [2]. Hyponatraemia in this setting is multifactorial and can occur due to the non‐osmotic release of AVP in response to perceived hypovolaemia, which leads to water retention and dilution of serum sodium levels, as well as the adverse effects of diuretic use [10].

For people who do not tolerate conventional diuretics or have persistent hyponatraemia, vasopressin V2‐receptor antagonists may potentially become a beneficial alternative [2]. By promoting free‐water excretion without significant sodium loss, vaptans help correct dilutional hyponatraemia while aiding in fluid management. Conivaptan, lixivaptan, satavaptan, mozavaptan, and tolvaptan are all agents belonging to the class of vasopressin receptor antagonists [15].

Description of the intervention and how it might work

Vaptans are potent vasopressin 2 (V2) receptor antagonists with aquaretic properties and minimal effect on sodium and potassium excretion [16]. Tolvaptan, conivaptan, lixivaptan, satavaptan, and mozavaptan are all vasopressin receptor antagonists that block the action of vasopressin at specific receptors (V1a and V2 for conivaptan; primarily V2 for lixivaptan, satavaptan, mozavaptan, and tolvaptan) to increase water excretion and raise serum sodium levels [17]. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has approved tolvaptan for hyponatraemia correction in conditions such as congestive heart failure (CHF) and syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone (SIADH) [18], but has not approved it for people with liver diseases [19]. Conivaptan has limited applicability in cirrhosis because of its risk of hypotension and its restriction to intravenous administration. Lixivaptan is an oral agent that was studied in early trials, but its development in cirrhosis was discontinued due to hepatotoxicity concerns. Satavaptan showed short‐term sodium correction in cirrhosis, but further development was halted due to adverse effects and lack of survival benefit. Mozavaptan was investigated as an oral option but has not progressed in cirrhosis owing to limited evidence and safety concerns [20]. However, tolvaptan is more commonly used in cirrhosis compared to the mentioned vaptans but is limited to short‐term use due to liver toxicity risk and high cost [15].

The typical starting dose of tolvaptan is 15 mg orally once daily, but it can be increased to 30 mg once daily after 24 hours to 48 hours if the initial response is inadequate [15]. Doses greater than 60 mg per day are not generally recommended due to the risk of adverse effects, e.g. too rapid sodium correction. A dose below 15 mg per day may not be sufficient to antagonise vasopressin effectively and is therefore not used in clinical practice [15].

Tolvaptan can commonly cause adverse effects like thirst, polydipsia, dehydration, and electrolyte imbalances (e.g. hypernatraemia, hyperkalaemia) due to its aquaretic action. Rare but serious adverse effects include hepatotoxicity (elevated liver enzymes, acute liver failure) due to potential idiosyncratic reactions or metabolic stress and rapid correction of hyponatraemia, potentially leading to neurological complications like osmotic demyelination [21]. Other reactions, such as headache, dizziness, gastrointestinal disturbances, and hypersensitivity (e.g. rash, anaphylaxis), result from fluid shifts, systemic dehydration, and immune responses. Regular monitoring of liver function, electrolytes, and gradual sodium correction is essential to minimise the risks [22, 23].

Vasopressin exerts its effects through three main receptor subtypes: V1a (predominantly located in the smooth muscle of blood vessels, liver, and platelets), V1b (primarily found in the pituitary gland), and V2 (predominantly located in the kidneys) [24]. The binding of vasopressin to V2 receptors triggers the insertion of aquaporin‐2 water channels into renal collecting duct cells, augmenting water reabsorption and concentrating urine [25], a key mechanism in fluid balance and osmolality regulation [25].

Tolvaptan, an oral V2 receptor antagonist, blocks this pathway, thereby reducing aquaporin‐2 expression, decreasing renal water reabsorption, and promoting aquaresis [16, 26, 27]. This corrects dilutional hyponatraemia and increases serum sodium [28, 29]. Furthermore, tolvaptan has been shown to activate the nuclear factor erythropoietin‐2‐related factor 2/haeme oxygenase 1 (Nrf2/HO‐1) antioxidant pathway, which may provide added benefit in people with cirrhosis with concurrent kidney injury [30].

In people with hyponatraemia in cirrhosis, the therapeutic efficacy of tolvaptan is reflected by an increase in serum sodium concentration (by approximately 5 mEq/L or normalisation) [31]. Tolvaptan indirectly reduces ascites volume and peripheral oedema by improving sodium‐water balance and promoting effective aquaresis, thereby resulting in weight reduction [30].

Why it is important to do this review

Hyponatraemia is a common and serious complication in cirrhosis, associated with poor outcomes such as hepatic encephalopathy, increased morbidity, and reduced quality of life. Management options include fluid restriction, albumin infusion, hypertonic saline, and vaptans, with tolvaptan being the most extensively studied [32]. However, there are very few trials evaluating other vaptans (e.g. lixivaptan, satavaptan) for this condition, which limits comparative data across available pharmacologic options [15, 33, 34, 35].

Tolvaptan is approved for hyponatraemia in people with cirrhosis in Japan [7] and China [10], and for broader indications in hypervolaemic and euvolaemic hyponatraemia in the USA [36]. Its use in cirrhosis, however, is constrained in many countries due to concerns about hepatotoxicity and regulatory limitations, particularly in Europe, where it is generally restricted to clinical trials or considered off‐label [2, 22].

Although multiple trials and systematic reviews have assessed the efficacy of tolvaptan, most have focused on placebo‐controlled comparisons or single‐arm outcomes [15, 32, 37]. A comprehensive and up‐to‐date synthesis comparing tolvaptan with other available interventions for hyponatraemia in cirrhosis is lacking. Treatment practices vary widely across regions, and clinical guidelines remain inconsistent. Furthermore, existing reviews are now outdated, and more recent trials have not been fully integrated into comparative analyses [10, 37]. Some reviews also lack a robust evaluation of alternative strategies such as albumin, hypertonic saline, or other vaptans [10, 33, 38]. The SALT (Study of Ascending Levels of Tolvaptan in Hyponatraemia) trials, which were pivotal in the regulatory approval of tolvaptan, demonstrated significant short‐term improvements in serum sodium levels compared with placebo [15].

Given the ongoing unmet need for effective and safe management of hyponatraemia in cirrhosis, and the heterogeneity in vaptan use across countries, a comprehensive and up‐to‐date synthesis of all available vaptan data, including optimal dosing, duration, and safety, is essential to inform clinical practice and health policy.

Importantly, the clinical presentation and outcomes of cirrhosis and hyponatraemia may vary across ethnic groups, healthcare settings, and genetic backgrounds. Ethnic and genetic differences can influence disease progression and treatment response, while access to care and regional practice variation may impact prognosis [39].

Objectives

To evaluate the benefits and harms of tolvaptan versus standard medical management, placebo, or other vaptans for the treatment of hyponatraemia in people with cirrhosis.

Methods

We will follow the Methodological Expectations of Cochrane Intervention Reviews (MECIR) when conducting the review [40], and PRISMA 2020 for the reporting [41, 42].

Criteria for considering studies for this review

Types of studies

We will include only randomised clinical trials with a parallel‐group design that compare tolvaptan with standard medical management, placebo, or other vaptans in adults with hyponatraemia in cirrhosis. We will include trials irrespective of objectives, studied or reported outcomes, blinding, setting, language, size, duration of participants’ follow‐up, and publication status. If we identify cross‐over trials, we will only include data from the first trial period, i.e. before the cross‐over, to avoid the risk of carry‐over effects [43].

Cluster‐randomised trials, where groups of individuals are randomised to different interventions, are unlikely to have been performed with the topic of this review. We will not include quasi‐randomised studies as the allocation in these parallel‐arm studies is determined by an approximation of randomisation (e.g. allocation by participant ID number or day of birth) [43].

Types of participants

We will include trials where participants are 18 years or older, have cirrhosis (as defined by the trial authors; for details on the definition of cirrhosis, please see Description of the condition), have low sodium levels (as described in Description of the condition), and come from any background or setting. We will include participants irrespective of their body mass index (BMI), comorbidities, or medical history.

If trial reports include mixed populations, we will contact study authors to request published or unpublished data for the participants with hyponatraemia in cirrhosis. We will exclude trials if fewer than 10% of the randomised participants had hyponatraemia in cirrhosis or if relevant data for the subgroup cannot be obtained.

Types of interventions

In our review, we plan to do three separate comparisons: tolvaptan versus standard medical care, versus placebo, or versus other vaptans.

Outcome measures

We will include eligible trials irrespective of their planned or reported outcomes.

For our primary meta‐analyses and conclusions, we will use the data reported at the maximal follow‐up, i.e. from the start of treatment to the end of the follow‐up period. However, we will extract data on the three critical outcomes as well as on the adverse events considered non‐serious (see Important outcomes) at the reported follow‐up times for each outcome. We will group the follow‐up times for the outcomes into short term (from the start of treatment up to three months), medium term (from more than three months up to six months), and long term (more than six months). If data permit, we will meta‐analyse the outcomes for each defined interval.

We will measure serum sodium concentration, body weight, and urine output, based on mean difference (MD) from baseline (change score), as reported in the individual trials.

Search methods for identification of studies

We will search for all published and unpublished randomised clinical trials of tolvaptan for hyponatraemia in people with cirrhosis, without language or date restriction. To minimise bias in our search results, we will follow the guidance in Chapter 4 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and in PRISMA‐S on planning and describing the search process for the review [54, 55]. The Cochrane Hepato‐Biliary Group Information Specialist will develop the search strategies and perform the electronic searches.

Electronic searches

We will search for randomised clinical trials in the Cochrane Hepato‐Biliary Group (CHBG) Controlled Trials Register via the Cochrane Register of Studies Web, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials in the Cochrane Library, MEDLINE ALL Ovid, Embase Ovid (Excerpta Medica Database), LILACS (Latin American and Caribbean Health Science Information database; Virtual Health Library (VHL) Regional Portal), Science Citation Index Expanded, and Conference Proceedings Citation Index ‐ Science. We will search for trials in the latter two databases simultaneously through the Web of Science.

We will use the Cochrane Highly Sensitive Search Strategies for identifying randomised trials in MEDLINE and Embase, modified to include relevant search terms for identifying retraction statements and errata, as recommended in Chapter 4 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [54, 56].

Supplementary material 1 shows the preliminary search strategies with the expected date range of the searches. We will provide the actual date of the electronic searches at the review stage.

Searching other resources

As appropriate, we will search for trials in trial registers and repositories of results stored on ClinicalTrials.gov (clinicaltrials.gov), the World Health Organization (WHO) International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP) portal (www.who.int/ictrp), and other sources for unpublished or ongoing studies.

We will search grey literature for publications relevant to our review, such as reports, dissertations, theses, and conference abstracts, e.g. in Shodhganga (shodhganga.inflibnet.ac.in/) and Google Scholar (scholar.google.com).

We will use the PubMed/MEDLINE ‘similar articles search’ tool on all included trials. We will also manually check citations and reference lists of the included trials.

We will search for and examine any relevant retraction statements (through the Retraction Watch Database at retractionwatch.com/retraction‐watch‐database‐user‐guide/) and errata for information, as errata can reveal important limitations or even fatal flaws in studies [56].

Supplementary material 1 shows a preliminary list of search terms to be used to search for articles in the above‐mentioned resources. At the review stage, we will provide the most current date for Searching other resources.

We will use the relevant sections of the PRISMA‐S checklist for our review to ensure that we have reported and documented our searches as advised (PRISMA‐S Checklist) [55].

Data collection and analysis

We will follow the guidelines provided in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [57]. We will perform our main analysis using Review Manager (RevMan) [58].

Selection of studies

At least two review authors (AKN and SSN) will independently select trials for inclusion using Covidence [59]. We will upload the list of all candidate studies to Covidence, and duplicates not retrieved through the initial de‐duplication of the search results in the Cochrane Register of Studies Web will be removed automatically. We will then screen titles and abstracts of all unique records obtained by electronic searches and searches in other resources for inclusion of potentially relevant trials using a coding system such as ‘retrieve’ for eligible or potentially eligible/unclear studies and ‘do not retrieve’ for studies that do not meet the inclusion criteria. We will retrieve the full texts of all relevant and potentially relevant records, and two review authors (ZM and CTV, AKN and AV, UG and SSN, or DB and BST) will independently screen these to identify trials for inclusion. We will include trials regardless of whether they report our outcomes of interest. Covidence allows blind screening in a way that each review author can assess the studies independently without seeing the other's decisions. We will identify ineligible studies and will record reasons for exclusions. We will correspond with study investigators as required to clarify trial eligibility. We will resolve any disagreement through discussion or, if required, we will consult a third review author (SS). We will identify and collate multiple reports of the same trial so that each trial, rather than each record, is the unit of interest in the review. We will record the selection process in sufficient detail to complete a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram (for new systematic reviews, which includes searches of databases, registers, and other sources) [41, 42].

If any non‐English language reports require translation, and if we have no command of that language, we will use Google translator (translate.google.com), then we will request the CHBG Information specialist to help us find a person with command of that language to ensure the accuracy of the translation. We will report and document our searches, using items from the PRISMA‐S checklist as advised (PRISMA‐S Checklist) [55].

Risk of bias assessment in included studies

We will assess the risk of bias in the included trials using the criteria described in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [62]. We will use the Risk of Bias Tool 2 (RoB 2) (www.riskofbias.info/) [63].

As we will assess the effect of the assignment to the intervention, we will use the data based on the intention‐to‐treat (ITT). The ITT principle includes all randomised participants, regardless of the interventions they received.

We will assess the risk of bias for all Critical outcomes and Important outcomes by answering signalling questions within five domains (see below). We will also present the results of seven of these outcomes, at the longest follow‐up, in a summary of findings table: all‐cause mortality; the proportion of participants with serious adverse events; health‐related quality of life; serum sodium concentration, body weight, urine output, and adverse events considered non‐serious.

Teams of two authors (AKN and SSN) will independently evaluate the risk of bias in the trials. We will resolve any decision disagreements by discussing with another review author (ZM).

Each of these domains can be assessed as ‘low risk of bias’, ‘some concerns’, or ‘high risk of bias’ [63]. The response options for the signalling questions are: yes; probably yes; probably no; no; no information.

In the case of randomised clinical trials with a cross‐over design, we will assess the risk of bias in the same way as for trials with a parallel‐group design. This is because we will use the data from the first period only (i.e. before the cross‐over period).

We will obtain the risk of bias judgements for each domain using the algorithm in the RoB 2 Excel tool (available at www.riskofbias.info), namely ‘low’ risk of bias, ‘high’ risk of bias, or ‘some concerns’. We will support our answers to the signalling questions and our risk of bias judgements by providing justifications. The overall risk of bias for the outcome result will be the least favourable assessment across the five bias domains. Both the proposed domain‐level and overall risk of bias judgements may be overridden by the review authors, with justification, and we will document this appropriately [62].

Measures of treatment effect

We will follow the guidance outlined in Chapter 6 and Chapter 10 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [64, 65].

Whenever possible, we will analyse dichotomous outcomes, such as all‐cause mortality, serious adverse events, and non‐serious adverse events, with risk ratios (RRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

When survival data (i.e. time‐to‐event) are available, but mortality data are not, and they cannot be obtained from the survival data, we will use hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs.

When trials report the number of adverse events (serious or those considered non‐serious) in the experimental and control groups of the trials, we will calculate the rate ratio based on the total number of events in the two groups using a formula in Excel as described in Chapter 6 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [64]. In RevMan, we will define the analysis with contrast‐level type of data, the inverse variance statistical method, and the rate ratio effect measure with 95% CI. Then, we will add the natural logarithm (ln) of the rate ratio obtained from the formula, along with the corresponding standard error (SE), into RevMan for the analysis [64]. We will present a table with each type of adverse event separately.

For continuous outcomes, such as health‐related quality of life, we will use the mean difference (MD) and its 95% CI when trials use the same measurement tool. When different validated tools are used to measure the same outcome, we will report the outcome using the standardised mean difference (SMD) and its 95% CI, and we will indicate the direction of the effect [65]. As the SMD method does not correct for differences in the direction of the scale, if necessary, we will multiply the mean values from one set of studies by −1 to ensure that all the scale points are in the same direction [64]. We will interpret SMD as follows: SMD less than 0.40 for small intervention effects; SMD between 0.40 and 0.70 for moderate intervention effects; and SMD greater than 0.70 for large intervention effects [66]. For the remaining continuous outcomes, such as serum sodium concentration (mmol/L), body weight (kg), and urine output (mL/day), we will extract the mean change from baseline to end of treatment, along with corresponding standard deviations (or other estimable measures of variance) for each trial group. If change‐from‐baseline data are not available, we will use end‐of‐treatment values where appropriate, provided that baseline values are comparable between groups. We will calculate pooled mean differences (or SMDs where scales differ) with 95% CIs. If any of our continuous outcomes are reported as dichotomous data, e.g. ‘increased or not’, we will contact trial investigators to obtain the required data format. In the case of no response, we will apply techniques to combine continuous and dichotomous outcomes [65]. We will indicate the direction of the effect on the forest plots.

We will summarise findings narratively when skewed data are reported as medians and interquartile ranges for continuous data, following Section 10.5.3 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [65]. We will conduct meta‐analyses only when the trial groups are sufficiently homogeneous [65].

We will not include analyses based on individual participant data (IPD), as these are not planned for this review.

When applicable, we will report the number needed to treat for an additional beneficial outcome (NNTB) and the number needed to treat for an additional harmful outcome (NNTH), both with 95% CIs [64, 66]. We will analyse participants in the intervention groups to which they were randomised, regardless of the intervention they received, and we will include all randomised participants in the outcome analyses (i.e. ITT).

Unit of analysis issues

We will consider the participants randomised to the intervention groups as the unit of analysis. For trials with one experimental and one control parallel‐group design, we will compare the experimental intervention group versus the control group. In the case of a multi‐group trial, we will include data from the experimental and control intervention groups relevant to our review [64]. Suppose the two experimental groups represent variants of the same intervention (e.g. different doses of the same drug), and are clinically and methodologically similar enough to be pooled. In that case, we will combine these two groups into a single group using methods described in Section 6.5.2.10 Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions (i.e. calculating a weighted mean and pooled standard deviation) [64].

When the two experimental groups represent distinct interventions and cannot be combined meaningfully, we will split the control group evenly between the two comparisons to avoid double‐counting participants, as recommended in Section 23.3.4 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [43]. We may need to combine the experimental groups into one group by calculating a weighted average of their means and standard deviations [43].

For count data, such as the number of adverse events per intervention group, we will calculate the rate ratio, as described in the Measures of treatment effect section.

As stated in Outcome measures, we will consider follow‐up times reported during the trial period as short term (up to three months), intermediate term (three to six months), and long term (longer than six months). However, depending on the availability of the data, we may need to consider alternative methods of grouping these time points.

We will only use the effect estimates reported from the first period (before the cross‐over) in trials with a cross‐over design to avoid carry‐over effects [43].

Though unlikely, if cluster‐randomised trials are identified, we will evaluate the unit of randomisation and the study design [43]. We will carefully consider any potential confounding factors that may impact the results unrelated to the intervention, e.g. heterogeneity in cohorts. We will use the intracluster (or intraclass) correlation coefficient (ICC) to convert trials to their effective sample size before incorporating them into the meta‐analysis [43, 64].

We will provide all treatment groups in the characteristics of included studies table, even if they are not used in the review.

Reporting bias assessment

If we have 10 or more trials included in a meta‐analysis of our critical outcomes, we can evaluate potential publication bias and possible small‐study biases in their results [69]. In this case, we will create and visually examine the funnel plot of an outcome for evidence of asymmetry. We will apply formal statistical tests, i.e. the regression asymmetry test [70], to investigate funnel plot asymmetry following the recommendations in Chapter 13 of the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [69]. Regarding reporting bias, we will check trial protocols and information on trial registration against published reports to evaluate whether selective reporting of outcomes is present.

Synthesis methods

In our review, we plan to do three separate comparisons: tolvaptan versus standard medical care, versus placebo, or versus other vaptans. Whenever feasible, we will conduct meta‐analyses for a given outcome, irrespective of risk of bias, using a random‐effects model as the primary approach because we anticipate clinical and methodological diversity across populations and trial designs [65, 66].

We will undertake (or display) a meta‐analysis only if participants, interventions, comparisons, and outcomes are judged to be sufficiently alike to ensure a clinically meaningful answer. If a meta‐analysis is not meaningful, we will present the results in a narrative synthesis, following the synthesis without meta‐analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline (training.cochrane.org/online‐learning/cochrane‐methodology/swim‐reporting‐guideline), which promotes transparent reporting of trial grouping, the standardised metric used for the synthesis, the synthesis method, how data are presented, a summary of the synthesis findings, and limitations of the synthesis.

We will use the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) method to estimate heterogeneity in meta‐analyses. To further assess the consistency of trial results, we will use the I² statistic to quantify inconsistencies amongst the trials in each analysis when the P value is equal to or less than 0.10. We will interpret a P value of 0.10 or less from the Chi² test as evidence of statistical heterogeneity. Given the potential for between‐study variability, we will apply the enhanced random‐effects model meta‐analysis to account for this heterogeneity and obtain more reliable estimates [65]. We will use the REML estimator to calculate a confidence interval for the heterogeneity estimate. We will use the Hartung‐Knapp‐Sidik‐Jonkman method to calculate a confidence interval for the meta‐analysis effect estimate when there are at least three trials and the estimate of heterogeneity is greater than zero. In other scenarios (i.e. two trials or where the estimate of heterogeneity is equal to zero), we will use the Wald‐type method. If we have at least five trials and there is no clear funnel plot asymmetry, or in the absence of heterogeneity, we will show prediction intervals calculated in RevMan [65].

Trial Sequential Analysis

We will conduct Trial Sequential Analysis as cumulative meta‐analyses are at risk of producing random errors due to sparse data and repetitive testing of the accumulating data [76, 77, 78, 79]. To control random errors, we will calculate the diversity‐adjusted required information size (DARIS) (i.e. the number of participants needed in a meta‐analysis to detect or reject a certain intervention effect) [78, 79, 80, 81, 82]. In our dichotomous outcome meta‐analysis (all‐cause mortality, serious adverse events, adverse events considered non‐serious), we will base the DARIS on the event proportion in the control group of our meta‐analyses; the assumption of a plausible relative risk reduction of 20% or the risk observed in the included trials at low risk of bias; a risk of type I error of 2.5% due to three critical outcomes and 2.0% due to four important secondary outcomes; a risk of type II error of 10% [83]; and the diversity of the included trials in the meta‐analysis [79, 84]. For our continuous outcome meta‐analysis (e.g. health‐related quality of life, serum sodium concentration, body weight, urine output), we will estimate DARIS using the SD observed in the control group and the minimal relevant difference of 50% of this SD; alpha of 2.5% due to three important outcomes [85]; beta of 10% [83]; and the diversity as estimated from the trials in the meta‐analysis [86].

We will also calculate and report the Trial Sequential Analysis‐adjusted CI if the cumulative Z‐score does not cross any of the trial sequential monitoring boundaries for benefit, harm, or futility [76, 79]. We will compare the Trial Sequential Analysis‐adjusted CI to the prediction interval and the naive 95% CI. The underlying assumption of Trial Sequential Analysis is that testing for statistical significance may be performed each time a new trial is added to the meta‐analysis. We will, therefore, add the trials according to the year of publication, and if more than one trial has been published in a year, we will add trials alphabetically according to the last name of the first author. Based on the DARIS, we will construct the trial sequential monitoring boundaries for benefit, harm, and futility [76, 78, 79]. These boundaries will determine the statistical inference one may draw regarding the cumulative meta‐analysis that has not reached the DARIS; if the trial sequential monitoring boundary for benefit or harm is crossed before the DARIS is reached, firm evidence for an intervention effect may be established, and further trials may be superfluous. However, if the boundaries are not crossed, it is most probably necessary to continue doing trials to detect or reject a certain intervention effect. If the cumulative z‐curve crosses the trial sequential monitoring boundaries for futility, no more trials may be needed. A more detailed description of Trial Sequential Analysis can be found at www.ctu.dk/tsa/ [76, 77, 79]. We will perform Trial Sequential Analysis with Trial Sequential Analysis software, version 0.9.5.10 beta [77]. We will use random‐effects meta‐analysis.

Downgrading for imprecision with Trial Sequential Analysis

Where the cumulative z‐value in the Trial Sequential Analysis does not cross the monitoring boundaries for benefit, harm, or futility, we will downgrade our assessment of imprecision by three levels if the accrued number of participants is below 33% of the DARIS, by two levels if between 33% and 65%, and by one level if between 66% and 99% of DARIS. We will not downgrade for imprecision if the cumulative z‐value reaches or crosses benefit, harm, futility, or DARIS.

Consumer involvement

Insights from consumers will guide the development of the review, obtain patient‐centred feedback, and identify key priorities and meaningful outcomes [94].

Recognising that consumer involvement ensures review questions are relevant to people requiring healthcare and that areas of high importance are prioritised, we actively involved consumers in the development of this protocol. We engaged a member of the Liver Foundation of Kerala (LIFOK) as a consumer representative. Their input was sought through a semi‐structured interview, which contributed to refining the research question and ensuring that the outcomes included are relevant to patients and carers. Consumer involvement will continue at an advisory and consultative level, including during interpretation of the findings and preparation of the plain language summary.
