How do pregnancy complications, intrapartum, and neonatal risks differ in bicornuate and didelphys uteri? A population-based study
Authors: Shahad Alharazi, Ahmad Badeghiesh, Haitham Baghlaf, Michael H Dahan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study researched if there are differences between didelphys and bicornuate uteri regarding obstetric and neonatal outcomes based on an analysis of a population database.Study design, size, and durationThis retrospective population-based cohort study utilized data from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project – Nationwide Inpatient Sample (HCUP-NIS) from 2010 to 2014, including 3,846,342 births and analyzing 1,146 women with didelphys uteri and 6,327 with bicornuate uteri.Materials and methodsHCUP-NIS is the largest inpatient database in the USA, encompassing hospitalizations nationwide. It represents over 96% of Americans and provides data on approximately seven million yearly inpatient stays. Multivariate logistic regression was utilized to evaluate associations between uterine anomalies and delivery outcomes, controlling for confounding factors. IRB approval was deemed unnecessary since the data were anonymous and publicly accessible.ResultsWomen with didelphys uteri experienced significantly higher rates of preeclampsia (aOR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.05–1.80), PPROM (aOR = 1.76, 95% CI: 1.32–2.34), preterm delivery (aOR = 1.39, 95% CI: 1.18–1.64), and cesarean delivery (aOR = 1.41, 95% CI: 1.20–1.64) compared to those with bicornuate uteri. Conversely, women with didelphys uteri had lower risks of placenta previa (aOR = 0.26, 95% CI: 0.08–0.84) and placental abruption (aOR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.31–0.87). No significant differences were noted in other maternal or neonatal outcomes.ConclusionThe study highlights critical differences in pregnancy complications between didelphys and bicornuate uteri, underscoring the need for tailored perinatal care for affected women.Lay summarySome women are born with a womb that has an unusual shape, which may affect pregnancy. Two types are a bicornuate uterus, where the womb has a heart-like shape, and a didelphys uterus, where the womb and cervix are divided into two parts. These conditions are often studied together, but it is not known whether they affect pregnancy in the same way. In this study, we used a national database of over 3.8 million hospital births in the US. We compared pregnancy outcomes in women with a didelphys uterus and a bicornuate uterus. We found that the risks were different. Women with a didelphys uterus were more likely to have high blood pressure, waters breaking early, early birth, and delivery by a cesarean section. Women with a bicornuate uterus were more likely to have placenta problems. These findings show that the two conditions are different and may need different care during pregnancy.

Introduction

Congenital uterine anomalies (CUAs) are rare developmental anomalies of the female reproductive tract. They have been found to affect 5.5% of an unselected population (Chan et al. 2011). Eight percent of infertile patients, 12.3% of patients with a history of miscarriage, and 24.5% of patients with miscarriage and infertility have been shown to have uterine anomalies (Chan et al. 2011). In other studies, of CUAs, the bicornuate uterus was found in 26% of the cases and the didelphys uterus was diagnosed in 8% of the cases (Grimbizis et al. 2001). Most defects are likely the result of a combination of multiple genetic factors. The bicornuate uterus and didelphys uterus are both lateral fusion defect congenital anomalies, and both represent a continuum of fusion. A didelphys and an extreme bicornuate uterus with a near-complete lack of fusion may be difficult to distinguish. However, their differences are in anatomical presentations in that a bicornuate uterus has an external fundus that is indented >1 cm and the vagina and cervix are generally normal (one cervix is present) (Troiano & McCarthy 2004). According to the ASRM 2021 criteria, a bicornuate uterus is diagnosed when the external fundal indentation is more than 1 cm (Pfeifer et al. 2021). On the other hand, uterus didelphys is a duplication of certain reproductive structures. In many cases, the duplication can be limited to the uterus and cervix (uterine didelphys and bicollis (two cervixes), but duplication can extend to include the vulva, bladder, urethra, vagina, and anus.

The structural differences between these anomalies might explain why they carry distinct obstetric risks. Because didelphys uteri lack midline fusion completely, they form two entirely separate and smaller cavities. This restricted functional volume may predispose these patients to premature rupture of membranes and preterm labor. In contrast, the partial fusion seen in bicornuate uteri might alter the intrauterine environment and local blood supply in a way that specifically affects how the placenta implants.

In the medical literature, both anomalies have usually been studied as a single combined group. Together, they are known to be associated with spontaneous abortions, recurrent miscarriages, preterm births, prelabor rupture of membranes, intrauterine growth restriction, antepartum and postpartum bleeding, placental attachment abnormalities, cervical insufficiency, fetal malpresentation, and higher rates of cesarean delivery (Grimbizis et al. 2001, Venetis et al. 2014). Since our study uses inpatient delivery records, early pregnancy complications, such as first-trimester miscarriages, fall outside the scope of this analysis.

Methods

A total of 3,846,342 births between 2010 and 2014 inclusively were screened for inclusion; 1,146 women with didelphys uteri and 6,327 women with bicornuate uteri were included in the study. None of these cases were operated on to correct the anomaly before pregnancy. Women were included only once per pregnancy. We included all women who delivered during this period regardless of their parity, since specific parity data are not tracked in the HCUP-NIS database.

We conducted a retrospective population-based study using data from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project – Nationwide Inpatient Sample (HCUP-NIS) over five years from 2010 to 2014, inclusively. The HCUP-NIS is the largest inpatient sample database in the USA and comprises hospital inpatient stays submitted by hospitals in 48 states and the District of Columbia. Each year, the database provides information relating to approximately seven million inpatient stays, including patient characteristics, diagnoses, and procedures. The data are representative of 20% of admissions to the US hospitals annually and geographically represent over 96% of the American population. These years of inclusion were chosen because they are the period with data on uterine anomalies.

We evaluated deliveries between 2010 and 2014 inclusively, by using the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM) codes for delivery-related discharge diagnoses (650.xx, 677.xx, 651.xx–676.xx, where the fifth digit is 0, 1, or 2) and birth-related procedural diagnosis (72.x, 73.x, 74.0–74.2). We limited our study group to only those admissions that ended with delivery or maternal death to guarantee that multiple admissions in the same pregnancy were excluded. Within this group, all women with a bicornuate, or didelphys, were identified using ICD-9 diagnostic codes 752.34 and 752.2, respectively.

Demographic characteristics, maternal baseline characteristics, and delivery and neonatal outcomes of all deliveries included in the study population were also identified using the appropriate ICD-9 codes. Baseline clinical characteristics included patient age, race, income quartile, insurance type, previous CD, multiple gestations, tobacco smoking, obesity (defined as body mass index (BMI) of at least 30 kg/m2), pre-existing hypertension (HTN), pre-existing diabetes, and pre-existing thyroid disease. Delivery outcomes included preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), PTD, placental abruption, CD, maternal infection, chorioamnionitis, postpartum hemorrhage (PPH), hysterectomy, pulmonary embolism, and deep vein thrombosis (DVT). Neonatal outcomes included SGA defined as birth weight less than the tenth percentile for gestational age, intrauterine fetal death (IUFD), and congenital anomalies.

The date range of 2010–2014 inclusively was chosen because this is when data on uterine anomalies first appeared in the database and, in 2015, the database switched from ICD-9 to ICD-10 codes, which are not comparable and as such could not be included.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS 28.0 (IBM Corporation, USA) software. We contrasted baseline clinical and demographic characteristics between women with the two uterine anomalies. Subsequently, logistic regression analyses were conducted to explore associations between each of the two uterine anomalies and delivery and neonatal outcomes through the estimation of odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI). The regression models were adjusted for baseline variables that differed significantly between the two groups (P ≤ 0.05). Based on this, pregnancy outcomes were adjusted for race, while delivery and neonatal outcomes were adjusted for race, preeclampsia, and placenta previa. Previous cesarean section was recorded but did not meet the threshold to be included as a confounder in the placenta previa model. Because the database lacks detailed obstetric history, we could not adjust for parity, prior preeclampsia, or previous PPROM/preterm birth. Per convention, any complications with less than 11 occurrences in any of the groups were referred to in the Results section, citing possible unreliable results that should be confirmed in further studies. Since this study used exclusively de-identified, publicly available data, institutional review board approval was not required.

No statistical difference in rates of pregnancy-induced hypertension (aOR = 1.12, 95% CI: 0.90–1.39, P = 0.33), eclampsia, or gestational diabetes was found between the two groups. Discussion

In this retrospective population-based cohort study, pregnant women with didelphys uteri and bicornuate uteri had mostly similar pregnancy outcomes with several notable exceptions.

In comparison with pregnant women with bicornuate uteri, patients with didelphys uteri were more likely to experience preeclampsia, preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), preterm delivery, and higher chances of cesarean delivery, after controlling for confounding demographics. Additionally, women with didelphys uteri had less risk of developing placenta previa and abruptio placentae than pregnant women with bicornuate uteri. No significant differences were seen between the groups regarding pregnancy-induced hypertension, eclampsia, gestational diabetes, chorioamnionitis, operative vaginal delivery, hysterectomy, postpartum hemorrhage, wound complications, blood transfusion, maternal death, maternal infection, venous thromboembolism, pulmonary embolism, or DIC. Moreover, there was no significant difference between the two groups when comparing neonatal outcomes.

Women with didelphys uteri were more likely to experience preeclampsia. This could be related to a higher incidence of renal anomalies in patients with didelphys uteri. This risk was reported in the literature, and it affects up to 29% of subjects in didelphys uteri and only 15% of patients with bicornuate uteri (Heinonen 2018). However, these women had an increase in preeclampsia only and did not have an increased risk of gestational hypertension or eclampsia in our data. Nevertheless, eclampsia is rare, and the study was underpowered to detect differences. It also can be hypothesized that there may be a blood flow difference in these two types of uteri affecting placentation and favoring the development of preeclampsia in the didelphys uteri group. Nevertheless, the specific difference in blood flow when comparing bicornuate and didelphys uteri as far as we can determine has not been investigated. Upon reviewing the literature, although it is still in the normal range, it is known that women with congenital uterine anomalies had higher blood pressure values than women with normal uteri in pregnancy (Tranquilli et al. 2004). This difference was linked to abnormal uterine vasculature (Long et al. 1980) in women with uterine anomalies as a group. Women with uterine anomalies were also found to have increased vascular tension in pregnancy, with abnormal systolic/diastolic ratios. Uterine artery ratio abnormalities occurred in 80% of subjects with uterine anomalies and only in 10% of controls (Leible et al. 1998), and differences between specific uterine anomalies were not tackled in these studies. Regarding delivery outcomes, our findings suggest that pregnant women with didelphys uteri had a higher risk of preterm premature rupture of membranes and preterm delivery, which could be explained by decreased functional uterine volume in comparison with bicornuate uteri. Smaller uterine volumes are known in congenital uterine anomalies as a group (Reuter et al. 1989, Pellerito et al. 1992, Braun et al. 2005, Puscheck & Cohen 2008); however, the exact difference in volume between the bicornuate and didelphys uteri needs investigation. Additionally, higher risks of preterm delivery could also be iatrogenic related to the increased rate of preeclampsia. Sadly, the HCUP database does not permit us to associate the week of delivery and preeclampsia. We also cannot determine the direct cause of the preterm delivery. The only known difference between the two anomalies is the presence of one cervix in the bicornuate uterus and two cervixes in the didelphys uterus; however, the effect of this anatomical variation cannot be determined as numerous normal and complicated pregnancies and deliveries of singleton and multiple pregnancies have been reported in both groups and few of them are cited in this article (Rezai et al. 2015, Adams & Minuzzo 2019, Elias & Amisi 2020, Karunaratne et al. 2023). Investigating the published literature, one study found that 50% of preterm deliveries in women with CUAs as a group were due to preterm labor. Histological evidence of placental malperfusion was present in 22% of all pregnancies and those subjects that delivered at an earlier gestational age (Lekovich et al. 2017). This also can give another explanation.

According to our data, bicornuate uteri had three times more risk of developing placenta previa and double the risk of abruptio placentae in comparison with women with didelphys uteri. However, the number of subjects with placenta previa in the uterus didelphys group was small and more study is needed. One explanation of these differences could be related to anatomy and the possible placement of the placenta. Lateral placement of the placenta and its association with abnormal vasculature in CUAs in general has been mentioned in the literature (Leible et al. 1998). However, the difference in placental placement when comparing these two anomalies has yet to be studied. We also noted that women with didelphys uteri had significantly more chances of having cesarean deliveries. This difference could be related to different rates of pregnancy complications in the groups. It could also be hypothesized to be attributed to possible contractility issues of the uterine muscles, and the smaller functional uterine size, possibly resulting in prolonged labor in didelphys uteri when compared to bicornuate uteri. In the literature, cesarean sections were found to be higher in all class U3 uterine anomalies when compared to other CUAs, according to the ESHRE classification (Panagiotopoulos et al. 2022). This finding was explained by inadequate uterine contractility due to anomalous uterine shape, labor prolongation, fetal malpresentation, fetal growth restriction, and other obstetric complications (Panagiotopoulos et al. 2022). These effects were likely increased in the didelphys as compared to the bicornuate uteri. The HCUP database does not permit us to determine the exact cause of the cesarean sections.

Strengths and limitations

This study has multiple strengths. It is one of the largest studies to date that included women with congenital uterine anomalies and followed them up during pregnancy. The utilization of an extensive dataset, encompassing over 3 million deliveries nationwide, grants the study considerable statistical power. The results of this study provide valuable information to clinicians regarding prenatal risks, delivery complications, and neonatal outcomes for women with these two specific congenital uterine anomalies. This knowledge can help guide the management of these patients during delivery and improve prenatal counseling for these women.

The limitations of our study are related to its retrospective nature and use of administrative database, which relies on the accuracy and consistency of the individual coding the data, and the masking of undetected bias. Both diagnoses are based on ICD-9 coding by treating physicians. Standard diagnostic criteria for these anomalies include an external fundal indentation greater than 1 cm for a bicornuate uterus and complete duplication of the uterine horns and cervix for a didelphys uterus, both typically confirmed by 3D ultrasound or MRI. However, the database does not specify the diagnostic method used in each case. As a result, the ICD-9 code for a bicornuate uterus may occasionally be applied to cases of septate uterus, which could, to some degree, influence the results.

Because the HCUP-NIS only captures inpatient hospital stays for delivery, early pregnancy losses, such as first-trimester miscarriages, are not reflected in our data. The database also lacks granular obstetric history, which meant we could not adjust our models for parity, prior preeclampsia, or previous episodes of PPROM and preterm birth. While we adjusted for all significant baseline variables, the inability to control for these specific obstetric factors remains a limitation. Finally, since didelphys uteri are known to be associated with renal anomalies, and renal disease can influence preeclampsia risk, stratifying by renal anomaly status would be an interesting avenue for future research, although our sample size did not permit it here.

However, the use of such a large database is probably the only way to tackle a topic of such a low prevalent pathology, it shows that they are different, and further research is needed.

Conclusion

The present study demonstrates that women with bicornuate and didelphys uteri have certain differences when it comes to pregnancy and intrapartum complications, including rates of preeclampsia, placenta previa, placental abruption, cesarean section, PPROM, and preterm delivery. While this analysis is limited to delivery-related admissions and does not capture early pregnancy losses, the findings still emphasize the importance of individualized perinatal care for these two congenital uterine anomalies. Future studies on uterine anomalies, their physiology, and their impact on maternal and neonatal outcomes are needed. More studies directly comparing bicornuate and didelphys uteri are needed.
