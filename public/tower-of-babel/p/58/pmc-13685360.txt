Health systems interventions for prevention of maternal peripartum infection in low and middle-income countries: a systematic review
Authors: Roseline Abwao, Job Mapesa, Wanja Tenambergen, David Odada, Nasra Gathoni, Felix Riunga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobally, there is growing recognition that a functional health system is key to improving maternal health and reducing maternal mortality. Maternal peripartum infection remains a major cause of maternal morbidity and mortality, particularly in low- and middle-income countries. This infection is a multifaceted problem with many players. Adopting a health systems approach could help avert this preventable infection by addressing the gaps across all the components of care.ObjectiveThe aim of this systematic review was to identify and evaluate interventions to prevent maternal peripartum infection in LMICs.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in Cochrane Library, CINAHL, MEDLINE (via PubMed) and Scopus, World Health Organization (WHO), and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) websites to identify primary articles reporting on interventions for preventing maternal peripartum infection using the PRISMA model. The article search was conducted for a period of 3 months (01/08/2022 to 30/10/2022). Search terms were “Peripartum”, “Infection”, “Genital tract”, and their MESH terms. The inclusion criteria were primary interventional studies that reported interventions for the prevention of maternal peripartum infection, studies from LMICs, and those written in the English language. Cochrane Risk of Bias tools were used to appraise the quality of the studies.ResultsOut of 1662 articles, 24 studies met the inclusion criteria comprising of 55,982 participants. The interventions were grouped into five domains: antibiotic prophylaxis 10(42%) of studies), self-care training 6 (25%), skin preparation 5 (21%), Traditional Birth Attendant training 2(2%) and use of Clean Delivery Kit 1(1%)0.11 studies reported a significantly lower risk of infection. Four studies reported a significantly improved knowledge and practice of women regarding maternal peripartum infection. While six studies reported no change in the risk of infection with the intervention.ConclusionA range of interventions targeting maternal peripartum infection prevention in LMICs were identified in this review. They were all targeting a single intervention without integrating other system components. The review highlights the need for integrated health systems interventions and further research to generate stronger evidence and inform effective implementation.Study registrationPROSPERO CRD42022342550.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12905-026-04710-w.

Background

Globally, there is increased recognition of the importance of a functional health system in the improvement of maternal health and the reduction of maternal mortality [1]. defines health system as “consisting of all organizations, people and actions whose primary intent is to promote, restore or maintain health in ways that are responsive, financially fair and make the best or most efficient use of available resources” with a goal of improving the health of the community. Within Health System Building Blocks Framework there are six components which help identify system gaps systematically. These components are service delivery, health workforce, information, medical products, vaccines and technologies, financing, leadership and governance. Additionally process elements (access, coverage, quality and safety) and outcomes (improved health and health equity, responsiveness, social and financial protection and improved efficiency) are included. According to [1], Health systems interventions refer to deliberate strategies aimed at strengthening one or more components of a health system to improve the quality, access, efficiency, and equity of health services. The focus of these interventions may be on any of the components of the health system to enhance overall system performance and health outcomes [1, 2]. Healthcare is a complex, high-risk system of interdependent parts, and effective interventions require a systems thinking approach that ensures all components work together toward a common goal [3, 4]. In the absence of strong health systems, even the best clinical interventions may fail to reach those in need or be delivered safely and effectively [5]. Therefore, tackling healthcare challenges requires sustainable interventions guided by a systems thinking approach [1, 6]. By considering a detailed understanding of the dynamics among processes, people, and technology, systems thinking helps in the adoption of effective interventions in the healthcare system [1].

Maternal peripartum infection is a significant and preventable cause of maternal morbidity and mortality [7]. According to [8], the pooled incidence is 4% during labor and between 1% and 2% during the postpartum period. Defined as ‘Bacterial infection of the genital tract and surrounding tissues occurring any time between the onset of rupture of membranes or labor and the 42nd day postpartum in which two or more of the following are present: Pelvic pain, Fever, Abnormal vaginal discharge, Abnormal smell/foul odor of discharge, Delay in uterine involution’ [7] maternal peripartum infection encompasses a range of infections. This is include chorioamnionitis, puerperal endometritis post-cesarean surgical site Infections (SSIs) and infections resulting from perineal trauma, including those associated with episiotomies or spontaneous perineal tears during vaginal delivery [7, 8]. High incidence rates of 15.6% [9] and up to 30.9% [10, 11] of Post-Cesarean Surgical Site Infections have been reported in LMICs. Chorioamnionitis, puerperal endometritis and episiotomy site infections shows a wide range from 2% to over 20% across regions like Tanzania, India, Kenya, and Ethiopia [12–15], creating a considerable burden for patients and health systems [11]. Maternal peripartum infection increases the potential for readmission or prolonged hospitalization, increased antibiotic use, and significantly higher treatment costs compared to uninfected cases and can disrupt the maternal-infant bonding [12]. In addition, the resource constraints, with limited access to clean delivery environments, shortages of trained health personnel, and weak health systems infrastructure intensify the risk and the burden of maternal peripartum infection in LMICs.

Maternal peripartum infection is preventable and evidence of specific interventions necessary to prevent and treat the infection such as antibiotic prophylaxis have been documented [16–28]. Being a complex concern influenced by multiple, interrelated factors—such as access to quality antenatal care, hygienic delivery practices, postpartum follow-up, and health system responsiveness—preventing maternal peripartum infection cannot rely on a simple, linear intervention [14]. Instead, it requires a systems-based approach that considers the broader social, economic, cultural, and healthcare infrastructure within which maternal care is delivered [1]. A systems approach enables the identification of core systemic components that enhance the impact of technical interventions [6]. This perspective underscores the importance of looking beyond clinical procedures to examine the structural and operational elements of health systems that support or hinder infection prevention efforts [1].

It is from this ground that a health systems evaluation of the available interventions of prevention is necessary. Drawing from maternal peripartum infection literature, this paper reviews the available evidence on health systems interventions to prevent maternal peripartum infection.

Methods

The systematic review was conducted in line with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [29]. The systematic review protocol was prospectively registered on PROPERO (CRD42022342550).

Search strategy and selection process

The study took place from 1st August to 30th Oct 2022 during which an expert subject librarian assisted with formulating the review search strategy. The databases used in the search were: The Cochrane Library, CINAHL, MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, and EBSCO Host Academic Search Complete and Websites, such as the World Health Organization (WHO) and the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE). Key terms (“Peripartum”, “Infection”, and “Genital tract”) in the research questions were checked for alternative terms using the controlled vocabulary Medical Subject Heading (MeSH) and free text searching (Supplement 1). References lists of the publications retrieved were manually searched and the search was limited to the English language. The findings from the online databases were transferred into the endnote. Two reviewers independently assessed the titles and abstracts (RA and DO) of the identified publications according to the predetermined inclusion criteria and differences were resolved through discussion by involving the third reviewer (AR). All full-text articles were double-screened by RA and DO and all the processes in study selection were recorded in sufficient detail to complete a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1) and a table created for ‘Characteristics of Included Studies’ (Table 1).

Criteria for study inclusion and exclusion

Inclusion criteria –The systematic review included primary studies that reported interventions for the prevention of maternal peripartum Infection. Studies from LMICs and those written in the English language were included. There were no year restrictions in order to maximize the breadth of data included.

Exclusion criteria -Primary studies were excluded if there was a composite outcome from which it was not possible to extract data on maternal peripartum infection alone, systematic reviews and meta-analysis, studies not in the genital tract and surrounding tissues, studies not in LMICs, and non-English studies.

Data extraction and critical appraisal

Data from the included studies were extracted using a predefined data extraction sheet. Fields included: first author; year of publication; country, study design, population, sample size, duration of study, intervention, and critical appraisal summary. Two reviewers (RA and DO) independently assessed the methodological quality of the studies. The Cochrane risk of bias tool [47] was used to appraise studies using randomized study designs and [48] ROBINS-I (Risk of Bias In Nonrandomised Studies - of Interventions) for studies using non-randomized study designs. Disagreements were resolved by consensus and the involvement of the third reviewer (AR).

Results

Search findings

The search process identified a total of 1662 titles and abstracts including those sourced through manual searching for screening. A total of 1260 remained following the removal of duplicates which were then screened by title and abstract applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria and 56 articles were included for full-text screening. 32 articles were excluded in the full-text screening and the reasons provided for exclusion. Supplement 2 shows the excluded studies with the reasons for exclusion. This left a total of 24 papers (Table 1) which were critically appraised by the research team and agreement was reached for their inclusion in the systematic review.

Characteristics of the included studies

The 24 papers that met the aim of the review are detailed in Table 1. The studies covered a wide geographical area with a majority in inpatient settings. Six studies were conducted in Egypt [23, 38–40, 42, 43], four studies in India [20, 41, 45, 46], four studies in Nigeria [31, 32, 49, 50], two studies each in Sudan [36, 51], Uganda [21, 45] and Bangladesh [45, 46]. While one study each in Sri Lanka [37], Tanzania [33], Rwanda [34], Gambia [35] and Malawi [44]. Of the 24 studies, only one was a multicenter done in India, Nepal, and Bangladesh [46]. The oldest study in the review was published in 1997 [44]. More than half of the studies (n = 16) were published after 2015 with most of the studies (n = 12) being published in 2020,2021 and 2022. The most employed design used was randomized controlled studies 19 (65.5%) followed by Quasi-experimental 7(24). The intervention duration varied widely with the shortest at 2 weeks which tested the effect of antibiotic prophylaxis and the highest was 36 months which tested the use of a clean delivery kit (CDK). The interventions were mainly implemented in hospitals except for one study that involved the use of CDK by TBAs in the community [46].

Domains of the interventions to prevent maternal peripartum infection

Figure 2 presents the domains of the interventions identified in the included studies. The interventions targeted either the woman or health care workers and were grouped into five domains: antibiotic prophylaxis, self-care training, skin preparation, Traditional Birth Attendant (TBA) training and the use of clean birth kits (CDKs).

Antibiotic prophylaxis 10(42%) was the most common intervention, followed by skin preparation 6 (25%), self-care training 5 (21%),TBA training 2(2%) and CDK 1(1%).Majority of the interventions (n = 23) were implemented in the inpatient settings and therefore the implementations were majorly by health care professionals and a few (n = 1) were by non-healthcare professionals.

Risk of bias

Of the 16 RCTs (Table 2) assessed by using RoB2 all 16 (100%) were considered low risk. Notably, three studies [39, 50, 51] had some concerns of bias due to the randomization process. The ROBINS-I tool was used to assess the risk of bias in non-RCTs in 8 non-RCTs (Table 3) and all were considered low risk.

Discussions

The aim of this study was to identify and evaluate interventions to prevent maternal peripartum infection in LMICs. We found 24 studies, and the summary of the findings and the effectiveness of the five domains of interventions found in the review are discussed.

Antibiotic prophylaxis

The largest number of studies identified were on antibiotic prophylaxis for women undergoing Cesarean Section (CS). Ten studies [20, 30–37, 50] with a total of 3273 women for CS while one study [35] focused on 829 women during labour. The studies were all randomized control covering mainly African countries (n = 8) and South Asia (n = 2). Two studies supported prophylaxis for all women undergoing CS. Regarding the timing of antibiotic prophylaxis for CS, there was evidence from two studies [20, 30] supporting the [7] recommendations for antibiotic prophylaxis before the start of operation. However [36], study failed to show significant differences in rates of infection of post-operative infectious morbidity with different antibiotic timings. In this opened RCT study, 90 women received intravenous 1 gm of ceftizoxime either pre-incision or after clamping of the umbilical cord, and the authors found no difference in the two timings of antibiotic use. We identified five studies [31–33, 37, 50] that evaluated the duration of antibiotic prophylaxis. These studies supported single and not multiple or prolonged doses. One study in Rwanda [34], compared different antibiotic regimens. The comparison was between first-generation cephalosporin (cefazolin) and penicillin (ampicillin). The study showed a significantly lower risk of infection with prophylaxis cefazolin than with ampicillin. The authors recommended the implementation of a general protocol for the first generation [7]. recommendations do not support the routine use of antibiotics for normal deliveries, only in high-risk deliveries. On the contrary use of antibiotic prophylaxis during labor was considered in a Gambian study [35] where women in labor were given either an oral dose of azithromycin 2gram or a placebo. Follow-up was conducted for 8 weeks after delivery. The authors found maternal infections to be lower in the azithromycin group (3.6% vs. 9. 2%) and recommended the routine use of antibiotics routinely in labor in view of high maternal and neonatal mortalities in Gambia.

Overall, most of the studies on antibiotic prophylaxis have supported the current [7] recommendation on the use of antibiotics in the prevention of maternal peripartum infection. Implications for policymakers and practitioners in health systems and better public health outcomes are to recognize the importance of protocols on prophylaxis antibiotic use in maternity units based on the best available evidence, conformance to the standards of infection prevention and control as well as observance of surgical procedures [52].

Skin preparations

Six studies [21–23, 43, 44, 49] evaluated the effect of skin preparation in the prevention of maternal peripartum infection with a total of 8538 women (Table 1). For the administration of the antiseptics, the studies used vaginal preparations via sterile gauze, sponge sticks, and a full bath with soap containing antiseptics. There was further recent evidence to support the [53] recommendations for vaginal preparation with chlorhexidine gluconate or povidone-iodine immediately before CS [49]. prospective RCT study in Nigeria of 322 women who were randomized into two groups; the interventional group received vaginal cleansing, and the control group did not. Women in the intervention group had fewer infections (12%) than those in the control (36.8%). In a similar study in Egypt [23], involving 54 women split into two groups with one group receiving preoperative vaginal cleansing with povidone-iodine and the other none. The rate of infections was reduced in the treatment group (7.4%) than in the control (37%) [27]. RCT trial in Egypt, 90 women were allocated to three groups: control, vaginal cleansing with povidone-iodine, or chlorhexidine. The authors found lower infection rates in the groups that had vaginal cleansing and no significant difference in the rates of infections between povidone-iodine or chlorhexidine [22]. a pilot randomized study comparing the efficacy of chlorhexidine and povidone-iodine in skin antisepsis before CS with 311 women: 153 -chlorhexidine group and 158- povidone-iodine group found the rate of infections lower in chlorhexidine (5.4%) group than in the povidone-iodine (8.6%). The researchers highlighted that it did not reach a statistical significance. Evidence of a preoperative bath before CS was identified in one study. In this Ugandan study [21], randomized 96 women into two groups: An intervention (preoperative bath with chloroxylenol) and a control (without antisepsis bath) group. Incidences of infections were lower in the intervention group (6.25%) than in the control group (54. 17%). The evidence supports the [53] recommendation of a preoperative bath with or without antisepsis. The effect of birth canal cleansing with antiseptic was evaluated in one study. In this trial involving 6965 women [44], intervention and control groups were enrolled in the trial time periods not individually due to the busy labor unit. The intervention group received manual wipes of the birth canal with chlorhexidine at every vaginal examination while the control group received routine delivery procedures. The authors found the admissions related to delivery were reduced in the interventional group.

Implications of the findings to policymakers and practitioners in health systems and public health outcomes are that preoperative vaginal cleansing with povidone or chlorhexidine is a simple and cost-effective intervention that may be considered before performing CS and should be part of the hospital routine care to reduce the risk of post-CS infection [21, 23, 43, 44, 49].

Self-care training

We identified five studies [38–42] that evaluated the effects of self-care training on women’s knowledge and practice about maternal peripartum infections: with a total of 504 women (Table 1). They were all quasi experimental with four pre-posttests and two posttest control studies Four of the studies were done in Egypt (n = 4) and one in India (n = 1).

The studies employed purposive sampling (n = 4) and convenient (n = 1) sampling with a focus on postpartum women, two studies targeting antenatal women before CS. The main intervention was teaching and provision of a booklet or written materials with information regarding self-care to prevent infection. The outcome measures were knowledge improvement (n = 4) with one study looking at the rate of wound healing and another the overall quality of life after CS. In support of the [54] self-care agenda in health, the training and provision of the booklet with information and instructions improved the knowledge and self-care practices regarding maternal peripartum infection prevention among women.

The implications of the findings for policymakers and practitioners are to recognize the importance of maternal peripartum infection prevention guidelines teachings during the antenatal period and immediately after delivery, as part of routine antenatal and postnatal care [39, 40, 42]. The women should be given written instructions to go home with four references. A similar concept can be used to develop self-care guidelines on other post-partum health complications. Moreover, policymakers and practitioners should embrace the empowerment of nurses and midwives working in maternity units to enable them to train women on self-care as part of postpartum education as well as streamline the induction of newly recruited staff and students in colleges, on attachment and interns.

TBA training

We identified two studies that evaluated the effects of Traditional Birth Attendants (TBA) training [45, 51] on infectious morbidity and knowledge levels with a total number of 837 TBAs (Table 1) [45]. Bangladesh study involved 800 TBAs and 2099 women, the training covered the three ‘Cs’ handwashing with soap, cord care, and clean surfaces. The researchers found no significant difference in the risk of infection between trained and untrained TBAs and concluded that other factors, such as pre-existing infection, long labor, and insertion of fingers to examine- the vagina was found to be highly significant. In a Sudanese study, the effectiveness of designed guidelines for TBAs regarding postnatal sepsis management was considered. In this semi-experimental pretest-posttest [51], found an improvement in knowledge of TBAs after the guideline training intervention.

The implications of the findings in health systems and public health outcomes is that every delivery, including those at home, should be assisted by a skilled birth attendant (a midwife, nurse, or a doctor) who has been trained to proficiency in basic techniques for a clean and safe delivery [55] In support of the World Health Organization’s recommendations on working with TBAs [56], policymakers and practitioners need to have protocols that define their new roles and ensure collaboration between TBAs, skilled attendants, and staff in the referral system. Moreover, they need to be trained, and the other team members sensitized on their new roles. National and subnational governments need to strengthen the referral system from the community to other levels of health systems and ensure periodic monitoring and evaluation of the status.

Use of clean delivery kit

One study [46] examined the impact of the use of a Clean Delivery Kit (CDK) on maternal peripartum infection. This cluster RCT multicenter study in Bangladesh, India, and Nepal with a population of 40,602 over 24,36, and 24 months respectively showed that CDK does not improve maternal survival [46]. This study had also focused on the effects of hand washing, the authors concluded that hand washing was critical for maternal survival among home deliveries in India and [46].

The implications of the findings to policy makers and practitioners in health systems and public health outcomes is that clean delivery practices should be adhered in all deliveries. The use of CDK should be implemented within the health system with consideration to resource availability, health care workers’ factors focusing on their knowledge and practice but not as a single intervention.

Research and practice implications

Research implications

To enhance infection prevention in maternal care, it is essential for future studies to explore cross-sectoral collaborations that bring together diverse stakeholders, including healthcare providers, policymakers, and community organizations. Additionally, there is a pressing need for large-scale randomized controlled trials to evaluate the effectiveness of multi-component health systems interventions across various LMIC settings. Such trials would provide crucial insights into the feasibility and impact of comprehensive interventions. Moreover, the incorporation of dynamic system modeling should be considered to assess how individual interventions interact with the broader health system. This approach would enable a better understanding of how these interactions influence maternal health outcomes, providing a more comprehensive view of the system’s response to health interventions.

Practice implications

Infection prevention and control in maternal health should be prioritized by both governments and global health stakeholders through efforts to strengthen health systems and implement quality improvement initiatives. These actions are crucial for reducing maternal infections and improving overall healthcare outcomes. Furthermore, health policymakers and practitioners should consider integrating system-wide strategies that address multiple facets of healthcare delivery. This includes capacity building, community engagement, and the improvement of infrastructure and supply chain management. Such strategies will play a vital role in preventing peripartum infections and ensuring a more effective and sustainable approach to maternal health care.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this review include the very extensive search conducted with no year restriction. However, studies published before 2012 backward have not contributed to the findings. The studies in the review comprised of current information considering that more than half of the studies were published after 2015 with a large proportion 3 years ago. The review adopted the [7] definition of maternal peripartum infection even though many studies used the term ‘Puerperal Sepsis’ which therefore required the correct identification in line with the [7] definition of the term ‘maternal peripartum infection’. A key limitation of this study lies in the focus on English publications, potentially excluding relevant non-English papers from the review. Another limitation is that the scope of the review did not consider challenges which are important factors in appraising interventions.

Conclusion

This systematic review has highlighted various interventions implemented in LMICs to prevent maternal peripartum infection. The interventions such as antibiotic prophylaxis, skin preparation, selfcare training with the issuance of instructional guidelines to the women show promising results. However, all of these interventions remain fragmented and focused on singular components of care rather than integrated, systemwide strategies. Therefore, there is need for broad, multi-level interventions and further research to generate stronger evidence and inform effective implementation. Health systems strengthening through policy, capacity building for staff, service delivery and infrastructure can play a vital role in lowering maternal morbidity and mortality caused by infections in LMICs. Adopting a holistic, systems-based framework has the potential for transforming maternal health outcomes across the World.
