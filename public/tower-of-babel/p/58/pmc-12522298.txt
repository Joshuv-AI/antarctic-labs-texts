The homogeneity of training for graduate students of psychiatry in different universities in China
Authors: Shi Yuan-Hong, Gao Xia, Lu Yang, Tong Ping
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe cultivation of psychiatry graduate students is essential to addressing the shortage of mental health resources in China. However, no systematic research has yet explored the homogeneity of their training programs across universities. This study investigates the homogeneity of both training outcomes and processes at different institutions, providing evidence-based insights that could inform policy-making.MethodsFrom June 2022 to December 2022, surveys were administered through an online questionnaire platform to selected universities. The survey encompassed general information, the training process, and the outcomes of the training.ResultsAmong the four types of universities, significant differences were observed in various aspects of the clinical training process, including teaching rounds (P < 0.05), case discussions (P < 0.01), small lectures (P < 0.01), teaching conditions (P < 0.01), and total scores (P < 0.01). During the research training process, significant differences were also noted in research conditions (P < 0.01) and total scores (P < 0.05). In the humanistic quality training process, only morality showed significant differences (P < 0.01). Additionally, the total score of the overall training process exhibited significant variation (P < 0.01). Regarding training outcomes, graduation examinations differed significantly among the four universities (P < 0.01). Correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between clinical conditions (r = 0.327, P < 0.01), research conditions (r = 0.453, P < 0.01), and total scores (r = 0.307, P < 0.05). However, no significant correlation was found for humanistic quality conditions (r = 0.044, P > 0.05).ConclusionThe training outcomes for psychiatry graduate students demonstrate a high degree of homogeneity; however, the consistency of the training process requires enhancement. Greater emphasis should be placed on both daily clinical practice and research integration to ensure comprehensive skill development.

Background

China’s mental health services are significantly underdeveloped compared to those in developed countries. China has only 5.2 mental health professionals per 100,000 residents, far below the average of 28.2 in developed countries. Specifically, there are merely 1.54 psychiatrists per 100,000 people in China, compared to 11.87 in Japan [1, 2]. Disparities also exist among provinces; Guangxi has 2.29 professionals per 100,000 residents [3], whereas Anhui has only 1.46 [4]. Furthermore, approximately 29% of psychiatrists in China lack a medical degree, and 14% have no formal education [1]. The lifetime prevalence of mental disorders in China is estimated at 16.6% [2, 5]. To address these challenges, the government has increased investment, raising the number of psychiatric beds per 100,000 people from 17.1 in 2010 to 31.5 in 2015, an increase of 89.9% [2]. Practicing psychiatrists play a critical role in mental health care and ideally should hold advanced degrees, such as a master’s degree in psychiatry. Currently, over 60 universities and training bases in China offer relevant master’s programs. However, there is an uneven distribution and allocation of resources among these universities. For example, they are primarily concentrated in Beijing, Guangdong, Jiangsu, Zhejiang, and Liaoning provinces. Each year, only a limited number of students are admitted, with the maximum intake being fewer than 20. This situation reflects an imbalance in China’s overall medical education and training system. In 2020, China had 445 higher medical education institutions, which collectively enrolled 70,480 new medical students [6]. The majority of these institutions are located in major cities, where educational resources are more abundant in economically developed regions. Upon graduation, few medical students specializing in non-psychiatry fields choose to pursue careers in psychiatry. However, the proportion of psychiatry students who express a willingness to work in the field remains relatively high. For instance, a survey conducted in Chongqing, China, found that 80.8% of 422 psychiatry students considered becoming psychiatrists as their future career path [7]. The quality and consistency of their training outcomes will significantly influence the future development of mental health services in China.

Homogenization refers to the phenomenon in which products within the same category become increasingly similar in terms of performance, appearance, and marketing strategies [8]. Gosselin et al. [9]. contend that although educational homogenization may suppress local demands and cultural diversity, its role in promoting standardized education outweighs these drawbacks. Homogeneity signifies a high level of consistency among individuals or groups with respect to specific attributes, behaviors, or characteristics, as observed in social, statistical, commercial, and linguistic contexts. It represents the outcome of the homogenization process. In the context of mental health master’s training, it pertains to the uniformity of processes and outcomes across universities.

There is limited research on the homogeneity of postgraduate training in psychiatry. A 1999 survey explored the attitudes of devout Muslims toward education, indicating that the Muslim community in Brussels increasingly embraced non-Islamic schools for basic education, which to some extent reflected the homogenization of educational practices beyond ethnic characteristics [10]. Another study evaluated the homogeneity of public health education across Italian medical schools, revealing uniformly structured public health courses with regional adaptability [11]. A recent study implemented standardized curricula to train residents in ultrasound theory and skills, achieving highly consistent outcomes [12]. However, in the field of medical education, China still faces significant challenges in standardizing resident training and qualification certification. A study comparing the training and certification systems for colorectal and anorectal surgeons in China with those in the United States, Europe, and Japan revealed that China’s standardized education in this specialty lags behind by several decades [13]. Similarly, a gap persists between China’s psychiatry graduate education and that of developed countries such as the United States and European nations.‌.

It is crucial to examine the homogenization of psychiatry graduate training across universities in China, as it can provide a robust foundation for informed decision-making by relevant departments. It may also provide some useful information for medical education practices on a global scale. However, research in this area remains scarce. This study investigated psychiatry graduate students from representative universities nationwide using a self-designed questionnaire that covered both training processes and outcomes. The questionnaire was distributed through Wen-Juan-Xing and administered centrally. Items were scored systematically, and statistical methods were employed to assess the degree of homogenization in psychiatry graduate training at Chinese universities.

Methods

Study design and subjects

From June 5 to December 6, 2022, a total of 71 master’s students in psychiatry completed and submitted the “Questionnaire on the Training Process and Outcomes of Graduate Students in Psychiatry” via Wenjuanxing. Five submissions were excluded due to issues such as duplication, inappropriate response times (< 300 s or > 2000 s), errors, or random answers, leaving 66 valid questionnaires for analysis. The participants were from 17 universities across nine provinces, including Sichuan, Liaoning, Jiangsu, Shandong, Anhui, Zhejiang, Hubei, and Beijing. The number of participants per university ranged from one to 26, with three universities contributing five or more respondents: University 1 (n = 26), University 2 (n = 9), and University 3 (n = 5). The remaining universities were grouped as “Other Universities” (n = 25). Survey content and scoring

The research team independently designed the questionnaire, drawing on prior studies and relevant policies for resident physician training and psychiatric master’s degree programs [14, 15]. The questionnaire demonstrated excellent reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.90. For each indicator, three to six multiple-choice questions were developed, each assigned a specific score. The weighting method employed in this study is the expert investigation method, a subjective weighting approach. The determination of the weights was based on relevant documents and standards concerning the standardized training of resident physicians and the education of master’s degree candidates in psychiatry. These standards and indicators represent the collective expertise of national-level professionals, rather than reflecting the personal opinions of the experts involved in this study.

The survey assesses the clinical, research, and humanistic qualities of both training processes and outcomes. Specific indicators and scoring criteria are illustrated in Fig. 1. The training process is structured into three components: clinical (50 scores), research (40 scores), and humanistic quality (10 scores), with a total score of 100 scores. Clinical training encompasses departmental orientation (6 scores), teaching ward rounds (8.5 scores), case discussion (7.5 scores), mini-lectures (5.5 scores), daily clinical work (11.5 scores), departmental exit examination (4 scores), and teaching conditions (7 scores). Research training involves literature reading (7 scores), research thinking training (11.5 scores), daily research work (11 scores), and research working conditions (10.5 scores). Humanistic quality training emphasizes mental health (3 scores), physical health (3 scores), and morality (4 scores).

The training outcomes are categorized into three domains: clinical competence (50 scores), research proficiency (40 scores), and humanistic qualities (10 scores), with a total score of 100 scores. Clinical competence includes the National Medical Licensing Examination (NMLE, 10 scores), departmental evaluations (9 scores), work achievements (12 scores), clinical skills (11 scores), and the graduation examination (10 scores). Research proficiency encompasses scholarly articles (14 scores), research projects (14 scores), literature review skills (3 scores), and foreign language proficiency (9 scores). Humanistic qualities cover psychosomatic health (4 scores), morality (3 scores), and self-assessment (3 scores).

Survey methodology

A meticulously designed survey was distributed through Wen-Juan-Xing. During the investigation period, members of the research team will contact the teaching directors of psychiatry departments at relevant universities. After providing a comprehensive explanation of the study’s content, objectives, and methodology, the directors will be invited to participate. If they agree, they will coordinate the involvement of all willing psychiatry master’s students. These participants will then complete the questionnaire collectively in a classroom setting at a designated time. This standardized procedure is designed to ensure data authenticity, validity, and a high response rate. Upon completion, invalid responses were systematically excluded according to the predefined criteria.

Statistical analysis

Count data are presented as percentages and analyzed using chi-square tests. Quantitative data that follow a normal distribution are expressed as mean (SD), with group differences evaluated using One-way ANOVA and intra-group comparisons conducted via LSD tests. Non-normally distributed data are reported as median (25th, 75th percentiles) and analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis H-test. The relationship between training outcomes and processes is assessed using Pearson Correlation analysis. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSSAU, with P < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of demographic and baseline characteristics

No statistically significant differences were observed among the four groups in terms of gender, age, grade, training category, and work experience (P > 0.05), as shown in Table 1. This suggests the groups were well-matched.

Comparison of the training processes

The comparison of the training process is presented in Table 1. For the clinical training process (Fig. 2A), statistically significant differences were observed among the four types of universities (P < 0.01). Pairwise comparisons revealed that University 1 scored significantly higher than University 3 (P < 0.01) and the other universities (P < 0.05). Subcategory results demonstrated the following: Significant statistical differences were found among the four groups in teaching ward rounds [P < 0.05; pairwise comparisons showed that University 1 was significantly higher than University 2 (P < 0.05) and the other universities (P < 0.01)], case discussions [P < 0.01; pairwise comparisons showed that University 1 was significantly higher than University 3 (P < 0.05) and the other universities (P < 0.01)], small lectures [P < 0.01; pairwise comparisons showed that University 3 and the other universities were significantly lower than University 1 (P < 0.05 and P < 0.01) and University 2 (all P < 0.05)], teaching conditions [P < 0.01; pairwise comparisons showed that University 1 was significantly higher than the other three university types (all P < 0.01)], and the total score of the clinical training process [pairwise comparisons showed that University 1 was significantly higher than University 3 (P < 0.01) and the other universities (P < 0.05)]. No significant statistical differences were observed in orientation upon entering the department, clinical work, and exit examinations (P > 0.05).

For the research training process (Fig. 2B), statistically significant differences were observed among the four groups in terms of research work conditions (P < 0.01). Pairwise comparisons indicated that University 2 had significantly higher scores compared to the other three types of universities (P < 0.05 or P < 0.01). Furthermore, significant differences were detected in the total score of the research training process (P < 0.05), with pairwise comparison results aligning with those for research work conditions. No significant differences were found in literature reading, research thinking skills training, or daily research activities (P > 0.05).

For the humanistic quality training process (Fig. 2C), no significant differences were observed among the four university types in the overall scores (P > 0.05). Subcategory analysis revealed that morality scores differed significantly among the universities (P < 0.01), with University 1 scoring higher than University 3 (P < 0.05) and the other universities (P < 0.01). No significant differences were observed for mental and physical health scores across the universities (P > 0.05).

A significant statistical difference was observed in the total scores of the training process among the four groups (P < 0.05), as illustrated in Fig. 2D. Further pairwise comparisons indicated that the scores of the other universities were significantly lower than those of University 1 and University 2 (all P < 0.05).

Discussion

The study’s broad scope renders conventional methods, such as literature reviews, expert opinions, and established scales, inadequate [16–20]. Consequently, our questionnaire was developed by referencing official state documents and policies. There are approximately 100 items in the questionnaire. The questionnaire is divided into sections, and integrating all results for analysis necessitates the implementation of a weighting system. This involves assigning differential weights to items based on their relative importance, thereby enabling more accurate statistical analysis. The primary challenge lies in determining appropriate weights, as weighting directly reflects the significance of each item within the evaluation framework [21]. Three common methods for determining weights exist: subjective weighting, objective weighting, and comprehensive weighting [22–24]. For this study, the expert survey method (a subjective approach) was deemed most suitable. Weights were determined using relevant documentation and standards for resident and psychiatric master’s training.

The differences in psychiatry postgraduate training across universities provide several critical insights: (1) Universities exhibit significant variation in their training processes yet achieve comparable outcomes, suggesting that multiple pathways can lead to the same destination. However, this outcome may also be attributed to the fact that, despite variations in implementation processes, standardized national examinations have led to a convergence of educational outcomes. (2) The degree of homogeneity varies by training type: clinical training shows the least homogeneity, followed by research training, while humanistic quality training demonstrates the highest level of homogeneity. (3) Core teaching activities (e.g., teaching ward rounds, case discussions, and small lectures) and teaching conditions in clinical training display low homogeneity. However, daily clinical work and the outcomes of clinical training exhibit high homogeneity, indicating that active participation in clinical work plays a crucial role in shaping training outcomes. (4) Universities emphasize different aspects depending on their strengths; for instance, University 3 (a 985 institution) excels in research training but scores lower in clinical and humanistic quality training compared to University 2 (a non-985/non-211 university). (5) Despite variations in working conditions, research training exhibits high homogeneity. (6) In humanistic quality training, morality is the only aspect with low homogeneity.

Correlation analysis not only validates the conclusions drawn from difference analysis but also highlights the following points: (1) Although university training outcomes exhibit no significant differences, the training process remains a critical factor influencing results. (2) Daily work serves as the predominant factor shaping outcomes in both clinical and research training. (3) Teaching conditions have a more limited impact on clinical outcomes than anticipated. (4) Literature-reading skills might be underappreciated in the context of research training.

According to currently available evidence, this study constitutes the first comprehensive analysis of the consistency of training programs for psychiatry graduate. The results of this research may provide a valuable reference for relevant governmental agencies in the formulation and development of educational policies. Based on the research findings, we propose the following recommendations: (1) Enhance the homogeneity of the training process, particularly in clinical training. Although no significant difference was observed in overall training outcome scores, a positive correlation exists between training outcomes and the training process itself. Moreover, the training outcomes presented in our survey data do not fully reflect the overall effectiveness of individual student development. However, it is clear that these outcomes are directly linked to the training process. Therefore, improving homogeneity remains critical. In 2022, China issued guidelines for resident physician training and began implementing them. With strengthened supervision mechanisms, teaching homogeneity is expected to improve significantly [25, 26]. (2) Place greater emphasis on daily clinical and research activities. This study demonstrates that both clinical practice and research are profoundly influenced by daily work routines. Medicine is a highly practical discipline; only through consistent and ongoing practice can theoretical knowledge be effectively applied and further refined [27–29]. While standardized teaching plays an essential role, it serves as a complement to, rather than a substitute for, daily clinical practice. (3) Emphasize the critical role of literature reading skills in research training. Our study demonstrates that robust literature reading skills significantly enhance research effectiveness. By engaging in extensive reading, researchers can broaden their perspectives, stay abreast of global advancements, refine their experimental techniques, and improve their ability to write professional papers [30, 31]. (4) Strengthen the cultivation of humanistic qualities. While most universities provide courses on humanistic qualities, the extent and quality of implementation differ widely. Although humanistic qualities may not directly correlate with technical skills, they form the foundation for all professional achievements [32–35]. Only individuals who possess altruism, selflessness, and a strong moral character can genuinely contribute to societal progress [36–38]. The importance of humanistic qualities is globally acknowledged; however, the results of this study reveal a relatively weak correlation with training outcomes. This discrepancy may stem from the fact that key components of humanistic qualities—such as moral judgment, empathy, and communication skills—were not sufficiently addressed in the design of the questionnaire. Despite this, the high consistency observed in the train of humanistic qualities underscores its significance and warrants further emphasis and integration in relevant educational frameworks. Therefore, it is recommended to incorporate structured training modules on humanistic qualities, including medical ethics, doctor-patient communication, and patient-centered care, to elevate the overall standard of medical services. (5) To further enhance training homogeneity, policy-level interventions are advised, such as establishing national certification standards or formulating unified curricula. (6) The findings indicate that certain universities have demonstrated outstanding performance in the training of psychiatry master’s students. For instance, University 3 has developed an effective scientific research training system, while University 1 has implemented a robust clinical training model. These examples should be promoted as benchmarks for other institutions to reference and adopt. Finally, although China’s current efforts in improving the homogeneity of psychiatry postgraduate training primarily focus on clinical skills, research capabilities, and humanistic qualities, equal attention should be given to training in cross-cultural psychiatry, which is increasingly essential in a globalized healthcare environment [39].

Certainly, this study recognizes several limitations. First, Despite efforts to ensure data quality, potential biases introduced by participants may still exist in the online survey. Second, the sample did not include all eligible postgraduate students from certain universities, resulting in a relatively small and potentially non-representative sample. Third, classifying universities with fewer than five student responses into a single category labeled “Other universities” may introduce inconsistencies in evaluating the homogeneity of the data. Fourth, the overall sample size is relatively small, and the distribution of samples across institutions is uneven; this imbalance in sample allocation may undermine the generalizability of the research findings. Fifth, the weighting method requires further refinement, and specific sections of the self-developed questionnaire need improvement for greater clarity and reliability. Sixth, the cross-sectional design precludes longitudinal insight into training evolution. Seventh, reliance on self-reported measures risks social desirability effects, particularly regarding humanistic qualities. Future research should prioritize on-site surveys, aim to expand the sample size, and focus on optimizing questionnaires and methodologies to enhance the robustness of findings.

Conclusion

Our research reveals statistically significant differences in certain aspects of the training processes and correlations in others across universities. The findings suggest a low level of homogeneity in training methodologies but a high degree of consistency in outcomes among psychiatry postgraduate students. Placing emphasis on daily clinical practice and research is crucial for enhancing training quality. Future studies should prioritize field investigations, broader scopes, larger sample sizes, and refined methodologies to further validate these conclusions.
