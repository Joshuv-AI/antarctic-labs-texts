Safer at school? Experiences of families on school closure during COVID-19 in an informal settlement in Nairobi, Kenya
Authors: Elizabeth Echoka, Violet Wanjihia, Prisca Otambo, Lilian Nyandieka, Zipporah Bukania
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic led to widespread closures of learning institutions globally. In informal settlements, unique and multifaceted challenges were encountered. This article examines the specific challenges faced by families following school closures due to COVID-19 in an informal settlement in Nairobi, Kenya.MethodsQualitative data were collected in December 2020. In-depth interviews with 30 household heads and 15 community representatives were conducted. Responses were recorded, transcribed and reviewed. Thematic analysis identified four major themes: strain on food and economic consequences, increased risk of teen pregnancy and exploitation, gaps in childcare and supervision and educational disruption and increased dropout risk.ResultsSchool closures during COVID-19 not only disrupted education but also exacerbated food insecurity and economic strain, as families lost access to school feeding programs and experienced reduced incomes. Limited supervision of children increased their exposure to risky behaviours, while vulnerable groups, particularly girls, faced heightened risks of exploitation and teen pregnancy. Educational disruptions further worsened the situation, with many students, especially girls, at risk of permanently dropping out.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the critical role of schools in marginalized communities and suggest the need for targeted support and inclusive educational policies that address the needs of informal settlement residents during periods of pandemic vulnerability.

Introduction

In March 2020, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared the spread of COVID-19 as a global pandemic and individuals and families across the globe were confronted with unprecedented disruptions and concerns. The first concern was the direct effect of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 infection. The second concern was related to coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) containment measures, which carried unique challenges. Among the most affected sectors was education, with governments implementing school closures to reduce social contacts between students to disrupt transmission.1 The COVID-19 pandemic caused the greatest disruption to education systems, with almost 1.6 billion students across >190 countries affected. This disruption impacted 94% of the global student population, with 99% of students in low- and lower-middle-income countries being affected.2,3 While school closure was critical in limiting transmission, it introduced significant challenges, particularly for families in informal settlements who face unique socio-economic vulnerabilities such as inadequate living conditions, overcrowding, restricted access to clean water and sanitation and insufficient healthcare and nutrition.4,5

Kenya underwent one of the most prolonged school closures during COVID-19 and was the last country in East Africa to fully reopen its schools. The nationwide closure, which lasted 9 months, had a profound impact on many families.6 In Nairobi's informal settlements, the sudden shutdown of schools had particularly far-reaching consequences. Schools in these communities often serve as safe spaces for children, providing meals and protection from harmful environments.7 As the closures dragged on, children were cut off from these vital resources, with concerns raised about extended closures.3

While extensive research has shed light on the impact of school closures on children, there is still a limited understanding of how families, particularly those in vulnerable communities, experienced the challenges of these closures. Much of the existing evidence on school closures during the COVID-19 pandemic comes from high-income countries.8–10 Most studies on COVID-19 and school closures have primarily focused on children's mental health7,11,12 and learning outcomes.13–15 There is limited information on the challenges faced by families, especially those living in informal settlements, who already contend with significant socio-economic hardships and are at higher risk of adverse health and social outcomes.4,5 Families in these settings often face greater economic strain, limited access to essential resources and increased caregiving burdens, issues that remain largely underexplored in the current literature. This article aims to contribute to addressing this research gap by examining the lived experiences of families related to school closures during COVID-19 in an informal settlement in Nairobi.

Informal settlements in Kenya, particularly in urban areas, are characterized by high population density, limited access to clean water and sanitation, substandard housing and insufficient public services. These structural disadvantages exacerbate the vulnerability of residents, especially children, to public health emergencies and disruptions in education during crises such as the COVID-19 pandemic.6 By examining these unique experiences, this article aims to provide key insights for informing future pandemic responses, ensuring that the needs of vulnerable populations are better addressed. Understanding the challenges families face is essential for the design of more equitable and inclusive interventions that safeguard children's education and well-being, particularly in marginalized communities during periods of crises.

Methods

Study design, setting and participants

This article is based on qualitative data from a mixed methods study whose aim was to assess challenges of social distancing, community perceptions and coping mechanisms during the early COVID-19 pandemic (Kenya Medical Research Institute [KEMRI] 2021, COVID 19 study in Kibera, unpublished). The study was conducted in December 2020 in the Kibera informal settlement in Nairobi, the capital city in Kenya. Kibera was selected primarily because it is the largest and longest established informal settlement in Nairobi. Kibera is densely populated, comprising 14 villages hosting approximately 500 000 people across an area of just 2.5 km2.

Qualitative data were collected through in-depth interviews (IDIs) with 30 household heads (HHs) and 15 community representatives (CRs) to explore perceptions on social distancing measures, willingness to conform to social distancing measures, challenges and inequities experienced and coping mechanisms of individuals and the community in observing social distancing measures. The interviewees were selected using purposive sampling to capture a diverse range of perspectives. HHs were recruited from different villages within Kibera to reflect variability in socio-economic status and household composition. CRs, including village elders, local youth and women leaders and community health volunteers, were identified through community networks and local liaison officers familiar with the area. Inclusion criteria included being >18 y of age and having lived in Kibera for at least 1 y. Recruitment continued until data saturation was achieved.

The IDIs were conducted face to face by researchers (EE, PO and LN). The interviews were conducted in either Swahili or English, documented through both audio recordings and written notes, and lasted 45–60 min. All interviews were conducted in secure and enclosed designated places within the community setting. The interview guide was pretested in the field and adjusted based on the pretest findings. The interviews were conducted until data saturation was achieved.

This study was approved by the KEMRI Scientific and Ethics Review Unit (KEMRI/SERU/4047). The National Commission for Science, Technology, and Innovation provided research clearance. Written informed consent was obtained from individual participants prior to their participation in the study.

Data analysis

All interviews were transcribed and translated verbatim from Swahili into English by a professional translator. Data were analysed manually, using an inductive thematic approach, whereby themes, codes and categories emerged from the data.16 The analysis team was well qualified to conduct the coding and analysis of the transcripts. EE and PO hold doctoral degrees and have extensive experience in qualitative research, while LN holds a master's degrees and has qualitative research experience. Three researchers (EE, LN and PO) independently read the transcripts and field notes several times to gain familiarity with the data. They coded randomly selected transcripts and created initial codes by labelling meaningful data sections and highlighting key concepts based on the participants’ responses to the initial open questions about how the containment measures had affected them. Similar codes were grouped into categories, which were re-examined to identify overarching themes. The analysts reviewed coded segments and themes to ensure accuracy, examining any contradictory cases. Discrepancies in coding were resolved by rechecking transcripts until saturation was reached. The entire team conducted an interpretative analysis to understand the context, themes and connections between them. Key findings are illustrated with selected excerpts from the transcripts.

Strain on food and economic consequences

The closure of schools during COVID-19 had significant food and economic impacts on families. With children at home, parents faced challenges providing adequate nutrition, as they could no longer rely on school meals to supplement their children's diets. One participant shared:You know when children are in the house, they must eat, and you have no option. So, they would rather be in school. Now you're in the house with the children, and this poses a big challenge. (HH 11)

You know when children are in the house, they must eat, and you have no option. So, they would rather be in school. Now you're in the house with the children, and this poses a big challenge. (HH 11)

Another parent echoed similar concerns, noting:It is hard…children used to go to school in the morning and eat lunch there, but now it's difficult because they are at home, and you have no income to take care of them. (HH 03)

It is hard…children used to go to school in the morning and eat lunch there, but now it's difficult because they are at home, and you have no income to take care of them. (HH 03)

These sentiments were reinforced by one CR:It has really affected many children at home…families used to depend on the children eating at school. So, that really affected them because now the children are home, and families don't have food. (CR 09)

The financial burden on families also increased significantly, as parents struggled to meet the needs of children who were home all day. Participants discussed the additional economic pressure brought on by school closures:You know, when children are in the house, you have such a burden because everything is required. (HH 29)

Gaps in childcare with discipline and safety concerns

The extended school closures during COVID-19 raised significant concerns about the discipline, safety and overall well-being of children. Parents and community members noted the challenges of managing children at home without the structured environment provided by schools. One respondent remarked:The schools are closed, and that is a challenge for parents. Children are not as safe at home as they are at school. (CR 06)

The schools are closed, and that is a challenge for parents. Children are not as safe at home as they are at school. (CR 06)

Another added:They don't have a teacher to correct them…The parent is there, but school offers more protection than home. (HH 17)

They don't have a teacher to correct them…The parent is there, but school offers more protection than home. (HH 17)

The lack of supervision and structure led to behavioural issues, with some children getting involved in delinquent activities, as one CR shared:Closing of schools has brought harmful side effects, to be honest. Many children have formed groups, and you find that your boy child is involved in theft. (CR 08)

Closing of schools has brought harmful side effects, to be honest. Many children have formed groups, and you find that your boy child is involved in theft. (CR 08)

I am even embarrassed to talk about it. The other day we had a village meeting, and we talked about our girls who are pregnant. (CR 09)

Educational disruption and increased dropout risk

Participants expressed significant concerns about the negative impact of school closures on academic progress, highlighting issues such as interrupted learning, diminished attachment to school and increased dropout rates.

Fear was expressed that many students would not return to school due to involvement in negative behaviours such as drug abuse and crime:The majority will not go back to school because some have gotten into drugs…Most of them do not see themselves going back to school. I know one who is in Form Three, and he has gotten into drugs. (HH 21)

Another added:They are engaging in theft and smoking hard drugs. Will this kind of child ever go back to school? I am a village elder, I have seen them, and I keep asking myself if they will really return to school. (CR 13)

Discussion

The findings from this study emphasize the broad and lasting impacts of school closures during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in informal settlements. School closures not only disrupted education but also deepened food insecurity and economic strain as families lost access to school feeding programs and faced reduced incomes. Children's safety and discipline were compromised due to limited supervision, leading to increased engagement in risky behaviours, while vulnerable groups, such as girls, faced heightened risks of exploitation and teen pregnancy. Educational disruptions further worsened the situation, with many students, especially girls, at risk of permanently dropping out of school.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this article is that it captures the experiences of families in an informal settlement, offering valuable and localized insights into how COVID-19 school closures uniquely affected marginalized communities. This focus enables a deeper understanding of the specific socio-economic and educational challenges these families faced. Despite being localized, the findings have broader relevance, as they resonate with global experiences of school closures in low-income and resource-poor settings, making them applicable in similar contexts. However, a limitation of the study is that it primarily addresses the immediate or short-term impacts of school closures, without exploring longer-term consequences, such as dropout rates or academic recovery after schools reopened. Also, while a participatory approach would have enriched the findings, it was not feasible due to the lockdown measures in place. Finally, while the study offers valuable insights into the lived experiences of families in Kibera during school closures, another key limitation is the small and purposively selected sample size relative to the population of the informal settlement. As such, the findings may not be generalized to all households in Kibera or other informal settlements. However, the themes identified offer critical perspectives that can inform targeted interventions and policy debates around education and child welfare in underserved urban areas, particularly in the context of public health emergencies.

Strain on food and economic consequences

Our findings underscore the significant impact of school closures during the COVID-19 pandemic on food security and economic strain, aligning with similar research. With schools no longer providing meals, many families, especially those in informal settlements, struggled to meet their children's nutritional needs, exacerbating food insecurity. A study in Kenya similarly found that families’ ability to provide food was compromised due to income losses following the implementation of COVID-19 containment measures.6 It has also been observed that students in low-income settings who depend on school feeding programs as their primary source of nutrition were particularly affected, as their families struggled to make up for the missed meals.17–21 Beyond food insecurity, it is documented that school closures also increased the financial burden on households due to the need to address other basic needs while children remained at home.6,21 School closures also disproportionately affected girls, whose access to essential resources like sanitary pads was lost, which were typically provided at school.22 These findings highlight that the effects of school closures extended beyond education, deepening food insecurity and economic hardship. Future policies must incorporate comprehensive support systems to ensure food security and access to essential resources for vulnerable households during similar crises.

Gaps in childcare with discipline and safety concerns

Our findings indicate that unsupervised periods during school closures raised significant concerns about child safety and discipline, with increased instances of drug use and other social vices. This aligns with other studies showing an increase in substance abuse among teenagers due to greater access, boredom and peer influence during extended periods at home.21,23 Additionally, parents, particularly those in informal employment, faced the dual pressures of childcare and economic strain, as pandemic-related restrictions reduced their income. Additionally, due to parents working long hours to meet basic needs, they had limited time to properly supervise their children.23 This highlights the critical role schools play in offering a safe and structured environment. Without this safety net during school closures, many children were left vulnerable to negative influences and unsafe conditions.20,21 This emphasizes the need for robust community support systems and alternative protective measures during such crises to ensure children's well-being is safeguarded, even in the absence of formal education. Moreover, these challenges call for interventions that prioritize both educational continuity and child protection in future emergency responses.

Increased risk of exploitation of girls and teen pregnancy

Our findings, in line with the global literature, suggest that prolonged school closures during the COVID-19 pandemic heightened the vulnerability of young girls to exploitation and teen pregnancy in informal settlements. Girls have been recognized as especially vulnerable during the pandemic.24,25 Other studies report a notable increase in teen pregnancies during the pandemic, raising concerns that many girls were unable to resume their education after becoming pregnant.26 Similar patterns were observed during the Ebola outbreak, where pregnant girls were denied re-entry to school following reopening.27 It has also been suggested that school closures deprived many students of access to comprehensive sex education, heightening their vulnerability to early and unintended pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections and gender-based violence.6 Additionally, there are views the loss of livelihoods in low-income households pushed some children, particularly girls, into income-generating activities to support their families, further increasing their risk of exploitation. The suspension of school programs, like meal provision and sanitary supplies, further heightened the vulnerability of disadvantaged children.22 These findings highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions to safeguard vulnerable girls during crises. Addressing both economic support for families and the continuity of girls’ education and well-being is crucial to mitigating the long-term consequences of such disruptions.28

Educational disruption and increased dropout risk

The findings reflect the far-reaching effects of school closures, from educational disruption to the long-term risks of dropout and increased engagement in negative behaviours. These findings are consistent with findings from other studies, where concerns regarding diminished attachment to school, academic stagnation and rising dropout rates were prominent during the pandemic.29,30 In resource-limited settings, the risk of learning loss is particularly severe, with children who lack access to remote learning at an increased risk of never returning to the classroom.28,31 Post-closure school data also reveal gender disparities, with girls at higher risk of dropping out in some contexts.30 In Kenya, for instance, 16% of girls and 8% of boys ages 15–19 y did not return to school within the 2 months following reopening.25 The concern over interrupted learning highlights the urgent need for interventions to address the long-term effects of such disruptions.28 Fears of increased dropout rates emphasize the importance of strategies to encourage re-enrolment. Collaboration between schools, communities and policymakers is critical to creating supportive environments that motivate students, especially those who may have fallen into negative behaviours during the closures, to return to school.

Conclusions

This study’s findings underscore the vital role of schools in informal settlements like Kibera, not only in providing education but also in supporting children's nutrition, safety and psychosocial well-being. The experiences of families during the COVID-19 school closures in this informal settlement reveal the deep interdependence between educational infrastructure and broader social systems in vulnerable urban settings. While the findings are specific to Kibera, they highlight issues likely shared across similar contexts, reinforcing the need for responsive policies that are context specific and prioritise the needs of informal settlement residents during future crises.
