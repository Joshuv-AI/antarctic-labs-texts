Body mass index and failed induction of labor: A cohort study
Authors: Lise Qvirin Krogh, Tine Brink Henriksen, Katja Albert Taastrøm, Sidsel Boie, Anne Cathrine Maqving Kjeldsen, Jim Thornton, Jens Fuglsang, Julie Glavind
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInduction of labor (IOL) is a common intervention in industrialized countries. Failed induction is frequently reported, yet there is no consensus on its definition. Since the primary goal of IOL is to initiate labor, progress to the active phase is a more relevant measure of success than the surrogate of caesarean birth. Previous studies on the influence of body mass index (BMI) on the risk of failed IOL are limited. Most rely on data from the United States and define failed IOL primarily by caesarean delivery. This study aims to explore the association between maternal BMI and failed IOL, defined as failure to progress to the active phase of labor.Material and MethodsWe studied 22 114 term, singleton women undergoing IOL in the Central Denmark Region from 2013 to 2022. Women with spontaneous prelabor rupture of membranes, uterine scar, or fetal demise were excluded. The main outcome measure was failed IOL, defined as not reaching cervical dilation of 6 cm or more. BMI, our exposure, was modeled as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines and as a categorical variable stratified according to the World Health Organization BMI groups. Adjusted logistic regression was used in both models to assess the association between BMI and failed IOL.ResultsProportions of nulliparous women with failed IOL ranged between 4% for normal weight and 10% for obesity class III. Adjusted odds ratios for nulliparous women for failed IOL were 1.5 (95% confidence intervals [CI] 1.3, 1.7) for overweight, 1.8 (95% CI 1.4, 2.3) for obesity class I, 2.7 (95% CI 2.2, 3.3) for obesity class II, and 2.9 (95% CI 1.4, 6.0) for obesity class III compared to women with normal weight. In parous women, there was a similar but less pronounced association between BMI and failed IOL with a <2% absolute risk of failed IOL. A similar pattern was found when BMI and failed IOL were modeled using restricted cubic splines.ConclusionsIn singleton women with induced labor at term, increasing BMI was associated with higher odds of failed IOL; this association was more pronounced in nulliparous women.Keywords: active phase of labor, body mass index, failed induction of labor, induction of labor, restricted cubic spline analysis

Key message

In this large, multicenter cohort from a Scandinavian country, higher body mass index was associated with a proportionally increased odds of failed induction of labor in singleton women at term. The association was less pronounced in parous women.

INTRODUCTION

Induction of labor (IOL) is defined by the World Health Organization as the process of artificially stimulating the uterus to initiate labor. 1 Labor can be induced by medical or mechanical methods or by a combination of these. 1 IOL is a common procedure in industrialized countries, with rates of more than 30% in some parts of Europe and in the United States. 2 , 3 However, in the Nordic countries, the rates range from 14% to 22%. 2

Maternal body mass index (BMI) is associated with complications in pregnancy and during labor, 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 and the risk increases with higher BMI. 4 , 6 , 8 Furthermore, higher BMI is associated with slower labor progress in spontaneous and induced labor. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12

A commonly studied outcome with IOL is the risk of “failed IOL.” The definition of failed IOL varies across the literature, with some definitions focusing on the inability to initiate labor, such as the inability to achieve a degree of cervical dilatation after oxytocin administration or the inability to perform amniotomy, whereas others rely on the failure to achieve vaginal delivery. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Various other definitions, based on the duration of the latent phase or the time from IOL to birth, have also been proposed. 17 , 18 , 19 Since the primary goal of IOL is to initiate labor 1 and enable a trial of vaginal birth, the objective should focus on whether labor is successfully initiated and progresses into the active phase. Once a woman reaches the active phase of labor, a range of factors—not necessarily related to the induction process—can influence the course of labor and the mode of delivery. 20 Therefore, whether labor fails to progress into the active phase appears to be a meaningful measure of failed IOL. 21

Previous studies examining the effect of maternal BMI on the risk of failed IOL are limited, with the majority conducted in the United States, and typically defining failed IOL by caesarean delivery. 15 , 22 , 23 , 24 There is a lack of larger studies from northern Europe, where the prevalence of obesity is much lower, 4 , 5 , 25 , 26 and where antenatal care and clinical handling of IOL may differ considerably. This study aims to explore the association between maternal BMI and failed IOL, defined as failure to progress to the active phase of labor.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Design and setting

We studied a cohort of women within the Central Denmark Region registered with a pregnancy course and birth from January 1, 2013 to December 31, 2022. Information was retrieved from the Business Intelligence Portal, a repository housing patient‐specific information on pregnancy and birth extracted from electronic medical records.

During the study period, the five delivery sites within the Central Denmark Region cared for some 14 000 deliveries per year, 27 corresponding to around one‐fourth of the total number of deliveries in Denmark.

Approval for retrieval of the information was obtained from the legal authority of the Central Denmark Region (identifier: 1‐45‐70‐98‐23), and data were handled according to Danish and European Union law, adhering to the General Data Protection Regulations. According to the Danish Research Ethics Committee Law, ethical approval is not required for register‐based studies. 28

During the study period, the Danish national obstetric guidelines recommended inducing labor in postterm women with a prepregnancy BMI of 35 kg/m2 or more at a gestational age of 41 weeks and 0 days and women with lower BMIs at 41 weeks and 3 days through 41 weeks and 5 days. 29 , 30 Throughout the study period, several induction methods were used, but oral misoprostol or, when possible, artificial rupture of membranes were usually the first choice. 31 The recommendations for IOL methods did not vary according to BMI. 31 Women with low‐risk pregnancies who were induced with misoprostol were primarily managed using an outpatient regimen. 32 We defined IOL as the use of any cervical ripening agent: medical (prostaglandin or oxytocin) or mechanical (cervical balloon catheter or artificial rupture of membranes).

Population

We identified women with induced labor, a single fetus in cephalic presentation at term, and no uterine scar, corresponding to Robson Ten Group Classification System 2a (nulliparous women) and 4a (parous women), who received antenatal care throughout their pregnancy within the Central Denmark Region. Women with prelabor rupture of membranes or intrauterine fetal demise were excluded. 33

Exposure

Prepregnancy or early pregnancy BMI was retrieved from the Business Intelligence Portal. We categorized BMI into six strata 34 ; underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24 kg/m2), overweight (25–29 kg/m2), obesity class I (30–34 kg/m2), obesity class II (35–39 kg/m2), and obesity class III (≥40 kg/m2).

Outcomes

The main outcome was failed IOL defined as failure to reach a cervical dilation of 6 cm or more (i.e., failure to reach the active phase of labor). Our secondary outcome was mode of birth.

Potential confounding factors and intermediate variables

From existing evidence and by using a directed acyclic graph (Figure 1), the following covariates were identified as potential confounders: maternal age at time of conception, parity, smoking, preexisting medical conditions (defined as diabetes mellitus type 1 or 2, hypertension, inflammatory bowel disease, or psychiatric disorder), pregnancy‐related medical conditions (defined as gestational diabetes mellitus and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy), year of delivery, and delivery site. Medical conditions were identified according to International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision.

Statistical analyses

The study protocol, including the plan for analyses, was registered in Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/VG2W8) on January 1, 2024, prior to data analyses.

Continuous variables were presented as means with standard deviations if normally distributed or medians with interquartile ranges if nonnormally distributed. Categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages.

Missing data for BMI, parity, year of delivery, and reaching the active labor phase were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations, under the assumption that data were missing at random. Fully conditional specification was applied. The imputation models included information from the variables with missing data as well as maternal age, smoking status, delivery site, preexisting medical conditions, obstetrical conditions, and induction methods. Fifty imputed data sets were made.

In two models, we examined the association between maternal prepregnancy and early pregnancy BMI and failure to reach the active labor phase. In the initial analyses of the association between BMI group and failed IOL, we used logistic regression to estimate adjusted odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals using normal weight as the reference. Separate logistic regression models were conducted for each mode of birth: spontaneous vaginal, instrumental birth, and caesarean birth, to assess the association between BMI and the likelihood of each specific outcome following IOL. In the second analyses, the association between BMI and failed IOL was modeled with BMI as a continuous variable using restricted cubic splines to allow for a potential nonlinear relationship with the middle value of the normal weight group as the reference, corresponding to a BMI of 21.7 kg/m2. Five knots were then placed at the BMI values for which BMI groups changed from one to the next. 34 Additionally, the restricted cubic spline model was reproduced with alternative knots determined by the data. All analyses were adjusted for prespecified potential confounders. Deliveries within the same delivery site were modeled through robust standard errors as clusters. As specified a priori, we stratified all analyses by parity after confirming that parity was an effect modifier in order to examine whether the effect differed between nulliparous and parous women.

All analyses were conducted using STATA 18.0 (College Station, TX, USA).

RESULTS

A total of 26 130 women with IOL gave birth in the Central Denmark Region during the study period (Figure 2). We excluded women with previous Caesarean section or a uterine scar (n = 2055), spontaneous prelabor rupture of membranes (n = 1932), or intrauterine fetal demise (n = 29). Baseline characteristics for the remaining 22 114 women are shown in Table 1. Most women had a normal weight. Overall, 55% were nulliparous, and this proportion decreased with increasing BMI. The number of women delivering at a regional hospital increased with higher BMI, while the number of women delivering at a university hospital decreased. Overall, the most frequent indication for induction was postterm pregnancy (25%).

In nulliparous women, the proportion of women experiencing failed IOL increased with increasing maternal BMI, for example, women with underweight failed IOL in 2% of cases, while it was 10% in women with obesity class III (Table 3). In parous women, the proportion of women with failed IOL was stable throughout BMI groups and was less than 2%. In nulliparous women, the adjusted odds of failed IOL increased with increasing BMI. Compared to normal weight, overweight, obesity class I, II, and III were associated with increased adjusted odds of failed IOL, with adjusted odds ratios of 1.5, 1.8, 2.7, and 2.9, respectively (Table 3). In parous women, compared to normal weight, overweight and obesity class I were associated with increased adjusted odds of failed IOL, with odds ratios of 2.0 and 1.8, whereas obesity class II and class III were not (Table 3). The findings presented in Table 3 are also illustrated in a Forest Plot (Figure 3). When we modeled the association between BMI and failed IOL with BMI as a continuous variable, there was a similar pattern (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

We found that nulliparous women with higher BMI had higher odds of failed IOL. We found a similar pattern between higher BMI and lower odds of vaginal birth. Still, 90% of women with obesity class III reached the active labor phase, with 58% giving birth vaginally. Among parous women, we found higher BMI was associated with increased odds of failed IOL; however, the trend was not progressively upwards and less pronounced than that observed in nulliparous women. Fewer than 2% of parous women never reached the active phase, and more than 90% gave birth vaginally.

Our study is the first of this size from a Nordic country to investigate the association between BMI and the odds of failed induction. Our findings are consistent with a large American study involving 80 887 women with IOL, which also found higher BMI to be associated with increased odds of failed IOL, with very similar estimates. However, our study focused exclusively on induced labor and stratified analysis by parity, whereas the American study included both induced and augmented labor and did not stratify analyses by parity. Further, it defined failed IOL as a caesarean section, regardless of the timing during labor. 15

Our finding that 5% or fewer had a failed IOL is consistent with previous studies which found that less than 4% failed to progress into the active labor phase after IOL, with definitions of the active phase varying from cervical dilatation of at least 4 cm and 90% effacement to cervical dilation of at least 6 cm regardless of effacement. 13 , 19 , 35 , 36 , 37 However, none of these studies investigated the association with maternal BMI.

A large American cohort study (n = 118 978) of women with spontaneous and induced labor found that labor progressed more slowly with higher BMI regardless of parity. Notably, women who did not reach full cervical dilation were excluded. 9 In another study of some 500 women with term IOL, maternal weight was inversely associated with the cervical dilation rate. However, the authors did not report the proportions of women who progressed into the active labor phase. 11 A multicenter cohort study of some 600 nulliparous women found that higher BMI was associated with a slower cervical dilation rate. 12 This association was found in women with both spontaneous and induced labor. By comparison, a recent large Norwegian multicenter cohort study of 47 942 women with spontaneous onset of labor found that increasing BMI was associated with a shorter estimated median duration of the active second stage of labor. However, the study included only women with spontaneous labor, which limits the applicability of their findings to populations undergoing IOL. 38 Finally, a recent cohort study of 448 women with term IOL investigated the duration of the latent and active labor phases according to BMI. In this cohort, and in comparison with women with normal BMI, women with obesity were more likely to give birth by caesarean section following labor arrest in the first stage of labor (defined as failed IOL by the authors), whereas women with a normal BMI were more likely to have caesarean section for arrested labor in the second stage. 10

In our study, we defined active labor as cervical dilation of 6 cm or more, consistent with more contemporary labor curves. 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 Although Danish practice during the study period followed the NICE guideline, 43 which defines active labor as regular contractions and progressive cervical dilation from 4 cm, data on contraction patterns were not consistently available in our records. Diagnosing the transition from the latent phase to the active phase of labor is inherently challenging, as it varies between individuals and no reliable tool exists to precisely determine its onset. Any definition is therefore based on clinical estimates with an inherent margin of error. 44 To ensure consistency and to avoid underestimating failed inductions, we adopted the 6‐cm threshold. We acknowledge the uncertainty associated with this diagnostic cutoff and its implications for interpreting our findings and comparing results across studies.

With a large and growing number of women with obesity worldwide undergoing IOL, understanding the association between BMI and the risk of failed IOL has important clinical implications. Our results suggest differences in the odds of failed IOL depending on maternal BMI. This information is valuable for planning IOL, as it can help guide expectations and preparations for birth. While this association should not discourage women and their caregivers from considering IOL, it is important to use this knowledge to better prepare both parties for the potential course of the induction process. We argue that understanding the likelihood of reaching active labor is essential when IOL is considered a clinical decision to which planned caesarean is the alternative, once the indication for delivery is established. This early phase of labor represents a critical decision point and offers patient‐relevant insight distinct from overall mode of delivery.

Given the association between maternal BMI and the risk of failed IOL, future studies should focus on exploring strategies to reduce the heightened risk of failed IOL in women with overweight and obesity. Our results suggest that the primary focus in this effort should be on nulliparous women.

Currently, recommendations on IOL regimes do not differ by BMI in clinical obstetric guidelines on induction management. 31 , 45 , 46 This is although some studies indicate requirements for higher prostaglandin dosages and the use of combined induction methods with increasing BMI. 47 , 48 , 49 Future studies are needed to evaluate if IOL regimes should vary by BMI, especially in nulliparous women, both regarding dosage and whether combined methods may lower the failed induction rate in women with higher BMI. It has also been suggested that high BMI is also in itself an indication for induction, and randomized controlled trials are ongoing. 50 , 51

The strengths of our study include its large sample size using patient record information reported real time on cervical dilation from induction start throughout labor and birth. Because of this, we were able to adhere to the definition of active labor based on 6 cm of cervical dilation. Although the rate of missing data was low, we performed multiple imputation, and we used two robust, predefined statistical models with adjustment for potential confounders; however, point estimates changed minimally after adjustment. Our analyses were stratified by parity. Labor induction during the study period was managed according to national guidelines, minimizing variation in practice across hospitals. Furthermore, IOL regimens were similar across BMI groups within hospitals, reducing the potential for confounding by indication related to differences in treatment approaches with different indications. Various induction methods and indications for induction were included, increasing the generalizability. Additionally, our reporting adhered to the STROBE guidelines. 52

Our study is subject to some limitations inherent to its design. First, we relied on routinely collected data retrieved from medical records, which may introduce a risk of measurement bias in the primary outcome. This also limited our possibility to adjust for potential confounding from variables that were not recorded in the medical records, such as socio‐economic status. Furthermore, BMI was primarily self‐reported, which may introduce information bias. If underreporting of BMI is more pronounced among women with the highest BMIs, this could lead to misclassification into lower BMI categories and bias our results towards no association. 53 We adjusted our analyses for gestational diabetes mellitus and hypertensive pregnancy disorders being two frequent pregnancy‐related conditions associated with overweight and obesity and thereby modifying the risk of confounding by indication. Despite this, the risk of confounding by indication is still high, and the association we have found could be an expression of the influence of the condition leading to IOL rather than the maternal BMI itself. Residual confounding from, for example, individual preferences regarding IOL and timing of birth at the provider level, but also preferences and requests from women in the cohort may also differ between BMI groups; for example, if women with higher BMI were more likely to request a Caesarean section in the latent phase, this would lead to an inflated proportion of these women not reaching the active labor phase. This would bias our results, entailing overestimation of the association. Additionally, the study size was determined by the available cohort rather than a prospective sample size calculation. Nonetheless, we believe that the sample provides meaningful insight. Finally, since we had no exact information on dosage and duration of the ripening and induction agents used, we are unable to determine if practice varied between BMI groups despite uniform guidelines.

CONCLUSION

In singleton pregnant women with induced labor at term, increasing BMI was associated with proportionally increased odds of failed IOL. The association was most pronounced in nulliparous women. However, the overall incidence of failed induction, defined as not reaching the active phase of labor, in the cohort remained low, ranging from 2% to 10% in nulliparous women and less than 2% in parous women.
