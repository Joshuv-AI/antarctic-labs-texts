Random subspace ensemble-based detection of false data injection attacks in automatic generation control systems
Authors: Sami M Alshareef
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutomatic Generation Control (AGC) systems in smart grids are increasingly vulnerable to cyber-attacks, particularly False Data Injection (FDI) attacks, due to their reliance on information and communication technologies. These vulnerabilities pose significant threats to the reliable operation of power systems. To address this challenge, this research paper introduces the machine learning (ML) based cyberattack detection technique designed to identify FDI attacks with the highest accuracy proficiently. The study involves a comprehensive analysis of three features: the original discrete signal feature, the cycle-to-cycle-based feature, and the sample-to-sample-based feature. These features are utilized for detecting FDI attacks and distinguishing them from normal load variations. The research meticulously collects data by simulating diverse FDI attacks, including step attacks, pulse attacks, random attacks, and normal load variation cases on an AGC power system. Four ML classifiers are selected and compared for the classification task. The simulation results reveal that the sample-to-sample-based feature proves highly effective in distinguishing FDI attacks compared to the original signal and cycle-to-cycle-based features. Notably, the results indicate that the random subspace ensemble (RaSE) classifier, utilizing sample-to-sample-based features, effectively identifies and classifies all normal and FDI attacks. This research provides valuable insights into the potential of ML techniques for enhancing FDI attack detection in the AGC of power systems. It provides a potential pathway for overcoming the limitations associated with traditional model-based FDI detection methods.Keywords: Data-injection attacks, Cyber-attacks, Automatic generation control, Machine learning, Cyber-physical systems, Cyber security, Situational awareness, Random subspace ensemble classifier

Introduction

Background

The rapid growth in smart grid technology has revolutionized the power industry, introducing significant advancements that hinge on seamless interconnectivity through sophisticated communication technologies [[1], [2], [3]]. At the core of these smart grids lies the AGC system, which plays an essential role in balancing power generation and load demand [4,5]. The AGC serves as the control system responsible for maintaining the grid's frequency and minimizing deviations in tie-line power. It achieves this by adjusting the output power of generators based on data collected from sensors distributed across the grid [6]. The AGC algorithm utilizes information on frequency deviation and tie-line power flow from the Supervisory Control and Data Acquisition (SCADA) telemetry system. Within the SCADA platform, a remote terminal unit (RTU) is responsible for measuring the both frequency and power within each area. This data is transmitted through the measurement channel (MC). Subsequently, the system calculates the Area Control Error (ACE) every 2–4 s, considering frequency and power exchange deviations. The ACE, representing the deviation from the desired operating point, is then utilized by the AGC to calculate the necessary control actions. These actions are subsequently dispatched to each grid area, adjusting its operational settings accordingly [7,8]. A control center periodically collects frequency and tie-line power flow data to calculate ACE, ensuring effective responsiveness of the AGC to maintain grid stability [9]. Control commands from the AGC are conveyed to power plant RTUs via the control channel (CC), managing power generation in response to area control imbalances. In essence, the AGC ensures grid stability by fine-tuning power generation based on real-time data, facilitating effective control and management of the entire power system [10,11].

In addition to AGC, Wide Area Monitoring Protection and Control (WAMPAC) systems are critical components of modern smart grids [12]. WAMPAC schemes rely on IEC 61850 communication protocols to enhance grid monitoring, protection, and control capabilities across wide geographic areas. These protocols utilize various communication media, including wireless, fiber optics, and microwave, to facilitate increased communication between local and remote substation devices. While WAMPAC systems significantly improve grid reliability and situational awareness, they also introduce new vulnerabilities [[12], [13], [14]]. The geographical dispersion and limited physical network protections at remote substations make them particularly susceptible to cyber threats.

The reliance on extensive communication networks, including those used by WAMPAC and AGC systems, increases the power grid's exposure to cyber-attacks [15,16]. An attacker gaining access to system measurements, such as frequency and power flow, can manipulate this data, leading to potentially devastating consequences for the grid.

Various types of cyber-attacks, including time-delay attacks [17], denial-of-service (DoS) attacks [18], resonance attacks [19], and FDI attacks [20], pose significant threats.

FDI attacks are particularly concerning as they involve the manipulation of sensor data within the power system to deceive control systems and operators into making erroneous decisions. Unlike overt physical attacks, FDI attacks exploit vulnerabilities in data handling and processing, making them harder to detect but with potentially severe consequences [21]. For example, deceptive frequency data injected into regular measurements could mislead AGC into issuing incorrect commands, leading to power imbalances and possible cascading failures. To comprehend the impact of an FDI attack on AGC, consider injecting deceptive frequency data into the regular measurements. This manipulation is intended to deceive the AGC, leading it into erroneous calculations. This could lead to AGC issuing incorrect commands based on miscalculated ACEs [2,22]. For instance, when the ACE is positive, signaling an excess of power generation in an area, the standard response from AGC would be to reduce power generation. However, the introduction of deceptive data that yields a negative ACE could mislead AGC into perceiving an under-generation scenario. This could lead to a counterproductive command to increase power generation, thereby worsening the already existing over-generating situation. Undetected attacks have the potential to cause harm to consumer electronics and utility equipment, trigger generator disconnections, and initiate cascading effects that ultimately lead to power interruptions or blackouts.

Given the critical nature of these threats, there is a pressing need for advanced detection techniques to safeguard power systems. This research focuses on developing innovative FDI detection techniques tailored specifically for power system applications. By leveraging ML algorithms and advanced signal processing techniques, the proposed detection framework aims to identify anomalous behavior indicative of FDI attacks, thereby enhancing the security and resilience of power grid operations.

Previous work

AGC becomes an appealing target for power grid attacks due to its extensively automated nature, and minimal reliance on human supervision. Once compromised, the impact of an attack can escalate rapidly. Fast identification of attacks targeting AGC is essential for upholding the power system stability and reliability [7]. Numerous publications have focused on enhancing the cybersecurity of power systems by addressing the detection of FDI attacks in AGC. These efforts aim to improve the detection and response mechanisms, ensuring the resilience of AGC against potential cyber threats. Several authors have proposed model-based FDI attack detection methods for AGC in power systems, with a notable emphasis on Kalman filter-based techniques. In these approaches, the AGC system states are estimated, and the residuals, denoting the disparity between measured and estimated signals, are analyzed. Cyber-attacks are identified when the residue exceeds a predefined threshold, often utilizing the norm of the residue for detection, as outlined in Ref. [11]. To improve the effectiveness of thresholds settings, researchers explore diverse statistical models for residual signals, including CUSUM based detector [10], design Chi square detector [20], Euclidean detector [23], χ2 detector [24], two stage kalman filter [25], and extended kalman filter [14] aiming to enhance FDI attack detection capabilities. Apart from the kalman filter application, Khalaf et al. have proposed utilizing particle filters for detecting FDI attacks in AGC systems [9]. Ameli et al. introduced the residual function of Unknown Input and Output (UIO) observer [26] and stochastic unknown input estimator (SUIE) [27] for FDI attack detection in power system. Additionally, methods such as probability density function (PDF) based approaches [28], and dynamic watermarking [29,30] have been suggested to further enhance attack detection capabilities. However, it's important to note that all these methods fall into the category of model-based approaches, and they share common limitations. Despite their real-time implementation simplicity, these model-based methods face challenges due to their reliance on accurate mathematical models. This constraint significantly restricts their applicability, particularly in large and complex power systems where achieving a precise mathematical state space representation is inherently challenging. Inaccurate modeling may compromise state estimation and lead to incorrect predictions. Moreover, these techniques often rely on thresholds to differentiate normal load variations from cyber-attack scenarios, posing challenges in selecting optimal thresholds under load disturbances [25]. Addressing these limitations may involve integrating model-based methods with ML algorithms to augment the cybersecurity resilience of AGC in power systems. ML based methods offer a robust framework for studying complex dynamical systems, providing a means to estimate nonlinear systems. Using ML for detecting FDI attacks offers numerous advantages. ML models demonstrate adaptability by adjusting to evolving attack strategies, ensuring ongoing protection as attackers modify their techniques. Additionally, ML algorithms efficiently process large datasets in real-time, enabling timely detection and response to potential FDI attack, even in complex systems. Moreover, ML algorithms enable early detection of FDI attack by identifying abnormal data patterns before significant damage occurs, thereby enhancing the chances of mitigation. Continuous learning allows ML models to improve over time and remain effective against emerging threats in the constantly evolving cybersecurity landscape. Automation of the detection process enables quicker response times and reduces reliance on manual intervention, freeing up security personnel to focus on more complex tasks. Overall, leveraging ML for FDI attack detection offers a proactive and adaptive approach to cybersecurity, enhancing the resilience of systems against emerging threats. In the realm of contemporary power systems characterized by complex operations, ML models have proven adept at tackling a myriad of challenges. Qu et al. [31] introduced a random forest-based ML technique for detecting FDI attacks in AGC systems. Their method was compared with other classifiers such as K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), linear Support Vector Machine (SVM), Decision Tree (DT) SVM, Naïve Bayes (NB), and linear discriminant reduction algorithms (LDA). The results showcased the heightened efficacy of the random forest technique. Modelling FDI attacks

This section deals with the modeling of FDI attacks, in the Power Systems Computer Aided Design (PSCAD) using Electromagnetic Transients including DC (EMTDC) as the simulation engine, focusing on their implications for AGC systems. The literature has extensively explored FDI attacks targeting both measurements and control signals, including frequency deviation, tie-line power, and ACE signals. These templates encompass strategies such as manipulating frequency deviation signals to distort measurements, injecting deceptive information into tie-line power signals to affect power flow judgments, and introducing spurious data to ACE signals to influence control actions. Before delving into FDI attack modeling, it's crucial to consider several key points. The security and resilience of power systems are paramount for ensuring the reliable delivery of electricity to consumers. With the increasing digitization and interconnectedness of power grid infrastructure, the vulnerability of these systems to cyber threats has become a significant concern. While overt physical attacks on power grid infrastructure, such as opening breakers or tripping generators, are well-recognized threats, covert attacks, such as FDI attacks, pose a subtler yet equally significant risk to system integrity. FDI attacks involve the manipulation of sensor data within the power system to deceive control systems and operators into making erroneous decisions. Unlike overt attacks that directly target physical components, FDI attacks exploit vulnerabilities in the data handling and processing systems, making them inherently harder to detect. However, the consequences of successful FDI attacks can be severe, potentially leading to disruptions in power system operations, grid instability, and even widespread outages. One might question the priority of focusing on FDI attack detection when adversaries capable of executing such attacks would likely have access to critical infrastructure, enabling them to perform more disruptive actions with greater ease. While this concern is valid, it is essential to recognize that FDI attacks serve as a stealthy threat that can amplify the impact of overt physical attacks. Furthermore, adversaries may employ FDI attacks as part of a broader strategy to undermine the integrity and reliability of power systems. By exploiting vulnerabilities in data processing and control systems, adversaries can create confusion, disrupt normal grid operations, and potentially cause cascading failures that lead to widespread outages. Therefore, while protecting critical infrastructure against direct physical attacks remains a top priority, addressing the threat of FDI attacks is equally important for safeguarding power system operations. Effective detection and mitigation of FDI attacks can enhance the resilience of power systems against a range of cyber threats, ensuring the continued delivery of reliable electricity to consumers. To conduct research on FDI attacks in real power systems, it's assumed that the attacker can obtain a subset of system measurements through the communication network. This subset includes data on frequency and power flow, which are transmitted wirelessly and accessible to the attacker. However, the attacker is unable to physically breach the system or manipulate control decisions within the control center or Energy Management System (EMS). This constraint is crucial to prevent potential cascading failures across the power grid if the network were compromised. Under these conditions, protecting the EMS becomes a top priority for energy providers. Safeguarding the integrity of the EMS is essential to defend against system-wide disruptions. Although securing every communication link in the power system is desirable, it's important to recognize the significant costs involved, especially in large power systems heavily reliant on communication networks that handle numerous signals. Furthermore, the techniques employed in the detection of FDI attacks must effectively identify stealthy variants of these attacks. For an FDI attack to be classified as stealthy, it must successfully bypass the bad data detection algorithms commonly utilized within control centers. Additionally, grid operators often conduct additional validation checks on incoming data, specifically monitoring for sudden and significant changes in measurements over short time intervals. To ensure efficacy, attackers must strategically select their attack parameters, including magnitude and rate of change, to achieve the desired impact while avoiding the triggering of data quality alarms. In the context of AGC systems, for instance, attackers typically refrain from inducing abrupt spikes or substantial fluctuations in the ACE to remain undetected.

One effective approach to executing stealthy FDI attacks is by introducing slowly varying attack signals, which align with the characteristics of stealthy attacks and are more challenging to detect. This method involves adding signals in the form of step inputs, pulse inputs, and random inputs to the original frequency error or power deviation measurements within the AGC system. By carefully adjusting the magnitudes and durations of these inputs, the step, pulse, and random attacks exhibit the subtle characteristics typical of stealthy FDI attacks. A detailed modeling of each type of FDI attack is provided in the subsequent sections.

In this section, the critical aspect of feature selection within the context of the AGC model is considered. The effective discrimination between normal load changes and FDI attacks relies heavily on the careful identification and utilization of pertinent features. The objective is to pinpoint features that not only distinguish between routine load variations and potential cyber threats but also ensure the robustness and reliability of the AGC system. For this purpose, five distinct FDI attack locations have been identified. For each scenario, five signals, namely ΔF1, D ΔF2, ΔPTie, ACE1, and ACE2, are continuously measured. The continuous-time representation of the original signal serves as a raw and unaltered reflection of instantaneous signal changes over time, providing a real-time snapshot of the generator's dynamics. B.Cycle-to-Cycle Difference Signal (XC)

Cycle-to-Cycle Difference Signal (XC)

The cycle-to-cycle feature, [XC], calculates the difference between sample values taken one cycle apart. This feature excels in capturing changes occurring over a cycle and exhibits lesser sensitivity to rapid variations. Particularly valuable for identifying cyber-attacks or anomalies with a cyclical pattern. For instance, the first sample extracted from the two cycles of the original signal is denoted as X2[N]−X1[0], where N represents the number of cycles. Subsequently, the second sample is represented by X2[N+1]−X1[1], and this sequential pattern continues until the last sample of the two cycles, denoted as X2[2N−1]−X1[N−1], as outlined in Table 3.

C.Sample-to-Sample Difference Signal (XS)

Sample-to-Sample Difference Signal (XS)

The sample-to-sample feature [XS], is derived by computing the difference between consecutive samples within one cycle. This feature aids in detecting rapid variations and anomalies, offering a high-resolution view of deviations between individual samples. During the steady state of power, the sample difference is expected to be constant, while during a cyber-attack condition, it will be significantly higher. For the sample-to-sample feature, the difference between consecutive samples within one cycle X1[1]−X1[0] is computed as depicted in Table 3. It's important to understand the key difference between the original discrete signal and the sample-to-sample signal. With the original discrete signal, each value is considered individually. However, with the sample-to-sample signal, the focus is on the difference between two consecutive values instead. The choice of features, including the original discrete signal, cycle-to-cycle features, and sample-to-sample features, was made based on several considerations. These features were selected for their relevance to the specific problem domain addressed in the study, each capturing different aspects of the signal behavior crucial for detecting FDI attacks in the system. While traditional time-domain or spectral features are commonly used in signal processing tasks, they might not fully capture the intricacies of system dynamics in cyber-attack detection contexts. The chosen features, such as cycle-to-cycle and sample-to-sample variations, offer a more nuanced representation of signal characteristics, essential for accurate classification. It's crucial to note that the effectiveness of features often depends on the specific dataset characteristics and problem requirements. The approach used in this study was tailored to the data's characteristics and the classification task's requirements. Therefore, the selection of signal features was driven by their relevance to the problem domain, their ability to capture intricate signal behaviors, and their demonstrated effectiveness through experimentation and validation. This proposed approach ensures that distinctive features are derived for each case, which are then fed into ML classifiers to discern potential cyber-attacks within the AGC system. The fundamental philosophy underpinning this concept is based on the premise that, under normal load changes, these differences will approach zero. In contrast, during cyber-attack scenarios, these differences will exhibit higher values. This nuanced distinction forms the basis for the effective detection of cyber-attacks within the AGC system. By comprehensively capturing the variations in the selected features, the system can proactively identify FDI attacks, contributing to its overall stability and security in the face of potential threats.

Machine learning

Classifiers

ML classifiers play a crucial role in strengthening the security of power systems, particularly in identifying FDI attacks. Leveraging historical data, these classifiers discern patterns and relationships, enabling the differentiation between normal system behavior and potentially malicious deviations. Their significance lies in enhancing the robustness and efficiency of intrusion detection systems, thereby upholding the integrity and reliability of power systems. In the context of FDI attacks, the precise differentiation between diverse normal load variations and FDI attacks is crucial for the accurate operation of the AGC system. After gathering diverse event characteristics, a ML approach is employed to train a model using these features. This training process enables the classifier to understand patterns and relationships, empowering it to provide precise responses for unseen data. Ultimately, this contributes to the overall security and stability of power systems. This research encompasses the evaluation of four prominent ML classifiers, namely NB, KNN, RaSE, and DT. Their performance assessment will utilize various metrics including precision, recall, accuracy, and F-measure. A comparative analysis will be conducted to highlight the efficacy of each classifier, emphasizing their individual strengths and weaknesses. Following sections provides concise yet comprehensive explanations of each classifier, elucidating their core concepts to lay the groundwork for the subsequent comparative analysis.

Training and testing of classifiers

This section outlines the methodology for generating a comprehensive dataset to train and test ML classifiers in the context of a two area AGC system. The goal is to simulate diverse scenarios, encompassing both normal load variations and various FDI attacks, using PSCAD/EMTDC software. The generated dataset will serve as the foundation for training and evaluating the effectiveness of ML classifiers in detecting FDI attacks within the AGC system. The analysis covers six different load variation (increment/decrement) scenarios. To evaluate how load increases and decreases affect the frequency deviation of Generator 1, initially a load increase and decrease of 0.1875 per unit is applied, followed by another load increase and decrease of 0.07 per unit at simulation time 30 s, as shown in Fig. 9. Load increments/decrements can be considered as activated/deactivated of charging events of a fleet of electric vehicles whether via extreme or fast charging stations. The subsequent application of these load changes induces alterations in the frequency response, showcased in Fig. 10. Upon examination of Fig. 10, it is apparent that during the load increment scenario, a reduction in frequency occurs. Conversely, in the load decrement scenario, an observable overshoot in the frequency change is evident. Crucially, for both load variations, the frequency deviation eventually returns to zero due to corrective actions implemented by the AGC. This observation signifies a restoration to a normal and stable state in the power system. The effective collaboration between the AGC system and governor control actions plays a pivotal role in restoring frequency deviations to zero, providing reassurance regarding the power system's resilience and its ability to maintain stability under varying load conditions. For each area, 25 scenarios of load increment and load decrement are simulated, with a repetition of the procedure for Area 2. Additionally, simultaneous load reduction in Area 1 and load increment in Area 2, and vice versa are also carried out. This approach results in a total of 150 load variation cases to be used for training and testing as indicated in Table 4. To distinguish between normal load changes and FDI attacks, three types of attacks (step, pulse, and random) are applied at five different locations. Each attack type results in 10 cases for each location, culminating in a total of 50 cases per attack and 150 FDI attack cases for all types of attacks as depicted in Table 5. Magnitude duration for step and pulse attacks ranges from 0 to 0.01 per unit, while for random attacks, it varies from 0 to 0.002 per unit. Pulse attack scenarios include variations in amplitude and pulse duration. This comprehensive approach results in a dataset of 300 cases, comprising 150 cases of normal load variations and 150 cases of FDI attacks, representing severe conditions that could lead to AGC performance degradation and potential power blackouts if not accurately detected.

This research has employed a 50 % data split to ensure a robust evaluation, as this ratio provides a substantial amount of unseen data for testing, which better reflects real-world scenarios in power systems. This entails randomly selecting 75 load variation cases and 75 FDI attack cases for training, leaving an equal number for testing. This strategy ensures that the testing data remains unseen by the classifier, facilitating an unbiased evaluation of its performance. The decision for a 50 % split, deviating from the conventional 80-20 split, is driven by specific considerations in the AGC domain. AGC datasets, especially those simulating diverse scenarios of normal load variations and FDI attacks, often face constraints in size. The 50 % division addresses this limitation, striking a balance between model learning and evaluation. Additionally, in situations of limited dataset size, the risk of overfitting is mitigated by maintaining a substantial testing set, crucial for assessing the classifier's generalization on unseen data. This tailored approach acknowledges the complexity of the AGC system, allowing for a more comprehensive evaluation of the classifier's ability to generalize across various scenarios, thereby contributing to the development of a robust and adaptable ML classifier for AGC system security.

Conclusion

This research paper introduces a ML based technique for detecting FDI attacks in the AGC of power systems. The study systematically explores three distinct features to identify the most suitable one for effective FDI attack detection. Four widely recognized classifiers, namely k-nearest neighbors (kNN), Naïve Bayes (NB), Decision Tree (DT), and Random Subspace Ensemble (RaSE), are employed for the analysis. Training and testing data are generated by simulating 300 cases of FDI attacks and normal load variations, including step attacks, pulse attacks, and random attacks with diverse characteristics. The dataset is divided, with 50 % allocated for training and an equivalent portion reserved for evaluating classifier performance in distinguishing between FDI attacks and normal load variation scenarios. Results indicate that, the sample-to-sample-based feature parameter emerges as the most suitable and effective in detecting both FDI attacks and normal load variation scenarios across all classifiers. Notably, the RaSE classifier demonstrates exceptional performance, achieving perfect precision, recall, accuracy, and F-measure scores of 100 % for the sample-to-sample-based feature. A comparative analysis with existing techniques reinforces the effectiveness of the proposed ML based approach. These attributes collectively position the proposed technique as a robust and versatile solution for FDI attack detection in the AGC of the power systems. Overall, this research contributes to advancing the field of cybersecurity in power systems by introducing a ML based approach that offers high accuracy and reliability in detecting FDI attacks, thereby enhancing the security and resilience of power grid operations.
