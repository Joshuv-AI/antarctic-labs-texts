A survey on the diagnosis and management of neonatal hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy in sub-saharan Africa
Authors: H E Naburi, S Pillay, F Houndjahoue, S Bandeira, G T J Kali, A R Horn
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSub-Saharan Africa (SSA) has the highest burden of neonatal hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) in the world. However, there are few descriptions of HIE management in SSA and therapeutic hypothermia (TH) is considered controversial. A web-based survey was distributed to doctors across SSA in 2023. Adequate responses were received from 136 doctors across 43 of 48 countries. Therapeutic hypothermia was available in 13 countries, most frequently in private institutions compared to other settings (69% vs. 28%; P = 0.004). Over 90% of respondents who provided TH, appropriately cooled neonates to rectal temperatures of 33.5 °C before age 6 h, for 72 h, and 79% used automated cooling methods. Intubated ventilation and electroencephalograms were more available where TH was used (81% vs. 55%; p = 0.004 and 65% vs. 8%; p < 0.001 respectively). Indicators of intrapartum hypoxia were more frequently defined with TH provision, including early pH (79% vs. 21%; p < 0.001), base deficit (76% vs. 20%; p < 0.001), and ventilation at age 10 min (87% vs. 53%; p = 0.001). Despite the variation in resources and management of HIE, most respondents had standardised protocols (76%). Introduction

Globally, 1.2 million cases of hypoxic ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) occur annually; the majority (96%) of these babies are born in low and middle-income countries (LMICs) with the highest incidence (14.9 per 1 000 live births) in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA)1. Perinatal asphyxia is a major cause of HIE in LMIC2, with pooled prevalence of 9.1% and 18% in Central and East African countries respectively3. However, most perinatal asphyxia is not associated with HIE and if used as a proxy for HIE will overestimate it2. Similarly, HIE estimates which are not based on standardised biochemical data are also likely to be inaccurate2. A population study in South Africa noted more than two-fold variation in the incidence of HIE in the same population, depending on the defining criteria and assessment methods. The authors suggested the term, “intrapartum-related neonatal encephalopathy” could be used to recognise the potential but variable contribution from intrapartum events, but will also overestimate the incidence of HIE4.

Meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials, showed that therapeutic hypothermia (TH) reduces mortality and neurological deficits in term and near-term newborns with moderate-severe HIE. There was no difference in outcome between selective cerebral hypothermia (SCH) and whole body hypothermia (WBH) methods, and there was a greater effect of TH on mortality reduction among neonates with HIE in low and low-middle income countries versus middle-income or high-income countries5. Low-cost cooling devices including phase-changing material cooling mattresses6, manual gel-packs7, and servo-controlled gel packs8, have been effectively used to induce TH. The use of solid ice caps9, and servo-controlled fans10, have also been explored, but not recommended. However, the TH for neonatal encephalopathy in low-income and middle-income countries (HELIX) trial in South Asian LMICs, showed increased mortality in cooled neonates with HIE, and no difference in survival without disability11. The HELIX study is the largest randomised controlled trial of TH in LMICs and may therefore be expected to be most representative. In addition, the participating neonatal units fulfilled the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) criteria for safe administration of TH11,12. The lack of benefit was therefore particularly concerning and the authors of the HELIX trial recommended that TH for HIE should not be used in LMICs. However, several authors have contested the recommendation, citing; potential inclusion of babies with fetal hypoxia before labour, cooling after the onset of seizures, lack of objective biochemical criteria for acute peripartum hypoxia, and different disease severity profiles compared to centres in other LMICs, particularly South Africa13–15. A recent systematic review of TH for HIE, which included the HELIX trial, showed a significant reduction in neurological disability in babies in LMICs, but no difference in mortality or seizures16.

The diagnosis and management of HIE and the use of TH has been reported in few SSA countries, with diverse strategies. Most studies are from South Africa13,15. Published management in the rest of SSA is limited to small pilot or feasibility studies or isolated cohort studies in Uganda17 and Ghana18.

The concerns arising from the HELIX trial11,13,15, the paucity of data documenting the epidemiology and management of HIE in SSA, and the variation in definitions and terminology1,3,4, demonstrate a substantial knowledge gap. The aim of this survey was to describe how definitions and management of HIE vary in SSA, including the use of TH, from the perspectives of individual clinicians, countries, cities, and types of institutions.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a cross-sectional multi-country web-based anonymous survey across SSA countries from August to October 2023. All doctors providing neonatal care in SSA countries were eligible. The study was approved by the University of Cape Town (UCT), Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC REF NO:150/2023) and it was carried out in accordance with their relevant guidelines and regulations. All subjects confirmed their informed consent to participate on the first page of the survey. The study was supported by the African Neonatal Association (ANA) a voluntary non-profit organization of neonatologists and paediatricians with members across nearly 40 countries in SSA.

Procedures

The survey consisted of 66 questions referring to five domains: Scope of TH practice, HIE definition and burden, HIE facilities and management, and Neuro-developmental follow-up. After pretesting by all authors, followed by further minor adjustments, the survey was distributed on the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), using a public link posted on the ANA social media platform (WhatsApp). The survey was also distributed to contacts obtained through other studies, LinkedIn, emails, and personal contacts. Contacts and participants were also requested to distribute the survey to other potential participants within their professional network.

The survey was developed in English, based on published challenges with definitions4, and previous HIE surveys conducted in India, Argentina, and Canada19–21, and then translated to French and Portuguese and reviewed by native speakers (FH and SB). The multilingual mode in REDCap was enabled, to allow the respondents to choose their preferred language. The first page explained the purpose, the voluntary and anonymous nature of the research, and the requirement for confirmation of consent before participating.

Since this was an anonymous survey, and TH practices can differ within institutions, we invited individual responses, rather than single responses from each institution. The identity of participating institutions and individuals was therefore protected by only identifying countries cities, and institution type, and not identifying institutions or individuals by name. The classification of institutions as public, private, or public-private was at the discretion of the participant. The intent was that that “public” referred to government run hospitals, “private” referred to hospitals run by private, non-government companies, and “private-public” referred to hospitals with both government and non-government management and funding. We did not aim to achieve consensus results per country since practice may vary substantially between institutions and cities within each country. Reminder messages were sent every two weeks. Personal and network contacts were encouraged to invite and remind potential respondents.

Statistical analysis

The survey was exploratory with no assumptions regarding results; hence sample size calculation and power analysis were not performed. The responses were captured directly into the REDCap, password-protected database hosted at UCT, accessible only to the authors. Data were exported to Stata version 18 [Stata Corporation; College station, USA] for analysis. Responses to the five domains were grouped and compared according to TH practice. Responses to scope of TH Practice were categorised by country, city and type of institution. The countries providing TH were cross-checked against the answers to the question asking if other centers in each country provided TH. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to test for normality of distribution. Categorical variables were summarised as percentages, and differences evaluated using Fisher’s exact test. Continuous variables were summarized as median and interquartile range (IQR), and differences were tested using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Statistical significance was assigned at p < 0.05.

Results

Distribution of survey responses by cities, practice, and facility type (public and private)

A total of 148 respondents from 43 of 48 SSA countries (89.5%) returned the survey, including responses from 37 of 40 ANA countries; the other countries were contacted through referral to personal contacts. Of these, 12 responses which did not capture information on TH practice were excluded. The remaining 136 responses from 43 SSA countries were analysed.

The countries with the most cities represented were, South Africa (11 cities, 28 respondents), Tanzania (six cities, 18 respondents) and Mozambique (five cities, five respondents), most countries were represented by 1–3 respondents per country. Responses from individuals indicated that TH was provided in 13 of the 43 responding countries (30%), in a total of 23 cities, and at least 31 separate institutions, 25 of which were represented by a single respondent. The survey respondents were from private (10%), public (78%), and private-public partnership (12%) institutions. Over two thirds (78%;106/136) were general paediatricians and 37% (50/135) neonatologists. A higher proportion of respondents in private institutions provided TH compared to private-public and public settings (69% vs. 28%; P = 0.004). The highest level of respiratory care where TH was provided, was more frequently HFOV (26% vs. 10%; p = 0.035) and iNO (40% vs. 1%; p < 0.001) versus where TH was not provided. Similarly, limitation of ventilation support was less frequent where TH was provided; no nCPAP (5% vs. 18%; p = 0.04), only nCPAP (7% vs. 23%; p = 0.029), basic intubation and ventilation (23% vs. 48; p = 0.008). There was also increased availability of equipment and expertise for invasive BP monitoring (56% vs. 14%; p < 0.001) and inotrope administration (88% vs. 67%; p = 0.011) where TH was provided.

Definition of HIE and indicators of intrapartum hypoxia

The variation in definitions of HIE and indicators of intrapartum hypoxia reported by respondents from institutions with TH vs. those without TH are shown in Table 3. Abnormal pH and abnormal base deficit (BD) in first 60 min of life, were both more frequently used as indicators of intrapartum hypoxia with TH than without TH (79% vs. 21%; p < 0.001 and 76% vs. 20%; p < 0.001 respectively). Lower pH thresholds were more commonly used by respondents who provided TH, however less than 50% of respondents answered that question. Lower base deficit thresholds of ≥12 or ≥ 10 mmol/l were most frequently (a combined total of 85.7%) used by respondents who provided TH regardless of intrapartum abnormalities, compared to 38.5% of those who do not provide TH, but very few participants who did not cool answered the question (14% vs. 65% of those who provided TH).

Most respondents (89%) used 5-minute Apgar score thresholds of 5, 6 or 7 to indicate suspected intrapartum hypoxia, and 56% indicated an abnormal 10-minute Apgar was also required. The use of Apgar score thresholds did not vary with TH practice, however, the use of IPPV at 10 min to indicate intrapartum hypoxia was more frequent where TH was provided (86% vs. 52%; p = 0.001).

Clinical indicators of moderate-severe HIE

The clinical indicators of moderate-severe HIE in term and near-term babies are shown in Fig. 2. Abnormal clinical assessment was used by 68% (90/133) of the respondents to define moderate-severe HIE. Respondents who did not provide TH more frequently had no fixed criteria and used only clinician discretion (14% vs. 5%; p = 0.042), while respondents who provided TH more frequently used a combination of abnormal clinical assessment and aEEG/EEG (29% vs. 5%; p < 0.001). The most frequently used methods to assign severity of HIE included Thompson score (57%) Sarnat grade (33%) and modified Sarnat grade (30%), and these did not differ with provision of TH.

The management of HIE

The management of HIE reported by respondents from institutions with TH versus those without TH is shown in Table 4. The availability of most neonatal care resources was higher where TH was provided; intubation and mechanical ventilation in the first 24 h (81% vs. 55%; p = 0.004), blood gas (81% vs. 35%; P < 0.001) and aEEG (56% vs. 8%; p < 0.001). Most respondents had access to cranial ultrasound (81%) and standardized management protocols (76%).

Fluid management varied with TH practice; initial fluid restriction to ≤ 50mls/kg was used more frequently with TH (74% vs. 48%; p = 0.008), and management that was too variable to comment on, was more likely where TH was not provided (16% vs. 2%; p = 0.02). Similarly, respondents who provided TH were more likely to prescribe ≥20mls/kg of milk feeds by 72 h of life (42% vs. 24%; p = 0.037) while volumes that were too variable to comment on were more likely for those without TH (38% vs. 14%; p = 0.004). Only 25% (32/130) of all respondents prescribed TPN for ≥50% of babies with moderate-severe HIE, and more frequently with TH (41% vs. 17%; p = 0.004).

Blood culture for all babies with moderate-severe HIE was reported by 46% (62/135) of respondents, and the proportion increased with TH provision (70% vs. 35%; p < 0.001). However, antibiotics were prescribed to all babies with moderate-severe HIE by 73% (99/134) of respondents and this was not influenced by TH practice.

The methods of TH are compared between public and private institutions in Table 6. Methods were similar across institutions, but a smaller proportion of respondents at private than public institutions provided details (50% vs. 74%). Automatic servo-control cooling mats were used most often (79%; 23/29), followed by manual gel bag (10%; 3/29), servo-control gel bag (3%; 1/29) and cool cap (3%; 1/29). Neuro-developmental follow-up

A neurological follow-up service for infants with HIE, was available for 68% (92/136) of respondents, and this was more available with TH (81% vs. 61%; p = 0.029). The Bayley scales of infant development were used most frequently following TH (26% vs. 9%; P = 0.015), followed by the Cape Town infant neurological assessment (16% vs. 3%; p = 0.01). Hearing screening was available to 39% (53/135) of respondents, and more often with TH practice (60% vs. 29%; p < 0.001). Brain stem auditory evoked potential (BAER) and otoacoustic emissions were both used variably; BAER was most frequent with TH practice (42% vs. 14%; p < 0.001). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was used in 10% or less babies with HIE by 82% (104/127) of all respondents; more frequent use (≥ 50% of babies) was reported more often with TH (34% vs. 5%; p < 0.001).

Discussion

This is the first survey of management and TH for neonates with moderate-severe HIE across SSA. Responses from 90% of SSA countries showed that almost a third have implemented TH in at least one city. Despite significant variation in definitions, resources, TH practice and follow-up, most respondents (76%) had standardised protocols and over 90% of those providing TH, did so using validated/recommended methods and protocols. More countries provided TH in public hospitals than in private hospitals, but only 10% of all respondents in the survey were from private hospitals and they had access to TH more frequently than those in public/public-private hospitals. Respondents at institutions without TH reported less standardised management, fewer NICU facilities, and less definition of indicators of intrapartum hypoxia. These settings coincided with the highest burden of moderate-severe HIE.

Variation in management

Similar to some SSA countries, significant variation in HIE and TH management during evolving implementation of TH has been reported in middle-income settings over the last decade19,20. A survey of neonatal level-two and level-three institutions in India conducted in 2015, found that approximately half the institutions provided TH; most providers were in the private sector, used clinical criteria alone to qualify for TH and used improvised cooling methods and providers were in areas with low infant mortality19. A survey of doctors and allied health professionals in Brazil conducted in 2019, reported unvalidated cooling methods and inadequate follow-up in the majority and inadequate encephalopathy assessment in 20%20. Heterogeneity in TH management has also more recently been reported in higher income settings, albeit to a lesser extent21.

Intensive care support

Limited intensive care resources at institutions in SSA without TH, is considered an appropriate reason to withhold TH12. The availabilty of intubated ventilation and inotrope administration was reported by all respondents at private institutions providing TH in our survey. However, NCPAP was the maximum level of respiratory support for 10% of respondents from public institutions (in Seirra Leone and Mozambique), 6% did not provide NCPAP (in Madagascar and Mauritiana) and 16.7% did not provide inotrope administration. Although tertiary-level cardio-respiratory support is not required by all babies with moderate-severe HIE22, the trials showing benefit of TH were conducted in settings where intensive care was available16, including trials using validated low-technology methods7. There is an urgent need for centres providing TH without full intensive care support, to report their data and review the appropriateness of TH in that setting, since it is not yet evidence-based.

Indicators of intrapartum hypoxia

Indicators of intrapartum hypoxia at institutions in SSA were more robustly defined in this survey when TH was provided, particularly blood gas parameters – we speculate this may be due to better resources and the subsequent resource implications of diagnosing HIE. Details of 5-minute Apgar score thresholds were similarly better represented at institutions with TH. However, the thresholds varied substantially from 5 to 7 and approximately half of respondents also required a 10-minute Apgar score. The American Academy of Paediatrics (AAP) and the American College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (ACOG) guidelines suggest significant intrapartum hypoxia is increasingly likely with one or more of: 5-minute Apgar score < 5, umbilical artery pH < 7, and/or BD ≥ 12 mmol/L23. However, several large cooling studies accepted a BD ≥ 10 mmol/L or pH ≤ 7.15 if sentinel events were present16, and a South African population study showed that 81% of babies with HIE and a 5-minute Apgar score < 7, had BD ≥ 12 mmol/L or pH < 7 in the first hour4.

Clinical indicators of moderate-severe HIE

Almost all respondents required defined clinical criteria to indicate moderate-severe HIE, particularly with TH. Clinical assessment methods included the Thompson score, Sarnat, and modified Sarnat grading in similar proportions regardless of TH practice. Although aEEG was used more frequently with TH, it was used by a minority overall (23%). While clinical signs of moderate-severe HIE alone were used to select babies in some TH trials, others also required aEEG16, and the combination of aEEG with any clinical signs of moderate encephalopathy can provide higher specificity with preserved sensitivity for poor outcome24.

The management of HIE

Although most respondents in SSA used standardised management protocols for moderate-severe HIE, this was less frequent at institutions without TH, particularly fluid and feeding management. The need for standardization is also highlighted by reports of variations in fluid and feeding management of babies with moderate-severe HIE in high-income countries25,26 and the association between improved outcomes and fluid restriction with negative fluid balance following HIE27. The SSA survey also showed suboptimal management of potential sepsis, particularly at institutions without TH; most respondents prescribed antibiotics; however, blood cultures were taken less frequently. Empiric blood culture and antibiotic treatment is recommended with HIE, but when cultures and infection markers are negative, antibiotics should be stopped28.

Therapeutic hypothermia methods

The servo-control cooling mat and manual gel-bag methods were used frequently in both private and public institutions in SSA. However, the servo-control gel-bag and CoolCap methods were used infrequently and exclusively in private institutions. Studies have shown no difference in benefit between SCH and WBH methods5 and some low-tech cooling methods have been validated6,8. However, regardless of the cooling method, the neuroprotective effect of TH depends on appropriate temperature management as recommended by ILCOR12: TH methods should be consistent with the protocols used in the RCTs, that is to commence cooling within 6 h, to a core temperature of 33.5 °C (WBH) or 34.5 °C (SCH) for 72 h and rewarm over at least 4 h at a speed of not more than 0.5 °C per hour. Responses from the SSA survey showed good adherence to the ILCOR recommendation, except for one respondent in private practice who indicated 8 h as the latest age for TH. Information on sedation/analgesia during TH was only available from 65% of respondents who provided TH; less than half prescribed sedation/analgesia routinely. Variation in practice has also been reported in high-income countries and equipoise persists regarding the risk or benefit of pre-emptive versus symptomatic treatment29.

Neurological follow-up

Neurological follow-up services including hearing screening were frequently, but not universally available in SSA where TH was practiced, and less available without TH. Cranial ultrasound was widely available and although magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was used more often with TH, most respondents used it infrequently. Cranial ultrasound is most useful to identify brain pathology at the bedside, while MRI is more useful for the timing, characterizing and prognostication of HIE30.

Strengths and weaknesses

The major strength of this study is the representation of most countries from SSA, the relevance to tertiary institutions across SSA, whilst recognizing the substantial variation in facilities and context. In addition, the binary nature and scope of questions, with high levels of engagement from respondents adds robustness to the findings. However, there were also significant limitations. Since the survey was not aimed at specific named institutions per country, there may be countries and institutions which are not represented in this survey due to non-response, however, only 12% of respondents indicated they did not know if TH was provided in other hospitals in their country, and we received responses from 90% of SSA countries. To protect the identity of participating institutions and individuals and to encourage participation, institution names were not included, thus only countries and cities were identified and multiple responses from the same institution could not always be excluded. However, most countries were represented by only one respondent representing a single city or a single institution-type per city, and multiple responses were only present for five cities in South Africa and one city in Nigeria. Similarly, data showing the extent to which national medical facilities were represented by the cities, were not available, and the classification of institutions as public, private, or public-private was at the discretion of participants, which could vary between countries. A further limitation to the survey, is the lack of information on the total number of doctors surveyed. Lastly, the reasons for not providing TH were not directly explored, leaving only speculation based on the associated limitation of resources where TH was not provided.

Conclusions

Despite the limitations, this study provides the most inclusive description to date of the resources and management of HIE and TH across SSA. Therapeutic hypothermia is available in at least 13 countries in SSA, the majority of whom follow evidence-based methods with stricter diagnostic criteria and more resources than institutions who do not cool. However, TH is being provided in several countries, without facilities for intubation or inotropes, which is not evidence-based and requires urgent review. The data present the case for considering the variation in resources and contexts before indiscriminately applying recommendations from other settings. The data also indicate the need to establish baseline definitions and minimum standards to allow for benchmarking and comparison of HIE epidemiology and outcomes in SSA. The described experience and variations in practice revealed by this survey can be used to inform research protocols and standardised management bundles in similar settings.
