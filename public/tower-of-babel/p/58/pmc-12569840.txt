Surgical retrieval of an embolized atrial septal occluder device from the abdominal aorta: a rare case report

Introduction

Atrial septal defect (ASD) is one of the most common types of congenital heart defects (CHD), accounting for about 10 %. ASD is characterized by a defect in the interatrial septum allowing deoxygenated blood from the right atrium to mix with oxygenated blood from the left atrium by shunting [1]. In general, the most common presenting symptoms include easy fatigability, dyspnoea, palpitations, syncope, heart failure or atrial arrhythmias. Small defects may spontaneously close; larger defects may persist and result in hemodynamic and clinical sequelae requiring percutaneous or surgical intervention [2].

Embolization of implanted ASO devices, despite being quite rare and reported in about 0.5 % of cases, can potentially be fatal and usually occurs in the first 72 h after device placement. Migration in the late postoperative period (>1 year) is an extremely rare occurrence. The most common embolization site was the main pulmonary artery (89 %) [3].

Percutaneous transcatheter retrieval of embolized ASO devices is difficult but successful in up to 75 % of cases in experienced hands. In cases where transcatheter retrieval fails, surgical rescue is indicated, especially in late rescue due to epithelization [4]. However, rarely in selected cases it can be managed conservatively. Regular follow ups and long-term antiplatelet and antithrombotic therapies are necessary in these cases [5].

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE Criteria [6].

Presentation of case

This case is of a 16-year-old female, who presented to the paediatric outpatient department with acute abdominal pain. On her past medical history, as a 9-year-old she was diagnosed with an ASD of 4 mm with left-to-right shunting on echocardiography, with a mild mitral regurgitation and a mild right ventricular hypertrophy. The ASD was deemed suitable for transcatheter closure. Transcatheter closure was performed successfully with an Occlutech Figulla® Flex II 6 mm occluder device (Occlutech International AB; Helsingborg, Sweden).

A month after the procedure, on control echocardiography correct positioning of the ASO device was confirmed. On the next control echocardiography at 5 months the ASO device was not found in its position. An urgent computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the aorta was performed, where the device was found embolized to the abdominal aorta between the celiac trunk (CT) and the superior mesenteric artery (SMA). On duplex ultrasound (USG), the ASO device partly occluded the orifice of the CT, blood flow near its orifice was turbulent with peak systolic velocity (Vmax) 220 cm/s and resistive index (RI) 0.55. Vmax and RI in the SMA were 180 cm/s and RI 0.8 respectively.

A month later, the patient was readmitted to the paediatric department, and a new Occlutech Figulla® Flex UNI occluder device 24 mm was successfully implanted, however transcatheter retrieval of the embolized ASO device transfemorally was unsuccessful, despite multiple attempts. Due to the very young age of the patient and her being asymptomatic, a common decision was decided between the family a multidisciplinary team (MDT), including paediatric cardiac surgeons, paediatric cardiologists, interventional radiologists and vascular surgeons, to initiate conservative management. The patient's family was educated on the matter and made aware of the need to seek immediate medical help in case of abdominal or lower limb symptoms in the future. The patient was discharged home on the 3rd post procedural day with long-term prescription of aspirin once daily, as seen appropriate by the MDT to avoid long-term full anticoagulation while maintaining antiaggregation to prevent possible vascular complications such as plague embolization. Regular half yearly USG follow ups for the first 2 years, and later yearly USG follow ups were performed, showing no changes in the location of the device.

The patient was asymptomatic for 7 years, eventually the patient presented to the paediatric outpatient department with acute abdominal pain and postprandial nausea. On clinical examination the patient was afebrile and normotensive. Physical examination revealed diffuse abdominal pain. Complete blood count and blood biochemistry were normal. ECG and chest x-ray findings were normal, abdominal ultrasound excluded common causes of acute abdomen. A CTA was performed which showed the ASO device between the orifices of the CT and SMA, occluding the lumen of CT by 40 % (Fig. 1A and B). The aortotomy was closed with Prolene 4/0 suture without lumen narrowing. Deairing was done by allowing backflow from the distal aorta. After releasing all the clamps, blood flow in the visceral branches was controlled with intraoperative doppler, which was optimal in all branches. Redon drain was inserted to the wound, Floseal haemostatic foam was applied to the wound to achieve perfect haemostasis, and the laparotomy was closed in layers.

The surgery ran without complications, the patient was extubated 3 h postoperatively in the intensive care unit. Redon drain was removed on the first postoperative day. Control USG confirmed adequate blood flow in the mesenteric arteries. The patient was discharged home on the fifth postoperative day with long-term prescription of aspirin 100 mg once daily, as indicated after most of vascular procedures. The early postoperative review showed wound healing by primary intention and the patient had no residual symptomatology. 2 years postoperatively USG studies showed no aortic lumen stenosis, with patent visceral branches. Clinically the patient is asymptomatic and living a standard life.

Discussion

Transcatheter closure of an ASD is an effective treatment with low complication rates and shorter hospital stay. Due to the self-expanding design of these devices, embolization even though quite rare, reported in up to 0.5 % of cases, can be potentially fatal, resulting in complications such as aortic perforation, dissection or organ ischemia. In most cases, embolization occurs in the first 72 h, and immediate transcatheter retrieval is recommended, with open surgery being an efficient alternative if endovascular retrieval fails.

The most common embolization site for the devices are the main pulmonary arteries. Only sporadic case reports describe the abdominal aorta as the site of occluder device migration. A total of 6 reported cases of device embolization to the abdominal aorta exist in English literature where surgical rescue through a midline laparotomy was performed just in one of the cases. One device was surgically retrieved from abdominal aorta through a femoral artery incision, and four devices were retrieved from the abdominal aorta endovascularly. [[7], [8], [9]]. Only one report describes the use of conservative management [10].

Many factors influence embolization of the devices, including type of device used, use of smaller devices than the size of the defects and thin or fragile atrial tissue. Failure of endovascular retrieval of the device was as high as 50 % in of all cases reported in literature [9]. This statistic goes in hand with our case, where after failure of an initial attempt of endovascular retrieval of the ASO, we were successful at retrieval of the device by open aortic surgery. Due to the very young age of the patient and her being asymptomatic, conservative management was indicated by a MDT, as also done in literature [10]. Furthermore, open aortic surgery for device retrieval was also found to be technically demanding in existing literature [9]. Regular half yearly for the first 2 years, and later yearly USG follow ups were performed without changes in the location of the device. The patient was asymptomatic for 7 years, where after the patient started having acute abdominal pain attacks and postprandial nausea, for which the surgical retrieval was indicated.

Conclusion

ASO device embolization should be promptly diagnosed and treated by a multidisciplinary team, including paediatric cardiac surgeons, paediatric cardiologists, interventional radiologists and vascular surgeons, to achieve the best results. Approach to every case is individual, with a variety of treatment options depending on the age, symptomatology and radiologic findings. From our experience, open surgical retrieval of the occluder devices is a safe and a highly effective alternative method to retrieve embolised devices when transcatheter retrieval fails.

In English literature, to the best of our knowledge, our case is 7th reported case of an ASO device embolization to the abdominal aorta and the 2nd case where the device was retrieved through open aortic surgery.
