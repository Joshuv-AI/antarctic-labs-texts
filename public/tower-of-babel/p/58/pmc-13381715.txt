Incidental Mandibular FDG Uptake on PET/CT Preceding Medication‐Related Osteonecrosis of the Jaw in a Patient Receiving High‐Dose Denosumab: A Case Report
Authors: Yuki Sakamoto, Shunsuke Sawada, Masahiro Umeda, Yuka Kojima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn a 59‐year‐old edentulous woman on high‐dose denosumab, incidental mandibular FDG uptake (SUVmax: 5.1) on PET/CT preceded Stage 2 MRONJ by 12 months, despite normal conventional imaging. This suggests that PET/CT may help detect early, subclinical MRONJ development.Keywords: case report, denosumab, edentulous mandible, fluorodeoxyglucose uptake, medication‐related osteonecrosis of the jaw, PET/CT

Introduction

Medication‐related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) is a serious adverse event associated with antiresorptive therapy, including bisphosphonates and denosumab. It is commonly linked to dental infection, tooth extraction, or local trauma, but some cases develop without apparent odontogenic triggers [1, 2]. Early detection is challenging because initial disease may be clinically silent and may not be evident on conventional imaging [3, 4]. Functional imaging, such as PET/CT, has a potential role in detecting early or subclinical metabolic changes associated with MRONJ before structural bone abnormalities become apparent. We report a case of MRONJ that developed in an edentulous mandible of a patient receiving high‐dose denosumab, in whom incidental focal fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on PET/CT was detected approximately 1 year before the onset of clinical and radiological disease.

Case Presentation

Case History and Examination

A 59‐year‐old Japanese woman with breast cancer and multiple bone metastases had received a total of 15 doses of high‐dose denosumab (120 mg subcutaneously; cumulative exposure: 1800 mg) at another institution. She was referred to our hospital for continuity of oncologic care.

Whole‐body PET/CT was performed for the systemic restaging and evaluation of the breast cancer and bone metastases. The scan incidentally demonstrated focal fluorodeoxyglucose uptake (SUVmax: 5.1) in the alveolar bone of the left mandibular molar region (Figure 1A). The site was fully edentulous; the teeth in this area had been extracted several years prior with no residual roots or residual infection. The patient had never worn dentures, ruling out denture‐related mucosal trauma. She had no pain, swelling, redness, ulceration, purulent discharge, or mucosal breakdown in the corresponding area.

(B) Concurrent panoramic radiograph shows no abnormal findings, highlighting the complete absence of structural disease at this early stage. (C) Axial CT image showed no abnormalities such as bone resorption.

She was subsequently referred to the Department of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgery for evaluation. On clinical examination, she again had no pain, swelling, redness, ulceration, purulent discharge, or mucosal breakdown in the corresponding area.

Approximately 12 months later, she presented with mild discomfort in the left posterior mandible. Intraoral examination revealed a small mucosal fistula with purulent discharge and surrounding inflammation (Figure 2A).

Progression to clinically evident MRONJ at 12 months. (A) Intraoral photograph showing a mucosal fistula with purulent discharge. (B) Panoramic radiograph and (C) axial CT images demonstrating progressive focal osteolysis and cortical bone destruction in the left mandibular molar region. These explicit anatomical changes starkly contrast with the initially isolated metabolic abnormality seen in Figure 1, demonstrating the clear progression to overt structural disease.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Investigations

Laboratory tests at the time of the initial PET/CT scan showed normal inflammatory markers, with a white blood cell (WBC) count of 4800/μL and a C‐reactive protein (CRP) level of 0.085 mg/dL. Panoramic radiography (Figure 1B) and maxillofacial computed tomography (Figure 1C) showed no evident abnormality, including osteolysis, sclerosis, or sequestration.

At the 12‐month follow‐up presentation, panoramic radiography showed focal osteolysis with irregular margins in the left mandibular molar region (Figure 2B). Computed tomography demonstrated osteolytic change in the lingual alveolar bone superior to the mandibular canal (Figure 2C).

Differential Diagnosis

Because there were no clinical or radiological signs of active disease at the initial evaluation, she was followed conservatively at 3‐month intervals. On reassessment 12 months later, and based on the clinical findings, imaging findings, and history of denosumab use, Stage 2 medication‐related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) was diagnosed.

Treatment

Because the lesion showed progression, marginal resection of the affected mandible was performed under local anesthesia while preserving mandibular continuity. Histopathological examination demonstrated non‐vital bone with multiple empty osteocytic lacunae. Bacterial colonies, chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, and granulation tissue were also observed, consistent with MRONJ with secondary infection.

The postoperative course was uneventful. At 12‐month follow‐up, the mucosa had healed completely, and there was no evidence of recurrence (Table 1).

Discussion

This case is notable because incidental focal mandibular uptake on PET/CT preceded clinically overt MRONJ by approximately 1 year in an edentulous patient without apparent odontogenic triggers. At the time of PET/CT, there were no symptoms, no mucosal abnormalities, and no radiographic or computed tomographic evidence of disease.

PET/CT findings in the jaw are nonspecific and may reflect inflammation, infection, bone remodeling, microdamage, or malignant processes. In this patient, histopathological confirmation of the initial PET‐positive lesion was not available, and subclinical infection cannot be completely excluded. Nevertheless, the absence of teeth, lack of denture use, absence of prior dental procedures or mucosal trauma, and normal conventional imaging argue against a typical odontogenic trigger at that stage.

Denosumab profoundly suppresses bone turnover by inhibiting RANKL‐mediated osteoclast activity. Although beneficial for preventing skeletal‐related events, this suppression may impair repair of localized microdamage and contribute to the development of jaw osteonecrosis [5]. This case highlights a stark contrast between functional and anatomical imaging modalities. The present case supports the possibility that metabolic or inflammatory change may precede structural change detectable on conventional imaging. This aligns perfectly with recent literature regarding the utility of PET/CT in MRONJ. Notably, Emmelheinz et al. recently demonstrated that FDG‐PET/CT can effectively identify early preclinical MRONJ lesions in patients with metastatic breast cancer prior to the appearance of definitive structural changes [6]. Our case strongly corroborates their findings and further expands upon them by highlighting that such preclinical PET positivity can occur even in a completely edentulous mandible lacking typical odontogenic triggers. However, it is important to emphasize that this single case demonstrates an association rather than causation; the FDG uptake may reflect a localized process that coincided with the subsequent development of MRONJ. Furthermore, while PET/CT demonstrated distinct metabolic activity (SUVmax: 5.1), concurrent panoramic radiography and CT were completely unremarkable, lacking any signs of osteosclerosis or osteolysis [3, 4]. The longitudinal progression from this isolated metabolic abnormality (Figure 1) to the overt clinical and radiological bone destruction 12 months later (Figure 2) explicitly links our imaging findings to our clinical hypothesis: functional imaging can capture the early, subclinical phase of MRONJ before anatomical changes occur.

The clinical implication is not that PET/CT can diagnose MRONJ. Rather, incidental jaw uptake on PET/CT in patients receiving high‐dose antiresorptive therapy should not be dismissed, even in edentulous patients. More explicitly, such findings should serve as an early warning sign that triggers proactive clinical management. This includes performing additional imaging modalities, such as magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or high‐resolution computed tomography (CT), to evaluate subclinical bone marrow or soft tissue changes that may not be visible on conventional radiographs. Furthermore, clinicians should implement professional oral hygiene management and educate patients on the early symptoms of MRONJ. Specifically, a strict follow‐up strategy should be established, comprising clinical oral examinations every 1–3 months and periodic radiographic assessments to monitor for emerging structural bone changes. Additionally, prompt multidisciplinary communication with the treating oncologist is crucial to evaluate the patient's overall status and reassess the risks and benefits of ongoing antiresorptive therapy.

Conclusion

Incidental mandibular FDG uptake on PET/CT may be associated with the subsequent clinical and radiological onset of MRONJ in patients receiving high‐dose denosumab, even in the absence of apparent odontogenic factors. Although PET/CT is not diagnostic for MRONJ, such findings may identify patients who require closer oral surveillance.
