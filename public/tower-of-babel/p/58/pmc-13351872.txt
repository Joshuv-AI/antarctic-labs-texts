Restorative neuromodulation for chronic lumbar spine pain secondary to a long construct spinal fusion: a case report
Authors: Lindsay Kate Wanner, Michael A. Fishman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersistent low back pain is a known complication of long construct fusions, often resulting in long term impacts on patients’ physical function. While available treatment options manage patients’ pain, there are few options available that also work to improve the patients’ physical function. Prior studies have demonstrated the benefit of restorative stimulation of the multifidus muscles on patients’ pain, function, and disability levels. This case hypothesizes that medial branch restorative neuromodulation at the L2 segment may be therapeutic for the patient’s chronic lumbar spine pain, and potentially protective through improved multifidi stability.Case DescriptionA 70-year-old female with a past medical history of rheumatoid arthritis, scoliosis status post multiple long-construct spinal fusions now fused to T10 to Pelvis, presented with significant chronic lumbar spine and leg pain. The patient presented to the clinic with 9/10 pain around the T9–T10 level and bilateral iliac crests. Her current pain management regimen included chronic hydrocodone and duloxetine, with minimal benefit for pain, and a home exercise program. Physical exam was significant for flat back kyphosis and S-shaped scoliosis with associated paraspinal muscle spasm. Prone instability and multifidus lift testing were positive bilaterally, and there was magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) evidence of multifidus atrophy. She underwent a permanent peripheral nerve stimulator placement, with lead placement at the L2 medial branches bilaterally, to provide restorative motor stimulation of the multifidus muscle. She completed 30-minute, twice daily restorative motor stimulation sessions. At her most recent follow-up visit, she reports pain is now 6/10, with improvement in several Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)-29 domains. Additionally, the patient reports experiencing notable strong contractions of multifidus muscles bilaterally during stimulation sessions. She reports improvements in her quality of life, ability to walk without her cane, and less difficulty with activities of daily living.ConclusionsRestorative neuromodulation using motor programming may represent a potential adjunctive intervention for management of post-fusion pain in select patients by improving segmental stability. The device implantation is minimally invasive, and therapeutic sessions are aligned with functional restoration. Further study is needed to better define its role, mechanisms, and expected outcomes in post-surgical populations.

Introduction

Background

Persistent post-fusion chronic low back pain is a common complaint, affecting over forty percent of patients (1). Restorative neuromodulation via medial branch stimulation has previously been shown to improve chronic low back pain in adults who have failed conservative management (2). Prior studies have shown that restorative neuromodulation can provide a significant decrease in low back pain in patients with multifidus dysfunction (3). Restorative stimulation uses motor programming to activate the multifidus muscle chain and improve pain and function (4). These studies have not delineated, the effectiveness of restorative neuromodulation on a multi-operated, long-construct spinal deformity patient as described in the following case report.

Rationale and knowledge gap

We hypothesized that motor programming of the bilateral medial branch nerves would be both therapeutic for pain and potentially protective via improved muscular control and stability. The device was selected to accommodate the patient’s prior fusion; and the approach allows for implementation by pain physicians and surgeons alike.

Objective

This study aimed to demonstrate the result of restorative neurostimulation in the management of chronic low back pain in a patient with long construct fusion. We present this article in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2026-0083/rc).

Case presentation

A 70-year-old female with a past medical history of rheumatoid arthritis, scoliosis status post multiple long-construct spinal fusions, presented with significant chronic lumbar spine and leg pain. She initially underwent a lumbar fusion for lumbar pain secondary to thoracolumbar scoliosis, which was complicated by severe post-operative pain, a dural leak, and hematoma formation requiring a second surgery. Following the initial fusion, she reports a change in her pain pattern, developing bilateral pain around the midpoint of her spine, as well as around the iliac crests and costal margins bilaterally, and persistent left leg weakness. She was found to have further spinal instability and tried managing her pain with physical therapy, lumbar epidural steroid injections, medial branch blocks, and radiofrequency ablation without significant relief. She underwent a fusion revision and extension, now with a T10 to Pelvis instrumented fusion. The patient presented to the clinic with 9/10 pain around the T9–T10 level, radiating across the costal margins as well as pain around her bilateral iliac crests. She noted severe impact on her ability to enjoy life and complete her daily activities, including walking, doing household chores, and working in her garden. Her current pain management regimen included chronic hydrocodone for more than 10 years and duloxetine, which provided some minimal benefit for pain, and a home exercise program.

On exam, she appeared healthy and well developed, standing 63.5 inches tall with a body mass index (BMI) of 31.4 kg/m2, with some discomfort with movement. Inspection of the spine revealed flat back kyphosis and S-shaped thoracolumbar scoliosis with an extensive thoracolumbar incision and associated paraspinal muscle spasm. Prone instability and multifidus lift testing were positive bilaterally. A sitting straight leg raise was negative bilaterally. Neurologic exam revealed decreased sensation to light touch on the right at the L4, L5, and S1 dermatomes. She had slight L2 weakness on the left, 4+/5 strength when compared to the right hip flexors. Review of a previous magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the Thoracic Spine demonstrated T9 adjacent segment vertebral edema to the T10 fusion, evidence of gliosis at multiple levels, and extensive multifidus muscle dysfunction and atrophy.

Following a review of the patient’s MRI and a comprehensive physical examination, the working diagnosis for this patient was determined to be multifidus dysfunction in the setting of post-laminectomy kyphosis. Cross sectional imaging demonstrated paraspinal muscle atrophy consistent with multifidus degeneration, which has previously been associated with impaired segmental stability and chronic low back pain. However, there was extensive metal artifact in the imaging, and the relationship between multifidus atrophy and symptom severity remains incompletely defined, and causality cannot be assumed in an individual case. Differential diagnosis included discogenic back pain, pseudoarthrosis and hardware related pain, and myofascial pain of the paravertebral muscles and ribs; and were all systematically evaluated. There was no radiographic evidence of disc degradation or herniation, or findings such as motion across fused segments on dynamic imaging or radiographic signs of nonunion, which would be suggestive of pseudoarthrosis. Hardware failure was considered, however the lack of radiographic evidence of hardware failure, including absence of screw loosening, rod fracture, or implant malposition on X-ray and MRI imaging. The patient’s clinical presentation was also not consistent with focal mechanical instability or hardware-related pain. Central sensitization in the setting myofascial pain was also considered, the patient lacked key symptoms of allodynia, widespread pain, global sensory hyperresponsiveness, and hyperalgesia, making this diagnosis less likely. Given these findings, multifidus dysfunction was considered a likely contributor, this diagnosis is inferential and should be interpreted within the broader context of a multifactorial pain syndrome.

After appropriate workup and counseling, she underwent a permanent peripheral nerve stimulator placement, with lead placement at the L2 medial branches bilaterally, to provide motor innervation of the multifidus muscle. Following lead placement, intraoperative muscle twitch testing was preformed using motor programming, and reproduceable twitch was seen in both multifidi. The peripheral nerve stimulation (PNS) device was initially activated three weeks after implant and the patient instructed in twice daily use. Unfortunately, one month after device activation, the patient experienced a ground-level fall, resulting in bilateral lower extremity fractures and right PNS lead migration with subsequent loss of efficacy. She stopped using her device for several months after the fall while she was recuperating from the fractures. Ten months after the initial lead placement, she underwent replacement of a new lead at the right L2 medial branch, and placement was confirmed with intraoperative twitch testing and X-ray (Figure 1).

The patient’s response was assessed using a Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS)-29 given at her first visit and following device implantation and activation. Global Pain was rated on a 10-point numeric rating scale. Pain Impact Score is representative of pain intensity [0–10], pain interference [4–20], and the inverted Physical Function Score [4–20]. Categories are scored, with scores ranging from 20 to 80. A score of 80 is indicative of maximum symptom severity for anxiety, depression, fatigue, pain interference, and sleep disturbance. For the physical function and social participation categories, a score of 80 is indicative of the highest level of function. A minimum clinically important difference (MCID) for a PROMIS domain is considered a 5-point change on the individual level. She also completed a Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC) questionnaire at the 14-week follow-up visit.

Discussion

Key findings

At baseline, the patient reported a 9/10 global pain; at her follow-up visit, 14 weeks following device reactivation after the revision, she reports pain is now 6/10. Her baseline Pain Impact Score improved from 44/50 at baseline to 31/50 at 14 weeks. PROMIS-29 scores at baseline, 6, 9, and 14 weeks post PNS reactivation after lead revision are presented in Figure 2. At the 14-week follow-up visit, changes exceeding the established minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds were observed in pain interference, anxiety, fatigue, sleep disturbance, physical function, and social participation domains.

The patient reported a score of 2, much improved, on the PGIC questionnaire at the 14-week follow-up visit. She has been using the device 30 minutes twice daily; and adherence to therapy was confirmed with device usage records. The patient also reported perceiving bilateral multifidus contractions during stimulation sessions.

In terms of functional status, the patient reported decreased reliance on a cane for walking, improved tolerance of activities of daily living, and resumption of her previously limited activities such as gardening. With the exception of the lead migration secondary to the mechanical fall, no adverse events were reported during the follow-up period.

Strengths and limitations

This case report has several limitations, as a single-case design, the observations are not generalizable and do not allow causal inference. Clinical improvement may reflect confounding factors, including prior surgery, ongoing medical management, and non-specific effects such as placebo response and symptom fluctuation. Outcomes were based on patient-reported measures without objective functional assessment, and follow-up duration was limited. Additionally, the patient’s complex surgical history introduces uncertainty regarding mechanisms and expected response. Adherence was confirmed via device logs, though variability over time and its relationship to outcomes were not formally analyzed.

Comparison with similar research

There is a limited amount of prior research investigating the role of restorative neurostimulation in patients with long construct fusions; however, neuromodulation has previously been used to treat failed back surgery syndrome and post-laminectomy syndrome. Peripheral nerve field stimulation has been shown to decrease visual analog scores at 12-month follow-ups in those patient populations (5,6). While the patient population is the same, the mechanism of peripheral nerve field stimulation significantly differs from restorative neuromodulation, instead using distal nerve targets to interrupt pain signaling via the gate control theory and sensory stimulation. It is possible that if this population can respond to distal peripheral nervous system modulation, there is the potential for response to proximal motor stimulation. However, it is unclear if patients with multifidus dysfunction and atrophy secondary to long construct fusions where the muscle may be denervated or atrophied would respond similarly to peripheral nervous system stimulation targeting the motor innervation.

Medial branch PNS has been found to reduce pain scores by 50% in patients with axial low back pain (7). Notably, these studies excluded patients with previous lumbar surgery, such as the patient in this case report. However, these results support the use of medial branch PNS in the management of axial low back pain and served to generate the initial hypothesis of this case report.

Beyond device specific studies, a body of literature supports the role of neuromodulation and multimodal approaches in complex chronic pain states. González-González et al. highlight the importance of interdisciplinary and multimodal pain management emphasizing that meaningful clinical improvement often arises from the integration of pharmacologic, rehabilitative, and interventional strategies, rather than any one modality (8). This framework is particularly supportive of the use of restorative neuromodulation as a component of a broader therapeutic approach for this patient.

Emerging mechanistic evidence suggests that effects of neuromodulation may extend beyond localized muscle control. Neuromodulatory interventions have been shown to influence autonomic regulation and central pain processing, and change somatosensory function following targeted stimulation, supporting the concept that peripheral neuromodulation may induce central neuroplastic adaptations (9,10). These findings provide a broader neurophysiological context for the present case, in which clinical improvements may reflect local motor effects and global modulation of pain processing pathways.

Transcutaneous neuromodulation has been shown to enhance muscle activation in patients with chronic low back pain, reinforcing the rationale for targeting the multifidus via medial branch stimulation (11). Similarly, improvements in both local symptoms and overall pain perception were reported following peripheral neuromodulation in a patient with low back pain and scar-related dysfunction, providing a clinically relevant parallel to the present case (12). Although not specific to neuromodulation, Martínez Pozas et al. further demonstrated that non-invasive interventions such as mobilization can modulate central pain processing and hyperalgesia, underscoring the importance of considering chronic pain within a broader neurophysiological framework (13).

The relatively rapid clinical improvement observed in this case warrants consideration, particularly given the patient’s history of extensive spinal surgery. Generally, response trajectories to restorative neurostimulation vary, and post-surgical patients could theoretically exhibit delayed or attenuated responses due to factors such as altered anatomy, paraspinal muscle disruption, or partial denervation. However, data from prior clinical trials in non-surgical populations suggests early improvement is not uncommon, with approximately 58% of implanted patients demonstrating at least a 2-point reduction in numeric rating scale pain scores at 90 days (14). While the present case represents a more complex post-fusion phenotype, this context suggests that the observed response at 14 weeks is not outside the range of biologically plausible outcomes.

Explanations of findings

The observed changes in patient reported outcomes require contextualization. While improvements were noted following initiation of restorative neurostimulation, the extent to which these changes can be attributed to the intervention remains uncertain. This patient underwent fusion using a skipped instrumentation level approach, which may have allowed partial preservation multifidi muscles and their innervation, potentially facilitating responsiveness to neuromodulation

The reduction in pain intensity from 9 to 6, meets commonly accepted thresholds for an MCID; however, this remains within a moderate range for pain, indicating persistent symptom burden. Improvements in PROMIS-29 domains similarly exceeded MCID thresholds but should be interpreted in the context of ongoing limitations.

The patient’s clinical course was complex, including prior revision surgery, pharmacologic management, and behavioral adaptation. Non-specific effects such as placebo response, regression to the mean, and natural symptom fluctuation may also have contributed. In the absence of control condition, the relative contribution of these factors cannot be determined, and findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis generating.

Implications and actions needed

The benefits of restorative neuromodulation in patients with chronic low back pain following a fusion suggest that this is a minimally invasive and possibly effective treatment for post-laminectomy pain in select patients with long construct fusions. Further studies examining restorative neurostimulation in a larger cohort of patients with persistent low back pain following spinal fusion should be performed. Specifically, the impact of fusion approach such as skipped instrumentation level should be further explored to determine if this previously excluded patient groups may also experience treatment benefit. The extent to which prior fusion surgery influences response trajectory remains unclear, and further investigation is needed to better characterize treatment response patterns in this population.

Patient perspective

After my first surgery, my pain was so severe and much worse than what I had before the surgery, and while the second surgery helped some, my pain was still severe and I had difficulty with everyday tasks. After having this procedure done, I’ve noticed improvement in my pain and I feel like my strength is getting better. The procedure and recovery were much easier than my other surgeries. I can walk most days without my cane now, and I’m grateful I can get back to gardening and working around my house.

Conclusions

Restorative peripheral nerve stimulation of the multifidus muscles may represent a potential adjunct within a multimodal pain management approach for select patients with persistent low back pain following multiple spinal surgeries. This minimally invasive procedure can be performed without impacting established hardware and is delivered via a brief, twice-daily treatment regimen.

In this case, the use of restorative neurostimulation was associated with improvements in patient-reported outcomes; however, given limitations of a single-case design, and complex clinical context, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further investigation is needed to better explore its role in post-fusion patient populations.
