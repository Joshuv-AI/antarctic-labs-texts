Validation of Tricuspid Annular Displacement by Tissue Doppler in Pulmonary Hypertension
Authors: Rongrong Chen, Shadi P. Bagherzadeh, Neev Patel, Patrick Khoury, Bettia E. Celestin, Frida N. Hermansson, Jennifer P. Woo, Francois Haddad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) is primarily measured using motion‐mode (M‐mode) echocardiography. However, image resolution doesn't always provide clear visualization. In these cases, tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) may offer a valuable alternative for right ventricular assessment. To validate TDI‐derived TAPSE (TAPSE‐TDI) against M‐mode TAPSE and compare their diagnostic utility for right heart catheterization‐confirmed pulmonary hypertension(PH). We analyzed 304 healthy volunteers and 310 PH patients in a case‐control study. M‐mode TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI were measured in the RV‐focused apical four‐chamber view. TAPSE‐TDI was calculated by averaging the systolic and diastolic signals from the lateral tricuspid annulus. Agreement between methods was quantified using bias, percentile precision, and intra‐class coefficient (ICC). The dependence of annular motion on body size, sex, and heart rate was evaluated with weighted regression. We assessed PH discrimination using receiver operating curve (ROC) analysis, comparing TAPSE, TAPSE‐TDI, and RV TDI peak systolic velocity (s′). Thresholds were further tested using Classification and Regression Tree (CART) analysis. TAPSE‐TDI demonstrated a strong linear relationship with TAPSE (r = 0.945, p < 0.001) and a slope of 1.02 in no‐intercept regression. TAPSE‐TDI demonstrated excellent reproducibility (ICC = 0.937), and its reference range fell within the guideline‐recommended values. For PH diagnosis, TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI showed good discrimination (AUC > 0.83), with the TAPSE/RVSP ratio outperforming (both AUC > 0.96). CART analysis identified optimal thresholds of 16.68 mm for TAPSE and 15.88 mm for TAPSE‐TDI to discriminate PH. TAPSE measured using TDI was closely related to M‐mode and both better discriminated PH than RV s′.

Tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) is a valuable method for assessing right ventricular (RV) function [1]. TDI offers good temporal resolution, enabling the assessment of different cardiac phases, including isovolumic contraction (IVC), systolic, isovolumic relaxation (IVR), and diastolic phases [2]. The recent Guidelines for the Echocardiographic Assessment of the Right Heart in Adults specifically recommend RV peak velocity (RV s′) as a measure of systolic function, with a reference limit of 9.5 cm/s [3]. Meluzin and colleagues were the first to demonstrate the relationship between RV s′ and RV function, showing a good correlation with RV ejection fraction measured by radionuclide angiography (r = 0.648, p < 0.001) [4].

Beyond measuring peak systolic velocity (RV s′), TDI can quantify annular displacement by integrating systolic or diastolic velocities over time [5]. This integration of the systolic signal should provide a measure comparable to M‐mode TAPSE [6]. Furthermore, based on the principle of conservation of motion, systolic and diastolic annular displacements should be approximately equal, offering an internal quality control mechanism. Despite these theoretical advantages, the reliability of TDI‐derived displacement for predicting TAPSE has not been well established. TDI displacement analysis may be particularly valuable when M‐mode is unavailable or when image quality is suboptimal [7, 8]. Moreover, because it integrates motion over the entire cardiac cycle rather than capturing a single peak velocity, TDI‐derived displacement may offer superior discrimination of pulmonary hypertension (PH) compared to RV s′ alone.

This study had three objectives. First, we sought to determine the bias and precision of TDI‐derived TAPSE (TAPSE‐TDI) versus M‐mode TAPSE measurements. Second, we aimed to assess the association between RV s′ and TAPSE‐TDI. Third, we evaluated whether TAPSE‐TDI provides superior discrimination of pulmonary hypertension (PH) compared to RV s′.

Methods

Population

The study was designed as a case‐control study and included individuals without known cardiovascular disease (“healthy cohort”) and patients with established PH (PH cohort).

The healthy cohort was prospectively recruited between December 2009 and December 2024 from participants enrolled in a healthy aging study. Participants completed standardized medical history questionnaires and physical examination as well as comprehensive echocardiography. Participants had no prior history of cardiovascular disease, lung disease, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or cancer. Of the 344 participants, we include 304 volunteers who had both M‐mode and TDI measurements. The PH cohort consisted of patients retrospectively identified from December 2006 to December 2023, all of whom had indications for right heart catheterization (RHC) and echocardiography within 90 days of each other. Patients with complex congenital heart diseases or those who had undergone heart transplantation were excluded from this cohort. From this group, we selected 310 patients based on the availability of high‐quality transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) images and a mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg confirmed by RHC. The study was approved by the Stanford Institutional Review Board (IRB# 25673) and was conducted in accordance with the Cardio Share registry protocol.

RHC Assessment and Classifications

RHC assessments included measurements of mPAP, pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), right atrial pressure (RAP), cardiac output (CO), and vital signs. Cardiac output was determined using the Fick method. In accordance with the 2022 European Society of Cardiology/European Respiratory Society (ESC/ERS) guidelines [9], the diagnosis of PH was established clinically and corroborated by RHC findings indicating mPAP > 20 mmHg. Patients within the PH cohort with mPAP ≤ 20 mmHg were classified as negative for PH and subsequently excluded from the cohort.

Echocardiographic Assessments

Echocardiographic images were obtained using Hewlett‐Packard Sonos 5500 or Philips IE33 ultrasound systems. All images were acquired following the guidelines established by the American Society of Echocardiography for chamber quantification [10]. Echo measurements were performed using the TomTec Imaging system and the Us2.ai system (Singapore) [11, 12]. Left ventricular diameters were assessed in the parasternal long‐axis and apical four‐chamber views, while RV measurements were evaluated on the RV‐focused apical four‐chamber view [3]. LV ejection fraction (LVEF) was calculated using the modified biplane Simpson's method. All parameters were measured in the research core laboratory by certified sonographers and confirmed by trained cardiologists (R.C., S.P.B.).

We measured M‐mode TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI for method comparison, as well as RV s′, in the same RV‐focused apical four‐chamber view (Figure 1). TAPSE was assessed using standard M‐mode measurements of annular excursion, with the M‐mode cursor placed along the lateral tricuspid valve (TV) annulus and aligned along the right ventricular free wall (RVFW) from apex to base, ensuring it was parallel to annular motion. Image brightness was optimized to enhance the contrast between the M‐mode signal of the tricuspid annulus and the background. TAPSE was defined as the maximum apical movement of the lateral TV annulus between end‐diastole and peak systole, averaged over two or three beats (Figure 1a). We then analyzed tissue Doppler imaging (TDI) mode using the same RV‐focused apical four‐chamber view, placing the pulsed wave Doppler cursor at the basal segment of RVFW along the lateral TV annulus, in accordance with current American Society of Echocardiography (ASE) guideline recommendations [3]. Gains and filters were set as low as possible to obtain an optimal signal‐to‐noise ratio, and the imaging sector width was minimized to improve frame rate. To ensure optimal accuracy, the angle between the contraction axis and the ultrasound beam was usually kept < 20°. The RV s′ velocity was measured as the peak systolic velocity of the tissue Doppler signal above the baseline(Figure 1b, yellow marker). The velocity time integral of the systolic and diastolic TDI signals was obtained by tracing the contours of their respective waveforms. The systolic TDI included the isovolumic contraction component, while the diastolic TDI excluded the isovolumic relaxation phase, as IVR typically contains bidirectional waves (Figure 1b, yellow lines). For waveforms with no clearly identifiable baseline crossing at the onset or termination, the crossing point was estimated by linear extrapolation to the baseline. For the contour, we chose a region with the most frequent thickness, typically within the upper 75th percentile when assessed visually [5].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Python v3.13. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when the data conformed to a normal distribution. In cases where the data exhibited significant deviation from normality, values were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables were summarized as the percentage of subjects (%). We compared differences between healthy and PH groups using independent samples t‐tests with effect size quantified using Cohen's d (0.2 often considered a small effect size, 0.5 (medium), and 0.8 (large)). A two‐tailed p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Agreement between TAPSE‐TDI and M‐mode TAPSE was analyzed using quantile regression, Spearman's correlation coefficient (r), and the coefficient of determination (R 2). Duplicate analysis was performed and technical differences were measured using (1) bias (median difference between the M‐mode and TDI measures), (2) percentile precision, (3) root mean square coefficient of variation, and (4) intra‐class coefficient. Percentile precision was calculated as one‐half of the 84th‐to‐16th percentile range of the inter‐measurement differences, divided by the square root of two, to account for the uncertainty associated with repeated measures. The coefficient of variation (COV) was calculated using the root mean square method to assess variability. Agreement between measurements was evaluated using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) derived from a two‐way random‐effects model with absolute agreement, classified as poor (< 0.5), moderate (0.50–0.75), good (0.75–0.90), or excellent (> 0.90) [13, 14].

Then, we used weighted least squares regression to establish equations and percentile‐based reference values in the healthy group. Subsequently, to evaluate the diagnostic performance of each parameter in identifying PH, we conducted receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and calculated the C‐statistic (area under the curve) for each parameter. A classification and regression tree (CART) model was developed to distinguish patients with and without PH and to identify clinically relevant diagnostic thresholds. The dataset was stratified and randomly split into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets while preserving class proportions.

Inter‐ and intra‐observer variability were assessed using 30 images. Two cardiologists (R.C., S.P.B.) independently performed measurements for inter‐observer analysis, and one cardiologist repeated the measurements 1 month later for intra‐observer analysis. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated using a two‐way random‐effects model with absolute agreement.

Results

Characteristics of the Study Groups

The study populations comprised 304 participants in the Healthy Group and 310 patients in the PH Group. A total of 344 volunteers were initially selected for the Healthy cohort, prospectively recruited from 2009 to 2024. Following our exclusion criteria, we eliminated two participants due to TDI suboptimal image quality and 38 participants who lacked M‐mode TAPSE measurements, as this was not a standard measurement protocol at the time of recruitment. The final cohorts included 304 echocardiographic studies from the Healthy cohort, which formed the basis of our healthy group for subsequent analysis. The contour diagram is provided in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1a. For the Stanford PH Cohort, we conducted a retrospective analysis of 440 patients screened from the RHC database, all of whom had an initial diagnosis of PH between 2006 and 2023. Among these, 345 patients were confirmed to have PH based on right heart catheterization results. After applying our exclusion criteria, we removed 35 studies due to poor image quality or the absence of M‐mode TAPSE measurements. Consequently, a total of 310 patients were included in our PH group for final analysis (Supporting Information S1: Figure S1b).

The Healthy Group had a mean age of 53.1 years and a mean body mass index (BMI) of 24.4 kg/m2; 21% of the group were classified as obese. In contrast, the PH Group had a mean age of 58.5 years and a mean BMI of 27.9 kg/m2, with 28% classified as obese. As expected, healthy individuals had lower RVSP (24.21 (7.73) vs. 59.78 (24.27)), higher RVFAC (44.03 (5.95) vs. 30.04 (11.44)), and smaller RV diameters (32.44 (6.58) vs. 43.71 (9.89)). Image Quality

Based on visual assessment, we first stratified image quality into three levels: good, moderate, and poor. Among the 614 total images, 394 (64.2%) TAPSE images were rated as good quality, 78 (12.7%) as moderate, and 142 (23.1%) as poor. For TDI images, 345 (56.2%) were rated as good quality, 173 (28.2%) as moderate, and 96 (15.6%) as poor. Among the poor‐quality images, diastolic‐phase TDI images were of relatively better quality than systolic‐phase images.

Comparing TAPSE, TAPSE‐TDI, and RV s′

First, we evaluated the relationship between TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI using quantile regression analysis, as illustrated in Figure 2a. In the healthy and PH‐combined cohort, TAPSE‐TDI demonstrated a strong linear relationship with TAPSE (Pseudo R 2 = 0.718, Pearson r = 0.945, p < 0.001). Quantile regression demonstrated stable associations across the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles. Additionally, a no‐intercept had a slope close to unity, a slope of 1.02 (Pseudo R 2 = 0.697, Pearson r = 0.945, SE = 0.004, p < 0.001). The spread between the 5th and 95th percentile regression lines suggests increasing variability in the relationship at the extremes of TAPSE‐TDI, supporting the presence of heteroscedasticity. To evaluate the agreement between TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI, we analyzed the difference between TAPSE‐TDI and TAPSE. As shown in Figure 2b, the median difference between TAPSE‐TDI and TAPSE was close to zero, indicating minimal systematic bias. Bias and Precision Between TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI

Next, we compared quantitative metrics, including bias, precision, root mean square error (RMSE), and ICC between TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI. We averaged systolic and diastolic TDI as they were strongly related to each other (ICC ≥ 0.937). Method comparison metrics are summarized in Figure 2c. The median bias between TAPSE‐TDI and TAPSE was −0.48 mm (−2.01% relative), with a precision of 1.08 mm, and a relative RMSE of 7.55%. For systolic TDI, the bias was similar (−0.43 mm, −2.47% relative), with slightly greater variability (precision 1.12 mm, RMSE 7.53%). Both methods demonstrated excellent reproducibility, with ICC values of 0.937 and 0.938, respectively.

Relationship Between TAPSE and RV s′

The quantile regression model and graph for RV s′ (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2) revealed that the association between RV s′ and TAPSE remained consistent across the 5th, 50th, and 95th percentiles (Pseudo R 2 > 0.20, Pearson r = 0.697, p < 0.001).

Diagnostic Performance for PH

To evaluate the diagnostic performance of echocardiographic parameters in identifying PH, we performed ROC curve analyses using RHC‐confirmed PH (mPAP > 20 mmHg) as the reference standard. As shown in Figure 3a, TAPSE‐TDI (AUC = 0.841, p < 0.001) closely mirrored TAPSE (AUC = 0.849, p< 0.001) performance (ΔAUC = 0.008, p = 0.17, DeLong test). In contrast, RV s′—a guideline endorsed marker of systolic velocity—showed substantially lower discrimination (AUC = 0.677, p < 0.001), p < 0.001 for comparison with TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI AUC. Figure 3b showed that systolic VTI and diastolic VTI yielded nearly identical AUC values (AUC = 0.835 vs. 0.846) when evaluated against the same RHC‐based reference.

First, we applied percentile analysis to establish reference values for RVSP‐related parameters across the 2.5th, 5th, 50th, 95th, and 97.5th percentiles. The estimated percentile values are summarized in Table 4. Next, we performed ROC curve analyses to evaluate the diagnostic performance of RVSP and the TAPSE/RVSP ratio in identifying RHC‐confirmed PH. As shown in Figure 5a, TAPSE alone demonstrated strong discriminative ability with an AUC of 0.866. RVSP performed better with an AUC of 0.945 (p < 0.001). Reproducibility

Inter‐ and intra‐observer agreement was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). For inter‐observer variability, ICC values were excellent across these measurements, with values of 0.957 for TAPSE, 0.988 for systolic TDI, 0.982 for diastolic TDI, and 0.988 for TAPSE‐TDI. Intra‐observer ICCs were similarly high (0.937–0.948) for systolic TDI, diastolic TDI, and TAPSE‐TDI, while TAPSE showed a lower intra‐observer ICC of 0.808, indicating moderate reproducibility.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates three key findings. First, TAPSE‐TDI showed excellent agreement with M‐mode TAPSE (minimal bias, narrow limits of agreement) and may offer advantages when M‐mode image quality is suboptimal. Second, TAPSE‐TDI outperformed RV s′ in discriminating PH. Third, CART‐derived thresholds aligned with guideline‐recommended cutoffs further support clinical reference values.

TAPSE reflects the longitudinal displacement of the tricuspid annulus, a key contributor to right ventricular (RV) systolic function [15, 16, 17]. M‐mode echocardiography remains the gold standard for measuring TAPSE, with a lower limit of reference of 17 mm [3] and well‐established prognostic value in heart failure and PH [18, 19]. TAPSE‐TDI offers a complementary approach by estimating longitudinal annular displacement through integration of annular velocity over time, reflecting the same physiologic motion as TAPSE. Importantly, TAPSE‐TDI can be obtained directly from the TDI waveform, eliminating the need for post‐processing methods that have been shown to underestimate annular motion [20]. Our results demonstrate excellent agreement between TAPSE‐TDI and M‐mode TAPSE, with minimal systematic bias and narrow limits of agreement. This approach may be particularly valuable when M‐mode acquisition is technically challenging. Moreover, the conservation of motion between systole and diastole provides an internal quality control mechanism for TDI measures. In selecting the optimal interface for TDI signal measurement, we chose the region with higher signal intensity, typically above the 75th percentile, guided by the methodology of Dhutia et al. [6] and initial quality control comparisons between TDI and M‐mode measurements. Additionally, our systolic TDI measurement incorporated the isovolumic contraction phase, which is often affected early in right ventricular contractility [21] and may be overlooked by traditional TAPSE. Reference ranges for TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI aligned closely with ASE guidelines [3] and WASE [22], supporting the clinical validity of TDI‐based measurements. The small underestimation may be secondary to the chosen interface or stability in the tissue Doppler sample volume position.

In classifying PH in our case‐control study, TAPSE‐TDI performed comparably to M‐mode TAPSE, confirming its reliability as a surrogate for longitudinal displacement. Importantly, TAPSE‐TDI outperformed peak RV s′, reinforcing the value of this integral measure beyond simple velocity assessment. Systolic and diastolic TDI demonstrated similar effectiveness, and considering both values can serve as additional quality controls. Furthermore, TAPSE‐TDI acquisition also allows simultaneous measurement of other metrics such as RV s′. Original to our study, we used CART analysis to determine thresholds for M‐mode TAPSE and TAPSE‐TDI. These aligned closely with guideline‐recommended thresholds of < 17–18 mm [3, 9], providing data‐driven support for reference value‐determined thresholds, incorporating RVSP further enhanced diagnostic performance. The TAPSE/RVSP and TAPSE‐TDI/RVSP ratios demonstrated superior classification accuracy, with CART analysis identifying a threshold of TAPSE/RVSp < 0.42 mm/mmHg as indicative of PH, consistent with previous findings [23]. Estimated percentile values for TAPSE/RVSP provide a distribution‐based framework for clinical interpretation.

Limitations and Future Directions

Our relatively small sample size of healthy subjects limits the establishment of definitive reference values. Most participants were referred for catheterization, which may influence generalizability and the study is based on a case‐control study design and not on a referral cohort. Future multi‐center studies should expand the spectrum of studied populations, validate outcome‐derived thresholds across clinical contexts, and standardize methodologies for RV functional assessment.

Conclusion

TAPSE‐TDI represents a valid, precise, and complementary alternative to M‐mode TAPSE for assessing RV longitudinal function. It offers superior discriminatory power for detecting PH compared with RV s′ and can be particularly useful when M‐mode imaging is technically limited. CART‐derived thresholds reinforce existing guideline thresholds, adding evidence‐based support for their clinical application. These findings support incorporating TAPSE‐TDI into routine echocardiographic assessment of RV function.
