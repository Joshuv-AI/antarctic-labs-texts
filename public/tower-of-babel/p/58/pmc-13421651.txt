Dense MASW shear-wave imaging of weak-zone networks and preferential leachate pathways beneath rumah landfill site, central saudi arabia
Authors: Kamal Abdelrahman, Saad S. Alarifi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Landfills in arid regions may contribute to potential soil and groundwater concerns, as episodic rainfall, heterogeneous cover materials, and fractured carbonate bedrock may create preferential infiltration pathways. In central Saudi Arabia, landfill sites commonly overlie carbonate formations where weathering, fracturing, and dissolution can produce strong lateral variations in near-surface stiffness and hydraulic susceptibility. This study presents a dense MASW-based stiffness model to screen for shallow weak zones and fractured/weathered carbonate intervals beneath Rumah landfill site in central Saudi Arabia. We acquired 26 MASW profiles using a 24-channel split-spread array with 1 m receiver spacing and a 10 kg weight-drop source, and processed the data to image the near surface to an effective depth of approximately 30 m. The resulting 2D shear-wave velocity sections define three Vs-based geoseismic units: (1) a surficial cover/near-surface low-stiffness unit with Vs of approximately 0.35–0.76 km/s and thickness of about 2–8 m; (2) an intermediate fractured/weathered carbonate interval with Vs of approximately 0.76–1.50 km/s and typical thickness of about 5–15 m; and (3) a deeper competent carbonate/limestone domain with Vs > 1.50 km/s, locally approaching approximately 2.3 km/s. Localized low-Vs weak zones (< 0.76 km/s) are repeatedly detected, mainly within the upper 2–12 m and commonly 2–6 m thick. These anomalies are interpreted as zones of reduced shear stiffness that may reflect disturbed ground, elevated moisture, increased porosity, weathering, fracturing, or combinations of these factors. The results, therefore, provide a defensible MASW-based pathway-susceptibility screening framework and identify priority targets for confirmatory drilling, hydrochemical sampling, and fluid-sensitive geophysical methods such as ERT/IP. The findings should be interpreted as evidence of susceptibility and subsurface heterogeneity, not as direct proof of active leachate migration.

Introduction

Over the past several decades, geophysical methods have been increasingly applied at various stages of waste management to support landfill site characterization, reclamation planning, and environmental risk screening. These methods provide non-invasive information on subsurface layering, structural heterogeneity, weak zones, and potential moisture- or contaminant-related contrasts that can guide targeted verification1–8. Nevertheless, landfill environments remain difficult to interpret because waste materials, cover soils, moisture conditions, and bedrock properties are commonly heterogeneous. MASW is useful in this context because it resolves shear-wave velocity (Vs), a stiffness-sensitive parameter that can delineate mechanically weak or fractured zones when acquisition geometry and inversion quality control are appropriate9–11. However, MASW does not uniquely determine lithology, saturation, permeability, or contaminant presence; therefore, its outputs are best used as a screening framework to identify plausible preferential-pathway zones that require confirmation by complementary methods and/or direct sampling. Accordingly, the objective of this study is to use dense MASW profiling to characterize the near-surface stiffness architecture beneath the Rumah landfill site and to identify Vs-defined weak/fractured zones that may be associated with potential pathways for leachate movement.

Despite the growing use of near-surface geophysics for landfill assessment, high-resolution, site-scale MASW evidence from arid carbonate landfill settings remains limited. This study addresses that gap by presenting 26 MASW profiles (Fig. 1) and by using consistent, explicitly defined Vs criteria to (i) delineate weak-zone distributions and their lateral persistence, (ii) map the depth to the competent carbonate/limestone domain using the Vs ≈ 1.50 km/s transition, and (iii) develop a reproducible susceptibility-screening synthesis. The resulting products are intended to guide verification, monitoring design, and risk prioritization; they are not presented as direct confirmation of active leachate pathways without external calibration.

In the Riyadh region12, have demonstrated MASW for (i) mapping laterally variable Vs structure to ~ 30 m depth and identifying fractured/weathered limestone intervals and localized weak zones in western Riyadh, supporting foundation design and site-class mapping, and (ii) profiling Vs in the Diriyah area to characterize fill, weathered limestone, and competent limestone and to highlight weak zones consistent with sinkhole-prone carbonate settings13. Landfill site

The Rumah landfill site is located in Rumah Governorate, Riyadh Region, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia. The landfill spans more than 2 square kilometers. Historical analysis of satellite imagery suggests that the site began receiving waste around 2004, marking more than two decades of continuous expansion (Fig. 2). The landfill remains active to date, serving as a repository for municipal and industrial waste, highlighting the critical need to assess its environmental impacts on surrounding soil and groundwater systems. A total of 26 MASW profiles were conducted across the landfill site. The profiles were strategically positioned to provide comprehensive coverage of the area, with particular emphasis on key locations in the central portion of the landfill, ensuring a representative characterization of the subsurface.

Geological setting of the Rumah area

Geologically, the landfill site is located within a carbonate-dominated sedimentary setting in the Riyadh Region (Fig. 3). The near-surface framework includes Upper Cretaceous to Paleogene carbonate units, locally mantled by Quaternary eolian, alluvial, and wadi-related deposits. For the MASW interpretation, the most relevant geological controls are the contrast between surficial cover and carbonate bedrock, the potential for weathering/fracturing and dissolution-related secondary porosity in carbonate units, and the local redistribution of sediments and runoff along wadis. These factors can influence both shear-wave velocity and the likelihood that infiltration is.

focused into mechanically weaker or more fractured intervals.

Stratigraphy and lithofacies

Aruma formation (Upper cretaceous–paleocene)

The Aruma Formation forms part of the regional carbonate succession exposed in the Riyadh-Rumah corridor and consists mainly of shallow-marine limestone and marl units24,25. For the present geophysical interpretation, the key implication is not the full regional stratigraphic subdivision, but the presence of carbonate bedrock that may be locally fractured, weathered, or affected by exposure-related diagenesis. Such conditions can produce lateral stiffness variations detectable by MASW and can influence preferential infiltration where fractures or dissolution-enhanced secondary porosity are connected.

The Aruma carbonates include shallow-ramp limestone and marl facies that can vary laterally in texture, cementation, and weathering state. These variations provide a geological basis for interpreting repeated Vs contrasts as stiffness-defined units. The carbonate areas shown on the geologic map in Fig. 3 are therefore used only as a regional geological context; the MASW-based layer boundaries remain velocity-defined and require independent calibration to confirm lithology.

Recent regional studies have refined the carbonate stratigraphy and karst-related context of the broader Riyadh area26. In the Rumah map area, these studies support the possibility of fracture- and dissolution-influenced carbonate heterogeneity, which is directly relevant to interpreting low-Vs zones and the variable depth to the competent carbonate/limestone domain.

Umm er Radhuma formation (Paleocene–early Eocene)

The Umm er Radhuma Formation is a Paleocene-early Eocene carbonate unit that regionally forms an important karst-prone aquifer system. In the context of this study, its relevance lies in demonstrating that carbonate aquifer units in central Saudi Arabia can develop dissolution features, collapse structures, and fracture-controlled permeability. These features provide a plausible geological mechanism for the stiffness heterogeneity imaged by MASW, although the presence, hydraulic activity, and contaminant significance of any specific anomaly at Rumah must be verified independently.

Hydrogeologically, regional carbonate aquifers in central and eastern Saudi Arabia are strongly controlled by carbonate dissolution, dolomitization, and fracture connectivity22,27,28. These processes are important for the conceptual model of landfill vulnerability because they may allow episodic infiltration to move preferentially through connected weak or fractured intervals, particularly where a shallow low-Vs zone overlies a thicker fractured/weathered carbonate interval.

Quaternary surficial deposits and geomorphology

Eolian sand (sheets and dunes)

The Rumah area is locally mantled by eolian sand sheets and dunes, along with other surficial deposits, that can mask bedrock and produce variable near-surface stiffness29–32. For the MASW interpretation, these deposits are treated as part of the surficial cover/near-surface low-stiffness unit when their Vs response overlaps that of disturbed cover or landfill-related materials.

Wadi gravels and alluvium

Active and abandoned wadi fills occupy shallow channels and depressions across the Najd plain. Their variable mixtures of gravel, sand, and muddy lenses can create pronounced near-surface heterogeneity and may focus runoff or infiltration during episodic rainfall, making them relevant to interpreting shallow weak zones.

Methodology

The multichannel analysis of surface Waves (MASW) is a widely used geophysical technique based on seismic refraction principles for evaluating the mechanical properties of subsurface geological formations and subgrade materials. The method relies on the dispersive behavior of surface waves, where phase and group velocities vary with frequency and are governed by the elastic and physical properties of layered media at different depths. As surface waves propagate, higher frequencies are more sensitive to shallow layers, while lower frequencies penetrate deeper strata, each traveling at its characteristic velocity. The resulting wavefield is recorded using multichannel geophone arrays that capture wave trains composed of multiple frequencies arriving at different times, with recorded amplitudes and travel times reflecting subsurface layering. Dispersion curves are extracted from these records by identifying dominant surface-wave modes generated by P- and S-wave interactions and are subsequently inverted to obtain depth-dependent shear-wave (Vs) and compressional-wave (Vp) velocity models34. Owing to its high near-surface resolution, typically extending to depths of about 30 m, MASW is particularly effective for shallow geotechnical and environmental applications. Consequently, it has been extensively applied to characterize the spatial distribution of mechanical parameters in municipal solid waste (MSW) and other heterogeneous near-surface materials, enabling detailed delineation of stratigraphic boundaries and detection of lateral and vertical heterogeneities35–38. Comprehensive descriptions of the MASW methodology and dispersion-curve inversion procedures are provided39–41.

Data acquisition

The Multichannel Analysis of Surface Waves (MASW) methodology adopted in this study involved systematic acquisition of seismic data using multichannel geophone arrays, followed by dispersion-curve extraction and inversion to derive depth-dependent shear-wave velocity (Vs) profiles and two-dimensional (2D) MASW sections. A total of 26 MASW profiles were conducted directly across the landfill site to ensure representative subsurface coverage (Fig. 5). Data acquisition was carried out using a seismograph connected to an array of 24 vertical geophones (natural frequency of 4.5 Hz, see Table 1) through connecting cables, with seismic energy generated by a 10-kg weight-drop hammer striking a 1-cm-diameter metal plate. The system was powered by a 12-V battery. The geophones were deployed in a split-spread configuration with a uniform spacing of 1 m. The seismic source consisted of a sledgehammer, positioned 10 m offset from the nearest geophone to maximize signal quality (Fig. 6a & b). Following proper setup, multiple forward shots were recorded to enhance data quality. All seismic records were stored in the seismograph for subsequent processing and interpretation. This acquisition procedure was repeated at various offsets to ensure sufficient data coverage and improve the reliability of the derived subsurface velocity models.

MASW data processing and analysis

The acquired multichannel surface-wave (MASW) records were processed and interpreted following the standard, widely adopted MASW workflow, implemented in the ParkSEIS software package (Geometrics/SeisImager). The overall processing sequence comprised (i) preprocessing and quality control of shot gathers, (ii) extraction of Rayleigh-wave dispersion information, (iii) inversion of dispersion curves to obtain 1D Vs profiles, and (iv) spatial assembly and interpolation of 1D solutions to generate the final 2D Vs sections along each of the 26 profiles. The approach follows the conventional MASW methodology established for near-surface applications [39,40].

Pre-processing and quality control of shot gathers

Raw shot gathers were imported into ParkSEIS and assigned the correct acquisition geometry, including 24 channels, 1 m receiver spacing, an approximately 10 m near-source offset, a 5 m shot interval, and profile-specific spread positions. Each record was inspected to verify coherent Rayleigh-wave energy, consistent trace polarity, and absence of clipped or dead channels. Noisy traces, if present, were edited conservatively by trace removal or muting to avoid biasing dispersion trends. Where required, basic preprocessing steps were applied to enhance surface-wave coherence while preserving true dispersion characteristics, including time-windowing/muting of contaminated arrivals, band-pass filtering over the accepted processing range, and amplitude scaling to improve the visibility of coherent wave trains.

Dispersion imaging in ParkSEIS and picking strategy

For each shot gather, ParkSEIS was used to compute a frequency–phase velocity (f–c) dispersion image (dispersion energy spectrum). Dispersion images were generated for repeated forward shots at each setup to confirm stability and repeatability of the modal trend. The fundamental mode was chosen because it is generally the most continuous and robust for near-surface MASW inversion. Higher modes were considered only when they were clearly separated, laterally consistent between adjacent records, and improved the inversion stability; otherwise, they were excluded to avoid mode misidentification.

Dispersion picking was performed along the maximum-energy ridge in the f–c image and was restricted to frequency bands with: (i) high spectral energy, (ii) coherent and continuous modal behavior, and (iii) physically plausible dispersion trends. Frequencies dominated by incoherence, interference, or spatial aliasing (a potential issue at 1 m receiver spacing at high frequencies) were excluded from the inversion. Because the MASW objective here is to image the shallow subsurface to ~ 30 m, low-frequency picks (which provide greater depth sensitivity) were retained where coherent energy was present, while ensuring that the picked bandwidth remained consistent with the array aperture and signal quality.

Inversion of dispersion curves to 1D Vs models

Each picked dispersion curve was inverted in ParkSEIS to obtain a corresponding 1D Vs profile beneath the midpoint of the active spread. The inversion used an iterative forward modeling and misfit minimization procedure in which theoretical Rayleigh-wave dispersion curves from a layered Earth model were matched to the observed fundamental-mode picks. The final starting parameterization used 10 layers extending to an effective investigation depth of approximately 30 m. Layer thicknesses increased gradually with depth to reflect decreasing resolution at greater depths, with thinner layers in the upper 8–10 m where the highest-frequency picks provide better sensitivity and thicker layers below. Initial Vs values were bounded within geologically reasonable ranges based on the repeated velocity structure observed across the dataset: approximately 0.35–0.76 km/s for the surficial cover/weak-zone range, 0.76–1.50 km/s for the fractured/weathered carbonate interval, and > 1.50 km/s for the competent carbonate/limestone domain.

To reduce non-uniqueness and suppress unrealistic oscillations, the inversion was regularized using smoothness/stability constraints and by preferring monotonic or gently varying Vs trends, where consistent with the picked dispersion curves and local geological expectations. Abrupt velocity reversals were retained only when supported by coherent dispersion picks and by neighboring 1D solutions. Inversion quality control used three criteria: (i) RMS misfit generally ≤ 5%, (ii) visual agreement between observed and modeled dispersion curves over the accepted picking band, and (iii) lateral consistency with adjacent inversion points along the same profile. Solutions exhibiting unstable behavior, such as nonphysical reversals, abrupt, isolated velocity spikes, or boundaries inconsistent with adjacent stations, were revisited by checking the dispersion pick, removing noisy/non-repeatable picks, simplifying the layer model, and rerunning the inversion. Solutions that remained unstable after these steps were not used as independent evidence for weak-zone interpretation.

Generation of 2D Vs sections for all 26 profiles

The final 2D Vs sections were produced by assembling the accepted 1D Vs profiles along each MASW line and interpolating between adjacent inversion points to form continuous 2D velocity images to the effective depth of investigation. A consistent linear interpolation scheme, a 0.5 m x 0.5 m display grid, and light lateral smoothing were applied to all profiles to enable direct comparison of Vs structure between lines without overinterpreting sharp boundaries that are below the inversion’s resolving capability. Iso-velocity contours were overlaid on each 2D section to support stratigraphic correlation and to emphasize key stiffness transitions and anomalous low-Vs zones relevant to landfill pathway-susceptibility screening. Velocity-threshold selection and weak-zone susceptibility framework

The velocity thresholds used in this study were selected as operational MASW interpretation markers from repeated breaks in the 26 inverted Vs sections and from the regional carbonate setting. The approximately 0.76 km/s contour consistently separates the surficial cover/near-surface low-stiffness response from the stiffer underlying carbonate interval and was therefore used to identify low-Vs weak zones and the base of the shallow unit. The approximately 1.50 km/s contour marks the laterally persistent transition to the high-stiffness competent carbonate/limestone domain and was used to map the depth to competent bedrock. These thresholds are not presented as universal lithologic boundaries; they are study-specific stiffness markers that support consistent correlation among profiles and require calibration where direct lithologic or hydraulic confirmation is needed.

The weak-zone occurrence index was derived from the 26 MASW sections as follows. For each profile, a weak-zone occurrence was counted where a localized anomaly had Vs < 0.76 km/s, vertical thickness of at least approximately 2 m, position mainly within the upper 12 m, and either lateral persistence over at least one inversion station or repeatability in an adjacent/nearby section. For every profile, we recorded the number of weak-zone occurrences, their typical depth range, approximate thickness, and whether they were isolated or laterally persistent. The profile-scale weak-zone susceptibility classification was then assigned qualitatively as low, moderate, or high: low where weak zones were absent or isolated/thin; moderate where one or more shallow weak zones were present but limited in thickness or persistence; and high where shallow low-Vs anomalies were thicker, laterally persistent, or coincident with a thicker fractured/weathered carbonate interval above the Vs ≈ 1.50 km/s boundary. This index is a reproducible screening tool for ranking monitoring and ground-truthing priorities, not a quantitative estimate of the probability of leachate presence.

Results and Interpretation

Figure 8 compiles the shear-wave velocity (Vs) models from the 26 MASW profiles across the Rumah landfill site, providing laterally continuous 2D images of near-surface stiffness to an effective investigation depth of approximately 30 m. Table 3 summarizes the Vs ranges, thicknesses, and depth intervals for the three Vs-defined geoseismic units and weak zones, as interpreted from Fig. 8. The velocity field ranges from approximately 0.35 to 2.30 km/s, and the sections repeatedly exhibit two diagnostic iso-velocity transitions at about 0.76 km/s and 1.50 km/s. These contours are used as operational interpretation markers because they recur across multiple profiles and correspond to clear changes in the stiffness architecture; they should not be treated as universal lithologic boundaries without independent calibration.

Across the 26 MASW profiles, the top of the competent carbonate/limestone domain, approximated by the Vs ≈ 1.50 km/s boundary, varies from approximately 8 m to 22 m depth, with a typical depth of about 15 m. This geometry implies that the thickness of the overlying fractured/weathered carbonate interval varies substantially across the site. Using the Vs-defined boundaries, this intermediate interval (Vs ≈ 0.76–1.50 km/s) is typically about 5–15 m thick and locally approaches about 18 m where the competent-carbonate surface deepens. Low-V weak-zone features (generally Vs < 0.76 km/s) occur repeatedly throughout the dataset and are concentrated mainly within the upper 2–12 m. Where present, these weak zones typically show about 2–6 m vertical thickness and are spatially associated with the surficial unit and the upper part of the fractured/weathered carbonate interval.

To provide a reproducible site-scale summary of the 26 sections, Fig. 9 synthesizes the depth to the top of the competent carbonate/limestone domain (Vs ≈ 1.50 km/s), and Fig. 10 summarizes the weak-zone susceptibility index by profile. The index was derived from profile-scale interpretation of low-Vs zones using the criteria defined in the Methodology: weak-zone occurrence was counted where Vs < 0.76 km/s anomalies were at least approximately 2 m thick, occurred mainly within the upper 12 m, and were traceable over at least one inversion station or repeated in adjacent sections. Higher susceptibility was assigned where these weak zones were shallow, thicker, or laterally persistent, and coincided with a thicker fractured/weathered carbonate interval above the 1.50 km/s boundary. The resulting index is a screening metric for prioritizing confirmatory investigation, not a direct measure of leachate concentration or confirmed hydraulic conductivity.

Vs-defined geoseismic architecture

Across the full dataset, the Vs sections consistently resolve three principal geoseismic units with variable thickness and continuity. The shallowest unit (Layer 1) is characterized by relatively low velocities, typically 350–760 m/s, and forms a discontinuous cap extending from the ground surface to a depth of about 2–8 m. The base of this surficial cover/near-surface low-stiffness unit commonly coincides with the approximately 760 m/s contour, indicating a transition from lower-stiffness near-surface materials to a stiffer subsurface domain. Lateral changes in Layer 1 thickness from about 2–3 m in some areas to about 6–8 m in others reflect local variability in surficial deposits, cover materials, disturbance, and compaction state.

Beneath Layer 1, the intermediate unit (Layer 2) is the most laterally variable part of the subsurface. It is defined mainly by Vs values of approximately 760–1500 m/s and is interpreted, in the regional carbonate context, as a fractured/weathered carbonate interval rather than as a directly proven lithologic unit. This unit generally begins at depths of about 2–8 m and extends downward to the top of the competent carbonate/limestone domain marked by the approximately 1500 m/s boundary. Its short-wavelength lateral variations are consistent with spatially variable weathering, fracturing, porosity, moisture effects, or dissolution-related heterogeneity within carbonate bedrock.

At greater depths, a laterally more continuous high-velocity domain (Layer 3) is observed, with Vs values ≥ 1500 m/s and commonly about 1700–2300 m/s. This unit is interpreted as a competent carbonate/limestone domain based on its high stiffness and regional geological context. Because the investigation limit is approximately 30 m, its basal boundary is not imaged; however, the upper surface delineated by the approximately 1500 m/s transition provides a practical proxy for mapping the top of higher-stiffness bedrock across the site.

Depth to competent limestone and thickness variability of the fractured zone

Figure 9 shows the undulating geometry of the competent limestone surface. The depth to Vs ≥ 1500 m/s varies markedly across the 26 sections, typically ranging from ~ 8 to 22 m. Where this boundary is shallow (e.g., near ~ 8–10 m), the fractured limestone zone is relatively thin (commonly ~ 5–8 m). In contrast, where the boundary deepens toward ~ 20–22 m, the fractured zone thickens substantially (commonly ~ 12–18 m). This pattern indicates a laterally irregular bedrock topography and suggests spatial variability in the intensity of fracturing/weathering or in the degree of carbonate bedrock alteration near the surface.

The repeated occurrence of localized deepening of the ~ 1500 m/s boundary is especially important for site characterization: thicker fractured-limestone intervals increase the vertical extent of mechanically heterogeneous material above competent bedrock, with implications for settlement susceptibility and the continuity of potential preferential-flow pathways in the subsurface.

Weak zones: depth, thickness, and mechanical meaning

In addition to the three-layer framework, Fig. 10 highlights multiple weak zones manifested as localized low-Vs anomalies. Quantitatively, these weak zones are most consistently associated with Vs values < 760 m/s, occur predominantly at depths between about 2 and 12 m, and generally have vertical thicknesses of about 2–6 m. For the susceptibility synthesis, only anomalies satisfying the Methodology criteria were counted: Vs < 760 m/s, minimum vertical thickness of about 2 m, shallow position within the upper 12 m, and either lateral persistence over at least one inversion station or repeatability in adjacent/nearby sections.

Mechanically, these anomalies represent zones of reduced shear stiffness relative to surrounding materials. They may reflect one or a combination of disturbed or poorly compacted near-surface materials, higher moisture content, increased porosity, weathering, enhanced fracturing, or incipient dissolution within the upper carbonate interval. Therefore, the anomalies are interpreted as indicators of weak zones and susceptibility to pathway migration rather than as direct evidence of active leachate migration. Their frequent occurrence in Layer 1 and the upper part of Layer 2 suggests that stiffness reductions are concentrated in the subsurface portion most susceptible to surface-water infiltration, anthropogenic disturbance, and weathering.

Environmental interpretation: implications for leachate migration pathways

From an environmental standpoint, the Vs structure indicates that the near-surface system combines (a) a variable surficial cover/near-surface low-stiffness unit about 2–8 m thick that includes low-Vs weak pockets, and (b) an intermediate fractured/weathered carbonate interval (Vs ≈ 0.76–1.50 km/s) that commonly extends to depths of about 8–22 m. This configuration is significant because zones of reduced stiffness and fractured/weathered carbonate may be more susceptible to preferential infiltration than the deeper competent carbonate/limestone domain. Areas where shallow weak zones overlie a thicker fractured/weathered carbonate interval should therefore be treated as priority targets for focused monitoring, ground-truthing, and mitigation planning. However, because low Vs is not uniquely diagnostic of leachate, these areas should be described as potential pathway-susceptibility zones until verified by fluid-sensitive geophysics, drilling, or sampling.

Discussion

Linking the MASW outcomes to the main target of the study

The objective of this study is to assess the usefulness of dense MASW profiling for near-surface stiffness characterization and pathway-susceptibility screening at the Rumah landfill site. We do not claim that MASW reliability has been formally validated by independent boreholes, hydraulic tests, or hydrochemical data. Instead, confidence in the interpretation is based on dense spatial coverage, repeatability of major velocity transitions across 26 profiles, consistency of the 0.76 km/s and 1.50 km/s boundaries, and lateral agreement among adjacent inversion points. This framing aligns the objective with the evidence in the dataset and avoids overstating the method’s performance.

Rumah dataset is a strength because the 26 MASW profiles provide dense lateral coverage and reduce interpretive ambiguity compared with isolated single-line surveys. The repeated appearance of the approximately 0.76 km/s and 1.50 km/s iso-velocity contours across Fig. 8 indicates that the mapped transitions are not merely profile-specific artifacts but laterally recurrent stiffness contrasts. Nevertheless, the interpretation remains a MASW-derived stiffness model and should be calibrated with boreholes, ERT/IP, and/or hydrochemical sampling before being used to confirm lithology, permeability, or active leachate impact [42,43].

Interpreting low-Vs anomalies in terms of hydro-environmental susceptibility requires a cautious link between mechanical stiffness and likely hydraulic behavior. In fractured carbonates, lower Vs can coincide with higher effective permeability where fracture density, open joints, dissolution-enhanced secondary porosity, and pore connectivity reduce stiffness and promote connected flow [44,45]. However, low Vs may also reflect fine-grained, clay-rich, disturbed, or moisture-softened materials that are not necessarily highly permeable [46,47]. Accordingly, the low-Vs weak zones mapped here are interpreted as indicators of pathway susceptibility in a fractured-carbonate setting, not as direct evidence of transmissive leachate pathways.

Interpretation of Vs-defined layers in a carbonate platform setting and implications for preferential flow

The MASW sections resolve a consistent three-unit Vs architecture: (1) surficial cover/near-surface low-stiffness material with Vs of approximately 350–760 m/s and thickness typically about 2–8 m; (2) an intermediate fractured/weathered carbonate interval with Vs of approximately 760–1500 m/s and thickness generally about 5–15 m; and (3) a competent carbonate/limestone domain with Vs ≥1500 m/s, commonly about 1700–2300 m/s, whose top occurs at approximately 8–22 m depth (Fig. 8). These are velocity-defined geoseismic units. Their geological labels are interpretations supported by the regional carbonate setting and should be confirmed with independent subsurface control.

The carbonate geology described in the manuscript (Aruma Formation carbonates and the karst-prone Umm er Radhuma aquifer system regionally) provides a defensible geological mechanism for the observed velocity heterogeneity: carbonate terrains commonly develop fracture networks and dissolution-enhanced secondary porosity, producing spatially variable stiffness and permeability. Recent work on karst development in southeastern Riyadh (central Saudi Arabia) highlights how hydrochemical and geological processes drive dissolution and karstification in regional carbonate sequences, supporting the inference that fracture- and solution-related features can be common and hydraulically important in the broader central Saudi setting [26].

From a leachate-migration perspective, the most consequential feature is the coexistence of shallow low-Vs weak zones (< 760 m/s, commonly ~ 2–12 m depth) with a thicker fractured limestone interval (~ 760–1500 m/s) extending down to ~ 8–22 m. In hydrogeological terms, this configuration is consistent with a conceptual model in which leachate-charged infiltration (especially during episodic rainfall) can be captured by shallow disturbed/porous zones and then transmitted downward through fracture-enhanced carbonate layers until the more competent limestone is encountered. Importantly, where the ~ 1500 m/s boundary deepens toward ~ 20–22 m, the fractured interval thickens, increasing the vertical extent of potentially transmissive material above competent bedrock and thereby raising the likelihood of sustained downward connectivity.

Significance of weak zones: mechanical softness, moisture, and potential pathways

The weak zones delineated in Figs. 8 and 10 (typically Vs < 760 m/s, thickness about 2–6 m, depth range about 2–12 m) are geotechnically relevant because they indicate reduced shear stiffness and may also be environmentally relevant because they may reflect higher void ratio, higher moisture content, material disturbance, weathering, or fracture/void development. International landfill studies show that moisture distribution and leachate accumulation can strongly influence geophysical contrasts; therefore, combining MASW with electrical methods is recommended to distinguish mechanically weak but relatively dry materials from soft, conductive, and potentially leachate-affected ground8,42,48.

A particularly relevant international example is the demonstration that joint seismic–electrical inversion can be used to estimate landfill water content and identify wetter zones that correlate with enhanced biogeochemical activity42. While Rumah focuses on host-ground pathways (fractured limestone and weak zones) rather than internal waste hydrology alone, the key implication transfers directly: water content and saturation strongly influence geophysical responses, and thus weak zones detected by MASW represent priority targets for integrated hydrogeophysical follow-up (ERT/IP), especially where they appear to connect into the fractured limestone.

Comparison with relevant studies

On a local/regional scale in Saudi scale, Rumah findings are consistent with landfill investigations that identify environmentally significant subsurface heterogeneity and highlight pathways for contaminant migration. A study southeast of Riyadh documented geo-environmental impacts associated with landfill settings in the central region and highlighted environmental risks linked to site conditions and subsurface transport. Although the Rumah study uses MASW rather than primarily hydrochemical/geo-environmental indicators, both converge on a common conclusion: landfill sites may influence groundwater quality when subsurface pathways exist.

At the national scale, groundwater monitoring around Saudi landfill sites in the Eastern Province demonstrated measurable impacts on shallow groundwater quality downstream of landfill footprints, underscoring that contamination detection in Saudi aquifer settings is not hypothetical and reinforcing the need for pathway-focused site characterization50. The Rumah MASW results complement such hydrochemical evidence by providing a physically grounded mechanism for weak zones and fractured limestone intervals that could plausibly explain rapid or focused transport where protective controls are inadequate.

Geophysically, the open dumpsite study near Khamis Mushait (southwestern Saudi Arabia) used an integrated geophysical assessment to evaluate subsurface conditions around a waste site and highlighted the roles of subsurface structures and anomalous zones in environmental interpretation51. The match with Rumah is conceptual rather than lithologic (carbonate platform vs. other settings): in both cases, geophysical anomalies are interpreted as zones where contaminant migration and groundwater vulnerability may be elevated, and both studies demonstrate that geophysical imaging can efficiently guide targeted ground-truthing.

More recently, a Saudi landfill evaluation at Kaakia (Makkah) combined environmental and geophysical inspection to identify potential environmental threats and emphasized the value of geophysical methods for screening landfill impacts in Saudi environments52. The Rumah study extends this national body of work by providing (i) denser MASW coverage (26 profiles) and (ii) a Vs-based layered model that explicitly maps the depth to competent limestone (~ 8–22 m) and the geometry of fractured/weak zones, both of which are directly actionable for monitoring design.

Internationally, the Rumah approach aligns with studies emphasizing that MASW is especially useful for resolving stiffness layering and locating low-velocity anomalies, particularly when survey design provides broad coverage10. The “multi-method” perspective is also consistent with global best practice: modern studies frequently combine MASW/SRT with ERT/IP/GPR to reduce ambiguity and to connect mechanical structure with moisture/leachate signatures8,10.

In addition, recent international applications demonstrate how electrical methods can directly image contaminant-related zones and even quantify contaminated volumes when criteria (e.g., chargeability thresholds) are established43. This is relevant for Rumah because MASW has already provided a spatial hypothesis for mechanically weak and potentially permeable zones; integrating ERT/IP would be the logical next step to determine whether these same zones are also electrically consistent with the presence of leachate or enhanced saturation. Similarly, machine-learning-supported interpretation of resistivity/IP data has recently been shown to improve the delineation of leachate accumulation zones and to validate predictions against well data with high accuracy48.

Practical implications for groundwater protection and monitoring at Rumah

Because the MASW results indicate that the mechanically “open” system (weak zones + fractured limestone) may extend from the surface down to ~ 8–22 m, monitoring strategies limited to only the uppermost few meters risk missing the most hydraulically relevant interval. The mapped depth to competent limestone provides an evidence-based constraint on the minimum depth range to consider for shallow groundwater risk evaluation in the immediate vicinity of the landfill. Furthermore, locations where (i) weak zones are thickest (~ 2–6 m) and (ii) the fractured limestone is thickest (where the ~ 1500 m/s boundary deepens toward ~ 20–22 m) should be prioritized for confirmatory drilling and for placement of monitoring points, because these locations represent the most plausible preferential pathways under the conceptual model supported by both carbonate hydrogeology26 and modern landfill hydrogeophysics42,48.

Importantly, the broader interpretation distinguishes pathway susceptibility from confirmed leachate impact. Weak or low-Vs zones may reflect disturbed materials, weathering, elevated moisture, increased porosity, fracture concentration, or combinations of these factors. Some of these conditions may promote contaminant transport, but none alone proves the presence of leachate. Accordingly, the MASW outputs are best treated as a first-stage screening tool that identifies where transport pathways are plausible and where verification is most needed. This perspective is consistent with current international practice, in which mechanical imaging is commonly integrated with fluid-sensitive approaches such as ERT/IP and subsequently validated through targeted drilling and hydrochemical sampling.

Conceptual model

The results support a conceptual model (Fig. 11) in which landfill vulnerability in arid carbonate terrains is governed by the potential connectivity of preferential pathways that may become active during episodic infiltration events. In this model, rainfall and surface drainage can focus infiltration into mechanically weaker near-surface zones that act as possible entry points linking the surface to a fractured/weathered carbonate interval. Transport may then proceed preferentially along connected fracture or porous pathways until it is impeded or redirected by more competent carbonate domains. This conceptualization implies that monitoring and mitigation should be prioritized where the subsurface architecture favors potential connectivity, while recognizing that active leachate migration must be confirmed independently.

Uncertainty and limitations of the MASW-derived synthesis: MASW-derived Vs models provide a robust stiffness framework but do not uniquely determine lithology, saturation, permeability, or the presence of contaminants. Uncertainty arises from non-uniqueness in surface-wave inversion, limited depth sensitivity due to weak low-frequency energy, possible mode misidentification in heterogeneous ground, and smoothing/interpolation when locally 1D inversions are assembled into 2D sections. Confidence is highest for the laterally repeated 0.76 km/s and 1.50 km/s stiffness transitions and for weak zones that recur across adjacent stations or profiles; confidence is lower for isolated, thin, or poorly laterally persistent anomalies. Boundary depths should therefore be treated as approximate, and the weak-zone susceptibility index should be used as a prioritization tool rather than a quantitative probability map. External calibration using boreholes or test pits, downhole/geotechnical measurements, ERT/IP, and hydrochemical or pore-water sampling is required to confirm lithology, saturation state, hydraulic connectivity, and any leachate impact.

Conclusions

The MASW investigation conducted across Rumah landfill provides a dense, internally consistent stiffness-based framework for near-surface characterization and pathway-susceptibility screening. The 26 profiles enable repeated correlation of major velocity transitions and weak-zone anomalies across the site, improving confidence relative to sparse single-line interpretation. However, in the absence of direct borehole, hydraulic, or hydrochemical validation, the results should be interpreted as evidence of subsurface heterogeneity and susceptibility, not as proof of active contaminant pathways.

The results resolve three Vs-defined geoseismic units. The uppermost unit is a surficial cover/near-surface low-stiffness unit with Vs values of approximately 350–760 m/s and a typical thickness of about 2–8 m. This unit overlies an intermediate fractured/weathered carbonate interval characterized by Vs of approximately 760–1500 m/s, with thickness commonly about 5–15 m and locally increasing where the competent carbonate surface deepens. The basal unit is a competent carbonate/limestone domain with Vs ≥ 1500 m/s, often reaching 1700–2300 m/s. The top of this high-stiffness domain varies significantly across the site, occurring at depths of approximately 8–22 m. This laterally undulating boundary and the variable thickness of the fractured/weathered interval highlight the heterogeneous nature of the carbonate subsurface beneath the landfill.

The main screening outcome is the identification of localized weak zones expressed as low-Vs anomalies (< 760 m/s) that commonly occur at depths of about 2–12 m and typically have vertical thicknesses of about 2–6 m. These anomalies represent zones of reduced shear stiffness and may reflect disturbed ground, elevated moisture, increased porosity, weathering, enhanced fracturing, or combinations of these factors. Their spatial association with the fractured/weathered carbonate interval suggests they may serve as entry points or conduits for infiltration during episodic rainfall, particularly where they coincide with a thicker intermediate carbonate interval above the competent domain. This interpretation remains a susceptibility model and requires independent confirmation before it can be considered evidence of active leachate migration.

Targeted confirmatory investigations are recommended at locations where weak zones are shallow, relatively thick, laterally persistent, and associated with a deepened competent-carbonate boundary of about 20–22 m. Integrating MASW with ERT/IP, boreholes or test pits, and hydrochemical or pore-water sampling is strongly recommended to distinguish mechanically weak but relatively dry materials from zones with elevated saturation or leachate influence. For groundwater and vadose-zone protection, monitoring designs should account for the full thickness of the fractured/weathered carbonate interval and be adjusted to the locally mapped depth of the competent carbonate/limestone domain.

Future work should focus on calibrating the Vs-defined units against borehole lithology, in situ geotechnical tests such as SPT/CPT or downhole Vs where feasible, and hydrochemical/pore-water observations. The 26 profiles provide a strong basis for developing a site-wide 3D susceptibility model that integrates depth to competent carbonate and weak-zone distributions into a decision-support map for monitoring placement and mitigation planning. Time-lapse MASW and/or ERT/IP could also be used to evaluate seasonal changes and the effectiveness of mitigation measures.
