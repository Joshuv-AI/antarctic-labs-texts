MANAGEMENT OF PERIFOVEAL EXUDATIVE VASCULAR ANOMALOUS COMPLEX
Authors: Maria Cristina Savastano, Claudia Fossataro, Gianni Gravina, Francesca Coccimiglio, Clara Rizzo, Stanislao Rizzo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The aim of this article was to report a case of a patient with perifoveal exudative vascular anomalous complex, trying to describe morphologic and vascular changes with optical coherence tomography and optical coherence tomography angiography before and after treatment. Because no therapy is still approved, the authors would like to propose their experience as a possible therapeutic scheme of management.

The retina evaluation has been completely revolutionized by the advent of optical coherence tomography (OCT), because of its support to the clinicians not only to make the correct diagnosis but also to detect further distinctive features of retinal diseases. The continuing upgrade of the devices and the introduction of the optical coherence tomography–angiography (OCTA) has led to a more detailed retinal and choroidal analysis and novel classifications of structural and vascular retinal conditions. As a matter of fact, Querques et al1 first described in 2011 a new vascular entity, not related with diabetes, hypertension, or other vascular chorioretinopathies, named as “perifoveal exudative vascular anomalous complex” (PEVAC), whose features did not match with any other known retinal vascular abnormality. Typically, patients with PEVAC complain about nonprogressive visual decline and metamorphopsia.2 As demonstrated by Sacconi et al,2 PEVAC appears as a unilateral, commonly unifocal, large aneurism, associated with hemorrhages and/or hard exudates at funduscopic evaluation, whereas the structural OCT shows a round lesion, characterized by hyperreflective margins, a dark lumen, with intraretinal cystic spaces and/or hard exudates nearby. Fluorescein angiography (FA) shows a well-defined hyperfluorescent lesion with variable leakage, whereas the indocyanine green angiography (ICG) does not show any choroidal vascular changes. The OCTA scans provide a flow signal at the aneurysmal lesion in the superficial capillary plexus (SCP) or deep capillary plexus or in both, associated with capillaries rarefaction in the perilesional area.2

The aim of this case report was to describe the multimodal imaging approach to make the diagnosis of PEVAC and to analyze the retinal changes using OCT and OCTA before and after different treatments.

Case Report

A 57-year-old male patient referred to our center complaining about an enduring decrease in his visual acuity in the right eye (RE), with a previous diagnosis of central serous chorioretinopathy under oral acetazolamide and vitamin supplement therapy. We performed a complete ophthalmologic examination, comprehensive of retinal multimodal imaging, including color fundus image, near-infrared acquisition, and structural OCT and OCTA analysis using Solix full-range OCT (Optovue Inc, Freemont, CA). The best-corrected visual acuity (BVCA) was 20/50 in the RE and 20/20 in the left eye (LE). The anterior segment examination was unremarkable in both eyes, as well as the fundus analysis of the LE. However, the fundoscopic evaluation of the RE showed a fluid rising in the macula region, associated with an intraretinal aneurysm between the macula and the optic disk. The structural OCT of the RE revealed a fluid lifting of the neuroepithelium, intraretinal cystic fluid associated with a perifoveal round hyporeflective lesion, defined by a hyperreflective circular outline. A flow signal was detected by the OCTA at the lesion level in the deep capillary plexus, in the absence of any macular neovascularization (Figure 1). Our final diagnosis was of PEVAC. We suggested the patient to check artery systemic pressure, which revealed higher diastolic artery pressure, thus controlled with systemic compensatory therapy.

At baseline, the retinography of the right eye (RE) showed a fluid rising in the macula region, associated with a small hemorrhage between the fovea and the optic disk (A). The structural OCT of the RE revealed a fluid lifting of the neuroepithelium in foveal region, intraretinal cystic fluid associated with a perifoveal round hyporeflective lesion, defined by a hyperreflective circular outline: the macroaneurysm (B). A circular focal flow signal was detected by the OCTA at the macroaneurysm level in the deep capillary plexus (DCP) (C). On the enface, the hyperreflective sign represented the outline of the PEVAC lesion, whereas the hyporeflective areas all around showed the intraretinal cystic spaces and the subretinal fluid, collected below the fovea (D).

Although no therapy is still approved, as reported by Herda et al,3 we opted for intravitreal anti–vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) treatment and performed three aflibercept (Eylea; Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Inc., Westchester County, New York) intravitreal injections in the RE. After 3 months, the patient referred blurred vision in the RE, with a BCVA of 20/100. The fundoscopic examination showed persistent microhemorrhages and some hard exudates at the posterior pole appeared. On structural OCT, the rise of the neuroepithelium was not observable any longer, whereas intraretinal fluid and the PEVAC lesion were still evident. Thus, we opted for subthreshold micropulse laser therapy (SMPL) (IRIDEX—IQ577, 577 nm, 200 mW power, 5% duty cycle, 200 ms duration, 200 µm spot diameter) in the RE. Two months after the laser treatment, the patient did not refer any changes in his visual condition. However, the structural OCT revealed a marked decrease in the intraretinal fluid and still detectable PEVAC lesion in the RE (Figure 2). Thus, the patient underwent a second SMPL. Three months later, the BCVA was increased, achieving 20/160 (with a gain of 40 letters), and the structural OCT showed only few cystic spaces nearby the PEVAC lesion and few hard intraretinal exudates. Since the partial response to the SMPLs, we decided for a new session of laser treatment with full threshold and the following setting: 100 mW power, 300 ms duration, 100 µm spot diameter (IRIDEX – IQ577, 577 nm). The choice of power laser was based on the spot tests with visualization of slight whitening beyond the upper vascular arcade. We start with power of 90 mW and it was not visible; bringing it to 110 mW, it was just whitening, so we decided to continue the direct treatment of the macroaneurysm with 100 mW.

Two months after the two subthreshold micropulse laser treatments (SMPL) with 5% duty cycle, the retinography showed hard exudates at the posterior pole, and the persistence of aneurysms (A). The structural OCT revealed a marked decrease in the intraretinal fluid (B), whereas a still detectable persistence of flow was observed in PEVAC lesion (C). On the enface, the outline of the PEVAC lesion was still evident, in association with few intraretinal hyporeflective cystic spaces and accumulation of hyperreflecting material arranged in a radial pattern all around, representing hard exudates (D).

At the last follow-up, it could be appreciated from the OCT examination that the retinal profile was restored, in the absence of any intraretinal or subretinal fluid and PEVAC lesion, whereas the OCTA showed a slight flow deficit at the previous lesion site (Figure 3). The BCVA was 20/20.

At the last follow-up, 3 months after the full-threshold laser, the retinography showed only few hard exudates, no hemorrhages, absence of intraretinal aneurysm (A). The structural OCT showed a restored retinal profile, absence of intraretinal or subretinal fluid, and extremely reduced of macroaneurysm lesion with full hyperreflective appearance: “aneurysmal thrombization feature” (B). The OCTA showed a slight flow deficit at the previous lesion site (C). On the enface, no sign of the PEVAC lesion was shown, whereas few hard exudates were still detected (D).

Discussion

Perifoveal exudative vascular anomalous complex was reported for the first time by Querques et al1 in 2011, described as unilateral, isolated, intraretinal aneurismal abnormality in otherwise healthy patient. Recently, Sacconi et al4 defined two different phases of this entity: a nonexudative stage, nonexudative perifoveal vascular anomalous complex (“nePVAC”), characterized by the presence of microvascular abnormalities, in the absence of exudative signs and visual impairment, which may represent the subclinical pre-exudative stage of the following exudative phase, defined as exudative perifoveal vascular anomalous complex (“ePVAC”). Currently, there are two main hypothesis for PEVAC pathogenesis. The former consists in a focal endothelial cells damage, whose progressive loss may be responsible of the evolution from the nonexudative stage to the ePVAC.1,2,4 The latter, proposed by Spaide and Barquet,5 hypothesized that the aneurysm development derived from a decrease in vascular wall strength, because of pericytes loss, after an increasing expression in matrix metalloproteinase-9. Pericytes play a crucial role in preserving the blood–retina barrier integrity; thus, their dropout could be responsible of edema, aneurysm, and ischemia. According to these theories, the cystic fluid accumulation may not depend on VEGF. As a matter of fact, in previous literature, there is no evidence of anti-VEGF intravitreal injection response in patients affected by PEVAC, with the only exception of one case of fluid resolution after 13 anti-VEGF therapy.1–3,6,7 Of note, spontaneous retinal recovery has been reported, and this may explain the resolution of PEVAC in Mrejen's experience, which may depend not on the anti-VEGF injections, however on the natural course of the disease.2,6,8 According to the scientific literature, we did not observe any improvement in our patient after three intravitreal injections of aflibercept. Full-threshold laser photocoagulation, focused on the aneurismal lesion, has been proposed by Mrejen et al.7 as a possible cure, even though it would not be exempt of risks in case of extreme proximity to the fovea. In contrast, SMPL with duty cycle at 5% has been proven by the experience of Gawecki9 to be safe and effective for the treatment of different retinal diseases, such as central serous chorioretinopathy, diabetic macular edema, and macular edema secondary to vein occlusion. The SMPL provides many repetitive short impulses, interchanged with brief interval periods, giving the opportunity to the retina to cool down.9Especially for the safety profile with the possibility to generate a scar because of the extreme proximity of the macroaneurysm to the fovea, we decided to start with the SMPL with duty cycle at 5% (577 nm, 200 mW power, 5% duty cycle, 200 ms duration, 200 µm spot diameter). Only after the first session, we could appreciate a marked improvement in our patient; for this reason, we decided for further similar treatments. However, the additional SMPL sessions were not resolutive in our case. Only full threshold laser treatment was effective to achieve visual acuity improvement, a complete resolution of the intraretinal fluid, a retinal profile recovery, and disappearance of PEVAC lesion on OCT and OCTA scans. To the best of our knowledge, as reported by the experience of Kang et al,10 there is only one reported case of multiple sessions of SMPL (532 nm, 100 µm spot diameter, 2 × 2 matrix pattern mode, power ranging from 300 to 400 mW, increased in 50 mW each 3 to 4 weeks, 5–10% duty cycle) for PEVAC, performed after three anti-VEGF intravitreal injections of two different drugs and one triamcinolone acetonide injection unsuccessfully. In this case, the aneurysm involution was obtained, although it was not clear if it should be considered as a nePVAC stage or complete resolution.10 In additionally, an ellipsoid zone interruption was detected below the previous lesion after SMPL.10 Using an SMPL at 5% of duty cycle with different wavelength and standardized parameters setting for each treatment session, we observed a partial retinal response. In our case, a complete restoration of the retina was obtained only with a full-threshold laser treatment, with all detectable layers and “thrombization.” In this case, the closure of the aneurysmatic PEVAC lesion corresponded to the outcome of the full threshold laser treatment, and it can be comparable with a phenomenon of “thrombization.” This procedure has been already described in other medical disciplines for the treatment of aneurysmatic lesions of great dimensions. The only difference is related to the vessel caliber of the different anatomic vascular districts because retinal circulation is characterized by vessels of small caliber. In our case, the full-threshold laser treatment induced a complete resolution of the PEVAC lesion, like the phenomenon of thrombization had caused an exclusion of the vascular lesion from the retinal circulation.

Conclusion

The PEVAC treatment is still unknown. Several approaches have been proposed for the treatment of PEVAC, some are more conservative with less possible side effects, like SMPL, some others are more damaged like laser treatment near the fovea. In our experience, we described a case of multiple therapies for unresponsive PEVAC syndrome. Aflibercept intravitreal injections and subthreshold SMPL duty cycle 5% were ineffective. In our patient, the only effective treatment resulted to be the focal full-dose yellow laser. We proposed our multiple management to share the heterogeneous response at PEVAC entity, which is particularly unresponsive to anti-VEGF injections and SMPL.
