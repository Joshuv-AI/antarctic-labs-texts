Comparative descriptive analysis of hospital disaster preparedness using WHO safety index: a multi-center study from Eastern India
Authors: Kumar Guru Mishra, Nabnita Patnaik, Nihar Ranjan Pradhan, Adityananda Mohapatra, Sheikh Mohd Saleem
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHospital disaster preparedness is crucial for maintaining healthcare delivery during emergencies, yet significant gaps persist in developing nations. Introduction

Disasters, whether natural or human-induced, represent significant challenges to healthcare systems worldwide. The United Nations Office for Disaster Risk Reduction (UNDRR) defines disasters as serious disruptions of community functioning that exceed local response capabilities, necessitating external assistance for recovery [1]. Recent global events, including the COVID-19 pandemic, have highlighted critical vulnerabilities in healthcare infrastructure and emergency response systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries [2].

Healthcare facilities serve as critical infrastructure during disasters, yet they frequently become casualties themselves. Between 2020 and 2023, approximately 84% of hospitals worldwide reported significant disruptions in service delivery during various disasters, emphasizing the urgent need for robust preparedness protocols [3]. The World Health Organization’s Health Emergency and Disaster Risk Management Framework (2023) emphasizes that healthcare facilities must maintain operational continuity during disasters while managing surge capacity for mass casualties [4].

Despite increasing recognition of the importance of hospital disaster preparedness, significant gaps persist in implementation, particularly in developing nations. A systematic review by Bazyar J et al. (2023) found that only 38% of hospitals globally have comprehensive disaster management plans, with even fewer conducting regular drills and updates [5]. This deficiency becomes particularly concerning as climate change intensifies natural disasters and urbanization increases the potential impact of technological hazards [6].

Hospital disaster preparedness encompasses multiple dimensions: structural safety, non-structural components, functional capacity, and emergency management protocols. The Joint Commission International (JCI) standards for hospital accreditation emphasize the need for integrated emergency management systems that address all hazard types [7]. Recent studies indicate that hospitals with comprehensive disaster preparedness plans demonstrate significantly better outcomes during actual emergencies, with up to 60% reduction in mortality rates during mass casualty incidents [8].

The Indian context presents unique challenges, with its diverse geographical vulnerabilities and varying institutional capacities. The National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) guidelines mandate hospital disaster management plans, yet compliance and effectiveness remain inconsistent [9]. Recent assessments indicate that only 45% of Indian tertiary care hospitals maintain updated disaster preparedness protocols, with even fewer conducting regular simulation exercises [10].

Our study aims to evaluate the emergency and disaster management preparedness at a tertiary care institution in Eastern India, utilizing the WHO Hospital Safety Index framework. This assessment focuses particularly on institutional protocols, staff readiness, and resource allocation for disaster response. The findings will contribute to the growing body of evidence on hospital disaster preparedness in developing nations and inform policy recommendations for strengthening healthcare resilience.

Materials and methods

Study Design and Setting: This observational cross-sectional study was conducted across thirteen tertiary care hospitals (A through M) in Khordha District, Odisha, India, during January 2020 to December 2023. The study sites were selected based on their significant healthcare delivery roles in eastern India, collectively serving populations from multiple states including Bihar, Jharkhand, West Bengal, and Chhattisgarh.

Study Period Rationale: The study period (January 2020-December 2023) was deliberately selected to capture hospital preparedness both during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. This timeframe provided a unique opportunity to evaluate how tertiary healthcare facilities adapted their disaster management protocols in response to a prolonged public health emergency. The pandemic served as a real-world stress test for hospital emergency systems, allowing us to assess both theoretical preparedness and practical implementation. Data collection was adjusted during peak pandemic waves to maintain research integrity while adhering to safety protocols.

Hospital Classification: For analytical purposes, the thirteen hospitals were categorized into three groups based on bed capacity: smaller tertiary facilities (400–450 beds: Hospitals C, F, I, M), medium tertiary facilities (451–500 beds: Hospitals B, E, H, J, L), and larger tertiary facilities (> 500 beds: Hospitals A, D, G, K). This classification was chosen as bed capacity serves as a standardized metric of institutional size and often correlates with organizational complexity, resource availability, and staff strength—factors that may influence disaster preparedness capabilities.

Study Context: The study location, Khordha District, has a documented population of 2,251,673 (Census 2011), comprising 1,084,316 urban and 1,167,357 rural residents. The participating institutions collectively manage approximately 39,000 outpatient consultations and 2,600 emergency department visits daily, functioning as major regional healthcare hubs.

Assessment tool: The evaluation was based on Module-4 (Emergency and Disaster Management) of the WHO Safe Hospital Checklist [11], which focuses on assessing hospital preparedness across seven critical sub-modules.(Appendix I) These include: the organization and functioning of the Emergency/Disaster Committee and Emergency Operations Center; the existence and adequacy of operational plans for managing internal and external disasters; contingency plans for the medical treatment of disaster-related cases; and protocols for the operation, preventive maintenance, and restoration of critical services. Additionally, the assessment covered the availability of essential medicines, supplies, instruments, and equipment required during disasters; established procedures for evacuation, intra-hospital transportation, and triage; and the implementation of emergency staff training programs and regular response drills. This structured approach enabled a comprehensive evaluation of institutional readiness for emergency and disaster situations.

Data collection and scoring methodology

A multi-modal data collection strategy was employed to ensure comprehensiveness and triangulation of findings. This included direct observation of facility operations for real-time appraisal of healthcare processes and infrastructure, alongside a detailed review of institutional documentation to assess policy adherence, standard operating procedures, and historical records. Semi-structured interviews were conducted with key stakeholders—administrative officials, healthcare providers, patients, and attendants—to capture diverse perspectives and contextual insights.

The assessment utilized a structured framework comprising 40 evaluation criteria, each with equal weightage and a maximum score of 2.5 points. A three-tier scoring scale—Low (0 points), Average (1.25 points), and High (2.5 points)—standardized performance grading. Cumulative safety indices were calculated by aggregating sub-module scores and applying appropriate weightings, resulting in a comprehensive performance metric that reflected compliance with safety standards and operational efficiency.

While the standard WHO Hospital Safety Index classifies hospitals into three categories (Class A: 66–100%, Class B: 36–65%, Class C: 0–35%), this study adopted a modified continuous scoring approach to enable finer differentiation in preparedness levels. This adaptation retained the core structure of the WHO tool but allowed for more granular comparison across facilities. Safety index values from our assessment can still be mapped to the WHO classification, with scores > 0.66 corresponding to Class A (high preparedness), 0.36–0.65 to Class B (moderate preparedness), and < 0.36 to Class C (low preparedness). This adapted approach has been validated in previous regional studies to better reflect context-specific variations in healthcare systems.

Data analysis

Safety index calculations adhered to the WHO-recommended methodology, incorporating individual criterion scores, sub-module aggregation, weighted calculations, and final index determination. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0. Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, and coefficients of variation, were computed for all variables. To compare safety indices across hospitals, one-way ANOVA was employed, followed by Tukey’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to explore relationships among sub-modules. For non-normally distributed data, non-parametric tests such as the Kruskal-Wallis test were applied.

Subgroup analyses were undertaken to assess variations in preparedness based on hospital characteristics, including bed capacity (categorized as 400–450, 451–500, and > 500 beds), NABH accreditation status (fully accredited, conditionally accredited, and non-accredited), and teaching status (teaching vs. non-teaching). One-way ANOVA with post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test was used to determine significant differences between subgroups (significance set at p < 0.05). Pearson’s correlation analysis further examined associations between institutional characteristics and preparedness levels, while multiple regression analysis identified predictors of high disaster preparedness scores.

In particular, subgroup analysis revealed notable variations in preparedness based on accreditation status. The hospitals were categorized into fully NABH-accredited (n = 5), conditionally accredited (n = 4), and non-accredited (n = 4) groups, and safety indices were compared using one-way ANOVA to detect significant differences.

To ensure objectivity, a series of rigorous quality control measures were implemented. A standardized training program was conducted for all evaluators before data collection to ensure consistent understanding of the assessment framework. Each facility was evaluated independently by a team of three assessors to reduce individual bias. Inter-rater reliability was established through pilot assessments at two non-study hospitals, promoting calibration among evaluators. Discrepancies in scoring were resolved through structured consensus discussions. Furthermore, a validation process involving secondary review of 25% randomly selected criteria was integrated to verify the consistency and accuracy of assessments. A comprehensive evaluation checklist with detailed scoring criteria was used throughout the assessment process and is available as Appendix A in supplementary file.

Ethical Considerations: The study protocol received approval from the Institute Ethics Committee, AIIMS Bhubaneswar (Approval No. IEC/AIIMS/BBSR/Research/2019-20/16) dated 8 May 2019. Written informed consent was obtained from all study participants prior to data collection. Institutional anonymity was maintained as per administrative requirements.

Results

This analysis evaluates the emergency preparedness and disaster management capabilities of thirteen hospitals (A, B, C, D, E, F, G, H, I, J, K, L, and M) across seven key sub-modules. The findings provide insights into the strengths and weaknesses of each institution’s readiness for emergency situations.

The thirteen participating tertiary care hospitals had bed capacities ranging from 400 to 550 beds (mean: 471.2 ± 39.8 beds): Hospital A (500 beds), Hospital B (450 beds), Hospital C (400 beds), Hospital D (550 beds), Hospital E (480 beds), Hospital F (425 beds), Hospital G (510 beds), Hospital H (460 beds), Hospital I (440 beds), Hospital J (495 beds), Hospital K (515 beds), Hospital L (470 beds), and Hospital M (430 beds). Collectively, these institutions manage approximately 39,000 outpatient consultations and 2,600 emergency department visits daily.

Discussion

This study evaluates emergency preparedness and disaster management capabilities across thirteen hospitals, highlighting both strengths and gaps in various sub-modules. The findings underscore the need for targeted interventions to enhance institutional resilience and response capacity.

Coordination of emergency and disaster management activities

“Significant variability was observed in coordination capabilities. Hospitals B, G, and K demonstrated the highest preparedness, reflecting well-structured emergency management committees and effective collaboration with external agencies. Conversely, Hospitals A, I, and M showed the lowest performance, emphasizing the need to establish stronger emergency management structures and foster inter-agency partnerships. These findings align with research by Adini et al. [12], who reported that hospitals with robust coordination mechanisms exhibit superior emergency response capabilities. Similar findings were reported by Djalali et al., who demonstrated that hospitals with established emergency committees and clear command structures showed significantly better performance during actual disaster events [13]. The variability in coordination capabilities observed in our study mirrors findings from a systematic review by Bazyar et al. [5], which revealed that only 42% of hospitals globally have functional emergency committees with well-defined roles and responsibilities. Furthermore, Paturas et al. highlighted that effective coordination between hospitals and external agencies was a critical determinant of successful emergency response in multiple case studies [14].

Emergency response and recovery planning

“Emergency response planning emerged as the most challenging area, with the lowest mean score (0.460) and highest variability (CV = 67.2%). Hospitals B, C, G, K, and L demonstrated the highest preparedness, showcasing comprehensive hazard-specific sub-plans and structured activation procedures. However, Hospitals A, I, and M exhibited critically low preparedness levels, highlighting the urgent need for enhanced strategic planning. These findings corroborate previous studies [15, 16] indicating that many hospitals lack comprehensive all-hazards preparedness frameworks, limiting their response effectiveness during crises. The high variability in emergency planning observed in our study is consistent with findings from Sheikhbardsiri et al. [17], who reported significant disparities in emergency planning across healthcare facilities. A comprehensive assessment by Achora and Kamanyire found that inadequate emergency planning is particularly prevalent in resource-constrained settings, with fewer than 30% of hospitals maintaining updated and tested emergency plans [18]. Our findings also align with an assessment by the National Disaster Management Authority [9], which identified significant gaps in disaster preparedness planning across Indian healthcare facilities.”

Communication and information management

“Communication preparedness varied significantly, with Hospitals B, C, G, and K excelling due to well-integrated internal and external communication systems. However, substantial inconsistencies were observed across other hospitals, with Hospitals A, I, and M reporting the lowest scores. The high variability (CV = 52.6%) suggests critical gaps in crisis communication strategies. Previous research by Sheikhbardsiri et al. [17] has emphasized the importance of standardized communication protocols in improving hospital emergency responsiveness. These findings are consistent with previous research [19, 20], in which they identified communication systems as a persistent vulnerability in hospital emergency preparedness. Park et al. conducted a multi-center analysis of hospital communication systems and found that facilities with redundant communication channels and regular system testing demonstrated significantly better performance during emergency simulations [21]. The inconsistencies in communication preparedness observed in our study highlight the need for standardized protocols and regular communication drills, as recommended by the Joint Commission International standards [7].

Human resource preparedness

“Hospitals B, C, G, K, and L demonstrated strong human resource preparedness, reflecting well-maintained staff contact lists and structured mobilization strategies. Moderate preparedness was observed in Hospitals D, E, F, H, and J, whereas Hospitals A, I, and M exhibited significant deficiencies in workforce readiness and emergency training. Addressing these gaps is crucial, as research suggests that well-prepared personnel play a pivotal role in mitigating disaster-related disruptions [21, 22]. The variable levels of human resource preparedness observed in our study align with findings from Khirekar J et al. [22], who identified substantial inconsistencies in emergency training across healthcare facilities. A systematic review by Hsu et al. revealed that only 38% of healthcare workers receive adequate disaster-specific training, with significant disparities in training quality and frequency across institutions [23]. Similarly, Burkle et al. [19] emphasized that personnel competency and mobilization strategies are fundamental components of effective disaster response, yet remain underemphasized in many healthcare settings.”

Logistics and financial preparedness

“Logistics and finance emerged as the strongest area across all hospitals, achieving the highest mean score (0.875) with perfect consistency (CV = 0%). This indicates well-established supplier agreements, secure emergency transport, and robust provisioning of essential resources. The uniformity of preparedness in this sub-module suggests effective institutional planning and resource allocation [24]. This finding contrasts with research by Khirekar J et al. [22], who reported significant variability in logistics preparedness across Indian hospitals. Zhong et al. similarly observed that logistics and supply chain management often demonstrate higher preparedness compared to other domains, attributing this to the critical importance of resource availability during emergencies [25]. The consistent performance observed in our study may reflect growing recognition of the essential role of logistics in emergency response, as highlighted in the WHO Health Emergency and Disaster Risk Management Framework [4].”

Patient care and support services

“Patient care preparedness varied, with Hospitals B, D, G, K, and L demonstrating the highest levels of readiness, particularly in maintaining continuity of critical care, mass casualty triage, and infection control. Moderate preparedness was observed in Hospitals C, E, F, H, and J, while Hospitals A, I, and M reported the lowest scores, indicating an urgent need to strengthen patient referral systems and emergency medical services. These findings align with Aacharya et al. [24], who emphasized the critical role of efficient triage and emergency care in hospital disaster response. Similar patterns were observed in Saadatmand et al.‘s assessment [8], which highlighted the correlation between robust patient care protocols and reduced mortality during mass casualty incidents. Ardalan et al. [26] conducted a comparative analysis of patient care systems in emergency settings and found that standardized triage protocols and clear referral pathways were associated with improved patient outcomes during disasters. The variability in patient care preparedness observed in our study underscores the need for standardized protocols and regular simulation exercises, as recommended by the NDMA guidelines [9].”

Evacuation, decontamination, and security

“Preparedness levels in evacuation, decontamination, and security varied, with Hospitals B, C, G, and K demonstrating the highest performance. However, moderate preparedness was observed in a majority of hospitals, indicating a need for improved emergency evacuation strategies and security protocols. Hospitals A, I, and M exhibited the lowest scores, highlighting weaknesses in chemical and radiological hazard responses. Previous studies [19, 27] have recommended regular simulation exercises to enhance preparedness in these areas. These findings are consistent with Ablah et al. [28], who identified significant gaps in evacuation planning and decontamination capabilities across healthcare facilities. A comprehensive assessment by Melnychuk et al. revealed that fewer than 40% of hospitals maintain updated evacuation plans and decontamination protocols, with even fewer conducting regular drills [29]. The variability in evacuation and security preparedness observed in our study highlights the need for standardized protocols and regular simulation exercises, as recommended by Nekoie-Moghadam et al. [30].

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study focused exclusively on Module-4 of the WHO Hospital Safety Index, while a comprehensive assessment would ideally incorporate all modules (structural, non-structural, and functional capacities). The interconnected nature of these components means our evaluation may not fully capture the complete disaster preparedness picture. Second, the cross-sectional design provides only a snapshot of preparedness status without longitudinal insights. Third, the subjective nature of some assessment criteria may introduce evaluator bias, despite our efforts to standardize the evaluation process. Fourth, the study did not capture patient outcomes during actual emergency events, which would provide validation of the preparedness measures. Future research should address these limitations through longitudinal designs and correlation with actual emergency response outcomes.

Policy Implications and System-Level Considerations: The findings of this study have significant implications for healthcare policy at state and national levels. The marked disparities in preparedness across institutions within the same geographical region highlight the need for standardized regulatory frameworks that establish minimum preparedness requirements for tertiary healthcare facilities. Currently, the National Disaster Management Authority (NDMA) guidelines lack specific enforcement mechanisms, resulting in inconsistent implementation. Our findings support the need for mandatory compliance with disaster preparedness standards as part of hospital licensing requirements.

At the state level, the establishment of a centralized disaster preparedness monitoring system could facilitate regular assessment and targeted intervention. The consistently high performance in logistics and finance across all hospitals suggests that Odisha’s existing policy framework effectively addresses resource management during emergencies. However, the critical gaps in emergency response planning indicate that current policies inadequately prioritize operational readiness for immediate disaster response.

A novel finding from our study is the disconnect between resource availability and operational protocols—hospitals with adequate resources often lacked corresponding procedural frameworks for deployment during emergencies. This suggests that national policy should shift from a resource-centric approach to an integrated model that equally emphasizes procedural readiness and resource allocation.

Furthermore, the variance in communication systems preparedness reveals a critical vulnerability in regional healthcare infrastructure that could significantly impair multi-institutional response during widespread disasters. This finding has particular relevance for eastern India, a region frequently affected by cyclones and floods, where coordinated response across healthcare facilities is essential for effective disaster management.

Our results also offer new insights into the relationship between institutional characteristics and disaster preparedness. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on urban-rural distinctions, our analysis suggests that within the same district, organizational factors such as leadership commitment, regular drills, and cross-institutional knowledge sharing may be more significant determinants of preparedness than geographical or financial factors.

Factors influencing preparedness levels

Our findings reveal significant disparities in disaster preparedness across the thirteen hospitals, prompting analysis of underlying determinants. Several institutional factors appear to influence preparedness levels:

Leadership Commitment and Organizational Structure: Hospitals with high preparedness indices (B, G, K) demonstrated strong administrative support for emergency planning, with dedicated emergency management units and clear reporting structures. Conversely, hospitals with low preparedness (A, I, M) exhibited fragmented responsibility assignment and limited leadership engagement, consistent with findings by Adini et al. [12].

These factors indicate that enhancing disaster preparedness requires a multifaceted approach addressing organizational, financial, regulatory, and educational dimensions. Targeted interventions addressing these specific determinants may be more effective than generalized recommendations in improving regional healthcare resilience.

Conclusion

Despite strengths in logistics, finance, and patient care, major gaps persist in emergency response planning, communication, and coordination. Standardizing protocols, strengthening workforce training, and fostering knowledge-sharing between high- and low-performing hospitals are critical to enhancing disaster preparedness. Future research should explore the underlying factors contributing to superior performance in top-tier hospitals and assess the long-term impact of targeted interventions on hospital preparedness levels. Additionally, implementing periodic drills and structured feedback mechanisms may further strengthen institutional response capabilities, ensuring a more cohesive and resilient healthcare system.
