Risk stratification and data-driven phenotyping of peripherally inserted central catheter-related bloodstream infection: an explainable machine learning analysis of a large critical care cohort
Authors: Yujia Liu, Chao Cong, Dongsheng Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeripherally inserted central catheter-related bloodstream infections (PICC-CRBSIs) are significant causes of morbidity and mortality in critically ill patients. Therefore, improved risk stratification is required for targeted prevention. This study aimed to identify key predictors and novel patient phenotypes of PICC-CRBSI using explainable machine learning.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study using data from the MIMIC-IV Critical Care database. Patients undergoing PICC insertion were categorized into PICC-CRBSI (n = 237) and control (n = 8,052) groups. A machine learning pipeline was developed incorporating LASSO regression for feature selection and SHAP analysis for model interpretation. Introduction

Peripherally Inserted Central Catheters (PICCs) are widely used, especially among patients in the ICUs, because of their relative safety and potential for medium-to long-term venous access that can offer useful pathways for the administration of medications, parenteral nutrition, and hemodynamic monitoring [1]. However, PICCs are not entirely free of risks and complications, and catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs), including PICC-CRBSIs, are among the most serious and common [2]. PICC-CRBSI leads to increased morbidity and mortality in patients, prolonged hospital stays, and increased healthcare costs, presenting a major challenge to patient safety and healthcare resources [2, 3].

Although prior studies have explored the associated risk factors of PICC-CRBSI, including the timing of PICC insertion, comorbidities, and immunosuppression [4], it is still difficult to stratify the exact risks for certain patients. Conventional risk assessment models often have certain limitations in reflecting the complex relationships between multiple factors in various scenarios. Therefore, it is necessary to discover more effective approaches to identify high-risk populations for PICC-CRBSI, which can further guide the development and application of preventive interventions.

In this study, we attempted to apply rich real-world data from a large clinical information database (MIMIC-IV) [5] in combination with advanced machine learning methods, including Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression, and unsupervised learning algorithms, such as Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) and Hierarchical Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise (HDBSCAN), aiming to discover different patient subtypes with high or low risks for PICC-CRBSI and explore key clinical predictors for these high-risk phenotypes. Ultimately, we expect to provide new evidence-based insights to improve PICC management, lower infection rates, and achieve better clinical outcomes in critically ill patients.

Methods

Data source and ethical approval

This retrospective cohort study used de-identified patient information from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC-IV v2.2), a publicly available critical care database. MIMIC-IV data can be accessed using PhysioNet (Credentialed Health Data Use Agreement, CITI Certification ID: 12102426). The Institutional Review Boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (Cambridge, MA) and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston, MA) approved the study protocol and waived the need for informed consent due to the retrospective nature and use of de-identified data.

Cohort definition and variable extraction

An initial cohort of 12,359 unique PICC insertion records was extracted based on the procedural codes from the MIMIC-IV database. Data extraction was performed using materialized views from the official MIMIC code repository implemented in PostgreSQL (version 16). The cohort was then further refined after applying the following exclusion criteria: (1) patients with multiple PICC insertion events in the study period, retaining only the first insertion to make the observations independent; (2) patients with < 18 or ≥ 90 years old, in accordance with the HIPAA de-identification requirements; (3) records with > 50% missing values across important clinical parameters.

Variables including patients’ demographics, laboratory parameters, vital signs, comorbidities (documented using International Classification of Diseases, ICD codes) and pharmacological interventions were systematically extracted. Variables with > 30% missing values across the entire cohort were excluded from further analyses. Our primary outcome of interest PICC-CRBSI was operationally defined using the composite ICD code (99932, T80211, T80211A, T80211D, T80211S) documented in the medical records proximal to the PICC event. Thus, we obtained the final analytical cohort, which included 237 PICC-CRBSI cases and 8052 controls. All cohort selection and initial data manipulation steps were performed using PostgreSQL (version 16).

Data preprocessing

Continuous variables were further subjected to winsorization at 1.5 × IQR thresholds to eliminate the impact of extreme outliers. Missing data were addressed using a single imputation approach tailored to variable types. Continuous variables were imputed using Iterative Imputer (MICE algorithm, 20 iterations), while categorical variables used KNN imputation (k = 3). For intergroup comparison of descriptive statistics, we implemented a non-parametric Mann-Whitney U test for skewed continuous variables and chi-squared (χ²) tests with Yates’ continuity correction for categorical variables.

Feature selection and importance ranking

All predictor variables were first standardized (Standard Scaler). Sample weights were computed for the logistic regression model to account for class imbalance. LASSO logistic regression model was employed for feature selection, configured with an L1 penalty, the ‘saga’ solver, max_iter = 10,000, tol=1e-4 and warm_start enabled. Optimal regularization strength for the logistic regression (C parameter) was found via grid search over a log space of values between 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻¹ (100 values). 10-fold stratified cross-validation was used as a scoring method (Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve, ROC AUC). Variables with nonzero coefficients from the optimal LASSO model were retained.

We aimed to reduce multicollinearity between features. Therefore, we computed a pairwise Pearson correlation matrix of LASSO-selected features. For each feature pair with an absolute correlation coefficient of > 0.7, we dropped one of the two features. We retained only a nonredundant set of features. We retrained the logistic regression on the refined set of features with class_weight=’balanced’. Finally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values were computed on this retrained model. LinearExplainer quantified the importance of each feature. We retained only features with a mean absolute SHAP value > 0.1.

Dimensionality reduction and cluster analysis

A sequential pipeline was developed for dimensionality reduction and cluster identification. The dataset was first split into training (70%) and test (30%) sets using stratified sampling to maintain class distribution. All continuous features were standardized separately for training and test sets using StandardScaler. To account for class imbalance before applying UMAP embedding, we applied a form of oversampling and undersampling (SMOTEENN). We employed supervised UMAP to project the data into 2-dimensional space (n_components = 2), configured with n_neighbors = 200, min_dist = 0.8, target_weight = 0.8, and metric=’euclidean’. The UMAP model was trained exclusively on the resampled training data. We employed supervised UMAP to project the data into 2-dimensional space (n_components = 2). Optimal hyperparameters were determined through grid search with 3-fold cross-validation, using the F1 score of logistic regression classifiers trained on UMAP embeddings as the primary evaluation metric. The final configuration was: n_neighbors = 200, min_dist = 0.8, target_weight = 0.8 and metric=’euclidean’. The UMAP model was trained exclusively on the resampled training data.

We calculated cluster characteristics and PICC-CRBSI incidence rates for each identified cluster. Statistical significance of incidence rate differences across clusters was assessed using chi-square tests (P < 0.05), and the results were visualized. For feature importance analysis, we implemented a comprehensive three-dimensional assessment: UMAP importance based on linear regression coefficients between original features and UMAP embeddings, Cluster silhouette correlation measuring the relationship between feature values and silhouette scores within clusters, and Cluster effect analysis using logistic regression coefficients to identify features distinguishing cluster 0 from other clusters. Additionally, interactive feature importance analysis and parallel coordinate plots were created using Plotly (Plotly Technologies, Inc., Montreal, QC, Canada) to facilitate clinical decision support.

All workflows for feature selection, dimensionality reduction, and clustering were implemented in Python (version 3.12.0) using scikit-learn, pandas, numpy, shap, umap-learn, hdbscan, and plotly libraries. These were primarily implemented within the scikit-learn pipeline architecture to ensure reproducibility. A flowchart of the process is shown in Fig. 1.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort and intergroup comparisons

A total of 8,289 patients who underwent PICC insertion were included in the final cohort, consisting of 237 patients (2.86%) who developed PICC-CRBSI and 8,052 patients (97.14%) who did not develop PICC-CRBSI (control group). The baseline characteristics of the study cohort stratified according to the PICC-CRBSI status are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

There were also large differences (P < 0.05) in the following variables between the PICC-CRBSI and control groups. The patients in the PICC-CRBSI group were significantly younger (median age: 63.17 years vs. 66.83 years, P < 0.001) and sustained their PICC catheters for significantly longer (median: 99.45 h vs. 78.10 h, P < 0.001). Patients who developed PICC-CRBSI had consistently lower hematologic parameters: this was manifested as a minimum hematocrit (median: 27.40% vs. 28.80%), maximum hematocrit (median: 30.70% vs. 32.70%), minimum haemoglobin (median: 8.80 g/dL vs. 9.40 g/dL) and maximum haemoglobin (median: 9.90 g/dL vs. 10.70 g/dL) (all P < 0.001). Markers of renal function were significantly elevated in the PICC-CRBSI group, i.e., maximum creati ninine (median: 1.30 mg/dL vs. 1.10 mg/dL, P = 0.002) and maximum blood urea nitrogen (BUN) (median: 27.00 mg/dL vs. 23.00 mg/dL, P = 0.005).

Hemodynamically, patients with PICC-CRBSI had higher mean heart rate (median: 92.00 bpm vs. 87.52 bpm, P < 0.001) and lower mean systolic blood pressure (median: 110.70 mmHg vs. 114.53 mmHg, P = 0.001). In terms of comorbidities, patients in the PICC-CRBSI group had a lower prevalence of cerebrovascular disease (10.97% vs. 17.91%, P = 0.006), but a higher prevalence of renal disease (31.65% vs. 22.02%, P < 0.001) and malignancy (22.78% vs. 15.96%, P = 0.005). Additionally, the Acute Physiology Physiology Score III (APSIII) was significantly higher in the PICC-CRBSI group (median: 50 vs. 47, P = 0.036). No significant differences (all p > 0.05) were observed for baseline weight, Charlson Comorbidity Index, platelet counts, most electrolyte levels, except for minimum anion gap (P = 0.006), coagulation profiles, or other recorded severity scores.

Feature selection and eXplainable AI (XAI) analysis

Based on SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) values, the top 19 features with non-zero coefficients were selected as predictors of PICC-CRBSI by LASSO regression (optimal C = 0.007) (Fig. 2A-B). It is important to note that this observational study identifies associative patterns for risk prediction rather than causal factors. We then used these selected features to train the random forest model. The SHAP value represents the contribution of each feature to the prediction of the model. The three top predictors based on the mean absolute SHAP values were admission age (mean |SHAP| = 0.30), maximum hemoglobin level (mean |SHAP| = 0.28), and PICC catheter duration (mean |SHAP| = 0.19) (Fig. 2C-D). Other features with significant contributions (mean absolute SHAP value > 0.1) to the occurrence of PICC-CRBSI included the presence of renal disease, mean body temperature, minimum BUN, maximum potassium, history of myocardial infarction, mean heart rate, presence of malignant cancer, mean systolic blood pressure, history of cerebrovascular disease, and minimum anion gap.

Patient subgroup identification based on UMAP-HDBSCAN clustering

We applied the UMAP-HDBSCAN clustering pipeline, as detailed in the Methods, to the preprocessed and feature-selected dataset. The clustering results on the original training set (before SMOTEENN) are presented in Fig. 3A. The UMAP projection revealed two distinct, densely packed patient subgroups, designated as Cluster 0 and Cluster 1. Notably, under the optimal parameter configuration, the HDBSCAN algorithm assigned all data points to one of these two clusters, resulting in zero noise points and indicating a clear partition of the patient population. Figure 3C further quantifies the clinical significance of this partition, demonstrating a significantly higher PICC-CRBSI incidence rate in Cluster 0 compared to Cluster 1 (p < 0.001, chi-square test). To rigorously assess the generalizability and robustness of the identified patient phenotypes, we projected the held-out test set into the same latent space using the UMAP model trained on the training set. As shown in Fig. 3B, the two-cluster structure was preserved in the independent test set, with clear separation between Cluster 0 and Cluster 1. Consistent with the training set, all test set patients were assigned to a cluster with no noise points. Most importantly, the difference in infection rates between the clusters remained statistically significant in the test set (Cluster 0 vs. Cluster 1, p < 0.001), as confirmed in Fig. 3D.

The quantitative evaluation metrics, as detailed in Table 1, provide a nuanced understanding of the clustering performance and its generalizability. The pipeline achieved excellent results on the training set, with a high Silhouette Coefficient of 0.914 and a very low Davies-Bouldin Index of 0.382, indicating well-separated and compact clusters. This is further supported by an exceptionally high Calinski-Harabasz Index of 22,575.95. Most notably, a classifier based on the cluster labels achieved a near-perfect F1-Score of 0.9969 (± 0.0044) on the training set. When the UMAP model and HDBSCAN clustering were applied to the independent test set, the two-cluster structure was successfully reproduced, as visually attested in Fig. 3B and D. All test set patients were assigned to a cluster, resulting in a noise point ratio of 0%. However, the quantitative metrics revealed a moderate but expected decline in clustering quality, with a Silhouette Coefficient of 0.765 and a Davies-Bouldin Index of 0.504. The cluster-based classifier’s F1-Score on the test set was 0.4879.

Multimodal feature interpretation and interactive clinical profiling tool

Complementary importance analyses were performed to understand what determined the identified patient subgroups (Fig. 4). Among all features, catheter duration time (absolute UMAP coefficient = 1.330) and admission age (absolute UMAP coefficient = 1.348) were the main determinants of the separability of UMAP embeddings. Feature correlation with silhouette scores of HDBSCAN clusters indicated that the separability of clusters mainly depended on maximum hemoglobin (Pearson’s r = 0.150) and minimum anion gap (Pearson’s r=-0.141). Furthermore, logistic regression analysis identified minimum BUN (β = 0.443), maximum hemoglobin (β=-0.554), and admission age (β=-0.383) as primary predictors for membership in Cluster 0, with higher BUN values and lower hemoglobin levels associated with increased likelihood of belonging to the high-risk subgroup.

To explore the clinical applicability and generalization of the identified patient clusters, an interactive parallel coordinate plot (Supplementary Fig. 1) was developed as a local web-based tool. This tool was piloted within a hospital intensive care unit to enable clinicians to dynamically explore patient profiles and interactively visualize the two risk-based subgroups. The tool was deployed locally for internal validation and feasibility assessment; as it runs on a local server without public hosting, a live web link is not available for external access.

Discussion

A large real-world critical care database (MIMIC-IV) and an intricate pipeline of machine learning methodologies were used to explore risk factors and identify distinct patient phenotypes associated with PICC-related bloodstream infections (PICC-CRBSI). Our findings not only support previously reported risk factors but also show novel associations with different patient subgroups, facilitating a more granular understanding to guide targeted preventive strategies.

Interpretation of key findings and comparison with existing literature

The analysis of baseline characteristics showed that patients who developed PICC-CRBSI were generally younger. It is well known that older age is a risk factor for infections because of immunosenescence and higher comorbidity burden [6]. However, for a group of critically ill patients, younger patients may be more likely to be admitted with more acute diseases that require more intervention (e.g., trauma or acute respiratory distress syndrome), which results in a higher net state of risk [7]. This underscores the complexity of age as a risk factor in the ICU setting.

Our analysis substantiates the predictive utility of longer PICC catheter dwell time for PICC-CRBSI within our multivariate model. This finding aligns with extensive literature and prevention guidelines, which identify catheter duration as a key modifiable risk factor [8, 9]. The integration of this factor into our explainable machine learning framework allows for the quantification of its relative contribution to risk stratification, reinforcing the critical importance of daily catheter necessity assessments and prompt removal when no longer indicated. Furthermore, our model identified lower hemoglobin levels, renal impairment, and higher APS-III scores as significant predictors. These variables are recognized markers of physiological derangement and susceptibility to nosocomial infections [10, 11], and their selection by our model underscores their utility in profiling high-risk patients. Similarly, the association with malignancy, a well-documented risk state due to immunosuppression and frequent vascular access [12], was corroborated. Collectively, the convergence of our data-driven approach with established clinical knowledge strengthens the validity of our model for identifying patients who might benefit from intensified surveillance.

In this study, we employed a two-stage analytical framework to enhance both the robustness and interpretability of our risk assessment. First, LASSO regression was utilized for feature selection, effectively distilling a complex set of clinical variables down to 19 non-redundant predictors. Subsequently, SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was applied to a Random Forest model built upon these selected features. This approach moves beyond identifying mere associations; it quantifies the directional contribution and relative importance of each predictor within the model’s decision-making process. This level of interpretability is paramount for building clinician trust—a prerequisite for the adoption of ML tools in practice [13].

Novelty of patient phenotyping and implications for targeted prevention

Our study demonstrates that an unsupervised learning pipeline integrating supervised UMAP for dimensionality reduction and HDBSCAN for clustering can effectively identify distinct, clinically meaningful patient subgroups from high-dimensional ICU data pertaining to PICC-CRBSI risk [14]. The pipeline successfully delineated two robust patient phenotypes (Clusters 0 and 1) that were consistently separable in both the training and independent test sets. The statistical persistence of a significantly higher PICC-CRBSI incidence in Cluster 0 across both datasets (p < 0.001) strongly suggests that this separation corresponds to a genuine, generalizable difference in patient risk profiles rather than an artifact of overfitting. This is similar to a recently published study that used a similar data-driven approach to identify distinct sepsis phenotypes with different treatment responses [15]. Quantitative metrics provide a nuanced assessment of the clustering performance. The exceptionally high internal validation scores on the training set (e.g., Silhouette Coefficient of 0.914) indicate that the learned low-dimensional embedding creates a space where the two subgroups are near-perfectly separable. The observed, expected decline in these metrics on the test set (e.g., Silhouette Coefficient of 0.765) reflects the natural generalization gap when applying a model to unseen data. Crucially, however, the preservation of a clear two-cluster structure and, more importantly, the statistically significant difference in the clinically relevant outcome (infection rate) in the test set confirm the robustness and potential validity of the identified phenotypes.

Previous research on predictions for central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI) relied on supervised machine learning (e.g., Random Forest) for prediction [16, 17]. However, our approach addresses two fundamental limitations inherent in conventional supervised machine learning models commonly used for predicting CLABSI. First, the low incidence rate of CLABSI (typically below 5% and approximately 2% in this cohort) [18] creates a severe class imbalance that often undermines the performance of supervised models. In contrast, our hybrid strategy combining supervised dimensionality reduction with unsupervised clustering does not aim to directly classify the rare positive events. Instead, it seeks to uncover the intrinsic, risk-associated data structure, rendering it more robust to extreme class imbalance. Second, and more critically for clinical translation, traditional supervised models typically output an individual patient’s probability of developing CLABSI. While this provides a risk ranking, it offers limited intuitive guidance for clinicians to identify and conceptualize a coherent, high-risk patient group sharing common characteristics for targeted intervention. Our method, through unsupervised clustering, directly defines a distinct and interpretable “high-risk phenotype” (Cluster 0). This phenotype-based stratification shifts prevention strategy from reacting to an abstract probability threshold to proactively managing a well-defined patient subgroup with a clear clinical profile.

Phenotyping has direct clinical application. Patients classified as having this high-risk phenotype could be candidates for more aggressive prevention protocols. This could include more site assessments, greater use of chlorhexidine-impregnated dressings, insertion of antimicrobial-coated catheters, and interventions that have shown efficacy in high-risk populations [19]. The patient phenotypes identified in this exploratory, single-center study suggest a potential pathway for targeted clinical intervention, though their generalizability and clinical efficacy require prospective, multi-center validation. To facilitate the local evaluation and iterative refinement of these data-driven insights, we deployed a secure, web-based interactive tool within the hospital’s internal network. This tool, developed using Plotly, serves two primary functions to aid clinician comprehension: (1) a feature importance dashboard that visualizes the key clinical variables driving the UMAP-HDBSCAN clustering, thereby demystifying the primary factors distinguishing the subgroups; and (2) an interactive parallel coordinate plot that allows clinicians to dynamically filter and explore individual patient profiles across these key features, aiding in the visual distinction between the lower-risk (Cluster 1) and high-risk (Cluster 0) phenotypes. While not a validated clinical decision support system, this proof-of-concept tool is designed to translate our computational findings into a tangible format for bedside review. Its immediate purpose is to support local clinical discussion about whether patients exhibiting the high-risk profile (Cluster 0) might benefit from a more vigilant assessment or intensified prevention strategies.

Limitations

The major strengths of this study are the use of a large and granular real-world dataset (MIMIC-IV) and its application to a robust modern machine-learning pipeline. However, This study had several limitations that must be considered. First, this was a retrospective single-center study, similar to many studies using MIMIC-IV; thus, our results need to be externally validated in other institutions and populations to ensure generalizability. Second, our cohort definition, which retained only the first PICC insertion for each patient and excluded pediatric (< 18 years) and very elderly (≥ 90 years) patients to comply with data de-identification guidelines and ensure a homogeneous adult cohort, may limit the generalizability of our findings. These specific populations—pediatric, very elderly, and patients requiring multiple PICCs—represent important groups at risk for CRBSIs, and their exclusion means our model may not be directly applicable to them. Future studies focused on these specific populations are warranted. Third, by design, we retained only the first PICC insertion for each patient to ensure the independence of observations, a key assumption for the machine learning models used. This precluded the analysis of the effect of multiple insertions, which is itself a known risk factor for infection. Fourth, although the database was rich, we were unable to account for all the potential unmeasured confounders. We were unable to analyze specific catheter insertion techniques or measure maintenance care bundle adherence, variables that are known to have a large impact on CRBSI rates [20]. Fifth, our model did not capture variations in local antimicrobial stewardship practices and prescription patterns, which are known to have a large impact on the epidemiology of infections [21]. Finally, although we applied standardized criteria, the diagnosis of PICC-CRBSI can be complicated, and there may have been variations in how PICC-CRBSI was ascertained in the source data.

Future research directions

This study sets a stage for several important future research directions. The first priority should be external validation of our predictive model and the identification of patient phenotypes in diverse multicenter cohorts. After external validation, it is important to perform prospective studies to determine whether implementing interventions targeting the identified high-risk phenotype can effectively and safely reduce PICC-CRBSI rates. Future modeling work could extend our analysis to include more granular data, such as more details on catheter characteristics (e.g., number of lumens), microbial data, and dynamic time-series measurements of physiology.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated the power of applying a sophisticated machine-learning framework to a large clinical dataset to enhance our understanding of PICC-CRBSI. We identified key interpretable risk factors and delineated distinct high-risk patient phenotypes. These findings provide a data-driven foundation for the development and deployment of more precise and personalized preventive strategies to mitigate the significant burden of PICC-related infections in critically ill patients.
