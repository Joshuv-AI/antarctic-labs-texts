ABSTRACT
When the acquired immunodeficiency syndrome emerged in the 1980s, the United States National Institutes of Health established research networks to conduct clinical trials with the pharmaceutical industry to identify effective antiretroviral therapeutics. The clinical trials networks included laboratory centers with academic pharmacology laboratories measuring drug concentrations to allow for the estimation of pharmacokinetic parameters and correlation with pharmacodynamic outcomes. Adoption of comprehensive quality assurance initiatives was key to ensuring the integrity of pharmacology sampling and laboratory data provided by clinical sites and laboratories. Subsequently, this infrastructure facilitated rapid responses to co‐infection pathogens such as hepatitis C virus, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2. In 2008, the Center for Integrated Global Biomedical Sciences at the University at Buffalo was awarded the initial National Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases contract for the Clinical Pharmacology Quality Assurance Program. Since 2015, over 4,500 tutorial certificates for research staff and laboratories have been awarded on topics including the conduct of clinical pharmacology research protocols and bioanalytical method validation for antiretroviral assays. A bioanalytical peer review program for ensuring the quality of the assay methods has approved over 350 assays for > 100 analytes in 21 human biological matrices. An ISO‐17043 accredited external proficiency testing program has completed 35 rounds for 15 analytes. Also, a laboratory assessment program was established that utilizes international laboratory and regulatory standards, and multiple mechanisms for training, assistance and guidance to participants. This report summarizes the development of the CPQA program over the last decade.

After identifying the human immunodeficiency virus‐1 (HIV‐1) as the causative agent for AIDS, investigations focused on different enzymes in the sequential steps of the HIV‐1 life cycle and drugs that inhibited viral entry, reverse transcriptase, protease, and integrase activity, and more recently inhibition of a structural component, HIV capsid inhibition. This unprecedented drug development initiative required that partnerships be strengthened among government, academia, industry, and the community. The recognition that combination antiretroviral therapy was needed to achieve sustained viral suppression and prevent the emergence of resistance created another new paradigm focused on identifying drug–drug interactions that would increase or decrease antiretroviral (ARV) drug concentrations during the conduct of a clinical protocol 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 (Figure 1 ). As a result of the growing complexity required to design regimens for treatment arms, clinical pharmacology laboratory capacity expanded. This led the National Institute for Allergy and Infectious Diseases/Division of AIDS (NIAID/DAIDS) and their HIV research networks to establish laboratory centers that included academic clinical pharmacology laboratories (in addition to immunology, virology, and genomics) and related proficiency testing programs (PTPs). 6

HIV antiviral translational and clinical pharmacology timeline.

In 2008, NIAID/DAIDS released a competitive request for proposals to support a clinical pharmacology quality assurance (CPQA) program that would work with NIH‐funded clinical pharmacology laboratories as well as provide clinical research sites with training in the conduct of clinical pharmacology protocols. The University at Buffalo (UB) was awarded the initial contract with its subcontractor, Frontier Science, creating the CPQA. The vision of CPQA was to provide a program that would evolve in parallel with emerging investigational agents for HIV and assure that research protocols would yield high quality drug concentration data for eventual inclusion in larger datasets to address concentration–effect relationships, evaluation of drug interactions, and pharmacogenomic analyses.

With an array of ARVs available to use for prevention and treatment regimens, the field began to investigate the role of long‐acting ARVs to optimize medication adherence and reduce the burden on individuals to take daily medications. Also, a new area within HIV research, HIV eradication (also called Cure research), was developing. Cure research has recently transitioned toward a goal of HIV reservoir reduction, emphasizing the need to target pharmacokinetically protected compartments and creating the need to quantitate ARVs in these compartments such as gastrointestinal tissue (GALT) and the central nervous system (e.g., cerebrospinal fluid, CSF). Concurrently, the global HIV pandemic began to emerge as one that could be managed in Europe and North America but could not be controlled in low‐ and middle‐income countries, 7 where HIV treatment is more dependent on older drug regimens and co‐infections, such as tuberculosis, hepatitis B, and malaria, are more prevalent. These factors prompted clinical pharmacology laboratories to expand the numbers of quantitative methods and analytes.

Leveraging innate immune responses against HIV and concurrent advances in biologics for cancer, the field began pursuing the clinical development of HIV‐1 broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs). Initially isolated from HIV‐infected individuals who sustained viral load below detection, so‐called nonprogressors, these antibodies were able to bind to different sites on the spike protein and were active against multiple clades. Mutational strategies for these early bNAbs resulted in prolonged serum half‐lives facilitating intermittent parenteral dosing. bNAbs are currently under clinical investigation spanning across phases II and III studies for prevention, treatment, and reservoir reduction. 8 , 9 , 10 Chromatographic mass spectrometry‐based bioanalytical methods are being developed and applied for the characterization and quantitation of bNAbs to support clinical investigations. 11 , 12

With numerous ARVs available for HIV infection prevention and treatment, the established infectious disease research community began to focus on the global challenge of prevention and treatment for Mycobacterium tuberculosis. Global partnerships were formed, and clinical trials of new agents began to increase in number, eventually yielding a new generation of anti‐TB (ATB) drugs. 13 , 14 , 15 These ATBs required extensive drug interaction research and magnified the importance of having a rigorous clinical pharmacology plan for developing new ATBs, particularly in the context of global HIV infection. From the clinical pharmacology laboratory perspective, the complexity of conducting these studies is compounded as the needs for validated ATB assays and ARV assays increased the workload across studies. This report provides an update from the original program review that was published in 2013. 16

MISSION

CPQA focuses on providing a comprehensive quality assurance program for clinical pharmacology research programs supported by NIAID/DAIDS. While the program concentrates primarily on the inclusion of preanalytic and analytic quality support, the breadth of this mission requires the CPQA program to monitor current and upcoming research efforts through its external activities and respond with appropriate alterations within its internal program operations (Figure 2 ). Although initially limited to a selected group of university‐based clinical pharmacology laboratories in the HIV Research Networks, additional laboratories have been added from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, NIAID‐supported Centers for AIDS Research, and LMIC programs with a focus on ATB research. Together, select laboratories from these programs comprise the current CPQA Participating Laboratories (CPLs).

CPQA program infrastructure indicating internal operations and external mechanisms for communication.

GOALS

CPQA goals include (i) supporting preanalytical quality by providing bioanalytical laboratories with a bioanalytical method validation tutorial and an impartial confidential review of its bioanalytical method validation (BMV) process and the associated validation data, (ii) supporting preanalytical quality by interacting with clinical trial stakeholders and providing a clinical pharmacology study conduct tutorial for clinical research unit professionals including laboratory processing centers, and (iii) supporting analytical quality by providing a PTP and a laboratory assessment program for bioanalytical laboratory operations. An integrated approach to achieving these goals is accomplished by cross‐cutting data management, training, assistance, and guidance infrastructure (Figure 3 ). CPQA seeks to publish and disseminate program results in a confidential and impartial manner to improve the quality of pharmacology measurements made in clinical trials.

PROGRAM ORGANIZATION

CPQA has established an organizational matrix infrastructure that facilitates program planning and implementation while also encouraging cross‐program collaboration. The CPQA management team has external communications with the Contract Officer's Representative (COR) at DAIDS, an external Advisory Board made up of government and academic experts, and the CPQA participating laboratories (CPLs) through a group forum. This approach facilitates rapid information exchange among the contract sponsor (NIH), advisors, and the program participants. Intra‐program communications are accomplished with five internal areas of operations that include Training, Assistance and Guidance (TAG), CPQA Laboratories, Quality Management, Operations and Administration, and Data Management and Biostatistics. The latter area of operations is delivered by the subcontractor, Frontier Science (Amherst, New York). (Figure 4 ).

CPQA PROGRAMS

Bioanalytical method peer review program

CPQA creates and provides its participating laboratories with a guidance document for bioanalytical assay validation: Guidelines for Bioanalytical Chromatographic Method Development, Validation, And Application. This guidance has been revised over the years to incorporate regulatory and white paper upgrades to the process. Whenever changes were made, a working group was formed from voluntary members for CPLs: Bioanalytical Revisions Advisory Group (BRAG). BRAG and CPQA bioanalytical specialists worked together to further revise and adopt the final document. This shared process afforded the CPLs an opportunity for input and comprehension of the final document. Currently, the CPQA is incorporating the ICH Harmonized Guideline Bioanalytical Method Validation and Study Sample Analysis M10 requirements (ICH 17 ).

To receive, review, and provide recommendations to ensure Analytical Validation Reports (AVRs) adequately meet the CPQA guidance requirements, an electronic submission utility was created to ensure an organized, confidential, and impartial process for reviews. CPQA oversees and manages the review program processes and acts as a reviewer‐submitter referee when necessary. CPQA employs several bioanalytical specialty consultants that provide CPLs with objective and unbiased feedback on laboratory method validation results and their initial method standard operating procedure (SOP). After internal review of approved AVRs by CPQA leadership, approved methods are posted as the CPQA drug assay directory on its public website.

Tutorials

The BMV tutorial is directed toward laboratory staff, graduate students, and early‐stage faculty investigators. The tutorial utilizes the concepts and requirements for FDA Bioanalytical Method Guidance (May 2018) and is currently being updated to include the ICH M10 concepts and requirements adopted by the FDA, EMA, and other international agencies. New CPLs that may not have previously completed AVRs and SOPs often benefit from having their staff complete the BMV tutorial in conjunction with technical guidance meetings with CPQA.

The Clinical Pharmacology Study Conduct tutorial was developed by UB prior to the initial contract award in 2008. When the contract was awarded, it was moved to the CPQA website with revisions needed for the platform as well as the content. As the software platforms have evolved over time, the need to better engage and enhance the learning tools for learners became evident. During the second 7‐year contract (2015), the tutorial was extensively updated to a more robust, interactive and immersive experience for the learners while the content was expanded and updated to illustrate the impact of clinical research scenarios on the pharmacological outcome measures. For example, the impact of missed drug doses could easily be seen by selecting a dosing scenario, and the learner observes the decrease in drug area under the curve (AUC) and increase in time to achieve steady state (see Figure 4 ). The tutorial has nine modules which may be taken separately, if desired.

Proficiency testing program

The CPQA program provides an ISO‐17043‐accredited Proficiency Testing Program for antiretroviral and select antitubercular agent quantitation. The PTP is administered through a series of confidential and impartial processes. Currently, 15 antiretroviral agents are provided twice annually in two plasma panels and antitubercular agents in a separate plasma panel. Each panel consists of five unknown target concentrations that span drug‐specific therapeutic ranges. Blank matrix is also included to ensure specificity of test methods related to matrix effects. Test panels are developed at low and high concentrations prior to incorporating new analytes. When PT scores are below 80% for one or more analytes, remedial panels, and guidance are offered. Initial and final reports with statistical tables and graphics by round and laboratory‐specific longitudinal graphics for analytes are provided in a de‐identified, confidential manner to CPL directors and HIV Research Network leadership teams.

The CPQA PTP is accredited by ANSI National Accreditation Board (ANAB) and utilizes the ISO‐17043 standards that include several key categories of requirements: (i) general requirements for impartiality and confidentiality for the protection of the PTP laboratories, (ii) structural requirements for the PTP, management, and personnel, (iii) resource requirements for PTP personnel, facilities, and environmental conditions, equipment, systems and support services, (iv) process requirements to ensure the integrity of the entire PTP process, from initial planning, communicating, packaging, handling, and storage to final reporting, as well as to assure confidence in the results provided to PTP participants (i.e., CPLs), and (v) management system requirements to ensure consistent operation, competencies, impartiality, confidentiality, document control, and continuous improvement for its PTP through internal audits and management reviews, as well as taking corrective actions to resolve nonconformities when identified.

The reference standards used to prepare the PT samples are acquired through vendors that provide ISO‐17034 reference materials with certificates of analysis. However, if an ISO‐certified vendor is not available and standard reference material can be provided, the CPQA will test the reference grade material to ensure identity and purity. Purity is determined by USP HPLC methods and thermogravimetric analysis for volatile content. Nuclear magnetic resonance is used to confirm molecular structure, and the molecular formula is confirmed by high resolution mass spectrometry.

Prequalification is completed for all panel sample preparation that is distributed to CPLs in each round. Panels are evaluated in triplicate for each analyte at CPQA prior to shipping to CPLs by testing analytes in each panel using validated quantitative assays. Like the CPLs, CPQA chromatographic assays use tandem mass spectrometry for sensitive, accurate, and specific detection of these analytes. This testing ensures that each panel is prepared within the targeted concentrations (within a measurement of error) and is homogeneous as required by ISO‐17043.

After all PT results are obtained from CPLs, the PT dataset is closed and analyzed, and successful performance is determined based on criteria formulated by CPQA. 18 For each analyte, round‐specific summary statistics are calculated and tabulated to provide the mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation, along with the number of CPLs. For context, weighed‐in values and final targets are also shown. Each CPL, the contract sponsor, and others (as specified by the sponsor) are provided with a Final Laboratory‐specific PT Performance Report (generated after review of results for completeness and accuracy and discussion with the sponsor). PT performance results are discussed with CPLs, and corrective actions from CPLs, if required, are reviewed. CPLs receive assistance with the implementation of these plans as needed. CPLs may also be provided with remediation samples for re‐testing when performance failure is identified.

Although there is not an ISO‐17043 accredited program available to provide external quality assurance, CPQA has developed a mechanism to achieve this goal. To ensure the CPQA proficiency testing samples are within a designated range for accuracy, the CPQA program utilizes an interlaboratory comparison (ILC) approach. Percent deviations between the CPQA laboratory prequalification results and the final target are compared with percent deviations between the mean of CPL‐reported concentrations and final target. Notably, the final target takes the value of the weighed‐in value or the mean of CPL‐reported concentrations using an algorithm based on the number of labs, how tightly the CPL‐reported concentrations are clustered, and how far the mean is from the weighed‐in value. This type of ILC is viewed by the ISO‐accreditation agency as an acceptable approach when external PT programs are not available to support the CPQA prequalification data. In 2015, CPQA developed an internal quality management system (QMS) that interfaces with the PT program. A quality monitor attends several of the internal CPQA committee meetings to maintain the quality of the PT program processes and, more recently, in accordance with ISO‐17043 requirements. By auditing each PT round, the quality monitor actively ensures that SOPs and policies are followed by reconstruction of activities using records and documents as evidence. The quality monitor reports any concerns or findings to CPQA leadership. In addition, the quality monitor maintains the QMS manual and its respective SOPs. The quality monitor is also a key point of contact for accreditation visits and vendor audits from the sponsor. Internal audits of CPQA laboratories are completed with each PT round, and a report is provided for senior management review and sign off. Throughout the year, the quality monitor tracks the progress of corrective actions and quality improvements until they are completed and then further monitors to ensure these actions maintain effectiveness.

Laboratory site assessment program

CPQA laboratory site assessments have multiple goals including (i) preparing CPLs for inspection by FDA or other regulatory agencies and technical audits by conducting on‐site or remote audits of operations to determine the degree of conformity to various regulatory requirements and identify areas for quality improvement, (ii) focusing on the individual CPL research by providing an assessment that considers general laboratory operations, CPL‐specific laboratory research foci, and when directed, study‐specific clinical pharmacology research objectives and the identification of operational gaps that may be cited during an external audit, and (iii) providing a structured assessment approach that includes opportunities for integrated instruction in quality management strategies to optimize CPL audit preparedness and audit outcomes. CPQA site assessments of CPLs include three phases: preassessment, assessment, and post‐assessment. The structure and process strive to minimize the use of the CPL efforts while meeting the goals of the assessment. The assessment events occur at the CPL, remotely through live communication platforms, or as a hybrid of both approaches.

During preassessment, information is gathered that will be reviewed prior to the assessment visit. This includes a selection of applicable laboratory documents specifically related to the assessment scope. Documents requested include: (i) laboratory and quality management policy manuals, (ii) assay validation reports and addenda, (iii) applicable SOPs, (iv) resource records (including personnel), and (v) preanalytical, analytical, and post‐analytical records. The assessment team develops a plan for the assessment during this period and communicates with the CPL as needed. Coordination of schedules occurs during this period to arrive at a mutually convenient time for the assessment to be planned.

The assessment phase usually is conducted on‐site at the CPL; however remote assessments have increased in number as the technology for accomplishing a successful visit has increased in the last several years. The assessment phase includes a presentation from CPQA to provide relevant program summary, a presentation by the CPL leadership, a tour of the CPL and review of relevant CPL‐specific documents (e.g., protocols, reports, records) that were identified during preassessment.

The post‐assessment phase includes provision of the CPQA recommendations from the assessment, review of the CPL response, and completion of a final report by CPQA. At this time, the report is provided to the sponsor, and any follow‐up actions are noted and scheduled.

In addition to the assessments of established laboratories conducting clinical protocol sample analyses (i.e., study‐specific), the CPQA has provided assessments for laboratories requested by the contract sponsor. These assessments have been conducted for the following purposes: (i) laboratories of potential collaboration seeking development and/or training (development) or (ii) a general laboratory assessment including relocation (general) or (iii) preimplementation (implementation) or during of a new laboratory workload conduct that impact outcomes for the sponsor's research.

Training, assistance and guidance (TAG) program

CPQA engages with research communities to provide TAG as requested by individual laboratories and research‐associated stakeholders such as scientific committees and clinical site research staff, with the direction and approval of the CPQA COR. CPQA has developed multiple approaches to providing technical guidance and training for CPL and clinical research staff that can be provided during professional meetings, research committees or protocol team calls, or on‐site at the CPQA Laboratory. Importantly, technical guidance is offered to CPLs during relocation, CPQA‐CPL meetings, as well as with clinical research processing laboratories or clinical trial teams during their respective leadership calls. As discussed previously, website‐based tutorials are available for Bioanalytical Method Validation and Study Conduct of Clinical Pharmacology Protocols.

As needed, CPQA provides one‐on‐one or laboratory support to CPLs conducting studies for the development, validation, and implementation of pharmacology assays (e.g., assays for novel biomatrices, new drugs, and drug metabolites). Specifically, CPQA advises CPLs on compliance with current Good Laboratory Practices (cGLP) [21 CFR part 58], and where appropriate, applicable to federal, state, and local regulations. CPQA also provides CPLs with quality control materials (QCMs) for drug stability studies. If necessary, bioanalytical consultants are available to determine the optimal procedures for the processing of nontraditional matrices (e.g., hair).

The CPQA Clinical Pharmacology Sciences Working Group includes individuals with expertise in pharmacology and analytical chemistry who meet monthly to evaluate published findings and ongoing research to guide program strategic direction and planning. Working group recommendations ensure the appropriate pretesting and inclusion of new analytes into PTP panels as well as stimulating other considerations for program growth. An additional monthly TB Clinical Pharmacology Working Group was created in 2023 and emphasized review of the current state of the TB research area and provision of recommendations for adding ATB analytes and potential testing laboratories to the CPQA PTP.

Bioanalytical method peer review program

The current database for active bioanalytical methods approved by CPQA includes 216 assays for 33 ARVs, 21 ATB, and 46 other analytes, including 35 other therapeutic agents (such as anti‐bacterial or oral contraceptive hormones), eight metabolites and three endogenous biomarkers. Many of the methods measure more than one therapeutic agent or a therapeutic agent plus its metabolites. Also, more than one CPL has likely submitted a method for the same agents. Furthermore, CPLs submit methods independently for each matrix. Figure 6 illustrates the impact on active methods by showing the greater number of drug classes measured. Notably, methods no longer utilized by the CPLs have been archived and are not accounted for in this report.

In addition to vascular compartment matrices (i.e., plasma, peripheral blood mononuclear cells), CPL bioanalytical methods have included six biofluids (breast milk, cerebral spinal fluid, semen, cervicovaginal fluid, urine, and pericardial fluid), and three tissues (hair, liver biopsies, gut‐associated lymphoid tissue). Methods for the analysis of dried bloods spots and peripheral blood mononuclear cells have also been reviewed.

Tutorials

Two hundred seventy‐one individuals have completed the Bioanalytical Method Validation tutorial since March 2016, and 2969 certificates have been issued to individuals completing the Clinical Pharmacology Study Conduct tutorial at clinical sites (2127), laboratories (699) and other locations (143) as of October 2025. Recently, a refresher course has been added for those who have completed the lengthier base tutorial within 2 years. Plans to revise the current base tutorial are underway within the CPQA TAG program.

Assessment program

Since 2014, 36 assessments have been completed in the participating United States and international laboratories. Most of the assessments (44%) have been completed with the intent of ensuring the quality of laboratory operations, resources, and management before, during, or after bioanalyses are completed for specific clinical protocols. General assessment (23%) often followed the relocation of a laboratory or for other reasons. The development (25%) or implementation (8%) of a laboratory necessitated assessment visits requiring appraisals of laboratory needs for operations and training. CPQA observed that since most CPLs were university‐based, unique complexities to their organizational structures and hierarchical processes often required substantial detailing and clarification to adequately prepare for audit readiness. Furthermore, space and infrastructure were allocated based on academic ranking of the lab director or space limitations, rather than the research needs of the site. Lastly, QMSs were unfamiliar to some of these research‐based laboratories, and the implementation of QMSs created administrative and cost burdens for certain sites.

Training, assistance, and guidance program

Both Clinical Pharmacology Working Groups have noted the emergence of new laboratory techniques as well as promising therapeutics. Increasing collection of dried blood spots and volumetric absorptive microsampling devices for protocol specimen sampling has also been recognized. 19 , 20 , 21 More recently, an interest in qualitative screening methods has developed related to medication adherence. With this awareness, CPQA is currently developing, with interested CPLs, supplemental CPQA guidance documents for the validation of the qualitative methods specific to the sampling technologies employed by CPLs.

Since 2014, the addition of new AVRs to the PTP included dolutegravir, bictegravir, and cabotegravir. Currently, planned pretesting and addition of lenacapavir, a capsid inhibitor, available as a long‐acting formulation used for prevention and treatment of HIV‐1 is underway.

Four US and six international laboratories were identified as potential ATB test panel participants. A test panel for four ATBs is planned for January 2026 shipment. These test panels will contain bedaquiline, isoniazid, and pretomanid, as recommended by the TB Clinical Pharmacology Working Group. Notably, rifampicin has been offered for the PTP since 2015. Recommendations for a second, future test round of ATB analytes include: ethambutol, pyrazinamide, linezolid, and moxifloxacin.

DISCUSSION

CPQA has expanded in scope since our initial program was introduced in 2008 and described in a publication in 2013. CPQA has matured since early research protocols were investigating single and dual antiretrovirals, followed by triple and quadruple drug regimens that led to complex pharmacology challenges with multiple drug interactions that required consideration.

Bioanalytical methods used to measure antiretroviral drug concentrations in multiple matrices collected during clinical trials required increased validation data to prove quality when the US FDA issued new revised guidance in 2018. 22 , 23 Likewise, the collection of these multiple matrices for an increasing number of drug analytes required updated training for clinical sites and processing laboratories responsible for obtaining and handling these specimens. CPQA responded to these changes by updating training tutorials, guidance documents, and peer review submission requirements. 24 , 25 , 26 Input from the stakeholders and CPLs was vital to expediting the updates and achieving commitments to the program. Current and future trials may need evolving approaches to support pharmacology testing.

The inclusion of ISO‐accreditation for the CPQA PTP in 2018 facilitated global CPL participation and created new opportunities for accelerating drug development and addressing regional research questions relevant to HIV prevention, treatment, cure, and co‐morbidities. This accreditation indicates that PT schemes are well‐planned and statistically valid, and furthermore indicates a commitment to quality by CPQA and its sponsor, thereby increasing confidence in CPL data reported for the associated clinical research trials. The addition of ATB analytes will extend CPQA contributions to the growing field of TB clinical pharmacology and drug development as investigational agents are evaluated with and without previously approved TB medications. In addition, proficiency testing for antibodies and long‐acting therapeutics is likely to be needed.

The CPQA assessment program strives to prepare CPLs for regulatory and sponsor audits and has contributed to increasing the quality of their operations. In the past few years, CPQA has observed continued quality commitments from the CPL institutions and professional staff as CPQA recommendations have been embraced and implemented to achieve improved outcomes from audits and inspections. The rapid innovation in artificial intelligence applications will likely impact the quality assurance practices in laboratories, and CPQA expects to include key related regulatory requirements in assessment practices and training programs.

CPQA programs will continue to evolve with the HIV research landscape that includes long‐acting agents, cure therapeutics, new sample collection and laboratory technologies, changes in regulatory requirements, and emerging strategies for outcomes assessment (e.g., artificial intelligence, pharmacometrics). In addition, the CPQA program has established a comprehensive foundation for quality assurance that has the potential to be broadened to include other institutes at NIH that focus on clinical pharmacology and therapeutics research (e.g., oncology, neurology, and others) to advance translational drug development programs. The education, guidance, and protocol support components provided by CPQA such as quality considerations for sample handling, assay performance validation, routine external proficiency achievement, and laboratory quality management are essential contributors to rigor and reproducibility that NIH strives for in all funded research programs. Lastly, CPQA has provided a strong mechanism to enhance the collaborative research among academic investigators and drug developers that can be conducted and enable key clinical pharmacology studies such as those linking pharmacokinetics and pharmacogenomics or identifying pharmacokinetic drug interactions in special populations.
