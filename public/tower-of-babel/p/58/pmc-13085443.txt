Lactylation in pulmonary fibrosis: current understanding and challenges
Authors: Bo Li, Xiao-hong Li, Yan Zhou, Dan-dan Feng, Zi-qiang Luo, Hai-peng Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a progressive, chronic lung disease, which is accompanied by epithelial cell damage, fibroblast activation, and macrophage polarization; the fibrotic niche is primarily composed of these three cell types. The local microenvironment, composed of a fibrotic niche, participates in disease progression by altering epigenetic modifications. Lactylation is a novel post-translational modification involving the writing, removal, and recognition of lactyl groups. Studies have demonstrated that lactylation promotes profibrotic cellular phenotypes by altering the epigenetics. At the microenvironmental level, lactylation affects PF progression by influencing lung epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages in the fibrotic niche.Main bodyLactylation may promote the development of PF by affecting epithelial cell autophagy, cellular senescence, expression of proliferation or repair genes, apoptosis, ferroptosis, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition and may influence the activation of fibroblasts and secretion of pro-fibrotic factors by macrophages. By focusing on the key cell types in the fibrotic microenvironment, in this review, we elaborate on the roles of lactic acid and lactylation in PF and the potential future research directions of lactylation in the field of PF.ConclusionThis review summarizes the potential pathways through which lactylation promotes PF, based on the current sites and roles of lactylation in epithelial cells, fibroblasts, and macrophages. In addition, by summarizing the current research methods of lactylation and their limitations, our review paves the way for future studies on lactylation and PF.Keywords: Pulmonary fibrosis, Lactylation, Post-translational modification, Fibrotic niche

Introduction

Pulmonary fibrosis (PF) is a progressive chronic lung disease that primarily affects the pulmonary interstitium. It often develops in the later stages of many lung diseases. Thus, the PF is better understood as a pathological disease state or stage rather than a single, distinct condition. The underlying causes of the occurrence and development of these diseases are mostly pathologically present in the pulmonary interstitium and are clinically referred to as interstitial lung diseases (ILDs) [1, 2]. The most common disease in this series is idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF), indicating that most cases of PF in clinical practice do not have a specific cause of onset, but instead result from the combined action of multiple factors. Changes in the environment, genes, aging, and epigenetics can affect the progression of PF [3]. IPF is characterized by dyspnea and the progressive deterioration of lung function. This condition is more common in elderly patients. Patients with IPF usually present with symptoms such as cough, exertional dyspnea with progressive aggravation of dyspnea, signs of inspiratory moist rales at the bilateral lung bases, and deterioration of pulmonary function. Many patients develop clubbed fingers because of hypoxia. Typically, a diagnosis is made within 6 months to 1 year of the onset of initial symptoms. Without medical intervention, the median survival time is only 3 years. The prognosis is related to the patient’s condition and the presence or absence of other comorbidities, such as advanced age (> 70 years), smoking history, low body mass index, impaired normal physiological function, large radiological extent of the disease, and pulmonary hypertension [4, 5]. The disease is widespread, and the estimated prevalence (per 10,000 people) ranges from 0.57 to 4.51 in countries in the Asia-Pacific region, from 0.33 to 2.51 in Europe, and from 2.40 to 2.98 in North America [6]. Pirfenidone and nintedanib were approved as first-generation anti-PF drugs more than 10 years ago, and no drugs have been proven to be applicable to the clinical treatment of PF [7].

Current evidence suggests that the fibrotic response is driven by injury and the subsequent abnormal activation of alveolar epithelial cells (AECs), which produce mediators that induce the formation of fibroblast and myofibroblast foci through the proliferation of resident mesenchymal cells, recruitment of circulating fibrocytes, and stimulation of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Fibroblasts and myofibroblast foci secrete excessive extracellular matrix, leading to the formation of lung scars and destruction of the lung tissue structure [5, 8]. The formation of fibrotic tissue, defined as the excessive accumulation of extracellular matrix (ECM) components, such as collagen and fibronectin, is a normal and important stage in the repair process of all organ tissues. When the injury is mild or transient, the levels of ECM components increase temporarily and are then rapidly eliminated, which is beneficial for the restoration of normal tissue structure. However, with repeated injury or severe tissue damage, ECM components continue to accumulate, disrupting tissue structure, affecting organ function, and ultimately leading to organ failure [9]. PF is a phenomenon in which lung tissue is continuously damaged, and in the process of repeated repair and damage, scar tissue gradually replaces normal tissue. Therefore, it is commonly observed in the later stages of various lung diseases. The primary pathological processes of PF include persistent damage to alveolar epithelial cells, activation of fibroblasts, and ECM deposition, leading to lung tissue remodeling and subsequent PF [1, 10, 11], Among them, the disordered regulation of related inflammatory factors is also one of the central links of PF [12].

Post-translational modification of proteins refers to covalent modifications that occur after protein translation [13], including palmitoylation, isoprenylation, and protein SUMOylation, as well as common methylation, acetylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, and glycosylation [14]. Post-translational modifications can regulate protein activity, localization, spatial structural properties, and interactions between proteins and other biological macromolecules. In recent years, researchers have attempted to explore new strategies for treating PF from the mechanisms of post - translational modifications and have achieved some results. For example, histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibitors have been shown to have therapeutic potential for fibrotic diseases [15, 16]; Ubiquitination-related enzymes, such as E3 ubiquitin ligases and deubiquitinating enzymes (DUBs), can regulate the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) pathway and Wnt pathway and affect metabolic activity, inflammatory response, aging, ferroptosis, endoplasmic and reticulum stress, thus regulating the progression of PF [17]. Increasing the SMAD3 small ubiquitin-like modifier mediated modification (SUMOylation) inhibits TGF-β signaling and affect the development of PF [18, 19]; Glycosylation of vacuolar H+-adenosine triphosphatase (V-ATPase) contributes to the degradation of collagen and inhibits the progression of PF [20]. Fucosyltransferase 3 (FUT3) and FUT5 are negatively correlated with PF [21]. A new post-translational modification, lactylation, was discovered in 2019 [22], and researchers have begun to study the relationship between post-translational modifications and diseases from a metabolic perspective. Lactylation is a process through which the body adapts to environmental changes during metabolic processes. To date, numerous diseases have been reported to be associated with lactylation, such as Alzheimer’s disease (AS), social stress, prostate cancer, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), preeclampsia, and myocardial infarction [23–28].

At present, research on lactylation and PF is limited; however, many lactylation-related studies have suggested that lactylation may play an important role in the process of PF. For example, in the tumor microenvironment (TME), the lactylation of MOESIN (a membrane-associated protein) regulates regulatory T cells (Tregs), enhances TGF-β signaling in the TME, and thus promotes tumorigenesis [29]. In TCMK-1 (mouse renal tubular epithelial cell line) cells, both lactate treatment and pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) overexpression can promote histone H3K18 lactylation to induce TGF-β1 expression [30]. There is also evidence suggesting that histone lactylation in macrophages may contribute to the secretion of cytokines by macrophages, activating the transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)/Smad2/3 and vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA)/ERK pathways in MLE12 cells, and thus triggering the EMT of lung epithelial cells [31].The TGF-β-Smad pathway is a classical pathway in PF [32], which plays an important role in promoting the occurrence and development of PF. Lactylation may also affect the development of PF by influencing key molecules in this pathway. Some lactylation roles in other diseases may also have similar effects on PF. For example, histone lactylation promotes ocular melanoma by enhancing the expression of the N6-methyladenosine (m6A) reader protein YTHDF2. Similarly, in the context of arsenic exposure, H3K18 lactylation is involved in arsenic-induced PF by promoting the m6A reader protein YTHDF1 [33, 34].

Recently, the concept of a fibrotic niche has been proposed, offering a framework for understanding the pathological mechanisms within the local microenvironment. The fibrotic niche is the local microenvironment formed during chronic tissue injury or inflammation. The interaction of cytokines with different cells can promote the development of various cells in the direction of fibrosis [35–37]. This review focuses on clarifying the mechanism of lactylation in the microenvironment, its relationship with various cells in the lung fibrotic niche, and the sites that may affect lung fibrosis, thereby providing ideas and insights for future research on the relationship between lung fibrosis and lactylation.

Research progress on lactylation

Lactylation is a novel post-translational modification in which lactic acid covalently binds to the lysine residues of proteins. Histone lactylation promotes transcriptional activation of chromatin-associated genes. Zhang, et al. first discovered protein lactylation in 2019. By analyzing the histones of human MCF-7 cells using high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (HPLC-MS/MS), a mass shift of 72.021 daltons was found on the lysine residues, which was consistent with the theoretical mass of the lactyl group (-CH₃CHOHCO-). Subsequently, the existence of lactylation was confirmed through synthetic peptide comparison, development of a pan anti-Kla antibody, and metabolic labeling experiments [22] Lactylation “writers,” “erasers,” and “readers” are the core regulators of the dynamic balance and function of this modification; the mainstream view holds that, consistent with several other PTMs, lactylation levels are regulated by adding and removing lactyl groups from histones [38]. Writing proteins (writers) - enzymes mediating lactylation

Enzymatic reaction

The writer proteins for lactylation modification are responsible for covalently linking the lactate group to the lysine residues of the target proteins [22]. Members of the histone acetyltransferase (HAT) family P300/CBP were the first proteins confirmed to exhibit lactyltransferase activity. It catalyzes lactylation of histone H3 at lysine 18 (H3K18) site in cells and is commonly present in human pancreatic adenocarcinoma cells, microglial cells, lung fibroblasts, and macrophages [22, 39–42]. KAT8 is a lysine acetyltransferase that acts as a pan-Kla writer protein and is responsible for installing Kla onto numerous protein substrates involved in various biological processes. Studies have shown that the lactyltransferase catalytic activity of KAT8 is independent of its properties as an HAT. In colorectal cancer cells, KAT8 regulates Kla independent of p300, thereby contributing to cancer development. Interestingly, when studying the activation of hepatic stellate cells (HSC), histone lactylation and acetylation may compete with each other, and class I HDAC inhibitors may impede HSC activation [43, 44]. Skin fibroblasts can promote the lactylation of latent transforming growth factor β-binding protein 1 at lysine 752 (K752) through KAT8 and then increase the protein levels of collagen I and collagen III in fibroblasts [45]. N-acetyltransferase 10 (NAT10) is lactylated by α-tubulin acetyltransferase 1 (ATAT1), which functions as the lactylation writer enzyme, promotes the translation of Kaposi’s sarcoma-associated herpesvirus lytic proteins and viral reactivation [46]. CBP primarily mediates the lactylation of Meiotic Recombination 11 Homolog, an important homologous recombination (HR) protein, which is lactylated by CBP acetyltransferase at the lactylation site K673 in response to DNA damage [47]. In bone marrow-derived macrophages, it was confirmed that GCN5/KAT2A, a HAT, is a lactylation writer and mediates the regulation of target gene expression [27, 48]. Recently, TIP60 was identified as the “writer” of Nijmegen breakage syndrome protein 1 (NBS1) lysine lactylation and NBS1 K388 lactylation. TIP60/KAT5 is a member of the MYST subfamily of histone acetyltransferases and is ubiquitously expressed in various cell types. It is an important enzyme directly involved in early DNA repair and cell survival, as well as a key player in multiple signaling pathways, including transcriptional regulation, steroid receptor function, chromatin remodeling, histone acetylation, DNA repair, and maintenance of stem cell function [49]. NBS1 is a crucial DNA repair protein encoded by the NBS1 gene on human chromosome 8 (8q21). It is a core molecule that maintains genomic stability and its lactylation can help cancer cells resist chemotherapy [50]. Furthermore, using Human Embryonic Kidney 293 (HEK293T) cells transfected with SV40 large T antigen as the research object, lactate promotes autophagy through the lactylation of vacuolar protein sorting 34 mediated by the acyltransferase KAT5/TIP60 (at lysine-356 and lysine-781), and is associated with cancer progression, linking autophagy and glycolysis [51]. Acetylation and lactylation overlap in protein writing. Studying the relationship between acetylation and lactylation or lactylation with acetylation as a reference may uncover new mechanisms of action and play a role in several diseases.

Acetylation and lactylation share overlapping enzymatic writer machinery. Lactylation often occurs alongside acetylation. However, for the same protein, competition may exist if the lactylation and acetylation modification sites are the same. Studying the relationship between acetylation and lactylation or lactylation with acetylation as a reference may uncover new mechanisms of action and play a role in several diseases [52]. In addition, in HEK293T cells, HBO1 possesses lactyltransferase activity and acts as a potential writer protein to mediate protein lactylation both in vitro and in vivo [53]. For the prokaryotic Kla regulatory system, regarding the Kla proteome in Escherichia coli MG1655, YiaC was concluded to function as a lysine lactylation enzyme [54]. When studying the cariogenic bacterium Streptococcus mutans, it was found that the Gcn5-related N-acetyltransferase (GNAT) superfamily enzyme GNAT13 exhibited lysine lactyltransferase activity in cells and lactylated lysines. GNAT13 can also be considered a novel writer protein in prokaryotes. In vitro, it can promote the lactylation of the RNA polymerase subunit α (RpoA), thereby regulating metabolic pathways and biofilm formation [55]. Lactylation writers regulate the extent and specificity of protein lactylation. Inhibiting or promoting the activity of writer proteases can regulate lactylation and participate in the regulation of pathophysiological processes.

Non-enzymatic reactions

In addition to the protease-mediated enzymatic mechanism of incorporation, lactylation can occur in an enzyme-independent manner. Lactylation is the transfer of a lactyl group from a specific donor to the lysine residues of proteins. Two primary sources of the lactyl group exist: L-lactyl-CoA derived from L-lactic acid is involved in the enzymatic mechanism of KL-la, and the glyoxalase pathway intermediate D-lactyl-GSH serves as a direct donor of the D-lactyl group through a non-enzymatic transfer mechanism, transferring it to lysine residues of proteins [56]. In human embryonic kidney cells, non-enzymatic acyl transfer of the lactate moiety from lactoyl glutathione to protein Lys residues has been identified, resulting in the lysine lactylation modification of proteins [57]. Recently, research has shown that SLG (S-D-lactoylglutathione) can directly induce protein D-lactyl modification in an enzyme-independent manner, forming an “GLO2/SLG/D-lactyl modification” immunometabolic feedback loop. After immune activation, this loop prevents immunopathological damage by suppressing the excessive inflammatory responses [58]. Thermal denaturation protein pretreatment further enhanced the SLG-induced lactylation reaction, which may be due to the fact that denaturation exposes more lysine residues on the protein and inactivates enzymes such as GLO2 and lactase.

Erasers - reducing lactylation modification

Lactylation eraser proteins (erasers) refer to enzymes that remove lactylated modifications from histones or other proteins. They catalyze the dissociation of lactate groups from lysine residues through hydrolysis or other biochemical mechanisms, thus restoring the original state of proteins.

Similar to lactylation, the lactylation erasers overlapped with those of acetylation. In pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), overexpression of HDAC2 significantly decreased the lactylation level, especially of H3K18la, whereas overexpression of HDAC1 and HDAC3 did not affect the levels of Pan Kla and H3K18la. These results suggest that HDAC2 acts as an eraser of histone lactylation [39]. In HeLa cells (Henrietta Lacks cervical cancer cells), Sirtuin (SIRT)1–3 (class III histone deacetylase) has a strong L-delactylating effect on global histone lactylation, as well as H3K18 (L - la) and H4K5 (L - la); HDAC1–3 significantly reduces the global Kac and Kla levels on histones, and significantly decreases the L-lactylation levels of H3K18 and H4K5 [59], confirming that SIRT1-3 and HDAC1-3 are lactylation erasers. HDAC1–3 also functions as a histone lysine depropionylase in the nervous system (in P19 Embryonal Carcinoma Cells (P19EC cells)) [60]. SIRT1 and SIRT3 are delactylases that regulate the level of lysine lactylation in mammalian cells [61]. HDAC2 and HDAC3 are the key regulators of histone H3 lactylation. They actively reduce H3K18la levels by promoting delactylation. Under anaerobic respiratory conditions in breast cancer cells, the decreased expression of HDAC2 and HDAC3 is beneficial to H3K18la, which in turn enhances the expression of PPARD (a transcription factor). Subsequently, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is activated to maintain the survival and proliferation of breast cancer cells [62]. The eraser protein activity varies at each locus. For example, SIRT3 exhibits higher eraser activity towards the H4K16la locus than other sirtuins [63] and activated HDAC3 reduces H4K12la in macrophages and inhibits collagen synthesis in fibroblasts [64], while SIRT2 functions as an eraser of lactyl-lysine PTMs, but SIRT2 catalyzes the deacylation of acetylated peptides at a faster rate than lactylated peptides [65]. CobB functions as a lysine demyristoylase in metabolic regulation [54], acts as a lactylation eraser, and participates in the metabolic regulation of prokaryotes.

Most identified erasers exhibit limited substrate specificity and also regulate other acyl modifications.

Identification of proteins (readers) - the mediators through which lactylation exerts its effects

Lactylation reader proteins that recognize histone lactylation marks are protein effectors that specifically recognize and bind to lysine lactylation modifications (Kla) on histones or non-histones. This type of protein senses the chemical signals of lactylation modification through its specific domain and then recruits downstream effector molecules (such as transcription factors and chromatin remodeling complexes) to regulate biological processes, such as gene expression, metabolic reprogramming, and cell fate determination [66]. Several proteins that contain reader proteins often form multi-subunit enzyme complexes. Reader proteins in the complex usually have specificity for different PTMs [67]. In cervical cancer cells, DPF2 binds to H3K14la and colocalizes with it in the promoters of oncogenes. Disruption of the DPF2-H3K14la interaction attenuates cancer-related gene expression and affects cell survival, revealing that DPF2 is a genuine H3K14la effector molecule that links histone lactylation to gene transcription and cell survival, providing evidence for the involvement of histone Kla in transcription and tumorigenesis [68]. While studying the reprogramming of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), H3K18la and Brg1 were found to be strongly correlated with iPSCs. Brg1 acts as a reader of H3K18la during early reprogramming; it is both a regulator and a reader of H3K18la and promotes the mesenchymal-epithelial transition (MET) process [69]. TRIM33, also known as transcriptional intermediary factor 1 gamma (TIF1γ), is a known transcriptional repressor [70]. TRIM33 can regulate several key cellular processes, such as macrophage polarization and the recruitment of bone marrow cells to the site of inflammation; among them, TRIM33 acts as a reader protein by recognizing the metabolism-derived histone Kla and is involved in the negative regulation of the transcription of inflammatory genes in M1 macrophages [71].

Physiological functions of lactylation-related enzymes and their research in the context of fibrosis

The lactylated writer proteins P300/CBP, KAT8, KAT2A, KAT5, HBO1, and ATAT1 have all been studied with regard to fibrosis. Notably, under physiological conditions, these proteins also play a role in promoting the acetylation of related proteins [72–76]. For example, P300 promotes histone acetylation, thereby promoting PF, and inhibiting P300 can alleviate the progression of PF both in vivo and in vitro [77]. Furthermore, KAT2A promotes H3K9 acetylation, thereby regulating the expression of genes related to hepatic stellate cell activation, such as fibronectin 1 (FN1) and plasminogen activator inhibitor 1 (SERPINE1), and ultimately promoting biliary fibrosis [78]. AARS1 is a cytoplasmic alanyl-tRNA synthetase that possesses both aminoacylation and lactyl transferase functions and regulates cell fate in response to lactic acid signals [79]. Lactylation eraser proteins, such as HDAC1-3 or SIRT1-3, which are also acetylation eraser proteins, regulate acetylation under physiological conditions and are often used in studies on the relationship between acetylation and fibrosis. Regarding reader proteins, DPF2 and Brg1 are core subunits of chromatin remodeling complexes, regulating chromatin targeting and ATP-dependent structural remodeling, respectively. TIF1γ, a transcriptional intermediary factor and E3 ubiquitin ligase, is involved in transcriptional regulation and signaling pathways. The discussion of lactylation writers and eraser proteins in prokaryotes is not expanded here. In a PF model, studies have shown that P300 is a writer protein that plays a role in the lactylation of both non-histones and histones. Other studies have shown that P300 promotes the lactylation of Snail1 in fibroblasts, promoting histone lactylation in macrophages and promoting H3K18 lactylation in airway epithelial cells [36, 80, 81]. SIRT3 is a lactylation-erasing protein in the macrophages [82]. No other eraser proteins have been studied in PF models, and there remains great research potential.

Correlation between PF and lactylation in the fibrotic niche

The fibrotic niche of the lungs is composed of myofibroblasts, macrophages, and alveolar epithelial cells. In this structure, cells communicate with each other through contact, paracrine, and other mechanisms, influencing the functions of one another and participating in the pathological process of PF [36]. In the fibrotic niche, cells communicate with each other through lactate and alter their functional state through lactylation of intracellular proteins. Association of lactylation with lung epithelial cell damage and EMT

Damage to the lung epithelial cells initiates PF. Under the stimulation of various factors, lung epithelial cells exhibit the phenotype of mesenchymal cells, that is, they undergo EMT, thus promoting the progression of PF.

Lactylation may promote PF by affecting epithelial cell injury

As a source of lactylation, lactic acid has long been shown to play a key role in shaping the TME through its effects on different cell populations [83, 84]. Research has found that, as a core regulator of autophagy and lysosomal gene expression, lactylation of transcription factor EB (TFEB) can regulate its own activity and is associated with high levels of autophagy in rapidly proliferating cells, such as cancer cells [85]. Autophagy plays an important role in PF [86]. In addition, the increased p53 transcription induced by histone lactylation leads to senescence of lung epithelial cells during the progression of 11-nitropyrene (1-NP)-induced COPD [87]. Histone lactylation in renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs) is involved in renal fibrosis and accelerates tubular senescence during the progression of diabetic kidney disease (DKD) [74]. The senescence of lung epithelial cells can also promote the progression of PF [88]. As a novel post-translational modification, lactylation is not only involved in pathogenic processes within the body but also plays a role in stimulating the proliferation and repair of epithelial cells. Heat shock protein A12A (HSPA12A) promotes c-Myc lactylation by increasing glycolysis-derived lactate production in a HIF-1α-dependent manner. c-Myc lactylation contributes to the HSPA12A-induced nuclear localization of c-Myc, expression of proliferation-related genes, and proliferation (tubular epithelial cell) proliferation [89]. The epithelial cell proliferation, repair, and damage cycle are necessary steps in the formation of pulmonary fibrotic lesions. Lactylation is the key link between lactate metabolism and mitochondrial dysfunction in renal tubular epithelial cells. Lactate promotes excessive mitochondrial fission by mediating the lactylation of Fis1 (especially Fis1 K20la), triggering ATP depletion, mtROS (reactive oxygen species) accumulation, and apoptosis of HK2 (Human Kidney-2 cells) [90]. Apoptosis is one of the pathogenic mechanisms contributing to PF that indicates the connection between lactylation and PF. In a study on diabetic nephropathy, lactylation at K182 in ACSF2 led to mitochondrial dysfunction, which may promote ferroptosis in renal tubular epithelial cells [91]. In lung epithelial cells, during sepsis-associated acute lung injury, lactate induces ferroptosis via the GPR81/H3K18la/METTL3/ACSL4 axis in alveolar epithelial cells [92]. Lactylation may also accelerate the progression of PF by inducing ferroptosis and damaging the lung epithelial cells.

Lactylation may also be one of the mechanisms through which lactic acid exerts its effects. Lactic acid induces apoptosis in alveolar epithelial cells and exacerbates the progression of PF [93]. Previous studies have shown that ER stress affects the secretion of Sonic Hedgehog (shh) in lung epithelial cells. Secreted SHH can activate the HH signaling pathway in fibroblasts in the niche, thus promoting the progression of PF [94]. However, lactate-induced apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells can also cause ER stress, leading to PF through the ATF4-CHOP-Caspase12 pathway [93]. In these lactate-induced processes, certain protein lactylations may have occurred, which requires further exploration.

Protein lactylation in other epithelial cell types may have similar effects in lung epithelial cells. Based on existing research, the lactylation of protein molecules may accelerate the process of PF by increasing autophagy in lung epithelial cells, promoting cellular senescence, upregulating the expression of proliferation and repair genes, promoting apoptosis, inducing ferroptosis, and promoting endoplasmic reticulum stress.

Lactylation may contribute to EMT of lung epithelial cells and affect the progression of PF

During PF, EMT is crucial for the fibrotic processes [95]. Some studies have shown that lactylation of certain proteins contributes to EMT. In cancer research, the epithelial-mesenchymal transition is beneficial for the metastasis of tumor cells. Among them, lysyl oxidase (LOX) secreted by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAF) promotes PD-L1 expression through histone lactylation and regulates gastric cancer EMT through the TGFβ/IGF1 signaling pathway [96]. Furthermore, lactate can also promote the EMT of liver cancer cells through Twist1 lactylation [97]. Overexpression of mesoderm-related factor 1 (MESP1) is associated with the promotion of EMT and apoptosis in pancreatic cancer cells, and MESP1 is a target protein regulated by H3K18la [98]. In gastric cancer cells, H3K18 lactylation upregulates the transcription of vascular cell adhesion molecule 1, activates the AKT-mTOR signaling pathway, and promotes tumor cell proliferation, EMT, and metastasis [99].

In addition, histone lactylation in renal tubular epithelial cells (RTECs) promotes EMT of RTECs by enhancing KLF5 expression. Renal-specific knockdown and pharmacological inhibition of KLF5 reduced EMT development and alleviated fibrosis in DKD [100]. High glucose and exogenous lactate induce histone lactylation and EMT in mouse podocyte cell line [101]. Furthermore, a pathway involving decreased GCGR/GLP1R expression, lactate accumulation, and histone H3K9 lactylation (H3K9la) exacerbates EMT in intestinal epithelial cells [102]. Other cell types undergo mesenchymal phenotypic transformations under lactylation conditions. For example, glycolytic lactate induces a correlation between Twist1 and p300/CBP, leading to lactylation of Twist1 lysine 150 (K150la). The increase in K150la promotes Twist1 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation and further regulates the transcription of TGFβ1, thereby inducing a fibrotic phenotype and exacerbating EndoMT - related fibrosis after flap ischemia [103]. IGFBP5 promotes NLRP3 inflammasome-induced EndoMT and renal fibrosis by regulating glycolysis-mediated histone lactylation, accelerating the progression of diabetic nephropathy [104]. These lactylation regulatory mechanisms may have similar effects on lung epithelial cells and hold great potential for research. Recently, H3K18la-mediated upregulation of SIX1 was shown to contribute to the mesenchymal transformation of airway epithelial cells, forming an inflammation-glycolysis-lactylation cascade. Dysregulation of lactylation-induced inflammation is one of the causes of EMT and a central link in PF [81]. During PF, alveolar epithelial cells may have a similar mechanism, promoting their own EMT through the lactylation of certain proteins.

Current research on the role of lactylation in PF of epithelial cells is limited. Based on the current research, after lung epithelial cells receive noxious stimuli, they may participate in the injury of lung epithelial cells through protein lactylation and regulate their EMT, accelerating the progression of PF. In future studies, the specific mechanism requires further exploration.

Lactylation is associated with myofibroblast foci formation

Fibroblasts undergo metabolic reprogramming during PF development. Enhanced glycolysis and lactate production can promote PF development. Studies have shown that enhanced glycolysis promotes the fibrotic phenotype of pulmonary myofibroblasts and facilitates their proliferation and differentiation. Moreover, lactate at physiological concentrations can activate fibroblasts, which depends on low pH to activate TGF-β [105], inhibiting the production of lactic acid by drugs can prevent the differentiation of myofibroblasts [106]. For example, alamandine (ALA) can induce mitophagy in fibroblasts, thereby inhibiting glycolysis and reducing lactate production, thus reducing the activation of TGF-β1-induced lung fibroblasts [107]. When stimulating pulmonary myofibroblasts with TGF-β to induce a fibrotic phenotype, the production of lactate by the cells also increases [36]. Lactate-induced myofibroblast differentiation depends on a high-serum environment and does not occur under low-serum or serum-free conditions [105]. This suggests that lactate may play a role in inducing fibroblast differentiation by mediating the lactylation of certain molecules. Lactic acid promotes the transformation of fibroblasts into myofibroblasts (FMT). Hypoxia is a common cause of enhanced glycolysis. Studies have shown that under hypoxic conditions, HIF1α can promote the transcription of GPR81. Lactic acid produced under hypoxic conditions acts on the lactic acid receptor, GPR81, to promote the progression of PF by inducing fibrosis in mesenchymal cells [108]. In tumors, lactate derived from lung cancer cells can promote the nuclear translocation of nucleolar and spindle-associated protein 1, subsequently leading to the recruitment of the transcription complex JUNB - FRA1 - FRA2 near the DESMIN promoter and promoting DESMIN transcriptional activation, thereby promoting the activation of CAFs [109]. Inhibition of the key glycolytic enzyme PFKFB3 can suppress the activation of lung fibroblasts and the NLRP3 inflammasome [41]. The mechanism through which lactic acid exerts its effects and whether it lactylates specific molecules require further investigation.

Previous studies have indicated that lactylation is involved in fibroblast activation. The hypoxia-induced glycolysis/lactate/histone lactylation cascade drives the FMT, thereby inducing myofibroblast differentiation [110]. Similarly, in the context of arsenic stimulation, lactate-induced H3K18 lactylation in epithelial cells can activate fibroblasts into myofibroblasts by secreting TGF-β. Myofibroblasts secrete lactate to form an acidic microenvironment, which further stimulates histone lactylation in epithelial cells and accelerates PF [34]. Recent studies have shown that Snail1 lactylation in fibroblasts is involved in FMT and contributes to the progression of PF [80]. Lactylation in fibroblasts and the mechanism through which lactylation promotes FMT in PF are key areas to be addressed in future research.

Lactylation is linked to macrophage cytokine secretion and pulmonary fibrosis progression

Macrophages in lung tissue are divided into tissue-resident macrophages (TRMs) and monocyte-derived macrophages. Monocyte-derived alveolar macrophages rather than tissue-resident interstitial macrophages, contribute to the macrophage pool in the fibrotic niche [37]. Macrophages can be divided into two types: proinflammatory and anti-inflammatory. Proinflammatory M1 macrophages undergo metabolic reprogramming and aerobic glycolysis and produce lactic acid. By contrast, anti-inflammatory M2 macrophages trigger a metabolic program with increased oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation, as observed in BMDM. M1-type macrophages often survive in hypoxic environments and produce pro-inflammatory cytokines and other mediators of the M1 phenotype, such as glycolytic enzymes and glucose transporters. Interestingly, oxidative metabolism and fatty acid oxidation do not appear to be dominant in human M2 macrophages. Instead, gluconeogenesis, driven by fructose-1,6-bisphosphatase 1 (Fbp1) seems to play a major role. Blocking oxidative metabolism drives macrophages to the M1 state, whereas inducing oxidative metabolism in M1 macrophages enhances the M2 phenotype. These findings indicate that metabolic reprogramming critically influences macrophage polarization [111, 112]. In addition, lactate supports the direct transformation of macrophages from M0 to M2 via mitochondrial pyruvate metabolism [113]. In addition, lactate can act as a chemical signal to induce the polarization of macrophages towards the M2 phenotype, which has been shown to be beneficial for blood vessel reconstruction and muscle regeneration in vascular endothelial cells [114].

Lactate in macrophages and lactate-induced lactylation play certain roles in muscle regeneration, central nervous system diseases, TME, and inflammatory diseases [115], For example, lactic acid and induced H3 lactylation can promote the expression of the repair phenotype of mouse microglia [116]; tumor-associated macrophages are more likely to polarize into M2 macrophages that inhibit the inflammatory response and promote immune surveillance evasion in the acidic environment created by lactic acid, thus exerting an immunosuppressive effect [117]. During the process of inflammation resolution after myocardial infarction and the activation of repair signals, lactylation also promotes the polarization of macrophages from the M1 pro-inflammatory to the M2 repair-type [27]. In sepsis, lactate can induce the lactylation of HMGB1 and its accumulation in the cytoplasm of macrophages, promoting its secretion and release through exosomes. Secreted HMGB1 disrupts endothelial integrity and increases vascular permeability. Decreased lactate levels can improve the survival outcomes of patients with sepsis [118]. Macrophages with different phenotypes play unique and crucial roles at each stage. Any interference during this process may lead to the production of uncontrolled inflammatory mediators and growth factors, which in turn may result in abnormal repair, ultimately leading to pathological fibrosis [119]. Lactate-induced histone lactylation can promote macrophage pyroptosis through pro-inflammatory programmed cell death induced by the NLRP3 inflammasome [120] and interfere with macrophage homeostasis. Increased levels of histone lactylation induce homeostatic genes, such as arginase 1 (Arg1), to participate in wound healing during the late polarization of M1 macrophages [22], indicating that lactylation can regulate the functional state of macrophages. An imbalance in macrophage homeostasis and fibrotic factors released by M2 macrophages also plays an important role in PF.

Based on the findings of recent studies, lactate, both inside and outside macrophages, can promote histone lactylation during PF. Histone lactylation at the promoters of related genes promotes the transcription of fibrosis-related genes [22]. Under the mediation of P300, intracellular or extracellular lactate in macrophages induces histone lactylation at the promoter, thereby promoting the expression of macrophage-related fibrotic phenotypes such as cytokines or enzymes such as ARG1, platelet-derived growth factor A, thrombospondin 1, and vascular endothelial growth factor A [36]. PM2.5 can also promote macrophages to secrete cytokines by inducing histone lactylation in macrophages, thereby affecting the process of PF; however, it has no direct effect on lung epithelial cells [31]. The lactylation of PKM2 regulates the transformation of the metabolic phenotype of macrophages, promoting the transformation of macrophages from the M1 pro-inflammatory phenotype to M2 macrophages with a profibrotic phenotype. The primary changes include a decrease in secreted pro-inflammatory factors and an increase in factors involved in profibrotic repair [121]. The transition of the macrophage M1 phenotype to the M2 phenotype is a key process in tissue repair [122] and an important link in the occurrence and development of PF. Based on the current research, lactylation may promote the transformation of macrophages into the M2 type, thereby secreting profibrotic cytokines that act on surrounding cells and cause them to progress towards fibrosis. However, the proteins that undergo lactylation to exert this effect, as well as which cytokines are secreted due to lactylation during the process of PF, and how exactly they act on the surrounding cells, still require further exploration (Fig. 2).

Drugs affecting glycolysis - verifying the role of lactylation by inhibiting lactate production to reduce lactylation modification

Lactic acid is produced by the anaerobic oxidation of glucose (glycolysis). Pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) converts pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, thereby limiting lactate production. The two maintain a dynamic balance. Abnormal lactic acid accumulation causes lactic acidosis [123]. Currently, drugs that affect glycolysis mainly include oxamate, dichloroacetate (DCA), and 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG), which reduce the production of lactate and decrease lactylation. 2-DG is a non-metabolizable glucose analog that can reduce lactate production and histone Kla levels [120]. Sodium DCA and oxamate inhibit lactate production by regulating the activities of PDH and lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA), respectively [81, 124]. Gene depletion by LDHA or stiripentol (a lactate dehydrogenase A inhibitor used clinically for antiepileptic treatment) can be used to reduce lactate [50]. Shikonin can inhibit PKM2 and has applications in the study of renal fibrosis [30], as well as increasing lactate accumulation by inhibiting enzymes downstream of aerobic oxidation; Rotenone is an inhibitor of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I, which can drive cells to undergo glycolysis, thereby increasing intracellular lactate and histone Kla levels [125].

Oxamate and 2-DG are widely used to study the role of lactylation, and current research primarily focuses on tumors and fibrosis-related diseases. GNE-140 is a small-molecule inhibitor that targets LDHA. LDHA inhibition affects conversion of pyruvate to lactate [31]. DCA has been used to explore the role of lactylation in diseases, such as sepsis-induced acute kidney injury, kidney fibrosis, and diabetic nephropathy. Galloflavin has also been shown to reduce lactate production; however, it has not yet been applied in lactylation research [126]. Because the mechanism of action of these drugs is primarily to inhibit the production of lactic acid, they have certain effects on different cell types.

Drugs affecting lactate transport - affecting the influx and efflux of lactate into and out of cells, thus influencing lactylation

To understand the drugs that affect lactate transport, it is necessary to clarify how lactate is transported and its effects. The lactate transporters across the plasma membrane are divided into proton- and sodium-dependent transporters. Among them, proton transporters are classified as MCT1–4 and sodium-dependent transporters are classified as SMCT1–2. These transporters act on various substrates, including pyruvate and ketone bodies. Currently, in research on lactylation and PF, more attention is being paid to proton transporters, such as MCT1. In cells, MCT1 primarily mediates lactate influx, whereas MCT4 primarily mediates lactate efflux; however, it ultimately depends on the lactate gradient [127]. Lactic acid acts as the ligand. GPR81 and GPR132 are common G protein-coupled receptors found in lactic acid. The GPR81 receptor is widely expressed in adipose tissue and the brain. GPR132 is expressed in various tissues, including the lungs and gastrointestinal tract, as well as in immune cells, particularly macrophages. Moreover, in addition to lactic acid, lipid oxides can bind to the GPR132 ligand [127, 128], offering the potential to explore the relationship between lipids and lactate. Whether the involvement of lactic acid in the process of lactylation is related to the lactic acid ligand requires further investigation. Both α-CHCA and AZD3965 can inhibit lactate transport by suppressing MCT1, reducing the intracellular lactate content [34, 127, 129]. Syrosingopine primarily inhibits MCT4, suppressing the efflux of lactate and increasing the intracellular lactate content [130].

Lactylation-specific inhibitors - inhibiting the generation of lactylation

A-485 is an inhibitor of P300, and P300 is the most studied lactylation writer protein, and can prevent protein lactylation [81]. Demethylzeylasteral can inhibit H3K9/H3K56 lactylation and H3K18 lactylation, which has been confirmed in the research of liver cancer stem cells and pancreatic cancer cells [98, 131]. There are also studies claiming that evodiamine can inhibit H3K18 lactylation [132]; however, the specificity remains to be discussed. Theoretically, reducing lactylation enhances the activity of lactylation erasers. For example, ITSA-1 promotes the activities of deacetylases and delactylases. However, no studies have been conducted on lactylation-related diseases. Knockdown certain writing proteins or erasing proteins is another way to explore the role of lactylation. At present, there are no reports on agonists or inhibitors that directly act on lactylation sites, and further exploration is needed. The study of drugs that act directly on lactylation sites is also the basis for applying lactylation-related research results in clinical practice.

Given the non-specificity of these inhibitors, researchers should fully consider their limitations and make careful choices when selecting drugs to intervene in lactylation. In the future, efforts should be made to develop specific lactylation inhibitors to meet scientific research and clinical needs.

Clinical translation potential of lactylation

Clinically, blood lactic acid levels in patients with IPF are significantly increased [133]. Lactic acid acts as the substrate for lactylation. According to current research, the lactylation of certain protein molecules can promote the progression of PF. With further research, the lactylation of key molecules may serve as clinical diagnostic indicators in the future. Although research on lactylation at the overall level is relatively lacking at present, some studies have shown that interventions in lactylation at the overall level may be beneficial for the alleviation of PF and the recovery of lung function. For example, at the overall level, the use of 2-DG to interfere with lactic acid production is beneficial for the recovery of cardiac function, alleviates cardiac fibrosis [72], and inhibits lactylation, thereby preventing the development of PF [134]. Overall, silencing YTHDF1 can alleviate PF in mice, and research evidence has shown that H3K18la regulate the expression of YTHDF1 [34]. Liraglutide reduces histone lactylation in mouse pulmonary fibroblasts by activating glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R), thereby slowing the progression of PF [41]. Traditional Chinese medicines, such as Jinshui Huanxian Formula (ECC-JHF) and wogonoside, can also slow the overall process of PF by reducing the lactylation of H3K18 or Snail1 [80, 135]. Studies have also shown that the LDHA inhibitor (GNE-140) alleviates inflammation and fibrosis in the lungs of mice by inhibiting histone lactylation [31]. Overall, oxamate can reduce PF in mice [136]. α-CHCA is also involved in PF models. It can block the effect of fibroblasts in promoting histone lactylation in lung epithelial cells through lactic acid, but has not been applied at the overall level to verify whether inhibiting lactylation can alleviate PF [34]. Protein lactylation may promote the progression of PF through different mechanisms. Controlling lactic acid and lactylation at appropriate levels within a specific period may help reduce pulmonary fibrotic lesions. Currently, clinical drugs used for the treatment of PF function by inhibiting multiple pro-fibrotic pathways. As research into lactylation in PF remains nascent, more studies on the lactylation of PF-related proteins are needed. In the future, the development of drugs that act on multiple targets and appropriate intervention periods will be beneficial for the prevention and treatment of PF.

Limitations of current lactylation research

At present, research on the lactylation of protein molecules primarily uses immunofluorescence, co-immunoprecipitation, and western blotting. Lysine lactylation sites were identified by anti-Kla antibody enrichment combined with high-resolution liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS). It is currently the most authoritative method. Site-directed mutagenesis targeting specific lysine lactylation sites also serves as a relatively robust method for the verification of protein lactylation. Currently, specific antibodies related to histone lactylation are available; however, specific antibodies against non-histone lactylation have not yet been developed. Currently, drug interventions for lactylation primarily use inhibitors that interfere with lactic acid production and transport. In vitro experiments can reduce lactylation, but interference with lactic acid production and transport at the overall level can cause a series of effects, which cannot directly explain why the improvement of lung function and alleviation of PF are caused by the reduction of lactylation. For example, inhibiting LDHA can reduce lactylation, but at the same time, it will extensively affect the physiological processes of cells. Point mutation of lysine at a certain site of a protein reduces its lactylation level, but other post-translational modifications of lysine may also be affected simultaneously, which may also alter the intrinsic properties of the protein. Intervention with lactylation writers or erasers at the genetic level can affect the level of lactylation; however, these writers and erasers also have other functions, such as acetylation, which will also be affected, making it impossible to specifically explain the problem. In future studies, we expect to discover lactylation-specific writers, erasers, and readers that will aid in the development of drugs that specifically regulate lactylation. There is still some controversy regarding whether specific interference with lactylation can be achieved, and the development of more specific lactylation inhibitors is an urgent problem. In addition, distinguishing the modification sites of protein lactylation from those of other types of post-translational modifications will be the focus of future research.

Conclusions and future perspectives

In conclusion, lactylation may promote the development of PF by affecting epithelial cell autophagy, cellular senescence, expression of proliferation or repair genes, cell apoptosis, ferroptosis, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and EMT and may influence the activation of fibroblasts and secretion of pro-fibrotic factors by macrophages, among other mechanisms. Currently, research on lactylation in PF is still in its early stages, and further studies are required to enrich our understanding of its role in PF. Clarifying the fundamental role of lactylation will help to fill these research gaps, for example, point mutations at specific protein lactylation sites are used to verify changes in the functional status of lung epithelial cells, changes in the production of extracellular matrix by fibroblasts, and the degree of pulmonary fibrosis at the overall level, etc. The lactylation sites of distinct proteins and their functional roles following lactylation may differ in the context of pulmonary fibrosis, thus requiring further experimental validation. Developing conditional genetic mouse models (e.g. target cell-specific or inducible knockout/knockin of lactylation-related enzymes such as p300/CBP) to enable precise spatiotemporal control of lactylation levels in vivo. Besides, employing site-directed lysine-to-arginine (K→R) mutagenesis approaches to functionally validate critical lactylation sites on target proteins (e.g., α-SMA, collagen I, or transcription factors). Complemented by CRISPR/Cas9-mediated knockin of non-modifiable or mimic constitutive lactylation mutants, these strategies would elucidate the mechanistic contribution of individual lactylation sites to fibroblast activation and ECM deposition. In the future, the discovery of new and more specific lactylation writers or eraser proteins is needed, enabling the knockout of these writers or eraser proteins at the genetic level to be used to confirm the relationship between lactylation and PF. In addition, new drugs for the specific intervention of lactylation are required, and the development of small-molecule inhibitors can advance research on lactylation and PF. Furthermore, a lack of clinical evidence for lactylation currently exists. Increased investment in in vivo experiments and clinical trials can fundamentally address these issues. Future research should focus on the design of drugs that interfere with lactylation to slow the progression of PF.
