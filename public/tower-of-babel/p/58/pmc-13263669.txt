Pediatric versus adult self-inflating bags for cardiopulmonary resuscitation of adult patients with cardiac arrest – a non-randomized controlled trial
Authors: BG Kowsthubha, S Manu Ayyan, D Anandhi, M Vivekanandan Pillai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExcessive ventilation during cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) may compromise hemodynamics by elevating intrathoracic pressure and reducing venous return. Standard adult self-inflating bags often deliver tidal volumes exceeding American Heart Association (AHA) and European Resuscitation Council (ERC) guidelines (6–7 ml/kg of Predicted Body Weight (PBW)). We hypothesized that employing a pediatric self-inflating bag during extended CPR in intubated adult cardiac arrest patients would yield tidal volumes more in line with guideline recommendations.MethodThis was a single blinded, non-randomized controlled study conducted from October 2022 to October 2023 at a tertiary academic Emergency Department (ED) after ethical committee approval (Trial registration: ISRCTN10909864). Asynchronous ventilation was delivered during CPR using an adult self-inflating bag (1600 mL) in patients who were endotracheally intubated. For patients meeting termination-of-resuscitation (TOR) criteria, extended CPR was delivered using a pediatric self-inflating bag (750 mL). Ventilatory metrics were recorded via a CITREX H3 Gas Flow Analyzer connected between the endotracheal tube and the bag. The primary objective was to compare the inspired (VTi) and expired (VTe) tidal volumes delivered by the two devices, while the secondary objective was to determine the VTi required to achieve visible chest rise.ResultsOf 224 patients screened for eligibility, 132 (59%) were enrolled after excluding 2 (1.5%) patients who achieved return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), 130 (98.5%) patients were included in the final analysis. The adult self-inflating bag delivered a median VTi of 8.7 mL/kg of PBW [IQR 7.5–10.3], significantly higher than the pediatric bag (6.4 mL/kg PBW [IQR 5.45–7.7] (p < 0.001). VTe delivered by adult bag was also higher (7.66 mL/kg PBW [IQR 6.23–9.13] vs pediatric 5.82 mL/kg PBW [IQR 4.59–7.07] (p < 0.001). Case-average VTi within the 2015 ERC guideline range (6–7 mL/kg per PBW) was achieved more frequently with the pediatric self-inflating bag than with the adult bag (32 [24%] vs 14 [11%] cases), with significantly higher odds of target VTi delivery (matched-pair odds ratio 2.29; 95% CI 1.23–4.25; p < 0.01). For VTe, there was no significant difference in target range achievement between the pediatric and adult bags (21 [16%] vs 24 [18%] cases; matched-pair odds ratio 0.88; 95% CI 0.49–1.57; p = 0.77). Additionally, the median VTi necessary for a visible chest rise was 190 mL [IQR 164.8–217.0].ConclusionIn intubated adults during CPR, pediatric self-inflating bags delivered case-average tidal volumes per predicted body weight closer to weight-based ventilation targets (ERC 2015 guidelines), with higher odds of achieving the target tidal volume range compared with standard adult bags. However, clinical outcomes were not evaluated.Keywords: Cardiopulmonary resuscitation, Ventilation, Tidal volume

Introduction

Ensuring appropriate ventilation is crucial in high-quality cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) to avoid adverse outcomes.1, 2 The frequency and ventilation method depend largely on the presence or absence of an advanced airway.1, 2 During adult CPR, current guidelines emphasize delivering enough volume to produce visible chest rise and avoiding hypo and hyper ventilation.1, 2 The European Resuscitation Council (ERC) 2015 guidelines suggested approximately 500–600 mL (≈6–7 mL/kg predicted body weight [PBW]) per breath as a practical target to achieve visible chest rise.3 Subsequent updates have not-emphasized fixed numeric targets, focusing on visible chest rise.1, 4 The latest ERC 2025 guidelines recommend delivering tidal volumes of 6–8 mL/kg PBW when using a mechanical ventilator, or sufficient to produce visible chest movement.1 Similarly, the American Heart Association (AHA) 2020 guidelines recommend approximately 500–600 mL or breaths sufficient to achieve visible chest rise,5 while the 2025 update continues to emphasize visible chest rise without a strict volume target.2 Although guidelines recommend delivering breaths to achieve visible chest rise, this visual endpoint is inherently subjective and may not reliably reflect the delivered tidal volume, healthcare providers have judged “adequate” chest rise at widely variable—and sometimes low—tidal volumes in anesthetized adults.6, 7 Furthermore, during ongoing chest compressions after advanced airway placement, visible chest rise can be difficult to assess, potentially leading to both inadvertent delivery of high or low tidal volume than the recommendation. Mechanical ventilator use during CPR adds operational and technical complexity, requiring active monitoring and adjustment in an already time-critical resuscitation environment.8 Excessive ventilation can hinder hemodynamics by increasing intrathoracic pressure, reducing venous return, and compromising cardiac output and organ perfusion.9

Adult self-inflating bags (1500–2000 mL capacity) deliver tidal volumes that vary considerably among providers during manual ventilation, often reaching 900–1000 mL.10 In contrast, pediatric self-inflating bags (500–1000 mL capacity) deliver lower tidal volumes, typically around 400–800 mL per squeeze (“bag squeeze = the rescuer’s typical hand compression of the bag”), which may be more consistent with recommended ventilation targets during CPR.10, 11

Previous simulation-based research suggests adult bags tend to hyperventilate in terms of tidal volume while pediatric bags provide appropriate tidal volume for effective adult CPR.12 Pediatric bags have also been effective for manual ventilation during anesthesia induction in healthy adult patients undergoing elective surgery.13 However, specific ventilatory metrics for adult self-inflating-bags during CPR are not well-documented. Evidence on the impact of smaller self-inflating bags on ventilation remains limited. O’Neill and Deakin demonstrated that hyperventilation during cardiac arrest is common, mostly due to high respiratory rates rather than excessive tidal volumes.14 Experimental data suggest that clinically plausible increases in respiratory rate and tidal volume may not invariably result in adverse hemodynamic effects under controlled conditions.15 More recently, Snyder et al. reported that the use of smaller adult ventilation bags in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest was associated with lower odds of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), highlighting that bag size may influence outcomes.16 In this context, our study compares the tidal volumes delivered by adult and pediatric self-inflating bags during CPR in intubated adult cardiac arrest patients.

This study aimed to compare the inspired tidal volume (VTi) and expired tidal volume (VTe) delivered by adult and pediatric self-inflating bags during CPR in intubated adult cardiac arrest patients, and to determine the VTi required to achieve visible chest rise. We hypothesized that pediatric bags can deliver ventilation consistent with guidelines for adult patients during CPR.

Methods

Study procedure

This trial was a prospective, single-blinded, non-randomized controlled crossover study comparing tidal volumes delivered by adult and pediatric self-inflating bags during CPR in intubated adult cardiac arrest patients. Each patient served as their own control in a fixed-order within-subject comparison: ventilation was first provided using an adult self-inflating bag during ongoing CPR, followed by a pediatric self-inflating bag during the brief termination-of-resuscitation (TOR) communication interval.

The study was conducted between October 2022 and October 2023 in the Emergency Department (ED) of an academic tertiary-care and research institute in Puducherry, India, which manages approximately 350–356 cardiac arrest cases annually. All adult patients aged ≥18 years with in-hospital or out-of-hospital cardiac arrest who were intubated in the ED were eligible. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy, bleeding or secretions in the endotracheal tube, and non-availability of a flow analyzer (Fig. 1).

Prior to patient enrollment, the principal investigator was trained using a simulated cardiac arrest scenario to standardize placement of the flow analyzer between the endotracheal tube and the self-inflating bag. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC No. JIP/IEC/2022/034), registered with the ISRCTN registry (ISRCTN10909864), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.17 Given the impracticality of obtaining prospective informed consent from unconscious cardiac arrest patients, the urgency precluding surrogate consent, and the potential societal benefit of improving resuscitation practices,18 a waiver of informed consent was granted.

All patients were resuscitated according to institutional and international guidelines,4, 5 including chest compressions at a rate of 100–120/min with a depth of 5–6 cm. For patients with an advanced airway, asynchronous ventilation was provided with continuous chest compressions at approximately one breath every 6 s. Ventilatory parameters—including VTi, VTe, inspiratory time, and respiratory rate—were recorded over 30 s–1 min, and mean values were documented.

Patients who achieved ROSC received routine post–cardiac arrest care. For patients who did not achieve ROSC and met TOR criteria according to AHA 2020 and ERC 2021 guidelines,4, 5 the TOR decision was made by the treating physician. In our institution, CPR is continued while the physician communicates the outcome to family or bystanders. The study intervention was conducted during this interval, ensuring that resuscitation was not prolonged solely for research purposes. During this phase, the adult bag was replaced with a pediatric self-inflating bag (750 mL), and ventilatory metrics were recorded for several minutes.

The VTi required to produce visible chest rise was noted. After completion of the intervention phase, chest compressions were stopped, and visible chest rise was assessed visually by the principal investigator and confirmed by the team leader.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using a precision-based approach for mean tidal volume, assuming a standard deviation of 87 mL12 and a desired precision of 15 mL, resulting in a required sample size of 130 participants. Although the study involved paired comparisons, a paired sample size approach was not adopted, as it yielded a very small sample size and required assumptions regarding within-subject correlation.

Outcome

Primary outcome

The case-average VTi (normalized to PBW mL/kg) delivered during CPR using adult versus pediatric self-inflating bags.

Secondary outcomes

(1) Case-average VTe, normalized to PBW (mL/kg). (2) Case-average VTi and VTe expressed in absolute volumes (mL). (3) Case-average guideline-consistent ventilation (yes/no) for each metric, defined using PBW-based targets of 6–7 mL/kg based on the ERC 2015 guidelines.3 (4) The VTi (mL) required to produce visible chest rise in intubated patients.

Definitions

PBW was calculated from height (cm) and sex using standard formulas: males, PBW (kg) = 50 + 0.91 × (height − 152.4); females, PBW (kg) = 45.5 + 0.91 × (height − 152.4).19

“Guideline-consistent” ventilation based on PBW refers to the ERC 2015 recommendation of 6–7 mL/kg PBW3. AHA 2020 recommends a tidal volume of 500–600 ml or a breath sufficient for a visible chest rise. ERC 2021 specify visible chest rise with no numeric band4, 5 and are cited for context only.

Measurement of ventilatory metrics

VTi and VTe were recorded directly by the ventilator flow sensor. Ventilatory metrics were measured using the CITREX H3 Gas Flow Analyzer and Ventilator Tester, which measures parameters like VTi, VTe, bi-directional flow, pressure, temperature, and optionally oxygen concentration. The CITREX H3 is compact and lightweight, making it suitable for emergency setups. The schematic representation of connecting it to the ventilatory circuit is shown in Fig. 2. During the study, asynchronous ventilation was provided during continuous chest compressions. The CITREX H3 analyzer utilizes integrated flow sensors to measure bi-directional airflow. But the device is not validated under chest compressions.20

Schematic representation of the connection of the flow analyzer with the ventilation circuit.

To minimize performance bias, the study was conducted under single blinded conditions. A flow analyzer was connected between the endotracheal tube and the self-inflating bag, with the display covered so that ventilation was performed without real-time feedback, thereby reflecting natural operator technique. Blinding to the type of ventilation bag (pediatric versus adult) was not feasible due to the inherent differences in device size and handling characteristics.

Statistical analysis

The analysis was performed using IBM® SPSS® Statistics version 19.21 Categorical variables (e.g., sex, place of arrest) are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables (age, height, and predicted body weight [PBW]) are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

As each patient received ventilation with both bag types, comparisons were paired. Normality of paired differences was assessed, and as the data were not normally distributed, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for continuous outcomes, which are reported as median [interquartile range, IQR] with corresponding p-values. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Paired binary outcomes (case-average within-range yes/no) were compared using McNemar’s test and are summarized as matched-pairs odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). No multivariable modeling was performed, as each patient served as their own control.

Comparison of tidal volume between two independent groups (sex) was performed using the Mann–Whitney U test, as the data were not normally distributed. Correlation between tidal volume and continuous variables (height and predicted body weight) was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Agreement between tidal volume required to achieve chest rise and guideline-recommended tidal volume was assessed using Bland–Altman analysis, with calculation of the mean difference (bias) and 95% limits of agreement.

Results

During the study period, 224 patients were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 92 (41%) patients were excluded (Fig. 1). Consequently, 132 (59%) patients were enrolled in the study. Out of these 132 patients, CPR was performed, and ventilation was provided using an adult self-inflating-bag. Patients who achieved ROSC following CPR (n = 2, 1.5%) were excluded and subsequently received post-ROSC care. The remaining 130 (98.5%) patients, who received extended CPR with a pediatric self-inflating-bag, were included in the final analysis. No missing data were observed.

Tidal volume (ml/kg of PBW)

The adult self-inflating bag delivered a case average median VTi of 8.7 mL/kg of PBW [IQR 7.5–10.3], pediatric bag delivered 6.4 mL/kg of PBW [IQR 5.45–7.7]; (z = 9.83, p < 0.001). When the pediatric self-inflating bag was used for ventilation, case average VTi (mL/kg of PBW) within the ERC guideline range was achieved in 32 (24%) cases, whereas the adult self-inflating bag achieved case average VTi within the ERC guideline range in 14 (11%) cases. No individual patient achieved VTi within the guideline range (6–7 ml/kg) with both bags. Paired analysis using McNemar’s test demonstrated significantly higher odds of achieving VTi within the ERC guideline range with the pediatric bag compared with the adult bag (matched-pair odds ratio 2.29; 95% CI 1.23–4.25; p < 0.01) (Table 2).

Absolute tidal volume

The median absolute VTi delivered by the adult self-inflating bag was 454.0 mL [IQR: 399.0–533.5], 326.5 mL [IQR: 282.5–386.0] with the pediatric bag, a statistically significant difference (z = 6.78, p < 0.0001). The median absolute VTe was 398.6 mL [IQR: 328.5–470.3] with the adult bag versus 323.5 mL [IQR: 267.3–390.8] with the pediatric bag, which was statistically significant (z = 5.02, p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4).

Tidal volume required for visible chest rise

Visible chest rise was observed at a median measured VTi of 190 mL [IQR 164.8–217]. The median VTi required to achieve visible chest rise was 194 mL [IQR: 174–220] in males and 180 mL [IQR: 154–212] in females (Fig. 5). There was no statistically significant difference between sexes (U = 2300.5, p = 0.129). The VTi required to achieve a visible chest rise was consistently lower than the guideline-recommended tidal volume based on predicted body weight. Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated a mean difference (bias) of −153.63 mL, with 95% limits of agreement ranging from −296.80 to −10.47 mL. Paired t-test analysis confirmed a statistically significant difference between the two methods (mean difference −153.63 mL; 95% CI −166.31 to −140.96; p < 0.001).

Discussion

In this prospective study of adult cardiac arrest resuscitation, we found that using a pediatric self-inflating bag for ventilation in intubated patients, delivered significantly smaller tidal volumes than the standard adult bag. The mean VTi with the pediatric bag was 6.4 mL/kg of PBW, compared to 8.8 mL/kg of PBW with the adult bag. These data indicate that while the pediatric bag did not reach the absolute target of 500–600 mL,3, 5 it delivered VTi within the guideline-recommended range when normalized to PBW3 derived from patient height. While broader ventilation literature emphasizes tailoring tidal volume to patient size to minimize lung injury, this concept is hypothesis-generating and lies outside current CPR guideline recommendations, which focus primarily on visible chest rise rather than lung-protective volume targets.22 Although current guidelines emphasize avoiding both hypo and hyper-ventilation,1, 2 the pediatric bag produced tidal volumes closer to recommended targets, whereas the adult bag consistently exceeded them, suggesting that bag size may influence ventilation delivery during CPR.

Current AHA and ERC guidelines emphasize delivering tidal volumes sufficient to produce visible chest rise while avoiding both hypoventilation and hyperventilation during CPR.1, 2 Classic physiologic and animal work links over‑ventilation to elevated intrathoracic pressure and impaired venous return,23 however, clinically plausible increases in rate or tidal volume have not consistently produced adverse hemodynamic effects in experimental models15 though ETCO2 decreases with higher minute ventilation. Collectively, these data suggest avoiding both over‑ and under‑ventilation. Early animal studies have demonstrated that excessive ventilation, particularly increased ventilation rates, can adversely affect hemodynamics during CPR.23 Although data during cardiac arrest are limited, experimental studies in mechanically ventilated animal models have demonstrated that increasing tidal volume is associated with greater intrathoracic pressure fluctuations and reduced intrathoracic blood volume during inspiration, suggesting a potential mechanism by which higher tidal volumes may influence hemodynamics.24 Newer clinical data show that using smaller adult bags (designed to reduce tidal volumes) may paradoxically decrease rates of ROSC after out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, compared to larger adult bags.16 The mechanisms are unclear, as ventilation rate did not differ between groups, but differences in minute ventilation and acid-base status may play a role.

Moreover, surveys among resuscitation providers reveal significant variation in ventilation practices, and no clear consensus on ideal rate or volume.8, 25 Guidelines continue to recommend avoiding excessive ventilation1, 2, 3, 4, 5 but emphasize the need for further research on intra-arrest ventilation parameters. Both the size of the ventilation bag and the rate of breaths delivered should be tailored to optimize patient hemodynamics and gas exchange, and more research is urgently needed to define best practices in intra-arrest ventilation.

Collectively, physiologic and clinical studies demonstrate that ventilation delivery during CPR is variable and clinically relevant. Small human physiologic work shows that tidal volume directly influences arterial CO2 levels during resuscitation, underscoring the potential consequences of both under- and over-ventilation.26 Randomized prehospital data indicate that increasing ventilation rate increases delivered minute ventilation without improving gas exchange or ROSC, highlighting the non-linear physiology of CPR ventilation.27 Patients in whom ventilations actually produced lung inflation more consistently (detected by thoracic impedance waveforms) had higher ROSC, survival to discharge, and better neurological outcomes.28 Observational clinical studies further suggest that ventilation delivery consistency and device characteristics may be associated with resuscitation outcomes, although causality remains uncertain.28, 16 Together, these findings support balanced ventilation and adherence to guideline targets while recognizing that optimal CPR ventilation remains an evolving area of investigation.

The difference between adult and pediatric self-inflating bags is intuitive: adult 600–1000 mL per full bag squeeze, far exceeding the ∼500 mL typically needed for adequate ventilation in most adults. In contrast, pediatric bags deliver stroke volumes closer to guideline targets, reducing the risk of over-ventilation. For an average adult male (175 cm, ∼70 kg PBW), pediatric bag ventilation produced ∼450 mL (∼6.4 mL/kg), which falls within the ERC’s recommended 6–7 mL/kg range, whereas adult bags delivered ∼616 mL (∼8.8 mL/kg), near or above ERC’s upper limit. Our findings extend existing simulation evidence to actual resuscitation scenarios, highlighting the facilitated adherence to guideline‑sized, PBW‑normalized VTi. However, no clinical outcomes were assessed, and observational data suggest that ventilation device characteristics may influence out-of-hospital cardiac arrest outcomes,16 warranting cautious interpretation of device-related implications. The ERC guidelines specify that each CPR breath should be delivered over one second, only enough to achieve a visible adequate chest rise, explicitly discouraging excessive tidal volume delivery.3 In our study, a VTi of approximately 190 ml was sufficient to achieve a visible chest rise. These findings describe the delivered volumes required to achieve chest rise in our cohort, however, this also highlights that relying solely on chest rise, as suggested by current guidelines1, 2, 3, 4, 5 may be an uncertain indicator of adequate tidal volume delivery or effective ventilation. Similar results were reported by Baskett et al.7 in intubated and anesthetized patients, highlighting ambiguity regarding what constitutes adequate chest rise.

How we deliver breaths during CPR is a critical component of high-quality resuscitation, and modifying ventilation strategy or equipment may influence adherence to best practices. While our data cannot determine the frequency of excessive tidal volumes, future studies should evaluate whether smaller ventilation bags could help to deliver guideline recommended tidal volume in adult resuscitation. This approach could be a possible solution to avoid some of the human factor errors that lead to guidelines recommended ventilation. Emphasis should be placed on delivering just enough volume to see the chest rise, as per guidelines, rather than emptying the bag with each breath. Tools or techniques to provide feedback on tidal volume like ventilation monitors or devices that limit bag squeeze may also help mitigate unintentional over-ventilation. Ultimately, integrating these findings into practice – whether by selecting appropriately sized equipment or reinforcing volume-targeted ventilation – could reduce the risk of hyperventilation-induced complications during CPR. It is also worth noting that limiting tidal volume may reduce gastric inflation in cases where an advanced airway is not secured. Prior research has shown that using a pediatric bag can significantly decrease the volume of air inadvertently delivered to the stomach when ventilating with basic airway adjuncts or a face mask.13 Our study primarily involved intubated patients, we did not directly assess gastric inflation, however, the principle from those earlier findings supports an additional rationale for smaller tidal volumes during basic life support ventilation. However, the clinical application requires careful consideration of the trade-off between reduced gastric insufflation and potentially inadequate ventilation, particularly in adult patients.16

Several strengths of this study enhance the validity of our conclusions. To our knowledge, this study adds to the limited prospective clinical evidence 29, 30 measuring delivered tidal volumes during adult CPR in intubated patients using standard resuscitation equipment. We recruited patients in cardiac arrest as opposed to manikins bench models or cadaver studies. This clinical approach is crucial as alternative models cannot fully replicate the dynamic lung compliance and the complex interactions between active chest compressions and airflow observed during human resuscitation. We employed a blinded assessment of the ventilation data, reducing the risk of observer bias in comparing the two bag types. Our study provides real-world confirmation of prior simulation findings that smaller bags yield smaller tidal volumes during CPR.

Future research should prioritize carefully designed randomized controlled trials comparing pediatric and adult ventilation bags from the initiation of CPR, with particular attention to safety concerns and potential risks of inadequate ventilation and also future randomized trials should align with the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation’s core outcome set for adult cardiac arrest (ILCOR’s COSCA).31 Outcomes of interest should include ROSC rates, survival, and neurological recovery. Additional studies could examine ergonomic factors, patient subgroup responses, and rescuer fatigue during prolonged CPR scenarios. Hybrid approaches—such as intermediate-sized bags or adjustable valves that allow fine-tuning of delivered volume—may offer a safer compromise and deserve exploration. Finally, implementation science research is needed to determine how best to translate these findings into resuscitation protocols while balancing efficacy and safety.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Allocation to pediatric versus adult self-inflating bag ventilation was not randomized, and the fixed within-subject sequence (adult followed by pediatric) introduces potential for selection bias, confounding, and time/period effects such as changes in thoracic compliance or rescuer fatigue. Tidal volumes were measured using a CITREX H3 flow analyzer, which has not been validated during ongoing chest compressions, and real-time measurement may have introduced a Hawthorne effect despite blinding to the display. We did not assess arterial blood gases or ETCO2, and therefore report tidal volume delivery and guideline adherence rather than adequacy of gas exchange. Although inclusion of real patients enhances clinical relevance, it also introduces variability related to chest compliance, airway patency, and ongoing compressions. All patients were intubated early-thus, findings may not be generalizable to bag-mask ventilation or supraglottic airway use. The study did not evaluate clinical outcomes such as ROSC, survival, or neurological recovery, and the sample size and single-system design may further limit generalizability. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

Pediatric self-inflating bags delivered case average VTi for PBW closer to ERC recommended volumes than standard adult bags during adult CPR, with higher odds of achieving PBW-normalized VTi within the 6–7 mL/kg range. Absolute tidal volumes were below 500–600 mL with both bag types. While these delivery characteristics are more closely aligned with guideline-recommended weight-based ventilation targets, interpretation is limited to tidal-volume standardization, as clinical outcomes were not evaluated.
