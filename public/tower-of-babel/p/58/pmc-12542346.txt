NRF2 expression level and estrogen function in BRCA1-mutated breast cancer
Authors: Zeinab Derismahafi, Dariush Farhud, Amirhossein Razavirad, Ahad Muhammadnejad, Behnaz Jahanbin, Shirin Farivar, Reza Shirkoohi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer (BC) is the most common malignancy diagnosed in women, with approximately 5–10% of cases attributed to hereditary factors. Although the tumor suppressor breast cancer susceptibility gene 1(BRCA1) is ubiquitously expressed, it is not clear why BRCA1 mutations overwhelmingly predispose to breast and ovarian cancers. Estrogen has been proposed to promote the survival of BRCA1-deficient cells under oxidative stress by activating nuclear factor erythroid-2-related factor 2 (NRF2), potentially driving malignant transformation. This study investigates the role of estrogen and NRF2 expression in BRCA1-mutated breast cancer.MethodsWe analyzed 70 formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue samples (tumor and adjacent non-tumoral) from 15 BRCA1-mutated breast cancer patients and 20 non-mutated controls. Both adjacent non-tumoral and tumoral breast tissue were paired samples from the same patient for the two groups. Background

Breast cancer (BC) is the most frequently diagnosed malignancy among women worldwide. It ranks second in global cancer incidence, closely following lung cancer, and is the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality, accounting for 666,103 deaths annually [1]. Around 5–10% of BC cases are hereditary, with 4–5% linked to high-penetrance genes inherited in an autosomal dominant manner [2, 3]. Breast cancer susceptibility gene 1 (BRCA1; OMIM #113,705; Chromosome 17q) [4] is a tumor suppressor that plays a fundamental role in repairing DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs). Mutations in this high-penetrance gene increase the risk of BC by 10–20 times, with a Lifetime risk of 60–85% [5, 6]. BRCA1-associated breast tumors often present at an early onset or bilaterally [7]. Despite BRCA1’s crucial role in maintaining genomic integrity across all cells, the specific predisposition of BRCA1 mutation carriers to breast and ovarian cancers remains unclear [8].

Estrogen (E2), the primary sex hormone in breast tissue, is essential for natural breast growth [9, 10]. However, it also contributes to the initiation, progression, and metastasis of BC via its receptors, such as estrogen receptor alpha (ERα), and downstream signaling proteins, including focal adhesion kinase (FAK), as illustrated in Fig. 1 [11]. The FAK protein is encoded by the protein Tyrosine Kinase 2(PTK2) gene [12]. While BRCA1 and BRCA2 mutation carriers often have elevated estrogen levels, which may contribute to increased BC risk [13, 14], paradoxically, the majority of BRCA1-mutated breast tumors (> 90%) are ERα-negative [15]. Moreover, normal breast tissues carrying BRCA1 mutations exhibit downregulation of estrogen-responsive genes, including cell proliferation-related genes [16]. However, when exposed to estrogen, these cells undergo rapid proliferation [17]. Notably, BRCA1-related tumors predominantly arise in hormone-responsive tissues, such as the breast and ovary [18]. Oophorectomy significantly reduces BC risk and recurrence in BRCA1 mutation carriers [19–21]. Furthermore, studies in mouse models have demonstrated that BRCA1-deficient precancerous cells exhibit estrogen-dependent growth [22], suggesting that ER may serve as a substrate for BRCA1’s ubiquitin ligase activity. Consequently, in the absence of BRCA1, ER accumulation can drive uncontrolled proliferation in breast tissues [23]. Ultimately, Wang et al. reported that estrogen plays a critical role in initiating tumorigenesis in BRCA1-deficient cells by promoting cell proliferation [24].

Estrogen exerts a paradoxical influence in BRCA1-associated breast cancer, functioning as both a tumor promoter and a survival factor. On one hand, estrogen drives proliferation in BRCA1-deficient cells by compensating for impaired DNA repair mechanisms, thereby accelerating malignant transformation [17, 24]. On the other hand, estrogen may also protect BRCA1-mutated cells from oxidative stress-induced apoptosis by restoring NRF2-mediated antioxidant defenses, enabling their survival despite high genomic instability [25]. This dual role underscores the complexity of estrogen signaling in BRCA1-related tumorigenesis and highlights the need for targeted strategies that either block estrogen’s proliferative effects or exploit its dependency in NRF2-deficient contexts.

BRCA1 is also a key regulator of antioxidant defense, primarily through its role in inducing and activating nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2) [26, 27]. NRF2, encoded by the gene nuclear factor, erythroid 2-like 2 (NFE2L2; OMIM #617,744; Chromosome 2q), is a master transcription factor belonging to the Cap’n’Collar family of leucine-zipper (B-ZIP) proteins. NRF2 protects cells from oxidative and xenobiotic stress. Under stress conditions, NRF2 translocates to the nucleus where it activates the transcription of multiple cytoprotective genes, including antioxidants, detoxifying enzymes, and transporters [28, 29]. However, in BRCA1-mutated human BC cells, NRF2-mediated antioxidant gene activation is impaired, resulting in the excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS). This leads to increased DNA damage, cellular senescence, and reduced cell survival [30]. This defect may explain why BRCA1-deficient tumors respond well to platinum-based chemotherapy, as the lack of NRF2 prevents them from effectively counteracting oxidative stress induced by treatment [31, 32].

Despite the accumulation of ROS, BRCA1-deficient cells often evade apoptosis. A potential explanation for this phenomenon is the role of estrogen, which rescues these cells from oxidative stress-induced cell death by activating NRF2. This activation facilitates the survival and malignant transformation of BRCA1-mutated cells. In BRCA1 mutation carriers, two distinct pathways have been identified: (1) In tissues with low estrogen levels, BRCA1 deficiency leads to impaired NRF2 signaling, increased ROS accumulation, and eventual cell death. (2) In estrogen-rich environments, such as the breast and ovaries, estrogen activates NRF2, thereby preventing oxidative stress-induced cell death and promoting malignant transformation [25]. However, A critical gap remains in our understanding of how BRCA1 deficiency interacts with estrogen signaling and oxidative stress pathways to drive tumorigenesis. Specifically, the role of NRF2, a key regulator of antioxidant response modulated by BRCA1, has not been thoroughly investigated in the context of BRCA1-mutated breast cancer progression and therapeutic response.

This study provides the first comprehensive analysis of NRF2 expression patterns in BRCA1-mutated breast tumors using clinically relevant FFPE samples, bridging a critical gap between experimental models and human patient data. Unlike previous studies that primarily focused on BRCA1’s role in DNA repair, our work uniquely explores its crosstalk with NRF2 and estrogen signaling—a key yet understudied axis in BRCA1-associated tumorigenesis. Furthermore, we uncover novel mechanistic insights into how estrogen’s dual role (pro-survival vs. pro-tumorigenic) may dictate the fate of BRCA1-deficient cells under oxidative stress, offering a fresh perspective on targeted therapeutic strategies. By integrating histopathological data with molecular profiling, our findings could facilitate the way for precision interventions that exploit NRF2 dysregulation or estrogen dependency in high-risk BRCA1 mutation carriers.

Materials and methods

Patients

A total of 35 patients who underwent breast cancer surgery between 2011 and 2023 were enrolled in this study. We analyzed 70 formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples, specifically comprising tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues from 15 patients with germline BRCA1 mutations (case group) and 20 patients without any known genetic predisposition to breast cancer (control group). For each patient in both groups, tumoral and adjacent non-tumoral breast tissues were collected as matched pairs. All FFPE samples were obtained through collaboration with the Pathology Department of the Cancer Institute of Iran, Imam Khomeini Hospital Complex, Tehran. Inclusion criteria included a confirmed diagnosis of primary breast cancer and availability of BRCA1 mutation status. Patients who had received neoadjuvant therapy were excluded from the study. To ensure tissue type specificity and avoid cross-contamination, immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis was performed for verification. Clinicopathological data of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The BRCA1-mutated cases harbored a variety of pathogenic germline mutations in the BRCA1 gene, including frameshift, missense, nonsense, and stop-gained variants. These mutations, which frequently result in truncated protein products, were identified through next-generation sequencing (NGS) using the Illumina MiSeq platform (Table 2). This study was approved by the Regional Research Ethics Committee (IR.TUMS.IKHC.REC.1401.160) and conducted following the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion in the study.

RNA extraction and cDNA synthesis

Total RNA was extracted from FFPE samples using a modified protocol [33]. Tissue sections were first deparaffinized by incubation in 1.5 mL of xylene at 37°C for 20 min. Samples were rehydrated by sequential centrifugation with decreasing ethanol concentrations (100%, 85%, and 75%) for 10 min at room temperature (RT). To digest proteins, 10–20 µL of Proteinase K (500 µg/mL, Unilong, China) and 400–450 µL of digestion buffer (10 mmol/L Tris–HCl, pH 8; 0.1 mmol/L EDTA, pH 8; and 2% SDS, pH 7.3) were added, followed by overnight incubation at 56°C. Total RNA was subsequently isolated using the phenol–chloroform method with TRIZOL reagent (Hyperazma, Iran). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized using the Reverse Transcription System in a 15 µL reaction volume, containing 1.5 µg of total RNA. Reactions were carried out on a Prime thermal cycler (Techne Thermal Cyclers, UK).

PCR and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

The polymerase chain reaction (PCR) included an initial denaturation step at 95°C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95°C for 30 s, annealing at 58°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 15 s, with a final extension step at 78°C for 5 min. PCR products were analyzed using agarose gel electrophoresis, where a single band of the expected size confirmed reaction specificity.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using the SYBR Green method with RealQ Plus 2 × Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark) on a Rotor-Gene Q system (QIAGEN, Germany) to assess NRF2 mRNA expression. The qRT-PCR thermal profile consisted of an initial activation step at 95 °C for 15 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 58 °C for 10 s, and extension at 72 °C for 15 s. The primer sequences used were: NRF2: Forward: 5′-CGCAGACATTCCCGTTTGTA-3′, Reverse: 5′-TGTCCTGTTGCATACCGTCT-3′ and GAPDH (Internal control): Forward: 5′-TCACCAGGGCTGCTTTTAAC-3′, Reverse: 5′-GACAAGCTTCCCGTTCTCAG-3′ (Table 3). Primers were designed using Primer3Web (version 4.1.0) and validated through sequence analysis using the NCBI Primer-BLAST tool. Gene expression was quantified using the 2^-ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH as the internal reference gene. All qRT-PCR reactions were performed in duplicate.

Immunohistochemical staining and analysis

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to evaluate the expression of FAK, a downstream target of estrogen signaling. FFPE tissue sections (2 µm thick) were prepared using a microtome, followed by deparaffinization and rehydration through standard protocols. Antigen retrieval was carried out by heating the sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Slides were then washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for blocking non-specific binding. After three PBS washes, sections were incubated with the primary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Following additional PBS washes, horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated antibodies were applied. The primary antibody against FAK (rabbit polyclonal, 1:800 dilution) was purchased from Cell Signaling Technology (Boston, MA, USA). Detection of immunocomplexes was performed using the MACH 1 Universal HRP-Polymer Kit (Biocare Medical, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Immunoreactivity scoring method

Staining results were independently evaluated by two blinded observers, who were unaware of the clinical and follow-up data. Each slide was evaluated using the ALLRED scoring system, a semiquantitative scoring system incorporating both the intensity of the stain and the proportion of positive malignant cells. FAK immunoreactivity was predominantly observed in the cytoplasm of malignant neoplastic cells. The Proportion score of immunoreactive tumor cells was assigned as follows: 1 (< 1%), 2 (1–10%), 3 (11–33%), 4 (34–67%), and 5 (≥ 67%). Staining intensity was graded as: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong). The final immunoreactivity score was calculated by summing both parameters, with total scores categorized as follows: 0–1: No expression, 2–3: Weak expression, 4–5: Moderate expression, and 6–8: Strong expression [34].

Statistical analysis

Student’s t-test was used to compare continuous variables between two categorical groups. To minimize potential bias, the normality of data distribution was assessed in adjacent non-tumoral tissues from both case and control groups using appropriate normality tests. Data were expressed as mean ± standard error (SE). The chi-squared (χ2) test was applied to assess associations between BRCA1 mutations and clinicopathological variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All Statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio (version 1.2.5033) and GraphPad Prism software.

Results

BRCA1 mutations in Iranian patients are associated with early-onset, ERα-negative, PR-negative, and triple-negative breast cancer

We systematically evaluated the association between BRCA1 mutation status and various clinicopathological characteristics, including patient age, tumor size, lymph node involvement, tumor grade, disease stage, KI67 expression, and breast cancer subtypes (Table 1). Our findings demonstrated a significant association between BRCA1 mutations and early-onset breast cancer. Patients in the BRCA1-mutated group were notably younger than those in the BRCA1 wild-type group (p = 0.02). Notably, approximately 80% of patients with BRCA1 mutations were under the age of 40, compared to a mean age of 44.31 years in the control group. This highlights a strong correlation between BRCA1 mutations and earlier breast cancer onset (Fig. 2). Furthermore, BRCA1-mutated breast cancer was significantly associated with more advanced pathologic staging (stage III; p = 0.001), as well as estrogen receptor alpha (ERα)-negative (p = 0.01) and progesterone receptor (PR)-negative (p = 0.02) status. In addition, 11 out of 15 patients (73%) in the BRCA1-mutated group were classified as triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), which was also statistically significant in our cohort (p = 0.01).

NRF2 exhibits a specific pro-oncogenic role in BRCA1-mutated breast cancer

To assess the role of NRF2 in BRCA1-mutated breast cancer, we analyzed its mRNA expression using quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). GAPDH was used as an internal control for normalization, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2^ − ΔΔCt fold-change method. Our results demonstrated that NRF2 expression in BRCA1-mutated tumors was approximately 7.93-fold higher than in BRCA1 wild-type tumors (Fig. 3A). This overexpression was statistically significant (p = 0.049; Fig. 3B), suggesting a possible functional role of NRF2 in BRCA1-associated tumorigenesis. To further investigate NRF2 dynamics, we compared expression levels across three groups: (1) Adjacent non-tumoral tissue, (2) BRCA1 wild-type tumors, and (3) BRCA1-mutated tumors. A statistically significant difference in NRF2 expression was observed among these groups (p = 0.036, Fig. 3C). Specifically, NRF2 expression was lowest in BRCA1 wild-type tumors, followed by adjacent non-tumoral tissues, whereas BRCA1-mutated tumors exhibited the highest NRF2 expression levels. To further clarify NRF2's specific role in BRCA1-mutated breast cancer and its potential involvement in tumor initiation, we next compared NRF2 expression in tumors and adjacent non-tumoral tissues within each group separately. In BRCA1-mutated patients, NRF2 expression was higher in tumor tissues than in adjacent non-tumoral tissues, although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.17, Fig. 3D). In contrast, in the BRCA1 wild-type group, NRF2 expression decreased in tumor tissues compared to adjacent non-tumoral tissues, but the difference remained non-significant (p = 0.57, Fig. 3E). These findings suggest that NRF2 may exhibit a context-dependent, pro-oncogenic role specifically in BRCA1-mutated breast cancer. While the overexpression trend is evident, its precise mechanistic contribution to tumor initiation or progression warrants further investigation.

Estrogen displays similar expression levels in brca1 mutation carriers and non-carriers

To investigate whether estrogen signaling contributes to BRCA1-mutated breast cancer, we examined the expression of Focal Adhesion Kinase (FAK), a downstream protein associated with estrogen receptor activity, using immunohistochemistry (IHC). FAK expression was primarily localized in the cytoplasm of tumor cells (Fig. 4). However, our analysis revealed no statistically significant difference in FAK expression between the BRCA1-mutated (case) group and the BRCA1 wild-type (control) group (p = 1). The proportion of FAK-positive samples was comparable between the two groups: 26% in the BRCA1-mutated group and 25% in the control group. Additionally, the overall FAK protein expression levels across all samples were generally weak to moderate, with no strong immunoreactivity observed in either group (Table 4).

Discussion

Breast cancer has surpassed lung cancer as the most frequently diagnosed malignancy worldwide [35], with an estimated one in eight women developing the disease during their lifetime [36]. BRCA1 and BRCA2 germline mutations are among the most significant genetic risk factors for breast and ovarian cancer [4, 37]. Mutations in the BRCA1 gene increase breast cancer risk by 10–20 times, with a Lifetime risk estimated at 60–85% [5, 6]. Although BRCA1 was first discovered in the early 1990s and its role in DNA repair is now well established, therapeutic options remain limited. Currently, poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors remain one of the limited targeted therapies for individuals with BRCA1 mutations [38]. However, the majority of patients ultimately relapse as a result of acquired resistance, which presents a growing obstacle and may limit the sustained clinical benefit of PARP inhibitor treatment [39, 40]. As a result, Prophylactic mastectomy and oophorectomy are the primary cancer preventive measures in these individuals; these procedures significantly impact the fertility and quality of life of women.

BRCA1 functions as a tumor suppressor, maintaining genomic integrity; yet, its mutations primarily predispose individuals to breast and ovarian cancer rather than a broader spectrum of malignancies [8]. This tissue specificity suggests the involvement of additional oncogenic pathways that cooperate with BRCA1 deficiency in driving tumorigenesis [41]. Our study explored the role of NRF2 and the function of estrogen in BRCA1-mutated breast cancer. We found a significant upregulation of NRF2 in BRCA1 mutation carriers, supporting a potential oncogenic role for NRF2 in this context. In contrast, estrogen signaling, assessed via FAK expression, appeared comparable between BRCA1-mutated and wild-type tumors, indicating no significant differential activity.

Estrogen is crucial for normal breast tissue development [9, 10], but it has also been implicated in tumorigenesis, particularly in BRCA1-deficient cells, by promoting proliferation and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [24]. However, our study found no significant difference in estrogen function between BRCA1-mutated and wild-type tumors, suggesting that alternative pathways may play a more dominant role in BRCA1-associated carcinogenesis.

NRF2, a key transcription factor essential for cellular defense against oxidative stress, is dysregulated in BRCA1-mutated cells. BRCA1-deficient cells typically exhibit impaired NRF2 signaling, leading to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), DNA damage, and cellular senescence [30]. Paradoxically, our results showed an overexpression of NRF2 in BRCA1-mutated tumors, suggesting a compensatory mechanism that enhances antioxidant responses in the absence of BRCA1 function. This unexpected high expression of NRF2 indicates a distinct molecular relationship between this transcription factor and BRCA1 mutations, possibly reflecting adaptive changes that allow tumor cells to survive under oxidative stress. In adjacent non-tumoral tissue, NRF2 expression appears to play its typical antioxidant and protective role. In sporadic (BRCA1 wild-type) breast cancer, NRF2 expression generally decreases, as observed in our control group and consistent with previous studies (Figure S1; https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/). However, in BRCA1-associated breast cancer, NRF2 expression increases, contrary to expectations. This suggests an alternative mechanism of NRF2 activation, possibly mediated by secondary mutations or cross-talk pathways.

Secondary mutations are frequent in BRCA1-mutated cells [42]. Notably, breast cancers with BRCA1 mutations exhibit a high frequency of phosphatase and tensin homolog gene (PTEN) mutations, with loss of PTEN expression observed in 60–90% of cases. PTEN catalyzes the reverse reaction of phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase(PI3K), converting phosphatidylinositol 3,4,5-triphosphate (PIP3) into phosphatidylinositol 4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2), thereby inhibiting PI3K/AKT signaling [43]. Loss of PTEN leads to constitutive PI3K/AKT activation, promoting cell survival, proliferation, and migration [44, 45]. Importantly, NRF2-dependent transcription is enhanced under the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway. Activated PI3K-Akt pathway increases nuclear NRF2 abundance. Interestingly, studies demonstrated that the increased nuclear accumulation of NRF2 is independent of Kelch-like ECH-associated protein 1 (Keap1) deficiency but dependent on the disruption of the PTEN gene [46]. Therefore, in BRCA1-mutated tumors where PTEN inactivation is prevalent, it is plausible that PI3K/AKT-mediated upregulation of NRF2 serves as a compensatory survival mechanism leading to the initiation and development of these cancers [47]. These findings suggest a mechanistic link between BRCA1 mutation, PTEN loss, and aberrant NRF2 activation, highlighting a potentially targetable axis in BRCA1-associated breast cancer.

Emerging evidence suggests that NRF2 may also regulate PTEN expression, creating a feedback loop. For instance, NRF2 activation has been shown to suppress PTEN transcription in certain contexts, further amplifying PI3K/AKT signaling [48]. This bidirectional interaction between NRF2 and PTEN could explain the sustained antioxidant and pro-survival phenotype observed in BRCA1-mutated tumors. Additionally, recent studies highlight the role of NRF2 in metabolic reprogramming, favoring glycolysis and glutamine metabolism, which may provide a selective advantage to BRCA1-deficient cells under oxidative stress [49]. Together, these findings underscore the multifaceted role of NRF2 in promoting tumor cell survival, not only through redox homeostasis but also via transcriptional and metabolic rewiring, particularly in the context of BRCA1 loss and PI3K/AKT hyperactivation.

Our findings suggest that the NRF2/PTEN/PI3K/AKT axis represents a promising therapeutic target in the BRCA1-mutated breast cancer. Given the paradoxical upregulation of NRF2 in these tumors, strategies to modulate NRF2 activity—either by inhibition to counteract its pro-tumorigenic effects or by controlled activation to restore redox balance—warrant further investigation. Recent studies have explored NRF2 inhibitors, including brusatol and ML385, in cancers characterized by NRF2 hyperactivation [50]. Additionally, combining PARP inhibitors with PI3K/AKT pathway inhibitors (e.g., alpelisib or AKT inhibitors like capivasertib) may enhance therapeutic efficacy, particularly in PTEN-deficient BRCA1-mutated tumors [51]. Another potential strategy involves targeting the crosstalk between NRF2 and estrogen signaling. Although our study did not find significant differences in estrogen function between BRCA1-mutated and wild-type tumors, recent evidence suggests that NRF2 can modulate estrogen receptor (ER) activity [52]. Thus, dual targeting of NRF2 and ER pathways may be beneficial in certain subsets of BRCA1-associated breast cancers. Additionally, functional experiments, such as CRISPR-mediated PTEN knockout in BRCA1-deficient models, could clarify the mechanistic link between PTEN loss and NRF2 activation.

Our study has several limitations, primarily the relatively small sample size and reliance on FFPE tissues. Moreover, estrogen function was assessed indirectly via FAK expression rather than through direct measurement of circulating hormone levels. Future studies should involve larger cohorts, integrate serum estrogen measurements at specific menstrual cycle phases, and perform comparative analyses between BRCA1 mutation carriers and healthy individuals to elucidate the role of estrogen in BRCA1-related carcinogenesis. Our study employed a comparative design involving breast cancer patients with and without BRCA1 mutations as well as a comparison between tumor and adjacent non-tumor tissues within each group, focusing on the NRF2 signaling axis. This experimental model also provides a valuable framework for investigating additional molecular interactions and tumorigenic pathways in the context of BRCA1 mutation. Notably, as BRCA1 functions as a predisposing gene rather than an oncogene, our model enables future exploration of co-occurring factors that may synergize with BRCA1 mutations in promoting tumorigenesis.

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to characterize NRF2 expression in BRCA1-mutated breast cancer FFPE tissues. Our findings reveal a significant upregulation of NRF2 in BRCA1-associated tumors, uncovering the “dark” side of this antioxidant regulator and highlighting its dual role in BRCA1-mutated versus sporadic breast cancer. This finding underscores the complex interplay between BRCA1 mutations, NRF2 activation, and oxidative stress responses, suggesting that NRF2 may function as a double-edged sword, potentially promoting tumor survival under genotoxic stress while exacerbating cancer progression. The dysregulation of the NRF2/PTEN/PI3K/AKT axis in BRCA1-deficient tumors presents a compelling therapeutic target. Further mechanistic studies are warranted to dissect the precise molecular pathways driving NRF2 overexpression and its downstream effects. Elucidating these mechanisms could pave the way for novel targeted therapies, offering BRCA1 mutation carriers more effective and personalized treatment strategies for breast and ovarian cancers. Ultimately, our findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the redox biology of BRCA1-associated malignancies and open new avenues for precision oncology and improving outcomes for individuals with high-risk hereditary breast and ovarian cancers.
