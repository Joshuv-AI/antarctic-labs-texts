Sex-Related Differences in Outcomes of Endovascular Treatment of Distal Medium Vessel Occlusion Strokes

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSex-related differences in outcomes after mechanical thrombectomy (MT) for distal medium vessel occlusion (DMVO) stroke remain uncertain. While unadjusted differences have been reported in stroke populations, it is unclear whether biological sex independently influences outcomes after accounting for major prognostic factors.MethodsWe performed a retrospective, multicenter analysis of the MAD-MT registry. Patients with acute DMVO who underwent MT were included. Propensity score matching (PSM) was employed. The primary outcome was functional independence (modified Rankin Scale [mRS] score 0–2) at 90 days. Secondary outcomes included excellent outcome (mRS 0–1), day 1 NIHSS shift, and reperfusion success. Introduction

Stroke is a significant public health problem. In the US, it is the fifth and fourth most common cause of mortality in men and women, respectively, and the leading cause of morbidity in both sexes [1]. Distal medium vessel occlusion (DMVO) constitutes 25% to 40% of all stroke cases [2]. It presents an unresolved challenge in neurointerventional management [3, 4].

Sex-related differences in stroke outcomes are well described in unadjusted populations but are largely driven by differences in age at onset, baseline functional status, and vascular risk profiles rather than biological sex itself. Women experience stroke at an older age on average, and age remains one of the strongest predictors of functional outcome and mortality after acute ischemic stroke [5, 6]. Historical underrepresentation of women in clinical research has further contributed to uncertainty regarding sex-specific treatment effects in cerebrovascular disease [7]. Although female inclusion in stroke studies has improved over time [8], extrapolation of evidence across stroke subtypes and treatment paradigms—particularly in distal medium vessel occlusion (DMVO) stroke—remains limited.

Mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has proven to be highly effective in managing large vessel occlusion (LVO) in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) [9–13]. However, growing research has been conducted on DMVO stroke treatment modalities, safety, and effectiveness [9, 14–16]. Two recently published large-scale randomized controlled trials showed no superior outcome in patients with DMVO treated with MT compared to those receiving standard medical therapy in both sexes [17, 18]. Despite these findings, data regarding sex-related differences in treatment outcomes have been limited [19]. This uncertainty emphasizes the importance of assessing whether biological sex has an independent impact on outcomes during DMVO thrombectomy when significant prognostic variables are effectively controlled.

Whether biological sex independently influences outcomes after mechanical thrombectomy for distal medium vessel occlusion (DMVO) remains unclear, particularly given the non-randomized nature of treatment selection in this population. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to evaluate whether sex is independently associated with functional and safety outcomes following DMVO thrombectomy after controlling for major prognostic confounders using propensity score matching. By isolating the effect of biological sex, this analysis aims to clarify whether sex should be considered a determinant of outcome or treatment selection in patients undergoing endovascular therapy for DMVO stroke.

Methods

Our study is an analysis within the Multicenter Analysis of primary Distal medium vessel occlusions: effect of Mechanical Thrombectomy (MAD-MT) registry [3, 4, 20–41]. This manuscript adheres to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [42].

Ethical Considerations and Study Approval

The institutional review board or local ethical standards committee at each of the 37 participating sites across North America, Asia, and Europe granted approval for this study. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, informed consent was waived. Data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population and Inclusion Criteria

We focused on patients with acute ischemic stroke due to DMVO as previously described by Saver et al. [43]. All DMVO stroke patients who underwent medical management (MM) and/or MT treatment from September 2017 to July 2023 were included. Patients who received intra-arterial thrombolysis alone were excluded from the study.

Data Collection Process

This was a retrospective review of prospectively collected data. Local neurointerventionalists or vascular neurologists assessed angiographic treatment success before forwarding data to the MAD-MT consortium. Each center self-reported the data.

Variables and Data Documentation

We recorded comprehensive baseline clinical and demographic characteristics, including sex, age, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, diabetes mellitus, atrial fibrillation, and smoking status. Hypercholesterolemia was defined as either a documented history of elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels or the use of lipid-lowering medications as documented in patients’ records. Furthermore, we documented the pre-stroke mRS score, the occluded vessel, and NIHSS at presentation. Additionally, patients were stratified into subgroups based on their occlusion location during the initial angiography, differentiating between medium (M2, A1, P1) and distal occlusions (M3, P2, A2, and others) [43].

Additional data included pre-procedural antiplatelet and anticoagulation medication status. Moreover, time from symptom onset to arterial puncture and recanalization, anesthesia type, access site (femoral or radial), and post-MT imaging modalities (CT, MR, or none) were recorded.

Mechanical Thrombectomy and Intravenous Thrombolysis Procedures

Treatment consisted of MT alone or MT + IVT (intravenous thrombolysis). MT access site, the number of passes, and selection of MT device (aspiration and/or stent-retriever) was left to the individual operator’s discretion.

IVT was given per local guidelines, using either Alteplase or Tenecteplase. Alteplase was given at a standard dose of 0.9 mg/kg, with 10% of the total dose delivered as an initial bolus followed by an infusion of the remaining 90% over 60 min. Tenecteplase was administered in a single intravenous bolus at a dose of 0.25 mg/kg, infused over 5–10 s.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was functional independence (mRS 0–2) at 90 days with secondary outcomes including excellent 90-day outcome (mRS 0–1), and day one post admission NIHSS score. Safety outcomes were 90-day mortality, hemorrhagic complications of any type, and symptomatic intracerebral hemorrhage (sICH), defined according to “The Heidelberg Bleeding Classification” [44].

Statistical Analysis

We used descriptive statistics to compare baseline, treatment, and outcome variables between females and males. Continuous variables were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR) and compared between groups using Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were reported as counts plus percentages and compared between groups using chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test as appropriate.

Propensity score matching (PSM) controlled for potential confounders [45, 46]. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression and optimal matching algorithms were employed with a 1:1 ratio. Variables likely to affect the outcomes according to previous studies [47–50] were included in the PSM, which included age, baseline NIHSS, pre-admission mRS scores, history of antiplatelet and anticoagulant medication use, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, atrial fibrillation, occlusion location, IVT administration, and admission model (mothership, drip and ship). Covariate balance post-PSM was assessed using the standardized mean difference, with < 0.1 indicating effective balance. A P-value of 0.05 or less was considered statistically significant. Effect modification by sex on the primary outcome was assessed through subgroup analyses across predefined subgroups, including age (< 65 vs. ≥ 65 years), hypertension, hypercholesterolemia, admission NIHSS, ASPECTS, diabetes, atrial fibrillation, smoking status, and treatment-related factors such as IVT and first-line thrombectomy technique. Interaction terms between sex and these covariates were included in the models to assess potential effect modification, and the statistical significance of interaction effects was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests.

R statistical software (version 4.3.0, R Project for Statistical Computing) and Rstudio statistical software (version 2023.03.0 + 386, Rstudio) were used for statistical analyses and data visualization.

Figure 1 shows the distribution of 90-day mRS scores in males vs females. Overall, 51% of females achieved a 90-day mRS of 0–2 compared to 54% of males (OR 0.9; 95% CI, 0.72–1.13; P = 0.38). In the subgroup analysis, the effect of sex on the primary outcome did not vary significantly across age, hypercholesterolemia, hypertension, ASPECTS, diabetes, or atrial fibrillation (Fig. 2).

Secondary Outcomes

The median total number of passes was 2.00 (IQR, 1.00–3.00) in both females and males (P = 0.084 after matching). The rate of achieving TICI 2c–3 recanalization was similar between females (54%) and males (57%) (P = 0.19), while TICI 2b–3 recanalization was achieved in 86% of females and 89% of males (P = 0.18). First-pass effect was achieved in 30% of females and 31% of males (P = 0.69) (Table 3).

Mortality and Safety Outcomes

The 90-day mortality rate was nominally higher in females (18.4%) compared to males (15.5%), but this difference was not statistically significant (P = 0.11 after matching). Subgroup analysis suggested that among patients without hypertension, females had a higher mortality risk (OR, 2.35; 95% CI, 1.35–4.23; P = 0.003), whereas no significant sex-based difference in mortality was observed in patients with hypertension (OR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.69–1.42; P = 0.95), although the interaction p-value was not significant (P = 0.56). Similarly, among patients without hypercholesterolemia, females had a higher mortality risk (OR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.01–2.16; P = 0.04), while no significant difference was found in those with hypercholesterolemia (OR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.60–1.65; P = 0.98), though the interaction p-value was not significant (P = 0.29). There were no significant differences in mortality based on other grouping variables as shown in Fig. 3.

Rates of sICH were similar between females (8.3%) and males (7.7%) (P = 0.67). Any type of ICH occurred in 36% of females and 35% of males (P = 0.53). Specific subtypes of hemorrhage, including HI1, HI2, PH1, PH2, and SAH, showed no significant differences between groups (all P > 0.1). Embolization in new territories was rare, occurring in 3.5% of females and 2.8% of males (P = 0.46). Vessel perforation (1.3% in both groups; P > 0.99) and artery dissection (1.0% in females and 1.1% in males; P = 0.79) were infrequent in both groups (Table 3).

Discussion

In our multicenter study, 1147 females and 1062 males were included to assess the sex-related differences in the effectiveness and safety of MT in DMVO strokes. After PSM, 748 females and 748 males, there were no significant sex-differences in the primary and secondary outcomes of interest.

In the unmatched cohort, women were older at presentation and had a higher burden of vascular risk factors compared with men. These differences are consistent with well-established sex-related epidemiological patterns in stroke populations and largely reflect differences in age at stroke onset rather than modifiable factors or treatment-related disparities. Accordingly, the less favorable unadjusted outcomes observed in women should be interpreted as descriptive of real-world patient characteristics rather than evidence of an independent effect of biological sex.

In the propensity score–matched cohort, arterial puncture-to-recanalization was modestly longer in women. Given the small effect size, the absence of this difference in the unmatched cohort, and the lack of accompanying differences in procedural characteristics, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and may reflect a chance observation rather than a clinically meaningful sex-related effect.

Previous studies, including a Chinese retrospective study, conducted on 289 men and 289 women after PSM, showed overall similar clinical outcomes between men and women with anterior circulation LVO [51, 52]. Moreover, a subgroup analysis of the ANGEL-ASPECT trial showed that the treatment effect of MT did not differ significantly between both sexes, with better outcomes observed in patients with MT compared to those treated medically [53]. These results align with a systematic review of 51 studies (4,316,668 AIS patients) that demonstrated no sex differences in MT effectiveness or functional outcomes [19]. Moreover, an analysis of SWIFT, STAR, and SWIFTPRIME cohorts [54] and the SOLSTICE registry [55] showed the same results. This is contrary to MR CLEAN [56], which indicated that men had better clinical outcomes, all of these mentioned study using multivariable logistic regression.

On the other hand, two studies on basilar artery occlusion strokes reported different findings. A multicenter study across five countries using multivariate logistic regression found comparable and favorable functional outcomes in females [57, 58]. In contrast, a study of 21 Chinese stroke centers after PSM suggested that MT in females was associated with worse outcomes [59]. However, despite differences in vessel location, all previous studies focused on LVOs rather than DMVOs. A summary of adjusted and unadjusted evidence from prior EVT studies [19, 51, 54–56, 59–62] is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest study to evaluate sex-specific outcomes following MT for DMVO. Our primary finding provides robust evidence that biological sex itself should not be used as a selection criterion for DMVO thrombectomy or trial enrollment. This directly informs clinical practice by affirming that treatment decisions must be based solely on clinical and imaging factors.

Although our primary analyses relied on dichotomized functional outcomes, prior work suggests that evaluating the full modified Rankin Scale spectrum may reveal more nuanced recovery patterns, including potential sex-specific prognostic markers that are not captured by binary endpoints alone [63]. Moreover, improving learning curves and increasing operator experience with distal thrombectomy techniques may further influence complication rates and technical success over time, potentially attenuating early variability in outcomes [64].

Our study has several limitations. First, as a retrospective, nonrandomized analysis, it is subject to selection bias and residual confounding despite the use of propensity score matching. Second, while the large, multicenter design enhances generalizability, the study is primarily confirmatory and may not detect subtle sex-specific effects beyond established prognostic factors such as age and baseline functional status. Third, multiple outcomes were evaluated without formal adjustment for multiple testing; therefore, secondary findings should be interpreted cautiously. Finally, center-specific practice patterns and incomplete capture of relevant variables may have influenced the observed results.

Conclusion

After adjustment for age, baseline disability, and vascular risk factors, no sex-related differences were observed in functional or safety outcomes following endovascular treatment of DMVO strokes. Biological sex alone does not appear to influence outcome after DMVO thrombectomy.
