L-Theanine ameliorates high-protein diet-associated hepatic glucose and lipid metabolic alterations by promoting fatty acid β-oxidation and suppressing lipid synthesis and gluconeogenesis in rats
Authors: Sha Liu, Lanlan Li, Kai Yao, Lin He, Yong Yuan, Yueping Tan, Feiyan Yin, Zhihua Gong, Wenjun Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHigh-protein diets (HPDs) have the potential to reduce body weight, yet excessive protein intake can induce hepatic metabolic dysregulation and insulin resistance. L-theanine (LTA), a unique amino acid abundantly present in tea leaves, regulates protein metabolism under HPD conditions. However, its influence on glucose and lipid metabolism remains unclear. In this study, Sprague-Dawley rats were fed either a standard maintenance diet (20% of energy from protein) or HPDs containing 30%, 40%, or 50% of energy from protein, and were administered LTA at different doses (0, 100, 200, or 400 mg kg−1·body weight−1) for 40 days. A combination of physiological and biochemical assessments, metabolomics, proteomics, and Western blot techniques was used to investigate the regulatory effects and mechanisms of LTA on glucose and lipid metabolism under HPD conditions. The 30% HPD did not cause notable metabolic changes, whereas the 40% and 50% HPDs led to hyperinsulinemia and hepatic lipid accumulation by enhancing gluconeogenesis and fatty acid synthesis while inhibiting fatty acid β-oxidation. LTA alleviated insulin elevation, hepatic steatosis, and lipid droplet accumulation caused by the 40% and 50% HPDs. Mechanistically, LTA enhanced peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) expression and increased acyl-CoA dehydrogenase medium chain (ACADM) expression to promote mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation, while decreasing acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACACA) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1 (PCK1) expression, thereby suppressing lipid synthesis and gluconeogenesis. These findings highlight the potential of LTA to mitigate HPD-associated metabolic alterations, supporting its application as a functional food ingredient for metabolic health management.Keywords: L-theanine, High-protein diet, Liver metabolism, Fatty acid β-oxidation, Lipid synthesis, Gluconeogenesis

Introduction

Glucose, lipids, and proteins are three fundamental nutrients that maintain energy homeostasis and support vital physiological activities. Glucose metabolism is the core pathway for cellular energy supply, primarily through glycolysis, gluconeogenesis, and glycogen metabolism. Glycolysis catalyzes the breakdown of glucose into pyruvate and generates adenosine triphosphate (ATP) via substrate-level phosphorylation to meet the immediate energy demands of cells (Han et al., 2016). Under nutrient deprivation, gluconeogenesis and glycogenolysis generate glucose to sustain energy supply (Agius, 2015; Shah and Wondisford, 2023). Lipid metabolism is the principal pathway for long-term energy storage, including de novo fatty acid (FA) synthesis in the cytoplasm and FA β-oxidation in the mitochondria. Protein metabolism contributes to carbon skeletons from amino acids participating in glucose and lipid metabolic processes, resulting in a highly interconnected metabolic network. Furthermore, several key regulators, such as mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), sterol regulatory element-binding protein (SREBP), and peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα), play critical regulatory roles in nutrient metabolism (Goul et al., 2023; Liao et al., 2024; Nguyen et al., 2021). Glucose, lipid, and protein metabolism pathways, together with their transcriptional regulatory networks, collectively maintain energy homeostasis; alterations in dietary composition can directly disrupt this balance, leading to metabolic disorders.

High-protein diets (HPDs) have attracted considerable attention due to their low carbohydrate content, strong satiety effect, and weight-reduction potential. According to evaluations by the Institute of Medicine (IOM) and the Food and Agriculture Organization/World Health Organization, the recommended protein intake for adults is approximately 0.8 g kg−1 body weight (BW)−1 per day, corresponding to approximately 15% of total energy intake (Protein and amino, 2007; Rand et al., 2003). The IOM further suggests an acceptable macronutrient distribution range for protein of 10%–35% of total energy. Therefore, when the protein contribution to dietary energy exceeds 30%, and even reaches 40% or higher, it is typically regarded as an HPD. HPDs may promote weight loss, reduce lipid accumulation, and improve blood lipid profiles in patients with type 2 diabetes (Yu et al., 2020). However, recent research suggests that excessive protein intake may adversely affect metabolic health in otherwise healthy individuals. For example, an HPD providing 46% of energy from protein increases serum total amino acids and elevates cardiovascular risk (Zhang et al., 2020). HPDs with protein exceeding 40% of energy can also increase serum ammonia and blood urea nitrogen levels, causing protein metabolic disorders and impairing liver and kidney function (Lin et al., 2023). Moreover, prolonged excessive protein intake may impair insulin action and increase the risk of type 2 diabetes. Therefore, maintaining metabolic balance under HPD conditions is critical for health.

Natural bioactive compounds are attracting increasing attention as safe and effective modulators of metabolic homeostasis. L-theanine (LTA), a non-protein-derived amino acid found in tea (Yu et al., 2025), exhibits multiple biological activities with minimal side effects, making it widely applicable in the food industry (Zhang et al., 2021). Under a normal diet, LTA can modulate glucose, lipid, and protein metabolism through adenosine 5′-monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and its downstream signaling pathways (Lin et al., 2020); under a high-fat diet, LTA intervention improves glucose and lipid metabolic disorders, suppressing hepatic steatosis and inflammation (Du et al., 2024). Additionally, LTA ameliorates protein metabolism under HPD conditions (Lin et al., 2023). However, it remains unclear whether LTA regulates glucose and lipid metabolism under HPD conditions.

Therefore, in this study, Sprague-Dawley rats were fed diets with varying protein energy concentrations (20, 30, 40, and 50%) and treated with different doses of LTA (0, 100, 200, and 400 mg kg−1) to investigate the effects of LTA on glucose and lipid metabolism under HPDs. Furthermore, combined metabolomic and proteomic analyses, supplemented with Western blot validation, were used to reveal the potential mechanisms by which LTA ameliorates HPD-induced glucose and lipid metabolic disorders at the multi-omics level. This study provides direct theoretical and scientific support for the development of LTA as a functional food or nutritional strategy to mitigate metabolic risks associated with HPD.

Materials and methods

Animals and diets

One hundred twenty-eight 6-week-old male specific pathogen-free Sprague-Dawley rats were obtained from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China).

A standard maintenance diet with a protein-energy contribution of 20% and three high-protein diets with protein-energy contributions of 30, 40, and 50% were purchased from Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). The protein levels in the diets were selected based on previous studies (Lin et al., 2023). Detailed diet compositions are provided in Table S1. The energy density of the maintenance diet was 3.42 kcal/g (Qu et al., 2022), whereas that of the three high-protein diets, estimated from their composition using Atwater factors, was approximately 3.81 kcal/g (Merrill and Watt, 1973).

LTA (purity ≥98%) was purchased from Hunan Sunfull Bio-tech Co., Ltd. (Hunan, China).

Animal grouping and experimental design

All rats were housed in a controlled environment (24 ± 2 °C, 55 ± 5% relative humidity, 12-h light–dark cycle). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (NIH Publication No. 85-23, revised 1985) and complied with the Animal Research: Reporting of In Vivo Experiments guidelines. The experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the Biomedical Research Committee of Hunan Agricultural University (Ethics Approval No. 2020032).

After a 1-week acclimation period, the rats were randomly assigned to 16 groups, with eight rats per group. Grouping was based on the protein content of the diets, including a standard maintenance diet (20% protein, P20) and high-protein diets containing 30%, 40%, or 50% protein (HP30, HP40, and HP50, respectively). Within each protein level, rats were further subdivided into four LTA treatment subgroups according to LTA gavage doses of 0, 100 (L100), 200 (L200), or 400 (L400) mg·kg−1·BW−1. These dosages were selected based on previous studies (J. Williams et al., 2019; J. L. Williams et al., 2020). The groups were designated as follows (Fig. 1A):

1)P20: P20, P20+L100, P20+L200, P20+L400.

P20: P20, P20+L100, P20+L200, P20+L400.

2)HP30: HP30, HP30+L100, HP30+L200, HP30+L400.

HP30: HP30, HP30+L100, HP30+L200, HP30+L400.

3)HP40: HP40, HP40+L100, HP40+L200, HP40+L400.

HP40: HP40, HP40+L100, HP40+L200, HP40+L400.

4)HP50: HP50, HP50+L100, HP50+L200, HP50+L400.

HP50: HP50, HP50+L100, HP50+L200, HP50+L400.

LTA was administered by gavage based on body weight. In the 0 mg kg−1·BW−1 groups (P20, HP30, HP40, and HP50), an equal volume of distilled water was administered. Oral gavage was performed daily on the rats for 40 consecutive days. Body weight was measured every other day, and the remaining diet was weighed at a fixed time each day with additional diet provided as needed.

Sample collection and processing

After 40 days of treatment, the rats were fasted for 12 h. On day 41, the rats were anesthetized with 2% pentobarbital sodium. Blood samples were obtained from the abdominal aorta and collected into 10 mL vacuum tubes. After incubation at room temperature (∼24 ± 2 °C) for 30 min, blood samples were centrifuged at 3500 r·min−1 for 10 min. The supernatant was aliquoted into 1-mL sterile Eppendorf tubes and stored at −80 °C for further analysis. Liver tissues were collected and immediately stored at −80 °C. For omics analyses, liver samples meeting predefined quality criteria, including complete records, sufficient tissue amount, correct labeling, and good preservation, were selected prior to data acquisition. Sample selection was independent of omics results and phenotypic trends. Six biological replicates per group were used for liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS)-based untargeted metabolomics, and three biological replicates per group were used for tandem mass tag (TMT)-based proteomics, with each replicate representing liver tissue from an individual rat.

Histological analysis of liver tissue

The right lobe of the liver was sampled from the same anatomical region of each rat and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h. The tissues were embedded in paraffin and sectioned for Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining, and frozen sections were prepared for Oil Red O staining. Oil Red O-positive area was quantified using ImageJ under the same analysis settings for all groups, and the values were normalized to the mean value of the P20 group.

Determination of serum lipid, glucose, insulin, and hepatic glycogen levels

The concentrations of triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and non-esterified FA (NEFA) in rat blood were measured according to the kit instructions from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Fasting blood glucose (FBG), blood insulin (INS), and hepatic glycogen were determined according to the kit manuals from ZCIBIO Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as previously described (Matthews et al., 1985).

Assessment of hepatic metabolic enzymes related to glucose and lipid pathways

The levels of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK), glycogen phosphorylase a (GPa), and pyruvate kinase (PK) were measured according to the kit instructions from ZCIBIO Technology. Acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) levels were determined following the kit instructions from Shanghai Enzyme-linked Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The levels of farnesyl-diphosphate farnesyltransferase 1 (FDFT1) were measured according to the kit manuals from Coibo Bio Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

LC-MS-based untargeted metabolomics

LC–MS-based untargeted metabolomics was performed according to previously described methods with minor modifications (Chi et al., 2021). Liver tissue (100 mg) was mixed with 500 μL of 80% methanol containing 0.1% formic acid, followed by vortexing, ice-bath incubation for 5 min, and centrifugation at 15,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was diluted to a methanol content of 53%, followed by a second centrifugation, and the resulting filtrate was used for LC–MS analysis. Quality control (QC) samples were prepared by mixing equal volumes of all experimental samples. Prior to sample analysis, pooled QC injections were used to monitor instrument performance and equilibrate the LC-MS system. During the analytical run, one QC injection was inserted after every six study samples for quality assessment. Blank samples consisted of 53% methanol containing 0.1% formic acid and were subjected to the same pretreatment process as the experimental samples. Chromatography was performed using a Hypersil GOLD C18 column at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.2 mL/min. In the positive ion mode, mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid in water and mobile phase B was methanol; in the negative ion mode, mobile phase A was 5 mM ammonium acetate buffer (pH 9.0) and mobile phase B was methanol. Mass spectrometry signals were acquired in positive- and negative-ion scanning modes with an m/z range of 70–1050. Raw data were imported into Compound Discoverer 3.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for data preprocessing, molecular formula prediction, and database matching using mzCloud, mzVault, and MassList. Blank samples were used to remove the background signals, and only compounds with a coefficient of variation <30% in QC samples were retained for further analysis (Dunn et al., 2011). Principal component analysis (PCA), orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA), volcano plot analysis, and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis were performed using OmicShare (www.omicshare.com/tools).

TMT-based proteomics analysis

TMT-based proteomic analysis was performed according to previously described methods with minor modifications (Gong et al., 2022). Liver proteins were extracted using a urea-based lysis buffer containing 8 M urea (Bio-Rad), supplemented with complete EDTA-free protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche). Protein concentrations were determined using the Bradford assay. For sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (4–20%), 20 μg of protein from each sample was loaded. After quantification, 100 μg of protein per sample was digested using the filter-aided sample preparation method. The resulting peptides were labeled with Tandem Mass Tag reagents, dried using centrifugation, resuspended in 100 μL of mobile phase A, and centrifuged before use. Column efficiency was tested using 400 μg of digested bovine serum albumin (BSA) (column temperature 45 °C, detection wavelength 214 nm), followed by loading the prepared samples for high-pH reverse-phase chromatography. Separated fractions were resuspended in 20 μL of 2% methanol/0.1% formic acid and centrifuged, and 10 μL of supernatant was analyzed using a Q Exactive HF mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher). The instrument was operated in data-dependent acquisition mode with a full-scan resolution of 120,000, scan range of 300–1400 m/z, MS/MS resolution of 45,000, and normalized collision energy of 30%. Raw MS data were processed using Proteome Discoverer 2.1 (Thermo Fisher) with the Sequest HT algorithm, searching against the UniProt_Rat database. Trypsin was used as the cleavage enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Proteins were filtered to retain only those with false discovery rate <1% to ensure identification reliability. Quality control analysis confirmed high data quality (Fig. S1), which served as the basis for the differential protein analysis. Heatmap and Sankey diagram were plotted, and gene ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment as well as Spearman analysis were performed using OmicShare (www.omicshare.com/tools).

Western blotting

Proteins were extracted from 30 mg of liver tissue using a total protein extraction kit (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), which contains lysis buffer, phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride, protease inhibitors, and phosphatase inhibitors, according to the manufacturer's instructions. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid protein assay kit (Sangon Biotech). Samples were normalized to 2 μg/μL, mixed with 5× reducing loading buffer (Servicebio), denatured at 100 °C for 10 min. Subsequently, 8 μL of each sample (16 μg protein per lane) was separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk or 5% BSA in Tris-buffered saline with Tween-20 for 1 h at room temperature, depending on the antibody used, then incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with the corresponding horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Antibodies against β-tubulin (1:20,000), PPARα (1:1000), acetyl-CoA carboxylase alpha (ACACA; 1:5000), acyl-CoA dehydrogenase medium chain (ACADM; 1:5000), fatty acid-binding protein 5 (FABP5; 1:5000), cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36; 1:4000), phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase 1 (PCK1; 1:4000), pyruvate kinase M (PKM; 1:2000), and glycogen synthase 2 (GYS2; 1:2000) were purchased from Proteintech Group, Inc. (Wuhan, China). Antibodies against β-actin (1:2000) and FDFT1 (1:2000) were purchased from Sangon Biotech. Goat-anti-mouse IgG-HRP (1:1000) and goat-anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (1:1000) were obtained from Cell Signaling Technology, Inc. (Danvers, MA, USA). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagent (Sangon Biotech) on X-ray film in a darkroom. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ 1.54p.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 18.0. Data were subjected to variance analysis and tested for homogeneity of variance. If variances were equal, group differences were compared using the least significant difference test; if variances were unequal, Dunnett's T3 test was applied. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05. Results were presented as means ± standard deviation. Graphs were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.

Results

Effects of LTA on growth performance and glucose homeostasis in HPD rats

Compared with the P20 group, daily food intake significantly reduced in the HP30, HP40, and HP50 groups (p < 0.05, Fig. 1C). However, after recalculation based on dietary energy density, the estimated total calorie intake was not significantly different (Table S2). In addition, body weight gain was significantly decreased in the HP40 group compared with the P20 group (p < 0.05, Fig. 1D), whereas compared with the HP40 group, body weight gain was significantly increased in the HP40+L200 and HP40+L400 groups (p < 0.05).

FBG levels were significantly decreased in HP40 and HP50 compared with P20 (p < 0.05, Fig. 1E), whereas INS levels and HOMA-IR were significantly elevated (p < 0.05, Fig. 1F and G). LTA intervention at L200 and L400 on the HP40 background significantly increased FBG (p < 0.05), whereas INS levels and HOMA-IR were significantly reduced in HP40+L400 (p < 0.05). H&E staining showed attenuated steatotic vacuolar changes following LTA treatment in the HP40 and HP50 groups (Fig. 1B).

Additionally, hepatic glycogen was significantly decreased in HP40 compared with P20 (p < 0.05, Fig. 1H). PK levels significantly decreased in HP40 and HP50 (p < 0.01, Fig. 1I), whereas PEPCK and GPa levels significantly increased (p < 0.01, Fig. 1J and K). Following L400 treatment, hepatic glycogen significantly increased (p < 0.05), and GPa and PEPCK levels significantly decreased in HP40+L400 (p < 0.05). PEPCK levels also significantly reduced in HP50+L400 (p < 0.05). These results suggest that high-dose LTA attenuated HP40/HP50-associated changes in hepatic glycogen metabolism, gluconeogenic enzyme activity, and insulin-related parameters.

Compared with HP40 and HP50, HP30 showed differences in several indicators of glucose homeostasis. INS levels were significantly lower (p < 0.05), and PEPCK and GPa levels were significantly reduced (p < 0.05) in HP30 than in HP40 and HP50. Hepatic glycogen levels in HP30 were significantly higher than those in HP40 (p < 0.05).

Effects of LTA on lipid metabolism in HPD rats

The lipid content was further examined. Compared with P20, the relative hepatic lipid droplet area significantly increased in HP40 and HP50 (p < 0.05, Fig. 2A and B). Serum TG levels were significantly higher in HP40 and HP50 (p < 0.05, Fig. 2C), while NEFA levels were significantly increased in HP40 (p < 0.05, Fig. 2D). HP40+L400 significantly reduced serum TG and NEFA levels compared with HP40 (p < 0.05). Serum TC significantly increased in HP30 (p < 0.05, Fig. 2E) but decreased in HP40 (p < 0.05) compared with P20. LTA treatment significantly reduced TC in HP30+LTA (p < 0.05).

The levels of key hepatic lipid-metabolizing enzymes were measured. ACC levels significantly higher in HP40 and HP50 than in P20 and HP30 (p < 0.05, Fig. 2F). FDFT1 levels significantly increased in HP30 (p < 0.05) but decreased in HP40 (p < 0.05, Fig. 2G) relative to P20. LTA intervention significantly reduced ACC levels compared with HP40 and HP50 (p < 0.05). FDFT1 levels significantly increased in HP40+L400 compared with HP40 (p < 0.05).

Overall, HP40 and HP50 were associated with increased hepatic lipid droplet accumulation, elevated serum TG and NEFA levels, and changes in lipid-metabolizing enzymes, whereas LTA intervention attenuated FA synthesis and partially restored lipid metabolic balance, with 400 mg kg−1·BW−1 LTA exhibiting the most pronounced effect. Based on the above serum and hepatic measurements, hepatic metabolites and proteins were further analyzed in P20, P20+L400, HP40, and HP40+L400.

Effects of LTA on key metabolites in HPD-induced glucose and lipid metabolic alterations

The high Pearson correlation coefficients among QC samples in both positive and negative modes indicated good analytical reproducibility and data reliability (Fig. S2). PCA showed a clear separation between P20 and HP40, indicating that the HPD significantly altered the hepatic metabolite profile, whereas L400 intervention markedly influenced metabolite composition under the HPD (Fig. 3A and B).

Differential metabolites among the groups were identified based on OPLS-DA analysis (Fig. S3). Compared with P20, relative abundances of FA esters of hydroxy FA (FAHFA) (14:1/16:3) and FAHFA (18:0/20:2) significantly increased, whereas relative abundances of quercetin, 2-oxobutyric acid, D(−)-threose, acrylic acid, S-lactoylglutathione, glucose 1-phosphate, acetylcarnitine, taurochenodeoxycholate, carnosine, and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate significantly decreased in the P20+L400 group (p < 0.05, Fig. 3C). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these differential metabolites were mainly involved in pathways related to energy metabolism and signaling, glucose metabolism, glucose-lipid crosstalk, and lipid metabolism (Fig. 3D and E).

Compared with P20, HP40 exhibited disrupted hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism, characterized by a significant decrease in nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADP+), D-(+)-mannose, D-(+)-maltose, D-(−)-fructose, UDP-N-acetylglucosamine, and inositol relative abundances, suggesting enhanced gluconeogenesis and impaired insulin signaling. Meanwhile, the relative abundances of lauric acid, oleoyl ethanolamide, arachidonoyl ethanolamide (AEA), FAHFA, lysophosphatidylglycerol (LPG) 16:1, and steroid hormones were significantly increased, whereas conjugated bile acids were decreased, suggesting alterations in bile acid metabolism and cholesterol-related pathways (p < 0.05, Fig. 3F). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that these differential metabolites were mainly enriched in glycerophospholipid metabolism, FA biosynthesis, bile secretion, cholesterol metabolism, galactose metabolism, and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism pathways (Fig. 3G and H). HP40 likely promotes carbon flux toward gluconeogenesis and FA synthesis, accompanied by impaired insulin signaling.

Compared with HP40, HP40+L400 significantly decreased the relative abundances of lysophosphatidylinositol (LPI) 18:2, LPI 20:3, lysophosphatidylcholine (LPC) 18:2, LPC 20:3, glutathione (GSH), 2-oxobutyric acid, N-acetylthreonine, and deoxyribose 5-phosphate, while significantly increasing the relative abundances of palmitoleic acid, acylcarnitine (ACar) 18:0, lysophosphatidylethanolamine (LPE) 18:0, D-pantothenic acid, and stearamide (p < 0.05, Fig. 3I). The differential metabolites were mainly enriched in amino acid and glucose metabolic pathways, including cysteine and methionine metabolism, valine, leucine and isoleucine biosynthesis, C5-branched dibasic acid metabolism, glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, as well as lipid metabolism pathways including FA biosynthesis, bile secretion, cholesterol metabolism, and primary bile acid biosynthesis (Fig. 3 K). The KEGG pathway network is shown in Fig. 3J. These results indicate that LTA suppressed de novo FA synthesis, modulated bile acid-related metabolic pathways, promoted lipid clearance, and improved antioxidant capacity, thereby regulating energy metabolism and improving HP40-induced hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism disorders.

Effects of LTA on key proteins in HPD-induced glucose and lipid metabolic alterations

PCA revealed a clear separation between P20 and HP40 groups, and L400 intervention induced significant differences in the protein profiles between HP40+L400 and HP40 groups (Fig. S4).

Compared with P20 group, P20+L400 group exhibited significantly decreased expression of CD36, FABP5, and LPIN1, and significantly increased expression of ACADM, carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A), PCK1, and PKM (p < 0.05, Fig. 4A). Differentially expressed proteins were mainly enriched in organic acid and carboxylic acid metabolic processes involving metabolic intermediates, such as pyruvate, short-chain FAs, and acetyl-CoA (Fig. 4B). KEGG enrichment of proteins corresponded with that of metabolomics results (Figs. 3E and 4C), indicating that LTA intervention promoted FA utilization and regulated glucose-lipid energy homeostasis under the standard maintenance diet.

Differentially expressed proteins were analyzed between P20 + L400 vs. P20 (A–C), HP40 vs. P20 (D–F), and HP40 + L400 vs. HP40 (G–I), presented as heatmaps (A, D, G), GO terms (B, E, H), and KEGG pathway enrichment (C, F, I). Compared with P20, HP40 significantly upregulated proteins related to FA transport and synthesis, including CD36, FABP5, and ACACA; proteins involved in the non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) pathway, including ubiquinol-cytochrome c reductase hinge protein (UQCRH) and Ras-related C3 botulinum toxin substrate 1 (RAC1); and proteins associated with the insulin signaling pathway, including protein phosphatase 1 (PP1) and mTOR. In contrast, HP40 significantly downregulated ACADM, GYS2, and PKM (p < 0.05, Fig. 4D). KEGG analysis showed enrichment in FA metabolism, glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, PPAR signaling pathway, insulin signaling pathway, and NAFLD (Fig. 4F). These results are consistent with the metabolomics data, confirming that HPD promotes lipid synthesis and gluconeogenesis, suppresses FA β-oxidation and glycolysis, and disrupts hepatic glucose-lipid metabolic balance.

Compared with HP40 group, HP40+L400 group showed significant downregulation of CD36, FABP5, ACACA, PCK1, PP1, FDFT1, and UQCRH, and significant upregulation of ACADM, PKM, and GYS2 (p < 0.05, Fig. 4G). Integrating the GO and KEGG enrichment results (Fig. 4H and I), LTA intervention was found to suppress HPD-induced lipid synthesis, lipid droplet accumulation, and gluconeogenesis, and promote FA oxidation and glycolysis, thereby improving HPD-induced hepatic glucose and lipid metabolic disorders.

LTA activates PPARα to promote FA β-oxidation and ameliorate hyperinsulinemia and gluconeogenesis abnormalities

To further investigate how LTA regulates glucose and lipid metabolism under HPD through the protein-metabolite-physiological network, Spearman's correlation analysis was performed, retaining only the results with p < 0.05. Significant correlations were observed among protein expression, metabolite abundance, and physiological indicators (p < 0.05, Fig. 5A).

Focusing on the key transcription factor, PPARα, which was enriched in both metabolomics and proteomics analyses, Western blot validation was performed. Compared with HP40 group, PPARα protein expression significantly upregulated in HP40+L400 group (p < 0.05, Fig. 5B). Additionally, the hepatic expression patterns of key glucose- and lipid-metabolism-related proteins, including CD36, FABP5, ACADM, ACACA, FDFT1, PCK1, GYS2, and PKM, were consistent with proteomic data (p < 0.05, Fig. 5C–J).

Integration of the Sankey diagram metabolic flux with protein and metabolite pathway enrichment revealed that HPD increased proteins involved in gluconeogenesis (PCK1) and elevated insulin and HOMA-IR levels. Meanwhile, proteins associated with FA uptake and synthesis, such as CD36, FABP5, and ACACA, were upregulated, accompanied by increased lipid accumulation. L-theanine intervention reduced hyperinsulinemia, suppressed FA synthesis and gluconeogenesis, and promoted FA β-oxidation, as reflected by changes in the β-oxidation-related enzyme ACADM and related metabolites (Fig. 5K).

Discussion

HPD is favored by consumers for its weight loss–promoting effects, primarily due to the strong satiety effect of protein and its high dietary thermogenesis (Magkos, 2020). In the present study, the energy density of the maintenance diet was 3.42 kcal/g, whereas that of the three high-protein diets was approximately 3.81 kcal/g, indicating that the dietary energy densities were within a relatively close range. Therefore, the main dietary difference among the HPD groups was the redistribution of macronutrient energy. As fat energy was kept essentially constant, the increase in protein-derived energy was primarily balanced by a reduction in carbohydrate energy. This protein–carbohydrate energy redistribution is consistent with previous nutritional intervention studies in which high-protein dietary patterns were achieved by increasing the protein-energy ratio while reducing the carbohydrate-energy ratio under controlled or isoenergetic conditions (Vu et al., 2017; Zhong et al., 2022). Thus, the metabolic effects observed in the present study should be interpreted in the context of the high-protein dietary formulation used here. In this study, a protein-to-energy ratio of 30% did not induce significant lipid abnormalities or hyperinsulinemia. Although FDFT1 levels and TC increased in HP30 group, these changes were not accompanied by elevated TG or NEFA levels, and no hepatic lipid degeneration or lipid droplet accumulation was observed, indicating that HP30 did not cause significant metabolic changes. Previous studies have also shown that HPDs with a protein energy ratio of 20%–35% exert beneficial effects in short-term human trials (Magkos, 2020). Therefore, from a health management perspective, a moderate protein level (30%) may be a more reasonable choice for HPD. In contrast, when the protein energy ratio reaches 40% or 50%, marked glucose and lipid metabolic alterations (hereafter, “HPD” refers specifically to 40% and 50% protein levels). Based on previous studies showing the beneficial regulation of LTA on protein metabolism under HPD (Lin et al., 2023), and the finding that LTA more comprehensively reversed metabolic alterations in HP40 than in HP50, this study selected the HP40 model to further investigate the effects and mechanisms of LTA on glucose and lipid metabolism.

Previously, we confirmed that under normal dietary conditions, LTA exerted no adverse effects on hepatic metabolism and instead showed potential protective effects on metabolic homeostasis. LTA supplementation did not significantly alter serum glucose, lipid levels, or hepatic glycogen content. However, integrated proteomic and metabolomic analyses revealed subtle yet coordinated metabolic adaptations, including the upregulation of endogenous signaling lipids FAHFA (14:1/16:3) and FAHFA (18:0/20:2). FAHFAs have been reported to enhance insulin sensitivity and promote exogenous glucose uptake (Saadati et al., 2025), which is consistent with the observed reductions in glucose-1-phosphate, 2-oxobutyric acid, and acrylic acid, collectively suggesting an enhanced glycolytic flux. In parallel, decreased levels of S-lactoylglutathione and carnosine indicated an attenuated redox metabolic burden. Notably, these metabolic alterations were accompanied by enhanced AMPK signaling following LTA supplementation, in line with previously reported AMPK-mediated metabolic regulation (Cordani et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2020). These findings indicate that LTA modulates hepatic glucose and redox metabolism without disrupting systemic metabolic balance under normal dietary conditions. Having established the metabolic safety and homeostatic effects of LTA under normal dietary conditions, we therefore focused subsequent analyses on its regulatory role in glucose and lipid metabolism under HPD conditions.

LTA effectively ameliorates lipid metabolic disorders under HPD conditions. Specifically, LTA promotes hepatic FA β-oxidation under HPD. In the context of HPD, upregulation of glycerophospholipids and FAHFA lipids, combined with increased serum NEFA, elevated ACC levels, and hepatic lipid droplet accumulation, was observed, consistent with previous reports (Charidemou et al., 2019). Protein KEGG enrichment indicated alterations in insulin signaling and NAFLD pathways, suggesting that chronic excessive HPD intake may be associated with insulin-signaling disturbances and NAFLD-related hepatic metabolic alterations. Previous studies have shown that insulin resistance underlies NAFLD pathogenesis because it promotes hepatic TG synthesis and accumulation under elevated glycerol and NEFA conditions (Chao et al., 2019). Restoration of FA β-oxidation has recently been considered a strategy to prevent NAFLD, regulated by enzymes, transporters, and transcription factors, including ACADM and PPARα (Zhu et al., 2022). Differential proteins enriched in the NAFLD pathway in the HP40 group mainly include UQCRH and RAC1, both of which are closely related to reactive oxygen species (ROS) levels. Notably, UQCRH is a major source of mitochondrial ROS, and its upregulation may lead to mitochondrial dysfunction (F. Gao et al., 2016). Considering that mitochondria are crucial for FA β-oxidation, these results align with the observed downregulation of ACADM protein in HP40, suggesting that HP40 may inhibit FA β-oxidation by inducing mitochondrial impairment.

In contrast, LTA intervention reversed these metabolic alterations. Our data indicate that this effect is largely mediated through activation of PPARα. Previous studies have demonstrated that PPARα promotes FA oxidation, improves mitochondrial function, and alleviates oxidative stress (Deng et al., 2025; Samuel and Shulman, 2018; Sun et al., 2021; Xu et al., 2021). Consistent with these findings, LTA downregulated the ROS-related proteins UQCRH and RAC1, which are known markers of mitochondrial stress (F. Gao et al., 2016), while also upregulating ACADM, a direct PPARα target gene critical for FA oxidation. These protein-level changes were further supported by metabolic evidence, including elevated levels of ACar, which is commonly used as an indicator related to mitochondrial FA oxidation (Arora et al., 2025). In addition, LTA increased the abundance of palmitoleic acid, a lipid species reported to stimulate FA oxidation in a PPARα-dependent manner and inhibit hepatic lipogenesis (Bolsoni-Lopes et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2024). This is consistent with the observed reductions in serum TG, NEFA, and hepatic lipid droplets after LTA treatment. These findings suggest that activation of the PPARα-mediated FA β-oxidation pathway represents a key mechanism by which LTA alleviates HPD-induced lipid metabolic disturbances.

Beyond FA metabolism, LTA also influenced cholesterol-related metabolism responses under HPD conditions. Changes in serum TC, together with alterations in FDFT1 and cholesterol and bile acid-related metabolic pathways, supported a role for LTA in cholesterol-related metabolic regulation. An interesting dissociation was observed in the HP40 group: the key cholesterol synthesis enzyme FDFT1 was upregulated, yet TC levels decreased. This contradicts reports of a positive correlation between FDFT1 and TC (Cai et al., 2025). LTA supplementation partially restored TC levels. This dissociation may be related to the altered hepatic metabolic environment under HPD stress, such as changes in the availability of cofactors (e.g., NADPH) required for cholesterogenesis (Bonora et al., 2024). Consistently, LTA partially restored the NADP+/NADPH redox balance.

LTA inhibits hepatic gluconeogenesis under HPD conditions. Unlike the classical insulin-promoted anabolic mode under normal dietary conditions (Jones, 2016), HPDs restrict carbohydrate intake. Under these conditions, intensified amino acid catabolism imposes substantial energetic demands, promoting hepatic glycogen mobilization to maintain metabolic homeostasis. Amino acids also serve as major substrates for gluconeogenesis, providing suitable conditions for its facilitation. In our study, HPD markedly reduced hepatic glycogen content, accompanied by decreased PK levels and increased levels of PEPCK and GPa. In the HP40 group, the expression of PCK1 was upregulated, whereas GYS2 expression was downregulated. These changes collectively suggest enhanced gluconeogenesis and accelerated hepatic glycogen depletion under HPD conditions. After LTA intervention, the levels of GPa and PEPCK were significantly reduced, accompanied by downregulation of PCK1 and upregulation of GYS2, with a concomitant restoration of hepatic glycogen content. These findings indicate that LTA suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis while promoting glycogen synthesis. The metabolite results further indicated increased lactate and pyruvate levels and decreased 2-oxobutyric acid, suggesting enhanced glycolytic activity. Previous studies have established that PPARα activation suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis (Gao et al., 2022; Huang et al., 2024) and can modulate glycolytic gene expression (Wan et al., 2018). Critically, Yang et al. (2024) showed that PPARα activation correlated with the downregulation of key gluconeogenic enzymes, and that this effect was significantly attenuated upon PPARα knockdown. Consistently, in the present study, multi-omics analysis and Western blot validation identified PPARα as a key upregulated factor in response to LTA treatment. Our findings suggest that LTA-mediated PPARα activation may be associated with the suppression of gluconeogenesis and the promotion of glycolysis under HPD conditions.

Despite these findings, several aspects warrant further investigation. The present study was conducted using an in vivo rat model, which provides valuable insight into the systemic metabolic effects of LTA under HPD conditions. However, whether these regulatory effects occur directly in hepatocytes or involve additional cell types within the hepatic microenvironment requires further clarification. Future studies using in vitro cellular models may help to further dissect the underlying molecular mechanisms. Although the metabolomics and proteomics analyses provided useful pathway-level information, larger omics cohorts, particularly for proteomics, and targeted validation of additional candidate proteins are needed to further confirm the pathway-level changes and strengthen the robustness of the proteomic findings. In addition, although multi-omics analyses together with Western blot validation consistently indicated the involvement of the PPARα pathway, additional studies employing pharmacological or genetic approaches (e.g., PPARα inhibition or knockout models) would help to further confirm the mechanistic role of this pathway. These investigations will provide deeper insight into the molecular basis of LTA-mediated metabolic regulation under high-protein dietary conditions.

Conclusion

A diet providing 30% of energy from protein did not induce notable hepatic metabolic alterations, whereas higher protein levels (40%–50% of energy) promoted hepatic gluconeogenesis and FA synthesis while suppressing FA β-oxidation, leading to hepatic lipid accumulation and hyperinsulinemia. LTA supplementation alleviated these HPD-associated metabolic alterations in a dose-dependent manner. Integrated proteomic and metabolomic analyses, along with biochemical assays, indicated that LTA enhanced PPARα expression and increased ACADM expression to promote mitochondrial FA β-oxidation, while suppressing lipid synthesis and gluconeogenesis pathways. Collectively, these findings suggest that LTA improves glucose and lipid metabolic homeostasis under high-protein dietary conditions and highlights its potential as a nutritional strategy to mitigate HPD-associated metabolic alterations.
