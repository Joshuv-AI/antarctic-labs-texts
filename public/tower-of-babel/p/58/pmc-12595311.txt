Comparative analysis of antioxidant and antimicrobial potentials of different extracts of Turraea vogelii leaves
Authors: Olorunfemi R. Molehin, Oluwasegun S. Dauda, Oluwakemi V. Adeleke, Joy F. David, Testimony O. Oso, Anne A. Adeyanju
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Turraea vogelii Hook.f. is a plant valued for its medicinal properties. The aim of this study is to investigate the comparative analysis of antioxidant and antimicrobial potentials of different extracts of Turrea vogelii Hook.f. leaves. The fresh leaves of the plant were harvested, air-dried, ground, and extracted with three solvents based on polarity (Dichloromethane (DCM), Ethyl Acetate, and n-hexane) via a maceration procedure. The antioxidant and antibacterial potential of these compounds was tested to assess their efficacy. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) and Gas Chromatography Mass Spectrometry (GC-MS) were used to analyse the functional groups and organic compounds present in the drug materials, respectively. Antioxidant screening identified flavonoids (24.63 ± 0.47, 19.96 ± 0.17, 21.04 ± 0.04), phenols (20.18 ± 0.4, 17.61 ± 0.11, 8.65 ± 0.15) in 100μg/mL of the DCM, ethyl acetate, and n-hexane crude extracts, respectively. The antibacterial evaluation showed that the DCM extract demonstrated potent activity, with minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values as low as 3.125 µg/mL and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) values ranging from 6.25 µg/mL to 50 µg/mL against pathogens such as Escherichia coli ATCC 35218, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 2921, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700303, and Salmonella typhi ATCC14028. FTIR analysis identified several functional groups in the extract, including hydroxyls, alkanes, amides, ethers, and amines, indicative of diverse bioactive compounds, and GC-MS identified key compounds like squalene and Hexanoic acid. The results highlight the potential of plant leaves to possess notable antioxidants and antibacterial properties, supporting their traditional use in folk medicine and paving the way for the development of novel antibacterial agents.

Introduction

Turraea vogelii is a plant commonly known as the "forest mahogany" or "wild apricot. It belongs to the family Meliaceae. It is a scandent shrub or woody climber which can grow up to 5 m high and is widely distributed in evergreen forests and their edges. The plant is mainly found in tropical Africa, Asia, Australia, and Madagascar [1]. In Nigeria, it is commonly known as Aha Omode in Yoruba. Ethno-botanically, it is used to treat stomach pains, eczema, treat snake bites, and intestinal worms. The leaves, roots, and bark are used for urogenital infections, intestinal complaints, and as a decoction for wounds and skin disease [2]. Turraea vogelii leaves possess a diverse range of bioactive compounds that contribute to its medicinal properties, such as alkaloids, flavonoids, triterpenoids, glycosides, steroids, phenolics, and essential oils, which possess anti-inflammatory activity, cytotoxic and antiplasmodial activity, antibacterial properties, and potent antioxidant properties [3].

Solvent extracts from the leaves of Turraea vogelii contain varying concentrations of active compounds such as tannins, alkaloids, flavonoids, terpenoids, anthraquinones, and steroids. These compounds, which contribute to the pharmacological benefits of the plant, have been revealed to be more concentrated in the methanolic extract with a higher yield for alkaloids [2]. They possess quality antioxidant activity, as evidenced by in vitro studies against DPPH radicals as well as high catalase activity. The extracts are effective against clinical isolates of Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhi, and Proteus mirabilis with good MIC and MBC values. Above all, they have shown promising properties as anti-inflammatory, antiproliferative, pain-relieving, and gastroprotective agents [2], [4]. Hence, this study aims to determine the antioxidant properties; antibacterial activities of different extracts of Turraea vogelii leaves

Materials and methods

Sample collection

Fresh leaves of Turraea vogelii were collected from their natural habitats in Forestry Research Institute of Nigeria (FRIN), Ibadan, Oyo state, Nigeria. Identification and authentication were carried out and, the voucher specimen number FHI-114017 was assigned. Primarily, the plants were identified by their local name (Aha omode).

Preparation of solvent extracts

Approximately 400 g of each of the powdered plant materials was soaked in a liter of analytical grade of n-hexane, methanol and ethyl acetate in a 2-liter capacity conical flask. The flasks containing each plant material were shaken regularly, corked, and left to stand for 48 h at room temperature. In each case, the menstruum was separated by filtration through Whatman filter paper No. 1. The filtrates were then concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 50°C and later in a hot-air oven at 35°C to dry completely. The concentrates were put in airtight containers and stored at 4°C prior to analysis.

Chemicals, reagents, and instruments

Distilled water, Folin-Ciocalteu's reagent, ethyl acetate, 7 % sodium carbonate solution, spectrophotometer, 5 % NaNO2 solution, aluminum chloride (AlCl3), 1 M NaOH solution, hot-air oven, Sodium EDTA (Na2EDTA), FeSO4 (0.125 M), ferrozine (0.3125 mM), 300 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10.0 mM tripyridyl triazine (TPTZ) solution, 20.0 mM FeCl3.6H2O solution, standard ascorbic acid, phosphate buffer (pH 6.6), 0.1 % potassium hexacyanoferrate [K3Fe(CN)6], water bath, 10 % trichloroacetic acid (TCA), 0.01 % FeCl3 solution, 0.3 mM DPPH ethanolic solution, Sterilized Mueller Hinton Agar, petri dishes, cork borer, ciprofloxacin, Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), p-iodonitrotetrazolium violet (p-INT) solution, nutrient agar (NA), potassium bromide (KBr).

Determination of total phenols

The total phenol estimation was carried out employing the Folin-Ciocalteu method [5]. Distilled water and Folin-Ciocalteu's reagent were added to 125 μl of the extract. After a 6-minute incubation period, 7 % sodium carbonate solution was introduced. Following a 90-minute incubation, absorbance was measured at 760 nm, and the outcome was quantified as gallic acid equivalents (GAE).

Determination of flavonoids

Roughly 0.25 g of the extract was dissolved in 1 mL of distilled water. To this solution, 5 % NaNO2 solution, 0.150 mL freshly prepared aluminum chloride (AlCl3), and 1 M NaOH solutions were added. After a 5-minute incubation period, absorbance was recorded at 510 nm using a SpectrumLab70 spectrophotometer. The outcome was reported as Ascorbic Acid equivalent (QE).

Determination of antioxidant properties

Nitric oxide (NO) scavenging activity

At physiological pH, sodium nitroprusside in an aqueous solution spontaneously generates nitric oxide (NO), which then reacts with oxygen to yield nitrite ions. The presence of nitrite ions can be quantified using the Griess Illosvosy reaction. Compounds that scavenge NO compete with oxygen, resulting in a decreased production of NO and the formation of a pink-colored chromophore. The absorbance of these solutions was assessed at 540 nm against their respective blank solutions. The percentage inhibition was calculated as follows:

NO scavenging activity (%) = {(A0 –A1) /A0} × 100

Where A0 is the absorbance of the control, and A1 is the absorbance of the sample.

Metal (Ferrous Ion) chelating activity

The ferrous ion chelating activity was assessed following the method of Ebrahimzadeh [6]. This involved evaluating the extracts' capability to chelate ferrous ions (Fe2 +). Various concentrations were prepared, and 1 mL of each concentration was mixed with 1 mL of FeSO4 (0.125 M) and 1 mL of ferrozine (0.3125 mM), followed by vigorous shaking. After a 10-minute incubation period at room temperature, the solution mixture was measured using a spectrophotometer at 562 nm against a blank sample containing the same components, except the extracts were replaced with 1 mL of distilled water. The blank underwent incubation under identical conditions as the test samples. Sodium EDTA (Na2EDTA) served as the control. The percentage inhibition of ferrozine (Fe2+) by the extracts was calculated using the equation:

Inhibition of ferrozine (%) = 100(Ac – As)/Ac

Where: Ac = Absorbance of control and As = Absorbance of sample

Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) Assay

The FRAP reagent was prepared by combining 300 mM sodium acetate buffer (pH 3.6), 10.0 mM tripyridyl triazine (TPTZ) solution, and 20.0 mM FeCl3.6H2O solution in a volumetric ratio of 10:1:1. To assess the Fe3+ reducing power of the plant extract, the method described by Oyaizu [7] was employed with slight modifications. Various concentrations (50, 100, 150, 200, and 250 µg/mL) of the sample (0.5 mL) and standard ascorbic acid were mixed with 0.5 mL phosphate buffer (pH 6.6) and 0.5 mL of 0.1 % potassium hexacyanoferrate [K3Fe (CN)6], followed by incubation at 50°C in a water bath for 20 min. Subsequently, 0.5 mL of 10 % trichloroacetic acid (TCA) was added to halt the reaction. The upper portion of the solution (1 mL) was combined with 1 mL of distilled water, and 0.1 mL of 0.01 % FeCl3 solution was introduced. After a 10-minute incubation at room temperature, the absorbance was measured at 700 nm against an appropriate blank solution. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3), and higher absorbance of the reaction mixture indicated greater reducing power. Ascorbic acid served as the positive control, and the reducing power of the extract exhibited linear proportionality to the sample concentration. Phosphate buffer (pH 6.6) was employed as the blank solution.

2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) scavenging assay

DPPH assessment was carried out following the protocols described by Pissochi [8]. The alteration in color within the reaction mixture was monitored at 517 nm. Briefly, 1 mL aliquot of 0.3 mM DPPH ethanolic solution was combined with 2.5 mL of various concentrations of ethanolic stem bark extract (ranging from 50, 100, 150, 200, to 250 µg/mL) and standard ascorbic acid. This mixture was then incubated at room temperature under dark conditions. After 30 min, absorbance was measured at 517 nm. Ethanol served as the blank solution, while a mixture of 1 mL 0.3 mM DPPH solution and 2.5 mL ethanol acted as the negative control. The extent of decolorization of DPPH from purple to yellow indicated the scavenging efficiency of the tested extract. All experiments were conducted in triplicate (n = 3), and the average values were calculated. Additionally, half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) values were determined. Lower absorbance of the reaction mixtures indicates higher free radical scavenging activity. The percentage inhibition of DPPH free radical scavenging activity was calculated using the following equation.

DPPH radical scavenging activity (%) = [(Abs control – Abs sample)]/(Abs control) × 100

Where Abs control is the absorbance of DPPH radicals + methanol; Abs sample is the absorbance of DPPH radical + sample or standard.

Antimicrobial susceptibility of Turraea vogelii leaves

Collection of organisms

Six isolates were used in this research. All the strains were collected, characterized and identified at the Department of Pharmaceutical Microbiology, University of Ibadan, Ibadan, Oyo state, Nigeria. These include Staphylococcus aureus 29213, Escherichia coli 25922, Escherichia coli 35218, Pseudomonas aeruginosa 27853, Klebsiella pneumoniae 700303 and Salmonella typhi 14028.

Preparation of Turraea vogelii leaf extract

500 g of dried plant material was extracted using 1 L of n-hexane, dichloromethane and ethyl acetate following standard procedures. 1 g of the extract was diluted into 10 mL of DMSO4 to get a 100 mg/mL of stock.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test (AST) of the organisms

Sterilized Mueller Hinton Agar (20 mL) from Oxoid was aseptically dispensed into sterile Petri dishes. After allowing the agar to set for approximately 10 min, the dishes were dried in a hot air oven to remove moisture from the covers and the agar surface. Using the spread plate method, a 10−5 dilution of an 18-hour broth culture, adjusted to the McFarland standard, was spread on the agar surface with a sterile cotton swab. A flamed and cooled 6 mm diameter cork borer was then used to create wells in the inoculated agar. Pre-prepared solutions of different concentrations of plant extract (100 mg/mL, 50 mg/mL, and 25 mg/mL) were added to these wells and labeled accordingly. Ciprofloxacin (10 mg/mL) served as the positive control and was placed in one of the wells. Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO4) was used as a negative control to confirm it had no activity against the organisms. The plates were left for about an hour to allow proper diffusion of the extracts, then incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The diameters of the inhibition zones were measured in millimeters.

Determination of minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC)

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) was determined using the broth microdilution method with 96-well plates. Extract samples were dissolved in double-strength Tryptone Soya Broth (MERK) to create a solution of 50 mg/mL. This solution was serially diluted in a sterile 96-well plate to achieve concentrations of 25 mg/mL, 12.5 mg/mL, 6.25 mg/mL, 3.125 mg/mL, and 1.563 mg/mL. Ciprofloxacin (10 µg/mL) was used as the reference drug for the antibacterial assay, and it was also diluted to obtain concentrations of 10 µg/mL, 5 µg/mL, 2.5 µg/mL, 1.25 µg/mL, 0.625 µg/mL, and 0.1325 µg/mL for the antibacterial assay. After checking for growth or turbidity in the test plates to determine the MIC, 10 µl of 0.2 mg/mL p-iodonitrotetrazolium violet (p-INT) solution was added to the wells. The plates were then incubated further at 37°C for 30 min. The color change from yellow to pinkish red indicated bacterial growth.

Determination of minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC)

Each well of the microplate was inoculated with 10 µL of the microorganism and incubated at 37°C for 24 h. The lowest concentrations that showed no growth or turbidity after incubation were streaked onto nutrient agar (NA). The lowest concentration that showed no trace of growth was recorded as the minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC).

FTIR sample preparation

The sample was mixed with a solvent commonly potassium bromide with a mixing ratio of 1–100. Then, the mixed powder is pressed in a dye at a load of 10 tons to form a pellet of 13 mm. The pellet is then inserted in the FTIR chamber for analysis. It should be mentioned that potassium bromide is usually used as a reference material because it does not interfere in the infrared window from 400 to 4000 cm−1. The pellet was washed three times with 10 mL of de-ionized water to get rid of the free proteins/enzymes that are not capping the samples. The samples were dried and ground with potassium bromide (KBr) pellets and analyzed. Also, it acts as a carrier for the infrared spectrum. Furthermore, the reference material of potassium bromide will be used to plot the transmittance axis in the FTIR graph. The data in FITR spectroscopy is given by the transmittance versus the wavelength. Zero transmittance means that the sample absorbed all the radiation while 100 % transmittance means that the sample absorbed the same amounts of radiation as the reference. The generated graph is unique for each molecule, and this can be used in identifying the functional groups. All FTIR spectra were corrected against the FTIR spectrum of air as background. In addition, FTIR was recorded in five replications as absorbance values at each data point. FTIR analysis was used as a qualitative technique for analyzing the functional groups of materials. This analysis was used to obtain the spectrum pattern from the sample.

Instrumentation and methodology of GC-MS analysis

Chemical fingerprinting of the samples was done through a Varian 3800/4000 gas chromatograph mass spectrometer GC-MS (Agilent Technologies, USA) equipped with an HP-5 (30 m × 0.25 mm) silica-based cross-linked column. The injector and detector temperatures were set at 300 °C. The temperature was increased from 50 °C to 100 °C with a ramp rate of 10 °C min−1. The temperature was held at 100 °C for 120 s and was consequently increased to 250 °C by heating the system at a rate of 5 °C min−1. After holding the temperature at 250 °C for 2 min, the temperature was elevated to 300 °C with a ramp rate of 3 °C min−1 and was maintained at 300 °C for 15 min. A sample volume of 1 μL was injected in the splitless mode. Nitrogen was used as a gas carrier at the rate of 1 mL min−1. MS was scanned from 30 to 400 amu at 1.562 u s−1. MS source and quadrupole temperatures were maintained at 150 °C and 230 °C, respectively. Calibration curves were prepared through multiple dilution 20 element multi-elemental total petroleum hydrocarbons (TPH) standard (Supelco, USA).

Statistical analysis

All the calculations were carried out using Microsoft Excel, 2013. All the assays were replicated thrice, and the result were denoted as mean ± standard deviation (SD).

Given the high flavonoid content, both the n-Hexane and Ethyl Acetate extracts are expected to have strong antimicrobial activity. The non-polar nature of these extracts enhances the solubility of flavonoids that target bacterial and fungal membranes, making them effective against a broad range of microorganisms. Studies by [11] have shown that flavonoid-rich extracts from plants can effectively inhibit bacterial growth, including strains such as Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli. While the DCM extract has slightly lower flavonoid content, it could still possess moderate antimicrobial effects due to the presence of certain bioactive flavonoids. These compounds interfere with microbial growth through a range of mechanisms, including inhibition of nucleic acid synthesis and enzyme disruption [12].

Phenolic compounds are another class of bioactive molecules that exhibit both antioxidant and antimicrobial properties. These compounds are characterized by their hydroxyl groups, which play a critical role in neutralizing free radicals and targeting microbial structures [13]. The DCM extract shows the highest phenolic content at 300 µg/mL (43.65 mg GAE/g), followed closely by Ethyl Acetate (42.42 mg GAE/g) and n-Hexane (33.68 mg GAE/g). The antimicrobial activity of phenolics often works synergistically with flavonoids, enhancing the overall bioactivity of the extract. This synergistic effect likely contributes to the broad antimicrobial potential of the DCM extract, especially in inhibiting bacterial strains such as Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [14]. The Ethyl Acetate and n-Hexane extracts, while lower in phenolics compared to DCM, are still expected to exhibit moderate antimicrobial activity due to the presence of phenolic acids. Based on the flavonoid and phenolic content in Turraea vogelii extracts, the n-Hexane extract may provide the most significant antimicrobial benefits based on its high flavonoid content, while the DCM extract could offer the strongest antimicrobial properties due to its high phenolic content. In Table 3, the inhibitory effect of the Turraea vogelii extracts significantly varied (P < 0.05) across different bacterial isolates. The extracts demonstrated the most potent antimicrobial activity against Klebsiella pneumoniae with an inhibition zone of 16.7 mm at 100 mg/mL. The effectiveness was moderate against Staphylococcus aureus (14.3 mm) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (12.3 mm). The extracts showed limited activity against Salmonella typhi (10.3 mm) and minimal inhibition for Escherichia coli strains, with zones of 5.3 mm and 6.7 mm, respectively. Based on the sensitivity of the test organisms, the effectiveness of the extracts can be ranked as follows: Klebsiella pneumoniae > Staphylococcus aureus > Pseudomonas aeruginosa > Salmonella typhi > Escherichia coli strains. These ranking highlights that Turraea vogelii extracts are most effective against Klebsiella pneumoniae and less effective against Escherichia coli strains. The antimicrobial susceptibility test of the ethyl acetate extract of Turraea vogelii leaves demonstrated varying inhibitory potentials against selected bacterial strains. The extract showed remarkable inhibitory zones against Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29213, Escherichia coli ATCC 35218, and Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700303, while Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, and Salmonella typhi ATCC 14028 displayed little to no sensitivity at the tested concentrations. At 100 mg/mL, the highest activity was recorded against Escherichia coli 35218 (18 ± 1.63 mm) and Staphylococcus aureus (16 ± 1.63 mm), which were comparable to ciprofloxacin, the standard drug used as control. The sensitivity of Staphylococcus aureus indicates that the extract may possess compounds targeting Gram-positive cell wall structures, a feature often associated with secondary metabolites like flavonoids, tannins, and alkaloids known to disrupt peptidoglycan integrity. The inhibition observed in Escherichia coli 35218 but absent in Escherichia coli 25922 suggests strain-specific susceptibility, which may be attributed to differences in resistance genes or efflux mechanisms. Similarly, the moderate inhibition of Klebsiella pneumoniae across concentrations (14.67 ± 0.94 mm at 100 mg/mL) shows that the extract can exert broad-spectrum activity, although less potent than ciprofloxacin.

These results align with previous reports on the antimicrobial properties of Meliaceae family plants, where extracts rich in limonoids, flavonoids, and terpenoids showed selective antibacterial activities [15]. For example, extracts of Azadirachta indica [16] and Trichilia emetica, [17] both members of the same family, have been reported to inhibit Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli strains, with variable effects on Pseudomonas species. This similarity suggests that the antimicrobial activity observed in Turraea vogelii may be attributed to related phytochemical constituents.

Generally, the results highlight that the ethyl acetate fraction of Turraea vogelii leaves demonstrates selective antibacterial activity with notable efficacy against Staphylococcus aureus Escherichia coli 35218, and Klebsiella pneumonia, while Gram-negative organisms like Pseudomonas aeruginosa and S. typhi were resistant. This indicates that the plant extract may serve as a potential source of antibacterial agents, particularly in the management of infections caused by Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, but its spectrum of activity remains limited compared to standard antibiotics. The n-hexane extract of Turraea vogelii leaves demonstrated selective antimicrobial effects, showing moderate inhibition against Staphylococcus aureus (13.0 ± 0.82 mm at 100 mg/mL) and Klebsiella pneumoniae (14.0 ± 0.82 mm at 100 mg/mL). Some degree of inhibition was also observed against Pseudomonas aeruginosa at higher concentrations, while Escherichia coli (both strains) and Salmonella typhi were completely resistant across all concentrations tested. Compared with ciprofloxacin, the zones of inhibition produced by the n-hexane fraction were significantly lower, highlighting its relatively weak antibacterial potency.

The observed activity suggests that non-polar phytochemicals extracted by n-hexane, such as terpenoids, steroids, and fatty acid derivatives, may be responsible for the inhibition of Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae These classes of compounds are often associated with membrane disruption and lipophilic interactions with bacterial cell envelopes, which can compromise cellular integrity. The inability of the plant extract to inhibit Escherichia coli and Salmonella typhi indicates that its active components may not effectively penetrate or act upon the complex outer membrane structures characteristic of these Gram-negative bacteria. Overall, the results show that while the n-hexane extract has some antimicrobial activity, particularly against Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae, its effects are less pronounced than those of the ethyl acetate fraction. This indicates that polar phytochemicals likely play a greater role in the antibacterial potential of Turraea vogelii, while non-polar constituents may contribute modestly or in a strain-specific manner.

The main activity against Staphylococcus aureus reinforces the view that Gram-positive organisms are generally more sensitive to plant-derived secondary metabolites due to the absence of a protective outer membrane [18]. The inhibition of Escherichia coli 35218 but not Escherichia coli 25922 again reflects strain-dependent variability, which may be linked to genetic resistance determinants. The sensitivity of Pseudomonas aeruginosa in this assay contrasts with the absence of activity in the zone of inhibition test, which may be due to differences in extract diffusion on solid agar compared to broth microdilution assays. Overall, these results suggest that the ethyl acetate fraction of Turraea vogelii has strong bacteriostatic and bactericidal potential against selected pathogens, particularly Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli 35218, supporting its role as a source of antimicrobial compounds. The n-hexane extract of Turraea vogelii leaves showed measurable antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, with MIC values ranging from 1.56 to 3.12 µg/mL and corresponding MBC values as low as 0.78 µg/mL. These values indicate that the extract not only inhibited growth but was also bactericidal at relatively low concentrations, particularly against Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae. In contrast, Escherichia coli (both strains) and Salmonella typhi displayed complete resistance, with MIC and MBC values exceeding 50 µg/mL. This lack of activity highlights the limitation of the n-hexane fraction against certain Gram-negative bacteria, likely due to their rigid outer membrane and efflux pumps that reduce the penetration of non-polar compounds. The MIC and MBC results confirm that non-polar compounds in the n-hexane extract possess bactericidal properties, especially against Staphylococcus aureus and Klebsiella pneumoniae However, compared with ciprofloxacin, the activity remains weaker and more selective, showing that while the extract has potential, its efficacy is highly dependent on bacterial type and assay conditions.

Conclusion

The antioxidant and antimicrobial potential of Turraea vogelii leaf extracts were demonstrated across the dichloromethane, ethyl acetate, and n-hexane extracts. The presence of bioactive compounds, such as n-Hexadecanoic acid, 9-Octadecenoic acid, and squalene, contributed significantly to the observed activities. The dichloromethane extract exhibited strong antioxidant activity, while the n-hexane extract showed notable antimicrobial effects, particularly against Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa highlighting the therapeutic potential of Turraea vogelii as a source of natural antioxidants and antimicrobial agents.
