Imparting the core competencies of a scientific scholar through a week-long focused course: a pre-post study in Swiss undergraduate medical students
Authors: Peter Brauchli, Arnold von Eckardstein, Gerhard Rogler, Vartan Kurtcuoglu, Jasmin Anita Lal, Sonja Metzger, Marco Riguzzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCompetency-based education is the prevailing standard in most medical schools. The ‘scholar’ is one of seven roles proposed by CanMeds. Its competencies are the basis for evidence-based medicine (EBM) and the translation of scientific evidence into practice. However, it is often underrepresented in medical curricula, and it faces challenges from societal discourse. This study evaluates the effectiveness of a new week-long Biomedical Research Module for second-year medical students.MethodsBased on the Translational Research Model and by using different didactic tools, the module was developed at the Medical Faculty of the University of Zurich in German-speaking Switzerland to enable students to participate in generating and translating scientific medical knowledge, independently evaluate the quality of research, and adopt a scientific attitude toward knowledge creation following the 5 A-cycle of EBM. A 14-item self-assessment rating questionnaire was developed reflecting the learning goals (plus four items on career planning) and completed by two independent student cohorts in 2023 and 2024. Using a repeated one-group pretest-posttest design, changes in students’ competencies and attitudes were analysed by inferential statistical methods.ResultsData from 303 to 350 students were analysed. Changes between baseline and module completion were robust. The participants’ understanding of the principles of the process of medical knowledge generation and their competency to participate in it, their competency to independently evaluate the quality of research, and their competency to handle uncertainties in medical knowledge increased strongly (Cohen’s threshold, p < 0.01). While students’ perception of the importance of the basic principles of EBM was already at a high level at baseline, improvements in their scientific attitude were modest, and science-scepticism remained in a relevant portion of students.ConclusionsThe Biomedical Research Module is effective in improving self-assessed competencies that are necessary for the application of EBM and the scholar’s role according to the 5-A cycle in second-year students. However, certain active skills require further training, such as statistics and development of a research plan. Furthermore, a minority perpetuated certain sceptical attitudes towards the scientific process. Alternative approaches to changing such attitudes require further research.Trial registrationThis is not a clinical trial. The responsible ethics committee waived the need for approval (BASEC-Nr. Background

Competency-based education has emerged as the prevailing standard for medical student education. One of the available frameworks is CanMEDS [1]. The ‘scholar’ is one of its seven roles. As scholars, physicians are expected to demonstrate a lifelong commitment to excellence in practice through continuous learning, teaching others, evaluating evidence, and contributing to scholarship. They are expected to cultivate and maintain the capacity for critical thinking towards the scientific progress in the field of medicine and health. These competencies are considered prerequisites for the application of evidence-based medicine (EBM).

A growing body of evidence suggests that medical students may not be acquiring the fundamental competencies necessary to fulfil the role of a scholar [2–4]. As a result, they may draw incorrect conclusions from preclinical models, observational studies, and anecdotal or historical experience that do not allow the differentiation between cause, effect, and confounding/concomitant variables. Thus, they may make false generalisations [5]. This poses a significant risk of misdiagnosis and inappropriate treatment. It also hinders the implementation of new EBM in practice.

Despite the need for clinical research to adhere to rigorous standards, the scientific literature is inundated with studies that are methodologically deficient [6, 7], and flawed data [8–10] are perpetuated in secondary and tertiary literature. The competency to read primary literature and to independently assess the quality of published studies is therefore an indispensable part of the scholar’s role, enabling an accurate assessment of validity and relevance. Even when high-quality research results are published and lead to the approval of interventional and diagnostic procedures, their implementation into practice is often ill-considered, resulting in overtreatment or undertreatment [11]. In particular, the importance of overuse in diagnostics [12] and treatment [13, 14] is enormous today. To halt and reverse this trend, it is imperative that medical students receive comprehensive education and training in how to conduct meticulous literature searches and critically appraise published research for specific clinical scenarios. This is essential to avoid the pitfalls of overdiagnosis and overtreatment.

There are also social and societal challenges that require adjustments in the competencies of the scholar’s role. Critical or ambivalent attitudes towards science and medicine, and even scepticism, are nowadays widespread among both health professionals and the general public [15]. This was particularly evident during the COVID-19 pandemic [16]. The spread of misinformation and disinformation has intensified [17], particularly through social media [18]. Physicians therefore need to understand how scientific evidence is generated and translated into medical practice. They should be aware of the strengths and limitations of scientific methods to identify misinformation in their medical practice [19] and communicate with their patients accordingly [20]. Moreover, critical thinking is essential for physicians to transfer their knowledge to patients, correct misconceptions, and dispel prejudices and exaggerated expectations to ensure the provision of appropriate care.

While many medical faculties have introduced the role of the scholar to their curriculum some time ago [21, 22], the challenges outlined above require a dedicated prioritisation. The Medical Faculty of the University of Zurich has therefore revised its curriculum for the education of medical students and anchored biomedical research as one of four pillars [23]. In the first year, mandatory modules establish basic knowledge in biostatistics, epidemiological and clinical research, ethics and on how to search scientific literature. In the second year, a week-long ‘Biomedical Research Module’ has been newly introduced.

The learning objectives and programme of our Biomedical Research Module were derived from a number of position papers [24, 25] and published programmes [26–29]. A modified version of PROFILES [30] - the Swiss national catalogue of learning objectives based on CanMEDS - was used as the foundation for delineating the learning goals:

Understanding and competency to participate in the process of generating and translating medical knowledge.

Understanding and competency to participate in the process of generating and translating medical knowledge.

Competency to independently evaluate the quality of knowledge generated by research and reference it for medical issues.

Competency to independently evaluate the quality of knowledge generated by research and reference it for medical issues.

Adoption of a scientific attitude defined as caring about evidence and being willing to change theories and practice on the basis of new evidence [31] and applying the 5 A-cycle of EBM [32].

Adoption of a scientific attitude defined as caring about evidence and being willing to change theories and practice on the basis of new evidence [31] and applying the 5 A-cycle of EBM [32].

The programme for the five half-day sessions comprising 20 h of lecture was structured according to the translational research model [33]. The majority of the lectures were conducted in German, some in English. Teaching formats included traditional lectures, panel discussion, blended learning sessions with interactive discussions, team-based learning activities, and case vignettes. A comprehensive description of the course content and didactic tools can be found in Supplementary file 1.

Methods

Aim

The aim of this study was to investigate whether the Biomedical Research Module leads to improvements in second-year medical students that align with the module’s learning objectives.

Study design

A one-group pretest-posttest design [34] was applied to two independent cohorts. A questionnaire was developed for medical students to assess their competencies in and attitudes towards science/EBM before and after the Biomedical Research Module in their second year of medical school. This approach was applied to second-year medical students (2023 and 2024), relating to two implementations of the Biomedical Research Module, to show whether there was a consistent improvement across cohorts/years.

Participants

All second-year medical students from the years 2023 and 2024 (independent cohorts) were asked to complete the first survey (T0) within one week before the beginning of the module and the subsequent survey (T1) within two weeks following the conclusion of the module. Completion of these questionnaires was mandatory to obtain certification of the compulsory module. Students were anonymised by codes that allowed the pairwise assignment of each student’s responses at T0 and T1 but did not allow identification of individual students.

Questionnaire

In accordance with the three learning goals, a 14-item self-assessment rating questionnaire was developed, reflecting:

Understanding of and competency to participate in the process of generating and translating medical knowledge (items 1–5).

Understanding of and competency to participate in the process of generating and translating medical knowledge (items 1–5).

Competency to evaluate the quality of research results (items 6–7).

Competency to evaluate the quality of research results (items 6–7).

Adoption of a scientific attitude towards knowledge (items 8–10) and the competency to deal with uncertainty (item 11).

Adoption of a scientific attitude towards knowledge (items 8–10) and the competency to deal with uncertainty (item 11).

Adoption of a scientific attitude towards the type of evidence required to inform medical decisions (items 12–14).

Adoption of a scientific attitude towards the type of evidence required to inform medical decisions (items 12–14).

The questionnaire also included questions on the pursuit of a scientific career (items 15–18). The items were rated on an integer scale from 0 to 5, with the extremes labelled ‘do not agree at all’ and ‘fully agree’, respectively. Each rating question also offered the option ‘I cannot answer’. The questionnaire was identical at T0 and T1 except for three questions on previous experience in medical research (yes/no), age (years), gender (f/m/d), which were added at T0.

The questionnaire was developed specifically for this study and was administered in German. It was piloted with 160 medical students in 2022.

Hypotheses

It was hypothesised that the rating of items 1–8, 10–11 and 14, would significantly increase from T0 to T1, and that the rating of items 9, 12 and 13 would decrease.

Statistical analysis

The T0 and T1 surveys were compared using paired data from students who participated at both time points. Pre-post differences in central tendency were tested using the non-parametric Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test [35]. The rank-biserial correlation was calculated to measure the effect size and compared with thresholds for a ‘small’ (0.1), ‘medium’ (0.3), and ‘strong’ (0.5) effect based on Cohen’s work on correlation coefficients [36]. Pre-post differences in the proportion of responses ≥ 3 (upper half of the integer scale, ‘positive assessment’) were tested using a 2-times-2 matched McNemar -test [37]. For the comparison of baseline responses (T0) between 2023 and 2024, the two-sample unpaired t-test with bootstrapping of the test statistic was used for age, the -squared test was used for gender and previous research experience, and the Mann-Whitney U-test [38] was used for ratings 1 through 14. Results with a type one error probability p < 0.01 (**) were considered statistically significant, and an effect of at least ‘strong’ size was considered a relevant improvement. Students with a missing value (or who selected the option ‘cannot answer’) for at least one of the two time points were excluded from the analysis of the item in question. No adjustment was made for multiple testing.

Results

In 2023 and 2024, respectively, 342 out of 345 (99%) and 384 out of 384 (100%) eligible students participated in at least one of the two survey time windows (T0, T1), and 303 and 350 students (88% and 91%) participated in both (which is the analysed sample). No evidence of a significant difference in the two cohorts’ characteristics was found. Table 2 compares the answers to the 18 self-assessment questions at baseline (T0) between the cohorts of 2023 and 2024. There was no significant disparity in the frequency distributions for any question as defined by the critical α = 0.01.

Prior to the start of the module (T0), 72%/74% (cohorts of 2023/2024) of students gave a positive assessment (≥ 3 on the integer scale) of their understanding of the principles of medical knowledge creation (Table 3, item 1). This item increased strongly (effect size ≥ 0.5) in both cohorts, with the descriptive mean (median) increasing by 0.8/0.9 (from 3 to 4), leading to a proportion of 97%/95% at T1. At baseline (T0), between 26 and 58% of the students expressed confidence in their competencies to participate in the process of medical knowledge generation (≥ 3 on the integer scale): defining a research question (Table 3, item 2) (29%/26%), having the statistical expertise necessary to evaluate the quality and relevance of clinical studies (item 3) (31%/33%), comprehending the significance of meta-analyses and systematic reviews (item 4) (37%/44%), and knowing how to search for publications and sources to assess clinical evidence (item 5) (57%/58%). All of these self-assessments had strongly increased by T1 (with the exception of item 5 in the cohort of 2024, where only a medium effect size of 0.49 was found), resulting in proportions of 55–87%.

The two items operationalising the self-reported competency to evaluate the quality of research results were positively assessed (≥ 3 on the integer scale) by around 40% of the students at baseline (43%/38% for item 6, 36%/39% for item 7) (Table 4). Both increased strongly during the module (means by 1.0 to 1.1, medians from 2 to 3), resulting in at least 75% positive self-assessments ex post.

Already at baseline, between 87% an 92% of students expressed agreement (≥ 3 on the integer scale) regarding the importance of the basic principles of EBM, i.e. the attitude that scientific evidence should always be the basis for medical actions (Table 5, item 8), that views and theories should be adapted to new data (item 10), and that this should be guided by the results of randomised controlled intervention trials (Table 6, item 14). Particularly the latter view was shared very strongly, with a median score equal to the maximum of the scale. Nevertheless, some small to medium-sized improvements in these attitudes were observed during the module. However, at baseline, 26/27% of students agreed that biological plausibility is sufficient justification for the use of new therapeutic or diagnostic procedures in humans (Table 6, item 12), and 46%/39% that results of epidemiological or clinical observational studies are sufficient justification for the use of new therapeutic or diagnostic procedures in humans (item 13). These proportions slightly increased between T0 and T1. Over 75% of students gave a positive assessment of the claim that role models were the best way to acquire knowledge, which remained largely unchanged (item 9).

Students’ confidence in their competency to deal with uncertainties in medical knowledge when making therapeutic decisions increased by a medium to strong degree, reaching a proportion of 85% by the end of the module (≥ 3 on the integer scale) (Table 5, item 11).

More than 60% of students already reported to be cognisant of potential career opportunities prior to the start of the module (Table 7, item 15). During the module, this proportion increased with a strong/medium-sized effect. At baseline, 13%/10% of medical students could imagine a career in science, while 38%/33% could imagine combining clinical work with clinical research (items 16 and 17). A medium-size increase was found regarding the former, and a small-to-medium-size increase in the latter.

Discussion

Lack of knowledge, biased data and uncertainties are considered as some of the main drivers of both overuse and underuse of health-care resources [39]. There is incomplete translation of the advances in the continuously evolving evidence base in medical science. To improve these shortcomings, it is important that medical students and hence future medical doctors do not only learn state-of-the-art facts but are prepared for life-long learning. This includes knowledge of the strategies and processes underlying EBM, the competencies needed for evaluation the evidence in accordance with the 5 A-cycle, and a positive attitude towards science-oriented medicine. The week-long Biomedical Research Module tested in this study aimed to instil the necessary competencies and attitudes in second-year undergraduate medical students.

The same self-assessment questionnaire was completed by two independent student cohorts in 2023 and 2024. Response rates were above 99% and the cohorts demonstrated consistent demographic characteristics and answers at baseline, indicating a high degree of homogeneity in their educational status at the start of the module. The observed changes from baseline to module completion were highly similar in both cohorts, underscoring the module’s consistency and the robustness of its impact.

Overall, the results indicate that the students assessed their targeted competencies as strongly increased. Changes in students’ self-reported scientific attitude, however, were modest by comparison. The achieved levels of self-assessed competencies and attitudes were partially satisfactory given the goals of the module.

A strong increase was observed in the students’ self-assessed understanding of the principles of (item 1) and competency to participate in (items 2–5) the process of generating medical knowledge, their self-assessed competency to evaluate the quality of research (items 6, 7), and their self-assessed competency to deal with uncertainties in medical knowledge when making therapeutic decisions (item 11). There was a gap, however, between their self-assessed understanding, which achieved a high level, and their self-assessed competency of generating medical knowledge, which remained below expectations despite strong improvements. This gap between knowledge and competency has been observed in other cohorts of medical students [40]. It suggests that students feel they have the potential but need to gain more experience in applying these techniques through training beyond the module to develop their competency.

The largest gap between the targeted and the achieved level of self-assessed competencies was recorded for statistical know-how (item 3), which remained despite a strong increase. Limited skills in statistics among medical students, but also among practicing physicians, are well known [41]. It is challenging for students to acquire sound statistical methodological knowledge. It is also time-consuming for teachers. Nevertheless, we recommend that medical students receive an education that ensures that they acquire sufficient skills to translate medical statistics into decision-making in practice. Otherwise, their use of EBM will be limited. While statistical education needs to be targeted and efficient, it cannot be covered together with the other objectives of the module in a one-week course. Furthermore, the students also reported limited competency to develop a research plan. However, it should be noted that the competency to carry out a scientific research project independently exceeds the profile of an EBM-practising physician who does not pursue a scientific career. It is typical for courses on scientific competency for medical students that only some of the students achieve the learning objectives. Vogt et al. in their survey of 860 German medical students found that more than half still had insufficient scientific competencies in their 5th year of study [42]. Only 30% of students reported to be able to perform a scientific literature search without supervision, 16% reported that they could critically analyse literature by themselves, and 7% that they were able to work independently on a research project.

The students’ attitude towards the importance of the basic principles of EBM was already at a high self-reported level before the module (items 8, 10, 14). This strong commitment to the principles of EBM is expected in such a population and is considered a basic requirement for specific learning according to the 5-A Cycle of EBM. In contrast, two of the attitudes tested, which in our opinion hinder the consistent application of the 5-A Cycle, scored unexpectedly high (albeit low compared to the items on scientifically favourable attitudes). These two items claim that biological plausibility (item 12) and epidemiological or clinical observation (item 13) are sufficient justification for the use of new therapeutic or diagnostic procedures in humans. However, such kind of evidence as claimed by these two items is hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. These attitudes therefore bear the risk that certain physicians may accept a procedure as an intervention before it was proven effective, which is a well-known phenomenon [39]. Acceptance of these two assertions therefore means that the module’s objectives were not achieved in this regard. Overall, the module did not fundamentally change students’ self-reported attitudes. Rather, it appeared to have reaffirmed existing attitudes and emphasised some components, such as basing medical decisions on scientific results and prioritising data from randomised controlled trials. This is evident in the fact that, after the module, between roughly one third and half of the students still considered biological plausibility and observational data to be sufficient for the use of new therapeutic or diagnostic procedures. This shows that the module needs to place even greater emphasis on study designs and data sources and their implications for validity.

The proportion of students who could imagine combining their clinical work with research or the pursuit of a full academic career was similar to previous studies at German-speaking universities, and it increased slightly during the module. Markun et al. [43] found a proportion of 15% of first-year medical students at ETH Zurich, Switzerland, who envisaged an academic career, and Fuerholzer et al. [44] found 11% in a small sample of fourth- and fifth-year students at the University of Ulm, Germany. Given the results, we believe that most young physicians want to be connected to research. While self-reported awareness of career opportunities strongly increased, it is unclear whether this can only be attributed to overall opportunities (as the item suggests), or whether this represents increased awareness of opportunities in research.

The results from this study of the week-long Biomedical Research Module are in line with other courses of similar total scope (approx. 1 credit according to ECTS) that are designed to impart individual competencies of the 5-A cycle [45–49]. Using a comprehensive approach, our module covers all the main areas of the scholar’s role according to the 5-A cycle in one course. The module is integrated longitudinally into the teaching of EBM competencies over the course of the medical education curriculum and is preceded by a first-year course on the fundamentals of medical science. It incorporates different teaching formats that have been proposed to improve the competencies of a scholar [50], such as integrating EBM with other courses and content and including clinical content into EBM training. A number of faculty members from different medical fields were involved in teaching. While they adhered to a clear framework regarding the general format and learning objectives, they were free to incorporate their own teaching methods, creativity and enthusiasm for their respective subject areas. Finally, our module does not require the effort of teaching in small groups but used plenary sessions instead.

Limitations

The self-constructed questionnaire generated reproducible answers in two large cohorts of medical students. However, its structural validity was not quantitatively tested. The pretest-posttest design does not include a control group. Therefore, unidirectional causality of the observed effects cannot be claimed. Potential sources of bias include temporal effects of other experiences students may have made between T0 and T1 as well as self-report, social desirability, or Hawthorne bias. It should be noted that longitudinal studies of medical students reported an increase in objective skill assessment scores over time, while self-assessment scores of the same skills remained nearly unchanged [42], which argues against an overestimation of one’s own abilities by the medical students or a social desirability bias. Random distribution of missing values in the sample regarding the analysed outcomes cannot be proven. However, missing values were rare and response rates were high. While the comparison of demographics and baseline responses between the two cohorts did not reveal a significant difference, this type of test, by design, does not allow for the conclusion of equivalence. As discussed, agreement with the two items claiming that biological plausibility (item 12) or epidemiological/observational data (item 13) were sufficient for the use of new therapeutic or diagnostic procedures in humans did not decrease as expected between T0 and T1. The question arises as to whether this indicates a shortcoming of the module or a flaw in the measurement of the two constructs. The latter would mean that these results should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusions

During the Biomedical Research Module, an increase in the self-assessed competencies necessary for EBM and the scholar’s role according to the 5-A cycle in second year medical students who had previously received introductions into basic biostatistics, epidemiological and clinical research, and ethics was observed. Students’ self-assessed understanding of the principles of medical knowledge generation and their competency to evaluate the quality of medical research to a satisfactory level increased. While also students’ self-assessed competency to actively participate in the generation of scientific medical knowledge, certain active skills remained below satisfactory levels, particularly statistics and the development of a research plan. These are skills that require more extensive training to develop fully, beyond what a week-long (or 1 ECTS) course can provide at this stage of their education. Furthermore, the sceptical attitudes reported by a minority of students towards the scientific process proved to be more persistent than expected. Practical solutions need to be developed. Further research should therefore focus on alternative approaches to changing medical students’ attitudes towards the scientific method, taking into account modern channels of social communication and how the scientific process is treated in the popular media.
