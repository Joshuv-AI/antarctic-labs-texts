Bacterial pathogens and antimicrobial resistance patterns in infections associated with stillbirths and under-five deaths in Ethiopia: a post-mortem surveillance study
Authors: Fentabil Getnet, Dadi Marami, Shikur Mohammed, Yenework Acham, Alemnesh H Mirkuzie, Awoke Misganaw, Yimam Getaneh, Esrael Solomon, Dagne Bodena, Fami Ahmed, Daniel Demissie, Mulu Berihun, Zelalem Teklemariam, Lola Madrid, Joe O Oundo, Lulu M Muhe, Nega Assefa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacterial infections remain major contributors to stillbirth, neonatal, and under-five mortality, particularly in low-resource settings where antimicrobial resistance (AMR) increasingly threatens the management of severe infections. Despite the growing burden of AMR in Ethiopia, data on bacterial pathogens and resistance patterns contributing to child mortality remain limited. This study aimed to characterize bacterial pathogens and antimicrobial resistance patterns among isolates obtained from stillbirths and under-five deaths in Ethiopia.MethodsWe analyzed microbiological data from two post-mortem surveillance studies conducted in Ethiopia: the Child Health and Mortality Prevention Surveillance (CHAMPS) and the Study of Illness in Preterm (SIP). CHAMPS investigated the causes of death in 416 stillbirths and children under five years of age between 2019 and 2022, while SIP examined 1,109 preterm neonatal deaths from five hospitals between 2016 and 2018. In both studies, bacterial pathogens were identified through culture of blood and cerebrospinal fluid samples—collected post-mortem in CHAMPS and ante-mortem in SIP. A total of 535 bacterial isolates were identified (302 from CHAMPS and 233 from SIP), of which 371 underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Descriptive statistics were performed using Stata 16/MP 16.1 to summarize bacterial pathogen distribution and patterns of antimicrobial resistance.ResultsAmong the 302 isolates identified from stillbirths and under-five deaths in the CHAMPS study, Klebsiella pneumoniae accounted for 44.0% of isolates, followed by vaccine-preventable pathogens (Streptococcus pneumoniae, H. influenzae and Neisseria meningitidis) and Escherichia coli with 15.5% and 12.9%, respectively. In the SIP study, Klebsiella pneumoniae was also the predominant pathogen (29.2%) in preterm neonatal deaths followed by Staphylococcus aureus (17.6%). Group B Streptococcus comprised 2.1% of all isolates. Klebsiella pneumoniae exhibited high levels of resistance (≥ 75%) to commonly used antimicrobials, while lower resistance levels were observed for Amikacin and Ciprofloxacin. Among 110K. pneumoniae isolates tested, 83.6% were susceptible to Amikacin and 67.8% to Ciprofloxacin.ConclusionKlebsiella pneumonia is the predominant bacterial pathogen identified among stillbirths and under-five deaths in Ethiopian healthcare settings, with high levels of resistance to commonly used first-line antimicrobials. These findings highlight the need to strengthen infection prevention measures, AMR surveillance, and revision of empirical treatment protocols and antimicrobial stewardship to improve management of severe pediatric bacterial infections.Keywords: Child mortality, Bacterial pathogens, Sepsis, Antimicrobial resistance, Post-mortem surveillance, Ethiopia

Introduction

Bacterial infections remain major causes of stillbirth, neonatal, and under-five mortality worldwide, particularly in low-resource settings [1, 2]. Antimicrobials are the most effective and well-established interventions to avert deaths from bacterial diseases [3]. Nowadays, however, the effectiveness of antimicrobial treatment is increasingly threatened by the emergence of antimicrobial resistant bacteria against broad-spectrum antimicrobials [4]. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) occurs when microorganisms develop the ability to survive exposure to antimicrobial agents designed to inhibit or kill them [5]. Children are disproportionately vulnerable to the growing threat of AMR due to their immature immune systems, which make them more susceptible to infections [6]. Children who live in low-resource environments and have limited access to healthcare are at a much greater risk because AMR is disseminated more readily in environments with poor sanitation, inadequate personal hygiene, and poor infection control [7]. The lack of information about the effects of AMR on children, especially in settings with limited resources, further exacerbates the problem [5].

Antimicrobial resistance accounts for a high burden of morbidity and mortality globally [8, 9]. In 2019 alone, an estimated five million deaths were attributable to treatable bacterial infections including lower respiratory, bloodstream, and intra-abdominal infections. In over one million of these cases, AMR directly compromised treatment effectiveness. Notably, one in five AMR-related deaths occurred in children under the age of five, including neonates [5]. This indicates that large numbers of children die of treatable bacterial infections, highlighting growing concerns regarding the adequacy of currently used antimicrobial treatments. Without decisive intervention, mortalities attributed to AMR are projected to reach 10 million annually by 2050, with Africa expected to bear the highest burden of deaths [9]. The commonest pathogens linked to AMR-associated deaths include resistant strains of Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Acinetobacter baumannii, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [5].

An extensive analysis from the Child Health and Mortality Prevention Surveillance (CHAMPS) network reported sepsis, pneumonia, and meningitis as the leading causes of neonatal and under-five deaths in the study countries [10]. In Ethiopia, at least one pathogen was implicated in two-thirds of neonatal deaths, with multiple pathogens found in one-quarter of cases. Klebsiella pneumoniae was the most frequently identified organism, present in 51% of neonatal deaths, followed by Escherichia coli (12%) and Salmonella species (10%) [11]. However, detailed characterization of antimicrobial susceptibility patterns across bacterial pathogens associated with stillbirths, under-five deaths, and preterm neonatal deaths in Ethiopia remains limited. Understanding the dominant bacterial pathogens and their antimicrobial resistance patterns is crucial to addressing the growing threat of AMR—particularly in settings with limited surveillance and sparse data. Despite the rising burden of AMR infections and the widespread misuse of antibiotics in Ethiopia, there remains a significant evidence gap regarding the bacterial causes of morbidity and mortality among children aged under five and their resistance profiles [12, 13]. Using data from the CHAMPS and Study of Illness in Preterm (SIP) studies in Ethiopia, this study aimed to characterize bacterial pathogens and antimicrobial resistance patterns associated with stillbirths and under-five deaths.

Methods

Study designs and settings

We analyzed data obtained from two independent studies, namely CHAMPS and SIP, conducted to investigate causes of stillbirths and under-five death in Ethiopia. The CHAMPS is an ongoing mortality surveillance program established to investigate causes of stillbirths and deaths among children aged < 5 years occurring in health facilities and communities within one urban (Harar) and two rural (Kersa and Haramaya) Health and Demographic Surveillance Sites (HDSS) in Eastern Ethiopia since 2019. For this analysis, we used data collected between 2019 and 2022 [14]. A minimally invasive tissue sampling (MITS) procedure [15] was used to collect specimens within 24 h of death or stillbirth delivery. The causes of death (COD) are then determined by a Determination of Cause of Death (DeCoDe) panel of experts and categorized as underlying, immediate, and other causes. The detailed methods are published elsewhere [10, 11].

The SIP was a prospective surveillance study between 2016 and 2018 to investigate causes of illness and death among preterm neonates in five referral hospitals located in Addis Ababa, Amhara, and Oromia regions of Ethiopia. Preterm neonates born alive before 37 weeks of gestation and admitted to Neonatal Intensive Care Units (NICU) were enrolled and followed up to 28 days after birth. Both complete diagnostic autopsy and MITS procedures were applied to collect specimens from preterm deaths occurring withinthe neonatal period. Microbiological investigations were done for preterm illnesses suspected of bacterial infections, and deaths with bacteriological data were included in this analysis. Similar to CHAMPS, causes of deaths were assigned by a DeCoDe panel [16, 17].

Although the CHAMPS and SIP studies differed in population scope, both were designed to investigate causes of child deaths in Ethiopia using comparable pathological and microbiological investigations and DeCoDe procedures. CHAMPS included stillbirths and all under-five deaths, whereas SIP focused specifically on preterm neonatal deaths, representing a narrower high-risk subgroup within the broader child mortality spectrum. Furthermore, although CHAMPS included community deaths by design, most deaths included in this analysis were facility-based, making the clinical contexts broadly comparable. Therefore, the datasets were integrated to provide a broader characterization of bacterial pathogens and antimicrobial resistance patterns associated with fatal under-five infections in Ethiopia. The studies covered central, eastern, northern, and southwestern Ethiopia over the period 2016 to 2022 (Fig. 1).

Study population and sample size

For the CHAMPS study, stillbirths and deaths among children aged < 5 years (neonates, infants and under-five) that underwent complete post-mortem investigations 2019 and 2022 (n = 416) were screened for inclusion. Deaths with confirmed bacterial isolates and available microbiological data were included in this analysis (n = 263). Detailed findings on causes of death from CHAMPS surveillance have been published elsewhere [11].

For the SIP study, preterm neonatal deaths occurring within 28 days after birth among neonates born before 37 weeks of gestation between 2016 and 2018 were screened for inclusion (n = 1,109). Cases with available bacterial isolate data were included in this analysis (n = 233). The details from the SIP study have also been reported elsewhere [17].

In total, 535 bacterial isolates were included for pathogen characterization, of which 371 underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST). Pathogens requiring specialized microbiological, clinical, and programmatic attention, such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis, were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 2).

Flow diagram of the sampling procedure from CHAMPS and SIP studies

Bacteria isolation and identification

Blood and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) were the primary specimens analyzed in both studies. In CHAMPS, additional specimens such as umbilical pus, eye discharge, skin, abscess, and other body fluids were also analyzed. Blood samples (approximately 5 ml) were inoculated into BACT/ALERT PF Plus bottles [Biomerieux, France], and CSF samples were cultured on appropriate media for aerobic and facultative anaerobic bacteria. Positive cultures were sub-cultured onto Chrom Agar, 5% Blood agar, Chocolate agar, Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate agar, and MacConkey agar. Incubation was performed at 35.5 ± 1.5 °C under aerobic and microaerobic conditions, as appropriate. Bacterial identification was conducted using Gram staining, oxidase tests, and either the DxM1040 MicroScan WalkAway ID/ AST System [18] or Analytical Profile Index methods [Biomerieux, France]) [18, 19].

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

AST was performed using the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method [20]. The inoculum turbidity was standardized to 0.5 McFarland standard using a Siemens turbidity meter. The antimicrobials tested in both studies included Ceftriaxone, Ciprofloxacin, Clindamycin, Ampicillin, Erythromycin, Gentamicin, Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole, Amoxicillin-Clavulanate, Penicillin, Amikacin, Chloramphenicol, Cloxacillin, and Vancomycin. In both studies, AST panels were selected based on the Ethiopian Ministry of Health empirical treatment guidelines and laboratory protocols used in the original studies. Further methodological details have been published elsewhere [10, 21]. Some antimicrobials included in the original testing panels, such as Clindamycin and Erythromycin, have limited or no clinical activity against Gram-negative organisms. Resistance observed for these organisms largely reflects intrinsic resistance and should be interpreted accordingly. Only a small proportion of identified S. aureus isolates underwent AST because AST availability depended on the original study protocols and isolate availability. Consequently, the reported resistance patterns may not be representative of all identified S. aureus isolates.

Results were interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) and European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (EUCAST) guidelines [20, 22]. Quality control procedures were performed using NCTC reference strains, and no major quality-control failures were reported during AST procedures, and no isolates were excluded because of quality-control concerns. Isolates were categorized as susceptible, intermediate or resistant according to standardized clinical breakpoints [23]. Because AST panels varied slightly across pathogens and study sites, the number of isolates tested differed by antimicrobial agent.

Data sources and analysis

Data were obtained from the national health data repository at the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) [24], with access secured prior to downloading the datasets and their associated codebooks. Microbiological and antimicrobial susceptibility datasets from the CHAMPS and SIP studies were combined to characterize bacterial pathogens and antimicrobial resistance patterns associated with fatal pediatric infections in Ethiopia. Study-specific findings were presented where appropriate. Antimicrobial susceptibility datasets were integrated by pathogen and antimicrobial type, as illustrated in Fig. 2. Variations in antimicrobial panels across the two studies resulted in differing sample sizes for some antimicrobial agents. Another methodological difference between the studies is the timing of specimen collection. In CHAMPS, blood and CSF specimens were obtained post-mortem through MITS, whereas in SIP, specimens were collected ante-mortem from preterm neonates during clinical care before death. These differences might influence pathogen recovery rates, contamination profiles, and microbial yield. Therefore, pooled findings should be interpreted primarily as a descriptive characterization of bacterial pathogens identified among fatal pediatric infections rather than direct comparisons of pathogen frequency between the two studies.

Data cleaning and analysis were done using Stata/MP 16.1.Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the distribution of bacterial pathogens and antimicrobial susceptibility profiles.

In addition to species-specific analyses, the study summarized the proportion of isolates categorized as susceptible, intermediate or resistant to each antimicrobial agent across all tested bacterial isolates. This descriptive approach was intended to provide an overall picture of susceptibility patterns in settings where empirical treatment is commonly used due to limited routine microbiological diagnostics. Aggregate susceptibility summaries were interpreted cautiously, as antimicrobial response varies across bacterial species and clinical syndromes.

Results

Overview of Included Isolates

A total of 535 bacterial isolates were identified from stillbirths and under-five deaths included in the CHAMPS and SIP studies. Of these, 302 isolates were obtained from 263 stillbirths and under-five deaths in the CHAMPS study, while 233 isolates were identified from preterm neonatal deaths in the SIP study. Blood was the predominant source of bacterial isolates, accounting for approximately 80% of all isolates, while the remaining isolates were recovered CSF and other specimen types.

Among isolates identified in the CHAMPS study, nearly two-thirds (66%) were recovered from neonatal deaths, followed by children aged 1–4 years (17%), infants (14%), and stillbirths (3%) (Table 1).

Distribution of bacterial pathogen

In the CHAMPS study, 26 different bacterial pathogens were identified from 263 stillbirths and under-five child (neonatal, infant and 1 to 4 years) deaths, with 35 deaths (13.3%) involving two or more bacterial infections, resulting in a total of 302 isolates. K. pneumoniae was the most frequently isolated pathogen, accounting for 44.0% of all isolates, followed by E. coli at 12.9%. Three vaccine-preventable pathogens, S. pneumonia, H. influenzae and N. meningitides, comprized 15.5% of the isolates, while Group B Streptococcus accounted for 3.0%. The latter included four isolates from stillbirths and five from early neonatal deaths.

In the SIP study, 17 bacterial pathogens were isolated from preterm neonates prior to death, yielding 233 isolates in total. The predominant pathogens were K. pneumoniae (29.2%), coagulase-negative Staphylococcus (CoNS) (18.0%), and S. aureus (17.6%). Nearly half (47.5%) of the isolates belonged to the Enterobacteriaceae group (Table 1).

Etiology of sepsis

Given that sepsis was the leading immediate cause of neonatal and under-five deaths in the CHAMPS study, we analyzed the bacterial pathogens associated with these cases. A total of 17 different pathogens were identified in deaths for whom sepsis was determined to be the immediate cause of death. In 31 cases, two or more pathogens were isolated, resulting in a total of 110 bacterial isolates. Among these, 46.4% were K. pneumoniae and 18.2% were E. coli (Fig. 3).

Antimicrobial resistance patterns

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was performed for 13 antimicrobial agents across 371 bacterial isolates (Table 2). Resistance patterns among Gram-negative bacteria; the predominant Gram-negative pathogens, K. pneumoniae and E. coli, showed low levels of resistance to Amikacin, with resistance observed in 4.8% and 2.1% of isolates, respectively. Similarly, resistance to Ciprofloxacin was relatively lower in K. pneumoniae (15.5%) compared with other tested antimicrobials. In contrast, K. pneumoniae exhibited high levels of resistance (≥ 88.5%) to Ceftriaxone, Clindamycin, Ampicillin, Erythromycin, and Gentamicin, while complete resistance to Penicillin was observed. Among E. coli isolates, resistance ranged from 26.9% for Amoxicillin-Clavulanate to 100% for Clindamycin. Moderate resistance was observed for Ciprofloxacin (30.8%), Gentamicin (40.4%), and Ceftriaxone (55.8%), whereas resistance to the remaining antimicrobials exceeded 75%. Similarly, Salmonella spp. and P. aeruginosa demonstrated high resistance to most tested antimicrobials, except Amikacin.

Among Gram-positive pathogens, resistance patterns varied across organisms. Only one of the nine tested S. aureus isolates was resistant to Vancomycin (nine isolates were tested for Vancomycin while only three for other antimicrobials). Coagulase-negative Staphylococcus demonstrated relatively low resistance to Ceftriaxone (8.8%), Clindamycin (11.8%), Ciprofloxacin (14.7%), and Ampicillin (14.7%), but higher resistance to Penicillin (88.2%) and Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole (50.0%) (Table 2).

Overall antimicrobial Susceptibility Patterns

Across all tested bacterial isolates, Amikacin demonstrated the highest proportion of susceptible isolates (84%), followed by Vancomycin (82%), and Ciprofloxacin (68%). In contrast, susceptibility to the remaining antimicrobials was generally low, with less than 31% of isolates categorized as susceptible. Penicillin showed the lowest proportion of susceptible isolates (11.1%) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Klebsiella pneumoniae was the predominant pathogen (44%) identified among from under-five deaths and preterm neonatal deaths (29%). Vaccine-preventable pathogens (S. pneumonia, H. influenzae and N. meningitidis) consisted of 15.5% of the isolates in under-five deaths, while Enterobacteriaceae and Staphylococcus groups comprised 53.9% of the isolates in preterm infections that ended up with death. Over three-quarters of K. pneumoniae isolates were resistant to all antimicrobials tested except Amikacin and Ciprofloxacin. Amikacin, Ciprofloxacin and Vancomycin demonstrated the highest proportions of susceptible isolates among antimicrobials tested.

In this study, K. pneumoniae was the predominant bacterial diseases in neonatal and under-five deaths and nearly a third of preterm infections that led to death. Related evidence also reaffirms that the pathogen is emerging as the commonest cause of sepsis and pneumonia-caused under-five deaths [25–27]. Rates comparable or higher than our findings have been shown from several African countries including Egypt [28], Equatorial Guinea [27], and South Africa [25]. This pathogen along with other Enterobacteriaceae is taking the lead as the main cause of sepsis in children from the long dominance of Gram-positive cocci in developing countries [29, 30]. Despite inclusion in Ethiopia’s routine immunization program [31]. S. pneumoniae and H. influenzae still accounted for a considerable proportion of bacterial isolates identified in infant and under-five deaths, emphasizing the importance of enhancing immunization services to reduce child mortality in Ethiopia. On the other hand, the predominance of Enterobacteriaceae and Staphylococcus species among preterm neonatal infections may partly reflect healthcare-associated transmissions in NICU settings, where prolonged hospitalization and invasive procedures increase infection risk [32]. Moreover, the group B Streptococcus infections in stillbirths and early neonatal deaths might typically be vertical transmissions from colonized mothers during pregnancy or at delivery. Studies in comparable settings have shown high levels of vertical transmission and stillbirth from colonized women [33, 34]. Although CoNS are increasingly recognized as important causes of healthcare-associated infection among preterm neonates and NICU patients, they also might be resulted from blood culture contaminations. Despite this, the isolates were retained as reported in the SIP data since the final determination of their contribution to death was considered alongside clinical, pathological, and microbiological results during DeCoDe panel review.

The high levels of antimicrobial resistance observed among major bacterial pathogens raise concern regarding the adequacy of commonly used empirical treatment regimens in these settings. K. pneumoniae demonstrated high levels of resistance to most tested antimicrobials, with comparatively lower resistance to Amikacin and Ciprofloxacin. Evidence suggests that the role of multidrug-resistant bacteria in childhood sepsis and pneumonia is steadily increasing [28, 35]. A study of sepsis in Egypt shows that K. pneumoniae was resistant to all antimicrobials, except fluoroquinolones, and had carbapenemase and extended-spectrum β-lactamase resistant genes [35]. Infections by multidrug-resistant Enterobacteriaceae, particularly K. pneumoniae, are commonly acquired in healthcare settings [36–38]. The continually growing threat of diseases and deaths from multidrug resistant infections undoubtedly alarms the need to prioritize infection prevention approaches and find new treatment alternatives. Studies from other settings have reported that culture surveillance of patients on admission followed by isolation of patients with multidrug resistant infections and infection prevention measures may help reduce transmission of highly infectious Carbapenemase-producing K. pneumoniae in endemic hospital settings [39–41]. These measures, along with basic infection prevention measures, can be tried in Ethiopia’s pediatric and neonatal intensive care units bearing in mind the burden. The uniformly high resistance observed among Salmonella isolates should be interpreted cautiously given the relatively small number of isolates included in the analysis. Whether these findings reflect healthcare-associated transmission, local clonal circulation, or broader community resistance patterns could not be determined from the available data.

The global efforts to minimize under-five mortality seem to be unattainable unless deaths due to bacterial infections are avoided in healthcare settings of poor countries through effective infection prevention and treatment measures in place. Misuse of antimicrobials by healthcare providers, untrained practitioners, and antimicrobial consumers are the primary drivers of resistance [12]. However, prescriptions are not based on definite diagnosis and susceptibility testing results in low-income countries due to limited laboratory capacity [42]. Non-prescribed use of antimicrobials has been a common medical malpractice in Ethiopia. This suggests that antimicrobials may be both overused and inappropriately used, which are the key lanes to antimicrobial resistance [43, 44]. Hence, the relatively low resistance observed for Amikacin suggests that it may warrant further consideration in antimicrobial stewardship and empirical treatment policy discussions, which was not included among the essential medicines supplies in Ethiopia [45]. Previous studies have reported favorable activity of Amikacin against gram-negative infections in children [46, 47]. Mechanisms to track antimicrobial sensitivity and underpin stewardship should also be in place to ensure the effective and sensible use of antimicrobials. Furthermore, expanding sepsis management to low-level health facilities may help to reduce nosocomial transmissions facilitated by high crowds in referral hospitals.

The generalizability of the current findings is subject to certain limitations. Because culture results were available for only half of SIP sepsis cases, pathogen distribution estimates may be subject to selection bias. If culture-positive cases differed systematically from culture-negative cases in clinical severity, timing of presentation, or other characteristics, the observed pathogen frequencies may not fully represent all fatal preterm infections. This may have introduced selection bias in pathogen distribution estimates toward organisms more readily recovered through culture-based methods. Furthermore, only 9.7% of deaths in the CHAMPS study occurred at home, whereas prior evidence from the same study sites indicated that approximately 75% of child and 94% of neonatal deaths occur in the community setting [48, 49]. Although community-based mortality surveillance using verbal autopsy methods identified sepsis and lower respiratory tract infections as leading causes of neonatal deaths [49], the bacterial pathogens responsible for deaths in community settings may differ significantly from those found in healthcare facilities [50]. Therefore, the findings primarily reflect pathogens associated with facility-based deaths and may not fully represent the broader burden of bacterial infections contributing to child mortality in the general population. The study sites were limited to CHAMPS surveillance areas in eastern Ethiopia and SIP hospitals located in selected regions. Consequently, the findings may not be nationally representative. Furthermore, community deaths, which account for a substantial proportion of under-five mortality in Ethiopia, were underrepresented in the present analysis.

Differences in specimen collection timing between CHAMPS (post-mortem) and SIP (ante-mortem) may have influenced pathogen recovery and species distribution. Consequently, differences in pathogen frequencies between studies should be interpreted cautiously. Moreover, Methicillin susceptibility testing was not performed as part of the original study protocols. Thus, the prevalence of methicillin-resistant S. aureus could not be determined, limiting the clinical interpretation of S. aureus resistance patterns. Moreover, the reported resistance patterns may not be representative of all S. aureus isolates since only a small proportion of identified S. aureus isolates underwent susceptibility testing.

Conclusion

Klebsiella pneumoniae is the predominant bacterial pathogen identified among under-five deaths in Ethiopian healthcare settings, followed by vaccine preventable pathogens, Enterobacteriaceae, and Staphylococcus species. High levels of resistance to commonly used first-line antimicrobials, including Ceftriaxone, Amoxicillin-Clavulanate and Gentamicin, standard treatments for sepsis and pneumonia in Ethiopia, were observed among major bacterial pathogens. These findings raise concern regarding the adequacy of current empirical treatment practices for severe pediatric infections in high-burden healthcare settings. Strengthening antimicrobial resistance surveillance, infection prevention measures, microbiological diagnostic capacity, and antimicrobial stewardship programs may help improve the management of severe bacterial infections and preserve the effectiveness of available treatment options.

Declarations

Ethical approval and consent to participate

The present study used anonymized secondary data from the CHAMPS and SIP studies, archived at the National Data Management and Analytics Center (NDMC) of the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI). According to NDMC policy, the use of anonymized secondary data does not require additional ethical approval, as all personally identifiable information is removed prior to data access. Thus, no separate ethical approval was required for this analysis. Both studies obtained ethical approval prior to data collection and were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki, as well as relevant national and institutional guidelines. The CHAMPS study was approved by the Institutional Health Research Ethics Review Committee (IHRERC) of Haramaya University (Ref. No.: CHMS/10.0/2467/18) and the National Ethics Review Committee (Ref. No.: 3.10/70/2018). Informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians before data collection and the MITS procedure. All procedures adhered to applicable ethical standards and regulatory guidelines. Similarly, the SIP study received approval from the institutional review boards of each participating hospital and Institutional Review Board of the College of Health Sciences, Addis Ababa University. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or legal guardians for participation, with separate consent for post-mortem examinations, complete autopsy and MITS procedures. All clinical procedures followed hospital protocols, and confidentiality of participant information was maintained.
