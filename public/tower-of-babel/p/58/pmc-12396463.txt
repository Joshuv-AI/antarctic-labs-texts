A near-infrared responsive photocaged amplified DNA nanodevice for precise and sensitive microRNA imaging
Authors: Yan Zhong, Zheng Li, Bo Li, Chang Xu, Tuya Bai, Hang Qian, Chunyan Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
MicroRNAs (miRNAs) play a critical role in early cancer detection, but traditional DNA probes are limited by the low abundance of miRNAs and their “always effective” property. Herein, we construct a photocaged amplified DNA nanodevice (PAD) by attaching DNA probes to upconversion nanoparticles (UCs). Upon remote near-infrared (NIR) light stimulation, the photocleavable DNA probes are activated by emitted UV light, and subsequently triggered by target miRNA. This process is further propelled by mRNA and DNA hairpin to achieve “one to many” signal amplification, enabling precise and sensitive spatiotemporal resolved imaging of miRNA. In vitro results demonstrate that the proposed dual-cycle amplification strategy effectively prevents undesired activation and minimizes non-specific signals, achieving a remarkable 20-fold improvement in miRNA detection limit compared to the one-cycle amplification strategy. Additionally, PAD enables spatiotemporally controlled miRNA imaging in tumor-bearing mouse models through NIR light-regulated in situ activation. The proposed approach extends the application of light-gated spatiotemporal controllable DNA nanodevice in nucleic acid imaging, thereby advancing its application in biological and medical imaging.

Introduction

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) have emerged as pivotal biomarkers for cancer diagnosis and treatment [[1], [2], [3]]. Given their low expression levels in cells [4], establishing reliable and sensitive methods for visualizing and quantifying intracellular miRNAs is essential for understanding their roles and potential implications in health maintenance and cancer progression. Classical RNA detection methods, including the highly sensitive polymerase chain reaction (PCR), suffer from limitations such as high cost, time consumption, and complicated operational protocols [5]. Conventional detection approaches like linear hybridization or molecular beacons exhibit one to one signal readout [6,7]. In recent years, various enzymatic signal amplification strategies have been developed, including loop-mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP) [8], strand displacement amplification (SDA) [9], and rolling circle amplification (RCA) [10], offering remarkable signal enhancement for miRNAs detection in vitro. Nevertheless, their application in living organisms is restricted due to the reliance on exogenous enzymes. Non-enzymatic signal amplification strategies, such as hybrid chain reactions (HCR) [11], catalytic hairpin assembly (CHA) [12], and entropy-driven catalysis [13] have made significant progress in the detection of miRNAs. The design of most DNA probes as “always active” makes them responsive to unexpected target during delivery, lacking precise spatiotemporal control, leading to compromised detection accuracy and reduced signal-to-noise ratio [14]. Therefore, the development of intelligent DNA nanodevices is highly desirable to achieve spatiotemporal miRNA sensing and imaging in living cells and animals.

Light serves as a powerful tool with promising prospects in the field of stimulus-responsive nanodevices, owing to its convenient operation in spatial-temporal resolution and its ability to regulate biosensor with accuracy [[15], [16], [17]]. Researchers have explored various approaches utilizing light for biological regulation, particularly ultraviolet (UV) light. For example, Sethi et al. prepared a mechanical DNA polymer capable of reversible contraction and relaxation in response to UV and visible light irradiation, which can induce changes in cell spreading, organization of F-actin filaments, and formation of focal adhesions [18]. Li et al. developed a UV light-activated Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) nanoflare for spatiotemporally controlled imaging of miRNAs in living cells and mice with high sensitivity [19]. Nevertheless, the limited penetration ability and biological toxicity of UV or blue light in biological systems impede their application [20]. Fortunately, upconversion nanoparticles (UCs) offer a solution by converting low-energy near-infrared (NIR) light into high-energy UV and visible light to overcome the inherent limitations of UV light. UCs possess several outstanding advantages, including reduced biological absorption, deeper tissue penetration, and excellent stability [[21], [22], [23]]. Leveraging the properties of UCs to absorb NIR light and emit UV light, combined with the photosensitive groups in DNA probes, various intelligent DNA nanodevices have been developed for biosensing [[24], [25], [26]] and cancer treatment [[27], [28], [29]]. For instance, Chu et al. employed HCR functional units combined with upconversion photochemistry to generate activatable HCR with precise spatiotemporal accuracy, achieving ultra-sensitive amplification imaging of mRNA [30]. Similarly, Duan et al. utilized a NIR light-controlled nucleic acid chain displacement reaction and nucleic acid exonuclease III (EXO III) assisted nucleic acid cascade recycling amplification, combined with UCs, to achieve spatiotemporal controllable signal amplification imaging of mRNA [31]. However, these strategies often rely on exogenous enzymes or complex cascades, which may lead to instability and limited applications in complex intracellular environments. Furthermore, achieving superior detection of miRNAs under precise spatiotemporal control remains particularly challenging, as miRNA abundance in early stage of cancer is relatively low. The urgency for internal driving force compatible with light activation for miRNA amplification in living systems has not been adequately addressed. It is worth noting that the high expression of GAPDH mRNA in cells can be harnessed as an endogenous driving fuel [32], along with the inherent amplification capability of chain displacement cycles, may fulfill the need for exogenous enzymes and extensive applications. Meanwhile, conventional methods for miRNA detection using DNA nanodevices are often susceptible to non-specific signals and lack of detection sensitivity and spatiotemporal control. Therefore, the development of a DNA nanodevice activated by NIR light and driven by endogenous mRNA holds significant promise for spatiotemporally controlled in situ imaging of miRNAs.

In this study, we designed and constructed a photocaged amplified DNA nanodevice (PAD) to achieve spatiotemporal controlled miRNA155 (miR155) imaging with high sensitivity. The PAD comprises UCs and cascade operational DNA probes. The DNA probes are activated by NIR light and driven by endogenous GAPDH mRNA, serving as a powerful and biocompatible fuel source. Compared to the one-cycle amplification strategy, our dual-cycle approach achieves a remarkable 44-fold increase in fluorescence signal and a 20-fold improvement in detection limit. Furthermore, the method enables sensitive detection of intracellular miR155, discriminates miR155 from mismatched variants, reflects its expression levels across diverse cell types. Importantly, PAD enables spatiotemporally controlled miRNA imaging in tumor-bearing mouse models through NIR light-regulated in situ activation. This light-gated DNA nanodevice provides a versatile platform for advancing nucleic acid imaging, with broad implications for biomedical research and clinical diagnostics.

Results and discussion

Principle of the PAD

The UV light-activatable DNA probes (including reporting probe C1∗/C2, driving probes D1/D2, and DNA hairpins (Hp)) were electrostatically adsorbed onto UCs surface to form PAD. The UCs act both as carriers and light transducers. Initially, the DNA probes remain inert before NIR irradiation. Upon 980 nm NIR irradiation, the activated reporting probe C1∗/C2 is specifically triggered by miRNA and driven by mRNA and Hp, resulting in a “one to many” signal amplification. As shown in Scheme 1, the dual amplification cycles operated based on toehold-mediated chain displacement. The reporter probe consists of C1∗/C2, wherein the incorporation of BHQ2 in the C1∗ and Cy5 in the C2, resulting in a low fluorescence background. The toehold region for miRNA recognition within C2 is initially shielded by a 6-nt tail attached PC linker in C1∗, rendering it inactive for sensing (OFF). When UCs emit UV light under NIR light irradiation, the PC linker is photolyzed, releasing the 6-nt tail in C1∗ and exposing the toehold domain of the reporting probe. This facilitates hybridization with miR155 (C) and activates the DNA probe (ON). DNA probes then undergo a series of chain displacements triggered by initiator C. In cycle 1, a small amount of C binds to C2, forming C/C2 and resulting in the fluorescence recovery of Cy5. Subsequently, a large amount of GAPHD mRNA (D) recognizes the toehold domain in D1/D2 to form D/D2 and releases D1, which replaces C from C/C2. This process facilitates the recycling of C for amplification rounds. In cycle 2, the addition of Hp induces the release of D from D/D2, allowing it to be utilized for the release of D1. Thus, cycle 1 and cycle 2 repeatedly and continuously proceed to generate C1, fulfilling the “one to many” signal amplification. In short, the initial activity of PAD can be precisely activated by NIR light irradiation. Low abundance disease related miRNAs within cells serve as initiators, while endogenous high-abundance mRNA and additional Hp are introduced to drive the DNA probes for constant operation. This process generates significantly amplified fluorescence signals, enabling precise and sensitive imaging of miRNAs with spatiotemporal resolution in living cells and tumors.Scheme 1Illustration of the dual-cycle amplified system. Verification of dual cycle amplified DNA probes

To confirm the feasibility of the UV-activated DNA probes, polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) was performed to verify the two-step cycling process. As shown in Fig. 1a and Fig. S1a, C1/C2 in lane 5 moved much slower than single-stranded C1 and C2 in lane 1 and 2, suggesting the high yield of C1/C2 duplex. Introduction of target strand C (miR155) triggers the formation of C/C2, liberating C1 (lane 6). Further addition of fuel chain D1 facilitates reaction with C/C2, yielding D1/C2 and releasing C (lane 8). In Cycle 2 (Fig. 1b and Fig. S1b), the addition of D engages in a reaction with D1/D2, resulting in the liberation of D1 at concentrations substantially higher than those of C (lane 6). This is attributed to the elevated expression levels of D relative to C within cells. The released D1 play a crucial role in driving the cycle 1. The introduction of Hp leads to the replacement of D in D/D2 (lane 8), initiating the cycling process of D and further promotes the release of D1. To assess the efficacy of cyclic amplifications, ratios of 1/25, 1/5, and 1/1 among C, D and Hp were examined. As depicted in Fig. 1c and Fig. S1c, lane 7 represented no amplification, lane 8 represented one-cycle amplification, and lane 9 represented dual-cycle amplification. The utmost release of C1 was observed in lane 9, indicating that the dual-cycle amplified DNA probes yielded the most favorable outcome. Quantitative analysis revealed that the release of C1 from the dual-cycle amplified DNA probes exceeded 80 % (Fig. S2). Additionally, the stability of C1/C2 and D1/D2 were demonstrated in Fig. S3, with no significant signal leakage observed. The strand displacement activated by UV irradiation was verified through PAGE and fluorescence experiments. Upon UV activation, the reporting probe specifically responds to target C, initiating a toehold-mediated chain displacement reaction, releasing C1, and forming C/C2 (Fig. S4a). As depicted in Fig. S4b, the peak signal is attained within 10 min of UV irradiation, consistent with PAGE analysis, suggesting the effective activation of the reporting probe with UV irradiation. The effectiveness of the DNA probes for miRNA detection in test tubes were further explored with fluorescence experiments. As depicted in Fig. 2a, Cy5 signal exhibited a significant enhancement upon the addition of miR155 following pre-UV treatment, indicating that the DNA probes were in an inactive state before UV irradiation. In the absence of miR155, minimal fluorescence intensity was observed under identical UV irradiation conditions. Moreover, without UV irradiation, the DNA probes remained inert, underscoring the remote controllability of their sensing performance and the ability to circumvent undesired positive signals. The responsiveness of the DNA probes to concentration variations of miR155 was evaluated. The DNA probes were initially treated with UV irradiation and then incubated with various concentrations of target miRNA. As a control, an identical treatment was applied to the DNA probes lacking UV irradiation. Upon activation with UV light, the fluorescence signal exhibited a progressive augmentation with increasing concentrations of miR155 (Fig. 2b, red line). The signal saturation was observed at 20 nM, without significant signal increase upon further concentration increase to 50 nM. Conversely, in the absence of UV irradiation, the lack of fluorescence signal increment was observed despite the concentrations increase of miR155 (Fig. 2b, yellow line). These findings highlight the light-responsive behavior of the DNA probes. Cycling amplification approaches were evaluated through fluorescence experiments. As illustrated in Fig. 2c, dual-cycle amplification produced significantly higher fluorescence intensity than the one-cycle amplification strategy at equivalent miR155 concentrations. Specifically, at a target concentration of 5 nM, the dual-cycle amplification strategy resulted in a remarkable 44-fold increase in fluorescence signal compared to the one-cycle strategy. The linear correlation between fluorescence intensity of Cy5 and the concentration of miR155 was demonstrated in the low concentration range in Fig. 2d. The specificity of the DNA probes for target miR155 and their ability to identify mismatched targets were then evaluated. Under UV light treatment, fluorescence signals generated by non-target miRNAs were negligible relative to miR155 (Fig. 2e). Conversely, in the absence of UV irradiation, changes in fluorescence signals triggered by miRNAs were negligible. The DNA probes exhibited the excellent ability to recognize both triple, double and single base mismatches at various positions (Fig. 2f). The target miR155 demonstrated the highest signal to noise ratio, outperforming all the other mismatched targets and non-targets (Table S2 and Table S3). These results demonstrate that the DNA probes have excellent sensitivity and specificity, facilitating effective discrimination of miR155 from other non-target miRNAs. Overall, these findings confirm the potential of the UV-activated DNA probes for miRNA detection in test tube.

Preparation and characterization of PAD

As shown in Fig. 3a, the synthesized UCs were originally stabilized by oleic acid (OA) and then the OA was removed with HCl for the coating of poly D-lysine (PDL). Subsequently, the DNA probes were electrostatically adsorbed onto the UCs surface to form PAD. A Tm-doped core-shell UCs capable of efficiently converting NIR light to UV light was utilized to overcome the low penetration depth of UV light in biological systems. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images confirmed the synthesis of uniform core and core-shell UCs (Fig. 3b and c). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image revealed a lattice spacing of 0.52 nm for the (100) crystallographic plane of UCs (Fig. 3c, inset). Combined with the characterization of powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. S5), it was determined that the synthesized core and core-shell UCs possess a standard hexagonal phase. Statistical analysis of TEM images revealed that the average particle size of UCs increased from 35.5 nm (core) to 37.5 nm (core-shell). Elemental mapping further confirmed the successful synthesis of the UCs, with component elements including Y, Tm, Yb, and Gd. The hollow distribution of Gd directly verified the formation of NaGdF4 shell (Fig. 3e). Core-shell UCs exhibited a significant fluorescence enhancement compared to the core UCs, attributed to the prevention of surface-induced quenching of emitters on the core UCs (Fig. 3f) [33]. Under 980 nm NIR light irradiation, UCs demonstrated two UV emission peaks at 347 nm and 363 nm, which align with the UVs wavelength required for the photo cleavage of the PC linker in DNA probes. This feature is advantageous for the remote activation of the DNA probes within the NIR window. We functionalized the surface of UCs with photocaged DNA probes to enhance cellular uptake efficiency. The assembly process was confirmed via dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis and zeta potential measurements (Fig. 3g and h). The hydrodynamic diameters for UCs, UC@PDL, and PAD were determined as 91, 110, and 163 nm, respectively, exhibiting a gradual increase with the coating of PDL and DNA probes. The Zeta potential values were 26.3, 42.9, and −13.4 mV, respectively. Both DLS and Zeta potential data provided additional affirmation of the surface modifications and assembly process. The sensing performance of PAD in solution after NIR light irradiation was investigated through fluorescence spectroscopy. Under NIR irradiation in solution, the PAD showed negligible fluorescence response to targets (Fig. S7), indicating that probe adsorption onto UCs severely restricts hybridization efficiency due to steric hindrance and altered reaction kinetics. Nevertheless, intracellular amphiphilic biomolecules (e.g., proteins, lipids) can competitively displace DNA probes adsorbed on the UCs surface, releasing functionalized probes for target recognition [34]. To evaluate the ability of PAD to avoid false positive signals, a serum stability study was conducted. PAD and free DNA probes were incubated in DMEM medium containing 10 % fetal bovine serum for 36 h. Compared to the free DNA probes, PAD exhibited a slight change in Cy5 intensity, while free DNA probes showed a significant increase in Cy5 signals (Fig. S8). This observation indicates that loading of DNA probes on UCs substantially enhances stability. With that in mind, the cytotoxicity of PAD was further evaluated using a CCK-8 assay (Fig. S9). The results indicated relatively low cytotoxicity of PAD towards HeLa cells, both in the presence and absence of NIR light irradiation. The hemolysis testing revealed a PAD hemolysis rate of 3.49 %, indicating excellent biocompatibility (Fig. S10). Then, we conducted a comparative analysis of the cellular uptake of PAD and free DNA probes (both labeled with Cy5, no quencher). As shown in Fig. S11, CLSM revealed that PAD was effectively internalized by cells, while free DNA probes exhibited negligible cellular uptake. Time-dependent co-localization study showed early-stage co-localization of Cy5 and Lyso-Tracker Green, followed by gradual escape from lysosome over time. The Pearson correlation coefficient decreased from 0.82 to 0.61, confirming efficient lysosomal escape, a critical requirement for intracellular miRNA imaging (Fig. S12). These results suggest that PAD exhibits good serum stability, low cytotoxicity, effective cellular uptake, and successful endosomal escape, assuring it as a promising platform for miRNA sensing in living cells.

NIR activated PAD for miRNA imaging in vitro

The feasibility of NIR light activated PAD for miRNA imaging in living cells was investigated. HeLa cells were pre-incubated with PAD for 2 h, followed by NIR light irradiation for 20 min (1.5 W cm−2, 5 min break after 1 min irradiation) and another h of incubation. Upon PC linker cleavage, C1∗/C2 transformed into C1/C2 with a toehold region capable of hybridizing with miR155. Signal amplification was attained through subsequent cooperative dual driving mechanism of mRNA and Hp. Additionally, the release of scissored strand from the PAD was investigated using CLSM. Comparison of the fluorescence intensity between the control PAD (C1/C2 (no PC linker and 6 nt tail), D1/D2, Hp) and the NIR-activated PAD (C1∗/C2 (with PC linker), D1/D2, Hp) revealed that 32 % of the scissored strand was released, thereby activating the DNA probes (Fig. S13). As shown in Fig. 4a, significant Cy5 signals were observed in the red channel after NIR light irradiation. Conversely, no notable changes in fluorescence were observed in the red channel without NIR light activation, indicating the spatial control of the NIR light mediated activation process. Similarly, in the control group (nPAD, no PC linker), negligible Cy5 fluorescence signals were detected regardless of NIR treatment or not (Fig. S14). This stresses the stability of the locked DNA probes within PAD, preventing recognition and unlocking by intracellular miRNAs prior UV activation. To further validate these findings, flow cytometry analysis was performed (Fig. 4b). The quantitative data obtained in flow cytometry demonstrated that the fluorescence signal was significantly enhanced after NIR irradiation (Fig. 4c). In the subsequent experiments, the potential of PAD for dual signal amplification in miRNA detection within cellular environments was the focus of interest. Adsorption of C1∗/C2 alone on UCs represented the no-cycle; adsorption of both C1∗/C2 and D1/D2 indicated a one-cycle; whereas adsorption of C1∗/C2, D1/D2, and Hp together comprise a dual-cycle probe. As shown in Fig. 4d, after NIR light treatment, no-cycle exhibited a weak fluorescence signal, while one-cycle showed a slight improvement. Conversely, the dual-cycle PAD treated group demonstrated a significant fluorescence signal, attributable to the dual-cycle signal amplification driven by mRNA and Hp. To validate the mRNA driving force of the first cycle, the expression levels of GAPDH mRNA and miR155 in different cell lines were quantified with qRT-PCR (Fig. S15 and Table S4). The results revealed a notably higher intracellular level of GAPDH mRNA compared to miR155, thus affirming the feasibility of GAPDH mRNA driving of the first cycle for cellular miRNA detection. CLSM results indicated a stronger fluorescence intensity for one-cycle compared to no-cycle. Moreover, the introduction of a substantial quantity of Hp facilitated the second cycle, as evidenced by CLSM, wherein dual-cycle PAD exhibited stronger fluorescence compared to one-cycle. Flow cytometry analysis, along with quantitative data (Fig. 4e and f), further validated these results. These results confirm that endogenous GAPDH mRNA and the addition of Hp initiate dual amplification cycles, thereby facilitating the desired signal amplification and enhancing the detection sensitivity of miRNAs in cells.

Detecting intracellular miRNA variations is essential for elucidating their roles in early tumor diagnosis, as miRNA expression exhibits stage-specific patterns during cancer progression. The capability for the PAD system to reflect the fluctuations of intracellular miR155 were investigated. Initially, we manipulated the expression level of miR155 using miR155 inhibitor or miR155 mimic. It was found that the introduction of miR155 mimic significantly enhanced the fluorescence signals, as observed in the CLSM images in Fig. 5a. Conversely, the downregulated group exhibited a notable reduction in the fluorescence signal. Flow cytometry analysis corroborated these trends (Fig. 5b), demonstrating a significant increase in fluorescence intensity upon miR155 mimic transfection, whereas miR155 inhibitor treatment markedly attenuated the signal. These observations were further quantified through flow cytometric analysis and validated by qRT-PCR (Fig. 5c and Fig. S16a), establishing a consistent positive correlation between miR155 expression levels and the magnitude of PAD-generated fluorescence. The universality of the NIR light activated PAD system was further explored. The relative expression levels of miR155 in three different cell line were already determined with qPCR. HeLa, MCF-7, and MCF-10A, represents high, medium, and low expression level of miR155, respectively (Fig. S15). These cell lines were pre-incubated with PAD and subsequently exposed to NIR treatment. As shown in Fig. 5d, CLSM images revealed the strongest fluorescence intensity in HeLa cells, followed by MCF-7, whereas MCF-10A exhibited negligible fluorescence signal. Flow cytometry data (Fig. 5e) confirmed the CLSM findings, with relative quantitative analysis indicating the miR155 abundance ranking as HeLa > MCF-7>MCF-10A (Fig. 5f). Additionally, we performed qRT-PCR to quantify miR155 expression levels across the three cell lines (Fig. 5f, Fig. S16b and Table S4), aligning with CLSM and flow cytometry results. These findings underscore the versatility of NIR light activated PAD system for precise miR155 imaging across different cell lines.

NIR activated PAD for miRNA imaging in vivo

Encouraged by the high spatiotemporal resolution achieved by PAD for miRNA imaging in living cells, we further evaluated the efficacy of PAD in vivo. To avoid the thermal effects generated by 980 nm NIR light irradiation, an interval irradiation strategy was employed (1.5 W cm−2, 5 min break after 1 min irradiation). With NIR treatment of 1 min, the instantaneous peak temperature on mouse skin reached 44 °C. This temperature rapidly returned to baseline in subsequent 5 min break (Fig. S17). The corresponding hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of the irradiated skin revealed no significant thermal damage, as compared with the control group (Fig. S18). Furthermore, no detectable ROS signal was observed in HeLa cells irradiated with NIR light (Fig. S19). Collectively, these results demonstrate the safety of the irradiation protocol. A HeLa xenograft tumor model on the right back of BALB/c nude mice was established. PAD or nPAD was administered via tail vein injection in mice to monitor the activation of miRNA in vivo. In the light activation experiments, tumor sites were either exposed to NIR light for 20 min or maintained in darkness. As shown in Fig. 6a, negligible fluorescence changes were observed in the tumor region of mice treated solely with PAD, whereas significant fluorescence enhancement was evident in tumors treated with PAD followed by NIR irradiation. Quantitative analysis revealed that after 2 h of NIR light treatment, the fluorescence intensity at the tumor site in PAD + NIR treated mice was 2.1 times higher than that in mice treated with PAD alone. Notably, ex vivo fluorescence imaging of organs revealed significant accumulation of the nanomaterial in liver and kidney. This biodistribution pattern is attributed to the particle size of PAD, which determines its predominant uptake by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), particularly within the liver [35]. In addition, high renal accumulation primarily results from the renal excretion of degraded DNA [36]. Finally, further validation of the biosafety of the system was provided through blood biochemical analysis (Fig. S21) and H&E staining of organs (Fig. S22) of mice after 7 days of administration. Overall, these results indicate the ability of NIR activated PAD for spatiotemporally controlled miRNA imaging in vivo.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed a photocaged amplified DNA nanodevice (PAD) that enables precise and sensitive imaging of miRNAs with spatiotemporal control under NIR light irradiation. The PAD employs the abundance of mRNA in living cells and excess of Hp as drivers. It can respond to external NIR light stimuli for DNA probes initiation at precise temporal and spatial coordinates. UCs can convert NIR light into UV light to overcome the limitations of traditional UV powered probes in terms of light penetration and phototoxicity. The NIR activated PAD system demonstrates remarkable spatial and temporal resolution of sensitive miRNA imaging in situ. Furthermore, the PAD system allows for the detection of fluctuations in miRNA expression levels in living cells, enabling precise miRNA imaging across various cell lines. We believe that the PAD system holds great potential in promoting the application of nanodevices employing different external stimuli and endogenous high-abundance molecules as fuels in biological and medical imaging in the future.
