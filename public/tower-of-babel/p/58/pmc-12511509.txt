The Effects of Cobicistat and Voriconazole on the Safety, Pharmacokinetics, and Pharmacodynamics of the TLR7 Agonist Vesatolimod in People with HIV
Authors: Yanan Zheng, Mary Wire, Robert Were Omange, Christiaan R deVries, Liao Zhang, Buyun Chen, Susie S Y Huang, Kimberly Cruz, Howard Hassman, Olayemi Osiyemi, Juan Carlos Rondon, Daina Lim, Steve West, Anita Wen, Jeffrey J Wallin, Devi SenGupta, Yanhui Cai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionVesatolimod (VES), a toll-like receptor 7 agonist, is being investigated as part of a strategy for human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) cure. VES is metabolized through the CYP3A pathway and is a substrate of the efflux transporters P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP). This study evaluated the impact of coadministration of VES with cobicistat (COBI; a P-gp, BCRP, and strong CYP3A inhibitor) or voriconazole (VOR; a strong CYP3A inhibitor with no effect on transporters) on the safety, pharmacokinetics (PK), and pharmacodynamics (PD) of VES in people with HIV (PWH).MethodsA total of 15 people with virally suppressed HIV on antiretroviral therapy received 2 mg VES (on day 1 in period 1, day 2 in period 2, and day 3 in period 3), 150 mg COBI once daily for 5 days in period 2, and 400 mg VOR on day 1 and 200 mg twice daily on days 2–6 in period 3. Blood samples were collected to measure VES PK, expression of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), cytokine/chemokine levels, and immune-cell phenotyping.ResultsCoadministration of VES with VOR did not impact VES PK. In contrast, coadministration of COBI increased VES maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), area under the concentration–time curve extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf), and half-life (t1/2) 7.5-, 4.3-, and 1.2-fold, respectively, but did not increase the mRNA expression levels of ISGs, serum cytokines/chemokines, or expression of activation markers on peripheral natural killer or T cells compared with VES alone. There were no serious adverse events, and no participants discontinued study drugs due to study-drug-related adverse events.ConclusionsVES was well tolerated when administered alone or in combination with COBI or VOR in PWH. Coadministration of COBI, but not VOR, increased plasma VES concentrations, suggesting that inhibition of transporters (P-gp and/or BCRP) may have a greater impact on VES PK than inhibition of the drug-metabolizing enzyme (CYP3A).Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT05458102.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40121-025-01227-x.Keywords: Vesatolimod, Toll-like receptor 7, Drug interactions, Safety, Cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A), Breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP), P-glycoprotein (P-gp), Interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs), Human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)

Introduction

Clinical care for approximately 39.9 million people with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV)-1 (PWH) relies mainly on chronic disease management with lifelong antiretroviral therapy (ART) [1, 2]. ART is an effective tool to fight HIV and has shown great success in prolonging life, lowering morbidity, and reducing risk of transmission [1]. Despite the benefits of ART, PWH remain at increased risk for chronic noninfectious inflammatory diseases, including liver and kidney problems, cancer, and depression/suicide [3–10].

The development of therapeutic interventions that can eradicate/clear and control virus reservoirs, leading to long-term, ART-free remission or cure in PWH, is an unmet medical need [11]. After viral entry into a target cell, HIV-1 stably integrates into the host’s genome, therefore establishing the basis for latent infection and the main barrier to HIV-1 cure. Neither the hosts immune system nor ART can eliminate transcriptionally inactive virus. Novel agents such as vesatolimod (VES; formerly GS-9620), a selective toll-like receptor-7 (TLR-7) agonist, that can modulate the immune system to enhance antigen presentation and T cell function may be necessary to maintain viral control as part of a strategy for cure or remission in PWH [12, 13].

VES, administered alone at doses of 4–8 mg in people with virally suppressed HIV who are on ART, has been shown to induce consistent and dose-dependent pharmacodynamic (PD) responses, including interferon-stimulated gene (ISG) expression, production of proinflammatory cytokines and chemokines, and activation of immune T cells that are critical to control HIV-1 infection [12, 13]. In non-human primate studies, sequential dosing regimens of VES with either therapeutic vaccines and/or broadly neutralizing antibodies (bNAbs) resulted in ART-free viral control in a subset of simian–human immunodeficiency virus (SHIV)-infected macaques [14–17]. In a phase 1b clinical trial in people with virologically suppressed HIV, a modest increase in time to viral rebound after ART interruption was observed in participants who received VES alone [12, 13]. In phase 2 clinical trials, VES has been evaluated for use with therapeutic vaccines and/or bNAbs in people with virologically suppressed HIV as part of a strategy for HIV cure (NCT04364035, NCT05281510, NCT06071767) [18–21]. A regimen of VES plus therapeutic vaccines in men with virologically suppressed HIV was safe, elicited robust T-cell responses, and 33.3% of participants who received the intervention remained off ART for 24 weeks [22]. In the first HIV cure trial conducted in Africa, with a unique population of women with virologically suppressed HIV, a regimen of VES plus two bNAbs was well tolerated with no serious study drug-related adverse events (AEs) reported, and 30% of participants remained off ART for more than 44 weeks [21]. These studies have demonstrated the safety of VES in HIV cure trials in diverse settings and highlight the potential of VES as part of a strategy for HIV cure.

Across clinical trials, the administration of VES alone has been shown to be generally safe and well tolerated in healthy participants, people with hepatitis B, people with hepatitis C, and in PWH on ART for multiple dosing up to 12 mg [12, 23–25]. Flu-like symptoms are known to occur after the initial dose of VES administration. Notably, dose-dependent increases in flu-like AEs of interest (AEIs) were associated with increased VES exposure and PD responses [26]. To optimize the balance between efficacy and tolerability, subsequent studies implemented a dose cap of VES 8 mg. In the HIV cure studies of VES [18–21], 6 or 8 mg of VES was administered every 2 weeks with intra-patient dose escalation and adaptive monitoring.

VES is metabolized through the CYP3A pathway and is a substrate of the efflux transporters P-glycoprotein (P-gp) and breast cancer resistance protein (BCRP). Medications that inhibit these transporters and/or drug-metabolizing enzymes may impact the pharmacokinetics (PK), PD, and the safety of VES. Two such medications, cobicistat (COBI), a PK enhancer used in combination with some HIV-1 protease inhibitors and integrase strand transfer inhibitors as part of a standard ART regimen, and voriconazole (VOR), an antifungal agent used to treat serious fungal infections such as invasive aspergillosis and deep tissue Candida infections that may affect PWH, are both strong CYP3A inhibitors. In clinical drug-interaction studies, COBI and VOR decreased oral clearance of the probe CYP3A substrate midazolam by ≥ 90% [27, 28]. In addition to being an inhibitor of CYP3A, COBI is an inhibitor of P-gp and BCRP in vitro [29], and coadministration of COBI increased the maximum plasma concentration (Cmax) of probe P-gp substrates digoxin 1.41-fold and dabigatran 2.27-fold in clinical drug interaction studies [30, 31]. In contrast, VOR did not inhibit P-gp or BCRP in vitro, and in a clinical drug interaction study, coadministration of VOR had no effect on the PK of digoxin [32, 33].

Here, we present the results from our study informing potential mechanism(s) of drug interactions of VES when coadministered with COBI or VOR in people with ART-suppressed HIV.

Methods

Study Design and Treatment

An open-label, multi-cohort phase 1 study (NCT05458102) was conducted across five clinical sites in the United States to evaluate the impact of coadministration of VES with COBI or VOR on the safety, PK, and PD of VES (Fig. 1). Participants received 2 mg VES in each of three study periods. A subtherapeutic dose of 2 mg was selected due to the potential for increase of VES exposure, leading to AEs. In period 1, participants received VES alone on day 1. In period 2, participants received 150 mg COBI once daily for 5 days and VES on day 2. In period 3, participants received 400 mg VOR twice daily on day 1, VOR 200 mg twice daily on days 2–6, and VES on day 3. All study drugs were administered orally. The study included a washout period of 7–14 days between periods 1 and 2 and of 14–21 days between periods 2 and 3. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki of 1964 and its later amendments. An institutional review board (Advarra IRB, Columbia, MD, USA) approved the protocol. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Study Population

This study enrolled adults (age ≥ 18 years) with documented plasma HIV-1 RNA levels < 50 copies/mL at screening and for at least 6 months before the screening visit (using all available viral load measurements) and a CD4+ T-cell count of ≥ 350 cells/μL. Participants were required to be on an ART regimen for ≥ 6 consecutive months, with no change in the ART regimen in the 2 months before screening. The ART regimen for eligible participants included medications not expected to interact with VES, COBI, or VOR (i.e., bictegravir, dolutegravir, raltegravir, or doravirine in combination with nucleos(t)ide reverse transcriptase inhibitors), and participants were advised to avoid a list of prohibited medications from 14 days before study drug dosing through to the follow-up visit. Participants with CYP2C19*2/*2, CYP2C19*2/*3, or CYP2C19*3/*3 (slow metabolizer genotypes) were excluded to avoid high VOR exposures. Genotyping of CYP2C19 was conducted at screening using the Illumina next-generation sequencing platform (HistoGenetics, Ossining, NY, USA).

Additionally, participants were excluded if they were pregnant or lactating; had active chronic hepatitis B and/or hepatitis C virus infection; were treated with systemic steroids, immunosuppressant therapies, or chemotherapeutic agents within 3 months before screening; or had any serious or active medical or psychiatric illness that, in the opinion of the clinical investigator(s), had potential to interfere with the participant’s treatment, assessment, or compliance with the study protocol.

Study Objectives and Assessments

The primary objectives were to evaluate the safety and PK of VES when administered alone or in combination with COBI or VOR. The exploratory objective was to assess the impact of these medications on the VES PD, immune-cell activation, and markers of inflammation.

Safety was evaluated in all participants who received at least one dose of study drug. Treatment-emergent AEs (TEAEs), including serious AEs (SAEs) and AEs leading to discontinuation of treatment, were recorded up to 30 days after the last administered dose of any study drug and summarized by treatment, preferred term, and coded according to MedDRA version 26.1. Severity grades were defined by the Gilead grading scale for severity of AEs and laboratory abnormalities [26]. Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0 was used for cytokine release syndrome (CRS). Additionally, flu-like AEIs were analyzed and included the following preferred terms: any grade of pyrexia, chills, influenza-like illness, influenza, and CRS, and grade ≥ 2 myalgia, headache, fatigue, and malaise, as reported previously [26].

Relative to each dose of VES, plasma samples for VES PK were collected predose, and at 0.25, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h postdose. Plasma concentrations of VES were determined using a validated bioanalytical method developed at QPS Holdings, LLC (Newark, DE, USA). After liquid–liquid extraction, samples were injected into the high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectroscopy using electrospray ionization in positive mode. Transitions for VES and its internal standard GS-714090 (VES-d8) were monitored at mass-to-charge ratio (m/z) 411.4 → 105.2 and m/z 419.4 → 105.2, respectively. The calibrated range was 50–10,000 pg/mL. The inter-day precision ranged from 4.0% to 4.5% coefficient of variation, and the inter-day accuracy varied between 6.0% and 9.3% relative error.

Relative to each dose of VES, blood/serum samples were collected predose and at 4, 8, 12, 24, 48, and 96 h postdose for PD analysis, immune-cell phenotyping, and evaluating markers of inflammation. Fold changes from baseline or absolute levels of PD biomarkers (including ISG mRNA and serum cytokines/chemokines) and immune-cell activation were evaluated. The expression level of ISG (ISG15, MX1, OAS1) and TLR7 mRNA was analyzed by TaqMan real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) method (Labcorp, Indianapolis, IN, USA). Levels of serum cytokines/chemokines, including interleukin (IL)-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1RA), CXC motif chemokine ligands (CXCL10, CXCL11), IL-6, and TNFα, were measured by multiplex bead array, and IFNα was quantified using SIMOA (both at Rules-Based Medicine, Austin, TX, USA); C-reactive protein was measured via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) at Meso Scale Diagnostics (Rockville, MD, USA). Immunophenotyping analyses included staining of whole blood specimens collected at multiple time points corresponding to VES dosage, including predose and at 24, 72, and 96 h postdose across the three periods. Flow cytometry was used to analyze the expression of activation markers on T or natural killer (NK) cells (CD69, HLA-DR, and/or CD38) (Labcorp, Indianapolis, IN, USA) (Table S1).

Statistical Analyses

PK parameters for VES were estimated using Phoenix WinNonlin software version 8.2.2 (Certara, Radnor, PA, USA) using standard noncompartmental methods. Means and standard deviations of VES concentrations were plotted versus time by treatment. VES PK parameters were summarized using descriptive statistics and as box plots by treatment. PK parameters were compared separately between VES + COBI versus VES and VES + VOR versus VES using a mixed-effects analysis of variance model fitted to the natural log-transformed values of VES area under the concentration–time curve (AUC) extrapolated to infinity (AUCinf), AUC to last quantifiable concentration (AUClast), Cmax, and half-life (t1/2) using SAS PROC MIXED (SAS, Cary, NC, USA). The statistical model included treatment as a fixed effect and participant as a random effect. Nonparametric analysis of time to maximum VES concentration (Tmax) was performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for fixed sequence between the test and reference treatments.

Nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed to compare predose and postdose biomarker levels within each period. Correction of P-values for multiple testing using false discovery rate (FDR) was performed for each analysis. A generalized linear mixed-effect model was used for exploratory analysis of PD biomarker data, in which time point and period were fixed effects and participant ID was used as a random effect. The input data were log-transformed and longitudinal plots with error bars, estimating the marginal means and confidence intervals, were generated along with P values. The objective of the analysis was to identify treatment effects between each period, or treatment effect at any postdose time point, using the baseline as a reference. Additionally, the linear mixed-effects model was used to compare treatment effects within and among three different periods, and to quantify the responses above baseline as a reference point. The relationship between VES PK exposure (ln[Cmax]) and maximum fold changes in ISGs and cytokines/chemokines was evaluated using Pearson correlation.

Results

A total of 15 participants were enrolled and received at least one dose of study drug. Most participants were assigned male at birth (86.7%) and were Black or African American (66.7%) and not Hispanic or Latino (80.0%) (Table 1). The median age of participants was 48 years (range, 25–69), and the mean (standard deviation) body mass index was 28.9 (4.7) kg/m2. Of the participants, 11 (73.3%) completed the study, while 4 withdrew prematurely: 2 after period 1 and 2 after period 2. Reasons for discontinuation from study were the participant’s decision (n = 2; 13.3%) and AE and protocol violation (n = 1 each; 6.7%).

Safety

Ten participants (66.7%) experienced at least one TEAE during the study (Table 2). Most TEAEs were grade 1 in severity, except one grade 2 TEAE of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) leading to premature discontinuation of study drug. There were no participants with grade 3–4 or serious TEAEs. No events of CRS were reported, and no deaths occurred during the study. Seven participants (46.7%) had TEAEs considered related to study drugs, all grade 1 in severity. Of these, one (headache) was considered related only to VES. One participant experienced nausea and decreased appetite after administration of COBI, and both AEs were considered related to both VES and COBI (Table 2). Another two participants after administration of COBI experienced grade 1 headaches considered related to COBI; five participants who were administered VOR experienced photopsia, vision blurred, visual impairment, photophobia, dizziness, and hallucinations. Additionally, flu-like AEIs were reported in three participants (20%) who received VES; all were grade 1 and included headache (13.3%; n = 2) and nausea (6.7%; n = 1).

Pharmacokinetic Evaluations

All 15 participants were included in the VES PK analysis set (15 in period 1, 13 in period 2, and 11 in period 3). Median Tmax values were 3.0, 1.5, and 2.5 h for VES 2 mg, VES + COBI, and VES + VOR, respectively (Table 3). After Tmax, VES plasma concentrations decreased biexponentially (Fig. 2a). When VES was coadministered with COBI, median Tmax occurred 1.5 h earlier, geometric mean VES Cmax, AUCinf, and t1/2 increased 7.5-, 4.3-, and 1.2-fold, respectively (Table 3; Fig. 2b). When VES was coadministered with VOR, there were no significant changes in VES Cmax, AUCinf, Tmax, or t1/2 compared with VES administered alone (Table 3, Fig. 2b).

Pharmacodynamic Evaluations

Administration of VES, alone or with COBI or VOR, led to significant increases above baseline (P < 0.05) in mRNA levels of ISGs (OAS1, MX1, ISG15) and TLR7 (Fig. 3a). The levels of OAS1 were significantly increased from baseline by VES alone and when it was coadministered with COBI, but did not increase with VOR. The significant upregulation of OAS1 was observed at 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h postdose (adjusted [adj.] P < 0.05) in period 1 (VES alone), and at 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h postdose (adj. P < 0.05) in period 2 (VES + COBI). The expression of ISG15 significantly increased relative to predose time points at 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h postdose (adj. P < 0.01) in period 1 (VES alone), and at 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h postdose in both period 2 (VES + COBI) (adj. P < 0.05) and period 3 (VES + VOR) (adj. P < 0.01). A significant upregulation of MX1 was observed at 8, 12, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h postdose (adj. P < 0.05) in period 1 (VES alone), but limited to 8, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h postdose (adj. P < 0.05) in period 2 (VES + COBI) and 12, 24, and 96 h postdose (adj. P < 0.01) in period 3 (VES + VOR). TLR7 mRNA expression after 12 h was significantly upregulated in all three periods (adj. P < 0.01) (Fig. 3a).

To compare treatment effects within and among three different periods and to quantify the responses above baseline as a reference point (Fig. 4), the linear mixed-effects model analysis was used. There was a significant increase in expression of ISG15 between 12 and 24 h in all three periods, MX1 between 8 and 24 h in all three periods, and OAS1 between 12 and 48 h in periods 1 and 2, while TLR7 mRNA expression increased at 12 h in all three periods, above baseline levels. However, the model comparison of the magnitude of these responses between the three periods showed no differences and found that ISG responses were comparable between VES only, VES + COBI, and VES + VOR (Fig. 4).

There were no significant changes from baseline in IFNα, IL-6, IL-1RA, and CRP CXCL11 and CXCL10 at postdose in any of the study periods (Fig. 3b). The linear mixed-effects model showed that levels of IFNα between 8 and 48 h and IL-1RA between 12 and 24 h increased above baseline in period 1 (VES only) but not when VES was coadministered with either COBI or VOR (Fig. 4). Overall, there were no differences in the frequencies of CD38+ HLA-DR+ CD4+ T cells, CD38+ HLA-DR+ CD8+ T cells, CD69+ NK cells, CD38+ CD4+ T cells, CD38+ CD8+ T cells, CD69+ CD4+ T cells, and CD69+ CD8+ T cells at baseline when compared with postdose, and among the three periods (Fig. 3c).

Collectively, these data show that administration of 2 mg VES alone or with COBI or VOR led to consistent increases in VES PD responses (ISG15, MX1, OAS1, IFNα, and IL-1RA) and TLR7 mRNA transcription, but had no significant impact on immune-cell activation or markers of inflammation (including IL-6 and CRP).

Correlation of VES PK and VES PD Responses

Maximum fold changes from baseline in ISG15, MX1, and OAS1 mRNA levels correlated positively with VES Cmax in period 1, but generally not in periods 2 or 3, or across all periods, showing a lack of correlation between systemic VES exposure and PD responses (Fig. S1). In addition, maximum fold changes from baseline in IFNα, IL-1RA, CXCL10, and CXCL11 were generally not correlated with VES Cmax (Fig. S1).

Discussion

VES is currently being studied in people with virologically suppressed HIV in phase 2 HIV cure clinical trials [18–20]. To avoid the potential for drug interactions with VES, HIV cure clinical trials prohibit the use of inhibitors of CYP3A, P-gp, and/or BCRP with VES. COBI and VOR are medications commonly used by PWH and were selected for this study based on their PK properties to allow an assessment of the impact of coadministration of inhibitors of P-gp and BCRP and/or CYP3A on the safety, PK, and PD of VES in people with ART-suppressed HIV. We found that VES 2 mg was well tolerated when administered alone or in combination with COBI or VOR in PWH. Coadministration of VES with COBI resulted in a greater increase in VES Cmax (7.5-fold) than AUCinf (4.3-fold), an earlier VES Tmax, and a minor change in VES t1/2 compared with VES alone, suggesting that increased absorption (due to the inhibition of efflux transporters), rather than reduced metabolism, was primarily responsible for the increased VES concentrations. In contrast, when VES was coadministered with VOR, there were no significant changes in VES Cmax, AUCinf, Tmax, or t1/2 compared with VES alone, further indicating that inhibition of transporters was primarily responsible for the increased VES concentrations observed with COBI. Coadministration of COBI or VOR did not increase the mRNA expression levels of ISGs, serum cytokines/chemokines, or expression of activation markers on peripheral natural killer or T cells compared with VES alone.

P-gp and BCRP (efflux transporters) are present in intestinal epithelia, hepatocytes, and kidneys (P-gp expression only). P-gp and BCRP transporters limit absorption of some oral drug substrates and facilitate transport of some drugs into bile and urine [34]. However, nonclinical data show that VES is not excreted to a significant extent in bile or urine (Gilead, data on file), suggesting that systemic inhibition of transporters minimally affects VES concentrations. Therefore, most of the interaction between VES and COBI likely occurs primarily in the intestine, where COBI enhances VES absorption by inhibiting P-gp and BCRP in the intestinal epithelia.

Single 2 mg doses of VES, administered alone or in combination with inhibitors of its transporters and/or drug-metabolizing enzymes, were observed to be safe in virologically suppressed adults with HIV-1 on ART. No new safety concerns were identified in this drug interaction study and most AEs were grade 1 in severity. Flu-like AEIs and their potential association with VES mechanism of action have been investigated previously [26]. In a pooled analysis of eight clinical studies (N = 606), the incidence of flu-like AEIs was 15% in participants who received the 2 mg VES dose and demonstrated a dose-dependent increase with VES dose (24%, 43%, and 58% in the 4, 8, and 10/12 mg dose groups, respectively) [26]. The reports of flu-like AEIs in our study (3/15; 20%) were consistent with the pooled analysis. The most commonly reported flu-like AEIs were headache and nausea. Lack of significant increases in markers of inflammation (TNFα, IFNγ, and CRP) in study participants, despite coadministration of VES with COBI or VOR, supports the choice of dosing 2 mg VES from a safety perspective.

TLR7, the receptor for VES, is highly expressed in the endosomal membranes of plasmacytoid dendritic cells. Plasmacytoid dendritic cells in the bone marrow can mature and migrate into specific tissues, including gut and lymph nodes, under steady-state conditions [35]. Intestinal plasmacytoid dendritic cells, unlike their counterparts in other tissues, show limited migratory capacity, both in steady state and upon TLR7 stimulation, suggesting that presystemic stimulation may be important for triggering downstream TLR7 signaling. Oral administration of 2 mg VES, either alone or when coadministered with COBI or VOR, resulted in comparable increases in expression of PD ISGs. However, no changes were observed in VES PD cytokines (IFNα, CXCL10, IL1RA, or CXCL11). Interestingly, the increased plasma VES concentrations when coadministered with COBI did not significantly enhance VES PD responses or immune-cell activation compared with VES alone. Moreover, no correlation was found between systemic VES exposure and PD responses across the three periods. One possible explanation for this lack of association between VES PK and PD responses is that the presystemic VES exposure in the gut tissues may play a role in the induction of PD responses and activation of immune response cells. However, the mechanism for VES to directly stimulate TLR7 in the gut tissue regardless of intestinal efflux transporter activity is unknown. Another possible explanation for the lack of PK-PD correlation is the use of a subtherapeutic dose of VES along with high variability in PD responses and the small sample size in the study.

The VES dosage used in this study (2 mg) is lower than the therapeutic dose (6–8 mg) typically used in PWH [13, 26]. This difference in dosage necessitates caution when extrapolating the findings to the therapeutic doses of VES, especially considering that VES’s PD responses and safety are dose-dependent [26]. In this study, despite increased plasma concentrations of VES when coadministered with COBI, there were no significant safety findings related to VES and no apparent changes in its PD effects. However, further research may be needed to confirm these results at clinically relevant doses (6–8 mg). Another limitation of the study is the relatively small sample size. While adequate for characterizing VES PK, the sample size might not be sufficient to assess PD effects, given the large variation in biomarker responses that were observed in this study population. Lastly, since COBI inhibits both transporters and CYP3A, further investigation of the impact of drugs that inhibit only P-gp or BCRP, and not CYP3A, would be useful to further characterize the mechanism of interaction important for VES PK.

Conclusions

The data from this study may help inform dosing strategies, drug combination selections, and safety monitoring protocols for HIV cure clinical trials of VES in PWH. In addition, these data may guide the design of future drug interaction studies, with a focus on assessing transporter-specific interactions and evaluating dose staggering (i.e., administering VES and the inhibitor at different times during the day) as a potential strategy to mitigate interactions.
