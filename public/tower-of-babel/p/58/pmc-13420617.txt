Case SummaryA 41-year-old man without any medical history presents to the emergency department with acute, episodic palpitations. Initial work-up with laboratory testing yielded negative results. Electrocardiography revealed sinus rhythm with intermittent pre-excitation. The patient was discharged home with follow-up.Take-Home MessageIntermittent pre-excitation still carries risk, often leading to consideration of an electrophysiological study to determine the presence of clinically significant anterograde conduction properties.

Clinical vignette

A 41-year-old man with no significant medical history presents to the emergency department with several episodes of palpitations over the course of 2 days and associated left arm pain. Laboratory testing, examination, and urine drug screening were unremarkable. Choice A is correct because intermittent pre-excitation does not guarantee a low-risk accessory pathway (AP). There is equipoise regarding whether an invasive test is justified in a patient with no documented arrhythmia and intermittent pre-excitation. However, although some patients may want noninvasive testing to assess for arrhythmia correlation, a conversation should be held with the patient to discuss the indication for EPS due to data suggesting some benefit of risk stratifying an intermittent pre-excitation pattern.1 Some patients still have rapidly conducting pathways potentially allowing for fast conduction during atrial arrhythmias and risk of sudden cardiac death (SCD). An EPS can reveal properties requiring catheter ablation.2

Choice B is incorrect because the presence alone of an intermittent pre-excitation pattern still carries the possibility of high-risk features. At minimum, an ambulatory ECG monitor would be helpful because the patient is symptomatic with palpitations.

Choice C is incorrect because flecainide is used in the setting of arrhythmias. This patient has a pre-excitation pattern with symptoms (palpitations) but no evidence of tachyarrhythmia.

Choice D is incorrect because his ECG already demonstrates an accessory-pathway block; thus, determining the rate of the block is not clinically relevant. In addition, sudden loss of pre-excitation during exercise stress testing has a low negative predictive value in excluding high-risk accessory pathways.3 Ambulatory ECG monitoring could be helpful before exercise stress testing to determine a persistent pre-excitation pattern or high-risk features. However, this patient is also symptomatic with palpitations, thus favoring a discussion regarding invasive testing.

This ECG shows sinus rhythm with intermittent pre-excitation, which reflects an accessory pathway without development of Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome in the absence of a symptomatic tachyarrhythmia. Although intermittent pre-excitation typically indicates poor anterograde conduction of an accessory pathway with a lower risk of fast ventricular response, this pattern still places the patient at risk for arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation and atrioventricular reciprocating tachycardia due to catecholamine responsiveness of some pathways, and there is little consensus regarding the long-term risk of developing a malignant ventricular arrhythmia.4 The presence alone of intermittent pre-excitation does not exclude the possibility of high-risk features determined via EPS, such as inducibility, rapid anterograde conductive properties, and multiple accessory pathways. Catheter ablation is recommended in patients with at least one of the following: documented atrioventricular reciprocating tachycardia, multiple APs, or rapid antegrade conductive AP properties (effective refractory period or shortest pre-excited RR interval <250 milliseconds at baseline or induced).5 This patient is having symptoms with Wolff-Parkinson-White pattern; thus, catheter-based evaluation is appropriate to consider and justifies a discussion with the patient regarding further electrophysiological evaluation to anticipate the presence of clinically significant anterograde conduction properties as a marker of high risk of SCD. Even if asymptomatic, SCD may be the first symptom for patients, and thus EPS can carry utility in risk stratification.6Take-Home Message•Intermittent pre-excitation still carries risk, often leading to consideration of an electrophysiological study to determine the presence of clinically significant anterograde conduction properties.
