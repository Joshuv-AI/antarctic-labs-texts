Highlight: A Tough Shell to Crack—Mineral Chemistry Drives Shell Evolution in Invertebrates
Authors: Pedro Andrade
Published: 2026

The origin and diversification of the major animal lineages throughout the Ediacaran and Cambrian periods are well-known examples of evolutionary success (Smith and Harper 2013). The relatively “sudden” appearance of modern animal body plans in the fossil record over 500 Ma coincides with the convergent evolution of biomineralized skeletons, i.e. animal hard parts such as shells, exoskeletons, or bones, composed of biologically produced inorganic minerals embedded in an organic matrix. Minerals make up the majority of the composite skeleton; however, diverse sets of matrix proteins play central roles in incorporating these minerals through the development of these hard parts.

The Lophotrochozoa—the diverse clade including mollusks, annelids, bivalves, brachiopods, and other marine groups—are particularly noteworthy for their skeletal diversity. “During the Cambrian, lophotrochozoans emerged as key representatives of early biomineralized animals, marking a pivotal shift from the soft-bodied Ediacaran biota to the widespread appearance of skeletal structures,” says Linlin Zhang, professor at the Institute of Oceanology of the Chinese Academy of Sciences. While calcium carbonate-based skeletons predominate in modern lophotrochozoans, some species have retained phosphate, iron, or even mixed mineralization systems.

Are these diverse strategies a result of independent evolutionary trajectories? This question motivated a new article led by Zhang and her team, now published in Genome Biology and Evolution (Chen et al. 2026). Previous studies had already suggested that biomineralization in animals is broadly associated with a shared “molecular toolkit,” i.e. common sets of ancestral genes that were independently co-opted for building skeletons (e.g. Murdock 2020). Most studies have focused on species with calcium carbonate-based skeletons. As a result, the degree to which the ancestral “toolkit” hypothesis explains broader evolutionary patterns of biomineralization remains unknown. In other words, do shells composed of different minerals share similarities at the molecular level?

To address this question from an alternative perspective, Chen et al. (2026) generated a complete reference genome and shell proteome for a unique lophotrochozoan: the brachiopod Lingula anatina (Fig. 1). Lingula anatina is often described as a “living fossil” due to its extreme morphological similarity to Cambrian brachiopods. Importantly for Zhang's team, its shell is made of calcium-phosphate. To the authors, generating this genome resource was pivotal to their work: “this high-quality genomic resource allowed us to integrate proteomics and comparative genomic approaches,” tells us Yaru Chen, cofirst author of the article (Fig. 2).

He pursued a combined master's and PhD degree in Marine Biology at the Institute of Oceanology, Chinese Academy of Sciences. During his studies, he focused on biodiversity in deep-sea extreme environments and the adaptive roles of modular traits in such conditions. Using Polychaeta as his research model, he systematically studied the biogeographic distribution patterns, evolutionary history of appendage modular traits, and the omics basis of the novel appendage modular trait, “branching gills,” which is key to adaptation in cold seep environments.

Next, the authors combined the L. anatina shell proteome data with those from six distantly related lophotrochozoan species whose shells are constituted by calcium, phosphate, or iron. Strikingly, nearly 40% of the L. anatina shell matrix proteins were unique, and from a combined set of 737 shell matrix proteins, not a single one was shared across all seven species. This low conservation at the sequence level contrasted with higher conservation at the protein domain level: specific structural domains—including a ubiquitous EF-hand domain responsible for calcium-binding—were repeatedly preserved. These results suggest that lophotrochozoan shell proteins may have high evolutionary lability while preserving specific functional domains.

If shell protein composition is highly labile, a natural follow-up hypothesis would be that environmental factors may explain the evolution of shell proteins across these major lophotrochozoan groups. Consistent with this hypothesis, Chen et al. (2026) found that the composition of shell matrix proteins correlates more strongly with mineralization chemistry than with phylogenetic relationships, at both the sequence and the protein domain levels.

These findings are consistent with environmentally mediated convergent evolution. But they also point to how evolutionary trajectories constrain the range of environments available to organisms. “These results highlight the dual role of environmental context in evolution as both a driver of innovation and a filter of possibilities,” according to Jie Li, also cofirst author of the study (Fig. 2). To him, “the intrinsic properties of minerals, such as solubility and crystal structure, impose biophysical constraints, requiring compatibility between matrix proteins and mineral chemistry, thereby narrowing the molecular tools available.”

According to the authors, these highly conserved functional protein domains across species indicate that modular evolution by co-option is a key mechanism driving adaptation in the Lophotrochozoa. “By modifying and recombining conserved modules in their biomineralization ‘toolkit’, these organisms could produce structures with distinct mechanical properties, compositions, and morphologies, thereby maximizing their evolutionary potential,” Chen explains.

This research by Zhang and her team underscores how remarkable phenotypic diversity may arise from repurposing an existing “molecular toolkit.” More broadly, it provides a new perspective on one of the most important stages in the evolution of animal life. Zhang concludes that “by linking molecular mechanisms to environmental changes, lophotrochozoans shed light on how biomineralization catalyzed the rapid diversification of life during the Cambrian, permanently transforming marine ecosystems.”
