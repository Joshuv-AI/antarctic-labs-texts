Zinc transporter ZnT5 is associated with epithelial mesenchymal transition via SMAD1 in breast cancer
Authors: Erina Iwabuchi, Yasuhiro Miki, Junyao Xu, Ayako Kanai, Takanori Ishida, Hironobu Sasano, Takashi Suzuki
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractZinc levels in breast cancer tissues have been reported to be higher than those in normal tissues. In addition, the expression levels of zinc transporters, including ZnT5 and ZnT6, are reportedly higher in breast cancer than in normal breast tissues. ZnT5 and ZnT6 also contribute to heterodimer formation and are involved in several biological functions. However, the functions of ZnT5 and ZnT6 heterodimers in breast cancer remain unknown. Therefore, we first investigated the immunolocalization of ZnT5 and ZnT6 in pathological breast cancer specimens and in MCF‐7 and T‐47D breast cancer cells. Next, we used small interfering RNA to assess cell viability and migration in ZnT5 knockdown MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells. Immunohistochemical analysis showed that the number of ZnT5‐positive breast cancer cells was inversely correlated with the pathologic N factor status. ZnT5 knockdown had no effect on cell viability in the presence of 100 μM ZnCl2 in MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells. In a wound healing assay, 100 μM ZnCl2 treatment inhibited cell migration of MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells, whereas ZnT5 knockdown promoted cell migration, decreased E‐cadherin expression and increased vimentin, slug and matrix metalloproteinase 9 expression. Antibody arrays showed that ZnT5 knockdown increased the expression of SMAD1, and that dorsomorphin treatment inhibited the promotion of migratory ability induced by ZnT5 knockdown. The results of this study revealed that both ZnT5 may be involved in less aggressive breast cancer subtypes, possibly through inhibition of cell migration.

BACKGROUND

Zn is involved in various processes such as transcription, intracellular signal transduction, cell proliferation and cell invasion in normal cells. In some cancers, zinc concentrations in the blood and tissues are different compared to that in physiological states. 1 , 2 Margalioth et al. 3 reported that zinc levels increased by 72% in breast cancer tissue and decreased in the carcinoma of the kidney. Zowczak et al. 1 reported that the mean serum zinc concentration decreases only in metastatic gynaecological cancers. Gumulec et al. 4 conducted a meta‐analysis of 22,737 cases using publications in the Web of Science, PubMed, Embase and CENTRAL and reported decreased serum zinc levels in patients with lung, head and neck, breast, liver, stomach and prostate cancers. More specific zinc patterns were evident at the tissue level, showing an increase in breast cancer tissue and a decrease in prostate, liver, lung and thyroid cancer tissues. Increased concentrations of zinc in breast cancer tissues have been reported in several studies. Geraki et al. 2 reported that zinc levels were higher in breast cancer tissue than in normal breast tissue as assessed using an Atube X‐ray system. In addition, they reported that the mean zinc concentrations in specimens obtained from healthy individuals differed significantly from the mean zinc concentrations in histologically normal tissues adjacent to breast tumours. 2 Therefore, cancer tissue‐specific elevation of zinc concentrations has been observed; however, the underlying mechanism remains unclear. In addition, zinc transporters are responsible for the intracellular and extracellular transport of zinc and the intracellular and intracellular organelle transport of zinc. The ZnT family of Zn transporters (SLC30A1–10 family) includes 10 members (ZnT1–10) that transport Zn from the cytoplasm directly across the cell membrane or into intracellular organelles. 5 The ZIP family of Zn transporters (SLC39A1–14 family) contains 14 members (ZIP1–14) and facilitates the transport of Zn across the cell membrane or from intracellular organelles to the cytoplasm. 5 Cellular zinc management is regulated by metallothioneins (MTs), which are zinc‐binding proteins that buffer cytosolic zinc. 6 The expression and function of several zinc transporters have been reported in breast cancer. Several types of transporters have been reported to be altered in the breast cancer cell lines T‐47D and MDA‐MB‐231 compared to the non‐malignant cell line MCF10A. 7 The expression levels of zinc transporters, including ZnT5 and ZnT6, in breast cancer were reported to be higher than those in normal breast tissue. 8 ZnT5 is expressed in the cell membrane and Golgi apparatus, whereas ZnT6 is expressed only in the Golgi apparatus. 9 In addition, ZnT5 and ZnT6 contribute to heterodimer formation and are involved in several biological functions. 10 Autotaxin, matrix metalloproteinase 9 (MMP9) and carbonic anhydrase IX, which play crucial roles in tumour formation, invasion and metastasis, require ZnT5–ZnT6 heterodimers for their activation and secretion in chicken DT40 cells. 11 However, the significance of the expression of ZnT5 and ZnT6 in breast cancer remains unknown.

Therefore, in this study, we performed immunohistochemistry on human breast cancer tissues to investigate the relationship between ZnT5 and ZnT6 immunoreactivity and clinicopathological factors. It has been reported that ZnT5 forms a heterodimer with ZnT6, and that ZnT5 also forms a homodimer with each other; however, it has also been reported that ZnT6 does not form a homodimer. 10 We examined the effects of ZnT5 on the viability and migration of breast cancer cells using small interfering RNA (siRNA) in cultured breast cancer cells. An antibody array was performed to examine the relationship between ZnT5 expression and signalling pathways.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Breast cancer tissues

Invasive ductal breast cancer tissue samples were collected from 114 patients during surgery (FFPE: 10% neutral formaldehyde embedded in paraffin). This study was approved by the ethics committee of Tohoku University (2021‐1‐553).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry was performed as previously described, 12 , 13 using the biotin–streptavidin method with a Histofine kit (Nichirei Biosciences, Tokyo, Japan). Paraffin‐embedded sections were prepared using heat‐induced epitope retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). After blocking non‐specific sites, the sections were incubated with a mouse monoclonal anti‐ZnT5 antibody (1:200; Abnova, Taipei, Taiwan) and rabbit polyclonal anti‐ZnT6 antibody (1:200; Atlas Antibodies, Stockholm, Sweden). Sections were then blocked endogenous peroxidases, incubated with a biotinylated secondary antibody and peroxidase‐labelled streptavidin. After staining with 3,3′‐diaminobenzidine, the slides were counterstained with haematoxylin. Both ZnT5 and ZnT6 immunoreactivities were evaluated as either high or low, with a cutoff value of 50% for positively stained cancer cells.

Cell culture

MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, VA, USA). The cells were maintained in RPMI‐1640 medium (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) supplemented with 10% foetal bovine serum (FBS) (Biosera, Nuaille, France).

Immunofluorescence

The MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells were seeded onto glass chamber slides and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then fixed, permeabilized and blocked with 10% goat serum prior to incubation with anti‐ZnT5 and anti‐ZnT6 antibodies and then incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 goat anti‐rabbit IgG and Alexa Fluor 594 goat anti‐mouse IgG (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Then, the reacted slides were mounted with a mounting medium with DAPI (4′‐6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole).

ZnT5 siRNA synthesis

ZnT5‐specific siRNAs were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich and Santa Cruz Biotechnology (Dallas, TX) and named siZnT5 #1 and siZnT5 #2, respectively. The sense sequence of the duplex of siRNA‐1 was 5′‐GAAUUAGACACUCACAUGU‐3′ and its antisense sequence was 5′‐ACAUGUGAGUGUCUAAUUC‐3′. ZnT5#2 was a pooled siRNA duplex (sc‐91622) containing 19–25 nucleotide sequences.

Quantitative reverse transcription real‐time PCR (qPCR)

Real Time‐qPCR analysis was performed as previously described, 12 using a LightCycler 96 and FastStart Essential DNA Green Master (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Total RNA was extracted using TRI Reagent (Molecular Research Center, Cincinnati, OH, USA) and cDNA was synthesized using a QuantiTect reverse transcription kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The PCR primer sequences used in this study were as follows: ZnT5 (NM_022902) forward: 5′‐agcaaagacaaggggagctg‐3′ and reverse: 5′‐ggcaatggctgtgtaaagca‐3′; E‐cadherin, forward: 5′‐tgaaggtgacagagcctctggat‐3′ and reverse: 5′‐tgggtgaattcgggcttgtt‐3′; vimentin, forward: 5′‐tcagaatatgaaggaggaaatggc‐3′ and reverse: 5′‐gagtgggtatcaaccagagggagt‐3′; Slug, forward: 5′‐aagcatttcacgcctccaaa‐3′ and reverse: 5′‐ggatctctggttgtggtatgaca‐3′; MMP9, forward: 5′‐tccagtaccgagagaaagcc‐3′ and reverse: 5′‐tcaactcactccgggaactc‐3′; and ribosomal protein L13a (RPL13A) (NM_012423), forward: 5′‐cctggaggagaagaggaaag‐3′ and reverse: 5′‐ttgaggacctctgtgtattt‐3′. The mRNA level in each case was represented as the ratio of the housekeeping gene RPL13A.

Cell viability assay

The cell viability assay was performed as previously described. 14 Cells were cultured in 6 cm dishes at a density of 3 × 105 cells/mL. After 24 h, the cells were transfected with 5 nM ZnT5‐specific siRNA (Sigma‐Aldrich) or negative control siRNA (siCTL) (Ambion, Austin, TX, USA) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After 48 h, cells were seeded into 96‐well plates and then treated with 100 μM ZnCl2. At 24 and 48 h after siRNA, the WST‐8 [2‐(2‐methoxy‐4‐nitrophenyl)‐3‐(4‐nitrophenyl)‐5‐(2,4disulphophenyl)‐2H‐tetrazolium, monosodium salt] method (Cell Counting Kit‐8; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan) was used and the cell count was determined. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an iMark Microplate Reader (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA).

Wound healing assay

Cell migration was examined using a wound healing assay with culture insert (Ibidi, Gräfelfing, Germany) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were cultured in 6 cm dishes at a density of 3 × 105 cells/mL. After 24 h, the cells were transfected with 5 nM ZnT5‐specific siRNAs or negative control siRNA, by using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX. After 24 h, cells were seeded in RPMI‐1640 medium containing 1% FBS and ZnCl2 at a concentration of 1.5 × 106/70 μL into individual compartments of the culture insert overnight. The culture plate was filled with RPMI‐1640 medium containing 1% FBS and ZnCl2 (100 μM) and the ibidi culture inserts were then removed. Dorsomorphin, a selective inhibitor of the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) type I receptors anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) 2, ALK3 and ALK6, prevents BMP‐mediated SMAD1/5/8 phosphorylation and was also added when removing the insert. The results are presented as the percentage of wound healing, which was calculated as follows: [Wound Area (initial) − Wound Area − (final)]/Wound area (initial) × 100.

Phosphorylation multi‐pathway profiling array

Human phosphorylation pathway profiling array (cat. no. AAH‐PPP‐1‐8) was purchased from RayBiotech (Peachtree Corners, GA); this array can detect 55 phosphorylated human proteins in five signalling pathways: MAPK, AKT, JAK/STAT, NFκB and TGF‐β. MCF‐7 cells were transfected with 5 nM ZnT5‐specific siRNA (Santa Cruz Biotechnology) or negative control siRNA and cultured in the presence of ZnCl2 (100 μM). After treatment, the cells were lysed using the cell lysis buffer with a protease and a phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. The phosphorylation array analysis was performed according to the manufacturer's protocol. The array was scanned and quantified using a Gel Doc XR Imaging System (Bio‐Rad).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using JMP 15 software (sas Institute Japan, Tokyo, Japan). Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

RESULTS

ZnT5 and ZnT6 expressions and their correlation with clinicopathological parameters of patients with breast cancer

The immunoreactivity of both ZnT5 and ZnT6 was detected in the cytoplasm (Figure 1). The number of ZnT5‐positive breast cancer cells was significantly lower in patients with pathological N factor (pN) status and significantly higher in human epidermal growth factor receptor type2 (HER2)‐positive patients. The number of ZnT6‐positive breast cancer cells was significantly higher in patients with a low histological grade and high progesterone receptor (PgR) expressions (Table 1). The number of ZnT5 and ZnT6 double‐positive breast cancer cells was significantly higher in HER2‐positive patients (Table 1).

Effects of ZnT5‐knockdown in breast cancer cell lines

We examined the expression levels of ZnT5 and ZnT6 in MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells. Immunofluorescence results revealed that ZnT5 was localized in the cytoplasm and cell membranes, whereas ZnT6 was localized in the cytoplasm in both cell types (Figure 2). We investigated the effects of ZnT5‐knockdown by using small interfering RNAs (siRNAs). The expression of ZnT5 was suppressed in MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells transfected with ZnT5‐specific siRNA (siZnT5), but not in those transfected with negative control siRNA (siCTL) (Figure 3A). ZnT5 knockdown exerted no effect on cell viability in the presence of 100 μM ZnCl2 in MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells (Figure 3B). The 100 μM ZnCl2 treatment inhibited cell migration, but ZnT5 knockdown promoted cell migration in MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells (Figure 3C). ZnT5 knockdown induced higher vimentin, slug and MMP9 levels and lower E‐cadherin levels in the presence of 100 μM ZnCl2 in MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells (Figure 3D).

ZnT5 knockdown induced the SMAD1 expression, and dorsomorphin inhibited the promotion of migratory ability by ZnT5 knockdown

To further characterize the effects of ZnT5 knockdown on the migratory capacity of breast cancer cells, we explored the levels of phosphorylated proteins in ZnT5 knockdown‐MCF‐7 cells using a phosphorylation pathway profiling array. As shown in Figure 4A, SMAD1, SMAD2, SMAD4 and c‐Jun levels were not statistically significantly different, and there was a trend towards higher levels in ZnT5‐knockdown MCF‐7 cells compared to control cells. Therefore, we focused on BMP signals, and we used dorsomorphin to prevent BMP‐mediated SMAD1/5/8 phosphorylation. We also examined the effects of ZnT5 on cell migration. ZnT5 knockdown‐induced promotion of migratory ability was suppressed by 10 μM dorsomorphin (Figure 4B).

DISCUSSION

Breast cancer is the most common cancer in women worldwide and is a highly complex, heterogeneous and multifactorial disease. Therefore, it is necessary to understand the characteristics of individual breast cancers and provide the appropriate treatment for each case. To understand the characteristics of breast cancer, this study focused on zinc transporters, particularly ZnT5 and ZnT6, that are expressed in the Golgi apparatus.

This is the first study to demonstrate ZnT5 and ZnT6 expression in breast cancer tissues. In this study, we performed immunohistochemistry to determine the expression of ZnT5 and Znt6 in human breast cancer tissues. The number of ZnT5‐positive breast cancer cells was significantly lower in patients with pN status and significantly higher in HER2‐positive patients. The number of ZnT6‐positive breast cancer cells was significantly higher in patients with a low histological grade, and high PgR expression. The number of ZnT5 and ZnT6 double‐positive breast cancer cells was significantly higher in HER2‐positive patients. In addition, a positive correlation was observed between ZnT5 and HER2 and ZnT6 and PgR, respectively. Because ZnT5 is expressed not only in the Golgi apparatus, but also in the cell membrane, it may be associated with HER2 expression in the cell membrane; however, further research is required. No statistically significant difference was found between pN‐ and ZnT6‐positive and double‐positive patients in this study. ZnT5 functions as a homodimer and heterodimer with ZnT6; therefore, transport of zinc can be evaluated by evaluating ZnT5 single‐positive and ZnT5 and ZnT6 double‐positive patients; however, this is an evaluation of ZnT5‐positive patients. As a result, regardless of the expression of ZnT6, zinc transport is believed to occur when ZnT5 is present; therefore, the evaluation of ZnT5 expression is important. However, as this study did not directly evaluate ZnT5 and ZnT6 heterodimers, evaluating ZnT5 and ZnT6 heterodimers in breast cancer tissues and examining their association with clinicopathological factors is necessary.

Two families of Zn transporters, SLC30 (ZnT) and SLC39 (ZIP), maintain Zn homeostasis. ZnT contributes to the cytoplasmic zinc balance by exporting zinc to the extracellular space and sequestering cytoplasmic zinc to intracellular organelles. Conversely, ZIP increased the cytoplasmic zinc concentration. 9 , 15 Some of these transporters are also expressed in cancer, and their functions have been reported. Several studies have reported the relationship between the immunoreactivity of zinc transporters and cancer malignancy. Decreased ZnT4 expression has been associated with prostate cancer. 16 In oesophageal cancer, the expression of Zip5 is increased in precancerous lesions and normal oesophageal squamous epithelium, and Zip5 knockdown suppresses cancer progression. 17 Zip4 is involved in the pathogenesis and progression of pancreatic cancer. 18 In breast cancer, the expression of LIV‐1, a zinc transporter family member, is inversely correlated with tumour size, grade, stage, recurrence‐free survival and overall survival. It has been suggested to be an indicator of good prognosis. 19 Phosphorylated Zip7 is positively correlated with patient age, menopausal status, tumour size, tumour grade, tumour stage and proliferation in breast cancer, and has been associated with hormone therapy resistance. 20 Zn contributes to the suppression of tumour growth under in vivo homeostatic conditions. However, Zn deficiency contributes to tumorigenesis and tumour growth when in vivo homeostasis is disrupted. 21 This study showed that ZnT5 contribute to the suppression of breast cancer lymph node metastasis when homeostasis is disrupted. However, we did not examine the relationship between Zn concentration and the expression of ZnT5 and ZnT6, and we evaluated ZnT5 and ZnT6 heterodimers in breast cancer tissues. Further studies are required to clarify the significance of ZnT5 and ZnT6 expressions, including their relationship with intratumoral zinc concentration in breast cancer tissues.

ZnT5 knockdown exerted no effect on cell viability in the presence of 100 μM ZnCl2 in breast cancer cell lines MCF‐7 and T‐47D. In contrast, cell migration of both MCF‐7 and T‐47D was inhibited by 100 μM ZnCl2. Furthermore, ZnT5 knockdown using two siRNAs significantly promoted cell migration in MCF‐7 and T‐47D cells in the presence of 100 μM ZnCl2 compared to controls. There have been several reports on the relationship between normal cells and their migratory abilities. Du et al. 22 indicated that ZnCl2 can inhibit cell migration and viability by decreasing the expression of TGF‐β1 and increasing the expression of TNF‐α in human lens epithelial cells. Morand et al. 23 suggested that zinc deficiency upregulates endothelin‐1 (ET‐1) signalling through hypoxia‐inducible factor‐1 (HIF‐1) activation and stimulates endothelial cell migration, suggesting an important role for zinc in the vascular consequences of intermittent hypoxia and obstructive sleep apnea mediated by HIF‐1‐ET‐signalling. Sahibdad et al. 24 reported that Zn promoted the adhesion, migration and self‐renewal of human umbilical cord‐derived mesenchymal stem cells. Regarding the relationship between zinc transporters and breast cancer, it has been reported that knockdown of Zip6 and Zip10, which transport zinc from the outside into the cells, suppressed the migration ability of cancer, and suggested ZIP10 as a possible marker for the metastatic phenotype of breast cancer. 25 , 26 Although the relationship between ZnT5 and ZnT6 and migration and invasion abilities in breast cancer and other cancers has not yet been investigated, these transporters are mainly expressed in the Golgi apparatus. Zip13, a transporter expressed in the Golgi apparatus and transports zinc from the Golgi apparatus to the cytosol in ovarian cancer, promotes the migration and metastasis of ovarian cancer cells via the Src/FAK signalling pathway. 27 In addition, ZnT5 knockdown induced higher vimentin, slug and MMP9 levels and lower E‐cadherin levels in the presence of 100 μM ZnCl2 in MCF‐7. Therefore, ZnT5 knockdown in breast cancer cells and prevention of zinc uptake into the Golgi apparatus may contribute to the promotion of migration and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT). These results suggest that ZnT5 knockdown promotes EMT and migration of breast cancer cells. Furthermore, antibody array analysis revealed that ZnT5 knockdown increased the expression of SMAD1, SMAD2, SMAD4 and c‐Jun. In addition, dorsomorphin, a selective inhibitor of the BMP type I receptors ALK2, ALK3 and ALK6, prevents BMP‐mediated SMAD1/5/8 phosphorylation and suppresses the migratory ability of ZnT5 cells. In contrast, protein arrays revealed that SMAD5 was expressed at very low levels. Moreover, SMAD1 phosphorylation promoted the migration of breast cancer cells upon ZnT5 knockdown. In colorectal cancer, SMAD1 increases the expression of EMT markers and promotes cancer cell migration. 28 The antibody arrays demonstrated that ZnT5 knockdown also increased SMAD4 expression, suggesting that SMAD4 was induced by SMAD1/5/8 phosphorylation. In addition, phosphorylation of SMAD1/5/8 is induced by BMP4; therefore, the increase in SMAD1 observed in this study may have been induced by BMP4. 29 In MDA‐MB‐231 breast cancer cells, BMP4 promoted EMT and stem cell properties; therefore, a similar pathway may have been activated by ZnT5 in this study. 29

In breast cancer, ZnT5 knockdown inhibited the formation of ZnT5 homodimers and heterodimers of ZnT5 and ZnT6, inhibited zinc influx into the Golgi apparatus, and promoted the accumulation of zinc in the cytosol, suggesting that it contributes to the promotion of EMT in breast cancer via SMAD1 phosphorylation, resulting in the promotion of migration ability.

In this study, immunohistochemistry of ZnT5 and ZnT6 revealed that pN was lower in cases positive for ZnT5 expression. In addition, in a wound healing assay, zinc treatment suppressed the migratory ability, whereas ZnT5 knockdown promoted the migratory ability, decreased E‐cadherin expression, and increased vimentin, slug and MMP9 expression. Protein arrays showed that ZnT5 knockdown increased the expression of SMAD1, and the addition of dorsomorphin inhibited the promotion of migration by ZnT5 knockdown. It has been suggested that the promotion of EMT in breast cancer via SMAD1 phosphorylation results in the promotion of the migration ability. This study reveals the biological significance of zinc transporters in breast cancer.
