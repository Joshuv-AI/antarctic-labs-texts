Forging the frontlines at home: integrating military–civilian systems for national readiness and resilience
Authors: Joshua Dilday, Christopher B Horn, Adam D Fox, Jay Johannigman, Jennifer Margaret Gurney, Brian Eastridge, Valerie G Sams, Jeffrey David Kerby, Rachel M Russo, Samir M Fakhry, Justin P Fox, Ryan Earnest, Jeffrey Bailey, Jay A Yelon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFuture large-scale combat operations, high-impact domestic disasters, and infrastructure-disrupting crises will generate casualty volumes and system stress far exceeding those experienced during the conflicts occurring during the previous two decades. These threats will transcend the traditional distinctions between military and civilian healthcare systems. They will necessitate an integrated national response capable of absorbing sustained casualty surges and preserving access to care for the civilian population. At the 2026 Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma Annual Scientific Assembly, a multidisciplinary short course entitled “Forging the Frontlines at Home: Integrating Military–Civilian Systems for National Readiness and Resilience” convened military, civilian, academic, and policy leaders to examine readiness gaps and propose actionable pathways to facilitate military–civilian integration. This article synthesizes key themes from that program across three domains: (1) defining the operational problem space, (2) coordinating a synchronized national response, and (3) understanding the financial and administrative consequences of large-scale casualty care. Together, these perspectives highlight that national casualty readiness for combat casualty care needs to be a core component of domestic resilience.

Introduction

During the past two decades, US civilian and military trauma systems have matured during the relatively limited-scale conflicts of the post-9/11 era. During these conflicts, advances in hemorrhage control, damage control resuscitation, balanced transfusion strategies, and rapid aeromedical evacuation dramatically improved battlefield survival.18 These achievements are rightly celebrated, but they were forged within a specific operational framework of rapid patient evacuation, early surgical intervention, and relatively limited daily casualty volumes. The conditions cited enabled historic survival gains but also shaped assumptions regarding evacuation timelines, system capacity, and workforce resilience that will not be generalizable to future large-scale conflict operations (LSCOs) or domestic crises.911 LSCO scenarios predict prolonged evacuation, contested logistics, degraded communications, and casualty volumes unlike recent wars that will overwhelm military treatment facilities (MTFs) and rapidly spill into civilian trauma systems.913 Survival in the LSCO environment will depend less on isolated clinical excellence and more on system capacity and coordination. The complexity of LSCO-associated patient burden will not be simply whether individual trauma surgeons can manage complex injuries, but whether the national healthcare system can absorb, distribute, sustain care, rehabilitate, and reintegrate large numbers of severely injured patients during prolonged periods of time.14

Simultaneously, US civilian healthcare infrastructure faces declining inpatient bed capacity, increasing occupancy rates, and workforce shortages.15 Mass-casualty incidents, natural disasters, and cyber vulnerabilities further underscore that the domestic healthcare system is already operating as a system at risk for degradation, loss of surgical capacity, and collapse. These converging pressures challenge the long-standing, but increasingly artificial, conceptual separation between “military casualty care” and “civilian trauma care”. The effects of future conflict will require integration of civilian trauma centers, as the stateside return of battlefield casualties will rely on civilian trauma capacity. Simultaneously, domestic disasters may compete with wartime demands for personnel, blood products, operating room access, and critical care beds.

Recognizing this reality, EAST convened a multidisciplinary course to examine how military–civilian integration can strengthen national readiness and resilience in the face of international conflict and domestic crises. This article synthesizes operational, systems, and policy insights from that forum into a cohesive framework relevant to trauma surgeons, healthcare leaders, and policymakers.

Setting the operational foundation: what are we preparing for?

Trauma at scale without borders

Future conflicts will not produce neatly separable “military” and “civilian” casualties. Instead, they will generate trauma at scale that crosses geographic, institutional, jurisdictional, and military–civilian boundaries. These complexities are different from recent conflicts seen during the Global War on Terror (GWOT), where most civilian trauma centers were unburdened by a combination of returning combat casualties and a deploying surgical force. Modern battlefields dominated by precision fires, unmanned systems, thermobaric weapons, and prolonged field care will produce high rates of blast injury, burns, hemorrhage, and complex polytrauma with casualty volumes and acuity that exceed those seen during recent wars and domestic mass-casualty incidents.10 12 16 The combination of increased injury complexity, increased casualty volume, and decreased casualty evacuation capability leads to the predictable clinical implications of increased infection, increased organ failure, increased transfusion requirements, longer intensive care stays, increased long-term rehabilitation needs, and increased mortality.

Historical experience reinforces this paradigm. World War era conflicts required substantial expansion of military medical infrastructure, including the development of additional MTFs, utilization of leased civilian infrastructure, and integration of healthcare personnel drawn from civilian practice.17 Although civilian hospitals contributed to wartime care, the military health system expanded significantly to absorb the burden of combat casualties. Future LSCO scenarios will likely require a similarly coordinated military–civilian approach, although in a healthcare environment characterized by fewer available beds, greater specialization, and significantly reduced surge margin.

Recent conflicts have masked systemic vulnerabilities through exceptionally rapid evacuations from the frontlines and lower overall casualty rates.3 6 11 12 18 19 During the GWOT era, the civilian healthcare system was relatively isolated from the burden of care of the direct wartime mission. This insulation fostered an implicit assumption that military medical systems could independently manage combat casualties. LSCO reintroduces delays in care, casualty volume, and uncertainty as dominant determinants of outcome, rendering prior performance metrics insufficient proxies for future readiness.11 12 20 The overwhelming casualty predictions are unlike those of the most recent conflicts. This fact highlights that recent prior experiences will not be universally generalizable to the future battlefield.

Capacity as the primary constraint

The scale of predicted casualties demands integration planning.91113 Modeling exercises simulating LSCO suggest daily casualty volumes that would overwhelm forward surgical capacity and quickly saturate stateside referral centers.9 10 Although the USA possesses unparalleled trauma expertise, its capacity to absorb sustained casualty surges is limited.142123 Civilian-staffed inpatient bed capacity has declined during the past decade and occupancy rates have increased, leaving minimal surge capacity. In fact, the anticipated casualty from LSCO would overwhelm trauma centers, burn units, rehabilitation facilities, and intensive care resources within days to weeks.10 14 24

These capacity constraints are compounded by workforce vulnerability. Activation of reserve/national guard personnel and the potential implementation of the healthcare personnel delivery system (a contingency draft for medical personnel) will augment military capacity, but would further disrupt civilian trauma care capabilities, would simultaneously increase demand for trauma care and remove clinicians from civilian practice. The paradox created by simultaneously increasing demand and shrinking workforce supply illustrates the critical value of readiness, which is not solely a function of clinical capability, but of system endurance under prolonged stress.

Military–civilian partnerships as readiness infrastructure

Military–civilian trauma partnerships have repeatedly demonstrated value in sustaining clinical readiness during peacetime and preserving wartime lessons across conflict cycles. Recently, embedded training models and rotational programs have preserved expeditionary skills during peacetime and facilitated bidirectional learning.2533 These partnerships support bidirectional learning, maintain expeditionary skill sets, and mitigate the well-described “peacetime effect”.33 34

However, their strategic value extends beyond training. When viewed as a longitudinal surge-capable infrastructure rather than episodic collaborations, military–civilian partnerships become essential components of national casualty capacity. Evolution from partnership to integration that is characterized by shared governance, data transparency, and operational alignment is central to future readiness.31 35 True military–civilian integration must incorporate a system of readiness in addition to individual skill sustainment.32 36

Strategic synchrony: coordinating the national response

Fragmentation as a systemic vulnerability

The USA lacks a fully integrated national trauma, emergency care, or casualty management system. Trauma and acute care remain organized primarily at state and regional levels, with substantial variability in standards, access, and coordination. Although effective during routine operations, this fragmentation becomes a critical vulnerability during mass-casualty events that exceed regional capacity.37

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed these weaknesses, including limited real-time bed visibility, inconsistent patient distribution, supply chain vulnerabilities, and competition between healthcare systems.38 39 Analyses suggest that a significant proportion of pandemic-related deaths may have been preventable with improved surge coordination, underscoring the stakes of system-level failure.40 We still lack a robust data repository that provides capacity, capability, and staffing information across the nation to be used in active crises as well as for modeling and planning. healthcare systems and collaboratives across the country must begin to understand how they will communicate, load balance, manage logistics of staffing, supplies and equipment, have contingencies for operating room and intensive care unit utilization, and think outside the box for postacute care rehabilitation and support for family units.

NDMS as a necessary backbone

The National Disaster Medical System (NDMS) can serve as a foundational framework for a national trauma system.41 Established in 1984, NDMS was designed as the primary federal architecture for casualty movement, definitive care, and surge response for mass casualty generating events. It originated as a joint Department of Defense-Veterans Affiars-Civilian (DOD-VA-Civilian) partnership for distributing the care of injured service members returning from foreign wars, with the secondary mission of supporting national surge during domestic disasters. Although it has been partially activated for natural disasters, it has never been exercised at full scale for its wartime mission. Renewed concern regarding global power competition and the risk of LSCO prompted Congress to fund a pilot project at five strategically located sites to simulate military casualty return and assess whether the civilian healthcare sector could operationalize this mandate.42 Despite its scale, recent analyses have identified persistent gaps in interoperability, staffing, training, and load balancing.42 Many participating hospitals remain unaware of their NDMS role until activation, limiting effectiveness during a crisis.41

Recent pilot initiatives demonstrate the need to improve awareness, address resource constraints, and clarify funding structures. For NDMS to realize its potential as a national safety net for surge response, it must align with military casualty flow planning and be embedded in routine operations rather than rely on episodic activation.43 That evolution will require sustained federal support to establish a regional command-and-control structure capable of translating national intent into patient-level decisions. This structure is operationalized through Regional Medical Operations Coordinating Centers (RMOCCs).44

RMOCCs as the operational “Unit of Action”

A central theme of the Eastern Association for the Surgery of Trauma (EAST) short course was the emergence of RMOCCs as the functional “unit of action” for national casualty management.44 45 RMOCCs are designed to serve as regional coordination hubs that integrate situational awareness, bed tracking, patient regulation, resource allocation, and interfacility communication across healthcare systems. RMOCCs enable real-time situational awareness, patient distribution, and resource coordination across regions, providing a scalable framework applicable to daily operations and crisis response alike.44 However, limiting the RMOCC model to serve as a reserve or contingency plan limits its efficacy in crises. Because systems existing only for emergencies fail under stress, daily coordination sustains disaster readiness. In addition to the NDMS alignment, embedding RMOCCs into routine trauma system operations preserves readiness, improves efficiency, and enhances resilience. Due to their familiarity with local/regional resources and capabilities, RMOCCs must own the “last mile” of combat casualty care evacuation in the LSCO contingency.

The impact at home: financial and administrative implications

Financial shock and workforce burden for civilian trauma systems

Integration planning is not merely operational, but also financial. Civilian hospitals operate on narrow margins, with approximately two-thirds of revenue derived from elective procedures.46 LSCO casualty events would necessitate the limitation of elective surgical care, disrupt supply chains, and generate prolonged inpatient stays reimbursed at rates that frequently fail to cover actual costs.47 Experience from the early COVID-19 pandemic illustrates the magnitude of this risk. Despite unprecedented federal support through programs such as the Coronavirus Aid, Relief, and Economic Security Act, healthcare systems across the country experienced multibillion-dollar revenue losses associated with reduced procedural volume, increased resource utilization, workforce disruptions, and prolonged inpatient care. Similar challenges can be anticipated during a future LSCO scenario, where casualty surges may persist for weeks or months rather than days.39 Current proposed reimbursement strategies for military casualty care will unlikely cover true costs, particularly when high-resource utilization and long ICU stays are required. Blood products, advanced implants, and rehabilitation services add further expense. Current NDMS reimbursement mechanisms provide participating hospitals with reimbursement at approximately 125% of Medicare rates for NDMS patients, recognizing the additional burden associated with disaster response. Although this incentive represents an important step toward offsetting surge-related costs, inconsistencies remain within the broader reimbursement landscape. Notably, similar financial incentives do not currently exist for many TRICARE and Veterans Affairs beneficiaries, despite these populations representing a significant proportion of potential wartime casualties. Absent preplanned financial mechanisms, civilian trauma centers may be forced to choose between fiscal viability and national service during a crisis.

But, the economic burden of LSCO casualty care will extend beyond reimbursement challenges. Future conflicts are expected to generate large numbers of patients requiring massive transfusion, prolonged intensive care utilization, complex burn management, advanced reconstructive procedures, and extended rehabilitation services. These resource-intensive requirements would increase expenditures related to blood products, specialty staffing, consumable supplies, rehabilitation infrastructure, and long-term follow-up care. Just as federal relief programs helped stabilize healthcare systems during the COVID-19 pandemic, future wartime or national disaster planning should incorporate pre-established stabilization funding mechanisms capable of rapidly supporting hospitals tasked with absorbing large casualty volumes.

Accordingly, national readiness planning must include deliberate financial preparedness strategies in parallel with operational planning efforts, to include the “non-reimbursable” administrative aspects of clinical care. Deployments (for active duty and reserve service members), personnel mobilization, healthcare workforce shortages, and potentially a medical draft introduce administrative complexity for institutions already facing staffing shortages. Transparent planning for compensation, call redistribution, credentialing, and institutional support is essential to sustaining workforce morale and continuity of care during prolonged crises. Together, such mechanisms would help preserve institutional solvency, maintain access to care for civilian populations, and ensure that participation in national casualty response efforts does not jeopardize the long-term sustainability of the healthcare systems on which both military and civilian patients depend.

Next steps and measuring the return on readiness investment

Sustainable integration requires measurable outcomes of individual and system readiness (table 1). Emerging readiness frameworks based on joint knowledge, skills, and abilities measures and partnership quality indicators allow systems to quantify readiness gains and justify investment.35 Measured quality indicators must shift from solely focusing on the clinical skill sustainment of the provider to the overall health capacity of the system. Military–civilian partnership quality indicators can track procedural exposure and threshold attainment. Data-driven evaluation aligns military, civilian, and policy stakeholders around shared objectives and enables scalability.

In this context, alignment with the NDMS and RMOCCs is essential. System readiness metrics should incorporate NDMS participation and activation capability, as well as RMOCC-driven indicators such as real-time bed availability, transfer timeliness, and regional load balancing.

Concurrently, integration must expand beyond military trauma surgeons to include auxiliary medical professional societies—such as emergency medicine, anesthesiology, critical care, nursing, and prehospital organizations—which are essential to scaling workforce capacity and standardizing multidisciplinary care during surge conditions. These societies can play a central role in developing cross-disciplinary staffing models, credentialing frameworks, and training pathways to mitigate anticipated workforce shortages. Professional trauma organizations should continue to embrace joint military–civilian educational opportunities to ensure a workforce capable of functioning in resource-constrained environments.

Operationalizing these metrics represents the next step in advancing military–civilian integration. Military–civilian partnerships should be identified within NDMS structures, with clearly defined surge roles and expectations for participation in routine exercises. Concurrently, RMOCCs should continue to normalize interfacility coordination and maintain readiness outside of crisis periods. Military–civilian workforce and financial planning must be aligned with NDMS surge requirements to ensure that civilian centers can sustain operations during prolonged casualty events without compromising institutional viability. Together, these steps transition military–civilian partnerships from conceptual collaboration to operational integration within a coordinated national framework.

Conclusion

The next major conflict or domestic catastrophe will test not only the technical skill of trauma surgeons and teams but the cohesion and resilience of the nation’s healthcare systems. Excellence in isolated silos will be insufficient to support the need. National readiness requires operational, financial, and administrative integration across military and civilian domains.

The EAST short course, Forging the Frontlines at Home, highlighted that trauma surgeons are uniquely positioned to lead this effort, bridging bedside realities with system-level design. Preparing for LSCO abroad and disaster at home is the same mission. National readiness and resilience will be contingent on a comprehensive systems-level integration across the military and civilian domains.
