“It doesn’t feel as much like my downfall for needing help”: patient perspectives on pathways for improving health through social health integration

Background

Social health integration (SHI) consists of health care systems’ efforts to identify and address patient social circumstances, such as housing instability, food insecurity, or limited transportation. Evidence suggests that these individual-level social needs, stemming from social determinants of health, contribute to poor health outcomes [1]. In response, health care regulators, accreditors, payors, and therefore, providers, are placing a growing emphasis on systematically screening for social risks and responding to patient social needs as a new “basic standard of care,” with the goal of improving health and reducing avoidable health care costs [2].

As health care systems expand SHI programs, research on the effectiveness of these initiatives, along with assessments of the experience of providing and receiving social health care, has proliferated as well. Even as expectations to implement SHI programs grow, the evidence around SHI programs’ effectiveness in successfully connecting patients to resources and/or improving health outcomes is mixed [3]. In a paper recently published by Gottlieb et al., the authors describe the logic model underlying initial efforts at social health integration and introduce new potential logic model pathways, based on emergent research findings [2]. The initial logic model pathway for SHI programs to improve health and health care utilization focused on systematically identifying social needs and subsequently connecting patients to social services. This connection is intended as a mechanism to reduce social needs, thereby supporting patients to prioritize their clinical health needs. However, new findings suggest that the mechanisms through which SHI influences health and health care utilization may be more multifaceted [2].

The Gottlieb et al. logic model builds on the initial pathway of reducing the burden of social risks, described above, by summarizing new evidence and offering three additional hypotheses for how social health integration might influence health and health care utilization. These include: (1) Emotional support/healing relationships pathway, (2) Health care services connections pathway, and (3) Tailored clinical care pathway [2].

Previous qualitative studies have described patient attitudes towards and experiences of SHI programs, but there are limited insights into how patient perspectives align with the newly proposed pathways described in the revised logic model. In this manuscript, we aim to advance the field by organizing our qualitative patient feedback findings from a mixed methods SHI evaluation using the revised logic model pathways. We explore the newly proposed pathways using the patient experience and improvement perspectives shared by patients who participated in a primary care SHI program. The findings of this evaluation add an essential perspective to the growing understanding of how SHI programs are experienced by the people they are intended to serve.

Methods

Setting and intervention

Kaiser Permanente (KP) is a national, not-for-profit integrated health care system. KP Washington (KPWA) is one of eight KP regions and serves approximately 650,000 members in Washington state. From October 2022-January 2023, we evaluated a pilot SHI program in two KPWA primary care clinics. The SHI program consisted of universal social health screening and follow-up. The two primary care clinics were selected based on the diversity of their patient demographics and size, and received practice coaching, implementation support, and both workflow and communication training to facilitate adoption of universal screening. The social health screener asked about social risks across the domains of housing, transportation, food, and finances. It also asked patients if they would like assistance with any of the following social needs: housing, food, transportation, finances, employment, childcare, utilities, loneliness or social isolation, and/or paying for medical care, medicine, or medical supplies.

Patients who screened positive for at least one social need were randomized to receive support from either a local, clinic-based community resource specialist (CRS) or a resource specialist operating out of Kaiser Permanente’s nationally-run, centralized Connections Call Center (CCC). Clinic-based CRS are members of KPWA primary care teams, live in the communities they serve, offer local knowledge about community-based resources, and assist patients with navigating the health care system. In contrast, the centralized CCC resource specialists utilize an online directory of community-based resources to assist patients. Following randomization, resource specialists from both the local and centralized programs conducted outreach to eligible patients, with the goal of assisting patients with identifying and accessing resources. [4]

In order to understand patients’ experiences with the SHI program, we conducted 30- to 45-minute semi-structured qualitative interviews with a subset of patients randomized as part of the broader pragmatic evaluation, which had the overall goal of comparing social needs resolution between local (CRS) and centralized (CCC) follow-up over 5 months [7]. This pilot program and evaluation was deemed a quality improvement project and not human subjects research by the KP Interregional Institutional Review Board (KPiIRB). Due to this designation, the KPiIRB waived the requirement to obtain participant consent.

Sampling and recruitment

Patients who completed a 5-month follow-up survey and spoke English were eligible to complete an interview, regardless of program adoption status. Program adoption was defined based on whether or not patients received resource information from their assigned CRS or CCC staff.

We categorized eligible participants by the following domains: (1) Assignment to social health support program (CRS or CCC); (2) program adoption status (receipt or no receipt of resource information from assigned CRS or CCC); and (3) change in count of social needs from baseline to the 5-month survey (increase/no change or decrease). We combined individuals who had an increase or no change in needs over time into a single category due to small sample size. This led to a total of 8 subgroups to capture all variations of these domains (See Additional File 2). Based on the number of individuals who fell into each subgroup, we developed a minimum target number of people to interview from each subgroup, with the goal of reaching saturation. This led to a final sample size of 50 participants in total.

We extracted demographic information from the electronic health record (EHR), including patients’ age (< 18; 18–40; 41–60; 60+), insurance status (commercial; individual; Medicaid; Medicare; no coverage), and clinic (A or B) at which the original primary care appointment took place, and race and ethnicity. Race and ethnicity was aggregated into six categories due to small sample size, including African American/Black, Hispanic, Multiracial, White, Other (American Indian/Alaska Native, Asian, and Hawaiian/Pacific Islander), and Unknown. To obtain a diverse sample, we oversampled patients who identified as non-white. For patients under 18 years of age, we conducted the interview with their parent or guardian.

Recruitment for qualitative interviews occurred between April – July 2023, with the goal of recruiting 50 patients to participate. Eligible patients were mailed an invitation letter asking if they would like to participate in a 30- to 45-minute semi-structured phone interview about their experiences interacting with their primary care team, being asked about their life circumstances in a health care setting, and their experiences working with a resource specialist (if applicable). A $50 gift card was offered as compensation for their time. Patients who responded with interest received a phone call from study staff to share additional information and schedule the interview. Study staff also conducted proactive phone outreach to patients who did not respond to the invitation letter. Five call attempts were made, with a maximum of three voicemails left.

Data collection and analyses

The study team developed an interview guide (see Additional File 1) that asked patients about their experiences with SHI across three SHI domains: (1) awareness through social health screening, (2) assistance through resource connection, and (3) adjustment by tailoring care to patients’ social circumstances [5]. We designed the interview questions to elicit patient perspectives on their general experience with SHI, perceived usefulness across these SHI domains, whether their needs had been met, and the impact of SHI on their overall health care experience. Two team members (AS and JE) conducted one-on-one semi-structured interviews using the Microsoft Teams phone function between April – August 2023. Interviews were audio recorded using Microsoft Teams and professionally transcribed.

We used a Rapid Assessment Procedure to analyze transcripts [6, 7]. AS, JE and SM developed a summary for each interview transcript and randomly selected a subset of transcripts to be summarized independently by two team members to check for internal consistency. Following the development of interview summaries, we created a summary matrix of interview responses by domain. AS identified overarching themes from the matrix of responses and reviewed these themes for consensus with JE and SM. We then organized the themes according to the four revised logic model pathways for SHI [2].

Results

Sample

Of those who received an invitation, we interviewed 50 patients (41% response rate). Most patients were between 18 and 40 years of age (N = 17, 34%) or over 60 years of age (N = 16, 32%) (Table 1). More than half of our cohort identified as non-Hispanic white (N = 28, 56%) and nearly half were commercially insured (N = 24, 48%). There were slightly more patients who were assigned to CRS than CCC (52% and 48%, respectively). Almost half of patients reported a decrease in social needs over time in the follow-up survey described above (N = 24, 48%). Most of our cohort (N = 33, 66%) received care from Clinic A, which was proportional to the size of the populations served by the two clinics. The characteristics of patients who participated in interviews were similar to the evaluation sample overall. Findings

Reducing the burden of social risks pathway: connect patients with social services to decrease risk

Pervasive availability and eligibility obstacles

In both the CRS and CCC programs, resource specialists provided patients with referrals to either internal or community-based resources based on the identified needs. Referrals typically consisted of the name of the organization providing the resource, address, phone number, and general categories of services offered. In interviews, very few patients reported being successfully connected to community resources or programs as a result of meeting with a resource specialist from either CRS or CCC. Many patients reported that the services they needed were either not available in their community or they were not eligible for the services that exist.

The few things that I thought I potentially was going to find were very inadequate for my needs …You have to go through a whole application process and then get approved and then you have to be within boundaries and this and that, or [apply at] certain times and dates.

Several patients also noted that the resources they were given were not well-tailored to their circumstances, in terms of both needs and eligibility, and that resource navigation would be more valuable if well-matched.

[The resource specialist] gave us a lot of resources, but [did] not really sit down and say, ‘We really think these three of the resources are best for you.’ Instead, it was kind of like, here’s a lot of documents, a lot of catalog type information, and it’s really up to us to still do the narrowing down on the research.

Although most patients did not report successful connection to resources, there were a few people who reported getting connected as a result of the program. One notable success was achieved with a patient experiencing homelessness: with support from CRS, he was able to enroll in Social Security Disability Insurance and Medicaid, thereby significantly improving his financial situation. While this was a substantial win for this patient, this level of successful navigation to material assistance was unique in this sample.

Mismatch between patient needs and SHI services

Some patients with more complex social or emotional needs reported feeling frustrated by the level of support available through the resource specialists, which was limited to brief intervention to provide resource information and connection. In particular, several patients who had experienced a significant medical event or personal loss reported feeling too overwhelmed to follow up on the resources shared with them by a specialist.

It was on me to follow up… I was easily overwhelmed so it was hard for me to do the legwork to take things to the next step.

I looked through what they sent me, it was good. They gave me jobs, trying to help, but sometimes on my side, it’s like God, do I have to do all this?…I work the nightshift and sometimes I only sleep four or five hours, I don’t get enough sleep, and my head hurts and I don’t want to deal with it.

Patients with significant social needs, such as patients who reported experiencing significant food insecurity or homelessness, also felt frustrated by the low-intensity effort to address their constraints. They reported feeling misled and cynical about the health system inquiring about their social health challenges and then following up with very limited support (e.g., flyers and lists of resources sent by mail, rather than tailored resources or direct provision of services) to help them improve their circumstances.

I got information on the Silver Sneakers program and things like that…That’s all great too, but that’s not what you need to live…I guess what would have been helpful would be things that people really need, like food, their water bill, like their gas bill, their electricity bill, help with their roof. When you’re living off nothing, there’s no way you can save anything to be able to pay for that.

They need to [think about] how they can manage people’s problems…[not] just calling us and talking to us over the phone… after that, we are waiting for an answer [but only] receive by mail a bunch of paper…No! They can close this program, if that’s how they do it. For me it’s not helpful at all. Because everybody’s got the same. If I call 211, they will send me a book too.

Others wondered why their health care provider was trying to connect them to limited community-based resources instead of investing resources into reducing their premiums and other health care costs. They expressed preference for the health system to either provide social health services more directly or to take more direct action to reduce their health care expenses.

They said somebody will help you with community resources to get whatever kind of help you need. I assumed that meant things like we’ll help you with your deductible….

Several patients shared that they had concluded this service must be for someone else, an imagined better- or worse-off person, depending on their circumstance. For example, the people experiencing the most significant resource challenges suggested that this approach must be for someone who was better off than they were, since the intervention was so inadequate to meet their needs.

I was really depressed, crying every day, dealing with this situation. We were five people in one room. Because I wasn’t able to afford more than one room… that was really stressful… But every time I tried to call your program, they just say oh, we cannot do anything about that, we [already] sent you resources.

Many people found that they were not eligible for services of any kind because of income thresholds or other eligibility criteria, and therefore concluded that the program must be for people who were worse off than them.

I thought like somebody in way worse circumstances are who they’re trying to help, somebody that doesn’t even have internet access or something.

Patient preferences vary on formats for outreach and resource support

Patients reported a wide range of preferences relating to format for receiving follow-up support. Some patients reported feeling overwhelmed or unsatisfied with interactions mediated by technology.

Everything nowadays is electronic. You can’t even walk in the office…because everybody’s busy. That’s the way it is. I wish it was back in the 70s.

I think [my conversation with the resource specialist] might have been better if it was in person…We do a lot of communication with our bodies, rather than our voice and the words…we do a lot of communication in other ways, and I think that would have maybe propelled me a little more substantially.

Many patients reported not answering phone calls from unknown callers. Some remembered receiving the outreach calls, but at times when they were not available to answer. Several noted that they would like an easy, self-service option for getting assistance online. Many patients concluded that offering outreach and resource support in multiple formats would be ideal.

If there were a variety of ways that resources were shared, the same resources - let’s say through a conversation or email or follow-up with my care team or whatever, that would have made those resources more accessible to me.

Emotional support/healing relationships pathway: connect patient with social service navigator to improve emotional support

Even without resources to offer, emotional support is meaningful

Many of the patients who did not receive any resources still found their interactions with a resource specialist to be valuable. In particular, patients appreciated the holistic approach to health and the reassurance of knowing there is someone to call if they need help. They shared that the SHI program demonstrated that KP cared about them as a whole person, and made them appreciate Kaiser Permanente as both their health insurance provider and health care provider.

I’m used to the old way, where you’re just a number, in and out, they treated you and not much else. But now there’s more social issues, so the idea of getting people the help they need is awesome.

Some patients described feeling reassured to know that there is someone to call if they have significant social needs in the future.

I just felt like there was support, that I would have a number to call when I needed that. I’m not ready, but it’s just nice to know it’s there. A real relief…not be so tense, not so worried.

Others felt reassured, after speaking with a resource specialist, to know “we’re not leaving anything on the table that could have helped.”

Patients who were randomized to both CRS and CCC follow-up consistently expressed that the resource specialists were kind and supportive. Specifically, they valued having a health system representative take the time to listen to their experience and make efforts to help them find assistance, even when material assistance was ultimately not available.

Even though I didn’t necessarily think I was going to get any assistance … I was interested that it was an option and that Kaiser cared enough to have someone who was a resource specialist who would reach out and work personally with families.

The way [resources] were shared was excellent, she did a good job of talking about needs and [that there] are different opportunities out there. Her method was great. It [was]… maybe not as rich an offering as one would hope, but she plated them well – let’s say it that way.

While most patients expressed feeling comfortable being asked about their social health as part of their primary care visit, many others were hesitant to disclose their social needs because of pride, privacy concerns, or feeling ashamed or embarrassed about their situation.

People don’t want to admit that they ain’t got enough food, or they ain’t got money. There’s too much pride involved. I have a lot of pride in what I do and what I’ve done, so it’s hard to get through the pride part and realize I do need help, and then ask for it.

On paper it’s always easier to answer the question… but when it comes to talking to someone about them… it was a little less comfortable to talk about that…because I’m not sure if we would qualify or if people would judge us for needing some things.

Offering a meaningful and reassuring follow-up conversation is especially important for the subset of patients for whom answering social health questions feels uncomfortable or risky. Some patients reported that their positive experience with follow-up made them feel better about disclosing their struggles, despite their initial discomfort.

It’s just hard sometimes with those explicit questions…if I was to answer honestly, it would be honest about struggling… And then with the specialist…we couldn’t find any resources specifically that fit what I needed, but he was really thorough and looked really hard, and I appreciated that a lot…I feel better about it, that there’s actually resources and people that do want to help me. It doesn’t feel as much like my downfall for needing help.

Health care services connections pathway: connect patients with outpatient health care services to increase outpatient health care services

Competing priorities and too little time in primary care

The quantitative results from this pragmatic evaluation found higher levels of primary care utilization among patients engaging with CRS [4]. Nonetheless, patients reported challenges accessing desired care, even though we did not directly ask about SHI-driven connection to outpatient services. When asked about the impact of SHI on their perceptions of their health care provider, or any other feedback, several patients reported frustration with inadequate health system capacity, especially for time and attention during their primary care visits. Patients described wanting more time and follow-up to support effective diagnosis and treatment of their health conditions, and were concerned about the focus on social needs competing for the scarce resource of clinician time and attention. Patients whose medical needs were not being met were particularly frustrated, such as a patient with a significant missed cancer diagnosis, a patient with significant chronic health problems but no clear diagnosis, and patients with mental health challenges who want better access to behavioral health services.

My experience is that, especially the primary care team, they are utterly maxed out and so tend to deal with things on the surface.

Tailored clinical care pathway: health care team changes/tailors care plan

Limited tailoring of clinical care

When asked whether their provider had adapted their care based on the social needs they disclosed on the questionnaire, most patients reported that their provider did not discuss their social circumstances despite having social health screening data available to them.

I don’t think that he asked me about that kind of stuff. I don’t remember talking to him about that.

Some interviewees described discussing social health circumstances with their children’s pediatric providers as more routine. Some patients reported their doctor talking to them about their life situation, but only one reported their care plan changing as a result.

Patients value whole-person focus

Among those who reported discussing their life situation with their provider, patients value being cared for in a holistic way.

The more information I can give to my doctor, the better she’s going to be able to treat me… So I enjoy talking with my provider about stuff like that because sometimes the stuff going on in our lives is really affecting our health and we just don’t realize it.

I felt very cared for, concern for me as just a human being.

Discussion

Although we did not design our interview guide using the revised logic model, organizing patient SHI experience data using the logic model pathways was an easy fit. This fit suggests that our findings align with the evidence underlying the model, as well as the value of the revised logic model for strengthening program evaluation. Our findings correspond with improvement recommendations made by patients and workforce members in previously published implementation literature on SHI, suggesting that common implementation barriers exist across settings, as well as common strategies for improving health system effectiveness and patient experience.

In interviews with patients on their experiences with and perspectives on SHI, we found that patient feedback most closely aligned with the emotional support/healing relationships pathway as the value-driver for SHI. Many patients reported not only that their experience was useful, but also the perception that their needs were met, even if they did not receive any material assistance. Although it is an unexpected result, this pattern corresponds with findings from multiple studies that social health interventions that include relational and emotional support can improve patient health and well-being, regardless of whether any connections to social services are made [2, 8]. This mismatch between intervention benefits and successful service connection may be the result of decreased social isolation, enhancement of feeling cared for, and the boosts to activation and well-being furnished by interpersonal support [2].

Some patients, however, reported that it was difficult to connect with the resource specialist in their preferred format. To overcome this obstacle to adoption, health systems can offer support in a variety of formats (e.g., self-service vs. directed, and online, text, and phone-based options), treat screening as an ongoing process rather than a one-time event, and ensure that there are designated and appropriately trained staff to follow up with patients. These staff can build trust between patients and their care team, and provide patients with ongoing support over time [9–12].

With regard to the original pathway proposed as the mechanism for social health integration (i.e., reducing the burden of social risks), few patients in this study reported that engagement with either CRS or CCC resulted in a connection to material assistance. Regardless of whether the resource specialist was located in their community or a centralized call center, patients seemed to value supportive conversation, along with personalized, accurate, and well-matched resources. Notably, there were no overall differences in the feedback between patients who had been assigned to CRS or CCC follow-up.

While some patients reported a perception that their needs had been met when asked, regardless of whether they had received material assistance, others expressed significant frustration with the mismatch between their level or type of need and the services being offered. This echoes previous findings that while some patients may not expect their health care team to provide in-depth support for social needs, others report frustration with screening that is not followed by material assistance, or at a minimum, acknowledgment of the real challenges to accessing material assistance [11, 13, 14]. While supportive conversation alone seems to be meaningful and meet the need for some patients, others feel that follow-up must be actionable to avoid coming across as naïve or paternalistic [9, 11].

To help mitigate this challenge of insufficient resources, health systems can invest in staff and technology to enable the relationship-building with community-based organizations that supports personalized and accurate information about resources, along with well-coordinated services, warm handoffs, and ongoing support for patient follow-up [9, 11]. Patients also recommend using shared decision-making and empathic communication to reduce the discomfort of discussing stigmatized personal circumstances and to acknowledge the difficulties of connecting with resources [14, 15].

For the health care services connection pathway, some patients articulated a conflict between their clinical care needs and the growing emphasis on SHI. Nonetheless, the quantitative results from this study show greater primary care utilization among patients who engaged with CRS, which may be the result of health system navigation offered by the clinic-based CRS as part of their role [4]. While some qualitative evaluations have found that social health interventions can enhance patient motivation and capability to connect with health care services, patients in this interview series more commonly expressed their concerns about social health competing for health system resources, including concerns about time constraints in primary care and access constraints for specialty care [13, 16]. Patient concerns about time constraints and competing priorities are echoed by workforce members in recent publications about social health integration [9, 10]. To address the challenges of insufficient time and access to health care services for complex health problems, health systems should consider developing explicit linkages between social needs screening and referral protocols and an overall population health management strategy [9].

Only one patient out of 50 reported an experience that aligned with the tailored clinical care pathway, in which their providers talked to them about their life situation and incorporated it into care planning. Tailoring clinical care requires significant change to clinician behavior, which was not a focus of this pilot program. To improve clinician-level responsiveness to social circumstances, health professional training programs and health systems will likely need to develop dedicated strategies for building the professional competencies and implementation support needed to consistently offer social risk informed care [15].

Limitations

This study has several limitations. The study was conducted with patients who receive care at two primary care clinics within an integrated health system in the Pacific Northwest region of the United States and the findings may not be generalizable to other health care settings, although evidence suggests that the individuals receiving care from KP clinics are representative of their local communities [17]. These findings reflect the experience of those who agreed to participate in the interview process, and while the interview sample was demographically similar to the study sample overall, it is unknown if patient perspectives are representative of the sample for the larger pragmatic evaluation. In addition, interviews were only conducted with patients who speak English.

There was also a significant time delay (minimum of 6 months) between intervention and interviews due to the design of the evaluation, which may have impacted patient recollection. Patients may have received similar support before, during, or after the evaluation period, and it was not uncommon for patients who were recruited as non-adopters to describe experiences with resource specialists during their interviews. In addition, these data were collected as part of an evaluation that included multiple follow-up data collection surveys about patient social health status, which does not represent the standard approach to social health integration.

Conclusions

Although few patients reported successful connection to social services as a result of participating in the SHI program, many more patients reported that their positive interactions with a resource specialist regarding their social circumstances led to an improved perception of their health care system. This mismatch emphasizes the value that health care organizations can offer by providing emotional support to patients who are facing stressful social circumstances, even when the need for social services outstrips the supply.

While this finding underscores the importance of the emotional support/healing relationships pathway in the revised logic model, there is far more to be learned about patient experience, effective program design, and implementation considerations across all four pathways in the logic model. In particular, understanding how to design and implement effective patient-centered social care programs amidst the competing pressures and priorities of real-world health care settings is a valuable next step in the evolving social health integration research landscape.
