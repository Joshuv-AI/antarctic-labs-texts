Long-term survival benefit of neoadjuvant oncolytic virus plus PD-1 blockade in acral melanoma: implications of STING in resistance and potential therapy

ABSTRACT

Background

Acral melanoma (AM) represents a biologically and clinically distinct subtype of cutaneous melanoma that arises on non-hear bearing (glabrous) skin. It is characterized by a significantly poorer prognosis compared with non-acral cutaneous melanoma (CM) [1, 2]. Despite curative-intent surgical resection, patients with stage IIC-IVA AM remain at high risk for relapse, with post-adjuvant therapy recurrence rates reaching approximately 50% [3–7].

Neoadjuvant immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has recently emerged as a standard-of-care for resectable stage III-IV CM. Landmark trials, such as S1801 and NADINA, have demonstrated superior event-free survival (EFS) compared with adjuvant alone [8, 9]. In contrast, the application of neoadjuvant immunotherapy in AM remains markedly underexplored. In the advanced disease setting, PD-1 blockade, administered either as monotherapy or in combination with LAG-3 or CTLA-4 inhibitors, has yielded limited efficacy, with objective response rates (ORR) significantly lower than those observed in CM [10–17].

The poor responsiveness of AM to ICB is largely attributed to its profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by a low tumor mutational burden (TMB), frequent chromosomal copy number aberrations, diminished PD-L1 expression, and sparse CD8+ T-cell infiltration, alongside an enrichment of regulatory and exhausted T-cell populations [18–24]. Oncolytic virus (OV) therapy provides a promising strategy to reshape this immune-desert TME [25–29]. In CM, combining OV with PD-1 blockade has shown encouraging clinical activity, outperforming either monotherpy [30, 31]. Orienx010, a genetically engineered OV expressing GM-CSF, has demonstrated clinical efficacy in advanced melanoma, including the AM subtype [32].

Previously, we reported the primary results of a phase Ib neoadjuvant trial (NCT04197882) evaluating Orienx010 plus the anti-PD-1 antibody toripalimab in patients with resectable stage III/IV AM. This regimen achieved a notable 77.8% pathological response rate, with 2-year relapse-free survival (RFS) and EFS rate of 81.5% and 73%, respectively [33]. Nevertheless, several critical questions remain unanswered, including the long-term durability of clinical benefit, the identification of robust predictive biomarkers, and the development of effective salvage strategies for non-responders.

Here, we present the landmark 5-year survival outcomes from this trial, integrated with longitudinal multi-omic analyses of paired pre- and post-treatment tumor tissues and serum samples. These data delineate the biological determinants of therapeutic response, identify STING pathway dysfunction as a potential mediator of primary resistance, and provide a mechanistic framework for biomarker-guided strategies to optimize neoadjuvant immunotherapy in patients with AM.

Methods

Tumor specimens and profiling

This study enrolled 30 patients diagnosed with AM at Melanoma and Sarcoma Department of Peking University Cancer Hospital between 2019 and 2021. Baseline clinical characteristics, including age at diagnosis, sex, and prognosis, were recorded at enrollment. Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissues collected before and after treatment were used for simultaneous DNA and RNA co-extraction. Paired peripheral white blood cell (WBC) genomic DNA (gDNA) was used as the germline control for WES. Pathological responses were assessed using the guidelines of the International Neoadjuvant Melanoma Consortium, in which a response was defined as having ≤ 50% residual viable tumor cells, while cases with > 50% viability were classified as non-responders.

WES, WTS and TBS were performed in 30 pre-treatment diagnostic biopsies, 27 post-neoadjuvant treatment surgical specimens, and 2 biopsy samples of radiological progression. After rigorous quality control, WES results of 26 baseline and 22 post-treatment samples, WTS results of 22 baseline and 24 post-treatment specimens, TBS results of 17 baseline and 14 post-treatment tissues were included for further analysis. Serum proteomic profiling was performed on 8 non-responders and 8 responders across three time points: pre-treatment, post-cycle 3, and post-treatment.

WES data processing

DNA extraction and sequencing

gDNA was extracted from FFPE tumor tissues and peripheral WBCs and subsequently fragmented into 200–300 bp fragments using the Covaris S220 ultrasonicator (Woburn, MA, USA). The concentration and purity of extracted DNA were measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and quantified with a Qubit 2.0 Fluorometer utilizing the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

Whole-exome libraries were constructed using a 53 Mb custom hybridization capture panel (Integrated DNA Technologies, IA, USA) designed to enrich for all coding sequences along with selected non-coding regions of the human genome. The enriched libraries were subsequently sequenced on the DNBSEQ-T7RS platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China) using 100 bp paired-end reads, following the manufacturer’s protocols.

Data preprocessing and quality control

Raw data obtained from the DNBSEQ-T7RS platform underwent quality control to eliminate adaptor contamination and low-quality sequences. The resulting high-quality reads were subsequently aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using BWA (v0.7.10). A total of 48 samples, including primary tumor tissues (mean coverage: 464×; range: 120–685×) and matched WBC controls (mean coverage: 223×; range: 136–309×), were subjected to WES. Following variant calling, several filtering steps were applied to ensure mutation quality: (1) Synonymous variants were excluded; (2) Variants supported by fewer than 5 high-quality reads (mapping quality > 30, base quality > 30) in FFPE gDNA or fewer than 2 high-quality reads in WBC gDNA were removed; (3) Variants exhibiting an allele frequency below 5% were excluded from downstream analysis.

Mutational signature analysis

TMB was calculated as the number of somatic single nucleotide variants (SNVs) detected within coding regions per megabase of genome examine [34]. Somatic mutational signatures were deconvoluted using the deconstructSigs R package [35], based on the 30 COSMIC reference signatures provided within the package.

Neoantigen predicting

Four-digit HLA genotypes for each patient were inferred using OptiTyp [36], which utilizes germline WES BAM files as input. Reads mapped to chromosome 6 were isolated and realigned to the HLA reference sequences obtained from the IMGT/HLA database. Non-synonymous somatic mutations were used to generate 8- to 11-mer peptide sequences, positioning the mutated residue at all possible locations within each peptide. The binding affinities of mutant and corresponding wild-type peptides to the individual’s HLA alleles were estimated using netMHCpan version 3.037. Peptides were classified as candidate neoantigens if they exhibited a predicted binding affinity of less than 500 nM and a mutant-to-wild-type binding affinity ratio below 0.7.

RNA-Seq data processing

RNA extraction and sequencing

RNA was isolated from 46 tumor specimens and subsequently reverse-transcribed into complementary DNA (cDNA) using Ribo-SPIA amplification technology (NuGEN, San Carlos, CA, USA). Construction of mRNA libraries, sequencing, and initial quality assessment of FASTQ files were carried out according to the protocol [38]. Sequencing reads containing adaptor sequences or exhibiting poor quality were filtered out, retaining only high-quality paired-end reads. Clean reads were then aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using HISAT2 (v2.0.4), and transcriptome reconstruction was performed with StringTie (v1.2.3).

Differential gene expression and functional enrichment analyses

To identify transcriptional differences between responders and non-responders, raw gene expression data were preprocessed and analyzed using the limma package in R [39]. Genes exhibiting an absolute fold change of ≥ 2 and a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Gene set enrichment analysis was carried out using the clusterProfiler R package [40] referencing curated gene sets from KEGG, Hallmark, Gene Ontology (GO), and Reactome collections within the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) [41]. Enriched pathways with adjusted P values less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

The immune microenvironment analysis

To evaluate immune infiltration patterns, the immune score of each tumor was calculated to estimate the relative abundance of immune cell subsets [42].

DNA methylation profiling and DMR characterization

DNA methylation analysis was carried out as previously reported [43]. In brief, genomic DNA was isolated from FFPE tumor sections and treated with bisulfite using the EZ DNA Methylation-Lightning™ Kit (Zymo Research, USA) to convert unmethylated cytosines. Targeted methylome enrichment was achieved with the SeqCap Epi CpGiant 4 M panel (Roche), and libraries were sequenced on the Illumina HiSeq platform. Raw data were demultiplexed with bcl2fastq (v2.20), trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) to remove low-quality bases and adapters, and aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh37/hg19) using Bismark (v0.23.1). Differential methylation analysis was performed using the methylKit package (v1.12.0) in R (v4.4.1). CpG sites were grouped into 100 bp non-overlapping windows, with regions having < 5× coverage filtered out. Methylation β-values were computed as the proportion of methylated reads relative to the total number of reads at each CpG site. DMRs were defined as regions with absolute β-value differences > 0.2 and FDR-adjusted q-values < 0.01 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction), using logistic regression models adjusted for relevant covariates. This analytic framework, benchmarked by Ziller et al. [44], ensures robust detection of biologically meaningful epigenomic alterations while minimizing PCR and coverage-related artifacts. Functional annotation of differentially methylated genes was conducted using the Metascape platform [45].

Olink proteomics

Serum concentrations of cytokines and chemokines were quantified in 16 patients at baseline, C3, and post-neoadjuvant treatment using proximity extension assays (PEA) provided by Olink Proteomics. Assay preparation followed the manufacturer’s protocol, including vortexing and centrifuging the Incubation Mix, transferring precise volumes to designated wells, adding internal and external controls, sealing the reaction plates, and incubating at specified temperatures. Thermal cycling was performed according to the standard protocol for PEA-based protein expression profiling. For detection, the Olink® 96.96 IFC chip was primed and loaded with Detection Mix and incubated samples. After bubble removal, the chip was processed on the Olink Signature Q100 platform. Data were acquired using the SIGNATURE 100 qPCR instrument and analyzed with Olink NPX Signature software. Protein expression levels were quantified as normalized protein expression (NPX) values, which were subjected to quality control and used for downstream statistical analyses. This workflow ensures sensitive and accurate quantification of protein biomarkers in clinical serum samples.

Animal studies and drug formulation

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. For cell preparation, B16 melanoma cells were subcultured into fresh flasks 24 h before inoculation to ensure they were in logarithmic-phase growth. Cells were then trypsinized, centrifuged, washed and resuspended to adjust cell density to 5 × 10^5 cells per 25 µL PBS. Mouse footpad tumor inoculation involved preparing C57BL/6J mice by ear-tagging them and disinfecting the right hind footpad with 75% ethanol, followed by injecting 25 µL of PBS containing 5 × 10^5 B16 cells using a sterile syringe with gentle massage to minimize leakage. Tumor growth was monitored every three days; tumors measuring 50–100 mm^3 typically formed within 14–21 days with a success rate exceeding 98%. Based on tumor size measurements, mice were randomly assigned to treatment cohorts (n = 5 per group). Treatments included oncolytic virus orienx010 dosed at 2–8 × 10^5 PFU/mouse (10 µL of 2–8 × 10^7 PFU/mL), STING agonist KL340399 administered intravenously at 2 mg/mL (50 µL/mouse), and PD-1 blockade (anti-mouse PD-1, BE0146, InVivoMAb) given intraperitoneally at 200 µg per 100 µL/mouse, following the dosing schedule depicted in Fig. 6a.

Drug Formulation: KL340399: 4 mg KL340399 was dissolved in 1,000 µL DMSO, mixed with 4,000 µL 40% PEG300 and 500 µL 5% Tween-80, then diluted with 4,500 µL saline to 0.4 mg/mL. PD-1 Blockade: Stock solution (9.67 mg/mL) was diluted with PBS (827 µL stock + 3,173 µL PBS) to 2 mg/mL.

Paraffin-embedded tumor transcriptome sequencing

Paraffin-embedded mouse tumor tissues were used for transcriptome sequencing analysis. Total RNA was extracted, and ribosomal RNA (rRNA) was first removed. Transcripts were then enriched using an exon-targeted capture strategy, and sequencing libraries were constructed in a non-strand-specific manner. After quality control, the libraries were subjected to high-throughput sequencing to generate gene expression profiles. Gene expression levels were quantified from the sequencing data and normalized for subsequent bioinformatic analyses. Immune infiltration analysis was then performed based on the transcriptomic expression matrix to evaluate the infiltration levels and compositional characteristics of different immune cell populations in the tumor microenvironment.

Flow cytometry analysis

Tumor tissue, approximately 50 mg, was dissociated into 1–2 mL PBS using a gentleMACS Dissociator with the Tumor Dissociation program and underwent two consecutive cycles to ensure thorough homogenization. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 300-µm mesh, achieving a concentration of 107 cells/mL. For viability staining, 100 µL aliquots of the cell suspension were incubated with 1 µL Zombie Aqua™ Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend, #423101; excitation/emission: 405/525 nm) for 15–30 min at room temperature away from light, followed by one wash with Cell Staining Buffer (BioLegend, #420201). In preparation for surface antibody staining, cells were resuspended in 50 µL of Cell Staining Buffer and incubated with pre-titrated antibody cocktails (details in Table. S1) for 30 min in the dark, then washed three times with Cell Staining Buffer. Finally, cells were resuspended in 500 µL staining buffer and analyzed on a BD LSRFortessa™ X-20 cytometer, with compensation controls and fluorescence-minus-one controls included to ensure accurate data acquisition.

Immunohistochemical staining

Immunohistochemical staining was performed on 4-µm formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded mouse melanoma sections. After deparaffinization and rehydration, heat-induced antigen retrieval was conducted in citrate buffer (Beyotime, P0081; pH 6.0). Sections were then blocked for endogenous activity and nonspecific binding with goat serum (Beyotime, C0265) and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against p-STING (Affinity, AF7416), p-IRF3 (HuaBio, HA722772), and p-TBK1 (CST, 5483). After washing with PBS, the sections were incubated with a secondary antibody detection reagent (ZSGB-BIO, PV6001), followed by signal development using an AEC substrate kit (ZSGB-BIO, ZLI-9036). Nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Two pathologists from the Peking University Cancer Hospital’s pathology department who were blinded to this study interpreted the sections. Immunohistochemical staining intensity was semi-quantitatively scored as 0, 1+, 2+, or 3+, where 0 indicated no detectable staining, 1 + indicated weak staining, 2 + indicated moderate staining, and 3 + indicated strong staining.

Statistics

Comparisons of categorical data between groups were conducted using either the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, depending on sample size and distribution. Continuous variables were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Survival curves were generated with the Kaplan-Meier method, and intergroup differences were evaluated using the log-rank test. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to assess linear associations. For unpaired analyses of continuous variables with normal distribution, the independent samples t-test was applied. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p value < 0.05. All statistical procedures and data visualizations were carried out using R software (version 4.4.1) and GraphPad Prism version 9.

Results

Study population and five-year survival update

A total of 30 patients were enrolled between 2019 and 2021 (Table. S2). At a median follow-up of 62.4 months (range, 12.0-74.7 months; data cutoff April 14, 2025), the estimated 5-year RFS, distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS), EFS, and overall survival (OS) rates were 70.7% (95%CI, 53.7–93.0%), 70.7% (95%CI, 53.7–93.0%), 70.0% (95%CI, 55.4–88.4%), and 80.0% (95%CI, 66.9–95.7%), respectively (Fig. S1a-d). While the median RFS, DMFS and OS were not reached, the median EFS was 62.2 months. Disease progression occurred in ten patients (33.3%), including three who developed surgically unresectable distant metastases.

Patients were stratified by treatment response into two cohorts: responders (n = 21), defined as those achieving a pathological response, and non-responders (n = 9), comprising six patients with pathological non-response and three with radiologic progression precluding surgery. Responders demonstrated significantly superior survival outcomes compared to non-responders. Specifically, median RFS, DMFS, EFS, and OS were not reached in the responder group, compared with 21.3, 21.3, 12.8, and 52.3 months in non-responders, respectively (all p < 0.01; Fig. 1a-d).

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying therapeutic efficacy and primary resistance of OV plus PD-1 blockade, we performed longitudinal multi-omic analyses on specimens collected at baseline and during treatment. These included pre-treatment tumor biopsies and matched peripheral blood, post-cycle 3 (C3) blood samples, and post-surgical tumor specimens with matched blood. Tumor samples were characterized via whole-exome sequencing (WES), whole-transcriptome sequencing (WTS), and targeted bisulfite sequencing (TBS). Additionally, serial peripheral blood samples from 16 patients were analyzed using Olink proteomics (Fig. 1e, Fig. S1e-f).

Comparative multi-omics profiling of responders and non-responders at baseline

Baseline WES profiles are summarized in Fig. 2a. NRAS and BRAF were the most frequent driver mutations (30.8% [8/26] and 15.4% [4/26], respectively), with no significant differences in frequency between responders and non-responders. Similarly, TMB and tumor neoantigen burden (TNB) were comparable between the two groups (Fig. 2b). In contrast, DNA methylation profiling revealed extensive epigenetic divergence, with 52 genes significantly hypermethylated and 7 genes hypomethylated in non-responders (Fig. 2c). These alterations affected several critical biological axes: (i) metabolic suppression via the silencing of metabolism-related genes (SDHB, PFKFB2, GFPT1, BCAT1, ACOX3); (ii) dysregulation of signal transduction and transcriptional programs through hypermethylation of ETS1, HOXB3, TLK2, and COPRS; (iii) impairment of immune recognition and inflammasome activation via the repression of AIM2, SELE, CLCF1, FAIM, and ANGPT2; and (iv) perturbation of cell-cell interactions and vascular dynamics through epigenetic modulation of SORT1, LTBP1, KLKB1, and MMP12.

To determine whether baseline methylation status shapes the TME, we analyzed the correlation between the expression of these 52 hypermethylated genes and immune infiltration features in baseline tissues. Among these, the expression of AIM2, SLC40A1, SELE, TMCC1, EST1, HOXB3, ANGPT2, SLSTN2, and SPORT1 significantly correlated with immune infiltration levels (Fig. 2d), suggesting a link between the baseline methylome and the TME landscape. Notably, AIM2, a cytosolic double-stranded DNA sensor involved in innate immune signaling, exhibited significantly higher methylation levels in non-responders (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, AIM2 expression positively correlated with STING expression in baseline tumor samples (r = 0.633, p = 0.016; Fig. 2f), further supporting a connection between baseline methylation status and DNA-sensing immune pathways.

Collectively, these data suggest that an integrated state of metabolic suppression, impaired signaling, and reduced immune activation driven by epigenetic remodeling that underlies therapeutic resistance in non-responders. Consistent with this, immune cell profiling revealed a marked reduction in NK cells and memory B cells in non-responders (Fig. S1g), highlighting intrinsic resistance as a key determinant of treatment failure.

STING dysfunction correlates with impaired baseline immunity and therapeutic resistance

To elucidate the mechanisms governing resistance to OV and PD-1 blockade, we compared baseline transcriptomic profiles between responders and non-responders. Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) revealed significant downregulation of innate immune activation and interferon-alpha (IFN-I) response pathways in non-responders (Fig. 3a), implicating impaired innate immunity in therapeutic resistance. Given the pivotal role of STING signaling in driving IFN-I-mediated antitumor responses, we subsequently stratified patients based on baseline STING expression levels into STING-high (n = 11) and STING-low (n = 11) groups. Notably, STING-low tumors exhibited profound downregulation of innate and IFN-I signaling pathways, mirroring the transcriptomic landscape of non-responders (Fig. 3b). Moreover, patients with STING-low tumors had significantly shorter RFS and EFS, with a trend toward reduced OS (Fig. 3c-e).

DNA methylation analysis further identified a cluster of immune-related genes were hypomethylated in STING-high tumors (Fig. 3f). GO enrichment analysis confirmed these genes are predominantly involved in immune activation pathways (Fig. 3g). Furthermore, STING expression showed strong positive correlations with infiltration of key immune cell subsets, including activated dendritic cells (r = 0.66, p < 0.001), NK cells (r = 0.59, p = 0.003), CD8+ T cells (r = 0.83, p < 0.001), and B cells (r = 0.64, p < 0.001) (Fig. 3h-i). Collectively, these findings establish that STING dysfunction is associated with an immune-suppressed tumor microenvironment and inferior clinical outcomes, highlighting STING signaling as a critical determinant of therapeutic efficacy in AM.

Longitudinal serum immune profiling reveals divergent immune response dynamics between responders and non-responders

Serum immune profiling revealed broad systemic immunological activation following neoadjuvant OV and PD-1 blockade therapy in majority of patients irrespective of pathological response (Fig. 4a). Functional clustering of significantly elevated soluble factors post-treatment at C3 demonstrated a robust increase in proteins involved in T-cell activation (CD4, CD28, CD27), cytokine signaling (IFNG, TNF), chemokine-driven lymphocyte recruitment (CXCL10, CXCL11, CXCL13, CCL13), immune co-stimulation and checkpoint regulation (CD274, TNFRSF9), and innate immune responses (CD83, KLRD1, LAMP, NCR1) (Fig. 4b).

Notably, responders maintained relatively sustained and stable cytokine levels throughout the treatment course. In contrast, non-responders exhibited a distinct “surge-and-decline” pattern, characterized by transient elevations in CCL13, CD4, CD8A, and TNFSF10 at C3, which subsequently declined to baseline levels by the post-treatment time point (Fig. 4c-d). Collectively, these findings suggest that the inability to sustain systemic immune mobilization, coupled with the lack of intratumoral immune remodeling, distinguishes non-responders from responders. These divergent systemic dynamics highlights the critical importance of durable immune activation for achieving therapeutic success and may provide a basis for identifying early predictive biomarkers of response.

Non-responders lack therapy-induced immune remodeling within the TME

To evaluate treatment-induced molecular alterations, we first examined longitudinal genomic changes. Notably, the majority of non-responders (4/8) exhibited an increase in TMB and TNB following neoadjuvant therapy. In contrast, most responders (12/14) showed a significant reduction in TMB and TNB (Fig. 5a, Fig. S2a). Patients whose TMB/TNB failed to decrease had significantly shorter EFS and OS (Fig. S2b-e), suggesting that a lack of therapy-induced genomic clearance is indicative of treatment failure.

DNA methylation analysis identified 4,903 differentially methylated regions (DMRs) between pre- and post-treatment samples, including 3,360 (68.5%) hypermethylated and 1,543 (31.5%) hypomethylated regions (Fig. S3a). Pathway enrichment analysis revealed that hypermethylated genes were enriched in stimulus response and metabolic regulation, whereas hypomethylated genes were predominantly associated with immune-related pathways, including leukocyte activation and innate immune signaling (Fig. 5b, Fig. S3b). Integrated DNA methylation and transcriptomic analysis identified 425 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) inversely correlated with 585 DMRs (|log₂FC| > 1, p < 0.05; Fig. S3c). Among these, 242 upregulated genes with hypomethylation were strongly enriched in immune activation pathways, including T cell receptor signaling (CD8A, LCK, ZAP70, CD247), antigen presentation (HLA-DMB, CD74, HLA-DPB1), and B cell activation (CD27, PTPRC, CD244, CD79B) (Fig. S3d). Given that most longitudinal samples eligible for TBS were from responders, these findings support a model in which neoadjuvant therapy drives targeted epigenetic de-repression to activate key immune programs.

Transcriptomic profiling further confirmed this extensive reprogramming in responders. Following therapy, non-responders exhibited a minimal transcriptional response only 136 DEGs, in stark contrast to the 2,731 DEGs observed in responders (Fig. S4a). GO enrichment analysis confirmed that immune-related pathways—including innate immune responses, T cell receptor signaling, and B cell receptor signaling—were upregulated in responders but not in non-responders (Fig. S4b; Fig. 5c). Immune infiltration analyses further underscored this divergence. Non-responders showed minimal changes in immune cell infiltration after treatment, maintaining a persistently “cold” TME (Fig. 5d), whereas responders exhibited significant increases in T cells, B cells, NK cells, and dendritic cells, consistent with a shift toward an inflamed TME (Fig. 5e, Fig. S4c-e).

STING agonist synergistically enhances the efficacy of OV plus PD-1 blockade

To functionally validate whether STING activation could overcome resistance to OV plus PD-1 blockade, we employed an immune-competent B16 melanoma footpad mouse model (Fig. 7a). Consistent with clinical observations, OV plus anti-PD-1 therapy failed to induce substantial tumor regression, whereas the addition of a STING agonist markedly enhanced tumor suppression (Fig. 7b). The triple therapy showed no apparent treatment-related toxicity compared with the control group and was generally well tolerated during the treatment period (Fig. 7c). To further assess STING pathway activation in vivo, we performed immunohistochemical staining of tumor tissues from the control, OV + anti-PD-1, and OV + anti-PD-1 + STING agonist groups. Compared with the control group, triple therapy increased the expression of p-STING, p-IRF3, and p-TBK1, indicating stronger activation of STING signaling within the TME (Fig. 7d).

Discussion

Our study provides the long-term clinical evidence that neoadjuvant OV combined with PD-1 blockade improves survival in AM. With a 5-year RFS of 70.7%, DMFS of 70.7%, EFS of 70.0%, and OS of 80.0%, the regimen demonstrates durable therapeutic benefit [33]. Compared to historical data from conventional surgery and adjuvant therapy, these results support the clinical value of incorporating neoadjuvant immunotherapy for resectable AM [5, 7, 46].

When compared to neoadjuvant strategies in CM, where PD-1 blockade alone achieves a 47% ORR and dual checkpoint blockade leads to 67–70% pathological response with 2-year EFS around 81–84% [8, 9, 47], and considering that adjuvant PD-1 therapy in stage III CM achieves a 5-year RFS of 56%6.

Recent studies further suggest that improving the efficacy of oncolytic virotherapy depends not only on strengthening antitumor immunity itself, but also on overcoming tumor-intrinsic and host-derived barriers that limit viral delivery, replication, and immune conversion. And multiple clinical studies have demonstrated that OV can potentiate the therapeutic efficacy of immunotherapy [48, 49]. In our AM cohort achieved a 77.8% pathological response rate and improved 5-year survival. This superior efficacy highlights the potential of OV and PD-1 blockade as a distinct and effective neoadjuvant strategy for AM.

Responders exhibited robust immune remodeling across multiple molecular layers. Genomically, these patients showed reduced TMB and TNB following therapy, indicating immune-mediated elimination of dominant tumor clone. Epigenetically, responders demonstrated selective hypomethylation of immune effector genes, which likely promoted immune gene activation and immune cell recruitment. Transcriptomic profiling confirmed a post-treatment shift toward an inflamed tumor phenotype, characterized by infiltration of CD8+ T cells, dendritic cells, NK cells, and B cells. In peripheral blood, responders maintained sustained elevations of inflammatory cytokines throughout the treatment course. These coordinated changes across genomic, epigenomic, transcriptomic, and proteomic layers suggest that effective response to OV and PD-1 blockade requires durable immune activation both systemically and within the TME. It suggests that responders underwent a transition of the tumor microenvironment from an immune-desert phenotype to an inflamed phenotype [50, 51].

In contrast, non-responders failed to undergo comparable remodeling. at baseline, hypermethylation of multiple genes was associated with altered immune cell infiltration and metabolic impairment, consistent with findings reported in previous studies. Increasing evidence suggests that metabolic reprogramming can influence the efficacy of immunotherapy [52, 53]. Notably, hypermethylation of the AIM2 gene, which encodes a cytosolic double-stranded DNA sensor involved in innate immune signaling, was significantly correlated with STING expression. And transcriptomic profiling revealed an innate immunosuppressive signature, including downregulation of IFN-I pathways. Post-treatment, these tumors showed minimal immune cell infiltration or transcriptional activation, indicating a failure to convert to an inflamed state. In peripheral blood, they exhibited only transient cytokine elevations, which declined to baseline levels post-treatment. This early systemic immune collapse, without sustained intratumoral immune engagement, highlights a functional disconnect between systemic and local immunity.

In non-responders, dysfunction of the STING pathway may represent a potential mechanism of therapeutic resistance. STING is a pivotal innate immune sensor that induces type I interferons, promotes dendritic cell maturation, and drives CD8+ T cell and NK cell activation, while suppressing the accumulation of myeloid-derived suppressor cells [54–62]. High STING expression correlated with superior RFS and EFS in our cohort. Supporting this, mouse models demonstrated that STING agonists enhanced activation and infiltration of both innate and adaptive immune cells, recapitulating the effects of PD-1 blockade and OV. These findings support a complementary rationale for the triple-combination strategy.

Mechanistically, this triple-combination strategy is designed to reinforce complementary steps of the cancer-immunity cycle: OV initiates immunogenic cell death and tumor antigen release [63, 64], the STING agonist amplifies antigen presentation and T-cell recruitment [65, 66], and PD-1 blockade sustains the function of newly recruited effector T cells by counteracting adaptive immune resistance, including PD-L1 upregulation [67]. Consistent with this model, recent preclinical evidence has shown that combining OV with STING agonist induces stronger and more durable antitumor immunity than either approach alone [68]. Although recent trials in unselected populations, including the phase III study of T-VEC plus pembrolizumab and early-phase STING agonist studies, have shown only modest benefit [68, 69], our results suggest that such an intensified strategy may be more effective when guided by biomarkers of baseline innate immune deficiency. Thus, this regimen should be viewed not as a simple escalation of treatment intensity, but as a biomarker-informed, mechanism-based approach to overcome resistance in tumors with impaired innate immune activation. However, independent validation of STING expression remains challenging at present, because publicly accessible external datasets are limited and currently available OV-related public gene-expression datasets do not include STING. Therefore, our STING-related findings should be interpreted in the context of this limitation and warrant further validation in larger publicly available OV-immunotherapy cohorts.

Peripheral immune profiling further identified potential biomarkers of response. Responders maintained elevated serum levels of inflammatory cytokines, whereas non-responders showed early but unsustained spikes in molecules such as CD4, CD8A, CCL13, and TNFSF10. Notably, recent evidence links CCL13 to effective immune checkpoint blockade responses, functioning through CD8+ T cell recruitment [70]. Histological analyses supported this dichotomy: responders displayed high intratumoral CD8+ T cell density and persistent IFN-I signaling, while non-responders remained immunologically cold. The discordance between systemic cytokine elevation and limited local cytokine upregulation indicates that peripheral immune activation alone may be insufficient for effective tumor control. Serum cytokine increases likely reflect systemic pharmacodynamic engagement, whereas clinical benefit appears to rely more on the establishment of an inflamed tumor microenvironment [64,71–74]. Therefore, tissue-level cytokine upregulation may represent a more informative indicator of therapeutic efficacy than serum cytokine changes alone. These observations also raise the possibility that defective immune-cell trafficking into the tumor contributes to therapeutic resistance. Although peripheral and intratumoral immune profiles showed encouraging concordance, larger datasets will be required to validate peripheral immune markers as reliable surrogates of intratumoral immune activity.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-arm, single-center phase Ib trial with a small sample size, the findings require validation in larger, randomized, and multicenter cohorts. Second, the correlative analyses were based on bulk tumor and serum profiling, which may obscure cell type-specific dynamics and spatial heterogeneity within the tumor microenvironment. Single-cell and spatial transcriptomic approaches will be important to dissect the precise cellular and molecular mechanisms underlying therapeutic response. Third, while our data implicate STING pathway dysfunction in resistance, functional validation using patient-derived models or in vivo experimental systems is needed to confirm its causal role and therapeutic potential.

Conclusions

In summary, OV combined with PD-1 blockade elicits durable clinical responses in AM by coordinating genomic and epigenomic reprogramming of the tumor-immune interaction landscape. Responders demonstrate sustained immune activation across tumor and blood compartments, while non-responders are characterized by baseline innate immune suppression and STING dysfunction. These findings offer mechanistic insight into response heterogeneity and provide a rationale for integrating STING agonists into future biomarker-driven immunotherapy regimens.
