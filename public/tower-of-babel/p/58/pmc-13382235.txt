Digital Technologies for Community Consultation and Public Disclosure in Exception From Informed Consent Trials
Authors: Henry E. Wang, Manish I. Shah, Shannon W. Stephens
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Community Consultation and Public Disclosure (CC/PD) are essential ethical requirements for clinical trials conducted under Exception from Informed Consent (EFIC). Traditional CC/PD approaches, such as advertisements, manual surveys, and inperson meetings, are often resource-intensive and limited in scale, geographic reach, and community representativeness. Digital technologies, such as social media campaigns, digital survey panels, and virtual video conferencing, offer new modalities to disseminate information, solicit feedback, and facilitate bidirectional dialogue with community populations. Digital CC/PD strategies can expand measurable reach, shorten implementation timelines, and provide quantifiable indices of engagement while maintaining acceptability among Institutional Review Boards. When deployed alongside local engagement activities, digital technologies offer additional tools that may strengthen the practical conduct of CC/PD. This review summarizes contemporary digital approaches to CC/PD and highlights their impact on modern emergency care research.

Introduction

Clinical trials are important for generating new knowledge and advancing clinical practices.1 In emergent conditions such as cardiac arrest, stroke, seizures, and trauma, the rapid deterioration of the patient’s condition and the pace of care make it impractical to obtain written informed consent from patients or legally authorized representatives.2 Exception from Informed Consent (EFIC) regulations (US Department of Health and Human Services 45 CFR 46.101 and Food and Drug Administration 21 CFR 50.24) allow investigators to carry out clinical trials under emergency conditions without the requirement for written informed consent.3 Since 1997 almost 100 trials have been conducted in the United States using EFIC.4,5

Community Consultation and Public Disclosure (CC/PD) are key requirements of EFIC. Community consultation is the process of engaging with representatives of the communities to obtain input and feedback on the planned research.2,6 Community consultation entails two-way interactive dialogue with community members to gather community opinions, values, and concerns, seek input on the study design and consent plan, and gauge community acceptance of the proposed research. Public disclosure involves providing information to the community about the research, including the study methods, its risks and benefits, the EFIC process, ways to opt out, the aggregate results, and the community impact of the study.

Conventional CC/PD methods face well-documented limitations, including high costs, substantial labor demands, extended timelines, limited geographic reach, and inadequate community representativeness.2,6, 7, 8 Modern digital technologies, such as social media and the internet, offer additional tools for reaching and engaging communities.6,9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Recent efforts have successfully integrated digital technologies into the CC/PD process, augmenting community reach and engagement while shortening timelines. We provide an overview of innovative approaches to EFIC CC/PD using digital engagement tools.

Traditional Framework For CC/PD

While not prescribed by the regulations or Institutional Review Boards (IRBs), study teams currently use a framework of CC/PD activities in the community encompassing three major elements 1) advertisements, 2) surveys, and 3) discussions.2 (Table 1) These complementary elements are designed to share information about the research with the community, measure community support for the research study, and assess community members’ opinions and concerns about the research study. IRBs usually expect all three realms of CC/PD to ensure wide dissemination of study information as well as structured and free-form feedback from community members about the proposed research study. Community advertisements entail activities to passively disseminate information about the proposed research study to the community population. Traditional modalities include radio, television, newspaper, posters, flyers, and postal mailings throughout the community. In current practice, these modalities have important limitations, and achieving wide reach throughout a community may be difficult. Radio and television advertisements may be expensive, and it is difficult to measure the number of individuals reached and the extent of engagement or reaction to the advertisements viewed. By design, community advertisements offer only one-way dissemination of information, with no mechanism for exchange of information.

Community surveys are commonly used as part of CC/PD efforts because they are relatively simple, offer measurable feedback, can sample varied segments of the community, and offer a structured way to assess community understanding of and support for a study.16,17 Community surveys usually consist of a brief synopsis of the study, questions regarding respondent support for emergency care research and for the proposed research trial, and information regarding respondent demographics. Surveys also usually provide the opportunity for free text responses.

Study teams have traditionally sought CC/PD community survey completion by approaching individuals in public settings such as health clinics, emergency department waiting rooms, or health fairs, among others. Manually disseminated community surveys are arduous and expensive to distribute; research teams to require 2 to 3 months to achieve 100 completed surveys.16,18 Manually disseminated surveys may also result in a biased respondent sample not representative of a community’s demographics. An alternative to manually disseminated surveys is random digit dialing, where specialized survey research units use telephone calling technology to randomly contact community members and solicit their responses to defined questions. While used successfully in many CC/PD efforts, random-digit dialing is expensive, often seen as intrusive by community members, and more difficult with many community members abandoning home lines or registering with the National Do Not Call Registry.19

Community discussions are intended to elicit deeper conversations with community members about the proposed research. Research teams typically share information at existing civic meetings or focus groups convened for the study. Research teams may also conduct semi-structured interviews with individual community members or gather individual feedback through interactions at community events, such as health fairs. Community meetings for CC/PD have traditionally faced major limitations, with many efforts experiencing poor attendance or eliciting only limited insights. Setting up community meetings is time-intensive for research teams. The demographics of attendees may not accurately reflect those of the community.

Social Media Campaigns

Research teams have used social media to enhance the advertising component of CC/PD.12,13,20, 21, 22 Social media has many appealing facets, including its relatively high speed, low cost, and broad community reach; most adults in the United States use social media on a daily basis.23,24 Social media enables targeted, scalable outreach through geo-fenced advertising, tailored messaging, and real-time, measurable engagement. In the context of CC/PD, social media offers a unique modality for study advertising, enabling wide dissemination potentially exceeding the scale and speed of traditional advertising modalities.

Deployment of social media advertisements focuses on targeted populations in the study community, with consideration of user age, sex, and geographic residence. For example, for the Pediatric Prehospital Airway Resuscitation Trial (Pedi-PART), a multicenter clinical trial of paramedic prehospital airway management strategies in critically ill children, the social media campaign targeted adults (ages 18-65) who were registered social media users 1) in the geographic catchment area of each participating research site, and 2) in zip codes within the catchment area with high frequencies of pediatric EMS calls.26 Because the trial would enroll children as participants, the social media campaign focused on adults ages 18-45 years who would be most likely to be parents of children ages 3-17 years. Meta does not allow targeting of individuals based on race/ethnicity or age <18 years. The team deployed social media advertisements for a period of 60 days, a period agreed to by the single IRB for the study. The social media advertisements linked to local websites for each of the 10 sites in the trial, with plans to maintain public availability of the websites for the duration of the study, plus one year.26

An important feature of social media is its ability to assess advertisement exposure and engagement.11 (Figure 1) For example, Meta can report advertisement “impressions,” the number of times an advertisement was displayed to registered social media users; this figure reflects the magnitude of advertisement dissemination through the targeted population. Meta can also report the “reach” of an advertisement; the number of unique registered users who saw the advertisement at least once. Unlike traditional advertising, users can react to advertisements through “reactions” (likes, loves), posting comments, or sharing the advertisement with other users. Meta can identify the “click through” rate, indicating the extent to which users accessed the study website through the advertisement, thus offering another measure of user/advertisement interaction. Advertisement “engagements” encompasses clicking on the advertisement, “likes,” comments on the advertisement, and sharing of the advertisement with other users. Additional perspectives can be obtained by using an analytics program (eg, Google analytics) to assess study web activity, identifying the specific study web pages examined by website visitors as well as the time spent on each page.

Current digital frameworks also allow for centralized development and deployment of social media advertisements and websites. While local sites could develop their own websites and social media websites, local technical, regulatory, and logistical barriers often pose important challenges. For example, many academic institutions exercise strong oversight over website design and deployment, and social media activity. Even paying for social media advertisements may be challenging, as Meta does not accept conventional institutional purchase orders. In evolving models, a single expert team develops and deploys CC/PD social media and internet materials for all study sites, including building a series of local community websites.11,26 By leveraging their expertise and experience, they are able to centrally develop, implement, and monitor CC/PD materials across sites.

Digital Community Surveys and Survey Panels

Research teams traditionally solicit completion of community surveys using printed survey instruments. Software such as Qualtrics (Qualtrics International, Inc., Provo, Utah) and Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap, Vanderbilt University, Nashville, Tennessee) offer digital platforms for disseminating and completing surveys.26 In addition to streamlining survey completion, data consolidation, and analysis, Uniform Resource Locator (URL) or Quick Response (QR) codes to the surveys can also be easily shared. In contrast to printed surveys, digital surveys can incorporate skip-logic to redirect individuals with a misunderstanding of the survey questions or content.

For CC/PD, a logical approach would be to place a survey link on local study websites, leveraging the large number of visitors referred from social media advertisements. However, from practical experience, few completed surveys result from visits to study websites. Thus, research teams have shifted to the use of marketing survey panels to solicit community survey completion.11,26 (Figure 2) Survey panels are groups of individuals prerecruited to answer ongoing surveys for marketing or other research. A prominent example is the Qualtrics Consumer Panel (Qualtrics International, Inc., Provo, UT).11,20 The service builds panels of respondents using multiple recruitment tools, including website intercept recruitment, member referrals, targeted email lists, gaming sites, customer loyalty web portals, permission-based networks, and social media. The resulting panel includes a range of participants representative of the given community. Panels typically compensate members for survey participation. While intended primarily for marketing research, these platforms have been leveraged for scientific research.20, 21, 22 Qualtrics’ survey platform incorporates multiple measures to ensure the quality and integrity of surveys; for example, screens for duplicate or fraudulent responses.23Figure 2Example of a digital community survey campaign for community consultation and public disclosure. In recent efforts, study teams have used survey panels to disseminate community surveys across study sites. The result is a large volume of community survey responses at a high response rate. The tempo of survey collection is also faster than with manual methods. For example, in the Pediatric Dose Optimization for Seizures in EMS (PediDOSE) Trial, study teams at 20 sites each required 3 to 5 months to collect a total of 2400 completed printed surveys.24 In contrast, in the Pedi-PART trial, through the use of the survey panels, each of the 10 study sites achieved the predefined target of 2000 completed surveys within 5 to 10 days. The overall Pedi-PART survey campaign resulted in over 6700 completed surveys with a survey response rate of 60%.25

With manual survey dissemination, the demographics of survey respondents may not accurately reflect the demographics of the targeted communities, failing to adequately include minorities or those with lower income or educational attainment.27 Survey panels may better address these limitations, offering improved approximations of community demographics and capturing key underrepresented populations.17,28 For example, in the community survey efforts of the TROOP trial, 43% of respondents reported minority race, 13% reported Hispanic ethnicity, and 12% reported lower than high school diploma education.17 In Pedi-PART, survey respondents and census populations showed similar age mix (age >35 years, 67.1% vs. 66.5%), but slightly differed for gender (female 60.0% vs. 50.9%), minority race (33.9% vs. 52.1%), Hispanic ethnicity (15.3% vs. 34.9%), household income (77.5% vs. 60.2% <$100,000/y) and education (23.2% vs. 34.9% high school education or higher).28

Digital Videoconferencing Platforms

Accelerated by the COVID-19 pandemic, modern video conferencing technology such as Zoom and Microsoft Teams has become more widely used for remote communication and collabroation.29 Video conferencing platforms have also offered new approaches for CC/PD meeting and interviewing efforts. Inperson CC/PD meetings or interviews can be logistically difficult due to the challenges of scheduling and the time and expense of travel. The use of video conferencing technology reduces these barriers and potentially enables broader participation. Features, such as the ability to share a video, comment via chat, and record and transcribe a discussion, can also stimulate dialogue with participants.

A challenge of CC/PD is that local study teams may have limited experience with qualitative research methods, such as leading discussions, probing topics, and identifying discussion themes. Recent efforts have addressed this challenge by centrally coordinating all CC/PD meetings and interviews. Under this model, all virtual meetings and interviews are facilitated by a national research team with expertise and experience in moderating CC/PD events, ensuring consistent conduct across all sessions. To maintain a local presence, local study teams still play prominent roles in these meetings, identifying the participants and attending the session to answer local level questions. At the conclusion of the CC/PD efforts, the national team distills all transcripts to produce consistent site level summaries for the IRB, reducing variation in report content and thematic interpretation.

An important question is whether IRBs accept CC/PD approaches incorporating digital engagement tools. There are no set standards for the scope or scale of CC/PD efforts.30 Federal regulations do not define “community,” and there are no standardized criteria for assessing the effectiveness of CC/PD. Study teams select CC/PD activities relative to the risk of a given study and the target population. Studies indicate that IRBs often rely on prior experience and investigators’ gestalt assessment of the appropriateness of CC/PD efforts.30 For the studies listed in Table 3, the IRBs have supported the centralized digital techniques, noting their ability to engage and elicit responses from large portions of the targeted communities.

Challenges and Limitations of Digital Approaches to CC/PD

While digital technologies provide additional options for CC/PD, they also have important limitations. Reliance on internet access and familiarity with online platforms may exclude people with limited digital literacy or inadequate broadband access.33 In addition, common digital metrics, such as advertisement impressions, clicks, and survey completion primarily measure interaction with the platform rather than deeper understanding, trust, or acceptance of EFIC research. Centrally deployed digital strategies also raise operational and ethical concerns, including dependence on commercial platforms, evolving privacy policies, and variable institutional acceptance of externally hosted websites or third-party survey vendors. Investigators should view digital methods as complementary tools rather than definitive solutions for CC/PD. Ongoing evaluation is needed to determine whether digital tools improve community comprehension, trust, and acceptance of proposed EFIC research.

Future Considerations

Further enhancement of CC/PD is possible with additional technological innovation. Current models use the social media platform Meta because of its wide use and ability to target specific populations. However, many individuals – particularly younger generations – do not actively use Meta. Exploration of other platforms such as Google, YouTube, and TikTok may be necessary in future efforts, particularly for studies involving adolescent age groups. The models described used Qualtrics survey panels, but the use of other digital platforms is possible; for example, SurveyMonkey Audience (Survey Monkey, Inc., San Mateo, CA), Dynata (Dynata, Inc, Shelton, Connecticut), and Ipsos iSay (Ipsos Interactive Services, Inc., Norwalk, Connecticut). Current CC/PD models use experienced moderators to identify themes from virtual community meetings and interviews. Artificial intelligence has been used to perform thematic analysis in qualitative research and could offer additional strategies to enhance the rigor and efficiency of CC/PD assessment.34,35

Conclusion

We provide an overview of novel approaches to EFIC CC/PD using digital technologies. These approaches may extend the reach and efficiency of CC/PD efforts, supporting the ethical conduct of emergency care research trials.
