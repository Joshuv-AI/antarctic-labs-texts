Evolution and diversification of Ca2+/H+ exchangers: insights into karst-plant adaption to high-calcium environment
Authors: Endian Yang, Yi Zhang, Hongwen Huang, Chen Feng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCalcium (Ca2+) is a critical mineral essential for plant growth, development, and signal transduction. The CAX (Ca²⁺/H⁺ exchanger) family represents a group of key transmembrane transporters that mediate Ca2+ transport and homeostasis, thereby playing a central role in regulating various physiological processes. Although previous studies have addressed the functional characterization of certain CAX proteins, a comprehensive understanding of their evolutionary origins and trajectory remains limited.ResultsWe identified 216 CAX proteins from 49 green plant species and performed in-depth phylogenetic and expression analyses. Our phylogenetic results suggest that CAX proteins originated early in plant evolution, with a presence detectable even in red algae. Notably, two major CAX clades diverged prior to the emergence of seed plants. Following the whole-genome duplication (WGD) events, CAX further expanded in seed plants, with the Clade 1 splitting into two distinct subgroups. Expression analysis revealed tissue-specific expression patterns within the CAX family, with Clade 1–1 potentially involved in the development of reproductive organs. Additionally, the genus Primulina exhibited lineage-specific variations in key structural domains, which may contribute to its adaptation to high-calcium environments.ConclusionsOur study provides new insights into the phylogenetic relationships of CAX family members across green plant lineages and highlights the significant role of CAXs in plant adaptation and diversification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07330-y.Keywords: Calcium, Ca2+/H+ exchanger, Phylogenomics, Primulina, Adaptive evolution

Introduction

Calcium (Ca2+) is an essential mineral element for plant growth and development, serving as a fundamental component of cellular structures. It plays an important role in stabilizing the cell wall and plasma membrane [1]. Beyond the structural function, Ca²⁺ functions as a secondary messenger in various physiological processes, including the signaling pathways that mediate plant responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses [2]. Maintaining precise regulation of cytosolic Ca²⁺ concentration is crucial for cellular functions. Unlike other mineral elements required in high concentrations, the free Ca²⁺ concentration in the plant cytoplasm ([Ca2+]cyt) is tightly regulated at ~ 100 nM, approximately 10,000 times lower than in the extracellular space [3, 4]. This necessitates a highly regulated system for Ca²⁺ storage and transport to prevent intracellular Ca²⁺ toxicity while enabling transient fluctuations for signaling purposes. The vacuole serves as the primary storage site for Ca²⁺, sequestering excess free Ca²⁺ to avoid cytotoxicity and releasing it as needed during signaling events [5]. Central to this process is the cation/H⁺ exchanger (CAX), a key transporter responsible for mediating Ca²⁺ uptake into and release from the vacuole.

CAX, a member of the CaCA (Ca²⁺/Cation antiporter) superfamily, mediates the transport of various cations. These transmembrane proteins consist of 11 transmembrane domains arranged in two pseudo-symmetric repeats [6]. CAXs are involved in a wide range of essential physiological processes, including Ca2+ signaling, ion homeostasis regulation, and responses to environmental stress. In Arabidopsis thaliana, six CAX genes have been identified. Notably, the CAX1 gene regulates the cytosolic Ca²⁺ homeostasis by transporting Ca²⁺ into vacuoles, thereby preventing cytosolic Ca²⁺ accumulation. This regulation is crucial for normal plant growth and responses to both biotic and abiotic stresses [7]. Similarly, the OsCAX4 gene in Oryza sativa (rice) enhances salt tolerance by modulating intracellular Ca²⁺ levels under saline conditions [8]. Beyond stress responses, CAX genes are essential for ion homeostasis and storage. For instance, AtCAX4 regulates the transport of Ca²⁺ as well as other divalent ions, such as Cd²⁺ and Mn²⁺, maintaining ion balance across plant tissues [9]. In crops, overexpression of the CAX1 in Solanum lycopersicum (tomato) has been shown to increase fruit Ca²⁺ content and improve fruit texture, highlighting its role in Ca2+ accumulation and potential applications in enhancing crop quality [10]. Recent studies have further illuminated the role of CAX-regulated Ca2+ signaling in abiotic stresses. In Arabidopsis, knockout experiments demonstrated that the atcax2 mutant prolonged Ca²⁺ signaling, activating hypoxia-responsive pathways and enhancing survival under flooding conditions [11]. The diverse functions of CAXs are likely driven by their complex gene structures. Functional assays in yeast have demonstrated that the presence of an autoinhibition domain in the N-terminal region of plant CAX proteins [12]. Activation of Ca2+ transport in yeast requires the expression of the N-terminal truncated AtCAX1, rather than the full-length protein. It is noteworthy that overexpression of the N-terminal pseudosymmetrical module containing the autoinhibition domain in wild-type Arabidopsis results in a phenotype resembling the cax1 mutant, likely due to direct interactions with CAX1 protein [6]. However, the precise mechanism remains unclear. A significant breakthrough identified the S-cluster, a group of four consecutive serine residues within the autoinhibition domain that is strictly conserved in CAX1/3 orthologues across angiosperms, as a key regulatory element. Phosphorylation of the S-cluster alleviates autoinhibition, activation the Ca2+ transport function of CAX proteins [13].

CAX family members are widely distributed across bacteria, fungi, and higher plants, and algae [14]. Previous studies classified the CAX family into three major clades: Type I, encompassing plant, fungal, and bacterial members; Type II, found in lower vertebrates; and Type III, primarily composed of bacterial members [14]. The advent of next-generation sequencing technologies has led to the identification of an increasing number of CAXs in plants. For example, A. thaliana and O. sativa contain six members [15], while Malus domestica and Populus trichocarpa harbor ten and seven members [16, 17]. CAX proteins in angiosperms are classified into two primary evolutionary clades, with AtCAX1/3/4 forming one clade and AtCAX2/5/6 another [18]. In contrast to lower plants like algae, which typically have a low copy number of CAXs, higher plants exhibit significant expansion and diversification of the CAX family [19]. Whole-genome duplication (WGD) events are considered a major driving force behind the expansion of gene families [20]. The extensive expansion of plant CAXs can be traced back to ancient WGD events in the common ancestor of land plants [21]. Gene family expansion typically increases gene copy number, enabling functional diversification and contributing to the adaptive evolution of plant species [22]. Species with varying life histories and ecological niches often exhibit distinct patterns of gene family expansion and contraction, allowing them to respond effectively to diverse environmental pressures [23]. Although research has focused on the functional characterization of CAXs in model plants like Arabidopsis, the evolutionary and adaptive roles of CAX gene families in non-model plants remain largely unexplored. This gap is particularly evident in studies addressing specific environmental adaptations, such as the response to high-calcium conditions in karst regions. Primulina (Gesneriaceae), a non-model genus characterized by widespread edaphic specialization and primary occurrence in limestone karst regions, is thus exceptionally well-suited for studying plant adaptation to these environments [24]. Research on Primulina CAXs can therefore serve as a crucial case study. Consequently, comprehensive phylogenetic analyses across green plants hold great potential to uncover the functional diversity and evolutionary mechanisms of CAX proteins. These analyses can provide valuable insights into how CAX family members contribute to plant adaptation and survival in varying environmental contexts.

This study aims to investigate the origin, evolutionary trajectory, and functional diversity of the CAX gene family in green plants through phylogenetic analysis and functional comparison. By tracing the evolutionary history of CAXs, we reveal species-specific expansions driven by multiple WGD events, and clarify the potential functions of CAX proteins. Our findings highlight the role of CAXs in plant adaptive evolution, particularly their mechanisms for thriving in high-calcium environments. This research provides valuable insights for enhancing crop stress resistance and offers a theoretical framework for understanding the evolution of plant biodiversity in high-calcium karst habitats.

Materials and methods

Acquisition and identification of CAX sequences in plants

The genomic data for 49 plant species were obtained from publicly accessible databases (Table S1), including China National GeneBank DataBase (CNGBdb, https://db.cngb.org/), Phytozome v13 (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/), and EnsemblPlants (https://plants.ensembl.org/index.html/). These data encompass GFF (General feature format) files, genome sequences, coding sequences, and protein sequences, representing the full phylogenetic breadth of green plants, including rhodophytes, chlorophytes, charophytes, bryophytes, ferns, lycophytes, and seed plants. The Hidden Markov Model (HMM) file for the Na_Ca_ex domain (PF01699) obtained from the Pfam database, was used as a query in HMMER3 (v3.3) software to identify CAX homologs across the plant genomes, with a stringent e-value < 10−5 [25]. Additionally, protein BLAST searches were performed to detect CAX homologs in other plants with e-value < 10−5, and the reference sequences were derived from O. sativa [26], A. thaliana [14], and P. trichocarpa [16]. Protein sequences shorter than 100 amino acids were excluded through manual filtering. The identified protein sequences were then validated for the presence of conserved domains using the SMART database (https://smart.embl.de/). For genes with multiple transcripts, only the longest transcript was retained as the representative protein sequence. As a result, 216 CAX protein sequences were identified across the 49 species.

Multiple sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis of CAXs

The identified protein sequences were aligned using MAFFT (v7.490) with the parameters --thread 12 --localpair --maxiterate 1000. The resulting multiple sequence alignments were subsequently trimmed with TrimAl using the -automated1 parameters to remove poorly aligned regions [27]. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using IQTREE (v1.6.12) based on the maximum likelihood (ML) method, with the best-fit evolutionary model determined according to the Bayesian information criterion (BIC), and the bootstrap value was set to 1000 [28]. To explore the phylogenetic relationships among various plant lineages, we constructed separate phylogenetic trees based on the taxonomy. These phylogenetic trees were visualized with FigTree (v1.4.4) and iTOL [29].

Conserved motif and selective pressure analysis

To identify conserved domains and functional sites within the CAX family, the protein sequences were analyzed using the MEME (v5.5.5, https://meme-suite.org/). The width of the conserved sites was set between 6 and 50, with the Maximum number of conserved sequences Limited to 15. The coding sequences (CDS) were aligned using MAFFT by employing the ‘--codon’ option, which ensures that the alignment process considers codon boundaries, preserving the integrity of the genetic code. The aligned sequences were refined by Gblocks (v0.91b), which removed poorly aligned regions while retaining codon structure. Phylogenetic trees were constructed using ML method implemented in IQTREE. HyPhy (v2.5.33) was used to perform branch-site models to detect positive selection across the phylogenetic tree [30].

Expression profiles of CAX genes

The gene expression profiles for A. thaliana were obtained from Arabidopsis RNA-seq database (https://plantrnadb.com/athrdb/). Expression data for the CAXs in O. sativa were obtained from RiceENCODE database [31]. For Z. mays, expression levels were retrieved from the ZEAMAP database [32], and the expression data for P. eburnea were derived from our previous study. The heatmap of gene expression levels was visualized using TBtools [33].

Results

Identification and classification of CAX gene family among various plant species

To identify the CAX gene family in plants and investigate their evolutionary relationships, a combined search strategy employing BLASTP and HMM was applied to screen for CAX protein sequences across 49 fully sequenced species (Table S1). CAX homologs were not found in three algal species (Cyanidioschyzon merolae, Ostreococcus lucimarinus, and Spirogloea muscicola). However, a total of 216 CAX proteins were identified in the remaining 46 species, representing a wide range of plant groups from algae to eudicots (Table S2). Overall, the number of CAXs exhibits a notable expansion from lower to higher plants. While algae typically possess only a single CAX protein, the basal streptophyte alga Chara braunii already harbors two CAX homologs (Fig. 1A). This indicates that CAX genes diversification originated early in the streptophyte lineage during the evolution toward land plants. Subsequently, the gene copy number significantly increases with the diversification of vascular plants (Fig. 1A and Table S2).

The phylogenetic analysis was performed using the ML method on full-length protein sequences to determine the evolutionary relationships among CAXs. Simplified phylogenetic trees are shown in Fig. 1B and C, while the complete trees, including sequence labels and bootstrap values, are provided in Supplementary files (Fig. S1). In the phylogenetic trees, CAXs from rhodophytes and chlorophytes form a distinct clade, with chlorophytes CAXs positioned at the basal-most part of the tree (Fig. 1B and C, Fig. S1). The CAXs of lycophytes and ferns cluster into another clade (Fig. 1B). Based on evolutionary relationships, plant CAX proteins are divided into two Main clades, Clade 1 and Clade 2 (Fig. 1C). The algal branch is independently positioned as an outgroup and is not included in either of the two Main clades. Clade 1 contains more genes than Clade 2 (122 vs. 87) (Fig. 1A, Table S2 and S3). Clade 2 is composed solely of CAXs that emerged after the divergence of seed plants, whereas all CAXs from lycophytes and ferns are classified into Clade 1 (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1).

The evolutionary relationships of CAX gene family in plants

The phylogenetic and structural analyses revealed critical insights into the origin and evolutionary history of CAX proteins. Excluding the algal CAX clade, the remaining clades were predominantly composed of land plant CAXs (Fig. 1B). The phylogenetic tree distinctly separated the algal CAXs from those of land plants, suggesting that all land plant CAXs share a common ancestor origin. Notably, CAXs from early land plants, such as bryophytes, ferns, and lycophytes, were grouped into Clade 1, indicating that this clade is more conserved (Fig. 1C and Fig. S1). To further elucidate the evolutionary relationships among seed plant CAXs, we constructed a ML tree focusing exclusively on seed plants, using ferns and lycophytes as outgroups (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2). In this analysis, seed plant CAXs were divided into two distinct clades, Clade 1 and Clade 2, both of which encompass representatives from all Major seed plant Lineages. Additionally, Clade 1 exhibited evidence of additional duplication events, leading to the formation of subclades 1–1 and 1–2 (Fig. 2 and Fig. S2). The basal position of gymnosperms within Clade 1 implies that gymnosperms represent an early-diverging Lineage, potentially predating the differentiation of subclades 1–1 and 1–2 (Fig. 2).

Expansion and loss of CAXs during the evolution of seed plants

The CAX gene family in seed plants has undergone significant diversification throughout evolution. To investigate the patterns of expansion and loss within this family, we constructed ML trees for each subclade of the phylogenetic tree of seed plant CAX proteins (Fig. 3). The monophyletic group of gymnosperms within the tree supports a topology that primarily reflects the phylogenetic relationships of CAXs in angiosperms. CAXs are widely distributed across angiosperms, with varying degrees of copy number expansion observed in different subclades. In basal angiosperms, Amborella trichopoda retains three CAX copies, each located at the base of separate subclades. In contrast, other basal angiosperms, such as Brasenia schreberi and Euryale ferox of the order Nymphaeales, exhibit significant expansion of CAX genes. The extensive distribution of CAX homologs across the three major angiosperms branches indicates that these branches likely originated from a common ancestor before the divergence of angiosperms. These ancestral CAX genes were subsequently retained in most extant species, contributing to the observed diversity and functional adaptation of the CAX gene family.

To better understand the evolutionary dynamics of the CAX gene family, we annotated the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3A–C) with key whole-genome duplication (WGD) events, including ϵ (ancestral angiosperms), γ (core eudicots), τ (monocots), σ/ρ (Poaceae-specific), and α/β (Brassicaceae-specific) [34, 35]. The Clade 1 clusters into two Major clades, each exhibiting distinct patterns of expansion and contraction. In Clade 1–1, early-diverging angiosperms, including Magnoliids, Chloranthaceae, and most monocots, possess a single copy of the CAX gene, suggesting that Clade 1–1 may be more conserved (Fig. 3A). Particularly, despite experiencing the lineage-specific σ/ρ WGD events, the Poaceae family retains only a single copy of the CAX gene within the Clade 1–1 (Fig. 3A). In contrast, significant expansion of the CAX family occurred in most eudicots, including A. thaliana, which contains three CAX proteins: AtCAX2, AtCAX5, and AtCAX6. Unlike the conserved pattern in Clade 1–1, Clade 1–2, the smallest subclade containing 49 CAX proteins (Fig. 3D), experienced substantial gene expansion, possibly driven by an ancient WGD (τ event) in early monocots. Additionally, further duplication events are observed in certain Poaceae species, such as Zea mays (Fig. 3C). Conversely, most eudicots show a reduction in the number of CAX gene copies in Clade 1–2, generally retaining only a single copy, and A. thaliana lacks CAX genes in this clade entirely (Fig. 3B). In Clade 2, a large-scale expansion of CAX genes is observed, likely due to an ancient γ WGD event in the core eudicots, leading to the formation of two smaller subclades (Fig. 3C). Notably, most monocots in this clade have two or more CAX copies, indicating that gene expansion also occurred in monocots, potentially linked to the more recent ρ WGD event in Poaceae.

Furthermore, we focused on the CAX genes in the genus Primulina, a taxon adapted to high-calcium environments, where significant expansion of the CAX gene family was observed. Notably, CAX genes from Primulina are absent in Clade 1–1 (Fig. 3A). In contrast, Primulina exhibits substantial CAX gene expansion in Clade 1–2 and Clade 2 (Fig. 3B and C), with multiple copies retained, suggesting a specialized functional role of these CAXs in Primulina species.

Gene expression analysis of CAX genes in plants

To explore the functional roles of CAX genes across different plant tissues and developmental stages, we performed a comparative analysis using transcriptome data from four representative species (A. thaliana, Z. mays, O. sativa, and P. eburnea). The expression profiles revealed distinct patterns across different evolutionary branches (Fig. 4A). Among these species, P. eburnea exhibited the highest number of CAX gene copies, with a total of eight. These CAX genes displayed tissue-specific expression in flowers, roots, and leaves. In Clade 1–1, although no CAX genes were identified in P. eburnea, CAX genes in the other three species were highly expressed in floral organs. In Clade 1–2, Z. mays CAX genes were broadly expressed across nearly all tissues, with no significant tissue specificity observed. However, in O. sativa and P. eburnea, CAX genes exhibited higher expression levels in roots compared to other tissues, suggesting that CAX genes in these species may have root-specific functions (Fig. 4A). In Clade 2, AtCAX3 in A. thaliana had the highest expression in siliques, with minimal expression in stems, while AtCAX1 and AtCAX4 showed low expression in siliques. In Z. mays, ZmCAX1a/b exhibited significantly lower expression in seed organs compared to vegetative and floral organs. In O. sativa, OsCAX1a/b/c were highly expressed in leaves. In P. eburnea, PebCAX1a/b/c showed elevated expression in above-ground tissues, whereas PebCAX3/4 were more highly expressed in roots (Fig. 4A). These findings suggest that while CAX genes within the same clade may have similar functions across different species, functional diversification exists among multiple copies of CAX genes, reflecting their specialized roles in tissue-specific processes.

Furthermore, in our previous study, we investigated the transcriptional response network of P. eburnea under 100 mM high-calcium stress over different time courses [36]. Based on this dataset, we Herein focused on analyzing the expression patterns of 8 CAX genes (Fig. 4B). Our results revealed that P. eburnea CAX genes belonging to Clade 1–2 exhibited similar moderate expression levels in both leaves and roots (Fig. 4B). In contrast, CAX genes from P. eburnea in Clade 2 displayed significant tissue specificity and showed marked responses to high-calcium stress. The PebCAX1a/b/c genes were highly expressed in leaves, with their expression levels increasing significantly after 12 h of high-calcium stress exposure (Fig. 4B). Additionally, PebCAX3/4 showed extremely low expression in leaves but responded to high-calcium stress in roots. Notably, the expression level of PebCAX4 was significantly downregulated following high-calcium stress (Fig. 4B). These expression patterns indicate that the CAX genes in P. eburnea have undergone distinct functional divergence, which likely contributes to the adaptive strategies of this species for coping with high-calcium environments.

Analysis of the motifs and key amino acid residues of CAX protein sequences

Multiple gene copies generated by gene duplication events may exhibit different motif structures, thereby affecting protein function [22]. To investigate the structural divergence of CAX proteins in seed plants, we analyzed the motifs of CAXs across different species. The results revealed that Clade 1 and Clade 2 share 11 conserved motifs, each containing two unique motifs specific to each clade (Fig. 5A and Fig. S3). For instance, AtCAX5 belongs to the Clade 1, whereas AtCAX1 is classified within the Clade 2. Using AlphaFold3 [37], we predicted the three-dimensional structures of these two proteins and found that Clade 1-specific motifs 8 and 14, together with Clade 2-specific motif 10, form a single alpha-helix. Notably, motif 12, unique to Clade 2, forms an alpha-helix at the N-terminus of the CAX proteins, distinguishing it from AtCAX5 in Clade 1 (Fig. 5B). It has been reported that the Ca2+ transport capacity of truncated AtCAX1/3 proteins in Arabidopsis, when expressed in yeast, is significantly enhanced after deletion of the N-terminal region, which is identified as an auto-inhibitory domain [38]. Further sequence alignment of CAX proteins in Clade 2 indicated that motif 12 contains a serine-rich cluster (S-cluster) composed of four serine residues, which is conserved across multiple species (Fig. 5C). The S-cluster, located in the auto-inhibitory domain, is phosphorylated by CBL-CIPK module in response to elevated external Ca2+ level [13]. Interestingly, mutations in the S-cluster have occurred in Primulina genus species (P. eburnea, P. huaijiensis and P. tabacum), where serine at position 1 is replaced by asparagine and serine at position 3 by proline (Fig. 5C). These mutations may influence how CAX genes respond to external Ca2+, potentially reflecting the long-term adaptation of Primulina species to the high-calcium soils in karst regions.

S-cluster variations widely exist within Primulina

To further investigate the prevalence of S-cluster variations in Primulina, we analyzed transcriptomic data from 12 species in this genus (Fig. S4). The results revealed that variations in the S-cluster were identified in 7 species (Fig. 6A), further confirming the widespread presence of S-cluster variations across the Primulina genus. Moreover, we constructed a synteny map of the CAX genes across four species: V. vinifera (grapevine), A. thaliana (a model plant), P. eburnea (a representative species of Primulina), and P. longipes (another Primulina species that coexists in karst high-calcium environments), as shown in Fig. 6B. Continuous colinear gene pairs were identified across the four species, indicating that these genes are highly conserved and originated from a common ancestor genome (Fig. 6B). Although AtCAX1 and AtCAX3 show synteny with two CAXs in P. eburnea, the S-cluster variations were present only in P. eburnea, highlighting the unique adaptive differentiation of this genus. The ability of Primulina species to thrive in high-calcium karst environments is likely attribute to the specialized S-cluster mutations, which could play critical role in maintaining cellular Ca2+ homeostasis in these challenging conditions.

Discussion

Ca2+ is essential for plant growth, development, and signal transduction, playing a central role in numerous physiological processes. The transport and mediation of Ca2+ are facilitated by a variety of transport proteins [39], with the CAX gene family being particularly important for maintaining cytosolic Ca2+ homeostasis [40]. Although the functional significance of CAXs is well-established, the origin and evolutionary history of CAX gene family duplications across green plants remain poorly understood. In this study, we systematically identified CAX proteins from the genomes of 49 plant species, spanning algae to eudicots. Through phylogenetic analysis, we aim to elucidate the evolutionary trajectories of CAXs across different plant lineages, providing valuable insights into the origin and duplication events, as well as the functional diversity of the CAX proteins.

The evolutionary origin and expansion of the CAXs

The CAX gene family, a member of the Ca2+/cation antiporter superfamily, exports cations from the cytosol to maintain optimal ionic concentrations within the cell. CAX sequences have been identified across a broad range of organisms, including bacteria, fungi, plants, and animals [41, 42]. Previous studies have classified the CAX gene family into three major groups: type Ⅰ (plants), type Ⅱ (animals), and type Ⅲ (bacteria) [14]. Plant CAX proteins were further divided into two distinct clades. In the present study, we conducted a phylogenetic reconstruction and sequence analysis of CAX gene in green plants. Consistent with previous findings, our analysis also identifies two Major groups of CAX proteins, Clade 1 and Clade 2 (Fig. 1C). Our phylogenetic tree shows that the CAXs in algae form a distinct branch at the base, suggesting a red algae origin for these genes. Rhodophytes and chlorophytes typically contain only a single CAX protein (Fig. 7 and Fig S1), whereas bryophytes, which represent plants transitioning from aquatic to terrestrial environments, exhibit a significant increase in CAX gene copies (Fig. 1A). Interestingly, Zygnematophyceae, considered the closest sister group to embryophyte land plants, shares similar ecological niches with the early-diverging bryophytes [43]. In Mesotaenium endlicherianum, a member of Zygnematophyceae, we identified a single CAX protein that, based on phylogenetic analysis, clustered at the base of the bryophyte CAX clade (Fig S1). We hypothesize that the common ancestor of CAX genes originated in early plant lineages and underwent gene amplification during the divergence of bryophytes. As plants adapted to terrestrial environments, the evolution from aquatic to land-dwelling species likely drove the evolution of the CAX gene family. This gene duplication enabled plants to regulate various cations more efficiently, helping them meet the complex demands of terrestrial ecosystems. The independent gene duplication events in seed plants and ferns suggest a parallel expansion of CAX genes in both groups (Fig. 7). Although ferns and seed plants diverged early in plant phylogeny [44], they appear to have evolved similar strategies to cope with terrestrial pressures. One such strategy is the expansion of the CAX gene family, which enhances the plants’ ability to regulate Ca2+ concentrations in the soil. This regulation is vital for stabilizing cellular functions and maintaining physiological integrity, thus supporting plant adaptation to challenging and complex environments.

With the emergence and diversification of seed plants, the CAX gene family underwent further expansion and differentiation. Our phylogenetic analysis showed that the CAXs in seed plants can be divided into two main branches, likely the result of an early gene duplication event that predates the appearance of seed plants (Fig. 2). Previous studies have indicated that gymnosperms have experienced fewer WGD events. For instance, the genome of Ginkgo biloba has only undergone an ancient polyploidization event shared by all seed plants [45, 46]. Our phylogenetic tree topology indicates that Ginkgo CAXs form a separate lineage from those of angiosperms, with gymnosperms possessing significantly fewer CAX copies than angiosperms (Figs. 2 and 6). These results strongly suggest that the Ginkgo genome has not undergone additional lineage-specific WGD events. In contrast, WGDs have played a crucial role in the diversification and origin of phenotypic novelty in many angiosperm lineages [47]. Our results suggest that a gene duplication event occurred in the most recent common ancestor of angiosperms, leading to the formation of Clade1-1 and Clade1-2 (Fig. 3). A. trichopoda, considered the earliest diverging lineage of angiosperms, did not experience extensive WGDs but only underwent an ε duplication event during the common ancestor of angiosperms [48]. In A. trichopoda, three CAXs were identified, one from each of Clade 1–1, Clade 1–2, and Clade 2, (Fig. 4). highlighting the three evolutionary branches of the early angiosperm CAX gene family. Another early angiosperm lineage, E. ferox, contains eight CAXs, with substantial expansion observed in Clade 1–1 and Clade 1–2, which together contain seven CAX copies. This expansion is likely a result of an ancient WGD event, followed by a whole-genome triplication in E. ferox [49]. In O. sativa, six CAX genes have been previously classified into two clades, suggesting that they originated from a shared WGD event in the Poaceae family [26]. In our study, we further classified the O. sativa CAX genes into three clades and found that Clade 1–1 in O. sativa did not undergo significant expansion compared to the other two clades. After the core-eudicot whole-genome triplication (WGT) event, CAX genes in eudicots underwent significant expansion. For instance, Vitis vinifera, a key species within this evolutionary branch, contains five CAX genes. Moreover, our phylogenetic analysis indicates that CAX genes in the Primulina genus have undergone further expansion within their lineage. Previous studies have shown that Primulina huaijiensis underwent two WGD events in addition to the γ event, with the gene family expansion mainly due to lineage-specific WGD events [50]. Interestingly, CAX genes in Primulina are absent from Clade1-1, which may reflect adaptive differentiation to karst calcareous soils.

In summary, the expansion of CAX genes in plants primarily driven by multiple WGD events, with lineage-specific WGD events playing a key role in promoting plant adaptation to diverse environments. The evolution and classification of CAXs suggest that they have been essential in the adaptation of plants from aquatic to more complex terrestrial environments. Specifically, the large-scale expansion of CAX genes in angiosperms is likely associated with the transport and storage of various cations, particularly Ca2+, which are essential for regulating key physiological processes in plants. This adaptation enables plants to thrive in a wide range of environmental conditions, highlighting the importance of CAX proteins in maintaining cellular homeostasis and supporting plant survival in terrestrial ecosystems.

Evolution and functional diversification of the CAX family in plants

CAX genes have been identified and functionally characterized across a range of plant species. These membrane proteins, typically contain 11 transmembrane domains, are predominantly located in the vacuolar membrane, though some are also found in the plasma membrane [15]. Among the best studied, AtCAX1 from A. thaliana exhibits low Ca2+ affinity but high transport capacity, serving a crucial function in regulating cytosolic Ca2+ [51]. The atcax1 mutants display increased sensitivity to Ca2+, with reduced Ca2+ content in the leaves compared to wild-type plants, suggesting its involvement in Ca2+ accumulation in the leaves. In contrast, AtCAX3, which share high sequence similarity with AtCAX1, is mainly expressed in the roots, particularly at the root tips. Double mutants of atcax1/atcax3 show elevated extracellular Ca2+ levels [38], further highlighting their roles in Ca2+ regulation. AtCAX2 is expressed in vascular tissues and floral tissues [52], underscoring its role in the transport Ca2+ within these tissues. In this study, we analyzed the expression profiles of CAXs across four representative species and confirmed tissue-specific expression patterns consistent with previous reports. Notably, AtCAX5 exhibited significantly high expression in floral organs (Fig. 4A). Similar expression patterns were observed in floral tissues in two other species, indicating a conserved role for CAXs in regulating Ca2+ distribution in flowers. We hypothesize that CAXs regulate Ca2+ distribution to trigger secondary signaling in flowers, although the exact regulatory mechanisms necessitate further investigation. Gene family expansion often leads to functional diversification. For example, AtCAX1 and AtCAX3 are primarily involved in Ca2+ transport [38], while AtCAX2 and AtCAX4 have broader cation substrate specificity, participating in the regulation of multiple mineral nutrients like Cd2+, Mn2+, Zn2+, and Na+ [53, 54]. Recent studies have further revealed that CAXs contribute to plant responses to various abiotic stresses [55]. For example, AtCAX1 participates in Ca2+ signaling under hypoxic stress, facilitating downstream metabolic changes that enhance hypoxia tolerance [56], while MdCAX3L-2 negatively regulates cold tolerance in apples [57]. Our findings reveal that the CAX gene family in angiosperms has undergone significant expansion and differentiation (Fig. 3), likely enhancing plant stress adaptation to various stress conditions. Phylogenetic analysis classified plant CAXs into two primary evolutionary branches (Fig. 1C), with genes within the same branch sharing structural and functional similarities. For example, AtCAX1 and AtCAX3, which belong to Clade 2, both contain an N-terminal auto-inhibitory domain that suppresses Ca2+ transport activity. This auto-inhibitory domain is highly conserved in CAXs across diverse plant species [15, 16, 58]. The N-terminal sequence contains an S-cluster of four consecutive serine residues, whose phosphorylation relieves the inhibitory effect on CAXs [13]. In this study, we found that CAXs in Clade 2 generally possess an S-cluster motif (Fig. 5A), conserved across multiple plants. However, in the Primulina genus, specific mutations were detected in the first and third serine residues of the S-cluster (Fig. 5C). Additionally, selective pressure on CAXs was assessed using the aBSREL model in Hyphy software. The results demonstrated that the CAXs branch in the Primulina genus has undergone significant positive selection throughout its evolutionary history (Fig. S4 and Table S4). We hypothesize that the S-cluster play a crucial role in the adaptive evolution in Primulina species, particularly in response to their high-calcium environment.

Although our study shed light on the multifunctional roles of CAXs and their adaptive evolution in Primulina, it is important to note that much of the existing research on CAX genes has been focused on model plants. Studies on non-model species, especially those adapted to specialized environments like Primulina, remain limited. Therefore, while the molecular features of Primulina provide intriguing insights into plant adaptation to high-calcium environments (Fig. 6), further experimental work is required to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms. Such studies could expand our understanding of Ca2+ transport and its regulatory role in plants adapted to extreme environmental conditions.

Conclusion

We conducted comprehensive phylogenetic and expression analyses of CAX genes in green plants, revealing their early origin and evolutionary trajectory. Our study uncovered lineage-specific variations in the CAXs of Primulina species, emphasizing the critical role of CAXs in the adaptive evolution of plants in high-calcium environments. This insight deepens our understanding of Ca2+ transport and regulation mechanisms, providing a clearer picture of how CAXs have evolved across different plant lineage to support adaptation to specialized ecological niches.
