Jab1 regulates HRR mRNA stability to modulate PARP inhibitor sensitivity in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Xin Peng, Yingying Wang, Zixiang Yu, Shengfan Huang, Shaolu Zhang, Zhenxing Zhong, Yongzhe Wang, Shanshan Liu, Kailin Wang, Christophe Nicot, Francois X. Claret, Dexin Kong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is a highly aggressive breast cancer subtype associated with the highest mortality rate among all breast cancer subtypes, primarily due to the absence of actionable therapeutic targets. Although poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) have shown promising therapeutic effects in TNBC patients harboring homologous recombination deficiency (HRD), their clinical benefit remains limited, highlighting an urgent need for novel targets that enhance PARPi efficacy. This study investigates the role of Jab1 in regulating the stability of homologous recombination repair (HRR)-related RNAs and evaluates its potential as a therapeutic target to enhance PARPi sensitivity in TNBC.MethodsRNA-Seq analysis revealed that shRNA-mediated Jab1 knockdown profoundly affected HRR and DNA replication processes in TNBC cells. Using Nuclear Run-On Assay, RNA Immunoprecipitation, RNA Pull-Down Assay, and RIP-Seq, we identified Jab1 as a potential RNA-binding protein (RBP) that stabilizes HRR-related mRNAs by competing with the exosome complex. Genetic and pharmacological inhibition of Jab1 (using CSN5i-3) were evaluated for their impact on HRR efficiency, ionizing radiation (IR) sensitivity, and PARPi sensitivity. A comprehensive panel of in vitro assays was performed, including clonogenic survival assays, PrestoBlue assays, apoptosis assays, DR-GFP reporter assays, qRT-PCR, Western blot, comet assays, and immunofluorescence. In vivo efficacy was assessed using zebrafish xenografts, nude mouse xenografts, and syngeneic orthotopic mouse models to examine the therapeutic effect of Jab1 inhibition in combination with PARPi.ResultsJab1 was found to be overexpressed in TNBC and correlated with poor clinical outcomes. Functional analyses revealed that Jab1 knockdown impaired HRR, increased DNA damage accumulation, and sensitized TNBC cells to IR and PARPi, irrespective of BRCA mutation status. Mechanistically, Jab1 functioned as an RBP through its MPN domain, stabilizing HRR-related transcripts by competitively antagonizing the RNA exosome complex. Pharmacological inhibition of Jab1 using CSN5i-3 recapitulated these effects and synergized with PARPi to induce synthetic lethality. In multiple preclinical models, this combination significantly suppressed tumor growth and promoted apoptosis.ConclusionThis study uncovers a novel role for Jab1 as an RBP, specifically through interactions between its MPN domain and HRR-related RNAs, regulating RNA stability and maintaining HRR competency. Targeting Jab1 represents a promising strategy to pharmacologically induce HRD and enhance the efficacy of PARPi therapies in TNBC. This combination approach may hold translational value for improving clinical outcomes in patients with TNBC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12943-025-02422-7.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most common malignancy among women worldwide, accounting for 23.8% of new cancer diagnoses and representing a major cause of cancer-related deaths [1]. Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), characterized by the absence of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), constitutes approximately 15–20% of breast cancer cases and is distinguished by its aggressive behavior and poor prognosis [2–5]. Due to the lack of effective targeted therapies, chemotherapy, primarily involving anthracyclines and taxanes, remains the cornerstone treatment for TNBC [6]. Despite initial responsiveness, TNBC patients frequently experience relapse, resulting in a median survival time of only 12–18 months, with fewer than 15% of patients surviving beyond five years [7, 8]. Therefore, identifying novel therapeutic targets and developing effective small-molecule inhibitors are crucial for enhancing outcomes for TNBC patients.

Currently, several targeted therapies for TNBC are undergoing clinical investigation, including poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors, PI3K-Akt pathway inhibitors, and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) [9–13]. Among these, PARP inhibitors (PARPi) capitalize on homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) by blocking single-strand break (SSB) repair [14]. This blockage leads to persistent double-strand breaks (DSBs) and results in synthetic lethality in tumor cells [15, 16]. Olaparib and Talazoparib have been approved by the FDA for treating HER2-negative advanced or metastatic breast cancer in patients with BRCA mutations [17, 18]. Furthermore, 34 ongoing clinical trials are actively assessing the efficacy of PARPi in TNBC (clinicaltrials.gov, accessed December 2024).

Despite these advancements, only 10–20% of TNBC patients harbor BRCA1/2 mutations [19–21]. Efforts to expand PARPi use to patients with broader HRD signatures have marginally increased the responder population to 20–30% [22, 23]. Consequently, a substantial proportion of TNBC patients remain ineligible for PARPi therapy [24, 25]. Addressing this challenge requires improved biomarkers for homologous recombination repair (HRR) competency and strategies to pharmacologically induce HRD, thereby enabling broader therapeutic applicability of PARPi in TNBC [26, 27].

We previously identified Jab1 (c-Jun activation domain-binding protein 1), which is encoded by the COPS5 gene, as a protein capable of activating c-Jun [28]. Jab1 serves as the fifth subunit of the COP9 signalosome (CSN), a conserved protein complex that regulates intracellular signaling, transcription, and protein stability [29, 30]. Notably, Jab1 can also function independently of the CSN complex. Its dysregulation has been implicated in various oncogenic processes, including enhanced cell survival [31], cell cycle progression (e.g., via p21, p27, p57) [31–33], and immune evasion (e.g., upregulation of PD-L1) [34]. Jab1 has also been implicated in DNA damage response (DDR) pathways. Our prior studies demonstrated that Jab1 enhances sensitivity to radiotherapy and chemotherapy [31, 35]. Moreover, emerging evidence indicates that Jab1 inhibition may work synergistically with PARP inhibitors (PARPi), enhancing the therapeutic efficacy of DNA damage-based treatments [36]. Despite these promising findings, the exact molecular mechanisms through which Jab1 contributes to the cellular response to DNA damage remain incompletely understood. Further investigation is needed to fully understand Jab1’s role in modulating DNA repair pathways and its potential as a therapeutic target in combination with PARPi.

In this study, transcriptomic analyses revealed that Jab1 inhibition significantly disrupted HRR and altered the expression of key HRR-related factors in TNBC cells. Mechanistic investigations indicated that Jab1 does not affect transcriptional levels but instead regulates the mRNA half-life of these genes, suggesting a previously unrecognized role for Jab1 in RNA stabilization. We further dissected the molecular basis of this regulation, providing new insights into RNA-based mechanisms of DNA repair control and proposing a new strategy to enhance PARPi sensitivity in TNBC.

Methods

Reagents and Antibodies

Olaparib, Talazoparib, and Actinomycin D were purchased from Selleck Chemicals. CSN5i-3 was generously provided by Novartis. The following antibodies were used: RAD54B (Santa Cruz, sc-101234), RAD21 (Abcam, AB217678), RAD51 (Abcam, AB133534), COPS5 (Cell Signaling Technology, 6895 S), Cleaved Caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, 9661 S), Cleaved PARP (Cell Signaling Technology, 5625 S), γ‐H2AX (Cell Signaling Technology, 80312 S), EXOSC4 (Santa Cruz, sc-166772), SKP2 (Cell Signaling Technology, 2652 S), p27 (Cell Signaling Technology, 3686 S), p21 (Cell Signaling Technology, 2947 S), β-actin (ABclonal, AC026), BrdU (Santa Cruz, sc-32323), anti-rabbit IgG HRP‐linked antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 7074 S), anti-mouse IgG HRP‐linked antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, 7076 S), Anti-mouse IgG Fab2 Alexa Fluor(R) 647 (Cell Signaling Technology, 4410 S), and Anti-rabbit IgG Fab2 Alexa Fluor (R) 488 (Cell Signaling Technology, 4412 S).

Cell Culture

Human TNBC cell lines, including MDA-MB-231, BT-549, HCC-1806, MDA-MB-436, and HCC1937, as well as human embryonic kidney 293 T cells and mouse mammary carcinoma 4T1 cells, were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Additionally, the culture medium for MDA-MB-436 cells was supplemented with 10 µg/mL insulin and 16 µg/mL glutathione, whereas HCC1937 cells were cultured in DMEM/F12 medium supplemented with 1 µg/mL hydrocortisone and 5 µg/mL insulin. All cell lines were incubated at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. Cell cultures were passaged every 2–3 days and used within 20 passages to ensure experimental reproducibility and consistency.

Cell Viability Assay

Cell viability assays were performed as previously described [37]. Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well in 200 µl of medium. After 24 h, the cells in each well were treated with DMSO, CSN5i-3, Talazoparib, or Olaparib. At each time point, 10 µl of PrestoBlue Cell Viability Reagent (Invitrogen) was added and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a Tecan plate reader, and cell viability was normalized to the DMSO vehicle control. Data represent an average of three independent experiments.

Establishment of shRNA-Stable Cells

The establishment of shRNA-stable cells was performed as previously described [35]. To generate stable cell lines with knockdown of Jab1, Jab1 shRNA oligonucleotides were cloned into a retrovirus pSIREN-RetroQ system (Clontech, Mountain View, CA, USA). The 293 T cells were co-transfected with Jab1 shRNA vector and helper plasmids (pCGP and pVSVG) using the Lipofectamine PLUS reagent. At 48 h post-transfection, viral supernatants were harvested, filtered through a 0.45-µm syringe filter, and supplemented with 1.2 mg/ml polybrene. Target cells were infected for 12 h, followed by selection in culture medium containing 0.8 mg/ml puromycin for two weeks. Stable knockdown clones were verified by immunoblotting and maintained subsequently in medium supplemented with 0.2 mg/mL puromycin.

Generation of Stable Overexpression Cell Lines

Stable overexpression cell lines were generated as previously described [38]. The full-length COPS5 sequence was synthesized, verified by Sanger sequencing, and cloned into the pLenti-CMV-Puro-DEST vector (w118-1). Lentiviral particles were produced by co-transfection of 293 T cells with the plasmid construct. Viral supernatants were collected 48 h post-transfection, filtered through a 0.45-µm hydrophilic PVDF membrane syringe filter, and used to infect target cells. Infected cells were selected with puromycin for 7 days before being used in experiments.

Ionizing radiation (IR) and Clonogenic Survival Assay

A clonogenic survival assay was performed as previously described [39]. Irradiation was conducted using SHARP 1000 (Raycision Medical Technology Co. Ltd) at doses ranging from 0 to 6 Gy. To determine the effects of shJab1 on IR-induced cell death, 500–1000 cells per well were seeded in triplicate in six-well plates and incubated overnight. The fresh drug-free medium was added to the plates and replaced every 3 days. After incubation for 10–15 days, cells were stained with 0.25% crystal violet (Sigma-Aldrich). The colonies in each well were visually quantified. The surviving fraction was calculated using GraphPad Prism 8 and normalized to that of non-irradiated control cells.

qRT-PCR

qRT-PCR was performed as previously described [39]. RNA was isolated using an RNeasy Mini kit (Qiagen) and used to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) with a cDNA Synthesis Kit (GenStar). qRT‐PCR was performed with aliquots of cDNA samples mixed with SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems). Reactions were performed in triplicates. The fold differences in transcripts were calculated using the ΔΔCt method, and GAPDH was used as a control to normalize RNA expression (Supplementary Table 1 shows the primers used).

Western Blots

Western blots were performed as previously described [40]. Cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well in 2 ml medium. After 24 h, the cells in each well were treated with either DMSO, CSN5i-3, Talazoparib, or Olaparib. Cells were then lysed, and total proteins were harvested. Equal amounts of protein (20–50 µg) were separated using 8% or 12% SDS‐PAGE and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Bio‐Rad). After blocking in 5% nonfat dry milk, the membranes were incubated with appropriate primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed, and incubated with respective HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected using a ChemiDoc XRS + System (Bio‐Rad) after exposure to chemiluminescence reagents (Bio‐Rad). β‐actin was used as the loading control.

Nuclear Run-On (NRO) Assay

Nuclear run-on transcription assays were performed as previously described [41]. Briefly, MDA-MB-231 cells were collected and incubated with NP-40 lysis buffer on ice for 5 min. The lysates were centrifuged at 300 g for 4 min at 4 ℃ to pellet the nuclei. The nuclei were resuspended in storage buffer and incubated with the reaction buffer mixture at 30 ℃ for 30 min. Nuclear RNA (NRO-RNA) was extracted and incubated with anti-BrdU antibody-coated protein G beads at room temperature for 30 min. The beads were captured using a magnet and washed before extracting NRO-RNA with TRIzol reagent. The extracted NRO-RNA was reverse-transcribed and analyzed by qPCR.

Alkaline Comet Assay

Alkaline comet assays were performed as previously described [39]. Briefly, after treatment with CSN5i-3 and/or Talazoparib/Olaparib, cells (1 × 105/ml) were collected and mixed with low melting point agarose at a ratio of 1:10 (v/v). Fifty microliters of the cell suspension were immediately added onto comet slides. The slides were incubated at 4 ℃ for 10 min, immersed in lysis solution for 30 min, and then transferred to an alkaline unwinding solution for 20 min in the dark. Following electrophoresis, the cells were stained with SYBR Gold (Invitrogen). The quantification of tail DNA was performed with CASP software (CaspLab).

Apoptosis Assay

Cell apoptosis analysis was performed as previously described [42]. Cells were treated with CSN5i-3 and/or Talazoparib/Olaparib. After harvesting, cells were resuspended in 100 µl of binding buffer and incubated with Annexin V/PI solution (Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Detection Kit, BD Biosciences) in the dark for 15 min. Samples were acquired on a FACS Verse Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo Software (BD Life Sciences).

Immunofluorescence Staining

Immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described [40]. Cells were seeded onto coverslips in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well in 1 ml of medium. After 48 h, cells were treated with CSN5i-3, Talazoparib, or their combination for the indicated durations. Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X‐100 in PBS. The cells were then blocked in 5% donkey serum containing 0.1% Triton X‐100 and stained with primary antibodies against γ‐H2AX or RAD51, followed by incubation with secondary antibodies conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488. After three washes with PBS, slides were mounted using VECTASHIELD mounting medium (Vector Laboratories) containing DAPI for nuclear staining. Images were captured using a ZEISS LSM900 laser confocal ultra-high-resolution microscope.

DR-GFP Reporter Assay

The DR-GFP reporter assay was performed as described previously [37]. Briefly, cells were plated at 2 × 105 cells per well in a 6-well plate. After 24 h, the HR repair reporter substrate direct repeat GFP (DR-GFP) plasmid and the pCBASceI plasmid were transfected into the cells using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent. A GFP-expressing plasmid (pEGFP-C1) was used as a transfection efficiency control. 24 h later, the cells were treated with CSN5i-3. After 48 h, cells were harvested and resuspended in ice-cold PBS, and GFP intensity was analyzed using a FACS Verse flow cytometer (BD Biosciences).

RNA Stability Assay Using 5-Ethynyluridine (EU) Labeling

RNA stability was analyzed using the Click-iT Nascent RNA Capture Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were treated with 0.1 mM EU for 24 h, followed by EU removal and continued incubation for an additional 24 h. Total RNA was extracted, and EU-labeled RNA was biotinylated using click chemistry reactions. The biotinylated RNA was isolated using streptavidin beads. The stability of mRNA was determined by calculating the percentage of labeled mRNA remaining after EU removal.

RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP)

RIP assays were performed using the Magna RIP RNA-Binding Protein lmmunoprecipitation Kit (Cat# 17–700, Millipore) as described previously [43]. Briefly, MDA-MB-231 were harvested by adding RIP lysis buffer and incubated with protein beads and Jab1 antibody complex overnight at 4 °C. After washing off unbound materials, RNAs binding to Jab1 were eluted and quantified. We used qRT-PCR to examine certain RNAs co-immunoprecipitated with the Jab1 antibody.

RNA-Protein Pull-Down Assay

RNA-Protein Pull-Down Assay was performed as previously described [44], using the Pierce™ Magnetic RNA–Protein Pull-Down Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To evaluate the interaction between RAD21 mRNA and Jab1 protein, biotin-labeled RNA probes targeting the 3’ untranslated region (3’ UTR) of RAD21 were synthesized, while non-specific oligonucleotide probes were used as negative controls. Linear RAD21 RNA transcripts were generated via in vitro transcription using the Biotin RNA Labeling Mix (Roche) and T7 RNA polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific), followed by circularization with T4 RNA ligase I. To enrich for circular RNA, samples were treated with RNase R to digest linear RNA. For transfection experiments, full-length and truncation constructs of Jab1 (Tsingke Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) were transiently expressed in cells using jetPRIME® transfection reagent (Polyplus). After 48 h, cells were lysed and incubated with biotin-labeled RAD21 RNA probes. The resulting RNA–protein complexes were captured using streptavidin-coated magnetic agarose beads at room temperature. Bound proteins were eluted and analyzed via Western blot with anti-Jab1 or anti-Flag antibodies to validate direct RNA–protein interactions.

RIP-seq Data Analysis

RIP-seq analysis was performed as previously described [45]. Briefly, raw sequencing data were quality-controlled, and mRNA levels were quantified based on BAM files using StringTie (v. 2.2.3). Gene annotations were performed using the human genome reference file (Homo_sapiens.GRC38.112). Standardized read counts and fold changes (log2-transformed) were calculated using DESeq2 (v. 1.42.1). Genes with a fold change greater than 1 and a P-value less than 0.05 were considered significantly enriched. Genes overlapping with transcriptome data were mapped to Gene Ontology (GO) terms using the clusterProfiler package (v. 4.10.1). For motif analysis, the MEME (v. 5.5.7) and STREME (v. 5.5.5) suites were used to identify novel sequence motifs or consensus elements, with default parameters and a search window length of 6–12 nucleotides. Identified motifs were assessed for statistical significance and relevance to Jab1-bound regions. Results were visualized as lollipop plots and scatterplots generated with ggplot2 (v. 3.5.1). Upstream analyses were conducted in Ubuntu (v. 20.04), while differential expression and enrichment analyses were performed in R (v. 4.3.1).

RNA–Protein Interaction Modeling and Analysis

RNA–Protein interaction analysis was performed as previously described [46]. The secondary structures of RAD21 and RAD54B RNAs were determined based on the minimum free energy (ΔG) calculated using the Mfold Web Server. The optimal secondary structures were then converted into dot-bracket notation and processed through RNA Composer to generate corresponding three-dimensional (3D) RNA models. Subsequently, molecular docking simulations between the modeled RNAs and CSN5 (Jab1) protein were performed using the HDOCK server, which supports hybrid template-based and free docking approaches. The highest-scoring RNA–protein complex models, based on the docking scores and interaction plausibility, were selected for further structural evaluation. These models represent the most likely binding conformations between Jab1 and the RAD21/RAD54B transcripts and serve to illustrate their potential molecular interactions.

RNA-seq Data Analysis

RNA-seq analysis was performed as previously described [39]. Briefly, total RNA was isolated from MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells with stable Jab1 knockdown (shJab1) or corresponding control (shCtrl). Additionally, orthotopic 4T1 tumors from BALB/c mice treated with either vehicle or combined therapy (20 mg/kg CSN5i-3 and 0.33 mg/kg Talazoparib) for 14 days were harvested for transcriptomic analysis. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, quantified with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher), and assessed for integrity using the RNA Nano 6000 kit on the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies). Samples with RIN values greater than 6.0 were used. Complementary DNA libraries were prepared and sequenced by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (https://en.novogene.com/) using standard Illumina protocols. Specifically, cDNA libraries were prepared using the Illumina NEBNext® UltraTM RNA Library Prep Kit. Following cluster generation, sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform, producing 150 bp paired-end reads.

Raw sequencing data (FASTQ format) were processed using custom Perl scripts to remove adapter sequences, poly-N stretches, and low-quality reads. Reference genome and gene annotations were downloaded directly from genome databases. Indexes of the reference genome were built using Hisat2 (v. 2.0.5), and paired-end reads were aligned to the reference genome with Hisat2. Mapped reads for each sample were assembled using StringTie (v. 2.2.3), and gene-level counts were quantified with FeatureCounts (v. 1.5.0-p3).

Differential expression analysis between treatment and control groups was performed using DESeq2 (v. 1.42.1). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs; P < 0.05) were visualized by volcano plots using ggplot2 (v. 3.5.1) and ggrepel (v. 0.9.6). Overlapping DEGs between different cell lines were calculated with VennDiagram (v. 1.7.3) and mapped to GO terms using the clusterProfiler package (v. 4.10.1). Results were visualized as lollipop plots generated with ggplot2 (v. 3.5.1). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed using clusterProfiler (v. 4.10.1). Genes were ranked by fold changes (FC), and heatmaps of enriched terms were created using the pheatmap package (v. 1.0.12). All analyses were conducted in R (v. 4.3.1).

TCGA RNA-seq Data Analysis

TCGA-BRCA RNA-seq data and corresponding clinical information were downloaded from the GDC Data Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). METABRIC RNA-seq data and clinical information were obtained from cBioPortal (https://www.cbioportal.org/). Expression data were merged to form a matrix, and TNBC subtypes were identified based on the “negative” status of ER, PR, and HER2 immunohistochemistry markers. Breast cancer molecular subtypes were classified using the PAM50 classifier implemented in the Genefu package (version 2.34.0). Gene expression profiles were visualized as boxplots and co-expression relationships as scatterplots using ggplot2 (v. 3.5.1). Optimal COPS5 expression cutoffs were determined with the survminer package (v. 0.5.0), and Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the survival package (v. 3.5-8). All analyses were conducted in R (v. 4.3.1).

Heterotopic Zebrafish Xenograft Model

The zebrafish tumor xenograft model was established as previously described [37]. Wild-type AB zebrafish (Danio rerio) were provided by the Academy of Life Sciences, Peking University. Adult zebrafish were maintained under standard laboratory conditions, and embryos were generated by natural pairwise mating. Two days post-fertilization, embryos were anesthetized with tricaine (Sigma-Aldrich) and positioned on a Petri dish for microinjections. Tumor cells were stained with CM-Dil Dye (Invitrogen) and injected into the abdominal perivitelline space of embryos at a concentration of 108 cells/ml. After injection, embryos were transferred to a 24-well plate, acclimated at 32 ℃ for 24 h, and incubated with CSN5i-3 or Talazoparib for 72 h. Tumor growth was imaged using an Olympus fluorescence inverted microscope, and fluorescence intensity was analyzed with ImageJ software. Tumor volume was determined as the percentage of tumor surface area relative to total yolk surface area.

Heterotopic Nude Mouse Xenograft Model

The murine tumor xenograft model was established as previously described [37]. MDA-MB-231 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right flanks of 4- to 5‐week‐old female BALB/c nude mice. When tumors reached 1000 mm³, they were excised, diced into 2 mm × 2 mm × 2 mm pieces, and implanted into the right flanks of BALB/c nude mice. Tumors were allowed to grow to 100 mm³ before random assignment into four groups (n = 5): vehicle, CSN5i-3 (10 mg/kg), Talazoparib (0.33 mg/kg), or a combination of CSN5i-3 and Talazoparib. Tumor size was measured every 3 days, and volumes were calculated using the formula: (length × width²)/2. On day 19, mice were euthanized, and tumors were harvested. Tumors were either formalin-fixed or paraffin-embedded for histological analysis.

Orthotopic Syngeneic TNBC Mouse Model

The orthotopic syngeneic triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) mouse model was established as described previously [47]. Briefly, 1 × 106 murine 4T1 cells were orthotopically injected into the mammary fat pads of 6- to 8-week-old female BALB/c mice. Four days after tumor implantation, mice were randomized into four groups (n = 6 per group) and treated daily with either vehicle, CSN5i-3 (20 mg/kg), Talazoparib (0.33 mg/kg), or the combination of CSN5i-3 and Talazoparib for 14 consecutive days. Tumor volumes were monitored every 3 days using digital calipers and calculated using the following formula: volume = (length × width²)/2. On day 15 post-treatment initiation, mice were euthanized, and tumors were harvested and subjected to RNA-seq analysis.

TUNEL and Immunohistochemical (IHC) Staining

IHC stainings were performed as previously described [48]. Apoptotic cells in tumor tissues were stained using a TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (Beyotime). For histological analysis, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumors were sectioned, and slides were deparaffinized using xylene (Thermo Fisher). Endogenous peroxidases were quenched with 3% hydrogen peroxide in methanol. Staining was performed using antibodies against Cleaved Caspase-3 (1:400), RAD51 (1:50), γ-H2AX (1:50), and Ki67 (1:500). Counterstaining was performed with Mayer’s hematoxylin (Dako). Slides were observed under an Olympus CX21 microscope and scanned with a high-resolution digital slide scanner (Pannoramic 250, 3DHistech) to capture images.

Statistical analysis

Data from three independent experiments are presented as mean ± SD. Two-group comparisons were analyzed using an unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-test, while multiple-group comparisons were analyzed using ANOVA with Bonferroni’s correction. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Drug interaction effects were quantified by calculating combination indices (CIs) using CalcuSyn software (Version 2.1, Biosoft). Prior to statistical analysis, data distributions and assumptions of tests were verified. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Jab1 Knockdown Disrupts DNA Repair Pathways in TNBC Cells

Analyses of COPS5 transcript levels in primary tumor datasets from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and the Molecular Taxonomy of Breast Cancer International Consortium (METABRIC) revealed a significant increase in COPS5 expression in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) compared to other breast cancer subtypes. Moreover, COPS5 expression was notably higher in basal-like breast cancer than in other subtypes, including luminal A, luminal B, HER2-enriched, and normal-like breast cancers (Fig. 1A). Elevated COPS5 expression was also associated with poor prognosis in both overall breast cancer and TNBC patients (Fig. 1B), indicating its potential role as a prognostic biomarker for adverse clinical outcomes. These findings suggest that targeting COPS5 may offer a therapeutic strategy for improving patient outcomes, particularly in aggressive subtypes such as TNBC. To elucidate the functional role of COPS5-encoded Jab1, we silenced its expression in MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells and analyzed differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Fig. 1 A). Transcriptomic profiling revealed extensive alterations in gene expression in both cell lines, with 4,551 genes upregulated and 4,506 genes downregulated in MDA-MB-231, and 4,661 genes upregulated and 4,667 genes downregulated in BT-549 cells (FDR < 0.05, Fig. 1C and Supplementary Fig. 1B, Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). The overlapping differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the two cell lines were identified and subjected to Gene Ontology (GO) analysis, which revealed significant enrichment in DNA repair-related pathways, particularly homologous recombination repair (HRR) and DNA replication processes (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table 4). These results suggest that Jab1 regulates critical components of DNA repair mechanisms, and its depletion could lead to impaired repair efficiency.

Jab1 Knockdown Compromises DNA Repair and Increases Sensitivity to DNA Damage

Based on the observed changes in DNA repair pathways, we next investigated the functional impact of Jab1 knockdown on DNA damage sensitivity and repair efficiency. Jab1 depletion impaired TNBC cell proliferation, underscoring its essential role in cell survival and growth (Supplementary Fig. 2 A). DR-GFP reporter assays revealed that the efficiency of HR was reduced by approximately 70% in both MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells following Jab1 knockdown (Fig. 2A). To assess DNA damage accumulation, γ-H2AX foci were examined at various time points post-irradiation. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed persistent γ-H2AX foci in Jab1-depleted cells, indicating impaired DNA repair kinetics (Fig. 2B and C). Clonogenic survival assays further demonstrated that Jab1 knockdown significantly reduced cell survival in response to incremental doses of IR (Supplementary Fig. 2B). Additional experiments, including comet assays, apoptosis assays, and Western blot analyses, consistently confirmed enhanced DNA damage accumulation and increased apoptotic cell death in Jab1-depleted cells following IR treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2C–E).

Given the established synthetic lethality interaction between PARPi and HRD, we further assessed the impact of Jab1 knockdown on cellular sensitivity to two FDA-approved PARP inhibitors, Talazoparib and Olaparib, both clinically utilized in breast cancer treatment, exhibiting high and low PARP-trapping activities, respectively. Jab1-depleted cells exhibited increased sensitivity to both PARPi, as evidenced by reduced viability (Fig. 2D, Supplementary Fig. 2 F). Notably, Jab1 inhibition similarly increased PARPi sensitivity in BRCA-mutated cell lines, such as MDA-MB-436 and HCC1937 cells, suggesting that Jab1 may modulate HRR efficiency independently of BRCA status (Fig. 2E, Supplementary Fig. 2G). Comet assays validated that Jab1 depletion markedly augmented DNA damage accumulation induced by Talazoparib treatment (Fig. 2F). Further mechanistic investigations through immunofluorescence staining revealed increased γ-H2AX foci and significantly reduced RAD51 foci formation upon Jab1 knockdown (Fig. 2G). Consistently, Western blot analyses showed elevated γ-H2AX levels coupled with decreased RAD51 protein expression, reinforcing the notion that Jab1 knockdown impairs HRR capacity (Supplementary Fig. 2H). Talazoparib treatment induced over a three-fold increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells in Jab1-depleted groups compared to control groups (Fig. 2H and I). These observations were validated by elevated cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase-3 levels, indicative of apoptosis induction (Fig. 2J). Conversely, Jab1 overexpression in the TNBC cell line HCC1806, which naturally expresses low levels of Jab1 (Supplementary Fig. 3 A), had minimal effects on cell proliferation (Supplementary Fig. 3B). DR-GFP reporter assays demonstrated that Jab1 overexpression enhanced HRR efficiency by more than 50%, suggesting that Jab1 facilitates HRR in HCC1806 cells (Supplementary Fig. 3 C). Jab1 overexpression also enhanced resistance to IR (Supplementary Fig. 3D) and significantly increased cell survival under PARPi treatment, indicating elevated PARPi resistance (Supplementary Fig. 3E). These results strongly support the potential of targeting Jab1 to sensitize TNBC cells to DNA-damaging therapies.

Jab1 Regulates the Stability of DNA Repair Gene Transcripts

To further investigate the mechanism by which Jab1 regulates HRR-related genes, we first analyzed whether Jab1 influences the transcription of RAD54B and RAD21 two HRR-related genes significantly downregulated upon Jab1 depletion. Nuclear run-on assays performed in MDA-MB-231 cells revealed no significant changes in the transcription levels of these genes (Fig. 3A). We then evaluated the impact of Jab1 on mRNA stability, we treated cells with Actinomycin D to monitor the decay of RAD54B and RAD21 transcripts over time. Following Jab1 knockdown, both mRNAs exhibited accelerated degradation, indicating reduced stability (Fig. 3B).

Next, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assays to determine whether Jab1 directly interacts with these mRNAs to regulate their stability. RIP analysis demonstrated that Jab1 specifically binds to RAD54B and RAD21 mRNAs, as confirmed by immunoprecipitation using a Jab1-specific antibody (Fig. 3C and D). To further validate this interaction, RNA pull-down assays were performed using in vitro biotin-labeled 3’ UTRs of RAD21 RNA, which revealed a strong binding affinity of Jab1 to RAD21 mRNA (Fig. 3E).

To define which structural region of Jab1 mediates its RNA-binding capability, we generated a series of truncation mutants and performed RNA pull-down assays (Supplementary Fig. 4 A). The truncated fragment encompassing amino acids 1–190 (Jab1 1–190) retained strong RNA-binding ability, similar to full-length Jab1. In contrast, deletion of the MPN domain (Jab1 ΔMPN) completely abolished Jab1’s ability to bind RAD21 mRNA (Fig. 3F). Notably, the MPN domain has previously been recognized as the catalytic core for cullin deneddylation, a process critical for regulating the activity of cullin-RING ubiquitin ligases (CRLs) [49, 50]. These findings suggest that, beyond its enzymatic role, the MPN domain may also contribute to Jab1’s RNA-binding activity.

Consistent with this observation, rescue experiments showed that re-expression of full-length Jab1 or Jab1 1-190 in Jab1-knockdown cells effectively restored RAD21 mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 3G). Moreover, when examining the effects on RAD54B and RAD21 mRNA stability after re-expression, we observed that restoration of full-length Jab1 significantly enhanced the stability of both transcripts, whereas the Jab1 ΔMPN mutant failed to rescue the stability of these mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 4B). These data clearly indicate that the MPN domain of Jab1 is essential for its direct interaction with target mRNAs, thus maintaining their stability.

To investigate whether Jab1 exerts global regulatory effects on RNA stability, we performed RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (RIP-seq) in MDA-MB-231 cells. A total of 2,751 RNAs were significantly enriched by Jab1 compared to the control (Fig. 3H, Supplementary Table 6). Further RIP-seq peak distribution analysis indicated that Jab1 predominantly mapped to introns (31.8%), promoter regions (30.4%), 3’ UTRs (8.5%), exons (4.2%), and 5’ UTRs (0.6%) (Supplementary Fig. 4 C). Cross-analysis with RNA-seq data identified 734 overlapping transcripts (Fig. 3I, Supplementary Table 7). GO analysis of these overlapping transcripts indicated these transcripts were enriched for key DNA repair-related biological processes, including “double-strand break repair”, “cell cycle checkpoint signaling” and “DNA replication” (Fig. 3J, Supplementary Table 8).

Next, we employed molecular docking analysis to investigate potential binding sites between Jab1 protein and RAD54B/RAD21 RNAs. Considering the lowest free energy secondary structures for RAD54B and RAD21 RNAs (ΔG = −81.90and − 49.1 kcal/mol, respectively), we established three-dimensional (3D) interaction models for Jab1 with RAD54B and RAD21 RNAs (Supplementary Fig. 4D and E). Potential RNA-protein interactions were identified when distances between RNA atoms and amino acid residues fell within 3.5 Å, providing structural insights into key RNA-binding residues of Jab1 (Supplementary Table 9). Additionally, motif enrichment analysis identified five significantly enriched RNA-binding motifs, suggesting a potential recognition consensus sequence for Jab1-RNA interactions (Supplementary Fig. 4 F).

Collectively, these results suggest that Jab1 increases HRR-related mRNAs stability through direct binding, shielding these transcripts from degradation. This stabilization ensures their sustained availability, thereby supporting efficient DNA repair processes at the post-transcriptional level.

As a core component of this complex, EXOSC4 possesses 3’−5’ RNA exonuclease activity and preferentially recognizes AU-rich elements present within mRNA 3’-UTRs [51–53]. To determine whether Jab1 stabilizes HRR-related target RNAs by interfering with EXOSC4-mediated RNA degradation, we first performed a stable knockdown of EXOSC4 in MDA-MB-231 cells (Fig. 4A). qRT-PCR analysis revealed that the mRNA levels of RAD54B and RAD21 were significantly increased following EXOSC4 depletion (Fig. 4B), suggesting that these transcripts are likely substrates for exosome complex-mediated degradation.

Further investigation using RIP assays demonstrated that Jab1 knockdown markedly enhanced the binding of EXOSC4 to RAD54B and RAD21 mRNAs (Fig. 4C). Conversely, EXOSC4 knockdown also promoted increased binding of Jab1 to these mRNAs (Fig. 4D), suggesting a competitive binding relationship between Jab1 and EXOSC4 for these transcripts. Rescue experiments further demonstrated that co-knockdown of Jab1 and EXOSC4 partially restored RAD54B and RAD21 expression levels (Fig. 4E). To eliminate the possibility that Actinomycin D itself might nonspecifically influence the stability of RAD54B and RAD21 mRNAs, we used the 5-ethynyl uridine (EU) labeling assay as an independent method to assess mRNA turnover. This approach directly measures newly synthesized RNA and monitors the decay kinetics of mRNA transcripts without global transcriptional inhibition. Consistent with the above observations, we found a significant decrease in EU-labeled RAD54B and RAD21 mRNAs following Jab1 depletion. Importantly, this destabilization effect was reversed upon EXOSC4 knockdown (Fig. 4H). Taken together, these results suggest that Jab1 competes with the exosome complex for binding to targeted RNAs, including key HRR elements RAD54B and RAD21, protecting them from degradation and preserving their stability.

Pharmacological Inhibition of Jab1 Impairs HRR and Sensitizes TNBC Cells to IR-Induced DNA Damage

To determine whether pharmacological inhibition of Jab1 mimics the effects observed with genetic knockdown, we treated MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells with the Jab1 inhibitor CSN5i-3. Initially, we examined the effect of Jab1 inhibition on CRLs. Consistent with previous reports, CSN5i-3 treatment caused the accumulation of cullin 1 in its neddylated, active state and decreased the levels of the substrate receptor module SKP2. Consequently, CRL substrates p21 and p27 were protected from degradation [54]. Notably, Jab1 protein levels themselves were also decreased following CSN5i-3 treatment, suggesting Jab1 may be directly regulated by active CRLs (Supplementary Fig. 5 A). Given our earlier observation that genetic knockdown of Jab1 reduced the expression levels of RAD54B and RAD21, we next investigated whether pharmacological Jab1 inhibition elicited similar effects. Treatment with CSN5i-3 significantly decreased both mRNA and protein expression of RAD54B and RAD21, consistent with genetic knockdown results (Supplementary Fig. 5B and C). Moreover, assessment of RAD54B and RAD21 mRNA stability revealed that CSN5i-3 treatment accelerated degradation of these transcripts, again mirroring the genetic depletion phenotype (Supplementary Fig. 5D).

Next, we observed a dose-dependent reduction in HRR efficiency following CSN5i-3 treatment (Fig. 5A). Western blot analysis confirmed a dose-dependent increase in γ-H2AX levels and a decrease in RAD51 expression, indicative of impaired DSB repair capacity (Supplementary Fig. 5E). Immunofluorescence analysis revealed that cells treated with CSN5i-3 and IR displayed persistent γ-H2AX foci and reduced RAD51 foci formation, demonstrating defective repair of DSBs and increased DNA damage accumulation (Fig. 5B and C). These results were further validated by Western blot analysis, which showed elevated γ-H2AX levels and reduced RAD51 expression in the combination treatment group (Fig. 5D). Comet assays confirmed increased DNA damage after CSN5i-3 treatment, which was further amplified when combined with IR, indicating impaired repair efficiency (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, clonogenic survival and apoptosis assays revealed that the combined treatment resulted in greater reductions in survival and enhanced apoptosis, as confirmed by cleaved PARP and caspase-3 levels (Fig. 5F–J). Collectively, these results demonstrate that pharmacological inhibition of Jab1 closely replicates genetic knockdown effects by reducing the expression and stability of HRR-related transcripts, impairing HRR functionality, and ultimately sensitizing TNBC cells to IR-induced DNA damage. Thus, our findings strongly underscore Jab1 as an attractive therapeutic target for enhancing the efficacy of DNA damage-based therapies in TNBC.

Pharmacological Inhibition of Jab1 Synergizes with PARP Inhibitors to Induce Synthetic Lethality in TNBC Cells

To further explore the potential therapeutic synergy between Jab1 inhibitor CSN5i-3 and PARPi, we treated MDA-MB-231 and BT-549 cells with CSN5i-3 in combination with either Talazoparib or Olaparib. Dose-response curves indicated that the combination treatments acted synergistically and significantly reduced cell viability compared to single-agent treatments (Fig. 6A and B). Clonogenic survival assays showed a marked decrease in colony formation following combined treatment (Fig. 6C and D). Similarly, apoptosis assays confirmed enhanced cell death in the combination groups, as demonstrated by the increased cleaved PARP and caspase-3 levels (Fig. 6E–G). Comet assays demonstrated greater DNA fragmentation following combination therapy, consistent with inhibition of HRR and accumulation of DNA damage (Fig. 6H). Consistent with these results, immunofluorescence staining revealed persistent γ-H2AX foci and reduced RAD51 foci formation after combined treatment, indicating defective DNA repair (Fig. 6I and J). Western blot analysis further validated these findings, showing increased γ-H2AX levels and reduced RAD51 expression after combined treatment (Fig. 6K). Furthermore, consistent with results from genetic Jab1 depletion experiments, pharmacological inhibition of Jab1 using CSN5i-3 combined with PARPi also demonstrated synergistic effects in BRCA1-mutated TNBC cells (Supplementary Fig. 6 A and B). Collectively, these results indicate that CSN5i-3, when combined with PARPi, induces synthetic lethality and enhances DNA damage, providing a strong rationale for synergistic therapeutic strategies in TNBC.

Jab1 Inhibition Enhances the Antitumor Activity of Talazoparib in Vivo.

To assess the in vivo therapeutic potential of Jab1 inhibition combined with PARP inhibitors, we employed a zebrafish xenograft model using MDA-MB-231 cells. Bioluminescence imaging revealed significantly reduced tumor signal intensities in models treated with either Jab1 knockdown or CSN5i-3 when combined with Talazoparib as compared to single-agent treatments (Fig. 7A and B).

We further validated these effects in a nude mouse xenograft model. Tumor growth curves demonstrated a marked decrease in tumor volume in the combination treatment group, with no significant changes in body weight, indicating favorable toxicity profiles (Fig. 7C). Immunohistochemical staining revealed reduced Ki-67 levels, indicative of decreased proliferation, and increased cleaved caspase-3 and TUNEL signals, confirming elevated apoptosis in the combination group. Furthermore, increased γ-H2AX levels and reduced RAD51 expression provided evidence of DNA damage accumulation and impaired DNA repair mechanisms (Fig. 7F and G). Furthermore, we employed a syngeneic orthotopic model using murine-derived 4T1 TNBC cells. Consistent with results from human xenograft models, combined treatment with CSN5i-3 and Talazoparib significantly enhanced antitumor efficacy (Supplementary Fig. 7A–C). Transcriptomic profiling coupled with GSEA revealed significant downregulation of multiple DNA repair-related pathways, including “homology-directed repair”, “double-strand break repair”, and “DNA replication”, in tumors-bearing animals receiving combination therapy (Supplementary Fig. 7D and E, Supplementary Tables 10 and 11). These results provide strong preclinical evidence supporting this combination as a potential therapeutic strategy for TNBC treatment.

Proposed Model of Jab1 Regulation in HRR and PARPi Sensitivity

Jab1 plays a critical role in maintaining the stability of HRR-related mRNAs and modulating PARPi sensitivity (Fig. 8). Under normal conditions, Jab1 prevents the degradation of HRR-related mRNAs by competing with the exosome complex, thereby sustaining efficient DNA damage repair and contributing to PARPi resistance. Pharmacological inhibition of Jab1 with CSN5i-3 disrupts its ability to stabilize HRR-related mRNAs, leaving them susceptible to degradation by the exosome complex. This degradation leads to HRD, compromised DNA repair, and increased DNA damage accumulation, making TNBC cells more vulnerable to PARP inhibitors. This creates a synthetic lethal interaction, further exacerbating DNA damage accumulation. This model highlights a novel RNA-regulatory mechanism by which Jab1 promotes HRR and suggests that pharmacological targeting of Jab1 could sensitize TNBC cells to PARPi, providing a promising therapeutic approach.

Discussion

PARPi have emerged as promising therapeutic agents for TNBC, particularly benefiting patients with HRD [55, 56]. Nevertheless, intrinsic and acquired resistance to PARPi poses a substantial challenge, restricting their broader clinical utility [57–59]. Hence, there remains an urgent need to identify novel therapeutic targets that can synergize with PARPi to enhance efficacy and circumvent resistance.

In this study, we focused on Jab1, a multifunctional protein whose amplification and overexpression have been reported in multiple cancer types [33, 60–62]. Previous studies primarily highlighted Jab1’s involvement in deneddylation and its regulatory roles in critical cellular processes including proliferation, apoptosis, hypoxia responses, cell cycle progression, and DNA repair [31, 63–66]. Despite accumulating evidence of Jab1’s oncogenic potential, its precise molecular functions, particularly in the context of TNBC biology and therapy response, remain incompletely elucidated.

Targeting DNA damage repair pathways is an effective strategy for treating TNBC [20, 67]. Prior studies demonstrated that CUL4–ROC1 ubiquitin E3 ligase complexes and the CSN complexes maintain genomic stability by facilitating CDT1 degradation in response to DNA damage [68]. Furthermore, CSN complexes have been shown to modulate the activity of DDB2 and CSA ubiquitin ligase complexes involved in nucleotide excision repair (NER) [69]. Complementing these findings, our previous research established that Jab1 contributes to DNA repair and modulates cellular sensitivity to chemotherapy and radiotherapy by regulating p53-dependent RAD51 transcription [31, 35].

Importantly, our current study reveals an entirely novel function of Jab1 as an RNA-binding protein (RBP), critically involved in the regulation of mRNA stability. Through detailed mechanistic analysis, we demonstrate that Jab1 stabilizes HRR-related mRNAs by directly competing with the exosome complex. This Jab1-mediated transcript stabilization is critical for maintaining DNA repair efficiency and promoting resistance to both IR and PARPi in TNBC cells. Pharmacological inhibition of Jab1 with CSN5i-3 disrupted this stabilization, thereby inducing HRD and sensitizing TNBC cells to PARPi treatment. Notably, the combined treatment of CSN5i-3 and PARPi resulted in synthetic lethality, significantly enhancing DNA damage accumulation, apoptosis, and tumor regression across both in vitro and in vivo models. Furthermore, we demonstrated that Jab1 inhibition also enhanced PARPi sensitivity in BRCA-mutant TNBC cell lines, highlighting a BRCA-independent mechanism of action. Supporting this notion, we identified several critical HRR transcripts regulated by Jab1, including RAD54B and RAD54L, both essential for RAD51 focus formation [70, 71], as well as RAD21, a critical mediator recruited rapidly to DNA damage sites, stabilizing DNA ends and maintaining sister chromatid cohesion for effective HRR [72]. Our findings align with previous reports identifying alternative PARPi-sensitizing targets independent of BRCA1 status [73–75], underscoring that Jab1-mediated HRR impairment via RNA stabilization represents a unique mechanism, distinct from the well-characterized BRCA- or p53-dependent DNA repair pathways [31, 35]. This novel mechanism may exert broader influences on the cellular DNA damage response. Taken together, our findings substantially expand the current understanding of RNA metabolism’s role in DNA repair processes. By identifying Jab1 as an RBP that controls mRNA stability, we highlight RNA stability as a previously underappreciated regulatory mechanism in maintaining genomic integrity [76, 77]. Clinically, this novel insight could complement existing therapeutic strategies aimed at inducing HRD, providing a rational framework for combination therapies that exploit vulnerabilities in RNA metabolism to enhance the effectiveness of DNA-damaging treatments.

Future investigations should focus on defining the structural basis of Jab1’s RNA-binding activity and identifying additional RNA targets. Further exploration into combination therapies involving Jab1 inhibition with other DNA-damaging agents, such as platinum-based chemotherapeutics (e.g., cisplatin or carboplatin) or topoisomerase inhibitors (e.g., topotecan or etoposide), would be particularly valuable. These combinations may amplify DNA damage responses through cumulative HRR disruption and potentiate tumor regression [27, 78]. Additionally, combining Jab1 inhibition with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) presents another promising therapeutic avenue. Specifically, HRD induced by Jab1 inhibition may enhance tumor immunogenicity by activating innate immune signaling pathways or by increasing tumor mutational burden (TMB), thereby improving responses to ICIs [79–81]. These potential therapeutic synergies merit systematic preclinical validation and subsequent clinical translation efforts. Furthermore, the mechanism identified here may extend beyond TNBC, with significant implications for other malignancies reliant on HRR pathways, such as ovarian and prostate cancers [82, 83]. Exploring the generalizability of Jab1-dependent RNA stability mechanisms and their role in modulating DNA repair across different cancer types will constitute an important future research direction, potentially expanding the clinical utility of Jab1-targeting strategies across multiple malignancies.

In summary, our study identifies a novel RNA stability-dependent mechanism through which Jab1 modulates DNA repair and therapeutic sensitivity in TNBC. Pharmacological targeting of this pathway offers a compelling strategy for enhancing the efficacy of PARPi-based therapies. More broadly, these findings suggest that RNA stability regulation may represent an underexplored regulatory layer within the DNA damage response, with potential implications for the development of precision cancer therapies.
