Evaluating Pakistani dental graduates’ preparedness for practice: a mixed methods study using the conscious competence model
Authors: Syed Jaffar Abbas Zaidi, Caitriona Dennis, Qaiser Ali Baig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDental graduates often feel underprepared for independent practice, particularly in challenging work environments. This mixed-methods study explored self-perceived preparedness among final-year dental students, recent graduates, and house officers training for full registration in Karachi, Pakistan. This study correlated preparedness with personality type and aligned it with the Conscious Competence Model (CCM), which is comprised of four stages of learner self-perception as they proceed toward acquiring new competencies: Unconscious incompetent (don’t know what you don’t know); conscious incompetent (now aware of skill deficits); conscious competent (performs skill but with conscious effort and deep concentration); unconscious competent (performs with minimal conscious thought).MethodsA total of 44 participants (86% female, mean age 23.2 ± 3.43 years) completed the Dental Undergraduates Preparedness Assessment Scale (DU-PAS), with high reliability (Cronbach’s alpha: overall = 0.89, Part A = 0.91, Part B = 0.82). Thirty of these 44 participants also completed a Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) inventory, provided a global rating for self-perceived preparedness on a scale ranging from 0%–not ready to 100%–completely ready, and also engaged in semi-structured interviews. Quantitative data were analysed using chi-squared, Mann-Whitney U, and Kruskal-Wallis tests, while qualitative data (themes identified from interviews) were thematically analysed and mapped to the CCM.ResultsThe mean overall DU-PAS score was 64.12 ± 5.18 (clinical skills 62.0 ± 5.04, non-clinical skills 65.95 ± 8.09). High confidence levels were reported for foundational skills, such as taking medical history (95%). Conversely, lower confidence was reported for specialised procedures, such as bitewing radiography (29.5%), and non-clinical skills, such as managing pediatric patients (25%) and anxious patients (31.8%). DU-PAS scores demonstrated a significant decline across training stages. Final-year students scored highest (67.9 ± 4.2), followed by house officers (62.4 ± 3.5), and recent graduates (59.4 ± 4.8). The differences were statistically significant (H(2) = 18.42, Kruskal–Wallis p < 0.001), indicating a progressive reduction in perceived preparedness with increasing training experience. Of the 30 participants who completed the MBTI, 60% were classified as extroverts, who reported a median global self-perceived preparedness score of 70% on the 0–100 scale, compared with a median score of 57% among introverts. Six themes were identified from the interviews: effective undergraduate experiences, deliberate professional development, patient care, emergency preparedness, practice management, and ethical reflection were identified that charted movement from unconscious to conscious incompetence once graduates faced real clinical demands.ConclusionsAlthough participants felt that basic skills contributed to some preparedness to practice, they did not feel equipped to manage complex clinical and managerial tasks. In relation to the CCM learning process, the progressively declining preparedness rankings among final year students, house officers and recent graduates suggests that the transition from unconscious to conscious incompetence did not occur until entering professional practice. Possible interventions were suggested by identifying enablers and barriers to preparedness. These include increased earlier supervised exposure to advanced endodontics, radiography, paediatric and anxious-patient care, along with structured modules in practice management and reflective mentoring, leading to consolidating conscious competence before graduation.

Introduction

Preparing for dental practice requires mastering a combination of skills, knowledge and attitudes [1]. Globally, dental education curricula focus on students’ acquisition of clinical competencies needed for unsupervised, entry-level professional practice. However, recent graduates are often not prepared for the challenging work environment of contemporary dental practice [2, 3]. In many nations, these challenges include some combination of saturated workplaces, unemployment, the poor economic viability of private practices, and low remuneration [2]. In Pakistan, as in other low to middle-income countries, additional challenges include time, faculty, and resource constraints in delivering an undergraduate dental curriculum, leaving students inexperienced in complex skills [4].

A systematic review of preparedness for dental graduates highlighted six domains: academic and technical competence, communication and interpersonal skills, protective mechanisms and adaptive skills, professional attitude and ethical judgement, clinical entrepreneurship and financial solvency skills, and social and community orientation [5]. Considering these challenges, some domains such as clinical entrepreneurship and financial solvency may not be covered, impacting students’ preparedness to practice [4, 6].

The Pakistan Medical and Dental Council (PMDC) has published dental graduate competencies; however, training is not standardised and varies among institutions [7]. This inconsistency leads to a sense of graduate unpreparedness, which adversely affects the standards of dental care. Therefore, research is needed to evaluate and enhance graduates’ preparedness for dental practice. Preparedness for practice encompasses several elements, including competence in performing tasks, self-efficacy, and confidence in one’s abilities. Students’ preparedness for the practice pathway, acquiring skills, knowledge, and attitudes can be aligned with the Conscious Competence model (CCM) [8]. This model describes the four stages of awareness in the transition to professional practice (Fig. 1). After graduation, practitioners are consciously incompetent or unconsciously competent, where they can safely accomplish tasks. For undergraduate training, aligning CCM with expected competencies, educators can tailor teaching strategies and learning opportunities to support dental students to progress in the model, thereby enhancing preparedness to practice.

The Pakistan Medical and Dental Council (PMDC) has published dental graduate competencies; however, training and assessment of practice-readiness are not standardised and varies among institutions [7]. This variation contributes to self-reported feelings of unpreparedness among final-year students, house officers, and recent graduates, which may undermine confidence and potentially affect patient care [4, 9]. Therefore, research is needed to evaluate graduates’ actual level of preparedness for dental practice, which can identify strategies for enhancement of the educational process. Preparedness for practice encompasses several elements, including competence in performing tasks, self-efficacy, and confidence in one’s abilities. Students’ preparedness for the practice pathway, acquiring skills, knowledge, and attitudes can be aligned with the Conscious Competence Model (CCM) [8]. This model describes the four stages of learner self-awareness in the transition to professional practice (Fig. 1). Based on the CCM, dental graduates are expected to move beyond unconscious incompetence during their training. After graduation, they should at least demonstrate conscious incompetence, where they recognise the limits of their skills and seek supervision or referral rather than attempting procedures beyond their capacity. With progressive experience and support, they then advance to conscious competence and eventually unconscious competence, where core clinical tasks can be performed safely and efficiently with minimal conscious effort. For undergraduate training, by aligning CCM with expected competencies, educators can tailor teaching strategies and learning opportunities to support dental students to progress in the model, thereby enhancing preparedness to practice.

The self-perceived preparedness of graduates is dependent on several factors, including experience and demographics, and can be influenced by personality characteristics. Traits such as high self-confidence, self-efficacy, conscientiousness, resilience, intrinsic motivation, and extrovertism can affect how individuals perceive their preparedness [3, 10]. Personality traits can be assessed using personality assessment tools such as the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator® (MBTI®). Based on Jung’s theory of psychological types, this tool has proven insightful in studies mapping medical students’ performances [11–18].

This study aimed to explore preparedness for dental practice among a group of dental graduates and undergraduates in Pakistan by harnessing insights based on the MBTI and aligning their perceived ability with CCM. By identifying enabling and hindering elements, this study sought to gain a further understanding of the factors that influence preparedness for practice and thus inform educational interventions. These insights have the potential to enhance the delivery of an authentic educational programme that equips graduates for successful professional practice.

Methods

Study design

This study used a mixed-method design to measure preparedness objectively and obtain narratives of experiences and emotions, thus capturing the complexities concerned with feelings of preparedness. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the parent university of each of the study participants.

Theoretical framework

The CCM forms a theoretical framework to map the pathway of skill mastery and competence and to understand participants’ perceptions of their preparedness for practice through awareness of their abilities [8].

Study site

This study was conducted at Dow University of Health Sciences, Karachi, Pakistan. However, students, interns and recent graduates were from multiple institutions.

Sample population and recruitment

Participants included final-year undergraduate dental students enrolled at the three dental colleges affiliated with Dow University of Health Sciences, dental house officers from various institutions undergoing a one-year internship after leaving university, and recent dental graduates from all over the country who have already completed their degrees and internships and are now in professional practice. Sampling was purposive, and the criteria were based on professional status (student/house officer/graduate). Final-year dental undergraduate students were invited by course representatives. Dental graduates and house officers were invited by standardised email. For those who expressed interest, a participant information sheet was provided, and written consent was obtained.

Quantitative data collection and analysis

The data collection process involved three assessments: the Dental Undergraduates Preparedness Assessment Scale (DU-PAS), the Myers-Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI), and the Individual Global Rating of Self-perceived Preparedness for independent dental practice.

Participants completed the DU-PAS questionnaire, a tool with previously demonstrated high validity comprising two sections - Part A (clinical procedures) and Part B (non-clinical and behavioural skills) [19].

Participants also completed an online MBTI personality test [20]. This test categorises individuals into 16 personality types based on their preferences across four dimensions: Extraversion (E) versus Introversion (I), Sensing (S) versus Intuition (N), Thinking (T) versus Feeling (F), and Judging (J) versus Perceiving (P) [13].

Finally, participants rated their self-perceived preparedness levels on a global scale (0%–not ready to 100%–completely ready).

Descriptive statistics were calculated using Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) software 23 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The reliability of the DU-PAS was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. Further analysis was performed using the chi-squared, Mann-Whitney U, and Kruskal-Wallis tests.

Qualitative data collection and analysis

Semi-structured interviews were conducted by a single interviewer. All interviews were recorded using the Audacity software (NCH Software Inc. Greenwood Village, CO, USA), converted to mp3 format, and transcribed manually. Notes taken during the interviews were matched with transcripts for accuracy. The guide used for interviews is shown in Table 1. Study data were reported using the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [21]. Participants had the opportunity to review and comment on the interview transcripts before thematic analysis.

Topic guide used in semi-structured interviews

Manual stepwise thematic analysis was conducted using Braun and Clarke’s six-step framework [22, 23]. Two researchers independently coded the data, focusing on themes related to CCM stages. Discrepancies were resolved through discussions. Codes were analysed for overarching themes and reviewed to ensure coherence and relevance by checking against the dataset [24]. A structured report was formed from the analysis, integrating the identified themes with supporting data excerpts. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the study.

The CCM was integrated into thematic qualitative analysis to explore participants’ perceptions of their level of competency based on the CCM’s four stages. Preliminary codes were assigned, and responses were analysed using the four CCM stages. Participants’ reflections were then mapped to these stages. For instance, those unaware of skill gaps before practice were mapped to unconscious incompetence, and those who recognised gaps upon entering clinical practice were mapped to conscious incompetence. Graduates with skills but lower confidence mapped to conscious competence, whereas those who could perform tasks effortlessly mapped to unconscious competence. The final synthesis of themes and alignment with CCM mapped the progression of dental graduates through different competency stages.

Results

Participants’ selection and characteristics

A total of fifty-three participants were invited to take part in the study. Forty-four completed the DU-PAS for a 83% response rate. Eighteen DU-PAS completers (41%) were final-year undergraduate dental students, 18 (41%) were dental house officers, and 8 (18%) were recent dental graduates. Thirty-eight DU-PAS completers (86%) were female and 6 (14%) were male. The age range for participants who completed the DU-PAS was 21 to 25 years (mean age 23.2 years (SD ± 3.43)).

Thirty study subjects completed the MBTI personality questionnaire, the Individual Global Rating of Self-Perceived Preparedness, and participated in one-on-one interviews. Among these 30 subjects, 12 (40%) were final-year undergraduate dental students, 12 (40%) were dental house officers, and 6 (20%) were recent dental graduates.

Quantitative results

DU-PAS questionnaire

The total DU-PAS score was calculated by summing the individual scores for all 50 items in the questionnaire. Reliability was high - overall Cronbach’s alpha was 0.89 (subscale A reliability 0.91 subscale B reliability 0.82).

The mean DU-PAS score for participants was 64.12 (± 5.18 SD). The mean score for Part A of the questionnaire was 62.2 (+ 5.04 SD) and 65.9 (± 8.08 SD) for Part B, as shown in Fig. 2.

Participant scores on Part B of DU-PAS by item

In this study, the correlation between DU-PAS scores and gender was statistically insignificant. However, there were noticeable discrepancies in particular clinical skills where those identifying as female reported more confidence than males in restoring teeth with tooth colour fillings independently (94.7% versus 66.7%) and performing multi-rooted root canal treatments (71% versus 33.3%).

A comparison of the DU-PAS scores across the entire cohort is presented in Table 4. There was a clear and statistically significant decline in overall DU-PAS scores across the three training groups. Final-year students reported the highest level of preparedness (67.9 ± 4.2), followed by house officers (62.4 ± 3.5), with recent graduates scoring the lowest (59.4 ± 4.8). This trend was similarly observed when comparing the clinical (Part A) and non-clinical (Part B) subscale scores separately. A Kruskal–Wallis test confirmed that these differences were statistically significant (H(2) = 18.42, p < 0.001). Post-hoc Dunn–Bonferroni comparisons further demonstrated that each pair of groups differed significantly, reflecting a stepwise decline in self-assessed competence as graduates progressed from student to practitioner roles.

Of the 30 participants who completed the MBTI, 60% were classified as extroverts and 40% as introverts. Extroverted participants reported higher self-perceived preparedness on the global rating scale (median 70%) than introverts (median 57%). These percentages represent median preparedness scores rather than proportions of individuals who felt prepared. Self-perceived preparedness declined in value according to experience: final-year students (72.5%), dental house officers (65%), and recent graduates (58%). This trend was statistically significant (Kruskal-Wallis test, p = 0.03), indicating an increasing awareness of competency gaps post-graduation and upon transition into practice (Table 5).

Discussion

This study found participants confident in foundational skills but less in complex procedures. Quantitative data showed that final-year students felt more prepared than house officers and recent graduates, revealing that skill gaps were realised once they encountered real-world clinical challenges. Qualitative findings, mapped to the CCM, showed participants often moved from unconscious incompetence to conscious incompetence in clinical practice settings, echoing quantitative results. The finding that extroverts reported a higher median preparedness score (70%) than introverts (57%) reflects differences in self-perception rather than categorical readiness. Extroverted participants may be more comfortable expressing confidence, seeking help, or engaging socially within clinical environments, which can positively influence self-ratings. However, given the small group size and modest difference, this should be interpreted as a trend in perceived preparedness rather than a definitive indicator of competence. From the results, insights into factors that hinder or enable preparedness for dental practice could be generated.

Enablers for preparedness for dental practice

The results of the DU-PAS questionnaire showed the participants’ high confidence in basic clinical skills (Table 1). This foundational training supports further skills development, similar to a spiral curriculum philosophy where students build on basic concepts with increasing complexity. Applying previous learning to new scenarios mirrors professional practice, promoting critical thinking and problem-solving [33]. The statistically significant decline in DU-PAS scores observed across the three training stages suggests that graduates experience a reduction in perceived competence as they transition from supervised learning to independent clinical practice. While final-year students reported the strongest sense of preparedness, this confidence diminished during the house officer year and declined further among recent graduates. This pattern may reflect the realities of early clinical work, including increased responsibility, reduced supervision, and limited structured feedback. The significant differences between all three groups indicate that this reduction is not incidental, but instead represents a consistent trend requiring attention. Aligning this enabler with CCM suggests conscious incompetence, where skill gaps are identified and developed with support and supervision for progression within the model [34, 35]. Consistent with earlier studies, our research confirms that theoretical knowledge alone does not translate seamlessly into confident practice [5, 6].

Self-awareness and reflection are key enablers. Understanding strengths and weaknesses and continuously reflecting on experiences is crucial for development. Participants who identified skills they needed to develop were aware of helpful strategies and opportunities (Table 4).

Specific personality traits can enhance preparedness for practice [36]. Growth mindsets highlight the role of psychological traits in development [37, 38]. Extroverts may be more likely to seek support, build networks and actively participate, creating learning opportunities (Table 5).

Authentic experiences boost confidence and are key enablers for preparedness for practice. Learning experiences with a real-world context hold educational value, as they integrate clinical theory with technical, communication, and ethical considerations, enhancing understanding [39–41].

Barriers to preparedness for dental practice

A significant barrier was curriculum gaps, particularly the absence of essential learning experiences. Individuals entering the workforce had reduced confidence in managing complex cases, radiographic procedures, and areas like fixed orthodontics and fixed prosthodontics, indicating inadequate curriculum coverage (Table 4).

Despite confidence in theoretical knowledge, lack of practical experience hindered preparedness. Without exposure to diverse clinical cases, competence progression was stalled [34]. This aligns with CCM’s unconscious incompetence, reflected in the drop in self-perceived preparedness when participants entered the workplace [33] (Table 4).

Globally, a disconnect exists between training outcomes and workplace needs, especially in orthodontics and radiology [2, 19]. Similar curriculum gaps in Pakistan affect clinical and non-clinical competencies [42]. Mapping these findings into CCM shows that recent graduates may enter the “unconscious incompetence” stage if training in specialised areas such as paediatric care, advanced endodontics, and radiographic safety is lacking [32].

Reduced interactions with senior faculty negatively impacted preparedness to practice. Senior faculty brings clinical and academic experience that can be imparted in teaching and supervision. They serve as role models, demonstrating the professionalism, skills, and knowledge required for safe and competent practices. Without this expert guidance, students miss feedback on performance and validation of competence, which are crucial for confidence and progress [26, 30, 43]. This interaction is necessary for CCM progression; without it, students may have unrealistic perceptions of their abilities, remaining unconsciously incompetent. This affects the provision of safe patient care.

Dental schools should realign curricula with workplace competencies integrating practice management, financial literacy, regulations, advanced communication skills, and emerging technologies such as clear aligner orthodontics, digital radiography, and cone-beam- computed tomography into clinical teaching. This would ensure faculty-guided exposure to complex procedures, thus closing the gap between perceived and actual competence [29]. Curricular reform should be complemented by robust mentorship and supervision, where senior faculty and experienced clinicians guide students through clinical tasks, supported by near-peer assistance that augments, rather than replaces, expert feedback [26, 30, 31]. Expanding experiential learning through community clinics and private practices will enhance clinical reasoning, practice management, and regulatory awareness in real-world contexts. Reflective practice with learning portfolios and self-assessment tools helps identify and address limitations before independent practice. Programs should align learning outcomes with competency-based assessments to verify preparedness in the clinical, managerial, and ethical domains, fostering lifelong learning and growth mindsets for resilient, adaptable, and continuously developing dental professionals.

This study has limitations, relying primarily on self-assessment measures (DU-PAS scores and global preparedness ratings), which can be subject to over- or underestimation and social desirability bias. The validity of the MBTI in predicting professional performance is debated, offering only an approximate view of complex behaviours [44]. The cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences about how preparedness for practice evolves. These findings may not be representative of dental graduates or practitioners in different regions or cultures. Longitudinal studies following cohorts from training to practice would offer more profound insights into the development of competence.

Conclusion

This study combined the Dental Undergraduates Preparedness Assessment Scale (DU-PAS), a validated preparedness scale, MBTI personality profiling, a global perception of practice readiness, and qualitative interviews mapped to the Conscious Competence Model (CCM) to construct a multidimensional picture of Pakistani dental graduates’ preparedness for clinical practice. Preparedness scores were highest in final year students but declined among house officers and recent graduates once they faced independent clinical duties, mirroring a shift from unconscious to conscious incompetence in complex endodontics, radiography, paediatric and anxious patient management, and clinic administration. Extroverted participants reported higher confidence than introverts; however, the small effect size suggests that sociability primarily facilitated help seeking rather than reflecting superior competence. Thematic analysis identified six experiential domains, enhanced clinical exposure, structured professional development, patient care mastery, emergency management, business and regulatory knowledge, and ethical reflection, which together explained progression through the CCM competence perception stages. These findings can guide educators in timing focused curricular interventions to help learners consolidate conscious competence before entering unsupervised practice, and longitudinal follow up is recommended to evaluate the influence of such reforms on early career performance and patient outcomes.
