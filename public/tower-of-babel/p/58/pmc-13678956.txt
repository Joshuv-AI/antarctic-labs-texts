Serglycin-ROS axis mediates synergistic lethality of PI3Kγ inhibition and Azacitidine in TP53-mutated AML
Authors: Guiqin Huang, Xiaoya Cai, Yeming Zhou, Ying Liu, Fankai Meng, Dengju Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate novel combination strategies for TP53-mutated acute myeloid leukemia (AML), focusing on the potential synergy between PI3Kγ inhibition and Azacitidine, and to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Materials and methods: We detected the expression of PI3Kγ (PIK3CG/PIK3R5) in TP53 mutant and wildtype AML cell lines using the CCLE database, RT-qPCR, and western blot. We evaluated the influence of Azacitidine (AZA) and PI3Kγ inhibitor (Eganelisib) in AML cells, conducted in vivo combination therapy experiments, and identified synergistic targets through RNA sequencing and siRNA technology. Results: TP53-mutated AML showed hypermethylation of PI3Kγ promoters and reduced PI3Kγ expression. AZA treatment selectively activated the PI3Kγ signaling in TP53-mutated cells. Eganelisib combined with AZA demonstrated potent synergy, suppressing proliferation and inducing apoptosis in TP53-mutated AML (ZIP synergy score > 10). This combination extended survival in xenograft models (33.0 vs. 18.0 days, p < 0.01). RNA-seq identified serglycin (SRGN) as a candidate mediator potentially linking AZA-induced transcriptional reprogramming to ROS-mediated cell death. SRGN knockdown elevated ROS levels and sensitized cells to AZA. The combinatorial treatment significantly increased ROS levels compared to monotherapy. Conclusion: We provide the first preclinical evidence that PI3Kγ inhibition sensitizes TP53-mutated AML to AZA through a mechanism potentially involving the SRGN-ROS axis, offering a translatable combinatorial strategy for patients with this aggressive leukemia subtype.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16547-5.Keywords: AML, TP53, PI3Kγ, Azacitidine, Synergy, Oxidative stress

Introduction

TP53-mutated acute myeloid leukemia (AML), characterized by resistance to conventional chemotherapy and a high relapse rate post-allogeneic stem cell transplantation, poses a significant therapeutic challenge with a dismal prognosis. Recent clinical trials exploring combinations of Venetoclax [1], p53 reactivators [2], immune checkpoint inhibitors [3], and other targeted agents [4] offer hope. These innovative strategies have thus far failed to significantly improve long-term survival outcomes for patients with TP53 mutations. Limitations of these approaches include the development of resistance, short-lived responses, and potential toxicities. Furthermore, the heterogeneity of TP53 mutations within the patient population necessitates tailored treatment approaches [5]. Addressing these challenges requires further research to develop more effective and targeted therapies, while also exploring novel combination strategies and personalized medicine approaches to optimize treatment for this difficult-to-treat disease.

In contrast to other Phosphoinositide 3-kinase (PI3K) isozymes, PI3Kγ (encoded by PIK3CG, PIK3R5/PIK3R6) is the only isozyme that uniquely regulates the immune-metabolic crosstalk [6, 7]. PI3Kγ has been suggested to promote leukemia progression by regulating the pentose phosphate pathway to maintain the metabolism and function of AML stem cells [6]. The activation of PI3Kγ has been reported to increase the polarization of immunosuppressive myeloid cells and to inhibit anti-tumor immune responses [8]. Furthermore, PI3Kγ possesses a non-classical function and can act as a molecular scaffold independently of its lipid kinase activity [9].

However, the specific expression of PI3Kγ in AML in a particular genetic background is far from clear. AKT, the downstream component of PI3Kγ, has been shown to inhibit p53 activity by phosphorylating MDM2, thereby promoting p53 degradation [10]. In turn, p53 has been observed to induce the expression of certain negative regulatory factors, thereby inhibiting the activity of the PI3Kγ pathway [11]. However, the expression and function of PI3Kγ in TP53-mutated AML remains to be elucidated. The genetic heterogeneity of AML is a major factor in determining its therapeutic complexity, and the development of precision therapeutic strategies requires an in-depth understanding of the functions and influences of the key signaling pathways in a specific genetic context.

In this study, we identified a specific expression pattern of PI3Kγ in TP53-mutated AML in different genetic backgrounds and hypothesized that TP53-mutated AML may acquire drug resistance through metabolic reprogramming of the PI3Kγ pathway. In addition, a PI3Kγ inhibitor was combined with Azacitidine (AZA) against TP53-mutated AML, with a view to identifying a novel therapeutic strategy. The aim of this study was to test this hypothesis and to investigate the synergistic mechanism of the combination of PI3Kγ inhibitor and AZA, thus providing a theoretical basis for the treatment of TP53-mutated AML.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and cell culture

The human AML cell lines SKM-1, THP-1, K562, KG-1, MOLM13, OCI-AML3, MV4-11, and OCI-AML4 were obtained from the China Center for Type Culture Collection (Wuhan, China). Authentication services were performed on all cell lines to confirm their identity, and the cytogenetic backgrounds are shown in Table S1. Gene expression levels for 14 TP53-wildtype and 29 TP53-mutant AML cell lines were provided in supplementary CSV file, obtained from the CCLE database (https://sites.broadinstitute.org/ccle). The MOLM13 TP53 knock-out (TP53KO MOLM13) cells were generated using the CRISPR/Cas9 system in accordance with the reported method [12], and selected as monoclonal cell lines (Figure S1A, B). The construction of the mutant cell lines involved cloning the full-length human TP53 cDNA into the lentiviral vector pCDH-EF1-MCS-T2A-copGFP. To strictly rule out potential artifacts caused by lentiviral transduction or copGFP expression, an empty vector (pCDH-EF1-MCS-T2A-copGFP lacking the TP53 cDNA) was concurrently used to establish a negative control cell line (MOLM13-NC). This MOLM13-NC cell line served as a baseline negative control in all subsequent functional assays. To confirm successful lentiviral transduction, we analyzed copGFP reporter expression via flow cytometry and validated the accurate integration of the TP53 R273H and R175H mutations using Sanger sequencing (Figure S1C). The subsequent culturing of the mixed clonal cell lines in a single-cell isolation process was undertaken to generate monoclonal cell lines exhibiting stable expression. The AML cells were cultivated in 90% RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) in a humidified condition of 5% CO2 at 37 °C. Cells exhibiting greater than 95% viability were selected for subsequent experimentation.

Primary samples

Peripheral blood (PB) samples were collected from AML patients and mobilized healthy donors. The collection of these samples was conducted in accordance with a protocol that has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (permit number TJ-IRB 20230228). Informed consent was obtained from the patients in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The primary clinical characteristics were delineated in Table S2. Mononuclear cells were isolated by density-gradient centrifugation (Ficoll-Paque, Solarbio, Beijing, China) and subsequently cultured in RPMI 1640 (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS for the purpose of drug treatments. The cells were then subjected to a series of analytical procedures, including apoptosis analysis, proliferation analysis, and flow cytometry analysis.

Chemicals

Eganelisib (HY-100716) and 5-AZA (HY-10586) were procured from MedChemExpress (MCE) for utilization in in vitro cellular experiments and in vivo experiments in mice. D-Luciferin potassium (HY-12591B) was purchased from MCE for the purpose of in vivo imaging in mice.

Cell proliferation assay

For proliferation assays, cells were seeded in a 96-well plate at 20,000 cells/100 µL per well. After the addition of inhibitors, the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 72 h. Cell viability was measured using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Targetmol, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Detection of apoptosis and oxidative stress levels

The cells were seeded at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL in the presence or absence of compounds for 48 h. Thereafter, the cells were collected, washed in a solution of phosphate buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in 200 uL of PBS solution. The occurrence of apoptosis was detected using a commercial kit (rh Annexin V-APC Apoptosis Detection Kit, Antgene, China), while the reactive oxygen level of the cells was evaluated using another commercial kit (ROS Reactive Oxygen Detection Kit, red fluorescence, CA1420, Solarbio, China). The samples were then subjected to a Beckman Coulter FC500 flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Pasadena, CA).

Reverse transcription quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted using the FastPure Cell/Tissue Total RNA Isolation Kit V2 (RC112, Vazyme, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. First strand cDNA synthesis was performed with cDNA Synthesis SuperMix for qPCR (11141ES60, Yeasen, China), and 1 µg total RNA was used for one 20 µl reverse transcription reaction. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis was conducted on a CFX Connect™ Fluorescent Quantitative PCR Detection System (788BR09364, Bio-Rad, Singapore) with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711, Vazyme, China). The qPCR primer sequences utilized were as follows (5′-3′): GAPDH (forward-TCGGAGTCAACGGATTTG; reverse-CAACAATATCCACTTTACCAGAG), PIK3CG (forward-AACACCGACCTCACAGTTTTT; reverse-CTCAAGCCACACATTCCACAG), PIK3R5 (forward-TGACATGCTACTCTACTACTG; reverse-GGAGTGGATGAAGATCTCTG), SRGN (forward-AGGTTATCCTACGCGGAGAG; reverse-GTCTTTGGAAAAAGGTCAGTCCT).

Methylation-specific PCR

The manufacturer’s instructions were followed in the extraction of DNA using the Rapid Purification of Total Cell/Tissue DNA Isolation Kit V2 (DC102, Vazyme, China). The DNA was then treated with the DNA Methylation Bisulfite Kit (EM101, Vazyme, China) and PCR amplification was performed using 2×EpiArt® HS Taq Master Mix (EM202, Vazyme, China) for PCR amplification. The conditions for DNA gel electrophoresis were as follows: 1×TAE, 2.0% agarose, 4 V/cm. The following sequence of methylation-specific PCR primers was utilized (5′-3′): PIK3CG M (forward-TAGTTGGGAGTTTTTTAATATTTCGA; reverse-CCTAAATCATTTTTAAAATAACGTT), PIK3CG U (forward-TTTAGTTGGAGTTTTTTTTAATATATTTTGA; reverse-TCCTAAATCATTTTTAAAATAACATT), PIK3R5 M (forward-GTTTCGACGGTTATTCGAATC; reverse-CAACTCTACCTAAAACTCCCATACG), PIK3R5 U (forward-GGTTTTGATGGTTATTTGAATTGT; reverse-ACTCTACCTAAAACTCCCATACATA).

Western blotting analysis

Proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (BioRad, USA). Samples were electrophoresed using SDS-PAGE (Bio-Rad, USA), and then transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad, USA) where 5% BSA was used to seal bands. The membranes were then incubated with the following antibodies: anti-GAPDH (A2066, Sigma-Aldrich, Sweden, 1:2000), anti-PIK3CG (sc-166365, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 1:500) and anti-PIK3R5 (sc-390916, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, 1:500). The image Lab™ Software (Bio-Rad, United States) was employed for strip exposure and analysis.

In vivo study

The animal experiments conducted in this study were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Medical College, Huazhong University of Science and Technology (Approve number: 4250). The procedures were carried out in accordance with the specified guidelines. All drugs used for in vivo treatment were formulated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. All mice were purchased from GemPharmatech LLC (Nanjing, China). The animals were bred under specific-pathogen-free conditions and were not privately owned; therefore, no additional owner consent was required. Corresponding carrier solutions were used for carrier control mice.

The establishment of a subcutaneous tumor model was achieved through the injection of SKM-1 cells into 5-week-old BALB/c nude Mice. Once the tumor volume reached approximately 150 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into four groups. Following grouping, mice were treated with vehicle, AZA (2.5 mg/kg, days 1–5, days 8–12, QD, ip), the PI3Kγ inhibitor Eganelisib (15 mg/kg, days 1–14, QD, po), or a combination of these treatments. The mice were continuously administered the drugs for approximately two weeks and euthanized once the maximum diameter of the tumor reached 15 mm. The tumors were meticulously dissected out and fixed, followed by immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining.

1 × 106 K562 cells were transplanted into 6–8 weeks NOD.Cg-Prkdc Il2rg/SzJ female mice via lateral tail vein injection. The body weight of mouse was recorded on a weekly basis. PB was collected post-orbital, and the implantation was assessed by measuring human CD33 (Hu CD33 PE WM53, 555450, BD Pharmingen) using flow cytometry. The mice were divided into four groups of 6 mice each, by stratified randomization based on body weight. After grouping, the mice were treated with solvent, AZA (2.5 mg/kg, days 1–5, days 8–12, QD, ip), the PI3Kγ inhibitor Eganelisib (15 mg/kg, days 1–14, QD, po), or a combination of the two. The experimental protocol involved weekly bioluminescence imaging, performed on mice after intraperitoneal injection of anaesthetics and potassium D-luciferin (150 mg/kg, HY-12591B, MCE). After 5–8 min, the in vivo detection of K562 cell growth in mice was conducted using small animal live optical imaging (Lago X).

Mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane (induction 3–5%, maintenance 1.5–2%). After loss of consciousness and absence of pedal reflex, death was induced by cervical dislocation. Loss of vital signs (permanent cessation of heartbeat and respiration) was confirmed before disposal of the carcass. This method is consistent with the current AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals and was approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tongji Medical College (protocol no. 4250).

RNA‑sequencing

MOLM13 WT and TP53KO MOLM13 cells (triplicate) were treated with dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), AZA, the PI3Kγ inhibitor Eganelisib, or a combination of these agents for 48 h. The RNA sequencing (RNA seq) was performed on 24 samples using the NovaSeq 6000 platform. The input data for differential gene expression analysis were read count data obtained from gene expression level analysis and analyzed using DESeq 2 software. Genes exhibiting altered expression with a false-discovery rate p < 0.05 and more than 1.5-fold changes were designated as differentially expressed.

Statistical analysis

The data obtained from this study were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. The results of the data were presented as the mean value ± standard deviation. The Student’s t test was used for comparisons between two groups. For multiple comparisons, the ordinary one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s multiple comparison test was used. Statistically significant results were considered when p < 0.05. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Regulation of PI3Kγ expression in TP53-mutated AML by Azacitidine

An analysis of AML patients in the TCGA database using the GEPIA website (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#index) revealed that the PI3Kγ-related genes PIK3CG and PIK3R5 exhibited elevated levels of expression in AML cases when compared to the controls (Figure S1D). As demonstrated in Figure S1E, the PI3Kγ high-expression group had a suboptimal prognosis in comparison to the low-expression group, when the subjects were stratified according to median gene expression.

In the analysis of PI3Kγ genes in human-derived AML cell lines with different genetic backgrounds, it was revealed that the mRNA expression of PIK3CG and PIK3R5 in TP53-mutated AML cell lines was significantly lower than that in TP53-wildtype AML cell lines (Fig. 1A). A comparison of the expression of PI3Kγ-related genes was made using gene expression data from the CCLE database. This revealed that the mRNA expression of PIK3CG and PIK3R5 was similarly lower in TP53-mutated AML cell lines than in TP53-wildtype AML cell lines, consistent with the experimental trend (Fig. 1B). Following a thorough analysis of the intergenic correlations present within the beat AML patient database, it was determined that the TP53 gene exhibited a significant linear negative correlation with the PI3Kγ-related genes PIK3CG and PIK3R5 (Fig. 1C). Combining the low expression of PI3Kγ-related genes in TP53 mutant cells with the negative correlation between TP53 and PI3Kγ-related genes, we speculate that there may be mutual influence between TP53 and PI3Kγ-related genes.

To assess the antiproliferative effect of AZA on AML cell lines, AML cell lines were treated with different concentrations of AZA. It was observed that the TP53-mutated AML cell lines SKM-1, THP-1 and K562 were all less sensitive to AZA than TP53-wildtype AML cell lines MOLM13 and OCI-AML3 (Fig. 1D). After a 48-h exposure to AZA, the expression levels of key genes within the PI3K pathway was analyzed using RT-qPCR. The expression of genes associated with PI3Kγ expression increased significantly in the TP53-mutated AML-containing cell lines SKM-1/THP-1/K562, whereas no up-regulation occurred in the wildtype TP53 AML cell line MOLM13/OCI-AML3 (Fig. 1E).

Synergistic effects of AZA and Eganelisib in TP53-mutated AML cell lines

Eganelisib and AZA were used in isolation or in combination with each other in AML cell lines to evaluate the combinatorial effects. While individual treatments with AZA or PI3Kγ inhibitors induced only marginal alterations in cell apoptosis rates, the combined administration of these agents resulted in a marked increase in cellular apoptosis (Fig. 1F). To further investigate the role of AZA and PI3Kγ inhibitors in TP53-mutated AML cells, we conducted a series of proliferation assays using the SKM-1, THP-1, and K562 cell lines. Following a 48-h treatment period with PI3K inhibitors and/or AZA, the combination therapy demonstrated a significantly antiproliferation effect compared to monotherapy with either agent (Fig. 1G). The synergistic antiproliferative effect of Eganelisib in combination with AZA was specifically observed in TP53-mutated AML cell lines (SKM-1, THP-1, and K562), as quantitatively assessed through ZIP (Fig. 1H), HSA (Figure S1F), BLISS (Figure S1G) modeling. However, this synergistic effect was absent in wildtype TP53 AML cell lines (MOLM13 and OCI-AML3), as demonstrated in Figure S1H.

Combined effects of Eganelisib and AZA in TP53-abnormal AML cells and patient progenitor cells

To explore the specificity of the synergistic interaction between Eganelisib and AZA, the TP53 gene was knocked down in the MOLM13 AML cell line using the CRISPR/Cas9 system. The resulting TP53KO MOLM13 monoclonal cell line is shown in Figure S1A, B. Monoclonal cell lines with mutations at the TP53 R273H and TP53 R175H loci were constructed by lentiviral overexpression technique on the TP53 KO MOLM13 monoclonal cell line (Figure S1C). As demonstrated in Fig. 2A, B, the successful knockdown and mutant overexpression of TP53 was evidenced using qPCR and protein blotting, respectively. In the TP53KO, TP53R273H, and TP53R175H locus-mutated MOLM13 cell lines, we further validated the low expression of PI3Kγ-related genes in TP53 aberrant AML (Fig. 2A, B). In the present study, we observed that AZA treatment led to the up-regulation of PI3Kγ-related gene expression in TP53-mutated AML cell lines. To ascertain the extent of methylation in the promoter region of the PIK3CG and PIK3R5, methylation-specific PCR was employed to differentiate between the mutated and control groups. The results revealed that the methylation level of the PIK3CG promoter region of TP53 KO MOLM13 was higher than that of MOLM13, and TP53 R273H and TP53 R175H MOLM13 were abnormally higher than that of MOLM13 NC PIK3CG promoter region. Levels of PIK3R5 promoter region methylation was found to be elevated in TP53 R273H and TP53 R175H MOLM13 samples in comparison to MOLM13 NC (Fig. 2C). TP53KO MOLM13, TP53R273H MOLM13, and TP53R175H MOLM13 cells exhibited relative resistance to AZA monotherapy compared to parental MOLM13. Eganelisib had a similar effect on all four cell lines (Fig. 2D).

Relative DNA methylation levels (β-values) of two representative CpG probes, located within the PIK3CG and PIK3R5 promoter region were analyzed using the public dataset GSE123140. AZA induces DNA demethylation at the promoter region (Figure S1I). AZA was employed on the MOLM13 cell line, which exhibited mutations at the TP53 KO, TP53 R273H, and TP53 R175H, for a period of 48 h. This resulted in a significant upregulation of the relevant genes of PI3Kγ (Fig. 2E). Furthermore, Eganelisib and AZA showed synergistic antiproliferative effects in MOLM13 cell lines with mutations at the TP53 KO, TP53 R273H, and TP53 R175H loci (Fig. 2F). The apoptosis induced by the Eganelisib and AZA was remarkably higher than that of either agent alone (Fig. 2G).

The subsequent investigation focused on the impact of the combination of AZA and Eganelisib on AML and healthy donor primary cells. PB samples were collected from 9 TP53-mutated AML patients and 5 TP53-wildtype AML patients. Mononuclear cells were isolated and incubated with AZA (1 µM) and/or Eganelisib (0.5 µM) for 48 h. Despite varying baseline sensitivities to the single agents, the synergistic antiproliferative effect of the AZA and Eganelisib combination was highly pronounced in TP53-mutated primary cells, but lacked significance in the TP53-wildtype controls (Fig. 2H). PB mononuclear cells from five healthy donors were collected [13, 14] and treated with 1 µM AZA in combination with Eganelisib at increasing concentrations for 48 h. The results suggested that the regimen exhibited limited toxicity to PB mononuclear cells from healthy donors (Fig. 2I).

Combination of Eganelisib and AZA demonstrates therapeutic efficacy in a xenograft model derived from TP53-mutated AML cell lines

To investigate the combined effects of Eganelisib and AZA in vivo, TP53-mutated AML cell lines were subcutaneously and intravenously implanted in two xenograft mouse models (Fig. 3A). The mice showed adequate tolerance to the drugs, both as monotherapy and in combination, with no evidence of weight loss or other indications of distress. Consistent with the in vitro outcomes, the combination regimen of AZA and Eganelisib significantly inhibited the growth of subcutaneous tumors in mice, in comparison to the individual agents (Fig. 3B). Subcutaneous tumor weight was significantly lower in the combination group than in the single-agent group (Fig. 3C).

The in vivo imaging (Fig. 3D) suggested a substantial reduction in leukemic load in mice treated with the combination of two agents in comparison with either agent administered individually. The weekly detection of hCD33 in PB by flow cytometry indicated that the combination treatment effectively inhibited the proliferation of leukemia cells in mice (Fig. 3E). Overall, mice receiving the combination treatment (33.0 days) had statistically prolonged survival relative to the control group (18.0 days, p < 0.01), the Eganelisib group (19.0 days, p < 0.01), and the AZA group (23.7 days, p = 0.029) (Fig. 3F). As demonstrated in Fig. 3G, the body weights of the mice in the combination group remained consistent at the commencement and throughout the duration of the therapy.

RNA-seq shows Serglycin (SRGN) involvement in the synergistic therapeutic effect of Eganelisib and AZA in TP53-mutated AML cells

Considering the significant synergistic anti-tumor effects of combination treatment with AZA and Eganelisib in TP53-mutated cells, a further investigation was conducted into the observed synergistic mechanisms. To identify genes responsive to the combination treatments, we performed RNA-seq using MOLM13 and MOLM13 TP53 KO cells after treatment with DMSO, AZA, PI3Kγ inhibitor, or Combo (AZA and PI3Kγ inhibitor) (Fig. 4A). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed segregation between MOLM13 and MOLM13 TP53 KO cells, as anticipated, thereby indicating divergent genetic backgrounds of the two cell lines. It is noteworthy that in both cells, AZA treatment with or without the addition of Eganelisib resulted in clustering of the samples by PCA, suggesting that AZA exerts a more profound influence on the transcriptome of both cell lines than Eganelisib (Fig. 4B). In view of the restricted scope of the changes in transcriptome profiles induced by Eganelisib in comparison to AZA, we sought to elucidate the subtle changes introduced by Eganelisib by comparing the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the AZA and Combo groups in the two cell lines. A comparison of the KEGG and GO enrichment analysis of DEGs between Combo and AZA revealed a significant enrichment of genes involved in oxidative phosphorylation, protein synthesis and modification, and apoptosis (Fig. 4C). This finding differed significantly from the enrichment analysis of MOLM13 WT cells (Figure S1J). A total of thirteen DEGs, including eight genes that were down-regulated in Combo and up-regulated in AZA, and five genes up-regulated in Combo and down-regulated in AZA, were identified (Fig. 4D). These genes were observed to be present in TP53 KO MOLM13 cells but not in the wildtype cells. Of these genes, SRGN was of particular interest. Relative DNA methylation levels (β-values) of two representative CpG probes, cg13645409 (left) and cg14898243 (right), located within the SRGN promoter region were analyzed using the public dataset GSE123140. AZA induces DNA demethylation at the promoter region of SRGN (Fig. 4E). We further verified the expression of SRGN in SKM-1, THP-1, and K562 cells post-treatment with DMSO, AZA, or Combo by qPCR. Consistent with the RNA-seq results, a significant increase in SRGN expression was observed after treatment with AZA, while cotreatment with the Eganelisib resulted in a reduction of SRGN expression to baseline levels (Fig. 4F). Moreover, in the independent dataset GSE41754 [15], SRGN expression was found to be increased by AZA treatment in the TP53-mutated cell line HL60, and decreased in the TP53-wildtype cell line HCT116 (Figure S1K). In addition, we observed the significant overexpression of SRGN in patients with AML in TCGA database (Fig. 4G) and a positive correlation between SRGN expression and PI3Kγ expression (Fig. 4H).

SRGN inhibition increases ROS levels and thus enhances TP53-mutated AML sensitivity to AZA

To further investigate the role of SRGN in the synergistic effects observed in the combination treatment with AZA and Eganelisib, siRNAs were used to knock down SRGN (Figure S1L). After treatment of SRGN knockdown SKM-1, THP-1, and K562 cells with AZA, SRGN-si cells exhibited an increased sensitivity to the antiproliferative and pro-apoptotic effects of AZA (Fig. 4I, J). In comparison with the control group, SRGN-si cells showed elevated levels of ROS (Fig. 4K). In addition, elevated ROS levels were observed in the combination group of AZA and Eganelisib, in comparison with the DMSO group or with agent administered individually (Fig. 4L). To establish a causal link between ROS accumulation and the observed synergistic lethality, we performed a functional rescue experiment using the ROS scavenger N-acetylcysteine (NAC). The AML cell lines (including SKM-1, THP-1, and K562) were pre-treated with 5 mM neutralized NAC for 2 h prior to co-incubation with the AZA and Eganelisib combination. Scavenging intracellular ROS with NAC significantly abrogated the synergistic anti-proliferative and apoptosis-inducing effects of the dual-drug regimen (Fig. 4M and N).

Discussion

In this study, we identified a lower baseline level of the PI3Kγ gene in TP53-mutated AML cells than in wildtype cells. TP53-mutated AML cells showed a substantial increase in PI3Kγ following AZA treatment. This increase was found to be inhibited by the PI3Kγ inhibitor Eganelisib. We demonstrated the synergistic anti-tumor efficacy of AZA and Eganelisib, with limited toxicity to healthy cells, across TP53-mutated cell lines, primary patient samples, and mouse xenograft models. Transcriptomic analysis identified SRGN as a candidate mediator of the observed synergy. SRGN is a granular proteoglycan predominantly expressed in hematopoietic cells, where it is implicated in the assembly of perforin complexes and granule-mediated apoptosis. SRGN has been identified as one of the genes highly expressed in AML cells [16], and has been shown to regulate the retention and release of platelet factor 4 (PF4), thereby influencing bone marrow microenvironment homeostasis [17]. Notably, suppression of SRGN has been reported to impede proliferation and induce apoptosis in AML cells [18], suggesting that its downregulation may represent a therapeutically exploitable vulnerability in this disease context. SRGN expression was significantly upregulated following AZA treatment and returned toward baseline upon co-treatment with Eganelisib, a pattern validated by RT-qPCR across three independent TP53-mutant cell lines. Functionally, SRGN knockdown elevated basal ROS levels and sensitized TP53-mutant AML cells to AZA, while scavenging intracellular ROS with NAC abrogated the synergistic lethality of the combination — collectively implicating the SRGN–ROS axis in the observed synergistic lethality. The upstream mechanisms governing AZA-induced SRGN upregulation and its suppression by Eganelisib, however, remain to be directly demonstrated. We hypothesize that AZA may de-repress SRGN transcription through promoter demethylation, analogous to the AZA-induced re-expression of PIK3CG and PIK3R5 observed in this study. The mechanism by which Eganelisib counteracts AZA-induced SRGN upregulation remains to be directly demonstrated and warrants future investigation (Fig. 5). Together, our observations delineate a previously unrecognized relationship between PI3Kγ signaling, SRGN expression, and ROS-mediated cell death in TP53-mutated AML, providing a mechanistic rationale for the clinical investigation of AZA and PI3Kγ inhibitor combination therapy in this high-risk patient population.

The proposed model illustrates the mechanism of the combined action of AZA and PI3Kγ inhibitors. In the baseline state, a high methylation level of the PI3Kγ gene promoter region was observed in TP53 aberrant AML cells, which led to the inhibition of the expression of the PI3Kγ genes PIK3CG and PIK3R5. AZA treatment, on the one hand, exerted part of the cytotoxic effect, inhibited AML cell proliferation, and promoted apoptosis; on the other hand, it caused a decrease in the methylation level of the promoter region of the PI3Kγ gene, resulting in elevated expression of PIK3CG and PIK3R5. This may represent a mechanism through which TP53-mutated AML cells can evade the effect of AZA treatment. The addition of the PI3Kγ inhibitor Eganelisib is associated with the attenuation of AZA-induced SRGN upregulation. This correlates with elevated intracellular ROS levels and enhanced synergistic anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects in TP53-mutated AML cells, through a mechanism that remains to be fully elucidated

The role of PI3Kγ in AML has attracted mounting attention in recent years, especially with regard to its potential association with TP53-mutated AML. The expression of PIK3CG and PIK3R5 has been observed to correlate positively with monocyte- and dendritic cell-like transcriptome profiles in AML cells [19] and negatively with progenitor cell-like or leukemia stem cell (LSC)-related gene profiles [20]. The activation of the PI3Kγ pathway may further exacerbate this process, especially in LSCs, moving them from a quiescent state to a self-renewal and proliferative phase, and ultimately leading to disease relapse. In addition, the efficacy of PI3Kγ inhibitors in TP53-mutated hematological tumors have been demonstrated in clinical trials [21]. However, the action mechanism of the inhibitors may extend beyond direct inhibition of PI3Kγ signaling. The triple inhibitor of PI3Kγ, δ, and DNA-PK, BR101801, enhanced the radiosensitivity of p53-deficient colorectal cancer cells by inducing G2/M arrest, apoptosis, and senescence in a p53-independent manner [22]. However, the complexity and heterogeneity of TP53-mutated AML means that single targeting of PI3Kγ may not be sufficient to overcome drug resistance completely, and further exploration of combination therapy strategies is required.

Inhibitors targeting PI3K isozymes have undergone several iterations and updates in tumor therapy, especially the development and clinical application of PI3Kγ inhibitors, which has become a research hotspot in haemato-oncology. The PI3K δ/γ inhibitor Duvelisib, the PI3Kδ inhibitor Idelalisib, and another PI3Kα/δ Copanlisib inhibitor were all withdrawn from treatment with FL due to risk-benefit concerns arising from clinical findings [23]. It is notable that all three of the discontinued inhibitors have high potency in term of their ability to inhibit PI3Kδ, which suggests that the toxicity observed in the clinical settings may be, at least in part, contributable to the target effect of PI3Kδ inhibition. Eganelisib, a selective PI3Kγ inhibitor, is currently being evaluated in clinical trials for its potential to modulate the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and has shown to be well tolerated [24]. The present study also found that the PI3Kγ inhibitor Eganelisib was less toxic to healthy cells, suggesting that precisely targeting PI3Kγ may avoid excessive toxicity. Furthermore, the researchers developed a Proteolysis-Targeting Chimera (PROTAC) targeting PI3Kγ that specifically degrades PIK3CG, leading to the sustained elimination of AKT signaling and a significant decrease in AML cell viability without influencing that of non-AML cell lines [25]. There are also several newly developed PI3Kγ-selective inhibitors, each of which exhibits physiological properties such as potent PI3Kγ inhibition selectivity, good blood-brain barrier penetration, and good oral bioavailability, among others [26, 27]. In conclusion, the iterative updating of PI3Kγ inhibitors is moving towards higher selectivity and lower toxicity.

In recent years, the combination of PI3Kγ inhibitors has made significant progress in the field of cancer therapy, providing novel ideas and strategies for the treatment of various cancers. In clinical studies, IPI-549 significantly enhanced anti-tumor activity and improved tumor regression when combined with anti-PD-1 or anti-CTLA-4 [24, 28]. Of particular relevance to the present study, Luo et al. demonstrated that single-agent Eganelisib prolongs survival in PIK3R5/PIK3CG-high leukemia PDX models via the noncanonical PI3Kγ–PAK1 axis, and that Eganelisib synergizes with cytarabine across leukemias regardless of baseline PIK3R5 expression, through a mechanism involving G protein-coupled purinergic receptor-driven PI3Kγ activation in cytarabine-resistant cells [29]. Since TP53-mutant AML cells exhibit constitutively low PIK3CG/PIK3R5 expression due to promoter hypermethylation (Figs. 1A–B and 2C), they are inherently poor candidates for Eganelisib monotherapy. Our findings extend this framework by demonstrating that AZA-induced promoter demethylation reactivates PI3Kγ signaling and concurrently upregulates SRGN, creating a targetable vulnerability: Eganelisib then suppresses this AZA-induced SRGN upregulation, permitting ROS accumulation that drives synergistic lethality — a mechanism distinct from PIK3R5-dependent monotherapy sensitivity. Anthracyclines, as a class of clinically used chemotherapeutic drugs for AML, are associated with significant cardiotoxicity. Evidence has shown that the suppression of PI3Kγ can serve as a preventative measure against anthracycline-induced cardiotoxicity, while concurrently attenuating tumor growth [30]. Selinexor, an inhibitor of the nuclear export protein XPO1, showed encouraging efficacy in relapsed/refractory AML, with the concomitant inhibition of PI3Kγ-AKT signaling potentiating the antileukemic effects of Selinexor [31]. A PROTAC degrader targeting PI3Kγ-ARM165 demonstrated potent anti-leukemia capacity against primary patient cells, markedly reducing the disease burden in the bone marrow, spleen, and blood, while enhancing the effect of Venetoclax in a mouse model [25]. It is recommended that future studies undertake a more comprehensive evaluation of the potential relationship between the PI3Kγ signaling pathway and other significant intracellular signaling pathways, with an especial focus on refractory AML such as TP53-mutated AML. This combination therapy strategy has the potential to overcome resistance at multiple levels and induce a durable adaptive immune response, providing a more effective treatment option for AML patients.

Despite its promising preclinical efficacy, this study has several limitations. First, while strongly supported by in vitro and ex vivo data, the TP53-mutation specificity of the observed synergy requires definitive confirmation in future TP53-wildtype in vivo xenograft models. Second, further investigations are needed to determine whether this synergistic effect correlates with specific TP53 mutation subtypes or expression levels. Third, although the small healthy donor cohort (n = 5) limits the statistical power of our toxicity assay, the inherent quiescence (G0 phase) of resting peripheral blood mononuclear cells renders them insensitive to AZA, which supports our claim of low baseline toxicity; future studies should assess combination toxicity in CD34 + hematopoietic stem and progenitor cells using colony-forming unit assays. Fourth, while we have demonstrated that SRGN suppression is associated with ROS elevation and that ROS accumulation is functionally required for the synergistic lethality, the upstream mechanisms by which AZA upregulates SRGN and by which Eganelisib counteracts this upregulation remain to be elucidated. Collectively, future work should focus on resolving the PI3Kγ/p53/SRGN regulatory network through SRGN promoter methylation profiling, chromatin immunoprecipitation approaches, and functional dissection of PI3Kγ downstream transcriptional targets, and ultimately, larger-scale clinical trials will be essential to validate the safety and clinical superiority of this combination strategy for TP53-mutated AML.
