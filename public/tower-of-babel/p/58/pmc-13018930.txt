Tannic acid supplementation exerts biphasic effects on growth performance, immune function, and gut microbiota in Pekin duck
Authors: Xue-qian Jiang, Ze-cheng Zou, Ye-lin Zeng, Hao-tian Yuan, Xin Feng, Yong-sheng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTannins, which are polyphenols present in several plant species, have been shown to exert several beneficial effects in livestock at specific levels. However, the optimal dosage for growth promotion in waterfowl like Pekin ducks remains unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the effects of dietary tannic acid (TA) supplementation on the performance of Pekin ducks. A total of 420 male Pekin ducks were assigned to to five groups and fed a basal diet supplemented with 0, 0.1, 0.2, 0.35, or 0.5% TA (control, TAS0.1, TAS0.2, TAS0.35, and TAS0.5, respectively). Growth performance, serum biochemical, oxidative parameters, immune parameters, and cecal microbiota were analyzed. Low-to-moderate TA levels (0.1–0.35%) increased the pancreatic index (P < 0.05) and enhanced systemic antioxidant capacity, as evidenced by elevated activities of glutathione peroxidase, catalase, and total antioxidant capacity. Notably,0.1% TA supplementation increased serum immunoglobulin (IgA, IgG, IgM) and complement (C3, C4) levels compared to the control (P< 0.05). Conversely, dietary supplementation with 0.5% TA significantly compromised feed efficiency, increasing the feed conversion ratio by 14.6% during the initial 14-day period (P < 0.01). ​​​Importantly, TA intervention induced a dose-responsive restructuring of the cecal microbiota, characterized by a significant decrease in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria (P< 0.05), a marked reduction in the potentially pathogenic genus Desulfovibrio (from 6.24% to 0.17–2.14%, P< 0.01), and a selective enrichment of beneficial taxa, including Succinispira andRuminococcus. Functional predictions indicated enhanced xenobiotic metabolism in the low-dose groups but stress-related dysregulation in the TAS0.5 group. Collectively, these results demonstrate that TA exhibits dose-dependent biphasic effects. Optimal inclusion levels (0.1–0.35%) enhance antioxidant capacity, immune function, and gut microbial symbiosis, whereas excessive supplementation (0.5%) induces metabolic dysregulation and microbiota dysbiosis. Overall, this study established a theoretical framework for strategically optimizing TA supplementation in poultry production systems to reconcile productivity and health outcomes.Keywords: Tannins, Pekin duck, Growth performance, Blood biochemistry, Gut microbiota

Introduction

Tannic acid (TA), a polyphenolic compound that is ubiquitous in the plant kingdom, has emerged as a promising phytogenic additive for animal nutrition owing to its multifunctional effects on growth performance, nutrient partitioning, and gastrointestinal homeostasis (Marchetti et al., 2024). Historically, TA has been viewed with caution due to its protein-binding affinity and potential antinutritional effects (Mueller‐Harvey, 2006). However, a growing body of evidence suggests that judicious dietary inclusion of TA can confer growth-promoting and microbiota-modulating benefits in poultry production systems (Xu et al., 2025). Previous studies on mammals have revealed that TA enhances antioxidant defense and organogenesis by modulating enteric enzyme activity and serum biochemical markers (Abiola et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2024). In contrast, TA supplementation reduced apparent protein digestibility, upregulated the levels of oxidative stress and inflammatory mediators, and maintained growth performance and ruminal microbial stability in Xiangdong black goats (Wang et al., 2022b).

Mechanistically, polyphenolic compounds like TA exhibit selective antimicrobial activity, preferentially inhibiting pathogenic bacteria while promoting beneficial microbiota, thereby improving nutrient assimilation and gut barrier integrity (Nagesh et al., 2020). Notably, the gastrointestinal ecosystem, which serves as the primary interface between dietary inputs and host physiology, harbors microbial consortia that critically influence nutrient metabolism, immune regulation, and colonization resistance (Hou et al., 2022). Although TA-enriched silver nanoparticles have demonstrated enhanced bactericidal activity against gram-negative pathogens (Do et al., 2011), the concentration-dependent effects of pure TA on the gut microbiome of waterfowl require further investigation. Moreover, the propensity of TA to form complexes with dietary proteins and digestive enzymes may perturb nitrogen homeostasis, potentially modifying clinically relevant biomarkers, including blood urea nitrogen and albumin concentrations (Zhou et al., 2019), which are key indicators of hepatic function and protein metabolism. Supra-nutritional doses may impair feed efficiency through appetite suppression and digestive compromise (Choi et al., 2022). In contrast, subtherapeutic concentrations appear to potentiate antioxidant enzyme systems and immune priming (Isa et al., 2020; Jiang et al., 2021). Notably, the causal relationships between TA-induced microbial shifts and systemic immune modulation, particularly between immunoglobulin dynamics and cytokine networks, remain poorly defined in avian models.

In Pekin ducks, a key commercial poultry species, the optimization of feed additives to enhance productivity without compromising health is of critical importance. However, the dose-response relationships governing the effects of TA on growth metrics, hematological profiles, immunological competence, and microbial ecology in this species remain poorly characterized. Despite accumulating evidence, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding the therapeutic window of TA in Pekin ducks.

Therefore, this study aimed to determine the dose-response relationship of dietary TA in Pekin ducks. We systematically evaluated the effects of graded TA concentrations on growth performance, serum biochemistry, immune status, and the cecal microbiome. The findings are intended to establish an evidence-based framework for the precise application of TA in poultry nutrition, identifying both the optimal dosage range and the potential risks of over-supplementation.

Materials and methods

The Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kunpeng Institute of Modern Agriculture at Foshan approved all procedures used in the study (Approval No: KIMA-2024-03-03).

Experimental design and diets

A total of 420 male Pekin ducks (one-day-old) with homogeneous body weight (BW) were randomly allocated to five groups (six replicates with 14 ducks per replicate): control (basal diet), TAS0.1 (0.1% TA), TAS0.2 (0.2% TA), TAS0.35 (0.35% TA), and TAS0.5 (0.5% TA) groups. The basal diet was formulated according to the China Agricultural Industry Standards (2012) (Table 1). The nutrient compositions of wheat flour, soybean, corn- distillers dried grains with solubles (DDGS), corn germ, corn gluten meal, and wheat bran were analyzed using near-infrared spectroscopy (Bruker Tang-R, Germany) at the time of procurement to determine their contents of crude protein (CP), crude fat, crude ash, calcium, total phosphorus, lysine, methionine, and threonine. TA was purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (T818845-2.5Kg, AR). TA and the premix were uniformly mixed and then added to the mixer to produce pelleted feed for feeding the ducks. The diameter of the feed particles was 2.0 mm.

Duck husbandry

Ducks were reared in cages (length × width × height, 90 cm × 80 cm × 60 cm). During the first week, all ducks were maintained at an ambient temperature of 33 ± 2°C, which was adjusted to 24 ± 2°C from day 8 to 21. Temperature and humidity were consistently regulated throughout the trial. Birds were exposed to 23L:1D from 0 to 7 d of age, followed by 16L:8D for the remainder of the experiment. Light intensity was set at 30 lux from 0 to 7 d of age, 10 lux from 8 to 14 d of age, and 5 lux from 15 to 21 d of age. Mortality and health status were monitored daily, and all ducks had ad libitum access to food and water.

Production performance and sampling

BW was recorded on days 1, 14, and 21 of the experiment. Residual feed was collected on days 14 and 21 to calculate the average daily feed intake (ADFI). Average daily gain (ADG) and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were determined for periods of 1–14 and 1–21 days. On the final day of the experiment, after 12 h of feed withdrawal, one duck per replicate (n = 6 per group) whose BW was closest to the cage average was selected for blood collection via jugular venipuncture.

A blood sample of 5 mL was obtained via the jugular vein, subsequently centrifuged at 3,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and stored at −80°C for serum biochemical index analysis. After electrical stunning and slaughter, the thymus, liver, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius were immediately excised and weighed. Cecal contents from both left and right ceca were aseptically collected into sterile cryovials, flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C for microbial diversity analysis.

Organ indices were calculated using the following formula: organ index (%) = (organ weight/BW) × 100.

Serum biochemical, antioxidant, and immunological parameters

Briefly, serum samples were diluted with physiological saline at a 1:20 ratio. Thereafter, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (γ-GT), total protein (TP), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), and acid phosphatase (ACP) levels were analyzed using a Hitachi 7600 automated biochemistry analyzer (Hitachi Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). Oxidative stress markers, including malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), and superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), catalase (CAT), xanthine oxidase (XOD), glutathione S-transferase (GSH-ST), and glutathione (GSH) activities, were quantified using commercial assay kits (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Serum immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), immunoglobulin M (IgM), complement component 3 (C3), and complement component 4 (C4) levels were quantified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and a Multiskan MK3 microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific). All assays were conducted in triplicate and followed established protocols.

DNA extraction and high-throughput sequencing analysis

Total genomic DNA was isolated from the samples using a modified CTAB/SDS protocol. DNA integrity and quantity were assessed using 0.8% agarose gel electrophoresis, followed by normalization to a 1 ng/μL working concentration with sterile water. Thereafter, the hypervariable V3–V4 regions of bacterial 16SrRNA genes were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using specific primers (0.2 μM each) in a 30-μL reaction system containing Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs) and 10 ng template DNA. The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98°C for 1 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 10 s, annealing at 50°C for 30 s, extension at 72°C for 30 s, and final elongation at 72°C for 5 min. Amplification efficacy was validated using SYBR Green-stained 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Sequencing libraries were constructed using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II DNA Library Prep Kit (E7645) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Quality assessment was performed using a Qubit® 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform, generating 250-bp paired-end reads.

Bioinformatics processing

Raw sequencing data were processed using FLASH (v1.2.11) for read merging and FASTp (v0.20.0) for quality trimming. Chimeric sequences were identified and removed using VSEARCH (v2.15.0) through reference-based alignment against the SILVA database. High-quality sequences were denoised using DADA2 module (2020.06 release) in QIIME2 to generate amplicon sequence variants (ASVs), with low-abundance ASVs (< 5 reads) excluded from subsequent analyses.

Alpha diversity indices (Chao1, Faith_pd, Shannon, Simpson, Goods_coverage, Pielou_e, and Observed_species) were computed using the QIIME2 software. Beta diversity was assessed using principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray-Curtis dissimilarity in the R (v2.15.3) packages ade4 and ggplot2. Taxonomic biomarkers were identified using LEfSe (v1.0) with a linear discriminant analysis (LDA) score> 4. Functional annotation of genes was performed using PICRUSt2 with the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes database.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 25.0 (IBM Corp.). Significant differences between groups were determined using one-way analysis of variance, followed by Duncan’s post-hoc test for multiple comparisons. Normality of data distribution and homoscedasticity were verified using the Shapiro-Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. Results

Growth performance and immune organ indices

Although not statistically significant (P > 0.05), BW was lower in the TAS0.5 group than in the control group at 21 days of age (Table 2). During the starter phase (Days 1–14), dietary supplementation with 0.1% TA (TAS0.1) improved feed efficiency, resulting in a significantly lower FCR compared to the control group (P < 0.01). Interestingly, ducks in the TAS0.5 group showed a significantly higher ADFI than those in the TAS0.1 and TAS0.35 groups (P < 0.05), suggesting a possible compensatory feeding response. Linear regression analysis revealed a dose-dependent impairment of feed efficiency (linear trend, P < 0.01) during the entire experimental period (Days 1–21), with the TAS0.5 group displaying a significantly higher FCR than the control group (P < 0.05). Ducks in the TAS0.1, TAS0.2, and TAS0.35 groups exhibited significantly elevated pancreatic indices compared with those in the control group (P < 0.05; Table 3).

Serum biochemical, antioxidant, and immunological parameters

Hepatic function biomarkers, including serum ALT(P = 0.05) and AST (P < 0.05) levels, were markedly downregulated in the TAS0.1 group. Similarly, serum AST levels were downregulated in the TAS0.2 and TAS0.35 groups compared with those in the control group (Table 4). Additionally, serum γ-GT activity as well as ALB and GLB levels were significantly lower in the TAS0.1 and TAS0.2 groups than in the control group (P < 0.05). Similarly, serum GLB levels were significantly downregulated in the TAS0.35 group (P < 0.05). ACP activity was suppressed in all TA-supplemented groups (P < 0.05), except for the TAS0.5 group. Antioxidant enzyme activity demonstrated dose-responsive patterns in response to TA supplementation. Specifically, TA supplementation at the four levels significantly enhanced GSH-PX activity (P < 0.01; Table 5,Table 6). Additionally, CAT and GSH-ST activities were significantly higher in the TAS0.1 and TAS0.2 groups than in the control group (P < 0.01). Moreover, GSH levels were significantly upregulated in the TAS0.1, TAS0.2, and TAS0.35 groups (P < 0.05). Notably, TA supplementation at 0.2 and 0.5% significantly improved T-AOC (P < 0.01). Importantly, TA supplementation improved the immunoglobulin profile of the birds. For instance, IgA, IgG, and IgM levels were significantly upregulated in the TAS0.1 group (P < 0.05); IgA and IgM levels were upregulated in the TAS0.2 group (P < 0.01); and IgM levels were significantly elevated in the TAS0.5 group (P < 0.01). However, no significant improvement was observed in IgM level in the TAS0.35 group. C3 levels were significantly upregulated in all TA-supplemented groups compared with those in the control group (P < 0.01).

Taxonomic composition of cecal microbiota

To analyze the cecal microbiota composition, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on digesta samples from all five groups. After data filtering, quality control, and removal of chimeric sequences, an average of 74,690 high-quality sequence tags per sample were obtained, with lengths ranging from 404 to 432 bp (average: 416 bp). Notably, the rarefaction curves confirmed adequate sequencing depth and operational taxonomic unit (OTU) coverage (Fig. 1A). In total, 18,081 amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were identified, of which 728 were common across the groups. Moreover, 4,221, 3,352, 3,602, 2,965, and 3,212 unique ASVs were identified in the control, TAS0.1, TAS0.2, TAS0.35, and TAS0.5 groups, respectively (Fig. 1B). PCoA using Bray-Curtis distances (PC1:12.40%, PC2:10.70%) revealed no distinct clustering by treatment group (Fig. 1C). Alpha diversity analysis revealed that the Simpson index, a measure of community evenness, was significantly lower in the TAS0.35 group compared to the control (P < 0.05; Fig. 2), indicating a shift in community structure. Microbial composition analysis demonstrated dose-dependent modulation of the cecal microbiota in 21-day-old Pekin ducks (Fig. 3, Table 7, Table 8). At the phylum level, the abundance of Proteobacteria was significantly reduced in all TA-supplemented groups (P < 0.05). Genus-level analysis revealed consistent suppression of Desulfovibrio across the supplementation groups compared with that in the control group (P < 0.01). Based on the LDA results from LEfSe analysis (threshold set at 4), this study revealed significant compositional differences in cecal microbiota between the control and TAS02 groups (Fig. 4). Importantly, the control group exhibited an enrichment of sulfate-reducing taxa, including the Desulfovibrio genus and its phylogenetically affiliated lineages Proteobacteria (phylum), Deltaproteobacteria (class), Desulfovibrionales (order), and Desulfovibrionaceae (family). In contrast, the TAS02 group showed a significant increase in the relative abundances of Succinispira and Succinispira mobilis. All differentially abundant taxa met the predefined LDA threshold (LDA > 4) and were statistically significant (P < 0.05).

Functional prediction of cecal microbiota

Fig. 5A illustrates the functional prediction of microbial communities based on the PICRUSt2 data. Taxonomic distribution analysis revealed intergroup differences in biological processes and system counts across six functional categories: Cellular Processes, Environmental Information Processing, Genetic Information Processing, Human Diseases, Metabolism, and Organismal Systems. Additionally, 11 subclasses were identified in the metabolic function category, including amino acid metabolism, glycan biosynthesis and metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, energy metabolism, biosynthesis of other secondary metabolites, lipid metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism, metabolism of cofactors and vitamins, metabolism of terpenoids and polyketides, and metabolism of different amino acids. The relative abundance of amino acid metabolism was significantly higher than that of other subclasses, followed by carbohydrate and lipid metabolism. Notably, xenobiotic biodegradation and metabolism exhibited prominent variations in abundance, forming a secondary high-abundance interval along with nucleotide metabolism. In contrast, energy metabolism and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins displayed relatively stable abundance distributions, whereas terpenoid and polyketide metabolism pathways and glycan biosynthesis and metabolism showed lower abundance. Intergroup comparisons highlighted the most significant differences in amino acid metabolism and xenobiotic biodegradation pathways. The carbohydrate and lipid metabolism pathways also demonstrated discernible fluctuations in abundance between the groups, whereas nucleotide metabolism exhibited consistent abundance across all groups.

PCoA of the functional profiles demonstrated that PC1 and PC2 accounted for 63.2% and 8.1% of the total variance, respectively (Fig. 5B). Although overlapping clustering patterns were observed among the control, TAS0.1, TAS0.2, TAS0.35, and TAS0.5 groups, subtle intergroup dispersion along PC1 suggested dose-dependent shifts in the predicted functional capacities, particularly in xenobiotic metabolism and amino acid biosynthesis pathways.

Discussion

Growth performance and immune organ indices

Although not statistically significant (P > 0.05), the reduced 21-day BW observed in the 0.5% TA supplementation group (TAS0.5) was consistent with previous findings of growth suppression in poultry administered high tannin doses owing to antinutritional effects (Choi et al., 2022). Notably, this effect may be ascribed to the antinutritional properties of elevated TA concentrations, which reduce feed intake, nutrient digestibility, and growth performance in monogastric animals through mechanisms such as protein-tannin complex formation and digestive enzyme inhibition (Choi and Kim, 2020). During the starter phase (Days 1–14), FCR was significantly improved in the TAS0.1 group compared with that in the control group (P < 0.01). Additionally, birds in the TAS0.5 group exhibited an elevated ADFI compared with those in the TAS0.1 and TAS0.35 groups (P < 0.05). This divergence suggests dose-dependent metabolic disruption. TA supplementation at lower concentrations (0.1%) may enhance intestinal barrier function by improving gut morphology (increased villus height and crypt depth), thereby regulating intestinal motility and nutrient absorption to optimize FCR (Wang et al., 2019). In contrast, higher doses (0.5%) likely impair nutrient absorption, triggering compensatory hyperphagia as animals attempt to meet their nutritional requirements (Özcan et al., 2017). Importantly, the linear increase in FCR across TA concentrations from days 1 to 21 (P < 0.01) indicated cumulative metabolic stress from prolonged tannin exposure, potentially mediated by altered energy allocation or upregulated detoxification pathways (Choi et al., 2022).

Serum biochemical, antioxidant, and immunological profiles

Elevated pancreatic indices in the TAS0.1, TAS0.2, and TAS0.35 groups (P < 0.05) suggested adaptive hypertrophy to counter tannin-induced enzyme suppression. Given that TA supplementation inhibits trypsin and amylase activity (Lou et al., 2018), glandular hyperplasia may be required to maintain digestive capacity. Hepatic biomarker analysis revealed subtle dose-dependent effects, as evidenced by TAS0.1-induced decrease in the levels of the hepatocellular injury markers ALT (P = 0.05) and AST (P < 0.01). Additionally, TA supplementation at 0.2 and 0.35% significantly reduced serum AST levels. These findings align with the hepatoprotective properties of tannins, which are potentially mediated by antioxidant-stabilized hepatocyte membranes (Chu et al., 2016; Li et al., 2020). Moreover, the decrease in γ-GT activity (P < 0.05) in the TAS0.1 and TAS0.2 groups indicate enhanced biliary function, as γ-GT serves as a cholestasis marker (Suzuki et al., 2020; Xing et al., 2022).

Reduced serum ALB and GLB levels in the TAS0.1 and TAS0.2 groups may reflect transient hepatic synthesis inhibition or accelerated proteolysis during tannin exposure (Sharma, 2022). In contrast, the absence of these effects in the high-dose groups (TAS0.35 and TAS0.5) suggests that threshold-dependent compensatory mechanisms preserve protein homeostasis (Zhang et al., 2025). Moreover, the suppressed ACP activity at TA concentrations of 0.1–0.35% (P < 0.05) confirmed reduced lysosomal stress, as ACP fluctuations are correlated with lysosomal function (Ashtari et al., 2016).

TA supplementation modulated antioxidant capacity in a dose-dependent manner. TA supplementation at all concentrations significantly enhanced GSH-PX activity (P < 0.01). Similarly, CAT and GSH-ST activities were significantly upregulated in the TAS0.1 and TAS0.2 groups (P < 0.01). Increased GSH levels at doses of 0.1–0.35% and elevated T-AOC in the TAS0.2 and TAS0.5 groups suggest that TA has a dual antioxidant role as a direct radical scavenger and Nrf2 pathway activator, enhancing intestinal antioxidant defenses (Wang et al., 2022a). Additionally, attenuated antioxidant responses (CAT and GSH-ST) in the TAS0.5 group indicated a biphasic hormetic relationship. Low to moderate doses activate adaptive redox pathways, whereas higher doses overwhelm cellular defenses, prioritizing detoxification over antioxidant synthesis (Oliveira de Veras et al., 2021; Li et al., 2020). This aligns with the biphasic dose-response paradigm, in which phytochemicals exert beneficial effects at low concentrations but become toxic at higher levels (Murakami, 2020; Mattson and Cheng, 2006).

Importantly, the dose-dependent effects of TA on immune parameters and cecal microbiota composition underscore its dual role as an immunomodulator and microbial community regulator (Xu et al., 2023). Additionally, the significant upregulation of immunoglobulins (IgA, IgG, and IgM) and C3 and C4 in the TAS0.1 group (P < 0.05) aligns with the known capacity of tannins to enhance humoral immunity via lymphocyte-B activation and cytokine-mediated antibody production (Feng et al., 2023). Complement proteins, which are critical for pathogen clearance and inflammatory regulation (Reis et al., 2019), further corroborate the immunostimulatory effects of low-dose TA. However, the absence of significant immune enhancement in the high-dose groups (TAS0.35 and TAS0.5) suggests a threshold effect potentially attributable to the antinutritional properties of tannins or pro-oxidative stress at elevated concentrations (Ojo, 2022; Labieniec, et al., 2003). Overall, this biphasic response underscores the importance of dose optimization in therapeutic applications.

Taxonomic composition and functional prediction of cecal microbiota

16S rRNA sequencing revealed nuanced dose-dependent shifts in cecal microbiota structure. Although no distinct clustering by treatment was observed in the PCoA (Bray-Curtis; PC1:12.40%, PC2:10.70%; Fig. 2C), the decrease in shared ASVs (728 core ASVs) and group-specific unique ASV counts (control: 4,221, TAS0.35: 2,965; Fig. 2B) indicated selective microbial modulation rather than a broad ecological disruption. These findings align with those of previous studies demonstrating phytochemical-driven microbial fine-tuning in poultry (Obianwuna et al., 2024). Alpha diversity, a key measure of community structure, was assessed using several indices (including Chao1, Dominance, Shannon, Simpson, Goods_coverage, and Pielou_e) to evaluate species richness and evenness (Wang et al., 2018). An elevated Chao1 index corresponds to greater species richness, whereas a higher Shannon index or a lower Simpson index indicates increased community diversity (Forbes et al., 2018). Compared to the Control, tannic acid supplementation significantly elevated the Simpson index in the TAS0.35 group, indicating higher microbial community diversity at the 0.35% supplementation level. It has been reported that dietary TA supplementation significantly reduces the cecal Simpson diversity index in poultry by selectively inhibiting over-proliferating dominant bacteria to vacate ecological niches and promote beneficial bacterial growth, thereby fostering a more even species distribution and a balanced community structure that enhances microbial diversity (Liu et al., 2023).Given that Desulfovibrio is implicated in hydrogen sulfide production and intestinal inflammation (Singh et al., 2023), the significant suppression of Proteobacteria (P < 0.05) and its sulfate-reducing genusDesulfovibrio (control: 6.24% vs. TAS0.5: 0.60%; P < 0.01) in all TA-supplemented groups suggests that TA has potent anti-inflammatory activity. Additionally, the enrichment of Succinispira (a butyrate producer) in the TAS0.2 group (LDA > 4, P < 0.05) underscores the ability of TA to promote taxa associated with mucosal health and metabolic functionality (Singh et al., 2022; Van-Wehle and Vital, 2024). Dual modulation, which involves suppressing pro-inflammatory pathways and enhancing gut barrier integrity, may synergistically underpin the immune benefits observed in the low-dose groups.

Unexpectedly, canonical anti-inflammatory genera, such as Faecalibacterium and Bifidobacterium, exhibited no significant changes (P > 0.05), suggesting that the effects of TA are taxon-specific. Moreover, the promotion of butyrogenic taxa (Succinispira and Butyricicoccus) may explain the improved gut integrity and IgA synthesis in the low-dose groups, as short-chain fatty acids regulate lymphocyte activity and mucosal immunity (Qu et al., 2023; Singh et al., 2022).

The immunomodulatory effects of 0.1–0.2% TA are likely due to synergistic interactions between microbial metabolites (butyrate) and direct host signaling. However, the reduced benefits at higher doses (0.35–0.5%) align with evidence that excessive tannins disrupt microbial equilibrium and induce oxidative stress (Huang et al., 2024a; Díaz et al., 2022). Future studies should investigate the longitudinal effects of TA on host-microbe metabolic crosstalk, particularly focusing on tannin-derived metabolites and their roles in sustaining immune-microbiota crosstalk.

Functional prediction analysis using PICRUSt2 demonstrated that TA supplementation induced significant shifts in microbial metabolic priorities (Fig. 5A), particularly in the metabolic category. Notably, amino acid metabolism emerged as the most prominently enriched pathway, consistent with microbial adaptation strategies for tannin-mediated nutrient sequestration. Plant polyphenols, including tannins, form complexes with dietary proteins and carbohydrates, necessitating the microbial upregulation of both hydrolytic enzymes and amino acid recycling pathways to circumvent nutrient limitations (Jakobek, 2015; Chubukov et al., 2014). The concurrent enrichment of xenobiotic biodegradation pathways across the TA groups (TAS0.1–TAS0.5) suggests a coordinated microbial detoxification response to tannin-derived compounds, corroborating observations in ruminant microbiomes exposed to phenolic-rich diets (Newbold and Ramos-Morales, 2020). Additionally, the observed upregulation of carbohydrate and lipid metabolism pathways may reflect compensatory energy-harvesting strategies under tannin stress. In contrast, the stability of nucleotide metabolism across groups implies preserved nucleic acid synthesis despite metabolic reconfiguration. These metabolic redistributions are likely mediated by TA-induced alterations in substrate availability and enzymatic stoichiometry, as evidenced by the increase in the expression of membrane transport system genes (Huang et al., 2024b). Specifically, the increased abundance of ABC transporter homologs supports the premise of microbial investment in nutrient-scavenging mechanisms to counter tannin-chelation effects (Do et al., 2021).

PCoA of the functional profiles demonstrated that PC1 accounted for 63.2% of the total variance (Fig. 5B), driven primarily by differences in energy metabolism and stress response pathways. Although overlapping clusters among groups suggest conserved core functions (genetic information processing and carbohydrate metabolism), the subtle dispersion of TAS0.5 samples along PC1 indicates dose-dependent shifts in niche metabolic processes, particularly in xenobiotic metabolism and amino acid biosynthesis. Similarly, low doses of phytochemicals enhance metabolic flexibility, whereas high doses activate stress-responsive pathways in broilers (Obianwuna et al., 2024). Notably, the similarity in amino acid metabolism across groups may reflect the microbial prioritization of protein turnover to counteract TA-induced protease inhibition, as reported in tannin-adapted herbivores (Barbehenn and Constabel, 2011).

Furthermore, the upregulation of signal transduction pathways in the TA groups likely corresponds to microbial sensing of tannin-derived metabolites, which activate bacterial two-component regulatory systems (Lazar and Tabor, 2021). These signaling cascades may prime microbial communities for stress tolerance and enhance their resilience to dietary perturbations (Liu et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2024). Conversely, the reduced capacity for xenobiotic biodegradation in the TAS0.5 group suggests metabolic overload (excessive TA exceeding microbial detoxification thresholds), leading to the accumulation of inhibitory metabolites. Such functional exhaustion parallels the "toxin treadmill" observed in antibiotic-exposed microbiomes, emphasizing the need for dose optimization (Kowallik et al., 2022).

Importantly, the modulation of amino acid metabolism aligns with the role of TA in the nitrogen cycle. Tannins may redirect microbial nitrogen utilization for de novo synthesis of essential amino acids by binding to dietary proteins, potentially compensating for host nutrient deficits under stress (Chandel, 2021). Moreover, enriched signal transduction pathways may facilitate microbial communication with host intestinal cells, influencing immune regulation via metabolites such as tryptophan-derived indoles (Fiore and Murray, 2021). Overall, these interactions may explain the elevated levels of immunoglobulins (IgA and IgG) in the low-dose groups, as microbial metabolites directly modulate B cell activation and antibody production (Kim et al., 2016).

The functional restructuring observed in this study underscores the dual role of TA as a metabolic modulator and stress inducer. Low doses (0.1–0.35%) enhance microbial detoxification and metabolic flexibility, fostering symbiosis that benefits host immunity and nutrient utilization. In contrast, high doses (0.5%) overwhelm microbial adaptive mechanisms and disrupt functional equilibrium. Future studies should integrate meta-transcriptomics to validate the predicted functions and elucidate the causal links between microbial metabolism and host phenotypes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence for the dose-dependent, biphasic effects of tannic acid in Pekin ducks. Low-to-moderate doses (0.1–0.35%) significantly enhanced antioxidant capacity and immune function by improving pancreatic development, elevating antioxidant enzyme activities (GSH-PX, CAT, and T-AOC), and increasing immunoglobulin levels (IgA, IgG, and IgM). Additionally, these doses optimized the gut microbiota composition by enriching beneficial genera (Ruminococcus and Butyricicoccus), suppressing sulfate-reducing pathogens (Desulfovibrio), and activating microbial metabolic pathways (xenobiotic degradation and amino acid biosynthesis). In contrast, high-dose TA (0.5%) induced antinutritional effects, as evidenced by a 14.6% increase in FCR, disruption of microbial homeostasis (aberrant Proteobacteria abundance), and diminished antioxidant benefits, indicating metabolic imbalance and oxidative stress. Overall, these findings confirm the biphasic regulatory properties of TA, with optimal doses (0.1–0.35%) improving production performance by balancing gut health, redox status, and immune responses. In contrast, excessive intake triggers enzyme inhibition and induces oxidative overload. Based on these results, we recommend dietary inclusion of TA at 0.1–0.35% as an effective strategy to enhance antioxidant capacity, immune function, and gut health in Pekin duck production. Conversely, levels exceeding 0.5% should be avoided due to risks of metabolic stress and impaired performance.
