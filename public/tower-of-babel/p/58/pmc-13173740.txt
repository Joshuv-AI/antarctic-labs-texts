SPARC upregulation mediates podocyte injury in Alport syndrome mice

Background

Alport syndrome (AS) is a childhood-onset hereditary disorder that constitutes the second most common hereditary kidney disease after polycystic kidney disease, caused by pathogenic variants in the COL4A3, COL4A4, and COL4A5 genes, which encode alpha 3–5 chains of type IV collagen [1]. AS is characterized by hematuria, proteinuria, and progressive kidney failure. The characteristic ultrastructural abnormality of AS is thinning, splitting, and a basket-weave appearance of the glomerular basement membrane (GBM) [2]. Podocytes, terminally differentiated cells with limited regenerative capacity, play a critical role in maintaining the glomerular filtration barrier function [3]. Podocyte injury, characterized by foot process effacement, apoptosis, and detachment from the GBM, is a central pathogenic mechanism in the development and progression of various kidney diseases, including AS [4]. Our previous study showed that an elevated level of podocin mRNA in the urine is direct evidence of podocyte injury and disease progression in AS [5]. Although renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS) blockers are recommended to be used to delay the progression to end-stage kidney disease in AS [6], the risk of symptomatic hypotension and hyperkaliemia limits the use of RAAS blockers. There is an urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies that specifically target the underlying molecular mechanisms of AS, and for identifying new biomarkers capable of predicting disease progression and individuals at high risk for rapid disease progression.

Secreted protein acidic and rich in cysteine (SPARC), also known as osteonectin or BM-40, is a matricellular protein that modulates cell proliferation, migration, tissue remodeling, and inflammation [7]. Previous studies have shown that SPARC is upregulated in several kidney diseases, including diabetic kidney disease, crescentic glomerulonephritis, and fibrotic chronic kidney disease, where it has been linked to extracellular matrix remodeling, inflammation, and tissue injury [8, 9]. In proteinuria kidney diseases, podocyte injury is a central event driving glomerular dysfunction [5]. However, the expression pattern of SPARC in podocytes across kidney diseases remains incompletely characterized, and its role in AS-associated podocyte injury is still unknown.

Adhesion G protein-coupled receptor B1 (ADGRB1), also known as brain-specific angiogenesis inhibitor 1 (BAI1), an adhesion G protein-coupled receptor involved in cell-matrix interaction, phagocytosis, and inflammatory regulation [10]. Although ADGRB1 has been studied mainly in the nervous system, tumor biology, and innate immunity, its expression and potential function in the kidney have been poorly investigated. In the present study, transcriptomic analysis identified Adgrb1 as one of the most significantly upregulated genes in Sparc-overexpressing podocytes, and we further detected increased kidney ADGRB1 expression in AS mice.

This study aimed to determine whether SPARC is upregulated in AS-associated podocyte injury and to explore whether ADGRB1 participates in SPARC related podocyte responses. We further analyzed transcriptomic data to identify candidate downstream signaling pathways and evaluated the clinical relevance of SPARC in samples from pediatric patients with AS.

Methods

AS mouse model

All experimental procedures were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (Animal Ethics Committee Approval Number: J2023102). Animal care and interventions were undertaken in accordance with the National Institute of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Experimental Animals. The study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. Col4a3 knockout (Col4a3−/−) mice are in a C57BL6/J background and were kindly provided by Sanofi-Genzyme (C57BL6/J-Col4a3tm1Dec). Col4a3−/− mice were housed in the specific pathogen-free environment of the laboratory animal facility (12 h light/dark cycle and room temperature at 25℃). The mice had ad libitum access to food and water. Urine, plasma, and kidney tissues were collected from the mice at 5, 8, 13, and 18 weeks for subsequent analyses. Mice were anesthetized with 0.5% pentobarbital sodium (50–60 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection, a whole blood sample was collected by cardiac puncture and plasma was separated by centrifugation at 2,500 rpm for 10 min at 4℃, and metabolic cages collected urinary samples.

Urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio

Urine albumin content was measured using a Mouse Albumin Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) Kit (Bethly, E99-134). The urine creatinine was assessed by QuantiChrom™ Creatinine Assay Kit (DICT-500, BioAssay Systems). Urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) values were calculated and expressed as µg albumin per mg creatinine.

Quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from the samples using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, United States). RNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (ND-1000 spectrophotometer, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, United States). Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from 2 µg of total RNA using FastKing RT Enzyme (KR118, TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China) for reverse transcription. Real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) reagents were prepared using the SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (FP209, TIANGEN Biotech, Beijing, China), and qPCR was performed on an ABI Vii7 System. Results were normalized to glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (Gapdh) and analyzed by the 2−ΔΔCt method. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table S1.

Western blot

Kidney proteins were extracted in RIPA buffer containing inhibitors. Then, 30 µg protein was separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to PVDF membranes, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA). Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies: SPARC (1:1000, Proteintech, 15274-1-AP), ADGRB1 (1:1000, Abcam, ab135907), Podocin (1:1000, Abcam, ab50339), Nephrin (1:1000, ProSci, 2265), p-PI3K (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, 17366 S), PI3K (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4292 S), p-AKT (1:2000, Cell Signaling Technology, 4060 S), AKT (1:2000, Proteintech, 10176-2-AP) and β-actin (1:5000, Proteintech, 20536-1-AP) overnight at 4℃, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37℃. Bands were detected via enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, United States), with β-actin as a loading control.

Immunohistochemistry

Kidney tissues were fixed for 48 h at 25℃ in 10% neutral buffered formalin, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 µm. For immunohistochemistry (IHC), kidney sections were dewaxed and rehydrated, and antigens were retrieved by autoclaving in citric acid buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked by incubation with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, and the tissue sections were washed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Non-specific binding was blocked with 1% BSA for 1 h at 25℃. Tissue sections were, respectively, incubated with ADGRB1 (1:100, Abcam, ab135907), goat-anti-mouse SPARC (1:200, R&D systems, AF942), rabbit-anti-human SPARC (1:100, Abcam, ab203284), overnight at 4℃. After washing thrice with PBS, the tissue sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated donkey-anti-goat and donkey-anti-rabbit secondary antibody (Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China) for 20 min at 37℃, respectively, then stained with a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) kit (Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China) and visualized under a Leica microscope (Germany). Image Pro Plus (IPP) version 6.0 was used to assess the location and intensity of the positive area.

Masson trichrome staining

Paraffin-embedded kidney sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained using a Masson trichrome staining kit (Beyotime, C0189S) according to the manufacturer’s instructions and visualized under a Leica microscope (Germany).

Immunofluorescence (IF)

For IF staining, kidney sections were dewaxed, rehydrated, washed thrice with PBS, and blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 1 h at 25℃. Sections were labeled with rabbit-anti-mouse Podocin (1:5000, Abcam, ab50339), rabbit-anti-mouse Wilm’s tumor gene 1 (WT-1) (1:200, Abcam, ab89901) and goat-anti-mouse SPARC primary antibody (1:200, R&D systems, AF942) overnight at 4℃. After washing, the sections were incubated with donkey-anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 594 (1:500, Invitrogen, A21207), donkey-anti-goat IgG (H + L) cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 488 (1:200, Invitrogen, A11055), and donkey-anti-mouse IgG (H + L) highly cross-adsorbed secondary antibody, Alexa Fluor™ 594 (1:500, Invitrogen, A21203) for 1 h at 25℃. The sections were mounted on cover slips in mounting medium containing 4’, 6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Zhongshan Golden Bridge Biotechnology, Beijing, China). The images were captured using a confocal microscope (Zeiss 780, Germany). For additional co-immunofluorescence analyses, antibodies against PDGFRβ (1:100, Abcam, ab69506) and CD34 (1:100, Abcam, ab81289) were used to assess the relationship of SPARC signal with mesangial and endothelial markers in glomeruli (Supplementary Figure S1).

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

For TEM samples, the samples were fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4℃ overnight and washed thrice in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, then fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 2 h, and dehydrated in 30, 50, 70, 80, 90, and 100% acetone solution. Samples were cut into 50 nm sections using an ultrathin microtome (Leica EM UC7, Wetzlar, Germany), contrasted with 2% uranyl acetate for 10 min and lead citrate for 5 min, and observed using TEM (JEM-1400, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan).

Mouse podocytes clone-5 cells culture and treatment

Mouse podocyte clone-5 cells (MPC5) were purchased from iCell Bioscience Incorporation (Shanghai, China) and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37℃ in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator. Sparc-overexpression was achieved via transfection with pcDNA3.1-SPARC using the Starvio reagent (Shanghai Starvio Biotechnology Company Limited, T21004). For Adgrb1 knockdown, siAdgrb1 were transfected (sequences were as follows: siAdgrb1-1, 5’-GGUCCGUUAUCCUCAUCAAUUdTdT-3,’ siAdgrb1-2, 5’- GCGACACCAAGACAUGUUCCA-3,’ siAdgrb1-3, 5’-GGACGGCAUCACGGACAAGAA-3’). As expected for transient transfection, plasmid delivery was not observed in every cell; thus, fluorescence microscopy together with increased mRNA and protein expression supported successful transfection.

Immunofluorescence staining of MPC5 on Transwell PET membranes

The polyethylene terephthalate (PET) membranes were carefully removed from the Transwell inserts and rinsed three times with PBS. The membranes were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min at room temperature. After fixation, MPC5 on the membranes were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min and washed three times with PBS. Nonspecific binding was blocked with 3% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. The samples were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with a primary antibody against SPARC (AF942, 1:50). After washing, the membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated donkey anti-goat secondary antibody (1:500) together with rhodamine phalloidin (1:1,000) to visualize F-actin. The membranes were subsequently mounted using DAPI-containing mounting medium (ZLI-9557, ZSGB-BIO, China) and covered with coverslips. Fluorescence images were acquired using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 780, Germany).

Fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-dextran assay

A stock solution of FITC-dextran (4 kDa) at a concentration of 25 mg/ml (Chondrex, Cat. no. 4013) was diluted to 1 mg/ml with DMEM and subsequently added to the upper chamber of the Transwell insert. Following incubation for 2 h at 37℃, the medium from the lower chamber was collected and diluted at a ratio of 1:5 with PBS for quantification of fluorescence intensity at an excitation wavelength of 488 nm using a Synergy H1 microplate reader (Gene Company Limited, Hong Kong, China). DMEM without FITC-dextran was used as the baseline control for fluorescence detection. FITC-dextran assay in this study reflects permeability changes across a cultured podocyte monolayer.

Cell migration assay

MPC5 were plated in 6-well plates. Each well was then scratched with a sterile 200 µl pipette tip, washed with PBS, and replenished with fresh medium. Images were captured at 0 h by using an inverted microscope connected to a DP72 digital camera (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The cells were immediately transfected with Sparc-overexpression plasmid with or without siAdgrb1, and images were collected again after 24 h. The images were analyzed using the ImageJ (NIH, Bethesda, MD, United States) software to quantify cell migration (expressed as a percentage of the migration area covered).

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

Total RNA from treated MPC5 was extracted using TRIzol reagent. Purity, concentration (NanoDrop 2000), and integrity (Agilent 2100/4200) were assessed before library preparation: mRNA was purified via polyT, fragmented (300–350 bp), and reverse-transcribed to cDNA (first/second strands). After end repair, adenylation, and adaptor ligation, the libraries were enriched by PCR and validated (Qubit qPCR for concentration; agarose gel for size distribution). Sequencing was performed by Berry Genomics (Beijing, China) using an NGS platform. Raw reads were filtered to remove low-quality reads (≥ 3 Ns, adaptor contamination, or ≥ 20% bases with Phred quality ≤ 5), then mapped to the Silva database (Bowtie2) to exclude rRNA. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (HISAT2), and gene counts were generated (FeatureCount). Differential expression was analyzed using EdgeR (DEGs: |log2FoldChange|>2, adjusted P < 0.05, Benjamini-Hochberg correction). KEGG enrichment analysis was performed using the KOBAS software [21–23].

Patients

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital (Approval No.: (2020[72]), 1179) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians before sample collection. Patients diagnosed with AS between 2020 and 2022 in the Department of Pediatrics were enrolled in this study. A total of 27 urine samples from male pediatric patients with AS were included in this study, with an age range of 4–12 years. Urine samples were collected from volunteers to use as controls. Kidney tissues on the opposite side of the resected kidney tumors from patients with kidney cancer were collected as kidney controls.

Level of SPARC by ELISA

The urinary levels of SPARC in patients with AS and the plasma and urinary levels of SPARC in AS mice were quantified using ELISA. Urine supernatant samples were collected and analyzed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. ELISA was performed using a commercial kit (Fantaibio, FT-P31584R) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, samples and standards were added to the wells and incubated as instructed, followed by washing and incubation with the detection antibody. After substrate development, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Concentrations were calculated based on the standard curve. The sample to be tested was diluted and standardized with sample diluent at a ratio of 1:2, 100 µl added to each well, incubated at 37℃ for 1.5 h, and read under the microplate reader at a wavelength of 450 nm.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± SEM. Comparisons among groups were made using the t-test or one-way ANOVA. GraphPad Prism version 8.3.0 (San Diego, CA, United States) and the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) (version 24.0; IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, United States) were used for statistical analysis. P < 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

AS mice develop progressive albuminuria and podocyte injury

Col4a3−/− mice are a well-established model of AS. To evaluate disease progression, urine samples were collected from wild-type (WT) and AS mice at 5, 8, 13, and 18 weeks of age, and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) was measured. The uACR of AS mice was significantly higher than that of WT mice from 5 weeks of age onward (Fig. 1a). Transmission electron microscopy of renal cortical tissue from 18-week-old AS mice revealed irregular glomerular basement membrane thickness and diffuse foot process effacement (Fig. 1b). In addition, immunofluorescence staining for podocin showed a continuous pattern in WT mice kidneys, whereas in AS mice the staining became discontinuous and diffusely distributed at 13 and 18 weeks (Fig. 1c). Immunofluorescence staining for WT-1 (used as podocyte marker) further demonstrated a significantly reduced number of podocytes in glomeruli from 18-week-old AS mice compared with WT controls (Fig. 1d, e). Together, these findings indicate progressive podocyte injury in Col4a3−/− mice.

SPARC is upregulated in AS kidneys and enriched in podocyte-associated regions

To determine whether SPARC is involved in AS-associated kidney injury, we examined its expression in kidneys from WT and Col4a3−/− mice at different ages. qPCR analysis showed that Sparc mRNA expression was significantly increased in AS kidneys at 8, 13, and 18 weeks compared with age-matched WT controls (Fig. 2a), and its expression level was positively correlated with uACR (Fig. 2b). In addition, with increasing age, kidney fibrosis became evident in AS mice, as shown by Masson trichrome staining at 18 weeks (Fig. 2c). Immunohistochemical staining for SPARC showed that, at both 8 and 18 weeks, SPARC expression in kidney tissue was predominantly localized to the glomerular region. Notably, in 18-week-old AS mice, SPARC expression was further increased in areas with evident kidney fibrosis (Fig. 2d). Semiquantitative analysis demonstrated that the mean optical density of SPARC-positive staining in the kidneys of 8- and 18-week-old AS mice was significantly higher than that in age-matched WT mice (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, immunofluorescence co-staining of SPARC with WT-1 supported enrichment of SPARC in podocyte-associated regions (Fig. 2f). Additional co-immunofluorescence staining with PDGFRβ and CD34 further showed less overlap of SPARC with mesangial and endothelial markers, respectively (Supplementary Figure S1). These findings indicate that SPARC is upregulated in AS kidneys and is closely associated with podocyte injury.

Kidney and urinary SPARC levels increase with disease progression in AS mice

Western blot analysis of kidney tissues showed that SPARC protein expression progressively increased in AS mice with age, whereas nephrin expression gradually decreased (Fig. 3a, b). In parallel, ELISA demonstrated that urinary SPARC/Cr and plasma SPARC levels were significantly higher in AS mice than in WT mice at 8, 13, and 18 weeks, with a progressive increase over time (Fig. 3c, d). These results further support an association between increasing SPARC expression and disease progression in AS mice.

Sparc-overexpression induces inflammatory and injury-related changes in cultured podocytes

To investigate the biological effects of SPARC in podocytes, MPC5 were transiently transfected with a Sparc-overexpression plasmid. As expected for transient transfection, transfection efficiency was not 100%; however, fluorescence microscopy demonstrated successful plasmid delivery in a substantial proportion of cells, which was further supported by increased SPARC mRNA and protein expression (Fig. 4a, b). Meanwhile, the mRNA expression levels of Tnfa, Il1b, Il6, and Ccl2 were significantly increased in the Sparcoe group compared with the vector group (Fig. 4c). In addition, F-actin staining showed marked cytoskeletal disorganization in Sparc-overexpressing MPC5 (Fig. 4d). Together, these findings suggest that Sparc-overexpression induces inflammatory and injury-related changes in cultured podocytes.

Transcriptomic analysis identifies Adgrb1 as a candidate mediator associated with SPARC induced podocyte injury

To identify candidate mediators involved in SPARC-associated podocyte injury, we analyzed the RNA-seq data from Vector and Sparc-overexpressing MPC5. Among the significantly upregulated genes, Adgrb1 showed one of the most prominent increases in the Sparcoe group (Fig. 5a). qPCR validation confirmed that Adgrb1 mRNA expression was significantly higher in Sparc-overexpressing MPC5 than in Vector transfected cells (Fig. 5b). Consistent with the in vitro findings, kidney Adgrb1 mRNA expression was also significantly increased in AS mice at 8, 13, and 18 weeks of age compared with WT controls (Fig. 5c). In addition, western blot analysis of kidney tissues from 18-week-old mice showed elevated ADGRB1 protein expression in AS mice compared with WT controls (Fig. 5d, e). These findings identify ADGRB1 as a candidate mediator associated with SPARC-induced podocyte injury.

Adgrb1 knockdown partially attenuates SPARC-induced podocyte injury in vitro

To further explore the role of ADGRB1 in SPARC-associated podocyte injury, Adgrb1 was silenced in MPC5 using small interfering RNA (siRNA), and knockdown efficiency was confirmed at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 6a). Compared with the Sparcoe group, co-transfection of Sparcoe with siAdgrb1 significantly reduced the expression of inflammatory mediators, including Il1b, Il6, Tnfa, and Ccl2 (Fig. 6b). In the wound-healing assay, Sparc-overexpression increased podocyte migration, whereas this effect was significantly attenuated by Adgrb1 knockdown (Fig. 6c, d). In addition, Adgrb1 knockdown reduced FITC-dextran flux across the cultured podocyte monolayer compared with the Sparcoe group (Fig. 6e). Western blot analysis further showed that nephrin and podocin expression was partially restored after Adgrb1 knockdown (Fig. 6f, g). Together, these findings suggest that ADGRB1 may participate in SPARC-induced podocyte injury, and that Adgrb1 knockdown partially attenuates this injury phenotype in vitro.

Transcriptomic findings suggest possible involvement of PI3K/AKT-related signaling

To further explore potential downstream signaling pathways, we analyzed the transcriptomic data obtained from Sparc-overexpressing podocytes. KEGG pathway enrichment analysis suggested that PI3K/AKT signaling pathway may be involved in SPARC-associated podocyte responses (Fig. 7a). Furthermore, Western blot analysis showed that Sparc-overexpression in MPC5 was associated with increased phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT compared with the Vector group, whereas Adgrb1 knockdown partially reduced the activation of this signaling pathway (Fig. 7b, c).

SPARC is increased in patients with AS, with a distribution pattern distinct from that in mice

To assess the clinical relevance of our findings, we further examined SPARC expression in samples from patients with AS. Immunohistochemical analysis showed increased SPARC staining in kidney biopsy section from pediatric patient with AS compared with adjacent normal kidney (Fig. 8a). Notably, unlike the predominantly podocyte-associated pattern observed in AS mice, SPARC staining in human AS kidney sample appeared more diffuse and was also present in tubular compartments. These results indicate that, although SPARC was increased in both AS mice and patients, its intrarenal distribution pattern differed between the two settings.

In addition, we performed immunohistochemical staining for ADGRB1 in kidney biopsy sections from pediatric patients with AS. ADGRB1 immunostaining was detected in kidney biopsy sections from pediatric patients with AS (Fig. 8a), indicating that ADGRB1 is expressed in human AS kidney tissue. As this analysis was intended primarily to assess localization, semiquantitative scoring of ADGRB1 staining was not performed.

Furthermore, urinary SPARC/Cr levels were significantly higher in pediatric patients with AS than in controls (Fig. 8b). Taken together, these findings support the clinical relevance of SPARC upregulation in AS while suggesting that its renal distribution may be context-dependent.

Discussion

In this study, we found that SPARC expression was increased in both AS mouse kidneys and samples from pediatric patients with AS. In Col4a3−/− mice, SPARC was enriched in podocyte-associated regions and increased with disease progression, whereas in human AS kidney samples, SPARC staining was also positive expression but showed a different intrarenal distribution pattern. In cultured podocytes, Sparc-overexpression induced inflammatory activation, cytoskeletal disorganization, and increased permeability across the podocyte monolayer. We further identified ADGRB1 as a candidate mediator associated with these changes, as Adgrb1 was markedly upregulated in Sparc-overexpressing podocytes and its knockdown partially attenuated the injury phenotype in vitro. In addition, phosphorylation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway was enhanced in Sparc-overexpressing podocytes, and this effect was suppressed by Adgrb1 knockdown, suggesting that PI3K/AKT may function downstream of ADGRB1 activation. Together, these findings suggest that SPARC is associated with podocyte injury in AS, and that the ADGRB1-PI3K/AKT axis may participate in SPARC-related podocyte damage.

Previous studies on SPARC in kidney disease have primarily focused on its role in fibrosis, extracellular matrix remodeling, and chronic tissue injury [9, 11]. SPARC has been reported to promote type I collagen deposition and extracellular matrix accumulation, two key pathological processes associated with disease progression [12, 13]. In diabetic kidney disease, SPARC deficiency has been shown to attenuate kidney fibrosis in experimental models [14]. In patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD), increased SPARC expression has been observed in kidney biopsy tissues, and circulating SPARC levels were positively associated with the severity of fibrotic kidney lesions [11]. Moreover, combining serum and urinary SPARC levels with estimated glomerular filtration rate improved the predictive value for renal fibrosis during CKD progression [11].

By comparison, much less is known about the role of SPARC in podocyte injury. Podocytes are terminally differentiated epithelial cells that are essential for maintaining the integrity of the glomerular filtration barrier, and their injury is a central event in the development of albuminuria and progressive glomerular disease [15–18]. Podocyte damage contributes to minimal change disease, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and other podocytopathies, and is closely linked to proteinuria [5, 19, 20]. In the present study, Sparc-overexpression in MPC5 induced inflammatory activation, cytoskeletal disorganization, and increased permeability across the podocyte monolayer. Together, these findings suggest that SPARC upregulation is associated with podocyte injury in AS.

ADGRB1 has been implicated in cell-matrix interaction, phagocytosis, and inflammatory regulation in non-renal systems, but its expression and function in the kidney have been poorly characterized [10]. In the present study, transcriptomic analysis identified Adgrb1 as a markedly upregulated gene in Sparc-overexpressing podocytes, and functional knockdown experiments suggested that ADGRB1 may participate in SPARC-associated podocyte injury. Because SPARC is not a classical DNA-binding protein, its upregulation of ADGRB1 is more likely mediated through intracellular signal transduction and downstream transcriptional regulators. Given that SPARC overexpression exerts a pro-inflammatory effect, we hypothesize that, in AS, SPARC may activate specific inflammation- or stress-related transcription factors, which then bind to the regulatory region of ADGRB1, thereby driving its aberrant transcriptional upregulation and contributing to podocyte injury. This potential mechanism requires further investigation.

The increase in urinary SPARC observed in patients with AS supports the potential clinical relevance of our findings and suggests that SPARC may serve as a candidate noninvasive biomarker. Interestingly, SPARC immunostaining differed in its intrarenal distribution between AS mice and human biopsy samples. This difference may be related to species-specific factors, variation in disease stage, chronic injury and fibrosis burden, or the characteristics of biopsy sample. In addition, technical factors such as antibody performance or section variability cannot be excluded. Therefore, SPARC expression in AS may be context-dependent and may not follow a uniform spatial pattern across experimental models and human disease.

It is worth noting the limitations of this study. First, although we showed that ADGRB1 was upregulated in Sparc-overexpressing MPC5 and that Adgrb1 knockdown partially rescued the injury phenotype, we did not determine how SPARC regulates ADGRB1 expression or activation. Therefore, the mechanism linking SPARC to ADGRB1 remains unresolved. Second, our functional analyses were mainly performed in cultured podocytes, and in vivo genetic or pharmacological studies are still needed to confirm the role of the SPARC-ADGRB1 axis in AS. Third, loss-of-function studies targeting SPARC itself in cultured podocytes would also help clarify whether SPARC is sufficient and necessary for the observed injury phenotype. Fourth, future studies are needed to explore the potential mechanisms underlying the increased SPARC expression in podocytes of AS mice. In addition, localization of slit diaphragm markers by immunofluorescence was not assessed in cultured podocytes, which limited evaluation of structural alterations at the slit diaphragm level. Finally, the number of patient samples was limited, and the different intrarenal distribution patterns of SPARC observed between AS mice and human samples require further investigation in larger, well-characterized cohorts.

Conclusion

In summary, SPARC is upregulated in AS kidneys and is associated with podocyte injury. In cultured podocytes, Sparc-overexpression induces inflammatory and injury-related changes, while Adgrb1 knockdown partially attenuates these effects, suggesting that ADGRB1 may participate in SPARC-associated podocyte responses. Elevated urinary SPARC in pediatric patients with AS supports its potential as a candidate biomarker. Further studies are needed to clarify the molecular mechanism linking SPARC to ADGRB1 and to investigate the upstream signaling molecules responsible for SPARC upregulation in AS.
