Malignant vs. Non-malignant Annotations on TCGA Breast Cancer Whole Slide Images for AI Analysis
Authors: Zahra Alidousti Shahraki, Otto Jokelainen, Mari Valkonen, Päivi Auvinen, Arto Mannermaa, Hamid Behravan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Identification of malignant and non-malignant regions in breast cancer whole slide images (WSIs) is essential for understanding tumor heterogeneity and histopathological evaluation. Annotated data allows deep learning models to learn features and analyze histological structures. In this study, we obtained 50 breast cancer WSIs from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and had an expert pathologist manually annotate malignant (n = 1,882) and non-malignant (n = 374) regions using QuPath. The annotated regions were independently reviewed by a second pathologist, achieving an inter-observer agreement of 99.95%. In addition, to assess annotations’ quality, we trained a hybrid contrastive-supervised machine learning pipeline for patch-level malignant vs. non-malignant classification. The model achieved a high F1-score of 0.90 indicating that our annotations are comparable in quality to those presented in public datasets. The proposed dataset provides expert-quality annotations and a unique resource for benchmarking and developing AI models for breast cancer histopathology.

Background & Summary

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained WSIs are the clinical standard for visualizing tissue morphology, offering rich structural detail at low cost1. However, accurately distinguishing malignant from non-malignant regions in these images remains challenging because H&E lacks molecular specificity and does not directly indicate malignancy2. This task therefore relies heavily on the nuanced expertise of pathologists, making it time-consuming and subjective. Artificial intelligence (AI) has emerged as a promising solution to assist in this process, but its success depends on access to high-quality, region-level annotations for training and validation. Currently, such curated datasets are scarce, underscoring the need for expert-annotated resources to enable the development of robust and reliable AI models for such tasks.

Several public histopathology datasets already exist, which widely vary in terms of cancer type, staining protocols, annotation details, and expert validation. For instance, CAMELYON163 and CAMELYON174 provide pixel-level metastasis annotations for lymph nodes in breast cancer, whereas the PANDA dataset offers prostate cancer WSIs labelled at the slide level5. TCGA-BRCA includes thousands of WSIs across various types of cancer but lacks consistent region-level annotation6. DigestPath dataset7 provides 600 H&E-stained colorectal cancer WSIs with region-level annotations. In addition, the PanoptiLS dataset8 provides high-quality, pixel-level and region-level annotations across TCGA slides and serves as a widely used benchmark for segmentation and classification studies. The TCGA-BRCA cohort includes approximately 800 WSIs with annotations that broadly separate tumor from non-tumor regions by drawing a boundary around the entire tumor mass9. While this approach distinguishes tumor tissue from surrounding area (non-tumor), it does not capture the heterogeneity within the tumor region, which often contains a mixture of malignant epithelium, stroma, adipose tissue, necrotic debris, and other non-malignant components. Such coarse annotations limit the ability of AI models to learn fine-grained morphological features critical for accurate malignancy detection. To address this gap, we developed BRCA-QuPath-Annot, a dataset derived from TCGA-BRCA9 WSIs, where an expert pathologist manually annotated malignant and non-malignant regions at a more granular level using QuPath (version 0.4.4)10. This resource provides the nuanced region-level annotations necessary for training and benchmarking advanced AI algorithms in breast cancer histopathology.

Methods

A hybrid contrastive-supervised learning pipeline

A graphical overview of the proposed pipeline for patch-level classification task is shown on Fig. 1. A contrastive learning framework11 was adopted to extract high-level feature embeddings from histopathological image patches for differentiating between malignant and non-malignant samples in the BRCA-QuPath-Annot dataset12. The architecture is based on a ResNet-50 backbone used as a feature encoder followed by an Adjustment Module consisting of a 3 × 3 convolution with stride 1 and no padding, which helps preserve spatial information. The combination of these components is referred to as the Ad_REd module. This module processes input patches of size 224 × 224 pixels and outputs 2048-dimensional feature vectors, called h-vectors, which capture semantic tissue information for downstream tasks.Fig. 1Flowchart of the learning framework for patch-level classification in H&E-stained WSIs. (Step 2) Use an Ad_Res module to extract features (h,h′) and project them into an embedding space (z, z′, …) via contrastive learning, minimizing the contrastive loss. (Step 3) Train a classifier network to distinguish malignant from non-malignant patches using the learned representations from augmented patches. (Step 4) Evaluate the trained model on the test set. These h-vectors are further projected into a 1000-dimensional latent space through a projection head, producing z-vectors. The z-vectors are optimized using a contrastive loss function13 and serve as feature representations within the framework. In this study, the h-vectors are employed to train a multi-layer classifier composed of four fully connected layers (2048 → 1024 → 512 → 256 → 1) with batch normalization, ReLU activation, dropout (rate = 0.5) between the layers, and a sigmoid activation function at the output layer for binary classification. The classifier’s role is to map the learned representations to malignant or non-malignant classes in the BRCA-QuPath-Annot dataset12 and tumor or non-tumor classes in the public TCGA-BRCA datasets.

The model is optimized end-to-end using a combined objective comprising a contrastive loss13 that encourages similarity between positive pairs and dissimilarity with negatives, and a binary classification loss. To improve robustness to staining and morphological variations within WSIs and to construct positive pairs for contrastive learning, we applied a set of data augmentations, including color jittering (brightness ± 0.2, contrast ± 0.2, saturation ± 0.2, and hue ± 0.05), random rotations between −15° and + 15°, Gaussian blur (kernel size = 3), and random resized cropping with a scale parameter ranging from 0.8 to 1.0, corresponding to selecting 80–100% of the original tissue area before resizing to 224 × 224 pixels. Each patch was augmented twice, producing two correlated views used as a positive pair in the contrastive learning framework. Patch-level augmentations were applied only to the training set, while validation and test sets were built based on unaugment patches.

Prior to training, all patches were normalized using the Macenko stain normalization method16, as implemented in TIAToolbox17. A representative slide (ID: B6-A0RT-01Z-00-DX1.6C2BA663-D61B-404B-B62C-B81747B6A3AC) from TCGA-BRCA dataset was selected as the reference for normalization. A patch extracted from this slide at coordinates (x = 28330; y = 44298) was used to estimate the stain vectors, and all other image patches were normalized accordingly.

We trained the model for 30 epochs, using the validation set for monitoring and early stopping to prevent overfitting. We evaluated model performance on the patches using accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and ROC-AUC.

In addition, for dataset interaction, OpenSlide was used for efficient slide handling. Image preprocessing and augmentation were managed with Torchvision, while deep learning models were developed and executed using PyTorch (version 2.1.2 + cu121). All experiments were conducted on a high-performance computing server from Finland CSC – IT Center for Science. This server, running Ubuntu 22.04.4 LTS, was configured with a 12-core AMD processor and 216 GB of RAM. GPU acceleration, powered by CUDA 12.1, was utilized for all training procedures.

Data Records

TCGA-BRCA sample selection for malignant vs. non-malignant region annotation

We selected 50 publicly available H&E-stained WSIs from the TCGA-BRCA cohort (available at https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) based on their image quality and molecular subtype diversity. To access them, we followed the standard download procedure described in the TCGA documentation (https://docs.gdc.cancer.gov/Data_Transfer_Tool/Users_Guide/Getting_Started/) using the GDC Data Transfer Tool. Specifically, we listed the filenames of all WSIs in a text file and executed the command-line tool with that file as input, which enabled efficient downloading of all WSIs.

According to the TCGA Data Access Policy, the WSIs are openly available and may be reused under the TCGA data usage terms. As stated on the TCGA website (https://gdc.cancer.gov/about-data): “Data made available through the GDC is for research purposes only. The GDC provides researchers with access to standardized clinical, proteomic, epigenomic, and genomic data from cancer studies to enable exploratory analyses that cannot be considered definitive for outcomes.” Furthermore, our re-annotation constitutes exploratory data analysis performed on publicly available TCGA Open Access WSIs, which is explicitly permitted under the GDC data use policy (https://gdc.cancer.gov/content/are-there-restrictions-use-gdc-data-publications). This policy clearly states: “All GDC data can be used in publications or presentations.”

The chosen slides, provided in. svs format, represent multiple molecular breast cancer subtypes, including Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-enriched, Triple-negative, Normal-like, and Basal-like. This diversity ensures subtype-representative region-level annotations for both malignant and non-malignant tissues. The BRCA-QuPath-Annot dataset, which includes the annotations performed in this study, has been deposited in a public repository and is accessible via 10.6084/m9.figshare.3025635412.

BRCA-Qupath-annot dataset structure

The annotations were originally performed in four parts by the pathologist, labeled as Parts 1 to 4. Each part corresponds to a subset of WSIs, with a few slides annotated within each part. Each part (e.g., part1/) contains the project.qpproj and data/ directory. The project.qpproj defines the overall project structure along with its dependencies. The data/ directory includes one subfolder for each WSI. These subfolders store image-specific annotation data, class definitions, and project metadata required for QuPath to display and manage annotations. In addition, a WSI/ folder is located next to the four parts, which contains the original WSIs in.svs format used for annotation. The names of the WSIs begin with the corresponding molecular subtype, allowing for subtype-based grouping. (e.g., LumA_TCGA-XX-XXXX.svs where XX-XXXX indicates the unique TCGA patient ID originally assigned to each slide with LumA indicates the subtype). Annotation strategy for non-malignant tissue

The annotated non-malignant regions include diversity of non-malignant histological components present in breast WSIs, including adipose tissue, stroma, and normal glandular structures. This approach was taken to ensure broad representation of non-malignant tissue types. Annotating all tissue outside malignant regions as “non-malignant” would have introduced a strong sampling bias toward adipose tissue, which is typically the most abundant component in breast specimens. Instead, selected regions were chosen to ensure representation of heterogeneous non-malignant tissue types.

Moreover, the annotated non-malignant regions were drawn from both intra-tumoral and extra-tumoral areas. As such, the dataset includes non-malignant tissue both adjacent to tumors and from regions distant from tumor involvement. This design supports the applicability of the resulting models to both tumor-containing and non-tumor WSIs.

Patch extraction script

To perform patch-level extraction from the annotated regions, a Groovy script compatible with QuPath is also provided (groovy_script_for_extract_patches.gvy). This script enables users to extract patches from the annotated malignant and non-malignant regions. Note that when extracting patches, QuPath may include patches whose area partially overlaps with an annotated region. As a result, a number of patches may be located on the boundary of the annotated region, where a part of the patch is either inside or outside of the annotation region. Thus, the script enables researchers to determine the proportion of each patch that should be included within an annotated region. By adjusting this script, users can decide whether patches located on the boundaries - only partially included within an annotation - should be used or excluded.

The dataset does not include pre-extracted patches, except for an additional set of 256 × 256-pixel patches provided in this study along with the main dataset. Users can generate patches of any desired size by running the provided script on the original WSIs. The script can be executed directly within QuPath and it saves the extracted patches as image tiles in a user-defined output folder.

Expert validation and inter-observer reliability

To ensure the diagnostic integrity and high quality of the BRCA-Qupath-Annot dataset12, the primary annotations underwent a secondary validation process by an independent expert pathologist. This step was performed to establish the reliability and reproducibility of the ground-truth labels provided in this work.

The validation process involved a blind, detailed review of the regions initially delineated by the first pathologist. The second expert either “accepted” or “rejected” the initial classification of each region as malignant or non-malignant. This cross-verification confirms that the data reflects a high level of professional consensus, which is critical for its use in training and testing diagnostic models.

Characterization of dataset limitations and boundary effects
