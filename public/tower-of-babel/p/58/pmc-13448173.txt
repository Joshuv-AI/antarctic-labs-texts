Cry1Ab toxicity against four species of the Diatraea sugarcane borer complex in Colombia
Authors: Juan Sebastián Ángel-Salazar, Germán Vargas, Caroline Placidi de Bortoli, Juan Luis Jurat-Fuentes, Claudia Echeverri-Rubiano, Jershon López-Gerena, Carolina Camargo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The multiple Diatraea (Lepidoptera: Crambidae) species comprising the stem borer complex are among the most damaging pests of sugarcane in the Americas, with D. saccharalis, D. indigenella, D. busckella, and D. tabernella considered as the most widespread species in Colombia. Entomological research in Colombia has focused on the use of egg and larval parasitoids to control populations and minimize their negative economic impacts. However, the declining effectiveness of these parasitoids on certain species has encouraged the exploration of complementary management strategies, including alternatives such as insecticidal proteins derived from Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt). This study evaluated the toxicity of the Cry1Ab protein from Bt against the four most widespread species of the Diatraea complex in Colombia using a bioassay protocol with fresh corn tissue. The mortality rate of Cry1Ab protein followed a concentration response in three of the four species tested, enabling the estimation of toxicity parameters [Lethal Concentration (LC) LC50 and LC95]. Growth inhibition (GI) and growth index (GInd - RGI) were higher than 90% and lower than 0.1, respectively, for all species tested. These results demonstrate that the toxicity of Cry1Ab is similar in all four Diatraea species tested in this study, supporting the potential use of this Bt protein as an alternative management for these pest species.

Introduction

Sugarcane borers of the Diatraea spp. complex are widespread pests and have a significant economic impact on crops in the Western Hemisphere. They attack at least 65 plant species including sugarcane, corn, millet, sorghum, wheat, and rice, as well as many other native grasses, which explains the considerable interest in their control1–4.

In Mexico and the southern United States, Diatraea saccharalis is restricted almost exclusively to sugarcane, corn, and sorghum1, although it appears to have adapted particularly well to corn where it is the predominant pest species5,6. In Brazil, D. saccharalis is the primary pest of sugarcane and only a secondary pest in corn. However, concerns have been raised about a possible shift in host preference, as observed in the USA6.

Sugarcane has been cultivated continuously in the Cauca River Valley region (CRV) of Colombia for more than 100 years, resulting in the availability of various sugarcane growth stages as host plants for a diverse range of pests7. In this area, sugarcane is threatened by a four-species complex of sugarcane stem borers, including D. busckella Dyar & Heinrich, 1927, D. indigenella Dyar & Heinrich, 1927, D. saccharalis (Fabricius, 1794), and D. tabernella Dyar, 1911. The abundance of these pest species can fluctuate as a consequence of crop practices and climatic fluctuations8.

The Diatraea spp. complex can attack sugarcane in all stages of its crop cycle, with losses depending on the crop stage and the damage generated9. In the larval stage, Diatraea spp. bore through and kill the younger and smaller stalks, resulting in the damage known as “dead heart”, and creating an access route for other damaging organisms such as red rot (Colletotrichum falcatum)4,10. Infested mature stems have reduced juice yield, decreased sucrose content, and high phenolic compound content10. It is estimated that for each percentage unit of Diatraea spp. damage (1% infestation intensity) there is a reduction of approximately 1 ton of sugarcane per hectare harvested11, and a 0.26% reduction in tons of sugar translates to a total reduction of 143 kg of sugar per hectare for each percentage unit of infestation intensity9,11. The devastating effects of Diatraeaspp. infestation highlights the need for effective yet environmentally safe management tools.

Managing the Diatraea pest species complex represents a challenge considering the need to establish methods that similarly manage all four borer species. Diatraea busckella and D. tabernella have been recorded in the CRV since 2013 and 2014, respectively4,12,13, and apart from displacing the species traditionally known in the region14, they have also shown relative resistance toward the larval parasitoids Lydella minense (Townsend, 1927) and Billaea claripalpis (van der Wulp, 1896)15. Ideally, management strategies should be equally effective across all four species. In this regard, Cotesia flavipes (Cameron, 1891) (Hymenoptera: Braconidae), a biological control agent commonly released for Diatraea spp., has demonstrated varying levels of parasitism when targeting D. saccharalis, D. busckella, D. tabernella, and D. indigenella16, highlighting the challenges associated with managing the sugarcane borer species complex.

The bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis (Bt) and its insecticidal proteins have provided an effective and environmentally safe tool for the control of a wide variety of insect pests for decades17. During sporulation, the Bt bacterium produces crystalline inclusions containing entomocidal proteins that disrupt the midgut epithelium in the insect host18. Sprays containing Bt spores and crystals represent more than 80% of the international market share for microbial pesticides19, and transgenic crops expressing Bt insecticidal protein genes are grown in more than 100 million hectares globally20. Importantly, these transgenic crops are considered environmentally safer than chemical alternatives21 and can lead to benefits for non-transgenic crops through regional pest suppression22,23 while are compatible with biological control tools20. In the case of boring insects, such as Diatraea spp., effective delivery of Bt insecticidal proteins is through transgenic expression. Several studies have reported toxicity of some Bt insecticidal proteins on D. saccharalis as the model stem borer species, including Cry1Ab, Cry1Ac, Cry1F, Cry2Ab, and Vip3Aa206,10,24–26. Transgenic sugarcane plants expressing Cry1Ab are already commercially available in Brazil, primarily to control D. saccharalis as the main pest species in that country10. In Colombia, according to the most recent update from the Colombian Agricultural Institute (ICA by its initials in Spanish), several Bt formulations are authorized for use in multiple horticultural and fruit crops, with Bt var. kurstaki and Bt var. aizawai products registered for lepidopteran control27. However, these formulations are not widely used for Diatraea control in sugarcane, and Bt sugarcane is not currently cultivated commercially in Colombia28. In contrast, Bt maize has been grown in the country since 2006 following the first regulatory approvals29. Early adoption expanded rapidly, and by the early 2010 s Bt maize was already established in major producing departments such as Valle del Cauca, Córdoba, Meta, Tolima, and Cesar30. Today, cultivation remains concentrated in the Caribbean region (Córdoba, Sucre, Bolívar, Cesar, Magdalena, La Guajira) and the Eastern Plains (Meta, Casanare, Vichada), with smaller adoption in inter‑Andean areas including Tolima, Huila, and Valle del Cauca31. Given this context, the potential adoption of Bt sugarcane in Colombia depends fundamentally on whether the available Bt proteins can effectively attack one of the country’s main sugarcane pests. However, in Colombia sugarcane is attacked by a complex of four Diatraea species (D. saccharalis, D. indigenella, D. busckella, and D. tabernella)13,32, presenting a unique challenge in the implementation of control with Bt proteins as a protein exhibiting similar efficacy across all four species would be needed. Addressing this question is critical for determining the suitability of Cry1Ab for managing the entire pest complex. Recently, a bioassay method was developed using tender corncob material that enabled the testing of Cry1Ac against all four Diatraea species33, as there is only a commercially available artificial diet for D. saccharalis34–36, which limits the ability to assess other species in the complex. In this study, the use of this method was extended to evaluate the susceptibility of D. saccharalis, D. indigenella, D. busckella, and D. tabernella to Cry1Ab. Results from this study identify Cry1Ab as a viable candidate for producing transgenic sugarcane plants protected against Diatraea spp. stem borers.

Materials and methods

Cry1Ab production and purification

The Cry1Ab protein was produced in cultures of B. thuringiensis subsp. kurstaki strain HD1-9 from the Bacillus Genetic Stock Center (BGSC, Columbus, Ohio). The bacteria were grown on 1/3 tryptic soy broth (TSB) agar for three days at 30 °C, and then a single bacterial colony was suspended in 1 mL of autoclaved water and heated for 45 min at 72 °C to kill vegetative Bt cells. An aliquot (500 µL) of the suspension was used to inoculate 500 mL of 1/3 TSB medium. The culture was incubated for 4 days at 25 °C with agitation (160 rpm) until sporulation was confirmed by microscopic observation. Cultures were then collected by centrifugation (14,981 g x 10 min at 4 °C) and pellets were washed three times with 1 M NaCl containing 0.1% Triton X-100 with a brief (5 min) sonication before centrifugation. Washes were repeated for another 3 cycles as before using distilled water. The final pellet was re-suspended in 50 mL of solubilizing solution (50 mM Na2CO3, 0.1% β-mercaptoethanol, 0.1 M NaCl, pH 10.5), sonicated as above, and then incubated overnight at 30 °C with agitation (160 rpm). After centrifugation at 27,167 g x 30 min, the supernatant containing solubilized Cry1Ab protoxin was processed with 0.2 mg/mL of trypsin for 2 h at 37 °C. The activated Cry1Ab toxin was purified using anion exchange chromatography. One major absorbance peak containing Cry1Ab toxin (based on band size in SDS-10%PAGE analysis) was detected during the elution, and fractions in that peak were pooled, protein concentration quantified by fluorometry (Qubit, Invitrogen), and then lyophilized (FreeZone 12–84 °C, LABCONCO) and kept at − 80 °C until shipped to the Entomology Laboratory of the Colombian Sugarcane Research Center (known by the Spanish acronym Cenicaña) in Valle del Cauca (Colombia).

Insects

The four Diatraea species (D. saccharalis, D. indigenella,D. busckella, and D. tabernella) were collected in larval stage from sugarcane fields in different zones of the Cauca River Valley region (CRV) of Colombia, as described elsewhere37. Species identification was based on Vargas et al.13. To ensure the same rearing conditions, collected Diatraealarvae were maintained as described by Echeverri-Rubiano et al.38, and Ángel-Salazar et al.39, using tender corn cobs throughout the whole larval stages in the Entomology Laboratory of Cenicaña located in the municipality of Florida, Valle del Cauca, Colombia. Rearing laboratory conditions in a humidity and temperature-controlled room were 25 ± 1 °C, 70 ± 4% relative humidity, and 12:12 h L: D of daily photoperiod33,38.

To enhance genetic variability within the tested populations, adults that emerged from field-collected material of each species were crossbred with individuals from various locations. This was achieved using the iso-female lines method described by Fogliata et al.37, from which families were established. In this context, a ‘family’ corresponds to the progeny derived from a single field‑collected female mated with a controlled number of males, where the offspring of each female constituted a defined genetic unit. In our study, the established families had a limit of three males per female and ratios female: male can be seen on the Table 1. The number of families varied according to the abundance of each species in the field. Diatraea indigenella and D. saccharalis had more geographically limited distributions13,15. Therefore, nine families of the F1 generation were used for the bioassays with D. indigenella and four families of the F2 generation for D. saccharalis. For D. tabernella and D. busckella, a total of sixteen F1 generation families were used in the bioassays (Table 1).

All larvae used in the bioassays were reared on tender corn cobs (cultivar SV 1035) harvested 75 days after sowing. Larvae were then transferred to plastic Petri dishes (5 cm in diameter and 1.5 cm in height) with ventilated lids. Petri dishes and food were replaced twice a week with corn cob slices approximately 2.5 cm in circumference and 0.5 cm in height. Larvae were fed until reaching pupation, and then they were transferred to PVC pipes (22 ± 1 cm in height and 10.2 cm in diameter) until adults emerged.

Bioassays

All bioassays were carried out in the Entomology Laboratory at Cenicaña. Bioassays with each borer species were conducted by exposing F1 and/or F2 neonate larvae (< 24 h after hatching) to tender corn cobs treated with the Cry1Ab activated toxin or sterile de-ionized (DI) water as a control. Corn was used instead of sugarcane because it supports the development of all four tested species33,38. Corn from the cultivar SV 1035 was purchased from a third party. This corn was grown conventionally, except that no insecticides were used, while other corn fields were kept at least two kilometers away. Corn cobs were prepared as described by Ángel-Salazar et al.33, and placed in individual wells of 128-well bioassay trays (each well Ø 1.6 cm and height 1.6 cm; CD International Pitman, NJ) containing a 2% agar (BD Difco™) plug at the bottom to preserve turgidity. The wells were sealed with air-permeable lids (CD International, Pitman, NJ) and stored in a refrigerator (temperature, 8 °C; relative humidity, 75%) during the protein preparation period to prevent moisture loss33. The diagnostic concentration previously determined to evaluate the Cry1Ac protein (24.14 ng/cm2 or 619.5 µg/mL)33was used as a baseline to select concentrations to evaluate susceptibility to Cry1Ab. Six concentrations of Cry1Ab (90, 180, 360, 500, 619.5, and 720 µg/mL) were prepared in distilled water, and this diluent was used as a control. As described by Ángel-Salazar et al.33, an aliquot of the test solution (60 µL) was dispensed on the surface of the corn disk in the laminar flow cabinet. The substrate was allowed to absorb the solution for one hour, and then a single larva was transferred to each well using a fine brush. The wells were resealed with air-permeable lids, and the trays were stored in a controlled conditions room with a temperature of 25 ± 1 °C and a relative humidity of 70 ± 4%33. Each treatment corresponded to one Cry1Ab concentration and its control. For each treatment, 16 larvae (one per well) were used per replicate, and the bioassay was repeated three times, yielding 48 larvae per treatment. Thus, for each species, 7 treatments (6 Cry1Ab concentrations plus the control) times 48 larvae per treatment resulted in a total of 336 larvae.

Study variables

The response variables evaluated were recorded seven days after infestation and included percentage mortality (%M), developmental stage (Relative Growth Index, RGI), and weight (Growth Inhibition, GI). Surviving larvae had their larval developmental stage (instar) recorded visually based on the description of Ángel-Salazar et al.33, and the weight (mg) was taken using analytical scales (precision ± 0.1 mg, Metter Toledo AB204, Switzerland). Percentage mortality (%M) was calculated using the formula (%M) = (m/N) * 100 where m is the total number of insects killed in the treatment (including those that had weight < 0.1 mg) and N is the total number of insects tested for that treatment39,40. The RGI was calculated considering the number of live and dead individuals and their developmental stages. To calculate RGI, the growth index (GInd) was estimated using the formula GInd = [(n1 × L1) + (n2 × L2) + --- + (nx × Lx)]/(N × Lx) where n1, n2, …, nx are the number of individuals at instars L1, L2, ..., Lx, respectively, while N and Lx correspond to the total number of insects tested with the treatment and the maximum instar reached in the control treatment, respectively. The RGI was estimated from the GInd ratio between treatment and control. An RGI of < 1 indicates that individuals remained or died in early instars, RGI = 0 indicates no development (or that all died in the first instar), and RGI = 1 that all individuals in the test group had not difference in development compared to the control group41.

The Growth Inhibition (%GI) for the larvae was calculated using the average larval weight of survivors from each bioassay replicate for each treatment relative to the average weights from their respective control treatment. It was calculated using the formula %GI = 1 – (TWIT/TNIT)/(TWIC/TNIC) x 100, where TWIT is the total weight of insects in the treatment, TNIT is the total number of insects in the treatment, TWIC is the total weight of insects in the water control, and TNIC is the total number of insects in the water control42.

Analysis of data

A comparison of susceptibility was developed through a probit analysis for each species. A comparative analysis of the dose-response curves for different Diatraea species was conducted using the method ANCOVA43. The complete model includes Cry1Ab concentration as a covariate, species as a categorical factor, and the interaction between Cry1Ab concentration and species, compared with a simplified model. The simplified model uses D. saccharalis as the reference species for comparisons and examines the interaction between species by testing for differences in the slope of the dose-response curves (also known as a test of the “parallelism hypothesis”). A significant change in slope, as evidenced by a significant change in species response to the Cry1Ab protein, supports differing susceptibility levels between the species.

The experiments followed a completely randomized design with the factorial structure considering the Diatraea species as one factor, and the treatments (protein and control) as the second factor, where the response variables of mortality, developmental stage (GInd and RGI), and weight (GI) were analyzed using a generalized linear model (GLIMM). Three replicates per treatment were used with a 16-larvae subsample per replicate, for a total sample number of 48 larvae per treatment (n = 48), and multiple comparisons were performed with Tukey’s HSD test (α = 0.05) using the statistical package SAS 9.4 (2012) (Proc GLIMMIX).

Results

Mortality

The percentage mortality in the four Diatraea spp. ranged between 43.8% and 100% depending on Cry1Ab protein concentration, with the most drastic differences in dose-response observed for D. indigenella (Fig. 1). There was a Cry1Ab concentration effect on mortality for all four borer species (F = 19.04; df = 3, 1316; P < 0.001), with D. indigenella presenting the highest susceptibility. In addition, a concentration effect was found (F = 197.87; df = 6, 1316; P < 0.001), where the mortality in the control treatment was negligible and mortality for concentrations of 90 µg/ml and 180 µg/ml was significantly different from other concentrations and from each other. Similarly, an interaction between the species and the concentrations was observed with the higher concentrations being associated with high mortality, except D. indigenella which showed high mortality even at the lowest concentration tested (F = 3.04; df = 18, 1316; P < 0.001) (Fig. 1), preventing accurate estimation of lethal concentration values. Lethal concentrations LC50 and LC95 were estimated from mortality data for the other three species and were not significantly different between species, based on overlapping confidence intervals (Table 2) and the parallelism hypothesis (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Growth index (gind) and relative growth index (RGI)

There was a significant effect due to borer species on the Growth Index (GInd) (F = 9.76; df = 3, 56; P < 0.001), with D. indigenella presenting the lowest index and a significantly higher GInd in the control treatment (F = 793.5; df = 6, 56; P < 0. 001). There was a significant interaction between borer species and treatment, with increasing Cry1Ab protein concentrations generating a more pronounced weight loss in all species except D. indigenella (F = 2.04; df = 18, 56; P = 0.022). This weight reduction trend stabilized for all species after reaching a concentration of 360 µg/mL of Cry1Ab, with higher concentrations not generating further weight loss (Fig. 3).

Based on the Growth Index (GInd) estimations, the analysis of the RGI values for the larvae treated with the Cry1Ab protein revealed no interaction between borer species and treatment (F = 1.75; df = 15, 48; P = 0.072). Growth inhibition (%GI)

The percentages of Growth Inhibition (%GI) were 94.3% for D. busckella, 97% for D. indigenella, 96.1% for D. tabernella, and 94% for D. saccharalis, with no differences observed between protein concentrations (F = 1.14; df = 5, 48; P = 0. 350), and no interaction between concentration and species (F = 0.61; df = 15, 48; P = 0.850). However, differences were observed between species (F = 3.06; d. f. = 3, 48; P = 0.037), where D. indigenella presented greater inhibition and differed significantly from D. saccharalis.

Larvae in the control treatment had significantly different weights depending on species (F = 14.64; df = 3, 1316; P < 0.001) with D. busckella (± 5.3 mg) and D. tabernella (± 5.45 mg) achieving the highest weights followed by D. saccharalis (± 4.32 mg), and D. indigenella larvae (± 3.87 mg) being the lightest (Fig. 5). Control weights significantly diverged from weights of larvae exposed to any concentration of the Cry1Ab protein (F = 1867.74; df = 6, 1316; P < 0.001). An interaction between borer species and protein concentration was also found (F = 12. 21; df = 18, 1316; P < 0.001).

Discussion

Several studies have been conducted on D. saccharalis and D. impersonatella to evaluate the impact of insecticidal proteins from B. thuringiensis6,10,24–26,44,45. In contrast, there is a lack of information on susceptibility to Cry proteins in other Diatraea spp. of economic relevance. The results presented in this study provide a new report on the susceptibility to the Cry1Ab protein of D. saccharalis and the first report of three other predominant Diatraea species in Colombia (D. indigenella, D. busckella, and D. tabernella). The baseline susceptibility was similar in all species, but D. indigenella was the most sensitive species, displaying a high mortality at the lowest Cry1Ab concentration tested (over 90%) (Fig. 1). The results also validate the use of the bioassay protocol in developing dose-response curves in lepidopteran species that primarily feed on corn and lack an available artificial diet. For instance, the toxicity of Bt strains against D. saccharalis and D. flavipennella46 could be tested with this protocol on other species of Diatraea, including the ones tested in this study. The tender corn cobs used as a food source in the protocol not only facilitated the development of all sugarcane borer species studied, but also significantly reduced the observation time needed to monitor for toxicity33. Moreover, as the corn fresh tissue could have a less uniform incorporation of the protein in comparison to the more uniform distribution in the artificial diet, our tests could result in higher LC50values compared to assays with artificial diets33,38,47,48. For instance, the LC50 obtained from this study for D. saccharalis (77.7 µg/mL, CI 39.4–113.0) is substantially higher compared to the LC50reported by Girón-Pérez et al.6, for the same species (17.55 ng/cm2, approximately 0.45 µg/mL). However, the distinct source organism used for Cry1Ab production (B. thuringiensis versus E. coli), Cry1Ab quantification methods (fluorometry versus densitometry), and differences between protocols could also help explain the LC50 discrepancy between this study and Girón-Pérez et al.6.

Survival of some larvae in the bioassays following exposure to Cry1Ab was observed, although not consistently across all Cry1Ab concentrations. Severe stunting was observed in surviving larvae across all species, with over 90% growth inhibition. This was reflected in significant reductions in weight (GI), limited mobility, and delayed developmental stages (GInd - RGI).

Girón-Pérez et al.6, reported up to 30-fold lower weight in D. saccharalis exposed to Cry1Ab compared to the control, similarly to the observations in this study, where larvae of D. saccharalisand the other species treated with Cry1Ab were up to 25-fold lighter than the average weight of the control. Analogously, the observations of growth inhibition are consistent with those reported by Huang et al.39, and Zhang et al.40, who observed 100% growth inhibition in susceptible strains of D. saccharalis at 32 µg/g of Cry1Ab. Based on these findings, the growth inhibition (GI) and growth rate (GInd - RGI) data complement the mortality data by likely detecting any sublethal effects of Cry toxins41,49. Previous studies on other lepidopteran pests have proposed that larvae that fail to grow beyond the first instar or weigh less than 0.1 mg after one week should be categorized as dead50,51. Although this study was not specifically designed to assess sublethal effects, larvae that survived Cry1Ab exposure were isolated and observed. It was found that abnormal development (GI, GInd, and RGI) was caused by the protein rather than a lack of food, eventually leading to death (data not shown). On the other hand, D. indigenella showed almost complete mortality even at the lowest concentration of the Cry1Ab protein. While this indicates a comparatively higher susceptibility to the Cry1Ab protein, a quantitative comparison of susceptibility among species is incomplete, as LC50 and LC95 values could not be estimated for this species. Although this does not diminish the value of the study presented here, it does limit our ability to fully characterize the interaction between Cry1Ab and D. indigenella. Moreover, this behavior raises questions about the potential effects of using this protein in the field against this species. It cannot be ruled out that local extinction could occur, given this species’ high susceptibility to the toxin. These observations highlight the need for further studies to better understand the interaction between Cry1Ab protein and D. indigenella, as well as the evaluation of potential sublethal effects and broader ecological consequences across all species assessed. Such knowledge will strengthen risk assessments and support the development of integrated pest management strategies that ensure the responsible use of this protein in the field.

In addition to these considerations, it is also important to point out how these species interact with Bt maize in Colombia. As mentioned before, Bt maize is grown in some regions of Colombia where populations of Diatraea species are present, since these insects can feed on both maize and sugarcane. However, D. indigenella, D. busckella, and D. tabernella have not been reported as major pests of maize. This means that most interaction with Bt maize would likely involve D. saccharalis, which could result in localized reductions of this species in maize-growing areas. Because D. tabernella and D. busckella are the dominant species in Colombian sugarcane systems13,14, it is unlikely that Bt maize cultivation will create strong selection pressure or cause significant population-level effects on these sugarcane-associated species.

The study results highlight the Cry1Ab protein as an effective tool for controlling the Diatraea complex in Colombia. Despite differences between species’ biology38, susceptibility to Cry1Ab does not vary significantly. Additionally, these results support the use of the corn cob protocol to test other insecticidal proteins against these pests. The findings are not only novel for this species complex but also provide valuable information for the development of effective biological control methods and genetic transformation studies in sugarcane, being relevant to the sugar industry in Colombia and worldwide, potentially improving its yield and productivity29,52,53.
