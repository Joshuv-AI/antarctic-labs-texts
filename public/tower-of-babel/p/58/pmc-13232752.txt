Selection for rapid embryonic development sculpted the new gene Shrew into an accelerator of Drosophila dorsal-ventral axis specification
Authors: Sudhindra R Gadagkar, Stuart J Newfeld
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssignment of cell fate along the dorsal/ventral (D/V) axis of the Drosophila embryo occurs during the 40 minutes immediately prior to gastrulation. The mechanism is the rapid redistribution of Decapentaplegic-Screw morphogens from lateral to dorsal regions. Redistribution requires the activity of Short gastrulation (Sog), Twisted gastrulation (Tsg), Tolloid (Tld), and Shrew. The function of Sog, Tsg, and Tld and their vertebrate homologs are well known. Shrew remains an enigma, though previous studies showed that Shrew is a truncated relative of Tsg. Our phylogenetic survey revealed that Shrew is Drosophila specific and likely derived from a different Tsg family member Crossveinless. A second survey identified a correlation between insects with Shrew and those with the fastest time to gastrulation. The correlation suggests the hypothesis that Shrew functions as an accelerator of D/V axis formation as an adaption to selective pressure for rapid egg development that reduces exposure to predators. We tested the hypothesis by slowing down egg development with reduced temperature generating the prediction that an accelerator would then be unnecessary. Consistent with the prediction, at reduced temperature shrew was nonessential, with two normally lethal shrew mutants surviving to adult. Together the evolutionary and experimental data suggest that Shrew is an exemplar of a new gene that modifies a developmental process as an adaptation to an identified selective pressure. From a broader perspective the analysis of Shrew reveals that developmental processes are adaptable phenotypes, and that new gene creation is an effective response to selection.

Introduction

In Drosophila, cell fate specification along the dorsal/ventral (D/V) axis takes place in a 40 minutes period during formation of the cellular blastoderm immediately prior to gastrulation (Shimmi et al. 2005a). D/V axis formation requires the activity of two secreted signaling molecules, Decapentaplegic (Dpp), and Screw that form a heterodimer. Upon heterodimer binding, a complex of transmembrane receptors activate signal transduction proteins that serve as transcription factors to initiate differentiation. Heterodimer signaling in the dorsal half of the embryo is responsible for two cell fates. The dorsal-most cells that receive a high level of heterodimer signaling will form the amnioserosa, while the dorsal ectoderm is specified in adjacent dorsolateral regions by a low level of heterodimer signaling. Overall, Dpp/Screw heterodimers act as a classic morphogen to specify two dorsal cell fates in a concentration dependent manner (Akiyama et al. 2024).

One feature of the Drosophila embryonic D/V specification mechanism is a sharp transition in signal intensity between the future amnioserosa and dorsal ectoderm (ie signaling is step-like). The sharp transition results from interactions between the heterodimeric signal and the extracellular proteins Tolloid (Tld), Short gastrulation (Sog), and Twisted gastrulation (Tsg), then enhanced by positive feedback from transcriptional targets such as zerknullt (Wang and Ferguson 2005). Sog is expressed ventrally, then moves dorsally and binds to Dpp/Screw heterodimers in lateral regions. Tsg joins the complex, and along with Sog, prevents the ligands from binding their receptors in lateral regions. Sog momentum keeps the complex moving dorsally, where Tld cleaves Sog to release the heterodimers. This cycle of binding, dorsal migration then cleavage, leads to a net increase of free heterodimers in the dorsal-most region (also called the dorsal midline), where Dpp/Screw heterodimers bind to their receptors to stimulate a high level of signaling to specify the amnioserosa (Shimmi et al. 2005a). A small fraction of heterodimers escape Sog in lateral regions stimulating low levels of signaling to specify the dorsal ectoderm (Künnapuu et al. 2014).

A similar mechanism specifies crossveins during Drosophila wing development (Shimmi et al. 2005b). In the wing, the role of Tsg is fulfilled by Crossveinless (Cv), a close relative. The same mechanism patterns the D/V axis in vertebrate embryos with Tsg performing the same role (de Robertis and Tejeda-Munoz 2022). Nevertheless, the function of shrew (shrew) in Drosophila melanogaster remains unknown. To date only four reports on shrew have been published. Mutations were identified in the original loss-of-function screen for defects in embryonic development (Jurgens et al. 1984). shrew was shown to contribute specifically to D/V axis formation by rescue of shrew mutants with overexpression of Dpp (Ferguson and Anderson 1992). shrew encodes a protein similar to the carboxy-terminal half of Tsg (Bonds et al. 2007). The newest paper, a phylogenetic survey from the early days of genome sequencing that examined Tsg family members in insects showed that only in the genus Drosophila a duplication of Cv was truncated to create Shrew (da Fonseca et al. 2010).

To suggest a potential function for Shrew we looked beyond insects at the evolutionary context for Shrew and its relatives Tsg and Cv. Our phylogenetic survey across the animal kingdom identified multiple gene gains and losses in the Tsg family as well as the origins of Cv and Shrew. We then identified a correlation between insects with Shrew and insects with the fastest time to gastrulation. The correlation suggested the hypothesis that Shrew functions as an accelerator of D/V axis specification as an adaptation to selective pressure for rapid egg development that reduces exposure to predators. Consistent with the hypothesis, experiments showed that shrew is not absolutely required for the slower paced development induced by reduced temperature. Taken together, the phylogenetic and experimental results suggest that selective pressure for rapid embryonic development shaped the new gene Shrew into an accelerator of D/V axis specification.

Materials and methods

Identification of species with Tsg family members

To create a query, the sequence (FASTA format) for each of the three D. melanogaster Tsg family members was retrieved from the D. melanogaster reference genome in GenBank: (i) Crossveinless (Cv; NP_536786; isoform A—three transcripts and one protein), (ii) Shrew (Shrew; NP_647886; isoform B—one transcript and one protein), and (iii) Twisted gastrulation (Tsg; NP_511135; isoform A—one transcript and one protein). These were aligned in Clustal Omega with default parameters (ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/clustalo). The alignment identified a 57 amino acid region toward the carboxy-terminus of each that is common to all three: Cv amino acids 166 to 223, Shrew 72 to 129, and Tsg 140 to 197. This region contains nine cysteines and is 66% identical (38/57) across the three proteins. As a control, BLASTp (blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) with this region against the D. melanogaster reference genome (NCBI taxonomy ID 6447) returned the three proteins.

To identify candidate species for analysis there were several steps. First, the detailed version of Lifemap (de Vienne 2016; lifemap-ncbi.univ-lyon1.fr) was employed with Metazoa (multicellular animals) as a starting point. Metazoa was chosen because one of its basal taxa (phylum Porifera; sponges) contains the earliest known version of the TGF-β pathway (Suga et al. 1999; Nichols et al. 2006). Moving along the Metazoan lineage in the direction of greater complexity allowed the identification of candidate higher taxa to examine (phylum, class, order, and family). Second, a BLASTp search with the fly query against each identified taxon was performed to determine if any candidate species with Tsg sequences were present. In some cases, there were none, but for other taxa multiple species were returned. Third, among species returned by BLASTp the species with the most complete genome sequence (Supplementary Table 1) in that taxon was chosen for analysis (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genome). Proper lineage and taxon nomenclature for each species was obtained from the NCBI taxonomy browser (ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Taxonomy/Browser/). For context, each species common names and major characteristics were gathered from Encyclopedia Britannica (britannica.com). The divergence time from D. melanogaster (noted as million years ago; mya) was retrieved from Timetree.org (Kumar et al. 2022). The analysis was completed with available data on 11/19/2023.

Identification of Tsg family members in selected species

Sequences were retrieved from individual candidate species by BLASTp with the fly query sequence. All retrieved sequences from each species were downloaded and manually compared with each other for the presence of identical sequence pairs or a triplet and for the presence of sequence pairs or a triplet differing only by an amino or carboxy-terminal extension that could represent an alternative initiator methionine or an artifact of polyA priming of the same gene. When an identical pair or triplet of sequences was noted and one was from the reference genome (NP or XP prefix), then the reference sequence was studied further. When two sequences were not completely identical along their length, then we investigated further to determine if they were variants of the same gene deposited by different researchers, sequencing errors in reads for the same gene or a duplication representing two different genes. To address these possibilities, we set an empirical threshold for different genes by comparing an alignment of the pair or triplet sequences to an alignment for mouse BMP2 (NP_031579) and BMP4 (NP_001303289). These BMPs are two different genes (chromosomes 20 and 14) that remain very similar (61% identical over their whole length). The probability associated with the BMP2/BMP4 alignment is 5 × 10−175. In our Tsg analysis, any pair or pair within a triplet that displayed an “align two” probability smaller than BMP2/BMP4, were considered the same gene and only one was studied further. Decisions on pairs or triplet sequences were cross-referenced with the genome assembly to confirm that each sequence selected for further study was encoded at a distinct locus. Supplementary Table 2 contains accession numbers of all validated proteins.

Phylogenetic analysis of Tsg family members in selected species

Analyses were conducted in MEGAX (Kumar et al. 2018). The full-length sequence of all Tsg family members in the selected species were aligned with the D. melanogaster and Mus musculus Tsg family members employing default settings in MUSCLE (Edgar 2004). If the resulting tree displayed an ambiguous relationship between the sequences of the selected species and the others, then each of the Tsg family members in the selected species were run through the pipeline individually (e.g. Nasonia vitripennis). Prior to initiating tree building, we removed all signal peptides (first 18 to 25 residues) from the alignment based on SignalP (Teufel et al. 2022) to minimize artifactual similarity as Tsg family members are relatively short (<225 residues). Then amino and carboxy overhangs present in less than 50% of sequences were trimmed. The remaining residues were aligned with default settings in MUSCLE (Edgar 2004). The Partial Deletion method was implemented with an 80% cutoff for best-fit amino-acid substitution model selection for each alignment based on the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion. Phylogenetic trees were constructed utilizing the Maximum Likelihood framework with sites containing alignment gaps excluded using an 80% partial deletion criterion. Nonparametric bootstrapping was applied with 500 replicates to evaluate the relative strength of each node.

Low temperature rescue of shrew mutant embryos

For the rescue experiment, two stocks with mutations in shrew were obtained from the Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center. These were shrew1 st e (stock #2265 balanced over a TM3 chromosome with the dominant bristle mutation Stubble and the recessive dark body color mutation ebony) and shrewB18 p (stock #5395 balanced over a TM3 chromosome with the dominant wing nicking mutation Serrate and the recessive eye color mutation pink). shrew1 is an allele from the original screens for genes impacting embryonic development. This allele is homozygous lethal (Jurgens et al. 1984). It contains a missense mutation of cysteine 123 to serine that interferes with disulfide bond formation and presumably proper protein folding (Bonds et al. 2007). shrewB18 is a homozygous lethal nonsense mutation generated independently (Arora and Nusslein-Volhard 1992). The stop codon is in glutamine 23 (Bonds et al. 2007).

Virgin females and males of each stock were collected at 18 °C and crossed. Virgin females of shrew1 were collected at 18 °C and crossed to males of shrewB18. Each of the three crosses was conducted at 18 °C to slow down development by roughly 50% and at 25 °C which is the normal rearing temperature. There were 10 females and five males per cross with passes every four days, to prevent progeny overcrowding, until each cross had four vials with eggs. The vials were cultured at their respective temperature and progeny were scored as adults for the presence or absence of TM3 dominant and recessive markers over an 18-day period at 25 °C and a 36-day period at 18 °C. Adults without any TM3 markers were seen as surviving shrew mutants.

Results

Shrew is more likely derived from Cv than Tsg

To shed light on the origin of Shrew, we first examined the evolutionary relationships between Tsg, Cv, and Shrew in Drosophila. A full-length alignment (Supplementary Fig. 1a) found that Shrew is 66% identical to both Tsg and Cv in a carboxy-terminal 57 amino acid region that contains ten cysteines (Fig. 1). We added Mus musculus Tsg to the alignment to serve as an outgroup. After trimming the signal peptides, we created a Maximum Likelihood tree that has two important features. One point is that the fly protein Tsg is most like mouse Tsg in 100% of the replicate trees created by the algorithm, reflecting their common ancestor (a point noted independently in Moore et al. 2026). The second point is that clustering of Shrew and Cv is well-supported and consistent with the prior insect phylogenetic analysis (da Fonseca et al. 2010) suggesting Shrew is derived from Cv, rather than Tsg as suggested previously by Bonds et al. (2007).

Overall, the branching pattern suggests the following birth order for Drosophila Tsg family members. First, Tsg was inherited from the common ancestor of fly and mouse. Tsg then duplicated in the fly lineage to generate Cv. Subsequently Cv duplicated, then the duplicate truncated to only its carboxy-terminal Sog-binding domain to become Shrew. As a next step in understanding Shrew evolution and function we conducted a phylogenetic analysis of Metazoans with the highly conserved carboxy-terminal region.

Shrew is a new Drosophila specific gene

Our phylogenetic study began near the base of the Metazoan lineage with two radially symmetric organisms. Their divergence from D. melanogaster was over 715 mya and they predate the existence of organisms with bilateral symmetry (Table 1). Exemplar species of the phyla Ctenophora (comb jellyfish) and Cnidaria (sea anemone) do not contain any Tsg family members. These species have reference genomes with 12- or 53-fold coverage providing confidence that the absence of Tsg family members is not an artifact (Supplementary Table 1).

Within the Bilateria (organisms with bilateral symmetry at least once during their lifecycle), the split between the Deuterostome and Protostome superphyla occurred roughly 710 mya. Protostomes, including Drosophila, contain a gut with two openings in which the original gastrulation pore becomes the mouth. Deuterostomes, such as humans, contain a gut with two openings in which the original gastrulation pore becomes the anus. We examined twelve Deuterostomes including two well-known model organisms (sea urchin and house mouse Table 1). All species have a single Tsg family member. We confirmed this assessment with a tree of the D. melanogaster proteins plus sea urchin Strongylocentrotus purpuratus and M. musculus sequences. The tree demonstrated that the sea urchin and mouse family members are most like D. melanogaster Tsg (Fig. 2a). The underlying alignment revealed that mouse Tsg has 52% amino acid similarity to D. melanogaster Tsg and sea urchin Tsg has 46% similarity (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b).

Within protostomes, higher taxa were both uneven in the number of species with identified Tsg family members (e.g. phylum Mollusca taxonomy ID 6447 with 49 species versus phylum Xenacoelomorpha taxonomy ID 1312402 with one species) and uneven in numbers of species with sequenced genomes (phylum Mollusca with four versus phylum Xenacoelomorpha with none). The fact that the taxa in Table 1 reflect multiple ranks in the Linnaean hierarchy is a reflection of uneven sequence availability. Sequenced genomes in the Protostome phyla Mollusca (e.g. octopus), Nematoda (e.g. roundworm), and Priapulida (e.g. marine worm), with a divergence time from D. melanogaster of 680 mya contain a single protein similar to Tsg. For example, a tree from the from Nematoda species Pristionchus pacificus that is not in the Drosophila lineage and has one Tsg proteins displays the same branching pattern as the sea urchin tree (Fig. 2a′). The underlying alignment revealed that P. pacificus Tsg has 45% similarity to D. melanogaster Tsg (Supplementary Fig. 1c).

Interestingly the nematode model organism Caenorhabditis elegans has a complete genome but no Tsg family members. This is the only example we found of a Bilaterian organism that lost the Tsg-like protein inherited from the Deuterostome-Protostome common ancestor. Within the Protostome phylum Arthropoda (divergence from D. melanogaster 563 mya), a survey of twelve sequenced species identified one with two Tsg family members. In the subphylum Chelicerata class Merostomata, the species Limulus polyphemus (horseshoe crabs) is not in the Drosophila lineage and contains two Tsg-like proteins.

Within the class Insecta, the order Hymenoptera (divergence from D. melanogaster 344 mya) is not in the Drosophila lineage and the species N. vitripennis (a parasitic wasp) has one protein like Tsg (NCBI last four 7111; Fig. 2b) and another like Cv (0362; Fig. 2b′). The underlying alignment revealed that N. vitripennis 7111 has 56% similarity to D. melanogaster Tsg and 0362 has 67% similarity to D. melanogaster Cv (Supplementary Fig. 1d). In N. vitripennis, and others, genome annotation defaults to naming all proteins resembling D. melanogaster Tsg as Tsg or Tsg-like. Our trees provide a more rigorous test of similarity. In the list of accession numbers (Supplementary Table 2), both the NCBI name and the closest D. melanogaster relative shown by our trees are noted for each sequence when they are different.

Our trees for N. vitripennis illustrates improvements in genome assembly and phylogenetics in the 15 years since the prior study of Tsg in insects (da Fonseca et al. 2010). First, genome assemblies have advanced considerably. Their notation of three N. vitripennis proteins based on early contigs was reduced to two in the current assembly (GCF_009193385; 02/11/2020). Second, phylogenetic trees with multiple sequences from non-Drosophila insects can have artifactual clusters of those proteins. Their tree showed the three N. vitripennis proteins clustered together within a cluster of all non-Drosophila insects distinct from the cluster of all Drosophila sequences. We can reproduce this artifact in a tree with both N. vitripennis proteins clustering distinctly from others. Thus, we show trees with individual N. vitripennis proteins to provide more rigor. All our trees with clusters of non-melanogaster proteins (eg Anopheles darlingi, below) were validated with single protein trees and the multi-sequence tree is shown for brevity.

Within the class Insecta, order Diptera and suborder Nematocera (Fig. 2c) the species Anopheles darlingi (American mosquito) is not in the Drosophila lineage and has three Tsg family members like Cv. The underlying alignment revealed that Anopheles darling NCBI 2551 has 70% similarity to D. melanogaster Cv, NCBI 8276 has 68% similarity to Cv, and NCBI 8285 has 68% similarity to Cv (Supplementary Fig. 1e). Mosquitos are elongated flies with aquatic larvae and the most distant fly from Drosophila that has the same number of Tsg family members. The absence of Tsg does not impede Anopheles gambiae (African mosquito) embryonic dorsal-ventral axis specification even though the process in mosquitos is highly conserved with flies (Goltsev et al. 2007). The discrepancy is explained by demonstrated functional interchangeability of Tsg and Cv in D. melanogaster. Transgenic Tsg rescued wing defects due to the loss of Cv and transgenic Cv rescued embryonic lethality due to the loss of Tsg (Shimmi et al. 2005b). Functional equivalence in mosquitos would explain how the absence of Tsg is accommodated during embryonic development.

Two further points about the Tsg family in the class Insecta are relevant. First, the body plan in most Insects contains two extra-embryonic tissues, the serosa at the anterior and the amnion at the dorsal midline. A single amnioserosa at the dorsal midline is present only in a subset of Acalypterate that includes D. melanogaster (Schmidt-Ott 2000). Second, across Insect species the function of Tsg is variable. In Tribolium castaneum (Coleoptera), their Tsg-like protein is required for Dpp redistribution independent of Sog (da Fonseca et al. 2010). The wasp N. vitripennis (Hymenoptera) lacks Sog and one of its Tsg family members is required for shaping the Dpp activity gradient without redistribution (Buchta et al. 2013). The milkweed bug Oncopeltus fasciatus (Paraneoptera) has one Tsg-like protein that suppresses Dpp activity ventrally rather than redistributing Dpp dorsally (Sachs et al. 2015).

Within the Dipteran suborder Brachycera (sibling to Nematocera; divergence 256 mya), there are sibling clades that diverged from each other 129 mya. In the clade Calyptratae, the species Musca domestica (house fly) is not in the Drosophila lineage and it has three Tsg family members, one like Tsg and two like Cv (Fig. 2c′). The underlying alignment revealed that M. domestica NCBI 7880 has 67% similarity to D. melanogaster Tsg, NCBI 6377 has 81% similarity to Cv, and NCBI 7270 has 69% similarity to Cv (Supplementary Fig. 1f). Our data clarifies the database annotation of all three as Tsg. As in A. darlingi, the M. domestica Cv-like proteins retain the amino-terminal Dpp-binding domain suggesting the possibility of functional equivalence with Tsg.

The other Brachycera clade is Acalyptratae. This is the Drosophila lineage and there appear to be two trajectories for the Tsg family in Acalyptratae. The first is seen in a Tribe that diverged from D. melanogaster 61 mya where one copy of Cv was lost leaving only Tsg and Cv. This is the Tribe Colocasiomyini (vinegar fly and white striped body fly) that shares numerous adult characteristics with D. melanogaster (Li et al. 2022). The second trajectory is seen in the genus Drosophila, where we examined over a dozen species with fully sequenced genomes. Each one contained the same three Tsg family members: Tsg, Cv, and Shrew. For example, a tree with D. grimshawi (divergence from D. melanogaster 43 mya; Throckmorton 1975) shows three well supported clusters (one each for Tsg, Cv, and Shrew plus a secondary cluster of Cv and Shrew and a tertiary cluster including Tsg (Fig. 2d), mimicking the tree of D. melanogaster sequences. The underlying alignment revealed that D. grimshawi NCBI 1879 has 81% similarity to D. melanogaster Tsg, NCBI 1474 has 86% similarity to Cv, and NCBI 3521 has 66% similarity to Shrew (Supplementary Fig. 1g). Upon examination, D. grimshawi Shrew is also missing its amino-terminal Dpp-binding domain.

From the data in Brachycera we infer that Shrew originated at some point between the divergence of Drosophila from Colocasiomyini 61 mya and the diversification of Drosophila species roughly 43 mya. During this window of 18 million years, one of the two Cv proteins in the Drosophila ancestor lost its amino-terminal Dpp-binding domain and became Shrew. Shrew correlates with reduced time to gastrulation in insects

Given that Shrew is Drosophila specific and that its sole function is in the rapid redistribution of Dpp/Screw heterodimers, our hypothesis is that Shrew is an adaptation to selective pressure for rapid embryonic development. This hypothesis originates in the larger view that minimizing time spent in immobile developmental stages such as eggs reduces a species exposure to predation, parasites, and environmental stress (eg Zalucki et al. 2002; Hermann et al. 2019) . While fewer insects have embryonic timing data than genome sequence, there are enough to test the prediction that Shrew is present only in those with the shortest time from fertilization to gastrulation.

This survey began by examining insects with one Tsg-like protein (Table 2). The group displays a range of “time to gastrulation” much longer than Drosophila, between 11 hours (beetle and mosquito) and 96 hours (dragonfly). Interestingly, the species with the longest time to gastrulation (dragonfly) does not have with the longest egg stage (42 days; aphid). Importantly, the species with the shortest time to gastrulation (11 hours; beetle) also has the shortest egg stage (3 days). In species that have two or three proteins similar to Tsg and/or Cv but not Shrew, the extra family members facilitate a marked decrease in both time to gastrulation and time to hatching. For example, in the multiple Tsg family member group the longest time to gastrulation is 11 hours (mosquito), matching the shortest time in the single Tsg family member group (beetle). The fastest time to gastrulation (4 hours to gastrulation; Schmidt-Ott and Kwan 2022) in the multiple Tsg family member group is found in Megaselia abdita a house fly relative with one Tsg-like and two Cv-like proteins. Megaselia abdita has a mechanism of D/V cell fate specification similar to D. melanogaster (Rafiqi et al. 2012; Kwan et al. 2016) and its time to gastrulation is roughly equal to D. virilis, a species that contains Shrew.

In Drosophila, where all species examined have Shrew, D. melanogaster displays a time to gastrulation and time to hatching that are the shortest among all insects at 3 hours to gastrulation then 22 hours to hatching (at 25 °C). Slower developing Drosophila species such as D. virilis that contain Shrew may have developed additional adaptions such as oviposition site, chorion strength, or increased fecundity to avoid predation on eggs allowing a more measured developmental pace (Markow 2015). Overall, data across insects is consistent with our hypothesis that selection for speed in embryonic development resulted in the generation of Shrew from Cv via truncation, then Shrew was sculpted by selection into an accelerator of D/V axis formation.

Shrew is partially dispensable when development is slowed by reduced temperature

To experimentally test the hypothesis that Shrew accelerates D/V axis specification we examined the response of shrew mutant embryos to slower development induced by reduced temperature. The prediction is that if the accelerator hypothesis is true, then slowing down embryonic development would avoid the need for an accelerator thus allowing normally lethal shrew mutants to survive. Three shrew mutant genotypes were tested as individual homozygotes and as a transheterozygote.

The mutations were shrew1 with a substitution in a cysteine impacting protein folding and shrewB18 with a stop codon shortly after the signal peptide creating a protein null. The mutants were isolated in distinct genetic screens. As a result, transheterozygotes are unlikely to have any second site mutations in common, providing confidence that the results are associated with shrew. From a practical perspective, the stocks in our test were maintained with the shrew mutation balanced over a chromosome with an adult dominant visible marker (either Stubble bristles or Serrate wings). Thus, an individual in the experimental progeny without a balancer marker was a homozygous or transheterozygous shrew mutant that had survived to adulthood.

We examined over 100 progeny for each genotype at each temperature (Table 3). Consistent with previous reports no shrew mutant flies were seen in the two homozygous genotypes, or in the progeny of the cross between shrewB18 and shrew1 at the normal rearing temperature of 25 °C. In the reduced temperature environment of 18 °C, shrew mutant adults were visible in substantial numbers. Note that the expected Mendelian fraction of shrew mutants in the progeny of each test is 33% due to the homozygous lethality of the balancer chromosomes. For the homozygous mutant genotypes, wild type adults were either 13.1% of shrewB18 or 15.6% of shrew1 progeny examined. In comparison to the Mendelian expectation the homozygous genotypes produced between 39.7% and 47.3% of the expected number of adult flies, respectively. For the transheterozygous mutant genotype, wild type adults were 16.6% of the progeny examined. This number reflects 50.3% of the Mendelian expectation. Overall, the experiment showed that in these three genotypes, at reduced temperature shrew was not absolutely essential for survival, consistent the hypothesis that Shrew's sole role is to be an accelerator of D/V axis formation.

Discussion

Specification of the two dorsal-most cell fates in the Drosophila embryo occurs within a 40-minute window immediately prior to gastrulation at approximately three hours post-fertilization. Among the conserved group of D/V patterning proteins, Shrew stands out as an exception with no reports in vertebrates or non-Drosophila insects. Here we report two phylogenetic surveys of Tsg family members across the animal kingdom that reveal that Shrew is a Drosophila specific protein, and that among insects the presence of Shrew correlates with the fastest time to gastrulation. Taken together, the data suggest the hypothesis that Shrew accelerates D/V axis specification in Drosophila as an adaptation to selective pressure to minimize immobile egg duration. The first part of this hypothesis, that Shrew functions as an accelerator of embryonic development is supported by our experimental data.

The second part, that Shrew originated as an adaptation to selective pressure to minimize immobile egg duration, has three alternative hypotheses. However, as described below each of the alternatives can be eliminated as inconsistent with existing data. One alternative hypothesis is based on the fact that D. melanogaster D/V patterning is dependent on Dpp-Screw heterodimers as the morphogen (Shimmi et al. 2005a). This dependence is in contrast to other insects such as Tribolium (da Fonseca et al. 2010) where Dpp homodimers are the morphogen. The alternative hypothesis is that Shrew arose to accommodate Screw. The alternative predicts that Screw and Shrew will co-exist in all species where Screw is present. In our hypothesis test of this alternative, we utilized the cystine knot region, reported as the most conserved portion of Screw (Wisotzkey and Newfeld 2020), to examine the genomes of all species in Table 1. We took special care in the analysis of the mosquito and sandfly genomes in the Nematocera, a sister suborder to the Brachycera. There is no evidence of Screw in any species outside Brachycera (maximum divergence from D. melanogaster 256 mya). But in Brachycera Screw is widespread (Fritsch et al. 2010). There are exceptions such as the white striped body fly where Screw appears to have been lost. Based on their species distribution, the alternative hypothesis fails since Screw is far more widespread than Shrew that is restricted to Drosophila.

In the context of selection for rapid development, our hypothesis for Screw origin is that it is also an adaptation that accelerates embryonic development. Consistent with this Screw origin hypothesis, there are many fast-developing genera in the Brachycera such as Musca, Megaselia and Drosophila that contain Screw. Experimental support for this hypothesis derives from a comparison of Dpp signaling assays in cell culture. An assay employing Dpp homodimers (e.g. Ross et al. 2001) showed maximum signal transduction intensity is induced three hours after exposure, whereas the exact same assay employing Dpp-Screw heterodimers resulted in maximum signal intensity at one hour after exposure (Künnapuu et al. 2014). The phylogenetic and experimental data suggest that Screw is a Brachycera-wide adaption for rapid embryonic development that was subsequently augmented in Drosophila by Shrew.

Returning to Shrew, a second alternative hypothesis is that Shrew is an adaptation to increasing egg size, or more specifically the increasing D/V distance of larger insect eggs. The alternative predicts that increased D/V distance as a proportion of egg length would correlate with the presence of Shrew. For the test, we extracted egg D/V distance (reported as width), and aspect ratio (length/width) from a public dataset of insect egg size (eggdataset.tsv; Church et al. 2019a, 2019b) Consistent with our results, a companion dataset shows that D. melanogaster has the shortest time to hatching of 38 species with egg duration data (development.tsv; Church et al. 2019a, 2019b). We extracted egg data for all species in Table 2 or their closest relative. If a species was present multiple times, then we calculated an average for each of the two measures. The data showed that the presence of Shrew does not correspond to the insect species with the smallest aspect ratio. Note that at an aspect ratio of 1, eggs are spheres with their width equaling their length. Drosophila species with Shrew have oval eggs with the anterior-posterior dimension longer than the D/V dimension producing aspect ratios of 2.722 (D. melanogaster), 3.294 (D. virilis), and 3.375 (D. picticornis). In contrast, Anopheles (2.212), Bombyx (1.192), and Tribolium (1.935) that do not have Shrew, have rounder eggs with greater relative D/V width. The hypothesis that Shrew is an adaptation to increasing insect egg size or simply greater relative egg D/V distance appears false.

In the context of selection for rapid development, a hypothesis for Cv origins can be derived from data on M. domestica eggs. These eggs have a larger aspect ratio (4.364; elongated oval) with a shorter relative D/V distance than a D. melanogaster egg. However, their absolute D/V distance is greater than D. melanogaster as a result of being 3.7-fold larger. Our survey found that M. domestica eggs display nearly as rapid embryonic development as D. melanogaster, with gastrulation achieved in roughly four hours (Hodar and Cambiazo 2018). Although M. domestica does not have Shrew, it has two Cv proteins. As Tsg and Cv are functionally equivalent in D. melanogaster (Shimmi et al. 2005b), if multiple M. domestica Tsg family members are expressed in the early embryo, then perhaps a higher effective concentration of Tsg is achieved leading to increased Tsg efficiency. If this scenario is supported by observations of Tsg/Cv expression in M. domestica early embryos, then this scenario suggests that expansion of the Tsg family could be insect's first response to selection for rapid development with Cv found in even more taxa than Screw. Subsequently, the advent of Shrew in Drosophila may have allowed the redeployment of Cv to wing vein specification.

Returning to Shrew, a third alternative hypothesis for Shrew origin is that Cv is the ancestral Tsg family member in insects. Those authors proposed that during insect diversification Cv duplicated twice to produce Tsg and Shrew, then these two family members lost their introns due to a common function in rapid early development (da Fonseca et al. 2010). Here we note that applying the default logic of Occam's razor to the common structure of Tsg and Shrew to conclude that they both have Cv as a common ancestor does not prevail over evidence to the contrary. There are in fact several independent lines of contrary evidence. First, the presence or absence of introns is not a reliable measure of gene age nor gene lineage (Gilbert et al. 1997; De Souza et al. 1998). The second is that a general feature of the D. melanogaster genome is deleting non-functional DNA leading to a disproportionate number of genes lacking introns (Petrov et al. 1996; Petrov and Hartl 1997). The third is that the depth and breadth of available genome assemblies in insects and throughout the animal kingdom has significantly expanded since 2010. Fourth, the strong similarity between Deuterostome and Protostome Tsg proteins (Fig. 2a) indicates that the ancestor of the family was a Tsg protein that originated early in Bilateria, as noted independently in Moore et al. (2026). While we agree with the previous study that Shrew derives from Cv, it is also clear that their hypothesis of Cv as the ancestral Tsg family member is false.

To shed light on how Shrew performs its accelerator role, we examined a recent report of the molecular interactions underlying Tsg facilitation of Sog cleavage in Sog-Dpp-Screw complexes to release the Dpp-Screw heterodimer morphogen (Moore et al. 2026). Our inferences are based on this study's demonstrated roles for regions in Tsg that are highly conserved in Shrew. One similarity is that Shrew's α2 helix is homologous to the α4 helix in Tsg that binds Sog, suggesting that Shrew could interact with Sog in the Sog-Dpp-Screw complex via this helix. On the other hand, there are two important structural distinctions between the Tsg and Shrew proteins. First, Shrew does not have an amino-terminal Dpp-binding domain that could form a second linkage to the Sog-Dpp-Screw complex. Second, Shrew does not have an extended carboxy-terminal tail that provides Tsg with a second Sog interface and its third connection to the Sog-Dpp-Screw complex. These three binding domains ensure that the Tsg is stably bound to the Sog-Dpp-Screw complex enabling it to facilitate Tld cleavage of Sog (Moore et al. 2026). The presence of just one of the three Tsg binding domains in Shrew suggested to us that Shrew may performs its accelerator function by transiently binding to Sog and increasing the efficiency of Tsg facilitation of Sog cleavage by Tld.

From a wider perspective, increasing Tsg efficiency in the cleavage of Sog as a way to accelerate D/V patterning in the early embryo could also be facilitated by the presence of multiple, functionally equivalent Tsg family proteins. Multiple Tsg family members are present in all fast-developing insects without Shrew, such as N. vitripennis, A. darlingi, and M. domestica. The widespread presence in Dipterans of multiple Tsg family members suggests that accelerating the pace of embryonic development has provided a selective advantage for many millions of years.

Together our experimental and evolutionary data for Shrew indicate that developmental mechanisms such as D/V axis specification are in fact phenotypes capable of adapting to selective pressure, in the same manner as adult phenotypes such as eyespots (Brakefield et al. 1996) or abdominal pigmentation (Kopp et al. 2000). Results consistent with the hypothesis that developmental mechanisms are adaptable phenotypes were recently reported from a laboratory selection experiment for reduced time to hatching in Tribolium. The adaption that emerged was an enhancer mutation impacting ecdysone expression that shifted forward the late stage developmental process of dorsal closure (Cheng et al. 2024).

In summary, our results suggest that as a result of environmental pressure for rapid egg development Shrew was created from a copy of Cv, then sculpted by natural selection to become a D/V axis specification accelerator. We conclude that Shrew is an exemplar of a new gene that modifies a developmental pathway as an adaptation to an identified selective pressure. From a larger perspective, Shrew data advances our understanding of gene-environment interactions by revealing that developmental processes are adaptable phenotypes and that complex genetic alterations, in addition to enhancer point mutations, can generate effective responses to selection.
