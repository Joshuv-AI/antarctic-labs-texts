Quantitative Study of Elbow Ligaments in Modern Humans (Homo sapiens) and Common Chimpanzees (Pan troglodytes)
Authors: Josep Maria Potau, Aroa Casado, Patrícia Rodríguez, Marcel García‐Cuesta, Neus Ciurana, Félix Jesús de Paz, Mercedes Barbosa, Isabel Julia San José, Roberto Cabo, Juan Francisco Pastor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesWe have analyzed the elbow ligaments in humans and chimpanzees in order to identify differences that may be related to the use of the upper limbs in these two species. Specifically, we expected to find adaptations to arboreal locomotion and knuckle‐walking in chimpanzees and adaptations to non‐weight‐bearing manual activities in humans.Materials and MethodsWe dissected seven upper limbs from extant humans and seven from common chimpanzees and performed anatomical and qualitative analyses of the elbow ligaments. Subsequently, we calculated the absolute and relative size of the mass of these ligaments in the two species.ResultsThe anatomical description of the elbow ligaments was very similar in the two species. The quantitative analysis found a relatively larger anterior fascicle of the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) in humans and a relatively larger lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL) in chimpanzees.DiscussionThe different ways humans and chimpanzees use their upper limbs could explain the quantitative differences we observed in the elbow ligaments. We can thus hypothesize that the more developed anterior fascicle of the UCL in humans may be related to a more pronounced carrying angle in the upper limb and to the valgus forces acting on the elbow during manual tasks. In contrast, the larger LUCL in chimpanzees may help stabilize the elbow during arboreal locomotion and during the support phase of knuckle‐walking.Keywords: lateral ulnar collateral ligament, primates, ulnar collateral ligament

Introduction

Primates of the superfamily Hominoidea (gibbons, orangutans, gorillas, chimpanzees, and humans) have diverse locomotor behavior, including arboreal locomotion like brachiation, vertical climbing, and arm suspension, and terrestrial locomotion like knuckle‐walking and bipedalism (Gebo 2014). Chimpanzees ( Pan troglodytes ) and bonobos ( Pan paniscus ), the hominoid primates most closely related to modern humans ( Homo sapiens ) (Prüfer et al. 2012), combine arboreal locomotion (vertical climbing and arm suspension) and quadrupedal terrestrial locomotion (knuckle‐walking) (Hunt 1991). In these primates, the elbow plays a key functional role as it must combine adaptations for mobility and resistance to tensile forces related to arboreal locomotion with adaptations for stability and compressive forces related to knuckle‐walking (Rose 1993). Some of the most important anatomical adaptations that increase elbow stability in extension during the support phase of knuckle‐walking in chimpanzees are the greater depth of the olecranon fossa and the large projection of the lateral border of the humeral trochlea (Figure 1) (Aiello and Dean 1990; Richmond et al. 2001). Humans, on the other hand, do not possess these stabilizing adaptations because bipedalism frees the upper limb from its locomotor function and allows it to specialize in the performance of precise manipulative tasks (Gebo 2014).

Joint stability is ensured not only by adaptations of the bones in the elbow joint (distal epiphysis of the humerus, proximal epiphysis of the radius, and proximal epiphysis of the ulna) but also by the ligaments and muscles that protect and mobilize the joint (Nordin and Frankel 2001). Because the elbow joint capsule is protected anteriorly by the brachialis muscle and posteriorly by the triceps brachii muscle, it has two main collateral ligaments: a medial—or ulnar—complex and a lateral—or radial—complex (Stroyan and Wilk 1993) (Figure 2).

The white arrow indicates the anterior subunit of the anterior ulnar collateral ligament; the black arrow indicates the posterior subunit of the anterior ulnar collateral ligament.

The medial ligamentous complex (MLC) of the elbow joint is represented by the ulnar collateral ligament (UCL) (Malagelada et al. 2014), which is the primary stabilizer of the elbow during daily manual activities (Terzini et al. 2017). In humans, the UCL consists of an anterior fascicle (UCLA), a posterior fascicle (UCLP), and a transverse fascicle (UCLT) (Neumann 2022) (Figure 2). The UCLA is the most highly reinforced fascicle of the UCL (Neumann 2022) and acts as the primary resistance against valgus forces acting on the elbow joint (Karbach and Elfar 2017; Malagelada et al. 2014; Tomsick and Petersen 2010). The UCLA extends from the medial epicondyle of the humerus to the medial surface of the coronoid process of the ulna (Karbach and Elfar 2017; Lühmann et al. 2022). The posterior fibers of the UCLA are taut during elbow flexion, while the anterior fibers are taut during elbow extension (Malagelada et al. 2014; Neumann 2022; Stroyan and Wilk 1993; Tomsick and Petersen 2010). The UCLP extends from the medial epicondyle of the humerus to the medial surface of the olecranon (Karbach and Elfar 2017; Lühmann et al. 2022) and is taut during extreme elbow flexion (Neumann 2022; Stroyan and Wilk 1993; Tomsick and Petersen 2010). Finally, the UCLT extends from the medial surface of the olecranon to the medial surface of the coronoid process. It connects the distal insertions of the anterior and posterior fascicles but has no significant stabilizing function (Lühmann et al. 2022; Malagelada et al. 2014; Neumann 2022; Stroyan and Wilk 1993; Tomsick and Petersen 2010).

The lateral ligamentous complex (LLC) of the elbow joint is comprised in humans of the radial collateral ligament (RCL) and the lateral ulnar collateral ligament (LUCL) (Figure 2) (Malagelada et al. 2014; Neumann 2022). The RCL extends from the lateral epicondyle of the humerus to the annular ligament (AL) (Lühmann et al. 2022). It tenses during elbow flexion and extension (Hackl et al. 2016; Tomsick and Petersen 2010), stabilizing the radial head (Karbach and Elfar 2017) and opposing varus forces acting on the elbow (Stroyan and Wilk 1993; Tomsick and Petersen 2010). The LUCL is thicker than the RCL in humans (Neumann 2022). It is located dorsally to the RCL (Figure 2) and extends from the lateral epicondyle of the humerus to the supinator crest of the ulna (Lühmann et al. 2022; Stroyan and Wilk 1993; Tokunaga et al. 2017; Tomsick and Petersen 2010). The LUCL interconnects with the annular ligament (Malagelada et al. 2014). It tenses during full elbow flexion and supination, opposing varus forces acting on the elbow joint (Karbach and Elfar 2017; Malagelada et al. 2014; Neumann 2022; Noriego et al. 2023; Tokunaga et al. 2017).

The elbow joint also has the annular ligament, which is the primary stabilizing structure of the proximal radioulnar joint (Stroyan and Wilk 1993; Malagelada et al. 2014). This ligament attaches to the anterior and posterior margins of the radial notch of the ulna and encircles the head of the radius, forming an osteofibrous ring that allows axial rotational movements of the radial head associated with supination and pronation. The anterior region of the AL becomes taut during supination, while its posterior region becomes taut during pronation (Morrey and An 2000).

Although the anatomical and functional characteristics of the elbow ligaments have been widely examined in humans, to the best of our knowledge, no studies have analyzed these structures in other primate species, despite their importance to our understanding of joint function (Nordin and Frankel 2001). Traditionally, comparative anatomy studies of the musculoskeletal system in primates have focused on the study of bones and muscles (Carlson 2006; Michilsens et al. 2009; Oishi et al. 2009; Van Leeuwen et al. 2018) but have not explored joint ligaments—with the exception of specific studies of wrist ligaments (Potau et al. 2022; Sarmiento 1988; Tuttle 1969) or the trapeziometacarpal joint (Shrewsbury 2003; Van Leeuwen et al. 2019).

In order to shed further light on differences in the elbow joint of humans and primates, we have quantitatively analyzed the elbow ligaments in humans and in common chimpanzees, which are closely related phylogenetically to modern humans (Almécija et al. 2021). Our main hypothesis was that the functional differences in the elbow of chimpanzees and humans, resulting from their different uses of the upper limb, would translate into anatomical differences in the ligaments between the two species. Specifically, we expected that the anatomical characteristics of the elbow ligaments of chimpanzees would be related to the greater stability of their elbow joint, which enables them to withstand the forces generated during arboreal locomotion and, especially, during the support phase of knuckle‐walking (Aiello and Dean 1990; Richmond et al. 2001). In contrast, we expected that the anatomical aspects of the elbow ligaments of humans would be related to their greater specialization in non‐weight‐bearing manual activities (Gebo 2014). With this study, we aimed to increase our knowledge of the anatomical aspects of the elbow joint in chimpanzees, which would provide us with a better understanding of the functional characteristics of this joint in Pan troglodytes , as well as of the anatomical and functional characteristics of the elbow in humans. Specifically, we believe that the results obtained from the study of the ligaments will allow us to determine whether there are additional anatomical adaptations that increase elbow stability in chimpanzees, beyond those that are strictly osteological or muscular. We believe that our results can be applied in different fields of knowledge, including comparative anatomy, evolutionary anatomy, and biological anthropology.

Materials and Methods

Dissections

We dissected seven upper limbs from modern humans ( Homo sapiens ) and seven upper limbs from common chimpanzees ( Pan troglodytes ). One chimpanzee and one human specimen were excluded from the study due to marked osteoarthritis of the elbow joint identified after dissection. All limbs were obtained from individuals cryopreserved at −18°C with no fixation method. The human limbs were obtained from the Body Donation Service of the University of Barcelona and were dissected at the Human Anatomy and Embryology Unit of the Faculty of Medicine and Health Sciences at the same university. The specimens were one right and five left upper limbs from male individuals aged between 81 and 91 years (mean age 85.7 years). The chimpanzee limbs were from adults from various Spanish zoos that had died from causes unrelated to this study. They were obtained from and dissected at the Anatomical Museum of the University of Valladolid. The specimens were three right and three left upper limbs from five females and two males. In both humans and chimpanzees, each specimen provided data from only one of its limbs, as specified in Table 1.

All dissections were performed by the same researcher (JMP), who dissected all the limb muscles and the elbow ligaments. Photographic images of all these anatomical structures and different regions of interest were obtained using a Canon EOS‐50 camera. The dissected muscles were identified and isolated, and the mass in grams of each muscle was obtained with a Sartorius PT610 precision balance with a resolution of 0.1 g. The ligaments of the elbow joint were also identified and studied, and their degree of tension during flexion and extension of the elbow joint was analyzed. Ligament tension was assessed qualitatively by visual inspection and manual testing with forceps at maximal elbow flexion and maximal extension. The ligament was considered adequately tensioned when no slack could be detected. Subsequently, each ligament (UCLA, UCLP, UCLT, RCL, LUCL, AL) was isolated and its mass in grams was determined using a Kern‐EW precision balance with a resolution of 0.001 g. Finally, after removal, the thickness of each ligament was also measured at its central region using a digital caliper. Using the masses of the ligaments that stabilize the humerus with the ulna and the radius (UCLAm, UCLPm, UCLTm, RCLm, and LUCLm), the total mass of the MLC (MLCm = UCLAm + UCLPm + UCLTm) and the LLC (LLCm = RCLm + LUCLm) were calculated, as well as the total mass of the elbow ligaments (ELm = MLCm + LLCm). The mass of the AL was not used to calculate the total mass of the elbow ligaments, as the AL does not contribute to the stabilization of the humeroulnar and humeroradial joints. Using these values, the following relative values were calculated: the percentage of the mass of each ligament relative to the total ELm (%UCLAm1, %UCLPm1, %UCLTm1, %RCLm1, and %LUCLm1); the percentage of the mass of each ligamentous complex relative to the total ELm (%MLCm and %LLCm); the percentage of the mass of each fascicle of the MLC relative to the total MLCm (%UCLAm2, %UCLPm2, and %UCLTm2); and the percentage of the mass of each ligament of the LLC relative to the total LLCm (%RCLm2 and %LUCLm2).

The relative mass of the elbow ligaments relative to the articular surface (AS) of the distal humeral epiphysis (ELm/AS), formed by the humeral trochlea and the humeral capitulum (Figure 1), was also obtained. This parameter was selected to normalize the total mass of the elbow ligaments and allow for comparison between chimpanzees and humans. To calculate the AS, three‐dimensional models of the distal humeral epiphysis of the dissected specimens were obtained using an EINSCAN‐SP V2 scanner, and the AS of each model was calculated using MeshLab 2021.05 software. Because the size of the distal humerus AS is related to functional parameters, such as locomotor type, as well as to body size, to corroborate the results obtained with the ELm/AS parameter, the total mass of the elbow ligaments was also normalized by the anteroposterior diameter of the radial head (RHD), and the ELm/RHD parameter was calculated for each of the specimens.

To avoid the limitations associated with ratio‐based normalizations and to properly assess size effects within an allometric framework, natural logarithmic transformations were applied to the total mass of the elbow ligaments (logELm) and to the size proxies used (logAS and logRHD). Allometric relationships were evaluated using log–log linear regression models of the form logELm ~ logAS + Species and logELm ~ logRHD + Species, where ELm was the dependent variable, the size proxy was included as a covariate, and species was included as a fixed factor. Model significance, parameter estimates, and explained variance (R 2 and adjusted R 2) were used to assess allometric patterns and interspecies differences in ligament mass after accounting for size.

To further assess deviations from the common allometric scaling pattern, residual analyses were performed using a pooled species model. A linear regression between logELm and logAS was fitted without including species as a predictor. Residuals from this model were extracted and used as size‐adjusted measures of deviation from the expected ligament mass. Interspecies differences in residuals were tested using Welch's t‐test and the non‐parametric Wilcoxon test.

In addition, to examine size‐independent variation in the proportion of individual elbow ligaments, a geometric mean normalization following Mosimann's approach was applied. The geometric mean was calculated for each specimen using the masses of the five main elbow ligaments (UCLAm, UCLPm, UCLTm, RCLm, and LUCLm), and each ligament mass was divided by this value to obtain size‐adjusted proportional variables. These normalized variables were analyzed using principal component analysis (PCA) based on centered and scaled data. Species differences in PC scores were assessed using Welch's t‐test and the Wilcoxon test.

Finally, for each dissected specimen, the mass of the muscles that exert valgus forces at the elbow (extensor carpi ulnaris, extensor digitorum, extensor carpi radialis longus, extensor carpi radialis brevis, and brachioradialis) and the mass of the muscles that exert varus forces at the elbow (flexor carpi radialis, flexor digitorum superficialis, pronator teres, and flexor carpi ulnaris) were obtained (An et al. 1981; Buchanan et al. 1998). With these values, the total mass of the valgus muscles of the elbow (VALm) and the total mass of the varus muscles (VARm) were obtained, as well as the relative proportion of the mass of each group of muscles (VALm/VARm).

Statistical Analyses

Sample normality was tested in SPSS Statistics 29 software using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Comparisons between humans and chimpanzees were carried out with the parametric t‐test. Statistical significance was set at p ≤ 0.05. Logarithmic transformations, allometric residual analyses, and geometric mean normalization were performed using R software version 4.6.0 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

The research complied with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Barcelona (IRB00003099).

Results

Qualitative Analysis of the Ligaments

The anatomical arrangement of the elbow ligaments in chimpanzees was the same as that in modern humans, with a MLC, a LLC, and an AL (Figure 3). The MLC is formed by the UCL, in which the UCLA, the UCLP, and the UCLT fascicles can be identified (Figure 3). All three fascicles have the same insertions as those in modern humans. The only difference between humans and chimpanzees in our study was that the UCLA typically had distinct anterior and posterior subunits in humans (Figure 2), while these two subunits were not clearly identifiable macroscopically in chimpanzees (Figure 3). The LLC consists of the RCL ventrally and the LUCL dorsally (Figure 3), which are closely related to the annular ligament. Both the RCL and the LUCL had the same insertions in our chimpanzees and humans, but the LUCL was visually more prominent and distinct within the complex in chimpanzees.

Dissection of the ligaments of a left elbow in Pan troglodytes in a medial (a) and lateral (b) view. 1 = anterior ulnar collateral ligament; 2 = posterior ulnar collateral ligament; 3 = transverse ulnar collateral ligament; 4 = radial collateral ligament; 5 = lateral ulnar collateral ligament; 6 = annular ligament.

The qualitative assessment of the degree of tension in the different ligaments during elbow flexion and extension also showed similar results in chimpanzees and humans. The anterior fibers of the UCLA and the RCL were tensed primarily during elbow extension, while the posterior fibers of the UCLA, the UCLP, and the LUCL were tensed during elbow flexion.

Quantitative Analysis of the Ligaments

The main quantitative results of the 12 specimens are summarized in Table 1, Figure 4 and the Table S1. The raw data obtained in the allometric analyses are available as Data S1.

Bar chart showing the values of the elbow ligaments relative to the total ligament mass in Homo sapiens and Pan troglodytes . UCLA = ulnar collateral ligament anterior; UCLP = ulnar collateral ligament posterior; UCLT = ulnar collateral ligament transverse; RCL = radial collateral ligament; LUCL = lateral ulnar collateral ligament; m1 = relative mass to the total ELm. *Statistical significance.

Chimpanzees had greater overall ligament mass, both in absolute values (ELm: 3.22 ± 0.58 g vs. 2.24 ± 0.36 g; p = 0.006), in values normalized to the AS of the distal humeral epiphysis (ELm/AS: 0.0016 ± 0.0003 vs. 0.0013 ± 0.0002; p = 0.044) and in values normalized to the RHD (ELm/RHD: 0.13 ± 0.02 vs. 0.10 ± 0.02; p = 0.027). Additionally, humans had a larger MLC relative to the total ELm (%MLCm: 54.7% ± 3.3% vs. 47.2% ± 6.2%; p = 0.025), while chimpanzees had a larger LLC relative to the total ELm (%LLCm: 52.8% ± 6.2% vs. 45.3% ± 3.3%; p = 0.025). The AL had a significantly greater absolute mass in chimpanzees (ALm: 2.47 ± 0.38 vs. 1.60 ± 0.17; p < 0.001) and its mass normalized by RDH was also significantly higher in chimpanzees (ALm/RHD: 0.10 ± 0.01 vs. 0.07 ± 0.01; p < 0.001). However, no differences were observed between the two species when comparing AL mass normalized by AS (ALm/AS: 0.77 ± 0.09 vs. 0.74 ± 0.19; p = 0.693).

The results of the allometric analyses indicated that interspecies differences in ELm could not be explained solely by joint size. In the model including distal humeral AS as a covariate (logELm ~ logAS + Species), Species had a significant effect (β = 0.2831, SE = 0.1114, t = 2.540, p = 0.0317), with higher ELm values in Pan troglodytes compared to Homo sapiens after accounting for size. The overall model was significant (F = 7.984, p = 0.01014) and explained a substantial proportion of the variance (R 2 = 0.6395; adjusted R 2 = 0.5594). The effect of logAS was not significant (p = 0.1948). This pattern is illustrated in Figure 5, where Pan troglodytes is consistently above Homo sapiens . A similar pattern was observed when radial head diameter was used as an alternative size proxy (logELm ~ logRHD + Species), with a consistent but non‐significant effect for Species (β = 0.3847, p = 0.0694). The model was significant (F = 5.775, p = 0.02434; R 2 = 0.5621), whereas logRHD was not (p = 0.8814).

The results of the allometric residual analysis showed patterns consistent with those of the regression models (Figure 6). Homo sapiens exhibited negative mean residuals (−0.1061), whereas Pan troglodytes showed positive mean residuals (0.1061), indicating higher‐than‐expected ELm values relative to size in the latter species. Differences between species showed a trend towards statistical significance (t = −2.1355, p = 0.05885; Wilcoxon p = 0.06494).

When we calculated the size of each ligament relative to the total ELm, we found that humans had a significantly larger UCLA (%UCLAm1: 25.2% ± 1.9% vs. 22.6% ± 2.0%; p = 0.046) and RCL (%RCLm1: 15.9% ± 2.5% vs. 12.3% ± 2.9%; p = 0.040), as well as a near‐significantly larger UCLP (%UCLPm1: 22.4% ± 3.9% vs. 17.2% ± 4.8%; p = 0.068). Conversely, chimpanzees had a larger LUCL (%LUCLm1: 40.6% ± 5.5% vs. 29.4% ± 3.5%; p = 0.002). No significant differences between humans and chimpanzees were observed in the relative size of the UCLT. These results are supported by data obtained from measuring ligament thickness (Table 1), as the UCLA had the greatest thickness in humans (mean of 3.3 ± 0.7 mm), whereas the LUCL had the greatest thickness in chimpanzees (mean of 4.7 ± 0.9 mm).

The PCA of variables normalized using the geometric mean revealed differences between the two species in the proportional mass of each elbow ligament (Figure 7). The first PC (PC1) explained 41.61% of the variance, and the second PC (PC2) explained 29.63%; together they accounted for 71.24% of the total variation. The remaining PCs were excluded from the analysis because each accounted for less than 20% of the total variance (PC3 = 19.67%; PC4 = 8.65%; PC5 = 0.44%). PC1 loadings indicated that negative scores were associated with a higher relative contribution of the UCLAm and RCLm, whereas positive scores were associated with a higher contribution of the UCLTm and LUCLm. PC1 scores differed significantly between species (t = −2.3009, p = 0.04527), with lower mean values in Homo sapiens (−0.8125) and higher values in Pan troglodytes (0.8125). The Wilcoxon test approaches statistical significance (p = 0.09307). No significant differences between species were observed for PC2 (p > 0.05).

Principal component analysis (PCA) biplot of elbow ligament proportions with 95% confidence ellipses. The squares and triangles represent individual specimens grouped by species, and arrows indicate the loading vectors of each ligament variable on the first two principal components.

The analysis of the size of each ligament relative to the overall size of each ligamentous complex (MLCm and LLCm) revealed no significant differences between humans and chimpanzees in the size of any of the three fascicles of the UCL relative to the total MLCm. However, relative to the total LLCm, humans had a significantly larger RCL (RCLm2: 35.2% ± 5.5% vs. 23.3% ± 5.3%; p = 0.003), while chimpanzees had a significantly larger LUCL (LUCLm2: 76.7% ± 5.3% vs. 64.8% ± 5.5%; p = 0.003).

Finally, humans had a larger relative size of the forearm muscles exerting valgus forces at the elbow, while chimpanzees had a larger relative size of the muscles exerting varus forces at the elbow (VALm/VARm of 0.91 ± 0.10 in humans vs. 0.78 ± 0.05 in chimpanzees; p = 0.024) (Table 1).

Discussion

Qualitative Analysis

Our qualitative findings highlight the anatomical and functional similarity of the elbow ligaments in Pan troglodytes and Homo sapiens . Anatomically, both species possess the same ligaments (UCL, RCL, and LUCL), with the same fascicles (UCLA, UCLP, and UCLT) and insertions, indicating a high degree of anatomical stability of the elbow ligaments in these two species, similar to that observed in other upper limb ligaments, such as the wrist (Potau et al. 2022). Moreover, although the osteological structures of the elbow joint exhibit anatomical adaptations for arboreal locomotion and knuckle‐walking in chimpanzees and for non‐weight‐bearing manipulative tasks in humans (Aiello and Dean 1990; Gebo 2014), these adaptations do not affect the gross functional characteristics of the ligaments. In both humans and chimpanzees, the anterior fibers of the UCLA and RCL become taut during elbow extension, which approaches 180° in hominid primates (Neumann 2022; Fannin et al. 2023), whereas the posterior fibers of the UCLA, UCLP, and LUCL become taut during elbow flexion, which reaches approximately 30° in hominid primates (Rose 1993; Neumann 2022).

Quantitative Analysis

In contrast, the quantitative analysis of the ligaments revealed differences between humans and chimpanzees that may be related to their different uses of the upper limb. The total ELm was larger in chimpanzees than in humans, in both absolute and relative terms (Table 1). This finding, added to the greater AL mass that we also observed, may be related to the greater degree of elbow stability in chimpanzees (Zihlman and Underwood 2019), which they need both to withstand the traction forces acting on the elbow during suspensory arboreal locomotion (Hunt 1991; Whitehead 1993) as well as to compensate for the forces acting on the elbow during the support phase of knuckle‐walking (Pontzer et al. 2014; Thompson et al. 2018). Thus, the greater mass of the elbow ligaments in chimpanzees complements other osteological adaptations that stabilize the elbow, such as the greater depth of the olecranon fossa and the projection of the lateral border of the humeral trochlea (Richmond et al. 2001).

The humans in our study had a relatively larger MLC (Table 1), formed exclusively by the UCL, which is the main stabilizer of the elbow during everyday manual activities (Terzini et al. 2017). This may be related to the more pronounced carrying angle in humans compared to other hominoid primates (Aiello and Dean 1990; Ibáñez‐Gimeno et al. 2014; Rose 1993). The carrying angle is the angle between the longitudinal axis of the humerus and the longitudinal axis of the ulna when the elbow is fully extended and the hand is supinated. The average carrying angle is 11°–14° in men and 13°–16° in women (Malagelada et al. 2014; Paraskevas et al. 2004; Stroyan and Wilk 1993). The more pronounced carrying angle in humans compared to other hominoid primates (Knussmann 1967) facilitates a functionally advantageous hand position during manipulative tasks and tool use (Aiello and Dean 1990; Rose 1993) and determines the abducted position of the hand when the upper limb is extended and supinated. This abduction of the hand may increase the valgus forces acting on the elbow in humans, which would explain their larger MLC. This is borne out by the fact that the UCLA, which is the main stabilizing element of the elbow against valgus forces acting on it throughout its functional range of motion (30°–120°) (Karbach and Elfar 2017; Malagelada et al. 2014; Tomsick and Petersen 2010), is the only fascicle of the UCL that had a significantly larger relative mass in humans than in chimpanzees (Table 1). Although the UCLP also had a higher relative mass in humans, these differences were not statistically significant (Table 1). The UCLP also resists valgus forces acting on the elbow, but unlike the UCLA, it only does so during maximal elbow flexion (Neumann 2022; Stroyan and Wilk 1993; Tomsick and Petersen 2010). Finally, the UCLT, which has no stabilizing function at the elbow (Lühmann et al. 2022; Malagelada et al. 2014; Neumann 2022; Stroyan and Wilk 1993; Tomsick and Petersen 2010), had a similar relative mass in humans and chimpanzees (Table 1).

Another factor that may explain the larger relative size of the MLC in humans is the fact that supination and pronation of the hand are accompanied by slight adduction and abduction, respectively, of the ulna (Rose 1993; Palmer et al. 1982; Pirela‐Cruz et al. 1991). From the supinated position to the pronated position, the ulna describes a semilunar movement, with an initial varus shift (supination), followed by a dorsal shift and ending with a valgus shift (pronation) (Kasten et al. 2004). The abduction movement that accompanies pronation of the hand, which is the functional position of the hand during manual tasks, could lead to an increase in valgus forces acting on the elbow, which would then be compensated for by a larger relative size of the MLC, particularly the UCLA (Table 1). However, this interpretation should be considered with caution, as no studies have reported that the abduction movement accompanying pronation is greater in humans than in chimpanzees. Finally, the larger relative size of the MLC in humans may also be related to the greater relative mass of the muscles that exert valgus forces on the elbow, as observed in our human specimens (Table 1).

The chimpanzees in our study had a relatively larger LLC than humans, but there were differences in the two species between the two ligaments of the LLC. The RCL was larger in humans, both relative to the total mass of the elbow ligaments (%RCLm1) and to the total mass of the LLC (%RCLm2). In contrast, the LUCL was significantly larger in chimpanzees, both relative to the total mass of the elbow ligaments (%LUCLm1) and to the total mass of the LLC (%LUCLm2). This larger LUCL in chimpanzees may be directly related to their greater need for elbow stabilization due to their use of the upper limb for arboreal locomotion and knuckle‐walking (Hunt 1992). The LUCL is known to be one of the main stabilizing elements of the elbow in humans (Noriego et al. 2023). It compensates for varus forces acting on the elbow and it tenses during full elbow flexion and supination (Hackl et al. 2016; Malagelada et al. 2014; Neumann 2022; Regan et al. 1991; Tokunaga et al. 2017). This may explain its larger size in chimpanzees, as it would provide increased elbow stability during arboreal locomotion and vertical climbing, two forms of locomotion in which elbow flexion and supination are highly important (Ibáñez‐Gimeno et al. 2014; Oishi et al. 2008; Schmitt 2003). Elbow flexion is particularly important in chimpanzee vertical climbing when they use small or medium‐sized substrates, especially during the early to mid‐support phase until the early swing phase (Neufuss et al. 2017), whereas forearm supination occurs in chimpanzees during the support phase of suspensory‐type arboreal locomotion (Stern and Larson 2001).

In addition to stabilizing the elbow during arboreal locomotion, the large size of the LUCL in chimpanzees may be even more important in stabilizing the elbow during knuckle‐walking. Indeed, knuckle‐walking is the most frequent form of locomotion among chimpanzees (Hunt 1991; Crompton et al. 2010; Arias‐Martorell et al. 2021) and is estimated to represent up to 85% of their entire locomotor repertoire (Crompton et al. 2010). During the support phase of knuckle‐walking, the upper limb of chimpanzees experiences a medially oriented ground reaction force, which produces a medial bending of the ulna that translates into a varus force acting on the elbow (Schmitt 1994). This varus force involves a lateral displacement of the olecranon that is resisted by the greater depth of the olecranon fossa, which has a highly developed lateral wall in chimpanzees, as well as by the greater prominence of the lateral border of the humeral trochlea (Aiello and Dean 1990; Richmond et al. 2001). In addition to these osteological adaptations, the larger relative mass of the LUCL in chimpanzees resists or reinforces against the varus forces acting on the elbow in this phase, given that the LUCL is the main compensatory element for varus forces (Malagelada et al. 2014). Finally, the larger size of the LUCL in chimpanzees would also compensate for the greater relative mass of the muscles exerting varus forces on the elbow in chimpanzees compared to humans (Table 1).

Limitations

One of the main limitations of our study is the small number of individuals analyzed. The difficulty inherent in obtaining chimpanzee cadavers meant that we had only six chimpanzee limbs available for inclusion in the study, and a similar sample size of humans was necessary to balance the two groups. Therefore, our findings must be interpreted with caution, especially in terms of statistical significance. Nevertheless, we believe that presenting results from six specimens provides a valid understanding of the anatomical differences in elbow ligaments between humans and chimpanzees arising from the different uses the two species make of their upper limbs. In addition, our results may be influenced by the fact that in our human specimens, five limbs were left and one was right, but no information was available on the handedness of the individuals. Another clear limitation of the study is the advanced age of the human subjects, which is inherent to the use of human cadavers donated to science. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution, as these specimens may not reflect the morphological and functional characteristics of younger, living adults with typical activity levels.

Additionally, our qualitative estimation of the degree of ligament tension in humans and chimpanzees should be interpreted as a preliminary approach that needs corroboration with quantitative methods, such as tensiometers or tension sensors. Finally, we had no information on the types of pathologies these individuals had suffered throughout their lives. It would be beneficial to confirm our findings in a larger study including female and younger individuals in the human sample.

Conclusions

The present study of elbow ligaments in humans and chimpanzees has identified their morphological and functional stability and has also revealed quantitative differences that can be related to the different uses these two species make of their upper limbs. We have confirmed our initial hypothesis that chimpanzees have a greater absolute and relative mass of elbow ligaments, which increases their stability against the forces acting on the elbow during arboreal locomotion and during the support phase of knuckle‐walking.

The human specimens in our study had a greater relative mass of the UCL, especially the UCLA, which can be related to a more pronounced carrying angle and the need to compensate for valgus forces acting on the elbow during non‐weight‐bearing manual tasks and tool use. The relatively large size of the UCLA in humans may also be clinically relevant, as it allows for anatomical reconstruction using tendon grafts to ensure valgus stability of the elbow, since it is composed of a large number of packed parallel collagenous fibers and few elastic fibers (Dunning et al. 2001; Lühmann et al. 2022). In contrast, the chimpanzees in our study had a greater relative mass of the LUCL, which is crucial for stabilizing the elbow during vertical climbing and suspensory arboreal locomotion as well as during the support phase of knuckle‐walking.

Our findings can contribute to a better understanding of the anatomy and function of the elbow joint in chimpanzees. Since these primates are the species most closely related to humans, we believe that these results will also increase our understanding of human anatomical adaptations to the use of the upper limb in manipulative activities. For example, the detailed analysis of the elbow ligaments and their attachment sites, particularly those located in specific regions of the distal humerus and proximal ulna, may enable the identification of these structures in fossil primate remains and provide insights into when these adaptations first appeared. Our findings can thus be used in several fields, including comparative and evolutionary anatomy and biological anthropology.
