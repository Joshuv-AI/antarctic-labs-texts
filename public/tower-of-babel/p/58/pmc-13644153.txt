Diagnostic confirmation of adult sinonasal rhabdomyosarcoma through comprehensive immunohistochemical profiling: a case report
Authors: Bijan Khademi, Alireza Yousefi, Milad Bagheri, Shayan Yousufzai, Negar Fatehi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAdult sinonasal rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is an exceptionally rare and aggressive malignancy originating from mesenchymal cells. It poses a significant diagnostic challenge due to its morphological and immunohistochemical overlap with other small round blue cell tumors, such as neuroendocrine carcinoma and olfactory neuroblastoma. Accurate diagnosis requires a high index of suspicion, advanced imaging, and comprehensive histopathology aided with immunohistochemistry (IHC) for specific myogenic markers like Myogenin and MyoD1.Case presentationA 27-year-old male presented with recurrent epistaxis and headache. MRI revealed an enhancing ethmoidal mass with intracranial extension. Following endoscopic surgery, initial pathology suggested neuroendocrine carcinoma. However, a detailed IHC panel showed strong nuclear positivity for MyoD1 and myogenin, confirming RMS. The tumor had a high Ki-67 index (~ 70%). FISH testing was negative for FOXO1 rearrangement, favoring embryonal subtype. Postoperative imaging confirmed complete resection. The patient received adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy.ConclusionThis case underscores the diagnostic difficulty of sinonasal RMS. A definitive diagnosis is contingent upon integrating imaging findings with histopathology and an extensive IHC panel to identify myogenic differentiation. Once confirmed, prompt, multimodal therapy administered through a specialized multidisciplinary center is critical for managing this aggressive neoplasm and optimizing patient outcome.

Introduction

Rhabdomyosarcoma (RMS) is a malignant soft tissue sarcoma originating from primitive mesenchymal cells that exhibit skeletal muscle differentiation, often arising in sites devoid of normal skeletal muscle tissue, such as the nasal cavity [1–5]. In adults, RMS of the nasal cavity represents an exceedingly rare entity, predominantly affecting pediatric populations where it constitutes a significant proportion of head and neck sarcomas, but comprising only a minor fraction of adult soft tissue malignancies [3, 4, 6]. The incidence of sinonasal RMS in adults is notably low, accounting for approximately 1.5-3% of all adult soft tissue sarcomas, with head and neck involvement in about 40% of RMS cases overall; however, nasal cavity localization is exceptional, with embryonal and alveolar subtypes being the most commonly reported, and only isolated case reports documented in the literature [4, 6–12].

Diagnostic evaluation typically involves advanced imaging modalities, including computed tomography (CT) for assessing bony involvement and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) for delineating soft tissue extension, tumor margins, and potential intracranial or orbital infiltration [1, 4, 6]. MRI characteristics in adult sinonasal RMS often reveal ill-defined, heterogeneous lesions with iso- to hypointense signals on T1-weighted images, hyperintense on T2-weighted images, and heterogeneous enhancement post-contrast, alongside low apparent diffusion coefficient values indicative of high cellularity [6]. Definitive diagnosis requires histopathological examination of biopsy specimens, supplemented by immunohistochemistry demonstrating positivity for markers such as desmin, myogenin, and MyoD1, to differentiate from other small round blue cell tumors [4, 7–9, 13, 14].

Surgical management forms a cornerstone of treatment, frequently entailing endoscopic sinus surgery for tumor resection, including procedures such as septectomy, ethmoidectomy, and antrostomy to achieve complete excision where feasible, often in conjunction with multimodal therapy [4, 8]. In more extensive cases, approaches like medial maxillectomy may be employed, particularly when combined with adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy to address the tumor’s aggressive nature and proximity to critical structures [1, 3, 11, 12, 14].

The prognosis for adult nasal cavity RMS remains guarded, with a reported 5-year survival rate of approximately 60%, influenced by factors such as age, tumor subtype, size, site, and extent of metastasis; adult cases generally exhibit poorer outcomes compared to pediatric counterparts due to higher rates of local invasion and distant spread [3, 4, 13]. Here we present a case of sinonasal rhabdosarcoma in a 27 years-old male patient. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2020 Criteria [15].

Case presentation

The patient, a 27-year-old male with a history of Epstein-Barr virus infection, tobacco use, and alcohol consumption, presented with recurrent epistaxis, headache, periorbital pain, rhinorrhea, and post-nasal drip persisting for one year. He was referred to an otolaryngologist, where functional endoscopic sinus surgery was performed. Diagnostic evaluations, including magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) scans of the paranasal sinuses, revealed a mass-like lesion. Given the patient’s symptoms, medical history, and clinical suspicion of nasal cavity hemangioma as a differential diagnosis, surgical intervention was scheduled in the otolaryngology department. Intraoperatively, the mass was debulked; however, due to its involvement at the skull base and proximity to the left eyeball, the lesion was excised from the ethmoidal sinus cavity and submitted to pathology for definitive diagnosis. Histopathological examination confirmed sinonasal rhabdomyosarcoma. Subsequently, the patient was referred for adjuvant chemotherapy and radiotherapy. He completed six cycles of chemotherapy, comprising cyclophosphamide, cisplatin, doxorubicin, actinomycin, and vincristine.

Preoperative imaging findings

Preoperative contrast-enhanced CT and MRI revealed an enhancing soft-tissue mass in the right ethmoid sinus measuring 24 × 11 mm, with polypoid extension into the right maxillary sinus and superior intracranial extension to the base of the right frontal lobe; no bony destruction was observed (Fig. 1A, B, C, D). Chest CT showed tiny bilateral miliary nodules suggestive of inflammatory changes rather than metastatic disease. Based on these findings, the patient underwent right functional endoscopic sinus surgery, nasal polypectomy, and endoscopic septoplasty. Postoperative MRI following complete resection demonstrated complete resolution of the intracranial extension with no residual or recurrent tumor.

Postoperative histopathological analysis

Initial histopathological evaluation of the lesion suggested grade III neuroendocrine carcinoma, prompting immunohistochemistry (IHC) studies (Table 1). Subsequent IHC confirmed poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinoma as the provisional diagnosis. In the initial immunohistochemical evaluation of the diagnostic biopsy (Table 1), Ki-67 positivity was observed in approximately 20% of tumor cell nuclei. This relatively low value likely resulted from the limited nature of the specimen, which was small, fragmented, contained crush artifacts, and included less cellular areas selected for assessment. Following complete tumor resection, a second, more comprehensive specimen was obtained, allowing for the review of the most viable and representative tumor regions. In this follow-up analysis (Table 1, the Ki-67 labeling index increased to about 70%, a value that more accurately reflects the proliferative activity of this aggressive neoplasm. Such discrepancies between biopsy and resection specimens are common in small round blue cell tumors, where intratumoral heterogeneity and variable tissue preservation can skew proliferation estimates. Therefore, the higher Ki-67 index (70%) is considered more accurate, as it was derived from a larger, better-preserved sample with more representative sampling of the most cellular areas.

Regarding the diagnostic reappraisal from neuroendocrine carcinoma to rhabdomyosarcoma, the initial hematoxylin and eosin sections revealed a densely cellular tumor composed of small, round to ovoid cells with hyperchromatic nuclei, scant cytoplasm, and occasional nuclear molding. These morphologic features are nonspecific and overlap with multiple small round blue cell tumors, including neuroendocrine carcinoma, olfactory neuroblastoma, Ewing sarcoma, and rhabdomyosarcoma. In the limited biopsy, the tumor predominantly consisted of uniform round cells with minimal cytoplasmic characteristics. In contrast, the subsequent complete resection specimen demonstrated focal rhabdomyoblastic differentiation, characterized by scattered larger cells with eosinophilic granular cytoplasm, occasional cytoplasmic cross-striations, and a subset of spindle-shaped tumor cells. Although these findings were subtle, they suggested a skeletal muscle lineage and prompted broadening the immunohistochemical panel to include myogenic markers (myogenin and MyoD1), which ultimately confirmed the diagnosis of rhabdomyosarcoma.

Overall, the primary morphologic feature was a biphasic pattern, characterized by primitive round cell areas transitioning into regions exhibiting early rhabdomyoblastic differentiation—an architectural pattern typical of embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma and absent in pure neuroendocrine carcinoma.

However, given the patient’s clinical presentation and the pathologist’s strong suspicion, additional IHC markers were assessed, ultimately revising the diagnosis to rhabdomyosarcoma of the nasal cavity, as detailed in Table 1. The immunohistochemical profile revealed a high proliferative index, with Ki-67 labeling approximately 70% of tumor cell nuclei. Tumor cells exhibited strong expression of myogenic markers, including myogenin and MyoD1, along with focal weak positivity for desmin, supporting skeletal muscle differentiation. CD56 positivity and weak chromogranin reactivity were observed, features that may occur in small round blue cell neoplasms but lack specificity. Aberrant SOX-10 and PAX-5 positivity was noted; nevertheless, the absence of lineage-specific markers for melanocytic, neural, or lymphoid differentiation—including negative results for S100, LCA, CD3, CD20, CD79, CD5, CD10, CD4, CD8, CD30, and TdT—excluded melanocytic, peripheral nerve sheath, or lymphoid neoplasms.

The tumor lacked cytokeratin expression beyond weak nonspecific CK staining and was negative for vimentin and MPO, ruling out epithelial, mesenchymal, and myeloid origins, respectively. CD138 negativity precluded plasmacytic differentiation. Collectively, the immunophenotype aligned most closely with rhabdomyosarcoma, bolstered by co-expression of myogenin and MyoD1, high proliferative activity, and lack of immunoreactivity for alternative lineages (Table 2). Despite morphological similarities to neuroendocrine tumors and initial IHC findings, secondary IHC evaluation prioritized rhabdomyosarcoma as the leading diagnosis over neuroendocrine tumor. Furthermore, fluorescence in situ hybridization testing showed no FOXO1 rearrangement, which disfavored alveolar rhabdomyosarcoma while not excluding embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma (Fig. 2A.A, B, C, D) (Table 2).

Postoperative imaging evaluations

Recent brain MRI with and without contrast, conducted for follow-up of sinonasal rhabdomyosarcoma, demonstrated complete resolution of the prior intracranial extension, with no residual or recurrent tumor evident. The right frontal sinus exhibited increased signal intensity and peripheral mucosal enhancement, indicative of frontal sinusitis with retained secretions; a developing right frontal sinus mucocele remains a consideration, warranting serial imaging. Mild mucosal thickening was observed in the right maxillary sinus. A small developmental venous anomaly was identified in the right high frontal subcortical region, without complications. Bilaterally prominent optic nerve cerebrospinal fluid sheaths with mild posterior scleral flattening suggested possible idiopathic intracranial hypertension, recommending clinical correlation. Postsurgical changes included right partial turbinectomy and a left concha bullosa with mucosal thickening.

Chest CT with intravenous contrast revealed tree-in-bud nodularity, mild septal thickening, and intraparenchymal bands in both lower lobes, likely attributable to prior inflammatory or infectious processes such as bronchiolitis, stable from prior imaging and not indicative of pulmonary metastasis. A new irregular opacity measuring 23 × 31 mm in the anterior segment of the left upper lobe was noted, most consistent with recent pneumonia or inflammatory changes. Paranasal sinus CT with intravenous contrast showed findings compatible with prior right ethmoid sinus tumor resection, with no residual tumor. The right middle turbinate had been resected, and enlargement of the right ostiomeatal complex was present. Complete opacification of the right frontal sinus persisted, likely due to chronic obstruction and unchanged from preoperative imaging.

Mild mucosal thickening was evident in the right maxillary sinus. A small left concha bullosa was observed, along with hypertrophic changes in both inferior turbinates. The nasal septum deviated to the left, contacting the lateral nasal wall, with two small (2-mm) anterior septal perforations. Overall, these results confirmed complete tumor resection without evidence of residual or recurrent disease. Discussion

Diagnostic challenges in adult sinonasal rhabdomyosarcoma

Adult RMS is an exceptionally rare and highly aggressive malignancy, accounting for less than 3% of all adult soft tissue sarcomas [1, 4]. Histopathologically, its features significantly overlap with other small round blue cell tumors commonly found in the adult sinonasal region, including neuroendocrine carcinoma, olfactory neuroblastoma, sinonasal undifferentiated carcinoma (SNUC), malignant melanoma, lymphoma, Ewing sarcoma, and biphenotypic sinonasal sarcoma [4, 7–9, 14]. This diagnostic challenge is further complicated by the frequent limitations of initial biopsy specimens, which are often small, fragmented, or affected by crush artifact, obscuring definitive morphological characteristics. In the presented case of a 27-year-old male with recurrent epistaxis, headache, and an enhancing ethmoidal mass with intracranial extension, the preliminary pathological diagnosis was grade III neuroendocrine carcinoma. The definitive diagnosis of RMS was established only after comprehensive evaluation of the entire endoscopic resection specimen, supplemented by an expanded IHC panel targeting myogenic markers. This case highlights the critical importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion and employing a systematic diagnostic approach to prevent misdiagnosis and subsequent treatment delays [4, 8, 9].

Morphological insights from complete resection specimens

The initial biopsy revealed a uniform population of small round cells characterized by hyperchromatic nuclei, scant cytoplasm, and occasional rosette-like formations—features morphologically indistinguishable from neuroendocrine carcinoma, olfactory neuroblastoma, or lymphoma. However, examination of the entire resection specimen disclosed focal yet unequivocal rhabdomyoblastic differentiation. Specifically, scattered larger cells exhibited eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and occasional cytoplasmic cross-striations—a hallmark of skeletal muscle differentiation—as well as spindle-shaped tumor cells merging with the round cell component. This biphasic pattern, in which primitive round cell areas transition into regions demonstrating early myogenic differentiation, is characteristic of embryonal RMS and is absent in pure neuroendocrine carcinoma. Recognition of these subtle morphological features prompted expansion of the IHC panel to include myogenin and MyoD1, both of which demonstrated strong, diffuse nuclear positivity, thereby conclusively confirming RMS. Concurrently, synaptophysin was negative, and chromogranin exhibited only weak, nonspecific staining, effectively excluding neuroendocrine carcinoma. A comprehensive panel of B-cell, T-cell, and myeloid markers (CD3, CD4, CD5, CD7, CD8, CD10, CD20, CD30, CD79, LCA, MPO) was entirely negative, ruling out lymphoma and myeloid neoplasms [4, 8, 9, 14].

Prioritization of diagnostic immunohistochemical markers

Among the extensive array of available IHC markers, myogenin and MyoD1 are recognized as the most specific and sensitive indicators of skeletal muscle differentiation. In a substantial study by Cessna et al. [16], these markers were positive in nearly all RMS cases and absent in almost all other small round cell tumors, including neuroendocrine carcinoma, Ewing sarcoma, and lymphoma. Consequently, myogenin and MyoD1 should be prioritized in the differential diagnosis when RMS is suspected. In the present case, their robust nuclear expression was the pivotal diagnostic finding. Desmin, another myogenic marker, exhibited only weak and focal positivity (approximately 15% of cells), consistent with poorly differentiated embryonal RMS and contrasting with the diffuse, strong desmin staining typically observed in alveolar RMS. The Ki-67 proliferation index increased from 20% in the initial fragmented biopsy to 70% in the resection specimen, a discrepancy attributable to intratumoral heterogeneity and sampling bias; the higher value from the larger, well-preserved specimen more accurately reflects the tumor’s aggressive behavior. FISH analysis using a dual-color FOXO1 break-apart probe revealed no rearrangement, effectively excluding alveolar RMS—which harbors FOXO1 rearrangements in approximately 80% of cases—and supporting the diagnosis of the embryonal subtype [17].

Addressing immunohistochemical pitfalls and atypical findings

Several IHC findings in this case could have caused diagnostic confusion if not interpreted within the appropriate context. First, vimentin, an intermediate filament protein typically expressed in sarcomas, was negative in tumor cells despite strong staining in internal controls such as stromal fibroblasts and endothelium. Although prolonged formalin fixation and decalcification of sinonasal specimens can impair intermediate filament epitopes, the intact internal controls argue against a technical artifact [18–20]. It is hypothesized that in a subset of poorly differentiated RMS, strong transcriptional activation by MyoD1 and myogenin may downregulate vimentin expression, a phenomenon previously documented in experimental models of myogenic differentiation. Second, SOX10 positivity was observed. SOX10, a transcription factor expressed in melanocytes, neural crest-derived cells, and certain salivary gland tumors, has been reported in sinonasal alveolar RMS with PAX3::NCOA1 fusion, leading to initial misdiagnosis as malignant melanoma [21]. In this case, despite SOX10 positivity, a comprehensive melanocytic panel (S100, HMB45, MITF) was entirely negative, and myogenic markers were unequivocally positive, effectively excluding melanoma. This underscores the necessity of employing a panel of markers rather than relying on isolated results. Third, PAX5 expression was detected. Although PAX5 is a B cell lineage-specific transcription factor commonly used to support lymphoma diagnosis, prior studies have reported PAX5 positivity in 67% of alveolar RMS cases and in embryonal RMS, likely due to cross-reactivity with other PAX family members (e.g., PAX3 or PAX7) involved in RMS translocations [22, 23]. In the present case, an extensive panel of B cell markers (CD19, CD20, CD79a, TdT, CD5, CD10, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD30, LCA) was negative, excluding lymphoma. Therefore, isolated PAX5 positivity should not be misconstrued as evidence of lymphoid differentiation when myogenic markers are positive and B cell markers are negative [4, 8, 9, 14].

Differential diagnosis of sinonasal small, round, blue cell tumors

The differential diagnosis of adult sinonasal small round blue cell tumors requires a systematic approach that integrates clinical, radiological, and pathological data (see Table 3). A structured diagnostic algorithm incorporating IHC profiles is presented in Table 1, comparing findings from initial biopsy and definitive resection specimens. In the present case, the combination of diffuse nuclear positivity for myogenin and MyoD1, a high Ki-67 proliferation index (70%), absence of FOXO1 rearrangement, and complete negativity for epithelial, melanocytic, lymphoid, and myeloid markers conclusively established the diagnosis of embryonal sinonasal RMS.

Therapeutic and prognostic considerations

The patient underwent complete endoscopic resection followed by six cycles of multi-agent chemotherapy (cyclophosphamide, cisplatin, doxorubicin, actinomycin D, vincristine) and adjuvant radiotherapy. This multimodal treatment approach aligns with current guidelines for adult RMS, which recommend aggressive local control combined with systemic therapy due to the high risk of local recurrence and distant metastasis [1, 3, 4, 8, 11, 12, 14]. The overall five-year survival rate for adult sinonasal RMS is approximately 60%, notably lower than the 70–80% survival rate observed in pediatric RMS. Poorer outcomes in adults are attributed to more advanced disease at presentation (e.g., intracranial extension, as seen in this patient), higher metastatic rates, and potential differences in tumor biology [3, 4, 13]. Prognostic factors include age over 10 years, alveolar subtype, FOXO1 positivity, tumor size greater than 5 cm, and presence of metastases at diagnosis. The patient’s tumor was embryonal (favorable), FOXO1-negative (favorable), but exhibited intracranial extension (unfavorable). Rigorous imaging surveillance is essential due to the potential for late recurrences.

Strengths and limitations

This report’s strengths include comprehensive preoperative and postoperative imaging, detailed histopathological evaluation of both limited biopsy and complete resection specimens, an extensive IHC panel comprising over 25 markers performed in duplicate with appropriate controls, and molecular subtype confirmation via FOXO1 fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH). The diagnosis was independently reviewed and validated. The principal limitation is the rarity of the disease, which restricts generalizability. Additionally, long-term follow-up beyond the immediate postoperative period is not yet available. Nonetheless, the diagnostic triad of strong nuclear myogenin and MyoD1 expression, coupled with morphological evidence of rhabdomyoblastic differentiation, remains highly specific for RMS and should inform clinical practice.

Clinical implications for surgeons and pathologists

For otorhinolaryngologists managing adults presenting with aggressive sinonasal masses, RMS should be considered, even when initial pathology suggests more common entities such as neuroendocrine carcinoma. For pathologists, any sinonasal small round blue cell tumor of uncertain lineage warrants routine staining for myogenin and MyoD1, regardless of cytokeratin or neuroendocrine marker status. When initial biopsy specimens are small or compromised by crush artifact, deferring definitive subtyping until evaluation of the complete resection specimen may help prevent misdiagnosis. Multidisciplinary collaboration among surgeons, radiologists, pathologists, and oncologists is essential to facilitate timely and appropriate multimodal therapy.

Conclusion

Adult sinonasal rhabdomyosarcoma is a rare, aggressive tumor often mistaken for similar small-cell malignancies, requiring a high level of suspicion. Accurate diagnosis depends on advanced imaging, histopathology aided by specific immunohistochemistry, and molecular testing for FOXO1 rearrangement. Given its rapid invasion and poor prognosis, patients should be urgently referred to specialized, multidisciplinary centers for precise diagnosis and multimodal treatment to improve survival.
