Forecasting COVID-19 inpatient mortality using fundamental parameters in resource-constrained settings: a countrywide multi-center cohort study
Authors: Ibrahem Hanafi, Marah Alsalkini, Alaa Almouhammad, Ghaya Salhab, Qamar Khoder, Taj Azzam, Bayan Hanafi, Sondos Sallam, Majd Abu Khamis, Ola Alnabelsi, Lyana Alzamel, Zen Afif, Manaf Jassem, Rahaf Alsoudi, Samaher Almousa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate mortality prediction was essential for guiding hospitalization and management during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly in low-resource settings with already fragile health systems. In such regions, political instability and weakened healthcare infrastructure amplified viral spread while constraining response capacity. Hospitalization was, therefore, mostly restricted to the most critical cases due to shortages of beds, staff, and equipment, leaving frontline physicians to make rapid triage decisions under pressure. Yet, most existing prognostic tools were unsuitable for use in such resource-constrained contexts. To address this gap, we developed and validated a simplified, resource-directed score for predicting COVID-19 mortality.MethodsThis nationwide multicenter cohort study involved prospective data collection and retrospective analysis of hospitalized and non-hospitalized COVID-19 patients in Syria. The study was conducted across nine healthcare centers in four major Syrian cities, reflecting the diverse levels of care available in the war-torn country. It included 3,199 hospitalized and 293 non-hospitalized patients. Comprehensive datasets were collected, including demographic characteristics, clinical presentation, vital signs, laboratory tests, and imaging findings. The primary outcome was in-hospital mortality prediction, with secondary outcomes including mortality prediction for intensive care unit (ICU) patients and those managed at home. To develop the scores, we employed a regression coefficient-based scoring system methodology. Finally, the performance of the developed score was compared with established mortality prediction scores.ResultsLR-COMPAK, utilizing six easily obtainable variables (age, comorbidities such as kidney disease and malignancy, pulse rate, oxygen saturation, and consciousness), showed superior predictive performance with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.88 [0.87–0.90] and explained 52% of mortality variance, demonstrating applicability to non-hospitalized patients as well. Regional and temporal disparities in severity scores and mortality highlighted variations in healthcare capacity. The LR-ALBO-ICU score, additionally incorporating lactate dehydrogenase and bicarbonate levels, effectively predicted ICU mortality, showing great value in critical care decision-making.ConclusionsLR-COMPAK and LR-ALBO-ICU offer practical, effective tools for mortality prediction in COVID-19 patients, particularly in resource-limited settings. These tools can guide hospitalization decisions, optimize resource allocation, and improve patient outcomes, facilitating translation between resource-rich and resource-limited healthcare environments.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11870-x.

Introduction

The emerging COVID-19 pandemic jeopardized healthcare systems worldwide, exposing their vulnerability and putting hospitals and medical centers at the frontline in an open war against this global health crisis [1–3]. Poor COVID-19 governorate surveillance and inadequate public awareness led to underestimation of the burden of the pandemic and failure in reducing the incidence and alleviating strain on healthcare systems [4–8]. The pandemic also created complex social, political, and economic constrains, which in turn exaggerated the situation, leading to severe shortages of essential needs for both patient’s care (e.g., ventilators and medications) and medical workers’ safety (e.g., personal protective equipment (PPE)) [1, 4–8]. As a result, more than 64 million cases were infected and 1.5 million deaths occurred in less than one year [9, 10]. In less wealthy nations, where public health systems and emergency preparedness were already fragile, the initial wave of the pandemic led to a more dramatic escalation of the crisis [3].

Political instability in some regions, marked by widespread conflicts, population displacement, and infrastructure destruction, was central to the weakened response to the pandemic [4, 11–15]. This exacerbated the situation in countries like Syria [12], where the decade-long war displaced more than half of the population [16, 17], devastated national economy, and pushed half of the healthcare facilities and personnel out of service [12, 18–20]. With 83% of the Syrian population living below the poverty line and under poor sanitary conditions, the country was unable to enforce self-quarantine, social distancing, or curfews [18, 21]. These circumstances created conditions highly conducive to viral transmission, posing significant risks not only within Syria but also to neighboring and more distant communities [11, 12, 22–24]. Mathematical simulations estimated that only 1.25% of COVID-19 deaths in Syria were reported [25]. A major factor was the pronounced gap between healthcare resources and patient load [26, 27]. As COVID-19 mortality correlated with hospital bed availability worldwide [28], hospitalization capacity in Syria was largely restricted to the most critical cases, forcing physicians to deny admission to thousands of patients [27, 29]. The severe under-staffing [11, 18, 19] left junior physicians—often overworked, undertrained, and inadequately supervised—making critical decisions under extreme pressure [30], while facing burnout, social isolation, and persistent PPE shortages [1, 31, 32]. Moreover, the limited personnel and resources curtailed follow-up and shortened hospital stays, hindering the timely evaluation of the consequences of these decisions [27]. Such challenges underscored the urgent need for standardized criteria to guide critical care decisions in Syria and other resource constrained settings.

Numerous prognostic scoring systems were reused or developed worldwide to predict mortality and stratify disease severity [33–39], offering selection criteria to lead such decisions in situations of limited hospitalization capacity [40]. A key limitation of most existing scores is their dependence on detailed patient data [41], which is often impractical to obtain in resource-limited settings. Under such constraints, swift and effective triage between inpatient and outpatient management is critical and such tools are most needed. However, most of them require at least one blood test or imaging study [34, 37–39], compounding barriers related to cost and time and restricting their use to facilities capable of performing rapid diagnostic investigations in the emergency department. Consequently, the effectiveness of these tools in well-resourced environments cannot be readily assumed to translate to equitable outcomes in areas with limited resources [42]. In this study, we retrospectively analyzed the first two waves of COVID-19 in major responding hospitals across Syria to develop a feasible scoring system to predict mortality, support decisions, and guide case allocation in low-resource settings with high patient load and staff shortages.

Methods

Study design and hospitals

This retrospective cohort study was conducted in the four major Syrian cities with the largest populations: Aleppo, Damascus (the capital), Homs, and Latakia [43, 44]. In each of these cities, we included the major 1–5 hospitals of these designated by the Syrian Ministry of Health to receive COVID-19 cases during the pandemic. The number of centers per city was determined by considering the size of the hospitals and the total number of cases in their respective cities [45] (Fig. 1a). These hospitals included the university hospitals of Damascus (National university and Al-Mouwasat Hospitals), Aleppo, and Latakia universities, which are affiliated with Ministry of Higher Education, as well as Ibn Al-Waleed and Damascus Hospitals as the largest public hospitals affiliated with the Ministry of Health in Homs and Damascus, respectively. All these medical centers are community healthcare facilities that offer entirely free healthcare services to adult civilians, except for the national university Hospital, where non-private patients were required to cover 10% of the overall care costs incurred during their hospitalization. We additionally included the three largest military hospitals managed by the Syrian Armed Forces in Damascus (Tishreen and Qatana Hospitals) and Homs governorates. These hospitals exclusively served military personnel and their families, delivering care without any associated charges. All participating hospitals designated isolation wards for suspected COVID-19 cases throughout the duration of this study. While the majority of patients originated from the included cities, these hospitals also admitted cases transferred from smaller cities and rural areas. This study received ethical approval from the Higher Commission for Scientific Research (HCSR) at the Syrian Ministry of Higher Education and the review boards of all participating centers. A retrospective screening of patient records from the COVID-19 isolation wards and ICU units was performed, encompassing the period from June 1 st to December 31 st, 2020, which coincided with the initial two waves of the pandemic in Syria; the first of them primarily concentrated in Damascus, while the second additionally involved the rest of the country including the three other cities included (Fig. 1b). The subgroup of our patients who presented to Damascus hospitals between June and August 2020 were included in a previous publication [27]. Patient confidentiality was maintained by anonymizing all records at data extraction, ensuring that no personal identifiers were collected or analyzed.

Inclusion criteria, diagnostic processes, and hospital care protocols

The criteria for admission, diagnostic procedures, and hospital management guidelines were detailed in our previous publication [27]. Briefly, we included all patients over 13 years old, who were admitted to the included centers within the defined time period.

Patients exhibiting acute respiratory symptoms associated with COVID-19 were admitted after clinical evaluation. Diagnosis confirmation typically took place within 48 h following admission and was primarily based on real-time Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction (PCR) tests or rapid antigen tests (RAT), depending on availability. Otherwise, patients were managed as COVID-19 cases based on computed tomography (CT) or chest X-ray (CXR) scans indicating a potential COVID-19 infection [46, 47]. Moreover, some patients who lacked evidence of COVID-19 on chest imaging or for whom imaging was not available, but exhibited typical symptoms were also managed as COVID-19 cases. All these patients were included in our dataset, irrespective of the diagnostic modality and without any further inclusion or exclusion criteria.

At the start of the study period, the initial protocol at all participating centers involved admitting patients to the isolation ward if their blood oxygen saturation was below 92%, their respiratory rate exceeded 30 breaths per minute, or they exhibited other admission indications such as acute kidney injury, shock, or blood electrolyte disorders. Patients with milder symptoms and no other admission indications were discharged for home isolation. However, due to the limited hospitalization capacity and the increasing influx of patients, inpatient care was dynamically allocated to the most critical cases that presented to each center at every time point, and patients who demonstrated improvement and no longer met the admission criteria were subsequently discharged.

Patient and public involvement

As this study was designed to retrospectively analyze fundamental aspects such as patient characteristics, vital signs, major interventions, and mortality, patients were not involved in the project’s planning or in interpreting the results. Nevertheless, the study’s objectives were informed by patient observations and reports, and the data-driven conclusions hold significant value for public understanding of the pandemic.

Data collection and included variables

The data collection process was carried out by a team comprising ten senior internal medicine residents, three fresh graduate physicians, and one internal medicine specialist, who were blinded to the study hypothesis and outcomes during data extraction. The data gathered through an electronic form encompassed demographic information and comorbidities as well as the vital signs and radiological and laboratory findings at admission. We also collected information regarding major interventions like oxygen support, ICU admission, and mechanical ventilation, as well as mortality and complications during hospitalization. Less than 5% of the total number of screened records lacked documentation of at least one of the basic variables: demographic characteristics (e.g., Age and Gender), comorbidities, diagnosis modality, major interventions (e.g., ventilation), or status at discharge and were therefore excluded at data extraction to maintain the integrity of the collected data.

The analysis included five demographic characteristics, 12 comorbidities, and the six vital signs parameters, in addition to nine radiological findings and 16 laboratory tests. The latter represents a subgroup of the laboratory tests collected after we excluded the ones that are available for less than a third of our sample to maintain the statistical power (Supplementary Table 1).

Development of the risk scores

The univariate association between nominal variables and mortality was assessed using χ2 and Fisher’s exact tests, while student t or Mann Whitney tests were used for the same analysis on continuous variables. All univariate tests were applied twice: once on the whole sample and once only on PCR positive patients, both using available-case approach. Only the variables that showed a statistically significant association with mortality in both steps were considered for the multivariate analyses. A significance threshold of α = 0.001 was applied when analyzing the full sample, whereas thresholds of 0.005 for categorical variables and 0.05 for continuous variables were used in the PCR-positive subset. A more lenient threshold was employed for continuous variables due to their lower availability in the dataset (e.g., laboratory tests) compared with categorical data (e.g., imaging findings). This limited the number of predictors fed into multivariate analysis. To estimate associations, odds ratios (OR) and their corresponding confidence intervals 95% (CI) were reported. Significant continuous variables were later divided into clinically meaningful ranges using both clinical relevance and statistical optimization. Briefly, the highest cutoff for blood oxygen saturation was set at ≥ 97%, as it provided high sensitivity for predicting ICU admission within one month, albeit with significantly poorer specificity (< 50%) than 96% [48]. Subsequent categories were defined in 5% increments to both exceed the technical error margin of most pulse oximeters (± 2–4%) [49, 50] and achieve statistical superiority while capturing clinically meaningful inflection points [48]. Similarly, the cutoff of ≤ 110 beats per minute for pulse rate was adopted as it represents the most widely accepted clinical baseline. In contrast, the threshold of >125 beats per minute was selected for its statistical superiority over alternative cutoffs. For both variables, statistical superiority was defined as greater predictive performance, as measured by Nagelkerke R² in univariate binary logistic regression, and more distinctive separation between categories in the regression coefficients.

For the multivariate predictive models for inpatient mortality, ICU admission, and mortality in the ICU, we employed binary logistic regression analyses with a complete-case approach. This approach is commonly used in similar retrospective studies due to its interpretability despite potential limitations [40]. When applicable, we also ran the regression on 1,000 bootstrap datasets. To build the risk score, we used a common regression coefficient-based scoring system methodology, which was also applied to develop similar risk scores for COVID-19 patients [34, 51]. Briefly, we multiplied the regression coefficient from the final model by ten and rounded it to the nearest whole number to make the points allocated to each significant parameter. When applicable, this calculation was performed only for parameters that remained statistically significant in the averaged values of the 1,000 bootstrap samples.

Validity and generalizability assessment of the risk scores

The scores developed were presented using medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) because they were not normally distributed. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and the area under the curves (AUC) were illustrated for the scores developed and their individual components to represent the ability of the scores to predict the outcomes and prove their superiority to the items included. Calibration of the prediction model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test and calibration plots comparing predicted and observed probabilities across deciles. We also compared our mortality score to all feasible mortality risk scores used during the COVID-19 pandemic even in more resourced systems [34]. Later, the AUC curves were used to estimate cutoffs with best performance for binary outcome predictions. Additionally, Kaplan Meier curves and log rank Mantel-Cox survival analysis methods were used to compare survival among patients with different levels of mortality scores.

To evaluate the generalizability of our mortality scores beyond hospitalized patients, we supplemented our sample with a sample of 293 COVID-19 patients who received home visits by a voluntary group of physicians in Damascus between August and September 2020. This period, included in the timeline of the retrospective study, allowed for a simultaneous overview of both hospitalized and nonhospitalized patients in Damascus during the most challenging phase of the initial COVID-19 wave in Syria [27]. These patients were drawn from a previously documented home-visit cohort that contained the necessary data for mortality score calculation [52].

In brief, the voluntary team publicized its services widely through official channels and social media, receiving calls from more than 10,000 individuals reporting COVID-19–like symptoms. A structured triage process classified callers by disease severity [53]: mild cases were managed with professional advice over the phone, moderate cases qualified for home visits, and critical cases were referred to community hospitals. However, some critical cases were included when hospital admission was not possible. Consequently, the patients reached through home visits are representative of a large cohort of non-hospitalized COVID-19 cases in Damascus and its suburbs, specifically those with moderate to critical illness who were unable to access inpatient care. During the visits, physicians documented demographic details, medical history, and clinical examination findings. Management strategies varied from oral medications to oxygen therapy and noninvasive ventilation. Subsequently, patients or their caregivers received follow-up phone calls two to three weeks post-visit, enabling documentation of hospitalization and mortality.

Software and data presentation tools

The data analysis in this study was conducted using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM Corp. Version 27.0. Armonk, NY, United States). All statistical tests used adhered to their respective assumptions without violations. The figures and illustrations were created using SPSS, Microsoft Excel 365 (version 2103, year 2021), and Adobe Illustrator (CS6). This manuscript was prepared following the checklist of STrengthening the Reporting of OBservational studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) for observational studies [54], ensuring transparency and adherence to established reporting guidelines in epidemiological research.

Results

Sample demographics

3,199 hospitalized patients were recruited for our study. Their demographic characteristics, vital signs, and laboratory and imaging findings are presented in supplementary Table 1. Their most common symptoms were fever, cough, and dyspnea, and the most common comorbidities were arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and ischemic heart disease (Supplementary Fig. 1). More than half of the sample (n = 1,725, 53.9%) were diagnosed based on positive PCR tests (Supplementary Table 2). About three quarters of the sample (n = 2,417, 75.6%) received oxygen support and a quarter (n = 807, 25.2%) was admitted to the ICU; half of whom (n = 389, 48.2%) received mechanical ventilation (MV). Patients who were transferred to another medical facility (n = 269) or discharged at their own request (n = 197) were excluded from the mortality analysis. The in-hospital mortality rate was38.1% (n = 1,041); about half of them (n = 455) died without being admitted to the ICU, mostly due to lack of capacity. The duration of symptoms ranged between four and ten days with a median of seven days, and it was not significantly different between patients who survived or died during hospitalization (p =.783). Complications developed among survivors during hospitalization included respiratory failure, electrolyte disturbances, and acute kidney injury (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Development of the mortality score

The univariate analysis of the association between all included variables and mortality are presented in Supplementary Table 1. Variables with statistically significant association with mortality in the whole sample and the PCR positive patients were included in the multivariate analysis (i.e., binary logistic regression; supplementary Table 3). We then excluded all the insignificant predictors to reach a final model that explained 52.2% of the variability in mortality (p <.001, Table 1). Bootstrap validation confirmed the robustness of the included predictors, as their coefficients remained statistically significant with 95% CIs that were consistent in direction and magnitude with the original regression estimates. The model included six independent predictors: peripheral blood Oxygen saturation, Consciousness, Age, history of Malignancy, Pulse rate, and chronic Kidney disease. Applying the same model only on the PCR positive cases also explained 51.4% of the variability in mortality (n = 1,022, p <.001).

In the context of laboratory tests, a comparable procedure resulted in limited predictability of mortality (Supplementary Table 4), likely due to the unavailability of the complete set of tests for every patient. Incorporating the independent predictors among laboratory tests in the previous model based on demographics, comorbidities, and vital signs reduced the sample size drastically (n = 329) and increased the predictability of mortality by less than 5%. Similarly, the ratios of infiltration in each lung by imaging studies were independent predictors of mortality among all the included radiological findings (Supplementary Table 5). Nevertheless, when added to the logistic regression in Table 1, they still gave an increase of less than 5% in the predictability of mortality (n = 1,227). A binary logistic regression was subsequently employed utilizing three sequential blocks of predictors: 1) the initial model outlined in Tables 1; 2) two laboratory tests with the highest adjusted ORs, namely CRP and LDH; and 3) the radiological infiltration in both lungs. The statistical significance of the model was maintained at each block, with the r2 values reaching 51.6, 53.6, and 60.0 for the respective blocks (n = 667, p <.001, Supplementary Table 6). The limited number of patients who had the accessibility to undergo clinical, laboratory, and imaging studies, compounded by the minimal contributions of the latter two to predictive accuracy, encouraged us to build our regression coefficient-based scoring system utilizing only the variables outlined in Table 1 (Appendix 1). The resulting risk score suggested for centers with Limited Resource, was named LR-COMPAK, and ranged from zero to 96 with a median of 31.

Performance of the mortality score

Among our sample, survivors had significantly lower scores 20 (9,31) than patients who died during hospitalization 48 (38,57; p <.001). Splitting the scores into ten-point bins showed that the higher scores the higher probability of mortality. LR-COMPAK had a sensitivity of 87.9% and a specificity of 72.1% in predicting mortality at the critical value of ≥ 30, and its AUC (0.88 [95% CI: 0.87–0.90]) was significantly higher than that of all the numeric components of the score. Calibration analysis showed no evidence of poor fit (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ²=10.9, df = 8, p =.206). The calibration plot demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed risks across deciles. Interestingly, the AUC, critical value, sensitivity, and specificity were comparable also when calculated for PCR positive cases. Development and performance of the ICU-specific mortality score

LR-COMPAK’s score and model showed poor prediction of mortality for patients admitted to the ICU (R2 = 15.8%, Supplementary Table 10) likely due to a low 22.5% survival rate in ICU-admitted patients, varying significantly across cities and hospitals (Supplementary Table 7). Thus, we decided to recalibrate the scoring system for better predictability. We entered first all significant patients’ characteristics and vital signs from the univariate analysis (R2 = 18.1), second the laboratory findings (R2 = 14.2), and third the radiological findings (R2 = 25.6, Supplementary Table 11). These three models found Age, peripheral blood Oxygen saturation, Bicarbonate (Hco3), Lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), lymphocytes percentage, and no imaging findings as significant independent predictors. Incorporating all these variables into one regression model (R2 = 24.4) led to the exclusion of lymphocytes (Supplementary Table 12).

The final regression model (Table 3) was used to create a Limited Resources risk score named LR-ALBO-ICU to predict ICU mortality (Appendix 2). The resulting score ranged from zero to 48 and had a non-parametric distribution with a median of 34. Patients who survived after ICU admission had significantly lower scores 25 (21,34) than patients who died there 35 (27,45; p <.001). LR-ALBO-ICU also had an AUC of 0.77 [0.70–0.85] in forecasting mortality, which was specific for the aim of ICU survival as using LR-COMPAK for the same aim achieve an AUC of 0.71 [0.65–0.76]. Conversely, if we apply LR-ALBO-ICU to predict mortality in the whole sample regardless of ICU admission is also leads to a poor AUC of 0.71 [0.66–0.75].

Discussion

Statement of principal findings

Resources needed for facing the COVID-19 pandemic were dear, and multiple severity scores were suggested to predict mortality and maximize the benefit from the available resources. Albeit the higher need for them in developing and war-torn countries, these scores proved to be less feasible or effective where resources are scarcer and when mortality is more dependent on the initial presentation of the patients [42]. This study was successful in developing a new simplified resources-sparing scoring system for predicting COVID-19 mortality during hospitalization. LR-COMPAK relied on six variables easily obtainable within a short timeframe and still outperformed the previously available scoring systems in our sample. Not only did it aid in predicting mortality on an individual basis, but it also highlighted variations among cities, hospitals, and pandemic peaks. Moreover, its applicability extended to non-hospitalized patients, suggesting significant potential to aid in hospitalization decision-making and in the allocation of cases or resources across different centers. Finally, a customized version of the score, LR-ALBO-ICU, demonstrated notable effectiveness in forecasting mortality for ICU-admitted patients, potentially assisting in the allocation of available intensive care resources to those with higher predicted survival.

Strengths and weaknesses in relation to other studies

With an AUC of 0.88 [95% CT: 0.87–0.90], our regression coefficient-based scoring system (LR-COMPAK) was able to explain more than 52% of the variability in mortality in our sample. This performance exceeded the discriminability of previously suggested scores, within our dataset, including OuR-ARDSs (AUC of 0.85) [34], CURB65 (AUC of 0.72) [56], BURDEN (AUC of 0.83) [35], NEWS2 score (AUC of 0.82) [59], Chest Computed Tomography Severity Score (CTSS) (AUC of 0.84) [60], qSOFA score (AUC of 0.74) [55], as well as the score developed by Altschul et al. (AUC of 0.82) [33]. On the other hand, although scores like CALL (AUC of 0.91) [57], SOFA (AUC of 0.89) [55], CMR (AUC of 0.90) [61], and COVID-19 scoring system (CSS) (AUC of 0.92) [38] performed slightly better in their own samples, they all included much more variables or at least one blood test or imaging study, which required incomparable longer time to acquire. Simplified to just six easily accessible factors, namely: age, history of CKD, prior malignancy, HR, room blood oxygen saturation, and disturbed consciousness, the LR-COMPACK score can be obtained within 60 s, even by non-medical personnel and outside formal healthcare settings. Device-dependent measures such as HR and blood oxygen saturation can be measured using affordable pulse oximeters, which remain widely available even in resource-limited environments [62]. This feasibility helps to overcome shortages of personnel and diagnostics, supporting the score’s applicability beyond Syria and providing a scalable solution for regions unable to perform extensive emergency room testing before admission [63].

The variables of our developed LR-COMPAK score included one demographic characteristic (i.e., advanced age), which was one of the most reported risk factors for COVID-19 mortality [64–66]. Additionally, two comorbidities, namely malignancy and CKD, showed independent predictability of mortality in accordance with previous reports [66–68] and were included in our score. The weakened immune system and the exaggerated susceptibility to more severe infections can explain the higher mortality in elderly [69, 70], as well as patients with malignancies [71, 72] or CKD [73, 74]. On the other hand, LR-COMPAK also included three vital signs proved as independent predictors of mortality. Disturbed consciousness and tachycardia were among them, and albeit associating with multiple confounding factors for mortality prediction, they were reported among the risk factors of mortality in numerous studies [75–80]. Lastly, peripheral blood oxygen saturation was the individual parameter that carried the highest value in predicting mortality in our study, and this association was in line with other studies especially in countries with limited resources like Brazil, Peru, and South Africa [81–83]. Hence, we utilized this correlation in our scoring system by categorizing the oxygenation percentage into eight bins with gradual mortality probabilities (appendix 1). Of note, all variables included in the LR-COMPAK score reflect general physiological measures related to the body’s preparedness to face respiratory distress, which suggests potential for higher generalizability across other infectious respiratory diseases and future pandemics. This is consistent with the overlapping clinical predictors of respiratory failure across different infections and, therefore, the maintained predictive value of scores based on such variables across diverse patient populations and settings [84].

It is worth mentioning that several additional factors in our dataset also carried independent contributions to the predictability of mortality such as blood tests and imaging, which is in line with other studies [85–89]. These factors proved high potential to be incorporated in multiple scoring systems to improve mortality prediction, decision making, and logistical planning during the pandemic [34, 58, 90]. However, the time, effort, resources they require rendered them unavailable for many patients in Syria. Moreover, the added value they brought to the scoring system did not justify dramatically reducing the sample size to include them. It is plausible that including them in higher-resource settings, where imaging is more accessible, can improve predictive performance. Nonetheless, such settings also differ in hospitalization capacity and staffing ratios, factors that can independently affect LR-COMPAK performance. In line, six of the scores that incorporated blood tests could be obtained in a subgroup of our sample and still performed significantly poorer than LR-COMPAK. This reflects a dependency of the performance of mortality scores on the settings, where the sample is collected, highlighting the need for establishing and customizing scores based on locally available predictors in limited resources [42].

The potential of LR-COMPAK in forecasting mortality in hospitalized and home-managed patients positions it as an objective valuable tool for assessing severity and expediting hospitalization decisions [91]. Although a cutoff could be proposed for daily implementation, in low-resource settings it may be more effective to use LR-COMPAK dynamically, prioritizing patients for admission based on their scores at each time point rather than relying on fixed thresholds. The scenario from Damascus (Fig. 5) illustrated how different thresholds with varying levels of stringency can rationalize and standardize admission policies. However, cutoffs derived from one city may not generalize to other regions or healthcare systems. Moreover, a fixed threshold over time risks overburdening resources during peak waves and underutilizing capacity during lulls [27].

On the other hand, we proved the predictive performance of LR-COMPAK across all included cities and medical centers, in both patients with and without positive PCR tests. This versatility underscores the score’s additional potential to govern coordination among healthcare facilities, a major challenge in enhancing COVID-19 hospitalization outcomes [27]. For instance, LR-COMPAK can support continuous monitoring of patient severity both in hospitals and at home, guiding admission and referral decisions and optimizing allocation of scarce supplies. This potential is reflected in several findings from our dataset. First, LR-COMPAK revealed substantial variation in patient severity across hospitals. This partially explained the high mortality at Al-Mouwasat Hospital, which admitted the most severe cases on average. Second, differences in mortality not fully explained by severity (i.e., high LR-COMPAK scores) likely reflect the interaction of hospital capacity, case flow, and available resources. For example, patients in Aleppo presented with higher severity scores compared with Damascus and Latakia yet had lower mortality rates. This may be attributable to the relatively large capacity of Aleppo University Hospital in the context of a markedly reduced population following the armed conflict, with about one third of residents displaced to other cities or countries [92]. This unique context may also explain the suboptimal performance of LR-COMPAK in Aleppo (Table 2), where patient outcomes were shaped not only by severity at admission, as in other centers, but also by management choices. Third, when comparing mortality rates with LR-COMPAK over time, mortality peaked during both pandemic waves, consistent with global patterns [93–95]. However, LR-COMPAK values did not rise in parallel, suggesting that excess mortality during peaks was driven more by resource strain than by changes in patient severity. This highlights the potential that a standardized severity scoring system such as LR-COMPAK can guide flexible resource allocation and inter-hospital coordination to mitigate avoidable mortality [27].

Our findings support the consideration of incorporating LR-COMPAK into Syrian healthcare guidelines for pandemic response, as it may standardize patient triage, optimize resource allocation, and further improve score calibration in real-world practice. Implementation could be also enhanced through brief targeted training programs aimed at healthcare workers to promote consistent and accurate use of the score in clinical decision-making. Although the score components are largely user-independent, the assessment of consciousness level requires clinical judgment and, while generally straightforward, may benefit from specialized training to maintain reliability and accuracy under high-pressure conditions.

Although patients’ flow was beyond hospitalization capacity, the mismatch was even wider between patients with an indication for intensive care and ICU beds in Syria [26, 27] and worldwide [28]. This constrained capacity forced the treating physicians in every center to subjectively triage cases selecting those more probable to benefit from ICU admission. However, the available guidelines developed to standardize this approach offered theoretical guidance rather than practical construction [96]. Also, LR-COMPAK did not exhibit an adequate predictability for mortality during ICU admission. Therefore, we developed a modified scoring system (LR-ALBO-ICU), which still included age and peripheral blood oxygen saturation but in combination with LDH and HCO3, two blood tests, which were repeatedly reported to reflect COVID-19 infection severity [97–100]. This score could satisfactorily isolate patients with high probability of survival in the ICU, with accuracy (AUC of 0.77 [0.70–0.85]) higher than three out of the four scores applied for the same aim in their own samples (RECOILS (AUC of 0.75), 4 C (AUC of 0.79), Sofa (AUC of 0.66), and SAPS-III (AUC of 0.72)) [58, 101]. LR-ALBO-ICU consisted of still less demanding components than all the other scores [58, 102]. Although this might not be sufficient to justify clinical decisions without further refinement on larger samples, these findings suggest that predicting mortality in resource-limited settings may be more straightforward and effective when compared to environments with ample resources. This difference may be attributed to the limited survival-protective impact of healthcare services, which may pale in comparison to the severity of cases upon presentation and the constrained care provided [27].

Strengths and weaknesses of the study

This study was not free of limitations. First, not all the patients included in our dataset had PCR positive tests due to the limited number of PCR kits [103], which might introduce a level of misclassification bias. However, we have shown previously a similar pattern of presentation and progression of cases with and without PCR tests in Syria [27]. Also, all the reported analyses were replicated for both groups of patients to ensure the translatability of the developed scores. Second, selection bias may have influenced our findings, as mild to moderate cases—typically denied admission at presentation—were underrepresented in the analysis. However, LR-COMPAK showed excellent performance on another set of patients who were treated at home due to the limited hospitalization capacity [52]. Third, LR-ALBO-ICU relied on variables measured upon first presentation, which might not reflect the status of the cases when transferred to the ICU. However, the median delay from presentation to ICU admission was 0 (0,2), reducing the probability of a major change of the used evaluations. Additionally, the sample size available to validate LR-ALBO-ICU was restricted by the limited capacity of ICU beds. Therefore, its broader generalizability remains contingent on further validation in larger cohorts. Fourth, we employed available- and complete-case analyses to handle missing data, which may have introduced information bias due to incomplete records. Although multiple imputation can reduce bias and preserve a larger sample size [40], it requires additional assumptions and methodological complexity, particularly as we applied several multivariable regression models to progressively reduce the number of incorporated variables. Similarly, advanced machine learning approaches have been increasingly explored for mortality prediction in critical care settings; however, recent evidence suggests they do not necessarily outperform traditional scoring systems [104]. Importantly, the complete-case approach is commonly used in similar retrospective studies and was able to achieve consistent and significant results in our case. Fifth, it has been internationally recommended that the triage procedure, which aims to prioritize patients in a way that maximizes benefits for the largest possible number of patients [105] should be carried out by either a dedicated triage committee or, at the very least, a senior intensivist [105]. Nevertheless, the limited human resources in our setting prevented this and such decision had to be sometimes taken by second- to third-year internal medicine residents [106]. Despite that, we believe that the large sample size we reported from multiple centers all over the country constrained the effect of these limitations and provided a comprehensive dataset that allowed the development of robust scores that proved applicable in all the included centers. A prospective validation of both scoring systems in similar low-resource settings is still warranted to confirm their reliability and support its integration into clinical practice and emergency preparedness protocols in other countries [107].

Conclusion

We provide the LR-COMPAK scoring system, a simplified and resource-efficient tool for predicting COVID-19 mortality. This score, relying on six easily obtainable variables, showed better performance than other applicable scores within our dataset and demonstrated versatility in aiding hospitalization decisions and resource allocation, particularly in resource-limited settings. Additionally, the tailored LR-ALBO-ICU score showed effectiveness in predicting ICU mortality, potentially optimizing intensive care resource allocation. Although the generalizability of both scores requires validation in different populations and healthcare settings, they offer significant potential for enhancing clinical decision-making and improving outcomes in diverse underserved environments.
