The complete mitochondrial genome of Neope bremeri (lepidoptera: nymphalidae)
Authors: Qian Gu, Yu Zhu, Jiusheng Wang, Mengen Chen, Ting Huang, Mingxia Ge
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present the first comprehensive sequencing and annotation of the mitochondrial genome (mitogenome) of Neope bremeri. The mitogenome spans 15,216 bp and includes a control region, along with sequences encoding 13 proteins, 22 transfer RNAs (tRNAs), and 2 ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), consistent with the genomic organization observed in other insect species. The nucleotide composition is as follows: 38.17% A, 41.23% T, 12.89% C, and 7.71% G, exhibiting a negative AT skew (−0.039) and a negative GC skew (−0.252). Phylogenetic analysis elucidates the species' evolutionary relationships within the genus, establishing a critical genetic foundation for future studies on Neope.

Introduction

Nymphalidae, commonly known as brush-footed butterflies, is the largest family of butterflies, comprising approximately 7,200 described species (Yan et al. 2023). They are widely distributed across the globe and dominate butterfly communities in a variety of habitats (Pyrcz and Wojtusiak 2002). As one of the most taxonomically diverse butterfly groups, the subfamily Satyrina (Nymphalidae) emerged later in evolutionary history compared to other lineages (Peña et al. 2006; Espeland et al. 2019). While most Satyrinae species exhibit neotropical distributions, the genus Neope represents an Asian-centric lineage within this subfamily (Lang 2012). In this genus, around nine species are catalogued in the NCBI taxonomy database, most of them being endemic to China.

Insect mitochondrial DNA, characterized by its simple structure and rapid evolution, serves as a valuable marker for systematics (Galtier et al. 2009). The typical butterfly mitogenome, ranging from 14 to 16 kb in size, contains a control region and 37 genes, coding for 13 proteins, 22 tRNAs and two rRNAs (Cameron 2014). To date, the mitogenomes of four Neope species have been sequenced and deposited in GenBank: Neope goschkevitschii Ménétriès, 1857 (Accession: LC541740), Neope muirheadii C. & R.Felder, 1862 (Accession: MN242789), Neope pulaha Moore, 1858 (Accession: KF590543) and Neope ramose Leech, 1890 (Accession: OP526829). Neope bremeri C. & R. Felder, 1862, a member of the Neope genus, is primarily distributed across various regions of China, including Taiwan, Guangdong, and Zhejiang (Huang 2002). However, its mitogenome has not yet been reported. In this study, we sequenced and characterized the complete mitogenome of N. bremeri. This provides essential baseline mitogenomic data that will contribute to understanding the phylogenetic relationships within Satyrinae, and will be valuable for species identification, phylogenetic analyses, and biogeographic studies within Nymphalidae.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

The specimen of N. bremeri (Figure 1) was collected in July 2024 from Yijiang distruct of Wuhu City (31.17 N, 118.21 E), Anhui Province, China. The specimen was identified morphologically by its dorsal/ventral wing patterns, with a basal symmetry system of four circular eyespot-like structures and a central symmetry system forming a vertically elongated band connected anteriorly as the loop (Otaki 2021). The specimen was deposited at Wannan Medical College (contact: Mingxia Ge, gemx@wnmc.edu.cn) under the voucher number GMX_001. Total genomic DNA was extracted from the leg of the specimen using the DNeasy Blood & Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was stored at −20 °C for subsequent analyses.

Sequencing, genome assembly, annotation and analysis

The DNA library of N. bremeri was constructed by Genepioneer Biotechnologies Co. Ltd (Nanjing, China) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with a paired-end (PE) read length of 150 bp. Raw data were filtered using Fastp v0.23.4 (Chen et al. 2018), yielding 23,237,956 clean pair-end reads with a clean data rate of 98.88%. To optimize computational efficiency and expedite the assembly process, we initially retrieved mitochondrial genome sequences from closely related species (within the same genus or family) from the NCBI database. These reference sequences served as a foundation for our downstream analyses. We employed Bowtie2 v2.2.4 (Langmead and Salzberg 2012) to map the sequencing reads to these local reference sequences, thereby identifying seed sequences that could be used as starting points for assembly. Subsequently, we utilized SPAdes v3.15.5 (Bankevich et al. 2012) to assemble these mapped reads into contigs, employing an iterative k-mer extension approach to enhance contiguity. To further improve the assembly, we applied SSPACE v2.0 (Boetzer et al. 2011) to scaffold the contigs, thereby connecting them into larger contiguous sequences. To validate the accuracy of the assembly, all quality-filtered reads were remapped to the mitochondrial genome, revealing high and uniform per-base sequencing depth—strongly supporting the consistency of the final assembly with the raw data (Figure S1). Annotation of the mitogenome was performed using Mitos2 (Bernt et al. 2013), with the following parameters: E-value Exponent = 5, Maximum Overlap = 100, ncRNA overlap = 100. The preliminary annotations were then manually curated in Geneious Prime (Version 2025.0. Auckland, New Zealand: Biomatters Ltd) by cross-referencing homologous features from the closely related species N. pulaha (GenBank accession number: KF590543) to ensure accuracy. A mitogenome map was created via the Proksee web server (Grant et al. 2023). Base composition analysis was performed using the ‘Biopython’ package (Cock et al. 2009) as implemented in Phylosuite v1.2.3 (Zhang et al. 2020).

Phylogenetic analyses

To determine the phylogenetic position of N. bremeri, four Neope species and 11 genetically close species from related genera (Mycalesis, Lopinga, Tatinga, Lethe, and Chonala), excluding Lopinga achine (MT117843) because of anomalous reverse-complementation in its mitochondrial rrnL and rrnS genes. Melitaea ambigua Ménétriès, 1859 (MK252271) (Kim et al. 2021) from the subfamily Nymphalinae and Neptis thisbe Ménétriès, 1859 (OK393687) (Liu et al. 2021) from Limenitidinae were selected as outgroups. The 13 shared protein-coding genes (PCGs) and two rRNAs were extracted using Phylosuite v1.2.3 (Zhang et al. 2020) and aligned using MAFFT v7.313 (Katoh and Standley 2013). Nucleotide sequence alignment results for these 13 PCGs were optimized using MACSE v2.03 (Ranwez et al. 2018). Subsequently, the 13 PCGs and two rRNAs sequences were concatenated using Phylosuite v1.2.3. The best-fit partitioning scheme for maximum likelihood (ML) analysis was determined using ModelFinder v2.2.0 (Kalyaanamoorthy et al. 2017) under the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC). For Bayesian inference (BI), the optimal partitioning strategy was selected with PartitionFinder2 v2.1.1 (Lanfear et al. 2017) based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The best-fit partitioning strategy is detailed in Table S1-S2. The ML tree was constructed using IQ-TREE v1.6.8 (Nguyen et al. 2015) with 5000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates. The Bayesian inference (BI) tree was constructed using MrBayes 3.2.6 (Ronquist et al. 2012) with 10,000,000 generations. The first 25% of sampled trees were discarded as burn-in after the split frequencies reached an average standard deviation of < 0.01, and the remaining trees were used to estimate posterior probability (PP) values. The final phylogenetic trees were visualized using ITOL v7.2 (Letunic and Bork 2024).

Results

The mitogenome of N. bremeri displays a circular conformation, with a total size of 15,216 bp (Figure 2). It comprises 37 genes, which encode 13 proteins, 22 tRNAs, and 2 rRNAs. Specifically, 14 tRNA genes and 9 protein-coding genes (PCGs) are encoded on the majority strand (J-strand), while the remaining genes are located on the minority strand (N-strand). Additionally, a putative control region (CR) was identified (Figure 2). The nucleotide composition of the mitogenome of N. bremeri was found to be 38.17% A, 41.23% T, 12.89% C, and 7.71% G, with a negative AT skew (−0.039) and GC skew (−0.252).

Regarding the PCGs, all start codons primarily utilize ATA, ATG, and ATT, with the exception of the cox1 gene, which uses CGA. Eight genes employ TAA as the stop codon, while the remaining genes use a single T. The 13 PCGs collectively span 11,198 base pairs, with an A + T content of 77.64%. The AT skew for the PCGs is −0.039, and the GC skew is 0.002. The lengths of the rrnL and rrnS genes are 1,336 bp and 785 bp, respectively. Their A + T contents are 84.58% and 86.5%, respectively. The combined length of the 22 tRNA genes is 1,445 bp, with an A + T content of 80.14%. Both the AT skew (0.026) and GC skew (0.143) for the tRNA genes are positive.

To investigate the phylogenetic position and relationships of N. bremeri, we constructed both ML and BI trees using concatenated sequences of 13 PCGs and two rRNAs. Both phylogenetic trees exhibited completely congruent topologies with strong statistical support (bootstrap ≥ 93%/posterior probability ≥ 0.999) (Figure 3). The tree showed that all Neope species clustered together in a single clade. Within Neope, N. bremeri is most closely related to N. goschkevischii.

Discussion and conclusion

For the first time, we have successfully sequenced and assembled the mitogenome of N. bremeri. The mitogenome of N. bremeri encodes 37 genes, with a gene arrangement that is consistent with other species within the Nymphalidae family. It is widely recognized that the start codon for the cox1 gene in Lepidoptera species is CGA (Bian et al. 2020; Nagata et al. 2020; Wang et al. 2021; Jiang et al. 2022; 2024), a feature also observed in N. bremeri. Within the phylogenetic tree, Neope indeed forms a sister clade with (Lopinga+Tatinga+Lethe+Chonala), which is largely consistent with the findings of Yang et al. (2020) (Yang et al. 2020) whereas Wu et al. (2022) (Wu et al. 2022) reported that Neope clusters with Ninguta. This discrepancy may stem from differences in taxon sampling or phylogenetic reconstruction methodologies.

In conclusion, the mitogenome sequence of N. bremeri not only enriches the genomic resources of Nymphalidae but also provides valuable data for phylogenetic and evolutionary studies of this family. These findings lay a foundation for further research on the genetic and ecological diversity within this group.
