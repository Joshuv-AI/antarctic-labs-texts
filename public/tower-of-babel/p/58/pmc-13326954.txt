Various inorganic phosphorus species in prebiotic Earth and extraterrestrial settings
Authors: Maheen Gull, Harold A. Cruz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Phosphorus (P) is a critical element for life, yet its primary geochemical form—orthophosphate—is poorly soluble and unreactive under early Earth conditions, presenting the longstanding “phosphate problem” in prebiotic chemistry. In this brief review, we give an overview of various prebiotic P sources from geochemical viewpoint. We then also discuss the feasibility of reactive P-N species in aqueous ammoniacal solutions on the early Earth. This review article also suggests the formation of inorganic P-N species in aqueous solutions. Reduced phosphorus compounds, such as phosphite produced by schreibersite corrosion and other terrestrial processes, as well as condensed phosphates and phosphites like trimetaphosphate and pyrophosphite, can react with aqueous ammonia under anoxic (or even oxic) conditions to yield amidophosphates and amidophosphites. These P–N species are more soluble, highly reactive toward organic substrates, and capable of driving phosphorylation under mild environmental conditions. Serving early Earth as an example, we further explore the potential for similar chemistry beyond Earth, particularly in ammonia-bearing aqueous environments on Mars, Enceladus, and other ocean worlds where reduced phosphorus and hydrothermal systems may coexist. We also briefly discuss the recent findings of various P species in asteroids Ryugu and Bennu. We further suggest that P–N chemistry could represent a widely accessible route to organophosphorus compounds, with important implications for the emergence of life in diverse habitable settings.

Introduction

Biological phosphates are central to life because they play critical roles in essential biological processes, including the formation of cellular membranes, key metabolic reactions, and the structure of genetic material. Therefore, to understand the plausible reactions that may have initiated the origin of prebiotic chemistry, it is important to investigate the pathways that could have led to the formation of biological phosphates. As a result, the prebiotic origins and synthesis of phosphate-containing compounds have received significant attention [1–4]. Phosphorylation reactions are inherently endergonic. Although these reactions typically occur in aqueous environments, they also require dehydration or condensation steps to remove water molecules in order to form C–O–P bonds [5].

Prebiotically relevant P minerals & their plasubility

Most prebiotic phosphorylation models rely on phosphate-bearing minerals. However, Ca²⁺- (e.g., apatite) [2] and Fe²⁺-bearing (e.g., vivianite) minerals are poorly soluble in water, making them relatively unreactive toward organic molecules. Because of this limited solubility, alternative phosphate mineral phases that may have been more prevalent on the early Earth have been considered. These scenarios often involve cation exchange processes, where calcium in apatite may be replaced by other cations more favorable for phosphate mobilization [5]. Experimental studies demonstrate that in plausible prebiotic, urea-rich solutions containing Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺, the preferential phosphate phases were not apatites, but more soluble orthophosphate minerals such as struvite (MgNH4PO4·6H2O), newberyite (MgHPO4·3H2O), and brushite (CaHPO4·2H2O) [5–7]. Moreover, widespread emplacement of mafic and ultramafic komatiitic lavas on the early Earth [7, 8] likely supplied prebiotic oceans and lakes with abundant Mg²⁺ and Fe²⁺ [7].

These environments, enriched in bicarbonate (HCO3⁻) due to CO2-rich atmospheric conditions, would inhibit apatite nucleation [9], promoting the formation and stability of minerals such as newberyite and struvite in subaqueous settings [10, 11]. These studies have also been complimented by the recent findings that modern carbonate lakes can exhibit high concentrations of dissolved phosphate (up to ~ 0.1 molal). Under steady-state conditions, where evaporation is balanced by stream inflow, these systems facilitate phosphate accumulation because calcium is preferentially sequestered into carbonate minerals. As a result, the removal of dissolved phosphate via apatite precipitation is effectively inhibited [12].

Similarly, vivianite is also considered prebiotically relevant, as it could have formed through processes comparable to those responsible for newberyite and struvite mineralization on the early Earth [13, 14] (Fig. 1).

Condensed P species relevance to the prebiotic chemistry

Another possibility involves the use of condensed phosphorus compounds as plausible prebiotic phosphorylation agents. Pioneering work by Yamagata and colleagues [15] demonstrated that volcanic activity can generate water-soluble polyphosphates—such as pyrophosphate, tri- and tetraphosphates, and trimetaphosphate—through partial hydrolysis of P4O10. However, the presence of P4O10 on the early Earth remains uncertain. The high-temperature, strongly acidic conditions required for its formation may have been difficult to achieve under prebiotic environments. For example, fumarolic systems would likely have lacked sufficient acidity due to the absence of biogenic O2 that, in a modern setting, oxidizes elemental sulfur or SO2 to sulfuric acid [5, 16].

Polyphosphates are widely considered plausible prebiotic phosphorylating agents [4, 5, 17, 18]. A major challenge in linking these species to prebiotic phosphorus chemistry, however, is their scarcity as minerals in the geological record [5]. The discovery of canaphite—the first mineral containing a pyrophosphate ion—in 1985 [19] provided support for the idea that polyphosphates could have been prebiotically relevant reagents. Nevertheless, reports of pyrophosphate or other polyphosphate minerals in nature remain exceedingly rare [17]. This limited occurrence is largely due to the fact that polyphosphates form from orthophosphate under strongly acidic conditions, and the equilibrium constant for this condensation reaction at pH 0 is approximately 10⁶ times greater than at pH 14 [5]. Condensation is more favorable when phosphoric acid is the precursor, because water is a more effective leaving group than OH⁻ or O²⁻, which must be eliminated from tribasic phosphates. However, under the low-pH conditions that favor condensation, phosphate would likely leach from apatite minerals before sufficient heating could drive the formation of polyphosphates. Trimetaphosphate has long been regarded as a prebiotically relevant polyphosphate and has been extensively applied in phosphorylation studies; a full review of these reactions is beyond the scope of this work [1, 20–22].

It is important to highlight the significance of ring-opening reactions of trimetaphosphate (and other cyclic polyphosphates), as these reactions are exothermic and have generated considerable interest regarding their potential energetic contribution to prebiotic chemistry. However, similar to P4O10, trimetaphosphate has never been identified as a naturally occurring mineral or detected in volcanic fumarole waters, raising questions about its prebiotic plausibility. Interestingly, compounds such as pyrophosphate, trimetaphosphate, and other polyphosphates can form during heat-driven oxidation of reduced phosphorus species, including phosphite [23] and hypophosphate [24], providing an alternative pathway for their production under early Earth conditions.

Non-aqueous solvents & prebiotic phosphorylation reactions

To overcome the challenge known as “the phosphate problem,” researchers have explored the use of alternative, prebiotically plausible non-aqueous solvents. Using water as the reaction medium can be counterproductive for phosphorylation because the process requires dehydration, and water must be removed to form P–O–C bonds. One of the most widely discussed alter-native solvents is formamide (HCONH2).

Schoffstall first proposed its use as a potentially prebiotic reagent in 1976 [25], and since then, formamide has been extensively examined in prebiotic chemistry. It forms through the hydrolysis of HCN [26] and readily promotes condensation reactions, often resulting in enhanced phosphorylation yields. Comprehensive reviews of phosphorylation reactions conducted in formamide have been provided elsewhere [4, 5, 17]. A deep eutectic solvent mixture of urea and choline chloride has also been proposed as a prebiotic solvent, although its relevance to early Earth conditions remains uncertain. Even in the case of formamide, its widespread availability on the early Earth is doubtful [27], as pure formamide formed from HCN in aqueous environments would be prone to hydrolysis back to ammonium formate. To address this limitation, Burcar and colleagues proposed a semi-aqueous system containing urea, ammonium formate, and water that, at 70–80 °C, facilitates the conversion of apatite to struvite and substantially improves nucleoside phosphorylation yields [6, 7]. While this scenario is compelling for localized environments—such as evaporative ponds or geothermal systems—it is unlikely that such a solvent system would have been ubiquitous enough to influence the chemistry of global oceans or large water bodies on the early Earth.

Significance of additives & prebiotic phosphorylation reactions

Another important approach involves introducing prebiotically relevant additives (including condensation agents) into aqueous systems containing both organic molecules and a phosphorus source [28–30]. These agents help promote dehydration reactions in water by decreasing water activity, thereby favoring phosphorylation.

In addition, the hydrolysis of condensation agents releases energy, which can help drive endergonic phosphorylation reactions. For example, urea has proven particularly effective as a condensation agent in nucleoside phosphorylation. Its hydrolysis enthalpy (− 14.8 kJ/mol) closely matches the energy required to phosphorylate a nucleoside (+ 14.2 kJ/mol) [5], making the overall process energetically favorable. Beyond supplying energy, condensation agents can also form reactive intermediates that facilitate phosphate transfer [5].

Notably, urea is one of the most successful and prebiotically plausible condensation agents identified to date. It enhances phosphorylation not only of nucleosides but also of other biologically relevant molecules—including glycerol, ethanolamine, and choline—yielding their corresponding phosphate esters. Additionally, condensation agents such as urea lead to improved synthetic yields of phospholipid precursors under plausible prebiotic conditions [30].

Reduced oxidation state P compounds: an alternative approach to the ‘phosphate problem

An alternative approach focuses on the use of reduced oxidation state phosphorus species (hereafter referred to as reduced P), which are more soluble and chemically reactive than orthophosphate. Schreibersite—a meteoritic mineral—serves as a primary source of reduced P; upon aqueous corrosion it produces phosphite along with a range of other inorganic P compounds [31–33]. Additionally, various terrestrial processes can generate reduced P, including lightning [34, 35], serpentinization [36], hydrothermal activity [37], biogeochemical cycling [38], and biological phosphate reduction by marine planktonic organisms [39]. Although phosphite represents a reduced form of phosphorus, it is relatively stable under natural conditions, and substantial evidence supports its relevance and likely abundance on the early Earth [40]. The re-cent discovery of a phosphite mineral (CaHPO3) further reinforces the ubiquity of reduced P in nature [41].

Reduced P compounds have been explored as potential prebiotic phosphonylating agents—that is, for direct incorporation into organic molecules to form P–C or P–O–C bonds. Gulick proposed that reduced P species are approximately 1000 times more soluble than their phosphate analogs [42], making them particularly attractive for early Earth chemistry. However, experimental H-phosphonylation using phosphite still typically requires condensation agents, mild heating, and low-water or anhydrous environments, as demonstrated in recent studies [43, 44]. Despite these constraints, reported yields are encouraging, suggesting that phosphite is a promising prebiotic phosphorus source. A key observation from these studies is that phosphite can condense to form the high-energy compound pyrophosphite under warm (70–80 °C), evaporative conditions in the presence of urea. These reactions proceed efficiently in open vials [44], and even under highly evaporative conditions on unsealed watch glasses, where greater surface area facilitates enhanced phosphorylation (as observed in our recent experiments).

Gull and colleagues have also examined the meteoritic mineral schreibersite analog, Fe3P, as a potential phosphorus source for forming organophosphorus compounds. To date, Fe3P has been demonstrated to phosphorylate glycerol [40], adenosine [45], and choline chloride [46]. However, the detailed reaction mechanisms remain largely unresolved. In addition, the reported product yields are relatively low (approximately 5–8%) and still require urea as an additive to facilitate phosphorylation.

Formation & significance of inorganic P-N species

Krishnamurthy and colleagues reported a valuable finding regarding the inefficiency of trimetaphosphate—despite being a condensed phosphate—as a phosphorylation agent for glycolaldehyde [47]. Building on earlier work by Quimby et al., which showed that ring-opening of trimetaphosphate in aqueous ammonium hydroxide produces amidotriphosphate [48, 49], they demonstrated that amidotriphosphate is a highly effective phosphorylation reagent in the formation of glycolaldehyde phosphate [47]. In a subsequent study, Krishnamurthy’s group further showed that amidotriphosphate efficiently phosphorylates α-hydroxy aldehydes (including ribose and a range of prebiotically relevant sugars) in aqueous solution [50], and they introduced diamidophosphate (DAP) as an additional P–N compound with strong prebiotic potential [50]. Later research revealed remarkable efficiency of DAP as a prebiotic phosphorylation agent, thereby presenting a promising route to alleviating the “phosphate problem.” Notable examples include: prebiotic synthesis of phosphoenolpyruvate [51]; phosphorylation of amino acids, ribonucleosides, and other biomolecules under wet–dry cycles and wet-paste conditions [52]; and regioselective 5′-phosphorylation of nucleosides under mild heating during wet–dry cycling [53, 54].

For a long time, one of the only viable routes to form inorganic P–N species, as previously suggested, has been the ring-opening reaction of trimetaphosphate in aqueous ammonia solutions (Scheme 1). In this context, corrosion studies of the meteoritic mineral schreibersite (or its synthetic analog, Fe3P) in aqueous ammoniacal solution (NH4OH) under anoxic conditions at room temperature have demonstrated the potential prebiotic synthesis of DAP, monoamidophosphate (MAP), and even a reduced phosphorus species, amidophosphite [55].

Recently, Gull and colleagues reported that ammonolysis of pyrophosphite—a prebiotically plausible dimer of phosphite—spontaneously produces amidophosphite, which rapidly undergoes oxidation under ambient conditions to yield MAP and DAP [56]. Interestingly, not all ring-opening reactions of condensed or linear phosphorus species in aqueous ammonia lead to P–N formation. For example, hexametaphosphate and Graham’s salt do not generate P–N compounds under similar conditions. Likewise, although ammonolysis of pyrophosphite readily forms P–N species, aqueous ammonolysis of pyrophosphate does not produce any. These contrasting outcomes can be largely explained by differences in the free energies of hydrolysis (ΔG°rxn) of the respective inorganic phosphorus species [56].

These P–N species (amidophosphates and amidophosphite) are not only more reactive toward organic substrates—enabling the formation of organophosphorus compounds under relatively mild conditions—but they also offer a potential solution to the longstanding “phosphate problem.” Scheme 1 briefly compares the major synthetic pathways under potential prebiotic conditions.

Experimental studies have shown that, in the presence of divalent cations, dissolved orthophosphate readily precipitates from solution, whereas amidophosphates and phosphite remain soluble and chemically available [55, 56]. This persistence in solution suggests that such reduced phosphorus–nitrogen species may have played an important role in facilitating phosphorylation chemistry on the early Earth. Presence of ammonia on the early Earth & its relevance to prebiotic chemistry

In this subsection, the presence and relevance of ammonia on the early Earth are briefly discussed. Ammonia is a significant precursor molecule, particularly from the perspective of prebiotic chemistry, as it is an essential component of amino acids, nitrogenous bases, and many important proto-metabolic precursors [57]. On the early Earth, nitrogen-containing biomolecules or their precursors would have been synthesized through the fixation of inert N₂ gas into various useful nitrogen compounds such as NH3, nitrite (NO2⁻), and nitrate (NO3⁻) [58]. Thus, N2 can be regarded as one of the primary sources of NH3 on the early Earth [59, 60].

Several studies suggest that N2 reduction could occur via multiple processes, including the reduction of NO2⁻ and NO3⁻ (formed from the electrical discharge of atmospheric N2) in the presence of oceanic Fe²⁺ [61], photoreduction of N2 on mineral surfaces [62], and crustal N2 reduction in submarine hydrothermal systems [63, 64].

Photochemical reduction of N2 to NH3 in the presence of TiO2 has been reported to be highly efficient and has been suggested to produce high concentrations of NH3. These findings are supported by investigations of naturally occurring titanium-rich sites (e.g., Imperial Sand Dunes, California), which show high concentrations of ammonia. It has been estimated that an area of approximately 4,047 m² of this desert sand generates between 1 and 10 kg of NH3 per year. Accordingly, global ammonia production has been estimated to range between 6.8 × 10⁹ and 6.8 × 10¹⁰ kg per year. This strongly suggests that photochemical reduction of N2 could have been a major source of ammonia on the early Earth [65].

Recent modeling studies suggest that abiotic N2 reduction could supply approximately 9.0–10.8 × 10¹⁰ mol·year⁻¹ of NH4⁺ to the prebiotic global oceans. Although these estimated amounts are modest compared to the large dissolved nitrogen inventory in modern oceans, they could still have provided sufficient ammonia to the early oceans [66]. The possible sources and sinks of ammonia and other dissolved nitrogen compounds have also been discussed previously [67].

More recently, it has been shown that mackinawite, a commonly occurring iron sulfide in submarine hydrothermal systems, significantly enhances its capacity to adsorb ammonia upon electroreduction to zero-valent iron. This process enables up to ~ 90% buildup of 1 mM ammonia in 1 M NaCl at neutral pH [68].

In addition to N2 reduction, extraterrestrial sources may also have contributed significantly to ammonia on the early Earth. For example, the meteorite Grave Nunataks (GRA) 95,229 has been shown to generate abundant free ammonia under hydrothermal conditions [69].

Overall, this discussion suggests that the early Earth had multiple reliable sources of NH3 (and other dissolved nitrogen compounds), supporting the formation of P–N compounds as well as other key metabolic precursors. Implications of P–N chemistry for other habitable environments

After establishing the significance of P–N species in aqueous environments on the Hadean or early Earth, it is valuable to consider their potential relevance on other habitable worlds. The key insight from prebiotic P–N chemistry is that the formation of reactive P–N compounds requires only a reduced phosphorus source—such as schreibersite or phosphite salts—or a condensed phosphate like trimetaphosphate, together with an aqueous (or ammoniacal) environment. Importantly, this chemistry does not require molecular oxygen, but instead proceeds favorably under anoxic conditions. Given that such reduced phosphorus phases are present in meteorites and may also be produced through geological or impact processes, and that ammonia and ammonium-bearing fluids are predicted or detected in several icy or ocean worlds, similar P–N species could plausibly arise beyond Earth. These compounds remain stable even in ammonia–water or ammonia–ice mixtures, suggesting they could drive phosphorylation pathways in environments where water activity is reduced and oxidative pathways are limited. Thus, the emergence of P–N chemistry may represent a broadly accessible route to organophosphorus compound synthesis in diverse habitable niches across the Solar System and potentially beyond. Other planets would require only a viable phosphorus source such as phosphite (or any reduced P compound) or alternatively condensed polyphosphates such as TMP (trimetaphosphate) or even pyrophosphite, and ammonia under anoxic conditions for similar P–N chemistry to occur. Alternatively, ammonia-rich hydrothermal systems could also provide a favorable environment for the formation and persistence of these reactive P–N species.

Rocky planets such as Mars are considered potential habitable zones. Strong evidence for surface ammonia is lacking, and data from the ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter suggest that atmospheric ammonia concentrations are likely below 14 ppbv [70, 71]. This scarcity may result from ultraviolet-mediated conversion of ammonia to N2 [72]. Derivatized ammonia has been detected in sand samples from the Bagnold Dunes in Gale Crater using the SAM instrument, though its endogenic origin remains uncertain [73]. Studies of Martian meteorites indicate that ammonia could have been preserved in the subsurface since the Noachian era, protected from ultraviolet radiation and cosmic rays [74]. Additional abiotic—and potentially biotic—sources of ammonia have also been proposed [75, 76], suggesting that its presence on Mars cannot be dismissed. Furthermore, orthophosphate and other phosphate minerals have been identified in Jezero Crater [77], pointing to the potential formation of condensed phosphorus compounds.

Hydrothermal systems on Mars have long been recognized as key targets for exploration. Geochemical analyses, gravity and topography data, and numerical models suggest that the Eridania region—once a vast inland sea potentially exceeding the combined volume of other Martian surface water—may have hosted a radiogenic hydrothermal system [78]. Evidence for hydrothermal activity is further supported by the detection of hydrated silicates, such as opal, chalcedony, and crystallized quartz. Based on their grain size and crystallinity, these minerals are interpreted to have a hydrothermal origin. These silicate deposits were identified by the SuperCam instrument onboard the Mars 2020 Perseverance mission using a combination of laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS), infrared, and Raman spectroscopy [79]. Furthermore, experimental studies suggest that phosphate release from orthophosphate minerals during water–rock interactions on Mars could be up to 45 times higher than on Earth, and dissolved phosphate concentrations in early Martian environments may have exceeded those on early Earth by more than a factor of two [80]. This indicates the potential availability of a viable phosphate source on Mars. This could possibly be the source of the polyphosphates to act as sources for P.

Data from the Cassini Ion and Neutral Mass Spectrometer (INMS) confirm the presence of ammonia in the plumes of Enceladus, alongside a variety of organic compounds [81]. Additionally, Cassini’s Cosmic Dust Analyzer detected sodium phosphates in ice grains ejected from the moon, indicating a potentially abundant phosphorus source in Enceladus’s subsurface ocean [82]. Enceladus is also thought to host ongoing high-temperature (> 90 °C) hydrothermal activity, which could rapidly transport hydrothermal reaction products from the ocean floor to the plumes [83]. Overall, the formation of P–N compounds appears highly plausible on these planetary bodies. A combination of ammonia, a reduced phosphorus source such as phosphite (delivered via meteoritic bombardment or schreibersite), or alternatively, condensed phosphorus compounds generated through hydrothermal activity, would likely suffice to produce P–N species such as amidophosphates and amidophosphites. These reactive, highly soluble compounds could readily phosphorylate nearby organic molecules. While their stability may be influenced by pH and ultraviolet radiation, the occurrence of P–N compounds remain plausible. Importantly, future exploration of these planetary environments should include targeted searches for P–N species, even in icy mixtures.

Phosphorus speciation and the potential for P–N bond formation in asteroids

In 2020, the JAXA Hayabusa2 mission successfully returned samples from the asteroid Ryugu. One of the key findings related to phosphorus compounds was the discovery of a special class of grains, up to a few hundred micrometers in size, composed of hydrated ammonium–magnesium–phosphorus (HAMP). These phosphorus-rich grains are embedded within an organic-rich phyllosilicate matrix. It has been speculated that, when exposed to primitive aqueous environments, such grains could have played a significant role in initiating prebiotic chemistry by releasing more soluble phosphorus- and nitrogen-bearing species. This process may have contributed to the onset of important synthetic reactions [84].

Quite recently, on September 24, 2023, OSIRIS-REx (Origins, Spectral Interpretation, Resource Identification, and Security–Regolith Explorer) successfully returned a capsule to Earth containing approximately 120 g of pristine carbonaceous regolith collected from the near-Earth asteroid (101955) Bennu, following a seven-year round-trip mission [85–88]. NASA’s interest in Bennu was driven in part by telescopic observations indicating a primitive, carbon-rich composition and the presence of water-bearing minerals [85–89]. These findings strongly suggested that Bennu may harbor key ingredients relevant to prebiotic chemistry, including water, thereby providing insights into pathways toward planetary habitability on Earth and other planetary bodies. Furthermore, the sample return mission offered a unique opportunity to investigate such ancient material in pristine condition [85].

Spectroscopic and microscopic analyses, including powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM), indicate that Bennu is predominantly composed of Mg-bearing phyllosilicates, particularly serpentine and smectite. These phyllosilicates are commonly associated with additional mineral phases, including iron sulfides, magnetite, and carbonates [85].

A diverse suite of phosphorus-bearing compounds has been identified within the returned samples. Minor and trace phases include Mg- and Na-phosphates, Ca-phosphates, and Fe–Ni phosphides, the latter potentially corresponding to schreibersite. Fe- and Cr-bearing phosphides, possibly analogous to andreyivanovite, have also been reported, along with various sulfides and related phases. Phosphates occur in multiple morphological forms, including isolated and dispersed particles, euhedral to anhedral grains, as well as veins and surface coatings. Notably, bright isolated aggregates are primarily composed of Mg–Na phosphate phases. Micro–FTIR analyses of individual mineral grains further indicate the presence of magnesium hydrogen phosphate (Fig. 4) [85].

On Bennu’s parent body, phosphate speciation and abundance were likely governed by an apatite–carbonate buffering system [82]. Evaporative processes may have increased sodium concentrations sufficiently to promote the precipitation of Mg–Na phosphate phases [90]. Additional studies have revealed that Bennu samples contain significant abundances of nitrogen-bearing compounds, including ammonia.

A recent study investigating the distribution of nucleobases and other N-heterocycles in asteroid Bennu, using a larger sample (~ 0.6 g; sample ID OREX-800107-108), reported the detection of various nitrogen-containing compounds, including imidazole, urea, and related derivatives [91]. These compounds are significant in prebiotic phosphorylation reactions and are also recognized as precursors to a variety of biologically relevant organic molecules. Furthermore, the presence of these nitrogen-containing species has important implications for the formation of inorganic polyphosphates and inorganic phosphorus–nitrogen (P–N) compounds under asteroidal conditions.

Enrichments in nitrogen-15 (¹⁵N) isotopes suggest that ammonia and related nitrogenous species likely formed in cold molecular clouds or within the outer regions of the protoplanetary disk. Analyses have also identified a wide range of essential amino acids, as well as nucleobases and other nitrogen-containing heterocycles, indicating their formation and possible alteration via low-temperature reactions, plausibly in NH3-rich aqueous environments [92, 93].

The identification of Mg–Na phosphate phases in Bennu has important implications for prebiotic chemistry, particularly regarding the formation of phosphorus–nitrogen (P–N) compounds. These phosphate phases bear compositional similarities to sodium phosphates detected on Enceladus [82], suggesting potential geochemical parallels between these extraterrestrial environments. On Enceladus, sodium phosphates have been identified in subsurface ocean-derived materials and cryovolcanic plumes, likely under alkaline conditions.

Comparable phosphate enrichment processes have also been documented in terrestrial soda lakes, such as Last Chance Lake and Goodenough Lake, where high concentrations of dissolved phosphate arise from the precipitation of calcium as carbonate minerals [94]. These systems illustrate geochemical pathways that facilitate the accumulation of soluble phosphate in alkaline aqueous environments [85, 94].

These observations are consistent with the work of Toner and Catling, who demonstrated that modern carbonate-rich lakes can accumulate phosphate concentrations of up to ~ 0.1 molal under steady-state conditions involving evaporation and inflow. This process is driven by the sequestration of calcium into carbonate phases, thereby inhibiting phosphate removal via apatite precipitation [12].

Collectively, these findings suggest that, on Bennu, Ryugu and similar planetary bodies, the coexistence of ammonia, dissolved phosphate species, and phosphide minerals could facilitate the formation of reactive P–N compounds such as monoamidophosphate (MAP), diamidophosphate (DAP), and amidophosphites [56]. Although such high-energy P–N species have not yet been experimentally demonstrated under Bennu-like conditions, their formation is plausible under environments characterized by elevated ammonia concentrations (potentially as low as ~ 0.4 N [55]) and alkaline pH.

Conclusions

Phosphorus chemistry is central to prebiotic processes, yet the low solubility and reactivity of phosphate under early Earth conditions—the so-called “phosphate problem”—posed a major challenge for the formation of biologically relevant organophosphorus compounds. Studies show that this challenge could have been overcome through multiple complementary pathways, including the use of reduced phosphorus compounds (e.g., phosphite from schreibersite or meteoritic sources), condensed phosphates (e.g., trimetaphosphate), and P–N species such as amidophosphates and diamidophosphate. The presence of condensation agents, non-aqueous or semi-aqueous solvents like formamide or urea-ammonium formate mixtures, and hydrothermal environments further enhanced the formation and stability of reactive phosphorus species under prebiotic conditions. Beyond Earth, these findings have important astrobiological implications.

Planets, icy moons with available ammonia, reduced or condensed phosphorus sources, and anoxic or hydrothermal conditions—such as Mars, Enceladus, or Europa—could plausibly support the formation of P–N compounds. These species are reactive, highly soluble, and capable of phosphorylating organic molecules, suggesting that prebiotic phosphorylation chemistry may be a widespread and accessible pathway in diverse habitable environments. The chemical environment of asteroid Bennu, containing carbonates, brine solution and accumulated phosphates can be comparable to the Soda lakes present on the Earth.

Future exploration targeting P–N compounds, even within icy matrices, could therefore provide crucial insights into the universality of prebiotic phosphorus chemistry.
