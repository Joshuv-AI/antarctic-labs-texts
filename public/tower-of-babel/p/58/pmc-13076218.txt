sc-rDSeq: a robust and cost-effective full-length total RNA sequencing method for single cells reveals multilayered heterogeneity in drug-resistant lung cancer cells
Authors: Xue Sun, Shir-Liya Dadon, Dena Ennis, Wenpeng Fan, Muhammad Awawdy, Eli Reuveni, Adi Alajem, Oren Ram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstractsc-rDSeq is a scalable, full-length total RNA droplet-based technology that captures both polyadenylated and nonpolyadenylated RNAs, including histone RNAs, small and long non-coding RNAs, and enhancer RNAs. It achieves a 10-fold increase in UMIs per cell compared to conventional scRNAseq like 10× Chromium and inDrops, while remaining simple and cost-efficient. Applied to lung cancer cells, sc-rDSeq uncovered hidden heterogeneity, divergent signaling pathways, and non-polyA RNA variations undetectable by 3′ end-based methods. Following EGFR inhibitor treatment, cell cycle arrest was detected through non-polyA histone messenger RNA expression, revealing seven distinct subpopulations of cells with upregulation of different persister-related programs, like migration, sterol synthesis and matrix formation. Additionally, by leveraging single-cell expression variability and pseudo-bulk analyses, sc-rDSeq unveiled alternative splicing events and single nucleotide variations that distinguished the drug resistant subsets. sc-rDSeq therefore opens the way for in-depth personalized medicine applications through massive-scale and multifaceted analysis of different RNA species, splicing events, and sequence variations.

Introduction

Over the last decade, single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) has become an essential tool for understanding cellular heterogeneity in complex biological systems such as tissue development and tumor environments [1, 2]. Significant efforts have been made to advance the technical aspects of scRNA-seq technology. Just eight years ago, only a few hundred cells could be sequenced at a time, but today, hundreds of thousands of single cells can be sequenced with greater sensitivity, enabling accurate characterization of cell types, states, and unique populations. However, most methods rely on oligo-dT priming, which captures only polyadenylated RNA (polyA+) and are limited to the length of reads with unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) deduplication [3], which covers up to 600 bp upstream to the 3′ end, or downstream to the 5′ end [4, 5]. As a result, nonpolyadenylated RNA (polyA−), including noncoding RNA (ncRNA), histone RNA, micro-RNA, and enhancer RNA (eRNA), remain undetected. Similarly, short read coverage provides insufficient information to accurately map reads to distinct alternative RNA isoforms. This limitation restricts our ability to study these crucial transcriptome fractions and prevents us from exploring key transcriptional features such as alternative splicing events and, potentially, the detection of RNA-based mutations.

Currently, the most widely used single-cell full-length profiling methods are Smart-Seq2 and Smart-Seq3 [6, 7], which offer high sensitivity. However, they are limited to polyA+ RNAs, cost-prohibitive, and scale-limited due to the use of Tn5 transposase and well-plate-based single-cell isolation. More recent methods, such as Smart-seq-total [8] and VASA-seq [9] extend coverage to polyA− RNA by polyadenylation of total RNAs, allowing the use of standard polyT primers for capturing. Of these, VASA-seq is the only method that captures full-length total RNA with UMI incorporation. However, it requires two microfluidic steps for droplet-merging, making it inaccessible for most microfluidics labs and not compatible with common droplet-based platforms like 10× Genomics.

To address these limitations, we developed sc-rDSeq, a droplet-based method for full-length, strand-specific total RNA sequencing in single cells, with high sensitivity and low cost. Built on the inDrops [4] platform, sc-rDSeq incorporates a major modification in primer design and library preparation. It employs in vitro transcription for linear amplification, incorporates UMIs for accurate quantification, and is fully compatible with high-throughput droplet systems.

A major limitation of current total RNA single cell sequencing methods is their co-capture of rRNA with non-rRNA transcripts, necessitating post-amplification rRNA depletion step. Because rRNA constitutes over 80%–90% of cellular RNA [10], it consumes a significant proportion of reagents, thereby reducing the sensitivity for detecting non-rRNA transcripts. To avoid this, we decided to apply a selective priming strategy to avoid rRNA binding during reverse transcription, therefore exclude the need for downstream removal steps. Armour et al. [11] demonstrated that “Not-So-Random” (NSR) primers reduce rRNA content from ~80% to ~13% in bulk RNA-seq. RamDA-seq subsequently adapted NSR primers for single-cell RNA applications, enabled the detection of recursive splicing and eRNA expression at the single-cell level [12]. However, it was limited in sensitivity (~17 000 transcripts per cell) and scalability (Fluidigm-based). Moreover, the lack of strand specificity limited the detection of bidirectional peaks, thereby constraining the identification of novel enhancer-associated eRNAs.

To bring the advantages of NSR priming strategies into high-throughput single-cell profiling, we integrated the NSR primers into the droplet-based inDrops platform. Although NSR primers perform efficiently in bulk RNA-seq, their performance changed when incorporated into single-cell barcoding sequences, likely due to the presence of reverse-complement pairs within the pool that interfered with barcode synthesis and for their ability to capture rRNA through 5-mers hybridization. To overcome this, we selected a subset of 220 primers from the original NSR set that showed optimal efficiency. We termed this refined set “ribosomal-depleted sequences” (rDS). These primers enable efficient rRNA exclusion during reverse transcription while maintaining broad transcriptome coverage, making them well suited for droplet-based single-cell sequencing. Unlike polyT primers, rDS primers can bind at multiple positions along RNA molecules, enabling full-length coverage and improved detection of nonpolyadenylated and low-abundance RNAs.

We validated the primers design in bulk experiments and then benchmarked sc-rDSeq against 10× Chromium, inDrops and other state-of-the-art single-cell RNA-seq methods. sc-rDSeq demonstrated superior sensitivity, broader RNA diversity, full-length coverage, and the lowest cost per cell (~$0.08) among comparable methods. To present the potential of sc-rDSeq as a sensitive and scalable method for multifaceted analysis of single cells, we focused on the case of nonsmall cell lung cancer (NSCLC). Despite the accumulation of new drugs for this cancer type, resistance to treatments remains a major problem [13]. Recent studies have shown that, in addition to somatic mutations, transcriptionally defined cellular states can play a major role in drug response, suggesting a complex epigenomic mechanism underlying transcriptional regulation in the development of drug resistance [14]. Hence, we decided to leverage sc-rDSeq for studying the heterogeneity of drug-tolerant “persister” (DTP) cells from an NSCLC cancer cell line. We profiled >7000 DTPs and control cells using sc-rDSeq and characterized the shift in cellular states from naïve to persistent, based on both polyA+ and polyA− RNA expression profiles. Notably, G1 arresting is observed in DTPs, supported by both polyA+ cell cycle marker gene expression and polyA− histone messenger RNA (mRNA) expression. Canonical cancer pathways were upregulated in DTPs, along with some known genes for resistance. DTPs showed different cellular states with differentially expression of mRNA, microRNA (miRNA), and other ncRNAs. Having access to full-length readouts enabled the inclusion of alternative splicing as an additional source of information for characterizing cellular states in the persister and responding populations. For example, despite similar overall expression levels of the MDK gene, a polyT-based single-cell RNA-seq method may miss a predominantly expressed transcript that encodes a functional protein, while other cells may express a truncated isoform—potentially resulting in altered protein function. Having access to strand-specific readouts further allowed us to identify putative eRNA, as regions with marked bi-directional transcription. Interestingly, analysis of eRNA expression levels showed strong association to the expression of their putative targeted genes. Taken together, sc-rDSeq is a cost-effective, high-throughput, and sensitive single-cell method that ensures full-length coverage of total RNA and enhances sensitivity, opening opportunities to explore the interactive information between mRNA and other types of RNA at the single-cell level.

Material and methods

Cell culture

Human PC9 lung adenocarcinoma cells were kindly provided by Prof. Ravid Straussman (Weizmann Institute, Israel). PC9 cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM) (Sigma–Aldrich, cat #D5671) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Biological Industries Israel, cat #04-007-1A), 50 μg/ml penicillin–streptomycin (Biological Industries Israel, cat #03031-1B), 2 mM L-glutamine (Biological Industries Israel, cat #03-020-1B), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Biological Industries Israel, cat #03-042-1B). Mouse embryonic stem cell (mESC) R1 were kindly provided by Prof. Eran Meshorer (The Hebrew University, Jerusalem). R1 cells were grown on 0.1% gelatin-coated standard tissue culture dishes and maintained in ESC medium [DMEM, 15% ESC-grade FBS, 50 μg/ml penicillin–streptomycin, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.1 mM nonessential amino acids (Biological Industries Israel, cat #01-340-1B), 0.1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma–Aldrich, cat #M3148)].

Viable cell counting by colorimetric assay and dose-response curve

The dose-response was assessed by counting viable cells using Cell Proliferation Kit (XTT based; Biological Industries Israel, cat #20-300). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a concentration of 5000 cells/well. Twenty four hours after plating, media was removed and replaced with media containing Osimertinib (AZD9291, Selleck Chemicals, cat #S7297) or Gefitinib (Selleck Chemicals, cat #S1025) for 72 h. Both drugs were tested at concentrations ranging from 1 nM to 100 μM, using three-fold serial dilutions. Absorbance at 450 nm (sample absorbance) and 660 nm (reference absorbance) was measured in two steps using a spectrophotometer (Biotek Synergy H1 plate reader). A blank control, containing only medium, was set up for each row. Drug treatment experiments

EGFR inhibitors were prepared as 10 mM stock solutions in Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO): Osimertinib and Gefitinib. Small aliquots (10–15 μl) were stored at −20°C to minimize freeze-thaw cycles. For drug treatment experiments, stock solutions were first diluted in DMSO and then in culture medium, maintaining a final DMSO concentration of 1% across all dilutions. Untreated cells were given 1% DMSO as a control. As started, 1 × 106 PC9 cells were seeded to a 10 cm2 plate. Twenty four hours after seeding, 1 μM Osimertinib and 1 μM Gefitinib were given to cells. Every 3 days, medium was changed with fresh drugs. After 9 days, remained cells were collected for sc-rDSeq. Control groups were treated with DMSO for 3 days.

Species-mixing experiments

To determine off-species contamination in the single-cell preparations, sc-rDSeq was performed with a suspension mixture of PC9/R1 mESCs cells. The suspension mixtures were prepared at a total concentration of 300 000 cells/ml (1:1 ratio).

Microfluidics device design, fabrication and operation

Two microfluidics devices were used: (i) to produce acrylamide hydrogel microparticles with acrydite-modified DNA primers for barcoding; (ii) to encapsulate single cells with barcodes, lysis buffer, and reverse transcriptase (RT) enzyme within droplet. Device design, fabrication, and operation protocols followed the protocol described previously [16, 17].

Production of sc-rDSeq barcoded hydrogel beads and quality control

Hydrogel beads carrying barcoded DNA primers were produced following the method described before [16, 17]. The acrydite-modified DNA primer and the hydrogel beads containing it were handled in darkness or under UV-filtered light. DNA primers on polymerized hydrogel beads were barcoded using a combination of the split-and-pool method and a primer extension reaction. This process involved three rounds of priming: the first two rounds assigned single-cell barcodes, and the third round extended the rDS/polyT5C8N primers. Each round consisted of primer loading, hybridization and extension, performed by the Biomek 4000 liquid handling robot (Beckman Coulter Life Sciences).

In the first two rounds, acrydite hydrogels with 2.5× isothermal amplification buffer (NEB, cat #B0537) and 0.85 mM dNTP (NEB, cat #N0446) were distributed in a 384-well plate at 6 μl per well (~40 000 hydrogel beads). For each round, 9 μl of 50 µM BC1 primers (PE1*-BC1*-Spacer1*, detailed sequences at Supplementary Table S1) or BC2 primers (Spacer1*-BC2*-UMI*-Spacer2*, detailed sequences at Supplementary Table S1) were added to each well, with each well containing a unique primer. After denaturation at 85°C for 2 min and hybridization at 60°C for 20 min, 5 μl of primer extension mix containing 1.8 U of DNA Polymerase (NEB, cat #M0537) and 0.3 mM dNTP (NEB, cat #N0446) in 1× isothermal amplification buffer (NEB, cat #B0537) was added, followed by incubation at 60°C for 60 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 20 μl of stop buffer [100 mM KCl, 10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 50 mM EDTA, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20] and incubating at room temperature for 10 min. The beads were then collected and washed for single strand as described previously [4]. At each round, before washing, 0.5 μl of beads were saved in 9.5 μl DDW in a 1.5 ml tube for later-on quality control. The split-and-pool process produced a barcode combination complexity of 147 456 (Supplementary Fig. S1). In the third round, a primer mixture was prepared for all beads, consisting of 50 µM rDS/polyT5C8N primers (Spacer2*-UMI*-rDS* primer/Spacer2*-UMI*- polyT5C8N* primer, detailed sequences at Supplementary Table S1) in a 4:1 ratio. The rDS primers were pre-mixed from 220 different sequences (Supplementary File S2, sc-rDSeq Barcode primers). The priming procedure was like the first two rounds, with the exception that, each well contained the same primer mixture (Supplementary Fig. S1).

To remove primers that failed to elongate at each step and did not end with rDS/ polyT5C8N, a cleanup process was performed. After priming, all beads were collected in a 50 ml tube and washed twice in T10E0.1T0.1 buffer [10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1% (v/v) Tween 20]. Next, 0.25 U/μl ExoI and 1× ExoI buffer (NEB, cat #M0293) were added. The tube was rotated at 37°C for 60 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped by adding 10 ml stop buffer and incubating on ice for 10 min, followed by washing as in the previous step.

After barcode synthesis, the barcoded hydrogel beads were filtered twice using a 70-µm cell strainer (pluriSelect, cat #43-10070-50) to obtain homogeneously sized beads with an approximate diameter of 60 µm and an average of 109 copies of fully extended DNA primers per bead [18]. Small batches of beads (~0.5 μl) saved per round were first exposed to UV for 10 min to release the primers, then run on a 2% agarose gel for quality control. Clear bands around 120 bp should be observed for the final products, while bands from earlier steps may show slight smears at lower sizes due to incomplete primer extensions.

Single-cell barcoding

For both inDrops and sc-rDSeq, the microfluidics operations following protocol described previously [16, 17]. The devices have four inlets: (i) Cell inlet: cells were loaded at 300 000 cells/ml in PBS containing 10% v/v OptiPrep (STEMCELL Technologies, cat # 07820) and maintained in suspension using a magnetic micro-stirrer bar placed within the tube. (ii) Barcoded hydrogel beads inlet: inDrop barcoding beads were prepared as previously described [18], and sc-rDSeq barcoding beads were prepared in the last step. Around 100–150 μl beads were centrifuged in a 1.5-ml Eppendorf tube at 1500 rcf for 2 min to obtain packed beads. After aspirating residual buffer from the pelleted beads, the tube was loaded onto the corresponding inlet in the microfluidics setup. (iii) Reverse transcription/lysis mix inlet: 150 μl RT/lysis mix consisted of 30 μl 10× StellarScript buffer, 15μl StellarScript RT enzyme 200 U/μl (StellarScript HT Reverse Transcriptase Kit, Watchmaker, cat #WM-7K0070), 10 μl murine RNase inhibitor (NEB, cat #M0314), 15 μl 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 8.0), 10 μl 0.1 M Dithiothreitol (DTT), 9 μl 10% (v/v) IGEPAL CA-630 (Sigma–Aldrich, cat #I8896), 6 μl 25 mM dNTPs (NEB, cat #N0446S) and 55 μl nuclease-free water. (iv) Carrier oil inlet: The carrier oil was 3 ml of HFE-7500 with 2% (w/w) fluorosurfactant. During microfluidics runs, cell suspension and collection tubes were kept on ice. The microfluidics chips generate monodispersed droplets with volumes in the range of 1–3 nl. inDrops collection emulsions were kept in 1.5 ml Eppendorf tubes pre-filled with 200 μl 2% HFE-7500, and sc-rDSeq collection emulsion were collected into 96-well plates pre-filled with 50 μl 2% HFE-7500 per well. After collections, 100 and 20 μl mineral oil were added on each collection, respectively.

Library preparation

The inDrops libraries were prepared following the protocol described previously [18]. The sc-rDSeq process was similar, with the following modifications: after the microfluidics collections, the collection well plate was exposed to 6.5 J/cm² from a 365-nm UV lamp while kept on ice for 10 min to release photocleavable barcoding primers from the barcoding beads. Next, the collection well plate containing the UV-exposed emulsion was transferred to a PCR machine for a ramping-cycling reverse transcription reaction under 20 cycles of the following conditions: 8°C for 12 s, 15°C for 45 s, 20°C for 45 s, 30°C for 30 s, and 50°C for 15 s. Then, emulsions from 2–3 wells were combined into a 1.5-ml Eppendorf tube, with each tube containing 1000–3000 cells per sample. The following protocol, up to and including in vitro transcription (IVT), was the same as for inDrop [18].

After IVT, resulting amplified RNA (aRNA) was purified using a 1.3× reaction volume of AMPure XP beads and eluted with 20 μl of 10 mM Tris–HCl. An aliquot of 9 μl was stored at −80°C as a backup. Next, RNA was fragmented using the RNA fragmentation kit (Invitrogen, cat #AM8740). Nine microliters of aRNA was combined with 1 μl of RNA fragmentation reagent and incubated at 70°C for 1min, then transferred to ice. A 40 μl fragmentation stop mix containing 5 μl fragmentation stop solution and 35 μl 10 mM Tris–HCl was added. Fragmented RNA was purified with a 1.3× reaction volume of AMPure XP beads and eluted in 10 μl of 10 mM Tris–HCl. The amplified and fragmented RNA was reverse transcribed using a random hexamer primer. Specifically, 12 μl RNA was mixed with 2 μl of 100 µM PE2-N6-v2 primer containing random hexamer (Supplementary Table S1), 1 μl of 10 mM dNTP, 2 μl of 10× StellarScript buffer, 1 μl of 0.1 M DTT, 1 μl Murine RNase inhibitor (40 U/μl), and 1 μl of StellarScript RT enzyme (200 U/μl). Samples were incubated at 25°C for 10 min, 50°C for 60 min, and 80°C for 10 min. Following reverse transcription, the reaction volume was increased to 40 μl by adding 20 μl of nuclease-free water. An aliquot of 20 μl was stored at −80°C for backup. The resulting complementary DNA (cDNA) was purified with a 1.2× reaction volume of AMPure XP beads and eluted in 11.5 μl of 10 mM Tris–HCl. The libraries were then PCR amplified using a standard mix. Primers containing Illumina library indices for multiplexing were added. Each PCR reaction contained 11.5 μl of the postreverse transcription cDNA library, 12.5 μl of 2× KAPA HiFi HotStart ReadyMix (Roche), and 1 μl of 25 µM PE1/PE2 index primer mix (Supplementary Table S1), and consisted of 10 amplification cycles, following conditions: 98°C for 2 min, 2 × (98°C for 20 s, 55°C for 30 s, 72°C for 40 s), 8 × (98°C for 20 s, 65°C for 30 s, 72°C for 40 s), 72°C for 10 min. Amplified libraries were purified using a 0.7× reaction volume of AMPure XP beads and eluted in 20 μl of nuclease-free water. Library quality was confirmed on the Agilent 2200 TapeStation nucleic acid system using the Agilent High Sensitivity D1000 DS DNA kit. The final libraries had an average size of 300–500 bp.

Protocol notes

IVT-aRNA is fragmented prior to random-primed reverse transcription to maximize library complexity and uniform coverage across transcripts. A ligation-based alternative protocol without fragmentation can better preserve shorter fragments but increases primer-dimer formation and the proportion of large fragments disfavored during PCR and cluster generation.

Bulk control experiments

Bulk control experiments were performed with PC9 cells using whole-cell lysate (2000 cells), total RNA (20 ng), or genomic DNA (2 ng) as input, with two biological replicates per condition. Total RNA was extracted with the NucleoSpin RNA kit (MACHEREY-NAGEL, cat #740955.50), yielding RNA of high purity (A260/A280 = 2.11) and integrity (RIN = 9.9). SIRV Set-3 RNA spike-in (Lexogen, cat. #051.0N, including ERCC) was added to the lysis buffer to achieve ~5% of reads mapping to SIRV transcripts.

Unless otherwise specified, bulk controls were processed following the sc-rDSeq protocol. Each reaction contained 20 μl of input material (cells in PBS, total RNA, or gDNA) mixed with 20 μl of RT/lysis mix. Primers were either supplied in the RT/lysis mix or released by UV from sc-rDSeq barcodes (0.5 μl barcodes per reaction). polyT and polyT5C8N priming were compared using T7-PE1-polyT and T7-PE1-polyT5C8N primers (detailed sequences in Supplementary Table S1) at a final concentration of 5 μM. For benchmarking, KAPA Stranded RNA-seq libraries were prepared following the manufacturer’s protocol (Roche, cat #07962142001), without prior rRNA depletion, to ensure consistency with the sc-rDSeq protocol for comparison. In-house polyT-based RNA-seq libraries were generated with T7-PE1-polyT primers, using reverse transcription at 50°C for 2 h.

scNSR barcoded hydrogel bead production

scNSR barcoded hydrogel bead were prepared in the same way as sc-rDSeq barcodes. Only the Spacer2*-UMI*-rDS* primer was replaced with Spacer2*-UMI*-NSR* primer. Full set of NSR primers were retrieved from [11].

Library sequencing

Two types of sequencing platforms were used in this work, based on the data size. inDrops libraries were sequenced using Illumina NextSeq 2000 instrument with NextSeq 1000/2000 P2 Reagent kit (100 Cycles). sc-rDSeq and bulk experiments were sequenced at Hadassah Genomic center using NovaSeq 6000 instrument with NovaSeq 6000 S1 Reagent Kit (100 cycles). Cycle distribution for single-cell experiments was 50, 70, 8, 8 cycles for Read 1, Read 2, index 1, and index 2, respectively, and 55, 65, 8, 8 cycles for bulk experiments.

Raw data filtering and demultiplexing

The raw sequencing data were paired-end fastq files, split into two lanes and four lanes per read for the NextSeq 2000 and NovaSeq 6000, respectively. In data QC, Read1 from all lanes was iterated through and filtered for reads possessing the correct barcode structure (for inDrops, search for the W1 adaptor; for sc-rDSeq, search for Spacer1 and Spacer2), and single-cell barcodes and UMIs were extracted. Barcodes that could be uniquely assigned to an accepted barcode with a Hamming distance of 2 nt or less were merged. UMI deduplication was performed by removing reads with duplicated UMIs under the same single-cell barcode. Unique UMI reads per barcode were counted and plotted in a histogram. Thresholds were determined based on the separation between the first and second peaks. This threshold was then applied in the filtering step, where a similar process was performed as in QC, but outputting reads with UMI counts greater than the threshold into barcode-specific fastq files. Single cell fastqs were then trimmed using cutadapt v4.0 [19] for Illumina adaptors, barcode spacers and repeated elements (20× A/T/G/C). Finally reads aligned to ribosomal RNA were removed using bwa mem and bwa aln v0.7.17 [20] with human 12S, 16S, 18S, 28S, and 5.8S rRNA sequences from NCBI. Detailed sequencing depth and UMI deduplication statistics are presented in Supplementary Table S2 for single-cell datasets and Supplementary Table S3 for bulk datasets.

Read alignments

inDrops reads were aligned as described previously [16, 17]. For sc-rDSeq, reads were aligned in multiple rounds to exclusively map to total RNA (Supplementary Fig. S11). Briefly, reads were first mapped to the whole genome (GRCh38, Ensembl Release 112), then counted with different gene annotation GTF files in each round. Only reads that failed to align proceeded to the next round. The alignment order was short RNA, long RNA exons, long RNA introns, and pseudogenes. GTF files used for each round were subsets of the full GTF, created by defining specific filters for gene biotypes. Alignments were performed in a strand-specific manner. Different alignment and quantification tools were applied in each step to maximize alignment efficiency, including STAR v2.7.11 [21], featureCounts v2.0.6 [22], RSEM v1.3.2 [23] wrapping Bowtie2 v2.5.0 [24]. Multimappers were processed with expectation maximization (EM) algorithm implemented in RSEM. Detailed GTF filter and alignment parameters for each round of alignment are listed in Supplementary Table S4. To analyze gene coverage and genomic location of the reads, BAM files from individual cells were combined and qualified by QoRTs [25].

Bulk sequencing data were aligned with STAR using a merged reference of human genome (GRCh38, Ensembl Release 112) and SIRV, with SIRV_Set3_Sequences_20210507a fasta and gtf files downloaded from the Lexogen website. Transcript quantification was performed with Cufflinks [26] (v2.2.1). ERCC and SIRV analyses were carried out using the SIRVsuite package provided by the manufacturer.

Benchmarking against 10× chromium

Benchmarking of sc-rDSeq against 10× Chomium methods was performed using publicly available datasets generated from the PC9 nonsmall-cell lung cancer cell line. Two 10× Genomics Chromium workflows were included: a standard singleplex dataset generated using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Kit v2 (GEO: GSE150949) [27] and a multiplexed dataset generated using the Chromium Single Cell 3′ Kit v3.1 in combination with the CellPlex CMO system (GEO: GSE247691 [28]). Raw FASTQ files were downloaded from GEO and processed using standard Cell Ranger pipelines. All datasets were processed using the GRCh38 reference genome provided by 10× Genomics, and BAM files were retained for downstream analyses. To enable direct cross-platform comparison, inDrops and sc-rDSeq datasets were converted into a 10×-compatible BAM format. Reads were first filtered based on expected barcode structure, and reads with invalid barcode composition were discarded. Corrected cell barcodes (CB) and UMIs (UB) were written into read headers, followed by adapter trimming. Reads were aligned to the GRCh38 genome using STAR and gene-level assignments were obtained using featureCounts, as described in the previous section. Barcode correction performance was assessed directly from BAM files. For each corrected cell barcode, the barcode error rate was calculated as the fraction of reads for which the raw barcode (CR) differed from the corrected barcode (CB). UMI complexity was calculated as the total number of UMI-deduplicated reads divided by the total number of raw reads at the sample level.

For gene detection sensitivity analysis, differences in sequencing depth across platforms were accounted for by downsampling. sc-rDSeq data were randomly downsampled to 10% prior to analysis to better match per-cell read depth with inDrops and 10× Chromium datasets. Gene detection sensitivity was evaluated by downsampling gene count matrices using DropletUtils [29], based on input read counts and specified target read depths. The median number of detected genes per cell was calculated at each depth.

Long-read RNA seq analysis

Long-read RNA-seq data of PC9 total RNA were obtained from the ENCODE consortium (ENCSR916JDV) and generated on the Pacific Biosciences Sequel II platform. Post-processed ENCODE outputs, including transcript-level count tables and the accompanying GTF annotation, were used for comparison with bulk rDSeq and KAPA Stranded RNA-seq libraries prepared from PC9 total RNA. De novo transcripts were identified based on the ENCODE-provided annotation and been excluded from the analysis according to their transcript classification with transcript status = “NOVEL” in the provided GTF. All RNA biotypes present in each dataset were included in the benchmarking analyses comparing long-read RNA-seq, bulk rDSeq, and KAPA-Stranded RNA-seq libraries.

Single-cell total RNA expression analysis

The single-cell matrices from four alignment steps were loaded into R v4.4.1 [30] and combined for processing using the Seurat v 5.1.0 [31]. Unnamed genes (gene symbols beginning with ENSG) were removed from the analysis for clarity. Data QC was processed using scCustomize v2.1.2 [32] to decide filtering thresholds for nUMI and nGene in order to remove doublets and abnormal cells, as listed in Supplementary Table S5. For naïve PC9 inDrop and sc-rDSeq data, the normalization, scaling, and variable feature selection were performed using default settings. Principal component analysis (PCA) was conducted on the scaled data, with the number of principal components listed in Supplementary Table S5. Clusters of cells were identified using a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) modularity optimization-based clustering algorithm, with resolutions specified in Supplementary Table S5. UMAP was used for visualization.

For PC9 Drug treatment data, the data analysis was split into two stages. First, all data were analyzed with integration to remove technical noises using Harmony v1.2.3 [33]. Then cell cycle phase scores of each cell were calculated based on canonical markers [34] with Seurat function CellCycleScoring. Histone mRNA were split into replication dependent and independent [35]. Replication dependent histone mRNA scores were added to single cell and plotted in histogram with density plot. Due to the big variance of coverage, first a harsh filter is applied to only keep high-quality cells, variable feature selection were selected using default settings, and kept as highly-variable genes for the next round of analysis, where a normal filter is applied to keep most of the cells (Supplementary Table S5), and PCA analysis was performed with the highly-variable genes defined previously. Clusters of cells were identified using an SNN modularity optimization-based clustering algorithm, with resolutions specified in Supplementary Table S5. UMAP was used for visualization.

Gene-set enrichment analysis

Gene-set enrichment analysis was performed with ShinyGO v8.0 [36] and clusterProfiler v4.12.6 [37]. Marker genes for each cluster were selected using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function, applying a filter for a log2-fold change greater than 0 and an adjusted P-value <.05. Customized background genes for the ShinyGO analysis included all expressed genes in the dataset. Marker genes for each cluster were mapped against the MSigDB Hallmark and GO Biological Processes pathway databases, using a filter with an FDR q-value <0.05.

Copy number variation analysis

The copy number variation (CNV) analysis was conducted using InferCNV [38] v3.2 using default parameters, with cells treated with DMSO served as the reference group, representing a normal karyotype, while cells treated with Osimertinib and Gefitinib constituted the observation groups, potentially harboring CNVs. Human genome (GRCh38, Ensembl Release 112) was used for gene location information.

eRNA analysis

eRNAs are found by searching for regions with enriched bidirectional peaks on the genome. sc-rDSeq data were realigned to the genome (GRCh38, Ensembl Release 112) using Bowtie 2, as STAR alignment results contain junction information that can confound the peak calling process. Alignments from each cluster, as defined by gene expression analysis in Seurat, were merged to identify enriched peak regions using HOMER v5 [39] findPeaks with parameters -style histone -region -size 150 -minDist 370 -F 0 -L 0 -C 0. The directionality of the peaks was calculated by dividing the number of reads from the plus strand by the total reads of the peak. Peaks with a directionality in the range of 5%–95% were defined as bidirectional. Peaks covering more than two genes in opposite directions were removed. Additionally, peaks with low expression (<10 reads per peak) were also filtered out. Bidirectional peaks from all clusters were merged to form a list of candidate eRNAs. These candidate eRNAs were characterized using HOMER annotation. The expression of eRNA in each cluster was determined by intersecting the candidate eRNA list with the merged alignment from each cluster, then normalized by read depth and scaled by row. The scaled expression matrix was clustered and reordered using AgglomerativeClustering from the Scikit-learn package [40] with the number of clusters set to match the input. Candidate eRNAs were then intersected with eRNAs defined by the GeneHancer database [41] and the connected gene list for each eRNA was retrieved. For each gene expression cluster, differentially upregulated genes (avg_log2FC > 0, p_val_adj < .05) were compared with the genes connected to eRNA expression clusters. Overlapping genes were identified as those upregulated in both gene expression and eRNA regulation. Whether the connected genes found in the list of differentially expressed genes were due to chance was tested. First, same number of eRNAs as the input eRNA per cluster were randomly sampled from GeneHancer. Then it was tested whether the number of overlap genes between cluster’s DE genes and the candidate eRNA connected genes was significantly greater than the number of overlap genes between cluster’s differentially expressed genes and random eRNA connected genes. The test was conducted by performing 50 sampling iterations to obtain a P-value, and this process was repeated 10 times for the distribution of P-values.

Isoform analysis

The alternative splicing (AS) events and junction counts were analyzed by rMATS [42] using default parameters for naïve PC9 data of inDrops and sc-rDSeq. The isoform analysis was performed on pseudo-bulk of cellular clusters. DTP cells were grouped according to the single cell clustering annotations, raw fastq files were merged as pseudo-bulk. For each cluster, the isoform calculation is based on the comparison between individual clusters against all the rest of the cells as background. In each dataset, three samples were randomly picked from all reads, with sampling size equal 70% of the number of reads of the pseudo bulk cluster. Then alignment for transcriptome (not including introns) using RSEM with estimation maximization (EM) algorithm was performed to get isoform counts. The expected counts were then used for isoform switch analysis with IsoformSwitchAnalyzeR [43] v2.4.0. In brief, isoform counts were imported and normalized [44], then isoform switch test was performed [45, 46]. Significantly switched isoforms were also checked for protein domains using Pfam [47] and signaling peptides with SignaIP [48] v6.0. As an independent validation, transcript-level quantification was also performed using Kallisto [49], and the same downstream isoform switch analysis workflow was applied for comparison.

Single cell isoform analysis was performed on isoform matrices obtained from single cell RSEM alignment. Data QC was processed using scCustomize v2.1.2 [32] to decide filtering thresholds, as listed in Supplementary Table S5. After filtering and default normalization, switched isoforms identified from pseudo-bulk isoform switch analysis were used for data scaling and PCA. Top 400 switched isoforms were selected from each cluster, independent from their gene-assignment, resulting in a total of 2245 isoforms corresponding to 1245 genes. Clusters of cells were identified using an SNN modularity optimization-based clustering algorithm, with resolutions specified in Supplementary Table S5. UMAP was used for visualization. Then gene expression-based cell cluster annotations were compared with that of isoform expression, fraction of cells that overlaps between each pair of clusters were calculated and presented in a heatmap.

Single nucleotide variants and indels analysis

Detection of mutations was performed on treatment-level pseudo-bulks using GATK [50] v4.5.0.0 Mutect2. Alignments of single cells from each condition were merged into condition pseudo bulks, then transferred to Mutect2 in tumor-only mode to call mutations. Gefitinib and Osimertinib mutation calling was done with default parameters, DMSO specific parameters were: --initial-tumor-lod 1 --tumor-lod-to-emit 1 --max-population-af 1 to allow detection of very rare instances that could potentially give rise to DTP cells. The following step was GATK v4.5.0.0 FilterMutectCalls, and eventually we filtered out sites with filters defined as FAIL, weak_evidence, map_qual, base_qual, and contamination. Additionally, sites were annotated with GATK v4.5.0.0 Funcotator with gencode source. We filtered any sites overlapping with known RNA-editing sites [51–53], only sites that were covered with at least one read in all three conditions were kept, and eventually only sites with at least 10 reads at either Gefitinib or Osimertinib were taken for testing enrichment/depletion compared to DMSO. Each site was tested with chi-square or fisher-exact depending on sample sizes for the allele frequencies, then FDR correction was applied and threshold for significance was defined as 0.05. Sites with variant classification of missense and splice-site were kept for further observation. We applied SComatic [54] SingleCellGenotype on DTP cells in these candidate sites with --min_mq 20.

To test the EGFR mutational profiles across the conditions we extracted EGFR-annotated sites after filtration of FAIL, weak_evidence, map_qual, base_qual, and contamination. We used DP (depth) and AF (allele frequency) to calculate the number of mutated reads. To measure overlap between mutations in each condition we used both genomic location and substitution information.

Results

sc-rDSeq enables high-throughput detection of total RNA with high sensitivity and full-length coverage

We have developed a high-throughput droplet-based total RNA sequencing method—sc-rDSeq (Fig. 1a). In brief, single cells are co-encapsulated with reverse transcription - lysis buffer and hydrogel beads carrying specific barcoded primers allowing for nonribosomal total RNA capturing. Two types of barcoded primers are employed for achieving this purpose, rDS and polyT5C8N primers (Supplementary Fig. S1).

sc-rDSeq showed high sensitivity for full-length total RNA in single cell with high sensitivity. (a) Single cell and barcode beads are co-encapsulated by microfluidics platform. The primers on the barcode beads are equipped with handles for library preparation and single cell barcode and end with rDS or polyT5C8N, which target both polyA+ and polyA− RNA transcripts. (b) Gene-body coverage across all genes based on merged single cells data showing strong 3′ bias for inDrops and mostly uniform with a small 3′ bias for sc-rDSeq. Each gene is divided into 40 segments and counts failing into each segment are calculated for coverage. Ribbon area shows the SEM around mean. (c) The boxplot displays nUMI/cell for sc-rDSeq and inDrops. On average, sc-rDSeq captures 90k nUMI/cell, inDrops captures 7.4k nUMI/cell. (d) Pie chart showing overlap of genes detected by sc-rDSeq and inDrops in merged single-cell dataset. (e) Stacked horizontal bars showing the number of genes uniquely detected by each method, grouped by gene biotype [mRNA/protein-coding, long noncoding RNA (lncRNA), miRNA, small noncoding RNA (sncRNA), mitochondrial genes]. The “other” category includes misc_RNA, vault_RNA, and MT_tRNA. (f) Histogram showing normalized distribution of the fraction of transcripts per biotype expressed in single cells, comparing sc-rDSeq to inDrops. (g) Cost comparison of sc-rDSeq and other single RNA-seq technologies. Top bars for instrument cost, and bottom bars for library and sequencing cost per cell. (h) UMAP of PC9 cells (n = 680) based on total transcriptome, revealing four clusters of populations. (i) Differentially expressed genes were identified using KNN unsupervised clustering of total transcriptome expression. The average expression of selected marker genes for each cluster are presented in dot plot. (j) Top five GO terms enriched in each cluster, ranked by fold enrichment. (k) The average expression of polyA− RNAs showed heterogeneity, including histone RNAs, lncRNAs, sncRNAs, and miRNAs.

Although NSR primers do not align directly to human rRNA, we sought to further reduce the chance that their last 5 nucleotides could anneal to rRNA and initiate reverse transcription (RT), which has 3956 alignments to rRNA sequences. Another concern was cross-hybridization. About 10% of NSR sequences have reverse-complement pairs within the set, which could interfere with single-cell barcode synthesis and reduce RT efficiency. In addition, synthesizing the full NSR set on single-cell barcodes is costly. To address these issues, we reduced the primer pool to a subset of 220 primers that balanced low rRNA mismatch rates with broad transcriptome coverage, while also reducing cross-hybridization to ~2%. We termed this optimized subset rDS. Random sampling simulations of 220 primers from NSR, followed by alignment to rRNA, confirmed that the rDS set minimizes potential rRNA hits from the last 5 nt, with four times lower number of hits to rRNA (Supplementary Fig. S2a). In silico alignment showed that per 100 nt, 4.41 and 4.67 rDS sequences aligned to mRNA and ncRNA, capturing 99.9% and 99.6%, respectively, ensuring ample priming coverage (Supplementary Fig. S2b)

We found that using rDS primers alone results in limited RT efficiency. However, when combined with polyT5C8N primers, the number of UMIs captured per cell increases fourfold (Supplementary Fig. S2c). The polyT5C8N primer complements random priming by supporting high efficiency polyT priming while also initiating RT from internal RNA regions. The polyT sequence anneals to polyA repeats, particularly the polyA tail of mRNA, while the CCC sequence acts as a bridge, facilitating the annealing of the random octamer to upstream regions of the mRNA. Notably, since mRNA naturally forms secondary structures, the folded RNA regions are spatially closer to the polyA tail, increasing their accessibility to the 8Ns for annealing. Enhanced internal priming with poly5C8N comparing to polyT was observed in both bulk and single-cell experiments. (Supplementary Figs S2d–g and S5a and b).

To find the optimal ratio between rDS-to-polyT5C8N, different proportions were also tested in bulk experiments. Due to the higher RT efficiency of polyT5C8N, the final read composition from each primer differs from the original input mix (Supplementary Fig. S2h). Although a high proportion of rDS (1000:1) yielded the most uniform gene-body coverage (Supplementary Fig. S2k), it showed reduced robustness by ERCC spike-in correlation and more than three-fold lower sequencing depth compared to 10:1 or 4:1 ratio (Supplementary Fig. S2i and j). Eventually, we selected a 4:1 ratio as the best balance. Interestingly, at the single-cell level, the proportion of rDS-derived reads was higher than in bulk, leading to more balanced gene coverage (Supplementary Figs S2h and S5a and b).

We incorporated the rDS and polyT5C8N primers into single-cell barcode beads, which also included handles for library preparation and UMIs to facilitate PCR duplication removal. To improve RT reaction efficiency, we implemented a ramping-cycling-short RT strategy. In this approach, the temperature is gradually ramped from 8°C to 50°C for 20 cycles to enhance annealing of initially unbound primers, and the total RT incubation at 50°C was limited to 5 min to prevent strand displacement [55, 56]. These modifications significantly improved RT efficiency and increased single-cell complexity from 6k to 116k nUMIs per cell (Supplementary Fig. S2l). Bulk libraries prepared with sc-rDSeq primers showed no amplification in the absence of RT or when genomic DNA was used as input, confirming that gDNA contamination was negligible (Supplementary Fig. S2m). Analysis of rDS priming sites revealed a subtle enrichment of thymidine in the first 5 nucleotides, likely an artifact introduced by polyT5C8N primers (Supplementary Fig. S3a). The distribution within the same transcript was less uniform than in random-primed libraries, a pattern also reported for NSR primers (Supplementary Fig. S3b). Importantly, these effects were reproducible across replicates and did not impact expression counts, confirming the robustness of rDS priming.

Benchmark against KAPA Stranded RNA-seq kit and polyT-based RNA-seq protocol (IVT based) in bulk revealed 6.8% of rRNA reads in bulk rDSeq libraries, compared to 81.6% in KAPA libraries that used random hexamer for RT (Supplementary Fig. S3c). Across methods, genome mapping rates of non-rRNA and gene-level expression correlations were comparable, and robustness, as measured by ERCC spike-in concentration correlation, was also consistent (Supplementary Fig. S3d–f). Bulk rDSeq libraries showed an improved distribution across the gene body compared with polyT-based libraries, which exhibited strong enrichment toward the 3′ end, whereas KAPA libraries showed the most uniform coverage overall (Supplementary Fig. S3g and h). We further compared with public long-read RNA-seq data for isoform detection across all RNA biotypes captured in each library. Among the top 10% highly expressed isoforms across bulk rDSeq, KAPA and long-read, 73% were uniquely captured by long-read sequencing. Approximately half of the isoforms detected by KAPA and bulk rDSeq were also observed in the long-read data, suggesting that with the limited sequencing depth and input RNA, short-read sequencing may introduce artifacts in isoforms detection (Supplementary Fig. S3i). Expression correlations between bulk rDSeq and KAPA with long-read data were higher on gene-level (R = 0.75 and 0.68) than at the isoform level (R = 0.45 and 0.50). Similar discrepancies between short-read and long-read data have been reported previously [57], underscoring the limitations of short-read sequencing, which cannot fully substitute for long-read technology for isoform detections (Supplementary Fig. S3j).

We also evaluated the performance of the full set of NSR primers in single cells. Overall, scNSR showed lower efficiency, with higher number of residual adaptors in the reads, lower mapping rates, and substantially fewer nUMIs per cell compared to sc-rDSeq (Supplementary Fig. S4a and b). The higher loss during adaptor trimming was might due to the cross-hybridization within the primer sets, which introduced more primer dimers into the libraries. Gene-body coverage was comparable, with a subtle 5′ bias with scNSR and 3′ bias with scrDS (Supplementary Fig. S4c). While scNSR captured a higher fraction of ncRNAs within the non-rRNA reads [10.9% versus 2.2% for sc-rDS, excluding long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs)], the majority (84.9%) of these were miscellaneous RNAs, mainly 7SK and 7SL small nuclear RNAs and their pseudogenes (Supplementary Fig. S4d and e). Thus, although full NSR primers modestly increase recovery of ncRNA species, this comes at the cost of reduced RT efficiency, lower sensitivity, and higher expense for barcode synthesis. For these reasons, we consider rDS to be the more balanced and practical choice for single-cell application.

Species-mixing experiment with mESCs and human PC9 cells confirmed that the mixing rate is on the same level as inDrops, which is around 5% (Supplementary Fig. S5c). sc-rDSeq showed superior performance in comparison to conventional polyT-based scRNAseq method and is comparable to other state-of-the-art single cell full-length RNA methods, such as VASA-seq and Smart-seq-total, based on the benchmarking results reported previously. We profiled the lung cancer-derived PC9 cells by sc-rDSeq and inDrops to evaluate their technical performance. sc-rDSeq demonstrated higher barcoding efficiency compared to inDrops, with 81% of reads exhibiting the correct library structure. The final mapping rate of reads with proper barcodes, following adapter and rRNA trimming, is 72%, comparable to VASA-drop (~70%) and higher than Smart-seq-total (~50%) [9] (Supplementary Fig. S5d and e). Data from all single cells were combined into pseudo-bulk to assess average gene body coverage across all genes. We observed a pronounced 3′ end bias with inDrops, while sc-rDSeq showed a relatively uniform distribution across the gene body, with a modest 3′ bias due to the polyT5C8N primers (Fig. 1b). A similar bias is observed in Smart-seq3 but is less pronounced in Smart-seq-total [11]. Notably, reads from rDS primers alone were evenly distributed (Supplementary Fig. S5a).

Due to the multiple annealing events enabled by sc-rDSeq, on average, sc-rDSeq yielded 90k nUMIs per PC9 cell, representing more than a 10-fold increase compared to the 7.4k nUMIs per cell obtained with inDrops (Fig. 1c). Gene body coverage analysis showed that sc-rDSeq captured a higher percentage of reads from coding sequences and introns, whereas inDrops predominantly captured untranslated regions (UTRs) (Supplementary Fig. S5f). Although 75.4% of genes were shared between both methods, sc-rDSeq identified 21.5% unique genes not captured by inDrops, compared to only 3.1% unique genes identified by inDrops (Fig. 1d). These unique genes identified by sc-rDSeq were primarily lncRNAs and non-polyA RNAs, such as miRNAs and sncRNAs, which were largely absent in inDrops (Fig. 1e and f).

We next benchmarked sc-rDSeq against 10× Chromium using publicly available PC9 datasets. sc-rDSeq showed a barcode error rate slightly higher than the 10× Chromium Singleplex workflow, but substantially lower than the 10× Multiplex workflow, which trades accuracy for reduced per-sample cost (Supplementary Fig. S5g). Using a unified definition of UMI complexity, sc-rDSeq recovered substantially more UMIs per cell than both 10× Singleplex and inDrops, despite modestly lower UMI complexity (Supplementary Fig. S5h). After downsampling to equal read depth, sc-rDSeq exhibited the highest gene detection sensitivity, followed by 10× Chromium Singleplex and inDrops (Supplementary Fig. S5i).

Despite its improved full-length coverage and total RNA capturing capabilities, the library preparation cost and instrument cost for sc-rDSeq are the same as inDrops. However, the sequencing cost depends on the required coverage, which is approximately doubled due to the method’s higher sensitivity in RNA detection. The estimated costs of sc-rDSeq are $0.08 per cell for library preparation and $0.30 per cell for sequencing (Supplementary File S1). The library preparation cost per cell for sc-rDSeq and is six times lower than 10× Chromium ($0.5) [58, 59] and Smart-seq3 (between $0.57 and $1.14) [7]. Since Smart-seq-total utilizes a similar library preparation protocol as Smart-seq3 with additional steps, its costs are expected to exceed those of Smart-seq3. VASA-seq has a slightly higher cost the sc-rDSeq for library preparation ($0.11), but the instrument cost is three times higher than sc-rDSeq due to its two-step microfluidic encapsulation deices. Consequently, sc-rDSeq is the most cost-effective method for single-cell full-length total RNA sequencing so far (Fig. 1g and Supplementary Fig. S5j).

sc-rDSeq reveals heterogeneous cell populations in cancer cell line by analyzing both polyA and non-polyA RNA

We further investigated the heterogeneity revealed by polyA and non-polyA RNAs using sc-rDSeq. Unique reads were aligned in four sequential rounds, with only unaligned reads passed to the next round: first to sncRNAs, followed by exons, introns, and finally pseudogenes. The expression matrices from each round were then combined for downstream analysis. Applying sc-rDSeq to the NSCLC cell line PC9 identified four distinct cellular states that inDrops could not detect (Fig. 1h–k and Supplementary Fig. S5k and l). Clusters 1 and 3 were characterized by more aggressive cancer-related features, such as increased lipid synthesis and cell migration. Cluster 2 displayed heightened activity in the cell cycle, while Cluster 0 exhibited the opposite, with overall lower coverage, indicating a slower metabolic state (Supplementary Fig. S5k). To be more specific, Cluster 0 was enriched for processes like heterochromatin assembly and negative epigenetic regulation of gene expression. Key genes been upregulated like WAPL, SMCHD1, and BAZ2A, which promote heterochromatin formation [60–63]. Cluster 1 showed enrichment for lipid synthesis and proteolysis. Proteases like CST3, CST4, and CTSH, promote tumor aggressiveness [64–66], while lipid synthesis genes such as FASN, SCD, and IDH1 support tumor growth and energy supply [28–30]. Cluster 2 was enriched for chromosome segregation and proliferation. Highly expressed genes, including TOP2A, AURKA, and PTTG1, were identified as regulators of chromosome instability and cancer progression [67–69]. Notably, various histone RNAs were highly expressed in Cluster 2 (Fig. 1k), further supporting roles related to chromosome segregation and rapid cell proliferation. Cluster 3 was linked to cell migration and motility, with genes like EFNB2, ANXA1, and LAMC2 promoting migration, invasion, and epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [70–72]. In addition to histone RNAs, other non-polyA RNAs were also differentially expressed across clusters (Fig. 1f–k). Cancer-related lncRNAs such as PVT1, GAS5, and EBLN3P [73–76] was identified. Antisense RNAs, including HOXA-AS2, TMPO-AS1, and ZFAS1, may regulate their sense RNA counterparts [77–79].

DTP cells exhibit G1 arrest and activate different pathways for drug resistance

Previous studies have shown the existences of a small subpopulation of cells, termed “drug-tolerant persisters” (DTPs), which survive short-term exposure to high-dose drug treatment [44]. Recent studies have highlighted distinct fates of DTP cells, but these investigations relied on the conventional scRNA-seq, which only captures polyadenylated RNA and with limited sensitivity [14]. Given the sensitivity of sc-rDSeq in detecting subtle cancer states, we aimed to investigate the emergence of DTPs. As PC9 cells are derived from NSCLC, we focused on two EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs), Gefitinib and Osimertinib. Gefitinib, a first-generation TKI, targets EGFR mutations but often faces resistance due to secondary mutations like T790M. Osimertinib, a third-generation TKI, overcomes this by targeting both activating and T790M mutations. PC9 cells, which carry an EGFR exon 19 deletion mutation, are highly sensitive to both drugs at low doses. To model drug resistance, we treated PC9 cells with Gefitinib or Osimertinib for nine days at a drug concentration 10-fold greater than the IC50 values (Supplementary Fig. S6a and b), eliminating most of the cell population and leaving only a rare subset of DTP cells (<1% of the population). As a control, DMSO was used at equivalent concentrations to mimic treatment conditions without selective pressure.

Using sc-rDSeq, we profiled 7 051 cells across all groups, including 2 982 Gefitinib DTPs, 1320 Osimertinib DTPs, and 2749 control cells (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. S6c and d). Integration analysis revealed major effects of drug resistance on cell states. The first major finding was that G1 arresting of DTP cells determined by the expression of both classical cell cycle gene and non-polyadenylated histone mRNAs (Fig. 2a–c). Consistent with previous studies [44], DTPs were mostly in G1 (fraction of G1 cells: Gefitinib 83%, Osimertinib 95.1%), while control cells were largely in G2/M and S phases (fraction of G1 cells: DMSO 4.77%; Supplementary Fig. S7b). Due to the total RNA detection ability, we were able to capture the replication-dependent histone mRNAs which are the only known cellular nonpolyadenylated mRNAs. Their expression was elevated in S and G2/M phases (Fig. 2b), consistent with histone protein accumulation before mitosis as preparation for division [35]. Cells were then categorized into S and non-S phases based on histone RNA expression levels (Supplementary Fig. S7a). Cell cycle annotation based on cell-cycle and non-polyA histone genes was consistent across both UMAP and population averages expression of histone RNA (Fig. 2a and c, and Supplementary Fig. S7b). To identify cellular expression programs associated with drug resistance, we analyzed differentially expressed gene ontology pathways between DTPs and control cells (Fig. 2d). Six proliferation-related pathways were upregulated in control cells (MYC targets v1, G2M checkpoints, E2F targets, MYC targets v2, and Mitotic spindle), consistent with their role in cancer drug responses [80]. We further investigated the heterogeneity within DTPs through subclustering treated cells. UMAP analysis revealed seven distinct clusters, with Gefitinib DTPs exhibiting greater cellular diversity, predominantly occupying Clusters 0, 2, 4, and 6. Osimertinib DTPs were primarily concentrated in Clusters 3 and 5, while Cluster 1 comprised cells from both treatments, with a higher proportion of Gefitinib-treated cells (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Fig. S7c–e). The differentially expressed genes enriched for different biological processes that suggests several strategies for cells to bypass the drug treatment. Clusters 0, 5, and 6 displayed elevated metabolic pathway expression, including cytoplasmic translation and ribosome biogenesis, indicating increased protein synthesis requirements for the development of resistance [81, 82] (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. S7g). These clusters also showed higher MYC target expression and oxidative phosphorylation (Supplementary Fig. S7f and h), consistent with previous findings in Gefitinib-resistant NSCLC cells [49]. Notably, several genes upregulated in these clusters, such as PPIA, S100A6, and GSTP1 (Fig. 2f and h), have been previously linked to EGFR TKI resistance in cancer [83–85]. Cluster 5 showed higher expression of tumor suppressor genes, including S100P, NPC2, SPINT2, and CLU [86–89] (Fig. 2f and h), suggesting reduced cellular aggressiveness. While Cluster 6 shares most differentially expressed genes with Cluster 5, it uniquely expressed four precursor miRNAs (MIR1244-1/2/3/4) that form mature mir-1244 (Fig. 2i). mir-1244 is involved in mRNA degradation and translation inhibition, which has previously been reported to sensitize cells to cisplatin, induce resistance, and cause cellular senescence in multiple cancers [90–92]. Clusters 1 and 3 exhibited upregulation of tumor-aggressive pathways, including EMT transition, cholesterol homeostasis, and extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling [93] (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. S7f–h). Genes been upregulated include ACLY for lipid metabolism [94], laminin subunits LAMA5, LAMB2, LAMC1, and the cell surface glycoprotein gene ICAM1 for ECM formation (Fig. 2f and h). Cluster 3 also present tumor suppressive activities with the expression of tumor suppressors like LINC-PINT, MAGI1, SSBP2, MACROD2, and EXT1 [95–99]. These findings suggest an intriguing phenomenon in which the persistent cells of Cluster 3 exhibit both tumor suppressor and aggressive activities. Interestingly JUND, part of the AP-1 transcription factor family, was expressed in both aggressive (Clusters 1 and 3) and less aggressive (Clusters 2 and 4) states (Fig. 2h). This highlights its dual role in promoting or inhibiting growth depending on the cellular context [100].

Finally, to investigate whether chromosomal CNVs act as genomic drivers of resistance, we analyzed single DTP cells using inferCNV [38] with DMSO-treated cells as a reference. Most Gefitinib- and Osimertinib-DTP cells exhibited chr19 gain and partial loss of chr2, except for two rare subpopulations, Clusters 2 and 3 (Supplementary Fig. S6e). Cells from CNV Clusters 2 and 3 were primarily concentrated in Cluster 3 and Cluster 1 on the gene expression UMAP, respectively. Other CNV-based clusters showed some tendency to be restricted to specific transcriptional clusters but did not display a clear correlation with gene expression (Supplementary Fig. S6e). Overall, subpopulations identified through RNA expression exhibited greater variation than those inferred from CNV analysis. It aligns with previous studies [101], suggesting that CNVs are just one of the mechanisms cancer cells use to develop drug resistance, and a deeper understanding of the underlying expression-based and mutational pathways is instrumental. These findings highlight the potential of sc-rDSeq in identifying drug-resistant cells and optimizing combination therapies that target cluster-specific pathways.

Enhancer RNAs align with gene expression variability

Enhancers, distal regulatory elements often disrupted in cancer, produce short ncRNAs called eRNAs that influence gene expression [102, 103]. However, detecting eRNAs at the single-cell level is difficult due to their short length, low abundance, and instability. Recent advances like scGRO-seq have improved detection but remain limited in throughput [104]. Given the strand-specificity of sc-rDSeq, we explored its potential for profiling eRNAs by identifying bidirectionally transcribed regions. Since the eRNAs are cell-type-specific and expressed in low amount, we extracted bidirectional peaks from aggregated reads per cluster (2%–4.4% bidirectional), merging them across clusters to compile a candidate eRNA list (Fig. 2j and Supplementary Fig. S8a). In DTP cells, we identified 9750 candidate eRNAs, with 6% in intergenic regions and 25%–30% previously annotated [41] (Supplementary Fig. S8b and d), mostly located near transcription start sites (TSSs) and extended up to 50 kb upstream or downstream of protein-coding genes and lncRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S8c, e, and f). We then quantified eRNA expression at the pseudo-bulk level. While intergenic eRNAs were not inherently cluster-specific, their expression patterns correlated well with gene expression clustering (Fig. 2k and Supplementary Fig. S8h). Based on GeneHancer [41], an experimentally validated regulatory database, we linked candidate eRNAs to their putative target genes and found significant overlaps with differentially expressed genes (Supplementary Fig. S8g). For instance, a Cluster-3-specific eRNA targeted F3 (coagulation factor III), which upregulated in the same cluster. Another Cluster-5-specific eRNA near ALPP (1 kb away), which showed highest expression in Cluster 5, consistent with its role in Gefitinib response [105] (Fig. 2l).

Extraction of alternative splicing events to dissect DTP subpopulations

Alternative splicing (AS) drives phenotypic diversity and cancer progression by generating multiple transcripts from a single gene [106–108]. Detecting AS at the single-cell level has been challenging due to limited transcript coverage in 3′ end methods and low sensitivity in full-length sequencing [109, 110]. Leveraging the full-length coverage provided by sc-rDSeq, we evaluated its capacity to quantify AS events. sc-rDSeq detected four times more splicing events than inDrops, primarily skipped exons, while capturing most inDrops-identified events (Supplementary Fig. S9a and b). To assess cell-state-dependent splicing in DTP cells, pseudo-bulk data from gene expression clusters were analyzed with RSEM [23], revealing significant isoform switching in 20%–38% of expressed genes per cluster (Fig. 3c). Comparable proportions were independently identified using the alignment-free isoform quantification tool Kallisto (Supplementary Fig. S9c). Isoform switches were further decomposed into alternative TSS/TTS usage and classical splicing events, with consistent distributions across clusters (Supplementary Fig. S9d). For example, while total MDK expression was comparable between Clusters 3 and 4, Cluster 4 predominantly expressed full-length MDK-203, whereas Cluster 3 favored MDK-204, which skips exon 3 and results in truncated tMDK (Fig. 3a). Midkine is a secreted protein that functions as growth factor involved in tumorigenesis [111]. Despite lacking the N-terminal domain, tMDK retains its functional C-terminal domain and was shown to accelerate tumor formation in mice [112, 113]. Notably, Cluster 3 displays higher EMT levels (Supplementary Fig. S7g), suggesting the unexplored role of tMDK in this process. Similarly, CTSH, encoding Cathepsin H, a protease promoting metastasis [66, 114], showed higher expression in Cluster 4, which primarily expressed full-length CTSH-207. Cluster 2 favored CTSH-236, a shorter isoform lacking the inhibitory propeptide domain, implying enhanced protease activity (Fig. 3b). Single-cell isoform analysis remains challenging due to low read depth, but PCA performed on isoform-switched genes largely recapitulated gene expression-based clustering (Fig. 3d and e, and Supplementary Fig. S9f–i). This analysis, based on 2245 unique isoforms corresponding to 1285 genes, was not dominated by genes with many switched isoforms, as 83.6% of genes contributed only one or two isoforms (Supplementary Fig. S9e). Intriguingly, Cluster 0 split into two subclusters in isoform UMAP, revealing AS-driven heterogeneity. Cluster 6 showed comparatively limited isoform switching and was characterized by expression of tumor suppressor-associated genes and MIR1244 precursor transcripts, consistent with a transcriptionally restrained cell state (Fig. 2i). Single-cell isoform expression validated pseudo-bulk trends for MDK and CTSH (Fig. 3f and g). Notably, the longer isoform CTSH-207 showed very low expression on single cell level due to the need of higher sensitivity over 5′ exons, suggesting the need of pseudo-bulk level analysis to better reveal the AS events. Together, these results indicate that pseudo-bulk analysis captures inter-cluster isoform differences, whereas detection of isoform heterogeneity at single-cell resolution remains constrained by read depth per isoform. Our findings underscore the utility of sc-rDSeq in resolving AS based on correlation with distinct DTP cell expression states. Specifically, truncated isoforms (e.g. tMDK, CTSH-236) may contribute to functional plasticity during adaptation. Ultimately, integrating isoform-level data refines the transcriptional landscape of tumors, highlighting AS as a layer of cell-state heterogeneity.

Genetic variation analysis reveals nondriver role in DTP heterogeneity

To evaluate genetic contributions to drug resistance, we analyzed single nucleotide variants (SNVs) and indels in DTPs and control cells using mutect2 [50] on pseudo-bulk data. We started with screening mutations in EGFR gene, and found the site for the canonical resistance variant T790M (c.2369C > T) was unmutated both before and after drug treatment, indicating other sources for drug resistance. On the other hand, a recurrent 3′ UTR SNV (chr7:55206391 T > C) dominated across groups, which might have an influence on translation efficiency of EGFR (Supplementary Fig. S10a). Moreover, most EGFR SNVs in untreated cells were absent in DTPs, suggesting these mutations are not contributing to drug sensitivity (Supplementary Fig. S10b). We then moved on to look for all SNVs that potentially contribute to the drug resistance. Given the short time of drug treatment (9 days), we do not anticipate the accumulation of de novo SNVs in the DTPs. Accordingly, we focused on the pre-existing SNVs and indels with altered allele frequencies post-treatment. To identify mutations that may have contributed to drug resistance but were pre-existing in the untreated (DMSO) state, we used the human genome assembly GRCh38 as the reference. We identified 857 033, 29 851, and 22 474 SNVs and indels in DMSO, Gefitinib, and Osimertinib DTPs, respectively. The SNVs are predominantly found in introns and 3′UTR (Supplementary Fig. S10c). After filtering low-expression variants and significancy testing, we identified 59 enriched and 41 depleted SNVs in Gefitinib DTPs, and 24 enriched and 21 depleted in Osimertinib DTPs, with 15 enriched and 4 depleted SNVs in both treatment groups (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. S10d). Although the 3′ UTR mutations could have significant impact in gene regulation, we focused on the 17 missense and splice-site mutations to reveal the direct functional relevance (Fig. 4b). We found that many (8/14) of these mutations were found in genes related to lung cancer [115–120]. For example, knockdown MUC16 is shown to activate the downstream targets of EGFR pathway [121], the enriched allele frequency in Gefitinib DTPs of MUC16 is observed and potentially reflecting compensatory adaptation to EGFR inhibition. Conversely, IGFBP6 mutation allele frequency was depleted, suggesting selective pressure against growth factor loss (Fig. 4c). We then performed single cell genotyping over these SNVs using SComatic [54] (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. S10e). However, it showed no clear correlation with transcriptional states, implying that SNVs are not the major drivers for transcriptional heterogeneity but rather a necessity to achieve resistance. Together, these findings, although still limited in coverage, highlight the limited role of genetic variation in shaping DTP cell states and underscore nonmutational mechanisms of resistance.

Discussion

sc-rDSeq is a ground-breaking droplet-based, rDS method designed to capture a wide spectrum of single-cell transcriptomes with high sensitivity and exceptional cost-effectiveness. It provides full-length coverage of total RNA, including both polyA+ and polyA− transcripts, which are often overlooked by traditional scRNA-seq methods. sc-rDSeq matches state-of-the-art methods like Smart-seq-total and VASA-seq in performance with improved scalability, accessibility, and cost-efficiency. Compared to the gold-standard polyT-based method inDrops, sc-rDSeq significantly improves full-length coverage, diverse RNA profiling, and sensitivity, achieving a 10-fold increase in UMIs. sc-rDSeq does not require sophisticated devices beyond basic microfluidic setups used in standard droplet-based scRNA-seq, making it highly compatible with most commercially available platforms while overcoming the lower encapsulation efficiency seen in VASA-drops due to the droplet merging step. By excluding rRNA transcripts at initial capture step, sc-rDSeq eliminates the need for dedicated rRNA depletion, reducing costs and enhancing total RNA amplification efficiency. In this study, we deliberately operated at lower cell numbers to maximize per-cell sequencing depth; sc-rDSeq is not intrinsically limited in throughput, which is instead determined by barcode complexity and experimental design. The high sensitivity of sc-rDSeq over full-length total transcriptome enabled us to investigate the emergence of drug persistence in cancer cells, revealing a complex landscape of cellular responses to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors. Specifically, we confirmed G1 cell cycle arrest in DTPs not only through conventional cell cycle markers but also via polyA− histone mRNAs. Additionally, we identified rare cell populations marked by ncRNA like miRNAs, and demonstrated that the expression of intergenic eRNAs is aligned with gene expression-based clusters. The full gene-body coverage facilitated the extraction of AS events, linking transcript diversity to functional protein potential. Furthermore, sc-rDSeq expanded the detection power of SNVs, allowing the identification of mutations with allelic fractions enriched upon drug treatments.

Despite these advantages, sc-rDSeq has several limitations. First, the current rDS primer set was designed for the human transcriptome; adapting the method to other species will require redesigning rDS primers. Second, while the combination of rDS and polyT5C8N primers improves transcriptome coverage, primer composition may introduce biases in quantification across RNA biotypes. Third, the detection of AS, eRNAs, and SNVs remains limited by sequencing depth and the inherent challenges of short-read sequencing. In particular, although sc-rDSeq significantly improves ncRNA detection, deeper sequencing is expected to further expand the diversity of the noncoding transcriptome that can be captured. Moreover, our benchmarking was performed primarily in cancer cell lines; testing sc-rDSeq in primary tissues or clinical samples will be essential to establish its robustness in more heterogeneous and low-input contexts. Finally, the complexity of analyzing full-length total RNA at single-cell resolution places additional demands on computational pipelines and annotation resources.

To address the current limitations, we plan to integrate sc-rDSeq with CloneSeq [16, 17] in future studies. Profiling 3D-cultured clones of untreated cells versus DTPs originating from individual cells should enhance sensitivity, improve coverage, and reduce signal-to-noise ratios by minimizing biological variability, such as cell cycle effects and transcriptional bursting. sc-rDSeq, particularly when integrated with CloneSeq, will significantly enhance our ability to dissect cancer biology and serve as a powerful tool for personalized cancer medicine and research. As we continue to refine sc-rDSeq and explore its applications, we anticipate it will become an indispensable tool in the field of single-cell sequencing.
