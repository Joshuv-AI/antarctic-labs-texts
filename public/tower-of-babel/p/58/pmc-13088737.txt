Impact of bariatric surgery and predictive factors for eating disorders before and after surgery: a prospective observational study
Authors: Sepideh Alijani, Elham Hojaji, Behrooz Ghanbari, Shadi Naderyan Fe’li, Saeed Safari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMorbid obesity is frequently associated with high-risk behaviors such as eating disorders (EDs).ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the prevalence, changes, and predictive factors of EDs before and one year after metabolic and bariatric surgeries (MBS) in Iranian adults.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 96 MBS candidates with class II (BMI 35–40 kg/m² with comorbidities) or class III obesity (BMI ≥ 40 kg/m²). Patients underwent sleeve gastrectomy (SG), Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), or one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB). Demographic data, nutritional status [assessed by a food frequency questionnaire (FFQ)], BMI, and EDs symptoms were collected before and one year after surgery via the EDs Questionnaire (EDE-Q 6.0), and psychological status was assessed via the Symptom Checklist-90-Revised (SCL-90-R).ResultsA total of 22.9% of the participants were identified as having EDs. These individuals had a significantly higher BMI compared to those non-eating disorders (non-Eds) (49.9 ± 9.1 vs. 45.4 ± 5.8; p = 0.039). All MBS types (SG RYGB and OAGB) led to significant reductions in EDs global and subscale scores (p < 0.05), with SG surgery group showed notable numerical improvements in weight and shape concerns. Female sex (β = 0.87; p = 0.002) and younger age (β = − 0.03 per year; p = 0.014) were significant predictors of higher EDs scores after surgery.ConclusionMBS reduce symptoms of EDs, with a trend towards SG showing the most prominent effect. Younger age and female sex were independently associated with greater severity of symptoms after surgery. These findings highlight the importance of targeted postoperative psychological support and underscore the interplay between biological and psychological factors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40337-026-01565-2.

Background

Obesity is considered a global epidemic [1]. According to the World Health Organization, obesity is defined as an abnormal or excessive accumulation of body fat that can occur as a result of the complex interaction of behavioral, biological, environmental, genetic, and psychological factors [2]. In more recent definitions, obesity is regarded as a chronic, relapsing, and progressive disease that requires long-term, multidisciplinary treatment approaches to improve health outcomes [1]. Recent evidence indicates that obesity not only contributes to the development of many chronic and inflammatory conditions but also represents an independent and systemic disease directly resulting from excessive adiposity. Within this framework, the concept of “preclinical obesity” has also been introduced, referring to a state in which excess body fat has not yet led to organ dysfunction or limitations in daily activities, but nonetheless places the individual at an increased risk of adverse health outcomes in the future [3, 4].

This condition leads to physiological consequences, misconceptions, and social stigma, which can negatively impact an individual’s quality of life and life expectancy [5] and, in some cases, may even result in high-risk behaviors such as EDs [6].

Weight loss is one approach that may reduce symptoms and improve disordered eating behaviors [7]. However, many individuals with severe obesity experience worsened conditions after repeated unsuccessful weight loss attempts [8, 9].

MBS, which are one of the therapeutic approaches for reducing the ability to consume excessive amounts of food and a means of promoting weight loss, are performed in individuals with severe and morbid obesity and can generally improve quality of life, increase self-esteem, and reduce symptoms of depression and anxiety [10].

Morbid obesity usually refers to obesity in which an individual has a body mass index (BMI) of 40 or higher, or a life-threatening obesity-related health condition such as diabetes, severe sleep apnea, or heart disease with a BMI of 35 or higher, or physical problems related to obesity that interfere with employment, walking, or family functioning [10, 11] For a more precise definition, waist circumference, waist-to-hip ratio, or waist-to-height ratio should also be considered in addition to BMI [4].

For selecting the appropriate bariatric procedure, a patient’s medical, psychological, and social status should be carefully considered. These surgeries generally aid weight loss by restricting food intake and/or reducing nutrient absorption [12, 13]. Commonly performed procedures include sleeve gastrectomy (SG), which primarily limits food intake is [14], Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB), which combines both restriction and malabsorption mechanisms [15], and one-anastomosis gastric bypass (OAGB), which is another effective surgical option for promoting weight loss and metabolic improvements [16].

High-quality evidence has shown that effective interventions, such as MBS, selected based on the individual’s medical, psychological, and social circumstances, not only result in greater weight loss but also provide long-term sustainability of results [17–19].

On the other hand, it is essential to consider that weight loss through MBS may not necessarily lead to the resolution or reduction of ED. According to previous studies, some patients may develop maladaptive behaviors post-surgery, including binge eating [20, 21], intentional vomiting [22], obsessive eating patterns [23], or a lack of motivation to adhere to dietary recommendations [24].

As a result, these maladaptive behaviors can lead to weight regain after surgery [25–27]. Considering the complexity of this condition, a comprehensive assessment of EDs before and after surgery, especially in individuals with a history of such disorders, is critically important.

According to our review, no study in Iran has yet examined the differences and predictors of EDs before and after MBS in the adult population. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the prevalence, changes, and predictive factors of EDs in Iranian adults before and one year after three types of MBS.

Methods

Participants

This was a prospective observational study, which included patients with or without comorbidities, who presented for MBS for treatment of their obesity. The study was carried out from April 2017 to 2019 at the MBS Clinic of Firoozgar Educational and Therapeutic Hospital in Tehran, Iran.

The surgical method for patients was made by the treating surgeon. This was based on the patients’ clinical conditions, degree of obesity, and overall health status; however, patients did not participate in the decision-making regarding the type of surgery. The reason for the choice of method, as well as the risks, benefits, and pre- and post-operative care were fully explained to the patients.

Sample size calculations showed that 37 participants per group were sufficient. The inclusion criteria included patients aged 18 to 65 years with a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 40 kg/m² (with or without comorbidities) or BMI between 35 and 40 kg/m² accompanied by chronic diseases or other obesity-related complications, and completion of a written informed consent form. The exclusion criteria included the use of psychotropic or antidepressant medications during the data collection period, either before or after surgery. Initially, 140 individuals were assessed for inclusion in the study. The surgical method for patients was made by the treating surgeon. This was based on the patients’ clinical conditions, degree of obesity, and overall health status; however, patients did not participate in the decision-making regarding the type of surgery. The reason for the choice of method, as well as the risks, benefits, and pre- and post-operative care were fully explained to the patients.

After initial screening, considering potential dropouts, 40 participants were allocated to each group: RYGB, OAGB, and SG; therefore, 120 patients who met the inclusion criteria and had provided informed consent to participate in the study were enrolled. After one year of follow-up, 24 patients withdrew from the study due to refusal to continue, dissatisfaction, distance, or lack of time, and ultimately 96 patients completed the study, including (RYGB = 37), (OAGB = 25), and (SG = 34) participants. The relevant questionnaires were completed before surgery and re-evaluated one year after the operation. Demographic information and anthropometric measurements were also recorded separately.

Nutritional assessment

Nutritional status was evaluated via a semiquantitative food frequency questionnaire (FFQ) comprising 148 food items, the validity and reliability of which had been previously confirmed [28]. The dietary data obtained from the FFQ were analyzed via a localized software program adapted for Iranian dietary patterns and based on USDA food composition Table [29].

Assessment of EDs and psychological status

To evaluate EDs symptoms, the EDs Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q 6.0), a 28-item self-report tool with four subscales (restraint, eating concern, shape concern, and weight concern), was used to assess cognitive and behavioral features over the past 28 days [30]. Subscale scores are averages of relevant items, and the global score is the mean of these four. Scores ≥ 4 indicate significant symptoms [30, 31]. The Persian version showed good reliability, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.91 and subscale alphas: restraint (0.78), eating concern (0.73), shape concern (0.81), and weight concern (0.69) [30].

Participants’ psychological status was assessed via the Symptom Checklist-90-Revised (SCL-90-R), a validated 90-item self-report tool rated on a 5-point Likert scale (0 = “not at all” to 4 = “extremely”). It measures nine psychological domains: somatization, obsessive‒compulsive symptoms, interpersonal sensitivity, depression, anxiety, hostility, phobic anxiety, paranoid ideation, and psychoticism. Responses were based on recent experiences, with interviewer assistance provided for illiterate participants. Subscale scores were calculated as the mean item values; scores > 1 indicate psychological distress, scores > 2 suggest severe depression, and scores > 3 imply psychotic features. The internal consistency across subscales ranges from 0.76 to 0.90 in MBS candidates [32].

Measurements of weight, height, and BMI

To assess anthropometric characteristics, patients’ weights before and after surgery were measured via a calibrated Seca digital scale (Germany) to the nearest 0.1 kg, with minimal clothing and no shoes. Height was measured in a standing upright position without shoes, with heels, buttocks, and shoulders touching a vertical surface via a wall-mounted stadiometer with a precision of 0.1 cm. BMI was calculated by dividing weight (kg) by the square of height (m²).

Ethical considerations

This study received ethical approval (IR. IUMS.REC1396.31076), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before their inclusion.

Statistical analysis

The frequency (percentage) and mean ± standard deviation were used to describe categorical and continuous variables, respectively. The normality of the quantitative variables was evaluated through a combination of visual assessments (Q–Q plots, Box plots and Histograms) and numerical indices (skewness and kurtosis). To compare categorical and continuous variables between individuals with and without ED, chi-square tests and independent sample t tests were used. To compare EDs and their subtypes before and after surgery, paired sample t tests were applied.

To identify factors associated with EDs, a linear regression analysis was conducted. A p value threshold of < 0.2 from univariable models was considered for variable entry into the multivariable model. Both crude and adjusted beta coefficients with 95% confidence intervals are reported. Statistical significance was considered at p < 0.05. All the statistical analyses were performed via STATA version 17.

Results

A total of 96 individuals were included in this study, 22 (22.9%) of whom were in the ED group and 74 (77.1%) of whom were in the non-ED group. There was no statistically significant difference in the mean age between the two groups (p = 0.630). Additionally, BMI was significantly greater in the ED group than in the non-ED group (p = 0.039).

The sex distribution between the groups approached no statistical significance (p = 0.05), with 40.9% male and 59.1% female participants in the ED group and 20.3% male and 79.7% female participants in the non-ED group. However, Analysis of other background variables showed no statistically significant differences between the two groups with respect to marital status (p = 0.957), education level (p = 0.107), smoking status (p = 0.531), or alcohol consumption (p = 0.316) (Table 1).

Discussion

According to the findings of our study, the scores of the subscales related to EDs, including weight concern, shape concern, eating concern, and the global EDs score, significantly decreased. However, the reduction in concerns related to weight was particularly notable. These findings align with those reported in some previous studies. In a prospective study assessing body shape and weight dissatisfaction in patients undergoing gastric bypass surgery, body dissatisfaction and concerns about shape and weight decreased significantly at 6 and 12 months postoperatively [33]. Another study examining psychiatric symptoms and EDs before and after MBS found significant reductions in eating-related problems, drive for thinness, and body dissatisfaction at the 6-month follow-up [34]. A further investigation of severely obese patients undergoing gastric bypass at three time points reported that concerns about body and shape decreased significantly over time. Improvements in certain body image measures were maintained up to one year after surgery, while weight-related concerns continued to decline steadily [35]. Nevertheless, these improvements are not observed in all studies.

In one study evaluating weight or body overestimation and loss of control over eating following MBS, approximately half of the patients remained dissatisfied and concerned about their weight and body shape six months after SG [36]. Another study examining post-MBS patients reported that concerns about body weight and shape were significantly greater at 24 months postoperatively compared to earlier follow-ups [37].

These differences are likely due to various factors, such as sample population, assessment tools, or differences in follow-up duration [34, 38–40].

Furthermore, all surgical procedures demonstrated significant reductions in EDs scores; however, based on the regression analyses, none of them were identified as independent predictors of postoperative EDs symptoms.

On the basis of our findings, it seems that the reduction in ED symptoms is not solely due to MBS. This finding is also in line with several other studies and may be influenced by various factors including depressive symptoms, stress, and pre-operative BMI [37, 41, 42] According to several studies, EDs often decrease temporarily in the early months following surgery, and symptom improvement has been observed. These changes are attributed to shifts in dietary habits, body image, and mental health [43–45].

Other studies have also shown that weight loss resulting from MBS does not necessarily lead to a long-term reduction or improvement in EDs, and some patients may continue to experience symptoms [46]. The evidence suggests that factors affecting EDs in MBS candidates may vary depending on the pre- or postoperative phase [47, 48]. These findings indicate that the presence of an EDs by itself cannot be regarded as the primary determinant for choosing surgery. These findings indicate that the presence of an EDs alone cannot be considered the primary determinant for selecting candidates for MBS. Rather, surgical decision-making should be based on a comprehensive assessment of clinical factors, including medical comorbidities, functional status, and risk for perioperative complications, as well as psychological and behavioral factors [49–52].

Although intentional most studies have focused on EDs related to binge eating (such as bulimia nervosa and binge EDs), which are commonly associated with overweight and obesity, obesity in this population may stem from a wider range of psychological factors, including excessive concerns about body image or emotional patterns of eating [53, 54]. The current study also showed that although EDs may be a contributing factor to excess weight, only about 22% of participants met criteria for an EDs.

In some cases, these disorders may decrease or even disappear with age. Moreover, the reduction in EDs symptoms with increasing age may be due to greater psychological maturity, more experience in managing eating behaviors, and less social pressure related to body image [55–57]. In the present study, younger age after surgery was identified as a predictor of EDs symptoms. Although this study was conducted on adults, similar findings have also been reported in studies on adolescents. These findings suggest that certain underlying mechanisms involved in the development and improvement of EDs may be shared across different age groups.

After surgery, female sex was also found to be a predictor of EDs [58]. In our study, the proportion of women in the EDs group was greater than that of men, and a greater number of women than men had undergone MBS; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Consistent with our findings, a population-based study in Australia demonstrated that women, more than men, had a history of or intended to undergo bariatric and cosmetic surgery, and these individuals often had a current or past EDs [59]. Similarly, another study reported comparable results, indicating a greater tendency for MBS among women [60].

Although men can also experience EDs, due to social taboos, a lack of public awareness, and concerns about stigma, they are less likely to be diagnosed or report symptoms [61]. In addition, due to cultural and social pressures, women are generally more sensitive to body image and eating-related concerns, which may persist or even intensify after surgery [62].

Multiple mechanisms have been proposed for the emergence of EDs, but what is consistently emphasized across studies is the significant role of psychological and social factors as the main contributors, whereas factors related to weight gain are usually secondary [63, 64]. The findings from the present study showed that, before MBS, one of the psychological factors associated with the onset of EDs was phobia. Other studies have also shown that such factors may reflect internal concerns, psychosocial stress, and feelings of social rejection or underlying psychological disorders. One study reported that 38% of obese patients met the criteria for at least one psychiatric disorder according to the DSM-IV, and 29% showed symptoms of Axis II disorders [65]. These findings suggest a greater level of psychosocial vulnerability in individuals with morbid obesity and confirm the strong association between physical conditions and psychosocial functioning. Therefore, psychological status plays an important role in the development of unhealthy eating patterns [66, 67]. To some extent, weight gain may also exacerbate psychosocial disorders and ultimately worsen the situation [9, 68]. However, on the basis of the results of the multivariate model, none of the psychological or behavioral variables alone were determining factors in EDs, and it appears that these disorders result from the complex interaction of individual, social, and psychological factors [41, 42].

One of the strengths of this study is its focus on a target population of patients with morbid obesity who are candidates for MBS. The use of validated instruments (FFQ, EDE-Q 6.0, and SCL-90-R) and the simultaneous assessment of nutritional, psychological, and anthropometric indicators also contribute to the reliability of the findings.

However, this study is not without limitations. These include a small sample size, reliance on self-reported measures that may lead to recall bias and socially desirable responses, the inability to infer causal relationships, and sampling from a single clinic in Tehran, which may limit the generalizability of the results.

Conclusions

Based on our findings, EDs were observed in approximately one-fourth of the participants and were associated with higher preoperative BMI. All three MBS procedures led to a significant reduction in overall ED scores and their subscales, indicating improved eating-related attitudes following surgery. None of the preoperative psychological factors were independent predictors of EDs in the multivariable model. Postoperatively, female sex and younger age remained independently associated with higher ED scores, suggesting that demographic characteristics may influence ED patterns after surgery. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to clarify these associations further and to evaluate the potential benefits of targeted psychological support in specific patient subgroups.
