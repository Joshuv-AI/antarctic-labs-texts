PGC-1α protects against MASH via Tim23-dependent inhibition of DRP1-mediated ferroptosis
Authors: Yanmian Zhao, Linzhong Zhang, Bairong Li, Menghan Liu, Yanting Zhang, Teng Li, Shoubin Ning, Xiuying Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1α (PGC-1α) is crucial for mitochondrial function and biogenesis. However, whether and how PGC-1α can regulate hepatocyte ferroptosis during the pathogenic process of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) has not been clarified. Hepatocyte ferroptosis was assessed in the liver of MASH patients and in vivo and in vitro MASH models. The mechanisms by which PGC-1α regulated hepatocyte ferroptosis during the process of MASH were examined by Western blot, reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), immunofluorescence, luciferase reporter, and co-immunoprecipitation assays. Hepatocyte ferroptosis, down-regulated Tim23 and up-regulated ACSL4 expression were observed in the livers of MASH patients, and in vivo and in vitro MASH models. PGC-1α deficiency exacerbated hepatocyte ferroptosis in mouse models of MASH induced by high-fat diet and methionine- and choline-deficient diet, and primary mouse hepatocytes that had been treated with palmitic acid. Conversely, PGC-1α over-expression mitigated hepatocyte ferroptosis in these models. Mechanistically, PGC-1α promoted the binding of nuclear respiratory factor (Nrf)1 to the Tim23 promoter, reducing Drp1 transcription and ACSL4 mitochondrial translocation, inhibiting hepatocyte ferroptosis and MASH. These findings indicated that PGC-1α inhibited hepatocyte ferroptosis and attenuated MASH by upregulating Tim23 and inhibiting the Drp1-ACSL4 axis.

Introduction

Metabolic-dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) currently is the most prevalent chronic liver disease and affects 30% of adult people in the world [1, 2]. MAFLD can progress to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), a condition characterized by hepatocyte ballooning, death, and liver inflammation. Within 10 years, approximately 40% of MASH patients progress into liver fibrosis, eventually into cirrhosis and even to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) [3]. As MASH has become a leading cause of cryptogenic liver cancer, understanding the complex molecular mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of MASH is critical for the management of MASH in the clinic [4].

Ferroptosis is a new form of programmed cell death and is caused by the iron-dependent accumulation of lipid-based reactive oxygen species (ROS) [5, 6], particularly for toxic lipid hydroperoxides. This process is governed by a complex network of molecular regulators. Glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) serves as the key enzyme detoxifying lipid hydroperoxides, forming the central antioxidant defense against ferroptosis. In contrast, acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) promotes ferroptosis by catalyzing the incorporation of polyunsaturated fatty acids into membrane phospholipids, the primary substrates for lipid peroxidation [7, 8]. Ferritin heavy chain 1 (FTH1) confines intracellular iron in a redox-inert form, thereby limiting the generation of iron-dependent ROS and restraining ferroptosis [9]. Conversely, transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) mediates cellular iron uptake, expanding labile iron pools that drive Fenton reactions and amplify lipid peroxidation [10].

Morphologically, ferroptosis differs from apoptosis, necrosis, and autophagy and is mainly characterized by changes in mitochondrial and crista structure [11]. Pathophysiologically, ferroptosis contributes to dysfunction of iron homeostasis and is involved in the pathogenesis of many diseases. At the early stage of pathogenic process of MASH, hepatocyte ferroptosis commonly occurs and progresses into hepatocyte apoptosis [12]. It has been known that mitochondria are pivotal players in the process of ferroptosis, with the mitochondrial electron transport chain serving as a major source of intracellular ROS. The impairment in the mitochondrial respiratory chain can promote ROS over-production and accumulation, leading to oxidative stress, phospholipid peroxidation, and ultimately ferroptosis [13, 14]. Hence, investigating the mechanisms underlying the process of ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of MASH may offer novel strategies for early prevention and treatment of MASH.

Peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) is a central regulator of mitochondrial function [15, 16]. Moreover, some single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the PPARGC1A gene for PGC-1α are associated with increased risk of MASH development in both adults and children. Specifically, the Gly482Ser mutation in the PPARGC1A gene presents in approximately 50% of the population, and can significantly reduce the protein lifespan of PGC-1α, especially among 482Ser/482Ser homozygotes [17, 18]. Given that PGC-1α is a key regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and an activator of antioxidant enzymes that protect mitochondria from oxidative stress, and that ferroptosis is characterized by mitochondrial alterations and phospholipid peroxidation, we hypothesized that PGC-1α may play a pivotal role in regulating ferroptosis in MASH.

Translocase of the Inner Mitochondrial Membrane 23 (Tim23) is the core channel component of the TIM complex, responsible for translocating precursor proteins from the cytosol into the mitochondrial matrix or inner membrane [19, 20]. As the principal translocator for matrix-targeted proteins, Tim23 plays a pivotal role in mitochondrial protein import. Alterations in its expression directly reflect the structural integrity of mitochondria [21, 22]. Dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1), a crucial regulator of mitochondrial fission, is typically located in the cytoplasm. Hypoxic stress can activate Drp1 and promote its translocation onto the mitochondrial surface, where its GTPase activity facilitates mitochondrial membrane severing, leading to fission and fragmentation [23–25]. Notably, impaired Tim23 function can lead to the dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨm) [26], which serves as a potent activating signal for dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) [27]. As previously reported, PGC-1α coordinates nuclear respiratory factors (Nrfs) to maintain mitochondrial function [28–30]. However, it remains unknown whether Nrfs can bind to the Tim23 promoter to regulate its transcription and the subsequent activation of Drp1, thereby influencing ferroptosis during MASH progression. It is still unknown whether PGC-1α regulates Drp1 expression through the mitochondrial membrane protein Tim23, consequently influencing ferroptosis during MASH progression.

In this study, our purpose is to explore whether and how PGC-1α regulates hepatocyte ferroptosis and the pathological progression of MASH. Finding revealed that hepatocyte ferroptosis commonly occurred in the livers of individuals with MASH, accompanied by down-regulated PGC-1α expression and that inhibition of ferroptosis significantly mitigated MASH progression in vivo. PGC-1α deficiency exacerbated the process of hepatocyte ferroptosis, while induction of PGC-1α over-expression alleviated hepatocyte ferroptosis. Mechanistically, we found for the first time that PGC-1α up-regulated Tim23 expression through co-activation of Nrf1 and inhibited hepatocyte ferroptosis by attenuating the Drp1-ACSL4 pathway. Collectively, these results indicated that PGC-1α was a critical and novel regulator of hepatocyte ferroptosis during the pathogenic process of MASH.

Results

Hepatocyte ferroptosis occurs in the livers of MASH and reversed by Fer-1

To explore the role of hepatocyte ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of MASH, human liver tissue specimens were collected from non-MASH controls and MASH patients (details in Table S2), and were subjected to H&E staining of morphology, Perls’ blue staining of iron deposition, and immunohistochemistry analysis of the levels of ACSL4, GPX4, TFR1, and FTH1 expression. Compared with the control liver tissues, the MASH livers showed a marked increase in hepatocyte death and iron deposition, along with elevated expression levels of ACSL4 and TFR1, and reduced expression levels of GPX4 and FTH1 (Fig. 1A). These highlighted that hepatocyte ferroptosis existed in the liver of MASH patients and contributed to the pathogenic process of MASH in humans. As shown in Table S2, the incidence of MASH was significantly higher in males than in females. To further explore the role of hepatocyte ferroptosis in the pathogenesis of MASH, male C57BL/6 mice were randomized and fed with HFD or normal chow for 16 weeks. In comparison with that of the control mice receiving normal chow, HFD feeding induced remarkable steatosis, hepatocyte ballooning, inflammation, and fibrosis in the livers of mice, accompanied by significantly increased levels of liver and serum irons, liver MDA, but lower levels of liver GSH. Consistently, ACSL4 expression was markedly elevated, whereas only a few cells were positive for cleaved caspase-3, phosphorylated MLKL, or the GSDMD N-terminal fragment, suggesting that ferroptosis, rather than apoptosis, necroptosis, or pyroptosis, predominated under HFD-induced stress (Fig. 1B-F, Supplementary Fig. 5A). Furthermore, immunofluorescence double staining revealed that most ACSL4-positive cells were also positive for HNF-4α, indicating that ferroptosis mainly occurred in hepatocytes at this stage of the experimental MASH mouse model. A small proportion of ACSL4-positive cells were positive for F4/80, suggesting that some Kupffer cells also underwent ferroptosis. In contrast, only a few desmin-positive (hepatic stellate cells) or LYVE-1-positive (liver sinusoidal endothelial cells) cells colocalized with ACSL4 (Supplementary Fig. 5B). Consistently, Western blot exhibited that HFD feeding significantly up-regulated the relative levels of IL-6, TNF-α, Collagen I, α-SMA, ACSL4, and TFR1 expression, but down-regulated GPX4 and FTH1 expression in the liver of MASH mice, relative to that in the control mice (Fig. 1G, G’). A similar pattern of mRNA transcripts in the livers of mice was detected by RT-qPCR (Fig. 1H). To assess whether ferroptosis inhibition could modulate the severities of MASH, C57BL/6 mice were fed with HFD or normal chow for 12 weeks and randomized to inject with vehicle or 5 mg/kg Fer-1, a synthetic inhibitor of ferroptosis, daily for 4 weeks (Supplementary Fig.1A). Treatment with Fer-1 did not obviously change the liver tissue morphology in the normal chow-fed mice while Fer-1 treatment dramatically alleviated the HFD-induced pathogenic changes in the livers of mice, relative to those receiving vehicle injection (Supplementary Fig. 1B, D). Additionally, Fer-1 treatment significantly mitigated the HFD-elevated serum ALT, TG, T-CHO, and LDL-C, insulin and blood glucose as well as insulin resistance (Supplementary Fig. 1E–K). The Fer-1 treatment also attenuated the HFD-elevated TNF-α, IL-6, Collagen I, α-SMA, ACSL4 and TFR1 expression, but restored the HFD-reduced GPX4 and FTH1 expression in the livers of mice (Supplementary Fig. 1C, C’, O-Q). Furthermore, Fer-1 treatment partially restored the HFD-decreased hepatic GSH, but mitigated the HFD-increased serum and liver irons (Supplementary Fig. 1L–N). Thus, inhibition of hepatocyte ferroptosis significantly reduced the severity of MASH in the HFD-fed mice.

PA induces the ferroptosis of primary hepatocytes and is inhibited by Fer-1

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from mice and treated with, control vehicle or PA for 48 h. Compared with the vehicle-treated control cells, PA treatment obviously increased the contents of oil red-stained lipid droplets and the Perls’ blue-stained irons, but decreased GSH levels in the cultured primary hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2A–C). Furthermore, PA treatment increased mitochondrial division and mitochondrial-derived superoxide in primary hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2D, E). C11-BODIPY 581/591 undecanoic acid fluorescent staining displayed the oxidation of its polyunsaturated butadienyl portion, shifting its red fluorescence to green color in the PA-treated hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2F). Moreover, treatment with PA up-regulated ferroptosis-promoting ACSL4 and TFR1 expression, but down-regulated ferroptosis-inhibiting GPX4 and FTH1 expression in primary hepatocytes, accompanied by increasing IL-6, TNF-α, Collagen I and α-SMA expression (Supplementary Fig. 2G, G’, H). Collectively, treatment with PA-induced hepatocyte ferroptosis in vitro. To assess whether Fer-1 could attenuate the PA-induced hepatocyte steatosis and ferroptosis, primary hepatocytes were treated with PA for 48 h and treated with vehicle DMSO or 5 µM Fer-1 for 24 h. The results exhibited that Fer-1 treatment inhibited the PA-induced iron deposition and steatosis and reduced the levels of IL-6, TNF-α, Collagen I and α-SMA expression in primary hepatocytes. In contrast, Fer-1 treatment obviously restored GPX4 and FTH1 expression in primary hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 2I, I’, J). Hence, Fer-1 treatment significantly inhibited the PA-induced hepatocyte steatosis, inflammation and fibrosis in vitro.

PGC-1α suppresses Drp1 via Nrf1-regulated expression of Tim23

PGC-1α is a key regulator of mitochondrial function and biogenesis. Next, we tested the hypothesis that PGC-1α could regulate hepatocyte ferroptosis during the pathogenic process of MASH. First, immunohistochemistry exhibited that the levels of PGC-1α, Nrf1, Nrf2 and Tim23 expression were remarkably down-regulated while the levels of Drp1 expression and phosphorylated Drp1 (Ser616) increased in the livers of MASH patients, relative to that of control subjects (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 6A). A similar pattern of liver PGC-1α, Nrf1, Nrf2, Tim23 and Drp1 expression and Drp1 phosphorylation was detected in a mouse model of HFD-induced MASH (Fig. 2B–D, Supplementary Fig. 6B–D). Similarly, treatment with PA decreased PGC-1α, Nrf1, Nrf2 and Tim23 expression, but increased Drp1 expression and phosphorylation in primary hepatocytes (Fig. 2E, E’, F, Supplementary Fig. 6E, E’, F). Bioinformatic analysis using the JASPAR database (http://jaspar.genereg.net/) predicted that both Nrf1 and Nrf2 could bind to the Tim23 promoter to regulate its transcription. In HEK293 cells, overexpression of Nrf1, but not Nrf2, significantly increased firefly luciferase activity driven by the wild-type Tim23 promoter (Fig. 2H, Supplementary Fig. 6G). Consistently, Nrf1 knockdown reduced the expression of both Nrf1 and Tim23, whereas Tim23 silencing decreased Tim23 expression but simultaneously increased Drp1 expression (Fig. 2I–J’). Interestingly, Nrf2 silencing led to decreased expression of Nrf2, GPX4, and FTH1, along with increased ACSL4 expression, but had no effect on Tim23 (Supplementary Fig. 6H, H’). These findings indicate that Nrf2 may modulate ferroptosis through a Tim23-independent mechanism in MASH. PGC-1α deficiency deteriorates Drp1 expression and hepatocyte ferroptosis in mice

To further explore the regulatory role of PGC-1α in hepatocyte ferroptosis during the pathogenic process of MASH, we generated hepatocyte-specific PGC-1α knockout (PGC-1α-KO) mice using the classical Cre-loxP recombination system and fed them with HFD to establish MASH in different strains of mice. First, PGC-1α-KO mice displayed liver morphology similar to wild-type mice (Fig. 3A). Compared to the HFD-fed control mice, HFD feeding obviously enhanced liver tissue damage, lipid accumulation, iron deposition, Collagen in the livers of PGC-1α-KO mice (Fig. 3A). Second, compared with that in the HFD-fed WT mice, PGC-1α- KO significantly elevated the levels of serum ALT, TG, T-CHO, and LDL-C in the HFD-fed mice (Fig. 3B-E). Loss of PGC-1α further increased the levels of Drp1 expression and phosphorylation, ACSL4 and TFR1 expression, and decreased the levels of Tim23, GPX4 and FTH1 expression in the HFD-fed mice (Fig. 3A, H, H’, I). Furthermore, PGC-1α deficiency further increased serum iron and decreased hepatic GSH levels in the HFD-fed mice (Fig. 3F-G). Similar effects of PGC-1α- KO were observed in another mouse model of the MCD-induced MASH (Supplementary Fig. 3). PGC-1α silencing enhances Drp1 expression and ferroptosis in primary hepatocytes

To further investigate whether PGC-1α decrease contributed to hepatocyte ferroptosis, primary hepatocytes were transfected with PGC-1α-specific or control siRNA and treated with, or without, PA to induce ferroptosis in vitro. Compared with that in the siRNA-transfected control cells, PGC-1α silencing did not change the morphology, lipid and iron contents, but significantly increased lipid, iron deposition and decreased Tim23 expression in the PA-treated primary hepatocytes (Fig. 4A). PGC-1α silencing further increased Drp1, ACSL4, and TFR1 expression as well as Drp1 phosphorylation, but decreased Tim23, GPX4 and FTH1 expression and GSH levels in the PA-treated primary hepatocytes (Fig. 4B-D). Finally, PGC-1α silencing further increased mitochondrial-derived superoxide and lipid peroxidation in the PA-treated primary hepatocytes (Fig. 4E, F). PGC-1α over-expression alleviates hepatocyte ferroptosis by down-regulating Drp1 expression in mice

To investigate whether PGC-1α over-expression could alleviate hepatocyte ferroptosis, wild-type C57BL/6 mice were fed with HFD for 12 weeks and randomized to inject intravenously with AAV8-PGC-1α or control AAV8-NC. After feeding with HFD for another 4 weeks, the mice were analyzed for hepatocyte ferroptosis (Fig. 5A). First, treatment with AAV8-PGC-1α, but not the control AAV8-NC, induced high levels of PGC-1α expression in the livers of mice (Fig. 5B, L, L’, M) and PGC-1α over-expression did not significantly alter the morphology and liver functions in mice (Fig. 5B-F). Importantly, PGC-1α over-expression obviously reduced HFD-induced liver tissue damage, collagen and iron deposition, liver functional impairments, MDA formation and restored liver GSH levels in mice, relative to that in the mice fed with HFD and injected with the control AAV8-NC (Fig. 5B-J). Notably, TEM analysis indicated that PGC-1α over-expression improved mitochondrial morphology in the livers of HFD-fed mice (Fig. 5K). Furthermore, PGC-1α over-expression reduced Drp1 and phosphorylation, ACSL4 and TFR1 expression, and partially restored Tim23, GPX4 and FTH1 expression in the livers of HFD-fed mice (Fig. 5B, L, L’, M). Similar effects of PGC-1α over-expression were observed in another mouse model of MCD-induced MASH (Supplementary Fig. 4). Discussion

MASH is a highly prevalent chronic liver disease and can ultimately progress to cirrhosis and HCC [31–33]. The incidence of MAFLD has been increasing globally, with recent estimates reaching approximately 4,613 new cases per 100,000 person-years [34]. Consistent with previous findings, males and overweight or obese individuals exhibit a higher incidence than females and those of normal weight [35]. This sex disparity may be explained, at least in part, by the hepatoprotective effects of estrogen, which interacts with PGC-1α via ERRα to maintain mitochondrial function and metabolic homeostasis [36, 37]. To minimize the confounding influence of estrogen, only male mice were included in the present study. Our data indicated that hepatocyte ferroptosis and up-regulated ACSL4 and TFR1 expression in the livers of MASH patients. This study demonstrates for the first time that PGC-1α-mediated upregulation of Tim23, together with the concomitant suppression of Drp1 and ACSL4, modulates hepatocyte ferroptosis and attenuates the progression of MASH. These findings uncover a previously unrecognized regulatory axis and provide new insights into the interplay between mitochondrial dynamics and ferroptotic cell death in the context of metabolic liver disease.

Pathologically, ferroptosis is marked by iron accumulation and excessive lipid peroxidation in the cell [38]. Hepatocyte ferroptosis is associated with the pathogenesis of MASH [39–41]. Consistently, we detected iron accumulation and up-regulated ACSL4 expression in the livers of MASH patients and MASH mice models (Fig. 1), and inhibiting hepatocyte ferroptosis effectively alleviated MASH (Supplementary Fig. 1). Our data indicated that Fer-1 treatment also reduced the expression levels of IL-6 and TNF-α in MASH. TNF-α directly activates the TNFR1 receptor on hepatocyte membranes, leading to hepatocyte membrane rupture and ALT release [42]. Fer-1 treatment restored iron homeostasis by downregulating the iron importer TFR1 and upregulating the iron storage protein FTH1, thereby limiting Fe²⁺-dependent ROS generation via Fenton reactions. Importantly, the improvement of insulin resistance by Fer-1 likely results from its dual ability to mitigate cytokine-driven IRS-1 dysregulation and reduce oxidative stress [43–45]. Together, these findings position ferroptosis as a central mechanism linking metabolic dysfunction and inflammation in the progression of MASH.

Recent studies have shown that PGC-1α inhibits ferroptosis in models of cerebral ischemia, myocardial ischemia-reperfusion, and kidney injury [46–48]. Its anti-ferroptotic effects are mainly attributed to AMPK/Sirt1 signaling and the activation of antioxidant pathways such as SLC7A11-GPX4 [49, 50], thereby maintaining mitochondrial function and redox homeostasis. However, whether and how PGC-1α regulates the mitochondrial inner-membrane protein Tim23 and its downstream signaling remain unknown. In the present study, both PGC-1α and Nrf1 were markedly downregulated in the livers of patients with MASH. PGC-1α physically interacted with Nrf1 to promote the transcriptional activation of Tim23 (Fig. 2), a core component of the mitochondrial inner membrane essential for maintaining mitochondrial structural integrity [51, 52]. Notably, we report for the first time that Tim23 expression is reduced in MASH livers, indicating compromised mitochondrial integrity. Mechanistically, activation of the PGC-1α/Nrf1 axis restored Tim23 expression in MASH livers and consequently suppressed Drp1 levels. Functionally, Tim23 silencing exacerbated palmitic acid-induced ferroptosis in hepatocytes, thereby uncovering a previously unrecognized mechanism by which Tim23 contributes to MASH pathogenesis through the regulation of ferroptosis. This is consistent with reports indicating that Tim23 deficiency disrupts mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) [26]. Our current study suggests that Nrf2 may modulate ferroptosis via GPX4, independently of Tim23, consistent with previous reports showing that Nrf2 regulates ferroptosis through the SLC7A11/GPX4 axis [53–55]. We further confirmed that Tim23 silencing enhanced PA-induced Δψm collapse (as indicated by reduced JC-1 aggregates) while promoting Drp1 mitochondrial translocation and fission (Fig. 7).

Previous studies have shown that disruption of mitochondrial homeostasis, such as excessive fission mediated by Drp1, promotes reactive oxygen species accumulation and lipid peroxidation, ultimately triggering ferroptosis [56–59]. In this study, we found that during HFD or MCD-induced MASH, up-regulated Drp1 expression and enhanced phosphorylation correlated with ACSL4 expression levels and hepatocyte ferroptosis severity in vivo and in vitro. More importantly, Drp1 directly interacted with ACSL4 to promote its translocation into mitochondria during MASH. Hence, Drp1/ACSL4 signaling may be a novel therapeutic target for MASH. Our data indicated that during MASH progression, PGC-1α coactivated Nrf1 to promote Tim23 transcription while simultaneously inhibiting Drp1 expression and its Ser616 phosphorylation. Consequently, mitochondrial translocation of Drp1 and ACSL4 was reduced. This impaired Drp1/ACSL4 interaction decreased mitochondrial ROS levels, suppressed lipid peroxidation, ultimately mitigating ACSL4-mediated hepatocyte ferroptosis (Fig. 8).

Our findings have translational potential. We found that ferroptosis occurred in the liver MASH patients. Given that ferroptosis can progress into apoptosis, inhibiting ferroptosis may be valuable to inhibiting the progression of MASH, particularly at its early stage. Our investigation revealed that PGC-1α inhibited hepatocyte ferroptosis and attenuated MASH by upregulating Tim23 and inhibiting the Drp1-ACSL4 axis. Thus, targeting PGC-1α may be a promising strategy for the early prevention and treatment of MASH.

Materials and Methods

Special reagents

Special reagents included primary antibodies against Drp1, Nrf1, P-Drp1ser616, alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA), collagen I, glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), cytochrome c oxidase subunit 4 (COXIV) and cleaved caspase-3 (Cell Signaling Technology, Beverly, USA); PGC-1α, ACSL4, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)α, Hepatocyte nuclear factor 4-alpha (HNF-4α), Desmin and interleukin (IL)-6 (Abcam, Cambridge, USA); Tim23 (Santa, TX, USA); glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), Ferroton heavy chain 1 (FTH1), transferrin receptor 1 (TFR1) and F4/80 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China); Lymphatic Vessel Endothelial Receptor-1(Lyve-1) (ABclonal, Wuhan, China); Nrf2, P-MLKL, GSDMD-N (HUABIO, Hangzhou, China); special kits for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Sirius Red, and Oil Red staining (Solarbio, Beijing, China); immunohistochemical staining kit (Maixin Biological Technology, Fujian, China); the kits for measurements of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), triglyceride (TG), total cholesterol (T-CHO), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), glucose, glutathione (GSH), malondialdehyde (MDA), and iron contents (Jiancheng Biological Engineering Institute, Nanjing, China); enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for measurements of insulin (Crystal Chem, Chicago, USA); Tissue mitochondria isolation kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China); Perls’ blue (Sbjbio, Nanjing, China); PGC-1α clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) activation plasmid (sc-400070-ACT) and Protein A/G plus-agarose (Santa Cruz Biotechnology, CA, USA); PGC-1α and Drp1 small interfering RNAs (siRNAs) (LIKELI, Beijing, China); dual luciferase assay kits and TnT® quick coupled transcription/ translation systems (Promega, Wisconsin, USA); Lipofectamine 2000, C-11 BODIPY 581/591, MitoSOX and Mitotracker (Invitrogen, CA, USA); JC-1 (Chemodex, SG, SUI); Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) (MedChemExpress, NJ, USA).

Animal study

Male C57BL/6 J (6–8 weeks old), Ppargc1αf/f (B6.Cg-Ppargc1αtm2.1Brsp/J) and transgenic (Tg) (Alb-cre)21Mgn/J (Alb-cre+/+) mice were from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology (Beijing, China) and the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA), respectively. Hepatocyte-specific PGC-1α knockout mice were generated based on the classic Cre-loxP recombination system. Briefly, Alb-cre+/+ mice were crossed with C57BL/6 mice to generate Alb-cre+/0 mice. Alb-cre+/0 mice were crossed with Ppargc1αf/f mice to generate Ppargc1αf/+Alb-cre+/0 mice. Alb-cre0/0 mice (Ppargc1α+/+) or hepatocyte-specific knockout mice (Ppargc1αf/fAlb-cre+/0) were selected by crossing Ppargc1αf/+Alb-cre+/0 and Ppargc1αf/+Alb-cre+/0mice. All mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free facility at 22 ± 2 °C, 40 ± 5% relative humidity, and a 12-hour light-dark cycle, with free access to water and food. The animal experimental protocols were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and approved by the Capital Medical University Animal Care and Use Committee.

To establish MASH models, the different strains of mice were randomized and fed with a high-fat diet (60% of fat, Trophicdiet, Nantong, China) and 20% fructose in drinking water (HFD) for 16 weeks or a methionine- and choline-deficient diet (MCD) with 18% protein, 36% carbohydrate and 46% fat (Biopike, Beijing, China) for 8 weeks. After feeding with HFD for 12 weeks or MCD for 4 weeks, some mice were randomized and injected intraperitoneally with vehicle control or 5 mg/kg Fer-1 daily and the mice were continually fed with the same diet for four weeks (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Similarly, some mice were injected intravenously with 1.1 × 10¹¹ virions of AAV8-PGC-1α or control AAV8 (100 μl, Hanbio, Shanghai, China). Four weeks post-viral injection, their peripheral blood samples were collected for the measurements of serum iron, ALT, TG, T-CHO, LDL-C, insulin and glucose using specific kits and their liver samples were collected for subsequent experiments.

Liver histopathology and immunohistochemistry

Liver specimens were fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution (pH 7.4) overnight, and paraffin-embedded. The liver tissue sections (5 μm) were routine-stained with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E), Sirius Red, Oil Red O, and Perls’ blue. In addition, biopsied liver samples were collected from 10 non-MASH patients and 22 MASH patients at the Air Force Medical Center of Chinese People’s Liberation Army. Individual MASH patients were diagnosed by pathologists and human study was carried out, in accordance with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, as amended in 1983 and approved by the Review Board of Air Force Medical Center of Chinese People’s Liberation Army (approval number: 2024-78-PJ01). The levels of interesting protein expression in liver tissue sections were examined by immunohistochemistry. Briefly, the liver tissue sections were dewaxed, rehydrated and subjected to antigen retrieval using a citrate antigen retrieval solution. After being blocked with 5% dry milk in TBST, the sections were probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against Collagen I (1:800), PGC-1α (1:1000), Nrf1 (1:200), Tim23 (1:50), Drp1 (1:200), P-Drp1ser616 (1:1000), ACSL4 (1:100), FTH1 (1:200), GPX4 (1:2000), TFR1 (1:500), Nrf2 (1:200), P-MLKL (1:200), GSDMD-N (1:200), Cleaved caspase-3 (1:50) and α-SMA (1:2000). The cell samples were incubated with antibodies against Tim23(1:50) overnight at 4 °C. After being washed, the sections were incubated with biotinylated secondary antibodies, and HRP-conjugated streptavidin, followed by developing colors with diaminobenzidine (DAB). The immunosignals were observed under a microscope.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The microstructure of liver tissue sections was examined by TEM. Briefly, the liver tissue specimens were sequentially fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmium tetroxide. After dehydration, the tissues were embedded in epoxy resin, and the ultrathin sections (70 nm) were mounted on copper grids and stained with uranyl acetate and lead citrate. Finally, the stained sections were examined under a transmission electron microscope.

Cell culture and transfection

Primary hepatocytes were isolated from adult mice, as described previously [15]. The cells (1×10⁶ cells/well) were cultured in 6-well plates that had been coated with collagen and transfected with either the PGC-1α Crispr activation (sc-400070-ACT) plasmid, control plasmid, PGC-1α (accession code: NM_001402987.1 siRNA, Drp1 (accession code: NM_001025947.3) siRNA, Tim23 (accession code: NM-016897.3) siRNA, Nrf1 (accession code: NM-001164226.2) siRNA, Nrf2 (accession code: NM-001399226.1) siRNA or control siRNA using Lipofectamine 2000, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. One day later, the cells were treated with vehicle or 300 μM palmitic acid (PA, Sigma, St. Louis, USA) for 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with vehicle DMSO or 5 µM Fer-1 for 24 h. The cells were harvested for subsequent mRNA and protein analysis, lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial ROS assessments.

Isolation of liver tissue fractionations

Liver tissues were collected from individual groups of mice and homogenized in ice-cold mitochondria isolation buffer. After being centrifuged at 600 g (4 °C, 5 minutes), their supernatants were collected and centrifuged at 11,000 g (4 °C, 10 minutes) to obtain the mitochondrial pellet. Similarly, the collected homogenates were centrifuged at 12,000 g (4 °C, 10 minutes) to remove the pellets containing the remaining residual mitochondria and collect the supernatants containing cytoplasmic fraction. The cytosolic and mitochondrial proteins were analyzed by Western blot.

Mouse metabolic study

The levels of blood glucose, serum ALT, TG, T-CHO, LDL-C and insulin were measured using specific kits as manufacturer’s instructions.

In vitro transcription/translation

The DNA fragments for the expression of Drp1 and ACSL4 were subcloned into the plasmid of pcDNA3.1-vector, respectively. After sequenced, the plasmids were used for in vitro transcription and translation. The in vitro transcription and translation reactions were performed using the TnT®-coupled reticulocyte lysate system with T7 RNA polymerase and biotinylated lysine-tRNA, according to manufacturer’s protocols.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from liver tissues and primary hepatocytes using Trizol reagent and reverse-transcribed into cDNA. The relative levels of a target gene to the control GAPDH mRNA transcripts were quantified by RT-qPCR using specific primers (Table S1).

Western blot

The relative levels of interesting proteins to the control GAPDH expression in liver tissue, primary hepatocytes and mitochondrial fractions were quantified by Western blot. We used primary antibodies against PGC-1α (1:4000), Nrf1 (1:1000), Nrf2 (1:2500), Tim23 (1:500), anti-Drp1 (1:1000), phospho-Drp1ser616 (1:1000), IL-6 (1:1000), TNFα (1:1000), ACSL4 (1:1000), α-SMA (1:1000), Collagen I (1:1000), GPX4 (1:1000), TFR1 (1:1000), FTH1 (1:1000), and GAPDH (1:4000).

Dual luciferase reporter assay

The potential sequences for the binding of transcription factors in the promoter region were predicted using the JASPAR database, and the sequences for Nrf1 promoter region designed as Supplementary methods. The sequence in the wild-type Nrf1, Tim23 or Drp1 promoter and its mutants (Table S1) were synthesized and cloned into the PGL4.17 plasmid, followed by sequencing. HEK293T cells (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) were cultured in 96-well plates and transiently transfected with the plasmid of PCDNA3.1-Ppargc1α, PCDNA3.1-Nrf1, PCDNA3.1-Nrf2 or control vector using Lipofectamine 2000. Two days later, the different groups of cells were harvested, and their luciferase activity was analyzed using a dual luciferase reporter assay system (Promega, Madison, WI).

Co-immunoprecipitation

The potential interactions of Drp1 with ACSL4 were analyzed by co-immunoprecipitation. Briefly, individual liver tissue homogenates or protein samples from the TnT®-coupled reticulocyte lysate system were incubated with anti-Drp1 or control mouse IgG and the immunocomplex was precipitated with Protein A/G beads. After being washed, the contents of ACSL4 in immunocomplex were characterized by Western blot using anti-ACSL4 (1:1000).

Measurement of lipid peroxidation, mtROS and JC-1

Primary hepatocytes were cultured in confocal plates up to 60–70% confluency and the cells were probed with 5 μM C11-BODIPY 581/591 solution for the measurement of lipid peroxidation or stained with 5 μM MitoSOX and 500 nM Mitotracker solution or 10 ug/ml JC-1 solution. After incubation at 37 °C for 30 minutes, the cells were washed and stained with 10 μg/mL Hoechst. Finally, the cells were examined under a confocal microscope.

Immunofluorescent staining

Frozen liver sections or primary hepatocytes were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. After permeabilization with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 10 min, the samples were blocked in immunostaining blocking solution for 1 h at room temperature. The liver sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with an anti-ACSL4 antibody (1:200) in combination with either anti-HNF4α (1:50), anti-F4/80 (1:200), anti-Lyve-1 (1:200), or anti-Desmin (1:200) antibody. Primary hepatocytes were incubated with combinations of primary antibodies against Drp1 (1:1000) and ACSL4 (1:1000), ACSL4 (1:1000) and COXIV (1:1000), or Drp1 (1:1000) and COXIV (1:1000). After washing, the samples were incubated with fluorescence-labeled secondary antibodies, counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and examined under a confocal microscope.

Measurement of MDA and GSH

The levels of hepatic MDA were quantified using a lipid peroxidation assay kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions. The optical density was measured at 532 nm, and the MDA levels were expressed as micromoles per gram of liver tissue proteins. Similarly, the levels of hepatic glutathione (GSH) were measured using a commercial quantification kit, according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The absorbance of the solution was measured at 405 nm. The results were calculated and reported as micromoles per gram of liver tissue proteins.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The difference between groups was assessed by the Student’s t-test for pairwise comparisons and the difference among groups was analyzed by one-way ANOVA using SPSS software, version 12.0. The graphs were generated using GraphPad software. A P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
