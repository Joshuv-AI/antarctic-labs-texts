Frequency of Pseudomonas's Antibiotic Resistance and Virulence Genes in Poultry Meat
Authors: Sajjad Abbasi, Ebrahim Rahimi, Noosha Zia Jahromi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground Pseudomonas aeruginosa can be transmitted to humans through food and cause infection. This bacterium is a Gram‐negative bacillus. Septicemia, significant financial losses in poultry production and respiratory diseases in humans are the complications of P. aeruginosa infection.ObjectivesThis study assessed the antibiotic resistance, and distribution of virulence factors in poultry meat. Multiplex PCR used to investigate the distribution of exo T3SS genes in P. aeruginosa.Material and MethodsBacteriological examination was performed on the 417 samples collected from poultry meat. The disc diffusion technique was employed to test antimicrobial susceptibility. The genetic pattern and distribution of antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes of the isolates were studied. The T3SS genes (exo T, Y, S and U) were amplified by multiplex PCR with specific primers.Results P. aeruginosa was found in 153 out of 417 (36.69%) examined samples. Penicillin had the highest resistance rate (100%), tetracycline (96.07%), gentamicin (81.69%) and streptomycin (70.58%). The antibiotic resistance genes with the most common detection were blaTEM (93.46%), blaSHV (19.60%) and blaDHA (18.95%). The virulence factor with the highest detection rate was nanI (69.28%), plcH (48.36%), plcN (37.90%), pilA (32.67%), phzM (32.02%), toxA (31.37%). The frequency T3SS genes (exoT, Y, S and U) of P. aeruginosa isolates were 100%, 97.38%, 94.77% and 73.85%, respectively.ConclusionIn the present study, P. aeruginosa recovered from poultry meat is a very dangerous bacterium that can be transmitted to humans through food. Bacterium is resistant to most therapeutic agents and shows a pattern of multidrug resistance. It is considered very dangerous in the food industry and the consumption of poultry meat, as well as for public health.Keywords: antibiotic resistance, P. aeruginosa, poultry meat, T3SS, virulence gene

Introduction

By 2050, the predicted increase in human population is around 9.15 billion, and developing countries are expected to have the highest increase. Poultry farming is the primary source of animal protein supply chains in rural communities, making it the most popular and cost‐effective source of high‐quality protein for humans (Hemathilake and Gunathilake 2022). Various bacterial species in poultry farms can result in multiple clinical sequelae. This negatively affects the poultry industry's investments, decreasing protein production and transmission of pathogens to humans (Wang et al. 2019). One of the opportunistic diseases affecting poultry is infection with Pseudomonas. The causative agent is Pseudomonas aeruginosa (P. aeruginosa). It spreads quickly through poultry flocks, increasing mortality rates and decreasing production (Astill et al. 2018). Food spoilage and foodborne diseases can be caused by pathogens that reside in broilers. Environmental sources such as soil and water are often where it is found. P. aeruginosa grows and multiplies at various temperatures when meat is kept in aerobic conditions (Wei et al. 2020). The storage of this microorganism in dairy samples and meat is easy and it can grow aerobically at low temperatures. Off‐flavours, pigmentation, slime and malodour production are a result of this bacteria's growth in meat and derived products (Vukić Lušić et al. 2021). P. aeruginosa’s involvement in foodborne infections is becoming more evident. The incidence of foodborne illness following consumption of poultry sample contaminated with P. aeruginosa is not well known. It is crucial to determine the role of poultry as a pool of P. aeruginosa (Heredia and García 2018). P. aeruginosa is composed of multiple virulence genes that are cause of infections. The intracellular oxidative effects are caused by three phenazine compounds encoded in precursor proteins secreted by the phenazine operons including (phzH, phzM and phzS) (Rocha et al. 2019). Among the virulence factors of P. aeruginosa, which causes diseases in humans and animals, we can mention lasA and B, exoS, T, U and Y, plcH and N, and alginate‐encoded genes D and U (Veetilvalappil et al. 2022). Pathogenic genes play a role in the survival and invasion of bacteria to the host. The type III secretion system (T3SS) is one of the central pathogenic systems in P. aeruginosa, which acts through the expression of four toxins (exoS, U, T and Y). The high virulence potential caused by exoT and Y secretion leads to lung tissue deterioration and higher mortality. According to in vitro assays, exoS and U are less important in virulence because they have low cytotoxicity (Sarges et al. 2020). Almost all strains contain exoS, exoT and Y. The frequency of exoU detection is lower because it is linked to pathogenicity (PAPI‐2) that absent in P. aeruginosa genome. The exoS and U tend to be exclusive, and it's uncommon to find the viral type that has both exoS and U genes (Lee and Jones 2018). Antimicrobial properties are needed to deal with all kinds of diseases in poultry flocks. The creation and spread of drug resistance in poultry farms can be influenced by the use of antimicrobial agents (Pang et al. 2019). Food‐producing animals can act as a source for the spread of resistance genes. The ways of transferring these genes to humans can be through directly (contact between humans and poultry infected with bacteria) or indirectly (food production chain) (Kunz Coyne et al. 2022). The high level of resistance to aminoglycosides, tetracycline, penicillin, quinolone, cephalosporin, macrolide and beta‐lactam antibiotics has been reported (Langendonk et al. 2021). Paying attention to antimicrobial resistance in poultry bacterial infections is very important. Because it may turn treatable and simple diseases into incurable and complex diseases. Beta‐lactams are used to treat human and animal infections and prevent serious diseases (Hawkey and Jones 2009; Bush and Bradford 2016; Dame‐Korevaar et al. 2019). This study aimed to investigate the antimicrobial resistance, virulence and T3SS genes of P. aeruginosa isolated from meat in broiler poultry.

Materials and Methods

Sample Collection

Over one year (from January 2023 to January 2024), 417 poultry meat were collected. The samples included chicken (162 samples), turkey (123 samples), duck (52 samples), quail (41 samples), and goose (39 samples) in Tehran. Approximately 100 g of poultry meat were collected from the muscle by sterile bags. Refrigerated containers were used to transport the samples at 4°C. Within 2 h after collection, the samples were processed.

Isolation and Biochemical Identification of P. aeruginosa

Approximately 25 g of the meat were placed in Stomacher bags that had 225 mL of peptone water (Oxoid, UK). For 4 min, bags were subjected to maceration within the stomacher (Seward 400 circulator) with 260 beats per minute. 100 µL homogenate samples were placed on CN Selective Agar (Oxoid SR 102E) supplemented with Pseudomonas Agar base (Oxoid). Aerobic conditions were used to incubate the media at 37°C for 24 h. Pseudomonas were identified by microscopic observation of morphology, catalase, oxidase and urease enzyme activity, ability to hydrolyse casein and starch, use of citrate and indole, and Methyl Red‐Veges Prosquare and gelatin liquefaction tests. P. aeruginosa were identified by PCR for detection of the 16S rRNA using (F: 5′‐GGGGGATCTTCGGACCTCA‐3′ and R: 5′‐TCCTTAGAGTGCCCACCCG‐3′ [956 bp]) (Spilker et al. 2004).

Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

The disc diffusion technique on Mueller–Hinton agar (Quelab, Canada) with antimicrobials recommended by CLSI 2021 was used to test all isolates for resistance (Lewis and James 2022; Gill et al. 2016). The antimicrobials used were penicillin (P, 10 µg/disk), tetracycline (TE, 30 µg/disk), streptomycin (S, 10 µg/disk), chloramphenicol (C, 30 µg/disk), sulphamethoxazol (SXT, 25 µg/disk), gentamycin (GM, 10 µg/disk), enrofloxacin (NFX, 5 µg/disk), cephalothin (CF, 30 µg/disk), ciprofloxacin (CIP, 5 µg/disk), trimethoprim (TMP, 5 µg/disk), nitrofurantoin (F/M, 300 µg/disk), erythromycin (E, 15 µg/disk) and ampicillin (AM, 10 µg/disk). The broth dilution technique was used to calculate the MIC of the aforementioned antibiotics against the isolates following the manufacturer's instructions and CLSI recommendations (Dame‐Korevaar et al. 2019). The positive control in this experiment was a standard strain of P. aeruginosa (ATCC 10145) (Safarpoor Dehkordi et al. 2017).

DNA Extraction and Quality Analysis

The P. aeruginosa isolates that were identified were incubated at 37°C under aerobic conditions in TSB media (Quelab, Canada) for 48 h. The DNA Extraction Kit (Cinaclone, Iran) was used to extract the genomic DNA from the isolates. Nanodrop (Thermo Scientific, USA) and 2% agarose gel was used to check the purity (A260/A280), concentration and DNA's quality (Dehkordi et al. 2020).

Detection of Virulence Genes Encoding T3SS

Multiplex PCR primers for T3SS gene amplification are shown in Table 1. A total of 25 µL PCR master mix reaction volume containing 2 µL of DNA, 12.5 µL of 2X Master Mix (Cinaclone, Iran) and 0.5 µL for each of the forward and reverse primer for all genes, with 9.5 µL dH2O to a final volume of 25 µL. All PCR reactions were conducted using a programmable DNA thermocycler (Eppendorf Mastercycler 5330, Germany). Genes amplified by PCR were loaded on agarose gel with a concentration of 2%. Agarose gel was stained with safe red dye. Electrophoresis (120 V/208 mA) caused the movement of genes on the gel (Rahi et al. 2020). UVI doc was used to analyse the gel image.

Data Analysis

By using SPSS 21.0 (SPSS Inc, USA) The data analysis was carried out. Chi‐square were performed to assess significant relationship between the prevalence of P. aeruginosa, virulence and antibiotic resistance pattern. p value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant (Ranjbar et al. 2019).

Results

P. aeruginosa Distribution

Out of 417 poultry meat isolates, 153 (36.69%) were P. aeruginosa positive. Different meat sources showed a statistically significant difference. The highest contamination is in duck meat (50%) chicken meat (44.44%), goose meat (41.02 %) turkey meat (24.39%) and quail meat (21.95%) was identified. The results shown in Figure 1.

Prevalence Detection of T3SS Genes in P. aeruginosa

The results revealed that exoT genes were present in all isolates (100%). Table 4 shown the prevalence of exoY, exoS and exoU genes among the 153 isolates were 97.38% (n = 149), 94.77% (n = 145) and 73.85% (n = 113). As shown in Figure 2, in Lanes 1, and 3–5, which correspond to chicken, quail, goose and duck meat samples, respectively, exoT, exoS and exoY are present. However, in the turkey meat sample, the exoU gene was not observed, which indicates its low frequency among the four genes.

Table 6 shows that the P. aeruginosa have a 100% resistance to penicillin, followed by tetracycline (96.07%), gentamycin (81.69%), streptomycin (70.58%), sulfamethoxazol (43.13%), erythromycin (42.48%), trimethoprim (38.56%), ampicillin (32.67%), enrofloxacin (28.75%), cephalothin (26.79%), ciprofloxacin (22.87%), chloramphenicol (17.64%) and nitrofurantoin (7.84%).

Discussion

The origin of bacteria in food is a complex topic to debate. The poultry production sector worldwide suffers significant economic losses due to P. aeruginosa, which is a primary cause of septicemia in broiler poultry (Abdelmoez et al. 2019). In this study, the prevalence of P. aeruginosa in the examined poultry including chicken, turkey, duck, goose, and quail was 36.69%. According to Abd El‐Tawab et al. (2018), the inspected broiler chickens had a 34% P. aeruginosa percentage. Furthermore, Shahat et al. (2019) reported a lower prevalence (20%). P. aeruginosa is a critical threat to poultry survival, leading to acute infections, reduced production, and death in broilers. The abundance of P. aeruginosa in the environment is very high. It can even exist on human skin surface damage due to wounds or burns. Environmental contamination or infected slaughterhouse personnel after contact with meat and carcasses can be the source of the isolated strain (Abd El‐Ghany 2021). P. aeruginosa strains are recognized as microorganisms that can spoil fresh meat. All the isolates collected from poultry were found to have the morphological and biochemical characteristics of P. aeruginosa, as highlighted by the bacteriological investigation (Sheir et al. 2020). The difference in the prevalence rate can be explained by the sanitary conditions of the slaughterhouse, the time of carcass sampling, geographical changes, the way of management, stress, the age of the animal and the level of immunity (Eraky et al. 2020). Preventing the spread of P. aeruginosa infection requires a high standard of hygiene measures. Better infection control results are often achieved through regular cleaning and disinfection of poultry farms. Furthermore, it is important to consider restrictions on antibiotic use in the poultry industry (Abdelmoez et al. 2019). Diverse classes of antimicrobials were found to have a high resistance rate against the P. aeruginosa strains. The high rate of resistance to penicillin was achieved (100%), tetracycline (96.07%), gentamicin (81.69%) and streptomycin (70.58%). In addition, blaTEM , blaSHV and blaDHA were some antibiotic resistance genes that P. aeruginosa had. Both phenotypically and genetically, P. aeruginosa showed resistance to antibiotic agents. The high prevalence of this genes can be explained by improper and unauthorized administration of antibiotics and overuse of antibiotics and disinfectants on farms (Meng et al. 2020). In Benie's report, P. aeruginosa showed the most resistance to kanamycin at 100% (Benie et al. 2017). Allydice‐Francis and Brown (2012) conducted a study and found that 79% of P. aeruginosa were resistant to ceftazidime, 93% to ciprofloxacin, 97% to gentamicin and 100% to imipenem. According to Odumosu et al. (2016), the rate of resistance against carbenicillin was 60%. Chloramphenicol, imipenem, aztreonam and clindamycin prescriptions are believed to be effective in treating foodborne infections caused by P. aeruginosa (Mir et al. 2016). The virulence genes of P. aeruginosa enable bacteria to colonize different niches in their hosts, making it an opportunistic pathogen. Acquired infections in hospitals and communities worldwide are mainly caused by it. The invasive and acute cytotoxic phenotype will be confirmed by the presence of exoS and U. ExoT and Y are present in most P. aeruginosa’s. The existence of exoS and U appears to be exclusive between the isolates, despite their significant differences (Khodayary et al. 2019). ExoT, Y, S and U were found to be 100%, 97.38%, 94.77% and 73.85% present in P. aeruginosa isolates in the present study. The frequency was lower in the study conducted by Khodayary et al. (2019) who reported exoU: 59% and exoS: 41% in P. aeruginosa. The different samples, types of strains and geographical regions investigated may explain the observed differences in the prevalence of exoS and U. The isolates had several putative virulence factors, particularly nanI (69.28%), plcH (48.36%), plcN (37.90%), pilA (32.67%), phzM (32.02%), toxA (31.37%), algU and phzII (26.79%), pvdA (23.52%), lasA (22.22%), pilB (18.95%), lasB (18.30%), phzS and phzH (15.03%) and apr (13.07%). Adhesion and invasion into host are largely due to these genes. Consuming food that contains P. aeruginosa may result in many foodborne disease. In Osman et al. (2019), toxA, aprA, plcH and las factors of P. aeruginosa were dominant.

Conclusion

P. aeruginosa were detected in 36.69% of poultry meat samples. A high level of resistance was observed in P. aeruginosa to common antibiotic agents, especially penicillin, tetracycline and gentamicin. The findings could indicate that P. aeruginosa has a high level of antibiotic resistance and that poultry meat samples could play a role in transmitting it to human. Different antibiotic resistance genes, such as blaSHV , blaDHA and blaTEM , and virulence genes, especially exoT, Y, nanI, plcH and N observed. It appears that consuming meat that is contaminated with P. aeruginosa result in many food‐borne illnesses that are resistant to antibiotic therapy. The risk of foodborne infection from contaminated meat has not been determined yet. To better understand the role of poultry meat in the transmission of infection and resistant bacteria P. aeruginosa, it is recommended to conduct more studies.
