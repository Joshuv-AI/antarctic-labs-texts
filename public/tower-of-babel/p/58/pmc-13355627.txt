Salvage of a Penetrating Bilateral Testicular Gunshot Injury Using an Autologous Free Tunica Albuginea Graft from the Excised Contralateral Testis: A Case Report
Authors: Anas Saad, Omar Al-Abed, Salem Hakouk, Mohammad Alaa Aldakak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPenetrating scrotal trauma is an uncommon but potentially devastating urogenital injury. Testicular rupture requires prompt exploration, particularly when associated with urethral or corporal trauma. When primary closure of the tunica albuginea is not feasible, reconstructive alternatives may be needed to preserve viable testicular tissue.Case PresentationWe report an 18-year-old male who sustained a ricocheting gunshot injury to the perineum and external genitalia. After initial cystostomy and wound packing at a non-specialized hospital, he was referred to our center approximately 12 hours after injury. Exploration revealed complete destruction of the right testis, severe rupture of the left testis with extrusion of seminiferous tubules and a large tunica albuginea defect, a partial distal bulbar urethral laceration, and a 1-cm laceration of the left corpus cavernosum. The right testis was unsalvageable and excised. The urethral and corporal injuries were repaired primarily. Because the left testis could not be closed primarily and the tunica vaginalis was inadequate, an autologous free graft harvested from the tunica albuginea of the excised right testis was used to close the defect.DiscussionPreviously described options for large tunica albuginea defects include tunica vaginalis-based techniques and biologic or synthetic grafts. Our approach used contralateral tunica albuginea as an immediately available autologous graft.ConclusionAt 3-month follow-up, the patient had preserved testicular vascularity and volume, normal voiding and erections, and reference-range testosterone. However, persistent severe oligozoospermia and elevated gonadotropins indicated impaired spermatogenesis, prompting fertility counseling and repeat sperm cryopreservation. This technique may represent a feasible testis-sparing option in carefully selected cases.

Introduction

Male genital trauma is uncommon, largely because of the mobility, elasticity, protected anatomic position, and relatively small surface area of the external genitalia.1,2

Within this spectrum, penetrating external genital injuries are relatively rare, although the genitourinary tract is involved in approximately 10% of all trauma cases and about 60% of penetrating genitourinary injuries affect the external genitalia.3 Scrotal and testicular trauma account for less than 1% of all traumatic injuries, yet these injuries may result in important long-term sequelae including infertility, hormonal dysfunction, impotence, voiding dysfunction, and psychological distress.1,4,5 Scrotal injuries are classically classified as penetrating, blunt, degloving, and thermal injuries, while blunt trauma remains the dominant mechanism overall and penetrating trauma is particularly relevant in civilian gunshot-related genital injury.2,3 Among penetrating external genital injuries, gunshot wounds are the commonest mechanism, scrotal injury is the most frequent anatomic presentation, and testicular injury occurs in a substantial proportion of scrotal wounds, with bilateral injuries representing a minority but clinically important subset.3,6 Clinically, patients may present with acute pain, swelling, ecchymosis, hematocele, or extrusion of seminiferous tubules, but physical examination alone is often insufficient for reliable diagnosis.2,7 Scrotal ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality, with heterogeneous parenchyma, contour loss, tunica albuginea disruption, hematocele, and altered vascularity among the most useful signs of rupture, whereas MRI is mainly reserved for equivocal cases.2,7 Current management emphasizes early surgical exploration when rupture is suspected, because repair within 72 hours is associated with markedly higher salvage rates than delayed intervention.2,7 Standard operative treatment consists of hematoma evacuation, conservative debridement of nonviable tissue, and tension-free closure of the tunica albuginea; however, extensive tunical defects may necessitate reconstructive substitutes such as tunica vaginalis or other graft materials to maximize preservation of viable testicular tissue.2,8–10 Here, we present a case of complex bilateral penetrating testicular injury caused by a ricocheting gunshot, associated with distal bulbar urethral and left corporal injury. The case highlights successful salvage of the remaining testis using an autologous free tunica albuginea graft harvested from the excised contralateral testis when primary closure was not feasible.

Case Presentation

An 18-year-old unmarried male with no significant past medical history sustained a ricocheting gunshot injury to the perineum and external genitalia in a remote area. He developed significant bleeding and was initially transferred to a non-specialized hospital, where first aid was provided, a cystostomy was performed, and the scrotal wound was packed with gauze to achieve hemostasis. He was subsequently referred to our tertiary specialized hospital for further management.

The patient presented to our emergency department approximately 12 hours after the injury in good general condition, with stable vital signs and adequate urinary output. Physical examination revealed a severe isolated injury involving the perineum and scrotum. The entry wound was located in the right perineal region, anterior and lateral to the anus, while the exit wound was identified at the anterior aspect of the scrotum. The scrotum was markedly enlarged, severely swollen, tense, and occupied by a large hematoma, with gauze compressing the exit wound. In addition, blood spots were noted at the external urethral meatus, raising suspicion of an associated urethral injury. No other injuries were identified elsewhere on the body. A general surgery consultation confirmed that the anus was intact and that no colorectal intervention was required.

Emergency scrotal exploration was performed by an experienced urological team. After removal of the gauze packing, the wound was thoroughly irrigated. Intraoperatively, the right testis was found to be completely destroyed, with only remnants of the tunica albuginea and epididymis attached to the spermatic cord; no identifiable seminiferous tubules remained (Figure 1). The left testis showed severe rupture at the inferior pole, with extrusion of seminiferous tubules and partial loss of the tunica albuginea (Figures 2 and 3). A partial sharp laceration of the distal bulbar urethra, approximately 1 cm proximal to the external urethral sphincter, was also identified. Additionally, a longitudinal laceration measuring approximately 1 cm was found in the left corpus cavernosum, causing significant bleeding within the operative field (Figure 4). Given the extent of destruction, salvage of the right testis was not feasible. Therefore, simple excision of the remaining right testicular and epididymal tissue was performed, followed by high ligation of the right spermatic cord. The laceration of the left corpus cavernosum was repaired using continuous 2–0 absorbable suture, after which the overlying tunica albuginea was closed. Primary repair of the urethral injury was then performed in two layers over a urethral catheter.

Reconstruction of the remaining left testis was particularly challenging because of a large tunica albuginea defect with a broad area of exposed seminiferous tubules that could not be closed primarily. Leaving the defect uncovered was not considered acceptable, and the tunica vaginalis was deemed inadequate for flap reconstruction. Therefore, an autologous free graft was fashioned from the tunica albuginea of the excised right testis and used to cover the defect in the left testis. After debridement of the defect margins and excision of necrotic testicular tissue until healthy viable tissue was identified, the graft was sutured in a tension-free manner using continuous 4–0 absorbable suture. A drain was placed beneath the dartos layer, the scrotal wound was closed in two layers, and the perineal entry wound was also irrigated and closed in two layers.

The postoperative course was uneventful. The patient was discharged on postoperative day 2 after drain removal. The urethral catheter was removed 2 weeks later. Follow-up cystoscopy demonstrated satisfactory healing at the site of urethral repair, and the patient regained normal voiding without urinary symptoms or complications (Figure 5). Scrotal ultrasonography performed 1 week after discharge showed a heterogeneous left testis of normal size (40 × 20 × 38 mm), preserved vascularity, and a volume of 16.7 cm3. At the one-month follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic and reported no urinary or erectile complaints. Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated preserved testicular volume and vascularity. Semen analysis revealed a volume of 1.5 mL, severe oligozoospermia with a sperm concentration of approximately 2 million/mL, and markedly reduced motility. Hormonal evaluation demonstrated elevated serum FSH and LH levels, while serum testosterone remained within the laboratory reference range. These findings were consistent with compensated primary testicular dysfunction and preserved early Leydig cell function. The patient was counseled regarding fertility prognosis and underwent sperm cryopreservation.

At the three-month follow-up, the patient continued to deny erectile or urinary symptoms. Doppler ultrasonography demonstrated a homogeneous, well-perfused left testis of normal size, measuring 32 × 36 × 22 mm, with a reported volume of 25 cm3. Repeat semen analysis showed preserved semen volume, persistent severe oligozoospermia with a sperm concentration of approximately 1 million/mL, and modest improvement in sperm motility. Hormonal evaluation showed elevated serum FSH and LH levels, with FSH of 28.4 mIU/mL and LH of 11.9 mIU/mL, whereas serum testosterone remained within the laboratory reference range at 267 ng/dL. These findings remained consistent with compensated primary testicular dysfunction and preserved Leydig cell function. Given the improvement in sperm motility and the patient’s young age, he was counseled again regarding fertility preservation and referred for an additional sperm cryopreservation attempt to maximize future reproductive options.

Discussion

Our case lies at the intersection of two difficult clinical scenarios: penetrating male genital trauma and reconstruction of a large tunica albuginea defect.1,3,9 Penetrating external genital trauma is uncommon, but when present it is most often caused by gunshot wounds and may involve the scrotum, testes, penis, and urethra.1,3 In a large published series, penetrating external genital trauma represented 0.57% of all trauma; gunshot wounds accounted for 93% of cases, scrotal injuries for 78%, testicular injuries for 63% of scrotal wounds, bilateral testicular injuries for 8%, and urethral injuries for 13%.3 Another series similarly showed that genital trauma frequently involves the scrotum and that associated urethral injury is not rare, occurring in 17% of genital trauma overall.1 These data highlight the complexity of our patient’s injury, which combined bilateral testicular destruction, distal bulbar urethral laceration, and ipsilateral corporal injury.

The diagnostic and operative principles in this case are consistent with the literature.2,7 Clinical examination may be limited by pain, swelling, and hematoma, while ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality for suspected testicular rupture; MRI is generally reserved for equivocal cases.2,7 However, when rupture is strongly suspected, surgical exploration should not be delayed, because repair within 72 hours is associated with higher salvage rates and lower orchiectomy risk.2,7,10 This is particularly relevant in penetrating gunshot trauma, where tissue loss, blast effect, hematoma, and contamination may rapidly compromise viability.3,6

Standard operative management includes hematoma evacuation, conservative debridement of nonviable seminiferous tubules, and tension-free closure of the tunica albuginea whenever possible.2,3,7 The reconstructive challenge arises when viable extruded seminiferous tubules cannot be reduced or covered without excessive tissue sacrifice or forced closure, which may increase intratesticular pressure, impair perfusion, and promote necrosis or later atrophy.8–10 Several alternative coverage techniques have therefore been described.8–10 These include creation of a tunica vaginalis “neocapsule,” with preserved viability at 3-month Doppler follow-up,11 vascularized tunica vaginalis pedicle flaps for large tunical defects,10 and pediatric repairs using pedicled tunica vaginalis with preserved blood flow and testosterone production.9 Other reports have described bovine pericardium and cadaveric dermis for complex ruptures, including penetrating trauma.8 The closest published comparison is a case of bilateral gunshot testicular rupture repaired using tunica vaginalis grafts.6 In that case, one testis became nonviable and required orchiectomy at one week, while the remaining testis had minimal atrophic change at four months; erections were preserved, but hormonal status was borderline and semen analysis was abnormal.6 Although focused on delayed unilateral rupture after blunt trauma rather than penetrating bilateral injury, a recent case report and literature review also emphasized the importance of prompt assessment, surgical exploration, follow-up, and consideration of fertility-preservation options when substantial testicular tissue loss is likely.12

In our patient, the right testis was completely destroyed and was excised at the index operation, while the left testis was reconstructed and remained viable with preserved vascularity, preserved volume, normal voiding, and preserved erectile function during follow-up. However, the reproductive outcome was more guarded: semen analysis showed persistent severe oligozoospermia, and hormonal testing demonstrated elevated FSH and LH levels with serum testosterone remaining within the normal range. These findings suggest impaired spermatogenesis with preserved early Leydig cell function, or compensated primary testicular dysfunction. Therefore, the value of this procedure should be interpreted as successful anatomical, vascular, and endocrine-preserving salvage of the only remaining testis, rather than restoration of normal spermatogenesis. Although follow-up remains short, these findings provide a realistic functional perspective on the limited published experience in bilateral penetrating testicular injury.

The distinctive feature of our case is that reconstruction was not performed with tunica vaginalis, but with an autologous free graft harvested from the tunica albuginea of the excised contralateral testis.6,9–11 Previously described substitutes for large tunical defects include free or pedicled tunica vaginalis, PTFE, bovine pericardium, cadaveric pericardium, and cadaveric dermis.8–11 Within the available references, we did not identify another published case using contralateral excised tunica albuginea itself as a free autologous graft to reconstruct the remaining injured testis.6,8–11 The significance of this technique is not that it replaces established approaches, but that it provides a biologically plausible and immediately available autologous option when one testis is unsalvageable, the remaining testis is partly viable, primary closure is impossible, and tunica vaginalis is inadequate.8–10 Because the graft is autologous and harvested from tissue already being excised, it also avoids implantation of foreign material, a relevant concern in contaminated penetrating trauma and in reports of synthetic graft infection or orchiectomy.9–11

An important lesson from this case is that testicular salvage after severe bilateral trauma should not be judged only by Doppler vascularity or testicular size. Preserved perfusion, maintained volume, and reference-range testosterone are clinically meaningful, especially in a young patient with a solitary remaining testis. Nevertheless, spermatogenesis may remain severely impaired because of parenchymal loss, seminiferous tubule disruption, local inflammation, ischemic or blast-related injury, and possible disruption of the blood-testis barrier. In our patient, persistent severe oligozoospermia despite preserved vascularity and testosterone illustrates the dissociation between structural viability, endocrine function, and reproductive potential. This distinction is essential for counseling and for reporting outcomes after complex testicular reconstruction. Early semen analysis, hormonal follow-up, fertility counseling, and sperm cryopreservation should therefore be integrated into postoperative care, particularly in adolescents and young adults. Because sperm motility improved modestly on repeat semen analysis, additional sperm cryopreservation was recommended to maximize future reproductive options.

Finally, our case also reinforces a principle emphasized in penetrating genital trauma series: associated urethral injury requires a high index of suspicion, careful evaluation, and either primary repair or diversion depending on injury extent and clinical context.1,3 In this patient, combined testicular salvage, primary urethral repair, corporal repair, preserved voiding, preserved erectile function, maintained testicular perfusion, and reference-range testosterone support the value of organ-preserving reconstruction even in complex genital gunshot trauma. At the same time, persistent severe oligozoospermia and elevated gonadotropins emphasize that anatomical salvage does not necessarily restore normal spermatogenesis after bilateral destructive injury. This balanced interpretation supports testis-sparing reconstruction when technically feasible, while acknowledging that longer follow-up is needed to assess endocrine durability, testicular volume stability, and future reproductive potential.1,3,5,6

Conclusion

Penetrating bilateral testicular injury with associated urethral and corporal trauma is rare and technically challenging. In this case, early exploration and single-stage repair allowed salvage of the remaining viable testis despite a large tunica albuginea defect. An autologous free tunica albuginea graft harvested from the excised contralateral testis provided a practical reconstructive option when primary closure and tunica vaginalis repair were not feasible. Short-term follow-up showed preserved testicular vascularity and volume, normal voiding and erectile function, and reference-range testosterone; however, persistent severe oligozoospermia and elevated gonadotropins indicated impaired spermatogenesis, underscoring the importance of fertility counseling, semen analysis, and sperm cryopreservation. This technique may represent a feasible testis-sparing option in selected cases, although longer follow-up is needed to assess durable endocrine and reproductive outcomes.
