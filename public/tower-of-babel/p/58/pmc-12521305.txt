A collagen-based bilayer nanocomposite scaffold for osteochondral tissue regeneration: integrating hydroxyapatite and cellulose nanocrystals
Authors: Fatemeh Mahmoudi, Sara Takallu, Mohammad Ali Derakhshan, Esmaeil Mirzaei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Regenerating osteochondral (OC) defects remains a significant challenge in regenerative medicine due to the complex nature of these tissues and their limited self-healing capacity. While tissue engineering offers a promising solution, developing a comprehensive, long-term regenerative strategy is still an active area of research. This study focused on fabricating of a biocompatible bilayer collagen (COL) based scaffold, designed with a bone layer containing hydroxyapatite (HAp) nanoparticles and a cartilage layer incorporating cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs) and hyaluronic acid (HA). Fabricated through COL fibrillation and gelation subsequent with plastic compression and freeze-drying, the scaffold provided proper environment for both bone and cartilage cells. The inclusion of HAp and CNCs not only enhanced the mechanical properties of the scaffolds but also provided structural similarity to the native matrix of these tissues, thereby improving bioactivity. The bone layer, featuring larger pores, structurally resembled hard bone tissue and supported excellent survival, adhesion, and proliferation of MG-63 cells (a human osteoblastic cell line). In contrast, the cartilage layer, provided a hydrated, bioactive environment similar to natural cartilage with smaller, well-distributed pores that promoted the adhesion and growth of C28/I2 chondrocytes (an immortalized human chondrocyte line). Together, these properties enhance the scaffold’s capacity for effective OC tissue regeneration. These findings highlight the scaffold’s potential for effective OC tissue regeneration, offering a promising step forward in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

Introduction

Osteochondral (OC) lesions are prevalent joint disorders that affect individuals of all ages and commonly involve the ankle, knee, and elbow [1]. These lesions can arise from trauma, degenerative diseases such as osteoarthritis (OA), or cartilage destruction. OC lesions and chondral abnormalities are interlinked risk factors, as OC lesions may contribute to OA development and inversely [2, 3]. The treatment of OC disorders is challenging due to the involvement of multiple tissues, including cartilage, the interface layer, and subchondral bone. These tissues collectively provide the framework required for weight-bearing and joint mobility [4].

Degenerated articular cartilage, as an avascular and aneural tissue, is incapable of self-healing after being damaged. Natural bone has an intrinsic healing capacity when compared to articular cartilage, while significant bone abnormalities cannot heal on their own and require surgical restoration. Prompt and effective healing is essential to halt the progression of chondral defects into the underlying subchondral bone, thereby reducing the risk of requiring joint replacement at advanced stages. Articular cartilage defects are divided into three types based on their depth: partial thickness chondral defect, full-thickness chondral defect, and OC defect. A range of therapeutic strategies have been devised to tackle these problems, encompassing reparative procedures like microfracture and drilling, regenerative treatments like autograft transplantation and autologous chondrocyte implantation, and palliative techniques like chondroplasty or debridement [5, 6]. OC tissue engineering appears to be a potential way to overcome the limitations of conventional approaches [1, 7]. Scientists are currently focusing on designing scaffolds that mimic the natural gradients seen in OC tissue. These scaffolds provide cell growth and differentiation. Cells, scaffolds, and bioactive substances are often employed in tissue engineering for OC regeneration [8, 9].

Hydrogels are three-dimensional networks of hydrophilic polymers that can absorb large amounts of water without losing their structural integrity. Hydrogels for tissue engineering are derived from synthetic or natural polymers. As an illustration, collagen (COL) is prevalent in the majority of biological tissues and has demonstrated favorable biocompatibility [10]. It also has several benefits for use in tissue repair, such as being easily reabsorbed in vivo without causing an adverse reaction, increasing cellular interaction because it contains ligands that may promote cell adhesion, and being able to co-polymerize with other biological materials to enhance their bio-functionality [11–13]. Type I COL is the predominant isoform in bone, contributing to the tensile strength and mineralization of the extracellular matrix. In contrast, type II COL is the major component of hyaline cartilage, forming a finer fibrillar network that supports compressive resistance and hydration retention [14, 15].

Nanoparticles are used to improve the mechanical and biofunctional characteristics of scaffolds based on COL [16, 17]. The availability, renewability, affordability, and tunable properties of cellulose nanoparticles—including bacterial nanocellulose (BNC), cellulose nanocrystals (CNCs), and nanofibrillated cellulose (NFC)—make them superior biomaterials [17, 18]. CNC is instrumental in the performance improvement of biomimetic scaffolds by reinforcing their strength and elastic modulus. In addition, CNC promotes cellular interaction and facilitates cell differentiation into a chondrogenic phenotype to promote tissue regeneration [19]. Hyaluronic acid (HA) is also a fundamental polysaccharide in the extracellular matrix (ECM) that is indispensable for the homeostasis of cartilage tissue [20]. There are various effects of HA on chondrocyte viability, glycosaminoglycan synthesis, preservation of phenotypic stability, and cell adhesion and proliferation [21, 22]. On the other hand, as we know, subchondral bone is a tissue located deep within the OC unit. For bone regeneration, calcium phosphate compounds like hydroxyapatite (HAp) and β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) are the ceramics under investigation [23]. These ceramics support new bone formation, chemically bond with native bone, and enhance scaffold mechanical strength [24]. Both HAp and β-TCP have been widely studied in bone tissue engineering and are already used clinically, with applications in commercial bone graft substitutes and orthopedic implants [25, 26].

Recent advancements have focused on designing bilayer or multilayer scaffolds, which are more effective than mono-layer configurations for addressing OC defects. These scaffolds aim to simultaneously regenerate both cartilage and bone [27, 28]. These endeavors are essential in order to progress tissue engineering methodologies and attain favorable clinical outcomes without the requirement for extended therapeutic interventions or additional surgical procedures [11].

In this study, a bilayer biodegradable scaffold was developed, consisting of a COL/HAp hydrogel for the bone layer and a COL/CNC/HA hydrogel for the cartilage layer. Although type II COL is the primary component of hyaline cartilage and crucial for its structure and function, type I COL was selected for both layers due to its abundant availability, proven biocompatibility, and superior gel-forming ability—making it a well-established biomaterial for both cartilage and bone tissue engineering. Additionally, type I COL can support chondrogenic and osteogenic differentiation depending on incorporated bioactive cues and the surrounding microenvironment [29, 30]. The scaffold was lyophilized to create a porous structure and subsequently evaluated for its internal architecture, mechanical properties, and biocompatibility. In vitro biocompatibility was assessed using distinct bone and cartilage cell lines.

Materials and methods

Materials

Ethanol, glacial acetic acid (purity ≥99.5%), and pure formalin were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany). Furthermore, gel-like suspensions of CNCs (2.5 wt.% in water) were purchased from Nano Novin Polymer Co. (Iran) and HA was also purchased from Sigma Aldrich. HAp was synthesized from calcium nitrate tetrahydrate [Ca(NO3)2.4H2O, Sigma Aldrich] and diammonium hydrogen phosphate [(NH4)2HPO4, Sigma Aldrich] and ammonia (NH4OH). Fetal bovine serum (FBS), Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM/Ham’s F-12), penicillin-streptomycin, trypsin-EDTA (0.25%), phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), fluorescein diacetate (FDA), and propidium iodide (PI), MTT assay reagent and H&E staining solutions were also purchased from Sigma Aldrich (Germany). Glutaraldehyde (25% in water, electron microscopy grade) was obtained from Fujifilm Wako Pure Chemicals (Tokyo, Japan). MG-63 and C28/I2 cells were obtained from the National Cell Bank of Iran (NCBI), Pasteur Institute of Iran.

HAp nanoparticles were synthesized using the wet chemical method. At the same time, in another container, 7.92 g of ammonium phosphate [(NH4)2HPO4] was dissolved in 250 ml of deionized water while the temperature was kept at 40 °C. Following that, ammonium phosphate solution was added to the calcium nitrate solution. Collagen hydrogel preparation

The hydrogel was prepared from COL type I extracted from adult rat tails. Twenty tails were rinsed in 70% ethanol, then cut with a scalpel and placed in PBS. Tendons were cut off stretched, and gathered after each tail was flexed to break. The tendons were dissolved in 0.02 M acetic acid at 4 °C for 48 h while stirring and then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 20 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was freeze-dried and kept at −20 °C for further use. COL hydrogel was prepared by dissolving dry COL sponges in 0.02 M acetic acid (3.7 mg/mL) at 4 °C with stirring, following the protocol of Torabi et al. [29]. The solution was mixed with equal amount of 2X PBS, adjusted to pH 7.4 with 5 M NaOH, molded into wells of SPL 24-well plate, and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. Finally, the samples were slowly exposed to 66% of the required force that is necessary to reach the target thickness. In fact, to improve strength and cohesiveness, the hydrogel scaffolds were compressed with a plastic compression machine to remove excess water before being lyophilized in a freeze dryer (LD Plus Alpha, Germany) for further investigation.

Bilayer scaffold fabrication

To prepare the bilayer scaffold, the bone layer hydrogel, composed of COL and HAp (COL/HAp), was first prepared. For this, HAp powder was initially dispersed in PBS using a sonication device. The COL/HAp hydrogel was prepared following the COL hydrogel synthesis method described earlier. Specifically, the COL solution was mixed with the HAp solution (in PBS), neutralized by adding 5 M NaOH on ice, and then incubated at 37 °C for 1 h to form a gel (in 12-well plate), in accordance with the COL hydrogel protocol. The amount of HAp used was 10 wt.% relative to the dry weight of the bone layer.

The cartilage layer hydrogel was subsequently formed on top of the previously formed bone layer hydrogel. The cartilage layer (COL/CNC/HA) composed of COL, CNC (5% based on the layer dry weight), and HA (20% based on the layer dry weight). To prepare this layer, CNC was dispersed and homogenized in 0.02 M acetic acid using probe ultrasonication, after which COL was incorporated and allowed to dissolve under stirring at 4 °C. Following neutralization with 5 M NaOH, a PBS-HA solution was introduced and after mixing by stirring at 4 °C, the mixture was added to the already prepared COL/HAp hydrogel and incubated at 37 °C until gelation occurred. The height ratio of the cartilage to the bone layer was maintained at 3:1. The bilayer hydrogels underwent compression using a plastic compression machine. This process efficiently removed excess water from the hydrogel. Finally, the hydrogels were stored initially at −20 °C, followed by −80 °C, in preparation for lyophilization. Characterizations

Dynamic light Scattering (DLS)

Light scattered from a suspension can be measured for hydrodynamic particle size by observing random variations in its intensity. The average size of produced HAp nanoparticles was measured utilizing DLS analysis at 25 °C by HORIBA Scientific ZS-100 particle size analyzer.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) analysis

To study the microstructure and porosity of freeze-dried scaffolds, SEM images (TESCAN VEGA 3, 20 kV) were captured at various magnifications. Samples were coated with a 10 nm gold layer using the Quorum Q150R ES system. Scaffold pore sizes were measured using ImageJ software. FESEM (TESCAN MIRA4) was used to image HAp nanoparticles. EDS analysis (TESCAN MIRA4) provided elemental maps and the composition of calcium (Ca) and phosphorus (P) at the bilayer interface and in the synthetic HAp powder.

Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

FTIR spectroscopy was performed using an FTIR spectrometer (Tensor 27, Bruker, Germany) in transmission mode and within the range of 4000–400 cm−1. Prior to analysis, to remove any contamination or extraneous material, all samples were washed 3 times with distilled water, each wash lasting 2 min.

X-ray diffraction analysis (XRD)

XRD analysis was employed to investigate the crystal structure of the samples. The locations and intensities of the recorded diffraction peaks resemble a fingerprint of a specific crystalline phase. XRD of powder samples of HAp, CNC, and COL/HAp, COL/CNC nanocomposite scaffolds were collected by (ASENWARE, AW-XDM300) with 2θ scale and step size of 0.05 degrees, diffraction from 10 to 70° with a wavelength of 1.54184 Å. It is crucial to wash the scaffolds with distilled water before analysis to avoid the formation of extraneous peaks linked to chemicals and possible impurities.

Gelation time

The gelation time was determined using the vial inversion test, commonly referred to as the flow test. In a way that, after each hydrogel solution was neutralized, it was rapidly moved to a glass vial that had been warmed up to 37 °C in an incubator. Every 5 s, the vials’ flow condition was checked by tilting them. The gelation period is defined as the amount of time that the pre-gel precursor did not experience any movement. Each sample performed this process at least three times, and an overall gelation time was measured.

Degradation analysis

The degradation of hydrogels in a PBS environment serves as an effective and valuable approach to assessing their performance and behavior under conditions that mimic biological systems. Pre-weighed freeze-dried hydrogel scaffolds (W1) were immersed in PBS at 37 °C and pH = 7.4 for 1, 3, 7, 10, and 14 days to evaluate their degradability in vitro. Following each time span, the samples were taken out of the PBS and vacuum-dried. Mechanical properties

The mechanical strength of the scaffolds was assessed through compression testing conducted with a universal mechanical testing machine (Zwick z020, Germany) with a 1 kg load cell on the scaffolds. The scaffolds were punched and cut to have thickness of 1 cm and the diameter of 1.6 cm. A compressive load was incrementally applied until the sample thickness decreased to 80% of its original value, and the corresponding compressive strength was measured. The tests were performed at a speed of 2 mm/min. Each sample was tested in triplicate, and the mean values were documented.

In vitro cytocompatibility analysis

The cytocompatibility of the scaffolds was evaluated using two distinct cell lines: MG-63 (as a human osteoblastic model) and C28/I2 (as a human articular chondrocyte model). Both cell types were cultured in a complete culture medium consisting of DMEM/F12 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, under standard incubation conditions of 37 °C and 5% CO2. The culture medium was refreshed every two days.

Live/Dead and MTT assays

Both Live/Dead and MTT assays were performed to independently assess the cytocompatibility of each scaffold layer. MG-63 cells were cultured on COL and COL/HAp scaffolds, while C28/I2 cells were seeded on COL, COL/CNC, and COL/CNC/HA scaffolds.

Cell viability was evaluated using the Live/Dead assay. MG-63 cells were cultured on COL and COL/HAp scaffolds, while C28/I2 cells were assessed on COL, COL/CNC, and COL/CNC/HA scaffolds. Prior to cell seeding, scaffolds were punched to fit 48-well plates and sterilized via UV exposure for 20 min. After scaffold placement in wells, cells were seeded at a density of 20,000 cells (MG-63 or C28/I2) per scaffold. Following 1 and 7 days of culture, the cells were stained with fluorescein diacetate for live cells and propidium iodide for dead cells. After washing with PBS, fluorescence microscopy (Varian Cary Eclipse, Agilent Technologies, USA) distinguished live (green) and dead (red) cells.

The MTT assay was used to assess cell viability and proliferation on the scaffolds. The scaffolds were punched and cut to fit 96-well plates. Pre-sterilized scaffolds, seeded with 10,000 cell/well and cultured for 1 and 7 days. After incubation, the samples were washed with PBS, and 100 μL of MTT solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C for 4 h to allow formazan crystal formation. For each scaffold condition, five replicates were analyzed. The optical density (OD) at 570 nm was measured using an ELISA microplate reader (Epoch, USA). A control group (cells without scaffolds) was included for comparison. To determine relative cell viability, the OD values of scaffold groups were normalized to the control group at each time point, and results were expressed as a percentage of the control.

SEM cells adhesion

MG63 and C28/I2 cells were seeded onto COL/HAP and COL/CNC/HA scaffolds, respectively. Three days later, scaffolds were washed with PBS, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 2 h, and dried. Cell-cultured scaffolds were sectioned longitudinally, and SEM imaging was performed using a TESCAN VEGA 3 microscope after sputter coating with gold.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) Cell staining

H&E staining was performed on both bilayer and monolayer scaffolds. To prepare the bilayer scaffold for co-culturing cartilage and bone cells, it was punched to fit a 24-well plate and sterilized under UV light for 20 min. After placing the scaffolds into the wells, cells were seeded onto them. First, MG63 cells were cultured on the bone layer. After 8 h, the scaffold was flipped upside down, and C28/I2 cells were seeded onto the cartilage layer (Fig. 1). Approximately 80,000 C28/I2 cells and 20,000 MG63 cells were cultured on each respective layer. The higher cell number for C28/I2 cells was chosen to account for both the greater thickness of the cartilage layer and their slower proliferation rate compared to MG63 cells. This seeding strategy ensured sufficient cell coverage and improved visualization in subsequent histological staining. Monolayer COL/HAp and COL/CNC/HA scaffolds were also cultured with MG63 and C28/I2 cells, respectively.

On the first and seventh days of culture, the scaffolds were fixed in a 10% formaldehyde solution prepared in PBS to preserve their structural integrity and compositional characteristics. Following fixation, the scaffolds were embedded in paraffin to facilitate microscopic evaluation. Thin sections of 10 µm thickness were prepared using a microtome, and H&E staining was performed.

Statistical analyses

All variables were analyzed statistically using Prism software (version 9.0.0) and are reported as mean ± SD. Paired t-tests and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test were used to compare the statistical significance between sample means. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001 was considered as statistical significance.

Result and discussion

Evaluating size and structural properties of HAp

As shown in Fig. 2a, SEM images reveal that the nanoparticles are roughly spherical with an average diameter of 70 nm. DLS analysis of HAp indicates a particle size distribution ranging from 80 to 100 nm (Fig. 2c), aligning closely with SEM findings. As demonstrated in Fig. 2d, the EDS imaging and elemental mapping results of HAp nanoparticles reveal that the primary components are calcium and phosphorus with a ratio of Ca/P = 1.76, slightly higher than the theoretical stoichiometric value of 1.67 for pure HAp. The elevated Ca/P ratio may indicate the presence of secondary calcium phases (e.g., CaCO₃) or carbonate substitution (Type B), which is not uncommon in wet-chemically synthesized Hap [33, 34]. The observed deviation in Ca/P ratio may also reflect the inherent limitations of EDS as a semi-quantitative analytical technique. Microstructural and elemental analysis of scaffolds

Figure 3a, b show the surface views of the mono-layers (COL, COL/HAp, and COL/CNC/HA) at different magnifications, and the cross-sections of the bilayer scaffold, revealing a porous microstructure. Compression and lyophilization may affect pore size accuracy, as these processes alter the scaffold’s structure, with compression leading to tighter, denser pores [12, 21, 35]. SEM images reveal that the COL scaffold has a sponge-like structure with irregular and scattered porosity. The bone layer (COL/HAp) has a rough, granular surface. The incorporation of HAp into the COL matrix seems to have increased the size and decreased the number of pores, which is indicative of the creation of a pore structure similar to natural bone tissue that plays a crucial role in improving nutrient exchange as well as enhanced osteoconductivity [36–38]. The cartilage layer (COL/CNC/HA) is more fibrous and porous. The addition of CNC and HA to the cartilage layer appears to result in a more uniform and smaller pore structure similar to the natural cartilage environment and capable of supporting cartilage growth [13, 39]. As seen from Fig. 3b, which is a cross-sectional view of the bilayer scaffold, the two layers of the scaffold have interconnected pores for cell penetration, nutrient exchange, and fluid flow. Additionally, there are no obvious spaces at the interface between the COL/HAp and COL/CNC/HA layers, indicating that the bilayer scaffold has a continuous and well-integrated structure. This indicates strong physical adherence, most likely because the COL matrix is similar in both layers, allowing for interfacial bonding through hydrogen bonds and electrostatic interactions between components.

Figure 3c shows the average pore size of the scaffolds as measured using Fiji software based on surface SEM images of scaffolds. It should also be noted that there were a good consistent between porosity from surface images (Fig. 3a) and cross-section images (Fig. 3b). Pores were identified manually by thresholding the SEM images and selecting interconnected voids within the scaffold structure. For each scaffold type (COL, COL/HAp, and COL/CNC/HA), at least 50 randomly selected pores from three different SEM images were analyzed. The average pore size was found to be 15.78 ± 1.1 µm for COL, 23.86 ± 6.2 µm for COL/HAp, and 10.2 ± 3.4 µm for COL/CNC/HA. Although these values are smaller than the ideal physiological ranges reported for native osteochondral tissues (100–200 µm for cartilage and 300–450 µm for bone) [41, 42], the porous microstructure was sufficient to support cell infiltration, as confirmed in cell culture and H&E assessments (Figs. 7 and 8).

The elemental composition and distribution in the bilayer scaffold were analyzed through the EDS mapping technique. Figure 3d illustrates the distribution of major elements across the bilayer scaffold. The elemental distribution reveals a higher concentration of calcium and phosphorus in the bone layer. At the boundary between the upper (cartilage) and lower (bone) layers, there is a gradual shift in elemental concentration, with calcium and phosphorus progressively increasing from the cartilage layer to the bone layer. This continuous gradient suggests a stable interlayer connection that enhances adhesion and overall mechanical integrity [39].

FT-IR analyses

The FTIR spectrum of the bone layer scaffold, composed of COL and HAp, reveals characteristic functional group vibrations (Fig. 4a). For pure COL, a broad band at ~3305 cm−1 corresponds to O–H stretching, indicative of hydrogen bonds, while peaks at 1641 cm−1, 1549 cm−1, and 1237 cm−1 represent amide I (C = O stretching), amide II (N–H bending), and amide III (C–N stretching), respectively [43, 44]. The HAp spectrum exhibits prominent P–O stretching peaks at ~1022 cm−1 and ~1118 cm−1, symmetric phosphate (PO43−) stretching at 969 cm−1, and bending vibrations of O–H and phosphate groups between 600 and 560 cm−1 [13, 45–47]. In the Col/HAp composite, the O–H stretching region (3500–3000 cm−1) shows reduced intensity, suggesting hydrogen bond interactions between COL and HAp. Shifts in amide I, II, and III peaks further support these interactions. The presence of P–O peaks confirms the integration of HAp into the COL matrix, validating the composite’s formation [44, 48]. For the cartilage layer scaffold (Col/CNC/HA), CNC shows peaks (Fig. 4b) at 2700–2950 cm−1 (C–H stretching), 1600–1620 cm−1 (O–H bending), and 1250–900 cm−1 (C–O–C pyranose ring vibrations). The Col/CNC composite spectrum combines features of both constituents, including hydrogen bonding (~3300 cm−1) and COL amide I and II peaks (1650 and 1550 cm−1) [18, 49]. Adding HA reduces peak intensities, particularly in the 3400–3200 cm−1 range, due to enhanced hydrogen bonding [20]. X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The XRD patterns of HAp nanoparticles powder (Fig. 4c) show sharp peaks at 2θ values of ≈25.8°, 31.05°, 32.5°, 34.35°, 39.8°, 46.7° and 49.5° [37, 50]. These peaks indicate the highly crystalline nature of HAp, attributed to its regular and periodic atomic arrangement. A minor peak observed at 2θ ≈ 29.4° may be attributed to trace amounts of CaCO₃, which occasionally forms as a secondary product during wet chemical synthesis [51]. Additionally, a weak peak around 2θ ≈ 27.8° may overlap with characteristic reflections of octacalcium phosphate (OCP) [52]. The lack of multiple sharp and well-resolved diffraction peaks characteristic of OCP strongly supports that HAp is the predominant crystalline phase present in the synthesized sample. Nonetheless, it should be noted that the XRD measurement was performed within the 10°–70° range, and thus, possible signals from OCP at lower angles (4°–10°) may have been missed. The COL/HAp composite shows broad peaks corresponding to a range of 2θ = 20–25°, which is, possibly, due to reduced crystallinity, and more uniform dispersion of nanoparticles within the COL matrix. In fact, these broadened peaks indicate an increase in the distance between HAp layers, which is probably due to the interaction with the COL matrix. In the CNC pattern, a prominent peak at around 22.5° is observed, attributed to the (002) plane of cellulose Iβ [53]. Additionally, a secondary peak about 16° is due to the (110) plane of cellulose [54, 55]. Nevertheless, this peak is not shown in the COL/CNC composite (Fig. 4d), which could be attributed to the dispersion of CNC in the COL matrix, and the low amount of CNC in the composite.

Degradation assessment

Pure COL scaffolds lost 78% of their weight after 14 days while scaffolds containing HAp, CNC, CNC/HA, and the bilayer scaffold lost 23, 36, 57, and 49%, respectively. The incorporation of HAp into the COL matrix results in the formation of strong chemical and physical bonds among HAp and COL molecules. They decrease water permeability and hydrolytic degradation to confer enhanced structural integrity and lowered degradation [56]. Similarly, the addition of CNC in cartilage layer scaffolds fixes the matrix and controls degradation. Nevertheless, at high CNC concentrations, there may be a limit on further performance improvement due to saturation of binding sites and nanocrystal aggregation [16, 57]. As HA is hydrophilic, it enhances water absorption or hydration in COL/CNC/HA composites and accelerates hydrolytic degradation [46]. Consequently, this composite exhibits a higher degradation rate compared to COL/CNC scaffolds [17, 40]. The synergistic interaction of components of the bilayer scaffold is responsible for the degradation profile and its rate lies in between that of upper and lower layers. The lower layer, composed of COL and HAp, has a stable structure that resists breakdown, reducing overall weight loss. Inversely, the upper layer, containing COL, CNC, and HA, has a higher degradation rate on account of HA, which renders it more vulnerable to moisture and hydrolytic (Fig. 5a). Gelation time

COL forms gels naturally and rapidly at physiological temperatures (37 °C), typically within seconds to minuets [58]. In this study (Fig. 5b), COL exhibited a gelation time of 58 ± 3 s. The addition of HAp reduced gelation time to 31 ± 3 s (n = 3, *P < 0.05). This decrease is attributed to strong hydrogen bonding between HAp’s hydroxyl (-OH) groups and COL’s carbonyl (-C = O) and amine (-NH₂) groups, as well as ionic interactions between HAp’s Ca2+/PO43− ions and COL’s COO⁻/-NH₃⁺ groups. These interactions give an impetus to molecular crosslinking to get faster and more stable gel formation. COL/CNC decreased the gelation time to 29 ± 3 s, compared to 58 ± 3 s for pure COL. Besides that, in CNC, there are additional active sites that allow molecular distribution and changes in solution viscosity, so networks are more stable [59]. The gelation time reached 31 ± 4 s after the addition of HA. This occurrence is probably caused by HA’s dual behavior. HA has a high-water retention capacity and ability for viscosity enhancement, resulting in a delayed gelation process. On the contrary, its interactions with CNC can modulate the structural arrangement of the gel network, potentially affecting its overall organization and stability [20, 22, 60]. The effects of CNC and HA on gelation time were statistically significant (n = 3, *P < 0.05).

Mechanical analyzes

The mechanical properties of the scaffolds were evaluated using stress-strain curves (Fig. 5c) and Young’s modulus measurements (Fig. 5d). COL scaffolds exhibited maximum compressive stress of 202 kPa, reflecting their limited mechanical strength due to fibrillar slippage [18]. On the contrary, the compressive strength of scaffolds in COL/HAp (980 kPa), COL/CNC (440 kPa), COL/CNC/HA (420 kPa), and bilayer scaffold (710 kPa) are all significantly higher in comparison. The Young’s modulus of COL (100 ± 4 kPa), COL/HAp (350 ± 4 kPa), COL/CNC (200 ± 2 kPa), COL/CNCN/HA (180 ± 4 kPa), and bilayer (250 ± 4 kPa) scaffolds were also calculated. The addition of HAp mimics the inorganic phase of natural bone, increasing scaffold rigidity and yielding a higher Young’s modulus [38]. Although COL offers elasticity, uniform and homogeneous dispersion of HAp in the COL matrix resulted in better bone-like mechanical properties, and increased compressive strength and stiffness. Similarly, CNC serves as structural reinforcements within the COL matrix, significantly improving load-bearing capacity and resistance to compressive forces due to their organized structure and unique physical properties [61, 62]. The COL/CNC/HA scaffold’s reduced mechanical strength and Young’s modulus may be the result of HA’s hydrophilic properties, which may lower the structural density and increase the scaffold’s viscoelasticity. This, in turn, causes the scaffold’s mechanical behavior to transform from a hard and stiff structure to a softer and more flexible network [63]. The bilayer scaffolds, combining a COL/HAp bone layer and a COL/CNC/HA cartilage layer, achieve a balance of strength and flexibility. The lower layer contributes structural integrity and rigidity due to HAp’s mineral-like properties, while, the upper layer, comprising COL/CNC/HA, enhances the scaffold’s flexibility and elasticity. CNC serves as a structural enhancer, increasing bending resistance, while HA, known for its superior hydration properties and effective interaction with COL, improves elasticity and augments flexibility. This bilayer configuration enables the scaffold to achieve a harmonious balance of rigidity and flexibility making it a perfect option for bone and cartilage regeneration.

As shown in Fig. 6a, the growth of MG-63 cells on the COL/HAp scaffold was higher than on the COL groups. Conclusion

The results of this study broadly suggest that a bilayer scaffold with a porous structure and irregular distribution of large and small pores developed will work effectively by mimicking the natural characteristics of OC tissue. The pore size in this design gradually increases from the upper to the lower layer in the same way as that of the natural tissues. Not only does the HAp incorporation as a reinforcing agent accurately replicate bone architecture, but it also improves both mechanical strength and biological behavior such as cellular growth, viability, and adhesion. Meanwhile, the inclusion of CNC and HA in the cartilage layer leads to a synergistic enhancement of properties such as biocompatibility, strength, and elasticity. The combination of these two materials within the surface layers significantly aids in the uniform distribution of mechanical stresses and the improvement of the scaffold’s flexibility. As this composite scaffold design combines higher mechanical performance with improved reversibility, it holds promise to be a high-performance scaffolding design for OC tissue engineering applications. Furthermore, freeze-drying and compression procedures applied during scaffold preparation to remove excess hydrogel water and to improve scaffold strength increase the potential for scaffold in vivo application and prevent scaffold degradation over time.
