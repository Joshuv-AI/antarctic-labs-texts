Isolation and characterization of an antibiofilm bacteriophage against Klebsiella michiganensis and evaluation of its therapeutic potential in an insect larval gut colonization model
Authors: Jhuma Samanta, Sibnarayan Datta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBacteriophages (phages) have therapeutic potential against AMR bacteria. Recently, Klebsiella michiganensis (a member of the Klebsiella oxytoca complex) was discovered. This pathogen is associated with nosocomial infection and sepsis and has the ability to neutralize the β-lactam class of antibiotics. This study focused on detailed characterization of the phage vB_KleM_kp20 (kp20) and evaluation of its in vitro and in vivo efficacy against K. michiganensis.MethodsPhage kp20 was isolated from sewage water, purified, and characterized via standard methods, including adsorption assay, one-step growth curve, host range determination, pH/temperature stability, in vitro lysis kinetics, transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and genome sequencing. The therapeutic potential of kp20 was also evaluated through in vitro antibiofilm activity and in vivo decolonization in a mosquito larval model (Aedes albopictus).Resultskp20 showed narrow host specificity, a rapid adsorption rate (> 60% adsorption within 10 min), a short latency period (~ 10 min), a large burst size (~ 100 PFU per infected cell), and strong in vitro antibacterial activity at a low MOI (0.1). Phage kp20 retained infectivity over a range of temperatures (4–40 °C) and pH values (6–10). In the in vitro antibiofilm assay, a 39% reduction in biomass was observed at an MOI of 1. Transmission electron micrography revealed a myovirus morphology, and genetic analyses revealed an ~ 174 kb dsDNA genome (GenBank PP993148) harboring at least 267 CDSs and 2 tRNA genes. No AMR/toxin- or integration-related genes were detected. Analyses of the proteomic tree revealed the affinity of kp20 for the genus Slopekvirus under the Straboviridae family of viruses. Compared with no treatment, treatment with kp20 significantly reduced survival (70% vs. 40%; P < 0.001) and growth (2.1 ± 0.38 mm vs. 1.4 ± 0.17 mm; p < 0.0001) in the mosquito larval gut colonization model.ConclusionThe results of our study suggest promising therapeutic potential for phage kp20. Furthermore, we demonstrated that the mosquito larval gut colonization model could be utilized as a convenient pre-murine model to evaluate phage efficacy in vivo.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-025-04457-8.Keywords: Bacteriophages, Klebsiella michiganensis, Anti-Microbial resistance, Phage therapy

Introduction

Klebsiella michiganensis was originally isolated from a toothbrush holder from a residential setting in Michigan [1]. It is an important member of the K. oxytoca complex (KoC) and has been associated with emerging antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) nosocomial infections [2]. KoC is known to share several antimicrobial genetic mechanisms with K. pneumoniae [2]. Accordingly, K. michiganensis strains harbor genes encoding metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) and carbapenemase enzymes, which can hydrolyze the β-lactam group of antibiotics [3, 4]. Compared with that of K. pneumoniae, the epidemiology of KoC/K. michiganensis remains understudied [2].

The emergence and expansion of extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) and carbapenemase-producing Enterobacterales (CPE) pathogens have become major threats not only to patient prognosis but also to the selection of therapeutics [5]. Members of the bacterial genera Enterobacter sp., Klebsiella sp., and Escherichia sp [6, 7]. and certain opportunistic pathogens belonging to the genera Pseudomonas sp., Serratia sp., and Acinetobacter sp. harbor a variety of genes encoding ESBLs and carbapenemases [8]. Among these strains, several MBL-producing bacterial strains are resistant to ceftazidime/avibactam therapy, which is currently the last line of antibiotic-based therapy [9, 10]. Collectively, these concerns have compelled researchers worldwide to aggressively search for alternative antibacterial strategies to combat emerging MDR pathogens.

The prevailing AMR situation has rekindled the interest of researchers in bacteriophages (i.e., phages or bacteria-eating viruses) as potential alternatives to antibiotics [11]. Phages are the most abundant and diverse natural entities that are suggested to outnumber bacteria [12, 13]. Unlike antibiotics, phages have a relatively narrow host spectrum, owing to which they selectively target bacteria without affecting nontarget microbiota or eukaryotic cells [14]. Phages and their specific proteins (e.g., holins, endolysins, and spanins) are being explored as additives or alternatives to antibacterial therapies [2]. During the last decade, extensive research in the field of phage biology has led to the increasing use of phages in the treatment of challenging bacterial infections [15, 16]. Recently, the WHO has called for research-based evidence to establish the safety, efficacy, and feasibility of phage application and therapy for use across all the One Health sectors [11].

Previously, researchers characterized several phages against K. pneumoniae and demonstrated their efficacy in various animal models as well as in challenging clinical cases [17–20]. In contrast, a limited number of studies on phages against other species of Klebsiella, including K. michiganensis, are available in the literature [21]. With increasing knowledge of the clinical importance of this bacterium, efforts to isolate and characterize phages against K. michiganensis have gained momentum in recent years [21–26]. Although these studies reported the biological characteristics and in vitro antibiofilm activity of various phages against K. michiganensis, none have evaluated their efficacy in vivo.

With the rapid increase in phage research, the need for suitable animal models for in vivo evaluation of phage efficacy has also increased. Although murine models are the most suitable preclinical animal models, strict animal ethical protocols restrict their large-scale application for preliminary screening of potential phages. Therefore, to reduce the use of murine models, researchers have explored the practical utility of various other animals as pre-murine animal models for high-throughput screening of phages in vivo [18–20]. Among the various nonmurine models tested, the majority of researchers have conducted phage experiments on different insect larvae and recommended them as suitable models for this purpose. Although wax moth larvae (Galleria mellonella) have been widely used as pre-murine insect larval model in phage studies [17, 18, 20], the utility of other insect larvae (e.g., fruit flies, houseflies, cabbage looper, silkworm, and superworm) has also been demonstrated through scientific studies [27–31]. Recently, phage-mediated decolonization of gut bacteria (especially those belonging to the Enterobacteriaceae family) has been demonstrated in two different insect larval gut-colonization models (Galleria mellonella and Zophobas morio) as a novel strategy for evaluating phages in vivo [27, 32, 33]. Additionally, the efficacy of phage therapy in other insect larvae (Anopheles gambiae and Musca domestica) has also been shown recently [34, 35].

To focus our research efforts on the fight against MDR bacteria, we recently isolated a lytic phage against Klebsiella michiganensis and assessed its therapeutic suitability through in vitro assessment of its biological, genetic, and antibiofilm properties. We subsequently evaluated its therapeutic potential in an Aedes albopictus larval gut colonization model. In the present manuscript, we report and discuss the findings of our studies.

Materials and methods

Isolation and identification of host bacteria

Klebsiella michiganensis, used as the host bacterium in this study, was isolated from a domestic sewage water sample collected from Tezpur, Assam, India (26.63°N 92.8°E). Pure cultures were obtained via standard microbiological methods by plating on MacConkey agar, followed by subculturing representative colonies in MacConkey broth.

An aliquot of the bacterial broth culture was used for genomic DNA purification via a commercially available kit (HiPurA, HiMedia, India). The purified DNA was then subjected to PCR amplification of the 16S rRNA gene using the universal primers fD1 5’-agagtttgatcctggctcag-3’ and rP2 5’-acggctaccttgttacgactt-3’ [36]. PCR-mediated amplification was carried out with the following thermal profile: 94°C for 3 min, followed by 30 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 56°C for 30 s, and 72°C for 90 s, followed by a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. 16 S rRNA gene amplicons (~ 1.5 kb) were then sequenced bidirectionally at a commercial facility (Eurofins, Bengaluru, India). Sequence chromatograms were checked and edited using the computer program BioEdit v7.2.5 [37]. The final sequence was subjected to a BLASTn search for homologous sequences in the GenBank RefSeq database [38].

Antibiotic susceptibility test of bacterial isolates

The antibiotic susceptibility profile of the K. michiganensis isolate was determined via the Kirby–Bauer disc diffusion method on Mueller‒Hinton agar [39] with a commercially available antimicrobial susceptibility assay system (Hexa Disc, Himedia). The following antibiotic discs were used: ampicillin (AMP; 10 µg), amoxyclav (AMC; 30 µg), vancomycin (VA; 30 µg), tobramycin (TOB; 10 µg), cefotaxime (CTX; 30 µg), colistin acid (CL; 30 µg), rifampicin (RIF; 15 µg), and gentamicin (GEN; 10 µg). Ofloxacin (OF; 5 µg), ciprofloxacin (CIP; 5 µg), amikacin (AK; 30 µg), netillin (NET; 30 µg), nalidixic acid (NA; 30 µg), kanamycin (K; 30 µg), erythromycin (E; 15 µg), cotrimoxazole (COT; 25 µg), ceftazidime (CAZ; 30 µg), and tetracycline (TE; 30 µg). In brief, a 100 µl aliquot of exponentially grown culture (~ 1 × 108 CFU mL⁻¹) was evenly swabbed onto Mueller‒Hinton agar plates. The antibiotic discs were subsequently carefully placed on the agar surface, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16–18 h. For each antibiotic, the zone of inhibition around the disc was measured using an antibiotic zone scale (HiMedia), with each measurement performed in triplicate. The mean of three measurements was used for interpretation of results. Sensitive, intermediate and resistance to the drugs were determined by comparing the zone of inhibition to the relevant zone interpretative chart provided in the disc manufacturer’s manual [40]. The interpretative criteria in the manual were based on CLSI and EUCAST standards [41, 42].

Phage isolation, enrichment, and purification

A bacteriophage against the K. michiganensis isolate was isolated from the same sewage water sample. Briefly, debris, coarse detritus, and nonviral microorganisms were removed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was then added to a culture of host bacteria in the early exponential phase and incubated overnight in a shaker incubator maintained at 37 °C and 180 rpm. After incubation, the host-phage culture was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet the host cells and cell debris, followed by collection of supernatants containing phage particles. Initially, phage lytic potential was screened by the standard spot test method, followed by enumeration of phage titer by the double layer agar (DLA) overlay assay, in which diluted phage‒host mixtures were added to TSA (trypticase soy agar) molten soft top agar (0.3% w/v) and overlaid onto solidified TSA plates (1.5% w/v), as described elsewhere [43].

For detailed analyses of the phage, the enriched preparation was further purified following a previously described laboratory protocol [43] with minor modifications. Briefly, phage particles from a single plaque (DLA plate) were picked and suspended in TM buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM MgCl₂, pH 7.4). The phage suspension was centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to separate the host cell debris and passed through a 0.2 μm syringe filter (Minisart, Sartorius AG, Germany), followed by infection and overnight enrichment in exponential phase host cultures. The purification process was repeated five times to obtain a pure phage preparation. The supernatant from the final phage enrichment step was then subjected to ultracentrifugation at 30,000 rpm for 2 h at 4 °C. Pelleted phage particles were gently resuspended in TM buffer, and the titer was evaluated by DLA method. The purified phage was stored at 4 °C for further characterization.

Transmission electron microscopy

To observe the morphology of the isolated phage, 5 µl of a 1014 PFU mL⁻¹ purified phage suspension was applied onto a carbon-coated copper grid (300 mesh) and incubated for 1 min at room temperature. Excess suspension on the grid was removed by gentle blotting with filter paper. The grid was subsequently negatively stained with a drop of 2% (w/v ) uranyl acetate and air dried. Stained phage particles on the grid were visualized under a transmission electron microscope (FEI Tecnai G2 20 S-Twin, operating at 200 kV) at an academic research instrumentation facility (Sophisticated Analytical Instrumentation Centre, Tezpur University, Tezpur). The dimensions of the phage particles were measured with the help of ImageJ software version 1.53e [44].

Host range of the phage

The infectivity of the isolated phage against various bacterial genera/species was evaluated to determine its host range, which is an essential prerequisite for successful phage therapy. Briefly, 10 µL of a 10⁹ PFU mL⁻¹ phage suspension was spotted on a bacterial lawn. After overnight incubation at 37 °C, the plates were observed for clear zones that indicate phage activity due to their ability to lyse bacterial cells.

Adsorption assay

A phage adsorption assay was performed following a previously described method with slight modifications [43, 45]. Briefly, an exponentially growing host culture was mixed with the phage at an MOI of 0.001 and incubated at 37 °C. Aliquots from the above mixture were collected at 0, 5, 10, 15, 20, and 25-min post-infection intervals and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 5 min to pellet host cells with adsorbed phages. The titer of unadsorbed free phage in the culture supernatant was determined through DLA as mentioned previously. The experiments were performed in triplicate, and the results are presented as the mean value along with the standard deviation.

One-step growth curve

The latent period, rise period, plateau phase, and burst size of the phage were determined through a one-step growth curve following a previously described method [43, 45], with slight modifications. To ensure complete infection, the phage was mixed with the host bacterium at an MOI of 10 for 5 min at 37 °C to allow adsorption. Afterward, unadsorbed phages were removed by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 5 min. The resulting host cell pellets were resuspended in 50 ml of fresh TSB (trypticase soy broth) and incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 120 rpm. Free phages in the culture were sampled at 5 min intervals and enumerated by the DLA method. The experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the results are presented as the mean value along with the standard deviation.

Thermal and pH stability assays of phage

To determine the effects of various temperatures and pH values on the stability of the phage, 100 µL of phage suspension (~ 107 PFU mL⁻¹) was incubated in 900 µL of TM buffer at different temperatures (−20 °C, 4 °C, 25 °C, 37 °C, 40 °C, 50 °C, 60 °C, and 70 °C) and variable pH values (pH 3.0 - pH 12.0). After 2 h of incubation, the stability of the phage was determined by the DLA method. The percentage of residual infective phages under different conditions was calculated by comparing the initial and post incubation PFU. Phage particles stored in TM buffer (pH 7.4) at 4 °C were used as a control [46]. The assays were performed in triplicate for each temperature and pH condition.

Phage sensitivity to chloroform

Chloroform sensitivity was studied to determine whether the phage contained lipids (enveloped phages). Chloroform sensitivity was assessed as described previously [47], with slight modifications. Briefly, phage suspensions were mixed with 1% chloroform, vigorously shaken, and then incubated at 4 °C overnight. The mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min, and the phage titer was calculated via the DLA method. The experiment was performed in triplicate.

In vitro bacteriolytic activity of phage

The in vitro bacteriolytic activity of the phage was studied following a previous study [48]. Briefly, 180 µL of exponentially growing host cells (in TSB) were mixed with 20 µL of phage suspension at different MOIs (10, 1, 0.1, 0.01) in a 96-well microplate, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h inside a temperature-controlled programmable multimode plate reader (Varioskan LUX, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Wells containing only bacterial culture (without phage) served as positive controls, whereas sterile broth served as a negative control. Changes in absorbance over time were automatically recorded at intervals of 10 min, analyzed, and plotted by the accompanying software (SkanIt™, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The experiment was conducted in triplicate.

Phage-mediated biofilm disruption assay

A biofilm assay was performed following a previously described method with minor modifications to determine the potential of phages to disrupt biofilms [26, 43, 49]. For biofilm formation, K. michiganensis was cultured overnight in 5 mL of tryptone soy broth supplemented with 1% glucose. Subsequently, ~108 cells were added to each well of a 24-well plate containing 1 mL of fresh TSB broth. The plate was incubated at 37 °C for 72 h without shaking, allowing the development of biofilms. After biofilm formation, planktonic cells were carefully removed, and 1 mL aliquots of phage suspension, serially diluted in TM buffer, were added to each well at different multiplicities of infection (MOIs) of 1.0, 0.1, and 0.01. The plate was then incubated for 12 h. Afterward, the supernatant was aspirated carefully, ensuring that the biofilm remained undisturbed, followed by washing the biofilms three times with 1 mL of 1x PBS (phosphate-buffered saline) to remove unattached cells. The biofilm was allowed to dry in air for 5 min prior to the quantification assay. TSB inoculated with K. michiganensis without phage served as a positive control, whereas uninoculated TSB broth was used as a negative control.

The quantification of adhered biofilms was accomplished through staining with crystal violet (CV) dye (Loba Chemie, India) following established protocols with minor modifications [24, 43]. In this approach, 1 mL of 1% (w/v) CV solution was added to each well and allowed to stain for 20 min at room temperature, followed by washing three times with 1X PBS buffer to remove excess CV. The CV-stained biofilm was solubilized by adding 1 mL of 33% (v/v) glacial acetic acid solution to each well and left to incubate for 20 min. After solubilization, the biofilm load was estimated by measuring the optical density of the extracted crystal violet at 540 nm [24] in a UV‒visible spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). The absorbance of both the control and test samples was measured in triplicate. The percentage of biofilm disruption was calculated according to a previous method [50].

Phage nucleic acid extraction, genome sequencing, and analyses

A purified phage stock (~ 2 × 1015 PFU mL−1) was subjected to DNase I (Sigma, St. Louis, USA) and RNase A (Himedia) digestion for 30 min at 37 °C to remove host-associated nucleic acids. Following inactivation of the nucleases, the phage suspension was treated with proteinase K at 56 °C for 20 min. The phage genome was subsequently extracted with the help of a commercially available viral nucleic acid purification kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The quantity and quality of the phage nucleic acid preparation were evaluated via agarose gel electrophoresis followed by fluorimetry (Qubit, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The nature of the viral nucleic acid was examined by incubating 500 ng of the nucleic acid with DNase I and RNase A, followed by visualization of the digestion patterns on agarose gels. The purified virus-associated nucleic acid was subsequently used for library preparation and sequencing on a long-read sequencing platform (MinION, Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK) following the manufacturer’s instructions. QC-passed reads obtained after sequencing were subjected to de novo assembly via the computer program Flye v2.9.5 [51] implemented on the CPT Galaxy server [52].

The resulting single contig was then subjected to a BLASTn homology search [38]. Analyses of various features of the genome, including gene annotation, were subsequently performed via the PHANOTATE computer program and the PRODIGAL gene caller implemented in the CPT Galaxy server [53]. A phage proteomic tree and genome sequence comparison alignments were generated via the ViPTree web server [54, 55]. PhageScope and PHASTER web servers were also used for determining other features of the phage genome [56, 57].

For reconstruction of evolutionary history, phage genome sequences along with reference sequences were aligned using the online MAFFT v7 program [58]. Evolutionary divergence among the sequences was computed by the maximum composite likelihood (MCL) model, and evolutionary history was inferred by the neighbor‒joining method. Bootstrap tests (1000 replicates) were performed to evaluate the reliability of clustering among the sequences. All the molecular evolutionary analyses were performed using the MEGA12 program v12.0.9 [59].

Evaluation of the gut bacteria decolonizing effect of the phage in a mosquito larval gut colonization model (Ae. albopictus)

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic potential of the isolated phage, we conducted experiments utilizing a mosquito larval model (Ae. albopictus). Initially, axenic larvae (microbe-free) were generated and maintained following a laboratory-optimized method described previously [60, 61]. For direct visualization of the bacterial cells within the larval gut, we labeled K. michiganensis cells with a fluorescent live bacterial cell stain (BactoView™ Live Green, Biotium, Fremont, USA). In brief, 1 mL (~ 108 CFU mL⁻¹) of fresh bacterial (K. michiganensis) culture was centrifuged, followed by resuspension of the cell pellet in fresh PBS (pH 7.4) buffer. The bacterial cells were then labeled for 30 min, following the manufacturer’s protocol. Excess label was removed by centrifuging the labeled bacterial cell suspension, followed by resuspension of the bacterial cell pellet in fresh PBS buffer. A fluorescently labeled bacterial cell suspension was then added (106 CFU mL−1) to culture plates containing axenic larvae to generate gnotobiotic larvae with bacteria colonized in their guts. The labeled bacteria were allowed to colonize the larval gut for 1 h. Stable bacterial colonization was confirmed by visualizing an intense fluorescent signal, which was specifically restricted to the anterior midgut of the larvae. K. michiganensis-colonized larvae were subsequently equally distributed into three subsets in different culture plates , each containing ~ 100 larvae. In one of the wells, the phage suspension was added (MOI of 1.0); another plate received antibiotic treatment (kanamycin at 50 µg mL−1) as a standard therapy control; and the third plate received an equal volume of sterile TM buffer (phage buffer) as a mock control. After overnight treatment (~ 16 h), 10–12 larvae from each of these experimental groups were examined, and images were acquired and processed via an upright fluorescence microscope and accompanying CellSens v2.1 imaging software (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Subsequently, ~ 50 larvae from each experimental group were gently washed three times with sterile distilled water and collected in fresh microcentrifuge tubes. After being euthanized, the larvae were ground in sterile PBS using micropestles. Larval lysates were then clarified by centrifugation, and the supernatants were collected in fresh tubes. A 100 µL aliquot of the supernatant was cultured on MacConkey agar plates. MacConkey-agar plates inoculated with lysate supernatants were incubated at 37 °C overnight.

In parallel, 200 µL of clarified larval lysate from each test group was processed for purification of total DNA using a commercially available kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer’s protocol. qPCR was performed to detect and quantify K. michiganensis DNA relative to the mosquito actin gene (housekeeping gene) using a hot-start SYBR Green qPCR mastermix (Bio-Rad, California, USA). For the qPCR assay, an in-house designed primer pair (AeF6 5’-atgtgaaagccccgggctcaacctgggaattg-3’ and Bac-Rev 5’-acatctcacgacacgagctgacgac-3’) generating an ~ 483 bp amplicon from the bacterial 16 S rRNA gene was used. Bacterial DNA-specific signals were normalized with reference to mosquito actin gene amplicon signals generated by the primer pair described previously (Act-2 F 5’-atggtcggyatgggncagaaggactc-3’ and Act-8R 5’-gattccatacccaggaaggadgg-3’) [62]. qPCR was performed; data was collected and analyzed via the CFX96 Real-Time PCR detection system and accompanying CFX Maestro™ 1.0 v4.0 software (Bio-Rad).

Survival and growth were assessed in the remaining larvae in each experimental group two days after treatment.

Statistical analysis

The data were initially compiled in a spreadsheet and subsequently analyzed via GraphPad Prism. The means of at least three experimental repeats were calculated for the CFU and PFU values, with error bars representing the standard deviation. Statistical analyses were performed via one-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. Differences at various significance levels—p < 0.01, p < 0.001, and p < 0.0001—are denoted in the figures by **, ***, and ****, respectively. All presented data are based on at least three replicates (n ≥ 3).

Results

Antibiotic susceptibility of K. michiganensis

The bacterial isolates were obtained from sewage water and exhibited typical characteristics of the genus Klebsiella, forming pink, hypermucoviscous colonies on MacConkey agar. Furthermore, molecular identification of this isolate was performed via 16 S rRNA gene sequencing and phylogenetic analyses, which confirmed that the bacterial isolate was a strain of K. michiganensis. The strain was resistant to multiple antibiotics, including vancomycin (VA), rifampicin (RIF), colistin (CL), and ampicillin (AMP). The MAR index was 0.2, suggesting that the bacteria had limited exposure to antibiotics.

Morphological properties of phage plaques and virions

Spot tests and DLA conducted with the isolated phage and K. michiganensis demonstrated the lytic activity of the phage. The plaques formed by the phage in the DLA assay appeared circular, measuring approximately 6.5 ± 3.1 mm, transparent, with a halo encircling a clearer plaque center (Fig. 1 A). TEM images revealed that the virion structure was composed of head (116.68 ± 4.1 nm × 87.10 ± 1.26 nm) and tail (108.81 ± 3.1 nm) structures (Fig. 1B). These structural features of the isolated virion revealed that the phage is morphologically a myovirus affiliated with the recently defined class Caudoviricetes of tailed viruses of bacteria and archaea [63]. Accordingly, the isolated phage was named vB_KleM_kp20 (kp20), following a recently recommended guideline for the nomenclature of phages [64].

Determination of phage host range

The host range of phage kp20 was examined against bacterial strains, including Pseudomonas paraeruginosa, Escherichia coli, and Klebsiella pneumoniae, obtained from the Microbial Type Culture Collection (MTCC, Institute of Microbial Technology, Chandigarh, India). Additionally, the phage host range was also evaluated against Bacillus cereus, Lysinibacillus sp., Aeromonas hydrophila, Microbacterium paraoxydans, and Leucobacter coleopterorum, which were isolated and identified via 16 S rRNA gene sequencing in the laboratory. Phage kp20 was found to be highly host specific, as it did not lyse K. pneumoniae or other bacteria (Table 1).

Phage life cycle

In the phage adsorption assay, 60% of the phage kp20 adsorbed to its host within 10 min, and 87% adsorption was recorded after 25 min (Fig. 2 A). In the one-step growth curve, a latent period of approximately 10 min was observed, with a rise period of approximately 20 min (Fig. 2B). The burst size was calculated to be approximately 100 phages per infected cell (Fig. 2B).

Influence of temperature and pH on phage viability

Phage tolerance to different temperature and pH conditions was studied to assess its stability and retention of infectivity under diverse environmental settings based on plaque-forming units (PFUs). Phage kp20 was found to be considerably stable (≥ 80% residual phage infectivity) between 4 and 40 °C. Approximately 75% of the phage particles retained their infectivity at 50 °C, whereas the viability of the phages decreased drastically above 60 °C (Fig. 3 A).

Phages were incubated for 2 h under different temperatures. B pH stability profile of phage kp20 assessed across a range of pH values. Phages were incubated for 2 h under different temperatures. Data (A-B) represent mean ± standard deviation (SD) from three independent experiments

On the other hand, phage kp20 was highly stable at pH 7 and 8 and relatively stable (≥ 80% residual phage infectivity) at pH 6 and 10. A substantial reduction in residual phage infectivity (≤ 60%) was observed after incubation at pH 4 and 11. In contrast, ≥ 80% of kp20 lost infectivity at extreme pH values of 3 and 12 (Fig. 3B).

Chloroform sensitivity test

Compared with no treatment, treatment with 1% chloroform did not significantly affect the infectivity of phage kp20 (p = 0.1230), suggesting that phage kp20 does not have a lipid envelope.

In vitro kinetics assay of the phage

The bacteriostatic activity of the isolated phage against the host strain was evaluated at different MOIs in comparison with that of uninfected host cells as control. Phage-infected bacterial cultures showed arrested growth due to lytic activity at an MOI of 0.1 and above for up to 24 h (Fig. 4).

Features of the phage genome

A single contig of 174.032 kb length was obtained after assembly of the QC-passed sequence reads, with 41.94% GC content. In terms of completeness, the genome assembly was reported as ‘high-quality’ and ‘intact’ (score of 150) in the PhageScope and PHASTER analyses, respectively. The lifestyle of the phage was reported to be ‘virulent’ in the PhageScope analysis. No host-associated gene, AMR gene, or anti-CRISPR protein was detected in the present phage genome. At least 2 tRNA sequences (tRNA-Met, tRNA-Leu) were detected in the genome. The phage genome was found to encode at least 267 CDSs, of which 152 were predicted as hypothetical proteins/unknown proteins and 115 were identified as functional proteins based on their homology with other phage genome sequences available in the GenBank/phage protein databases (Supplementary Table S1). The sense strand encoded 121 (45.3%) ORFs, whereas the complement strand encoded 146 (54.7%) ORFs. Functionally associated ORFs were predicted to encode proteins involved in infection, DNA replication and modification, virion structure, assembly and packaging, immunity, host lysis, etc.

A BLASTn homology search revealed high similarity (up to 98.91% nucleotide identity over 98% query coverage) to Klebsiella phage genome sequences belonging to the genus Slopekvirus (family Strabdoviridae, class Caudoviricetes). The top hit GenBank reference phage genomes that matched the kp20 genome sequence included Klebsiella phage Matisse (NC_028750, USA), Miro (NC_041981, USA), PMBT1 (NC_042138, Germany), KP27 (NC_020080, Poland), KP15 (NC_014036, Poland), PKO111 (NC_031095, South Korea), Marfa (NC_048798, USA). In the phage proteome-based phylogenetic tree, the present phage genome clustered with phage proteomes belonging to the genus Slopekvirus of the family Strabovoridae (Fig. 5 A and B). The morphology of the isolated phage particles visualized via TEM corroborated with these findings.

Effects of phage kp20 on biofilm disruption

The phage kp20 showed the potential ability to disrupt preformed biofilms produced by K. michiganensis. As illustrated in the figure (Fig. 6), coculturing phage and bacteria at different multiplicities of infection (MOIs) resulted in a marked increase in biofilm disruption compared with bacterial cultures alone. The extent of disruption varied according to the MOI in a dose-dependent manner. In the coculture setup at an MOI of 1.0, there was a significant (p < 0.0001) loss of biomass (approximately 39%) after 12 h. In contrast, at MOIs of 0.1 and 0.01, the reductions in biomass were 15% and 8%, respectively (p < 0.001), compared with those of the nonphage-treated control.

In vivo evaluation of phage-mediated gut decolonization of K. michiganensis

Experiments conducted in a mosquito larval model demonstrated the potential of the isolated phage to decolonize K. michiganensis from the larval gut. An intense fluorescent signal in the AMG of untreated larvae demonstrated gut colonization of labeled bacteria, whereas no fluorescent signal was observed in the larval gut after treatment with either phage or kanamycin (Fig. 7 A). When the larval lysates were cultured on MacConkey agar plates, there was a significant difference in the abundance of bacteria between the untreated and treated groups of larvae (Fig. 7B C). These results were further confirmed through qPCR-based relative quantification of bacterial DNA in different experimental groups (Fig. 7D). The efficiency of the phage in gut decolonization was comparable to that of antibiotic treatment. Since the survival and growth of mosquito larvae are directly dependent upon the presence of gut-colonizing bacteria, we examined these two parameters in posttreatment larvae as additional indicators of phage-mediated bacterial decolonization from the larval gut. Compared with that of larvae growing with gut-colonized K. michiganensis, the percent survival of phage-treated gut-decolonized larvae was significantly lower (70% vs. 40%, p < 0.001). On the other hand, larval survival was lowest (2%) in the antibiotic-treated group (Fig. 7E). Additionally, the growth of the surviving larvae in the untreated and phage-treated groups also differed significantly (p< 0.0001), indicating the efficacy of the phage in gut decolonization in vivo (Fig. 7 F).

Discussion

Owing to their increasing resistance to carbapenems, several CPEs have been classified as ‘critical group’ pathogens on the WHO Bacterial Priority Pathogen List [65]. Among them, carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae has risen to the 1st rank in the priority list 2024 because of the rapid increase in morbidity and mortality associated with this bacterium. MDR bacteria belonging to the order Enterobacterales (e.g., Klebsiella, Escherichia, etc.) colonize the gut and act as reservoirs of antibiotic-resistant pathogens [66]. Such colonization is associated with an increased risk of transmission, often leading to severe outcomes such as sepsis, pneumonia, and urinary tract infections [66, 67]. Bacteriophage-mediated selective elimination of gut-associated MDR pathogens has been suggested as a practical approach to reduce the burden of such infections [66].

The application of phages as therapeutics against various drug-resistant bacteria has emerged as a potential alternative to antibiotics in recent years [15]. In the present study, we reported the isolation and characterization of a lytic phage, kp20, against K. michiganensis. The phage kp20 exhibited strong lytic activity against K. michiganensis but did not infect the other bacterial strains tested in this study, indicating a relatively narrow host range. While narrow host specificity can limit its broad-spectrum applications, it also reduces the risk of off-target effects on beneficial microbiota. Moreover, considering the frequent misidentification of K. michiganensis as K. oxytoca, kp20 could be included in phage cocktails to expand their clinical relevance.

The biological properties and stability profile of the phage kp20 suggest its suitability for practical applications, including therapeutics, biocontrol, and other environmental applications [68]. Detailed analyses of the phage kp20 genome and proteome revealed its phylogenetic affiliation with the genus Slopekvirus of the virus family Strabdoviridae. Phages belonging to the genus Slopekvirus are known to have highly conserved genomes, and other members of this phage family have therapeutic potential [25]. Phage kp20 shares the highest coverage and maximum sequence identity with the species Slopekvirus matisse, suggesting that kp20 is its variant, as per the ICTV-recommended criterion for species demarcation [64]. In addition to genes associated with structure, genome replication, transcription, viral packaging, etc., genes encoding proteins associated with bacterial cell lysis, such as holin, endolysin, and spanin, were also detected in the kp20 genome. Nonetheless, genetic regions encoding possible toxins or antibiotic resistance mechanism-related proteins were not detected in the kp20 genome.

Like several other species of the genus Klebsiella, K. michiganensis can establish biofilms, making standard antibiotic therapy extremely challenging [14]. Certain Klebsiella phages are known to encode depolymerase enzymes that degrade extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix components, leading to disruption of biofilms and facilitating phage penetration [69, 70]. Owing to these properties, phages/phage-derived enzymes alone or in combination with antibiotics are increasingly considered potential therapeutics for the treatment of various biofilm-producing bacterial infections [56, 69, 71]. Although most of the Klebsiella michiganensis phages characterized thus far have shown antibiofilm activity, the depolymerase-encoding genetic region can be detected in only some of them [24–26, 72]. The phage kp20 genome also lacks depolymerase but encodes a baseplate-associated tail lysozyme, which is suggested to have high peptidoglycan-degrading ability [73]. In addition to their direct antibiofilm activity, phage infection-associated stress may also induce bacterial enzymes that disturb biofilm community structures [74]. We further assume that some regions of the genome with unassigned activity (hypothetical genes) might contribute to the antibiofilm activity of phage kp20.

With the increasing interest in phage therapy, preclinical evaluation of candidate phage(s) in suitable animal models has become indispensable [75]. However, large-scale use of conventional murine models during the preliminary phase of phage screening is not practically feasible because of the costs and strict ethical concerns associated with these animal models [17–20]. Therefore, to reduce the use of murine models, researchers have resorted to the use of various invertebrate and lower vertebrate animals as cost-effective and ethically sustainable pre-murine models for in vivo evaluation of phages [17–20]. Among the different animals tested thus far, insect larvae offer convenient pre-murine model systems for in vivo high-throughput assessment of phages because of their simple physiological processes, robust immune systems, and nonrequirement of strict ethical protocols [17, 20]. While the wax moth larvae (Galleria mellonella) model has been frequently used in phage studies [17–20, 76], other insect larvae (e.g., fruit fly, housefly, cabbage looper, silkworm, superworm, etc.) have also been shown to be useful as experimental models for phage studies [27, 30–32, 34].

Previously, researchers used systemic infection-based insect larval models (primarily wax moths) for phage evaluation [17–20]. Very recently, Mirza and colleagues established a bacterial gut colonization model of G. mellonella and demonstrated its utility in evaluating the efficacy of phage treatment in gut-associated Enterobacteriaceae (K. pneumoniae and E. coli) infections [66]. Eddoubaji and colleagues [27, 32] also established another gut colonization model of Zophobas morio larvae (superworm) and demonstrated its utility in the in vivo evaluation of phages against MDR E. coli. These studies demonstrated phage-mediated decolonization of gut-colonizing bacteria as a novel approach for rapid evaluation of phage efficacy for proof-of-concept investigations [27, 32, 66]. Additionally, the efficacy of phages in modulating complex gut microbiota has also been demonstrated in mosquito and housefly larvae in two distinct biocontrol studies [34, 35], suggesting that other insect larval gut colonization models can also be used for evaluating phage efficacy in vivo.

Taking a cue from the previous studies, we attempted to evaluate the efficacy of phage kp20 in a mosquito larva (Ae. albopictus) gut colonization model. Mosquito larvae present certain characteristic features that make them a prospective pre-murine model. Mosquito larvae have a short life cycle; eggs are relatively easy to collect, sturdy, can be stored for longer durations and can withstand chemical sterilization procedures, larvae have simple dietary requirements, and are easy to rear, maintain, and manipulate under laboratory conditions. Extensive information is available on their physiological, biochemical, microbiological, and genetic aspects. Mosquito larvae are also known to harbor complex microbiota and are therefore tolerant to experimental colonization by various human pathogens, including Pseudomonas sp., Klebsiella sp., and Escherichia sp. Mosquitoes are also known to have complex and highly evolved immune systems, which are stronger during the larval stages [77, 78]. As they are aquatic in nature, experimental materials (e.g., bacteria, phages, etc.) can be introduced into mosquito larvae simply through the addition of these materials to rearing water. Unlike other insect larval models, mosquito larvae do not need stress-triggering methods such as force-feeding or injection for the introduction of test materials. Additionally, owing to their translucent body, mosquito larvae allow easy tracking of appropriately labeled experimental materials, enabling live localization studies. Furthermore, in the mosquito larva gut-colonization model, the characteristic ionic microenvironment (pH >9) of the mosquito larva gut [79] allows evaluation of phage activity, stability, and antibacterial effects under extreme pH conditions in vivo. Additionally, we and other researchers have shown that growth and development in mosquito larvae are reciprocal to midgut colonization by certain bacterial species (including various human pathogenic bacteria), which permits quantitative evaluation of phage treatment of these animals [35, 60, 80]. Therefore, the mosquito larval model may prove useful as an alternative, low-cost, easy-to-manage pre-murine animal model in phage therapy studies, especially those focused on enterobacterial infections. However, further research is needed to reveal the true potential of this animal model. Although the choice of an animal experimental model should be based on the site of infection/colonization of the pathogen being studied, our results endorse the mosquito larvae model as an alternative for pre-mammalian evaluation of phage treatment, especially for gut-colonizing bacteria.

We acknowledge that one of the limitations of the present study is that we could not test the lytic potential of the phage kp20 in clinical Klebsiella spp. isolates due to the requirement of ethical approvals. However, we plan to conduct further studies, which will include evaluation of kp20 lytic activity against clinical isolates.

Conclusion

In conclusion, in the present study, we report the isolation and detailed biological and genetic characterization of a bacteriophage against K. michiganensis that has potent bacteriolytic and antibiofilm activity, short latency, high burst size, and good environmental stability while lacking virulence/antibiotic resistance and lysogenic elements in its genome. We also demonstrated the efficacy of this phage in a rather nonconventional in vivo insect larval model. We acknowledge that evaluation by other researchers is needed to validate this model for antimicrobial efficacy.
