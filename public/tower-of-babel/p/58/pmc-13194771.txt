Investigating the association between marital status and survival outcomes in patients with follicular thyroid carcinoma: a population-based analysis
Authors: Zhiying Yao, Rongbing Jiang, Rongrong Dai, Qingquan Chen, Shangbin Huang, Rupin Chen, Honggeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFollicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) represents a distinct subtype of thyroid malignancy; however, the prognostic significance of marital status, a recognized social determinant in various other cancers, remains inadequately characterized within this specific patient population. This retrospective cohort study leveraged data from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database to explore the relationship between marital status and survival outcomes among 7954 patients diagnosed with FTC from 2004 to 2015. Patients were classified as either married or unmarried, and overall survival (OS) as well as cancer-specific survival (CSS) were assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis, log-rank tests, and Cox proportional hazards models. To address potential confounding variables, propensity score matching (PSM) was utilized, and further subgroup analyses were conducted based on age and sex. Prior to and following PSM, unmarried patients exhibited significantly inferior OS and CSS compared to their married counterparts (all P < 0.05). After PSM, unmarried status was found to be independently associated with poorer OS (HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.34–1.74, P < 0.001) and CSS (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.23–2.03, P < 0.001) after multivariate adjustment. A significant interaction between marital status and age was identified (P for interaction = 0.02), with the beneficial effects of married status on survival outcomes being particularly pronounced among patients aged 65 years and older (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.48–0.83), while no significant interaction was noted with respect to sex. Introduction

Follicular thyroid carcinoma (FTC) is a malignant neoplasm arising from the follicular cells of the thyroid gland. It is categorized as a differentiated thyroid carcinoma, with a notable increase in its global incidence over the past four decades1. Alongside papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), FTC is classified within the spectrum of differentiated thyroid carcinomas. Although FTC represents a relatively minor proportion of these carcinomas, approximately 5%, extant literature indicates a greater propensity for recurrence and metastasis in FTC compared to PTC. This phenomenon can be attributed to its distinctive biological traits, which include a heightened capacity for vascular invasion and the ability to penetrate the thyroid capsule. As a result, FTC is associated with increased invasiveness and mortality rates, thereby constituting a considerable public health concern2,3.

FTC is a multifactorial disease shaped by a confluence of environmental, physiological, immunological, and genetic factors. Established risk factors identified in prior studies include advanced age, genetic predisposition, iodine deficiency, exposure to radiation, and a familial history of FTC4,5. Furthermore, additional variables warrant consideration in the exploration of their association with FTC, such as gender, dietary inflammatory index, socio-economic status, tumor size, vascular invasion, extra-thyroidal extension (ETE), widespread infiltration (WI), cervical lymph node metastasis (CLNM), and distant metastasis (DM)1,6,7. Recently, there has been a growing focus on psychosocial determinants in the context of health and disease. Among these, marital status has identified as a social factor influencing cancer survival outcomes8. Research conducted by Mao Y et al. suggests that marital status may function as an independent prognostic indicator for cancer-specific survival (CSS)9. Similarly, investigations in other domains of oncology have demonstrated that marital status can independently correlate with enhanced survival rates across various cancer types10,11. Nonetheless, there remains a notable deficiency in systematic data analyses with adequate sample sizes to comprehensively assess the effect of marital status on survival among patients with FTC.

This study was conducted to address existing gaps in the literature by systematically examining the influence of marital status on survival outcomes in FTC, a specific subtype that is frequently overlooked in aggregated analyses. To enhance the robustness of the association estimate and mitigate confounding variables, we employed propensity score matching (PSM). Additionally, we aimed to investigate whether this association is moderated by demographic and clinical factors, including age and gender, through stratified interaction analyses.

Materials and methods

Patient selection from SEER database

FTC patient data were obtained from the National Cancer Institute’s Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) program. The SEER project collects demographic information, cancer diagnosis details, and outcomes from 18 cancer registries across the United States, providing data coverage for approximately 28% of the U.S. population. It is important to note that the SEER database does not contain any identifiable personal information and is publicly available for cancer epidemiology studies. This particular study utilized data from the SEER-Stat program for analysis (version 8.3.5).

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) patients diagnosed with primary cancer between 2004 and 2015, and (2) patients with a histological diagnosis of FTC (codes 8290, 8330, 8331, 8332, and 8335). The exclusion criteria included: (1) patients below the age of 20, (2) patients with incomplete clinical features and treatment records, (3) patients with incomplete demographic information, (4) patients who were lost to follow-up, had an unknown survival time, or survived less than 1 month, and (5) patients with unknown cause of death. The detailed study process is depicted in Fig. 1. Ultimately, a total of 7954 eligible patients were screened and analyzed based on the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Study variables

The study variables included age at diagnosis, gender, race, marital status, tumor stage, surgery, months of overall survival, and patient vital status. Age was categorized into three groups: <35 years, 35–65 years, and ≥ 65 years. This stratification was based on clinical relevance, considering distinct prognostic profiles, life stages, and social support needs across these age ranges12,13. Race was divided into three groups: white, black, and others (including Asian or Pacific Islander, American Indian/Alaska Native). Marital status was classified as married or unmarried (divorced, separated, widowed, single (never married), unmarried, or domestic partner). Thyroid surgery was grouped into two categories: with or without surgery. For tumor (T), lymph node (N), and metastatic (M) status, TNM classification of thyroid carcinoma, sixth edition, was used for assessment. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) of all eligible patients were analyzed. OS was defined as the interval between the date of initial diagnosis and the date of death from any cause or the last follow-up, while CSS was estimated from diagnosis to cancer-specific caused death.

Statistical analyses

Baseline characteristics of FTC patients were analyzed using the Pearson’s chi-square test to compare clinicopathological factors between the married and unmarried groups. The study endpoints included OS and CSS. Kaplan-Meier curves were used to analyze survival before and after PSM for marital status, age subgroups, and gender subgroups. The differences were assessed using the log-rank test.

The independent prognostic factors and hazard ratios (HR) for OS and CSS were determined using univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard models. Variables that showed prognostic significance (P < 0.05) in the univariate analysis were included in the final multivariate analysis. Additionally, to adjust for significant clinicopathological covariates such as age, gender, and race between the married and unmarried groups, we implemented a 1:1 PSM using logistic regression modeling. This approach aimed to reduce selection bias. Age, gender, race, tumor stage, and surgery were utilized as matching criteria, and the propensity score was defined as the predicted log odds of being unmarried. The married and unmarried patients were matched within their respective risk groups, based on nearest neighbor matching. The caliper used for matching was set at 0.00114. This strict caliper was used to ensure high comparability between married and unmarried patients on the matched variables, aiming to minimize residual confounding between the groups, albeit at the potential cost of leaving some patients unmatched. The balance of covariates after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs) and a Love plot.

We conducted all statistical analyses using R software (Version 4.2.2). All statistical tests were two-sided, and a significance level of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethics review

As the SEER database contains de-identified, publicly available data, no informed consent from participants or ethical approval from institutional review boards was required.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included a total of 7,954 eligible patients diagnosed with FTC between 2004 and 2015. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics are presented in Table 1. Among these patients, 5,025 (63.2% of the cohort) were married, and 2,929 (36.8% of the cohort) were unmarried, indicating a predominance of married patients in the cohort. There were statistically significant differences (all P < 0.001) between the married and unmarried groups in terms of age, gender, race, and T-stage. It was found that married patients were more likely to be in the age group of 35–65 years (62.7% vs. 48.9%), male (36.1% vs. 21.4%), white (83.0% vs. 76.6%) or from other races (9.67% vs. 7.75%), and have tumors in T1 stage (25.6% vs. 21.9%) or T2 stage (39.6% vs. 38.9%).

Effects of marital status on OS and CSS

The relationship between marital status and OS and CSS was explored using Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. The results demonstrated that married patients had significantly better OS and CSS compared to unmarried patients (P < 0.0001) (Fig. 2). To further assess the prognostic factors associated with FTC, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed. The analysis revealed that marital status, age, gender, T-stage, N-stage, M-stage, and surgery were all significant prognostic factors for OS and CSS (all P < 0.05), whereas race was not identified as an independent prognostic factor for OS or CSS. Based on the results of the univariate Cox regression analysis, a multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted. Consequently, marital status remained a significant prognostic factor for OS and CSS (all P < 0.001). Specifically, unmarried patients exhibited significantly worse OS (HR 1.54, 95% CI 1.38–1.72, P < 0.001) and CSS (HR 1.66, 95% CI 1.35–2.05, P < 0.001) compared to married patients. Additionally, age, gender, T-stage, N-stage, M-stage, and surgery continued to be important prognostic factors for OS. However, when considering gender, no significant difference in CSS was observed in married patients compared to unmarried patients (HR 1.01, 95% CI 0.81–1.26, P = 0.933). Apart from this, all other prognostic factors were consistent with the findings for OS (Table 2).

Propensity score matching for patients with follicular thyroid carcinoma

In order to account for potential confounding variables between the married and unmarried groups and to ensure the reliability of our results, we employed a 1:1 PSM method. This matching method resulted in a total of 2615 pairs of patients who were perfectly matched (Table 3). To quantitatively assess the balance achieved, we calculated the SMDs for all covariates before and after matching. As detailed in Supplementary Table 1, the absolute SMD for all variables after matching was reduced to below the recommended threshold of 0.1 (range: 0.000–0.010), indicating excellent balance between the groups. The Love plot (Supplementary Fig. 1) provides a visual confirmation of this substantial improvement, showing all covariates closely aligned around the zero-difference line after matching, in contrast to their pre-match dispersion. Following the matching process, all p-values for the variables were > 0.05, suggesting that the differences between the two groups were minimal and the obtained results were robust.

Effects of marital status on OS and CSS after propensity score matching

After conducting 1:1 PSM, we examined the impact of marital status on OS and CSS using Kaplan-Meier survival curves (Table 4). The results demonstrated significant differences in OS and CSS between married and unmarried patients (OS: P < 0.0001, CSS: P = 0.008). Specifically, the OS and CSS of married patients were superior to those of the unmarried population (Fig. 3). Moreover, multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate the relationship between marital status and OS or CSS. The results indicated that marital status remained a significant prognostic factor for OS and CSS (both P < 0.001). Unmarried individuals exhibited poorer OS (HR 1.52, 95% CI 1.34–1.74, P < 0.001) and CSS (HR 1.58, 95% CI 1.23–2.03, P < 0.001) compared to their married counterparts. These findings imply that married patients had better overall and cancer-specific survival compared to unmarried patients (Fig. 4).

Subgroup analyses of the effects of different marital status on CSS in patients with follicular thyroid carcinoma before PSM

Our study highlights the significant impact of marital status on the survival rate of patients with FTC. Through a single multivariate Cox regression analysis, we identified age as a significant factor influencing the survival rate of these patients. Interestingly, gender was found to be statistically significant in the univariate Cox regression analysis but not in the multivariate Cox regression analysis. Consequently, we conducted a comprehensive investigation into the effects of age and gender on the survival of patients with FTC.

In the subgroup analysis stratified by age, a significant interaction was observed (P for interaction = 0.02, Fig. 4A). Married status was significantly associated with a reduced risk of cancer-specific death in patients aged 35–65 years (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.49–0.99, P = 0.046) and those aged ≥ 65 years (HR 0.63, 95% CI 0.48–0.83, P = 0.001). The proportional hazards assumption was tested and confirmed for both of these subgroups (Supplementary Table 2). For patients aged < 35 years, all eight cancer-specific deaths occurred in the unmarried group, with no events in the married group. This complete separation precluded reliable hazard ratio estimation with the Cox proportional hazards model.

In the subgroup analysis stratified by sex, no significant interaction was found (P for interaction = 0.244). The intervention showed a significant protective effect in both females (n = 5,533, 69.3%; HR 0.67, 95% CI 0.52–0.87, P = 0.003) and males (n = 2,453, 30.7%; HR 0.57, 95% CI 0.41–0.79, P = 0.001).

Discussion

Our population-based study contributes to the existing knowledge in several critical areas. Firstly, it represents one of the largest and most focused analyses on FTC, specifically examining the association between marital status and survival outcomes within this histological framework. Secondly, the application of PSM in our study helps to minimize the influence of confounding from essential baseline variables, thereby supporting the robustness of the observed associations. Most importantly, our investigation extends beyond average effect reporting by rigorously examining effect modification, revealing that the association between marital status and survival outcomes is not uniform across subgroups, with a more pronounced association observed in patients aged 65 years and older, as well as those under 35 years.

The impact of marital status on follicular thyroid carcinoma patients stratified by age and sex variables

This study sought to evaluate the influence of marital status on the survival outcomes of patients diagnosed with FTC. The findings indicated that marital status is a significant independent prognostic factor, with married individuals exhibiting markedly improved OS and CSS both prior to and following PSM. To investigate potential variations beyond an average effect, interaction tests were conducted within a Cox proportional hazards model. Previous studies have established that the effects of marital status on thyroid carcinoma outcomes differ by age15. Our analysis identified a statistically significant interaction between marital status and age (P for interaction < 0.05), thereby confirming that the strength of this association is not uniform across different age demographics. Notably, a significant protective association was observed in patients aged 35 to 65 years and those aged 65 years and older. Conversely, a reliable hazard ratio could not be determined for patients under 35 years, likely due to the limited number of cancer-specific deaths in this subgroup, which hinders robust statistical analysis. This trend suggests that the relationship between marital status and survival is most pronounced among middle-aged and older adults.

In contrast, no significant interaction was detected between marital status and sex (P for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the overall association remains consistent across genders. The absence of a differential effect by sex may be attributed to the similarities in the advantages of marital support within the context of this disease or may reflect the compensatory support provided by women’s generally broader social networks outside of marriage. These results highlight that the relationship between marital status and survival in FTC is influenced by age but not by biological sex, with a clear association evident in the majority of patients over the age of 35. Future investigations should delve deeper into the specific psychosocial and behavioral mechanisms by which marital relationships affect outcomes in various patient subgroups15,16.

The significance of marital status in follicular thyroid carcinoma patients

In a previous study, it was observed that marital status significantly affects the prognosis of thyroid carcinoma, with married patients exhibiting a notable survival advantage17. Li et al. illustrated that among patients with various forms of thyroid carcinoma, those who were widowed or divorced experienced inferior OS and CSS outcomes18. Additionally, a study focusing on patients with differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC) reported elevated tumor mortality rates among widowed individuals17. In research conducted by Roche et al., marriage was identified as having a protective effect on the five-year survival rates of patients diagnosed with medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC)19. The robustness of this association is further emphasized by the consistency of the hazard ratio observed both before and after rigorous PSM in our analysis. This stability indicates that the survival benefit associated with marriage is unlikely to be merely a result of demographic or clinical confounding, but rather may reflect a more independent influence of the socio-economic and psychosocial resources linked to marital partnerships.

Despite these findings, there exists a paucity of research specifically examining the relationship between marital status and survival prognosis in patients with FTC. Most prior studies have primarily focused on thyroid carcinoma as a whole, as well as on DTC and MTC. Therefore, the present study aims to explore the association between marital status and survival outcomes in FTC patients, employing the PSM method to mitigate the effects of confounding variables and to ensure the reliability of the findings.

Cancer, a condition that currently lacks a definitive cure, is recognized for its psychological repercussions on patients. Approximately two-thirds of cancer patients endure varying degrees of depression, with symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, pain, and insomnia manifesting to differing extents20. Thus, it is imperative to underscore the significance of emotional support, particularly from close relatives and spouses, in mitigating the adverse effects of depressive symptoms. Notably, stress related to parenting has been shown to influence breast cancer prognosis through similar mechanisms21, highlighting the broader importance of social and familial factors in cancer survivorship. Therefore, integrating marital status into risk stratification frameworks could enhance personalized supportive care for patients with FTC.

Married patients exhibit a higher propensity for undergoing early tumor screening. A responsible spouse can assist in monitoring the regular medical check-ups of potential FTC patients, aiding in the early detection of lesions and prompt initiation of treatment22. At the same time, married individuals may have greater access to social support that is associated with healthier behaviors, such as reduced smoking rates and lower alcohol consumption. This network of support may also improve adherence to standardized treatment protocols (e.g., surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy), ultimately contributing to improved long-term survival outcomes.

Notably, our data reveals an intriguing nuance: after rigorous matching, the initially robust association between surgery and cancer-specific survival diminished, becoming statistically insignificant. This observation suggests that the survival advantage attributed to surgery in FTC may primarily be explained by the patients’ baseline clinical characteristics. In stark contrast, the relationship between marital status and survival remained largely unchanged post-matching. This critical divergence implies that the survival benefit associated with marriage is not solely a reflection of patients’ overall health or disease stage at diagnosis. Instead, it supports the hypothesis that distinct psychosocial or behavioral mechanisms, such as enhanced support, care coordination, and treatment adherence, may significantly influence survival outcomes.

Moreover, the encouragement and support provided by spouses can contribute to a reduced incidence of depression and psychological distress among married patients with FTC. It is important to recognize that psychological distress may contribute to tumor progression through disruptions in cortisol circadian rhythms and elevated levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and C-reactive protein (CRP)17–23. Chronic stress activates neuroendocrine pathways that foster a pro-inflammatory state. Emerging evidence in thyroid cancer indicates that gene signatures related to oxidative stress are associated with poorer prognoses and reduced immune infiltration, establishing a biological link between psychosocial factors and disease progression24. However, it is essential to consider that the influence of marital status on patient outcomes is closely tied to the quality of the marital relationship. Changes in marital status may occur during the study period, necessitating a careful examination of both the stability and quality of the marriage, as these factors can significantly influence OS and CSS. Unfortunately, the SEER database currently lacks comprehensive assessments of marital details, underscoring the necessity for further research in this domain.

In addition to the psychosocial factors linked to marital status, married patients often experience a diminished financial burden due to the pooling of resources, underscoring the substantial material implications of this arrangement. The economic status of individuals serves as a vital component of social support interventions, significantly influencing patients’ treatment decisions and the types of medications they utilize, which in turn enhances their quality of life and overall survival rates. Therefore, it is reasonable to hypothesize that adequate financial support for unmarried patients could also correlate with improved prognoses to some degree.

It is imperative to emphasize that our study reveals a notable association rather than a prescriptive solution. Marital status should not be viewed as a modifiable treatment target; instead, it functions as a marker that enables clinicians to identify patients who may benefit from intensified psychosocial and supportive care. The objective is to incorporate the evaluation of social support into comprehensive patient management, rather than to oversimplify the intricate relationships between social connections and health outcomes.

Limitations

Firstly, the present study is constrained by the variables accessible within the SEER database. Notably, it lacks comprehensive information regarding the quality and duration of marital relationships, as well as the reliance on a static marital status at the time of diagnosis, which fails to account for any subsequent changes. For example, research conducted by Yang et al.25 indicated that individuals in distressed relationships report elevated overall stress levels compared to those in healthier partnerships, potentially resulting in increased dysfunction. Additionally, individual-level data concerning socioeconomic status, health behaviors, and psychological conditions were not available, which could serve as confounding factors or mediators in the association under investigation.

Secondly, in line with a prevalent trade-off inherent in large-scale database studies, we opted to simplify complex treatment information. Surgical intervention was categorized as a binary confounder, while detailed data regarding the extent of surgery, radioactive iodine therapy, or TSH suppression therapy were absent from the SEER database and thus excluded from the primary analysis. This methodology accounts for the fundamental influence of treatment receipt; however, it may not fully encompass the intricacies or progression of treatment modalities throughout the study period.

Thirdly, the SEER population is predominantly composed of White and Black individuals, which may restrict the generalizability of findings to other racial demographics. Our cohort was diagnosed between 2004 and 2015, a timeframe preceding significant alterations in treatment guidelines. Consequently, while the identified association is robust within this historical context, the absolute survival estimates and effect sizes may not be directly applicable to contemporary patients managed according to newer protocols.

Fourthly, the retrospective observational design inherently limits the ability to establish causal relationships, and residual confounding may remain despite the implementation of PSM. Comprehensive treatment data, including details on the extent of thyroidectomy, radioactive iodine therapy, and TSH suppression, were not available in the SEER database. These unmeasured variables may vary according to marital status and could partially confound the observed association. Although the robustness of our findings following PSM indicates that such confounding is unlikely to completely explain the effect of marital status, this limitation warrants acknowledgment. Furthermore, the exclusion of patients with very short survival (< 1 month) may have resulted in an underrepresentation of individuals with the most aggressive metastatic disease, which could contribute to the observed distant metastasis rate being lower than estimates from some historical literature, thereby affecting the generalizability of our findings. Additionally, cancer-specific survival is susceptible to potential misclassification bias in cause-of-death coding. Notably, the consistent results regarding overall survival, which are not influenced by such misclassification, provide compelling evidence that the association between marital status and survival is robust and not merely a byproduct of coding bias in cancer-specific survival.

Moreover, the subgroup analyses conducted in this study are exploratory in nature. Although interaction tests help address the challenge of multiple comparisons across subgroups by assessing heterogeneity through a single statistical test, the results should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Further validation through independent prospective studies is essential to corroborate the observed associations.

Finally, it is important to emphasize that this study utilized a retrospective design and lacked a prospective component. Despite the application of PSM to adjust for baseline characteristics, significant differences remained. Therefore, it is imperative that our conclusions be validated in other cohorts, particularly through large-scale randomized controlled trials.

Conclusion

In conclusion, utilizing a large population-based cohort, this study identifies marital status as an independent prognostic factor for survival in patients with FTC. Compared to unmarried patients, married individuals demonstrated significantly better overall and cancer-specific survival. This association was particularly pronounced in older patients (≥ 65 years). These findings highlight the need to integrate social support assessments into the comprehensive clinical management of patients with FTC.
