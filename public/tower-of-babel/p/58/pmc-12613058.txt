Study on the differences in the effects of sulfite and protease on the structure and processing quality of high purity corn starch☆
Authors: Qing Su, Linlin Sun, Xingya Wang, Yuqiu Guo, Kuijie Gong, Kaichang Liu, Lirong Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to investigate the effects of sulfite and protease treatments on the molecular structure and processing quality of starch. Analyses showed that starch purity exceeded 90 % for both extraction methods. Compared with protease-extracted starch, sulfite-extracted starch exhibited a 0.05 % reduction in residual protein content. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy demonstrated that sulfite treatment hydrolyzed starch granular-associated proteins (SGAPs), which enhanced molecular ordering within the starch matrix. Sulfite-extracted starch also had higher solubility (over 4.8 %) and swelling degree (over 11.29 g/g) than protease-extracted starch. Electron microscopy revealed protein residues on the surface of protease-extracted starch granules. Additionally, protease-extracted starch exhibited higher peak viscosity, trough viscosity, and final viscosity than sulfite-extracted starch, with a lower hydrolysis rate in the first 30 min. These findings provide a foundational basis for advancing the functional applications and molecular-level investigations of these two starch types.

Introduction

Corn starch is abundant in the endosperm, where it comprises concentric layers of amorphous and crystalline starch molecular chains, all encapsulated within a protein matrix and proteinoplast (Zhang, Ma, et al., 2021). The polar functional groups (hydroxyl, amino, and carboxyl groups) of endosperm proteins readily interact with starch polymers through both hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions (Qiao et al., 2022). These molecular forces drive protein adsorption onto the surfaces of starch granules, forming stable interfacial complexes. Notably, starch granular-associated proteins (SGAPs) exhibit particularly strong binding affinity for starch molecules (Zhu et al., 2024), and the removal of these proteins exerts a significant impact on the molecular structure and processing quality of starch.

The sulfite-based extraction method offers advantages such as high starch purity, minimal raw material loss, and inhibition of bacterial proliferation (Zhang, Wu, et al., 2021). For 14 maize varieties (with total starch contents ranging from 69.9 % to 72.9 %), this method achieved starch yields of 62.8 %–70.1 % (Singh et al., 2024). Wang et al. (2023) further demonstrated that the sulfite method yielded starch with purity exceeding 84.58 % (on a dry weight basis [d.b]) for two corn cultivars. However, this approach has notable drawbacks: sulfur dioxide (SO2) acts as both an environmental pollutant and a processing hazard, causing equipment corrosion and complicating wastewater treatment. These limitations have spurred research into enzymatic protein hydrolysis as an alternative for corn starch extraction. Recent studies highlight the superior extraction yields of proteolytic (enzyme-based) methods. Liu, Xu, et al. (2024) reported a starch recovery rate of 65.18 % using enzymatic treatment, compared to 54.73 % with the sulfite method. Similarly, Somavat et al. (2021) achieved a 71.4 % yield from common corn (with a total starch content of 75.2 %) via enzymatic processing. While both the sulfite and protease methods can achieve approximately 90 % extraction efficiency and 85 % starch purity, method-dependent differences in the physicochemical properties of starch are likely to exist. Current literature primarily focuses on optimizing extraction yields, whereas systematic comparisons of structural and functional property differences remain understudied.

Maize endosperm proteins impede water absorption, thereby attenuating the gelatinization characteristics and viscosity of starch (Dang et al., 2025). Both sulfite and proteases facilitate starch granule extraction by hydrolyzing these proteins (Ozturk et al., 2021; Singh et al., 2024). Tester et al. (2008) confirmed that starch extracted via the proteolytic method exhibits reduced amylose content, attributed to the influence of α-amylase. In contrast, Liu, Zhao, et al. (2024) et al. hypothesized that the enzymatic method increase amylose content through acid-mediated amylopectin degradation, suggesting that, beyond yield differences, structural variations between enzyme-extracted and sulfite-extracted starch are minimal. However, molecular-level distinctions between sulfite-extracted and protease-extracted starch remain uncharacterized, with current literature providing limited evidence of method-dependent effects on the molecular structure of starch. Emerging research highlights substantial structural alterations in starch following protein removal: for instance, wheat starch showed decreased relative crystallinity but enhanced short-range molecular ordering after waxy protein extraction (Qiang et al., 2025). Similarly, rice starch demonstrated decreased crystallinity, increased surface porosity, and elevated susceptibility to α-amylase following SGAPs removal (Ma et al., 2020). Given these findings, it is worth investigating whether sulfite and proteases are able to alter the molecular structure and processing quality of starch by hydrolyzing proteins in different parts of the starch.

This study employed both sulfite and protease extraction methods to obtain high-purity corn starch samples. The molecular structure of the extracted starch was analyzed using Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Comprehensive quality evaluation encompassed: scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging, granule size distribution profiling, assessment of pasting properties via rapid viscosity analysis (RVA), evaluation of thermal behavior using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC), and in vitro digestibility simulations. These analytical approaches establish a fundamental understanding of structure-function relationships, providing critical data for industrial applications and future research on differentially extracted starch products.

Materials and methods

Materials

LY296, C9929, ND372, JK999 and LY106 were mature maize kernels harvested from Zhangqiu test field in Jinan City, Shandong Province (Base components are shown in 3.1). Total starch kit (K-TSTA-100 A), amylose test kit (K-RSTAR, 100 Assays per Kit) and resistant starch test kit (K-RSTAR, 100 Assays per Kit) were purchased from Megazyme International Ireland Ltd. (Wicklow, Ireland). Acid protease (C10260281, 700 U/mg) was bought from Shanhai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Pepsin (P7000, 250 U/mg) was bought from Sigma-Aldrich Chemical Co.(St. Louis, MO, USA). Other chemicals and solvents were all of analytical grade.

Determination of basic components of maize grain

Total starch content (d.b) was determined using the Total Starch Detection Kit (Megazyme, Ireland). Prior to analysis, corn kernels were ground to a fine powder and passed through an 80-mesh sieve. A 100 mg aliquot of the sieved powder was treated with 0.1 mL of thermostable α-amylase, followed by hydrolysis in a boiling water bath for 30 min to ensure complete starch degradation. Subsequently, 0.1 mL of starch glucosidase was added, and the mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 30 min. Color development was facilitated using GOPOD reagent, with absorbance measured at 510 nm. For protein determination, a Kjeltec 8200 nitrogen analyzer (FOSS LTD., Hillerød, Denmark) was used in accordance with the protocol described by Jia et al. (2023). Water content was determined following the method established by Su et al. (2025). while crude fat content was analyzed via the Soxhlete extraction method as outlined by Jia et al. (2023).

Corn starch extraction by sulfite method

The method described by Timm et al. (2023) was adapted with modifications. Briefly, 300 g of cleaned corn kernels were mixed with purified water at a ratio of 1:3 (w/v). Relative to the dry weight of maize kernels, 0.5 % sodium bisulfite (NaHSO3) and 0.5 % lactic acid were added, followed by soaking at 53 °C for 39 h. Once the reaction was complete, the corn kernels were rinsed with distilled water. The kernels were then ground using a JYL Y92 homogenizer (Joyoung Company Limited, Shandong, China), operated at medium speed (25,000 r/min) for 3 min. The resulting mixture was passed through a 50-mesh sieve to remove the germ fractions. The remaining corn slurry was further ground at high speed (35,000 r/min) for 3 min, then sieved sequentially through 200-mesh and 300-mesh screens. The resulting starch slurry was filtered and allowed to settle for 3 h, after which the supernatant was discarded. Using a Baumé hydrometer, the specific gravity of the settled starch precipitate was adjusted to 1.04°Bé. The slurry was then processed through an inclined starch tank (slope: 0.015 cm/cm; flow rate: 300 mL/min) to remove zeaxanthin, with purified corn starch retained in the tank. The recovered starch was oven-dried at 45 °C for 24 h, then milled and passed through an 80-mesh sieve to obtain sulfite-extracted starch, which was stored in a desiccator. Starch purity was determined following the protocol outlined in Section 2.1 (total starch content, d.b). Starch extraction yield was calculated using the following formula:Extractionrate%=C1×m1/C0×m0×100where: C0 is the total starch content in maize grain, m0 is the corn grain mass, C1 is the total starch content in the extracted starch, and m1 is the corn starch mass.

Extraction of corn starch by protease method

The method described by Liu, Zhao, et al. (2024) was adapted with modifications. An appropriate amount of clean corn kernels was weighed, and the kernels and purified water were immersed in a water bath at 52 °C for 3 h at a ratio of 1:2 (w/v). Following initial coarse grinding (3 min), the pH of the soaking solution was adjusted to 3.5. Acid protease (700 u/g substrate) was introduced and enzymatic digestion proceeded at 52 °C for 20 h. The reaction was terminated by pH adjustment to 7.0. The remaining steps were performed as per the sulfite method to obtain protease-extracted starch.

Protein content

This is consistent with method 2.2.

Amylose content

Amylose content was determined using an Amylose Assay kit (K-AMYL, Megazyme). This method relies on the selective binding of concanavalin A (Con A) to branched polymers, forming insoluble complexes, while linear amylose remains soluble in the supernatant. Amylose concentration was calculated by comparing the absorbance (measured at 510 nm) of the supernatant (containing unbound amylose) to that of the original starch solution, as described in reference (Su et al., 2024).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

Samples were evenly spread on conductive adhesive, then coated with gold. They were subsequently examined using an Apreo SEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) at an acceleration voltage of 20 kV, as described by Su et al. (2024).

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Corn kernels were soaked in deionized water (DIW) at 4 °C for 12 h. The pericarp and germ were removed using a sharp blade, after which the endosperm was cut in half. The relatively loose, powdery endosperm from the center was carefully scraped off, and the adherent region between the cutin endosperm and powdery endosperm was excised with a surgical blade, yielding isolated cutin endosperm fractions. Both the cutin and silty endosperm components were dried overnight at 45 °C in a convection oven, then mechanically ground and passed through a 100-mesh sieve for subsequent analysis.

For starch samples: 1.00 mg of starch was thoroughly mixed with 150 mg of potassium bromide (KBr) and pressed into a transparent pellets. FTIR spectra were recorded using a Nicolet-iS20 FTIR spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA) over a spectral range of 4000–400 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1.

Grain size distribution

A measured aliquot of the sample was uniformly dispersed in DIW via 5-min ultrasonic treatment. The suspension was then added gradually to the sample cell, with real-time adjustment of opacity. The particle size distribution of starch granules was determined using a Microtrac S3500 laser diffraction analyzer (Microtrac Inc., Pennsylvania, USA), and data are reported as the mean of three independent measurements.

Solubility and swelling power

The experimental procedure was adapted from Liu, Xu, et al. (2024). A 2 % (w/v) starch suspension was stirred continuously in an 80 °C water bath for 30 min. After cooling to ambient temperature (25 °C), the mixture was centrifuged at 3000 ×g for 20 min to separate the phases. The resulting supernatant was carefully decanted into a pre-weighed Petri dish and dried at 105 °C until a constant mass was achieved. The mass of the residue from the dried supernatant and the mass of the centrifuged precipitate were recorded. Solubility and swelling power were calculated using the following formula:Solubility%=m1m0×100Swelling powergg=m2m0×1−Solubility%where: m0 is the mass of cornmeal, m1 was the quality of water-soluble corn flour, m2 is the mass of expanded cornmeal.

Pasting properties

The pasting properties of the extracted starch samples were determined using a RVA-TecMaster rapid viscosity analyzer (Perten Instrument Company, Stockholm, Sweden). Thermal processing conditions followed the protocol established by Dai et al. (2025), with a standardized temperature profile applied during testing.

Thermal properties

A precisely weighed starch sample (3 mg, d.b) was mixed with 6 μL of DIW in a stainless-steel DSC pan. The hermetically sealed pan was equilibrated at 24 °C for 20 h. The gelatinization curve of the sample was obtained using a DSC204F1 differential scanning calorimeter (NETZSCH Geraetebau GmbH, Selb, Germany), with a temperature range of 30–90 °C and a heating rate of 10 °C/min.

In vitro digestion

This assay followed Method 3 described by Pautong et al. (Pautong et al., 2022). Briefly, 300 mg of sample was accurately weighed and mixed with add DIW at a sample-to-water ratio of 1:2, then heated to boiling in a water bath for 23 min. After allowing the mixture to stand at room temperature for 5 min, it was placed it a 37 °C water bath for an additional 5 min. Subsequently, 1 mL of sodium maleate buffer (0.1 mol/L, pH 6) was added, followed by 2 mL of pancreatic α-amylase (60 U/mL; from RS Test Kit). The mixture was stirred at 60 rpm in a magnetic stirrer at 37 °C for 75 s. Next, 3 mL of pepsin (19.5 U/m in 0.1 mol/L potassium phosphate buffer) was added, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C with stirring at 60 rpm for 30 min. The reaction was terminated by adjusting the pH to 6.9 with 15 mL of sodium hydroxide (0.1 mol/L). A 15 mL aliquot of enzyme solution, containing pancreatin (28.4 μ g/mL; P1750, Sigma) and amyloglucosidase (13 U/mL; from RS Test Kit) in 0.1 M sodium potassium phosphate buffer (pH 6.9), was then added, and incubation continued for another 180 min (37 °C, 120 rpm). Aliquots (0.70 mL) withdraw at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, and 180 min and placed on ice prior to centrifugation (13,500 rpm, 10 min, 4 °C). Glucose content in the supernatant was determined using the GOPOD method (Megayme International Ireland). The percentage of hydrolyzed starch was calculated by multiplying the measured glucose content by a conversion factor of 0.9.

Data analysis

All results were carried out in triplicate. The Origin 2024 and Tukey's test was used to analyse the significance in the difference at a 0.05 level of confidence.

Results and discussion

The basic component of corn seed

As shown in Fig. S1, the moisture content of multiple high-starch corn varieties averaged approximately 10 %, while crude fat content was relatively consistent at 0.55 % across all cultivars. Among the tested varieties, C9929 exhibited the highest total starch content (72.12 %), with the other samples containing no less than 65 %. Protein content ranged from 6.76 % to 8.03 % across the five corn varieties. Elevated protein content typically correlates with greater kernel hardness, whereas increased starch content enhances paste viscosity. These compositional differences directly influence starch extraction efficiency (Qiao et al., 2022), softer kernel texture facilitates wet grinding, thereby improving starch extraction rates (Singh et al., 2024). The minimal compositional variation among the five selected corn varieties helped minimize the impact of varietal differences on both the quality and extraction efficiency of corn starch, thereby reducing errors when analyzing how sulfite and protease treatments affect extraction rates and starch quality.

C9929 and ND372 displayed superior starch purity and extraction yield compared to the other varieties, potentially due to their lower protein content. For each variety, there were no significant differences in starch purity and extraction rates between the protease and sulfite treatments, with both methods achieving values exceeding 90 %. These results outperformed those documented in previous studies (Liu, Zhao, et al., 2024; Singh et al., 2024; Somavat et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023). Protein content of starch extracted via sulfite and protease methods

As shown in Fig. 1A, protease-extracted starch exhibited a higher residual protein content than sulfite-extracted starch. While both sulfurous acid (H2SO3) and protease degrade and remove surface-bound proteins from starch granules, their distinct mechanisms lead to differences in residual protein levels. Proteolytic enzymes require access through microporous channels to hydrolyze internal structures (Zhu et al., 2024). Experimental evidence indicates that proteases primarily act on surface proteins, as their ability to penetrate starch granules limited. In contrast, the smaller molecular size of H2SO3 allows for more effective diffusion through starch micropores, potentially enabling it to interact with internal protein-starch complexes. This proposed mechanism merits further investigation through characterization of starch chain modifications. Difference in protein content may also stem from residual protease. Given that the water-soluble protease used in this experiment was largely removed during the washing process, residual protease contributed minimally to differences in protein content.Fig. 1Extraction starch protein content (A) and amylose content (B). Fig. 1B demonstrates that amylose content was comparable between sulfite-extracted and protease-extracted starch samples, indicating that neither treatment effectively degrades amylose molecules. This observation is consistent with findings of Dang et al.(Dang et al., 2025), who reported that while removing SGAPs enhances amylose extraction yield by weakening intermolecular binding forces, it does not change the relative amylose content of starch.

Starch particle surface characteristics and size distribution

Electron microscopy revealed protein separation on the surface of starch granules. As depicted in Fig. 2, all starch granules exhibited irregular structures with smooth surfaces, micropores, and trace amounts of residual protein. Consistent with this, laser confocal microscopy analysis by Bae et al., 2020, Dai et al., 2025 detected comparable protein fluorescence on the surface of protease-extracted starch granules. Furthermore, Fig. 2 demonstrates that sulfite-extracted starch granules had substantially more surface micropores than their protease-extracted counterparts. Protease treatment primarily degrades proteins associated with the granule exterior, resulting in limited microporous formation; the additional porosity observed may stem from endogenous amylase activity (Zhang, Ma, et al., 2021) In contrast, sulfite treatment induces more extensive microporous channel formation through direct chemical action, effects that extend beyond those caused by protease or endogenous enzyme activity. During starch extraction, as proteins in the outer layer of starch granules are degraded, SO32− (due to its small molecular structure) penetrates the granules and continues hydrolyzing SGAPs within existing microporous channels. Following SGAPs hydrolysis, additional microporous channels form on the granule surface (Hu et al., 2025).Fig. 2Starch particle microstructure and particle size distribution: (A) LY296; (B) C9929; (C) ND372; (D) JK999; (E) LY106. Particle size distribution analysis revealed a leftward shift for protease-extracted starch relative to sulfite-extracted starch. Across the five varieties, the median diameter (D50) of sulfite-modified starch granules ranged from 16.43 μm to 17.18 μm. In contrast, protease-extracted samples exhibited higher D50 values, along with increased mean volume (MV) and mean area (MA) parameters. These elevated metrics correlate with greater retention of surface proteins in protease-extracted starch.

FTIR spectral analysis of extracted starches

As depicted in Fig. S2, the FTIR spectra of the sulfite-extracted and protease-extracted starches from the five maize cultivars were nearly identical, indicating that neither sulfite nor protease introduced new functional groups to the starch. FTIR analysis revealed broad absorption peaks near 3310 cm−1 across all five endosperm powder samples. Both sulfite-extracted and protease-extracted starches exhibited peak shifts toward lower wavenumbers relative to native endosperm powder. The spectral region of 3600–3020 cm−1 corresponds to -OH stretching vibrations, which reflect hydrogen bonding interactions. The observed blue shift in this region for extracted starches demonstrates stronger hydrogen bonding compared with native endosperm powder, an enhancement that correlates with the improved starch purity discussed in Section 3.2. The FTIR spectrum also displayed characteristic absorption bands at 1340 cm−1 and 996 cm−1: the former corresponds to -CH2 and -COO stretching vibrations, while the latter is associated with C—O vibrations of α-1,4-glycosidic linkages (Ma et al., 2023). Both sulfite-extracted and protease-extracted starches showed greater peak intensities at these wavenumbers than native endosperm powder, confirming the higher purity achieved via these extraction methods. Notably, the 1530 cm−1 absorption band, observed in endosperm powder and attributed to N—H vibrational modes of protein components, was absent in extracted starches. This disappearance confirms that both sulfite and protease treatments effective removed proteins from the starch.

The ratio of the absorbance at 1047 cm−1 to that at 1022 cm−1 (IR 1047/1022) is an indicator of the short-range molecular order of starch granules. As shown in Fig. 3, there was no significant difference in the IR 1047/1022 ratio across the different corn endosperm powder varieties. Similarly, within each variety, the sulfite-extracted and protease-extracted starches exhibited comparable IR 1047/1022 values, demonstrating consistent modification effects across genotypes. Notably, sulfite-extracted starch had a significantly higher IR 1047/1022 ratio than native endosperm powder. Two potential mechanisms may account for this phenomenon: (1) Endogenous α-amylase activity could induce starch chain hydrolysis, followed by molecular rearrangement (Liu, Zhao, et al., 2024), or (2) H₂SO₃ treatment might promote the formation of linear double helices in amylose components after protein polar group removal, thereby enhancing short-range structural order. For protease-extracted starch, only the IR1047/1022 ratio of ND372 was significantly higher than that of its corresponding endosperm powder; no significant differences were observed for the other varieties. This is because protease treatment fails to hydrolyze proteins embedded within starch granules that interact with starch chains, thus exerting no impact on granule short-range structural order. Experimental evidence further indicates that intrinsic enzymatic activity in starch granules cannot enhance structural order, except in the ND372 variety. The enhanced short-range order observed in sulfite-extracted starch stems exclusively from sulfite-mediated effects, rather than endogenous enzymatic hydrolysis. Solubility and swelling behavior of extracted starches

As shown in Fig. 4, sulfite-extracted starches from all five varieties exhibited higher solubility than their protease-extracted starches. Bae et al. (Bae et al., 2020) reported that starch granules with SGAPs removed display greater solubility than those retaining SGAPs, an effect attributed to enhanced leaching of amylose and amylopectin molecules following SGAPs depletion. Additionally, the abundant surface micropores of sulfite-extracted starch granules facilitate water molecule penetration, further, increasing solubility.Fig. 4Solubility, and swelling of starch. The different lowercase letters indicate the significant difference between the same color P < 0.05.Fig. 4

Solubility, and swelling of starch. The different lowercase letters indicate the significant difference between the same color P < 0.05.

Similarly, Fig. 4 demonstrates that sulfite-extracted starch had higher swelling degree than protease-extracted starch. As temperature increases, water molecules penetrate the microporous channels on the surface of the starch granules, prompting starch chains to depolymerize and bind with water molecules to form a stable gel structure (Xu et al., 2025). Sulfite-modified starch granules feature more extensive surface microporosity, which enhances water penetration. This structural trait, combined with the higher molecular ordering of starch chains in sulfite-extracted samples, promotes greater swelling capacity: the disruption of interchain hydrogen bonds allows for more extensive water molecule binding. In contrast, protease-extracted starch exhibits reduced surface porosity, limiting water absorption during gelatinization. Its native starch-protein structure further restricts swelling, as protein polar groups remain encapsulated within starch chains. This stable configuration inhibits chain expansion and subsequent interaction with water molecules.

Pasting viscosity characteristics of extracted starches

As shown in Fig. 5A, the viscosity curves of sulfite-extracted starches from the five varieties exhibited minimal variation. Their peak viscosity ranged from 4314.67 to 4536.00 cP, trough viscosity from 3017.00 to 3093.00 cP, and final viscosity from 4875.33 to 5147.67 cP. This observation correlates with the comparable amylose content observed across these sulfite-extracted starches. Notably, Fig. 5B demonstrates that protease-extracted starches consistently exhibited higher viscosity parameters than sulfite-modified starches. For protease-extracted samples, peak viscosity ranged from 4354.33 to 4508.33 cP, trough viscosity from 3028.00 to 3238.67 cP, and final viscosity from 4876.33 to 5602.37 cP. The differences in viscosity during heating stem from distinct structural behaviors of the two starch types. Water-absorbed starch granules with weakened intermolecular forces undergo easier disintegration under shear stress as temperature rises. In contrast, protease-extracted starch maintains higher viscosity due to residual surface proteins: these protein components enhance structural rigidity and afford protection against shear forces (Dang et al., 2025), thereby increasing the viscosity of starch.Fig. 5Viscosity curves of sulfite starch (A) and protease starch (B).Fig. 5

Thermal properties of extracted starches (DSC analysis)

As shown in Fig. 6, the DSC gelatinization curves of sulfite-extracted and protease-extracted starches were nearly identical. Table S1 reveals that the gelatinization onset temperature (To) of sulfite-extracted starches across all five varieties was approximately 1 °C higher than that of protease-extracted starches, while the peak temperature (Tp) and conclusion temperature (Tc) were essentially the same. This observed difference in To correlates with the enhanced molecular ordering of sulfite-modified starch. The two extraction methods resulted in comparable gelatinization enthalpy (ΔH) values for the LY296 and C9929 varieties, consistent with their similar amylose contents. However, sulfite-extracted starches of the ND372, JK999, and LY106 varieties exhibited significantly higher ΔH values than their protease-extracted counterparts (P < 0.05), which aligns with previous findings reported by Liu, Xu, et al. (2024). This thermal behavior is consistent with proposed structural reorganization mechanisms. According to Huang et al. (2025) SGAPs removal promotes starch molecular rearrangement: liberated amylose chains, no longer constrained by the polar groups of proteins, preferentially reorganized into linear double helices. This structural transition explains the higher gelatinization enthalpy observed in sulfite-extracted starch relative to protease-extracted samples.Fig. 6DSC curves of sulfite starch (A) and protease starch (B).Fig. 6

In vitro starch hydrolysis kinetics

The hydrolysis profile of the LY296 variety is presented in Fig. 7. The starch hydrolysis rate curve showed an upward trend and had not reached completion by 180 min. This hydrolysis kinetic pattern aligns with findings by Pautong et al. (2022), where the use of a minimal enzyme dosage enabled clear detection of variations in hydrolysis rates. Notably, Fig. 7 demonstrates that sulfite-extracted starch exhibited significantly higher initial hydrolysis rates than protease-extracted starch, though this difference diminished after 60 min. This can be attributed to the enhanced solubility of sulfite-extracted starch(Fig. 4A): dissolved starch molecules are more accessible to α-amylase in the simulated oral fluid, explaining the pronounced rate difference observed at initial time points (e.g., 0 min). Previous studies on recrystallized starch (Su et al., 2025), confirm an inverse relationship between enzymatic digestibility and molecular ordering, while highly ordered starch structures exhibit reduced water absorption capacity and gelatinization potential, thereby limiting enzyme accessibility. However, after gelatinization of ordinary starch, nearly all starch double helices unwind and are subsequently hydrolyzed by amyloglucosidase, making the hydrolysis rate independent of the degree of molecular order (see Fig. 2). Consistent with the swelling degree (Fig. 4B), following gelatinization, sulfite-extracted starch chains bind with more water molecules, providing additional active sites for amylase. In addition, the starch-protein matrix in protease-extracted starch impedes enzymatic hydrolysis, further contributing to the lower hydrolysis rate of protease-extracted starch in the first 30 min. After 60 min of digestion, the hydrolysis profiles of sulfite-extracted and protease-extracted starch became nearly identical. This is associated with the lack of significant difference in amylose content between the two starch types (Fig. 1B).Fig. 7Hydrolysis rate curves of sulfite starch and protease starch.Fig. 7

Conclusions

Under conditions where the sulfite and proteolytic methods yielded comparable starch extraction rates (> 90 % yield) and purity (> 90 % purity), this study investigated variations in the physicochemical properties and molecular structural modifications of starch to inform future extraction optimization strategies. Notably, sulfite-extracted starch exhibited a 0.05 % lower residual protein content than starch extracted via the proteolytic method. FTIR spectroscopy revealed that H2SO3 hydrolyzed SGAPs; by disrupting the starch-protein structure, this hydrolysis enhanced the molecular ordering of starch. Findings from swelling power and DSC analyses further confirmed that sulfite-extracted starch contained more double-helical structures. This structural enhancement significantly expands the applicability of sulfite-extracted starch, for instance, as a resistant starch, or as a substrate for preparing composite materials that interact with fatty acids, phenolic acids, or polypeptides‌. In contrast, protease-extracted starch featured fewer microporous channels and lower solubility than sulfite-extracted starch. It also retained the natural, stable starch-protein structure within starch granules, reducing its hydrolytic susceptibility of amylase; this explains why protease-extracted starch exhibited a lower hydrolysis rate than sulfite-extracted starch in the first 30 min. Furthermore, protease-extracted starch displayed superior pasting viscosity profiles, which may be positioning it as an optimal candidate for direct incorporation into low glycemic index (GI) food systems requiring high viscosity product processing. Additionally, the protease-based extraction method facilitates the high-value utilization of whole corn components, making it well-suited for future applications in the corn starch extraction industry.
