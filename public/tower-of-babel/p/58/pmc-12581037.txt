Prevalence of risk factors of major non-communicable diseases among adolescents in schools of Rapti-Sonari rural municipality of Banke district: a cross-sectional study in Nepal
Authors: Bigyan Subedi, Paras Modi Pangeni, Shrija Rijal, Prashanna Man Maharjan, Manish Rajbanshi, Kshitij Kunwar, Lokendra Oli, Khem B Karki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAbout 70% of premature adult deaths are linked to health-related behaviours that begin in childhood and adolescence. This study aimed to assess the prevalence of major non-communicable disease (NCD) risk factors and their associated factors among school-going adolescents in Rapti-Sonari Rural Municipality, Banke, Nepal.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted among 630 adolescents selected using a two-stage cluster sampling technique. Data were collected through a self-administered questionnaire. Descriptive statistics summarised the data, while the χ² tests, bivariate and multivariate logistic regression identified associated factors. Statistical significance was set at p<0.05 at a 95% CI.ResultsThe mean age of participants was 15.5±1.3 years. Insufficient fruit and vegetable intake (98.4%) and physical inactivity (83.8%) were highly prevalent. Physical activity was significantly associated with male sex (adjusted OR (AOR): 2.1, 95% CI 1.3 to 3.2) and monthly pocket money >Nepalese Rupee (NRs) 500 (AOR: 1.9, 95% CI 1.2 to 3.0). Current alcohol use (29.0%) and smoking (8.1%) were also reported. Alcohol consumption was significantly associated with male (AOR: 2.6, 95% CI 1.6 to 3.3), Tharu ethnicity (AOR: 2.5, 95% CI 1.3 to 3.6) and pocket money >NRs 500 (AOR: 2.8, 95% CI 1.8 to 4.1). Smoking was significantly associated with male (AOR: 2.7, 95% CI 1.8 to 3.9) and pocket money >NRs 500 (AOR: 2.8, 95% CI 1.8 to 4.3).ConclusionsNearly all adolescents exhibited at least one behavioural risk factor for NCDs. The findings highlight inadequate diet, insufficient physical activity and substantial levels of alcohol and tobacco use among adolescents. As these behaviours often persist into adulthood, comprehensive interventions, promotion of extracurricular activities and stricter enforcement of alcohol and tobacco regulations in schools are urgently needed.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) are defined as diseases or conditions that occur in or are known to affect individuals over an extensive period and for which no known causative agents are transmitted from one affected individual to another.1 Cancer, diabetes, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), cardiovascular disease and mental health conditions are the leading causes of death worldwide.2 3 According to the WHO, “NCDs are primarily driven by four key modifiable risk factors: tobacco use, unhealthy diets, physical inactivity, and excessive alcohol consumption”.4 These behaviours result in physiological alterations such as obesity, hypertension, elevated blood sugar and high cholesterol levels. The impact of these risk factors is further intensified by factors such as urbanisation, the global spread of unhealthy lifestyles and an ageing population.4 5

The WHO reports that NCDs cause 41 million deaths annually, accounting for 71% of global deaths, including over 15 million premature deaths (75% of all premature deaths), with 77% of these deaths and 85% of premature deaths occurring in low-income and middle-income countries, primarily due to four major NCDs: cardiovascular diseases (17.9 million deaths, 44% of all NCD deaths and 31% of global deaths), cancers (9 million deaths, 22% of NCD deaths and 16% of global deaths), chronic respiratory diseases (3.8 million deaths, 9% of NCD deaths and 7% of global deaths) and diabetes (1.6 million deaths, 4% of NCD deaths and 3% of global deaths).4 The Global Response for NCD prevention aims for a 25% reduction in premature mortality from major NCDs (cardiovascular diseases, cancer, diabetes and chronic respiratory diseases), alongside targeted reductions in harmful alcohol use (10%), insufficient physical activity (10%), salt intake (30%), tobacco use (30%), raised blood pressure (25%) and efforts to halt the rise in diabetes and obesity.6 7

Nepal’s Multi-sectoral Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs 2021–2025 demonstrates a clear prioritisation of adolescents through specific interventions and dedicated monitoring indicators.8 The plan mandates actions such as stopping the marketing of unhealthy foods to children. This focus is further substantiated within the monitoring framework, which includes a specific indicator for physical inactivity among adolescents, tracked through the Global School-based Student Health Survey.8 Nepal’s Multi-sectoral Action Plan for the Prevention and Control of NCDs 2021–2025 demonstrates a clear prioritisation of adolescents through specific interventions and dedicated monitoring indicators.8 The plan mandates actions such as stopping the marketing of unhealthy foods to children.8 This focus is further substantiated within the monitoring framework, which includes a specific indicator for physical inactivity among adolescents, tracked through the Global School-based Student Health Survey.8 Additionally, national reduction targets for tobacco and alcohol are established for the population aged 15 and above, formally integrating adolescents into national surveillance efforts.8 The Stepwise approach to NCD risk factor surveillance (STEPS) 2019 indicated a high prevalence of tobacco consumption (28.9%), alcohol consumption (23.9%), low consumption of fruits and vegetables (96.7%), raised blood pressure (24.5%), overweight and obese (24.3%), and raised cholesterol (11.1%).9 The Nepal Burden of Disease 2017 study showed that NCDs are the leading causes of death with two-thirds (66%) of deaths attributed to NCDs.10 A 2019 population-based nationwide cross-sectional study in Nepal highlighted a significant burden of non-communicable diseases, revealing high prevalence rates of COPD (12%), diabetes (8.5%), chronic kidney disease (6%) and coronary artery disease (3%). This growing NCD burden could present a substantial challenge to the health system in the coming years.11 12

Nepal is experiencing a rapid nutrition transition, which poses a significant risk of escalating the prevalence of overweight and obesity among adolescents.13 Investments in this field should be a part of the whole-of-society approach as well as the life-course approach.14 More than 25% of obese adolescents have signs of diabetes by age 15. Also, nearly three out of four obese adolescents remain obese as adults, increasing their risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, stroke and cancers.15 Again, when they become adults, overweight adolescents are twice as likely to develop cardiovascular disease and have a seven times greater risk of developing atherosclerosis.16 17 It is increasingly recognised that framing NCD risk factors as purely individual choices is insufficient.9 The rapid global increase in NCDs is largely driven by high-level societal, environmental and commercial determinants of health. As highlighted by the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 Adolescent Body Mass Index (BMI) Collaborators and the Lancet Commission on Obesity, forces such as globalisation and commercialisation have reshaped food, transport and media systems, creating environments that can exacerbate existing health inequalities, particularly among vulnerable populations.16 17 Therefore, to develop effective and equitable interventions, it is crucial to understand how NCD risk varies across different sociodemographic groups. This study investigates factors such as sex, ethnicity, school type and economic status (pocket money) not merely as individual characteristics but as essential indicators of how these broader structural determinants manifest locally, shaping the health risks and opportunities available to adolescents. Considering NCDs are most prevalent among older populations and adolescents are perceived as healthy, there is limited research carried out to assess the prevalence of risk factors of NCDs among adolescents.17 18

This research will aid evidence to the limited literatures that could be useful for local government, educational institutions and developmental partners to work jointly to plan and implement effective programmes to reduce the prevalence of risk factors of NCDs among adolescents. This research also helps to make informed decisions among educational stakeholders to effectively implement rules and regulations in educational settings through a whole-school approach including students, teachers, parents and the local community around the school that will promote adolescent health.

The objective of the research is to measure the prevalence of risk factors of major non-communicable diseases and their association among school-going adolescent students in Rapti-Sonari Rural Municipality.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

A cross-sectional study was conducted among adolescents in selected schools of Rapti-Sonari Rural Municipality, Banke district. The study was conducted between 15 April and 15 May 2022 in the selected government and private schools of the rural municipality. There were 36 government schools and 4 private schools. It is the largest rural municipality by population in Nepal.

Study population

This study included school-going adolescents of grades 8, 9 and 10 in Rapti-Sonari Rural Municipality. According to the Education Section of Rapti-Sonari Rural Municipality, a total of 1310 students were enrolled in grades 8 to 10. To evaluate sample representativeness, the demographic profile of school-going adolescents was compared with the 2021 National Census data for Rapti-Sonari Rural Municipality.19 The sample showed a similar sex distribution (58.1% females vs 52.9% in the census) and closely matched religious composition (97.0% Hindu vs 95.2%).19 However, the Tharu ethnic group was over-represented (65.4% vs 51.8%), while the Chhetri group was under-represented (15.9% vs 23.6%).19

All the students who were present during the period of data collection and fulfilling selection criteria were taken in this study. Students with a history of mental disability, speech problems, hearing problems and unable to communicate were excluded from the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

The sample size was calculated by using the Cochrane proportionate formula, n=Z2pq/d2 by taking the prevalence of at least one risk factor of NCDs (p)=45% taken from the study conducted by Tandon K et al,16 and by assuming the margin of error (d) of 0.05, 95% CI and design effect of 1.5 and 10% non-response rate, the final sample size determined as 630 in this study.

The cluster sampling method was used to recruit the participants. Type of school was considered as clusters in this study. Then, a proportionate allocation technique was carried out to determine the required number of students from each private and governmental school. According to the type of schools as cluster, out of 36 government and 4 private schools, 18 government and 2 private schools were selected using simple random sampling based on the average cluster size. Then, participants were selected from each selected school according to the school’s serial number until the required number of sample was met.

Tool and measures

The study tool was obtained from the “Global School-based Student Health Survey 2015 Nepal” (GSHS),13 which was already used in Nepal to determine the prevalence of major NCD risk factors among school-age adolescents. Pretesting was done among the 10% non-sampled schools (n=63) via a self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire items were evaluated for internal consistency using the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient that is, 0.815, which was comparable to the previous survey, GSHS.

The tool consisted of two sections: the first section consisted of questions related to the socioeconomic and demographic characteristics of the participants. It included age groups (in completed years), sex (male/female), school type (government/private), grades (8, 9 and 10), ethnicity (Tharu, Chhetri, Magar, Kami, Brahmins and others), religion (Hindu, Christian, Buddhist, Muslim and others) and average monthly pocket money (in NRs).

The second section consisted of questions related to behavioural risk factors of NCDs which included: fruit and vegetable intake, cigarette and other tobacco use, alcohol consumption, physical activity and BMI.

Operational definition

Major NCDs: The most commonly recognised NCDs include heart disease, cancer, diabetes, chronic respiratory diseases and mental health conditions.3 4

Insufficient physical activity: It is defined as being physically active for less than at least 60 min per day for 7 days before the survey.13

Insufficient consumption of fruit and vegetables: Consumption of fruit and vegetables less than five times per day is defined as insufficient consumption of fruit and vegetables.13

Current cigarette and tobacco use: This study defines current cigarette and tobacco use as smoking or use of any tobacco products for at least 1 day during the 30 days preceding the survey.13

Current alcohol users: Current alcohol users were defined as those who had at least one drink of alcohol during the 30 days preceding the survey.13

Height: It was measured in centimetres.

Weight: It was measured in kilograms.

Data collection

Data were collected through a self-administered structured questionnaire. We collected the data between 15 April and 15 May 2022. All eligible students from the selected classes (grades 8, 9 and 10) who met the inclusion criteria were approached for participation in the study. Written informed consent was obtained from the students and their guardians. All the students provided the written consent form/parental consent form to participate in the study to minimise the non-response rate. During the period of data collection, some students were absent. We conducted follow-up visits to the schools and contacted their parents via phone to ensure their participation. Each participant took around 25 to 30 min to complete the questionnaire. Principal investigator and school teachers were responsible for the collection of the data. Orientation regarding the study and data collection techniques was provided to the school teachers.

Anthropometric data were obtained using height and weight; standing height was measured using a stature metre and weight was measured using UNICEF electronic weighing scale. The weight and height of the participants were measured three times and their average value was computed in this study.

Data management and analysis

The collected data were entered, validated, cleaned, coded and cross-checked systematically into Epi-Data 3.1 software, and exported to IIBM Statistical Package of Social Sciences (IBM SPSS) version 25 for data analysis. Descriptive statistics, including frequencies, percentages, means and SD, were used to summarise participants’ socioeconomic and demographic characteristics. χ² tests and bivariate binary logistic regression were performed to examine the association between individual characteristics and risk factors of NCDs. The events per variable included in the multivariate regression model were at least 10. Multiple regression models were built using different inclusion criteria (p values <0.1, 0.2 and 0.3) and compared with a full model based on theoretical considerations. Variables with a p value <0.2 were then included in the multivariable logistic regression model to adjust for potential confounders. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test and the Nagelkerke R-squared value. A p value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and adjusted ORs (AOR) with 95% CIs were reported to reflect the strength and precision of associations.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of the participants

Out of 630 participants (online supplemental dataset), the majority of the participants (58.7%) were females. The mean age of the participants was 15.5±1.3 years. The majority of the participants were from government schools (86.2%). Most of them (65.4%) belonged to the Tharu ethnic group, followed by Chhetri (15.9%). A high proportion (96.9%) of them followed the Hindu religion. Over two-thirds of the participants (73.5%) had average monthly pocket money less than or equal to $3.76 (table 1).

Prevalence of NCDs risk factors among school-going adolescents

The study identified four behavioural and one metabolic risk factor associated with major non-communicable diseases: insufficient physical activity, inadequate fruit and vegetable intake, alcohol consumption, smoking and overweight/obesity. Most students (98.4%) did not take sufficient intake of fruits and vegetables. A high proportion of students (83.8%) were physically inactive. Discussion

This study showed that insufficient intake of fruits and vegetables (98.4%) is similar to the studies conducted in Nepal (95 to 97%).5 11 In contrast, studies conducted in India (85.1%), Indonesia (75.2%), Myanmar (83.3%), Sri Lanka (77.1%) and Thailand (67.1%) reported that a much lower prevalence of insufficient fruits and vegetables intake among adolescents.13 The lower proportion of fruits and vegetable intake in our study can be explained by the culturally diverse dietary behaviours, insufficient awareness of the potential benefits of dietary diversity and limited access to fruit and vegetables in various geographical regions of Nepal.13 20 21

This study observed a high prevalence (83.8%) of low physical activity among adolescents, a finding consistent with a previous nationwide survey in Nepal (84.7%).13 Furthermore, our result aligns closely with recent global evidence; a large-scale pooled analysis in The Lancet Child & Adolescent Health, covering 1.6 million adolescents, reported that 81% were insufficiently physically active.22 The challenge appears particularly acute in the region, with a recent study among Indonesian adolescents reporting an even higher prevalence of insufficient physical activity at 96.1%.22 Taken together, these findings confirm that the high prevalence of inactivity in our study is not just a local issue but reflects a widespread and public health challenge affecting adolescents, particularly within the Asian context. This study reported twice higher odds of physical activity among males than females and similar to the study conducted among adolescents in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs).23 It might be due to societal norms, fewer opportunities for sports and concerns about body image for girls. Participants who had an average monthly pocket money of >NRs 500 were more likely to have sufficient physical activity compared with those receiving ≤NRs 500. Children with higher pocket money are more physically active due to better access to sports facilities, equipment and extracurricular activities.24

Our study demonstrated that nearly one-third of adolescents were current alcohol users. One of the studies conducted in Southeast Asian countries, particularly in Bangladesh (1.4%), Indonesia (2.5%), Maldives (4.9%) and Myanmar (0.9%) reported a lower proportion of current alcohol users among adolescents.13 However, this finding is higher than the other studies conducted in Nepal.16 25 The main reasons for the high prevalence might be the cultural and ceremonial acceptance of Tharu ethnicity for the homemade cereals brewed alcohol. Additionally, this study suggests that 13% of food is used for preparing alcohol in Tharu community which will ultimately lead to a higher consumption of alcohol.25 26

Alcohol consumption was significantly associated with sex, ethnicity and pocket money. Male adolescents were 2.6 times more likely to drink alcohol than their counterparts due to social and peer influence, greater exposure to alcohol and a tendency toward risk-taking behaviours. This study reported 2.8 times higher odds of current alcohol drinker among adolescents having higher average pocket money as more pocket money increases affordability, social opportunities and peer influence, leading to higher alcohol use.24 This finding is consistent with the GSHS conducted in 57 countries.23

Our study found that a notable proportion (8.1%) of adolescents were current smokers, which is slightly higher than a similar study conducted among Nepalese adolescents (6.04%).13 Meanwhile, the proportion of current smokers among early adolescents was low in a study conducted in India (1.1%) and Myanmar (1.7%).27 Participants who were males (AOR=2.7) and having higher average pocket money (AOR=2.8) had higher odds of current smoking.13 28 This result is in line with a similar study conducted in Kalaiya, Nepal.29 As increased financial resources make cigarettes more affordable, while social norms and peer influence among males further reinforce smoking behaviour.24 29

Despite a global decline in tobacco smoking, the proportion of tobacco smoking among adolescents has been increasing in Nepal. It is driven by aggressive marketing, easy access, peer pressure and a lack of targeted antismoking campaigns.30 31 Our study reported a lower prevalence of obesity (3.8%) which is coherent with the systematic review conducted in Nepal (4.6%), India (6.8%), and the global age-standardised study (5.6%).3234

Our study found that almost all of the adolescents had at least one clustered behavioural risk factor. This finding is comparable with the Nationwide School Survey (NSS).13 This indicates a high prevalence of multiple behavioural risk factors among adolescents, which underscores the need for comprehensive interventions to prevent and control in reducing NCD-related risk factors among adolescents.

Limitations

The study had several limitations. The key risk-factor variables (tobacco, alcohol, fruit/vegetable intake and physical activity) were based on self-reported data, which may be susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias, especially within a school environment. It involved a very small sample size, restricting subgroup analyses. As a cross-sectional design, it cannot establish causation. The sample was drawn from one region of Nepal, which limits generalisability; although its demographic representativeness was briefly compared with Census data. In addition, unmeasured confounding factors may have influenced the findings.

Conclusion

This study concludes that almost all the participants exhibited at least one behavioural risk factor related to NCDs. It highlights inadequate fruit and vegetable intake and insufficient physical activity among adolescents. Moreover, the prevalence of alcohol drinkers and smoking among adolescents is notably higher than reported in other surveys conducted in Nepal. This is a serious issue, as many of these risky behaviours acquired during adolescence tend to remain in the adulthood period of their life and each additional risk factor increases future health risks. The findings underscore the introduction of comprehensive interventions to tackle key risk factors. Schools and local authorities are key implementation platforms. Existing efforts (eg, mid-day meal programmes that currently reach mainly lower grades, school-based agriculture initiatives, mental health sessions and other extracurricular offerings) could be expanded and adapted for students in grades 8–10 to reinforce healthy eating, regular physical activity and broader life-skills development. Integrating age-appropriate nutrition and activity content into the secondary-school curriculum, increasing structured and unstructured opportunities for physical activity and strengthening school-community partnerships would add reach and sustainability. Policy enforcement is equally important: stricter implementation of age restrictions on alcohol and tobacco sales supported by routine inspection, vendor accountability and fiscal measures such as taxation has the potential to reduce youth uptake.
