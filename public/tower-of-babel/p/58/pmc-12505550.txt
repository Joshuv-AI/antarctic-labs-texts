Prevalence of migraine in Iceland
Authors: Larus S. Gudmundsson, Sunna D. Arnardottir, Dawn C. Buse, Ann I. Scher, Lenore J. Launer, Gretchen E. Tietjen, Richard B. Lipton, Jon H. Eliasson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere are few studies assessing the prevalence and characteristics of migraine in the Icelandic population. Previous Icelandic studies have methodological limitations and no study has been performed using full ICHD-3 criteria.MethodsThe study is based on data from a pilot for the SAGA (Stress-And-Gene-Analysis) cohort study conducted in 2014. The participants were women invited to cancer screening and men randomly sampled from the National Registry of Iceland. Participants answered an online health questionnaire including standardized, validated questions, used to identify migraine as defined by the ICHD-3 criteria. Estimates are provided for the one-year period prevalence of migraine overall, and the migraine subgroups migraine with aura, migraine without aura, and probable migraine.ResultsOf 1390 individuals invited to participate, 916 responded (65.9%). There were 402 men (44.0%) and 514 women (56.0%), 21 to 75 years of age with a median age of 50 years. The 1-year period prevalence of migraine was 14.4% overall, 19.3% in women and 8.2% in men with a female-to-male ratio of 2.3:1. The prevalence of subjects with migraine without aura (MO) but not migraine with aura (MA) was 6.3%, Conversely, the prevalence of subjects with MA who did not meet the criteria for MO was 4.9%. A total of 3.2% of the participants met the criteria for both migraine with and without aura (MA + MO). The prevalence of probable migraine was 2.6% overall, 2.7% in women and 2.5% in men.ConclusionsThe 1-year prevalence of migraine in Iceland was 14.4% overall, 19.3% in women and 8.2% in men. These rates are in line with European prevalence estimates and are slightly higher than United States estimates. The 1-year prevalence of probable migraine was 2.6%, 2.7% in women and 2.5% in men.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s10194-025-02092-2.

Background

Migraine is a common disorder worldwide [1]. The disabling headaches and other symptoms associated with migraine can pose a great burden on the patients as well as their families and the community [2]. Migraine ranks second globally for the most disabling neurological disorder after stroke [3] and it often severely reduces quality of life [4]. Nevertheless, migraine is underdiagnosed and undertreated [5].

The prevalence and characteristics of migraine in the Icelandic population has been little studied to date and previous Icelandic studies have methodological limitations. The Reykjavik Study used a modified version of the diagnostic criteria set by the International Headache Society in 1988 the International Classification of Headache Disorders, first edition (ICHD-1). The 1-year prevalence of migraine among women was 14.1% and among men 5.2% [6]. Another study from 2002 also used a modified version of ICHD-1. The prevalence of migraine was 13%, 19% in women and 7% in men. Migraine without aura (MO) was identified in 9.5% of participants and migraine with aura (MA) was identified in 6% of participants [7].

The aim of the present study was to determine the 1-year prevalence of migraine and probable migraine (PM) in the Icelandic population using the ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria [8]. This is the first migraine prevalence study in Iceland using full ICHD criteria.

Methods

Survey

The study is based on data from a pilot for the SAGA (Stress-And-Gene-Analysis) cohort study which was conducted in 2014. All women in Iceland aged 21 to 65 years are invited for cervical cancer screening and all women aged 40–75 years are invited for breast cancer screening. All women living in Iceland are included in the Icelandic universal health insurance. A sample of women, living in the Reykjavik capital area, who had accepted a screening invitation during February to April 2014 (n = 1870) and were attending regular breast and/or cervical cancer screening at the Icelandic Cancer Society were invited to participate in the study (n = 734). Of those the following were excluded due to: no registered telephone (n = 19), reduced sample when participating women surpassed the capacity for conducting clinical visit and bio sampling in current study (n = 21), residency outside the capital area (n = 11), had participated in a face validity study prior to current study (n = 2). Of the remaining 681 women invited 514 (75.4%) submitted a questionnaire. In 2014 the proportion of invited women attending cervical cancer screening was 73% and 60% for breast cancer screening (The Icelandic Cancer Society, annual report 2015).

Since a comparable systematic cancer screening among men did not cover the age below 50 years a random sample of men aged 21–75 years was selected from the National Registry of Iceland. A sample of 898 men was retrieved, of those 189 did not have a registered telephone number, of the remaining 709 invited 402 men agreed to participate (56.7%). The national average age in 2014 was 37.8 years for women and 36.6 years for men and life expectancy at birth 83.9 years for women and 80.8 years for men (Statistics Iceland). Participants answered an online questionnaire containing questions regarding their health. The part of the questionnaire which focused on headaches was used to diagnose migraine and PM.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire included specific closed-ended questions based on the 2013 ICHD-3 beta criteria which was used to diagnose migraine with aura, migraine without aura, and probable migraine. The migraine criteria in ICHD-3 beta from 2013 and the final ICHD-3 from 2018 are identical, so for sake of simplicity we refer to the criteria as ICHD-3, in this article. The validation of the questionnaire has been reported previously. Briefly, 150 subjects with and without migraine were interview by a neurologist (JHE): the sensitivity and specificity for migraine overall were 82.1% and 86.4%, respectively [9], Supplemental Table 1. The sensitivity and specificity for MA were 61.5% and 86.3%, respectively. The sensitivity and specificity for MO were 60.3% and 78.3%, respectively, Supplemental Tables 2 and 3. The questionnaire started with a screening question to establish one year headache prevalence: “Have you had a headache in the past 12 months which was not caused by hangover, fever, cold or other illnesses?”. A total of 39.6% answered the screening question with a yes, 45.5% of the women and 31.8% of the men.

Both men and women participants answered an extensive online questionnaire on trauma history, life stressors, psychological morbidities, and history of major diseases. The questionnaire included 16 screening questions on headache symptoms (based on ICHD-3 criteria) [10].

Of those 16 questions there were two screening questions on visual symptoms (migraine visual aura symptoms). Participants were asked if they had experienced any of the following visual symptoms before or during their headache attack: Bright spots, zig-zag lines, flashes of light, mirage, reading difficulties or blind spots (scotoma). Those who answered “yes” were then asked if the symptoms had occurred twice or more. Participants with at least two episodes of visual symptoms were asked about each of the visual symptoms above and about speed of onset, duration and frequency of visual symptoms. The migraine with aura prevalence calculations were solely based on the visual aura symptoms, but not sensory symptoms or speech disturbances as 98–99% of people with migraine aura have visual aura. Furthermore, visual aura is more reliably ascertained compared to other aura symptoms [11–13].

Case definition

An algorithm was used to identify people meeting criteria for migraine based on answers from the questionnaire. The respondents identified with migraine were further categorized by subtype, MO, MA, both MO and MA, probable migraine, or not migraine.

Participants met the ICHD-3 criteria for MO if they had experienced a severe headache attack in the past year and reported five or more lifetime attacks with the duration of 4–72 h that fulfilled any two of the following features: unilateral location, pulsatile pain, moderate to severe pain (at least a 40 on the scale of 0–100) and exacerbation with routine physical activity; and either nausea, vomiting or phonophobia with photophobia.

Participants met the ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria for MA if they reported at least two lifetime attacks of visual disturbances shortly before or during a headache attack and had experienced visual disturbances that developed gradually over 5–60 min or lasted between 5 and 60 min. Participants fulfilling ICHD-3 criteria for both MA and MO were classified as MA + MO.

In this study we also identified those with probable migraine using ICHD-3 criteria for PM where all but one of the ICHD migraine criteria were fulfilled.

Statistics/analysis

Frequencies of participants experiencing or meeting criteria for headache, migraine, MO, MA, and MA plus MO (MA + MO) are presented as a percentage of the total number of participants in the study. The analytic software used was R® version 1.1.383. The age standardized prevalence of migraine was also calculated using the Icelandic population in 2014 as a reference, with data from Statistics Iceland (www.statice.is). Due to a few subjects aged 70 years and older (n = 16) the direct age standardization was limited to age 69 years and younger, Supplemental Table 4.

Results

A total of 1390 individuals were invited to participate; 916 answered the questionnaire making the response rate 65.9%. There were 514 women (56.0%) and 402 men (44.0%). Survey participants were between 21 and 75 years of age with the median age of 50 years, average age for women was 52.3 years (SD 10.8) median 53 years and average age for men was 45.6 years (SD 13.2) median 46 years.

The overall 1-year prevalence of migraine was 14.4%, 19.3% in women and 8.2% in men as shown in Table 1. This included MA, MO and probable migraine. The female to male ratio was 2.3:1. The prevalence of subjects with MO who did not meet the criteria for MA was 6.3% and conversely prevalence of subjects with MA who did not meet the criteria for MO was 4.9%. A total of 3.2% of the participants were identified as suffering from MA + MO. Table 2 shows further distribution of those identified with different subtypes of migraine compared to other European and North American studies using ICHD criteria. Migraine with aura occurred in 34.3% of those with migraine (Table 3). The prevalence of probable migraine was 2.6% overall, 2.7% in women and 2.5% in men (Table 4).

The age standardized prevalence values were slightly higher 15.3% (95% confidence interval (CI) = 13.2–17.7%) overall, 22.6% (95% CI = 19.4–26.2%) for women and 9.2% (95% CI = 6.9–12.3%) for men (Supplemental Table 4).

Discussion

We provide the first estimates of the prevalence of migraine and PM in the adult population of Iceland using the ICHD-3 diagnostic criteria. Our results are in line with previous published results from other countries where migraine prevalence is higher among women compared to men for both MA and MO. The prevalence of migraine in the Icelandic population was 14.4%, which was similar to the prevalence in other European countries [19], higher than in the US population based American Migraine Prevalence and Prevention study which had reported prevalence of 11.8% [20] and similar to a previous Icelandic study from 2002, using modified ICHD-1 criteria, which showed migraine prevalence of 13% [7]. The review by Stovner et al. on the epidemiology of headaches in Europe showed prevalence of migraine in the range of 9.6% and 24.6%, with the median of eight different studies being 13.7% with a female to male ratio of 2.2:1 [19]. Most of these studies used the ICHD diagnostic criteria for migraine. These criteria are useful in comparing the prevalence of migraine between different studies and countries [19].

Among those with migraine, the proportion of those with migraine with aura in our study was 34.3% which similar to what was found by Zivadinov et al. (35.3%) [16] and by Rasmussen and Olesen (36.6%) but relatively high compared to what Matias et al. (25.0%) [15], Launer et al. (17.7%) [17] and Ferrante et al. (12.5%) [14] found but within the range of what population-based studies using clinician interviews reported (20–40%) [21]. The proportion of MA among people with migraine varies considerably between studies, ranging from 12.5% for women in the study by Ferrante et al. [14] to 36.6% in the study by Rasmussen and Olesen [18] (Table 3).

The summary in Table 3 shows the proportion of those identified with MA in our study compared to other similar studies where subjects of MO and MA were identified. The division of migraineurs into different subgroups of MO, MA and MA + MO may be important to facilitate better understanding of different subtypes of migraine. There are some known differences between individuals with MA and MO, for example MA is a known risk factor for cardiovascular events and cardiovascular mortality while most studies have not shown any increased risk among patients with MO [22–25].

The 1-year prevalence of probable migraine in our study is 2.6%, which adds to the burden of disease in the Icelandic population. Prior estimates of PM prevalence from Iceland are not available. Doing more studies on PM worldwide could be important to fully understand the burden of disease since many patients with PM have severe disabling attacks causing productivity loss [26]. In the US population based AMPP study the prevalence of PM was 5.3% among women and 3.9% among men [20] which is higher than the PM prevalence estimate in current study with 2.7% among women and 2.5% among men.

The results should be interpreted in the context of the strengths and limitations of the study. A strength of our study is the design of the questionnaire with questions allowing diagnosis using full ICHD-3 criteria. Furthermore, our questionnaire has been validated and has high sensitivity and specificity for migraine diagnosis [9]. Also, about two-thirds of Icelanders live in the Reykjavik capital area (Statistics Iceland), so this study represents an area where the majority of the Icelandic population lives.

A limitation of this study is the relatively small sample size, but since this is a pilot study a sample of 1390 was considered sufficient. One could argue that our screening question, for one year period prevalence of headache: “Have you had a headache in the past 12 months which was not caused by hangover, fever, cold or other illnesses?”, has too high a threshold for migraine diagnosis, which could lead to under-estimation of the one-year headache prevalence. A total of 39.4% of participants, in the current study, reported having headache as defined in this question that eliminated some common causes of secondary headache. In other studies this ratio has varied between 29 and 77% with the mean value of 51% [19]. Also, the response rate, in certain age groups, was low. Among women 21–29 and 30–39 years it was 20% compared with 65.9% overall response rate, leading to some under-representation of younger women. This is reflected in higher average age among women (52.3 years) vs. men (45.6 years). This low participation rate among women younger than 40 may explain why the age standardized migraine prevalence value (22.6%) was higher than the study prevalence value (19.3%). The standardized prevalence among men was 9.2% versus 8.2% and overall standardized value was 15.3% versus study prevalence of 14.4%. However, the age standardized value was within the 95% confidence interval for the study prevalence value, for women, men and overall. This low participation rate among women age under 40 may be explained by the fact that the age range for breast cancer screening among women is 40–74 years. A larger sample of men and women, including residents outside the Reykjavik capital area, is needed to get a better overview of the true prevalence of migraine representing the whole population of Iceland. A study on characteristics of women attending breast cancer screening in Iceland in 2018–2019 showed that neither educational status nor living in rural areas did affect participation. But less participation was observed among women with low income [27] and this may reduce the migraine prevalence values for women in the current study since low income has been associated with higher prevalence of migraine [28, 29]. It is of note that the sensitivity and specificity of detecting migraine overall in our study were 82.1% and 86.4%, respectively but sensitivity and specificity for MA were 61.5% and 86.3%, respectively and sensitivity and specificity for MO were 60.3% and 78.3%, respectively. The lower sensitivity for detecting MA and MO indicates a potential misclassification among participants between MO and MA. Since our estimates of MA prevalence are high in comparison to other studies, and since MO is more common than MA, the direction of misclassification may be from MO to MA. This may explain why the prevalence of MA with MA + MO, as a proportion of all with migraine, is relatively high in our study 34.0% + 22.2% = 56.5%. Thus, it seems that we may have overestimated the prevalence of MA. In supplemental Tables 2 and 3 we can see that during our validation process about half of the participants who got MA diagnosis by our algorithm did not get a MA diagnosis by a neurologist telephone interview, most of them were diagnosed with MO instead of MA. This indicates some misclassification from MO to MA in our study.

Many patients who have migraine attacks with aura also have attacks without aura; they should be coded as both 1.2 Migraine with aura and 1.1 Migraine without aura [8]. It must be kept in mind that ICHD criteria only require 2 or more lifetime experiences of aura for the diagnosis of MA and there are no time limits on when the aura was experienced so it could have been a long time since the aura experience or somewhat isolated experiences. This may further overestimate the one-year prevalence of MA in our study. However, as this study assesses one-year prevalence, by asking about attacks in the previous 12 months, emphasis should be placed on the one-year time interval. It is also possible that some subjects with non-migraine headache and aura (ICHD-3 coed 1.2.1.1) may be included as MA in our study. Finally, the study assessed only visual aura symptoms, so it is possible that people who experienced non-visual aura symptoms were not classified with aura. However, prior work suggests that 99% of people with MA have visual aura, alone, or in association with other aura types. Therefore, we are unlikely to have missed a large number of aura cases by confining our attention to visual aura [12].

A retrospective study, in primary care clinics in the capital area of Iceland, during the period 2010–2019, showed that 4.4% (95% CI 4.0–4.9%) of the population had visited a primary care center and received a migraine diagnosis [30]. In context with the present study showing a prevalence of 14.4% it seems that only one third of migraine patients seek treatment in primary care. Lower prevalence of migraine in general practice compared to population studies is understandable, since not all people with migraine seek medical care, this finding is consistent with other studies [31, 32].

A worldwide systemic review of 6.2 million people reported higher migraine prevalence in urban setting 11.2% vs. 8.4% in rural setting [33]. There are only a few studies that report urban vs. rural migraine prevalence in the Nordic countries. A Finnish study reported minimal differences in headache prevalence between people living in urban vs. rural areas a difference of + 0.9% among women and − 2.9% among women [34]. A population-based study in Sweden reported that prevalence of migraine in Sweden did not differ between the northern, middle and southern part of Sweden, or between urban and rural areas or different income groups [35]. This indicates that differences in migraine prevalence in the urban vs. rural population may be smaller in the Nordic countries compared to other countries. Still, further studies are needed in Iceland, with a nationwide sample, to estimate the prevalence rates of migraine in both the urban and rural population.

In conclusion, this study showed that the prevalence of migraine in Iceland was 14.4%, 19.3% in women and 8.2% in men, which is similar to other Western countries. The prevalence of probable migraine was 2.6% and it further adds to the burden of disease. Further prevalence studies are needed in Iceland, with a larger nationwide sample including rural as well as urban populations.
