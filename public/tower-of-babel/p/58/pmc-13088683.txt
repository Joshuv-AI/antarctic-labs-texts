Induction immunochemotherapy before definitive chemoradiotherapy versus definitive chemoradiotherapy alone in limited-stage small-cell lung cancer: a multicenter propensity score–matched analysis
Authors: Xin Hou, Yihan Guo, Meng Yan, Yangyang Zhang, Zheng Zhang, Yanglin Sun, Kai Ren, Xue Li, Xin Ding, Lujun Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThe purpose of this study is to evaluate the efficacy and safety of induction immunochemotherapy before definitive chemoradiotherapy (CRT) in patients with limited-stage small-cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC).Materials and methodsThis multicenter retrospective study included patients with LS-SCLC treated with definitive CRT, with or without induction immunochemotherapy from January 2021 to July 2024. The primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to control potential confounders. Survival was analyzed with Kaplan-Meier method and log-rank tests, and Cox regression was employed for prognostic analysis.ResultsOverall, 372 patients were included: 275 received definitive CRT alone (CRT group) and 97 received induction immunochemotherapy followed by definitive CRT (I-CRT group). Before PSM, the median OS was 30.3 months in the CRT group vs. not reached (NR) in the I-CRT group, with a 2-year OS of 59.4% vs. 72.2% (P = 0.006). The median PFS was 12.0 months vs. 22.5 months, with a 2-year PFS of 27.1% vs. 47.5%, respectively (P < 0.001). After 1:1 PSM, the median OS was 24.4 months in the CRT group vs. NR in the I-CRT group, with a 2-year OS of 50.1% vs. 70.4% (P = 0.002). The median PFS was 11.8 months vs. 21.9 months, with a 2-year PFS of 25.4% vs. 45.0%, respectively (P = 0.001). There were no significant differences in adverse events between the two groups.ConclusionFor LS-SCLC, the addition of induction immunochemotherapy to definitive CRT significantly improves clinical outcomes without increasing toxicity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13014-026-02819-8.Keywords: Limited-stage small-cell lung cancer, Induction immunochemotherapy, Chemoradiotherapy, Radiotherapy, Survival, Propensity score matching

Introduction

Small-cell lung cancer (SCLC) comprises about 10–15% of lung cancers and is noted for a rapid clinical course. Approximately 30% of patients present with limited-stage small-cell lung cancer (LS-SCLC) [1, 2]. Before the introduction of immunotherapy, the standard management of LS-SCLC centered on chemoradiotherapy (CRT). Despite favorable initial responses, the intrinsic aggressiveness and early metastatic potential of LS-SCLC resulted in suboptimal long-term outcomes. Several phase III trials have demonstrated that adding immune-checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) to first-line chemotherapy improves survival in extensive-stage SCLC (ES-SCLC) [3–6]. Likewise, ICIs have shown promise in LS-SCLC. For example, durvalumab consolidation is recommended for patients with LS-SCLC who achieve disease control after CRT, given the survival benefit observed with durvalumab in the phase III ADRIATIC trial [7]. However, the NRG-LU005 trial, which evaluated concurrent atezolizumab with CRT followed by atezolizumab consolidation, did not meet its primary survival endpoint [8]. Therefore, further investigation is needed to determine the preferred strategy for combining immunotherapy with definitive CRT in LS-SCLC.

Many patients with LS-SCLC present with substantial tumor burden at diagnosis, typically requiring chemotherapy before radiotherapy. However, whether adding immunotherapy to this induction regimen further improves efficacy remains uncertain. Motivated by this uncertainty, we considered the established efficacy of induction immunochemotherapy in non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) as a conceptual framework. In resectable NSCLC, this approach has been shown to increase pathological complete response (pCR) rates [9–12]. Similarly, in unresectable, locally advanced NSCLC, induction immunochemotherapy followed by CRT has demonstrated favorable efficacy with a manageable safety profile [13–16]. Based on these rationales, this multicenter retrospective cohort evaluated outcomes and tolerability of induction immunochemotherapy followed by CRT in LS-SCLC.

Materials and methods

Patient population

Clinical data were retrospectively collected from patients with LS-SCLC treated at three centers (Tianjin Medical University Cancer Hospital, Tianjin; The Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University, Xuzhou; The First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China, Hefei) from January 2021 to July 2024. Inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically or cytologically confirmed stage I–III LS-SCLC not amenable to surgical resection (American Joint Committee on Cancer, 8th edition); (2) receipt of definitive CRT with or without pre-radiotherapy induction immunochemotherapy; (3) Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) 0–1; and (4) age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria were: (1) prior treatment with any anticancer therapy; (2) synchronous second primary malignancy; (3) participation in another interventional drug trial; (4) incomplete medical records; (5) concurrent immunotherapy during radiotherapy or consolidation immunotherapy after CRT. Ethical approval was secured from the Institutional Review Board. (No. bc20254192). The requirement for informed consent was waived for its nature of retrospective study.

Treatment and toxicities

All patients received induction chemotherapy with etoposide plus cisplatin or carboplatin regimen, in combination with or without ICIs every 3 weeks. The ICI agents included adebrelimab, atezolizumab, durvalumab, serplulimab, tislelizumab, and toripalimab, all of which have regulatory approval in China for use in ES-SCLC. The number of induction cycles was determined at the treating physician’s discretion based on tumor response. For patients receiving concurrent chemoradiotherapy, the etoposide–platinum regimen was generally used during the radiotherapy course, typically for 1–2 cycles.

Radiotherapy was delivered with 6-MV photons using intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT) or volumetric-modulated arc therapy (VMAT). The gross tumor volume (GTV) encompassed the primary tumor and involved lymph nodes. The clinical target volume (CTV) included the primary tumor and nodal stations with involvement, constructed by expanding the GTV by 0.5 cm and incorporating involved nodal regions. The planning target volume (PTV) was defined as an isotropic 0.5 cm expansion from the CTV. Conventional radiotherapy (60 Gy/30 once-daily fractions), hyperfractionated radiotherapy (45 Gy/30 twice-daily fractions), or hypofractionated radiotherapy (45 Gy/15 once-daily fractions) were selected by the treating radiation oncologists based on the individualized treatment plan and clinical status. Patients achieving a complete or partial response (CR/PR) after CRT were advised to receive prophylactic cranial irradiation (PCI).

Endpoints

The primary endpoints were progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and treatment-related safety. PFS was measured from treatment initiation to the earliest of radiographic progression, death, or last follow-up, adjudicated per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 OS was measured from treatment initiation until death from any cause or the date of last follow-up. Adverse events (AEs) were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

Comparisons of clinical characteristics and adverse event rates were conducted using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to estimate survival, and log-rank tests were applied to compare survival curves. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted to identify prognostic factors, with variables meeting P < 0.10 in univariate analyses included in the multivariate model. Confounding was addressed using 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) with a caliper of 0.1. Subgroup analyses used an unstratified Cox proportional hazards model with treatment as a covariate. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided P < 0.05. All analyses were conducted in SPSS version 25.0 and R version 4.1.3.

Results

Patients clinical characteristics

In total, 554 patients with stage I–III LS-SCLC, unresectable, and treated with definitive CRT were screened. After applying inclusion rules, 372 were deemed eligible and analyzed (Fig. 1). Of these, 275 (73.9%) received definitive CRT alone (CRT group) and 97 (26.1%) received induction immunochemotherapy followed by definitive CRT (I-CRT group). By September 30, 2025, the median follow-up duration for the entire cohort was 29.8 months (IQR, 22.3–38.5).

We defined early radiotherapy as that initiated within 3 cycles (≤ 3 cycles) of induction chemotherapy or immunochemotherapy, and late radiotherapy as that initiated after 3 cycles of systemic therapy. The CRT modality was defined by the treatment received after induction therapy: radiotherapy alone (Sequential) or radiotherapy administrated concurrently with chemotherapy (Concurrent). In the I-CRT group, the median number of induction immunochemotherapy cycles was 4 (IQR, 2–5). The immunotherapy agents included atezolizumab (n = 28), serplulimab (n = 26), durvalumab (n = 19), and others (Supplementary Table 1).

Compared with the I-CRT group, the CRT group exhibited higher rates of smoking, early radiotherapy, concurrent CRT, and PCI utilization (all P < 0.05). After PSM for sex, age, smoking history, ECOG PS, clinical stage, radiotherapy modality, radiotherapy timing, CRT modality, and PCI, 90 matched patients were retained in each group for further analysis. The median time from diagnosis to treatment initiation was 6 days in the CRT group and 7 days in the I-CRT group, and the median time from diagnosis to radiotherapy initiation was 2.1 and 2.6 months, respectively. Survival outcomes

Before PSM, the median OS was 30.3 months (95% CI, 25.8–34.4) in the CRT group vs. not reached (NR) (95% CI, 32.0–NR) in the I-CRT group, with a 2-year OS of 59.4% vs. 72.2% (HR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.40–0.86; P = 0.006) (Fig. 2A). The median PFS was 12.0 months (95% CI, 11.0–14.0) vs. 22.5 months (95% CI, 17.0–37.5), with a 2-year PFS of 27.1% vs. 47.5%, respectively (HR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.41–0.75; P < 0.001) (Fig. 2B).

Kaplan-Meier curves for OS (A) and PFS (B) comparing the I-CRT and CRT groups before PSM. Kaplan-Meier curves for OS (C) and PFS (D) comparing the I-CRT and CRT groups after PSM. OS, overall survival; PFS, progression-free survival; CRT, chemoradiotherapy; I-CRT, immunochemotherapy plus chemoradiotherapy; PSM, propensity score matching

After PSM, the median OS was 24.4 months (95% CI, 20.6–31.0) in the CRT group vs. NR (95% CI, 32.0–NR) in the I-CRT group, with a 2-year OS of 50.1% vs. 70.4% (HR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.33–0.80; P = 0.002) (Fig. 2C). The median PFS was 11.8 months (95% CI, 8.9–14.0) vs. 21.9 months (95% CI, 15.1–31.1), with a 2-year PFS of 25.4% vs. 45.0%, respectively (HR, 0.55; 95% CI, 0.38–0.79; P = 0.001) (Fig. 2D).

Risk factors of OS and PFS

Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified induction immunochemotherapy (yes vs. no, P < 0.001), PCI (yes vs. no, P = 0.010), and clinical stage (IIIC vs. IIB–IIIA, P = 0.021) as independent predictors of OS; induction immunochemotherapy (yes vs. no, P < 0.001) and PCI (yes vs. no, P < 0.001) independently predicted longer PFS. These factors remained independently associated with survival after PSM (Tables 2 and 3).

The impact of timing of radiotherapy on the role of immunochemotherapy

The impact of the number of induction immunochemotherapy cycles was evaluated within the I-CRT cohort. Using the median number of induction immunochemotherapy cycles as the cutoff, I-CRT patients were stratified into two groups: those receiving radiotherapy within 3 cycles (≤ 3-cycle group; n = 48) and those initiating it after 3 cycles (> 3-cycle group; n = 49). After 1:1 PSM for sex, age, smoking history, ECOG PS, clinical stage, radiotherapy modality, CRT modality, and PCI, baseline features were comparable across the groups (Supplementary Table 3). The median OS was NR (95% CI, 32.0–NR) in the ≤ 3-cycles group vs. 35.6 months (95% CI, 22.6–NR) in the > 3-cycles group (P = 0.021) (Supplementary Fig. 1A). The median PFS was NR (95% CI, 26.3–NR) vs. 17.0 months (95% CI, 10.1–NR), respectively (P = 0.065) (Supplementary Fig. 1B).

Subgroup and exploratory analyses

In subgroup analyses, induction immunochemotherapy was associated with improved OS and PFS across most strata (Fig. 3A; B). In an exploratory analysis of the I-CRT cohort, PFS and OS were similar in patients who received PD-1 inhibitors (serplulimab, tislelizumab, toripalimab; n = 35) and those who received PD-L1 inhibitors (atezolizumab, durvalumab, adebrelimab; n = 62) after PSM (OS: HR = 0.99, 95% CI, 0.39–2.55; P = 0.995; PFS: HR = 1.34, 95% CI, 0.63–2.82; P = 0.443) (Supplementary Table 4; Supplementary Fig. 2).

To evaluate the impact of fractionation heterogeneity, we repeated the primary analysis in the propensity score-matched cohort after excluding the 12 patients who received hypofractionated radiotherapy. The survival benefit of I-CRT remained significant (PFS: HR = 0.48; 95% CI, 0.33–0.71; P < 0.001; OS: HR = 0.47; 95% CI, 0.29–0.74; P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 5; Supplementary Fig. 3).

To evaluate potential confounding from differences in CRT modality (sequential vs. concurrent), we performed an exploratory analysis stratified by CRT modality. In the sequential CRT stratum after PSM, I-CRT was associated with improved OS (HR = 0.41; 95% CI, 0.24–0.68; P < 0.001) and PFS (HR = 0.48; 95% CI, 0.31–0.75; P < 0.001) compared with CRT (Supplementary Table 6; Supplementary Fig. 4). In the concurrent CRT stratum, no patients in the I-CRT group received PCI. We therefore restricted subsequent analyses to patients who did not receive PCI. In this subgroup, outcomes numerically favored the I-CRT group but were not statistically significant (OS: HR = 0.49; 95% CI, 0.16–1.54; P = 0.213; PFS: HR = 0.49; 95% CI, 0.20–1.18; P = 0.102) (Supplementary Table 7; Supplementary Fig. 5).

Safety profile

In the overall cohort, a single episode of Grade 5 pneumonia was recorded in each of the groups. Adverse event rates did not differ between the two groups (Supplementary Table 8). Similarly, in the matched cohort, there was no significant difference in the incidence of AEs between groups (Table 4). Furthermore, the incidence of treatment toxicities were also similar both in the induction therapy phase and in the CRT phase (Supplementary Tables 9 and 10).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first multicenter retrospective analysis evaluating the efficacy and safety of induction immunochemotherapy plus definitive CRT in LS-SCLC. Our findings demonstrate that adding induction immunochemotherapy to definitive CRT significantly improves survival outcomes in LS-SCLC, with an acceptable safety profile.

Immunotherapy has expanded the treatment landscape for LS-SCLC. The phase III ADRIATIC study showed that among LS-SCLC patients who remained progression-free after CRT, durvalumab consolidation prolonged median OS and PFS by 22.5 and 7.4 months, respectively, compared with placebo [7]. In this study, the I-CRT group had a median OS that was NR, and the median PFS was 21.9 months. The 2-year OS and PFS rates appeared numerically similar to those reported in the ADRIATIC trial (OS: 70.4% vs. 68.0%; PFS: 45.0% vs. 46.2%), underscoring a favorable survival performance. However, differences in time origin for OS and PFS (treatment initiation in our study vs. randomization in ADRIATIC), patient populations, and treatment regimens warrant caution in cross-trial comparisons. Notably, the negative results of the NRG-LU005 trial, which evaluated concurrent atezolizumab with CRT, suggest that the benefit of immunotherapy in LS-SCLC may depend on its sequence relative to radiotherapy. Consistent with this concept, two phase III trials [17, 18] in unresectable stage III NSCLC likewise failed to show a survival benefit with concurrent immunotherapy plus chemoradiotherapy (followed by consolidation), supporting the importance of treatment sequencing. Preclinical and clinical evidence indicates that radiotherapy to tumor-draining lymph nodes may impair the function of dendritic cells and antigen presentation, potentially blunting the systemic immune response to ICIs [19, 20]. Accordingly, the concurrent approach in NRG-LU005 may have inadvertently limited atezolizumab efficacy via this mechanism. By contrast, our strategy of administering immunotherapy first may circumvent this issue by delivering immunotherapy before substantial irradiation of the lymphatic basin. In addition, induction immunochemotherapy often reduces primary tumor volume, enabling smaller, more focal radiation fields that spare immune-competent tissue. Collectively, our findings suggest that induction immunochemotherapy may yield survival outcomes numerically similar to those reported with durvalumab consolidation in the ADRIATIC trial, although such cross-trial comparisons should be interpreted cautiously. Confirmation of these observations will require future prospective, randomized controlled trials.

At present, clinical evidence for induction immunochemotherapy in LS-SCLC remains limited. Of the two studies evaluating surgery after induction immunochemotherapy in LS-SCLC, one demonstrated significantly higher rates of pCR (P = 0.016) and major pathological response (MPR, P < 0.001) with induction immunochemotherapy versus induction chemotherapy [21]. The other study reported a downstaging effect in the induction immunochemotherapy group [22]. Although surgery following induction immunochemotherapy is not the conventional approach in LS-SCLC, prior studies [21, 22] suggest that induction immunochemotherapy may play a key role in tumor downstaging and micrometastasis eradication. Furthermore, a phase III trial is evaluating adebrelimab administered across all phases of therapy (induction, concurrent and consolidation immunotherapy) for LS-SCLC [23], which showed promising efficacy with a manageable safety profile in the safety run-in phase. A prospective phase II trial reported that, in LS-SCLC, induction immunochemotherapy followed by concurrent CRT and consolidation immunotherapy improved median OS (NR vs. 23.9 months; P = 0.044) and PFS (NR vs. 11.4 months; P = 0.047) versus induction chemotherapy followed by concurrent CRT alone [24], aligning with our findings. However, the two prior prospective trials may not identify which phase(s) of immunotherapy contributed to the observed survival benefit. For further analysis, we excluded patients who received concurrent immunotherapy during radiotherapy or consolidation immunotherapy after radiotherapy, thereby isolating the effect of induction immunochemotherapy plus CRT. This design provides real-world evidence for the specific benefit of induction immunochemotherapy in LS-SCLC.

Before the immunotherapy era, multiple studies established that earlier initiation of radiotherapy was associated with superior survival outcomes compared to delayed radiotherapy in patients with LS-SCLC [25–28]. In the immunotherapy era, some evidence from NSCLC indicates that initiating radiotherapy after 2–3 cycles of induction immunochemotherapy optimizes survival outcomes [13, 15, 29]. In our exploratory analysis of induction immunochemotherapy cycles, we found that initiating radiotherapy within no more than 3 cycles was associated with improved OS and PFS (Supplementary Fig. 1). These findings suggests that, even with induction immunochemotherapy, radiotherapy should be initiated as early as feasible. This observation may be attributable to the intrinsically aggressive biology of LS-SCLC, in which an extended induction phase can postpone definitive local control, thereby allowing for disease progression.

The addition of induction immunochemotherapy did not appear to increase hematologic toxicity. The incidence of grade 3–4 pneumonia was 3.1% reported in the ADRIATIC [7], which is slightly lower than our rate of 5.6%. This discrepancy likely reflects differences in patient populations. Our I-CRT cohort comprised exclusively stage III patients (100%), versus 87.5% in the ADRIATIC durvalumab arm. More advanced disease typically entails larger radiotherapy target volumes, which may increase pneumonia risk. Additionally, in NRG-LU005 [8], the reported rate of grade 3–4 pneumonia was similar to ours. Xu et al. also reported that camrelizumab-based induction did not increase the risk of radiation pneumonitis [24]. Moreover, the incidence of grade 5 events in our induction immunochemotherapy cohort was 1.1%, similar to the 0.8% observed in the ADRIATIC durvalumab arm. Overall, the safety profile of induction immunochemotherapy followed by definitive CRT for LS-SCLC was acceptable.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample size of I-CRT group was relatively small, which may limit our findings’ generalizability. In addition, subgroup analyses in the matched cohort may have been underpowered. For example, in the “CRT modality (concurrent vs sequential)” subgroup, the OS estimate favored I-CRT but the confidence interval crossed 1, and subgroup results should therefore be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory. Second, in the I-CRT cohort, multiple PD-1 and PD-L1 inhibitors were used. Although an exploratory analysis comparing PD-1 versus PD-L1 inhibitors showed no significant differences in OS or PFS, agent-specific comparisons were underpowered, so residual confounding due to ICI heterogeneity may remain. Finally, a low proportion of patients in the I-CRT group received concurrent CRT, which differs from the conventional concurrent CRT used in many historical LS-SCLC studies. Although CRT modality was included in the propensity model and exploratory analyses were stratified by this factor, the small number of patients, in combination with limited use of PCI in the I-CRT group, produced imprecise estimates. Consequently, prospective studies are warranted to further clarify the role of induction immunochemotherapy in patients with LS-SCLC.

Conclusion

In this multicenter retrospective analysis, it was found that induction immunochemotherapy before definitive CRT was associated with improved survival and a manageable safety profile compared with definitive CRT alone. However, given the retrospective design, treatment heterogeneity, and limited size of sample numbers, these findings should be interpreted cautiously and warrant validation in future prospective, randomized controlled trials.
