A rare drug reaction: Toxic epidermal necrolysis following polymyxin B administration in a post-Bentall procedure
Authors: Bhavin B. Patel, Rachana B. Patel, Nisarg R. Hajariwala, Pankaj Maheshwari, Anil Ganju, Chetan Trivedi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Polymyxin B is a last-resort antibiotic used to treat multidrug-resistant (MDR) Gram-negative infections. While nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity are well-documented, severe dermatologic adverse reactions such as toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) are exceedingly uncommon but clinically significant. A 54-year-old hypertensive male underwent an emergency Bentall procedure for DeBakey type I aortic dissection. The patient developed acute kidney injury requiring dialysis. The patient subsequently developed polymyxin B-sensitive Acinetobacter lwoffii septicemia, for which intravenous polymyxin B was initiated. On day 6 of therapy, the patient developed a widespread erythematous rash that progressed to bullous eruptions and skin sloughing involving over 30% body surface area. A clinical diagnosis of TEN was made. Polymyxin B was discontinued immediately, and the patient was managed with corticosteroids and wound care. The patient demonstrated complete clinical recovery. This case underscores the importance of heightened clinical suspicion for polymyxin B-induced toxic epidermal necrolysis and early withdrawal of the offending agent to ensure prompt detection and recovery.

Introduction

Toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) is a life-threatening mucocutaneous disorder characterized by widespread epidermal necrosis and detachment involving more than 30% of the body surface area (BSA).[12] The pathogenesis involves cytotoxic T lymphocyte activation and release of granulysin, and leading to massive keratinocyte apoptosis.[34]

While TEN is classically associated with sulfonamides, antiepileptics, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, uncommon but clinically significantly used antimicrobials like polymyxin B are not commonly implicated.[56] With increasing global use of polymyxins in intensive care units (ICUs) for treating MDR Gram-negative infections, awareness of uncommon but severe adverse drug reactions is critical.[7]

Case Report

A 54-year-old male with known hypertension presented with acute bilateral lower limb weakness and jaw pain to other hospital, patient presented to us in an arrested state so cardiopulmonary resuscitation was given as per recent American Heart Association guideline for 3 min. The patient was diagnosed with acute DeBakey type I aortic dissection involving the entire aorta, arch vessels, and iliac arteries, confirmed through computed tomography aortography [Figure 1]. Emergency Bentall procedure with a 21 CAVGJ-514 SJM Masters Series valved graft was performed on the day of arrival. Immediate postoperative care included mechanical ventilation, vasopressor support, and prophylactic antibiotics (meropenem, teicoplanin) as the procedure was in emergency with multiple lines in situ. By postoperative day 5, the patient developed oliguria and worsening creatinine (peak 5.24 mg/dL), necessitating dialysis. Blood culture was sent in view of new onset of fever which grew Acinetobacter lwoffii, sensitive only to polymyxin B. Intravenous polymyxin B (15,00,000 IU loading, followed by 5,00,000 IU twice a day) was started.

On day 6 of polymyxin B therapy, the patient developed an erythematous rash over the upper limbs and chest, which rapidly spread to the abdomen and groin, forming bullae and widespread skin detachment (>30% BSA). Nikolsky’s sign was positive. Dermatology consultation confirmed a clinical diagnosis of TEN. The patient had a SCORTEN score of 2 (age >40 years and >10% body surface area involvement), predicting an approximate 12% mortality risk. Mucosal involvement was absent.

Polymyxin B was continued for two to three doses after the onset of skin lesions, initially the skin lesions were suspected to be some local allergic reaction. After diagnosis of TEN, polymyxin B was stopped, and the patient was started on methylprednisolone 60 mg IV once daily for 3 days; later, oral prednisone was administered for 10 days in a tapering dose [Figure 2]. From a sepsis perspective injection levofloxacin was started as it was intermediate in culture sensitivity report and continued for 14 days. Wound care was managed with dry collagen sheet applications under plastic surgery supervision. The patient showed clinical improvement with resolution of skin lesions and gradual re-epithelialization. The patient was weaned off dialysis, and renal function improved. The patient was discharged in a stable condition with advice for follow-up after 15 days. On follow up serum creatinine was within normal limits, and skin lesions were in the healing stage. After 1 month, serum creatinine continued to be normal, and skin lesions were nearly healed to normal skin.

Discussion

TEN is among the most severe forms of cutaneous adverse drug reactions. It is commonly drug-induced and carries a mortality risk of 30%–50%.[12] Pathophysiologically, drug-specific T-cells initiate keratinocyte apoptosis via granulysin, Fas–FasL, and tumor necrosis factor-alpha.[34]

Polymyxin B is reserved for MDR Gram-negative infections and is known for dose-dependent nephrotoxicity and neurotoxicity.[5] Its cutaneous adverse effects are uncommon but clinically significantly reported. Skin hyperpigmentation and urticaria are described more often than severe reactions like TEN.[89] In this case, the temporal association with polymyxin B therapy initiation, lack of alternate triggers, and resolution upon drug cessation establish a probable causality (Naranjo score 6).[10]

The SCORTEN score is a validated severity tool for TEN. The patient had a score of 2 (age >40 years and >10% BSA), predicting a ~12% mortality risk.[11] Although systemic corticosteroid use in TEN remains controversial, early short-course therapy was used successfully in this case, in line with recent evidence.[1012]

This case is uncommon but clinically significant reported instances of polymyxin B-induced TEN, highlighting the potential for uncommon but clinically significant but severe adverse reactions in postoperative ICU the patients.[13]

Conclusion

Polymyxin B-induced TEN is an extremely uncommon yet life-threatening adverse drug reaction. Clinicians should remain vigilant for early skin symptoms in the patients on polymyxin therapy. Prompt recognition, drug withdrawal, and intensive multidisciplinary care – including dermatology, wound management, and systemic immunosuppression – can lead to full recovery.
