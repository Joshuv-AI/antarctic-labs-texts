Redox chemistry of early Earth and the origin of life
Authors: Nurbubu T. Moldogazieva, Alexander A. Terentiev, Innokenty M. Mokhosoev, Dmitry V. Astakhov
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Multiple controversial scenarios have been proposed explaining the emergence of life, both from environmental and chemical perspectives. These scenarios discuss environmental settings including deep-ocean hydrothermal vents, volcanic lakes, hot springs, and geysers, which could have served as the cradle for life. Environmental conditions varied by energy, chemical elements, minerals, transition metals, dynamic light-dark, hot-cold, and wet-dry cycles for the first organic synthesis. Other controversies concern the type of organisms that could have appeared first: either heterotrophic or autotrophic. The heterotrophic theory suggests that the first organic compounds were abiotically synthesized from simple inorganic molecules accumulating in the oceans and concentrating in coacervates, which functioned as early pre-cellular structures. The chemoautotrophic theory argues that life originated from inorganic compounds, rather than from organic matter establishing proto-metabolic pathways in primary producers. All of the above-mentioned theories rely on redox reactions that created necessary conditions and molecules for the origin of life. This review explores evidences that reconcile the dominant theories, including that: (i) the Hadean atmosphere was weakly oxidizing, but transiently reducing, (ii) hydrothermal systems provided energy for organic synthesis supporting both heterotrophs and chemoautotrophs, (iii) organic compounds were transported between different environments, and (iv) life emerged in multiple local environments.

Introduction

The Earth’s long history is divided into distinct time units according to a geological time scale that is hierarchically organized into eons, eras, periods, and epochs1. This division is based on the age of rocks, fossils, and sediments, which is determined using absolute (for example, radiometric) or relative (for example, stratigraphic) methods2. Studying the geological, chemical, and biological processes that occurred in the Earth’s terrestrial, atmospheric, and aquatic environments over millions of years is used to determine the boundaries between these time units1. However, challenges still exist in the chronometric division of these time intervals, and therefore, the current system of geological time scale is being systematically improved to eliminate these disadvantages3. According to the current geological time scale, approximately 88% of Earth’s history comprises the Precambrian, which spans from ca. 4.567 to 0.541 billion years ago (Ga). The Precambrian is an informal time interval classified as a supereon and composed of three eons: the Hadean, Archean, and Proterozoic. The Hadean eon was the earliest of them and lasted from ca. 4.567 to 4.031 Ga, being marked by the initial formation of the Solar System’s planets, including Earth4. The Archean eon, which lasted from ca. 4.031 to 2.420 Ga was followed by the Proterozoic eon, which spanned from ca. 2.420 to 0.541 Ga3. The Archean and Proterozoic eons were characterized by the most dramatic transformations of the Earth’s environments and biosphere5,6. Terrestrial and atmospheric conditions during the Archean eon led to the emergence of first unicellular organisms and oxygenic photosynthesis7,8, whereas the Proterozoic was distinguished by permanent oxygenation of the Earth’s atmosphere and oceans9. However, it was during the Hadean eon, known as the early Earth, that physicochemical conditions favorable for prebiotic synthesis of organic molecules probably already existed in the atmosphere, hydrosphere, and lithosphere, which eventually led to the emergence of life10,11.

The initial redox state of prebiotic Earth was probably established by the creation of a magma ocean during accretion, with subsequent differentiation of the metallic core and mantle through sequestration of iron into the core and oxidation of the mantle12. Later, plate tectonics, volcanic outgassing, the escape of gases into space, gas exchange between the atmosphere and the ocean, and between the deep-ocean and the mantle, as well as UV radiation, asteroid impacts, and weathering influenced the redox conditions of Earth’s environments13,14. These processes controlled geochemical cycles of redox-active elements including carbon, oxygen, sulfur, nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, and others15,16. Volcanic activity could have outgassed sulfur dioxide (SO2) and hydrogen sulfide (H2S), key components of the global sulfur cycle17. In prebiotic conditions, the sulfur cycle involved the photolysis of volcanic SO2 by UV radiation in an anoxic atmosphere, the release of sulfide-rich fluids from the Earth’s mantle into oceans through hydrothermal circulation, their burial in sediments as minerals such as ferrous disulfide (FeS2), also known as pyrite, and the weathering of sulfides and sulfate minerals in the oceanic crust18.

Over long geological time scales, sulfur and iron cycles could have been coupled through redox reactions, involving the formation of iron sulfides. This controlled the circulation of other gases and, consequently, the redox chemistry of the atmosphere-ocean system, which was associated with the emergence of life. Indeed, FeS2 could be formed under hydrothermal and volcanic conditions by the reaction between H2S and ferrous sulfide (FeS)19. In deep-sea hydrothermal environments, ultramafic rocks undergo serpentinization, a process that involves the oxidation of ferrous ion (Fe2+) to ferric ion (Fe3+), coupled with the reduction of H2O to H220,21. These observations allowed Günter Wächtershäuser to formulate his hypothesis of the autotrophic origin of life, known as the “iron-sulfide world”22. The hypothesis is based on the assumption that the reaction of FeS2 formation is exergonic enough to provide energy and electrons for carbon fixation through CO2 reduction by H2, which leads to the production of various organic molecules. The discovery of iron-sulfur (FeS) cluster-containing oxidoreductase enzymes, such as ferredoxin in the first universal common ancestor (LUCA), which existed in the late Hadean, and the key role of sulfur in cell metabolism support this theory23,24.

Carbon fixation is a part of the global carbon cycle and is the process in which inorganic carbon, in particular, CO2, is converted into a variety of organic compounds25,26. Under modern conditions, biological carbon assimilation primarily occurs through photosynthesis, using energy of light utilized by photoautotrophs and photoheterotrophs27 and chemosynthesis using energy of chemical reactions utilized by chemoautotrophs28. Modern chemoautotrophs include methanogenic and acetogenic archaea and bacteria that are found in various extreme habitats such as deep-sea hydrothermal vents as well as acidic and high-pressure environments29–31. Their metabolism has been used as the basis for the chemoautotrophic theory of the origin of life, which suggests that chemoautotrophs were the first life forms on Earth32. On the prebiotic Hadean Earth, the carbon cycle likely included various long-term geological processes, such as rock weathering, metamorphism, and diagenesis33. The volcanic activity, plate tectonics, and mantle subduction contributed the concentration of CO2 in the atmosphere and influenced climate change34,35. Under Hadean conditions, outgassing from oxidized silicate mantle could have served as a source of CO2 in the atmosphere, which was further dissolved in ocean water36. This led to slightly acidic seawater, creating a pH and redox gradient between alkaline seafloor hydrothermal vents and acidic ocean waters, which could drive the synthesis of organic compounds37.

Nitrogen fixation, the conversion of inert N2 gas into metabolically active nitrogen compounds such as NH3, nitrite (NO2-), and nitrate (NO3-), is an essential part of the nitrogen cycle38. From a chemical point of view, nitrogen fixation is the activation of N2 through redox reactions, which require overcoming thermodynamic barriers due to the presence of a triple bond in N239. In modern conditions, this process is carried out by nitrogen-fixing bacteria, which use FeS cluster-containing enzymes, known as nitrogenases, that decrease the activation energy of the reaction40. On prebiotic Earth, nitrogen fixation may have occurred with the involvement of various energy sources such as UV radiation, lightning, and meteorite impacts41,42. The rate of abiotic nitrogen fixation was likely lower than that of the biogenic process but still comparable43. On early Earth, various high-energy events could have caused the reaction between NH3 and CH4 delivered by impacts to produce hydrogen cyanide (HCN), thereby creating HCN-rich atmosphere44. HCN is a biochemically accessible form of nitrogen, which could have been rained into aquatic environments for further reactions of nucleotide synthesis45. These data support the heterotrophic theory of the emergence of life and contribute to the “RNA world” hypothesis, which advocates the “replication first” principle46. Alternatively, the “metabolism first” principle argues that the origin of life began with the emergence of simple metabolic cycles, catalyzed by minerals and transition metals, and occurred in the early Earth’s oceans47. These primitive metabolic cycles likely created organic building blocks, which eventually led to the formation of biopolymers such as proteins and RNA, and then the genetic replication capacity could have been developed. The “metabolism-first” principle allows combining heterotrophic and chemoautotrophic theories by synchronizing the evolutionary routes of these two types of organisms48,49.

In our review, we explore the redox conditions that may have existed in the Hadean lithosphere, atmosphere, and hydrosphere, in order understand the origin of life. We consider various oxidation-reduction reactions, which activated inert inorganic molecules essential for prebiotic organic synthesis. We discuss the dominant theories of the emergence of life from both an environmental and chemical perspectives. We examine how initial prebiotic redox reactions formed the basis and forced the diversification of proto-metabolism, which triggered the origin of life. Life could have emerged from different interplayed environments on early Earth, including deep-ocean hydrothermal vents, atmosphere, and subaerial environments such as shallow lakes, ponds, and hot springs. These environments acted as chemical reactors for prebiotic synthesis of organic molecules.

Hadean lithosphere and geodynamics

There are significant uncertainties, controversies, and debates regarding the formation of the Solar System’s planets. The dominant scenario proposes the formation of planets and stars from a protoplanetary disk composed of gases, mostly H2 and helium (He), and dust50. The abundance of solid material in the protoplanetary disk probably caused its clustering and compression under the influence of self-gravity, leading to the formation of bodies known as planetesimals51. It has been suggested that Earth was formed from the accretion of these planetesimals, which were melted to create a magma ocean due to heat, which was produced by accretional impacts and the decay of short-lived radioactive elements52. Giant impacts could have contributed immense heat sufficient to melt a significant portion of the planet’s interior, depending on the impact speed and size53. The Moon-forming event is the most recognized hypothetical giant impact by an object called Theia, which may have occurred between ca. 4.50 and 4.35 Ga54,55. This impact probably melted the Earth’s mantle contributing to the production of magma ocean and vaporizing water from the mantle. Within the first tens to hundreds of millions of years after this event, re-homogenization of the Earth could have occurred followed by differentiation into the core, mantle, and crust56,57. The U–Th–Pb dating of zircon (ZrSiO4) grains aged 4.4 Ga-old provided evidence for the existence of a magma ocean by an early Moon-forming impact at ca. 4.5 Ga58. Subsequent cooling and solidification of a magma ocean led to the formation of primordial crustal and mantle structures and the release of volatile elements that contributed to the Earth’s atmosphere59.

It has been suggested that the Earth’s earliest crust consisted of mafic (basaltic, Mg- and Fe-rich) minerals, which underwent a transition to a modern-like felsic (silicate) composition through oceanic crust subduction and remelting60. This transition from mafic/ultramafic to felsic crust composition has been linked to the onset of plate tectonics, based on isotopic signatures in zircon, an ancient mineral that crystallized from a magma and is preserved in felsic igneous and metamorphic rocks61. Isotopic signatures of water-soluble elements such as O and Si in zircon may record changes in the mantle-crust interaction, consistent with melting of hydrated mafic oceanic crust producing felsic magma in the Archean eon62. This suggests that zircon crystallized in a wet, melting environments characteristic for subduction zones, which allowed recycling of surface rocks into the mantle, a key process in plate tectonic. Reassessment of O and Hf isotopic signatures in the Hadean zircon grains showed that there were four zircon growth events between ca. 4.4 and 4.0 Ga that supracrustal reservoirs might not have been involved in the zircon genesis before 4.15 Ga63. Isotopic and geochemical signatures of parent magmas of zircons suggested that the major transition in crustal geochemistry and the global onset of mobile-lid tectonics occurred between ca. 3.8 and 3.6 Ga64,65.

Hartnady et al. also argued that the tectonic regime at ca. 3.6 Ga was different from modern tectonism and that the Earth’s continental crust was formed from altered mafic protocrust through partial melting66. However, Vezinet et al. conducted geochemical analysis of melt inclusions in Mg-high olivine crystals and proposed that massive lithospheric subduction and continental growth could have occurred ca. 4.31 Ga67. These findings evidence chemical similarities to modern magmas implying that plate tectonic activity may have existed at that time. Additionally, recent data obtained by Turner et al. suggest that the Hadean protocrust was not necessarily mafic and could contain felsic components with a SiO2 content ranging from 58 to 78 wt% in the parent melt68. The authors proposed that the Hadean protocrust could have possessed a composition of trace-elements incompatible with a crystal structure of silicate minerals (incompatible trace elements), and this is similar to modern continental crust. Therefore, the obtained data challenge arguments of other researchers regarding the onset of plate tectonics. Machine learning in multidimensional space, incorporating an array of zircon trace elements, suggests that subduction-driven plate tectonics could have been active during the Hadean, resembling a modern mobile-lid tectonism69.

There are debates about the origin and chronology of continent formation on early Earth. According to the plate tectonics hypothesis of continent formation, mantle convection was caused by energy from tremendous heat released from the core and radioactive decay, leading to movements of plates70. When plates interact at convergent boundaries, one tectonic plate subducted beneath another, diving into the mantle. Magma upwelled and created new silica-rich crust, which gradually accumulated to form stable landmasses71. At divergent boundaries, the basaltic magma fills the gap created as plates move apart, solidifies, and creates new oceanic crust. Arc-trench complexes are formed over subducting tectonic plates either on continental or oceanic crust. These complexes include volcanic arcs where eruption of magma occurs due to a lowering of its melting point in the upper mantle by water released from minerals72. This contributed to the growth of continents and the formation of mineral deposits. Subduction zones facilitate the recycling of chemical elements into the mantle where they exist in different states such as liquids, aqueous solutions, and supercritical fluids73. Recently, an alternative model was proposed arguing that towering columns of molten rock from deep within the Earth’s mantle (mantle plumes) played a key role in the formation of the earliest continents74. The mantle plumes, continental overturns, or lithospheric peels could have contributed to thickening of mafic oceanic crust that underwent the partial melting at the base causing the formation of oxidizing and wet magmas75.

By exploring evidence from O and Li isotopes in detrital zircons, Valley suggested that physical and chemical conditions such as the formation of continents, oceans, and atmosphere could have created habitable conditions on early Earth by ca. 4.3 Ga76. Plate tectonics could play significant roles in global climate change through the transfer of heat, carbon, and water from the lithosphere into the oceans and atmosphere, associated with volcanic activity at divergent and convergent plate boundaries77. The distribution, storage, and movement of carbon between the Earth’s surface and subsurface affect long-term climatic conditions, and are linked to volcanism, mountain-building, and subduction. Volcanic activity caused eruption of ash and aerosol particles as well as degassing of CO2 into the atmosphere, contributing to the greenhouse warming effect78–81. Climate conditions over long geological time scale also depend on long-term geochemical cycles including recycling of greenhouse gases such as CO2 back into the mantle through subduction82. The formation of continents and plate tectonics provided the conditions necessary for geochemical cycling, which was crucial for the emergence of life on our planet83.

Plate tectonics facilitates carbon-silicate cycling, in which CO2 is dissolved in rainwater to form carbonic acid (H2CO3) that falls on land and oceans (Fig. 1a), thereby causing weathering of silicate and carbonate rocks over long time scales. This weathering leads to the consumption of atmospheric CO2 and, consequently, decreases surface temperatures. Silicate rocks are weathered by H2CO3, and, ultimately form carbonates (CO32-) and silicon dioxide (SiO2), which precipitate and are buried in sediments on the seafloor (Fig. 1b). In reverse weathering, CO2 can be regenerated through reactions between SiO2 and CO32-, facilitating further carbonate weathering from oceanic crusts (Fig. 1c). Alternatively, subduction of carbonated oceanic basalts and pelagic sediments recycles CO32- into the mantle84,85. Volcanic activity at mid ocean ridges and island arcs outgasses CO2 back into oceans and atmosphere, completing the carbon-silicate cycling. Under modern conditions, the carbon-silicate cycle provides a dynamic link between the atmosphere, hydrosphere, and biosphere. Silicate weathering is a temperature-dependent process because mineral dissolution rate increases with growth of temperature87. However, this process consumes CO2 and modulates its concentration in the atmosphere, thereby serving as a negative feedback mechanism for climate control88,89. The long-term carbon cycle is crucial for maintaining liquid water in the oceans over geological time scales. A positive feedback mechanism may exist, as cool surface temperatures strengthen plate tectonics90. Plate tectonics acts as bidirectional link between the Earth’s interior and surface, facilitating exchange of chemical elements essential for life91. Therefore, the Hadean geodynamics could have significantly influenced the Earth’s atmosphere, oceans, and climate, ultimately creating conditions for the emergence of life on early Earth.

Hadean atmosphere

Atmospheric composition

During planetary accretion, the primordial atmosphere of Earth and other terrestrial planets was likely composed of gases gravitationally attracted from the solar nebula, primarily H2 and He92. However, as planetesimals collided, their kinetic energy heated the accreted material, causing the outgassing of volatiles such as H2O and CO2 into the Earth’s atmosphere93. It is probable that high-energy sources such as heavy bombardments, high temperatures, and solar activity caused hydrodynamic escape of H2 to space94. In the escape outflow, CO2 underwent rapid photolysis to CO and O, which oxidized H2 to HO•- and H2O, and this decreased the rate of H2 loss95. The Earth’s secondary atmosphere could have been formed by volcanic outgassing dominated by carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrogen gas (N2), and water vapor (H2O) and thereby being slightly oxidizing96.

Some efforts have been made to infer the Hadean secondary atmosphere from the mantle redox state97. Experimental data showed that under high pressure, Fe2+ can undergo disproportionation to Fe3+ and metallic iron (Fe0). This allowed a suggestion that during Earth differentiation, the dense Fe0 partitioned into the core whereas Fe3+ remained in the mantle98. This could have increased the oxidation state of the upper mantle and caused the transformation of a reducing atmosphere into a slightly oxidizing one. Measuring the oxygen fugacity (ꝭO2) of magmatic melts by determining the relative amounts of iron in different oxidation states within minerals such as fayalite [(Mg,Fe)2SiO4] and magnetite (Fe2+Fe23+O4) and comparing with the quartz-fayalite-magnetite (QFM) buffer allowed constraining the Hadean magma redox state and confirming that it could be relatively oxidized99. Probably, the Earth’s mantle became relatively oxidized shortly after the Moon-forming impact, which occurred between ca. 4.50 and 4.35 Ga54,55. At that time, outgassing of volatiles including CO2, which were partially dissolved in the mantle, could have formed a dense and deep atmosphere around Earth100,101. In this atmosphere, CO2 began to dominate over carbon monoxide (CO), whereas H2O began to dominate over H296.

The partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2) in the atmosphere may have varied from several bars to tens of bars or even up to 100 bars in the early Hadean before declining over time102. It was controlled by a combination of various processes including asteroid impacts, recycling into the mantle, dissolution in ocean waters, reactions with minerals, and photochemical reactions in the atmosphere, which led to the reduction of CO2 to CO, CH4 and other organic compounds103. Sossi et al. calculated the ꝭO2 in quenched liquids of silicate Earth-like compositions and established that the redox state of the Earth’s magma ocean initially produced a CO-rich atmosphere, which, upon cooling and water condensation, transitioned to a Venus-like atmosphere dominated by CO2 and N2104. However, unlike on Venus, as Earth cooled, water vapor condensed to form oceans, removing CO2 from the atmosphere and eventually rising O2 concentration. Therefore, the Earth’s interior may have acquired its current oxidation state early in the Hadean, at least ca. 3.9 Ga, leading to the volcanic outgassing of oxidized volatiles such as H2O, CO2, and SO2 rather than CH4, H2, and NH3 into the Earth’s atmosphere105.

Although geochemical data suggest that volcanoes did not emit reducing gases during the Hadean eon, modeling experiments carried out by combining trace-element data from zircon found in igneous rocks show that the Hadean continental crust may have been more reduced than the modern one, with progressive oxidation toward the early Archean, ca. 3.6 Ga106. This could have favored reduced magmatism while degassing of hot reduced basaltic melts contributed to the H2-rich Hadean atmosphere. Additional evidence for the possibility of a transient reducing atmosphere during the Hadean eon comes from theoretical calculations that suggest that asteroid and meteorite impact after the completion of planetary accretion may have caused outgassing of the impact material to produce a large fraction of early atmosphere. These calculations showed that the atmosphere generated by impact ejecta on ocean-covered Earth may have been rich in H2, CH4, and ammonia (NH3)107,108. Outgassing of material contained in ordinary chondrites could have released volatiles such as N2, NH3, H2, and CH4109 along with substantial amounts of metallic iron and iron sulfides110.

Possibly, Fe2+ was contained in the “late veneer” extraterrestrial material delivered to Earth to form its final layer, primarily from chondritic meteorites, after the Earth’s core had already been formed111. The “late veneer” hypothesis was proposed to explain why dense, metallic, and highly siderophile (with affinity to iron) elements (HSE) are found in the Earth’s mantle if they have sunk into the core during Earth differentiation112. It has been suggested that a body the size of the Moon, likely a differentiated chondritic object, collided with early Earth. The iron-rich core of the impactor was absorbed into the Earth’s core, and its rocky mantle, rich in highly siderophile elements, was dispersed throughout the mantle, becoming the late veneer113. H2 was a major product of the atmospheric reaction of oxidation of Fe2+ contained in the “late veneer” coupled with the reduction of H2O in vapor produced by ocean-vaporizing impacts. The produced H2 could have been used for the formation of CH4 that along with NH3 created the reducing atmosphere44. This transient reducing atmosphere could have then evolved to a weakly oxidizing atmosphere due to the photochemical generation of CO2 and N2114.

The investigation of nitrogen evolution throughout the Earth’s history using of a geochemical model showed that plate tectonics has significantly influenced the partial pressure of dinitrogen (pN2) in the atmosphere, biosphere, and geosphere115. This model showed that during the Hadean eon, high mantle temperatures may have led to the outgassing of N2 from the mantle into the atmosphere. Over time, cooling of the mantle enhanced the efficiency of nitrogen subduction, allowing nitrogen to be stored in geological reservoirs. Thermodynamic calculations conducted by Mikhail and Sverjensky suggest that under relatively oxidized mantle conditions in subduction zones, nitrogen predominantly exists as N2 in fluids, facilitating its release through volcanic activity116. In other parts of Earth’s upper mantle, where conditions are more reducing, ammonium ion (NH4+) is predominant in aqueous fluids (Fig. 1).

Atmospheric carbon fixation

As Bada suggested, the intense lightning discharges associated with erupting volcanoes provided a concentrated source of energy, which along with volcanic outgassing of volatile substances such as H2O, CO2, and SO2 could have created conditions for atmospheric synthesis of organic molecules in the Hadean eon117. The exposure of critical elements on the surface of primordial continents after the solidification of the magma ocean and meteorite impacts, which also delivered volatile compounds such as CO2, N2, and H2O likely contributed to the conditions necessary for the atmospheric organic synthesis118. Lightning and UV radiation may have enabled carbon fixation from the CO2-dominated atmosphere facilitated by geochemical cycles involving redox-active elements such Fe and S. These elements could have provided their catalytic surfaces and energy transfer mechanisms for carbon fixation, necessary for life to begin on early Earth environments. This was probably facilitated by the interaction between the atmosphere, land mass, and oceans, the habitable trinity proposed as an essential factor for the emergence of life on early Earth119.

Direct CO2 conversion to CH4 (methanation) through the reduction by H2 (hydrogenation), known as the Sabatier reaction, which occurs in a gas phase, could have taken place on the surface of minerals and cosmic dust grains composed of silicates and iron condensates, from which a protoplanetary disc was formed120. Organic molecules produced in this process could be delivered to Earth by meteorite impacts and could provide precursors for the life’s building blocks leading to the emergence of life121. Under experimental conditions, the Sabatier reaction proceeds in a reactor with inorganic catalysts such as nickel (Ni), ruthenium (Ru) and aluminum oxide (Al2O3)122. The reaction occurs under high pressure and at temperatures between 300 and 400 °C and is highly exergonic with a free energy change of ∆G = −27.25 kcal mol−1 and therefore is thermodynamically favorable123. The Sabatier reaction allows the process to take place through a continuous closed-loop system, recycling the reactants and generating renewable fuel in the form of CH4, which is useful in space missions124.

For the H2 production from H2O, a significant amount of reducing material could have been provided by ejection and subsequent re-accretion of iron-bearing giant-impact fragments125. This may have led to a shift from a CO2-rich weakly oxidizing atmosphere to a H2-rich and CH4-rich reducing atmosphere. In the anoxic Hadean and Archean environments, CH4 production through CO2 reduction by H2 (Fig. 1d) could have occurred due to photochemical oxidation of Fe2+, dissolved in high-temperature water vapor126. The oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+ was probably driven by short-wavelength UV light, which caused the H2O reduction to H2 (Fig. 1e). In the absence of stratospheric ozone (O3) layer, the most energetic UV rays of wavelengths between 100 and 280 nm could penetrate the early Earth’s atmosphere127. Sufficiently large impacts that affected the Hadean atmosphere and hydrosphere could have generated high pressure and temperature, which evaporated ocean waters128. Under experimental conditions simulating giant impact-driven reactions between Fe2+ and H2O and CO2, the production of H2 and CO, respectively, by oxidation of Fe2+ to FeO has been shown129. The CO2 hydrogenation could have occurred if there was a high H2/CO2 ratio in the early CO2-rich atmosphere130. The Fe2+-rich particles from meteorites and volcanic ash reduced H2O to H2 and caused the CO2 hydrogenation not only to C1 hydrocarbons such as CH4, formaldehyde (HCOH), and methanol (CH3OH), but also to C2 hydrocarbons such as ethanol (C2H5OH), acetaldehyde (CH3COH), and various alkanes and alkenes. This has been shown under the experimentally simulated conditions, in high-pressure autoclave experiments with broad ranges of pressure (9–45 bar) and temperature (150–300 °C) in both neutral (CO2 balanced with H2 and N2) and H2-rich reducing atmospheres131.

Under experimental conditions, HCOH can interact with hydroxymethylene (CHOH) generated from the pyrolysis of glyoxylic acid (HCO-COOH) in a gas-phase reaction with almost no thermodynamic barrier132. This leads to generation of glycolaldehyde and glyceraldehyde, the building blocks of ribose for the nucleotide synthesis. This reaction is analogous to the formose reaction also known as the Butlerov reaction, which involves the formation of sugars, polyols and hydroxy acids from HCOH. This is a series of autocatalytic self-condensation reactions that often occur in aqueous solution at alkaline pH and in the presence of divalent metal ions133. Thermodynamic analysis of the reactivity of molecules in a gas phase indicates the plausibility of synthesis of formic acid (HCOOH) from CO and H2O (Fig. 1f), and formamide (HCONH2) from CO and NH3 (Fig. 1g)134. H2O and NH3 act as a proton shuttle to lower the thermodynamic barrier. Both HCOOH and HCONH2 can further give rise to a set of intermediates for amino acid synthesis on the early Earth’s atmosphere driven by energy sources such as lightning.

Atmospheric production of HCN and nitrogen fixation

One of the most prominent molecules featured in some hypotheses on the origin of life is HCN, which could have been produced through photochemistry in CH4- and NH3- dominated atmospheres135, lightning shock in CO2- and N2-dominated atmospheres136, and meteorite impacts in CO-dominated and N2-rich atmosphere137. Experimentally simulated short-wavelength UV radiation between 147 to 180 nm has been shown to cause the photolysis of CH4 and NH3 mixtures followed by the formation of nitriles such as HCN (Fig. 1h), when the NH3 proportion is low, and the generation of amines as the NH3 mole fraction increases138. Additionally, both experimental and theoretical investigations have shown that under conditions simulating a gas mixture of a reducing Hadean atmosphere, HCN is produced by cold plasma discharges, a controlled laboratory analog to shortwave UV radiation139. In the N2-dominated atmosphere, HCN could have been formed with the use of various energy sources such as photochemistry, lightning, asteroid impacts, and volcanic activity143. N2 could be delivered by impacts and volcanic outgassing, accumulating in the early Earth’s reservoirs such as the atmosphere, oceans, crust, and mantle. Accumulation of N2 in a specific reservoir depends on abiotic nitrogen cycling including nitrogen fixation144. Reduction of N2 to NH3 during nitrogen fixation requires high energy input to overcome the high thermodynamic barrier arising from the strength of the triple covalent bond in N2. Theoretical studies showed that under extremely hot surface conditions of 1000 K in combination with a reducing steam environment, significant atmospheric production of NH3 through abiotic nitrogen fixation could be effective (Fig. 1k)145. Near the Earth’s surface, the NH3 production may be thermodynamically favorable for further incorporation into oceanic sediments or for dissolving into the mantle in the form of NH4+. However, in the upper atmosphere NH3 was probably susceptible to photolysis by UV radiation to N2 and H2 (Fig. 1l). This likely kept its concentration low even in the H2-rich environment146.

Experimental evidence of photochemical nitrogen fixation to NH3 in the Earth’s atmosphere containing N2 and water vapor has been observed on a FeS2 surface that can absorb UV radiation at wavelengths between 200 to 400 nm147. The shock heating of a CO2- and N2-rich atmosphere under experimentally simulated early Earth’s conditions showed the plausibility of nitrogen fixation to nitrate (NO3-) and nitrite (NO2-) in both the gas phase and the liquid phase148. In the gas phase, N2 was converted to nitrogen dioxide (NO2), whereas in the liquid phase, HNO was produced as an intermediate product. NO2- and NO3- could have rained into prebiotic oceans from the atmosphere for further reduction in acidic conditions on the surface of FeS to NH3149. Shang et al. showed that under experimental conditions that simulate the Hadean serpentinization system, NH3/NH4+ is produced in the presence of N2, indicating the occurrence of N2 reduction by H2, which in turn was produced through the H2O reduction by Fe2+ oxidation to Fe3+ (Fig. 1m)150. Therefore, CO2, N2, and H2O vapor may have dominated the Hadean atmosphere. N2 could have been captured from the atmosphere to be fixed to NH3 whereas H2 could be released to the atmosphere for N2 fixation.

The synthesis of HCN, cyanoacetylene (HCCCN), and urea (CO(NH2)2) has been shown to occur in the atmosphere consisting of CO, CH4, N2, and H2O and created by experimentally simulated high-velocity impacts. These impacts created a shock wave, which triggered chemical reactions in the presence of montmorillonite as a catalyst151. Numerical modeling of the Hadean atmosphere suggests that under highly reducing conditions, HCN and its derivatives such as HCCCN and cyanomethylene (NCCN) can be produced through UV-driven photochemistry152. The products of these reactions gave rise to the RNA canonical nucleobases and the simplest amino acid glycine produced in the vapor plume, which cooled rapidly, and this was important for preserving synthesized molecules. Ianeselli et al. suggested that Hadean rock pores may have been filled with water. Under the CO2-dominated atmosphere and high temperatures, water would have continuously evaporated and condensed as acidic dew droplets, creating humidity, salt, and pH values conducive to the polymerization of nucleotides into oligonucleotides and polynucleotides153.

Amino acid and nucleotide synthesis

Shang et al. suggested that serpentinization could have provided the production of large quantities of CH4 and NH3, which were released into the atmosphere150. These gases reacted with each other in water vapor, eventually forming amino acids. The amino acids may have been washed down by heavy rainfall and concentrated at the atmosphere-ocean interface, where H2O existed in a supercritical state154. The polymerization of these amino acids with the production of peptides and proteins necessary for life could have occurred at the interface between the silicate surface of the Earth and supercritical H2O. Further cooling of Earth may have led to collapse of the supercritical water layer causing the amino acids to fall into the ocean. It has been hypothesized that polymerization of amino acids to proteins occurred on the Hadean Earth under anhydrous (dry) thermal conditions allowing oligomers to form crystals155. The polymerization of amino acids such as glycine through dehydration condensation at 200 °C due to the formation of conventional amide bonds has been observed under experimentally simulated conditions156. Some mineral matrices such as phyllosilicates changed the thermal properties of glycine, increasing the stability, protecting from degradation, and the formation of polymers157.

Heating crystals of amino acids causes their cyclization into diketopiperazines (DKPs), six-member cyclic dipeptides produced through a dehydration reaction occurring in the absence of H2O on mineral surfaces158. DKPs are stable products that initiate further polymerization reactions, and they play a role of DPKs in the origin of biochemical chirality, an enigmatic problem in the synthesis of native peptides and an essential prerequisite for the origin of life. DKPs exist in an equilibrium of cis and trans forms, but naturally occurring DKPs are formed from L-α-amino acids and exist in cis configuration159. The process of thermal oligomerization uses heat as a source of energy and heat-cold and wet-dry cycles, which facilitate dehydration reactions by concentrating molecules in environments created by geological networks of thin interconnected cracks through increasing their reactivity160. Geological networks of interconnected cracks could have selected molecules required for reactions from a complex mixture of amino acids, nucleotides and other building blocks of life.

Under prebiotic atmospheric conditions, powerful pathways for the synthesis of complex organic molecules, such as nitrogen bases, amino acids, and monosaccharides from HCN could have occurred through a photochemical reduction, homologation and polymerization161. The UV radiation served as a source of energy to overcome high energy barriers and provided chemical selectivity favoring the production of thermodynamically stable molecules. UV light-induced reduction of HCN by H2S or sulfite (SO32-) by reacting intermediates in the presence of catalysts such as Cu2+ for photochemical redox cycling has been shown in various models162,163. The initial substrate then reacted with the active intermediates to give rise to stable products.

Computational calculations showed that HCN could have reversibly reacted with H2O in water droplets under the early Earth atmosphere conditions in a forward gas-phase reaction thermochemically and photochemically producing HCONH2 (Fig. 2a) or with NH3 producing hydroxylamine (NH2OH)164,165. Free-energy maps of the reactions with the involvement of HCN, NH3, H2O, and HCONH2 showed that HCONH2 is thermodynamically the most stable product under standard conditions, but the initial hydration of HCN has a relatively high activation energy barrier in a gas phase165. When H2O served as a catalyst, thermodynamic barrier drastically decreased, and ∆G became −5.73 kcal mol−1166,167. Trimerization of HCN in an aqueous solution with the formation of a planar, sp2-hybridized six-membered aromatic product (Fig. 2a) is exergonic (∆G = −29.44 kcal mol−1). In contrast, the trimerization of formamide and its imino tautomeric form (NHCHOH) to produce non-planar, sp3-hybridized cyclic products is endergonic with ∆G = + 55.81 kcal mol−1 and ∆G = +5.74 kcal mol−1, respectively. Experimental confirmation of the conversion of HCONH2 to nucleobases, for example, adenine has been demonstrated on the surface of a TiO(2) (001) single crystal, oxide semiconductors169. In this process, UV light excites the semiconductor surface, but does not adsorb substrate molecules and, therefore, does not cause product decomposition. Using a ferrocyanide-ferricyanide redox system, the possibility to synthesize 2-aminoazoles such as 2-aminooxazole and 2-aminoimidazole from simple precursors, thiourea and HCN (Fig. 2b), under plausible prebiotic conditions has been shown170. Thiourea undergoes photoinduced oxidation to produce cyanamide (NH2CN) whereas HCN is photochemically reduced and homologated, ultimately giving rise to 2-aminooxazole and 2-aminoimidazole, precursors of ribonucleotides. Additionally, using a continuous flow chemistry that mimics the stream environments of early Earth, the multi-step photochemical reduction of HCN for the synthesis of 2-aminooxazole has been demonstrated171. The 2-aminooxazole can be produced through a reaction between HCN and HCOH to generate glycolonitrile (HOCH2CN) and various other products via different routes. In the presence of NH3, HOCH2CN undergoes condensation to form aminoacetonitrile (H2NCH2CN) that is hydrolyzed to amino acid glycine (Fig. 2b) in the process known as the Strecker synthesis172.

The Strecker synthesis involves the condensation reaction of aldehydes with HCN and NH3, forming racemic α-aminonitriles (RCH(NH2)CN) and α-hydroxy nitriles (RCH(OH)CN) as a function of substrate concentration and temperature173. Subsequent hydrolysis of α-aminonitrile in the presence of HCl yields racemic mixtures of α-amino acids; however, the modified asymmetric synthesis can produce chiral α-amino acids174,175. The formation of N-protected α-amino acids during the Strecker synthesis in the atmospheric aerosol-liquid cycle or on ice grains has been suggested to proceed on prebiotic Earth176,177. For example, spontaneous generation of amino acids in interstellar ice under UV radiation could have occurred, and this can explain the presence of prebiotic organic molecules in carbonaceous meteorites178–180. The synthesis of amino acids and nucleotides may have co-evolved with self-replicating machinery, which included short peptides and oligonucleotides. Mineral surfaces could have been used for polymerization whereas oligonucleotides served as templates for the formation of peptides with weak catalytic activity181.

Atmospheric aerosols and raining could have facilitated molecular co-evolution by transferring products from one environment to another, for example, between gas and aqueous phases182. Aerosols are primarily formed directly at the sea surface as a result of interaction between wind and waves (sea spray aerosols) or from emitted gases that condense to produce secondary marine aerosols. They carry a variety of inorganic and organic molecules to the atmosphere, where these molecules enter the chemical reactions183,184. On early Earth, they could be released from the atmosphere and deposited into the oceans through raining and gravitational settling. It has been hypothesized that atmospheric aerosols could have acted as prebiotic chemical reactors, providing an environment, where various compounds could have concentrated and undergone chemical reactions under atmospheric conditions185,186.

Hadean hydrosphere

Plausible aquatic habitats on early Earth

Deep-ocean hydrothermal vents

The formation of a rocky planet with liquid water, a stable atmosphere, and a magnetic field is considered to be a key factor in making the planet habitable187. Earth could have become habitable as early as 10–20 million years after the end of Moon-forming impact, when the magma ocean cooled and solidified to form the proto-crust188. Hadean volcanoes and extraterrestrial sources released large amounts of water vapor and CO2 into the atmosphere. After the Earth’s surface cooled, atmospheric water condensed and precipitated, eventually resulting in the formation of oceans. This caused the dissolving of CO2 in ocean waters and a dramatic decrease in its atmospheric concentration. However, the pCO2 level was probably enough to maintain the greenhouse effect, leading to a surface temperature of about 500 K189. Data from stable oxygen isotope ratios (δ18O) in detrital zircons from metamorphosed sediments in the Jack Hills of the Yilgarn Craton, Western Australia, dating back to ca. 4.4 and 4.3 Ga, provide evidence for the existence of oceans in the early Hadean eon190. The δ18O values in these zircons indicate that the formation of continental crust may have occurred in a watery environment near the Earth’s surface, consistent with the existence of oceans at ca. 4.3 Ga191. Data from the Earth silicate differentiation and the presence of a massive early crust and terrestrial hydrosphere indicate habitable conditions on our planet by at least ca. 4.15 Ga192.

The deep-ocean hydrothermal vents have long been considered to be the earliest type of aquatic environment, which existed at least ca. 4.2 Ga and was suitable for the origin of life193. Hydrothermal vents have been found on the seafloor on modern Earth, mainly along mid-ocean ridges and volcanic arcs194. Two major types of modern hydrothermal vents have been identified including: (i) acidic vents known as black smokers and located at ridge axes, driven by magma chambers with hot effluents (approximately 350–400 °C and higher) with pH values between 2 and 3, and (ii) alkaline vents with pH values between 9 and 11, known as Lost City-type, which are driven by serpentinization and have cooler effluents (50–90 °C)195. Additionally, the Von Damm Vent Field (VDVF) type of seafloor hydrothermal vents with fluids of moderately low pH (6–7) and temperatures up to 215 °C have been identified196. The VDVFs are located away from the ridge axis and unlike other hydrothermal vents, contain 85–90% precipitates composed of clay mineral, talc, which is hydrated magnesium silicate.

Hadean alkaline hydrothermal systems are considered to be capable of driving prebiotic chemistry due to their thermal and electrochemical proton gradients across mineral pores, catalytic mineral surfaces, and the mixing of reduced, alkaline fluids with acidic ocean water197. However, modern systems are highly influenced by microbial communities and are not equivalent in their chemistry to those that would have existed in the Hadean eon198,199. Under modern conditions, atmospheric CO2 is absorbed by the oceans and consumed by photosynthetic organisms, and this makes surface seawater oxic with pH values ranging from 8.1 to 8.4200. The Hadean oceans were anoxic and acidic with pH values ranging from 5 to 6, due to the dissolution of CO2 and the equilibrium between its concentration in the ocean and the atmosphere, which could have reached up to 100 bars188,193. Above the mantle plumes and near ocean ridges, there were probably acidic hydrothermal springs with temperatures of 350–400 °C. In contrast, deep-sea hydrothermal systems contained relatively cool (≤ 100 °C) and alkaline fluids with pH values between 10 and 11201. The difference between deep-see fluids and surface waters created a chemiosmotic gradient that could have served as a proton motive force for organic synthesis. The Hadean deep-ocean hydrothermal vents were probably UV-protected, providing stable, mineral-rich environments for serpentinization and far-from-equilibrium conditions that facilitated the origin of life202.

Westall et al. summarized the hydrothermal and sedimentary conditions of early Earth, which could have favored organic synthesis, including temperature, pressure, salinity, and pH values, and the availability of minerals203. They concluded that porous and reactive sedimentary layers between the oceanic crust and seawater could have served as a network of tiny, interconnected chemical reactors for the concentration, stabilization and polymerization of molecules. During the Hadean eon, Mg- and Fe-rich silicate minerals dominated an oceanic crust, which was formed by a hydrated and chemically heterogeneous mantle204. The Hadean hydrothermal vents may have been enriched in ultramafic rocks such as komatiites enriched in Mg and Fe, associated with deposits of transition metals, primarily, Ni and Co205. Minerals in these rocks reacted with oceanic water for H2 production through serpentinization. During the serpentinization process, ferromagnesian minerals such as olivine [(Mg,Fe)2SiO4] and pyroxenes [(Mg,Fe)SiO3] in mafic and ultramafic igneous rocks undergo metamorphic transformation206,207. As a result of their interaction with seawater, Mg, Fe, and Si are precipitated in different proportions in the form of serpentines [(Mg,Fe)3Si2O5(OH)4], brucite [Mg,Fe(OH)2], and often magnetite (Fe2+Fe23+O4).

The serpentinization is a low-temperature geochemical process that occurs with negative ΔG values, providing chemical energy in the form of pH-, temperature-, and redox gradients208. The redox state of minerals is controlled by buffers such as the QFM buffer, which maintain oxidation states and solubility of multivalent elements, providing a fixed ꝭO2 even at high temperatures existed in hydrothermal systems209. During the serpentinization, Fe2+ is oxidized in minerals to Fe3+. This is coupled with the reduction of H2O to H2 that can be used to produce CH4 and other organic molecules210. The concentration of dissolved Fe2+ could have reached up to 100–250 ppm in the Hadean oceans, which is equivalent to 2 to 4 × 10−3 M, that is significantly higher than current levels in oxic oceans, ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 × 10−9 M211. However, some studies have shown that Fe2+ can be lost to the ocean with diffuse fluids emanating from hydrothermal systems, and this can limit CO2 reduction and carbon fixation212,213. The synthesized organic molecules could also have been diffused into the dilute ocean and underwent hydrolysis. Although, the porous, mineral-rich structures of the vents and nearby sediments may have acted as protected systems providing conditions for concentration and chemical conversions203. Hadean ocean waters are suggested to be supersaturated in silica that precipitated in sediments forming layers of microporous silica gel, which could have incorporated various minerals214. This enabled the concentration, selection, and polymerization of organic molecules under non-equilibrium conditions of hydrothermal vents215. On the other hand, diffusion of synthesized organic molecules also allowed transporting prebiotic molecules into surrounding seawaters, where they could have been further processed or delivered to other locations216.

Subaerial environments

A fundamental conflict that arises from the hypothesis of the emergence of life in deep-ocean hydrothermal vents is the “water paradox”217. This conflict implies that, despite the fact that water is needed as a solvent for chemical reactions, some essential organic compounds for life are poorly soluble in aqueous solutions. Additionally, macromolecules including proteins and nucleic acids are produced by the condensation polymerization of amino acids and nucleotides, respectively, in reactions that require the removal of water218. Furthermore, water molecules thermodynamically favor the hydrolysis of covalent bonds in polymers over condensation, and this requires strategies to favor condensation over hydrolysis219. Various strategies could have been employed on early Earth to resolve these challenges including: (i) the absorption of amino acids and nucleotides on surfaces of minerals, which serve as templates for polymer formation even in the presence of water220; (ii) the use of nanopores in minerals as spaces where the physical and chemical properties of water change221; (iii) the formation of some organic molecules in a nonpolar phase prior to transfer to the water phase222,223; and (iv) the involvement of wet-dry cycles that could have occurred in the Hadean shallow water environments, enabling polymerization of macromolecules224,225.

The Hadean terrestrial hot springs, which probably used energy from both hydrothermal activity and UV radiation could have provided the water-gas (wet-dry) cycling and the concentration of elements for the synthesis of organic molecules have been proposed as an alternative to deep-sea hydrothermal vents226. The hot spring hypothesis of the origin of live was proposed by Damer and Deamer based on data showing that lipid-encapsulated polymers can be synthesized through cycles of hydration and dehydration during protocell formation227. The formation of hot springs results from the emergence of groundwater heated by either the Earth’s mantle, shallow magma in volcanic regions or radioactive decay of elements located in the mantle228. Modern hot springs often have water temperatures from 35 to 110 °C, differ from each other by their composition and can be: (i) alkaline chloride, enriched in silica minerals that play a role of catalysts; (ii) acidic formed due to the oxidation of H2S to H2SO4; (iii) enriched in CO2 and carbonate minerals, which serve as sources of carbon, and (iv) iron-rich that are habitats for microbes using iron oxidation as a source of energy in a low-oxygen environment229.

The intriguing property of hot springs is their elemental composition, which can be identical to that of living cells230. Jenewein et al. suggested that on early Earth, the synthesis of organic molecules could have been concurrent with the creation of protocells through a mechanism of bubbling and precipitation at the water-gas interface of subaerial environments231. In a laboratory model, the ability of aqueous aerosols to transform into proto-cellular structures upon entering an aqueous solution-containing lipid molecules has been demonstrated232. Such processes could provide a pathway for the assembly of building blocks of life within cell-like vesicles containing lipid bilayer. The concept of “protocells” as precursors of modern cells explains the formation of compartments that could contain a primitive genetic material, allowing for replication and accumulation of molecules essential for survival and evolution233. Replication of both the protocell and its encapsulated genetic material would have enabled natural selection to operate234.

In addition to hot springs, ancient shallow waters such as volcanic crater lakes and ponds formed in the impact or volcanoes craters could be cool enough, but contained numerous hydrothermal vents that emitted streams of hot water providing an environment with cold-hot and wet-dry cycles along with influxes of both saltwater and freshwater235. It has been suggested that these fluctuating temperature environments played an important role in the polymerization of amino acids and nucleotides to form proteins and RNA, respectively236. Mulkidjanian et al. suggested that shallow ponds were lined with porous silicate minerals mixed with metal sulfides and enriched in K+, Na+ Zn2+, Mn2+ ions and phosphorous compounds, having a composition conducive to the origin of the first cells under the anoxic, CO2-dominated primordial atmospheres237. Cool geothermal fields enriched with metallic zinc and radioactive potassium have been suggested to result from the Moon-forming impact to maintain the reduction of CO2 and N2 yielding numerous organic molecules released with the geothermal vapor condensate238.

Chemoautotrophic vs heterotrophic theories of the origin of life

The heterotrophic theory of the origin of life is based on the assumption that early organisms were heterotrophs and consumed abiotically synthesized molecules from their environment to use them as structural components and energy sources239. This theory was proposed by the Russian biochemist Alexander Oparin in 1924, arguing that autotrophic organisms were too complex to be considered primordial forms of life240. Oparin suggested that hydrocarbons were either synthesized on early Earth or delivered to it by meteorites, comets, or even interstellar gas and dust241. Extraterrestrial sources such as asteroid and meteorite impacts could have provided Earth with a variety of materials including transition metals, gases, inorganic and organic molecules, which also influenced the origin of life242–244. According to Oparin, coacervate droplets, resembling protoplasm and working in accordance with the laws of colloidal chemistry could have been the union of the constituents of the first cells245. The Oparin’s hypothesis was supported by the theory of abiogenesis in a “primordial soup” proposed by the British biologist John Haldane. He argued that many sources of energy such as electric discharges (lightning), UV radiation, and volcanic eruption could have enabled prebiotic organic synthesis from simple chemical compounds such as CH4, NH3, H2S, and H2O vapor in the atmosphere246. The products of such reactions could be then transferred to aquatic environments, where more complex compounds including proteins and nucleic acids could be produced in the presence of inorganic catalysts.

In support of the Oparin-Haldane hypothesis, the American physical chemist Harold Urey stated that the early Earth’s atmosphere was strongly reducing and this created favorable conditions for prebiotic synthesis of organic molecules leading to the origin of life247. By calculating equilibrium constants of reactions that could have occurred at that time, he suggested that early Earth’s atmosphere was rich in CH4, N2, NH3, H2O, and H2. If CO2 is generated, it was probably utilized through a reaction with silicates to produce carbonates that were then sequestered into the mantle by subduction. The Urey’s ideas greatly influenced the Miller experiments, which showed that carboxylic acids and amino acids can be produced from CH4, NH3, H2O, and H2 passing through an electric discharge in an apparatus with boiled water248.

Alternatively, the autotrophic theory of the origin of life has been proposed to explain that the first organisms were primary producers, capable of carbon fixation from CO2, which served as an electron acceptor in the prebiotic synthesis of organic molecules249. The first hypothesis on the chemoautotrophic origin of life, known as the “iron-sulfide world”, was proposed by Günter Wächtershäuser in 198822. A fundamental difference between the autotrophic and heterotrophic theories is that the former argues that life originated from inorganic compounds, rather than from organic matter250. The hypothesis postulated a pioneer organism that could be characterized by a composite structure with an inorganic substructure and an organic superstructure251. Wächtershäuser supposed that life emerged on the surface of inorganic minerals such as FeS2 formed in a redox reaction between FeS and H2S. This assumption was based on the fact that the reaction of FeS2 formation is exergonic enough with a standard free energy change (∆G°) of –9.18 kcal mol−1 to provide energy for CO2 reduction by H2252. Later, the reaction between FeS and H2S in the presence of CO2 in an aerobic aqueous environment has been experimentally demonstrated to yield H2 at temperatures above 50 °C253. In these experiments, the FeS/H2S system reduced CO2, producing a variety of organic sulfur compounds including thiols such as methanethiol (CH3SH) and thioethers such as dimethylsulfide (CH3SCH3). The reaction rate was dependent on temperature and substrate concentration, and HCl can be used in place of H2S.

A central problem of the chemoautotrophic theory is how the first carbon fixation could have occurred on Hadean Earth. Wächtershäuser hypothesized that the reaction of CO2 reduction was an autocatalytic one, and FeS2 formation could give a reductive power254. He suggested that, on early Earth, the reduction of CO2 or CO by FeS/H2S could have led to the formation of CH3SH with its subsequent carbonylation to thioacetic acid (CH3COSH). This compound was further activated through the production of acetyl-CoA (CH3COS-CoA), as part of the extant reductive (reverse) tricarboxylic acid (rTCA) cycle. In support to this theory, the ability of CH3SH to react with CO in the presence of FeS and NiS at a temperature of 100 °C in an aqueous solution with formation of S-methyl thioacetate (CH3COSCH3), which was then cleaved to CH3COSH, has been experimentally shown255. Therefore, this reaction could be a primordial initiation reaction for the chemoautotrophic origin of life.

Prebiotic carbon fixation in aquatic environments

Carbon fixation in moderate temperature shallow waters

The first hypothesis about the origin of life in a “warm little pond” environment has been proposed by Charles Darwin in 1871256. He suggested that “a protein compound was chemically formed’ with the use of various energy sources such as “light, heat, electricity”. Such terrestrial shallow pools could be exposed to the atmosphere and underwent wet-dry cycles allowing organic molecules to concentrate and polymerize on clay mineral surfaces. The role of minerals in low temperature catalysis is provided by their ability to decrease the activation energy of reactions257. Additionally, minerals provide their surfaces for absorption and concentration of diluted substrates, compartmentalization, creation of pH and electron potential gradients, and protection of synthesized organic molecules from UV and γ-radiation258. The Fe2+-bearing minerals have been shown to catalyze the formation of CH4, H2 and NH3 even at temperatures between 25 and 70 °C through a reaction of olivine with H2O during weathering259,260. Transition metal sulfide minerals have been suggested to serve as structural analogs of metalloenzymes in modern living organisms261. The role of minerals as templates for polymerization reactions is provided by arranging substrate molecules into specific orientations and facilitating bond formation between monomers of nucleic acids and peptides262.

Low temperature environments could have existed on early Earth due to weathering of impact ejecta, which could have occurred during the Late Heavy Bombardment (LHB), when the pCO2 could have decreased to 0.1–0.36 bars263. The LHB is a cataclysmic event that is hypothesized to occur in the inner Solar System between ca. 4.1 and 3.8 Ga due to the impacts of asteroids rather than comets264,265. Over last years, this hypothesis has been revisited implying that there was a prolonged bombardment of Moon and Earth between ca. 4.2 and 3.4 Ga with no a spike in the impact rate ca. 3.9 Ga as it has been suggested earlier266. Modeling of impact ejecta generated from intense bombardments on both terrestrial and aquatic environments showed that it could have altered the global carbon cycle by increasing CO2 sequestration in the form of carbonates267. The decrease in atmospheric pCO2 caused a lowering of the global mean surface temperature (GMST), which ranged from 0 to 50 °C whereas dissolved cations changed seawater pH towards near neutral and alkaline values between ca. 4.3 and 4.0 Ga268. Additionally, the assessment of mineral fingerprints and elemental composition of LUCA, from which both bacteria and archaea were descended, showed a moderate Hadean climate with dry and wet seasons and a lower atmospheric abundance of CO2 than today269. These data allow suggestion that large-scale, low temperature carbon fixation reactions could have occurred in Hadean cold and weakly acidic to neutral environments with wet and dry cycles observed in shallow waters, volcanic crater lakes and hot springs.

Volcanic hot springs are enriched in minerals such as olivine and iron sulfides and elements released from the Earth’s interior, creating a chemical disequilibrium228. These could have fueled reactions of proto-metabolism on early Earth. Under Hadean aquatic environments, low temperature shallow water settings could have enabled carbon fixation at the interface between the atmosphere, water, and minerals270. Under these conditions, production of CH4 by hydration of olivine or orthopyroxene without H2 production was observed in the presence of a carbon source at 50 °C (Fig. 3a). If FeS is used as a catalyst, H2 was generated for the reduction of CO2 to CH3OH at temperatures between 80 and 120 °C (Fig. 3b)271. The resulting products could be found in both atmosphere and groundwater. In geothermal conditions, organic compounds could have been absorbed on the surface of clay minerals for self-assembling272. In ancient aquatic environments, the abiotic reduction of NO2− to NH3 through the intermediate formation of NO and N2O could have occurred. A supercritical CO2 is a unique state of CO2 that lies between liquid and gas at temperatures above 31.1 °C and pressures exceeding 73.8 bar294. Mayer et al. hypothesized that a cyclic process involving repeated phase transitions can occur between supercritical CO2 and gaseous CO2 caused by periodic pressure and temperature changes296. This could have occurred in networks of channels and cavities at a depth of approximately 1 km. Such interconnected channels and cavities in the lithosphere could be generated by tidal influences and geyser activities, which trigger tectonic faulting297. The supercritical CO2 induces the formation of water droplets covered by a monolayer of amphiphilic compounds at the interface between the liquid/supercritical CO2 and H2O185. During the interaction with water, the droplets turn into vesicles, which have a bilayer membrane and can undergo morphological changes such as growth, fusion, fission, and budding, probably, being a prototype of protocells298. The disintegration of the vesicles due to their thermodynamic instability caused the release their organic contents into the bulk water. Therefore, such cycles of repetitive compartmentalization could have provided the synthesis of organic molecules under variable environmental condition299. Harrison et al. argue that carbon fixation from CO2 or CO within the protocells created a reaction network with the topology of a universal core of autotrophic metabolism300. This, probably, led to the emergence of genetic information through a direct biophysical interaction between amino acids and nucleotides, where oligonucleotides served as template for peptides, giving rise to the LUCA.

Organic synthesis in deep-sea hydrothermal vents

Deep-ocean alkaline hydrothermal systems could have provided natural proton gradient that served as an electrochemical potential or natural proton motive force to be used for the synthesis of organic molecules such as CH4, HCOOH, and others301,302. The first in situ pH measurements of hydrothermal vent fluids at mid-ocean ridges using solid-state electrochemical sensors have been reported by Ding et al.303. They showed that mixing and cooling could decrease fluid acidity, especially at intermediate temperatures between 250 and 300 °C and suggested that pH minimum can be explained by a reaction between FeS and H2S with the production of FeS2 and H2. FeS2 is precipitated whereas H2 can be further used for the reduction of CO2. The prebiotic reduction of CO2 by H2 to produce CH4 could have triggered the generation of massive banded iron formations (BIFs) due to the Fe2+ oxidation as shown by the investigation of δ56Fe value in Transvaal Supergroup in South Africa304. This conversion was accompanied by H+ release and, therefore, could have contributed to the control of seawater alkalinity. BIFs are composed of precipitated iron oxides such as Fe2+Fe23+O4 and hematite (Fe2O3) produced by oxidation of Fe2+ by oxygen generated by cyanobacteria305. This could have occurred due to the ferruginous character of seawater, enriched in dissolved Fe2+, prior to the permanent oxygenation of the atmosphere and oceans, known as the Great Oxygenation Event ca. 2.4 Ga306.

Plausible proto-metabolic pathways

In modern deep-sea microorganisms, six major metabolic pathways of autotrophic carbon fixation have been identified. These include the Calvin-Benson-Bassham (CBB) cycle, rTCA cycle, Wood-Ljungdahl pathway (WLP), 3-hydroxypropionate (3-HP) bicycle, 3-hydroxypropionate/4-hydroxybutyrate (3-HP/4-HB) cycle, and dicarboxylate/4-hydroxybutyrate (DC/4-HB) cycle328. Two of these pathways, the rTCA cycle and WJP (the acetyl-CoA pathway), are thought to have been characteristic of at least some Hadean environments and, therefore, they are the most plausible proto-metabolic processes329. The central carbon-fixing reactions in these reductive pathways could have been universal and occurred before the emergence of genes, being the anabolic core for all life forms330. These reactions produce key intermediates such as succinate, α-ketoglutarate and acetyl-CoA, which are necessary for the biosynthesis of building blocks of proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids.

The Hadean deep-ocean hydrothermal vents could have created conditions enriched in iron sulfides that acted as catalysts for the non-enzymatic cofactor-assisted rTCA cycle331. It has been shown that 6 of 11 reactions in the rTCA cycle in consecutive sequence are promoted by Zn2+, Cr3+ and Fe0 in an acidic aqueous solution332. In these reactions, the reductive amination of ketoacids to corresponding amino acids occurs under similar conditions. Unlike the oxidative TCA cycle, which is a catabolic process occurring through reactions of oxidation and decarboxylation (CO2 removal), the rTCA cycle is an anabolic process occurring through reactions of reduction and CO2 consumption333. During the course of rTCA, the reductive synthesis of CH3CO-SCoA from CO2 and H2O occurs through the carbon fixation using electron donors and, this makes the process a perfect proto-metabolic candidate for the origin of life.

The WLP is another pathway of reductive synthesis of CH3CO-SCoA, which in a variety of chemoautotrophic bacteria and archaea, including acetogens and methanogens, is probably an ancestral carbon fixation pathway under anaerobic conditions29,334. The WLP is a free energy-harvesting process with ∆G = −14.1 kcal mol−1 (see Eq. 9). In the biological pathway, tetrahydrofolate (THF) is used as a single-carbon group carrier, and its formyl-derivative (HCO-THF) is reduced to methyl-derivative (CH3-THF) in a methyl branch336. In a carbonyl branch of the pathway, CO is produced from CO2, which then reacts with CH3-THF. It has been proposed that minerals could be precursors of enzymes that catalyze the CH3CO-SCoA synthesis and that enzymatic processes were preceded by abiotic exergonic reactions catalyzed by naturally occurring minerals337. This reductive acetyl-CoA pathway allows a suggestion that metabolism could have emerged before the genes and that there could be a potential evolutionary link between mineral-based reactions and enzyme-catalyzed reactions, being an example of ‘pattern evolution”335.

Indeed, native iron Fe0 has been experimentally shown to promote the H2-dependent reduction of ferredoxin in aqueous solution at 40 °C and pH 8.5 or higher338. The Fe0 could have served as a catalyst in the primordial reactions of reduction and could be a precursor of modern electron-bifurcating oxidoreductase enzymes found in microorganisms. Such enzymes have multiple redox centers including FeS-clusters and a flavin-containing cofactor to enable the thermodynamically unfavorable reactions to occur by coupling the endergonic and exergonic reductions339. During the process of electron bifurcation, oxidation of two-electron donor such as H2 occurs. In this process, one electron is used for exergonic reduction of a high-potential acceptor such as NAD+ whereas a second electron is used for endergonic reduction of a low potential donor such as ferredoxin340. Therefore, an endergonic reaction can be driven by an exergonic reaction and this mechanism may be an ancient one that enables the energy production in deep-sea microorganisms. Ferredoxin is the strongest reductant involved in metabolism of anaerobic chemotrophic cells and uses electron bifurcation process for carbon fixation through CO2 reduction341. The reduced low potential ferredoxin is formed by acceptance of electrons from H2 with the use of Fe0 to transfer one electron from one FeS-cluster to other FeS-clusters. Some acetogens use the native Fe0 instead of H2 as an electron donor for CO2 reduction to CH3OH and CH3COOH utilizing the mechanism of electron bifurcation342. Additionally, the ability of methanogens for direct uptake of electrons from the environment associated with Fe0 oxidation to Fe2+ and H2 generation by hydrogenases for further carbon fixation to CH4 and HCOOH has been shown343,344.

In modern organisms, both thioesters such as acetyl-CoA and phosphate esters such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) are high-energy molecules that are used as sources of energy released upon the hydrolysis of ester bonds345,346. The hydrolysis rates showed that thioesters can tolerate hydrolysis under acidic conditions being stable at temperatures above 65 °C, whereas alkaline pH conditions favored phosphate esters347. In addition, thioesters are reported to efficiently support amino acid interactions, potentially leading to the formation of proto-peptides348 and ribosomal protein biosynthesis349. On the contrary, phosphate esters, such as pyrophosphate and polyphosphates, as well ATP and other nucleotide triphosphates provide chemical energy for the protein synthesis and are involved in DNA replication and RNA transcription350. Therefore, the solution to the discrepancy between the “metabolism-first” and “gene-first” hypotheses could lie in the environmental conditions that determine either the development of metabolism pathway first or the emergence of genes first.

Thermodynamic and environmental constraints for the origin of life

Living organisms are open systems capable of transforming energy to maintain their highly organized structures against the constant increase in universal entropy caused by the loss of energy as heat351. The evolution of metabolism in living cells involves capturing energy from the environment and transforming it into chemical energy in the form of ATP, thereby enabling a variety of cellular processes to occur352. The concept of LUCA enables understanding the origin of primordial metabolism by investigating the core reactions of biosynthesis that are universal for modern bacteria and archaea353. By calculating ΔG values, 95–97% of conserved and universal core reactions have been estimated to be exergonic at pH 7–10 and 80–100 °C under nonequilibrium conditions with H2 replacing biochemical reductants. These reactions trace back to the synthesis of amino acids, nucleotides and cofactors from H2, CO2, NH3, H2S and phosphate in an aqueous highly reducing, far-from-equilibrium environmental conditions. The LUCA, which probably existed between 4.3 and 4.1 Ga, has been shown to be a CO2- and N2-fixing H2-dependent thermophilic organism with a Wood-Ljungdahl pathway354,355. It was probably a chemoautotrophic, acetogenic organism that consumed CO2 and H2 from both geochemical and biotic sources355. The synthesis of the LUCA’s chemical constituents reveals a thermodynamic tendency of metabolism in living organisms. Life may have emerged as a self-sustaining chemical system that involves the free energy conversion through the coupling of endergonic anabolic reactions with more thermodynamically favorable exergonic catabolic reactions to maintain metabolism and structural organization of organisms353.

Unlike many modern biological processes, which require an energy input, the abiotic synthesis of cellular building blocks on the early Earth could have been thermodynamically favorable, yielding a net positive energy output356. Kua and Miller calculated ΔG values using quantum chemistry approach to analyze the thermodynamic characteristics of autocatalytic reactions involved in the prebiotic formation of C1 and C2 compounds from CO2, H2, and H2S in aqueous solutions357. The reaction of CH4 formation from a redox coupling of CO2 and H2 is exergonic, with ΔG = –39.5 kcal mol−1, when carbon is fully reduced from its oxidation state of +4 to –4. If carbon is not fully reduced, as in the formation of CH3OH, its oxidation state changes to –2 and ΔG = –11.2 kcal mol−1. A reaction of CO2 with a mixture of H2S and H2 resulted in the formation of CH3SH, the carbon oxidation state is also changed to –2, but ΔG is equal to −19.2 kcal mol−1. Although both of the latter reactions are exergonic, the last reaction leads to the formation of thiol that is a more stable compound. However, the formation of thioesters has positive ΔG values, indicating that these reactions are thermodynamically less favorable than the formation of their oxygen-containing counterparts. Calculations performed through modeling of hydrothermal conditions showed that in mixing zones, reduction of CO2 to a variety of organic compounds including carboxylic acids, alcohols, and ketones at temperatures between 50 and 250 °C is thermodynamically plausible358. These naturally established redox disequilibrium conditions created by mixing hot and alkaline hydrothermal fluids with cold acidic ocean water masses could have driven the synthesis of organic compounds from inorganic reactants.

Although thermodynamic calculations allow us to asses which reactions are energetically favorable, they do not fully reflect the reactions that actually occur in nature. For example, the reduction of CO2 (HCO3−) and CO to CH4 and other hydrocarbons by H2 is thermodynamically favorable; however, spontaneous reactions proceed very slowly under hydrothermal conditions due to high activation energy and require the presence of catalysts to promote electron transfer359. Thermodynamic calculations have been caried out for the synthesis of cellular building blocks, such as amines, amino acids, fatty acids, nucleotides, and saccharides, occurred at the interface between two fluids: low temperature (25 °C), mildly acidic (pH 6.5), relatively oxidized seawater and moderately hot (140 °C), alkaline (pH 9) and reduced hydrothermal vent fluids360. These calculations showed that moderate temperatures between 60 and 90 °C are most favorable for the organic synthesis. However, the proportion of thermodynamically favored products depends on the availability and concentration of substrates, reaction temperature, and the pH value of the environment.

An additional example is the WLP, exergonic process thermodynamically favorable for carbon fixation361; however, its feasibility under conditions on early Earth has been questioned. Nitschke and Russell proposed that modern methanotrophic acetogenesis is more likely than methanogenesis and reductive acetogenesis occurred in the WLP, the most ancient process that could have occurred under the alkaline hydrothermal vent conditions362. They argued that conventional reactions within the WLP process do not enable the entropy decrease necessary for the origin of life, since they are characterized by extremely low free energy output. Methanotrophic microorganisms utilize a single-carbon compounds such as CH4, CH3OH, and HCOH as carbon and energy sources for metabolism363. They require primarily the methane monooxygenase enzymes that catalyze the oxidation of CH4 to CH3OH and then to HCOH364. In the proposed methanotrophic acetogenesis pathway, the endergonic oxidation of CH4 to CH3OH requires additional exergonic reaction of reduction of NO3− to NO2−, and further to NO as an oxygen source365. At low temperatures, NO reacts with CH4 to produce CH3OH and N2O (see Eq. 10), and this allows overcoming the thermodynamic barrier. Indeed, methanotrophy has been shown to induce nitrogen fixation to NH3, which further gives rise to NO2− and NO3−366. Thermodynamic calculations of hypothetical prebiotic redox reactions at the interface between simulated acidic seawater and alkaline deep-sea hydrothermal fluids have revealed that methanotrophic acetogenesis is energetically less favorable than methanogenesis and acetogenesis. Some of these sediments contained 13C-depleted graphite microparticles, which allow suggesting their biogenic origin and indicate the existence of life at that time. The δ13C signature in primary graphite inclusions in detrital zircons from the Jack Hills in Western Australia allowed the identification of a probable organic origin for these inclusions, which were dated back to ca. 4.10 Ga371. Therefore, organic molecules could have been synthesized around that time, although one of most important factors influencing isotopic signatures is contamination by abiotic sources and extraterrestrial flux372. Due to the scarcity of the Hadean rock records, most data on the first organic synthesis on prebiotic Earth has been obtained by through modeling and reconstruction approaches373,374. However, there is still no credible model that explains how organic molecules were synthesized abiotically and how living cells emerged from non-living components. Additionally, laboratory experiments that simulate specific conditions that could have existed on early Earth do not enable the creation of regimes fully compatible with and observed in a definite environmental setting.

Efforts have been made to constrain experimentally simulated hydrothermal conditions favorable for serpentinization-driven H2 formation and carbon fixation through CO2 reduction to CH4 and other organic molecules. For example, it has been shown that mineral stability in ultramafic rocks and the distribution of Fe among mineral alteration products are temperature-dependent and likely major factors controlling the extent of H2 production. At temperatures below 150 °C, the H2 formation is decreased due to slow reaction kinetics and Fe2+ partitioning into minerals, whereas at temperatures above 350 °C, serpentinization occurs rapidly375. The H2 production has been linked to Si deficiency in serpentines, and it has been suggested that this may be limited by low concentrations of SiO2 in water at temperatures below 200 °C376. Additionally, the use of 13C-labeled inorganic carbon has shown that CH4 production at temperatures between 200 and 320 °C is very limited377. This is because abiotic CH4 production is kinetically slow under these temperatures even at high H2 concentrations. However, more extensive production of CH4 was observed under conditions that allow the formation of a H2-rich vapor phase, suggesting that shallow serpentinization environments with a separate gas phase as observed in shallow waters, may be more conducive to abiotic synthesis of CH4377.

An important challenge is the availability of chemical elements and molecules in sufficient quantities in a given environment. Especially, this concerns the availability of water and other inorganic molecules required for the synthesis of organic compounds, environmental settings where reactions could have occurred, and locations for the emergence of life214. In this context, it has been suggested that precipitation on mineral surfaces provided high concentrations of substrates for the synthesis of organic compounds and their polymerization to macromolecules378. Maruyama et al. proposed a nuclear geyser system as the most plausible environment, which provided the availability of elements, simple molecules, and energy for the synthesis of organic compounds379. The advantages of this environment include a high diversity of conditions such as cold-hot, dry-wet, and day-night cycles. Stüeken et al. hypothesized that life may have originated from the cumulative effect of multiple, recurrent, and interconnected processes216. They suggested that various organic compounds synthesized under specific conditions could have been transferred to other environments and underwent further reactions. The authors argued that each unique environment in the Hadean favored the synthesis of a few key compounds, and their interactions occurred on both large and small spatiotemporal scales.

Outlook

Despite the fact that many data remain controversial and continue to be debated, over the past decades, there has been a remarkable advancement in our understanding of the processes that may have occurred on early Earth. The explosive influx of new geological, geophysical, and geochemical data allowed proposing terrestrial, atmospheric, and aquatic conditions, which favored the emergence of life. Investigating the history of our planet and the origin of life is the most fascinating scientific field. This research field allows us to understand who we are and how humans evolved from simple life forms. Redox reactions played significant roles in geological and biological evolution of Earth. The abiotically occurring redox reactions contributed to the formation of the Earth’s atmosphere and oceans, driving geochemical cycles of redox-active elements and the synthesis of organic molecules, leading to the origin and evolution of life. The dominant theories of the origin of life are based on redox reactions, which could have occurred in the Hadean atmosphere, deep-ocean, and shallow waters emphasizing the crucial roles of minerals and energy sources. allowing these reactions to proceed.

The first life forms probably emerged as early as between 4.1 and 3.8 Ga, at the boundary between the Hadean and Archean eons. However, it was during the Hadean eon known as the early Earth, that atmospheric and aquatic conditions were favorable for the first organic synthesis. Based on recent advances in the field, some hallmarks of the origin of life on early Earth can be distinguished.

The Hadean atmosphere was likely slightly oxidizing with dominance of CO2, H2O, and SO2; however, CH4, NH3, and HCN could have been delivered to Earth by meteorite impacts, and transient reducing atmosphere could have existed during the Hadean eon. Moreover, meteorite could have delivered transition metals such as iron and organic compounds from extraterrestrial sources. Atmospheric composition and reactions and oceanic processes influenced each other, and the Hadean environments could have created conditions that favored redox reactions. Geothermal gradient created in hydrothermal systems provided energy, fueling organic synthesis underlying both Oparin-Haldane heterotrophic theory and mineral surface-based chemoautotrophic theory proposed by Wächtershäuser.

The first organic molecules necessary for the synthesis of the building blocks of life could have been formed in various environmental settings including the atmosphere, deep-ocean hydrothermal vents, and terrestrial shallow waters such as lakes, ponds, and hot springs. These environments utilized different energy sources such as UV radiation, volcanic activity, lightning, and geothermal heat. Depending on the availability of elements and energy sources, each environmental setting may favor the formation of specific organic molecules. However, these compounds could have been transported between different environmental settings for further processing. Therefore, life could have emerged in multiple interplayed environments, which allowed for diverse organic molecules to converge, eventually leading to self-replication and life.

There are two contrasting principles: “metabolism-first” and “gene first”, each with its own controversy. The “metabolism-first” principle’s controversy is that an early network of metabolic chemical reactions could not be sustainable without programmed replication-based genetic instructions. The “gene-first” principle’s controversy is that, for the formation of genetic molecules such as RNA, thermodynamically stable and self-replicating systems could not have emerged under severe conditions on early Earth. However, these two principles are not completely exclusive and can be reconciled because metabolic processes are necessary for the synthesis of nucleotides, which are the building blocks of RNA and DNA. It is probable that both metabolic pathways and the genetic system are evolved in an interdependent manner.

Heterotrophs and autotrophs could have originated through synchronizing evolutionary paths, and therefore both heterotrophic and chemoautotrophic theories of the origin of life may complement each other. According to chemoautotrophic theory of the origin of life, carbon fixation from CO2 using mineral surfaces in early Earth’s hydrothermal environments relies on reactions of reduction by H2 produced with the involvement of Fe2+ or H2S. This includes exergonic and thermodynamically favorable reductive metabolic processes such as the WLP and rTCA, which could prebiotically produce precursors for building blocks of proteins and nucleic acids. These metabolic cycles are considered as self-sustaining chemical systems, and organic molecules produced in these processes could be consumed by first heterotrophic organisms.
