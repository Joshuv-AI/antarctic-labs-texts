Authors: Alexandre Vallée

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis work proposes a unified framework for causal digital twins, integrating Structural Causal Models (SCMs), the Potential Outcomes Framework, and reinforcement learning. The objective is to enable individualized counterfactual reasoning and dynamic treatment policy optimization, moving beyond prediction toward actionable, ethical, and adaptive decision support.Materials and methodsThis work formalizes causal digital twins by embedding causal inference principles into digital architectures, modeling both observed and counterfactual outcomes for each patient. Individualized Treatment Effects (ITE) are estimated through causal modeling, and sequential decision-making is optimized using reinforcement learning techniques. This work is a methodological and conceptual research contribution. We propose a unified framework for the construction and evaluation of clinical digital twins and a practical checklist covering validation, safety, and ethical requirements. No new dataset or algorithmic benchmark is introduced.ResultsThis work discusses methodological requirements, learning objectives, evaluation strategies, and ethical considerations necessary for deploying causal digital twins in real-world clinical contexts. Introduction

In the evolving landscape of precision medicine, digital twins (DTs) have emerged as powerful tools for modeling, simulating, and forecasting individual health trajectories [1, 2]. Traditionally, DTs are deployed to replicate the physiological and pathological states of patients by leveraging high-dimensional data from clinical, biological, and behavioral sources [3–5]. These models are primarily focused on descriptive and predictive analytics, where machine learning algorithms are trained to anticipate future health outcomes based on historical data [6, 7]. While such approaches are undeniably valuable, they often fall short in addressing a fundamental question at the heart of clinical decision-making: “What would have happened if a different intervention had been chosen?”.

This question pertains to the realm of causal inference, which distinguishes correlation from causation and allows the estimation of counterfactual outcomes [8, 9]. In medical practice, treatment decisions hinge on counterfactual reasoning, comparing the observed outcome of a patient who received treatment “A” with the unobserved, hypothetical outcome had they received treatment “B” [10]. Traditional DTs, despite their predictive prowess, lack the theoretical structure to answer such questions reliably [11].

This gap motivates the integration of formal causal frameworks into the digital twin paradigm. Specifically, this work proposes the development of causally informed digital twins by embedding (1) structural causal models (SCMs), as introduced by Judea Pearl [8], which use directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) and structural equations to represent and manipulate causal relationships, (2) and the potential outcomes framework, or Rubin Causal Model [9, 12], which defines treatment effects as comparisons between potential outcomes under different treatment conditions.

By enriching DTs with causal inference capabilities, we can transition from “what is likely to happen?” to “what would happen if.?“, a shift that is essential for personalized, ethically grounded, and evidence-based medical decision-making [13, 14].

This article aims to establish a methodological and conceptual foundation for causal digital twins. This article argues that these hybrid models are not only technically feasible but necessary for the future of AI-driven healthcare. In the following sections, this article explores the theoretical frameworks that underpin causal inference, describes how these can be operationalized within digital twin architectures, and presents methodologies for training, evaluating, and deploying causally-enabled DTs in real-world clinical contexts.

Although this manuscript follows the structure of a research article, its contribution is methodological rather than empirical. The objective is not to introduce a new dataset, develop a novel machine-learning architecture, or report benchmark performance.

Instead, this article aims to:

(i)Formalize an integrative conceptual framework for clinical digital twins,(ii)Synthesize current methodological requirements across modeling, data integration, and validation, and.(iii)Propose an operational evaluation and ethical checklist to support clinical deployment and regulatory assessment.

Statement of Significance:

Problem or issue

Traditional digital twins in healthcare are predominantly predictive and do not support causal reasoning or individualized counterfactual simulation, limiting their utility for clinical decision-making.

What is already known

Structural causal models (SCMs) and the potential outcomes framework allow rigorous estimation of treatment effects and counterfactuals. However, they have not been systematically integrated into digital twin architectures.

What this paper adds

This paper proposes a unified framework for causal digital twins, combining SCMs, potential outcomes, and reinforcement learning to enable dynamic, individualized treatment optimization. It defines methodological pipelines and evaluation metrics for real-world deployment.

Who would benefit

Researchers and developers in biomedical informatics, precision medicine, and AI-driven healthcare systems seeking to build interpretable, adaptive, and ethically sound clinical decision-support tools.

Theoretical framework

In contrast to purely predictive models, which estimate what is likely to happen given observed data, causal inference aims to answer intervention-based questions such as: “What would happen to this patient if we actively changed the treatment?” [15].

For example, a predictive digital twin may learn that patients receiving treatment A have worse outcomes than those receiving treatment B. However, this association alone does not imply that treatment A is harmful, as patients receiving A may be systematically sicker. Causal inference explicitly models such confounding mechanisms and seeks to estimate outcomes under hypothetical interventions, answering questions such as “What would the outcome have been for this same patient had treatment B been given instead?”.

Structural causal models formalize this reasoning by separating observed associations from causal effects, enabling digital twins to simulate counterfactual outcomes that are essential for individualized treatment decisions [10, 14].

Structural Causal Models are mathematical frameworks used to model and analyze causal relationships between variables. They consist of structural equations that express each variable as a deterministic function of its direct causes (or parents) and a stochastic noise term, capturing unobserved factors (Fig. 1).

A key tool within SCMs is the do-operator, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:do(T=t)$$\end{document}, which represents an intervention that forcibly sets T to t, thereby breaking its natural dependencies. The do-operator, introduced by Judea Pearl, is a formal notation used in causal inference to represent interventions in a system.

This expression means that the variable T is forcibly set to the value ttt, as if by intervention, independent of its natural causes.

This allows the estimation of causal effects:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{Y}\mid\:\:\mathrm{d}\mathrm{o}(\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{t})\ne\:\mathrm{P}(\mathrm{Y}\mid\:\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{t})$$\end{document}

This inequality illustrates that observing someone who received \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{t}$$\end{document} does not reveal the causal effect unless we intervene. The causal effect of T on Y is best described using the Average Treatment Effect (ATE):\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:ATE=\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid\:\:\mathrm{d}\mathrm{o}(\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{t})\right]\:-\mathbb{E}\left[Y\mid\:\:\mathrm{d}\mathrm{o}(\mathrm{T}=0)\right]$$\end{document}

The notation \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\mathrm{d}\mathrm{o}(\mathrm{T}=\mathrm{t})$$\end{document} indicates an intervention, breaking all natural (possibly confounded) causes of T.

This represents the average expected change in the outcome Y if the entire population were to receive treatment compared to no treatment. The do-notation emphasizes that these expectations are based on intervention, not just statistical associations.

This equation captures the expected difference in outcomes if everyone in the population were to receive treatment T = 1 versus if they received T = 0.

The ATE measures the causal effect of the treatment across a population. If: ATE > 0, then the treatment has a positive effect on the outcome on average. If: ATE < 0, then the treatment has a negative (possibly harmful) effect on average.

Potential outcomes framework

The Potential Outcomes Framework, also known as the Rubin Causal Model, is a cornerstone of modern causal inference. It frames the causal effect of a treatment as a comparison between the potential outcomes that would occur under different treatment assignments.

Definition of potential outcomes

For each individual, define two potential outcomes:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}\left(1\right)$$\end{document}: the outcome if the individual receives te treatement.

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}\left(0\right)$$\end{document}: the outcome if the individual does not receive the treatment.

However, we can only observe one of these outcomes for each person, either \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}\left(1\right)$$\end{document} or \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}\left(0\right)$$\end{document}, depending on the actual treatment assignment \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}$$\end{document}. This is known as the “fundamental problem of causal inference”.

Causal estimands

Individual treatment effect (ITE):\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{ITE}_{i}={Y}_{i}\left(1\right)-{Y}_{i}\left(0\right)$$\end{document}

Where:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{Y}_{i}\left(1\right)$$\end{document} is the potential outcome for individual i if treated.

The ITE quantifies the causal effect of a treatment on a specific individual. It is defined as the difference between two potential outcomes for the same person: one under treatment and one under no treatment.

And for interpretation:

ATE captures the causal effect of a treatment on average across the population.

It is useful for policy decisions and public health planning.

Thus, the ATE measures the expected difference in outcomes between a population that receives a treatment and the same population if it had not received the treatment.

Digital twins as causal digital twins

Digital twins, when enriched with causal inference frameworks, become powerful tools not only for prediction but also for simulating alternative realities — that is, counterfactuals. In this role, digital twins act as “causal digital twins”: computational systems capable of estimating both observed and unobserved potential outcomes for an individual, thereby quantifying the effects of interventions.

Developing and deploying causal digital twins in healthcare raises significant ethical considerations that must be addressed systematically within the methodology.

First, transparency and explainability are essential to ensure that clinicians and patients understand the basis of treatment recommendations. Models must integrate interpretability tools, such as SHAP or ICE plots, to make the causal mechanisms and individualized treatment effects comprehensible to non-technical users.

Second, fairness and bias auditing must be an integral part of model validation. Digital twins should be evaluated across demographic subgroups (e.g., age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status) to detect and mitigate disparate impacts. Strategies such as subgroup-specific performance metrics, fairness-constrained optimization, and bias mitigation techniques should be employed to ensure equitable model behavior.

Third, privacy and data protection are paramount. Given that digital twins simulate sensitive health trajectories, robust measures must be implemented to safeguard patient data. Approaches such as data de-identification, federated learning, and differential privacy mechanisms can be used to minimize the risks associated with centralized data storage and model training.

Then, the deployment of causal digital twins must uphold the principle of human oversight. Clinical decision-making should not be fully delegated to automated systems; rather, digital twins should serve as advisory tools that augment, but do not replace, physician judgment. Clear accountability frameworks must be established to delineate the roles and responsibilities of model developers, clinical users, and institutional governance bodies.

Synthesize

Having outlined the key theoretical frameworks and methodological advances underpinning causal digital twins, we now synthesize these concepts into a unified formalism that captures the essential structure of individualized counterfactual modeling and decision optimization.

The convergence of causal inference, individualized simulation, and adaptive decision-making heralds a paradigm shift in precision medicine. By embedding SCMs and the Potential Outcomes Framework within dynamic digital twin architectures, and extending these models with reinforcement learning for policy optimization, we create computational systems capable of both simulating and prescribing personalized health trajectories. This synthesis bridges prediction and intervention, allowing digital twins to not only foresee clinical futures but to shape them through counterfactual reasoning and optimal action selection.

The development of digital twins as causal digital twins marks a transformative step in personalized medicine and data-driven healthcare [5, 16, 17]. Unlike traditional predictive models, causal digital twins not only forecast likely outcomes but also simulate alternative scenarios, allowing clinicians to answer “what if” questions with greater confidence [1]. One of the principal strengths of causal digital twins lies in their ability to estimate individualized treatment effects [18]. This capacity enables tailored medical decisions that go beyond average treatment effects by accounting for the heterogeneity in patient characteristics and responses (Table 2) [19].

By incorporating formal causal frameworks such as Structural Causal Models and the Potential Outcomes Framework [8, 20], digital twins can overcome common biases in observational data and provide plausible simulations of counterfactual outcomes [14]. Furthermore, digital twins integrate seamlessly with modern machine learning architectures, ranging from neural networks to tree-based methods [7], which allows for their scalable deployment across large and complex clinical datasets. Their compatibility with interpretability tools, such as SHAP values or ICE plots [21, 22], supports transparency and builds clinician trust in decision-support systems.

Despite these advantages, several challenges remain. The reliability of a digital twin model depends heavily on the quality, completeness, and granularity of the data it uses [23]. Missing data, measurement error, and unmeasured confounding can significantly compromise model performance [24]. Moreover, because counterfactual outcomes are inherently unobservable, validating these predictions remains methodologically difficult [13]. Although synthetic benchmarks and simulated data provide partial solutions [25, 26], they cannot fully substitute real-world evidence. Another challenge is the computational complexity involved in training robust models, particularly those that rely on generative or adversarial architectures [27]. Furthermore, models trained in one healthcare setting or population may not generalize well to others without further calibration or domain adaptation [28]. Finally, deploying digital twins in clinical environments raises important regulatory, legal, and ethical questions [29, 30]. These include concerns about model transparency, fairness across patient populations, and the degree of human oversight required in high-stakes decision-making [31].

Looking forward, several promising directions are emerging. The integration of digital twins into real-time clinical decision support systems could bring simulated insights directly to the point of care [32]. The development of longitudinal and dynamic twins, capable of updating as patient trajectories evolve, may further enhance the timeliness and relevance of recommendations. Hybrid models that combine expert clinical knowledge with data-driven inference are likely to improve causal validity while maintaining interpretability [33]. Digital twins could also be used to simulate public health interventions at scale, helping policymakers assess the potential outcomes of healthcare reforms before implementation. Moreover, the use of federated learning and privacy-preserving computation will be key to enabling secure model training across institutions, ensuring that sensitive health data remains protected (Fig. 2) [34].

In parallel, interpretability is a critical component in the deployment of causal digital twins, particularly in clinical contexts where decision support must be transparent and actionable [35, 36]. While digital twins can emulate both factual and counterfactual outcomes, understanding why a model recommends a specific treatment or predicts a certain response is essential for clinical trust and regulatory acceptance [29].

Tools such as SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) and ICE (Individual Conditional Expectation) plots can be integrated into causal digital twins to reveal the contribution of individual covariates to predicted treatment effects [21, 22]. SHAP values, derived from cooperative game theory, provide a local explanation by quantifying the marginal impact of each feature on the model’s output for a specific individual [37]. In contrast, ICE plots show how the predicted outcome changes as a single feature varies, holding others constant [38].

Beyond transparency, interpretability also facilitates model validation, error analysis, and bias auditing [39]. By visualizing and interpreting treatment effect heterogeneity, digital twins can highlight outliers, identify sources of confounding, and support counterfactual reasoning in complex clinical scenarios. Thus, integrating explainability methods not only enhances clinical usability but also reinforces the scientific credibility of causal digital twin models [31, 40].

Methodological limitations and causal assumptions

The validity of counterfactual predictions generated by causal digital twins relies on a set of causal assumptions that warrant careful consideration. Chief among these is the assumption of no unmeasured confounding, which posits that all variables jointly influencing treatment assignment and outcomes are observed and appropriately modeled [8, 15]. In real-world clinical data, this assumption is inherently strong and often unverifiable, as latent factors such as clinician judgment, patient preferences, or unrecorded disease severity may influence both treatment decisions and outcomes [15, 41].

Violations of this assumption may lead to biased estimates of treatment effects, potentially compromising the reliability of individualized recommendations [42, 43]. As a result, causal digital twins should not be interpreted as definitive decision-makers, but rather as decision-support tools whose outputs must be contextualized within clinical expertise.

Additional assumptions, including positivity (sufficient overlap between treatment groups), consistency, and correct model specification, further constrain applicability. Limited overlap may restrict inference to specific patient subgroups, while model misspecification can distort both factual and counterfactual predictions.

To mitigate these limitations, several strategies can be employed, including sensitivity analyses to assess robustness to unmeasured confounding, incorporation of domain knowledge in causal graph specification, triangulation across multiple data sources, and prospective validation where feasible. Ultimately, maintaining human oversight and emphasizing interpretability are essential to ensure that causal digital twins enhance, rather than replace, clinical judgment [23].

Causal assumptions, failure modes, and mitigation strategies

Individual-level counterfactual inference using digital twins rests on a set of structural assumptions that are both necessary for identification and fragile in realistic clinical environments. Making these assumptions explicit is essential for interpreting estimated individual and conditional average treatment effects as causal quantities rather than purely predictive constructs.

The assumption of consistency requires that the potential outcome under a given intervention corresponds to the outcome observed when that intervention is implemented in practice. In clinical settings, however, interventions are rarely atomic: treatment effects may vary with dose, timing, adherence, concomitant therapies, and provider behavior. Violations of consistency induce ambiguity in the definition of the causal estimand itself, rendering individualized effect estimates ill-posed. A principled mitigation strategy consists in formulating interventions as well-defined dynamic treatment regimes and modeling treatment intensity and adherence explicitly within the state transition equations of the twin.

Exchangeability (or ignorability) conditional on observed covariates is required for causal identification [8, 44]. This assumption is particularly vulnerable in healthcare, where latent constructs such as frailty, clinician judgment, subclinical disease severity, and socioeconomic context often influence both treatment assignment and outcomes. When violated, estimated ITEs and CATEs may exhibit systematic bias that is not detectable through conventional predictive validation. Beyond high-dimensional adjustment, methodological safeguards include the incorporation of negative and positive control outcomes or exposures, formal sensitivity analyses to quantify the magnitude of unmeasured confounding required to overturn conclusions, and partial identification approaches that yield bounded rather than point estimates [45].

The positivity assumption requires sufficient overlap in treatment assignment across the covariate space. In practice, clinical contraindications, institutional protocols, and strong physician preferences may create structural violations whereby certain patient profiles are deterministically assigned to a single treatment. In such regions, individualized counterfactual predictions become extrapolations unsupported by empirical evidence. Diagnostic evaluation of overlap, restriction of inference to regions of common support, and explicit reporting of effective sample size are therefore necessary to prevent overconfident decision support.

Digital twins implicitly assume correct model specification at multiple levels: statistical risk models, mechanistic submodels, and their coupling through state-space representations. Misspecification may arise from incomplete biological knowledge, dataset shift, or simplifications introduced for computational tractability. Importantly, low predictive error on observed outcomes does not guarantee valid counterfactual reasoning. Model criticism should therefore extend beyond discrimination metrics to include calibration of predicted trajectories, posterior predictive checks, invariance testing across environments, and stress testing under controlled perturbations of physiological parameters [46].

Causal digital twins presuppose a degree of transportability and temporal stability of causal mechanisms [47]. Shifts in population case-mix, standards of care, diagnostic practices, or healthcare infrastructure may invalidate previously learned relationships. Formal transportability analysis, reweighting schemes, domain adaptation techniques, and continual learning frameworks are thus required to maintain causal interpretability under deployment drift. Collectively, these considerations imply that causal digital twins should be conceptualized as epistemic instruments whose outputs are contingent on unverifiable assumptions and model abstractions [48]. Their safe clinical integration therefore depends not only on algorithmic performance, but on systematic assumption auditing, uncertainty propagation, and transparent communication of the limits of causal interpretability (Table 3).

Conclusion

Causal digital twins represent a pivotal advancement in precision medicine, enabling not only the prediction of future health trajectories but also the simulation of individualized counterfactual scenarios. By integrating structural causal models, potential outcomes frameworks, and reinforcement learning into dynamic digital architectures, this work offers a unified foundation for optimizing personalized treatment decisions. While challenges related to data quality, counterfactual validation, and fairness remain, the methodological synthesis proposed here moves digital twins beyond prediction toward actionable and adaptive decision support systems, paving the way for the next generation of AI-driven healthcare. Moreover, while causal digital twins offer powerful counterfactual insights, their clinical impact ultimately depends on careful validation of causal assumptions and responsible integration into human-centered decision-making workflows.
