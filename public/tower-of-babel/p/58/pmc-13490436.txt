Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio of the gut microbiota and its association with abdominal obesity and insulin resistance (METS-IR) in Korean adults
Authors: Jae-Min Park, Ja-Eun Choi, Yu-Jin Kwon, Ji-Won Lee, Kyung-Won Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The gut microbiota has been increasingly implicated in the pathogenesis of obesity and insulin resistance. The Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio may serve as a marker of metabolic health; nonetheless, evidence for its association with abdominal obesity and insulin resistance in Asian populations remains scarce. This retrospective cross-sectional study included 186 Korean adults undergoing health check-ups. Gut microbiota composition was assessed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing, and the phylum-level F/B ratio was calculated. Abdominal obesity was defined using waist circumference criteria for Asian populations, and insulin resistance was evaluated using the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR). Participants were categorized into F/B ratio quartiles. Associations between the F/B ratio, abdominal obesity, and insulin resistance were examined using linear and logistic regression analyses with adjustment for potential confounders. Higher F/B ratios were inversely associated with waist circumference and METS-IR. Abdominal obesity prevalence decreased progressively across increasing F/B ratio quartiles. In multivariable linear regression analyses, the F/B ratio remained independently associated with lower METS-IR (β = − 0.911, p = 0.040). In fully adjusted logistic regression models, participants in the highest F/B ratio quartile had significantly lower odds of abdominal obesity than did those in the lowest quartile (OR = 0.17, 95% CI 0.05–0.59). In Korean adults, the gut microbial F/B ratio was inversely associated with abdominal obesity and insulin resistance, as assessed by METS-IR, a composite index reflecting adiposity-related metabolic dysfunction. These findings suggest that gut microbiota composition may be metabolically relevant to visceral adiposity–related metabolic dysfunction and that the F/B ratio could serve as a complementary indicator for metabolic risk stratification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56849-z.

Introduction

The human gut microbiota is key to host metabolic regulation and has been increasingly implicated in the development of obesity and insulin resistance. Alterations in gut microbial composition can influence energy harvest, lipid metabolism, systemic inflammation, and insulin signaling pathways, thereby contributing to metabolic disorders1. Among various indices of gut microbiota composition, the relative abundance of the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, commonly expressed as the Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes (F/B) ratio, has been widely investigated as a potential marker of metabolic health2,3.

Notably, several studies have reported that an increased F/B ratio is associated with obesity and metabolic dysfunction; however, others have demonstrated inconsistent or population-specific findings2–4. These discrepancies may reflect differences in ethnicity, dietary patterns, lifestyle factors, and methodological heterogeneity across studies. Specifically, data on the association between the F/B ratio and metabolic outcomes in Asian populations, including Koreans, remain limited and inconclusive4. Given the distinct metabolic profiles and body fat distribution patterns observed in East Asian populations5,6, further investigation in this context is warranted.

Abdominal obesity, characterized by excess visceral adiposity, is a key determinant of metabolic risk and is more strongly associated with insulin resistance and cardiometabolic complications than general obesity7. Moreover, visceral fat accumulation is closely associated with dyslipidemia, chronic low-grade inflammation, and impaired insulin sensitivity, all of which may be influenced by gut microbiota composition1,8. Therefore, examining the relationship between the F/B ratio and abdominal obesity may provide critical insights into the metabolic relevance of gut microbiota beyond body mass index alone.

Insulin resistance is commonly assessed using surrogate indices derived from fasting glucose and lipid parameters9,10. More recently, composite indices such as the Metabolic Score for Insulin Resistance (METS-IR), which incorporates fasting glucose, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and body mass index, have been proposed as markers of insulin resistance that capture lipid- and adiposity-related metabolic features, particularly in population-based studies11–13.

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate the association between the gut microbial F/B ratio and abdominal obesity, as well as adiposity-related metabolic dysfunction assessed by METS-IR, in Korean adults.

Methods

Study overview and study participants

This retrospective, cross-sectional study included adult volunteers who participated in a medical health check-up program at Gangnam Severance Hospital, Seoul, Korea, between December 2019 and December 2021. Individuals aged 19 years or older were eligible for inclusion. Participants with missing data or incomplete questionnaires were excluded. Ultimately, 186 participants (54 men and 132 women) were included in the final analysis. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Gangnam Severance Hospital (approval number: 3–2019-0226).

Participants were enrolled during routine health check-ups. At enrollment, clinical and anthropometric measurements were obtained, and questionnaire-based information, including smoking status and alcohol consumption, was collected. Blood samples were obtained during the visit for metabolic assessments. Fecal samples were collected separately using a standardized stool collection kit and returned for gut microbiota analysis.

Fecal sample collection, DNA extraction, and 16S rRNA sequencing

Fecal samples (approximately 200 mg per participant) were collected using a stool collection and transfer kit (PDXen, Korea) according to standardized procedures. After collection, samples were stored at 4 °C and transported to the laboratory. Upon arrival, samples were processed for DNA extraction as soon as possible, typically within approximately 1–2 days. Genomic DNA was extracted according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with minor modifications. Briefly, fecal material was mixed with 1.4 mL of ASL buffer in a 2-mL microcentrifuge tube and vortexed until completely homogenized. The homogenate was then combined with 0.2 g of sterile zirconia/silica beads and subjected to mechanical disruption using a TissueLyser at 30 Hz for 6 min. Samples were subsequently incubated at 95 °C for 5 min to complete cell lysis.

DNA extraction was performed using the QIAamp DNA Stool Mini Kit, and DNA was eluted in 100 μL of the provided elution buffer. The V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified, and libraries were prepared for next-generation sequencing according to the Illumina 16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library Preparation protocol. DNA concentration and purity were assessed using PicoGreen and NanoDrop, respectively. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of the V4 region was performed using the primer pair 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), with 12.5 ng of input genomic DNA per reaction.

Following initial amplification, limited-cycle PCR was performed to attach dual-index barcodes and Illumina sequencing adapters. Amplicon libraries were normalized and pooled according to PicoGreen quantification, and the library size distribution was confirmed using the LabChip GX HT DNA High Sensitivity Kit. The final pooled libraries were sequenced on the Illumina MiSeq platform using standard paired-end sequencing chemistry.

Sequence data processing and taxonomic assignment

Raw sequencing reads were processed using a standardized bioinformatics workflow. Primer sequences corresponding to the 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) primer pair were removed using cutadapt. Paired-end reads were merged using FLASH, with the maximum overlap parameter set to 120 bp. The merged reads were then quality-filtered using Sickle with a Phred quality threshold of 20.

After preprocessing, the quality-filtered merged reads were imported into Quantitative Insights Into Microbial Ecology 2 (QIIME 2, version 2020.11) as single-end sequences14 Because paired-end reads were merged before QIIME 2 import, DADA2 was performed using the denoise-single workflow rather than the paired-end workflow15. During DADA2 denoising, the first 12 bases were trimmed from each read and reads were truncated at 150 bp (–p-trim-left 12 and –p-trunc-len 150). DADA2 was used for denoising, error correction, chimera removal, and amplicon sequence variant (ASV) inference.

Sequencing depth and microbiome quality control metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Sequencing depth was calculated from the feature table generated after DADA2 denoising and chimera removal, before rarefaction. The sequencing depth per sample before rarefaction ranged from 145,340 to 4,321,592 reads, with a median of 1,100,688 reads and a mean of 1,240,019 reads. The feature table was rarefied to 10,000 reads per sample for downstream diversity and taxonomic analyses, and all 186 samples were retained after rarefaction. Although no universal rarefaction threshold applies to all 16S rRNA studies, a depth of 10,000 reads has been commonly used in previous 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing analyses. The final number of ASVs retained after DADA2 processing was 22,568.

Taxonomic classification of representative ASV sequences was performed using a naïve Bayes classifier trained on the SILVA reference database (version 138) for the 515F/806R V4 region16. Taxonomic assignments were summarized across hierarchical taxonomic levels, including the phylum level. Sequences classified as mitochondria, chloroplasts, or non-bacterial taxa were removed before downstream analyses. Alpha diversity indices, including the Shannon index and Pielou’s evenness, were calculated using the rarefied ASV table.

Negative controls and batch controls were not included in this study. All samples were processed together using the same standardized bioinformatics workflow, and no separate batch-effect correction was applied.

The F/B ratio was calculated from phylum-level relative abundance data derived from the rarefied feature table. For each participant, the relative abundance of Firmicutes was divided by that of Bacteroidetes. No participant had zero Bacteroidetes abundance; therefore, no pseudocount or sample exclusion was required for calculation of the F/B ratio.

Definition of clinical variables

Abdominal obesity was defined according to the International Diabetes Federation criteria for Asian populations, using waist circumference cutoffs of ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 85 cm for women5.

Insulin resistance was assessed using multiple surrogate indices. The METS-IR was calculated using fasting plasma glucose, triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, and body mass index (BMI), with higher values indicating greater insulin resistance11. The single-point insulin sensitivity estimator (SPISE) was calculated based on triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and BMI, with lower values indicating reduced insulin sensitivity17. The homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was calculated as fasting insulin (µIU/mL) multiplied by fasting plasma glucose (mg/dL) divided by 40518.

Visceral adiposity was further assessed using the visceral adiposity index and the new visceral adiposity index. The visceral adiposity index was calculated using sex-specific formulas incorporating waist circumference, BMI, triglycerides, and HDL cholesterol19. The new visceral adiposity index was calculated using previously published formulas that incorporate waist circumference, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, and fasting plasma glucose, with higher values indicating greater visceral adiposity–related metabolic risk20,21. This index is defined on a probability scale ranging from 0 to 1, and therefore relatively small numerical values are expected.

Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and waist circumference, were obtained using standardized protocols during the health check-up examination. BMI was calculated as weight (in kilograms) divided by height (in meters) squared. Blood pressure was measured by trained personnel after participants had rested for at least 5 min in a seated position.

Smoking status and alcohol drinking status were assessed using self-reported questionnaires at enrollment. Smoking status was categorized as never smoker, former smoker, or current smoker based on the participants’ responses. Former smokers were defined as participants who reported that they had smoked in the past but were not currently smoking; no specific cut-off time since smoking cessation was applied. Alcohol drinking status was assessed using a self-reported yes/no question and categorized as drinker or non-drinker. Detailed information on smoking cessation duration, alcohol intake frequency, amount, or duration was not available. Fasting blood samples were collected after an overnight fast of at least 8 h and were analyzed for glucose, lipid profiles, and insulin using standard laboratory methods.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcomes of this study were abdominal obesity and the metabolic score for insulin resistance (METS-IR). Additional metabolic indices, including SPISE, HOMA-IR, VAI, and NVAI, were examined in supplementary analyses.

Continuous variables are reported as medians with interquartile ranges, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Participants were categorized into quartiles according to the F/B ratio distribution. Differences in baseline characteristics across F/B ratio quartiles were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

The F/B ratio was analyzed both as a continuous variable and as a categorical variable based on quartiles. Continuous analyses were used to evaluate the overall association between the F/B ratio and metabolic outcomes, whereas quartile-based analyses were used to examine patterns across the distribution of the F/B ratio. The associations between the F/B ratio and waist circumference and METS-IR were evaluated using linear regression analyses and visualized using scatter plots with fitted regression lines.

Multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to examine the association between the F/B ratio and METS-IR after adjustment for age, sex, smoking status, and alcohol consumption. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for abdominal obesity across F/B ratio quartiles, with Q1 used as the reference group. All four quartiles were presented separately. Three models were specified: Model 1 was adjusted for age; Model 2 was adjusted for age and sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted for smoking status and alcohol consumption.

BMI was not included as a covariate in models for abdominal obesity due to its conceptual and statistical overlap with waist circumference, which may lead to multicollinearity and overadjustment.

Because the F/B ratio showed a right-skewed distribution, sensitivity analyses were performed using the natural log-transformed F/B ratio. The distribution of the original and log-transformed F/B ratio was assessed using descriptive statistics and skewness. No participant had an F/B ratio of zero or below; therefore, no pseudocount was required for log-transformation. Sensitivity analyses using the log-transformed F/B ratio were conducted for waist circumference, METS-IR, and abdominal obesity.

In supplementary analyses, Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated between the natural log-transformed F/B ratio and insulin resistance-related indices, including METS-IR, SPISE, and HOMA-IR. Steiger’s Z-test was used to compare dependent correlation coefficients sharing a common variable, the log-transformed F/B ratio. Specifically, the correlation between the log-transformed F/B ratio and METS-IR was used as the reference and was compared with the correlation between the log-transformed F/B ratio and SPISE and with the correlation between the log-transformed F/B ratio and HOMA-IR. This test accounts for the correlation between the two metabolic indices being compared and evaluates whether two dependent correlation coefficients differ significantly. The resulting p-values indicate whether each correlation coefficient differs significantly from the correlation between the log-transformed F/B ratio and METS-IR, rather than the statistical significance of each individual correlation.

Detailed results of the log-transformed sensitivity analyses and supplementary correlation analyses are provided in the Supplementary Material. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2), and a two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study participants according to quartiles of the F/B ratio. Participants in the lowest F/B ratio quartile (Q1) were older than those in the highest quartile (Q4) (median age, 39.0 vs. 29.0 years; p < 0.001), and the proportion of men was higher in Q1 than in Q4 (44.7% vs. 19.1%; p = 0.017). Gut microbial diversity increased across higher F/B ratio quartiles, as reflected by both the Shannon index and Pielou’s evenness (both p < 0.001). Overall, the gut microbiota was predominantly composed of the phyla Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, consistent with typical human gut microbial composition. Alcohol drinking status did not differ significantly across quartiles (p = 0.250), whereas smoking status differed significantly among groups (p = 0.035). Among anthropometric and metabolic parameters, waist circumference was significantly different across F/B ratio quartiles and was lower in Q4 than in Q1 (median, 74.3 cm vs. 86.0 cm; p = 0.030). Body mass index, blood pressure, fasting plasma glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol, insulin, and HOMA-IR did not differ significantly across quartiles. METS-IR differed significantly across F/B ratio quartiles and tended to decrease with higher F/B ratio groups, from 36.05 in Q1 to 30.89 in Q4 (p = 0.024). In contrast, SPISE values tended to increase across higher F/B ratio quartiles (p = 0.037), consistent with a more favorable insulin sensitivity profile. Visceral adiposity-related indices also differed across quartiles. The visceral adiposity index was significantly different among groups (p = 0.003), and the new visceral adiposity index decreased across higher F/B ratio quartiles, with the lowest value observed in Because the original F/B ratio showed a right-skewed distribution, sensitivity analyses were performed using the natural log-transformed F/B ratio. The original F/B ratio had a mean of 1.49, median of 1.03, and skewness of 1.33, whereas the log-transformed F/B ratio had a skewness of 0.13, indicating substantially reduced asymmetry (Supplementary Table 2). In scatter plot analyses using the log-transformed F/B ratio, inverse associations were observed with waist circumference (R2 = 0.106, p < 0.001) and METS-IR (R2 = 0.075, p < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 1).

In fully adjusted sensitivity analyses, the log-transformed F/B ratio was significantly associated with lower waist circumference (β = − 2.511, SE = 0.927, p = 0.007) and lower odds of abdominal obesity (OR = 0.491, 95% CI 0.294–0.821, p = 0.007). The association with METS-IR remained inverse in direction but was attenuated to borderline statistical significance (β = − 1.152, SE = 0.599, p = 0.056; Supplementary Table 3).

In supplementary correlation analyses using the log-transformed F/B ratio, the log-transformed F/B ratio was negatively correlated with METS-IR (r = − 0.274) and positively correlated with SPISE (r = 0.214), whereas its correlation with HOMA-IR was minimal (r = 0.030). Steiger’s Z-test showed that the correlations of the log-transformed F/B ratio with SPISE and HOMA-IR differed significantly from that with METS-IR (both p < 0.001; Supplementary Table 4). Given the opposite directionality of SPISE, where higher values indicate greater insulin sensitivity, these findings are consistent with an association between higher F/B ratio and a more favorable adiposity-related metabolic profile.

Discussion

In this cross-sectional study of Korean adults, the gut microbial F/B ratio was inversely associated with abdominal obesity and insulin resistance, as assessed by METS-IR. Given that METS-IR incorporates adiposity- and lipid-related components, this association may reflect broader adiposity-related metabolic dysfunction rather than insulin resistance alone. Participants with higher F/B ratios had lower waist circumference, lower METS-IR values, and a substantially lower prevalence of abdominal obesity. These associations remained significant after adjustment for major demographic and lifestyle factors, supporting an independent relationship between the F/B ratio and adverse metabolic profiles.

Notably, our findings are consistent with those of previous studies reporting an association between gut microbial composition and obesity-related phenotypes. Nonetheless, the direction and magnitude of the association between the F/B ratio and metabolic outcomes have varied across populations3,22–24. While several studies conducted in Western populations have linked gut microbial composition, including phylum-level shifts, to obesity and metabolic dysfunction23,25, data from Asian populations remain limited and have yielded inconsistent findings4. For instance, a study in a Japanese population did not observe significant differences in the F/B ratio between individuals with and without obesity26; additionally, evidence from Korean cohorts remains scarce and largely confined to small, disease-specific populations27.

Notably, the F/B ratio demonstrated a stronger association with METS-IR than with other insulin resistance-related indices in supplementary analyses. Given that METS-IR incorporates lipid parameters and adiposity-related components, this finding may reflect an association with broader adiposity-related metabolic patterns, rather than a specific link to insulin resistance per se.

The F/B ratio is a simplified and reductionistic metric that does not capture the complexity of the gut microbiome. While it has been widely used in epidemiological studies due to its interpretability and comparability, it compresses diverse microbial communities into a single phylum-level ratio and may yield inconsistent findings across populations3,4. In addition, the F/B ratio is sensitive to methodological factors, including DNA extraction protocols, which may lead to underrepresentation of Gram-positive bacteria and thereby influence the relative abundance of Firmicutes28,29. These methodological variations may partly explain discrepancies across studies.

In the present study, the F/B ratio was selected as a pragmatic summary metric to facilitate comparison with prior population-based research4. However, given its inherent limitations, our findings should be interpreted cautiously and considered exploratory, rather than as evidence supporting a definitive microbiome marker of metabolic dysfunction. Accordingly, the interpretation of the present findings has been deliberately restricted to an association-level inference, and no causal or mechanistic conclusions are implied. Future studies incorporating more detailed taxonomic and functional analyses are warranted to better elucidate the relationship between gut microbiota and metabolic health.

The strengths of the present study include a relatively large sample size compared with that of previous studies conducted in Korean populations; moreover, it included generally healthy Korean adults. Unlike previous Korean studies, which were often limited by small sample sizes or restricted to specific disease populations27,30, the present study evaluated a broad range of metabolic phenotypes in a community-based setting. Additionally, the comprehensive assessment of metabolic phenotypes, including abdominal obesity, multiple insulin resistance indices, and visceral adiposity-related markers, permitted a more nuanced evaluation of the metabolic relevance of the F/B ratio.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. Second, gut microbiota composition was assessed at a single time point, which may not reflect longer-term microbial dynamics. Third, detailed dietary information was unavailable, limiting our ability to adjust for dietary influences on gut microbiota composition. Fourth, the single-center design may restrict the generalizability of the findings. In addition, stratified analyses by age and sex were not performed due to the sample size of the present study, and potential effect modification could not be evaluated. Future studies with larger sample sizes are warranted to explore potential effect modification by age and sex. Finally, although major demographic and lifestyle factors were adjusted for, residual confounding due to unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Detailed information on medication use (including antibiotics, metformin, and lipid-lowering agents) and physical activity was not available, all of which may influence both gut microbiota composition and metabolic outcomes. The lack of information on antibiotic use represents an important limitation, as antibiotic exposure may substantially alter gut microbiota composition and influence the F/B ratio. Furthermore, negative controls and batch controls were not included in this study, which may limit the ability to assess potential contamination or batch-related variability in the microbiome data.

In this study, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was used to characterize gut microbiota composition, as it enables comprehensive profiling of microbial communities at the population level. While targeted PCR-based approaches may allow precise quantification of specific taxa, they are limited in their ability to capture overall microbial diversity and community structure. Therefore, 16S sequencing was considered appropriate for the exploratory and population-based design of the present study. However, the taxonomic resolution of 16S rRNA sequencing is limited, particularly at the species and strain levels, and functional inferences cannot be directly derived. In addition, methodological factors, including primer selection and amplification bias, may influence the observed microbial composition. These methodological limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

The F/B ratio is known to be influenced by various factors, including diet, lifestyle, and metabolic status, and may change over time or in response to interventions. Previous studies have suggested that gut microbiota composition is dynamic rather than static1,3. However, given the cross-sectional design of the present study, temporal changes in the F/B ratio and its responsiveness to treatment could not be evaluated. Therefore, caution is warranted when interpreting the findings in a longitudinal or interventional context.

Despite these limitations, this study provides evidence that the gut microbial F/B ratio is associated with abdominal obesity and insulin resistance, particularly in phenotypes characterized by visceral adiposity and dyslipidemia in Korean adults. Clinically, these findings suggest that gut microbiota composition, as reflected by the F/B ratio, may provide complementary information for metabolic risk stratification when used alongside established clinical parameters. Although the F/B ratio is not currently suitable as a standalone clinical biomarker, these findings support the potential relevance of the gut microbiome as a target for lifestyle or therapeutic interventions aimed at improving metabolic health. However, further longitudinal and interventional studies are required before clinical implementation can be established.

In conclusion, the gut microbial F/B ratio was inversely associated with abdominal obesity and insulin resistance, as assessed by METS-IR, in Korean adults. Given the composite nature of METS-IR, these findings may reflect adiposity-related metabolic dysfunction captured by this index and should be interpreted as reflecting broader metabolic profiles rather than a direct measure of insulin resistance. These findings underscore the potential metabolic relevance of gut microbiota composition, particularly regarding visceral adiposity-related metabolic dysfunction, and suggest that the F/B ratio may represent a microbiota-related indicator associated with metabolic risk, warranting further evaluation in larger and longitudinal studies.
