Case report: An unusual case of sliding para peritoneal inguinal bladder hernia: A diagnostic challenge
Authors: Kalkidan Kibret Mekoya, Mukasa Mohammed, Shabbir Murtaza Adamjee, Justus Kamara Anacleth, Berhane Weldegergis Yohannes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInguinal bladder hernia is a rare and often underdiagnosed condition. Due to its non-specific presentation, it is frequently identified only during surgery.Case presentationWe report the case of a 54-year-old man who presented with a longstanding right inguinoscrotal swelling. He had no urinary symptoms and no identifiable risk factors for hernia. Preoperative ultrasonography did not reveal bladder involvement. The diagnosis was made intraoperatively when the bladder was identified as part of the hernia sac.DiscussionInguinal bladder hernia is typically misdiagnosed preoperatively due to its rarity and subtle clinical presentation. Preoperative imaging can aid in diagnosis and help prevent intraoperative complications.Keywords: Inguinal hernia, Inguinal bladder hernia, Sliding hernia, Inguinoscrotal hernia

Introduction

Inguinal bladder hernia (IBH) was first described by Levine in 1951 as a rare variant of inguinal hernia. [1] The urinary bladder is involved in approximately 1–4 % of all inguinal hernias, with complete herniation into the scrotum accounting for a smaller subset of these cases. [2] Herniation occurs when the bladder and a sheath of peritoneum are pulled through a weak point in the abdominal fascia, forming the hernia sac. [3] While the inguinal canal is the most common site, the bladder can also herniate into the femoral canal, accounting for 23 % of bladder hernias, while IBH represents 75 % of cases. The remaining 2 % involve rarer routes, including the perineum, ischiorectal fossa, obturator canal, and other abdominal defects. [4]

IBH is a rare and often difficult condition to diagnose, particularly in high-risk groups such as older, overweight men, leading to frequent misdiagnosis. [5] Only 7 % of cases are diagnosed preoperatively, with the vast majority identified intraoperatively and approximately 16 % diagnosed postoperatively due to complications such as bladder injury or urinary leakage. [6]

Here, we present a case report of a sliding para peritoneal inguinal bladder hernia. To our knowledge, this is the first such case reported from Tanzania. We aim to highlight the diagnostic challenges and the intraoperative complications encountered during management. This case report has been prepared in accordance with the SCARE Criteria. [7]

Case presentation

A 54-year-old male patient presented to our clinic with a complaint of a right-sided inguinal bulge, which reduces spontaneously when supine. He reported no associated pain, no difficulty in urination, urinary symptoms, or changes in bowel habits. There were no identifiable risk factors for hernia on history or typical risk factors for Inguinal bladder hernia like advanced age, obesity or lower urinary tract symptoms.

Physical examination revealed a large, reducible right inguinoscrotal swelling. Routine laboratory investigations were within normal limits. Abdominopelvic ultrasonography showed a right-sided inguinoscrotal hernia but did not clearly differentiate whether it was direct or indirect. Bladder Involvement in the Inguinal canal was not detected by ultrasonography preoperatively.

Following informed written consent, the patient was scheduled for elective hernia repair. An oblique inguinal incision was made 1 cm above the inguinal ligament to approach the inguinal canal. During dissection, the herniated bladder wall, which was para-peritoneal and located medial to the hernia sac, was mistakenly identified as the hernia sac and was inadvertently injured. The bladder wall was immediately repaired in two layers. This intraoperative bladder injury was graded as Calvien Dindo Grade IIIa, as it required immediate repair under regional anesthesia. The true hernia sac was then separated from the bladder, and the bladder was repositioned medially. Hernia repair was completed using mesh (Fig. 1 and 2).

Postoperatively, the complication was communicated to the patient and urinary catheter was retained for seven days. On follow-up, the patient reported no urinary complaints and showed no signs of recurrence up to the time of this report's preparation.

Discussion

Bladder hernia is a multifactorial condition, with its incidence increasing progressively with age. It is frequently associated with well-documented comorbidities such as obesity, weakened abdominal wall musculature, and most notably bladder outlet obstruction—particularly due to prostatic hypertrophy. [8] The pathophysiology of an inguinal bladder herniation involves pulling of the bladder and a sheath of peritoneum that forms its sac through a weak point in the abdominal fascia [6].

The majority of inguinal bladder hernias occur in males, with a reported male predominance ranging from 70 % to 95 %. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated a tenfold higher incidence in males compared to females. [9] This disparity is attributed to the larger inguinal canal in males, which contains the spermatic cord and provides a more permissive pathway for abdominal contents to herniate. [10]

The clinical presentation of inguinal bladder hernia is variable and may include non-specific urinary symptoms, inguinal pain or swelling, or may even be asymptomatic in many cases. Due to this variability, a high index of suspicion is required to make the diagnosis. [1] Mery's sign—a two-stage urination pattern in which the patient must compress the hernia to complete bladder emptying, and where the hernia visibly reduces after voiding—is a highly suggestive, though inconsistently present, clinical indicator of bladder involvement in the hernia. [8]

Bladder hernias are classified into para peritoneal, intraperitoneal, and extraperitoneal types based on their anatomical relationship to the peritoneum. In para peritoneal hernias—the most common type—the bladder remains extraperitoneal and lies medial to the hernia sac. In intraperitoneal hernias, the bladder is completely enclosed by peritoneum within the hernia sac. In extraperitoneal hernias, only the bladder herniates, with no associated peritoneal covering. [10] Predisposing factors for bladder herniation include male sex, advanced age, obesity, bladder outlet obstruction, and weakness of the abdominal wall. These hernias occur more frequently on the right side and are most often diagnosed incidentally during imaging or intraoperatively, with fewer than 7 % identified preoperatively. [1]

Similar to our case report, the majority of bladder hernia cases are diagnosed intraoperatively. Studies show that up to 93 % of inguinal bladder hernias are only identified during surgery. [3] A high index of suspicion should be maintained in high-risk individuals, particularly obese men over the age of 50. [11] Given the low number of bladder hernias that are diagnosed preoperatively, the clinician should have a high index of suspicion especially in older, obese, male populations in whom incidence has been reported as high as 10 % of all inguinal hernia. CT scanning may be beneficial in obese males over 50 with inguinal swelling and lower urinary tract symptoms, followed by cystoscopy to confirm diagnosis and rule out additional pathology of the bladder [6] A voiding cystourethrogram (VCUG) is also considered one of the gold standard imaging modalities for diagnosing bladder herniation. It may reveal the characteristic “dog-ear” or “dumbbell” appearance of the bladder extending into the hernia sac. [12]

A review of 14 published case reports revealed that preoperative CT imaging identified bladder involvement in all but one case, allowing surgeons to avoid intraoperative bladder injury. In contrast, a case from Indonesia—similar to ours—lacked preoperative CT or VCUG, and bladder involvement was missed. In that case, bladder injury was avoided intraoperatively by instilling saline through a Foley catheter to differentiate the bladder from the hernia sac.

Open surgery remains the most commonly performed approach for inguinal bladder hernia, used in approximately 80.4 % of cases. Among these, Lichtenstein repair is the most frequently utilized technique (32.6 %), followed by Bassini (15.2 %), McVay (4.3 %), and Shouldice (2.2 %) repairs. [9] Robotic-assisted laparoscopic surgery offers improved dexterity and enhanced visualization of pelvic structures, potentially reducing the risk of iatrogenic bladder injury. Although this technique is associated with longer operative times and higher surgical costs, these may be outweighed by the reduced risk of complications in selected patients. [13]

Bladder resection is currently indicated only in specific cases, such as when there is bladder wall necrosis, a true herniated bladder diverticulum, a tight hernia neck, or a tumor involving the herniated portion of the bladder [5]. In select patients, conservative management with urethral catheterization to decompress and reduce the bladder may be sufficient. [6] In our review, we also encountered a case in which IBH was managed conservatively with tamsulosin alone, without catheterization. The patient declined surgical intervention and opted for outpatient follow-up. He experienced complete resolution of symptoms, including abdominal and chest pain, prior to discharge. [12]

Failure to recognize bladder involvement during inguinal hernia repair can lead to significant postoperative complications, including gross hematuria, fistula formation, urinary tract infection, and sepsis. [13] Early reports documented a morbidity rate of approximately 6–8 % in adults with sliding hernias through the inguinal ring. More recent literature suggests a slight decline in this rate, likely due to improved surgical techniques and imaging.

Intraoperative identification of bladder injury is critical, as it allows for immediate repair and catheterization, which can often lead to full recovery without long-term sequelae. [14] Despite these advances, the overall rate of bladder injury during surgery remains relatively high at around 12 %, but this rate is significantly lower in cases where IBH is diagnosed preoperatively. [1]

Conclusion

In conclusion, sliding inguinal bladder hernia is a rare condition that is frequently missed during preoperative evaluation, as illustrated by our case. Our clinical recommendation based on our experience is that clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion in patients with risk factors such as advanced age, obesity, or urinary symptoms presenting with inguinoscrotal hernia. Preoperative imaging is strongly recommended to aid in diagnosis and to minimize the risk of bladder injury and other associated morbidities.
