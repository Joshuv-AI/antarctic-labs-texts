Fractal dimensions for tumour-related cell types of prostate cancer on histopathology images using multiple-threshold box counting algorithm
Authors: Anton Schwarz, Hidetaka Arimura, Yunhao Cui, Shun Shimabukuro, Qijing Lin, Yu Jin, Satoshi Kobayashi, Takashi Matsumoto, Masaki Shiota, Masatoshi Eto, Yoshinao Oda
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe malignancies of prostate tumour cells are assessed by pathologists as grade groups (GGs) from 1 (least aggressive) to 5 (most aggressive) on histopathology images. GGs are associated with the degree of tumour cell differentiation and may have different self-similarities depending on GG and tumour-related cell types, which are neoplastic epithelial, inflammatory, connective tissue, necrotic, and non-neoplastic epithelial cells. We investigated the associations between GGs and fractal dimensions (FDs) for five types of prostate tumour-related cells using a multiple-threshold box counting algorithm (MTBC). We showed the association of FDs of 9 channel images (eosin, hematoxylin, normalised images for red, green, and blue colour channels) with multiple threshold values on histopathology images (patch images) and the feasibility of FD-threshold images in an artificial intelligence model to classify patients into low (GG≤3) and high (GG≥4) GGs. We constructed FD-threshold images based on MTBC algorithm for characterizing prostate tumour cells. A shallow-convolutional neural network (sCNN) model to classify patients into low and high GGs was trained with input data of the FD-threshold images for all 9 channels and evaluated using the area under receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). There were statistically significant correlations between the FD of non-neoplastic epithelial cells and GG [Pearson correlation coefficient=–0.849, p=0.001]. Significant correlations also existed for connective tissue and the original images. The AUC for the sCNN classification model into high and low GGs was 0.811. FD can characterise physical properties of prostate tumour-related cells for low and high GGs.Keywords: Fractal dimension, prostate cancer malignancy, histopathology image, multiple-threshold box counting

Significance

We have studied the associations between grade groups (GGs) of prostate cancer on histopathology images and fractal dimensions (FDs) of five types of prostate tumour-related cells, i.e., neoplastic epithelial, inflammatory, connective tissue, necrotic, and non-neoplastic epithelial cells using a multiple-threshold box counting algorithm. We created the associations of FDs of 9 channel images (eosin, hematoxylin, normalised images for red, green, and blue colour channels) with multiple threshold values on histopathology images as FD-threshold images. We demonstrated the feasibility of FDs in an artificial intelligence model to classify patients into low (GG≤3) and high (GG≥4) GGs.

Introduction

In 2022, 1.47 million people worldwide were diagnosed with prostate cancer, which is the fourth most common cancer in terms of incidence rate [1]. The malignancies of prostate tumour cells are assessed according to morphological patterns (phenotypes) of prostate cancer [2] by pathologists as Gleason grade groups (GGs) from 1 (least aggressive) to 5 (most aggressive) on histopathology images. The Gleason grading system for prostate cancer is the strongest predictor of outcome in men diagnosed with prostate cancer. It consists of structural patterns which can be identified in a prostate biopsy core. The two most prominent patterns can be mapped to a Gleason score, which was recently simplified to the GG system [3,4]. Thus, a multitude of different structural patterns in tumour architecture can lead to the same GG. The GGs are associated with the degree of tumour cell differentiation and biochemical recurrence [2,5]. However, the Gleason GG system has an inherent level of subjectivity and inter- and intra-observer variability [6].

Automated Gleason grading approaches have been investigated for the prediction of GGs in prostate cancer [7–10]. Previous studies employed deep learning technologies with histopathological images from diagnostic needle biopsies. There are two major drawbacks: low explainability [11] and unreliable references (GGs) [12]. The low explainability is because deep learning systems can hardly explain how and why outputs, such as GGs, are reached [11]. Unreliable references are caused by the limited sampling of prostate tumour regions with several needle biopsies. Using whole prostate specimens from radical prostatectomy provides more reliable references. We have targeted higher explainability from a biophysical perspective while developing an automated classification approach by investigating the associations between physical properties of prostate tumour-related cells and GGs using histopathological slides taken from whole prostate specimens in tumour regions.

Cells in prostate cancer regions on histopathology images can be mainly classified into five cell types of tumour-related cells: neoplastic epithelial, inflammatory, connective tissue, necrotic, and non-neoplastic epithelial cells. Karram et al. reported that the maximum percentage of cancer per biopsy core per case, including intervening benign prostate tissue, was more predictive of stage and margins than ignoring intervening benign tissue [13]. This result motivated us to explore not only the neoplastic epithelial cells, but also the inflammatory, connective tissue, necrotic, and non-neoplastic epithelial cells. The results of Sessa et al. suggested an association between prostate inflammation and cancer at the tissue level [14]. Pizurica et al. found that histopathology images predicted to contain a TP53 mutation include relatively more connective tissue and fewer neoplastic epithelial cells than those predicted not to contain the mutation [15]. TP53 is a gene that produces a protein that is present inside the nucleus of cells and controls cell division and cell death [16]. Van Leenders et al. noted that different invasive growth patterns can be characterised by the adjacency of neoplastic epithelial cells with connective tissue [17]. Gordetsky et al. discussed that the necrosis could affect the evaluation of GGs [18]. Grypari et al. reported a change from PRMT1 to CARM1 crosstalk with androgen signals in the shift from non-neoplastic epithelial cells to neoplastic cells [19]. PRMT1 and CARM1 are enzymes that are associated with cancer progression. Therefore, we have decided to dive into the physical properties of five cell types of tumour-related cells.

A higher GG indicates poor differentiation in tumour cells, meaning that tumour cells exhibit less similarity to non-tumorous cells [20]. Consequently, we assumed that GG patterns for prostate cancer may have scaling self-similarities depending on GG and tumour-related cell type, which can be evaluated by the fractal dimension (FD). Cross already argued that the scaling self-similarity of tumour cells can be measured with FD in the histopathological field more than a quarter of a century ago [21]. Taverna et al. discovered that vascular surface FDs were associated with tumour aggressiveness, e.g., prostate specific antigen (PSA) level, tumour volume, and biochemical recurrence [22].

Recent studies applying FD analysis to histopathological images of prostate cancer have focused on segmenting the images and only calculating the FD on a subset of the original image. By using Gömöri staining to highlight the tumour cell architecture [23] or digitally isolating the cancer cell nuclei [24–26], the structural analysis was limited to the most relevant areas of the image. These studies stratified the histopathological images into newly defined classes based on their FDs, where the classes were related to GGs but not necessarily equal to them. Furthermore, the studies have in common that they compressed each 8-bit image into a single binary image and used only the binary box counting algorithm to compute the FD.

The novelty of this study is the proposal of applying FD analysis to histopathology images segmented into five types of tumour-related cells and using a multiple-threshold box counting algorithm (MTBC) to calculate the FDs from multiple binary images. We hypothesised that MTBC could unveil physical properties of prostate tumour-related cell types related to GGs, which could have different self-similarity and complex structures depending on threshold value. This study aimed to explore the associations between FDs of the five tumour-related cells and GGs of prostate tumour cells using MTBC. Besides, we created FD-threshold images and demonstrated their feasibility by developing an artificial intelligence (AI) model to classify patients into low (GG ≤ 3) and high (GG ≥ 4) GGs.

Materials and methods

Figure 1 illustrates an overall workflow for the exploration of associations of FDs of five types of tumour-related cells with GGs of prostate tumour cells and classification of prostate cancer patients into low and high GGs. Five patch images were selected from each cancer region. Pixel values of each patch image were normalised, and the patch image was decomposed into 9-channel images (eosin, hematoxylin, normalised images for red, green, and blue colour channels). By applying a deep learning architecture to all patch normalised images, cell nuclei were segmented and classified into five cell types: neoplastic epithelial, inflammatory, connective tissue, necrotic, and non-neoplastic epithelial cells. An FD-threshold diagram was obtained for each patch image using MTBC. The FD-threshold diagrams for five cell types and the original image of five patch images within the same region were rasterised as FD-threshold images and combined into a region-based FD-threshold image by stacking 9-channel images and combinations of cell types. The region-based FD-threshold images were leveraged to train and test shallow convolutional neural network (sCNN) models using the area under receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity and specificity.

Overall workflow for the exploration of associations between grade groups (GGs) of prostate cancer on histopathology images and fractal dimensions (FDs) of five types of tumour-related cells, and the investigation of a classification model [shallow convolutional neural network (sCNN)] of prostate cancer patients into low and high GGs.

Patient information

This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of our university hospital (approval number: [24314]).

Table 1 lists the patient characteristics. Seventy-five patients [median age: 65; range: 51–74 years; median prostate-specific antigen (PSA): 7.46 ng/ml; range: 3.57–33.65 ng/ml] with prostate cancer who underwent radical prostatectomy between July 2010 and September 2014 were selected for this study. Radical prostatectomy specimens were processed as previously described [27,28]. All cancer regions were outlined on hematoxylin and eosin-stained histopathological images, taken at 20 × magnification and a pixel size of 0.4 μm using a whole slide scanner (Nanozoomer-SQ; Hamamatsu Photonics KK). The GGs were determined by two urologists with over 6 years of experience (S.K. and T.M.). All cancer regions were stratified into a low GG (GG ≤ 3) and high GG (GG ≥ 4) following the International Society of Urological Pathology (ISUP) guideline [2] after radical prostatectomy. In this study, GGs were considered homogenous within one region, irrespective of the region’s size.

In total, a dataset of 996 patch images from 147 prostate cancer regions was selected from the 75 patients. The dataset was separated into four folds for a four-fold cross-validation (CV) test to train and test the classification models. Between 5 and 17 patch images with 1000 × 1000 pixels were chosen from each region to create a set of patch images that was balanced with respect to the number of patches in each fold and GG. During the model training and evaluation, the number of patch images per region is set to five by randomly sampling five patches without replacement.

Pre-processing of whole slide images

Pixel values of each patch image were normalised, and the patch image was decomposed into 9-channel images (eosin, hematoxylin, normalised images for red, green, and blue colour channels). Figure 2 shows the workflow for preprocessing of whole slide images (WSIs) to produce 9 types of pathology images (9-channel images). The WSIs were tiled into non-overlapping 1000×1000-pixel patches with a 0.4 μm pixel size at a 20× magnification. The staining colour variations due to different stain manufacturers and procedures were normalised by means of the Macenko approach with a singular value decomposition into “normalised”, “hematoxylin”, and “eosin” patches [29]. The “hematoxylin” patches allowed us to focus on cell nuclei, while “eosin” patches were involved in cell cytoplasma and extracellular matrices. Normalised, hematoxylin, and eosin patches could encompass neoplastic epithelial, inflammatory, connective tissue, necrotic, and non-neoplastic epithelial cells. These three types of stain images were decomposed into red, green, and blue (RGB) images to obtain greyscale images for calculation of FDs. In the end of this preprocessing, 9 channel images for each patch image were yielded with a quantisation level of 8 bits: normalised-R (red channel of normalised image), normalised-G (green channel of normalised image), normalised-B (blue channel of normalised image), hematoxylin-R (red channel of hematoxylin image), hematoxylin-G (green channel of hematoxylin image), hematoxylin-B (blue channel of hematoxylin image), eosin-R (red channel of eosin image), eosin-G (green channel of eosin image), and eosin-B (blue channel of eosin image).

Workflow for preprocessing whole slide images to produce 9 types of pathology images (9-channel images).

Segmentation of five tumour-related cell types and their combinations

By applying a deep learning architecture of Hover-Net [30] to all patch normalised images, cell nuclei were segmented and classified into five tumour-related cell types: neoplastic epithelial, inflammatory, connective tissue, necrotic, and non-neoplastic epithelial cells. Figure 3 illustrates the segmentation of five tumour-related cell types. The segmentation and classification were performed using the Hover-Net [30], which was retrained with a PanNuke dataset, a large and diverse dataset containing 19 different tissue types, including the prostate [31].

Segmentation of five tumour-related cell types using a deep learning architecture of Hover-Net [30], neoplastic epithelial, inflammatory, connective tissue, necrotic, and non-neoplastic epithelial cells. The original image was 1000×1000 pixels and the subimage was 150×150 pixels.

FD-threshold diagrams obtained with MTBC

Figure 4 illustrates binary images obtained using MTBC from a patch image of a GG of 1 at thresholds of 1, 80, and 160 when focusing on non-neoplastic epithelial nuclei. The advantage of MTBC is to delve into the FDs of internal cell nuclear structures for the GG prediction. For instance, for low thresholds, the relative positions of the cell nuclei are dominant for FD calculation, while for higher thresholds the internal structure of the cell nuclei becomes increasingly important.

Binary images obtained using multi-threshold box counting algorithm (MTBC) to delve into the fractal dimensions of internal cell structures from a patch image of a grade group (GG) 1 at thresholds of 1, 80, and 160 when focusing on non-neoplastic epithelial nuclei.

The motivation for using multiple thresholds is that while global thresholding techniques can determine the single binarisation threshold that leads to optimal contrast in the output binary image, there is a loss of information when reducing 256 possible pixel values to only two [32]. To extract the FD information from the input image, we employed a multiple-threshold approach [33].

Figure 5 shows a workflow of FD-threshold diagram obtained with MTBC. The FD-threshold diagram was obtained for each patch image using MTBC. MTBC is a method that binarises a channel image using multiple thresholds and applies a box-counting algorithm for each binary image. This resulted in one FD for each threshold. Since each channel image has pixel values of 0 to 255, 254 different thresholds T are applied from 1 to 254. To create binary images, pixel values higher than T were assigned a value of one, while all other pixel values were set to zero.

Workflow of fractal dimension (FD)-threshold diagram obtained with multi-threshold box counting algorithm (MTBC) based on a binary box-counting algorithm. This is the example for a red channel image with MTBC.

The FD was calculated from the slope of the linear relationship between the natural logarithm of the number of lattice cells N(ε) required to cover a binary object and the natural logarithm of inverse of lattice size ε [34]. The FD can be written as a limit:

In the actual calculation, the discrete lattice size εi was determined according to {εi∈Z|εpixel≤εi≤εimage}, where εpixel=1 and εimage=1000 measured in pixels, and εi=2,5,10,20,50,100,200,500. Each lattice size is a divisor of the image side length. The FD is the slope of the linear function fitted to the relationship of ln N(εi) and ln 1/εi [35]. By changing the threshold value and applying the binary box counting for each channel image, the FD-threshold diagram was obtained as a function of the threshold.

FD-threshold images

Figure 6 shows a workflow describing how five patch images from a region are combined into a stacked region-based FD-threshold image. The FD-threshold diagrams for five tumour-related cell types of five patch images within the same region were rasterised and combined into a region-based FD-threshold image, stacking 9-channel images and combinations of cell types. The rasterised diagram representing the FD-threshold relationship for one patch image, stain, colour channel and cell type image was an FD-threshold image of 128 × 128 pixels [36]. The five FD-threshold images belonging to the same region were combined into a region-based FD-threshold image using pixel-wise averaging. The 9 region-based FD-threshold images belonging to the same cell type image were stacked to give a 9-channel region-based FD-threshold image.

Calculation of the stacked region-based fractal dimension (FD)-threshold images.

To identify the significant cell types associated with GG, we explored the performances of the FD-based models for all 63 combinations (= 26 − 1) of one or more cell type images selected from the original image and five cell type images. After selecting N, 1 ≤ N ≤ 6 cell type images, the 9-channel region-based FD-threshold images were blurred using a two-dimensional (2D) Gaussian filter with the standard deviation σ corresponding to the degree of blurring. Finally, they were stacked into an image with 9N layers of 128 × 128 pixels each.

Construction of FD-based models

We demonstrated the feasibility of FD-threshold diagrams obtained from MTBC in an AI model to classify patients into low (GG ≤ 3) and high (GG ≥ 4) GGs. The region-based FD-threshold images were leveraged to train and test sCNN models using AUC, accuracy, sensitivity and specificity. Supplementary Figure S1 shows the sCNN model architecture. The purpose of the model was to validate the usefulness of the previously determined correlations between FD and GG. The input to the model consisted of the region-based FD-threshold images, i.e., 9N greyscale images that were used as feature maps in the first convolutional layer, where N is the number of cell type images. After passing through three convolutional layers and one fully connected layer, an activation function was used to return a probability corresponding to the degree of the low and high GGs. This probability was thresholded to determine the final prediction. During the training of the model, the dropout method was deployed to prevent the overfitting of the sCNN. Furthermore, a weighted mean squared error function was used to determine the training loss, because the training was conducted on region-wise data, but the dataset was only balanced on the patch level. The first convolutional layer took 9N feature maps with square greyscale images of side length 128 pixels and returned three feature maps of 64 × 64 pixels. The second convolutional layer returned three feature maps of 55 × 55 pixels. The third and final convolutional layer returned one feature map of 42 × 42 pixels. A maxpooling layer further reduced this to a matrix of 7 × 7 values, which were passed to the fully connected layer, which then returned a single logit. A sigmoid function was used to convert the logit into a probability. The standard deviation of the Gaussian filter was optimised for 0 ≤ σ ≤ 3 with a step of Δσ = 0.1.

Comparison model based on a single-threshold box counting algorithm

To compare MTBC with a single-threshold approach, comparison models with support vector machine (SVM) models were trained and tested on the FDs extracted from 9-channel images of combinations of cell types using a single-threshold box counting (STBC) algorithm. Supplementary Figure S2 describes the construction of the region-based STBC feature vector. For each stain and colour channel of each patch image, the image was binarised at the ideal threshold value using Otsu’s method [37]. The FD of each binary image was calculated using the binary box counting algorithm. The FD values were averaged over all five patch images of the same region, resulting in the mean FD for a region and 9 channels. As for the region-based FD-threshold images, 63 combinations of one or more cell type images were considered. After selecting N cell type images, 1 ≤ N ≤ 6 cell type images, the 9N mean FD values for the 9 channels and the N images were collected into a feature vector which we call the region-based STBC feature vector. SVM models using radial basis function kernels were trained and tested with the feature vectors constructed in this manner.

Statistical analysis

The correlations between FD and GG were analysed for each cell type image, stain, colour channel and threshold using the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC). For each fold in the CV test, the PCC was computed between the mean FD and the GG, while controlling for the remaining variables. The mean PCC over the four folds was calculated, and the correlation with the greatest absolute value was considered the best. A two-sided Student’s t-test was employed for computing the p-value because the PCC could be positive or negative. The significance threshold was set at p < 0.05.

The sCNN model was evaluated using the AUC, as well as accuracy, sensitivity and specificity for prediction of low and high GGs. The model evaluation was performed four times based on a four-fold CV test. During the evaluation of the sCNN and SVM models, the AUCs were aggregated over the four-fold CV test and compared to the AUC of a random classifier (AUC = 0.5). A two-sided Student’s t-test was used to compute the p-value because the measured AUC could be greater or less than 0.5.

Discussion

We have studied the associations of FDs for five types of tumour-related cells with GGs of prostate tumour cells for neoplastic epithelial, inflammatory, connective tissue, necrotic, and non-neoplastic epithelial cells. Besides, we created the associations of FDs of 9 channel images (eosin, hematoxylin, normalised stain images for red, green, and blue colour channels) with multiple threshold values on histopathology images as FD-threshold diagrams. We demonstrated the feasibility of FDs in an artificial intelligence to classify patients into low (GG≤3) and high (GG≥4) GGs.

The non-neoplastic epithelial cell type images were found to have significant correlations in all 9 channels, while the original images and connective cell type images had at least 2 of 9 channels with significant correlations. Furthermore, the importance of the FD extracted from non-neoplastic cell type images was highlighted by the sCNN-models, which were trained on 63 combinations of cell type images: non-neoplastic cell type images were present in all models with AUCs higher than 0.750 (p<0.05).

This study showed that the FD of non-neoplastic epithelial cells was more indicative of GG than that of any other cell types. The best PCCs determined for each combination of non-neoplastic epithelial cells with stain and colour channel images evidenced a negative linear functional relationship between FD and GG, with all PCC<–0.75, as shown in Fig. 9. This finding suggests that as the cancer progresses and GG increases, the non-neoplastic epithelial cells are replaced by neoplastic epithelial cells, which in turn decreases FD of non-neoplastic epithelial cells. However, there was no significant evidence that FD of neoplastic epithelial cells increased with GG. Furthermore, FD is a measure of self-similarity and does not directly determine the cell count. In a previous study, Grypari et al. reported that a change from PRMT1 to CARM1 crosstalk with androgen signals can be observed in the shift from non-neoplastic epithelial cells to neoplastic cells [19]. If this shift would influence FD of the segmented image, it might explain why the FD of non-neoplastic epithelial cell type images was correlated with GG. Further studies are needed to prove the effects of PRMT1 and CARM1 enzymes on the FD of histopathological images.

FD of neoplastic epithelial cells was not significantly correlated with GG, which would be linked to the structure of tumour cells by definition of the Gleason system. The reason for this may lie in the multiple different patterns that comprise the Gleason system [5]. The subpatterns of Gleason pattern 5 may be composed of solid sheets, cords or single cells [38], each of which could have a different FD. Our results suggest that complex correlations may exist between the FD of neoplastic epithelial cells and GG, considering the existence of multiple patterns and associated FD characteristics. Besides, there have been reports on inter-observer variability of Gleason patterns, especially Gleason pattern 4 [39], which affect the grade groups of 2 to 5 and could hinder the correlation between GG and fractal geometry.

The discovery of the significant PCC between the FD of connective cell type images and GG was supported by van Leenders et al., who linked the adjacency of neoplastic epithelial cells to connective tissue to cancer progression [17]. However, their study categorised tumour structures by the interplay of tumour cells with the surrounding tissue in three dimensions, which could not be reproduced in this study, due to the limitations of two-dimensional histopathological images. The lack of a third dimension in the FD-analysis might explain why the correlation was less pronounced (PCC=0.299±0.154, p=0.030), and was not used in any of the sCNN models. Further investigation into the relationship between FD of connective tissue in three-dimensional samples of prostate cancer and GG is warranted.

Similar to the connective cell type images, the original images indicated significant correlations between FD and GG, but they were not consistently leveraged by the best-performing sCNN models. The reason for the high PCC of the original images may lie in the fact that the information of the segmented cell type images is entirely encoded within the original image. In particular, the eosin stain of the original images had a significant positive PCC. This indicates that the overall cell cytoplasma and extracellular matrices are related to GG, independent of tumour-related cell type. Despite this, the original images had a subordinate role in the best-performing sCNN models because of the lack of cell type information. Further research is necessary to uncover the best usage of the FD information of the original images.

Using MTBC means that both the overall positions of the nuclei as well as the internal structure within the nuclei are analysed. The choice of cell type image, stain and colour channel leads to different thresholds having the highest PCC. The best threshold value for non-neoplastic epithelial cell type images was determined to be high (in the 95th percentile of possible threshold values) for the eosin stain, indicating that the internal structure of the nuclei as highlighted by the eosin staining agent conveys the abundant information on the cancer nuclei. In contrast, the optimal threshold was at the lowest possible value of T=1 for all colour channels of the hematoxylin stain, indicating that the overall placement of the nuclei was dominant in this study.

The Royal Society defines explainability as a characteristic of AI that implies that a wide range of users can understand how a conclusion was reached [11]. In addition to verifying the usefulness of FD-based features in classification models, we interpreted the features in our study, justifying their use in models because of their proven correlations with GG. Therefore, the proposed MTBC approach achieved a higher explainability from a biophysical perspective for developing an automated classification approach.

Our study had several limitations, the first of which was the assumption that GG was homogenous within each region of interest and that each patch image within the region must accordingly have the same GG. Note that the definition of the GG itself challenges the assumption of homogeneity throughout an extended area: each GG encompasses a set of Gleason scores, each of which can be realised by several subpatterns of the numbered Gleason patterns. As van Leenders et al. discovered, subpatterns of the same Gleason pattern may be distinguished by whether the tumour cells are in contact with the surrounding tissue (stroma) [17]. By considering variations in FD, we could develop an approach to calculate the spatial distribution of the FD within patch images in a future work, which may lead to better characterisation of GGs.

Second, the dataset for this study consisted of only 75 individual patients, which were divided into 15 patients for GGs of 1 to 3 and 3 or 4 patients for GGs of 4 and 5. This means that the FD-threshold distributions used in classification were influenced by outliers and individual patient characteristics. With such a limited dataset, the number of patients was comparable to the number of individual patterns to be considered. In addition, there was a risk of overfitting during the model training, because there are only 147 regions of interest. Since each fold of the CV test only contained 36 or 37 regions of interest, the evaluation process was prone to statistical fluctuations due to a limited sample size. Therefore, the number of patients should be increased for further investigations. Besides, from a clinical point of view, we should collect more data of patients with higher GGs of 4 and 5 to achieve a better balance of GGs. The overfitting risk due to the unbalanced GGs can be mitigated by such a large balanced dataset. Increasing the number of patients and regions of interest in each CV fold could lead to more stable performance across folds.

Third, this study did not assess the interplay of different cell type images in the region-based FD-threshold images passed to the sCNN models. Clark et al. investigated intercellular communication in the prostate cancer tumour microenvironment and discovered that cell signaling events can reduce proliferation and migration of prostate epithelial cells [40]. We derived the FD relationships between pairs of the five cell type images and original image (Supplementary Figure S6). Further investigations are needed for these cell type correlations in future works using similar ways to the phenotyping and regional analysis of histology (PHARAOH) [41].

Fourth, the Gleason system itself is a limitation, because at its most basic level it is structured as a discrete set of morphological patterns. The GGs built on top of these patterns retain this discrete nature, in contrast to more recent studies that suggest a continuity in patterns [17,42]. Thus, we initially attempted to classify the histopathological images into five GGs by applying a continuous regression model to the FD-threshold diagrams, but this proved ineffective. Since our purpose of this study was to investigate the associations between GGs and FD for five types of prostate tumour-related cells using the MTBC algorithm, the AI model was simplified for proving the usefulness of the associations. Nevertheless, we will try to expand the simplified AI model for the binary classification to novel regression models for five GGs classification based on the associations in future works.

Finally, we did not have access to three-dimensional histopathology images, as the number of parallel slices was too low (e.g. 7 slices) to allow a volume reconstruction. Verhoef et al. characterised the three-dimensional architecture of prostate cancer growth patterns using a confocal laser scanning microscope, identifying two major subgroups [42]. We will seek opportunities to deploy three-dimensional histopathology images in future studies, which could result in more accurate predictions.

Conclusion

The associations of FDs of 9 channel images (eosin, hematoxylin, normalised images for red, green, and blue colour channels) were studied with MTBC on histopathology images. We found that the FDs of non-neoplastic epithelial cells, connective tissue and the original images had statistically significant associations with the GG. A classification model with histopathology images indicated that these associations could be useful in classifying patients into low (GG≤3) and high (GG≥4) GGs.
