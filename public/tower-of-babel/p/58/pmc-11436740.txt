Application of remote sensing and electrical resistivity technique for delineating groundwater potential in North Western Nigeria
Authors: Joseph Orojah Osumeje, Daniel Eshimiakhe, Adetola Sunday Oniku, Kolawole Muideen Lawal
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Improving the living condition of residents of Palladan and Basawa community requires access to drinking water. The main objectives of this paper are to identify suitable groundwater zones for productive drilling and to assess groundwater mineralization in the coastal aquifers of the study area. Geographic Information Systems (GIS) and Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP) were used in the methodology to generate the groundwater potential map. Slope, landcover/land use, lineament density, rainfall, soil cover and drainage density were taken into account to characterize the groundwater potential zones. Weights were assigned to the various parameters and their characteristics according to their impact on groundwater recharge. The groundwater potential map was classified into five zones namely: poor, fair, moderate, good, excellent. Based on the lineament density map, the distribution of these lineaments reveals the degree of porosity or permeability in each area and, consequently, its groundwater potential. Aeromagnetic data filtering permits the construction of a structural map that illustrates various geophysical lineaments that are known to be fault systems in the research area. These faults are the main routes via which groundwater seeps into the subsurface and granitoid-type magnetic rocks intrude into the basement. The research region is badly fractured/failed and made up of four lithologic units, including the aquifer layer (clayey sands in the cracked basement) with thicknesses varying from 12–55 m, according to the vertical electrical sounding (VES) applications. According to geoelectric cross-sections, the subsurface structures are made of granitic rocks that are surrounded by normal faults that trend both NW and NE. It is believed that groundwater flows into the hard rock aquifers in the studied locations through these notable geological features, such as faults and fractures. Two phenomena are responsible for the mineralization of water: a process of interaction between water and rock; and a process of salinization resulting from natural phenomena or anthropic activities. The present study could guide hydrogeological investigations and groundwater resource management planning in the study area.

Introduction

Any community's ability to grow depends on its ability to access necessities like reliable transportation, clean water, and energy. Water is the most plentiful and used element on Earth and is necessary for all living things to survive1–3. Although water is abundant in the natural world, getting access to enough of it in a suitable amount and quality is still a major problem4,5. Because surface water pollution is mostly caused by human activity in metropolitan and peri-urban settings, the problem is more urgent there6. The study region involves two local communities; Palladan and Basawa community, with a plethora of activities such as farming and fishing. The lack of a sustainable water delivery network poses a major difficulty for these communities, in terms of availability of clean drinking water. Although the local population has access to surface water sources such as rivers, streams, lakes, and ponds, they are insufficient due to their seasonality, unpredictability, and frequently low quality. Economic activities such as agriculture are the major occupation of the population, and as such improper disposal of solid waste from this activity has led to water pollution in some vicinity. This contaminant from waste disposal can be carried by runoff into surface water sources, thereby affecting drinking water quality. According to7,8, waterborne infections, some of which are lethal, are disseminated by these contaminants. Hence, this study was required to determine the groundwater potential in these localities to provide quality groundwater for the population.

Groundwater is in high demand as a viable and sustainable solution to the local population's water-related problems and to increase access to a dependable and secure water supply. Aquifers are water-bearing permeable rocks located beneath the surface of the earth that can hold groundwater9,10. Because of its inherent chemical composition and microbiological quality, groundwater is suggested for a variety of uses. According to2,11, it is a comparatively safe and dependable supply of drinking water that requires little to no purification before consumption in both rural and urban regions. With regards to aquifer formation, fractured and weathered basements are typically home to groundwater in basement complexes like the one under study. Within these fractured and weathered strata, its distribution is not uniform and may be limited to particular zones or pockets2,12,13. Hence, good knowledge of the conduits or pathways for groundwater flow, such as fractures, faults, and joints is crucial for the successful exploitation and management of groundwater resources2,14,15.

Geophysicists sometimes mix various disciplines, including topographic analysis, geology, hydrogeology, remote sensing, and geophysics, to find groundwater at depths. With recent advances in satellite imaging for mapping and surveying, remote sensing has become a very effective tool for detecting potential aquifers in hard rock conditions1,15. Groundwater potential zones have been assessed by several researchers using various statistical models, including the Analytical Hierarchy Process (AHP)1,16–18, Frequency Ratio19,20, Shannon Entropy21,22, Weight of Evidence23,24, and MultiInluencing Factor25. Because of the AHP’s positive results in numerous groundwater studies, it was chosen for this investigation26,27. The Landsat 9 which is the latest satellite in the Landsat program provides consistent and continuous Earth observation data. This continuity is crucial for long-term studies, monitoring changes in Landcover, and understanding environmental trends over time. The existence of a lineament may be closely related to an unexposed fault and may indirectly reflect a hydrothermal alteration area, a spatial distribution of groundwater28. Employing remote sensing and geophysical techniques for detecting groundwater potential in areas with basement areas have been carried out by researchers29–31. Geospatial approaches were employed in the investigations for groundwater potential zones in the crystalline basements of North-central Nigeria32. Furthermore, an integrated investigation using remote sensing for groundwater potentiality mapping was conducted in the alluvial environment of southeast Asia, and the prediction map created was around 81.3% in agreement with the ground truth data33. The most popular method in aeromagnetic interpretation for identifying groundwater presence is the filter technique, which aims to highlight the edges of rocks. Analytical signal and horizontal gradient filters32,34 are less efficient at detecting deep source edges when compared to more modern filter types like balanced horizontal derivatives, balanced window standard deviations, and balanced analytic signals32.

An old geophysical exploration approach that has been widely applied to groundwater research is the electrical resistivity method. It is a non-invasive method for assessing subsurface depths. Many authors have shown that this approach is essential to ensuring long-lasting boreholes and a sustainable water supply35–43. Among the numerous electrode configurations used in resistivity measurements, the vertical electrical sounding (VES) technique with Schlumberger electrode configuration is highly preferred. This is because the VES technique is relatively fast, dependable, and cost-effective in acquiring details of subsoil electrical characteristics.

The goal of this study's groundwater potential modeling is to identify places in the study region that are accessible to groundwater. The lack of comprehensive geophysical knowledge among individuals in the study area has resulted in unproductive boreholes, borehole failure, and contaminated groundwater with associated health risks. A review of previous literature reveals that only a limited contribution has been made toward the effort extracting lineaments, interpreting the structural features, and correlating these features with electrical resistivity parameters, in Western Nigeria. Most of the groundwater potential investigations around the study area have been directed towards one geophysical technique, and not a proper study of the structures responsible for the flow path of the aquifer44. To our knowledge, no detailed studies have been carried out on the structural lineaments associated with groundwater resources and their relationship to the aquifer pathways in the two study localities. Using a combination of remote sensing, aeromagnetic, and electrical resistivity technologies, this study aims to examine the hydrogeological setting and subsurface structural dispositions of the Bassawa and Palladan villages, located in the Sabon-Gari region of Kaduna state. This comprehensive approach allows us to evaluate effectively the groundwater resources in the study region.

Location and geological background

The study area consists of two localities namely Palladan and Bassawa, which are situated within the Sabon-Gari Local Government Area of Kaduna State, North-western Nigeria (Fig. 1).Figure 1Location map indicating the study area.

Location map indicating the study area.

Two distinct seasons are identifiable in the Zaria area, and they are the dry and rainy seasons. The wet season lasts from April to October, with the maximum rainfall in August. Zaria has a mean annual rainfall of 1082 mm). Land use/cover change assessed by45 through a time-series analysis of Landsat Thematic Mapper 1990, Enhanced Thematic Mapper + ,1999 and NigeriaSat-1 2009 satellite imageries of the study area suggests that the major changes in land use/cover were in the plantation and cultivated areas with very significant increase in areal extent by (19.8% and 13.8% gain, respectively) while cultivated land and water bodies depreciated with a loss of about 386 km2 (− 2.73% loss) and 20.7 km (− 5.5% loss).

Nigeria's three primary geological units are the Younger Granites, the Cretaceous to Quaternary Sedimentary Sequences, and the Crystalline Basement Complex46,47. The Basement Complex covers more than half of Nigeria's subsurface. Rocks from the Precambrian and Phanerozoic eras, which are located in the Eastern and North Central areas of Nigeria, are the main constituents of the basement complex.

The Precambrian basement complex is comprised of three major lithologic successions: the metavolcanic-sedimentary rocks of late Proterozoic age (5420 Ma), the Older granite complex of Late Precambrian to lower Paleozoic age (5420–2510 Ma), and the undifferentiated Migmatite-Gneiss-Quartzite complex of Archaean to early Proterozoic origin (270 0–20 0 Ma). According to48 a good aquifer is not naturally produced by foundation rock with a poor transmissivity of about 31 m2/day.

Generally speaking, the subsurface of the Sabon-Gari Local Government Area consists of the crystalline Basement Complex and the granite suite, which includes ancient granites, foliated granodiorite, and foliated microgranites (Fig. 2). The gneiss complex (augen gneiss, banded gneiss, and granite gneiss) and veins (pegmatite and quartz veins) are the others. These crystalline rocks are usually the main subject of any geophysical investigation because they were deposited throughout the Precambrian epoch49–53. Changes in temperature, pressure, and chemical composition sparked processes like metamorphism causing decay which produced sedimentary rocks, even if the basement complex still dominated the area. Moreover, the surficial layer is composed of laterites, clayey soil, and alluvial deposits54,55.Figure 2Geological map covering the study area (after48).

Materials and methods

To determine the optimal study region for aquifer reserves, which includes Palladan and Bassawa towns, we took into account the groundwater potential of the entire Sabon-Gari Local Government region.

This resulted in the application of both remote sensing and aeromagnetic data before ground truthing was done. Figure 3 displays a workflow diagram that outlines the steps involved in gathering, processing, and analyzing data.Figure 3Flowchart methodology.

Remote sensing

The Landsat 9 OLI image scene, path 184, and row 55 served as the basis for this investigation's satellite image. Between 2023–03-29 and 2023–04-05, this image was downloaded from the US Geological Survey website (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov). The classification of land use and land cover was made possible by the use of multi-spectral Landsat photos56. The57 image pre-processing method was directly applied to the gathered Landsat 9 image's area of interest.

A common Universal Transverse Mercator (UTM) coordinate system (WGS84 Zone 32N) was projected onto the datasets to prevent errors in the linear measurement and to preserve the geometric qualities of the records. The thematic layers were resampled using the closest neighbor resampling technique and decreased to 30 m to maintain compatibility with the spatial resolution of the Landsat images. The Landsat imagery was enhanced in the ENVI software v5.3 environment by generating a false-color composite with three spectral bands in the sequence of band 6 (short-wave infrared), band 5 (near infrared), and band 4 (red).

Geoelectric data

Using Schlumberger's array, the Ohmega O resistance meter was employed for the VES survey. To investigate the subsurface setting of the area, the current electrode spacing (AB) varies with increasing distance, ranging from 3 to 200 m. To cover all of the study locations, a total of 22 VES points were chosen in both study area (Fig. 4a and b). To link the VES results with known geological and hydrogeological data, some additional VES stations were created close to existing wells. These VESs had a maximum current electrode (AB) separation of 200 m, enabling a depth of inquiry range of 50 to 100 m below the surface of the earth. Data processing

The production of the land use/land cover (LULC), lineaments, slope and drainage density maps, and the creation of maps for the total magnetic intensity (TMI) and its derivatives were all part of the data processing. From the aforementioned layers, the analytical hierarchy process (AHP), a multi-criteria decision-making method, was used to create the Groundwater Potential Zonation (GWPZ) map. In the parts that follow, the crucial steps used in creating the GWPZ map will be discussed.

Land use/Land cover (LULC) extraction

Following Anderson's Level I classification scheme62 for remotely sensed data, the following LULC classes—built-up area, water body, barren land, and agricultural land—were determined by interpretation of the Landsat image composite. The composite was put through supervised image classification using the “maximum likelihood classification” (MLC) method to extract LULC classes. To recognize patterns, the MLC algorithm employs a statistical method and assumes that the statistics for each class in the image band are normally distributed63. To assign a pixel to a class, the MLC method first calculated the likelihood that the pixel belongs to each class57,64.

Lineament extraction

The Landsat image composite was subjected to a directional convolution filter with a \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$5$$\end{document}5 kernel size to enhance its spatial frequency. The ENVI software environment was used to make this improvement. The filter was applied in the four main directional angles of N-S (180°), NW–SE (135°), NE-SW (225°), and E-W (270°), following the methodology of65. Four sets of spatially filtered images with improved brightness values were the convolution's result. The 2018 edition of PCI Geomatica was then opened after the filtered photos had been imported. The Line module was used to perform edge detection, thresholding, and curve extraction to extract the lineaments. The number and length of the extracted lineaments were defined by parameters used in the Line module's implementation.

Slope map generation

A slope map (in degrees) was produced from the SRTM DEM using the slope tool in ArcGIS 3D Analyst. The slope function, which has a range of 0—90°, determines the gradient, or rate of maximum change in z-value from each DEM cell. A cell's greatest rate of change about its neighbors was determined using the slope tool. The sharpest downhill fall from the cell was determined by the highest elevation change over the distance between it and its eight surrounding cells.

Weighting and overlay of the thematic layers

The64 method's analytical hierarchy process (AHP) was used to weigh the thematic levels. For managing both qualitative and quantitative multi-criteria decision-making elements, the AHP is a well-structured instrument. It simplifies computations and decisions because of the pairwise comparison of matrices of the identified criteria (i.e., the topic layers, as in this study). The final weights of the various parameters and their classes were determined by applying the following procedures to the AHP: normalizing the weights, assigning weights to the various criteria and subcriteria, and testing the consistency of the resultant solution. Based on their influence on groundwater recharge25,66, the AHP requires a pairwise comparison matrix (PCM) of the various criteria by the relative relevance of one criterion compared to another67. In a decision analysis, assigning the weights to each criterion and its features is crucial68,69, since the choice depends heavily on choosing the right weights70. In his study, a thorough literature review and expert opinion were taken into account in the selection and classification of the criteria.

Processing of magnetic data

Oasis Montaj TM software, a data processing and analysis package, was used to process the data. Filters were applied to the magnetic data (i.e., raw total magnetic intensity) to increase some characteristics of the magnetic sources to interpret the aeromagnetic anomalies related to the study area. During the interpretation process, various enhancement techniques, such as the first and second vertical derivatives (FVD and SVD), and analytical signal (AS), are utilized to either accentuate or suppress specific magnetic abnormalities. Below is a quick explanation of the idea, creation, and implementation of several of these enhancing approaches.

Residual magnetic intensity

After the geomagnetic gradient was removed from the aeromagnetic data using the International Geomagnetic Reference Field (IGRF) epoch 2005, the total magnetic intensity (RMI) was produced. The RMI image was given some image enhancement filters to help in visualization and interpretation. Image augmentation is required to detect small magnetic signals, particularly those connected to edge detection and lithological contrasts.

First and second vertical derivatives

The First Vertical Derivative (FVD) is one derivative that has been used to highlight the edges of magnetization boundaries, notably when mapping contacts, faults, dykes, and ore bodies. The FVD, which employs a rapid Fourier transform, boosts the high-frequency component of magnetic fields while decreasing the low-frequency content induced by the regional field40. To highlight the near-surface geological characteristics, the RTE data produced from the gridded TMI data was improved with an FVD filter.

The second-order derivative of the FVD is called the Second Vertical Derivative (SVD). Although it is susceptible to cultural noise and artifacts41,42, the SVD aids in further improving the magnetization boundaries. The SVD can be used to improve the bounds of anomalous sources.

Analytic signal

To improve near-surface characteristics and produce maxima directly over magnetic sources, an analytical signal (AS) filter was applied. The AS provides more information about the short-wavelength abnormalities than does FVD, and it can be used to more precisely trace the structures that FVD has revealed. The AS is therefore most effective for locating contacts43. The geometry of magnetic sources is thus determined by AS using the Hilbert transform. Geoelectric data

The field geoelectric data were processed and interpreted using the IPI2win software44 according to the inverse problem and Gauss–Newton algorithm for the inversion process. The interpretations' results categorized the investigated subsurface sequence into 4–5 sequential geoelectric zones with varying resistivity ranges, thickness, and depth. It is worth noting that the simplified interpreted curves of the IPI2WIN program, as well as the familiarity with the geology and hydrology of the study areas, were utilized to build a preliminary model that fit the observed field resistivity curves reasonably well. Results and analysis

Remote sensing

For each of the four thematic layers, maps were created. Below, we discuss the significance of each thematic layer in relation to groundwater recharge, runoff, occurrence, and storage. Depending on regional idiosyncrasies, factors for detecting groundwater potential are nearly unlimited, claim48. However, the most widely used ones are rainfall, geology, slope, drainage density, lineament density, and land use/land cover.

Lineament density

Lineaments are typically connected to faults or fractures and can also be a sign of subsurface features like lithological boundaries or the intersection of drainage and land use lines49. In groundwater potentiality mapping, lineaments are significant because they are linear structures that serve as routes or conduits for groundwater transport and accumulation.

The geology of the study area, as well as the drainage map was used in correlation with the lineament map so as to understand the permeability of rocks and the occurrences of structurally controlled streams.

The lineament density map of the study area was divided into five categories: 5.24–8.9 km/sq.km (very high), 5.24–3.16 km/sq.km (high), 3.16–1.75 km/sq.km (moderate), 1.75–0.63 km/sq.km (low) and 0–0.63 km/sq.km (very low). The highest normalized weight (0.444) was assigned to a very high lineament density.

According to Fig. 6, the north-central and northeastern regions of the study area had high lineament density, whereas the north-western and southern regions had low lineament density. While the Bassawa study area has a low lineament density, this would suggest that subsurface features that could serve as groundwater repositories are absent or only very minimally present there. In contrast, the Palladan study area has a medium lineament density, which would suggest that subsurface features (such as fissures, joints, and fractures) that could serve as groundwater repositories exist in that area. The regions closest to the active area (NNW) have the lowest potential for groundwater storage. As a result, it is believed that the aquifer systems in Bassawa are not actively being recharged, indicating that fossil water is most likely the area's primary groundwater supply.Figure 6Lineament density map.

Lineament density map.

Slope

The various slope classes and their spatial distribution are shown in Fig. 7. Whether a zone may retain water through infiltration or promote runoff depends on the slope. While a steep slope reduces the rate of water infiltration into the ground, a gentle slope slows surface runoff, which in turn speeds up infiltration. This indicates that one of the elements affecting how much water seeps into the earth is the slope. Surface water infiltration is inversely proportional to slope. The slope in the study area has been categorized into five categories: surface level or very low slope (slope zone 0 to 1 degrees), low slope (slope zone 1 to 2 degrees), gentle slope (slope zone 2 to 3 degrees), mild to moderate slope (slope zone 3 to 5 degrees), and steep slope (slope zone > 5 degrees). The highest normalized weight (0.504) was assigned to the very low slope zones. Since slope and runoff are inversely proportional, steeper slopes have received less weighting than shallower ones.Figure 7Slope classification of the study area.

Slope classification of the study area.

Land use/land cover (LULC)

Land use refers to human activity on the land and includes a wide range of activities, whereas land cover refers to characteristics that come from land change. Land use information must be derived from information on the land surface captured mostly by remote sensing data. Using the maximum likelihood method, the Landsat 9 data were classified into five categories: bare landscape, built-up area/settlement, vegetation, and waterbody. According to50, LULC conveys a sense of an urban environment and serves as a sign of anthropogenic influences and potential effects on the hydrogeology of any zone. According to the map (Fig. 8), the area is primarily made up of built-up/settlement, which makes up around 60% of the total area. The process of soil formation and groundwater infiltration is influenced by the kind of LULC since it also has an impact on how water is distributed for various purposes. The ability of the land to withstand surface runoff and erosion as well as the potential for evapotranspiration of the land under investigation can all be determined by looking at the land cover. In places with agricultural and forest land, there is a higher rate of infiltration. Built-up areas reduce surface water iniltration due to the lack of permeable surfaces49. The lowest normalized weight was assigned to built-up areas due to their high water retention capacity50. The highest weights were assigned to water bodies, followed by cultivated land and vegetation.Figure 8LULC of the study area.

LULC of the study area.

Drainage

To identify groundwater potential zones in the research region, it was given a normalized weight of 0.263. Drainage density values vary from 0 to 6.55 km / sq.km in the study area. The drainage density map of the study area was reclassiied into five distinct classes: 0–0.66 km/sq.km (very low), 0.66–1.46 km/sq.km (low), 1.46–2.34 km/sq.km (moderate), 2.34—3.52 km/sq.km (high) and 3.52–6.55 km/sq.km (very high). The classes cover 835.5 km2 (30.3%), 759.2 km2 (28.6%), 662.5 km2 (24%), 374.5 km (13.5%) and 124.3 km2 (4.5%), respectively. The highest normalized weight (0.438) was assigned to zones with very low drainage density due to their low runof rate.

Drainage maps are used to describe the direction of water flow and accumulation in catchments. Figure 9 illustrates a drainage density map. It shows that the Palladan research area has a low drainage density, resulting in little surface runoff, whereas the Basawa study area has a high drainage density, which implies that surface runoff will be high. As a result, there is a high likelihood that water (precipitation or irrigation) will be kept at the Paladan study location, aiding in groundwater recharge.Figure 9Drainage map of the study area.

Aeromagnetic data

Derivative maps are the end product of the processing of the aeromagnetic data covering the two research areas. These consist of the analytic signal (AS) map, the first and second vertical derivative maps (FVD and SVD), and the residual magnetic intensity (RMI) map. These maps served as a tool for processing the magnetic data and supplying details about the local tectonic tendencies and structural settings. The structural details of lithological contacts, faults, and dykes (or pre-tertiary intrusives) could be discovered by using edge detection algorithms to the aeromagnetic data. According to7 and12, these structures influence groundwater circulation and accumulation, serve as hydrocarbon traps and aid in the assessment of geologic hazards. The deeper magnetic sources characterized by low magnetic amplitudes are probably of Precambrian basement rocks while shallow magnetic sources associated with high magnetic amplitudes depict basic intrusives and/or magnetized bodies within the sedimentary cover and shallow subsurface43.

Geoelectrical characteristics

Vertical electrical sounding (VES) points were taken at each of the study areas to represent the extension of layers. Due to the influence of fault systems, the generated geoelectric layers show different thicknesses in lateral distribution. In general, there are 3 to 4 lithologies in both study areas (Palladan and Basawa localities) according to the range of resistivity, thickness, and depth. These consist of the weathered bedrock (18 to 200 Ω-m), fractured bedrock (250 to 2958 Ω-m), collapsed zone (48 to 1350 Ω-m), and fresh bedrock (3000 to 3500 Ω-m). Telford et al., (1998) state that in a three-layered earth model, the distribution of resistivities of various subsurface layers can be categorized based on curve shapes into H type (ρ1 > ρ2 < ρ3), K type (ρ1 < ρ2 > ρ3), A type (ρ1 < ρ2 < ρ3), and Q type (ρ1 > ρ2 > ρ3); these can then be combined to create HA type (ρ1 > ρ2 < ρ3 > ρ4), HK type (ρ1 > ρ2 < ρ3 > ρ4), KH type, QH type (ρ1 > ρ2 > ρ3 < ρ4), and so on in a four layer case.

Correlation of sounding result with geology Vertical electrical sounding data interpretation results obtained from the area showed three to four geoelectrical layers namely: top layer, weathered bedrock, fractured bedrock and fresh bedrock. For better delineation with the underlying geology, results from two vertical electrical soundings, each from the two study areas, conducted in close proximity to two water borehole/ well have been correlated with known lithology.

Discussion

Surface or image lineaments that are surficial and extend to a depth of roughly 1–10 m are revealed by the remote sensing data52, which may indicate groundwater recharge zones. This supports the growing use of aeromagnetic surveys for ground validation (also known as ground truth) and regional and subregional spatial confirmation of remote sensing data. Aeromagnetic data doesn't always identify the existence of water, but it can infer numerous underlying geologies that regulate groundwater flow, quality, and presence based on differences in the rock's magnetic properties.

On a magnetic map, when magnetic contrast and abrupt changes in magnetization within rock units are visible, geological structures like faults are observed. This results from either the secondary growth or destruction of magnetic materials or from the structural juxtaposition of litho-units caused by the primary difference in magnetization. Aeromagnetic data has a higher degree of research and precision than remote sensing data because of altitude variations. In addition, the magnetic lineaments show possible faults, deep fractures, and intrusive bodies—structural components crucial for mapping basement regions' groundwater reserves.

Denser lineament zones, according to53, suggest that the formation of fissures and channels for groundwater movement requires a certain degree of intensity in the rock fracture system. Low surficial lineaments comprise the majority of the northwest section of the area of interest, according to the lineaments map, which was created using both remote sensing and aeromagnetic data. If no subterranean fissures are serving as groundwater conduits or passageways, the zone may be weak. However, the central and southern regions of the study area have a moderate to high lineament density. As a result, they have a high potential for groundwater extraction and output. Medium lineament densities predominate in the investigated Palladan village region, with the potential for groundwater accumulation due to porosity and permeability. The Bassawa research region was determined to have low-medium lineament density.

The groundwater potential zone and the aeromagnetic analysis were cross-verified with the Geo-electrical cross-sections. Results from the electrical survey were narrowed down to the Palladan and Bassawa localities. The results of VES from both study sites indicate the existence of four lithologies and structures among which are very resistant formations situated at depth and exempt of fractures and faults, and conductive discontinuity zones, with low resistivity, consisting of faults/fractures. These are upper (clayey sand) and lower aquifers (fractured basement) of both study area.

The varying thicknesses of the aquifer layer significantly impact groundwater availability and quality. Thicker aquifers generally offer higher storage capacity, sustainable yields, and better natural filtration, leading to more stable and higher-quality groundwater supplies. Thinner aquifers, while easier to access, may face challenges related to lower storage capacity, quicker depletion, and higher vulnerability to contamination. Effective groundwater management must consider these factors to ensure sustainable and safe water resources in the study area. Greater thickness can enhance natural filtration processes, reducing the concentration of contaminants by the time water reaches deeper levels.

The groundwater potential map indicated a good yield in the Palladan study area, and the results from the VES analysis and isoresistivity plots validated this. Low resistivity which consists of faults and conductive zones were found, and the best point was decided for a possible drill. Considering the Basawa study area, the groundwater potential map indicates a moderate-fair yield, and the results from the VES curves shows high resistivity in weathered basement layer. This formation lacks of water and corresponds to the basement rocks composed of granitic and metamorphic rocks.

Conclusion

In order to comprehend the groundwater potential in the Palladan and Basawa communities in the Sabon Gari local Government area of Kaduna state, northwest Nigeria, this study attempts to define the subsurface structures and hydrogeological aspects. An integrated strategy that included aeromagnetic, vertical electrical sounding, and remote sensing was used to accomplish this. By using a Sobel directional filter and pre-processing techniques, remote sensing was applied to create four theme layers that functioned as groundwater regulating elements. An overall overview of the groundwater map zone of the research areas was obtained by overlaying and analyzing these layers. Because of the low surficial lineaments in the Basawa research region, there may not be as many groundwater conduits there. The Palladan research area, however, has a potentially high groundwater potential and a moderate to high lineament density. The lineament directions (NW–SE, NNE-SSW, ENE-WSW, NE-SW, and E-W) that define the tectonic events of the research area can be extracted through the use of the aeromagnetic data. These orientations align with the principal faults, fissures, and deformations noted in the area.
