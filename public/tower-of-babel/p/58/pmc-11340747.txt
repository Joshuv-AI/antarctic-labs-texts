Expression patterns and clinical value of key m6A RNA modification regulators in smoking patients with coronary artery disease
Authors: Huanwei Zhuang, Hua Ouyang, Yangfei Peng, Shuji Gong, Kun Xiang, Le Chen, Jinlan Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEven though N6-methyladenosine (m6A) RNA modifications are increasingly being implicated in human disease, their mechanisms are not fully understood in smokers with coronary artery disease (CAD). Thirty m6A-related regulators’ expression (MRRE) in CAD individuals (smokers and non-smokers) were analyzed from GEO. Support Vector Machine, random forest, and nomogram models were constructed to assess its clinical value. Consensus clustering, principal component analysis, and ssGSEA were used to construct a full picture of m6A-related regulators in smokers with CAD. Oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) and qRT-PCR were used to validate hypoxia’s effect on MRRE. A comparison between smokers with CAD and controls revealed lower expression levels of RBM15B, YTHDC2, and ZC3H13. Based on three key MRREs, all models showed good clinical value, and smokers with CAD were divided into two distinct molecular subgroups. The correlations were found between key MRRE and the degree of immune infiltration. Three key MRREs in HUVECs and FMC84 mouse cardiomyocytes were reduced in the OGD group. Through hypoxia, smoking might reduce the expression levels of RBM15B, YTHDC2, and ZC3H13 in smokers with CAD. Our findings provide an important theoretical basis for the treatment of smokers with CAD.KEYWORDS: Coronary artery disease, smoking, N6-methyladenosine RNA modification, hypoxia, epigenetic modification

Introduction

Worldwide, coronary artery disease (CAD) is the principal reason for cardiovascular deaths, accounting for 43.2% of all deaths [1]. It is a cardiac disease characterized by ischemia and hypoxia [2]. Smoking is considered as an independent hazard factor for CAD [3]. It adds to the incidence of all stages of atherosclerosis, beginning with endothelial dysfunction to various types of cardiovascular disease [4]. Moreover, daily cigarette consumption, smoking initiation age, and smoking years are negatively correlated with CAD risk and status [3]. Smoking leads to increased carboxyhemoglobinemia and decreased oxygen utilization by hemoglobin, resulting in impaired oxygen release from tissues [5–7]. In addition to raising heart metabolism’s oxygen demands, smoking reduces oxygen availability [8] and even impairs vascular endothelial function and stability, promoting myocardial hypoxia [9]. A randomized controlled trial showed that even e-cigarettes could induce arterial hypoxemia in smokers suffering from CAD [10]. Smoking can also affect gene expression [11]. CAD-related genes should be studied with smoking effects, as Horne et al. showed that the genetic risk from genes was modified by smoking [12]. Therefore, studying the molecular alterations in CAD associated with smoking-induced hypoxia can be beneficial in gaining a more thorough comprehension of the pathogenesis of CAD, which may lead to a new therapeutic target for smokers with CAD.

Recent progress in understanding epigenetic mechanisms has uncovered a link between epigenetic modifications and cardiovascular disease, providing novel possible diagnostic and therapeutic targets [13]. Among more than 170 RNA modification types, N6-methyladenosine (m6A) modification is the most prevalent, reversible, and dynamic eukaryotic mRNA transcriptional modification, which comprises 50% of all methylated ribonucleotides [14]. m6A modifications influence a diverse range of cellular processes, including RNA stability, export, splicing, and translation [14]. Additionally, immune infiltration, which has been linked to a significant role in the progression of CAD [15], is strongly correlated with m6A modification [16]. A dynamic interaction among m6A’s methyltransferases (‘writers’), demethylases (‘erasers’), and binding proteins (‘readers’) determines the abundance and roles of m6A on RNA [14].

Several independent studies have revealed that dysregulation of m6A-related regulators is strongly correlated with specific cardiovascular diseases, such as hypertension, cardiac hypertrophy, heart failure, and CAD [13]. For example, FTO-dependent N6-methyladenosine played an essential regulatory function in cardiac function, which was found by Mathiyalagan et al. in the remodeling and repair of the heart [17]. Song et al. identified METTL3 as a major factor involved in aberrant m6A modifications in hypoxia/reoxygenation-treated cardiomyocytes [18]. Zhang et al. revealed that ALKBH5 expression was significantly lower in TNF-α-treated HUVECs, suggesting a role in protection against cell injury [19]. In a specific stage of CAD, the above discoveries may provide insights into the mechanism of RNA modification for m6A. Nevertheless, there have been no studies evaluating m6A-related regulators’ expression (MRRE) or molecular types in smokers with CAD.

In order to understand the expression patterns of m6A RNA modification regulators and its clinical value, we identified three key m6A-related regulators in 72 CAD individuals (smokers and non-smokers) from a public database and established three models with good clinical value. We also identified two distinct molecular subgroups and revealed MRRE-related immune cell infiltration. Finally, we verified that hypoxic environments could lower MRRE. These findings will lead to a potential therapeutic target for smokers with CAD.

Materials and methods

Data source and pre-processing

Platform and probe data were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) [20] database. Peripheral blood cell gene alignments and clinical data (Table S1) were obtained from the GSE20681 dataset [21], which contained 99 CAD individuals and 99 controls. Gene expression levels in the dataset were normalized by signal intensity of Log2 transformation. Probes were annotated by GPL4133. For the present study, 134 individuals were selected based on whether they smoked or not. The data were organized using Perl software, and the ‘limma’ package of the R program was used to analyze the data.

Comparison of peripheral blood MRRE between smokers with CAD and non-smokers with CAD

According to the relevant literature [22], a total of 30 regulators (including 3 erasers, 8 writers, and 19 readers) related to m6A were selected (Table 1). R software ‘limma’ package was used to extract MRRE data, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare MRRE in smokers and non-smokers with CAD. Visualize the results by using the R ‘pheat’ and ‘ggplot2’ packages. The m6A-related regulators were annotated on the location of human chromosomes in the form of a circle map using the ‘RCircos’ software tool. The correlations among 30 m6A-related regulators in the two groups were analyzed separately.

Construction of the support vector machine and random forest models

To explore the clinical value of the key m6A-related regulators, Support Vector Machine (SVM) [23] and random forest (RF) [24] models were constructed as training models. The SVM model and RF model were developed using R packages ‘caret’ and ‘randomForest,’ respectively. To determine which model is preferable, three factors were taken into consideration: the boxplots of residual, the inverse cumulative distribution of the residual error, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) [25]. The model with a low residual value and a large AUC value is more stable and accurate. Based on the key m6A-related regulators, the RF approach was used to filter CAD individuals as opposed to normal individuals. In the present study, ntrees and mtry were set to 500 and 3, respectively. After 10-fold cross-validation, the ntree with the fewest cross-validation errors was chosen. The genetic importance plot was constructed based on the optimal ntree. Mean Decreasing Gini (MDG) was used to assess the importance of the selected gene to the model, with a higher value indicating greater importance.

Construction of the nomogram model

Based on key m6A-related regulators, a nomogram model [26] was constructed by the R ‘rms’ package. The model was used to forecast the occurrence of CAD patients. A calibration curve was analyzed to determine whether the predicted values matched the actual values and to study accuracy and consistency. An analysis of the intervention effect of the m6A-related regulators was conducted using decision curve analysis [27]. A clinical impact curve was generated to determine whether the model could be exploited to reduce CAD incidence rates.

Identification of molecular subgroups

Consensus clustering is commonly applied to identify the optimum number of clusters for a given dataset and can be implemented using the R package ‘ConsensusClusterPlus.’ A resampling-based approach for assessing cluster stability determined the reasonableness of the consensus clustering [28]. Combining the results of the consistency matrix heat map, the cumulative distribution function (CDF) and the delta plot, the optimal number of clusters was determined [28]. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare 30 MRRE between molecular subgroups. A threshold of |log2FC| > 1 and q value < 0.05 was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between molecular subgroups. The biological characteristics of DEGs were searched in GeneCards®: The Human Gene Database (https://www.genecards.org/).

Calculation of m6A score and analysis of immune cell infiltration

Principal component analysis (PCA) [29] prioritizes the genes in the set that are closely linked (or unrelated) and weakly considers the genes that do not track each other. To quantitatively assess the molecular subgroup results, PCA was performed to compute m6A scores for each individual. Based on the immune.gmt data set, a single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) and a Gaussian discriminant analysis (GDA) were conducted to generate a score for each sample [30]. By using this method, the degree of immune cell infiltration could be measured in each sample, as well as how MRRE correlated with the amount of immune cell infiltration.

Cell culture and processing

FMC84 mouse cardiomyocytes and Human Umbilical Vein Endothelial cells (HUVECs) were a kind gift from Prof. Keshen Li. Both cells were placed in high glucose DMEM (Thermo Fisher Scientific, San Jose, USA) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (PBS) and 1% double antibodies and incubated at 37°C in an incubator containing 5% CO2. Cells were allowed to undergo oxygen-glucose deprivation (OGD) [31] to simulate a hypoxic environment. The HUVECs and FMC84 mouse cardiomyocytes were removed from the cell medium in the OGD group. The cells were re-cultured in DMEM medium without glucose after being rinsed three times with PBS. Finally, the cells were placed in an incubator containing a mixture of 0.1% O2, 5% CO2, and 94.9% N2 and incubated at 37°C for 3 hours. The cells were used for subsequent experiments.

Tube formation assay

HUVECs were divided into OGD and control groups to examine the influence of hypoxia on tube formation. The substrate gel (Corning, #CB-40230C) was pre-melted at 4°C overnight and placed on a plate the following day. After spreading the substrate gel, 70 ul of the gel was pipetted into a 24-well plate by a pre-cooled suction head and incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes to allow its formation. 200 ul of cell suspension (density: 4 × 104/ml) was taken from each of the two groups and spread onto the gel. Using a phase contrast microscope, images were taken at 50× and 200× magnification after 3 hours to determine the extent of intercellular tubular structure formation. To determine the efficiency of tube formation, total tube length, and total branches were calculated using NIH ImageJ software and plotted (GraphPad PRISM software).

Scratch assay

An assay for scratch wound healing migration was carried out. HUVECs from the OGD and control groups were first inoculated into 6-well plates and allowed to grow until fully fused. The tip of the sterile 1000 uL gun was then used to draw a straight line through the center of the cell to form the two separated parts. After the cells were rinsed twice with PBS, the scratched cells were removed, and a serum-free DMEM culture medium was added. The incubation temperature was maintained at 37°C. Using a phase-contrast microscope (Nikon Eclipse Inverted Phase Contrast Microscope Spectra Services) at 100X magnification, images were captured at 0 and 3 hours. Cell migration rate was calculated based on the area covered by the cells over time, and data were analyzed and visualized using the NIH ImageJ software (GraphPad PRISM).

Quantitative real-time PCR

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) were performed to examine mRNAs’ expression. Total RNA was isolated from the HUVECs and FMC84 mouse cardiomyocytes using Trizol reagent (Invitrogen). To generate the first-stranded cDNA from mRNA, total RNA was used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. According to the manufacturer guidelines, CFX96TM Real-Time System (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) was used to perform qRT-PCR with an initial step at 95°C for 15 s for denaturation and 60°C for 60 s for annealing, followed by 40 cycles, using β-actin as an internal control. Using the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt), the data were assessed and then normalized to β-actin. The primers’ sequence in the present study were shown in Table S2.

Statistical analysis

The data calculations and statistical analyses were conducted using R programming (version 4.1.3). A t-test was used for variables that satisfied the normal distribution, whereas a Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used for variables that did not. A chi-square test was used to compare and analyze the statistical significance between the two sets of categorical variables. A Spearman correlation analysis was used to compute the correlation coefficients between different genes. p values were bilateral, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Comparison of MRRE between smokers and non-smokers with CAD

To identify 30 m6A-related regulators differentially expressed in peripheral blood between smokers with CAD and non-smokers with CAD, we collected 72 patients with CAD from the GSE20681 dataset. There were 25 individuals in the smoking group and 47 individuals in the non-smoking group. Age and gender did not differ statistically between the groups (p = 0.329 and p = 1.000, respectively) (Table S3). Two writers [RNA binding motif protein 15B (RBM15B) and zinc finger CCCH domain-containing protein 13 (ZC3H13)] and one reader [YT521-B homology domain containing 2 (YTHDC2)] differed between the two groups (Figure 1(a)), and they were thought to be key m6A-related regulators in smokers with CAD. All three key MRREs were reduced in smokers with CAD (Figure 1(b)). All 30 m6A-related regulators could be annotated in the human chromosome, and RBM15B, YTHDC2, and ZC3H13 were distributed on chromosomes 3, 5 and 13, respectively (Figure 1(c)).

Correlation analysis among m6A-related regulators

To assess whether smoking affected the interactions among m6A-related modulators in CAD, we analyzed the correlation among them in the smoking with CAD and non-smoking with CAD separately. As shown in Figure 2(a–b), the correlations among the 30 m6A-related regulators in the two groups were indeed different, implying that smoking might influence the function of m6A-related regulators. Moreover, we observed the effects of RBM15B, YTHDC2, and ZC3H13 on other m6A-related regulators in the smoking with CAD group by linear regression analysis method. The results revealed that RBM15B correlated positively with WTAP, IGFBP3, CPSF6, FTO, and YTHDF1 but correlated negatively with IGF2BP1; YTHDC2 correlated positively with METTL3 and RBMX; ZC3H13 correlated positively with FMR1 (Figure 2(c)).

Construction of the SVM and RF models

To analyze whether the key MRRE could forecast the occurrence of CAD, we established an SVM and RF model. The boxplots of residual and the residual reverse cumulative distribution plot (Figure 3(a–b)) demonstrated that the residual value of the RF model was lower. The AUC of SVM and RF was 0.755 and 1.000, respectively (Figure 3(c)). The above results showed that it was more accurate and stable to use the RF model than the SVF model to obtain results. As a result, the RF model was found to be the most suitable. Moreover, the optimal ntree was determined by 10-fold cross-validation. The random forest plot (Figure 3d) showed that the optimal ntree was 17. To assess the importance of three key m6A-related regulators in the RF model, the genetic importance plot was established based on the optimal ntree. The MDG values of RBM15B, YTHDC2, and ZC3H13 were all above 15, which implied the important role of three key m6A-related modulators in the occurrence of CAD (Figure 3(e).

Construction of the nomogram model

The ‘rms’ R package was used to develop a nomogram model that contained three key m6A-related regulators. Based on the model (Figure 4(a)), we were able to predict the rate of CAD incidence. An analysis of the calibration curve (Figure 4(b)) showed that the nomogram model is capable of predicting events well. According to decision curve analysis (Figure 4(c)), the model’s judgments may be useful to individuals with CAD. It was clear from the clinical impact curve (Figure 4(d)) that the nomogram model has good predictive power. Again, these results indicated that RBM15B, YTHDC2, and ZC3H13 played a role in CAD development.

Identification of two distinct m6A molecular subgroups

To identify different m6A molecular subgroups, we used consensus-clustering methods based on the key m6A-related regulators. By combining clustered heat map (Figure S1), the CDF plot (Figure 5(a)), Delta area plot (Figure 5(b)) and tracking plot (Figure 5(c)), 25 smokers with CAD were recognized into two m6A molecular subgroups. Twenty-one patients belonged to subgroup A and four patients belonged to subgroup B (Figure 5(d), Table S4). PCA results (Figure 5(e)) showed that subgroups A and B could be completely distinguished. The 30 MRRE in subgroup A and subgroup B were shown in Figure 5(f–g). ZC3H13, WTAP, RBM15B, YTHDC2, and XRN1 were upregulated in subgroup A, and IGF2BP1 was upregulated in subgroup B. A total of eight genes (GFRA4, ANK2, PCP2, CHODL, LOC100131845, GHRHR, AQP2, and PRR17) were considered as DEGs between the two groups, all of which were highly expressed in type B (Table 2). Among them, the biological processes of Ankyrin 2 (ANK2) were closely related to cardiac function, such as heart rate regulation, ventricular myocardial cell action potential, and myocardial contraction (Table S5).

Reduction in endothelial cell function and MRRE under hypoxic environment

To verify the effect of hypoxia on endothelial cells, we tested the functions of HUVECs treated with OGD. Compared to the controls, the total tube length and total branches of HUVECs in the OGD group decreased significantly at both 50× and 200× after 3 hours of OGD (Figure 9(a–b)). The migration distance of HUVECs in the OGD group was not different from the control group at 0 h, but it was statistically significant that the migration distance of HUVECs in the OGD group was significantly lower than that in the control group after 3 hours (Figure 9(c–d)). The results demonstrated that hypoxia affected endothelial cell function and a hypoxia model was successful in being manufactured. After that, to verify the effect of hypoxia on the three key MRREs, we further treated HUVECs and FMC84 mouse cardiomyocytes with OGD. qRT-PCR results showed that ZC3H13, YTHDC2, and RBM15B expressions were downregulated in both HUVECs and FMC84 mouse cardiomyocytes after OGD (Figure 10(a–b)), implying that hypoxia had the same effect on endothelial cell and cardiomyocyte.

Discussion

CAD is an ischemic-hypoxic disease [32]. The heart is the body’s primary pump and biggest consumer of oxygen, yet it has a limited capacity for ATP generation and utilization [33], making it more vulnerable to hypoxia. Additionally, myocardial gene expression is profoundly influenced by oxygen, and as myocardial oxygen levels decline, gene expression patterns in the heart change dramatically, both during hypoxia alone and during ischemia-associated hypoxia [34]. Meanwhile, smoking not only can cause systemic hypoxia [35] but also leads to genetic changes in smokers with CAD [32]. Based on this, the present study, through bioinformatics and in vitro experiments, found that the three key MRREs differed between smokers and non-smokers with CAD, the RF model had good clinical value, two different molecular subgroups existed in smokers with CAD and the three key MRREs were down-regulated in the OGD group.

According to the GEO database data, ZC3H13 (‘writer’), RBM15B (‘writer’), and YTHDC2 (‘reader’) had statistically significant expression levels in smokers with CAD compared to non-smokers with CAD, indicating they played an important role in the smokers with CAD. Meanwhile, all three MRRE levels were also reduced after hypoxia treatment of HUVECs and FMC84 mouse cardiomyocytes in vitro. We provided preliminary evidence that smoking might modulate key MRRE levels in smokers with CAD through hypoxia. This was consistent with previous research. Liang et al. discovered that peripheral blood expression of ZC3H13 was significantly lower in the myocardial infarction (MI) group [36], while MI occurs when the coronary arteries are ischemic or hypoxic for an extended period of time [37]. As reported by Hao et al. [38], RBM15B was overexpressed in the aerobic exercise group and had an important function in reducing cardiovascular disease risk. A remarkable positive correlation was also found between RBM15B and FTO (r = 0.662, p < 0.001). Mathiyalagan et al. discovered that reduced FTO expression could enhance m6A level on RNAs and decrease myocardial contractility, which was validated in failing mammalian hearts and hypoxic myocardial cells [17]. The mRNA translation capacity of hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) can be enhanced by YTHDC2 [39]. HIF-1α not only plays a core function in the regulation of cardiac energy availability and utilization but also has significance in impaired cardiac oxygen transport [36] and is even related to coronary collateral formation capacity and clinical performance of CAD [40].

RF is often used for classification tasks because of its inherent resistance to overfitting and integrated learning capabilities [41]. It can capture complex dependency patterns between results and covariates, so it is commonly used for histological analysis and feature screening [24]. A RF model was developed by Hao et al. using ZC3H13, RBM15B, YTHDC2, and nine other genes to predict the risk of cardiovascular diseases [38]. Furthermore, Li et al. established the Forest model to forecast the progression of CAD using YTHDC2, ZC3H13, and two other genes [22]. The RF model constructed in the present study successfully identified CAD patients. Moreover, our model performance was further improved compared with the previous model [22]. The MDG of the Genetic importance plot showed that the three key m6A-related regulators played an important role in the model. As a result of these findings, the RF model based on three key m6A-related regulators appeared to be clinically useful.

Twenty-five smokers with CAD were effectively divided into two m6A molecular subgroups in the clustering analysis. Furthermore, PCA results demonstrated that the two m6A subgroups could be clearly distinguished. Six of the thirty m6A-related regulators exhibited statistically significant expression differences between the two subgroups, with type A expressing them more strongly than type B. Notably, the expression patterns of ZC3H13, RBM15B, and YTHDC2 between the two subgroups were similar to the subset of smokers/non-smokers with CAD, indicating that type B patients might smoke at larger dosages than type A patients. Meanwhile, there were eight DEGs between the two subgroups, among which the biological characteristics of ANK2 were closely related to cardiac function. Moreover, ANK2 was associated with the development of Cardiac Arrhythmia [42], Ankyrin-B-Related [43], and Long QT Syndrome 1 [44]. Consequently, the aforementioned findings showed that two distinct patterns of m6A modification might occur in smokers with CAD.

Atherosclerosis is an autoimmune disease with an inflammatory response [45], and CAD is a coronary ischemic heart disease mainly caused by atherosclerosis [46]. Emerging researches focus on the function of epigenetic regulation in immunity, specifically N6-methyladenosine RNA modification [47]. In smokers with CAD group, correlation analysis between key m6A-related regulator expression and infiltrating immune cell score suggested that ZC3H13 downregulation might inhibit the infiltration of Plasmacytoid.dendritic.cell but promote the aggregation of Activated.B.cell; RBM15B downregulation might inhibit the aggregation of CD56bright.natural.killer.cell and Activated.CD8.T.cell; YTHDC2 downregulation might inhibit the aggregation of Activated.CD4.T.cells, but promote the infiltration of macrophages. These suggested that aberrant expression of ZC3H13, RBM15B, and YTHDC2 played an important linking role in epigenetic modification and immune infiltration in smokers with CAD.

The study is, however, restricted by a few limitations. Firstly, we mainly used publicly available microarray data for our bioinformatics analyses, and we only validated m6A-related regulators in a single set of samples. To further investigate and validate the results of this study, Randomized Controlled Trials with a larger sample size are recommended. Secondly, smoking can affect the expression levels of CAD genes through a variety of mechanisms. Only hypoxia was used in our study to confirm smoking’s effects on CAD gene expression. Therefore, the differences of the key MRREs were more significant between the two groups in vitro than that by bioinformatics. Additional research and validation on other mechanisms of smoking affecting CAD gene expression were recommended.

Conclusion

We found RBM15B, YTHDC2, and ZC3H13 were abnormally expressed in smokers with CAD, evaluated the clinical value of RF model, and identified different expression patterns. These findings provide insight into the molecular processes behind epigenetic modifications in smokers with CAD and provide possible novel therapeutic options for smokers with CAD.
