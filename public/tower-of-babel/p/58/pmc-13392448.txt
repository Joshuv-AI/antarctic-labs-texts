Atomically dispersed iron on carbon nitride with enhanced oxygen adsorption for efficient and scalable photooxidation
Authors: Zhen Su, Hua Long, Siyi Song, Rui-Rui Zhao, Liang Zhou, Ang Gao, Ke Gao, Wen-Jing Xiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Selective oxidation of C-H bonds represents a pivotal transformation for upgrading hydrocarbon feedstocks into value-added chemicals, yet achieving this under mild conditions remains a formidable challenge. We herein disclose a single-atom photocatalytic system featuring nonsymmetric Fe-N3Br sites anchored on melem that enables efficient photooxidation of C(sp3)-H bonds under mild conditions. The coordination environment facilitates Griffiths-type O2 adsorption and realizes a high toluene conversion rate of 9.27 × 104 μmol g−1 h−1. Experimental and theoretical studies indicate that the Fe-N3Br configuration reduces the Fe 3d splitting energy, enhances the O2 adsorption, and facilitates O2 activation, thereby promoting superoxide radical (•O2-) generation. The catalyst demonstrates broad functional group tolerance (43 examples, up to 97% yield), and good scalability, enabling a 1000 mmol-scale continuous-flow reaction. This work demonstrates the importance of the single-atom site coordination in governing oxygen activation and provides a scalable, green photocatalytic platform for C-H functionalization.

Introduction

Direct aerobic oxidation reactions are widely used in chemical synthesis in both academia and industry1,2. Among these transformations, the selective oxidation of toluene to benzaldehyde holds particular industrial significance, as benzaldehyde serves as a key intermediate in the manufacture of pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, and fragrance compounds3. Conventional industrial processes, including chlorination-saponification, suffer from severe limitations such as harsh reaction conditions, environmental toxicity, and inadequate selectivity4. The field has been significantly advanced by pioneering homogeneous catalytic systems, such as those employing Co/Mn salts for the oxidation of xylene and cyclohexane5. These foundational strategies demonstrate the feasibility of catalytic C-H oxidation, though the energy requirements, including elevated temperature and pressure, alongside conversions typically under 15%, present challenges for broader applicability. This work was later extended to sophisticated heterogeneous catalysts like Au-Pd/TiO26,7. The high activity of these systems is commendable, yet their practical benefits can be offset by the demanding operational conditions required to maintain high efficiency.

Photocatalysis has emerged as a highly promising alternative strategy for organic transformations, owing to its ability to generate reactive species under mild and environmentally benign conditions8. Heterogeneous photocatalysts, in particular, have attracted considerable interest for toluene oxidation using molecular O2 as the oxidant, benefiting from their robustness, recyclability, and ease of separation. Since the seminal report on TiO2-mediated photocatalytic oxidation in 19819, substantial efforts have been devoted to developing diverse catalytic architectures. These include conventional semiconductors (e.g., Bi2WO6/BiOCl), polyoxometalates (e.g., Ti4Fe1 cocrystal clusters), metal-organic frameworks (e.g., Fe-UiO-66), halide perovskites (e.g., CsxMA3-xSb2Br9, MA = CH3NH3), single-atom catalysts (e.g., Pd-O3/Bi2WO6-x/BiOCl), etc3,10. Despite these noteworthy advances, the overall performance of existing photocatalytic systems still falls short of industrial requirements. Limitations such as moderate toluene conversion rates, small reaction scales, and restricted substrate scope hinder practical implementation. In a typical photocatalytic toluene oxidation pathway, photogenerated holes oxidize toluene to form benzyl radicals, which subsequently combine with reactive oxygen species such as superoxide radicals (•O2−) to yield benzaldehyde. It follows that enhancing the O2 activation process-including adsorption and conversion to •O2−-could kinetically promote the oxidation of benzyl radicals11,12. Thus, engineering highly active sites to facilitate O2 activation represents a critical direction for overcoming the current limitations in photocatalytic benzaldehyde synthesis.

With well-defined atomic structures and highly tunable electronic properties, single-atom photocatalysts provide an ideal platform for O2 activation. On single-atom metal surfaces, two O2 adsorption models have been established: the end-on (Pauling-type) and side-on (Griffiths-type) configurations. In the Pauling model Fig. 1a), O2 chemisorbs in an end-on fashion via electron donation into its π* antibonding orbital13. In contrast, the Griffiths-type side-on adsorption (Fig. 1b) generally exhibits stronger binding affinity, which is attributed to the formation of two metal-oxygen bonds compared to only one in the end-on model14. This enhanced adsorption capability makes the side-on configuration particularly attractive for promoting O2 activation, yet it remains unexplored in photocatalytic oxidation reactions. Notable efforts have been made to engineer single-atom sites favoring the side-on adsorption geometry. For example, Zhu and colleagues designed a hybrid catalyst comprising Fe single atoms (Fe-N-C) and ultrafine Pd nanoclusters, in which electron transfer from Fe to Pd led to the redistribution of Fe 3d-electrons15. This electronic modulation partially occupied the formerly empty dz2 orbital of low-spin Fe(II), facilitating improved orbital overlap with O2 and thereby enabling side-on adsorption. In another elegant approach, Wang et al. developed tip-like Fe-N4 sites that induced a local microenvironment conducive to the end-on to side-on transition16. These studies underscore the importance of orbital interactions between metal centers and O2 in dictating the adsorption configuration. Despite these advances, the majority of strategies for tailoring the coordination environment of metal single atoms remain limited to carbon-based supports and often require high-temperature pyrolysis17–19. In this work, we report a melem-supported single-atom photocatalyst with nonsymmetric Fe-N3Br coordination for efficient toluene oxidation. Melem (2,5,8-triamino-tri-s-triazine, C6N10H6), the structural unit of graphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4)20–22, offers a favorable combination of semiconducting properties and abundant nitrogen sites for metal anchoring, and it can be prepared under mild conditions23. The Fe-N3Br sites are shown to modulate the Fe 3d-orbital electron distribution, achieving side-on O2 adsorption (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Data 5 and 6) and boosting O2 activation. The resulting photocatalyst demonstrates good performance in toluene conversion rate, scalability, and applicability across a wide range of substrates.

Results

Catalyst synthesis and characterization

The Fe-N3Br catalyst supported on melem was synthesized via a straightforward one-step pyrolysis method, involving annealing of iron bromide (FeBr3) and melamine at 450 °C (Fig. 2a). When melamine alone was pyrolyzed at 450 °C, the resulting product was identified as pure melem based on its XRD pattern and FTIR spectrum (Supplementary Fig. 2)24. The XRD pattern of the Fe-N3Br material closely resembles that of pristine melem, indicating that the melem framework remains intact and serves as a host for dispersed Fe species. X-Ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis provided insights into the surface composition and chemical bonding of catalysts. Deconvolution of the N 1s spectra (Fig. 2b) yielded three components at 398.7, 399.9, and 401.0 eV, characteristic of sp2-hybridized nitrogen (C=N-C), tertiary nitrogen (N-(C)3), and amino functionalities (C-NHx), respectively25. The consensus of these nitrogen types with the sp2 carbon signal (N-C=N) in the C 1s region (Supplementary Fig. 3d) firmly establishes the heptazine (C6N7) framework in all samples. A key observation is the positive binding energy shift experienced by the C=N-C and N-(C)3 nitrogen atoms upon Fe incorporation. This electronic perturbation provides direct evidence for the coordination of Fe species to the nitrogen-rich matrix, effectively forming metal-nitrogen bonds18. The Fe 2p spectrum for Fe-N3Br (Supplementary Fig. 4), with its doublet at 710.7 and 723.8 eV, is assigned to Fe2+ 25, while the peak shift is indicative of the specific N/Br coordination sphere. Furthermore, the slightly elevated Fe content in Fe-N4 (XPS: 2.16 at%; ICP: 10.82 wt%) compared to Fe-N3Br (XPS: 2.00 at%; ICP: 7.97 wt%) aligns with the thermal conversion of melem to g-C3N4 at higher temperature, which typically facilitates greater metal retention (Supplementary Fig. 5)26.

The oxidation state and local coordination of iron were elucidated by X-ray absorption spectroscopy. The Fe K-edge XANES spectra (Fig. 2c) reveal that both Fe-N3Br and Fe-N4 exhibit nearly identical pre-edge regions (Region A) to that of standard FeO, indicating similar average valence states dominated by Fe2+ 27, which is in full agreement with the XPS Fe 2p analysis. Specifically, Fe-N3Br shows a slight positive shift in the pre-edge region and a higher white line intensity at ~7127 eV (Region B) compared to Fe-N4, suggesting a slightly elevated valence state of Fe-N3Br disrupted by Br doping28. This corroborates the introduction of Br atoms into the first coordination shell, where they bond with Fe centers by replacing N atoms. Detailed fitting of the EXAFS spectra (Supplementary Fig. 7), supported by XPS results, allows determination of the precise atomic configurations around the Fe single atoms. We further performed the ion time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) to qualitatively investigate the surface composition of Fe-N3Br. While not quantitative, this technique enables qualitative analysis via the mass position and relative intensity. The mass spectrum in Fig. 2d shows the existence of massive Fe-N3 fragments (FeN3+ and FeN3H+) and a minor amount of Fe-N4 (Fe-N4H+). Notably, the Fe-Br bond is not detectable by TOF-SIMS due to its cleavage during the sputtering process. The Fe-N3Br configuration can therefore be deduced in this sample. Furthermore, TOF-SIMS mapping images reveal its uniform distribution in the carbon-nitrogen matrix (Supplementary Fig. 8).

The XAFS measurements at Br K-edge were conducted, which reveal that Br atoms are predominantly coordinated to Fe rather than in residual iron bromide species in the Fe-N3Br sample (Supplementary Fig. 9). To eliminate the influence of residual iron bromide species on the catalytic performance, a well-defined Fe-N4 coordination was constructed in the Fe/g-C3N4 sample following reported procedures29,30, by annealing ferric chloride and dicyandiamide at 550 °C (Supplementary Fig. 10). The comparable catalytic performance of Fe/g-C3N4 and Fe-N4 (with residual FeBr3) rules out the performance contribution from iron bromide species, suggesting the Fe-N3Br coordination structure as the active sites of the Fe-N3Br catalyst. Morphological examination shows that Fe-N3Br possesses a dense, block-like structure, in contrast to the porous texture of Fe-N4 (Supplementary Figs. 11 and 13). The atomic dispersion of Fe is directly confirmed by HAADF-STEM (Fig. 2e, f), where individual Fe atoms appear as bright spots against the support. The homogeneous co-localization of Fe, Br, C, and N signals in elemental mapping (Fig. 2g) provides compelling evidence for the successful and uniform incorporation of Fe-N3Br sites within the melem matrix.

Photocatalytic toluene oxidation

The Fe-N3Br catalyst exhibits high activity in the photocatalytic oxidation of toluene, achieving 90% selectivity and 60% conversion to benzaldehyde under mild conditions (room temperature, 390 nm LED, O2 atmosphere). This performance is comparable to the benchmark catalyst Bi2WO6-x/BiOCl4 and other reported heterogeneous systems3,10, despite its micron-sized bulk morphology. The reaction is proven to be photocatalytic, requiring both the catalyst and light illumination (Supplementary Fig. 19), and the active site is unequivocally identified as the Fe-N3Br moiety coordinated to the melem support (Supplementary Fig. 20). A key feature of this system is its strong reaction controllability driven by a sequential oxidation pathway. Selective production of benzaldehyde (62% conv., 84% sel.) is achievable at short reaction times (6 h), while prolonged irradiation (>36 h) quantitatively yields benzoic acid (Supplementary Fig. 21). Furthermore, the catalyst operates efficiently even under an air atmosphere, underscoring its practical potential (Supplementary Fig. 19).

The apparent quantum efficiency (AQE) of Fe-N3Br (13.74%) significantly outperformed those of melem (0.81%) and Fe-N4 (0.61%) (Fig. 3a). Increasing the toluene concentration to 10 mmol increased the AQE to 23.07%. Fe-N3Br achieved a toluene conversion rate of 3.64 × 104 μmol g−1 h−1 at the 1 mmol scale, an 18-fold enhancement over the reference materials (Fig. 3b). At 10 mmol, the rate reached 9.27 × 104 μmol g−1 h−1. These AQE and conversion rate values are competitive with those of previously reported catalysts (Supplementary Tables 5 and 6)10. Moreover, Fe-N3Br exhibited a high turnover frequency (TOF) of 106 h−1, demonstrating its good intrinsic catalytic activity (Supplementary Table 7). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 24, the catalytic performance of TFE was inferior to that of HFIP (2.95 × 103 vs. 3.64 × 104 μmol g−1 h−1), revealing a potential influence of solvent hydrogen-bonding effects on catalytic activity31. Notably, Fe-N4 showed no catalytic activity in TFE, further underscoring the high activity of Fe-N3Br. Across five consecutive photocatalytic cycles, both conversion efficiency and benzaldehyde selectivity remained stable without significant degradation (Supplementary Fig. 25). A 200 mmol batch reaction gave 76% conversion and 87% selectivity but required 117 h. A continuous-flow setup reduced this time to 42 h by improving light absorption and mass transfer. Finally, a 1000 mmol flow reaction produced benzaldehyde at 6.2 mmol h−1 (73% conv., 82% sel.) over 96 h (Fig. 3c, d). The system’s energy efficiency (0.118%) and low cost (0.076 USD g−1) compare favorably with reported systems (Supplementary Table 11). Mechanistic insights

Mechanistic investigations were conducted to elucidate the reaction pathway. Radical trapping experiments confirmed the involvement of holes (h+), electrons (e−), superoxide radicals (•O2−), and benzyl radicals (R•), with the C(sp3)-H bond activation by photogenerated h+ being a critical step (Fig. 4a)3. 18O2 isotope labelling experiments showed 81% 18O incorporation into the benzaldehyde product, unequivocally confirming that molecular oxygen is the source of the carbonyl oxygen atom via the reaction of R• with •O2− (Supplementary Fig. 32). In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) monitored the reaction progress: under dark conditions, peaks corresponding to toluene adsorption (e.g., δ(C-H) of the aromatic ring at 3075 and 3033 cm−1, ν(C=C) at 1616 and 1499 cm−1, and ν(C-H) of the methyl group at 2934 and 2881 cm−1)32 increased in intensity over time (Supplementary Fig. 33). The potential involvement of bromine radicals was rigorously examined. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy using N-tert-butyl-α-phenylnitrone (PBN) as a spin trap (Fig. 4b) showed that UV irradiation of CBr4 generated signals corresponding to PBN-•Br and PBN-•CBr3 adducts (Supplementary Fig. 35). In contrast, the EPR spectrum of the Fe-N3Br system under irradiation displayed only a characteristic 2 × 3 splitting pattern from a PBN-carbon-centered radical (R•) adduct, with no evidence of bromine radical species. This was further supported by control experiments where FeBr3 alone or combined with potential ligands (e.g., 2,2’-bipyridine (L1) and 1,10-phenanthroline (L2)) showed negligible activity, ruling out a photoinduced ligand-to-metal charge transfer (LMCT) process generating bromine radicals as a viable pathway (Supplementary Fig. 20).

Based on these findings, a photocatalytic mechanism for Fe-N3Br is proposed (Fig. 4c). Upon light absorption, electrons are excited from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB). The photogenerated holes in the VB (located at 1.69 V vs. NHE) oxidize adsorbed toluene to R• radicals, a thermodynamically feasible step as the VB potential is more positive than toluene’s oxidation potential (Supplementary Table 12)33,34. Concurrently, electrons in the CB (located at −1.23 V vs. NHE) reduce O2 (E(O2/•O2−) = −0.28 V vs. NHE) to form •O2− radicals. The subsequent coupling of R• and •O2− yields benzaldehyde.

Optical, photoelectrochemical and EPR characterizations were performed to elucidate the enhanced activity of the Fe-N3Br single-atom catalyst. Ultraviolet-visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-vis DRS) revealed enhanced and broadened light absorption for both Fe-N4 and Fe-N3Br compared to melem, attributed to host-guest interactions between the Fe sites and the carbon nitride support (Supplementary Fig. 40). A marked decrease in photoluminescence intensity (Supplementary Fig. 41) and a substantial increase in photocurrent density (Supplementary Fig. 42) were observed for the single-atom catalysts, indicating significantly improved charge separation and suppressed recombination due to new electron transfer pathways introduced by the Fe atoms12,35. Time-dependent EPR (Supplementary Figs. 43 and 44) revealed that Fe-N3Br generated a more pronounced •O2⁻ signal without an increase in the R• signal. This indicates that its higher catalytic activity is primarily attributable to enhanced •O2⁻ generation.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations provide atomic-scale insight into the critical O2 activation step, with detailed calculation information provided in Supplementary Data 1–6). Notably, these calculations are performed on idealized structural models and do not fully capture the dynamic surface reconstruction or the practical reaction environment. The results should therefore be considered as qualitative insights that complement the experimental observations. While both Fe-N4 and Fe-N3Br models were initialized with an end-on O2 adsorption geometry, structural optimization revealed a fundamental difference: Fe-N3Br spontaneously relaxed into a more stable side-on configuration, whereas Fe-N4 retained the end-on model (inset of Fig. 4d and Supplementary Data 5,6). This side-on adsorption on Fe-N3Br is accompanied by a substantially lower free energy of O2 adsorption (−3.10 vs. −1.27 eV for Fe-N4). The introduction of Br into the coordination sphere significantly modulates the electronic structure of the Fe center, leading to a redistribution of the 3d-orbital electrons (Supplementary Fig. 47a). The projected density of states (PDOS) shows a reduced energy splitting (2.40 eV) between the spin-up and spin-down Fe 3d states in Fe-N3Br compared to Fe-N4 (3.02 eV), shifting the d-band center closer to the Fermi level (Fig. 4e). This electronic modulation is associated with enhanced overlap between Fe 3d and O2 π* orbitals, as reflected by the upshift of the O2 π* states toward the Fermi level (Supplementary Fig. 47b) and the more negative integrated crystal orbital Hamilton population (ICOHP) value (Fig. 4 f), the latter indicating stronger covalent Fe-O bonding in Fe-N3Br. Such enhanced orbital coupling facilitates greater electron transfer (0.70 e−) from Fe to the O2 π* orbital, revealed by Bader charge and charge density difference analyses (inset of Fig. 4 d), resulting in effective O2 activation to •O2−. Consistent with this, the O-O bond on Fe-N3Br is significantly elongated to 1.40 Å, a characteristic feature of superoxide species (O2−ads)36.

Experimental evidence corroborates the theoretical predictions. Raman spectroscopy of post-reaction catalysts identified a band at 873 cm−1 for Fe-N3Br, characteristic of side-on O2 adsorption, whereas a band at 843 cm−1 for Fe-N4 was assigned to an end-on Fe-O2 species (Supplementary Fig. 48 and Supplementary Data 5 and 6)35,37. The remaining peaks at 710, 743, and 982 cm⁻1 originated from melem (Supplementary Fig. 49). In situ DRIFTS showed a peak at 1044 cm−1, further supporting side-on O2 adsorption on Fe-N3Br (Supplementary Fig. 33)38. This peak emerged over time in the dark and persisted under illumination, indicating that O2 adsorption is unaffected by the light irradiation. Kinetic measurements confirmed a •O2− yield rate for Fe-N3Br (0.0957 spins mm−3 s−1) that was over six times higher than that of Fe-N4 (0.0155 spins mm−3 s−1, Fig. 4 g). The facile detection of the DMPO-•O2− adduct by EPR even under an air atmosphere underscores its good oxygen activation capability, consistent with its high performance in air (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy measurements were performed to investigate the charge transfer dynamics. Time-dependent EPR measurements of •O2− and R• as well as calculational results, indicate that only the electron transfer dynamics can be observed by TA experiments, while the hole behavior does not alter the TA signals. Consequently, the subsequent analysis focuses on electron relaxation39. Under 325 nm excitation in air, Fe-N3Br presents negative ground state bleaching (GSB) signals (370–610 nm, Supplementary Fig. 50a, b). The GSB recovery kinetics (Fig. 4 h) reveals two time constants of τ1 = 24.14 ± 1.75 ps (65%) and τ2 = 179.29 ± 17.49 ps (35%)12,30. These lifetime components may correspond to the trapping of CB electrons into the near band-edge trap states induced by the O2 adsorption on the surface of Fe-N3Br (τ1), and the recombination of electrons with VB holes (τ2), respectively30,35,39,40. On the other hand, Fe-N4 exhibits significantly weak GSB signals (Supplementary Fig. 50d, e) and a much shorter decay time (30 ps, Supplementary Fig. 50f). The comparison between Fe-N3Br and Fe-N4 reveals that photoinduced electron is highly concentrated at the surface of Fe-N3Br in the presence of O2, and the surface O2 adsorption may further prolong the lifetime of photoinduced electrons on Fe-N3Br40,41.

Based on the above characterizations and analysis, a proposed reaction route is illustrated in Fig. 4i. The strong Fe-O2 adsorption promotes electron transfer from the CB to the Fe sites12,39, enhancing charge separation and the utilization of holes localized on nearby nitrogen atoms35. The synergistic effect between efficient carrier dynamics and enhanced O2 activation underpins the good photocatalytic performance of Fe-N3Br.

Substrate scope and functional group tolerance

The broad applicability and synthetic utility of this photocatalytic protocol were demonstrated through the successful oxidation of a diverse range of alkyl aromatic substrates, yielding valuable aldehydes and ketones with high selectivity (Fig. 5). The system proved effective for synthesizing key chemical intermediates across multiple industries. For instance, 4-fluorobenzaldehyde (2b) and 4-trifluorobenzaldehyde (2i), which are precursors to the insecticides cyfluthrin and hydramethylnon, respectively, were obtained in moderate conversions and high selectivity. Pharmaceutical intermediates such as 4-chlorobenzaldehyde (2c, for the analgesic chlormezanone) and 4-bromobenzaldehyde (2d, for antitumor isoquinoline alkaloids) were also efficiently synthesized. In materials chemistry, methyl 4-formylbenzoate (2g, >98% sel.) serves as an intermediate for fluorescent whiteners, and the PEEK monomer 2ab (>98% sel.) was produced with high efficiency. The catalyst showed good functional-group tolerance (2b-2o) and minimal sensitivity to steric effects. Uniformly high performance was observed for ortho-, meta-, and para-substituted toluenes (2e, 2j, 2l, 2n), achieving 63-68% conversion with selectivities up to 99%. Substrates containing multiple methyl groups underwent exclusive mono-oxidation to the corresponding aldehydes with good conversion (2j, 2l, 2p). Ethylbenzenes were selectively oxidized to acetophenones (2r–2v), valuable intermediates in the flavor and fragrance industry, with moderate conversions and good selectivity. For molecules possessing both primary and secondary benzylic C-H bonds (2o, 2t, 2u), the reaction demonstrated a preference for the more electron-rich benzylic position. The ortho-, meta-, and para-substituted ethylbenzenes were efficiently oxidized to desired ketones (2v, 2 u, 2t). Substrates with longer alkyl chains (2u-2y) afforded ketones in moderate to high conversion, albeit with moderate selectivity due to competing β-scission pathways leading to aldehydes (Supplementary Fig. 54). Notably, the method was also suitable for synthesizing some homogeneous photosensitizers, including benzophenones (2aa–2ac) and anthraquinone derivatives (2q, 2ad, 2ag), in high conversions (64–98%) and selectivity (95–99%). Substrates containing multiple reaction sites (1z and 1ae) underwent highly selective mono-oxidation to the corresponding ketones. The over-oxidized by-products 2z-1 and 2ad were also detected by gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) (Supplementary Fig. 51). Challenging substrates such as inert cycloalkanes were successfully oxidized to ketones (2ah–2ak) without ring-opening byproducts (Supplementary Fig. 55). This high selectivity, observed for cyclopentanone (2ah, a key intermediate for rubber and insecticides) and cyclohexanone (2ai, a nylon precursor) with 78–90% selectivity, likely stems from the stability of the corresponding cycloalkyl radicals. Furthermore, the oxidation of decane (2al) and n-tridecane (2am) yielded mixtures of aldehydes and ketones, with limited selectivity observed (Supplementary Table 13). Moreover, the protocol was extended to heteroatom-containing substrates (2an, 2ao) and complex molecules, such as the anisomycin intermediate 2ap and the fragrance ingredient celestolide (2aq), all of which were oxidized efficiently with good selectivity. The broad substrate scope and high functional group tolerance underscore the significant potential of this transformation for applications in pharmaceutical, agrochemical, and specialty chemical synthesis.

Discussion

In conclusion, we have developed a melem-supported single-atom catalyst with nonsymmetric Fe-N3Br coordination that enables highly efficient photocatalytic toluene oxidation. This configuration, verified by experimental and theoretical evidence, optimizes the Fe electronic structure for strong O2 binding and efficient •O2− generation. The catalyst achieves good activity and scalability, establishing the critical link between local coordination geometry and O2 activation efficiency in C-H photooxidation. This insight opens avenues for designing single-atom catalysts for challenging photochemical reactions.

Methods

Materials

Iron bromide (FeBr3, 99%), iron chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, ≥99.0%), zinc chloride (ZnCl2, 98%), tungsten chloride (WCl3, 99.5%), ferric nitrate nonahydrate (Fe(NO3)3·9H2O, 98%), iron fluoride trihydrate (FeF3·3H2O, 98%), urea (≥99.0%), cyanoguanidine (≥99.5%), hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP, ≥99.5%), and decane (99%) were purchased from Shanghai Adamas Reagent Co., Ltd. Manganese chloride tetrahydrate (MnCl2·4H2O, 99.0%, Cat No.1102135, Leyan, Shanghai, China) was purchased from Shanghai Haohong Scientific Co., Ltd. Nickel chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2·6H2O, 98%), cobalt chloride hexahydrate (CoCl2·6H2O, 99%), and chromic chloride hexahydrate (CrCl3·6H2O, 99%) were purchased from the Rhawn reagent. Iron sulfate heptahydrate (Fe2(SO4)3·7H2O, 98%) was purchased from the Shanghai Bide Pharmatech Co., Ltd. Melamine (C3H6N6, 99%), and cupric chloride (CuCl2, 95%) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. 18O2 (10 vol.% 18O2/N2) was purchased from Wuhan Isotope Technology Co., Ltd. Toluene and anhydrous solvents were taken from the JC-Meyer solvent purification system.

Synthesis of photocatalysts

The Fe-N3Br was prepared through a simple one-step pyrolysis. In a typical experiment, 4.38 g FeBr3 and 10 g melamine were mixed, ground, and annealed at 450 °C for 4 h at a ramp rate of 2 °C min−1. For comparison, melem was prepared using the same conditions without the addition of FeBr3. Fe-N4 was synthesized by annealing the mixture of FeBr3 and melamine at 550 °C. The catalysts are shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Characterizations of photocatalysts

The crystallographic structures of the products were determined using a Bruker D8 Advance X-ray diffractometer (XRD; Cu Kα radiation, λ = 1.5418 Å) with an operating voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra were collected on a Nicolet iS50 FT-IR spectrometer. The field-emission scanning electron microscope (FESEM, JSM-7900F), and the transmission electron microscope (TEM, Thermo Scientific Talos F200X G2 operating at 200 kV) were used to analyze the sizes and morphologies of catalysts. The AC-HAADF-STEM was performed by Titan Cubed Themis G2 300 (FEI, USA). The surface components and valence band spectra were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo ESCALAB QXi). The Fe content in catalysts was determined by the inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES; PerkinElmer Optima 3300DV). Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface areas were tested using a nitrogen adsorption apparatus (MICROTRAC BELSORP MAX G). Fe K-edge XAFS measurements were collected at the BL14W1 beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (3.5 GeV storage ring, 220 mA injection current, transmission mode) using a Si (111) double-crystal monochromator. Br K-edge XAFS measurements were conducted on a RapidXAFS 2 M instrument (Anhui Absorption Spectroscopy Analysis Instrument Co., Ltd., transmission mode, 20 kV, 30 mA) using a Si (880) spherically bent crystal analyzer with a 500 mm curvature radius. The obtained spectra were processed using Demeter software. Time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) and the mapping imaging with a sampling area of 500 × 500 μm2 were recorded by ION-TOF TOFSIMS 5.

Photocatalytic reaction towards toluene oxidation

Photocatalytic oxidation of toluene was carried out in a dried 10 mL Schlenk tube connected with an oxygen balloon (Supplementary Fig. 14). In a typical process, 5 mg photocatalyst and 1 mmol toluene were put into the Schlenk tube, and 2 mL HFIP was employed as the solvent for the reaction. The photoreactor system was irradiated by a 120 W 390 nm LED light at a distance of ~5 cm (light intensity = 50 mW cm−2) under vigorous stirring. The irradiation intensity was determined by a Perfectlight PL-MW 2000 photo-radiometer. The temperature was maintained at room temperature (around 298 K) with an electronic fan. After irradiation, the suspension was collected and filtered, and then analyzed by GC-MS for the identification of oxidation productions, and by gas chromatography (GC) for further quantitative analysis. The GC-MS analysis was carried out on a Shimadzu GC-MS-QP2020NX using a capillary column (DB5MS, from Shimadzu, length 30 m, i.d. 0.25 mm, film 0.25 μm). The GC analysis was conducted on a Shimadzu GC-2030AF series GC with an FID detector using a capillary column (SH-5, from Shimadzu, length 30 m, i.d. 0.25 mm, film 0.25 μm) and using decane as the internal standard substance.

Flow reaction scale-up experiment

The catalyst ink was prepared by sonicating and dispersing 600 mg of catalyst for 30 minutes into a solution containing 6 mL of ethanol, 2.1 mL of deionized water, and 0.96 mL of 5% Nafion solution. The ink was evenly coated onto the homemade photoreactors and then dried at 120 °C to form a heterogeneous catalyst film on the bottom of the photoreactors, with a catalyst loading of 2.3 mg cm−2. Subsequently, in a flame-dried 250 mL three-neck flask, toluene was added to HFIP with a stir bar under an air atmosphere. The resulting reaction mixture was pushed through the flow system using a pump. The photoreactor was irradiated by 120 W 390 nm LED lights at room temperature (ca. 298 K) with fan cooling. After irradiation, the suspension was filtered and concentrated in vacuo using a rotary evaporator. The resulting residue was then purified by silica gel column chromatography to obtain the pure product.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

The commercially available VASP (Vienna Ab-initio Simulation Package) software package developed by the University of Vienna42, was used for the DFT calculations. The affixed plane wave method (PAW) was used to deal with the effective interactions between electrons and valence electrons in the nucleus43. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation general function of the Generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was used to solve the equations as well as to obtain energy-dependent information about the system44. The Kohn-Sham electronic wave function was extended using a plane wave function with a truncation energy of 500 eV. The optimization was considered to be convergent when the difference in the spring force applied between adjacent images was less than 0.01 eV Å−1, and the total energy change between adjacent electron steps was less than 10−5 eV, which proved to be sufficiently accurate under the convergence criterion. Brillouin zone integration was conducted using 1 × 1 × 1Γ-centered k-point sampling to simultaneously optimize the geometry and lattice size. For the adsorption of small molecules (e.g., O2), Ead is defined as the adsorption energy. Ead = Eads/sup−(Eads + Esup), where Eads, Esup, and Eads/sup are the energies of the free gas-phase molecules, the catalyst carrier, and the carrier after adsorption of the molecular gas, respectively. In addition, Bader charge analysis was also applied to this work. The data on differential charge density were calculated using Vaspkit. The corresponding optimized atomic configurations are provided in Supplementary Data 1-6.

Femtosecond transient absorption (fs-TA) spectroscopy

Fs-TA measurements were performed on a pump-probe system (Helios, Ultrafast Systems) coupled with a regeneratively amplified Ti:sapphire laser (Coherent Astrella, 800 nm, 35 fs, 5 mJ, 1 kHz) under ambient conditions45. 20 mg catalyst was ultrasonically dispersed in 20 mL acetonitrile to form a clear solution, which was then sealed in a 2 mm quartz cuvette for measurements. Data were collected under an air atmosphere, and attempts to change the atmosphere resulted in no detectable signal. The obtained decay curves were analyzed by fitting to a multi-exponential equation.

General procedure for the synthesis of aldehyde products

In a dried 10 mL Schlenk tube, Fe-N3Br (5 mg), toluene (92.2 mg, 1 mmol), and HFIP (2 mL) were added. The suspension was irradiated by 120 W 390 nm LED light (light intensity = 50 mW cm−2) at room temperature for 6 h with an oxygen balloon. The reaction progress was monitored by GC and GC-MS analysis. After irradiation, the suspension was purified by column chromatography over silica gel to give the corresponding product.

General procedure for the synthesis of ketone products

In a dried 10 mL Schlenk tube, Fe-N3Br (5 mg), ethylbenzene (106.2 mg, 1 mmol) and HFIP (2 mL) were added. The suspension was irradiated by 120 W 390 nm LED light (light intensity = 50 mW cm−2) at room temperature for 6 h with an oxygen balloon. The reaction progress was monitored by GC and GC-MS analysis. After irradiation, the suspension was purified by column chromatography over silica gel to give the corresponding product.
