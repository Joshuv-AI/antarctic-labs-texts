Stacking the Deck to Make Materials With Atypically Short Heterometallic d 8 M(II)···Au(III) Bonds
Authors: Leanna M Karn, Samyadeb Mahato, Adlih Britton, Mojtaba Abbasi, Scott Kroeker, Tim Storr, Daniel B Leznoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe first PtII···AuIII metallophilic interaction is reported in K4[Pt(CN)4][Au(CN)4]Cl·6H2O, which consists of a 1D chain of stacking [Pt(CN)4]2− and [Au(CN)4]− units bridged by potassium cations, with extremely short PtII···AuIII distances of 3.0024(4) and 3.0057(4) Å at 80 K. The chloride ions and water molecules are bound to the potassium cation. Addition of KCl to the reaction mixture is necessary for the metallophilic PtII···AuIII chain to assemble. The isostructural [Pd(CN)4]2− and [Ni(CN)4]2− analogs can be rationally synthesized in a similar manner, which feature the first PdII···AuIII and NiII···AuIII interactions, with the NiII analog having the shortest metal‐metal distances of 2.968(3) and 2.978(3) Å. The bromide versions of all compounds were also synthesized by substituting KBr for KCl in the reaction mixture. 195Pt solid‐state NMR of the title compound revealed highly anisotropic, axially symmetric magnetic shielding which resembles that found in systems with PtII···PtII interactions, such as K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O. The PdII and PtII analogs are emissive, with λ max of 555 and 570 nm. Theoretical calculations indicate that the positive charge of the K+ ions effectively neutralizes the electrostatic repulsion between the cyanometallates, resulting in significant metal‐based donor‐acceptor interactions. These novel d 8···d 8 interactions provide a conceptual framework for the rational design of future metallophilic compounds.Keywords: donor‐acceptor, heterobimetallic, luminescence, metallophilic interactions, solid‐state 195Pt NMR

Introduction

Metallophilicity was first discovered in 1988 by Schmidbaur, who reported an interaction between two AuI atoms with a distance of 3.033 Å, described as an aurophilic interaction, or “Aurophilicity” [1]. Since then, numerous homometallic Au(I) interactions have been identified in many compounds using various AuI‐containing building blocks [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. Similarly, homometallic d 8 PtII compounds are considered to have platinophilic interactions when the Pt···Pt distance is within 3.6 Å [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Metallophilic interactions most commonly occur between d 10 or d 8 metals, such as PtII and AuI, but can occur between other metals as well [16, 17, 18, 19]. They are non‐covalent van der Waals‐type forces with similar strengths to hydrogen bonding (20–50 kJ mol−1) [20, 21].

Traditionally, metallophilic interactions are identified by the M···M distance from an X‐ray crystal structure being less than the sum of the van der Waals radii (for AuI, sum of vdW radii = 3.4 Å) [22]. They may also be established through Raman spectroscopy [23, 24, 25], nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) [26], and supported through density functional theory (DFT) calculations, where the energies of the interactions can be probed.

Such interactions can be a useful tool in supramolecular chemistry and crystal engineering to rationally design functional materials with increased dimensionality [17]. Metallophilic interactions can also lead to interesting properties, such as catalysis [27], conductivity [28, 29], and most notably luminescence [30, 31, 32, 33, 34, 35]. For example, Ba[Pt(CN)4]·4H2O features a Pt···Pt interaction of 3.321 Å and is strongly fluorescent, with a quantum yield of 65% [36]. The luminescence is often sensitive to changes in the M···M distance, which can be harnessed for sensing and stimuli‐responsive applications [37, 38, 39, 40, 41].

While less common, metallophilic interactions can also occur in heterobimetallic complexes between d 8···d 10 metals, for example PtII···AuI, such as in [Pt(terpyridine)Cl][Au(CN)2] and {[Eu(4,4’‐bipyridine N,N’‐dioxide)(H2O)2][Pt(CN)4]}·[Au(CN)2]·H2O (PtII···AuI distance = 3.349 and 3.528 Å, respectively) [42, 43].

In contrast, aurophilicity between d 8 AuIII metal centers is significantly understudied. We recently demonstrated how rare AuIII···AuIII interactions can be induced with complementary packing through careful selection of the cation shape and size [44, 45]; the shortest reported AuIII metallophilic distance is 3.3351(3) Å in [Sc(2,2’‐bipyridine‐N,N’‐dioxide)4][Au(CN)4]3·H2O.

While homometallic d 8···d 8 AuIII systems are quite rare, the analogous heterobimetallic d 8···d 8 PtII···AuIII compounds are unknown, having only been speculated to exist through calculations [46]. With this in mind, we considered the possibility of synthesizing materials with PtII···AuIII metallophilic interactions by combining the simple cyanometallate PtII and AuIII building blocks K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O and K[Au(CN)4]. This approach differs significantly from the computationally proposed systems, as they consist of complementary cation‐anion pairs that have an initial attractive electrostatic interaction between them. However, for a metallophilic interaction to form between two anions, such as [Pt(CN)4]2− and [Au(CN)4]−, the inherent Coulombic repulsion must be overcome. Previously, this has been achieved with additional support through contact with cations, such as using hydrogen bonding to delocalize the charge and reduce the Coulombic repulsion between the anions [3, 47].

Herein, we report the synthesis and characterization of the first MII···AuIII (MII = Ni, Pd, Pt) metallophilic interactions with very short distances. DFT calculations reveal the interaction is donor/acceptor in nature, with bridging potassium cations supporting the formation of a one‐dimensional (1D) chain with very short MII···AuIII interactions.

Results and Discussion

When equivalent amounts of K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O and K[Au(CN)4] were combined in water, the resulting solution was clear and colorless. Both starting materials are white powders, but when the solution evaporated to dryness, orange rod‐shaped crystals were produced, which were determined to correspond to the structure of K4[Pt(CN)4][Au(CN)4]Cl·6H2O (PtAuCl) by X‐ray diffraction (Figure 1). Additionally, when powders of the initial potassium cyanometallates were mixed, adding a few drops of water gave an orange powder of the same structure (Video S1). The source of the chloride unit was determined to be an impurity in the K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O starting material by confirmation with X‐ray fluorescence (XRF), which indicated the presence of a 4%–6% chloride impurity; this is likely to be residual KCl from the commercial synthesis of potassium tetracyanoplatinate, which uses K2[PtCl4] as the PtII starting material. Note that use of impurity‐free K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O only yielded crystals of the two starting materials upon solvent evaporation, but a high yield of PtAuCl could be obtained rationally by the addition of one equivalent of KCl along with the two cyanometallate salts in aqueous solution. Adding an excess of KCl still produced the same PtAuCl product.

Reaction scheme for the synthesis of K4[MII(CN)4][Au(CN)4]X·6H2O compounds (MII = Pt, Pd, Ni; X = Cl, Br). Orange crystals of K4[Pt(CN)4][Au(CN)4]Cl·6H2O (PtAuCl) (right) grow when the solution reaches near dryness.

The crystal structure consists of an infinite 1D chain of alternating units of [Pt(CN)4]2− and [Au(CN)4]− with extremely short PtII···AuIII distances of 3.0057(4) and 3.0024(4) Å at 80 K (Figure 2, Table S1) (sum of the vdW radii = 3.41 Å). XRF analysis confirmed both PtII and AuIII were present in a 1:1 ratio. The 1D chain is supported by K+ centers that are bound to the N‐termini of the cyano groups from both [PtII(CN)4]2− and [AuIII(CN)4]− units, bridging the two anions. Aqua ligands are bound to the K+ centers and interact with the cyanometallates through hydrogen bonding interactions (2.40(6)–2.46(5) Å) with the N‐cyano units. Additionally, the chloride coordinates to the potassium cation, protruding from the layer of cations and also forms hydrogen bonds with the bound water molecules, with a distance of 2.50(6) Å (Figures 3 and 4). The potassium and chloride ions have coordination numbers of 8 and 4, respectively. The chloride ions and H2O units do not interact directly with the PtII···AuIII 1D chain. PtAuCl crystallizes in a similar crystal type to K2[Pt(CN)4]X0.3·3H2O (X = Cl, Br) with identical atom coordinates and very similar unit cell dimensions [48, 49].

The thermogravimetric analysis of PtAuCl shows an initial 5% mass loss between 60°C and 80°C, consistent with the loss of approximately six water molecules. The compound is then thermally stable until its decomposition at 300°C, which occurs via cyanide loss, likely in the form of cyanogen (Figure S1) [50]. When the material was partially dehydrated, the characteristic orange color changed to white, and all crystallinity was lost, resulting in an amorphous powder. When this partially dehydrated material was exposed to moisture, the powder returned to its original orange color, indicating the sample can be readily rehydrated.

Solid‐state 195Pt nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy of PtAuCl is shown in Figure 5. The spectrum shows highly anisotropic chemical shielding with nearly axial symmetry, as previously observed for square‐planar [Pt(CN)4]2− [51] and other homoleptic square‐planar Pt complexes [52]. The best fit to the experimental powder pattern yields an overall shielding span, Ω, of 2820(25) ppm and a skew, κ, of −0.97(2) (Table 1). This high degree of symmetry at the Pt center reflects a local C4 rotation axis, with the most shielded components lying in the plane of the [Pt(CN)4]2− group, and the least shielded component directed toward the bonded Au. These parameters correspond to shielding tensor principal components of δ 11 = −2950 ppm, δ 22 = −5730 ppm, and δ 33 = −5770 ppm, and are similar to those measured for K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O, (δ11 = −3030 ppm, δ22 = −5710 ppm and δ33 = −5780 ppm), which features a long Pt···Pt interaction of 3.478(1) Å (Table 1, Figures S2 and S3). They are less similar to those of [nBu4N]2[Pt(CN)4], which lacks a Pt···Pt interaction (δ 11 = −2955 ppm, δ 22 = −5540 ppm, and δ 33 = −5605 ppm). Although the experimental uncertainty is sizeable, the largest shielding differences between systems with and without a metal···metal interaction occur in the δ 22 and δ 33 components, which lie in the [Pt(CN)4]2− plane.

Since the PtII···AuIII distance is very short, we wanted to probe it by substituting [Pt(CN)4]2− with the lighter, isostructural [Pd(CN)4]2− and [Ni(CN)4]2− building blocks. Stoichiometric amounts of K2[MII(CN)4] (MII = Pd, Ni), K[Au(CN)4] and KCl were combined in an aqueous solution. When the solvent was evaporated to near dryness, white and yellow rod‐shaped crystals grew from the PdII and NiII reactions, respectively. The structures of the crystals were revealed to be K4[Pd(CN)4][Au(CN)4]Cl·6H2O (PdAuCl) and K4[Ni(CN)4][Au(CN)4]Cl·6H2O (NiAuCl), both being isostructural to PtAuCl (Figure 6, Tables S2 and S3). When the synthesis was performed in the absence of KCl, crystals of the starting materials were produced instead.

PdAuCl and NiAuCl feature the first PdII···AuIII and NiII···AuIII metallophilic interactions, with very short distances of 3.004(2) and 3.0009(2) Å in PdAuCl and 2.968(3) and 2.978(3) Å in NiAuCl at 80 K (sum of vdW radii for both is 3.29 Å). All structures exhibit the same packing motif, featuring the 1D chain of MII···AuIII interactions supported through the potassium and water scaffolding (Figure 4). Hydrogen bonding is also prevalent in both structures, with the water molecules hydrogen bonding with the chloride ions (2.63(9) and 2.57(11) Å in PdAuCl and NiAuCl) and the N‐cyano units (2.21(11) and 2.39(3) Å for PdAuCl, 2.05(18) and 2.42(12) Å for NiAuCl). There are no close Cl···Cl interactions in any of the three structures.

The bromide analogs of all three compounds can be synthesized by replacing KCl with KBr. Thus, when KBr was added to an aqueous solution of pure (Cl‐free) K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O (confirmed by XRF) and K[Au(CN)4], dark orange rod‐shaped crystals formed when the solution approached near‐dryness. The crystals were isostructural to PtAuCl, with the chloride ion being replaced by a bromide ion to give brown‐orange crystals of K4[Pt(CN)4][Au(CN)4]Br·6H2O (PtAuBr) (Table S4).

K2[Pd(CN)4] and K2[Ni(CN)4] also crystallize with KBr and K[Au(CN)4] to give isostructural compounds of K4[Pd(CN)4][Au(CN)4]Br·6H2O (PdAuBr) and K4[Ni(CN)4][Au(CN)4]Br·6H2O (NiAuBr) (Tables S5 and S6). The MII···AuIII distances increase slightly in the bromide analogs, most likely as a result of the increase in ionic size from Cl to Br. There is no visible color change between the Cl and Br analogs for the PdII or NiII compounds.

The orange color of PtAuCl is consistent with a metal‐metal charge‐transfer (MMCT) band between the MII and the AuIII centers. Both starting materials, K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O and K[Au(CN)4], are white powders and therefore do not individually contribute to the color of the final product. The strong color arises from the charge‐transfer band, which is facilitated by the exceptionally short PtII···AuIII distance. The reflectance spectrum shows a maximum at λ = 610 nm, confirming the observed orange color (Figure S4). PtAuBr is also orange but appears slightly darker, with a brownish‐orange hue. Its reflectance maximum at 601 nm (Figure S4) confirms this and shows a small 9 nm shift in the reflectance maximum relative to PtAuCl. Interestingly, both compounds are also thermochromic, and the reflectance maxima of both shift to 660 nm when cooled to 77 K, with both compounds visually having a deep red‐orange color. Crystals of NiAuCl and NiAuBr also exhibit a yellow‐orange color, likely influenced by the very short NiII···AuIII distances.

All compounds display a linear correlation between the average MII···AuIII distance and temperature (Figure S5). The MII···AuIII distances expand at a rate of 1.5%–1.9% between 80 and 298 K, with the Pt compounds having the smallest expansion rate, and the Pd compounds having the largest. The coefficient of thermal expansion along the metallophilic interaction direction is 7.21, 7.43, and 8.56 ppm K−1 for PtAuCl, NiAuCl, and PdAuCl, respectively. The bromide compounds displayed similar thermal expansion coefficients. When comparing the chloride and bromide analogs for each metal, the Pt and Ni compounds display ∼0.5% expansion, whereas the Pd compounds show the smallest amount of change when increasing from Cl to Br with ∼0.29%.

When the MII···AuIII interaction distances are compared across all six compounds and four temperature points (80, 100, 150, and 298 K), no consistent trend is observed between the PtII or PdII analogs. In most cases, the distances follow the order NiAuCl < PdAuCl < PtAuCl; however, at several temperature points, the PdII analog exhibits a longer interaction distance compared to the corresponding PtII compound (Figure S6, Tables S7 and S8). The ionic radii of Ni2+, Pd2+, and Pt2+ in four‐coordinate, square planar environments are 0.49, 0.64, and 0.60 Å, respectively, which may suggest that Pd2+ would display a longer MII···AuIII interaction compared to Pt2+ [53]. However, the similarity in size of Pd2+ and Pt2+ may account for the observed variability and lack of a clear, systematic trend.

The infrared spectra of all compounds are almost identical, showing ν C≡N stretches from both [Au(CN)4]− and [MII(CN)4]2− between 2120 and 2185 cm−1 (Figures S7 and S8). The single ν C≡N band associated with [Au(CN)4]− is extremely weak, appearing at 2187–2191 cm−1, consistent with K[Au(CN)4] (ν C≡N = 2190 cm−1) and other tetracyanoaurate(III)‐based materials [54, 55]. A second, much stronger single band at 2125–2140 cm−1 corresponds to the cyanide stretch of the second cyanometallate unit, [MII(CN)4]2− (MII = Ni, Pd, Pt). The band is almost identical in frequency to the cyanide stretch in the K2[MII(CN)4] salts (ν C≡N = 2123 and 2135 cm−1 for Pt, 2135 cm−1 for Pd, and 2120 and 2126 cm−1 for Ni). No major differences are observed between chloride and bromide analogs of the same cyanometallate (e.g., NiAuCl vs. NiAuBr), supporting the crystallographic conclusion that the halide does not interact with the cyanide groups and therefore does not influence the ν C≡N stretching frequencies.

As was noted for the infrared spectra, the Raman spectra of all reported compounds are very similar and exhibit ν C≡N stretching bands for both [MII(CN)4]2− (MII = Ni, Pd, Pt) at 2135–2169 cm−1 and [Au(CN)4]− units at 2204–2209 cm−1 (Figures S9 and S10). The AuIII‐based peaks are significantly stronger in the Raman spectra than in the IR and are stronger than most of the [MII(CN)4]2− groups as well. All observed stretching frequencies are consistent with the corresponding potassium cyanometallates.

PdAuCl, PdAuBr, PtAuCl, and PtAuBr are all emissive, with emission wavelengths varying between 550 and 650 nm (Figure 7 and Figure S11, Table S9). Luminescence measurements were collected at room temperature and at 77 K to examine how the MII···AuIII distance influences the photophysical properties. NiAuCl and NiAuBr were found to be non‐emissive at both temperature points. PdAuCl and PdAuBr emit a light yellow color when excited with broadband UV light. At room temperature, they exhibit nearly identical emission profiles, with maxima at 555 and 560 nm, respectively (Figure S11). Upon cooling, both compounds display slightly red‐shifted emission maxima at 570 and 580 nm. At lower temperatures, the PdII···AuIII distances are shorter and may be responsible for the red‐shifted emission, although the shift is small, consistent with the small thermal expansion coefficient mentioned above (8.56 ppm K−1). PdAuCl and PdAuBr have phosphorescence lifetimes of 2.3(0.06) and 2.4(0.07) µS, respectively (Table S10). This is slightly faster than the K2[Pd(CN)4] starting material, which has a lifetime of 8.8(0.5) µS.

Emission spectra of (a) PtAuCl and (b) PtAuBr measured at 298 K (black) and 77 K (red). The excitation wavelength for all measurements was 455 nm.

PtAuCl and PtAuBr have emission maxima of 570 nm for both compounds at room temperature when excited with 455 nm light. The asymmetric, broad profile of the emission possibly suggests a second unresolved emission band. Both compounds are weakly emissive, with a quantum yield of 4% for PtAuCl. At 77 K, the emission peaks shift very slightly to 568 and 569 nm, respectively, indicating the overall emission wavelengths do not change significantly with temperature (Figure 7). At 77 K, a second emission peak appears at 650 nm (λ ex = 550 and 530 nm for PtAuCl and PtAuBr, respectively) (Figure S12), which could be attributed to a PtII based d–d (metal‐centered) transition that commonly occurs in other PtII‐based materials between 650 and 750 nm at lower temperatures [56, 57]. PtAuCl and PtAuBr also decay with phosphorescence lifetimes of 7.5(0.9) and 6.6 (0.01) µS, respectively (Table S10), which are significantly longer than K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O, which has a measured lifetime of 0.8(0.01) µS, consistent with reported values [58].

Additionally, PtAuCl and PtAuBr exhibit pleochroism when examined under polarized light. The planar geometry of both [Pt(CN)4]2− and [Au(CN)4]− anions, and the highly ordered 1D‐chain in the resulting compounds, can produce strong anisotropic optical properties [59]. The single crystals display an orange color when viewed under unpolarized light, but under plane‐polarized light, the crystals change to a yellow color upon rotation by 90°. The crystals can be classified as dichroic, which would be expected for tetragonal crystals (Figure S13). All crystallographic faces appear to exhibit the dichroic behavior equally.

Theoretical calculations were conducted on a simplified dimeric model system of PtAuCl, both in the presence and absence of K+ ions, based on the original crystal structure geometry (Figure S14).

The primary K+ containing potential energy surface (PES) model corresponds to the formally cationic cluster [K4{Pt(CN)4}{Au(CN)4}]+ (Figure S14), which was selected to preserve the local crystallographic arrangement (and symmetry) around the PtII···AuIII contact. Additional models containing two K+ ions and a formally neutral three K+ system were also examined. This provided an opportunity to further investigate the role of the bridging K+ ions in mediating the MII···AuIII interactions.

The PES was mapped by systematically varying the PtII···AuIII distance within the dimer composed of [Au(CN)4]− and [Pt(CN)4]2− units (for experimental and model details, see Supporting Information). In the absence of added K+ ions, no energy minimum was observed along the PES. However, with the inclusion of K+ ions in the model system, an energy minimum of approximately 14 kcal mol−1 was found at a PtII···AuIII separation of ∼3.1 Å (Figure 8A).

Additional models containing two K+ and a formally neutral three K+ system also exhibit minima near ∼3.2 Å, although with smaller stabilization energies of ∼6 and ∼9.5 kcal mol−1, respectively (Figures S15 and S16). These results indicate that progressive electrostatic compensation by the K+ ions is a significant factor enabling access to the experimentally observed short‐contact geometry.

A similar PES minimum was observed for a tetrameric arrangement (Figures S17 and S18). The PdII···AuIII and NiII···AuIII systems afforded analogous results, although with slightly lower stabilization energies on the PES (Figure S19).

To further investigate the effect of K+ ions, an electrostatic surface potential (ESP) analysis was conducted on the dimer (Figure S20). Notably, the inclusion of K+ ions led to a minor charge separation within the dimer, with a partial negative charge on the Pt unit, and a partial positive charge on the Au unit.

Having established the role of K+ cations, we proceeded to examine the orbital interactions between the two metal centers to gain insight into the nature of the metallophilic stabilization. Using a dimer unit from the experimental solid‐state structure, Second‐Order Perturbation Theory (SOPT) from Natural Bond Order (NBO) analysis [60, 61] revealed significant donor‐acceptor interactions [62, 63, 64].

For the PtAuCl system, we analyzed the stabilization energy (E (2)) between different donating (filled) and accepting (empty) orbitals associated with the PtII···AuIII interaction. This analysis predicts a strong and highly polarized donor–acceptor interaction dominated by σ‐donation from the filled dz2 orbital of Pt into the empty p z orbital of Au (E (2) = 54.34 kcal mol− 1), accompanied by a much weaker reverse donation from Au to Pt (7.84 kcal mol− 1) (Figure 8B, Table 2, Figure S21). This donor‐acceptor interaction reflects efficient orbital overlap enabled by the radially expanded Pt 5d orbitals, resulting in significant metal–metal interaction.

For PdAuCl, both the magnitude and asymmetry of the donor–acceptor interactions are reduced. The dz2(Pd) → p z(Au) donation decreases to 14.04 kcal mol− 1, while the reverse interaction remains modest (6.09 kcal mol− 1), consistent with the less diffuse nature of Pd 4d orbitals and weaker orbital overlap with Au.

In the NiAuCl system, the Ni dz2 → Au p z donation is the weakest in the series (3.67 kcal mol− 1), while the Au → Ni interaction (6.68 kcal mol− 1) is larger, indicating a weak and slightly polarized AuIII → NiII donor–acceptor interaction. This reflects the limited radial extension and poorer energy matching of Ni 3d orbitals.

Additional SOPT calculations performed at a compressed MII···AuIII separation of 2.8 Å show substantially larger donor–acceptor stabilization energies due to increased orbital overlap (Table S11), although these larger SOPT terms do not correspond to greater overall thermodynamic stability because electrostatic and Pauli repulsion also increase at compressed separations. Furthermore, analogous SOPT calculations performed using PtAuCl models containing varying numbers of K+ ions show only minimal changes in the donor–acceptor stabilization energies at fixed PtII···AuIII separation (Table S12), supporting the conclusion that the primary role of the K+ ions is electrostatic, enabling sufficiently close approach of the anionic cyanometallate units for donor–acceptor orbital interactions to occur.

Thus, to summarize, these metallophilic interactions can be described as donor‐acceptor in nature, in which, for example, electron density is donated from the PtII center (donor) to the electron‐poor AuIII (acceptor), stabilizing the interaction between the anionic units [65, 66, 67, 68, 69]. The bridging K+ ions play a key role in reducing electrostatic repulsion between the anionic cyanometallate units, thereby enabling a sufficiently close MII···AuIII approach for significant donor–acceptor orbital interactions to emerge. These donor–acceptor interactions further stabilize the experimentally observed short‐contact motif and give rise to the metal–metal charge‐transfer band responsible for the color of the material and the formation of the linear 1D chain.

Conclusion

We have synthesized materials containing the first PtII···AuIII metallophilic interaction via slow evaporation of an aqueous solution containing K2[Pt(CN)4]·H2O, K[Au(CN)4], and KX (X = Cl, Br). The resulting orange crystals were structurally characterized as K4[Pt(CN)4][Au(CN)4]X·6H2O, revealing a 1D chain of alternating [Pt(CN)4]2− and [Au(CN)4]− units with an exceptionally short Pt···Au distance of ∼3 Å. The isostructural [Pd(CN)4]2− and [Ni(CN)4]2− analogs were also successfully prepared, each exhibiting comparably short MII···AuIII distances. The PdII and PtII structures exhibit luminescent properties, consistent with previously reported systems featuring metallophilic interactions. Solid‐state 195Pt NMR spectroscopy provides further support for a metallophilic interaction. Computational studies predict a stabilizing interaction between the PtII···AuIII units at a separation of 3.1 Å, with complementary NBO analyses indicating a significant donor/acceptor interaction between the d 8 metal centers. Collectively, this series of compounds represents a new class of heterobimetallic d 8···d 8 interactions, providing fundamental insight into the factors governing short M···M interactions and offering a framework for the rational design of future metallophilic compounds.
