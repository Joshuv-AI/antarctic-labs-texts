Association of the ratio of non-HDL-cholesterol to HDL-cholesterol with clinical and pathological characteristics in diabetic kidney disease
Authors: Liang Wu, Dong-Yuan Chang, Ming-Hui Zhao, Sydney C.W. Tang, Min Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionDiabetic kidney disease (DKD) is a severe complication of diabetes (DM), and dyslipidemia has a vital role in its progression. It is necessary to identify lipid-related biomarkers related to disease severity and prognosis in DKD to facilitate early risk stratification and prevent progression to advanced renal disease. The non-high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (non-HDL-C) to HDL-C ratio (NHHR), an emerging composite lipid metric, has demonstrated potential in assessing metabolic and cardiovascular risks. This investigation was designed to examine the link between NHHR and clinicopathological characteristics, as well as renal outcomes in individuals with DKD and T2DM.MethodsA total of 521 individuals with DKD and T2DM were retrospectively enrolled. Demographic, laboratory, and renal outcomes data were collected. Renal histopathology was assessed in 208 individuals with biopsy-proven DKD. Linear, ordinal logistic and Cox regression analyses were utilized to assess the link between NHHR and clinicopathological parameters and the risk of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD), respectively. Nested models were utilized to compare the prognostic value of NHHR against conventional lipid parameters.ResultsHigher NHHR levels were significantly associated with poorer glycemic control, advanced glomerular lesions, and a higher risk of progressing to ESKD. Multivariable Cox regression analysis considered NHHR as an independent indicator of progressing to ESKD after the adjustment for conventional risk factors. Furthermore, the nested model analysis demonstrated better prognostic value of NHHR compared with conventional lipid parameters. Subgroup analysis illustrated that high NHHR was significantly related to high ESKD risk across subgroups, such as varied ages, sexes, BMI classifications, and in patients with established renal dysfunction or albuminuria.ConclusionNHHR could act as a potential biomarker for risk stratification in DKD and predicting renal outcomes, meriting prospective validation for clinical implementation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12882-026-05162-y.

Introduction

Diabetic kidney disease (DKD), affecting about 40% of diabetes mellitus (DM) patients, is a devastating complication of DM [1]. Globally, DKD has become the main cause of chronic (CKD) and end-stage kidney diseases (ESKD), accounting for nearly half of all ESKD cases in many developed countries [2]. In China, DKD has emerged as the primary cause of CKD in urban areas, accompanied by a steady rise in the proportion of patients initiating renal replacement therapy attributable to DKD [3, 4]. Once ESKD develops, patients require lifelong renal replacement therapy, which carries substantial healthcare costs and reduced life expectancy. The clinical impact of DKD extends beyond progressive kidney function loss. Patients face substantially increased risks of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, with cardiovascular events accounting for approximately 50% of deaths in this population [5].

The pathogenesis of DKD is multifactorial and complex, involving intricate interactions between metabolic, hemodynamic, inflammatory, and fibrotic pathways. Beyond traditional risk factors (e.g., hyperglycemia and hypertension), increasing evidence underlined the vital function of dyslipidemia in the occurrence and progression of DKD [6]. Qualitative and quantitative lipid abnormalities (e.g., high triglycerides (TG), low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and altered lipoprotein subfractions) contribute to kidney injury by exacerbating oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction [7]. The accumulation of lipid droplets in renal cells, a process known as renal lipotoxicity, promotes podocyte apoptosis, mesangial cell proliferation, and tubular interstitial fibrosis, thereby accelerating the decline in kidney function [8]. Although conventional lipid parameters are associated with the risk of DKD development, their predictive value for disease progression is limited [9, 10]. This is likely because the overall impact on the kidney is determined by the integrated effects of multiple lipid pathways, rather than by any single lipid fraction [8].

To address this limitation, the non-HDL-C to HDL-C ratio (NHHR) is considered an innovative composite biomarker [11, 12]. NHHR integrates information on atherogenic non-HDL-C, comprising LDL-C, very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) remnants, and lipoproteins, and anti-atherogenic HDL-C, offering a highly comprehensive assessment of lipid metabolism than individual parameters alone [13–15]. Recent studies demonstrated that NHHR was a robust predictor of metabolic disorders (e.g., insulin resistance, DM, and cardiovascular diseases) [16, 17]. Notably, NHHR showed a stronger association with the risk of DM than conventional lipid parameters [18]. In prediabetic populations, elevated NHHR was associated with the progression to DM, partly mediated by obesity [19]. A cross-sectional study illustrated a non-linear link between NHHR and DKD risk [11]. These findings suggested that NHHR might hold great promise as a biomarker for metabolic dysregulation and renal injuries.

However, existing studies on NHHR in DKD are limited to cross-sectional designs and cannot establish its prognostic significance. Hence, this investigation aimed to evaluate the link between NHHR and clinicopathological features as well as renal outcomes in individuals with DKD.

Methods

Recruitment of patients

Herein, individuals with DKD and type 2 DM (T2DM) were enrolled from two sources: the multicenter Cohort Study of Chronic Kidney Disease (C-STRIDE) [20] and the institutional patient registry of Peking University First Hospital’s electronic medical records (EMR), covering the period from 2009 to 2019. DM was identified as at least one of these: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or the administration of glucose-lowering drugs [21]. For asymptomatic patients, the diagnosis of DM requires conducting tests at two or three different time points with results meeting the diagnostic thresholds, as recommended by clinical guidelines. Cases suggestive of type 1 or other specific types of DM were excluded. DKD was identified as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or a urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratio (uACR) ≥ 30 mg/g, documented for a duration of > 3 months in individuals with DM, with exclusion of other known kidney diseases, such as membranous nephropathy, lupus nephritis, IgA nephropathy, hypertensive nephrosclerosis, and uric acid-related nephropathy [22]. We excluded individuals with baseline eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m². The C-STRIDE cohort is a multicenter observational study of chronic kidney disease designed to reflect real-world clinical practice, and it did not mandate renal biopsy for enrolled participants. The EMR cohort consisted exclusively of patients with biopsy-proven DKD from our hospital. In these patients, renal biopsy was performed based on: (1) rapid decline in renal function (e.g., annual eGFR decline > 5 mL/min/1.73 m²); (2) active urinary sediment (e.g., microscopic hematuria, leukocyturia); (3) heavy proteinuria (uACR ≥ 3000 mg/g) or nephrotic-range proteinuria; (4) absence of diabetic retinopathy or short diabetes duration (< 5 years) with significant kidney damage; (5) clinical suspicion of concurrent non-diabetic glomerular disease. Figure 1 illustrates the participant selection process through a flow diagram. Ultimately, 521 eligible individuals participated in the investigation, among whom 208 (39.9%) received a renal biopsy and had biopsy-proven DKD. The investigation was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Peking University First Hospital. The C-STRIDE cohort study was approved under approval number 2011[363], and the use of EMR data was approved under approval number 2017 − 1280. The biological sample donors signed informed consent forms, agreeing that the donated samples and related information could be used for all medical research. Given the retrospective, observational design of this analysis, which used only anonymized clinical data without any intervention or re-contact with patients, the Institutional Review Board of Peking University First Hospital granted a waiver of informed consent and conducted a new ethics review specifically for this study, with approval number 2025R0541.

Data collection

The collection of demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters was conducted at baseline, comprising sex, body mass index (BMI), uACR, serum creatinine (Scr), eGFR, age, hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), serum uric acid (SUA), duration of DM, blood pressure, and lipid profiles (total cholesterol [TC], LDL-C, TG, and HDL-C). The NHHR was assessed as: NHHR = (TC-HDL-C)/HDL-C [23–25]. Baseline medication use was recorded, including lipid-lowering agents (statins and fibrates) and renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) inhibitors (ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers). ESKD was defined as a persistent requirement for renal transplantation, hemodialysis/peritoneal dialysis for > 3 months, or death resulting from uremia [26, 27]. Patients who reached ESKD during follow-up were defined as progressors, while those who did not were defined as non-progressors.

Kidney histopathology

Kidney samples were collected via percutaneous needle biopsy and processed using standard methods (immunofluorescence and light and electron microscopy). Histological evaluation was performed independently by two experienced pathologists blinded to clinical data and group allocation. Glomerular lesions were graded I-IV by intensity and distribution as per the Renal Pathology Society (RPS) categorization [28]. Glomerulosclerosis was quantified as the percentage of globally or segmentally sclerotic glomeruli [29]. Subendothelial spacing was graded by the existence of widening (0–1) [30], and mesangiolysis/microaneurysm was scored by mesangial matrix dissolution and tuft dilation from capillary destabilization (0–1) [28, 30].

Tubulointerstitial injury was scored by IFTA (interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy) based on the proportion of affected interstitium/tubules (0 = none; 1 = < 25%; 2 = 25%-50%; 3 = > 50%). Interstitial inflammation was graded semi-quantitatively (0 = none; 1 = IFTA-related infiltration only; 2 = infiltration in non-IFTA areas) [28]. Vascular lesions were scored by arteriolar hyalinosis (0–2) and arteriosclerosis (0–2) [28].

Statistical analyses

Continuous data were represented as mean ± SD (normal distribution) or median (interquartile range (IQR); abnormal distribution). Categorical variables were represented as counts and percentages. Between-group comparisons used an unpaired Student’s t-test (continuous) or Chi-square test (categorical). Associations between NHHR and clinicopathological parameters were assessed using linear regression for continuous variables and ordinal logistic regression for ordinal variables (e.g., RPS classification). Unstandardized coefficients (β) and odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported accordingly. Lipid parameters were evaluated for their association with the kidney endpoint using univariable and multivariable Cox regression, with hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals; both the global test and individual covariate tests were performed, and no violation was detected. To account for competing risk of non-uremic death, Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models were performed as a sensitivity analysis for the primary outcome of ESKD. Missing data were observed only for HbA1c and uACR (eight patients missing both measurements, one additional patient missing uACR only, accounting for less than 2% of the total sample). Complete-case analysis was therefore used for all multivariable models. Nested Cox proportional hazards models assessed the additive predictive value of lipid parameters beyond traditional ESKD predictors, with model improvement evaluated by Nagelkerke’s pseudo R², likelihood ratio tests, and Akaike information criterion (AIC). Stratification analyses examined NHHR-ESKD associations in subgroups by age (< 45, ≥ 45 years), BMI (< 25, ≥ 25 kg/m²), sex (female, male), HbA1c (< 5.7, 5.7–6.4, ≥ 6.5), uACR (< 30, 30–300, ≥ 300 mg/g), and eGFR (15–29, 30–59, 60–90, ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m²). Interaction tests evaluated subgroup heterogeneity (P < 0.05). All subgroup analyses were pre-specified as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. The results of these subgroup analyses should be interpreted with caution. Analyses were conducted via SPSS (24.0; IL, USA) and R (v4.3.2; Vienna, Austria). P < 0.05 was deemed significance.

Results

Features of individuals stratified by NHHR levels

Among the 521 individuals with DKD recruited in this investigation, 337 were men, and 184 were women, with an age of 55.98 ± 10.97 years at diagnosis. The distribution of baseline eGFR by CKD stage was as follows: stage G4 (eGFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73 m²) in 153 patients (29.4%), stage G3 (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73 m²) in 260 patients (49.9%), stage G2 (eGFR 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m²) in 71 patients (13.6%), and stage G1 (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m²) in 37 patients (7.1%). The median level of NHHR was 3.56 (IQR: 2.58–4.89). Based on this median value, individuals were categorized into high-NHHR (≥ 3.56) and low-NHHR (< 3.56) groups for comparative analysis. Table 1 illustrates that, compared to the low-NHHR group, the high-NHHR group was significantly younger (P = 0.003), had a higher percentage of males (P = 0.005), and exhibited higher BMI (P < 0.001), higher diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (P = 0.002), higher HbA1c (P = 0.016), as well as shorter DM duration (P = 0.013). No significant variation was detected in baseline uACR (P = 0.281) or eGFR (P = 0.115) between the two groups.

Among the 521 enrolled patients, 208 (39.9%) underwent renal biopsy. A comparison of baseline characteristics between patients with and without renal biopsy is presented in Supplementary Table 1. Compared with patients who did not undergo biopsy, those who underwent biopsy were younger (P < 0.001), had a higher proportion of males (P < 0.001), had a shorter diabetes duration (P < 0.001), and exhibited more severe disease manifestations, including higher uACR (P < 0.001) and higher eGFR (P < 0.001). NHHR levels were significantly higher in the biopsy group compared with the non-biopsy group (P = 0.003). These findings indicate that the biopsy subgroup represented a higher-risk population with more advanced DKD.

A total of 208 out of 521 patients received a renal biopsy. These 208 patients exhibited similar demographic patterns to the whole cohort (Table 2). Compared to individuals in the low-NHHR group, those in the high-NHHR group demonstrated significant higher levels of DBP (P = 0.006), BMI (P < 0.001), and HbA1c (P = 0.022). No significant variation in uACR (P = 0.180) or eGFR (P = 0.476) was detected between both groups. In renal histology, compared to the low-NHHR group, individuals in the high-NHHR group had more severe glomerular lesions, including higher proportion of advanced RPS classification (class III/IV: 70.80% [80] vs. 47.37% [45], P < 0.001) (Table 3). Nonetheless, no significant variation in tubulointerstitial injury (IFTA grades 2–3) was observed between these two groups of patients (53.98% [61] vs. 53.68% [51], P = 0.966).

Association of NHHR levels with clinicopathological parameters

We further evaluated the correlation between the NHHR level and clinicopathological parameters. NHHR level showed a positive correlation with HbA1c (β = 0.132, P = 0.008), BMI (β = 0.187, P < 0.001) and DBP (β = 0.181, P = 0.002). In addition, ordinal logistic regression showed that higher NHHR was significantly associated with more severe RPS classification (OR [95%CI]: 1.26 [1.10 to 1.45], P < 0.001). Nevertheless, there was no significant link between NHHR and baseline eGFR or uACR.

NHHR levels and renal outcomes

Throughout a median follow-up of 18.0 (IQR 10.0-34.5) months, 153 (29.37%) patients progressed to ESKD. A comparison of baseline characteristics between ESKD progressors and non-progressors is presented in Supplementary Table 2. Compared with non-progressors, progressors had numerically higher NHHR levels, although the difference did not reach statistical significance. They also had higher systolic blood pressure, more severe proteinuria, and higher LDL-C levels, confirming the high-risk nature of the cohort. Table 4 illustrates that univariable Cox regression analyses found that individual lipid components LDL-C, TG, TC, and NHHR, but not HDL-C, were significantly linked to the occurrence of ESKD. Multivariable Cox regression analysis showed that after adjusting for age, gender, DM duration, BMI, HbA1c, eGFR, uACR and SUA, LDL-C, TC and NHHR were independently linked to the occurrence of ESKD (HR [95% CI]: 1.31 [1.12 to 1.53], P = 0.001; HR [95% CI]: 1.19 [1.08 to 1.31], P < 0.001; and HR [95% CI]: 1.20 [1.09 to 1.32], P < 0.001, respectively). After further adjustment for RAAS inhibitors and lipid-lowering agents (statins and fibrates), NHHR remained significantly associated with ESKD (HR [95% CI]: 1.21 [1.10 to 1.34], P < 0.001), as shown in Model 2 of Table 4. The proportional hazards assumption was tested using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violation was detected; the detailed results are provided in Supplementary Table 3. In addition, restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed an approximately linear association between NHHR and ESKD risk (P for non-linearity = 0.482), supporting the validity of using NHHR as a continuous variable in our primary Cox regression analyses. To address potential heterogeneity between the two data sources, we performed multivariable Cox regression stratified by data source, presented in Supplementary Tables 4 and Supplementary Table 5. In the C-STRIDE cohort, NHHR remained significantly associated with ESKD after multivariable adjustment (HR [95% CI]: 1.19 [1.07 to 1.33], P = 0.002). Similarly, in the EMR cohort, the association was also significant (HR [95% CI]: 1.35 [1.05 to 1.74], P = 0.018). These results indicate that the association between NHHR and ESKD was consistent across both data sources. In sensitivity analyses accounting for competing risk of non-uremic death, Fine-Gray subdistribution hazard models confirmed the association between NHHR and ESKD (subdistribution HR [95% CI]: 1.16 [1.07 to 1.26], P < 0.001). Furthermore, the nested model analysis demonstrated better prognostic value of NHHR compared with conventional lipid parameters (Table 5). Specifically, NHHR showed the greatest model improvement (ΔR2 = + 0.0304, ΔAIC= -11.36) and highly significant likelihood ratio test results (P < 0.001).

Subgroup analyses

To examine the correlation between NHHR and renal outcomes, we stratified the patients depending on the basic features (Table 6). The correlation between NHHR levels and ESKD remained significant in different age groups (< 45 and ≥ 45 years), sexes (male and female), and BMI categories (< 25 and ≥ 25 kg/m²). Similarly, the effect persisted in individuals with eGFR 30–59 and 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m², and individuals with uACR 30–300 and ≥ 300 mg/g. However, the association was not significant in patients with HbA1c < 5.7% (P = 0.178), uACR < 30 mg/g (P = 0.245), eGFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73 m² (P = 0.529), or eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m² (P = 0.512). A significant interaction was observed between NHHR and eGFR subgroups (P for interaction = 0.006), indicating that the prognostic effect of NHHR on ESKD differed across eGFR strata. No significant interactions were observed for the remaining stratification variables (P for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

Epidemiological investigations have found a connection between dyslipidemia and the occurrence and development of DKD. Previous multinational cross-sectional and prospective cohort investigations have indicated that the development of DKD is linked to high TG levels and low HDL-C levels in individuals with T2DM [9, 31]. However, the association between conventional lipid parameters and DKD progression was inconsistent across major clinical cohorts [10]. This inconsistency likely stems from the fact that DKD risk is associated with a complex interplay of atherogenic and atheroprotective lipids, which cannot be fully captured by any parameter in isolation. NHHR, a composite parameter integrating atherogenic non-HDL-C and protective HDL-C, is considered a more effective indicator of metabolic and cardiovascular risks. However, the association between NHHR and clinicopathological characteristics as well as renal outcomes in DKD patients remains largely unexplored.

Our study demonstrated that DKD patients with elevated NHHR levels exhibited higher levels of BMI and poorer glycemic and blood pressure control. Importantly, patients with higher NHHR levels demonstrated more advanced glomerular lesions, evidenced by higher proportion of RPS class III-IV pathologies. These findings indicated that elevated NHHR level reflects more severe metabolic dysregulation and renal injuries. However, no significant correlation was detected between NHHR and baseline uACR or eGFR, which was in line with previous cross-sectional studies [32]. This discrepancy may be explained by several considerations. First, glomerular lesions represent cumulative structural damage resulting from chronic metabolic disturbances, including dyslipidemia. In contrast, eGFR and uACR are functional parameters that capture current kidney status and are readily influenced by short-term factors such as hemodynamic fluctuations, volume status, and acute interventions. Therefore, the degree of structural injury and functional impairment may not be concordant when assessed at a single time point. Second, NHHR may be closely associated with glomerular structural remodeling, including mesangial expansion, podocyte dysfunction, and K-W nodule formation, that typically precedes functional decline [33]. This temporal sequence may explain why NHHR correlates with structural lesions but not with baseline eGFR or uACR. Third, the relationship between NHHR and proteinuria is complex and potentially non-linear. While nephrotic-range proteinuria can trigger hepatic compensatory lipoprotein synthesis (which may elevate NHHR), this mechanism is most prominent in patients with massive proteinuria. In our cohort, patients exhibited a wide range of uACR values, and the cross-sectional correlation may have been attenuated by this biological complexity. Notably, the longitudinal analysis demonstrated a significant association between NHHR and ESKD even after adjusting for baseline uACR, supporting NHHR’s independent prognostic value.

Longitudinally, our study discovered that individuals with higher NHHR levels had a significantly increased ESKD risk, even after the adjustment for traditional risk factors, including gender, age, eGFR, uACR, serum uric acid, lipid-lowering medication, and RAAS inhibitors. In the stratified analyses, the association between NHHR and ESKD remained significant across various subgroups, including different ages, gender, and BMI categories. Notably, the prognostic value of NHHR was evident in patients with eGFR 30–59 and 60–89 mL/min/1.73 m², as well as in those with uACR 30–300 or ≥ 300 mg/g. However, no significant association was observed in patients with normoalbuminuria (uACR < 30 mg/g), preserved renal function (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min/1.73 m²), severe renal impairment (eGFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73 m²), or excellent glycemic control (HbA1c < 5.7%). We interpret these findings as reflecting a stage-dependent role of NHHR in DKD progression. In the early stages of DKD, characterized by normoalbuminuria or preserved eGFR, the intrinsic risk of progression is low, and the limited number of events may have constrained statistical power. Furthermore, other risk factors such as elevated blood pressure, suboptimal glycemic control, and exposure to nephrotoxic medications may contribute more substantially to kidney function deterioration than dyslipidemia in this subgroup, potentially overshadowing the prognostic value of NHHR. In patients with severe renal impairment (eGFR 15–29 mL/min/1.73 m²), the lack of significant association may be attributed to the overwhelming burden of uremic milieu and competing risks, which may overshadow the relative contribution of lipid abnormalities. Moreover, intensive glycemic management in patients with excellent glucose control may attenuate the impact of dyslipidemia on renal outcomes. By contrast, once kidney injury becomes clinically manifest, as evidenced by moderately reduced eGFR (30–89 mL/min/1.73 m²) or overt proteinuria, the prognostic value of NHHR emerges significantly, suggesting that dyslipidemia plays an increasingly important role as a risk marker for disease progression as DKD advances. These findings support the utility of NHHR as a risk stratification tool, particularly in patients with established DKD.

It is worth noting that our cohort included patients with baseline HbA1c below 6.5%, which may appear inconsistent with a diagnosis of diabetes. This reflects two clinically relevant scenarios. First, intensive glucose-lowering therapy prior to enrollment, resulting in HbA1c levels within the normal or near-normal range despite a clear history of diabetes. Second, diagnosis of diabetes based on fasting plasma glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or the presence of classic hyperglycemic symptoms rather than HbA1c. The presence of such patients underscores an important clinical insight, that is, DKD can develop and progress even in the setting of optimal glycemic control, highlighting the independent role of non-glucose factors, such as lipid dysregulation, in the pathogenesis of DKD. Pan et al. presented a significant link between NHHR level and high DKD risk in the American adult diabetic population in a cross-sectional investigation [11]. Our investigation further observed that NHHR level was associated with ESKD, suggesting its potential as a prognostic marker for kidney disease progression in patients with DKD; however, prospective validation is needed to confirm these findings. Furthermore, the nested model analyses demonstrated the relatively superior prognostic value of NHHR compared with conventional lipid parameters.

These clinical observations likely reflect the collective association of dyslipidemia with renal injury in DKD. A high NHHR reflects a pro-atherogenic imbalance (non-HDL-C predominance over HDL-C), which is closely linked to inflammation, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction. Oxidized LDL (oxLDL) induces podocyte damage and thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, and high albumin leakage [34]. The diminished function of HDL further limits renal cholesterol efflux, thereby aggravating lipid overload and inflammatory cytokine release, and causing microvascular impairment [35]. From a clinical perspective, NHHR offers several advantages over conventional lipid parameters. First, as a composite metric, it integrates both atherogenic and anti-atherogenic components, providing a more holistic assessment of lipid metabolism. Second, NHHR can be easily calculated from routine lipid profiles without additional cost, making it readily implementable in clinical practice. Third, its superior prognostic value for ESKD, as demonstrated in our nested model analyses, generates the hypothesis that NHHR could serve as a tool for identifying high-risk DKD patients. However, whether these patients would genuinely benefit from intensified lipid-lowering strategies strictly requires prospective interventional validation.

The key strength of this investigation is identifying NHHR as a novel biomarker that reflects the severity of glomerular lesions and predicts long-term renal prognosis in DKD patients. However, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design and the biopsy subgroup selected based on clinical indications may have enriched patients with more severe or atypical disease, limiting the generalizability of histopathological findings to the broader DKD population. This is further reflected by the high ESKD event rate despite a relatively short median follow-up, indicating that our cohort comprised a high-risk population with advanced DKD. Consequently, our findings may mainly apply to a relatively advanced and high-risk DKD population. The prognostic value of NHHR observed in this cohort may not be generalizable to patients with early-stage DKD or those with a lower intrinsic risk of rapid progression. Second, NHHR was measured only at baseline. Given that NHHR may change with disease progression, nutritional status, or lipid-lowering therapy, particularly in patients approaching ESKD, the prognostic value of a single measurement should be interpreted with caution. Third, the subgroup analyses were exploratory by design and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, and the sample size for histopathological analyses may have limited statistical power for detecting certain associations. Fourth, a significant imbalance in lipid-lowering agent use was observed between the low-NHHR and high-NHHR groups in the biopsy subgroup (12.63% vs. 1.77%, P = 0.002), which may have introduced potential confounding in the association between NHHR and RPS classification. This limitation should be considered when interpreting the histopathological findings. Furthermore, as only final consensus pathology reports were retained, formal inter-rater agreement statistics for histopathological grading could not be calculated, which should be considered when interpreting the reproducibility of the biopsy-based findings. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes, serial biomarker measurements, and diverse populations are warranted to validate our findings. Moreover, the concept of NHHR-guided interventions remains purely hypothesis-generating at this stage. Future prospective and interventional studies are required to investigate whether NHHR-guided interventions can attenuate renal lipid accumulation or inflammatory responses, which would further validate their clinical utility.

Conclusion

Our investigation demonstrates that NHHR is linked to adverse renal outcomes in DKD, independent of traditional risk factors. As a composite lipid parameter integrating both atherogenic and anti-atherogenic lipid profiles, NHHR represents a convenient and comprehensive risk marker that warrants further evaluation for potential clinical application. The hypothesis that NHHR may assist in identifying high-risk patients for intensified lipid management is strictly exploratory. Further mechanistic, prospective, and interventional investigations are essential to confirm these observational findings and determine if there is any true clinical utility in targeting NHHR for the treatment of DKD.
