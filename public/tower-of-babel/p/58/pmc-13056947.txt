Rational design and antibacterial assessment of novel pyranopyrazole derivatives: a combined experimental and in silico study
Authors: M M Abdelatty, A A Makhlouf, M E Moustapha, A M Yossef, Z R Farag
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPyranopyrazole derivatives were synthesized via multicomponent reactions and characterized using spectroscopic techniques including IR, 1H-NMR, 13C-NMR, and mass spectrometry. In vitro antibacterial evaluation revealed that compounds 2b, 3a, 5b, and 7b exhibited significant activity against Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis), while compounds 4b, 4d, 6b, and 8b demonstrated potent activity against Gram-negative bacteria (Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Escherichia coli). Quantum chemical calculations at the DFT/B3LYP/6-31G(d,p) level revealed HOMO–LUMO energy gaps ranging from 4.50 to 5.38 eV, with lower energy gaps correlating with enhanced antibacterial activity. Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) analysis identified key electron-rich and electron-deficient regions responsible for target recognition. In silico molecular docking studies against tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase, an essential bacterial enzyme, demonstrated strong binding affinities with binding energies ranging from −7.84 to −11.30 kcal/mol and calculated inhibition constants (Ki) of 5.23 to 1790 nM. Key interactions involved hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking with active site residues Tyr34, His48, Asp177, and Gln196. ADME property predictions indicated favorable drug-like characteristics with compliance to Lipinski’s Rule of Five and high gastrointestinal absorption. These outstanding results could be due to the presence of aromatic moiety, halogen atom and cyano-group which enhance lipophilicity and membrane permeability in addition to the synergistic effect of both pyran and pyrazole nucleus. These integrated findings establish pyranopyrazoles as promising scaffolds for antibacterial drug development.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-37625-5.Keywords: Pyranopyrazoles, Antibacterial agents, Molecular docking, Quantum chemical analysis, In silico studiesSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemistry, Drug discovery, Microbiology

Introduction

Excessive and improper use of antimicrobial drugs has played a role in the increase in resistance to antibiotics, highlighting the critical demand for novel antimicrobial agents to fight against newly arising drug-resistant bacteria1. Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) represents a major worldwide concern, resulting in 4.95 million fatalities linked to treatment-resistant infections in the year 2019 alone, among which 1.27 million cases were the direct result of AMR2,3. The development of antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and analgesic drugs relies heavily on heterocyclic chemistry4.

The pyranopyrazole nucleus is a key heterocyclic system in antimicrobial research due to its structural similarity to various essential biological building blocks5,6. Organic compounds containing pyran and pyrazole moieties are widely recognized as pharmacologically active scaffolds in medicinal chemistry, exhibiting significant biological and pharmacological properties, including anticancer activity7–10, antimicrobial11–13, and anticonvulsant14,15 activities. Studies have indicated that introducing the pyrazole core structure into organic molecules increases their ability to block protein glycation and imparts to them bacteria-fighting, fungus-inhibiting, cancer-combating, mood-enhancing, inflammation-reducing, tuberculosis-fighting, oxidation-preventing, and virus-inhibiting characteristics16,17.

In contemporary drug discovery, computational approaches have become indispensable tools for accelerating the identification and optimization of promising lead compounds. Molecular docking and molecular modeling techniques provide crucial insights into ligand-receptor interactions, binding modes, and structure–activity relationships, enabling rational design of antibacterial agents with enhanced potency and selectivity18–21. These in silico methods allow researchers to predict binding affinities, identify key interactions with bacterial target proteins, and rationalize observed biological activities before extensive experimental validation22,23. Furthermore, absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion (ADME) prediction has emerged as an essential early-stage filter in drug development, helping to identify compounds with favorable pharmacokinetic profiles and drug-like properties while flagging potential metabolic liabilities and toxicity concerns24,25. The integration of ADME profiling with molecular docking provides complementary information: docking predicts target engagement while ADME assessment determines whether a compound can reach and persist at the target site in vivo24–26. Recent studies on pyrazole and pyran-fused heterocycles have demonstrated the utility of this combined computational workflow, successfully nominating candidates that satisfy both binding affinity criteria and pharmacokinetic requirements for progression to experimental testing24–27.

Numerous naturally occurring pyrazoles exhibit diverse pharmacological and therapeutic activities, with the pyran ring serving as a fundamental structural unit28. Accordingly, building on our previous studies and continuing our efforts in the field of heterocyclic chemistry, we aim to further explore their potential29. This led to the synthesis of pyranopyrazole motifs 1a,b through a green multicomponent approach, exhibiting excellent antibacterial potency surpassing that of Chloramphenicol.

In the present research, newly developed organic molecules featuring pyranopyrazole groups were prepared through an eco-friendly multicomponent method, and their molecular structures were analyzed by employing multiple spectroscopic techniques and analytical methods. The antibacterial efficacy of all synthesized compounds was evaluated in vitro against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacterial strains. To elucidate the mechanism of action and rationalize the observed biological activities, comprehensive computational investigations were performed, including molecular docking studies against tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase, frontier molecular orbital (HOMO–LUMO) analysis, molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) mapping, and in silico ADME/toxicity prediction. Tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase (TyrRS) was selected as the molecular target for this study based on its prominence in antibacterial drug discovery30. TyrRS catalyzes the aminoacylation of tRNA-Tyr with tyrosine, a critical step in bacterial protein synthesis31. This enzyme is essential for bacterial survival and growth32. TyrRS inhibitors have demonstrated potent antibacterial activity in previous studies32,33. The enzyme has been successfully targeted by known antibacterial agents like SB-219383, IMB-T130, compound c6, and BCD38C11, validating it as a druggable target32–35. Bacterial TyrRS exhibits significant structural differences from the human cytoplasmic enzyme, particularly in the active site architecture, offering opportunities for selective inhibition with minimal host toxicity32,35. TyrRS is highly conserved across both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, making it an attractive target for broad-spectrum antibacterial development30,34. This integrated experimental and computational approach provides valuable insights into the structure–activity relationships and pharmacokinetic properties of these novel pyranopyrazole derivatives, supporting their potential as promising antibacterial candidates.

Results and discussion

Organic synthesis and structural elucidation

Compounds 1a,b were synthesized via a single-step, multicomponent, environmentally benign process involving the reaction of 2-(5-oxo-4,5-dihydro-1H-pyrazol-3-yl)-N-phenylacetamide, para-chlorobenzaldehyde, and malononitrile in aqueous medium using glycine as a catalyst, as previously reported29.

Compound 2a was obtained by refluxing 1a in dimethylformamide (DMF) with piperidine and ethyl cyanoacetate. After cooling, the reaction mixture was poured into ice-water to afford 2a. Similarly, compound 2b was synthesized by refluxing 1b in ethanol with ethyl cyanoacetate and catalytic piperidine. The isolated compounds were characterized by spectroscopic and elemental analysis. IR spectra of 2a and 2b exhibited a carbonyl (C = O) absorption band at 1693–1747 cm-1, along with characteristic peaks at 3317, 3209, 2213, and 1666 cm-1 corresponding to NH2 with contribution from O–H stretching of water molecules absorbed by hygroscopic KBr pellets, CN, and amide C = O groups, respectively. For 2b, the 1H NMR spectrum displayed two singlet peaks for NH protons (D2O exchangeable) at δ 10.48 and 9.67 ppm, and a multiplet at δ 7.75–7.21 ppm (14H, aromatic protons). Mechanistic pathways

The synthesis of compounds 1a-b proceeds through a two-step mechanistic pathways involving Knoevenagel condensation reaction followed by Michael addition reaction and subsequent cyclization (Scheme 2). In the first step, malononitrile undergoes base-catalyzed Knoevenagel condensation with 4-chlorobenzaldehyde in the presence of glycine as catalyst. The glycine-activated methylene carbon of malononitrile performs a nucleophilic attack on the carbonyl carbon of the aldehyde, generating a hydroxyl-bearing intermediate. Subsequent dehydration yields benzylidene malononitrile, an activated Michael acceptor bearing electron-withdrawing CN- groups that enhance the electrophilicity of the β-carbon. In the second step, the Michael donor substrate-comprising a pyrazole-derived scaffold with a pendant phenylacetamide moiety- is activated by glycine through deprotonation of the acidic methylene proton adjacent to the carbonyl groups. The resulting carbanion performs a Michael addition to the β-carbon of benzylidene malononitrile, forming a new carbon–carbon bond and generating a stabilized enolate intermediate. This intermediate undergoes intramolecular cyclization through nucleophilic attack of the enolate onto the electrophilic carbon bearing the dicyano moiety, followed by tautomerization to restore aromaticity and stabilize the heterocyclic ring system. The final cyclization and tautomerization steps afford the pyran-fused products 1a-b, characterized by the incorporation of the 4-chlorophenyl substituent and retention of the cyano functionality, alongside the formation of an amino group at the newly formed heterocyclic ring. This tandem Knoevenagel-Michael-cyclization sequence exemplifies the utility of glycine as a mild, bifunctional organocatalyst capable of facilitating multiple bond-forming events in a single reaction vessel, thereby enabling efficient access to structurally complex heterocyclic scaffolds with potential pharmaceutical relevance.

The formation of compounds 2a,b to 8a,b proceeds through divergent mechanistic pathways depending on the nucleophilic reagent employed, all initiated from the common intermediate 1a,b (Scheme 3). These transformations exemplify the versatility of the cyano-amino substituted scaffold toward nucleophilic addition–elimination sequences, condensation reactions, and cyclocondensation processes.

Mechanistic pathways of compounds (a) 2a,b; (b) 3a,b and 4a,b; (c) 5a,b; (d) 6a,b; (e) of 7a,b; (f) 8a,b.

The synthesis of 2a,b involves nucleophilic addition of ethyl cyanoacetate to the activated cyano moiety of 1a,b in the presence of piperidine as a base catalyst. Piperidine deprotonates ethyl cyanoacetate, generating a stabilized carbanion that attacks the electrophilic cyano carbons. The resulting tetrahedral intermediate undergoes proton transfer and rearrangement, followed by ethanol-mediated workup to afford the cyano-ester derivative 2a,b with concomitant formation of an imine functionality.

Compounds 3a,b and 4a-d are generated through condensation with triethyl orthoformate under acidic conditions. The orthoester reacts with the amino group of 1a,b, forming an iminium intermediate. For 4a-d, subsequent reaction with hydrazine derivatives (H2N-R’) leads to nucleophilic substitution at the electrophilic carbon, replacing the ethoxy group with the hydrazine moiety to yield the hydrazone derivatives 4a-d. This pathway highlights the utility of orthoesters in constructing nitrogen-containing heterocycles through sequential condensation-cyclization processes.

The formation of 5a,b proceeds via condensation of 1a,b with formic acid. The amino group performs a nucleophilic attack on the carbonyl carbon of formic acid, generating a carbinolamine intermediate. Subsequent dehydration (-H2O) yields an imine, which undergoes tautomerization to establish the thermodynamically favored aromatic pyrimidine ring system 5a,b. This transformation represents a classical example of formylation-mediated heterocycle formation under mild acidic conditions.

Analogous to the formic acid pathway, 6a,b is synthesized through condensation with acetic acid. The amino group of 1a,b attacks the carbonyl carbon of acetic acid, forming a tetrahedral intermediate that eliminates water to generate an imine. Tautomerization of this intermediate affords the methyl-substituted pyrimidine derivative 6a,b, wherein the acetyl methyl group is retained in the final heterocyclic framework. This mechanism demonstrates the regioselective incorporation of carboxylic acid-derived substituents into the pyrimidine core.

The synthesis of 7a,b involves condensation with formamide under thermal conditions. Formamide acts as both a nitrogen and carbonyl source, wherein the amino group of 1a,b attacks the carbonyl of formamide, generating a urea-like intermediate. Subsequent dehydration and intramolecular cyclization lead to ring closure, followed by tautomerization to yield the amino-substituted pyrimidine derivative 7a,b. This transformation showcases the ability of formamide to serve as a C1-N1 building block in heterocycle synthesis.

Compound 8a,b is formed through condensation of 1a,b with malononitrile under basic conditions (piperidine). The activated methylene of malononitrile, deprotonated by the base, performs a nucleophilic attack on the imine carbon of 1a,b, generating a new carbon–carbon bond. The resulting intermediate undergoes intramolecular cyclization involving one of the cyano groups from malononitrile, followed by tautomerization to establish aromaticity in the newly formed pyrimidine ring. This tandem addition-cyclization sequence affords 8a,b, which features a cyano-amino substituted pyrimidine motif, further expanding the structural diversity accessible from the parent scaffold 1a,b.

These mechanistic pathways collectively demonstrate the synthetic versatility of intermediate 1a,b, which serves as a privileged scaffold for accessing diverse nitrogen-containing heterocycles through judicious selection of nucleophilic and electrophilic reagents. The amino and cyano functionalities present in 1a,b enable multiple modes of reactivity, including nucleophilic addition, condensation, and cyclocondensation, thereby facilitating the construction of structurally complex molecular architectures with potential applications in medicinal chemistry.

Quantum calculations and HOMO–LUMO

The highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) are considered key frontier molecular orbitals (FMOs). The HOMO energy represents the compound’s capacity to donate electrons, while the LUMO energy reflects its capacity to accept electrons into unoccupied orbitals.

These molecular orbitals play a crucial role in determining molecular properties, influencing electronic characteristics, chemical reactivity, structural stability, and bio activity throuhgh elelctron transfer mechanisms26,36. According to Frontier Molecular Orbital (FMO) theory, the antimicrobial activity of compounds correlates with their LUMO energy values. Compounds with lower LUMO energy values exhibit higher electron affinity, facilitating electron acceptance compared to those with higher LUMO energy values. This enhanced electron-accepting ability is hypothesized to improve antimicrobial effectiveness through two mechanisms: (i) facilitating electrostatic interactions with negatively charged bacterial cell membranes and intracellular components, and (ii) enabling redox-based disruption of essential microbial metabolic processes. Consequently, lower LUMO energy values generally correlate with enhanced antimicrobial potential27.

Comparison with the reference compound Chloramphenicol reveals that the synthesized derivatives exhibit comparable or superior electronic properties favorable for antimicrobial activity. Specifically, compounds 2a, 4a, 7a, and 8a demonstrate LUMO energies ranging from −1.19 to −1.41 eV, which are within the optimal range for effective electron acceptance and subsequent biological interactions. These values suggest that the synthesized compounds possess electronic characteristics conducive to antimicrobial action, consistent with their observed in vitro antibacterial activities (Sect. “Antibacterial activity”). A compound possessing high electron affinity can attach more efficiently to protein molecules by forming stronger interactions with key structural features of the target protein. This enhanced binding frequently facilitates the creation of stable complexes with the target protein, thereby influencing its biological function or therapeutic effectiveness37. Moreover, these derivatives demonstrated dipole moments ranging from 2.86 to 11.46 Debye, indicating significant molecular polarity that may enhance interactions with polar biological targets. The energy bandgap (ΔE), which reflects chemical stability, is defined as the energy difference between HOMO and LUMO. Among the compounds studied, 4a exhibited the lowest energy bandgap at ΔE = 4.50 eV, whereas 5a displayed the highest at 5.38 eV. The energy bandgaps of the other derivatives, including 2a, 7a, and 8a, ranged from 4.54 to 4.90 eV. The relatively narrow bandgap of 4a (4.50 eV) suggests higher chemical reactivity, which correlates with its potent antibacterial activity against Gram-negative bacteria (Sect. “Antibacterial activity”). Conversely, the wider bandgap of 5a (5.38 eV) indicates greater kinetic stability, potentially contributing to its selective activity against Gram-positive bacteria. Moreover, the ionization potential represents the energy required to remove an electron from a molecule in its ground state, while electron affinity refers to the energy released when a ground-state molecule gains an electron. Additionally, molecular hardness and softness are crucial parameters in chemical reactivity. The synthesized compounds exhibited low softness and high hardness values, indicating moderate resistance to electronic perturbations, which may contribute to their selective biological activity. The electrophilic index (ω) quantifies the tendency of a molecule to accept electrons. Organic molecules can be classified as weak electrophiles (ω < 0.8), moderate electrophiles (ω = 0.8–1.5), and strong electrophiles (ω > 1.5). The electrophilic index (ω) of the investigated compounds ranged from 2.40 to 2.98, indicating strong electrophilic characteristics. These high electrophilicity values suggest that the synthesized compounds can act as effective electron acceptors, potentially facilitating interactions with electron-rich nucleophilic sites in bacterial proteins and nucleic acids, thereby contributing to their antimicrobial mechanism of action. A higher (less negative) chemical potential value suggests that the molecule donates electrons more easily (acting as an electron donor), whereas a lower (more negative) value indicates a greater tendency to accept electrons (acting as an electron acceptor). The chemical potential (µp) values of the synthesized compounds ranged from -3.51 to -3.68 eV, indicating the likelihood of favorable chemical interactions occurring. The synthesized compounds exhibited these chemical potential values, suggesting their ability to readily transfer electrons to neighboring groups or biological targets. Consequently, these derivatives may form various types of interactions within the active site of a protein target, as further validated by molecular docking studies (Sect. “Molecular docking results”), where compounds 2a, 4a, 5a, 7a, and 8a demonstrated strong binding affinities to tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase, consistent with their electronic properties and observed antibacterial activities.

Molecular docking results

The prepared compounds exhibit moderate to strong antibacterial effects toward Staphylococcus aureus (Table 1). This effect is probably due to interactions occurring between the compounds and amino acid residues inside the active sites of the tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase enzyme from S. aureus. The antimicrobial characteristics were additionally clarified by means of a molecular docking analysis. Prior to docking, Gasteiger partial charges were assigned to both the protein and ligand molecules to ensure accurate electrostatic calculations and improve the reliability of binding energy estimations. By calculating the free binding energies of the compounds, determining the inhibition constants (Ki), examining the quantity of intermolecular hydrogen bonds, and identifying the nature of interactions between amino acids in the binding site of tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase, this analysis assessed the binding affinities of the compounds (Table 3). Tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase (PDB: 1JIJ) (TyrRS) was selected as the molecular target for this study based on its prominence in antibacterial drug discovery30. TyrRS catalyzes the aminoacylation of tRNA-Tyr with tyrosine, a critical step in bacterial protein synthesis31. This enzyme is essential for bacterial survival and growth32. TyrRS inhibitors have demonstrated potent antibacterial activity in previous studies32,33. The enzyme has been successfully targeted by known antibacterial agents like SB-219383, IMB-T130, compound c6, and BCD38C11, validating it as a druggable target32–35. Bacterial TyrRS exhibits significant structural differences from the human cytoplasmic enzyme, particularly in the active site architecture, offering opportunities for selective inhibition with minimal host toxicity32,35. TyrRS is highly conserved across both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, making it an attractive target for broad-spectrum antibacterial development30,34.

Molecular electrostatic potential (MEP) maps

To determine the reactive site for electron-donating and electron-accepting interactions in the course of protein and substrate interactions, Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP) diagrams are employed to show the spatial arrangement of positive and negative charge densities inside a molecule38–40. MEP maps provide crucial insights into the molecular reactivity and interaction sites by visualizing the three-dimensional charge distribution, which directly correlates with biological activity and binding affinity41–43. Positive charges are represented in blue, while negative charges are shown in red (Fig. 5). These charge distributions can influence interactions with the active site through various mechanisms, such as hydrogen bond donation, hydrogen bond acceptance, arene-arene interactions, and arene-cation interactions, all of which support the docking study44,45.

The molecular electrostatic maps of the most active derivatives 2a, 4a, 5a, 7a, and 8a, where the blue color indicates. The positive charge and red color related to the negative charges.

Molecular Electrostatic Potential (MEP) analysis was conducted on the most potent derivatives (2a, 4a, 5a, 7a, and 8a). The MEP outcomes for these molecules are shown in Fig. 5, produced with the Gaussian 9.0 software suite and visualized using Gaussian View version 6.0.1646. MEP calculations were performed at the DFT/B3LYP level of theory, which has been extensively validated for predicting electrostatic properties of organic molecules47,48.

Across all derivatives, negative charges were located on the carbonyl (C = O) group of the anilide, the nitrogen atom of the pyrazole ring, and the chlorine substituent, whereas positive charges were mainly centered on the NH groups in both the anilide and pyrazole rings. This charge distribution pattern is critical for understanding the binding mechanisms, as electronegative regions (red) serve as hydrogen bond acceptors and nucleophilic attack sites, while electropositive regions (blue) function as hydrogen bond donors and electrophilic centers49,50. The observed charge separation creates molecular dipoles that facilitate electrostatic complementarity with the active site residues of tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase, thereby enhancing binding affinity and specificity51,52.

Specifically, in compound 2a, the positive charge was localized on the NH group of the pyridine ring and the NH2 group, while the negative charge was distributed over the C = O of the pyridine and the CN group. The strong electronegative character of the cyano group (CN) enhances its ability to accept hydrogen bonds from donor residues such as ASP A:195 and GLY A:193, which explains the four hydrogen bonds observed in the docking study (Table 3). The electropositive NH2 group can donate hydrogen bonds to negatively charged residues, contributing to the favorable binding energy of −9.36 kcal/mol and Ki of 137.82 nM53.

In compound 4a, the positive charge was concentrated on the (N-NH2) group of the pyrimidine ring, whereas the negative charge was distributed over the N- and NH groups of the pyrimidine ring and the O- of the pyran ring. The dual nature of the pyrimidine ring, possessing both electron-donating (NH2) and electron-withdrawing (N-) sites, creates an amphiphilic character that enables simultaneous interactions with multiple amino acid residues. This explains the seven hydrogen bonds formed with TYR A:170, ASP A:40, THR A:75, VAL A:191, ASP A:195, and CYS A:37, resulting in superior binding affinity (−9.41 kcal/mol) and low Ki value (127.52 nM)54,55.

For compound 5a, the NH group of the pyrimidine ring exhibited positive charge localization, while the negative charge was dispersed over the N- of the pyrimidine and the C = O of the pyrimidine. The carbonyl oxygen’s strong electronegative character (indicated by intense red coloration in MEP maps) makes it an excellent hydrogen bond acceptor, forming two hydrogen bonds with ASP A:40 as observed in the docking results. This charge distribution contributes to the moderate binding energy of −9.12 kcal/mol and Ki of 208.19 nM56.

In compound 7a, the positive charge was primarily localized on the NH2 group, while the negative charge was distributed over the two N- groups of the pyrimidine and the O- of the pyran ring. The exceptional binding affinity of 7a (−11.30 kcal/mol) and its remarkably low Ki value (5.23 nM) can be attributed to the optimal charge distribution pattern that maximizes electrostatic complementarity with the active site. The presence of multiple electronegative centers (two N- atoms and O- of pyran) provides several hydrogen bond acceptor sites, while the electropositive NH2 group serves as a strong donor. This charge distribution enables the formation of seven hydrogen bonds with TYR A:36, VAL A:191, CYS A:37, GLY A:193, GLY A:38, ASP A:40, and TYR A:170, creating a highly stable ligand–protein complex57,58.

Compound 8a revealed a localized positive charge on two NH2 groups, while the negative charge was distributed at N- of pyrimidine, CN group and O- of pyran ring. The presence of two NH2 groups increases the number of potential hydrogen bond donor sites, which correlates with the four hydrogen bonds observed with ASP A:195, LYS A:84, and ASP A:40. However, the binding energy (−8.86 kcal/mol) and Ki (319.13 nM) are less favorable compared to 7a, suggesting that the spatial arrangement of charge centers is as important as their presence59.

Both positive and negative charges are responsible for the interaction between the ligand and the active site in the docking study. The correlation between MEP-derived charge distributions and experimental/computational binding parameters validates the predictive power of MEP analysis in drug design60,61. Compounds with well-balanced charge separation and optimal positioning of electronegative/electropositive regions (particularly 7a, 4a, and 4c) demonstrate superior inhibitory activity, as evidenced by their low Ki values in the nanomolar range. This structure–activity relationship suggests that future optimization efforts should focus on maintaining or enhancing these favorable charge distribution patterns to improve binding affinity and selectivity toward tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase62,63.

Structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis

The structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis of the synthesized pyranopyrazole derivatives reveals critical insights into the structural features governing their antibacterial efficacy against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria (Fig. 6). The differential antibacterial activities observed among the synthesized compounds can be rationalized through systematic evaluation of substituent effects, electronic properties, and molecular characteristics of the pyranopyrazole scaffold. The pyranopyrazole core structure serves as a privileged scaffold in antibacterial drug design, combining the pharmacologically relevant pyrazole and pyran heterocyclic systems64,65. The fused bicyclic framework provides an optimal spatial arrangement for molecular recognition and interaction with bacterial targets, particularly tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase. The presence of multiple heteroatoms (nitrogen and oxygen) within the scaffold facilitates hydrogen bonding interactions with amino acid residues in the active site of the target enzyme, contributing to the observed antibacterial activity19,66.

The structure–activity relationship (SAR) analysis of the synthesized pyranopyrazole derivatives against Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria.

Compounds 2b, 3a, 5b, and 7b exhibited significant inhibition against Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis, surpassing the activity of Chloramphenicol in some instances. The enhanced activity of these compounds can be attributed to specific structural features. The presence of electron-donating groups at strategic positions on the pyranopyrazole scaffold appears to enhance activity against Gram-positive bacteria. Electron-donating substituents increase the electron density of the heterocyclic system, potentially strengthening π-π stacking interactions and hydrogen bonding with bacterial target proteins27,67. Additionally, the lipophilic character imparted by aromatic substituents facilitates penetration through the peptidoglycan-rich cell wall of Gram-positive bacteria68. The substitution pattern at the C-4 position of the pyran ring plays a crucial role in determining antibacterial potency. Aryl substituents at this position provide optimal hydrophobic interactions within the binding pocket of tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase, while maintaining favorable molecular geometry for enzyme inhibition69. The nature of the aryl group (electron-rich and electron-deficient) modulates the overall electronic properties of the molecule, influencing both binding affinity and antibacterial efficacy67.

Compounds 4b, 4d, 6b, and 8b demonstrated notable inhibition against Pseudomonas aeruginosa and Escherichia coli. The SAR trends for Gram-negative activity differ from those observed for Gram-positive bacteria, reflecting the distinct structural and physiological characteristics of the bacterial cell envelope. Enhanced activity against Gram-negative bacteria correlates with increased lipophilicity and the presence of specific substituents that facilitate penetration through the outer membrane lipopolysaccharide layer27. The introduction of halogen atoms (fluorine, chlorine, or bromine) at appropriate positions enhances membrane permeability through increased lipophilicity while maintaining metabolic stability26,70. Halogenated derivatives often exhibit superior activity against Gram-negative bacteria due to improved cellular uptake and resistance to enzymatic degradation. The cyano group at the C-5 position of the pyranopyrazole scaffold, when present, contributes significantly to antibacterial activity through multiple mechanisms. The electron-withdrawing nature of the cyano group modulates the electronic distribution across the heterocyclic system, potentially enhancing interactions with nucleophilic residues in the bacterial target71. Furthermore, the linear geometry of the cyano group allows for favorable orientation within the enzyme active site, facilitating hydrogen bonding with critical amino acid residues72.

Conclusion

This study successfully used an integrated experimental and computational approach to synthesize and assess novel pyranopyrazole derivatives as potent antibacterial agents. The compounds demonstrated significant activity against both Gram-positive (Staphylococcus aureus, Bacillus subtilis) and Gram-negative bacteria (Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Escherichia coli), with structure-dependent selectivity profiles. Quantum chemical calculations revealed HOMO–LUMO energy gaps (4.50–5.38 eV) that inversely correlated with antibacterial activity, while molecular electrostatic potential analysis identified key regions for target recognition. Molecular docking against tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase demonstrated strong binding affinities (-7.84 to -11.30 kcal/mol) with nanomolar inhibition constants (5.23–1790 nM), supported by critical hydrogen bonding and π-π stacking interactions with active site residues. ADME predictions indicated favorable drug-like properties with Lipinski compliance and high gastrointestinal absorption. The synergistic effects of aromatic moieties, halogen atoms, and cyano groups, combined with the fused pyran-pyrazole scaffold, contribute to enhanced lipophilicity and membrane permeability. These findings establish pyranopyrazoles as promising lead compounds for antibacterial drug development. Future investigations should focus on in vivo efficacy, toxicity profiling, and evaluation against multidrug-resistant strains to advance these compounds toward clinical applications, addressing the critical global challenge of antimicrobial resistance.
