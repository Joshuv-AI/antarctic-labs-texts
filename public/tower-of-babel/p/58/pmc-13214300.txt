Microglia depletion alleviates the disruptions in circadian rhythms and anxiety caused by bmal1 deficiency
Authors: Song Gong, Yuxue Chen, Lihan Su, Ensheng Yao, Zhanfei Li, Xinghua Liu, Shi Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroglia, the intrinsic immune cells of the brain, play a crucial role in maintaining neuronal health and circadian rhythms. BMAL1 is a core transcriptional regulator of the molecular circadian clock that coordinates daily rhythms in physiology and behavior. In this study, we investigated the impact of microglial modulation on circadian disruptions and behavioral abnormalities observed in bmal−/− mice. Our results revealed disruptions in circadian rhythms and microglial activation in bmal−/− mice, characterized by altered activity patterns and increased microglia numbers. Inhibiting microglia with PLX5622 effectively ameliorated circadian disruptions and improved behavioral performance in bmal−/− mice, as observed in open field and elevated plus-maze tests. Additionally, microglia inhibition restored structural and functional abnormalities in neurons of bmal−/− mice, including dendritic spine reduction and impaired long-term potentiation (LTP). Furthermore, we observed a normalization of proinflammatory cytokine levels (IL-1β and TNF-α) upon PLX5622 treatment, indicating a potential link between microglial modulation and neuroinflammation. Our findings highlight the intricate interplay between circadian rhythms, microglia, and neuronal health, offering insights into potential therapeutic interventions for circadian-related and neuroinflammatory disorders.Keywords: Microglia, Circadian rhythms, Bmal, LTP, Behavioral abnormalities, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

Circadian rhythms are endogenous, approximately 24-h oscillations that coordinate a wide range of physiological and behavioral processes, including sleep-wake cycles, metabolism, immune activity, and locomotor behavior (Patke et al., 2020). In mammals, these rhythms are generated by a conserved molecular clockwork composed of transcriptional-translational feedback loops operating in both the suprachiasmatic nucleus and peripheral tissues (Patke et al., 2020). At the core of this system, the BMAL1/CLOCK heterodimer activates the transcription of Period (Per 1, Per 2) and Cryptochrome (Cry 1, Cry 2) genes through E-box elements, whereas PER/CRY complexes subsequently inhibit BMAL1/CLOCK activity, thereby generating self-sustained circadian oscillations (Kondratov et al., 2006; Cao et al., 2023). As a central component of the molecular clock, BMAL1 is essential for maintaining normal rhythmicity in physiology and behavior.

Disruption of Bmal1 has profound consequences for organismal homeostasis and neural function. Bmal1-deficient mice exhibit marked circadian abnormalities and develop multiple age-related and neurobiological phenotypes, including oxidative stress, synaptic alterations, and neurodegenerative changes (Kondratov et al., 2006; Musiek et al., 2013). These findings suggest that BMAL1 is not only required for circadian timing itself, but also plays a broader role in preserving neuronal integrity and brain homeostasis. However, the cellular mechanisms linking Bmal1 deficiency to behavioral and neural dysfunction remain incompletely understood.

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system, are increasingly recognized as dynamic regulators of synaptic remodeling, inflammatory signaling, and neural circuit function (Colonna and Butovsky, 2017). Accumulating evidence indicates that microglia also exhibit circadian properties, including time-of-day-dependent changes in morphology, inflammatory factor release, and interactions with synapses (Takayama et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2024). In parallel, clock-related pathways influence immune cell function, and BMAL1 has been implicated in the regulation of inflammatory responses in myeloid cells (Spengler et al., 2012; Nguyen et al., 2013). Recent studies further suggest that microglia contribute to sleep-wake regulation, hippocampal synaptic transmission, and stable wakefulness across the light-dark cycle (Corsi et al., 2022; Liu et al., 2021). Together, these observations raise the possibility that microglia may participate in the circadian and behavioral abnormalities associated with Bmal1 deficiency.

Nevertheless, whether microglia act as causal drivers of circadian disruption, rather than merely secondary responders to Bmal1 deficiency, remains unclear. In the present study, we used global Bmal1 knockout mice to examine alterations in locomotor circadian rhythms, microglial status, anxiety-like behaviors, synaptic structure, and hippocampal synaptic plasticity. We further tested whether microglial depletion with the CSF1R inhibitor PLX5622 could ameliorate these abnormalities. Our findings suggest that microglia are an important contributor to the circadian, behavioral, and synaptic phenotypes associated with Bmal1 deficiency.

Materials and methods

Animals

Six-month-old male wildtype and bmal−/− mice were sourced from Huafukang Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China. This age was selected based on previous studies showing that neuropathological changes in Bmal1-deficient mice are age dependent and become more evident at 4-6 months, before severe peripheral pathology develops (Musiek et al., 2013). The mice were housed under controlled temperature and humidity conditions, with ad libitum access to food and water, and maintained on a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with lights on at 08:00. A minimum acclimatization period of one week was provided before the experiments. All experimental procedures were approved by the Biology Ethics Committee of Shihezi University (Approval Number: A2024-363).

Microglia depletion induced by PLX5622

PLX5622 is a brain-penetrant colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) inhibitor widely used to deplete microglia in vivo (Spangenberg et al., 2019). PLX5622 (MedChemExpress) was formulated into AIN-76A standard chow (1200 ppm). Male bmal−/− mice aged 6 months were fed this food for 20 days in our study. To control for nonspecific effects of CSF1R inhibition, we additionally included a wildtype (wt) + PLX5622 group. Circadian locomotor activity was recorded for 24 h at baseline (day 0), day 10, and day 20. Behavioral tests were performed at the end of the 20-day treatment period. Tissue collection for histological, electrophysiological, and molecular analyses was conducted after completion of behavioral testing.

Open field test (OFT)

All behavioral tests were recorded using a camera and analyzed through DeepLabCut and Python (Mathis et al., 2018). Behavioral testing was conducted at 18:00-20:00 during the light phase. The OFT was performed in a square arena measuring 50 × 50 cm, enclosed by a 50 cm high wall. The arena floor was divided into 25 equal-sized grids with lines, and the central area consisted of the central 9 grids. Each mouse had a 5-min exploration session in the arena, and the arena was cleaned with 75% ethanol between each trial. The time spent exploring the central area was measured, and the peripheral duration was calculated as (time spent exploring the peripheral area/300 s) × 100%.

Elevate plus maze test (EPMT)

The elevated plus maze, with a height of 40 cm, consisted of a central square and four arms: two open arms and two closed arms. All arms extended from the central square, which had a side length of 10 cm. The two closed arms were 40 cm in length, 6 cm in width, and enclosed by walls with a height of 20 cm. In contrast, the open arms had no walls. Each mouse was placed in the central square, facing one of the open arms, and allowed to explore individually for 5 min. After each trial, the maze was cleaned with 75% ethanol. The recorded parameters included the total time spent in the open arms and the number of entries into the open arms. An entry was defined as when all the paws of a mouse entered an arm.

Immunohistochemical staining

The mice were anesthetized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital and then underwent transcardial perfusion with normal saline, followed by perfusion with a pre-cooled 4% paraformaldehyde solution. Subsequently, the brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 12 h and then transferred into a 30% sucrose solution for cryoprotection. Sections were incubated in 3% H2O2 for 30 min, and then were incubated with the primary antibody (IBA-1,1:1000, Abclonal, China) overnight at 4 °C. After primary antibody incubation, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Beijing Biosynthesis Biotechnology Co. Ltd.; Beijing, China) at a dilution of 1:500 for 30 min at room temperature. Immunoreactivity was then visualized using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), followed by hematoxylin counterstaining.

Circadian rhythm analysis

Mice were individually housed and recorded using an infrared camera under a 12 h light/12 h dark cycle, with lights on at 08:00. Locomotor activity was recorded continuously for 24 h on day 0, day 10, and day 20. The traveled distance of each mouse was extracted from video recordings using DeepLabCut-based tracking and custom Python scripts, and hourly activity was calculated for circadian analysis. Circadian rhythm analysis was performed using the CircaCompare R package (Parsons et al., 2020; Yao et al., 2023). The code is available on GitHub at https://github.com/RWParsons/circacompare/. To summarize, the traveled distance at each time point was fitted to a cosine curve. The parameters, such as peak time and amplitude, of these curves can be used to describe the characteristics of the circadian rhythm. A circadian rhythm was indicated by a rhythmic p-value below 0.05. In the plotted time axis, 0 corresponds to 00:00 (midnight), and peak phase is therefore reported in 24-h clock time.

Long-term potentiation (LTP)

Electrophysiology procedures were performed on anesthetized mice. The brain was quickly removed within 1 min and placed in ice-cold, oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF) with the following composition in mM: 124 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 2 CaCl2, 2 MgSO4, 1 NaH2PO4, 25 NaHCO3, and 10 Glucose, adjusted to pH 7.4. Using a precooled vibratome, the brain was horizontally sliced into 350 μm sections. These slices were then incubated in oxygenated ACSF at 29 °C for a minimum of 90 min to allow for recovery. After recovery, a single slice was placed on a MED probe (MED-P515A, 8 × 8 array, interpolar distance 150 μm, Panasonic), with careful positioning to cover the DG region over most electrodes. The slice was immersed in 29 °C oxygenated ACSF, stabilized with a meshed anchor, and allowed to equilibrate for at least 30 min. Continuous perfusion of oxygenated fresh ACSF at a rate of 2 ml/min was maintained throughout the experiment using a peristaltic pump. Field excitatory postsynaptic potentials (fEPSPs) were recorded at CA3-DG synapses. Electrical stimulation was applied to one channel within the PP, while evoked fEPSPs were monitored and recorded from the other 63 channels. The stimulation intensity was set at approximately 40% of the intensity inducing maximal fEPSPs. For LTP recording, baseline responses were evoked for at least 30 min until stabilized. Subsequently, a high-frequency stimulation (HFS) protocol, comprising 10 bursts with each burst containing 4 pulses at 100 Hz and an inter-burst interval of 200 ms, was delivered at a stimulation intensity adjusted to elicit 40% of the maximal response. Following HFS, the test stimulus was repeatedly delivered once every minute for at least 1 h to record LTP.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

For the detection of IL-1β and TNF-α levels in brain tissues, we collected samples and quantified cytokines using ELISA (IL-1β, DY401; TNF-α, MTA00B-1; R&D Systems), following to the guidelines provided by the manufacturers.

Golgi staining

Brains were immersed directly in Golgi solution containing 1 g potassium chromate, 1 g mercuric chloride, 0.8 g potassium chloride, and 100 ml double-distilled water. The bottles containing the brains were kept in the dark for 6 weeks. Following this period, the solution was successively exchanged with 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions in light-protected jars to preserve histological structure. Brain sections, with a thickness of 100 μm, were obtained using a vibratome and transferred onto gelatin-coated glass slides. After rinsing with double-distilled water, slides underwent a 30-min incubation in ammonium hydroxide. Subsequently, the slides were immersed for 30 min in a black and white film developer solution diluted at a ratio of 1:9 with water. After a final rinse with double-distilled water, slides were mounted with resin and cover-slipped. ImageJ software was utilized for all subsequent quantitative analyses.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc multiple-comparison tests was used. A value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Bmal−/− mice display disruptions in circadian rhythms and activation of microglia

We first assessed locomotor activity in wild-type and Bmal1−/− mice in their home cages. DeepLabCut was used to track animal movement, and hourly locomotor activity was calculated and fitted using a cosine curve. Although Bmal1−/− mice retained a detectable daily activity pattern, their activity rhythm was markedly disturbed. The fitted peak of locomotor activity occurred at 0.82 h after midnight, approximately 00:49, in Bmal1−/− mice, compared with 5.07 h after midnight, approximately 05:04, in wild-type mice (Fig. 1A; rhythmicity p-value: wild-type, 2.9 × 10−17; Bmal1−/−, 4.1 × 10−11). In addition, Bmal1−/− mice showed reduced total daily activity compared with wild-type mice (Fig. 1B). We next examined microglial changes in the hippocampus. Compared with wild-type mice, Bmal1−/− mice showed an increased density of Iba1-positive microglia (Fig. 1C and D). Sholl analysis further revealed reduced microglial process complexity in Bmal1−/− mice, as indicated by fewer and shorter processes (Fig. 1E). Together, these findings suggest that Bmal1 deficiency is associated with disrupted locomotor activity rhythms and reactive microglial changes.

Microglial depletion ameliorates anxiety-like behaviors and partially normalizes locomotor activity rhythms in Bmal1−/− mice

We next performed the open field test (OFT) and elevated plus maze (EPM) to assess locomotor and anxiety-like behaviors. In the OFT, Bmal1−/− mice showed increased locomotor activity and spent more time in the peripheral regions compared with wild-type mice (Fig. 2A–C). In the EPM, Bmal1−/− mice spent less time in the open arms and showed fewer open-arm entries than wild-type mice (Fig. 2D–F), indicating increased anxiety-like behavior. Given the increased microglial density and reactive morphological changes observed in Bmal1−/− mice, we next examined whether microglia contributed to the behavioral and rhythm-related abnormalities. Bmal1−/− mice were treated with the CSF1R inhibitor PLX5622 for 20 days to deplete microglia. PLX5622 treatment improved behavioral abnormalities in Bmal1−/− mice. In the OFT, PLX5622-treated Bmal1−/− mice showed total locomotor activity and peripheral-zone duration that were closer to those of wild-type mice (Fig. 2A–C). In the EPM, PLX5622-treated Bmal1−/− mice showed increased open-arm entries and longer open-arm duration compared with untreated Bmal1−/− mice (Fig. 2D–F).

To examine the temporal evolution of locomotor activity rhythms during PLX5622 treatment, we recorded 24-h locomotor activity at day 10 and day 20 after treatment and compared these data with baseline Bmal1−/− and wild-type rhythms. At day 10, the fitted peak activity time in PLX5622-treated Bmal1−/− mice shifted from 0.82 h after midnight, approximately 00:49, to 2.22 h after midnight, approximately 02:13, suggesting a trend toward phase recovery, although the phase difference from untreated Bmal1−/− mice did not reach statistical significance. By day 20, the peak activity time further shifted to 4.89 h after midnight, approximately 04:53, approaching the wild-type peak time of 5.07 h after midnight, approximately 05:04. This phase shift was statistically significant and was accompanied by improved MESOR and amplitude estimates, indicating partial normalization of locomotor activity rhythms after microglial depletion (Fig. 2G and Table 1). WT mice treated with PLX5622 retained significant rhythmicity, although a modest phase shift was observed compared with untreated WT mice.

Microglial depletion partially rescues neuronal structural and functional abnormalities in Bmal1−/− mice

We next examined whether PLX5622-mediated microglial depletion could improve neuronal structural and functional abnormalities in Bmal1−/− mice. Electron microscopy revealed impaired synaptic ultrastructure in Bmal1−/− mice, as shown by reduced postsynaptic density (PSD) thickness/length compared with wild-type mice (Fig. 3A and C). Consistently, Golgi staining showed a significant reduction in dendritic spine density in Bmal1−/− mice (Fig. 3B and D). These structural abnormalities were partially restored after PLX5622 treatment.

We further assessed synaptic function by electrophysiological recording of long-term potentiation (LTP) in the hippocampal CA3-DG pathway. LTP was markedly impaired in Bmal1−/− mice, whereas PLX5622-treated Bmal1−/− mice showed a partial recovery of LTP (Fig. 3E). Because PLX5622 is a CSF1R inhibitor that depletes microglia, we next examined whether the observed neuronal improvement was associated with reduced neuroinflammation. ELISA analysis showed that IL-1β and TNF-α levels were significantly elevated in Bmal1−/− mice. In contrast, PLX5622 treatment reduced IL-1β and TNF-α levels in Bmal1−/− mice to levels comparable to those in wild-type mice (Fig. 3F and G). These findings suggest that microglial depletion alleviates neuroinflammation and partially rescues synaptic structural and functional deficits in Bmal1−/− mice.

Discussion

In the present study, Bmal1 deficiency caused marked alterations in daily locomotor activity patterns. Compared with wild-type mice, which showed a peak activity time at approximately 5.07 a.m., Bmal1−/− mice exhibited an advanced activity peak at 0.82 a.m., indicating a substantial disturbance of circadian phase. In parallel with this circadian disruption, Bmal1−/− mice showed an increased density of Iba1-positive microglia and altered microglial morphology, including reduced process number and process length. These morphological changes, together with the increased microglial density, suggest an altered reactive state rather than a simple binary activation phenotype. PLX5622-mediated microglial depletion was associated with improvements in anxiety-like behavior, as assessed by the OFT and EPMT, and with a partial normalization of circadian locomotor activity. Specifically, after PLX5622 treatment, the peak activity time of Bmal1−/− mice shifted progressively toward that of wild-type mice, with a partial shift at day 10 and a more evident correction by day 20. Importantly, the WT + PLX5622 group did not show obvious abnormalities compared with WT controls, suggesting that PLX5622 treatment alone did not reproduce the behavioral or circadian phenotypes observed in Bmal1-deficient mice. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence showing that cortical diurnal gene expression rhythms remain largely intact in wild-type mice after microglial depletion, and that elimination of microglia with PLX3397, another CSF1R inhibitor, does not disrupt circadian locomotor activity in wild-type mice (Matsui et al., 2023).

To quantitatively characterize circadian alterations, we used the CircaCompare method to estimate and compare rhythmic parameters, including mesor, amplitude, and phase. This approach allowed us to move beyond visual inspection of activity curves and directly quantify the phase advance observed in Bmal1−/− mice, as well as the partial phase normalization after PLX5622 treatment. In particular, reporting fitted peak activity time provided a clearer description of the temporal shift in daily locomotor activity across groups and treatment time points.

The influence of microglia on neuronal structure and function was further supported by the effects of PLX5622 treatment in Bmal1−/− mice. In the present study, Bmal1−/− mice exhibited notable synaptic structural abnormalities, including reduced dendritic spine density and decreased postsynaptic density, as shown by Golgi staining and electron microscopy. These alterations suggest impaired synaptic connectivity and are consistent with the electrophysiological deficits observed in Bmal1-deficient mice. Importantly, PLX5622-mediated microglial depletion partially restored dendritic spine density and postsynaptic structural integrity. These findings suggest that altered microglial reactivity may contribute to synaptic abnormalities in the context of Bmal1 deficiency, although the present results do not distinguish between direct microglia-mediated synaptic remodeling and indirect effects secondary to changes in the inflammatory microenvironment.

Our findings are consistent with previous studies linking microglia to circadian regulation and synaptic remodeling. A previous study reported that microglia-specific Bmal1 deletion in aging mice accelerated cognitive decline, reduced hippocampal long-term potentiation, and increased immature dendritic spines (Iweka et al., 2023). Although our study used global Bmal1 knockout mice rather than microglia-specific deletion, the observed changes in microglial density, morphology, inflammatory cytokines, dendritic spine density, and LTP support the idea that altered microglial function may participate in the synaptic consequences of circadian clock disruption. In addition, microglia have been shown to engulf synaptic material through a CR3-dependent pathway in the presence of soluble Aβ oligomers, highlighting their capacity to directly regulate synaptic structure under pathological conditions (Hong et al., 2016). Together, these studies provide a broader context for our findings and suggest that microglial reactivity may represent an important link between Bmal1 deficiency, neuroinflammation, and synaptic dysfunction.

We further examined whether PLX5622-mediated microglial depletion affected synaptic function. Electrophysiological analysis of LTP in the CA3-DG projections revealed a marked impairment of synaptic plasticity in Bmal1−/− mice. After PLX5622 treatment, LTP was partially restored, suggesting that microglia-associated changes may contribute to the functional impairment of neural circuits in the absence of Bmal1. This interpretation is consistent with previous studies showing that microglial alterations can directly influence synaptic transmission and plasticity. For example, the microglia-specific pathogenic Trem2 variant enhances glutamatergic neuronal transmission and inhibits LTP by increasing brain TNF-α concentrations, thereby linking microglial dysfunction to neuronal impairment (Ren et al., 2020). Conversely, in the absence of microglia, synaptic transmission is reduced both at baseline and after LTP induction (Du et al., 2022). These findings indicate that microglia are not merely inflammatory cells, but active regulators of synaptic function under both physiological and pathological conditions.

In Bmal1−/− mice, elevated levels of IL-1β and TNF-α were observed, indicating an enhanced inflammatory response in the absence of functional Bmal1. Together with the increased density of Iba1-positive cells and the reduced complexity of microglial processes, these findings support the presence of altered microglial reactivity in this model. Because microglia are key regulators of neuroimmune homeostasis and synaptic remodeling, such changes may contribute not only to the inflammatory phenotype observed here but also to the structural and functional neuronal abnormalities in Bmal1-deficient mice. In our study, PLX5622 treatment markedly reduced IL-1β and TNF-α levels, suggesting that microglia-associated inflammatory signaling was attenuated after CSF1R inhibition. This reduction in inflammatory burden was accompanied by improvements in anxiety-like behavior, dendritic spine density, and LTP, supporting the idea that neuroinflammation is an important component of the pathological cascade triggered by Bmal1 deficiency.

Inflammation and circadian disruption are closely interconnected, and inflammatory signaling can disturb rhythmic physiology while circadian dysregulation can amplify inflammatory responses (Scheiermann et al., 2013). The expression of TNF-α exhibits a circadian rhythm controlled by clock proteins (Onoue et al., 2019). In turn, TNF-α can influence Bmal1 expression through calcium-dependent pathways, involving up-regulation of Rorα in the presence of Ca2+ influx and down-regulation of Rev-erbα in its absence (Yoshida et al., 2018). In addition, Bmal1 controls Nrf 2 mRNA expression by directly binding to its promoter E-box, thereby regulating antioxidant and inflammatory responses. Macrophages lacking Bmal1 show increased production of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β (Early et al., 2018). These findings provide a mechanistic context for our observation that IL-1β and TNF-α levels were elevated in Bmal1−/− mice and reduced after PLX5622 treatment. Thus, the attenuation of inflammatory cytokines after microglial depletion may contribute to the partial recovery of synaptic and behavioral abnormalities in this model.

Our study has several limitations. First, we focused on a 20-day period of PLX5622 treatment. Although the circadian activity pattern of Bmal1−/− mice shifted toward that of wild-type mice after treatment, it did not necessarily fully return to normal. Future studies should examine the long-term effects of PLX5622 treatment, as well as potential rebound effects after treatment cessation, to better understand the durability of microglia-related modulation of circadian and behavioral phenotypes. Second, this study used global Bmal1 knockout mice. Because global Bmal1 deficiency affects multiple cell types and peripheral tissues, the present study cannot determine whether the observed phenotypes are driven by cell-autonomous effects in microglia, neurons, astrocytes, or by systemic circadian disruption. Future studies using microglia-specific Bmal1 knockout mice, as well as other clock gene models involving Clock, Per, or Cry, will be useful for clarifying the cell-type-specific contribution of circadian machinery to microglial and neuronal dysfunction. Finally, only male mice were included in the present study to reduce variability associated with the estrous cycle. However, given that sex differences have been reported in microglial function, circadian regulation, and anxiety-related behaviors, future studies will be needed to determine whether the phenotypes and PLX5622 responses observed here are also present in female mice.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that Bmal1 deficiency is associated with circadian phase disruption, increased microglial density, altered microglial morphology, elevated inflammatory cytokines, and synaptic structural and functional abnormalities. PLX5622-mediated microglial depletion partially ameliorated anxiety-like behavior, shifted circadian locomotor activity toward the wild-type pattern, reduced IL-1β and TNF-α levels, and improved dendritic spine density and LTP. These findings support the view that altered microglial reactivity and neuroinflammatory signaling contribute to the pathological consequences of Bmal1 deficiency. Rather than identifying microglial depletion itself as a direct therapeutic strategy, our results highlight microglial reactivity as a potential modulatory pathway linking circadian disruption to neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction.
