Scutellarin mediates cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in liver cancer through a novel circSRBD1/miR-2682-5p/ESR1 ceRNA axis
Authors: Ze Li, Yimin Ling, Wei Xiao, JiaZheng Li, JiaHui Rong, Zewen Zhang, Yan Wei, Zhongchao Huo, Dong Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundScutellarin (SCU) is a natural flavonoid compound exhibiting anti-tumor activity. This study aimed to elucidate the molecular mechanism underlying SCU’s therapeutic effects in liver cancer.MethodsAn integrated approach combining network pharmacology and transcriptomic analysis was employed to identify SCU-related targets, followed by the identification of core targets through protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction and topological analysis using MCC and MCODE algorithms. A circular RNA (circRNA)-mediated competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) regulatory network was constructed by integrating data from databases including miRWALK and circBANK with whole-transcriptome sequencing. Molecular interactions were experimentally validated using Western blotting, dual-luciferase reporter assays, and RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP). In Huh7 and HepG2 cells, overexpression and knockdown models of key RNAs were established to evaluate their functional roles, with cell proliferation, cell cycle distribution, and apoptosis assessed via CCK-8, colony formation, and flow cytometry assays. To investigate in vivo anti-tumor efficacy and associated mechanisms, a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model was established in nude mice.ResultsIntegrated network pharmacology and transcriptomic analysis identified 11 core genes significantly enriched in the estrogen signaling pathway, among which ESR1 was validated as a key target. SCU downregulated ESR1 expression in a dose-dependent manner. Mechanistically, SCU upregulated miR-2682-5p while downregulating circSRBD1. Dual-luciferase reporter assays confirmed that miR-2682-5p directly binds to the 3′UTRs of both circSRBD1 and ESR1, supporting the existence of a circSRBD1/miR-2682-5p/ESR1 ceRNA regulatory axis. Functional studies demonstrated that overexpression of circSRBD1 attenuated SCU-induced cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and suppression of proliferation, whereas silencing circSRBD1 enhanced these effects. Furthermore, SCU and fulvestrant exhibited synergistic anti-tumor activity both in vitro and in vivo.ConclusionThis study identifies a novel circSRBD1/miR-2682-5p/ESR1 ceRNA regulatory axis that plays a critical role in mediating SCU’s inhibitory effects on liver cancer progression, highlighting the potential therapeutic value of combining SCU with fulvestrant.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07931-7.

Introduction

In 2020, primary liver cancer emerged as the sixth most prevalent malignancy and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide, underscoring its formidable global health burden [1]. The majority of liver cancer incidence occurs in developing countries. Early-stage disease is frequently asymptomatic, resulting in delayed diagnosis in approximately 80% of patients. Advanced liver cancer is associated with a mortality rate exceeding 80%, a median survival time of less than 12 months, and an overall 5-year survival rate below 20% [2]. China bears nearly half of the global liver cancer burden, with approximately 360,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths annually [3, 4]. Current therapeutic interventions for liver cancer face substantial challenges. Although surgical resection is the standard of care for early-stage (BCLC stage A) liver cancer, the 2-year postoperative recurrence rate remains as high as 60.1% [5]. The current standard of care for intermediate and advanced (BCLC B/C) liver cancer frequently involves combination therapy based on targeted agents and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). However, persistent challenges include high rates of drug resistance and significant adverse effects [6]. In recent years, the natural flavonoid scutellarin (SCU) has demonstrated potential antitumor activity [7–9].

SCU as a standalone therapeutic agent exhibits potent antitumor efficacy, unveiling broad-spectrum therapeutic promise in diverse human malignancies, including non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), breast cancer, and colorectal carcinoma [10, 11]. SCU modulates the STAT3 and AKT/mTOR/4EBP1 signaling pathways, significantly inhibiting NSCLC cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis [12]. SCU exerts potent regulatory effects on MCF-7 breast cancer cells by significantly inhibiting cell proliferation and inducing apoptosis. This anticancer potency is largely attributable to its role as an autophagy inducer, a function executed through its disruptive effect on the Hippo-YAP signaling pathway, thereby linking upstream signaling to downstream cytotoxic effects [13]. Moreover, SCU emerges as a highly promising therapeutic candidate for colorectal cancer, demonstrating its ability to suppress cell viability while simultaneously orchestrating apoptotic cell death. Mechanistically, SCU orchestrates its effects by concurrently promoting apoptosis through Bax/caspase-3 upregulation and p53 activation, while inhibiting key survival pathways such as NF-κB-mediated inflammation and Hedgehog signaling [14, 15]. However, its precise molecular mechanisms against liver cancer remain incompletely elucidated.

Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are covalently closed, circular RNA molecules that constitute a ubiquitously expressed and evolutionarily conserved class of endogenous noncoding RNAs, underscoring their essential regulatory functions in cellular processes. Their biogenesis unfolds during pre-mRNA splicing, where a dynamic process of back-splicing joins a downstream 5’ splice site to an upstream 3’ splice site, culminating in the formation of a seamless, covalently closed RNA loop [16]. The functional roles of circRNAs are diverse, encompassing those of competing endogenous RNAs (ceRNAs), which sequester miRNAs, as well as participation in direct binding and regulation of protein activity [16, 17]. A growing body of evidence has implicated numerous circRNAs in the pathogenesis of a spectrum of cancers, with well-established roles in lung, liver, gastric, and breast malignancies [16, 18, 19]. Substantial evidence indicates that the ceRNA regulatory network extensively modulates tumor-related gene expression across diverse malignancies—including lung, gastric, and breast cancer—thereby critically regulating tumorigenesis, progression, and prognosis [20]. By governing the expression of PBK, KIF5B, and SLC7A11 via competitive endogenous RNA mechanisms, LINC00337 modulates the immune landscape of lung adenocarcinoma, an effect that translates into significant consequences for patient outcomes and therapeutic efficacy [21]. The potential of LINC00511 as an anticancer target is highlighted by its ability to drive pro-tumorigenic phenotypes like proliferation and invasion in gastric cancer, largely through its regulatory interaction with miR-515-5p [22]. The oncogenic functions of circZFAND6, whose expression is induced by EIF4A3, are primarily orchestrated through its action on the miR-647/FASN signaling axis, thereby promoting breast cancer progression. These findings elucidate the molecular mechanisms underpinning breast cancer development and progression, offering potential therapeutic targets [23].

This study systematically investigates the molecular mechanisms underlying SCU’s anti-liver cancer effects through an integrated approach combining multi-omics profiling with experimental validation. Convergent bioinformatics analysis, intersecting network pharmacology predictions with transcriptomic DEGs, followed by PPI topology examination, identified ESR1 as the core target operative within the estrogen signaling pathway. Subsequent miRNA sequencing and bioinformatics prediction enabled the first construction of the “circSRBD1-miR-2682-5p-ESR1” ceRNA regulatory axis. In vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated that SCU significantly suppresses liver cancer cell proliferation and induces apoptosis by targeting circSRBD1. This study reveals that SCU modulates liver cancer progression via a circRNA-dependent regulatory pathway, thereby providing a novel strategy for synergistic targeted therapy utilizing natural compounds and potentially improving the prognosis of liver cancer patients.

Results

Delineation of SCU’s putative targets and central genes in liver cancer

A comprehensive screening of SCU’s potential target genes was conducted across seven well-established network pharmacology databases: SEA, GeneCards, PubChem, Batman, SwissTargetPrediction, ChemBL, and STITCH. Following integration of all identified targets and removal of redundant entries, 201 unique target genes were ultimately curated (Fig. 1A). Comparative RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) analysis between SCU-treated (huh7 cell concentration: 300 µg/mL) and control groups revealed statistically significant differential expression, identifying 452 DEGs, comprising 113 upregulated and 339 downregulated transcripts. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering heatmap and volcano plot analyses confirmed substantial inter-group differences in gene expression profiles (Fig. 1B, C). To identify core targets potentially mediating SCU’s pharmacological effects, the 201 target genes derived from network pharmacology were intersected with the 452 DEGs identified via RNA-Seq. This analysis yielded an intersection of 11 overlapping genes, designated as high-confidence candidate targets for subsequent investigation (Fig. 1D). To delineate the biological roles and pathway involvements of these 11 overlapping genes, we employed GO and KEGG enrichment to assess functional implications. Specifically, GO enrichment analysis revealed that the significantly enriched biological processes were primarily linked to estrogen signaling and cellular responses to estrogen stimuli, including regulation of intracellular estrogen receptor activity and responses to estrogen and estradiol. Cellular Component enrichment analysis revealed a predominant association with plasma membrane structures, with the highest representation in terms such as the basolateral plasma membrane and integral components of the plasma membrane. Analysis of Molecular Function, Key molecular functions involved steroid hormone binding and ligand-dependent estrogen receptor activity.KEGG Pathway Enrichment. The predominantly enriched pathway was the estrogen signaling pathway. These enrichment results indicate that SCU might achieve its anti-liver cancer effects through functional regulation of the estrogen signaling pathway (Fig. 1E, F). A PPI network was generated from the 11 overlapping candidate genes. The Maximal Clique Centrality (MCC) and MCODE algorithms within Cytoscape were applied to identify core regulatory factors. Integration of the top-ranked genes from both algorithms revealed five hub genes: ESR1, AKR1C2, SLCO1B1, UGT1A1, and SLCO1B3 (Fig. 1G, H). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was employed to determine the diagnostic potential of these hub genes, which yielded the following area under the curve (AUC) values: ESR1 (AUC = 0.61), AKR1C2 (AUC = 0.52), SLCO1B1 (AUC = 0.53), UGT1A1 (AUC = 0.49), and SLCO1B3 (AUC = 0.51) (Fig. 1I). To investigate the expression level of ESR1, this study selected THLE-2 cells (a normal hepatocyte control) and two commonly used hepatoma cell lines, Huh7 and HepG2, for RT-qPCR analysis. The results showed that the relative expression level of ESR1 in Huh7 and HepG2 cells was significantly higher than in THLE-2 cells, consistent with previous sequencing data (S_Figure1C). As a final step, a unified regulatory network was established. This framework delineates the relationships between the drug SCU, its candidate targets, and the relevant signaling pathways, thereby facilitating a holistic prediction of its mechanistic basis (S_Figure1A).

Construction of the ceRNA regulatory network of SCU in liver cancer

MiRNA sequencing analysis identified 61 statistically significant differentially expressed miRNAs (DEMs), comprising 23 significantly upregulated (logFC ≥ 1.2, P < 0.05) and 38 significantly downregulated (logFC ≤ -1.2, P < 0.05) miRNAs. The distribution of these DEMs is depicted in a bar graph (Fig. 2A). Integration of predictions from the miRWALK and DIANA-microT databases facilitated the screening of potential miRNAs targeting ESR1, followed by intersection analysis with the upregulated miRNAs. Thus, hsa-miR-2682-5p was identified as the key miRNA regulating ESR1 (Fig. 2B). To further investigate the expression level of miR-2682-5p, RT-qPCR analysis was conducted using THLE-2 cells (as a normal hepatocyte control), Huh7 cells, and HepG2 cells—two commonly used hepatoma cell lines. The results showed that miR-2682-5p expression was significantly lower in both Huh7 and HepG2 cells compared to THLE-2 cells (S_Figure1D). qRT-PCR analysis confirmed that SCU treatment led to a concentration-dependent upregulation of miR-2682-5p expression in two liver cancer cell lines (Fig. 2C). To validate the exact molecular interplay between miR-2682-5p and the 3’ untranslated region (3’UTR) of the ESR1 gene, luciferase reporter vectors were engineered to incorporate either the wild-type ESR1-3’UTR sequence (ESR1-3’UTR-WT) or a mutated variant featuring targeted disruptions in the seed binding region (ESR1-3’UTR-Mut). Luciferase reporter assays revealed that transfection with the miR-2682-5p mimic led to a significant reduction in luminescent signal in cells bearing the wild-type reporter construct, whereas no notable suppression was detected in cells harboring the mutant vector. These findings collectively confirm that miR-2682-5p directly silences ESR1 expression through specific binding to its 3’UTR (Fig. 2D). To further investigate the interaction between ESR1 and miR-2682-5p, RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) was performed to validate the association. The results showed that in Huh7 cells, both ESR1 mRNA and miR-2682-5p were significantly enriched in Ago2-containing complexes, indicating their direct binding to the Argonaute 2 (Ago2) protein (S_Figure1G). Circular RNA sequencing analysis unveiled a total of 180 differentially expressed circRNAs, comprising 69 upregulated species exhibiting a log fold change ≥ 1.2 (P < 0.05) and 111 downregulated variants with a log fold change ≤ − 1.2 (P < 0.05). These data were visualized as a rank plot (Fig. 2E). Intersection of the circBANK database (containing 9,174 circRNA-miRNA interactions) with 34 significantly downregulated circRNAs (logFC < -3.1, P < 0.05) identified hsa_circ_0000997 (circSRBD1) as the core circRNA binding miR-2682-5p (Fig. 2F). To further investigate the expression level of circSRBD1, RT-qPCR analysis was performed using THLE-2 cells (as a normal hepatocyte control), Huh7 cells, and HepG2 cells—two commonly used hepatoma cell lines. The results showed that circSRBD1 expression was significantly higher in both Huh7 and HepG2 cells than in THLE-2 cells, which is consistent with our previous sequencing data(S_Figure1E). qPCR analysis demonstrated that SCU dose-dependently suppresses circSRBD1 expression, providing mechanistic evidence that circSRBD1 downregulation serves as a key mediator of its anticancer effects in liver cancer cells (Fig. 2G). Moreover, luciferase reporter assays demonstrated that transfection with miR-2682-5p mimics elicited a marked reduction in luminescent output from the wild-type circSRBD1 (circSRBD1-WT) reporter construct, while the mutant circSRBD1 (circSRBD1-Mut) vector displayed no significant change in activity. These findings firmly establish that miR-2682-5p directly engages circSRBD1 through sequence-specific binding, thereby suppressing its expression with high precision (Fig. 2H).

Identification and characterization of circular RNA

Through comparative analysis of the Transcirc and circBase databases, circSRBD1 was mapped to chromosome 2 (chr2: 45546732–45562756). The mature circRNA molecule originates from the circularization of exons 1 through 5 of the human SRBD1 pre-mRNA. The circSRBD1 sequence was verified via Sanger sequencing, which confirmed the presence and accuracy of its back-splice junction site (Fig. 3A). To assess circSRBD1 stability, Huh7 and HepG2 cell lines were treated with actinomycin D (Act-D) to inhibit transcription. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis demonstrated that circSRBD1 exhibited significantly greater resistance to Act-D-mediated degradation compared to the linear SRBD1 transcript or the housekeeping gene GAPDH (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, qPCR analysis of total RNA isolated from HepG2 and Huh7 cells following RNase R treatment revealed that circSRBD1 exhibits remarkable resistance to RNase R exonuclease digestion, in stark contrast to its linear counterparts SRBD1 and GAPDH, which were rapidly degraded under the same conditions (Fig. 3C). To verify the circular nature of circSRBD1, primers targeting the back-splice junction in a divergent orientation were designed alongside convergent primers specific to the linear mRNA transcript. Agarose gel electrophoresis revealed that circSRBD1-specific amplicons were robustly amplified using divergent primers in cDNA, as well as by convergent primers, whereas amplification in genomic DNA (gDNA) occurred exclusively with convergent primers. In striking contrast, the control gene GAPDH yielded amplification products only with convergent primers across both cDNA and gDNA templates, underscoring the circular nature of circSRBD1 and the specificity of its back-splicing event (Fig. 3D). To further verify whether miR-2682-5p targets the linear SRBD1 transcript, NC mimics and miR-2682-5p mimics were transfected into Huh7 and HepG2 cells, respectively. RT-qPCR results revealed no statistically significant difference in SRBD1 expression between the two groups, indicating that miR-2682-5p does not directly target the linear SRBD1 transcript (S_Figure1F).

circSRBD1 overexpression alleviates the anti-tumor phenotypes triggered by SCU in liver cancer cells

qRT-PCR analysis confirmed a robust and efficient induction of circSRBD1 expression in both Huh7 and HepG2 cells upon transfection with the OE-circSRBD1 plasmid, exhibiting substantially heightened RNA transcript levels in comparison to cells transfected with the empty vector control (Fig. 4A). Western blot analysis confirmed that SCU monotherapy significantly downregulated ESR1 protein expression. Conversely, combined treatment with OE-circSRBD1 markedly attenuated SCU’s inhibition of ESR1 expression. These findings mechanistically elucidate that circSRBD1 restores ESR1 expression by competitively binding miR-2682-5p (Fig. 4B). Flow cytometry revealed that SCU treatment alone triggered a pronounced arrest in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle. In contrast, the combinatorial treatment with SCU and OE-circSRBD1 exhibited a markedly diminished G2/M arrest relative to the SCU monotherapy group, thereby substantiating that circSRBD1 overexpression effectively counteracts the cell cycle blockade induced by SCU (Fig. 4C). Apoptosis assays indicated that SCU monotherapy significantly increased the apoptotic rate, whereas co-treatment with OE-circSRBD1 resulted in a significantly lower apoptotic rate, suggesting circSRBD1 overexpression counteracts SCU’s pro-apoptotic effect (Fig. 4D). Convergent evidence from both the CCK-8 proliferation assay and colony formation assay revealed that SCU treatment alone exerted a profound suppressive effect on both cellular proliferation and clonogenic capability. Strikingly, co-treatment with OE-circSRBD1 substantially restored proliferative activity, thereby providing functional validation that circSRBD1 overexpression effectively counteracts the growth-inhibitory effects induced by SCU (Fig. 4E, F).

Knockdown of circSRBD1 in Huh7 and HepG2 cell lines potentiates SCU-induced cell cycle arrest, apoptosis, and proliferation inhibition

Transfection was performed in Huh7 and HepG2 cells using a panel of three unique siRNAs (si-1, si-2, si-3) directed against circSRBD1. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis demonstrated that si-2 and si-3 elicited over 50% knockdown efficiency relative to controls, with si-3 exhibiting the highest efficacy. Consequently, si-3 was selected for the ensuing functional experiments (Fig. 5A). Western blot analysis revealed that SCU monotherapy downregulated ESR1 protein expression. Co-transfection with si-circSRBD1 significantly augmented this inhibitory effect on ESR1 expression (Fig. 5B). A distinct G2/M phase cell cycle arrest was triggered by SCU monotherapy, as revealed through flow cytometric analysis. This arrest was markedly potentiated by co-transfection with si-circSRBD1, indicating that circSRBD1 knockdown synergistically enhances SCU-induced cell cycle arrest (Fig. 5C). Apoptosis analysis indicated that SCU monotherapy increased the apoptotic rate, which was further significantly elevated by co-transfection with si-circSRBD1. This confirmed that circSRBD1 knockdown synergistically enhances SCU-induced apoptosis (Fig. 5D). Concordant data derived from both CCK-8 proliferation assay and colony formation assays revealed that SCU monotherapy profoundly suppresses cellular proliferation and clonogenic potential. Notably, co-transfection with si-circSRBD1 markedly intensified these inhibitory effects on cell growth, providing compelling evidence that silencing circSRBD1 significantly enhances the anti-proliferative potency of SCU (Fig. 5E, F).

Experimental validation of ESR1-mediated mechanisms and synergistic effects between SCU and fulvestrant in Huh7 and HepG2 cell lines

Quantitative PCR analysis demonstrated concentration-dependent downregulation of ESR1 mRNA expression in both Huh7 and HepG2 cell lines following 72-hour exposure to low or high concentrations of SCU. Elevated SCU concentrations resulted in significantly reduced ESR1 transcript levels (Fig. 6A). Western blot analysis revealed that SCU induced a dose-dependent downregulation of ESR1 protein expression, with significantly more pronounced suppression observed in the high-dose group compared to the low-dose monotherapy regimen. These protein-level findings offer robust validation that SCU mediates its antitumor activity through the targeted inhibition of ESR1 expression (Fig. 6B). The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) of fulvestrant (an antagonist of ESR1 [24]) was determined for both cell lines, yielding values of 19.62 µM for Huh7 cells and 20.5 µM for HepG2 cells after 72 h, confirming liver cancer cell susceptibility to estrogen receptor antagonism (S_Figure1B). Flow cytometry-based cell cycle analysis unveiled that SCU monotherapy triggered a significant halt in the G2/M phase of the cell cycle, disrupting normal cell cycle progression. The combination therapy group displayed a synergistic enhancement in cell cycle blockade, intensifying G2/M phase arrest beyond the capacity of either individual treatment, thereby highlighting a markedly more potent inhibition of proliferative advancement(Fig. 6C). The apoptotic rate in both Huh7 and HepG2 cell lines was markedly elevated following treatment with SCU alone, as determined by apoptosis assays. The combination therapy group exhibited a markedly higher apoptosis rate than the SCU monotherapy group, confirming synergistic pro-apoptotic effects (Fig. 6D). The anti-proliferative effect of SCU monotherapy was confirmed across both functional assays—the CCK-8 proliferation assay and the colony formation assay. The combination group exhibited stronger inhibitory effects on proliferative capacity and clonogenicity compared to single-agent treatments, functionally validating synergistic antitumor efficacy (Fig. 6E, F). Furthermore, the anticancer effects of SCU were corroborated in ex vivo experiments. Consistent with in vitro findings, SCU significantly attenuated tumor growth rates relative to the control group, with the combination group demonstrating even greater suppression. These results collectively indicate that fulvestrant enhances the antitumor activity of SCU against liver cancer (Figs. 7A-C).

Discussion

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) represents a promising reservoir of multi-component, multi-target therapeutic agents for modern antitumor drug discovery, grounded in its extensive clinical experience in cancer management [25, 26]. Modern pharmacological investigations have substantiated that endogenous bioactive constituents within traditional Chinese medicine—including flavonoids, alkaloids, polysaccharides, polyphenols, and saponins—demonstrate significant efficacy in anti-inflammatory, anti-radiation, antioxidant, anti-aging, anti-tumor, and immunomodulatory effects [27, 28]. SCU, the principal bioactive compound derived from scutellaria baicalensis, has garnered considerable scientific interest owing to its extensive pharmacological profile, encompassing potent anti-inflammatory and antioxidant activities. Growing evidence highlights its antitumor potential, positioning it as a promising candidate for novel anticancer therapies [10, 29].

The antitumor effects of SCU, particularly in liver cancer, are well documented [7, 30, 31]. However, existing research primarily focuses on phenotypic observations or broadly implicated signaling pathways (e.g., STAT3,JAK2/STAT3,STAT3/Girdin/AKT) [31–34]and the inhibition of CYP1A activity [35], with additional reports suggesting inhibition of liver cancer growth via the IDH1-α-KG-HIF1α axis [36] and reversal of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) by nano-formulations like PLGA-PEG-AEAA.SCU [37]. Although these findings highlight the multi-target potential of SCU, its mechanism of action has remained largely confined to the traditional framework of protein-coding gene and signaling pathway regulation. By elucidating a novel non-coding RNA-dependent regulatory mechanism, we have expanded the mechanistic understanding in this field. For the first time, it has been demonstrated that SCU exerts its anti-liver cancer effects through modulation of the specific competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) network “circSRBD1–miR-2682-5p–ESR1” offering new insights into its mechanism of action in liver cancer.

To systematically identify the core targets and mechanisms underlying SCU’s antitumor effects in liver cancer, we employed an integrated approach combining network pharmacology and transcriptomic analysis. Intersecting predicted targets with differentially expressed genes identified core genes significantly enriched in the estrogen signaling pathway, Functional Enrichment Analysis. Comprehensive Profiling of Protein-Protein Interaction Networks further pinpointed ESR1 as a pivotal hub gene, whose expression level demonstrated significant diagnostic value in liver cancer. These findings provide robust bioinformatic evidence linking SCU’s antitumor activity to the estrogen signaling pathway. In addition to the in vivo validation, our in vitro experiments confirmed that SCU dose-dependently downregulates ESR1 protein expression in Huh7 and HepG2 liver cancer cells, validating the bioinformatic predictions and identifying ESR1 as a key direct target of SCU in liver cancer.

Estrogen receptor 1 (ESR1), a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily, primarily mediates estrogen signal transduction [38]. Accumulating evidence indicates that ESR1 plays a critical role in the development and progression of liver cancer [39]. ESR1 is widely expressed in various human tissues, including the breast, prostate, uterus, liver, and bone [40]. As a target organ of estrogen signaling [39], the liver exhibits marked sexual dimorphism in tumor incidence: liver cancer occurs at significantly lower rates in females than in males, suggesting a potential protective effect of estrogen in women. Indeed, the liver is one of the most sexually dimorphic organs [41]. The incidence of liver cancer in males is approximately 2–3 times higher than in females, and male patients exhibit significantly lower overall survival rates compared to females—findings that further support the involvement of sex hormones in the pathophysiology of liver cancer [42]. However, emerging studies suggest that estrogen may promote hepatocyte proliferation under certain conditions, indicating its dual and context-dependent role in liver cancer development [43]. Notably, aromatase expression is frequently upregulated in human liver cancer tissues and cell lines, where tumor-promoting effects may be mediated through estrogen receptor-regulated growth-related signaling pathways. Thus, ESR1 may act as a tumor suppressor during early carcinogenesis but could contribute to tumor progression in advanced stages [44, 45]. ERα exists predominantly in two isoforms: ERα66, the full-length form, and the truncated variants ERα46 and ERα36 [46]. ERα46 has been associated with cell cycle arrest [47], whereas ERα36 promotes tumor growth through multiple mechanisms, including the EGFR/Src/ERK signaling pathway [48]. Moreover, the aromatase-induced inflammatory microenvironment may facilitate a splicing switch from ERα66 to ERα36 [49]. The functional activity of ERα depends on post-translational modifications [40]. Environmental pollutants such as hydroxylated polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (OH-PAHs) can bind to ERα, activate downstream signaling, induce epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and thereby enhance the migratory and invasive capacities of liver cancer cells [50]. A growing body of evidence demonstrates that certain compounds exert anti-liver cancer effects via modulation of ESR1. For instance, hederagenin markedly inhibits HepG2 cell proliferation and downregulates ESR1 protein expression in both cultured cells and xenograft tumor tissues, suggesting that its anti-tumor effects may be partially mediated through ESR1 regulation [51]. Similarly, oxyresveratrol suppresses liver cancer growth by inhibiting ESR1 expression [52]. The anti-tumor effect of fulvestrant involves estrogen receptor α and suppression of the Wnt signaling pathway [53]. In summary, the role of ESR1 in liver cancer is highly context-dependent and influenced by multiple factors, including receptor isoform expression, genetic background, gene polymorphisms [54], sex, environmental exposures, and disease stage. Given its complex and sometimes contradictory functions, further in-depth investigation is warranted to fully elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms [55].

A key finding of this study is the identification of a novel ceRNA axis, circSRBD1/miR-2682-5p/ESR1, that mediates the antitumor activity of SCU. Mechanistically, SCU significantly downregulated the stable circRNA, circSRBD1, while upregulating miR-2682-5p expression. Dual-luciferase reporter assays conclusively validated the direct binding interaction between miR-2682-5p and the 3’ untranslated regions (3’UTRs) of both circSRBD1 and ESR1 mRNA, offering compelling evidence for their functional integration into a competitive endogenous RNA regulatory network. Importantly, we further confirmed that miR-2682-5p does not target the linear SRBD1 mRNA transcript, thereby highlighting the specificity of this interaction for the circular RNA isoform. Functionally, circSRBD1 serves as a molecular sponge by capturing miR-2682-5p, which alleviates the post-transcriptional suppression of ESR1 mRNA induced by miR-2682-5p. Notably, the overexpression of circSRBD1 effectively reversed SCU-induced cell cycle arrest and apoptosis in liver cancer cells, while targeted silencing of circSRBD1 markedly enhanced these antitumor effects. These findings robustly affirm the central regulatory role of the circSRBD1/miR-2682-5p/ESR1 axis in orchestrating the multifaceted biological responses elicited by SCU.

In summary, our findings reveal that SCU downregulates circSRBD1, leading to increased bioavailability of miR-2682-5p, which consequently enhances the repression of its target gene, ESR1. This study systematically elucidates the molecular mechanism—specifically the novel circSRBD1/miR-2682-5p/ESR1 ceRNA axis—underpinning SCU’s anticancer effects in liver cancer. Collectively, our findings establish a mechanistic rationale for advancing the clinical translation of SCU, while also revealing key nodes within this regulatory network as potential targets for informing future therapeutic strategies.

Materials and methods

Experimental materials

Cell Culture: The human liver cancer cell lines Huh7 and HepG2 were acquired from the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China), and their identities were verified by STR profiling.

Key reagents

SCU (purity ≥ 98%, MedChemExpress - MCE), Fulvestrant (5 mg, MCE), RNA rapid extraction kit (ES Science, Yishan Biotech, Shanghai, China), Reverse transcription kit (All-in-One First-Strand Synthesis MasterMix, Yugong Biotech, Jiangsu, China), SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Yugong Biotech, Jiangsu, China), Actinomycin D (Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology, China), RNase R (LGC Biosearch Technologies, USA), Cell viability, apoptosis, and cell cycle distribution were assessed using commercial CCK-8, Annexin V-FITC/PI apoptosis detection, and PI-based cell cycle detection kits, respectively. Western blot antibodies against ESR1 (Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting circSRBD1, miR-2682-5p mimic and siRNA targeting ESR1 (designed and synthesized by Jipeng Biology, Jinan, Shandong, China), circSRBD1 overexpression plasmid (purchased from General Biology, Anhui, China), Lipofectamine 3000 (Invitrogen, California, USA) for cell transfection, used according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Instruments

Real-time quantitative PCR system (Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 5, Thermo Fisher Scientific), Flow cytometer (Beckman CytoFLEX), Multimode microplate reader (Biotek Cytation5), Microplate reader (Tecan NanoQuant M200 PRO) - Assuming “NanoQuant M200 PRO” is the model from Tecan, Western blot imaging system (Tanon 4800).

Experimental methods

Cell Culture and Drug Treatment, Cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM, enriched with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, and maintained in a humidified atmosphere at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. During pharmacological intervention, Huh7 and HepG2 cell lines were treated for 72 h with SCU (low concentration: 100 µg/mL, high concentration: 300 µg/mL), fulvestrant (3 µM) as monotherapies, or their respective combinations, following predetermined concentration gradients.

Transcriptome sequencing and analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cells in both the SCU-treated group and the control group. The purity, concentration, and integrity of RNA samples were rigorously assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer, a Qubit 3.0 fluorometer, and an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Only RNA samples meeting quality control criteria were used for library construction. Strand-specific mRNA libraries and small RNA libraries were prepared separately. For small RNA libraries, a Small RNA Sample Prep Kit was used following a standardized protocol involving adaptor ligation, reverse transcription, PCR amplification, and PAGE gel purification. For strand-specific mRNA libraries, ribosomal RNA was first depleted using a Ribo-off rRNA Depletion Kit. Library preparation was then performed using a U-mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit, including mRNA fragmentation, first-strand cDNA synthesis, adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification. DNA fragments of 300–600 bp were selected using AMPure XP beads. All constructed libraries underwent preliminary quantification with Qubit, insert size assessment on the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and precise quantification via qPCR. Paired-end sequencing (PE150) was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform. Raw sequencing data were processed using fastp for quality control, including trimming of adaptor sequences and filtering of low-quality reads, yielding high-quality clean data (Q30 > 90%). Clean data were aligned to an rRNA database using bowtie2 to remove residual ribosomal RNA sequences. The remaining reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 to determine mapping efficiency and analyze read distribution across exonic, intronic, and intergenic regions. Transcriptome data quality was comprehensively evaluated using RSeQC to assess sequencing saturation, potential RNA degradation, and sequence duplication levels. Transcript assembly and abundance quantification were performed using StringTie. Differential expression analysis was conducted using edgeR, with significantly differentially expressed genes defined by P.adj < 0.01 and |log2FC| > 1.25. The identification and quantification of circRNAs were performed using the CIRIquant software. The start and end positions of circRNAs and their host gene annotations were predicted by precisely identifying the back-splicing junction (BSJ) sites. The expression levels of circRNAs were counted based on the BSJ-supported reads and normalized using the SRPBM (spliced reads per billion mapping) method.This portion of the analysis was completed by KAITAI-bio (Kaitai Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China).

Total RNA and genomic DNA extraction

Total RNA was meticulously extracted from cellular samples using the ES Science RNA Rapid Extraction Kit (Yishan Biotech, China), strictly adhering to the manufacturer’s protocol. Concurrently, genomic DNA was isolated from identical specimens through the TIANGEN Genomic DNA Extraction Kit (Beijing, China), ensuring consistent sample origin and experimental integrity. The concentration and purity of both nucleic acids were precisely assessed using the NanoDrop One spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), providing reliable quantification essential for downstream molecular analyses.

RT-PCR and qPCR experiments

Gene expression levels were quantified via reverse transcription followed by quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR). First-strand cDNA was synthesized from total RNA using the All-in-One First-Strand Synthesis MasterMix or the Evo M-MLV RT Kit, following the enclosed protocols. Subsequently, gene amplification was conducted on a real-time PCR system utilizing a qPCR Premix reagent containing SYBR Green fluorescent dye. All gene-specific primers were designed and supplied by Jipeng Biotechnology (Shandong, China). GAPDH and U6 small nuclear RNA were used as endogenous controls for normalizing circRNA/mRNA and miRNA expression levels, respectively. The amplification process was monitored in real-time, and cycle threshold (CT) values were acquired on a QuantStudio 5 system. Relative gene expression was calculated employing the comparative 2^(−ΔΔCt) method.

RNase R resistance assay

A total of 2 µg of RNA was incubated with RNase R (3 U/µg) at 37 °C for 30 min. Following treatment, both RNase R-digested and undigested RNA samples were analyzed by RT-qPCR to measure the levels of linear SRBD1 and circSRBD1, thereby verifying the stability of circSRBD1 against RNase R-mediated degradation.

Actinomycin D assay (Act-D)

To assess the stability of linear SRBD1 and circSRBD1 RNAs, Huh7 and HepG2 cells were treated with 2 µg/mL actinomycin D for 24 h. Total RNA was then extracted and analyzed by RT-qPCR to measure remaining transcript levels.

Determination of fulvestrant IC₅₀

Huh7 and HepG2 cells were treated with a range of fulvestrant concentrations for 72 h. Cell viability was measured using the CCK-8 assay, and the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC₅₀) was calculated.

CCK-8 proliferation assay

Cell growth at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h after plating the cells in 96-well plates. At each time point, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added per well, followed by a 2-hour incubation. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan NanoQuant M200 PRO), and inhibition rates were calculated from the data.

Colony formation assay

Treated cells were seeded at low density (500 cells per well) into 6-well plates and cultured for 14 days to enable colony development. We then fixed the colonies with methanol, stained them with crystal violet, and counted them by microscopic observation.

Flow cytometric analysis of cell cycle

Cells were fixed with cold 70% ethanol and permeabilized, then incubated with RNase A to remove RNA and stained with propidium iodide (PI) in the dark for 30 min at room temperature. We measured DNA content using a Beckman CytoFLEX flow cytometer and then analyzed the cell cycle distribution with dedicated software.

Flow cytometric assessment of apoptosis

Apoptosis was analyzed by dual staining with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI). Cells were resuspended in binding buffer and stained with both reagents for 15 min at room temperature in the dark. then ran the labeled cells on a Beckman CytoFLEX flow cytometer to quantify viable, early apoptotic, late apoptotic, and necrotic populations.

Western blot analysis

Total cellular proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer containing a protease inhibitor cocktail and quantified via a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay. Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per sample) were separated by SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes using electrophoretic blotting. The membranes were then blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at ambient temperature, followed by an overnight incubation with specific primary antibodies at 4 °C. After thorough washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were detected using ECL substrate and quantified with ImageJ software.

Functional validation of circSRBD1

CircRNA knockdown was achieved via siRNA transfection employing Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent, while overexpression was accomplished through plasmid transfection, to assess the functional impact on cell cycle progression, proliferation capacity, and apoptotic activity.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

The pmirGLO reporter vectors (Jiejin Biotechnology, Jinan, Shandong, China) containing either wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) sequences of circSRBD1 and ESR1 were co-transfected into Huh7 cells together with the miR-2682-5p mimic or a negative control (NC) mimic. Luciferase activity was measured using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

An RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay was performed using a commercial RIP Kit (GENESSEED, Guangzhou, China). Cell lysates were incubated with magnetic beads conjugated to either a human anti-Ago2 antibody (Proteintech China, Wuhan, China) or a negative control IgG antibody in RIP buffer, following the manufacturer’s protocol. After sequential washes with the provided wash buffer, protein components of the bead-bound complexes were digested with Proteinase K. The co-precipitated RNA was then purified and subjected to quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) analysis.

Animal experiments

This study utilized five-week-old male BALB/c nude mice, weighing 18–22 g, housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions. A subcutaneous xenograft model was established by injecting a single-cell suspension of 1 × 10⁶ Huh7 cells into the right flank of each mouse. Once tumor volumes reached the target range of 280–450 mm³, the animals were randomly divided into three experimental groups (n = 4 per group). Randomization was stratified based on individual tumor volume to ensure a comparable baseline tumor burden across all groups prior to treatment initiation. The control group was administered an equivalent volume of solvent (200 µL) via oral gavage. The SCU treatment group (SCU), administered SCU solution (50 mg/kg, 200 µL) via oral gavage. Combination treatment group (SCU + FUL), administered SCU solution (50 mg/kg, 200 µL) via oral gavage combined with an intratumoral injection of fulvestrant (5 mg/kg in 50 µL per mouse). The experimental duration was 7 days. The longest (L) and perpendicular shortest (W) tumor diameters were recorded daily using a digital caliper. The tumor volume (V) was subsequently calculated using the standard formula: V = (L × W²) / 2, and expressed in cubic millimeters (mm³). Body weight was monitored daily to assess potential drug toxicity. At the experimental endpoint (day 7), all animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation. The entire tumor masses were then surgically resected, photographed for documentation, and precisely weighed. Tumor tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde as needed. Animals were housed under standard conditions.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three biologically independent experiments. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (Version 10). Two-group comparisons used a two-tailed Student’s t-test; for more than two groups, one-way ANOVA was applied with an appropriate post-hoc test after confirming equal variances. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001 indicating significance levels.
