Cost-effectiveness of non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) versus direct amniocentesis for screening of fetal chromosomal aneuploidies in Brazilian private health system
Authors: Janaina Cardoso Nunes Marinho, Junior Vitorino Fandim, Ricardo dos Santos Simões, Julia Simões Correa Galendi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT) has emerged as a highly accurate screening modality for fetal aneuploidies. However, its cost-effectiveness in resource-constrained settings remains unclear. This study evaluated the cost-effectiveness of NIPT versus direct amniocentesis for aneuploidy screening in pregnant women with variable risk profiles from the perspective of the Brazilian Private healthcare system.MethodsA decision tree model with a one-year time horizon compared NIPT-based screening followed by confirmatory amniocentesis versus amniocentesis. The analysis included pregnant women with singleton pregnancies at risk due to abnormal first trimester screening. Clinical parameters were derived from systematic literature review, while costs and resource utilization data were obtained from the UNIMETRICS database. Outcomes included cost per Quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), cost per procedure-related loss avoided and cost per amniocentesis avoided. Deterministic and probabilistic sensitivity analyses assessed parameter uncertainty using 10,000 Monte Carlo simulations.ResultsIn the base case analysis, NIPT implementation resulted in an incremental cost of IntI $ 4.874,52 (BRL 2,790,99) per patient per year. The strategy reduced amniocenteses by 18.4% and procedure-related losses by 0.06% annually. The incremental cost-effectiveness ratio was IntI $ 34.064,44 (BRL 21.453,96) per QALY. Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent results across risk strata. Sensitivity analysis revealed that utility values, amniocentesis acceptance rates and procedure-related loss risk were the most influential parameters affecting cost-effectiveness outcomes.ConclusionsThe NIPT-amniocentesis strategy might be cost-effective, because NIPT implementation slightly reduces invasive testing and associated complications, and incurs moderately incremental costs within the Brazilian supplementary healthcare system. These findings provide crucial evidence for healthcare policy decisions regarding NIPT assessment in prenatal screening programs in middle-income healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12962-026-00732-1.Keywords: Non-invasive prenatal testing, Cost-effectiveness analysis, Aneuploidy screening, Amniocentesis, Health technology assessment, Brazil

Introduction

Numerical chromosomal abnormalities, or aneuploidies, are defined by deviations from the normal chromosome count, such as the presence of extra chromosomes (trisomies) or missing chromosomes (monosomies) [1]. Recurrent examples of aneuploidy syndromes include Patau’s syndrome (trisomy 13 – T13), Edward’s syndrome (trisomy 18 – T18), Down’s syndrome (trisomy 21 – T21), and commonly encountered sex chromosome aneuploidies (SCA) [1, 2].

Given the substantial morbidity associated with these conditions, prenatal screening has become an integral element of obstetric care [1]. Traditional screening approaches rely on maternal serum biochemical markers, ultrasound assessments, or combinations of these modalities [2]. To identify fetal aneuploidies invasive diagnostic procedures, notably amniocentesis or chorionic villus sampling, may be pursued [1, 3]. However, these invasive procedures carry risks, such as pregnancy loss, preterm birth, and infection. The discovery of cell-free fetal DNA in maternal plasma has enabled the emergence of non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT), which analyzes fetal genetic material and markedly reduces the risks associated with conventional invasive techniques [3, 4].

Current NIPT platforms utilize advanced technologies such as next-generation sequencing (NGS), massively parallel sequencing (MPS), chromosome selective sequencing, and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis to identify fetal chromosomal abnormalities with high sensitivity and specificity [5]. NIPT has demonstrated excellent performance, with reported sensitivities close to 99% for T21 and specificities above 99.8%, although effectiveness for less frequent aneuploidies (such as T18, T13) may be reduced [3, 4]. Despite its strengths, NIPT is considered a screening rather than diagnostic tool, and positive findings require confirmation via invasive diagnostic procedures [4].

Previous economic evaluations show significantly divergent cost-effectiveness results [6]. The heterogeneity in findings reflects differences in model structures, time horizons, health outcomes, contextual factors, and analytical approaches across studies [7]. The objective of this cost-effectiveness analysis was to evaluate the value of NIPT as a screening tool of fetal chromosomal aneuploidies in pregnancy from the perspective of the Brazilian Supplementary Health System.

Methods

Study design

This study followed the recommendations of the Methodological Guidelines of the Ministry of Health [8]. We reported this study according to the checklist of the Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) Task Force Report [9]. This is a cost effectiveness study from the perspective of the Brazilian Private Health, which provides supplementary coverage for the Brazilian population. Ethical approval was not required according to resolution (No. 510/2016).

Population

Pregnant women with a singleton pregnancy at risk for fetal chromosomal aneuploidies due to abnormal first trimester screening. Women with multiple pregnancies were not included in this evaluation.

Subgroup populations of women at low and high risk for aneuploidieswere evaluated. High-risk pregnancies were defined as at least one of the following criteria: advanced maternal age (> 35 years old) and a personal or family history of aneuploidies. Women were considered low risk if maternal age (≤ 35 years old) and no personal or family history of aneuploidies. Both higher risk and lower risk pregnant women should have either a positive standard biochemical screening or fetal abnormalities detected by ultrasound that justified continuing aneuploidies investigation.

Interventions and comparators

Upon clinical indication to pursue investigation for aneuploidies after positive first trimester screening (e.g., biochemical screening including alpha-fetoprotein (AFP), human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and PAPP-A and nuchal translucency (NT) evaluation in ultrasound), the comparator group underwent direct amniocentesis, which is currently the standard diagnostic procedure. The intervention group underwent next-generation sequencing (NGS)-based NIPT as an additional screening test strategy, and in the case of a positive result, proceeded with further investigation through an invasive test (amniocentesis).

Context

Brazil’s private healthcare system operates alongside the universal Unified Health System (SUS), providing supplementary coverage for approximately 25% of the population [10]. The beneficiaries represent mostly higher-income urban residents. Within the scope of the private healthcare system, UNIMED is the largest health insurer, representing 35% of the private market, serving over 20 million beneficiaries through 354 cooperatives and maintaining significant market presence across 83% of the national territory [10].

Model structure

A decision tree was developed to compare NIPT-amniocentesis-strategy versus amniocentesis-strategy (Fig. 1) in Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond WA, USA). The decision tree was designed to reflect the diagnostic accuracy of NIPT from a systematic literature review [11] detailed in Table 1A, Figs. 1A and 2A and Table 2A (supplementary material). Patients who test positive undergo amniocentesis and may either experience procedure-related loss or have no complications. In the amniocentesis-strategy group, patients may proceed without complications or may experience loss because of the procedure. A 1-year time horizon was considered to be sufficient to capture the effects of the aneuploidies investigation through the end of pregnancy. We assumed that all pregnancies would result in births because there is no clear legal framework in Brazil for indicating pregnancy termination in case of aneuploidy detection. No discount rate was applied due to the short-term time horizon.

Probabilities

A rapid systematic review was conducted to estimate the specificity and sensitivity of NIPT, as well as the prevalence of aneuploidies, which was previously published elsewhere [11]. A summary of the rapid review is provided in the supplementary material. The prevalence of aneuploidies, including T13, T18, T21 and ACS, in pregnant women at risk for aneuploidy due to abnormal first trimester screening considering 22 studies with 306,641 patients was estimated at 0.8%. The prevalence for two subgroups was estimated: higher risk (pregnant women > 35 years-old or with personal or familial history of aneuploidy in previous pregnancies) and lower risk (age ≤ 35 and no personal or familial history of aneuploidy in previous pregnancies). The prevalence in studies including higher risk women was estimated at 0.97% (12 studies, 116,873 patients), and in lower risk women was estimated at 0.2% (4 studies, 87,753 patients) [11].

A rapid literature search was conducted to estimate the likelihood that a pregnant woman with variable risk would choose to pursue further investigation of fetal aneuploidies in scenarios with and without the use of NIPT. No Brazilian studies were identified that reported on the acceptability of amniocentesis or NIPT. An additional search was performed in the ANS database to determine the absolute number of amniocenteses performed in women aged 35 years or older, in comparison to the number of vaginal deliveries or cesarean sections performed in this age group [13]. In 2023, a total of 237 ultrasound-guided amniocenteses were performed in this population, compared to 161,960 births, indicating that only a minority of women currently opt for invasive testing for fetal aneuploidies [13]. Based on these findings and mostly on expert opinion, it was assumed that 80% of women currently opt out when offered direct amniocentesis in the base case and alternative.

The potential acceptability of NIPT was derived from a study involving Croatian women aged 35 years or younger without a family history of Down syndrome. In that study, the acceptability of NIPT was found to be 91% [14].

Finally, the probability of procedure-related loss following amniocentesis was obtained from a previously published systematic review, which identified the procedure-related loss risk to be 0.3% (95% CI 0.11 to 0.49%) [12].

Utility values

Health state utility values were obtained from Kuppermann et al. (2016), who elicited preferences regarding outcomes associated with contemporary prenatal genetic testing [15]. In that study, utilities were derived from a sample of women desiring prenatal testing recruited in the United States, using time-trade off method [15]. Because no utility estimates derived from a Brazilian population were available for these health states, the potential impact of this assumption was explored in sensitivity analyses.

Costs and resources used

All prices and costs are presented in 2025 Brazilian reais (BRL). For international comparability, costs were converted to US dollars (USD) using the World Bank purchasing power parity (PPP) conversion factor (GDP-based), using the most recently available for 2024: BRL 2.48 per international dollar [17].

Real world resource use and costs were collected from UNIMETRICS, a large database from UNIMED, a private healthcare payer in Brazil [18]. UNIMETRICS Panel contains data regarding practiced procedures in the exchange system of all UNIMED cooperatives, and data collection included resource consumption and cost data from the last three years [18]. Cost of NIPT were estimated assuming the use of massively parallel/targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) technique. Other methods such as PCR-based counting methods, SNP-based genotyping without sequencing and microarray-based methods (non-NGS) were not investigated.

Costs associated with first-trimester prenatal assessment were included: at least two prenatal consultations, costs of morphological ultrasound and nuchal translucency, and costs of two genetic counseling sessions (one pre-test and one post-test) [19]. To estimate the cost of amniocentesis, the costs of the procedure itself, as well as biochemical laboratory evaluation and karyotyping, were considered. Unitary data cost for each procedure is detailed in the supplementary material (Table 3A, 4A and 5A).

Model outputs and sensitivity analyses

We calculated the incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER). Model outcomes were expressed as cost per quality-adjusted life years (QALY) gained, cost per procedure-related loss avoided and cost per amniocentesis avoided. Structural sensitivity analysis was conducted to test the assumption of the proportion of women who opt out in the amniocentesis arm: 50%, 60% and 90% opt out probabilities were tested.

A deterministic sensitivity analysis was conducted, in which the parameters and costs used in the model were varied to assess changes in the final ICER. For deterministic sensitivity analysis, all clinical parameters varied in their 95% confidence interval, and cost input parameters varied in 20%. The results were displayed in a tornado diagram. For the probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA), 10,000 second-order Monte Carlo simulations were performed. For PSA, beta distributions were applied to probabilities and utility values, and gamma distributions were applied to costs.

There is no willingness-to-pay threshold established for the Brazilian private healthcare setting. Therefore, a cost-effectiveness acceptability curve was built to assess probability of cost-effectiveness for several different thresholds.

Model validation

To validate the model, experts were invited to check the appropriateness of the model concept and input data (i.e., face validation). In addition, cross validation was performed by comparing the assumptions applied in our model to those of similar models and by comparing the model outputs with the results of other modeling studies.

Results

Base case results

In the base case analysis, the incremental cost per patient per year was IntI $ 2.842,44. The intervention was associated with 0,083 QALYs gained, an annual reduction of 0.06% in procedure-related loss and an 18.4% reduction in the number of amniocenteses performed. The ICER was estimated at IntI $ 34.064,44 per QALY gained, IntI $ 5.147.649,51 per procedure-related loss avoided and IntI $ 15.442,95 per amniocentesis avoided. Subgroup and sensitivity analyses

Subgroup analyses are shown in supplementary material (Table 6A). The high-risk subgroup showed slightly higher incremental costs per patient (Intl $ 2.797,23 vs. 2.858,31) and marginally higher ICERs for both outcome measures. Both subgroups achieved similar reductions in amniocenteses performed (approximately 18–19%), while the impact on procedure-related loss prevention in both groups (0.05–0.06%) and QALY gains (0,083 − 0,084) were similar.

The structural sensitivity analysis examined the impact of varying opt-out probability rates for amniocentesis on model outcomes, supplementary material (Table 7A). When the amniocentesis opt-out probability was reduced to 50%, representing a scenario where more women accept invasive testing, the NIPT-amniocentesis strategy was dominant strategy with cost savings, and corresponding improvements in both procedure-related loss avoidance (0.14%) and amniocentesis reduction (48.2%) and QALYs (0,37). Conversely, when the opt-out probability increased to 90%, incremental costs rose to IntL $ 3.858,48per patient, resulting in an ICER of IntI $ of 38.973, 19 per QALY gained.

The DSA results are presented in Fig. 2. The utility values for women who opt out from screening and for negative NIPT result were the parameter with higher impact on the ICER leading to scenarios in which the NIPT- amniocentesis is a dominated strategy (increases costs and reduces overall QALYs). The probability of opting out of amniocentesis was the third most influential parameter on model outcomes, with upper and lower bound estimates causing substantial variation in the ICER. Other parameters, including the cost of NIPT, cost of amniocentesis, and test specificity, had a comparatively minor effect on the ICER estimates within the ranges tested.

Discussion

The results of this economic model demonstrate that a strategy incorporating NIPT for fetal aneuploidy screening in pregnant women at risk leads to QALY gains, as well as small reductions in both the number of amniocentesis performed and procedure-related losses. However, the NIPT strategy also results in incremental costs for the Brazilian private healthcare system. These findings highlight the complex trade-offs inherent in implementing screening technologies within healthcare systems.

The exceptional negative predictive value of NIPT makes it an invaluable screening tool, as it reliably excludes true negative results while maintaining a low false-negative rate [3, 4]. As anticipated, the introduction of NIPT was associated with a substantial reduction in amniocentesis procedures [3, 4]. However, the implementation of NIPT as a screening modality has the potential to increase patient acceptability and interest in pursuing chromosomal investigation, which is not currently routinely sought within the supplementary healthcare setting. This dual effect of reducing invasive procedures while potentially expanding screening uptake explains the observed incremental costs.

It is also noteworthy that the proportion of women who choose to pursue amniocentesis in the comparator arm has high impact on the ICER. Notably, in scenarios where amniocentesis uptake is higher, the value of the screening test becomes correspondingly greater. The deterministic sensitivity analysis revealed that the utility values related to opting out of screening (e.g. not knowing) and the utility related to a negative NIPT result are very influential, leading to scenarios in which the NIPT-amniocentesis strategy is dominated. Previous preference studies have shown that women place high value on knowing if the pregnancy is related to aneuploidy, more than avoidance of invasive procedures [15].

Our analysis did not include downstream costs for raising a child with T13, T18 or T21, because these are out of scope considering the payer perspective. Moreover, Brazil’s Penal Code (from 1940) generally criminalizes pregnancy termination, with penalties for the woman and anyone performing the procedure, unless the pregnancy fits one of the strictly defined exceptions authorized by a Supreme Federal Court decision (ADPF 54) (e.g., pregnancy resulting from rape, risk to the pregnant woman’s life or health or fetal anencephaly). Because there is no clear legal framework in Brazil for indicating pregnancy termination in case of aneuploidy detection, these costs are not covered by healthcare insurance. For consistency, utility values related to raising a child with T13, T18 or T21 were also not included.

The exclusion of pregnancy termination option of the model can have a large impact on the ICER, explaining discrepancies related to conclusions regarding cost-effectiveness, as has been reported in a previous systematic review of prenatal testing models [7]. However, similar to previous evaluations, we found NIPT consistently reduced invasive procedures by approximately 18%, though this reduction was lower than the 85–95% reductions reported in populations with higher baseline amniocentesis uptake [6, 20, 21]. Our results parallel findings from Thailand, where NIPT cost-effectiveness was highly sensitive to test pricing [21]. Importantly, our high ICER per procedure-related loss avoided (BRL 3,242,016) is consistent with systematic reviews showing that NIPT’s primary economic value derives from reducing invasive procedures rather than preventing pregnancy loss [6, 20, 21].

The model incorporated multiple outcome measures, including cost per procedure-related loss avoided, cost per amniocentesis avoided, and cost per QALY gained. Although QALYs are the outcome most closely aligned with standard health technology assessment frameworks, the limited availability of context-specific utility values introduced substantial uncertainty into these estimates. In this context, alternative outcomes—such as procedure-related loss avoided and amniocentesis avoided—offer information that is directly relevant to health system organization, as they inform the expected demand for invasive diagnostic procedures and, consequently, the planning of service capacity, including the number of facilities, trained specialists, and procedural resources required.Beyond traditional effectiveness and cost offsets, there are novel elements of value such as reduction in uncertainty, insurance value (financial and health risk protection), the value of hope, improved equity, and scientific spillovers that may augment traditional cost-effectiveness analysis [22]. In the context of NIPT, these unmeasured elements could include the psychological benefit of reduced anxiety associated with the non-invasive nature of testing, the insurance value provided by having access to genetic information for family planning decisions, and the broader societal value of advancing precision medicine approaches [22]. While our analysis provides important insights into the direct clinical and economic impacts of NIPT implementation, a more comprehensive value assessment incorporating these additional elements might yield different conclusions regarding the overall value proposition of NIPT screening in prenatal care.

One strength of this analysis is the cost data applied to this analysis represents real world data from the largest private healthcare payer in Brazil [18]. In addition, a thorough systematic review was conducted to inform probabilities of testing positive with NIPT [11]. However, several limitations of this cost-effectiveness analysis warrant acknowledgment.

First, patient acceptance of direct amniocentesis, despite being a parameter of substantial impact on model outcomes, was based on assumptions derived from indirect data from the National Supplementary Health Agency (ANS) database rather than direct patient preference studies [13]. To counter this limitation, we tested several alternative assumptions in structural sensitivity analyses, but future research incorporating primary data on patient decision-making would strengthen these projections.

Second, patient acceptability of NIPT was taken from previous experience from Croatian population [14]. However, it should be noted that the acceptability of amniocentesis in this population was 53% prior to receiving genetic counseling and increased to 74% after counseling [14], substantially higher than the estimate obtained from ANS data. Therefore, it should be expected that preference of Croatian women might not be representative of Brazilian women, and it would be opportune to update model results with Brazilian real-world data when it becomes available.

Lastly, the analysis considered only amniocentesis as the invasive diagnostic procedure, although chorionic villus sampling represents an alternative approach [1]. However, the costs associated with chorionic villus sampling are comparable to those of amniocentesis. Furthermore, validation with clinical specialists confirmed that amniocentesis is currently preferred in Brazil due to its association with slightly lower procedure-related loss rates [12].

Other non–NGS-based approaches to NIPT, which typically target the most common whole-chromosome aneuploidies (particularly trisomies 21, 18, and 13), rely on alternative methodologies such as targeted amplification assays, quantitative PCR-based techniques, or microarray-based platforms rather than massively parallel sequencing. These methods generally focus on detecting dosage imbalances in specific chromosomes, allowing assessment of selected copy number variations (CNVs), such as recurrent microdeletions or microduplications, depending on the platform design. These methods are currently not routinely available in Brazil, limiting their relevance to current context, but may offer a more cost-effective alternative to sequencing-based NIPT, particularly in resource-constrained settings that could be explored in future studies.

Conclusion

This cost-effectiveness analysis demonstrates that while NIPT implementation as a screening strategy for fetal aneuploidies might be cost-effective, as it reduces invasive testing and associated procedure-related losses, even though it incurs moderately incremental costs within the Brazilian supplementary healthcare system. The high negative predictive value and excellent screening performance of NIPT support its clinical utility; however, the resource planning for downstream procedures can be heavily influenced by potential for increased testing uptake.
