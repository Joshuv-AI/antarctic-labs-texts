Hygiene and sanitation-related socio-ecological contexts and constraints of communities in districts with persistent trachoma, Southern Ethiopia
Authors: Dawit Seyum Buda, Naomi Lorrain Nkoane, Thinavhuyo Robert Netangaheni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersistence and recrudescence of trachoma remain major challenges delaying progress towards elimination of the disease in many countries, including Ethiopia. Limited evidence exists regarding factors contributing to the persistence and recrudescence of trachoma globally. This study highlights the importance of understanding socio-ecological characteristics of communities within persistent and recrudescent districts.AimTo explore the socio-ecological contexts and constraints of communities in districts with persistent trachoma.SettingTwo districts were selected from South Omo and Gofa Zone, representing lowland pastoralist and highland farming communities, respectively from the South Ethiopia Regional State.MethodsA community-based qualitative study was conducted in 2023, involving eleven key informant interviews involving District Health Office Heads, Integrated Eye Health Workers, Health Extension Workers, Ophthalmologist, Optometrist and Community leaders. In addition, six focus group discussions (FGDs) were conducted with local leadership and community representatives. Interviews and discussions were digitally recorded and thematically analysed using Atlas.ti 23.FindingsEnvironmental factors affecting latrine ownership, such as negative experiences with poor latrines and perceptions of open defecation as freedom, hinder favourable hygiene and sanitation practices. Factors hampering the effective delivery of the F&E strategy include negative attitudes towards latrines, viewing them as imposed interventions, and using open defecation as a rodent control strategy. The creation of open defecation-free villages has been affected by mismanagement and the destruction of community latrines. Farming and pastoralism disconnect individuals from latrine access, and women face various concerns related to latrine use, including health, safety, comfort, and privacy.ConclusionSeveral socio-ecological factors, ranging from individual to societal levels, contribute to conditions and behaviours that worsen sub-optimal hygiene and sanitation practices, hence contributing to the persistence of trachoma. Reinforcing both existing and innovative facial cleanliness and environmental improvement activities tailored to community contexts is essential to address persistent trachoma.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11777-7.

Introduction

Initially aimed for elimination by 2020, trachoma is a public health problem for over 136 million people, mostly living in sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia and is responsible for the blindness or visual impairment of about 1.9 million people [1]. Since the pre-historic period as indicated by Taylor, in his seminal work about the history of trachoma, congestion, over crowdedness and settlements were responsible for the ancient transmission of the disease [2]. Historical and current evidence worldwide show that higher rates of infectious trachoma are often linked to poor facial cleanliness, overcrowded settlements, and limited access to sanitation facilities at both household and community levels. Therefore, access to water and sanitation is crucial in determining the prevalence and endemicity of trachoma in communities [1, 3, 4].

While progress has been made, Ethiopia bears more than half of the global trachoma burden with over 67 million people living in trachoma-endemic areas in 2022 [1]. Among 886 districts which were endemic by 2013 and 2014 at baseline, 320 districts achieved the WHO elimination threshold of infectious trachoma prevalence of less than 5% in children aged 1–9 years and had stopped mass drug administration (MDA). Currently, there are 566 endemic districts that require intervention [5]. Despite all the efforts, Ethiopia is far away from achieving the elimination goal mainly due to persistence and recrudescence of Trachoma [6].

The issue of persistence and recrudescence related to trachoma came into prominence since the WHO informal consultations held in Decatur Atlanta in 2021 [a7]. Persistence refers to an intervention unit, often a district, that has had at least two impact surveys showing TF1–9 rates of 5% or higher, without ever achieving rates below 5%. Recrudescence means an intervention unit has had at least one surveillance survey with TF1–9 rates of 5% or higher [7]. Both imply an ongoing presence of trachoma in a specific intervention unit despite the recommended rounds of MDA [6, 8–11]. Since the WHO informal consultation, there is an evolving body of literature about the social and environmental contexts of communities living in areas with persistent and recurring trachoma in Ethiopia [6, 8–11].

The importance of hygiene and sanitation for Trachoma Elimination has been recognized in the Surgery, Antibiotics, Facial cleanliness and Environmental improvement (SAFE) strategy [1]. Improvements in water and sanitation, and reduction in unclean faces are associated with significant reduction of trachoma within affected communities [4]. Another study found that having a washing station in schools is associated with lower rates of trachoma prevalence in communities around the school [12]. A systematic review found that children with unclean faces are at a higher risk of contracting infectious trachoma [13, 14]. More importantly, consistent latrine utilization is more effective than latrine ownership, which is defined as physical access to the facility [13, 15]. Based on the transmission pathways, there is clear biological plausibility to appreciate the role of water and sanitation. Especially when such interventions are complemented with proper hygiene and sanitation practices, would contribute towards transmission breaking [4, 12, 16].

On the other side, there are differing opinions and inconclusive conclusions about the impact of water and sanitation for trachoma elimination. Some argued that investing on water and sanitation for trachoma elimination is not evidence-based intervention [17]. Recent evidence from various countries and regions within Ethiopia, based on community-based randomized trials, shows either weak or no associations [1, 18]. While the public health benefits of improved access to water, sanitation services, and better hygiene practices are undeniable, evidence questioning the direct link between these factors and trachoma elimination creates significant challenges for programmatic and policy efforts. Community-based trials in Western Africa and Ethiopia have not provided sufficient evidence to support the claim that latrines contribute to reducing trachoma [1, 4, 18].

In this study, the researchers aimed to provide important evidence to accelerate trachoma elimination efforts in Ethiopia, inform policy and practice, and help accelerate the elimination process [6]. This research expands existing knowledge and provides new insights by bridging public health and social sciences through qualitative approaches [6, 8–11]. The study draws on information-rich participants from nomadic/pastoralist who rely on extensive livestock raring in arid and semi-arid lowlands and agricultural communities in Southern Ethiopia. Therefore, this study aims to explore the socio-ecological contexts of communities within districts characterized by persistent trachoma in Southern Ethiopia.

Methods

The study setting

The study was conducted in two districts of South Ethiopia, namely Nyangatom and Uba Debretsehay. Nyangatom is predominantly pastoralist lowland while Uba Debretsehay is predominantly farming highland communities, respectively. The two districts are characterised by persistently high trachoma prevalence above the WHO threshold for elimination (Fig. 1).

The initial Trachoma baseline surveys in Uba Debretsehay and Nyangatom were conducted in 2009 and 2013, revealing TF prevalence rates of 56.2% and 20.2%, respectively. Between 2011 and 2023, eight rounds of MDA were administered in Uba Debretsehay and six rounds in Nyangatom. Despite regular WASH interventions, there was limited implementation of facial cleanliness and environmental improvement (F&E) interventions in both districts. Surveys in 2016 and 2021 revealed a significant reduction in TF prevalence to 0.4% and 9.9% in Uba Debretsehay and Nyangatom, respectively. Consequently, MDA was halted in Uba Debretsehay but continued in Nyangatom. However, subsequent surveys in 2020 and 2022 indicated a resurgence in TF prevalence to 15.5% and 20.1% in Uba Debretsehay and Nyangatom, respectively.

Study design and study period

A community-based cross-sectional exploratory study was conducted between June and October 2023. Diverse groups of participants were purposively selected for key informant interview (KII) and focus group discussions (FGD).

Study participants and recruitment procedure

Sixty-seven participants were purposively selected and recruited based on their direct engagement in Trachoma Program activities such as coordination, treatment delivery and supervision. Participants include service providers, local authorities and community representatives identified for their robust engagement and understanding of the trachoma context in the districts.

Sample size determination and sampling technique

Participants were enrolled to explore and describe opinions and perceptions to address the research question. In this study, six focus group discussions (FGDs) and eleven in-depth interviews were conducted with participants from diverse socio-economic, academic, and professional backgrounds, including community members and local authorities. The final sample size was determined by the saturation of information.

Data collection procedure

After reviewing relevant literature, we developed interview and discussion guides to explore factors that might increase exposure to infectious trachoma, such as water access, hygiene and sanitation practices, community interactions, and population dynamics. These guides were translated into local languages and pretested, leading to minor adjustments. Data collection involved face-to-face interviews using open-ended questions and probes, lasting 30–45 min. Interviews were digitally recorded and supplemented with field notes, ensuring participant privacy throughout the process. From the extensive data set generated through key informant interviews and focus group discussions, the treatment-related characteristics of the communities have been previously reported elsewhere [11]. Data collection continued until information saturation was achieved. The qualitative guides are attached in a supplementary file (Supplementary File 1).

Data quality assurance

To ensure study credibility, evidence based, expert-reviewed and context-informed tools were used [19, 20]. Key informants and focus group discussion (FGD) participants were selected for their deep understanding of the context. Furthermore, the researcher facilitated FGDs and KIIs.

Qualitative data management

In this study, qualitative data analysis began at the data collection sites, incorporating field notes, voice recordings, and debriefings. The recordings were accurately labelled along with the field notes, and the audio was translated verbatim by a social science expert. Using ATLAS ti, an AI-generated summary was reviewed to gain an overall understanding of the data elements and key areas reported by participants [21]. This was followed by rigorous manual coding by two coders and AI coding to ensure quality (Supplementary File 2). Themes and sub-themes were then generated and analysed accordingly. Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) requirements were adhered and reported [22].

Ethical consideration

Ethical clearance was obtained from the University of South Africa, research, and ethics board (NHREC Registration number: Rec-240816-052 CREC Reference number: 45378932-CREC_CHS_2023). In addition, Public Health Institute of the Southern Nations supported the fieldwork (ZS7-16/9900 _2023). Participation was entirely voluntary, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw and discontinue their involvement at any time during the data collection process. All collected data was handled with the highest level of confidentiality.

Findings

Socio-demographic characteristics of key informant interviews (KIIs) and focus group discussions (FGDs) participants

A total of 67 participants across the two study districts participated in Key Informant Interview or FGDs. The sociodemographic background of the FGDs and KIIs are presented below.

The KII participants were diverse, with different qualifications and experiences. Most participants from the health system had work experience ranging from one to 16 years. A total of 11 participants (6 males and 5 females) were included. The mean (SD±) age of the participants was 31.5 (7.4) years. Seven (63.6%) of the participants were from Nyangatom, while the remaining four (36.4%) were from Uba Debretsehay. All respondents were literate individuals working in the health system or community service as community leaders. In terms of educational qualifications, the majority held a bachelor’s degree (36.4%), followed by those with a Diploma (27.3%). A smaller proportion had completed primary education (18.2%) or high school (9.1%), and only one participant had a master’s degree or higher (9.1%). Regarding their positions, the largest group were community leaders (36.4%), with others working in the District Health Office and as Integrated Eye Health Workers (both 18.2%). Smaller groups included Health Extension Workers, Ophthalmologists, and Optometrists, each representing 9.1% of the participants.

Overall, six FGDs involving 56 participants were conducted in the two study districts, with three FGDs in each district. The participants included community representatives and local leaders from different sites and zones. Of the total participants, 27 (48.2%) were from the pastoralist Nyangatom district, while the remaining 29 (51.8%) were from Uba Debretsehay. The number of participants in each FGD ranged between 9 and 10. Among the 56 participants, 37 (66%) were women and 19 (34%) were men. The mean (SD±) age of the participants was 33.3 (5.4) years.

Theme 1: Individual-level characteristics

In the following section, sub-themes relevant to the SAFE strategy at the individual level, as identified from interviews and focus group discussions, will be presented. Participants highlighted significant challenges related to safe hygiene and sanitation practices, as well as the disproportionate risk of trachoma exposure among community members. The nomadic lifestyle and low literacy levels constrain the implementation of the WHO’s SAFE strategy, making it difficult to educate pastoralists on sanitation. Additionally, the perception of flies as harmless further complicates efforts to promote proper sanitation practices. Limited awareness of children as high-risk groups exacerbates transmission, with water rationing practices impacting optimal hygiene and sanitation.

Key concerns include limited literacy often associated with the pastoralist lifestyle. Theme 2: interpersonal level contexts and barriers in persistent districts

Participants identified that pastoralist households often characterized by shared living with animals, congested living conditions of migrant workers and considerations to social relationships regarding ownership of latrines contribute to lower level of hygiene and sanitation practices. These conditions often lead to disproportionate exposure to infectious trachoma.

Sub-theme: selective mobility of adult members of households

The participants identified that pastoralist lifestyle poses a challenge for delivering adequate interventions due to mobility and lifestyle associated with livestock. As indicated active members of the society often move from place to place, and live with their animals, and animals are part of their life making a favourable environment for breeding flies.“Because mobile pastoralists often travel long distances following their livestock, it can be challenging to provide them with essential education on sanitation” FGD3NYT.

Theme 3: institutional and health system level contexts and constraints to sanitation

Participants highlighted that institutional and health systems related barriers and contexts such as limited capacity to deliver culturally, and contextually relevant tools and alternatives inhibited latrine adoption among the study communities.

Participants highlighted that for effective promotion of latrine use and hygiene practices, it is crucial to shift from external recommendations to fostering community ownership and engagement, as current advice from health extension workers is often disregarded.“Latrine promotion needs to be internalised. So, we think we may not need to worry about it.” FGD2NYT.

In summary, various socio-ecological factors, contexts, and constraints create a conducive environment for trachoma transmission in the two districts, explaining its persistence (Fig. 3).

Persistent trachoma transmission in certain districts is influenced by various socioecological factors. At the individual level, the nomadic lifestyle and low literacy rates hinder proper hygiene practices, while the normalization of flies as harmless and the prioritization of water rationing over hygiene exacerbates the issue. These personal characteristics contribute significantly to the persistence of trachoma. Interpersonal factors also play a crucial role. The selective mobility of adult household members, coupled with poverty and marginalization, impacts on the community’s ability to maintain hygiene and sanitation standards. Social relationships further influence these practices, often leading to inconsistent hygiene behaviors that facilitate trachoma transmission. Institutional and health system constraints are another critical aspect.

Summary of the socioecological factors at various levels

The health system’s top-down approach to hygiene and sanitation promotion often lacks cultural and contextual competence, making interventions less effective. This disconnects between the health system and the community hinders the successful implementation of sanitation initiatives. Community-level barriers include substandard private and public latrines which set poor examples, while the management and maintenance of public latrines remain problematic. Indigenous knowledge that justifies open defecation as a rodent control strategy, along with limited access to water, further complicates efforts to improve facial cleanliness and environmental conditions. Finally, societal and policy-level considerations highlight the vulnerabilities exacerbated by gender roles and occupations. The lack of enforcement of hygiene and sanitation standards as a public health concern perpetuates the cycle of trachoma transmission. Addressing these multi-layered factors is essential for effective trachoma control and prevention.

Discussion

In this we identified several socio-ecological contexts and constraints contributing to persistent trachoma through their impact on hindering progress on favourable hygiene and sanitation practices [23]. These contexts and constraints range from an individual context to environmental and societal level barriers. In the following sections those barriers and constraints are discussed.

Accessibility and availability of water at a household are pillars for effective hygiene and sanitation practice. However, water scarcity in both districts presents significant challenges for different reasons. In the mountainous highlands of Uba Debretsehay, the terrain makes water development very difficult, while the Omo Valley’s semi-desert environment results in limited rainwater and surface water. The most frequently cited cause of trachoma endemicity is limited access to water [24]. Abebe and Tucho [25] reported that there is an increased odds of active trachoma among children from households without access to improved water sources. The availability and accessibility of water at the household level, along with prioritization and decision-making regarding its allocation, are more crucial than the sheer abundance of water. Due to limited access, the two most effective practices recommended by the World Health Organization’s SAFE strategy, F&E, are often compromised. In situations where water is scarce, face washing is frequently deprioritized.

The availability and accessibility of water at the household level, as well as prioritization and decision-making regarding its allocation, are more critical than the sheer abundance of water. Limited access often compromises the two most effective practices recommended by the World Health Organization’s SAFE strategy, F&E. Consequently, in water-scarce situations, face washing is frequently deprioritized, exacerbating trachoma transmission [15]. Access to water within close distance is also found to be associated with reduced risk of getting trachoma among children. In this case more water often creates opportunities for the use of water for hygiene and sanitation practices within the household [24].

This study revealed that, poor awareness, and misperception about the importance of trachoma prevention was highly evident which highlights the prevailing problem in the community. Studies found that misconceptions may hinder the effectiveness of various trachoma related interventions and might deter acceleration towards trachoma elimination [26]. As a result of low knowledge and awareness, participants indicated that there are tendencies to minimize risk related to the disease. This is consistent with a finding in a study conducted in a trachoma hyper endemic semi-pastoralist setting of Southern Ethiopia. Moreover, others minimize the importance of face washing, a critical pillar of the SAFE strategy to address persistence and recrudescence [27].

The issue of limited access and utilization of latrine among the study communities is consistent with other studies which reported lower coverage of improved latrine coverage in South Ethiopia [28, 29]. Several factors at household level affect ownership and utilization of latrines, ranging from physical conditions to pyscho-social contexts [30]. The current study identified several reasons that inhibit latrine construction and use. These include topography, soil type, and location of latrines. These findings are consistent with results reported in earlier studies [25]. This is partly attributable to limited technological and technical alternatives adaptable to various contexts [27]. This study underscores the significant role of substandard and inadequate latrines as obstacles to their use. Previous research has similarly found that poor-quality latrines, marked by unpleasant odors and lack of privacy, often deter users [25]. Accordingly, there needs to be investment on quality latrine promotion.

Occupational livelihood related contexts, especially among pastoralist homesteads often characterized by shared living with animals, congested living conditions increase vulnerability to the disease. Pastoralists live with their animals, and animals are part of their life making a favourable environment for breeding flies. Accordingly, there is normalization of the fly human relationship, though there is substantially large number of evidence showing the increased risk of acquiring trachoma due to fly-face-eye contact [1, 24, 31, 32].

In our study we found that congested villages in Uba Debretsehay related to terrain and camps of migrant workers in Nyangatom are other barriers that facilitated the reproduction of fly population and prevalence of suboptimal sanitation conditions. As indicated in previous studies, these conditions often lead to disproportionate exposure to infectious trachoma [1].

Community-level themes that contribute to persistent trachoma transmission include practices that hinder facial cleanliness and environmental improvement, the widespread justification and practice of open defecation, and challenges related to water access. Additionally, competing indigenous and cultural practices that perpetuate and reintroduce trachoma are detailed [33, 34]. In a contagious disease context these are critically important systems that impact both the risk and preventive capacity of individuals.

In this study, from the community perspective, participants indicated that ownership of bad latrine is not only a health challenge, but it might also have implications on livelihoods and positive neighbourhood. Under such conditions, sustaining latrines and latrine utilization is a challenge. The challenge of space has been reported in previous studies [25]. However, in this study, they did not report the social implication of challenges latrines constrained duet to space.

Participants also indicated that there are no enforcing mechanisms for creation of open defecation free communities. A participant indicated that latrine promotion is often considered as an external intervention and lacks community buy-in. Many participants feel that it is imposed by the Health Extension Worker, without a justified health related utility. A large study conducted to see the effect of a health extension program on improving water, sanitation, and hygiene practices in rural Ethiopia concluded that households exposed to Health Extension Program were five times higher odds of having a latrine [35]. This is partly due to the expected responsibility of Health Extension Workers in relation to their environmental health package.

Accordingly, latrine promotion often faces resistance from communities. As a result, regressing to open defecation is a widespread practice in those communities characterized by facing persistent and recrudescent trachoma. And several studies reported a higher slippage from open defecation free communities to practicing open defecations. In a latest systematic review, it was reported that the ODF slippage rate in Ethiopia was high [25].

Open defecation may be preferred by some individuals who find latrines uncomfortable and restrictive. Such sentiments were reported and echoed in Ghana, where such individuals are referred to as “free rangers.“ [23]. In a study conducted in Southern Ethiopia, community members reported that latrines may be scary and strange, often preferring open defecation [36].

Many people are hesitant to use latrines due to some beliefs. The participants identified that open defecation is a strategy to control invasion of rodents into granaries and crops. Moreover, others believe that it simply dries up and should be a concern. The service of pit latrines as a reservoir for rodents has been a conventional knowledge and has been reported even in scientific studies [37].

In the current study we highlighted the importance of making acceptable hygiene and sanitation practices as everyone’s business is for the good of family, friends, neighbours, and the society in general. It was found that maintenance and appropriate use of community latrines was a daunting practice, that led to open defecation. A recent systematic review clearly reported the discomfort of using shared latrines, where over a dozen of studies from the African countries confirmed frustration among users [25]. The concept of the tragedy of the commons is adapted from a decade old article in Garrett Hardin’s seminal work which describes how common properties being neglected or overused and eventually destroyed [38]. The notion is that in the absence of ownership, there is no custodianship to common properties. In the context of this study, we found that several initiatives to address ODF, including construction of considerable number of communal latrines along the roads, marketplaces and communal locations eventually failed because of lack of ownership, failed operation, and maintenance as well as inappropriate use.

The findings related to disproportionate impact and risk exposure of marginalised communities to trachoma is consistent with other studies from Ethiopia [27]. The findings are similar to what has been reported from Oromia, Ethiopia, where researchers established that Trachoma remains a disease associated with lower socio-economic status, emphasizing the need for continued implementation of control measures in addition to poverty reduction interventions in this region [31].

At the societal level distinctive vulnerabilities related to gender roles, occupation, poverty and marginalization were found to disproportionately contribute to sub-optimal hygiene and sanitation practices. We found that men, women and young people face different challenges in accessing latrines. For instance, while women have a health concern arising from substandard latrines which is consistent with other study [39], men and young boys do not have access to latrines at all around their farm or in the fields and might contribute more for open defecation [23]. However, there are also various other studies that reported the preference of women to practice open defecation due to physical and pyscho-social concerns [36]. Karikari et al. [23] indicated that women are often a group of disguised practitioners (offenders) of open defecation, while men are an outright culprit (outright offenders). This implies the need for designing targeted strategies considering gender soles as well as occupational variations.

Strength and limitation of the study

As the issue of persistence and recrudescence of Trachoma is an evolving agenda which gained momentum after the WHO informal consultation in 2021, this study contributes to the growing body of evidence to address these critical challenges to achieve trachoma elimination [6, 8–11]. The momentum to study persistence and recrudescence is set mainly after the realization of the challenge after decades of program implementation, which led to limited evidence for researchers, policy makers and program implementers. As a qualitative study, the study is prone to the challenges of generalizability.

Conclusion

In terms of generating evidence, this is study shed light on socio-ecological factors that contributed to persistence and recrudesce of Trachoma in Ethiopia using a qualitative approach. At the individual level, we found that there is a widespread poor awareness, knowledge and misconceptions regarding the prevention of trachoma. Several factors that impede effective prevention practices, including environmental factors affecting latrine ownership, negative experience associated to bad latrines, considerations that reinforce open defecation as freedom and latrines as constraining and strange are barriers for practicing favourable hygiene and practice are widely practice in the study areas.

There are several community level contexts and constraints that exacerbated disease transmission or hampered effective delivery of the SAFE strategy. Negative attitudes towards latrines as goods that might affect livelihood and neighbourhood, perception of latrines as imposed interventions without clear health utility and maximizing open defecation as strategy for rodent control are widespread contexts in persistent and recrudescent districts. Furthermore, creation of Open Defecation Free villages has been affected by mismanagement and eventual destruction of community latrines, a tragedy of the commons.

The societal and policy level considerations prevalent within the study communities include gender roles and responsibilities affecting hygiene and sanitation practices. We found that while being a man or a boy engaged in farming and pastoralism disconnects individuals from access to latrine, being at home never gives a woman a privilege to access latrines. Women and girls have various concerns related to latrine utilization, including health and safety, comfort, and privacy. Finally, we observed that trachoma programs in these districts are characterized by limited engagement of stakeholders and centralization of the elimination strategies.
