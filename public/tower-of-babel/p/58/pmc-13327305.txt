Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease and cardiovascular disease: Emerging insights into the liver–heart axis
Authors: Seung‐Hwan Lee
Published: 2026

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly known as nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), has emerged as the most common chronic liver disease worldwide and is increasingly recognized as a major component of systemic cardiometabolic disease 1 . The recent shift from NAFLD to MASLD reflects a conceptual transition from a liver‐centered disorder to a metabolic disease closely intertwined with obesity, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and cardiovascular disease (CVD) 2 , 3 . Importantly, CVD represents the leading cause of mortality in patients with MASLD, exceeding liver‐related complications in many populations. Accumulating evidence suggests that MASLD is not merely a bystander of metabolic dysfunction, but an active contributor to cardiovascular injury through complex inter‐organ communication networks 4 .

The association between MASLD and CVD is supported by substantial epidemiologic evidence. Individuals with MASLD exhibit increased risks of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), heart failure, atrial fibrillation, and cardiovascular mortality independent of traditional cardiometabolic risk factors 5 , 6 . Moreover, the severity of liver disease, particularly hepatic fibrosis, appears to correlate strongly with cardiovascular outcomes 7 . Patients with advanced fibrosis or metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH) show markedly elevated risks of major adverse cardiovascular events compared with those with isolated steatosis. These findings suggest that progressive liver injury may amplify systemic pathogenic processes relevant to cardiovascular disease.

Insulin resistance represents a central mechanism linking MASLD and CVD 8 . In insulin‐resistant states, impaired suppression of adipose tissue lipolysis increases fluxes of free fatty acids to the liver, promoting hepatic triglyceride accumulation and lipotoxicity. Hepatic insulin resistance also enhances gluconeogenesis and dyslipidemia, characterized by increased very‐low‐density lipoprotein production, hypertriglyceridemia, reduced high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and formation of small dense low‐density lipoprotein particles. This atherogenic lipid profile substantially contributes to cardiovascular risk. Simultaneously, ectopic fat deposition in the myocardium, skeletal muscle, and visceral adipose tissue further aggravates systemic metabolic dysfunction.

Beyond metabolic abnormalities, chronic low‐grade inflammation plays a critical role in the liver–heart axis 4 , 9 . Steatotic hepatocytes activate inflammatory pathways through oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and endoplasmic reticulum stress, leading to the release of pro‐inflammatory cytokines and chemokines. Elevated circulating levels of tumor necrosis factor‐α, interleukin‐6, and C‐reactive protein contribute to endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, and plaque instability. Hepatic inflammation may therefore serve as an amplifier of systemic inflammatory responses driving cardiovascular injury.

Recent studies have also highlighted the importance of hepatokines in mediating liver–heart communication 10 . The diseased liver actively secretes numerous bioactive molecules that influence distant organs. Fetuin‐A, fibroblast growth factor 21, angiopoietin‐like proteins, and selenoprotein P have been implicated in insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, vascular calcification, and cardiac remodeling. Some hepatokines may exert protective effects under specific conditions, whereas others appear to promote cardiometabolic injury. The complex and context‐dependent roles of these circulating factors remain an active area of investigation. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) have recently emerged as another important mediator of inter‐organ communication in MASLD 11 . EVs are membrane‐bound particles released by cells that carry proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids, including microribonucleic acids (miRs). Hepatocyte‐derived EVs released under lipotoxic conditions can modulate inflammatory and metabolic pathways in distant tissues. Experimental studies suggest that EV‐associated miRs may promote endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, macrophage activation, and myocardial remodeling. These findings support the concept that the steatotic liver actively participates in cardiovascular pathogenesis through EV‐mediated signaling networks. Furthermore, circulating EV profiles may serve as novel biomarkers reflecting both liver disease activity and cardiovascular risk.

Cardiac dysfunction associated with MASLD extends beyond traditional ASCVD. Increasing evidence suggests strong associations between MASLD and heart failure, particularly heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) 12 . Shared mechanisms include systemic inflammation, myocardial insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, epicardial adipose tissue expansion, and myocardial fibrosis. Patients with MASLD frequently exhibit subclinical cardiac remodeling, impaired diastolic function, and increased left ventricular mass even in the absence of overt coronary artery disease. The coexistence of obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, and MASLD may further accelerate progression toward the broader spectrum of cardiometabolic‐kidney disease.

The recognition of MASLD as a systemic cardiometabolic disorder has important therapeutic implications. Lifestyle intervention remains the cornerstone of treatment. Weight reduction through dietary modification and physical activity improves hepatic steatosis, insulin resistance, and cardiovascular risk factors simultaneously. Even modest weight loss can reduce liver fat content, whereas greater reductions may improve hepatic inflammation and fibrosis. Importantly, regular exercise provides cardiovascular benefits independent of weight reduction. Several glucose‐lowering agents with cardiovascular benefits also demonstrate favorable effects on MASLD 13 . Sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 inhibitors improve insulin resistance, reduce visceral adiposity, and decrease hepatic fat accumulation while simultaneously lowering risks of heart failure and renal outcomes. Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists have shown promising effects on body weight, hepatic steatosis, and MASH resolution, accompanied by significant cardiovascular risk reduction 14 . Emerging dual and triple incretin‐based therapies may further transform the therapeutic landscape of MASLD and cardiometabolic disease 15 . However, whether direct improvement of liver disease independently reduces cardiovascular events remains to be fully established.

The concept of the liver–heart axis highlights the need for integrated multidisciplinary management strategies. Patients with MASLD should undergo comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment, including evaluation of metabolic comorbidities, subclinical atherosclerosis, and heart failure risk 1 , 4 . Likewise, clinicians caring for patients with diabetes, obesity, or cardiovascular disease should recognize MASLD as an important systemic complication rather than an isolated hepatic disorder.

In conclusion, MASLD has evolved from a liver‐specific condition to a central component of systemic cardiometabolic disease. Growing evidence supports bidirectional interactions between the liver and cardiovascular system mediated by insulin resistance, inflammation, hepatokines, dyslipidemia, and extracellular vesicle signaling (Figure 1). Understanding these interconnected mechanisms may facilitate development of precision‐based therapeutic approaches targeting both hepatic and cardiovascular outcomes. As the global burden of obesity and type 2 diabetes mellitus continues to rise, unraveling the liver–heart axis will become increasingly important in the prevention and management of cardiometabolic disease.

The steatotic liver subsequently promotes cardiovascular disease through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including atherogenic dyslipidemia, systemic inflammation, hepatokine secretion, extracellular vesicle (EV)‐mediated signaling, endothelial dysfunction, and prothrombotic pathways, leading to a broad spectrum of cardiovascular complications.
