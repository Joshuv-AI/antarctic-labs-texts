Primary Ewing sarcoma of the pancreas: A rare extraosseous presentation
Authors: Elias Lugo-Fagundo, Connor W Smith, Felipe Lopez-Ramirez, Elliot K Fishman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEwing sarcoma is an aggressive neoplasm that is common among children and adolescents, most frequently affecting the pelvis and long bones. Although rare, Ewing sarcoma is known to also occur in extraosseous locations, affecting a broad range of soft tissue structures. The diagnosis of extraosseous Ewing sarcoma is difficult given the nonspecific symptomatology, often related to local tumor burden. Radiological features of this entity overlap with other common malignancies, as Ewing sarcoma typically presents on computed tomography (CT) as a heterogeneous mass containing areas of necrosis and calcification. Imaging plays a central role in the evaluation of this entity, but histologic and molecular analysis remain necessary for definitive diagnosis. We report the case of a 40-year-old female with a primary pancreatic extraosseous Ewing sarcoma. We correlate clinical indicators with imaging findings and pathological analysis to discuss this patient’s long-term outcome following multimodal therapy.Keywords: Computed tomography, Ewing sarcoma, Pancreas

Introduction

Ewing sarcoma is a highly aggressive neoplasm that commonly arises from bone and, less frequently, soft tissue, primarily affecting children and adolescents [1]. As the second most common primary bone malignancy affecting children, classic Ewing sarcoma typically involves the pelvis and long bones [2]. However, approximately 20% of cases are extraosseous, occurring in a wide range of anatomic locations [1,3]. Specifically, primary involvement of the pancreas by Ewing sarcoma is exceptionally rare, accounting for just 0.3% of primary pancreatic malignancies [4]. Clinical presentation often overlaps with that of more common pancreatic neoplasms such as neuroendocrine tumors and pancreatic ductal adenocarcinomas, making diagnosis challenging [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7]]. Consequently, imaging, particularly computed tomography (CT), plays a central role in the detection, characterization, and staging of these tumors [3]. Ultimately, a definite diagnosis requires histopathologic and molecular confirmation [1,2,8]. In this article, we present a rare case of a primary pancreatic extraosseous Ewing sarcoma in a 40-year-old female patient, highlighting the clinical presentation, diagnostic workup, imaging findings, and long-term outcome following multimodal treatment.

Case report

A 40-year-old female with no significant past medical history presented with progressively worsening, new-onset abdominal bloating and pain over several months. The patient reported sharp pain localized to the left lower quadrant, occasionally waking her from sleep. Over time, the patient developed a range of associated symptoms, including early satiety, gastroesophageal reflux, constipation, emesis, fever, and night sweats. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated an exophytic mass arising from the anterior aspect of the pancreatic body and tail, compressing the gastric body and antrum, with tumor encasement of the splenic artery and associated narrowing of the splenic vein (Fig. 1A–1C). There was no evidence of metastatic disease within the abdomen or pelvis. Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) revealed a malignant neoplasm with immunohistochemical positivity for CD99 and NKX2.2. Furthermore, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) confirmed an EWSR1–FLI1 fusion gene, consistent with extraosseous Ewing sarcoma. Images of the histological slides were unavailable for this report as they were returned to an outside practice following review at our institution. A follow-up positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET–CT) scan showed no evidence of metastatic disease (Fig. 1D). The patient was started on vincristine, doxorubicin, and cyclophosphamide alternating with ifosfamide and etoposide (VDC–IE) chemotherapy, followed by external beam radiation therapy, and ultimately underwent distal pancreatectomy with splenectomy. The postoperative course was unremarkable, and the patient has survived without evidence of disease for six years on routine oncologic surveillance with CT imaging performed every six months.

Discussion

Ewing sarcoma is an aggressive malignancy that typically arises in children and young adults, with a peak incidence at approximately 15 years of age [1]. As the second most common primary bone tumor in children and adolescents, Ewing sarcoma accounts for roughly 2% of pediatric malignancies and has a slight male predominance [1]. The tumor has an incidence of approximately 1 case per 1.5 million, with individuals of European descent being nearly 10 times more frequently affected than those of African ancestry [1,2]. Most cases arise in the pelvis and proximal long bones; however, in approximately 20% of patients, tumors are extraosseous. Extraosseous Ewing sarcomas exhibit a broader age distribution and a lower male predilection compared with osseous Ewing sarcoma [3]. These extraskeletal neoplasms have been encountered in the bladder, adrenal glands, lungs, esophagus, ileum, kidney, and, as presented in this case, the pancreas [1].

Pancreatic Ewing sarcoma is an extremely rare extraosseous manifestation, with approximately 45 cases reported in the literature, a median age at presentation of 20 years, and no clear sex predilection [3,4]. Clinical presentation is nonspecific, with reported symptoms including abdominal and flank pain, dysuria, anemia, nausea and vomiting, and occasionally, jaundice [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Although rare, paraneoplastic syndromes have also been described, with some cases reporting hyperglycemia, exophthalmos, and precocious puberty [4,6]. These tumors tend to be large and locally invasive, with approximately 25%-30% of cases presenting with extensive local involvement and early metastatic spread to the lungs, bone, and bone marrow [9]. Five-year survival rates range from 70%-80% in localized disease but decline to approximately 30% in patients with metastatic disease [2]. Furthermore, diagnosis can be challenging as these neoplasms may resemble other pancreatic tumors on imaging, including pancreatoblastomas and neuroendocrine tumors [4], with one study reporting that 22% of gastrointestinal Ewing sarcomas were initially misdiagnosed as neuroendocrine neoplasms [7]. However, when located in the pancreas, extraosseous Ewing sarcoma is known to exhibit an expansive growth pattern, unlike the infiltrative growth pattern characteristic of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [3].

Given its propensity for metastasis and associated poor prognosis, prompt and definitive diagnosis is crucial in patients with extraosseous Ewing sarcoma. For example, as seen in the case presentation, the immunohistochemical markers CD99 and NKX2.2 are highly informative, with their combined expression achieving a specificity of up to 98% for diagnosing Ewing sarcoma [8]. At the molecular level, the disease is characterized by recurrent, balanced chromosomal translocations involving the EWSR1 gene on chromosome 22q12. This results in an EWSR1-FLI1 fusion gene in 85% of cases, which functions as an oncogenic driver [1,2,6]. Molecular confirmation of the EWSR1-FLI1 fusion protein via FISH, polymerase chain reaction, or next-generation sequencing is required to achieve a definitive diagnosis [10].

CT also plays a crucial role in the detection, staging, and management of pancreatic Ewing sarcoma. Imaging may reveal an expansile rather than infiltrative growth pattern, a characterization useful in distinguishing these tumors from other common pancreatic malignancies [3]. Most instances of pancreatic Ewing sarcoma are located in the head and demonstrate heterogenous enhancement on contrast-enhanced CT, with regions of low attenuation corresponding to necrosis. Calcifications, which are present in roughly 25%-30% of cases, are usually faint and amorphous, but are more readily detected on CT than other imaging modalities [3,11]. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is also a viable imaging technique for primary tumor evaluation in Ewing sarcomas across all anatomical sites. On MRI, extraosseous Ewing sarcomas typically appear with hyperintense T2-weighted signaling which suggests areas of necrosis or cystic components [3].

Following confirmation of diagnosis and staging, multimodal therapy consisting of systemic chemotherapy and local control with surgery and radiation therapy is employed, with reported survival outcomes comparable to those of osseous Ewing sarcoma [3]. Ultimately, although rare, the presence of an undifferentiated round-cell tumor in the soft tissue should prompt the inclusion of Ewing sarcoma in the differential diagnosis [1]. Imaging such as CT, MRI, and PET-CT are useful in anatomical delineation, assessment of metastasis, and surgical planning, but molecular and histological study are ultimately required for a definitive diagnosis. Considering the aggressive nature of this entity, prompt evaluation in cases of suspected extraosseous Ewing sarcoma is crucial to enhance patient outcomes.
