Cartilage Scaffolds Implanted Without Subchondral Drilling Are Associated With Improved Outcomes and Fewer Complications Compared to Scaffolds Implanted With Subchondral Drilling in the Treatment of Isolated Patellar Cartilage Lesions
Authors: Romir P. Parmar, Sailesh V. Tummala, Sachin S. Pathuri, Jainam Shah, Jai Khurmi, Kostas J. Economopoulos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare clinical outcomes between augmented subchondral drilling (aSCD) and nonsubchondral drilling (NoSCD) techniques, both using a cartilage scaffold, in the treatment of isolated patellar cartilage lesions.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted on patients who underwent surgical treatment for isolated patellar cartilage using either an aSCD technique (a subchondral drilling augmented with a particulated cartilage allograft scaffold or a flexible osteochondral allograft scaffold) or a NoSCD technique (a particulated juvenile articular cartilage scaffold or a flexible osteochondral allograft scaffold implanted without performing a subchondral drilling). Patients were included if they had a minimum 2-year follow-up and excluded if they underwent concomitant meniscal or ligamentous procedures. While tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) was commonly performed, it was not a strict inclusion criterion. All procedures were performed through arthrotomy with a standardized postoperative rehabilitation protocol. Clinical outcomes included International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) and Lysholm scores, reoperation rates, conversion to total knee arthroplasty, and complications.ResultsA total of 65 knees were included: 31 in the aSCD group and 34 in the NoSCD group, with a mean follow-up of 50.4 months (range, 24-88 months). In the aSCD group, particulated allograft cartilage hydrated with platelet-rich plasma was used in 80.7% of cases and flexible osteochondral allograft in 19.3%, while the NoSCD group was treated with Cartiform (Arthrex) in 70.6% and particulated juvenile articular cartilage in 29.4% of cases. TTO was performed in 87.0% of aSCD and 91.2% of NoSCD cases. Average defect sizes were 3.7 cm2 (aSCD) and 4.0 cm2 (NoSCD). At final follow-up, the NoSCD group had significantly better outcomes, with higher median IKDC scores (81.0 vs 74.0; P < .001) and median Lysholm scores (83.0 vs 77.0; P < .001). A significantly greater proportion of NoSCD patients exceeded the minimal clinically important difference for IKDC (100% vs 80.6%; P = .024) but not for Lysholm (97.1% vs 93.5%; P = .935) at final follow-up. In multivariable regression adjusting for scaffold type, TTO, and defect size, aSCD remained a significant independent predictor of lower final IKDC (β = –8.97; P = .001) and Lysholm (β = –12.71; P < .001) scores. The aSCD group had a significantly higher rate of repeat surgery (45.2% vs 14.7%; P = .015). There was no significant difference in conversion to total knee arthroplasty between groups (P = .432).ConclusionsFor the treatment of isolated patellar cartilage lesions, a nonsubchondral drilling technique with scaffolding was associated with improved patient-reported outcomes and fewer complications compared to a subchondral drilling technique with scaffolding, although there was no difference in the rate of conversion to total knee arthroplasty.Level of EvidenceLevel III, retrospective comparative study.

Patellar cartilage injuries may necessitate surgical restoration due to the avascular nature of cartilage with limited intrinsic healing capacity.1,2 Treatment options range from scaffolds to advanced techniques, such as autologous chondrocyte implantation.3 A common approach is marrow stimulation, which can be done via microfracture, using a sharp awl, or subchondral drilling, using a surgical drill, to create holes in the subchondral bone.4,5 Of note, a systematic review by Kraeutler et al.6 compared those 2 techniques and found that despite subchondral drilling resulting in less damage to the subchondral bone, neither technique achieved the characteristics of native hyaline cartilage. These marrow stimulation procedures can also be augmented with a scaffold, such as a chondral or osteochondral allograft, designed to promote true hyaline-like cartilage formation rather than the fibrocartilage typically produced by marrow stimulation alone.7,8 Despite this, studies have shown comparable outcomes between scaffolded microfracture and microfracture without scaffolds.9,10 A notable limitation of microfracture is its potential to cause subchondral bone alterations, which can lead to postoperative failure.11 Consequently, emerging techniques involve the use of scaffold material alone, without the need for marrow stimulation. Among these, particulated juvenile allograft cartilage (PJAC) employs juvenile articular cartilage to facilitate hyaline-like cartilage repair while preserving the subchondral bone.12 Basic science research has shown that juvenile chondrocytes exhibit greater restorative potential compared to adult chondrocytes,13 and clinical studies have shown significant improvements in pain and function with PJAC for focal knee cartilage lesions.14 Furthermore, a clinical trial by Altschuler et al.15 revealed that an aragonite-based scaffold alone provided significantly superior clinical outcomes compared to microfracture alone in patients with chondral knee lesions.

Despite extensive research on augmented microfracture and microfracture alone,16,17 evidence comparing scaffold use with and without adjuvant subchondral drilling remains limited. The purpose of this study was to compare clinical outcomes between augmented subchondral drilling (aSCD) and nonsubchondral drilling (NoSCD) techniques, both using a cartilage scaffold, in the treatment of isolated patellar cartilage lesions. We hypothesized that avoiding subchondral penetration, as required in aSCD, would result in superior outcomes for patients treated with the NoSCD technique.

Methods

Patient Selection and Outcome Measures

A retrospective review was conducted of all adult patients who underwent patellar cartilage surgery with either aSCD or NoSCD between August 2013 and December 2022. Institutional review board approval was obtained, and all procedures were performed by a fellowship-trained sports medicine orthopaedic surgeon (K.J.E.). Inclusion criteria consisted of patients treated for isolated patellar cartilage injuries using an allograft cartilage scaffold, with or without subchondral drilling, and a minimum 2-year follow-up. Patients were excluded if they underwent any previous cartilage procedure (aside from shaving chondroplasty), concurrent meniscal surgery, anterior cruciate ligament reconstruction, or distal femoral osteotomy at the time of their cartilage surgery. Of note, patients who underwent tibial tubercle osteotomy (TTO) were still included in the study, as this was not considered a strict inclusion/exclusion criterion.

Demographic data, operative details, scaffold type, chondral defect size, and osteoarthritis grade were collected through a review of patient charts and radiographs (sunrise and lateral views). Osteoarthritis severity was graded using the Kellgren-Lawrence classification system, which ranges from 0 to 4 in increasing severity.18,19 The Caton-Deschamps index was also recorded to assess patellar height.20 Both radiographic measurements were performed by K.J.E., who was not blinded to the study design.21

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs), including the subjective International Knee Documentation Committee (IKDC) and Lysholm scores, were collected at 4 time points: preoperatively, 6 months postoperatively, 1 year postoperatively, and at final follow-up via face-to-face or e-mail.22,23 Additional outcomes included complications, specifically rates of arthrofibrosis requiring a manipulation under anesthesia with or without lysis of adhesions, cartilage overgrowth requiring arthroscopic debridement, conversion to total knee arthroplasty, and infection. Arthrofibrosis was specifically defined as a flexion contracture >10° or an inability to flex beyond 70° at 6 to 8 weeks postoperatively, or persistent loss of ≥5° of extension or flexion <110° at final follow-up. Cartilage overgrowth was diagnosed via second-look arthroscopy and when the cartilage fill was above the level of the native cartilage.

Procedure and Scaffolding Selection

Patients in the aSCD group had particulated allograft cartilage hydrated with platelet-rich plasma (BioCartilage; Arthrex) or flexible osteochondral allograft (Cartiform; Arthrex) as the extracellular matrix scaffold, while patients in the NoSCD group either had Cartiform or PJAC (DeNovo Zimmer Biomet) as the extracellular matrix scaffold. PJAC consists of minced juvenile hyaline cartilage, while BioCartilage is derived from minced adult articular cartilage. Cartiform is composed of articular cartilage on a thin, perforated bony base, which enhances flexibility and integration. Given that the purpose of BioCartilage is to act as a scaffold over a microfractured defect, this option was considered only in patients in the aSCD group and not in the NoSCD group.8 Cartiform could be used in both cases. The technique for PJAC does not require microfracture and can be placed on top of the subchondral bone after proper preparation. Overall, no strict criteria dictated which technique was performed. Initially, the senior author (K.J.E.) employed aSCD with BioCartilage and Cartiform predominantly in the patellofemoral (PF) joint. However, as the outcomes in the aSCD group failed to meet the senior author’s clinical expectations, alternative approaches, including treatment without subchondral drilling using Cartiform and PJAC, were explored. Over time, it became evident that avoiding subchondral drilling in the PF joint resulted in superior outcomes, leading the senior author to discontinue microfracture for PF joint defects entirely, and in turn, the NoSCD and aSCD were not contemporaneous. Regarding scaffold selection, there was no strict indication for the choice of the scaffold, as it depended on institutional availability. Thus, the choice of treatment was based on intraoperative findings, a discussion with the patient, and clinical judgment at the time of surgery.

Surgical Procedure and Arthrotomy

All procedures were performed with the patient in the supine position, utilizing a nonsterile tourniquet applied around the proximal thigh, under general anesthesia, and with a femoral nerve block. A diagnostic arthroscopy was conducted through standard anteromedial and anterolateral portals to assess the chondral injury and evaluate any associated ligamentous or meniscal pathology. Once the cartilage damage had been assessed arthroscopically, the arthroscopic equipment was removed from the knee. A parapatellar arthrotomy was performed by making an incision either medially or laterally, depending on the location of the injury. Once the defect was clearly visualized and measured with a ruler, a curette was used to remove any remaining cartilage, scar tissue, and the calcified cartilage layer while creating 90° margins around the defect. Vertical walls were then prepared by trimming loose cartilage edges with a No. 15 blade, and the prepared defect was measured. Next, based on the study group, patients underwent one of the following procedures: subchondral drilling with BioCartilage augmentation, Cartiform augmentation, or direct scaffolding using Cartiform or PJAC without subchondral drilling.

The indication for TTO was a tibial tubercle–trochlear groove distance ≥20 mm or a Caton-Deschamps Index (CDI) ≥1.4. Anterior medialization was performed with a 45° osteotomy cut, leaving a thin distal shingle and translating the tubercle ∼1 cm medially to correct the tibial tubercle–trochlear groove to ∼10 mm. For distalization (patella alta), a complete osteotomy was performed, removing ∼1 cm of tibia distal to the tubercle to correct the CDI to ∼1.1. In all cases, fixation was achieved with 2 headless screws.

BioCartilage With the Microfracture Procedure

Once the bony bed was prepared, a subchondral drilling of the entire bed was performed using a 1.6-mm K-wire on power. Each hole was 3 to 4 mm from each other, and the depth of each was about 8 to 9 mm. Irrigation was used to minimize thermal necrosis. For BioCartilage implantation, 1 mL of prepackaged BioCartilage was combined with 1 mL of previously prepared leukocyte-poor platelet-rich plasma in the Arthrex Mixing and Delivery Syringe. The microfracture site was dried in preparation for the BioCartilage. The BioCartilage/platelet-rich plasma mixture was then injected into the defect through the Tuohy needle, with careful attention to avoid overfilling the defect, keeping the level below the surrounding cartilage margin. A Freer elevator was used to smooth the BioCartilage, and fibrin glue was applied over both the defect and the adjacent cartilage. The fibrin glue was allowed to set for 5 minutes. Finally, after removing all instrumentation, compressive force was applied to the knee to contour the BioCartilage against the opposing articular surface.

Cartiform Implantation With or Without the Microfracture Procedure

For patients who underwent subchondral drilling, the procedure was performed as previously described. For patients who did not undergo subchondral drilling, Cartiform implantation was performed immediately. The Cartiform allograft was placed directly over the defect, ensuring proper alignment of the osseous side with the subchondral bone, and then trimmed to precisely fit the lesion. Then, 4-0 Vicryl (Ethicon) sutures were passed in a mattress fashion at the anticipated anchor sites. A drill was used to prepare the subchondral bone at the superolateral margin of the defect for the insertion of a 2.5-mm mini-PushLock anchor (Arthrex). This process was repeated at the superomedial and inferior margins to secure the graft with 3 anchors, taking care to avoid overtensioning. Finally, fibrin glue was applied over the graft and allowed to set for 5 minutes.

PJAC Implantation Without the Microfracture Procedure

Patients undergoing PJAC did not receive subchondral drilling. After the bed was prepared, a thin layer of fibrin glue was placed at the base of the defect. One package of DeNovo NT graft was allocated for every 2.5 cm2 of defect area. The nutrient medium was aspirated, and the particulated cartilage pieces were transferred evenly on top of the thin layer of fibrin glue. A second layer of fibrin glue was placed over the PJAC and allowed to dry for 5 minutes.

Postoperative Protocol

Both groups followed an identical rehabilitation protocol postsurgery. Each patient was placed in a postoperative brace locked in extension for the first 2 weeks following surgery and weight bear as tolerated. Following this, patients were allowed to be 50% weightbearing with the knee locked in extension from weeks 2 to 6. After 2 weeks, the brace was unlocked, and patients were able to begin passive knee flexion up to 90° when not weightbearing. However, the brace was locked in extension for the first 6 weeks while weightbearing. The reasoning for limiting range of motion initially was to allow for the soft tissue envelope to subside and begin healing. Formal physical therapy began at 2 weeks postoperatively and focused on passive range of motion and quad activation. After 6 weeks, patients were weaned out of their brace and off their crutches. Jogging was allowed to begin at 4 to 6 months, and return to sports typically was allowed at 7 to 9 months after surgery.

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables, including patient demographics, patient-reported outcomes, and complications. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Comparisons between the aSCD and NoSCD groups were made using either the unpaired Student t test or χ2 test, with a significance level of .05. Additionally, for categorical data and nonnormally distributed data, a nonparametric Mann-Whitney U test was performed. The minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was determined using the distribution-based method, defined as half the standard deviation of the change in outcome scores from preoperative to 1-year follow-up and preoperative to final follow-up. MCID thresholds were calculated separately for the IKDC and Lysholm scores. Threshold analyses were then performed to evaluate the proportion of patients in each cohort who exceeded the MCID, and these proportions were compared using the χ2 test to identify any clinically meaningful differences between groups. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing) and Excel (Microsoft).

Results

A total of 65 patients were included in the study, with 31 patients undergoing aSCD and 34 undergoing NoSCD (Fig 1). At the 1-year follow-up, the MCID threshold for IKDC was 6.85 points in both groups. All NoSCD patients (100%) surpassed this threshold compared to 87.1% of aSCD patients, although the difference was not statistically significant (P = .100). For Lysholm scores, the MCID was 6.16 in the NoSCD group and 6.90 in the aSCD group. A significantly higher proportion of NoSCD patients exceeded the Lysholm MCID compared to aSCD patients (100% vs 83.9%; P = .049).

At the final follow-up, the MCID for IKDC was 6.50 in the NoSCD group and 7.16 in the aSCD group. All NoSCD patients (100%) again exceeded the threshold, while only 80.6% of aSCD patients did, representing a statistically significant difference (P = .024). For Lysholm scores, the MCID was 5.46 in the NoSCD group and 6.85 in the aSCD group, with both groups showing high rates of achievement (97.1% vs 93.5%) and no significant difference between them (P = .935).

Discussion

The main finding of this study was that patients with isolated patellar cartilage defects treated with scaffold without microfracture exhibited statistically significant improved IKDC and Lysholm scores compared to those treated with microfracture. Additionally, patients in the NoSCD had a lower frequency of total postoperative complications. Both groups had an overall similar rate of patients who were converted to arthroplasty. In multivariable regression adjusting for scaffold type, TTO, and defect size, the use of subchondral drilling was independently associated with a mean 8.97-point lower final IKDC (P = .001) and a 12.71-point lower final Lysholm score (P < .001).

Previous studies on microfracture in various locations of the knee have highlighted suboptimal long-term knee function and degenerative changes, despite favorable short-term outcomes.24,25 For example, a prospective study by Solheim et al.25 involving 110 patients undergoing microfracture for focal chondral defects found that 43 patients (39.1%) required reoperation. In response to these high reoperation rates, recent literature has increasingly focused on scaffolds to augment microfracture procedures. Cole et al.26 conducted a multicenter study on augmented microfracture for focal knee cartilage defects, showing that 90% of patients achieved clinically improved outcomes at a 2-year follow-up, with a low complication rate of 2.1% and an additional 2.1% treatment failure rate. They also found an improvement in IKDC scores from a mean of 41.5 preoperatively to a mean of 74.7 at the 24-month follow-up. Our study showed similar, but smaller, improvements in IDKC (74.0 to 81.0) and Lysholm (53.0 to 76.2) scores from preoperative to final follow-up for patients undergoing aSCD. However, our study’s aSCD group had a higher rate of complications (45.2%) compared to the study group in Cole et al.26 A randomized controlled trial by Kim et al.27 comparing augmented microfracture with microfracture alone found that augmented microfracture resulted in improved defect filling and integration.

A systematic review by Fortier et al.9 comparing augmented microfracture with microfracture alone found that augmented microfracture led to greater improvements in Lysholm and radiographic magnetic resonance observation of cartilage repair tissue scores, but with similar improvements in other measures. Despite these advantages, they observed a comparable complication rate for augmented microfracture (16.4%) and microfracture alone (16.7%), suggesting that augmenting microfracture does not necessarily reduce complications, even though it may improve other outcomes.9 The higher complication rate for the aSCD group of this study (45.2%) may be explained by the mean chondral defect size being 3.7 cm2, compared to 2.4 cm2 in Cole et al.26 and 1.3 to 4.8 cm2 in Fortier et al.9 A systemic review by Gopinatth et al.28 showed that using microfracture for chondral defects, with included studies reporting mean defect sizes ranging from 2.3 to 4.0 cm2, was associated with long-term osteoarthritis progression in 40% to 48% of patients, as well as low return-to-sport rates (17.2%-20%).

The high complication rate and lower PROMs in the aSCD group of this study may also be attributed to the inherent compromise of the subchondral bone during both augmented and nonaugmented subchondral drilling procedures. Prior research has emphasized the important role of subchondral bone integrity in the healing of patellar cartilage injuries.29 It has been observed that decreasing subchondral bone volume has a negative effect on cartilage repair tissue.30 A study by Minas et al.31 found that cartilage defects previously treated in a manner that affected the subchondral bone had a 3-fold higher failure rate after autologous chondrocyte implantation, supporting the notion that microfracture-induced dysfunction of the subchondral bone compromises patellar cartilage restoration in subsequent procedures. In the present study, penetration of the patellar subchondral plate that occurred in the aSCD group may have contributed to subchondral bone dysfunction and impaired cartilage repair as compared to the NoSCD group.

In recent years, scaffolding techniques without microfracture have gained popularity, primarily due to the avoidance of subchondral bone penetration. Although research in this area is limited, results have been promising, especially with particulated cartilage.14 A recent case series by Pearsall et al.32 investigating the use of PJAC for patellar cartilage injuries found a 100% return-to-sport rate with reasonable PROMs and complication and reoperation rates of 29.3% and 14.6%, respectively. Another case series by Marmor et al.33 using PJAC showed significant improvements in PROMs at the 2-year follow-up and over 66% defect filling on magnetic resonance imaging at 3 months. These results align with our study, where patients undergoing NoSCD showed significant improvements in median IKDC (74.0 to 81.0; P < .001) and Lysholm (77.0 to 83.0; P < .001) scores from preoperative to final follow-up, with values significantly higher than those in the aSCD group. With respect to MCID, at final follow-up, a significantly greater proportion of NoSCD patients exceeded the IKDC MCID threshold compared to aSCD patients (100% vs 80.6%; P = .024), while Lysholm MCID achievement remained high but comparable between groups (97.1% vs 93.5%; P = .935). This suggests that despite achieving statistical significance, the 2 groups may report similar outcomes, as tested by the Lysholm score, beyond 2 years postoperatively. The PJAC technique may have advantages due to its adaptability for irregular lesions and its preservation of subchondral bone integrity.34 Importantly, although the NoSCD group had higher overall PROMs, multivariable regression revealed that BioCartilage scaffold use was independently associated with improved Lysholm scores compared to Cartiform, even after adjusting for microfracture status, TTO, and defect size. This may reflect within-group differences in scaffold performance, underscoring the importance of scaffold selection when microfracture is performed.

In this study, patients undergoing NoSCD had a lower rate of total complications compared to those undergoing aSCD (14.7% vs 45.2%, respectively; P < .05). Of all complications, arthrofibrosis and cartilage overgrowth were most common. There was no statistically significant difference between the groups in the proportion of cases with cartilage overgrowth, likely due to the study being underpowered (P = .234). Mithoefer et al.11 identified that subchondral bone overgrowth occurred in 62% of patients undergoing microfracture, which was associated with an increased rate of postoperative failure. Importantly, only 2 patients in the aSCD group (6.5%) were converted to a total knee arthroplasty, and 0 patients in the NoSCD group (0%) were converted to an arthroplasty (P = .432). The current literature on patellar cartilage lesions and progression to arthroplasty is limited, but there is literature evaluating the rate of total knee arthroplasty following microfracture of other cartilage knee defects. Solheim et al.25 reported a 6.4% rate of knee replacement within a median 12-year follow-up. Although this is numerically similar to the 6.5% conversion rate observed in our aSCD group, the shorter mean 52.7-month follow-up in our study limits direct comparison. However, prior studies evaluating conversion rates to arthroplasty after microfracture have primarily focused on degenerative lesions of the knee, with reported rates ranging from approximately 6.2% to 38.1%, and may not be directly comparable to the present cohort, which included only traumatic lesions.35,36 Despite NoSCD with PJAC procedures being shown to have a low rate of conversion to arthroplasty, they have been associated with revision surgery for other reasons, including persistent pain or debridement due to graft hypertrophy. For instance, consistent with the present study, Tompkins et al.12 report a 31% rate of graft hypertrophy and a 2% rate of arthrofibrosis in their study on PJAC allograft for patellar chondral defects. These findings suggest a potential benefit to avoiding subchondral bone penetration; however, this observation should be interpreted with caution, as differences in scaffold type between groups may also have influenced outcomes.

Limitations

Important limitations within the study need to be addressed. Given the retrospective, observational design, there is an inherent risk of selection bias, particularly as the transition from aSCD to NoSCD techniques occurred over time without structured randomization, potentially confounding the comparison between treatment groups and introducing performance bias. Specifically, although participants of both cohorts had similar demographics, the overall makeup of the entire study was predominantly female, reflecting the natural patient presentation and treatment pattern at our institution but making it less representative of the general population. Another limitation of this study is that although the CDI was similar between both groups, it was not included as a covariate in the analysis, and its potential influence on clinical outcomes was not formally assessed. Additionally, the high frequency of TTO in both groups introduces a potential confounder. However, multiple linear regression confirmed it was not a confounder. A further limitation is the absence of postoperative Caton-Deschamps measurements, knee surgery histories, and precise injury-to-surgery intervals in the available records, which precluded analysis of these potentially influential factors. Additionally, the small sample size of 65 patients may have limited the statistical power of the results, potentially increasing the chance of a type II error occurring. While consistency was maintained by having a single surgeon perform all procedures at 1 institution, this limits the generalizability of the findings to other clinical settings. Additionally, this study included only subjective measures via PROMs without objective assessment, such as advanced imaging or histologic outcomes, limiting the ability to characterize the mechanical properties of the repaired tissue. Furthermore, only 1 assessor measured the radiographic variables, and the patient-reported outcome assessments were unblinded. Although the primary focus was to evaluate NoSCD versus aSCD, the study did not include a direct comparison of the specific techniques within these cohorts, partly due to some overlap between the 2 groups. Furthermore, different scaffolds were utilized between the aSCD and NoSCD groups. While a regression analysis was run, BioCartilage was shown to be independently associated with improved Lysholm scores compared to Cartiform. Although the aSCD cohort had a mean defect size of 3.7 cm2, exceeding the typical <2.0 cm2 indication for microfracture, this likely contributed to the higher complication rates and may have negatively impacted clinical outcomes. This distinction provides important context when comparing groups and highlights defect size as a potential confounding factor when evaluating the relative efficacy of microfracture versus nonmicrofracture techniques. Additionally, follow-up in this study was limited to a mean of 50.4 months, which may not fully capture the long-term durability or failure of these cartilage restoration techniques. Finally, the data could not be disaggregated by sex and/or gender due to the nature of the data set and the small sample size.

Conclusions

For the treatment of isolated patellar cartilage lesions, a nonsubchondral drilling technique with scaffolding was associated with improved patient-reported outcomes and fewer complications compared to a subchondral drilling technique with scaffolding, although there was no difference in the rate of conversion to total knee arthroplasty.
