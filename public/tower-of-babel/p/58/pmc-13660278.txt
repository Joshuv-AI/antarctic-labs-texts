Mid-ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm causing left main coronary artery compression in a patient with a history of tuberculosis
Authors: Hyun-Ah Lim, June Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAortic pseudoaneurysm secondary to Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection is exceedingly rare. Extrinsic compression of the left main coronary artery by an aortic pseudoaneurysm is also uncommon, and cases attributable to tuberculosis are particularly rare. This report describes a mid-ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm causing left main coronary artery compression resulting in cardiac arrest in a patient with a history of tuberculosis, which was successfully managed with emergent surgical intervention.Case presentationA 76-year-old man with a history of tuberculosis and radiologic findings suggestive of previous tuberculous disease experienced cardiac arrest prior to transfer and was referred to the authors’ institution. Background

Pseudoaneurysm most commonly arises in association with prior cardiac surgery or vascular trauma, whereas infectious pseudoaneurysm, defined as infection-related dilation of the aorta, is exceedingly rare. According to Raavi et al. [1], cases caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection are particularly uncommon.

In contrast to true aneurysms, which involve dilation of all three layers of the arterial wall without disruption, pseudoaneurysms result from disruption of the layers, permitting blood to extravasate into adjacent tissues and form a contained hematoma encapsulated by surrounding structures [2]. This structural instability predisposes to rapid expansion, with potential compression or invasion of adjacent anatomical structures.

Pseudoaneurysms compressing the coronary arteries have been reported, with clinical presentations ranging from asymptomatic incidental finding to acute myocardial infarction and cardiogenic shock [3, 4].

An exceptionally rare case of mid-ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm causing extrinsic compression of the left main coronary artery (LMCA), culminating in cardiac arrest, is presented in a patient with a history of tuberculosis.

Case presentation

A 76-year-old man was transferred to the authors’ institution following cardiac arrest with return of spontaneous circulation after approximately 48 min of cardiopulmonary resuscitation.

The patient had a history of treatment for tuberculosis at another facility, and preoperative imaging showed findings compatible with previous tuberculous disease. Pericardiocentesis had been performed for pericardial effusion, which revealed dark, blood-tinged fluid. Prior to the cardiac arrest, sudden-onset chest pain developed, and electrocardiography demonstrated ST-segment elevation in leads aVR and V1–V3 (Fig. 1A). Elevated cardiac enzyme levels were reported. Coronary angiography (CAG; Fig. 1B) revealed extrinsic compression of the LMCA by an apparent pseudoaneurysm of the ascending aorta. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a marked reduction in left ventricular ejection fraction, from 63% two days earlier to 33.5%, with severe global hypokinesia and akinesia. Chest pain persisted until the onset of cardiac arrest, and there was no interval change in the characteristics of the pericardial effusion suggestive of aortic rupture.

Preoperative electrocardiogram and coronary angiography. (A) The electrocardiogram shows ST-segment elevation in leads aVR and V1–V3. (B) The coronary angiogram shows extrinsic compression of the left main coronary artery (yellow arrow) by the ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm

The patient was intubated upon arrival and had an indwelling pericardial catheter, through which no evidence of fresh or active bleeding was observed. He was tachycardic and hypothermic, with initial vital signs of blood pressure of 111/48 mmHg, heart rate of 145 beats/min, respiratory rate of 20 breaths/min, and temperature of 34.9 °C. Laboratory evaluation demonstrated a hemoglobin level of 11.9 g/dL and neutrophil-predominant leukocytosis (white blood cell count, 34.90 × 10³/µL; neutrophils, 86.7%), with elevated inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein, 7.86 mg/dL; procalcitonin, 0.21 ng/mL), cardiac biomarkers (creatine kinase-MB, 184.81 ng/mL; troponin I, 38.669 ng/mL), and pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (6,496.1 pg/mL). Computed tomography (CT) performed immediately after transfer (Fig. 2) revealed a 6-cm pseudoaneurysm arising from the aortic root and extending to the mid-ascending aorta, projecting toward the lesser curvature. A small amount of pericardial effusion was present. A diagnosis of mid-ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm with acute myocardial infarction secondary to LMCA compression was established, and urgent surgical intervention was undertaken.

Preoperative computed tomography. The contrast-enhanced imaging shows a large pseudoaneurysm (white arrow) arising from the mid-ascending aorta along the lesser curvature, extending from the aortic root to the mid-ascending aorta, with associated pericardial effusion

A full median sternotomy was performed, revealing mild pericardial adhesions. Following pericardiotomy, the ascending aorta was noted to be discolored, with a bruise-like appearance and a saccular dilation protruding toward the main pulmonary artery (Fig. 3A). Direct aortic cannulation and dual-stage right atrial cannulation were performed under intraoperative epiaortic ultrasound guidance to establish cardiopulmonary bypass. A left ventricular vent catheter was inserted via the right upper pulmonary vein, and an aortic root cannula was placed. Antegrade del Nido cardioplegia was administered, and the mid-ascending aorta was transected. Intimal disruption was identified along the lesser curvature (Fig. 3B) and a pseudoaneurysm containing blood and purulent-appearing material was identified on the posterior aspect of the ascending aorta, protruding toward the roof of the left atrium. Specimens were obtained for microbiological analysis. The aortic valve demonstrated partial cusp calcification with preserved coaptation and no evidence of infection. The affected segment of the ascending aorta was resected, and aortic replacement was performed using a 30-mm Gelweave graft (Vascutek, Terumo, Inchinnan, UK). The distal anastomosis was completed using a continuous 3 − 0 Prolene suture, reinforced on the lesser curvature with pledgeted 4 − 0 Prolene sutures. The proximal anastomosis was constructed 1 cm above the sinotubular junction using a Teflon felt strip and 4 − 0 Prolene (Fig. 3C). Total cardiopulmonary bypass and aortic cross-clamp times were 96 min and 67 min, respectively. Transesophageal echocardiography at the conclusion of the procedure demonstrated no residual abnormalities. Two chest tubes were placed, and the operation was completed.

Intraoperative findings and surgical repair. (A) Discoloration and saccular dilation of the ascending aorta (black star) are seen protruding toward the main pulmonary artery. (B) Intimal disruption (white star) is identified along the lesser curvature following transection of the ascending aorta. (C) The completed ascending aortic replacement using a 30-mm Gelweave graft

Because of postoperative oliguria on the day of surgery, continuous renal replacement therapy was initiated in the intensive care unit. The patient was extubated on postoperative day 1. CAG on postoperative day 2 (Fig. 4A) confirmed resolution of extrinsic compression of the LMCA, although persistent tubular stenosis (50% to 70%) of the proximal left anterior descending artery (LAD) was identified and managed conservatively. The chest tubes were removed on postoperative day 5, and the patient was subsequently transferred to the general ward. Histopathological examination of intraoperative specimens revealed aortic dissection and rupture with myxoid degeneration and necrosis of the tunica media, along with necrotizing suppurative inflammation of the tunica adventitia (Fig. 4B). No granulomatous inflammation or caseating granulomas were identified, and acid-fast bacillus (AFB) staining was negative. Polymerase chain reaction testing was not performed. Nevertheless, in the appropriate clinical context, the presence of a nontraumatic pseudoaneurysm in the mid-ascending aorta was highly suggestive of an infectious etiology. Given the patient’s history of tuberculosis and radiologic findings suggestive of previous tuberculous disease, tuberculosis-related involvement was considered clinically possible. However, because microbiological and histological confirmation was not obtained, the etiology could not be definitively established. Follow-up CT on postoperative day 7 (Fig. 4C) demonstrated no residual pseudoaneurysm or anastomotic complications, and the patient was discharged home in stable condition.

Postoperative evaluation and histopathology findings. (A) Postoperative coronary angiography shows resolution of the extrinsic compression of the left main coronary artery. (B) At histopathology, the resected aortic wall shows necrotizing suppurative inflammation (hematoxylin and eosin staining, ×400). (C) Follow-up computed tomography on postoperative day 7 shows no residual pseudoaneurysm or anastomotic complications following replacement of the affected ascending aorta

Discussion and conclusions

Tuberculous aortic aneurysms predominantly affect men and are associated with factors such as travel, migration, multidrug-resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains, and immunosuppression [5].

Tuberculous pseudoaneurysm typically arises from the contiguous spread of infection from periaortic tissues into the aortic wall. Given the role of lymphatic dissemination, the distal aortic arch and descending thoracic aorta, being anatomically adjacent to mediastinal lymph node chains, are most commonly involved [6]. While involvement of the ascending aorta is exceedingly rare, it has been reported in the setting of disseminated tuberculosis and may lead to life-threatening complications because of its proximity to critical structures, including the pulmonary and coronary arteries.

In the present case, the ascending aortic location was atypical, and definitive microbiological or histological confirmation of tuberculous aortitis was not obtained. AFB staining was negative, and neither granulomatous inflammation nor caseating granuloma was identified. Nevertheless, tuberculosis-related involvement was considered clinically possible because the patient had a history of tuberculosis and radiologic findings compatible with previous tuberculous disease, while the pseudoaneurysm was nontraumatic and showed intraoperative and histopathological features suggestive of an infectious process. Importantly, the central clinical relevance of this case lies in the rare anatomical and physiological presentation: a mid-ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm causing LMCA compression, acute myocardial infarction, and cardiac arrest, which was successfully treated by ascending aortic replacement without coronary artery bypass grafting.

Previous reports have suggested that radiologic proximity between a pseudoaneurysm and a tuberculous lesion may support a tuberculous etiology [7]. In the present case, however, such direct anatomical continuity was not demonstrated. Therefore, tuberculosis was interpreted as a relevant clinical background and possible contributing etiology rather than a proven cause. Moreover, failure to detect M. tuberculosis in vascular or extrapulmonary tissue may reflect a low bacterial burden, prior anti-tuberculous therapy, or limitations in specimen handling and testing [7–11]. Accordingly, a negative acid-fast bacillus result does not exclude tuberculous involvement. However, alternative infectious or inflammatory etiologies could not be completely excluded, and this diagnostic uncertainty remains an important limitation of the present case.

Histopathological findings demonstrated rupture with myxoid degeneration and necrosis of the tunica media, accompanied by necrotizing suppurative inflammation of the tunica adventitia. These findings are consistent with prior observations that noninfectious inflammatory aneurysms typically exhibit chronic inflammation and adventitial fibrosis, whereas infectious aneurysms are characterized by a more intense acute inflammatory response involving the entire vessel wall [11].

Extrinsic coronary artery compression caused by pseudoaneurysm of the ascending aorta has been reported most commonly as a complication of prior cardiac surgery or catheter-based intervention [4, 12–14]. Nemec et al. [3] reported a case of near-total occlusion of the LMCA due to external compression caused by a proximal ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm, with Takayasu arteritis identified as the underlying etiology. Surgical repair and coronary artery bypass grafting were performed. In the present case, the coronary artery compression was focal, and the LMCA orifice was intact on intraoperative assessment. Residual stenosis of the LAD was considered hemodynamically acceptable and restoration of LMCA flow was established through pseudoaneurysm resection without coronary artery bypass grafting.

Moukarbel et al. [4] have described a strategy involving initial percutaneous intervention prior to surgical management. However, surgical intervention remains the gold standard for definitive management of infectious aortic pseudoaneurysm [15, 16] because complete excision of infected tissue is critical to prevent recurrence and improve long-term outcomes. Regardless of anatomical location, extensive debridement and reconstruction of the affected aortic segment are required. Optimal management of an infectious aortic pseudoaneurysm requires en bloc resection of all involved aortic segments and surrounding inflammatory or necrotic tissue, and successful outcomes have been reported with combined aortic root and valve replacement in cases of tuberculous pseudoaneurysms involving the aortic root and ascending aorta [8]. Prolonged antimicrobial therapy is also essential, particularly in tuberculous aortitis, in which multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant strains are increasingly encountered [6].

The practical implication of this case is that an ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm causing left main coronary artery compression should be treated as a life-threatening condition requiring urgent intervention. When myocardial ischemia, hemodynamic instability, or cardiac arrest occurs, definitive surgical repair should not be delayed while awaiting microbiological confirmation of tuberculosis. In the present case, aggressive surgical resection of the affected aortic segment and ascending aortic replacement successfully relieved left main coronary artery compression and resulted in a favorable clinical outcome. When a tuberculous or other infectious etiology is suspected, complete removal of infected or necrotic tissue, intraoperative sampling for microbiological and histopathological evaluation, postoperative pathogen-directed antimicrobial therapy, and close imaging surveillance should be considered. These measures should be regarded as adjuncts to, rather than substitutes for, timely surgical source control in patients with life-threatening anatomical complications.

In patients with prohibitive operative risk or contraindications to open surgery, endovascular repair may serve as a temporizing or adjunctive option, although it is limited in its ability to eradicate infection and is associated with a substantial risk of recurrence [17, 18]. Because of the high risk of rupture associated with pseudoaneurysms [1, 9], surgical approaches such as median sternotomy must be undertaken with caution, and establishment of cardiopulmonary bypass via femoral or axillary cannulation prior to sternotomy may be advisable in selected cases [19]. The combination of surgical and medical therapy is associated with the most favorable prognosis in patients with tuberculous aortic aneurysms. Extended postoperative surveillance, typically ranging from 6 months to 2 years depending on individual clinical circumstances, is recommended [5].

This case is notable for a mid-ascending aortic pseudoaneurysm causing LMCA compression and cardiac arrest in a patient with a history of tuberculosis. Although tuberculosis-related involvement could not be definitively proven, the case highlights the importance of timely diagnosis using CAG and CT and supports urgent ascending aortic replacement as a successful treatment strategy in this rare but life-threatening presentation.
