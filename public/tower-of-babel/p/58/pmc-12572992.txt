Sulfate-Reducing Bacteria-Induced Corrosion Behavior of L415 Pipeline Steel in Southern Jiangxi, China
Authors: Yangqin Shangguan, Minghao Liu, Xuyu Liu, Ke Mei, Baocheng Shi, Hao Chen, Ruiquan Liao, Kai Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC) caused by sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) poses a severe threat to oil and gas transmission pipelines. This study investigated the corrosion behavior of L415 pipeline steel in an SRB environment in the acidic soil of southern Jiangxi, China, by employing weight loss measurements, electrochemical measurements, and surface analysis. The growth curve revealed that the SRB, isolated and purified from soil samples in the Southern Jiangxi region, exhibited high activity in an acidic soil environment and further adhered to the surface of the specimen, ultimately leading to MIC of the pipeline steel. The experimental results showed that by reducing SO4 2–, SRB could enhance cathodic reactions. The Fe2+ from anodic dissolution reacted with S2– to form FeS, significantly accelerating corrosion. Additionally, the formation of FeS-SRB/Fe galvanic couples, formed on the surface of L415 pipeline steel specimens, promoted the localized corrosion. The depth of the pitting corrosion increased with the extended immersion time. These experimental results would provide theoretical references for the practical application of pipeline engineering in China and the corrosion control of the SRB environment in southern Jiangxi.

Introduction

China’s continuous economic growth and national security concerns have heightened the demand for a stable energy supply. Against this backdrop, energy infrastructure projects have emerged as the core pillar for ensuring national energy security. The West-East Gas Pipeline network, a vital component of this system, plays a crucial role. Jiangxi Province, situated in the hinterland of southeastern China, serves as a strategic link connecting the two major economic engines of the Yangtze River Delta and the Pearl River Delta. These two economic zones contribute over 35% of the national GDP and exhibit their large-scale and highly stable energy demands. The second and third pipelines of the West-East Gas Pipeline Project run through the entire territory of Jiangxi, establishing it as a crucial “energy corridor” that connects central and western energy production areas with eastern consumer markets.

In Jiangxi, acidic red soil covers 70.7% of the area. Southern Jiangxi’s soil, with low oxygen, a pH of 3–6, and high sulfate ions, typifies Southeast China’s acidic red soil. This unique soil environment fosters sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB) proliferation, leading to severe microbiologically influenced corrosion (MIC) in pipelines. MIC is highly damaging to oil and gas transportation and production systems and is a key factor in reducing the service life of long-term pipelines. − Statistics indicate that MIC contributes to 15%–30% of total losses in the oil and gas industry. Thus, studying SRB-induced corrosion of pipeline steel in acidic red soil is vital for preventing and controlling MIC in such regions.

In recent years, research on corrosion in acidic red soil environments has made significant progress. Du et al. investigated the short-term corrosion behavior of API X100 steel in acidic simulated soil from Yingtan, Jiangxi, revealing X100 steel’s high susceptibility to pitting corrosion. Wang et al. explored the impact of pH and dissolved oxygen on X80 steel’s corrosion behavior in simulated acidic red soil solutions from Yingtan. They found that under oxygen-free and low-pH conditions, hydrogen reduction is nearly the sole cathodic process, forming small, dense pits. As pH increases, pit numbers decrease but their sizes grow. Li et al. studied Q235 steel’s corrosion behavior in saturated acidic red and yellow soils from Xiangtan, Hunan, identifying low pH, high corrosive anion content, and high-valence iron oxide content as key factors for high corrosion rates and local corrosion sensitivity in red soils. Ma et al. compared the full-immersion corrosion and polarization behaviors of B + F duplex X80 pipeline steel in simulated near-neutral and acidic soil solutions. They found that X80 steel’s corrosion rate and current are significantly higher in acidic soil solutions.

The performance of pipeline steel during service is affected not only by the soil environment but also by microorganisms in the environment. As one of the main species causing MIC and failure, SRB can reduce sulfate ions in the environment to low-valence sulfides through its metabolic activities. − Zhao et al. analyzed the corrosion behavior of SRB under the simulated solution. They found that in the presence of iron oxides, sulfate-reducing bacteria can interact with CaCO3, causing microbial dissimilation, reducing iron oxides, forming a loose FeS product film, and accelerating the corrosion process of steel pipes. Guo et al. studied the corrosion behavior of X80 steel in near-neutral solutions under SRB stress and found that SRB accelerates the transformation of Fe3O4 into sulfide compounds and may lead to film rupture of X80 steel products. Wang et al. studied the coupled effect of SRB and dynamic AC interference on buried metal pipelines in near-neutral soil environments and found that sinusoidal AC significantly reduces SRB growth activity. Initially, the microbial film inhibits corrosion, but as the microbial film detaches, corrosion intensifies. Moreover, the formation of corrosion products like sulfides and iron phosphides accelerates the corrosion process. Sun et al. studied the corrosion behavior of X70 pipeline steel under the combined action of SRB and constant load in alkaline earth solutions and found that plastic stress and SRB significantly increase the stress corrosion cracking sensitivity of X70 pipeline steel. SRB promotes pitting corrosion of pipeline steel, and both exhibit a synergistic effect in promoting pitting. Due to the complex life activities of SRB and differences in SRB species across regions, their impact on pipeline steel corrosion varies. Chen et al. studied the effect of SRB on the corrosion behavior of X80 pipeline steel in the soil environment of Yingtan, Jiangxi, and found that in the early stages of the experiment, highly bioactive SRB biofilms inhibit pipeline steel corrosion. Nevertheless, in the later stages, SRB accelerates pipeline steel corrosion. Qi et al. isolated a sulfate-reducing bacterium SRB-Z from the Pearl River Estuary in Guangzhou and found that it can induce severe pitting corrosion on Q235 carbon steel and enhance the cathodic reaction rate of Q235. Dong et al. isolated and cultured a sulfate-reducing bacterium SY-1 from the rust layer of the South China Sea and found that it differently affects the corrosion process of Q235 carbon steel across metabolic stages. In the first stage, SY-1 cells begin to adhere to the Q235 surface, promoting iron’s anodic dissolution. In the second stage, cell growth is inhibited, and SY-1 directly obtains energy from Fe, further intensifying pitting, with the maximum pitting depth approaching 15.7 μm.

However, based on the above-mentioned literature review, we found that current research on acidic red soil corrosion mainly focuses on high-strength pipelines like X80 and X100, while studies on L415 pipeline steel commonly used in medium and low-pressure transmission lines and branch networks of the West-East Gas Pipeline are relatively scarce. Moreover, there are relatively few studies on the southern Jiangxi region where the red soil coverage rate is high and the pipeline infrastructure is dense. Systematic research on the influence of SRB in the red soil of southern Jiangxi on the corrosion of pipeline steel is even rarer. Hence, to investigate the corrosion behavior of L415 pipeline steel in the SRB environment of southern Jiangxi, China, this study utilized SRB isolated and purified from soil samples collected around buried pipelines in the southern Jiangxi region. The research results could provide certain guiding significance for the safety of energy engineering in China.

Results and Discussion

SRB Growth Curve

The growth curve of SRB in the solution following immersion of L415 pipeline steel for varying durations is presented in Figure . The growth profile of SRB can be broadly categorized into three distinct phases. In the initial phase (0–7 days), abundant carbon sources in the solution supported a remarkable increase in the SRB population, marking the exponential growth phase. During this period, heightened reproductive activity of SRB was observed, with the maximum measured concentration reaching approximately 1.1 × 106 CFU/mL. From day 8 to day 10, SRB population growth plateaued, indicative of the stationary phase. Here, the rates of new bacterial proliferation and decay achieved a near-equilibrium state. Subsequently, from day 11 to day 14, nutrient depletion became pronounced as organic matter was progressively consumed. This propelled the SRB population into the decline phase, with counts dropping significantly to 1.15 × 103 CFU/mL by day 14.

Corrosion Rate

The corrosion rates of L415 pipeline steel after immersion for 7 and 14 days in both inoculated and sterile environments were depicted in Figure . The rates in the inoculated medium were substantially higher than in the sterile environment. Specifically, in SRB inoculated environment, the corrosion rate after 7 days of immersion was as high as 0.182 mm/y, classified as severe corrosion. By 14 days, due to the temporary protective effect of the corrosion product film, the corrosion rate decreased to 0.135 mm/y, remaining in the severe corrosion category. In contrast, in the sterile environment, the corrosion rate after 7 days of immersion was 0.157 mm/y, which was also considered severe corrosion. However, after 14 days, the corrosion rate dropped to 0.115 mm/y, transitioning to moderate corrosion. These results indicate that SRB presence significantly accelerates L415 pipeline steel corrosion.

Microscopic Morphology and Analysis of Corrosion Products

The microscopic morphology and EDS analysis results of L415 pipeline steel specimens immersed for 5 days in the simulated soil solution of southern Jiangxi with and without SRB were shown in Figure . In the SRB-inoculated solution (Figure a), after 5 day immersion, rodshaped SRB cells densely adhered to the specimen surface. They intermingled with loose corrosion products, forming clustered aggregations. The specimen surface was covered with discontinuous flocculent corrosion products, which were more densely attached than in the sterile environment (Figure c). EDS analysis indicated that the corrosion products mainly consisted of Fe, C, O, and P, with trace amounts of S. This sulfur may have been the reducing S produced by SRB through dissimilatory reactions. In the sterile solution (Figure c), after 5-day immersion, the specimen surface was relatively smooth, with only a small amount of corrosion products accumulated.

SEM morphology and EDS elemental analysis of specimens after 5 days of immersion: (a,b) SRB; (c,d) Sterile.

As the immersion time increased, corrosion products continued to form and gradually accumulated. After 14 days of immersion under the SRB-inoculated condition (Figure a), the specimen surface was nearly entirely covered with clustered FeS and laminated corrosion products. EDS analysis revealed that the surface film’s carbon (C) content had risen significantly to 15.57%, compared to 5.17% in the sterile environment. This implies that carbon-rich extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), produced during SRB metabolism, accumulated extensively on the specimen surface. Meanwhile, the sulfur (S) content in the corrosion products increased from 0.96% to 3.81%, indicating a substantial rise in FeS formation due to SRB activity. In the sterile environment (Figure c), after 14-day immersion, the specimen surface was uniformly coated with a thin corrosion-product film. This film exhibited fine-crack patterns, with more corrosion products present than after 7-day immersion.

XRD Analysis

To gain a deeper understanding of the composition of the corrosion products, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed on specimens immersed for 14 days in southern Jiangxi simulated soil solutions, both with and without SRB. The diffraction patterns were qualitatively analyzed using HighScore Plus, and the results were shown in Figure . The analysis revealed that in both environments, the corrosion products comprised Fe2O3, Fe3O4, and FeO­(OH). However, under SRB-inoculated conditions, due to the growth and metabolic activities of SRB, the products also contained FeS and FeSO4(H2O)4. This indicates that SRB activity alters the corrosion-product composition, potentially accelerating the corrosion process through the formation of additional compounds such as FeS.

XRD analysis results of surface corrosion products of L415 pipeline steel after 14 days of immersion in environments with SRB and Sterile conditions.

Considering the growth processes of the microbial film and the corrosion product film, the cathodic reaction process of L415 pipeline steel in the environment of SRB in the southern Jiangxi region was mainly manifested as the hydrogen ions (H+) produced by water electrolysis combining with the electrons provided by the dissolution of Fe. This interaction led to the cathodic hydrogen evolution reaction Subsequently, the SRB promoted the reaction of SO4 2– in the solution with hydrogen atoms to undergo a cathodic depolarization reaction. This process lowers the cathodic-reaction activation energy, accelerating both the cathodic and anodic metal-dissolution reactions. The anodic reaction involved the Fe2+ generated from the anodic dissolution of the metal combined with the OH– provided by hydrolysis reactions to form unstable Fe­(OH)2. This Fe­(OH)2 consumes residual dissolved oxygen near the electrode surface, creating an anaerobic environment favorable for SRB growth and promoting the formation of yellow, porous FeO­(OH). Pitting Depth Analysis

To further explore the corrosion behavior of L415 pipeline steel influenced by SRB, specimens from the inoculated environment were cleaned to remove the corrosion-product film. Their surface condition was then examined via laser confocal scanning microscopy (CLSM), with pitting-corrosion depth measurements taken. The CLSM images and maximum corrosion pit depths of L415 pipeline steel after 5- and 14-day immersion under SRB-influenced conditions were shown in Figure . Per formula (), the maximum pitting depth after 5-day immersion was 5.32 μm (Figure a), corresponding to a maximum pitting rate of 0.3884 mm/y. After 14-day immersion, these values increased to 24.61 μm (Figure b) and 0.6416 mm/y, respectively, indicating extremely severe corrosion. In contrast, no significant pitting was observed in the sterile -environment specimens after rust removal. This confirms that SRB is the primary driver of pitting corrosion, with pitting depth increasing markedly with longer immersion times.

Maximum corrosion pit depths of L415 pipeline steel under SRB conditions: (a, b) 5 d; (c, d) 14 d.

In the initial stage of the immersion experiment, the carbon source in the solution was abundant. SRB utilized the culture medium in the solution as a carbon source to obtain electrons, thereby maintaining the energy required for their life activities. During this phase, SRB rapidly reproduced and formed clusters on the specimen surface (Figure a). These aggregated areas exhibited higher SRB activity and greater initial adsorption density. They produced significant amount of FeS and EPS on the surface, which preferentially initiated and promoted pitting at the aggregation sites. Simultaneously, the high concentration of EPS adsorbed Fe2+ to form metal complexes. When these complexes adhered to the metal surface, they created a corrosion cell with the metal matrix, inducing FeS-SRB/Fe galvanic corrosion. In this process, the metal matrix served as the anode, accelerating overall metal corrosion.

As the immersion time increased, the carbon source in the solution was gradually depleted. The metabolic products of SRB interwove with the corrosion products to form a complete and continuous film. This film had a certain mitigating effect on uniform corrosion. However, SRB beneath the film, facing local carbon-source exhaustion, entered a starved state. These SRBs are directly obtained obtaining electrons from Fe or indirectly used FeS, biofilms, nucleic acids, etc., to conduct electrons to meet their metabolic energy needs. ,, Coupled with the chelation of iron ions by the film, this further promoted local corrosion beneath the film.

Electrochemical Measurement Results

The Nyquist and Bode plots of L415 pipeline steel in both inoculated and sterile environments were shown in Figure . In the SRB-inoculated environment, the impedance value peaked at 10 days of immersion. This may be attributed to the stationary growth phase of SRB between 8 and 10 days, during which the corrosion products and biofilm formed by SRB adhere to the metal surface, creating a protective barrier that temporarily reduces corrosion rates. However, by 14 days, the radius of the impedance arc decreased. This decrease could be attributed to two factors: (1) As immersion time increased beyond 10 days, the continued accumulation of corrosion products on the specimen surface led to increased internal stress among the products, resulting in crack formation (Figure a). Consequently, the protection of the corrosion product film diminished, and the corrosive media (e.g., Cl–, HS–) could more readily diffuse into the metal matrix, thereby accelerating metal corrosion. (2) During SRB growth and metabolism, significant amounts of iron sulfide and corrosive H2S were produced. Iron sulfide can function as a cathode for the metal substrate, forming an electrochemical corrosion cell and increasing the corrosion rate. , Simultaneously, the formation of H2S promoted electrochemical reactions, leading to severe localized corrosion. The Bode plot (Figure b) indicated that at 14 days, the peak phase angle shifts toward the high-frequency side, suggesting the beginning of degradation of the corrosion product film and biofilm on the specimen surface (Figure a). In the sterile environment, the impedance increased with immersion time during the first 10 days. This increase may be due to the gradual accumulation of corrosion products on the specimen surface, which hinders the penetration of corrosive media. Subsequently, as immersion time further increased, the impedance capacitance arc slightly decreased and gradually stabilized. The Bode plot (Figure d) also showed that with increasing immersion time, the modulus gradually increased and then tended toward a more stable range.

Nyquist plots and Bode plots of L415 Pipeline Steel: (a, b) SRB; (c, d) Sterile.

In both environments, the Bode plots displayed a single peak in the phase-frequency diagram, indicating the presence of only one time constant. This corresponds to the single capacitive arc observed in the Nyquist plots. The impedance arc radii measured in the inoculated environment were smaller than those measured in the sterile environment (Figure a,c). Furthermore, the low-frequency impedance obtained in the presence of SRB (Figure b) was lower than that in the sterile environment (Figure d). These findings demonstrated that the presence of SRB intensifies the corrosion of L415 pipeline steel.

The equivalent circuit that was found to reproduce most closely the impedance diagram is shown in Figure . The impedance spectrum data were fitted using these equivalent circuit model. In this model, R s represented the solution resistance, Q dl was the constant phase angle element associated with the double layer, R p was the polarization resistance, and n was the dispersion index. The polarization resistance (R p), defined as the sum of the charge-transfer resistance (R ct) and the film resistance (R f), reflects both the kinetics of electrochemical reactions and the protective effect of the biofilm/corrosion-product layer. The metal corrosion rate is inversely proportional to R p: a larger R p value indicates a slower corrosion rate. The dispersion index n reflects the compactness of the corrosion product film on the electrode surface, with a larger n value indicating a denser and more uniform film, enhancing the electrode’s corrosion resistance. As seen from Figure a, in the inoculated environment, the anodic polarization curve of L415 pipeline steel showed an increased slope in a “passivation zone,” likely due to the thicker biofilm formed on the steel surface. However, overall comparison of the polarization curves in both environments reveals a more significant rightward shift in the inoculated environment, indicating enhanced electrochemical activity and a greater corrosion tendency of L415 pipeline steel in the presence of bacteria. Despite the SRB-formed biofilm partially reducing direct contact between the steel and corrosive medium, the H2S from SRB metabolism and Cl– in the solution still cause significant matrix corrosion. Thus, the electrochemical corrosion impact on L415 pipeline steel exceeds the biofilm’s isolating effect.

By comparing the fitted results of the polarization curves (Table ), it was evident that the corrosion current density (I corr) in the SRB inoculated environment was higher than that in the sterile environment. This indicated that the corrosion resistance of L415 pipeline steel was weaker in the SRB inoculated environment, making it more susceptible to corrosion.

Conclusions

Employing weight loss testing, surface analysis, potentiodynamic polarization, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), and X-ray diffraction, the study thoroughly investigated the impact of SRB on the corrosion behavior of L415 pipeline steel in the acidic soil of southern Jiangxi. The specific conclusions are as follows:(1)L415 pipeline steel corroded faster in the SRB inoculated environment than in the sterile environment, with a thicker corrosionproduct film forming on the steel surface. This demonstrated that SRB could reduce SO4 2–, and the resulting S2– reacted with Fe2+ produced by anodic dissolution to form FeS.(2)In the SRB inoculated environment, SRB not only accelerated uniform corrosion but also markedly enhanced the occurrence of localized corrosion. Pitting depth increased with immersion time, reaching 24.61 μm after 14 days. SRB biofilms and FeS created a galvanic cell with the metal matrix, inducing FeS-SRB/Fe galvanic corrosion and promoting pitting.(3)In the SRB inoculated environment, the polarization resistance of L415 pipeline steel was lower than that in the sterile environment, indicating that the presence of SRB promotes the corrosion process of L415 pipeline steel. Additionally, the self-corrosion current density was higher in the inoculated environment, suggesting reduced corrosion resistance of the pipeline steel. This further confirmed SRB’s role in accelerating steel corrosion.

SRB Culturing and Inoculation

The experiment utilized SRB isolated and purified from soil samples around buried pipelines in the southern region of Jiangxi. The strain isolation and purification procedure was performed according to the following protocol: 20 g of collected soil was placed into a 250 mL conical flask containing 100 mL of sterile dilution water (0.85% NaCl, autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min). The mixture was shaken at 200 rpm for 30 min and then allowed to stand for 12 h, after which the supernatant was used as the initial suspension. For enrichment culture, 20 mL of this supernatant was inoculated into a 250 mL anaerobic bottle containing liquid medium and incubated at 35 °C for 3–5 days until the medium turned black and produced a characteristic rotten-egg H2S odor, indicating the presence of SRB in the mixed culture. The enriched culture was serially diluted 10-fold and plated using the double-layer method, which involved a 1.5% agar base layer overlaid with 0.7% soft agar mixed with 1 mL of diluted culture. The plates were incubated under strict anaerobic conditions at 35 °C for 7 days until typical black colonies appeared. Selected single colonies were subjected to three rounds of streak purification. A single smooth colony was then inoculated into Postgate’s C sulfate-reducing bacteria-specific culture medium to prepare multiple tubes of enriched SRB broth, which were further incubated at 35 °C.

The cultured strains were stored at low temperature (4 °C). For a long-term refrigerated pure bacterial solution, the dormant bacteria need to be activated before use. Before inoculation, the strains were placed in a 35 °C incubator for 12 h to activate dormant bacteria and improve biological activity. The composition of the culture medium is as follows: 0.5 g/L KH2PO4, 1.0 g/L NH4Cl, 1.0 g/L Na2SO4, 0.05g/L CaCl2, 2.0 g/L MgCl2·6H2O, 1.0 g/L yeast extract, 1.1 g/L sodium-D-lactate, 0.1 g/L ascorbic acid, 0.1 g/L sodium mercaptoacetate, 0.5 g/L FeSO4·7H2O. The experimental soil simulation solution for the southern Jiangxi region was mixed with Postgate’s C culture medium at a volume ratio of 9:1. Then, high-purity N2 gas was employed to purge it for 2 h. This mixture was sterilized in an autoclave at 121 °C and 1.03 × 105 Pa for 20 min. It was then placed in a sterile laminar flow cabinet under ultraviolet light for 30 min to obtain the SRB culture medium simulating the soil solution of the southern Jiangxi region. After cooling to room temperature, SRB were inoculated at a ratio of 1:20, which served as the SRB soil simulant solution for the southern Jiangxi region.

Weight Loss Measurements

The weight loss experiment was conducted in a sealed glass bottle with a capacity of 800 mL. Before the experiment, the sample mass was measured and recorded using a precision balance, and the surface area was measured with a vernier caliper. The samples were then exposed to ultraviolet light for 20 min and suspended in a glass bottle containing the soil simulation solution inoculated with SRB. The bottles were placed in a biochemical incubator at a constant temperature of 35 °C for immersion periods of 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 days (with two parallel samples for each group). The culture liquid was regularly collected and counted using the Most Probable Number (MPN) method. This method involves the serial dilution of samples, which are then inoculated into liquid culture media. The concentration of live bacteria in the original sample is estimated based on the number of positive reacting tubes. By measuring the MPN values at different time points (1, 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 days), a growth curve of SRB can be plotted. This curve reflects the different growth phases of SRB, including the lag phase, logarithmic phase, stationary phase, and death phase.

After the immersion periods, the surface product film layer was removed using a rust removal solution (500 mL HCl + 500 mL deionized water + 3.5 g hexamethylenetetramine), rinsed, and dried. The samples were then placed in a drying oven for 24 h to ensure thorough drying before being weighed again. Characterization of Corrosion Surface Morphology and Corrosion Products

Following immersion testing, specimens were meticulously prepared for surface characterization to preserve the integrity of biofilms and the morphology of corrosion products. Samples were initially fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde (in 0.1 M cacodylate buffer, pH 7.2) for 24 h at 4 °C, followed by a graded ethanol dehydration series (25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% v/v) with 15 min exposure at each concentration. SEM (TESCAN MIRA LMS) was used to observe the morphologies of corrosion products on the specimen surfaces, with elemental compositions analyzed using integrated energy dispersive spectrometry (EDS).

For specimens from which surface corrosion products were removed, derusted specimens were examined using laser confocal microscopy (Keyence VHX-7100) for pitting analysis. Additionally, the composition of the corrosion products was characterized using XRD (Bruker D8 Advance).

Electrochemical Measurements

Prior to the experiment, the experimental solutions and electrochemical apparatus were subjected to high-temperature sterilization under the conditions described in Section . The experiment was divided into two groups: (a) the inoculated group with SRB; (b) the sterile control group without SRB. For the inoculated group, under a vacuum sterile laminar flow hood, SRB bacterial solution was added to the high-temperature sterilized and deoxygenated soil simulation solution from the southern Jiangxi region at a volume ratio of 5%. In contrast, the sterile control group did not receive any SRB bacterial solution.

Electrochemical testing was conducted using a CH760E electrochemical workstation. The L415 pipeline steel base metal specimen served as the working electrode, a platinum sheet as the auxiliary electrode, and a saturated calomel electrode (SCE) as the reference electrode. Samples immersed in the inoculated southern Jiangxi soil simulation solution for 3, 5, 7, 10, and 14 days were tested at 35 °C. Initially, the open-circuit potential (OCP) of the working electrode was measured. Once the OCP stabilized (≥1 h), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was performed on the specimens. The EIS parameters were as follows: an AC perturbation signal of 10 mV sinusoidal wave with a frequency range of 0.01 to 1 × 105 Hz. All measurements were repeated at least three times to ensure reproducibility. The results were fitted using ZSimpWin software. The dynamic potential polarization curve test parameters were: a scan rate of 0.5 mV/s and a test potential range of −0.5 ± 0.5 V (vs OCP). All the electrochemical tests carried out in this work were oxygen-free corrosion tests.
