Clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes of pediatric achalasia: a 10-year experience from a tertiary center in Iran
Authors: Abtin Mazhari, Hosein Alimadadi, Behdad Gharib, Sara Memarian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to examine demographic patterns, clinical presentation, diagnostic methods, and therapeutic outcomes of achalasia patients in a tertiary hospital in Tehran, Iran. Background

Achalasia is a motility disorder of unknown etiology, characterized by aperistalsis in the esophagus and failure of the lower esophageal sphincter (LES) to relax. It results from the loss of ganglion cells in the myenteric (Auerbach’s) plexus. This rare disease has an incidence of 1.6 per 100,000 children per year [1, 2]. Children with achalasia may have a lower quality of life than those with chronic inflammatory bowel disease. Achalasia can also be associated with conditions such as Down syndrome, Allgrove syndrome, and congenital central hypoventilation syndrome [3, 4].

Patients with achalasia can experience variety of symptoms such as dysphagia, nausea/vomiting, chest pain, and cough. Due to the nonspecific nature of the symptoms, there is often a delay in diagnosis, and the disease can be misdiagnosed, especially gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD) [5–7].

Achalasia is usually diagnosed via barium swallow and confirmed by manometry or endoscopic evaluation. The “bird’s beak” sign in barium images is a hallmark of disease [8]. The treatment is usually palliative and aims to decrease the pressure of the LES. Pneumatic dilatation (PD) is a frequent treatment method with a success rate of up to 70–90% [9]. Heller myotomy is a more definitive treatment approach, but has more complications. The peroral endoscopic myotomy (POEM) technique has recently become a less invasive alternative [10, 11].

Due to the limited number of studies on achalasia in the Middle East, especially in Iran, we conducted a retrospective study to evaluate the demographic and clinical characteristics and treatment approaches for children with achalasia.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective cross-sectional study at the Children’s Medical Center, a tertiary pediatric referral hospital in Tehran, Iran, covering the period from 2013 to 2022. Data were extracted from patients’ electronic medical records available in the hospital’s database system. In cases of incomplete data, families were contacted by phone to obtain missing information. We included 100 patients aged < 18 years with symptoms consistent with achalasia and a diagnosis confirmed by barium swallow and endoscopy. Patients with other diagnoses or incomplete data were excluded.

Definition of variables and data collection

The following variables were collected for all patients: age, gender, presenting signs and symptoms, diagnostic methods, duration of diagnostic delay, therapeutic interventions, comorbidities, and number of PD sessions.

Patients presenting with initial symptoms such as dysphagia, nausea and vomiting, chest pain, cough, or weight loss underwent a barium swallow study. Particular attention was given to findings such as esophageal dilatation, air-fluid levels due to retained food, tertiary esophageal spasms, and the characteristic “bird’s beak” sign. All barium images were reviewed by a board-certified radiologist, and diagnostic confirmation was made by a board-certified pediatric gastroenterologist. We specifically looked for a dilated or tortuous esophagus with retained saliva and/or food, along with a tight esophagogastric junction (EGJ), and longitudinal superficial wrinkles of the esophageal mucosa observed during endoscope passage.

Patients were treated at the same hospital with either pneumatic dilation or Heller’s myotomy. The choice of initial treatment modality was determined by the treating physician based on individualized clinical assessment. A definitive referral for surgical consultation was routinely made for patients who required more than three PD sessions. Post-treatment follow-up was conducted through scheduled outpatient clinic visits every three months to assess symptom status and any need for further intervention. Treatment success was defined as the absence of dysphagia (difficulty swallowing) and the ability to eat a normal diet without the sensation of food sticking.

Heller myotomy

Heller myotomy was performed via open or laparoscopic approaches. In the open method, a midline laparotomy allows mobilization of the hiatus and proximal stomach. The myotomy extended 4–5 cm proximally from 1 cm below the gastroesophageal junction to the stomach. The muscle layers were divided over 180°, followed by partial Toupet fundoplication to reduce reflux. The laparoscopic technique followed the same surgical steps as the open approach.

Pneumatic dilatation

Pneumatic dilatation was done by board-certified pediatric gastroenterologists under general anesthesia. The diameter of the balloon was selected based on the age of the patients, ranging from 15 to 40 mm. After the procedure, an endoscopic evaluation was done for possible complications such as perforations.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarized using frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were presented as means and standard deviations if normally distributed. Independent t tests were used to compare mean values between groups. Associations between categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-square test. A p value of < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Results

Demographics

One hundred patients entered the study, including 59 boys and 41 girls, with a male-to-female ratio of 1.43:1. The average age was 7.45 ± 4.58 years, ranging from 4 months to 16 years.

Signs and symptoms

Dysphagia was seen in 83 patients (83%), nausea/vomiting in 78 (78%), weight loss in 49 (49%), cough in 43 (43%), and chest pain in 28 patients (28%). Weight loss was significantly more common in females than males. (p = 0.004) (Table 1).

Diagnostic delay

The average delay between symptom onset and definitive diagnosis was 22.04 ± 32.75 months in all patients. No significant association was found between gender and diagnosis delay. (p = 0.928; boys: 22.32 ± 33.40 months, girls: 21.71 ± 32.69 months)

Diagnostic tools

Among the 100 patients, 72 underwent a barium swallow study. Data were missing for the remaining 28 patients. In all 72 patients, a dilated esophagus and delayed contrast passage from the esophagus to the stomach were observed. The classic “bird’s beak” sign was identified in 44 out of 72 patients (61%). All barium findings were confirmed by endoscopic evaluation.

Treatment

Heller myotomy (HM) was the primary and sole treatment for 5 patients, none of whom experienced recurrence. Among the 95 patients who received pneumatic dilation (PD) as first-line therapy, 30 (31.6%) responded to a single session, while 65 (68.4%) experienced recurrence requiring multiple sessions. Consequently, 25 patients from the recurrence group subsequently underwent HM as definitive treatment. Recurrence was significantly more frequent after PD (68.4%, 65/95) than after primary HM (0%, 0/5) (p = 0.004, Fisher’s exact test). Among children receiving PD, younger patients were significantly more likely to require multiple sessions (mean age 5.76 ± 3.75 years) compared to those responding to a single session (9.03 ± 4.66 years; p = 0.001, Table 3). The mean number of pneumatic dilatation sessions among all patients was 2.51 ± 2.66. No significant association was found between sex and the number of PD sessions (p = 0.69; boys: 2.59 ± 3.15, girls: 2.39 ± 1.78).

All pneumatic dilation procedures were performed on an outpatient basis. Patients were discharged after a standard observation period in the recovery unit, with no instances of hospitalization required for post-procedural care. Furthermore, no major complications, including esophageal perforation, significant hemorrhage, or mortality, were recorded.

Discussion

In this retrospective study, we examined the clinical features and treatment methods of achalasia patients in a middle-income country. The mean age was 7.45 ± 4.58 years; other studies reported an age range of 5.2–12 years [5, 12–15]. We found a slight male predominance, similar to larger multicenter reports, although smaller studies have shown variable sex distributions [12–16]. Due to nonspecific signs and symptoms, there is usually a delay between symptom onset and definite diagnosis. In our study, the mean diagnostic delay was 22.04 ± 32.75 months, whereas it was 12–15 months in similar studies [13–15]. Other low- and middle-income settings have also reported prolonged diagnostic delays. For example, a pediatric series from Morocco documented a mean delay of approximately 36 months [12]. The extended delay in our study was largely resulted from initial misdiagnoses, particularly GERD. The diagnosis was eventually made when symptoms worsened, and the patients were referred to tertiary centers several years after onset. Dysphagia and nausea/vomiting were the most common symptoms. The prevalence of dysphagia was 83%, similar to that reported in the international literature with large sample sizes [14–16]. In contrast, studies with smaller samples reported lower rates of 35% and 50% [12, 13]. The prevalence of nausea/vomiting was 78% in our study, other studies reported rates ranging from 5 to 100% [12–16].

Achalasia can be associated with a variety of comorbid conditions. In our study, 23% of patients had at least one comorbidity. This rate is comparable to that reported by Hussain et al. (27.5%), although the spectrum of associated conditions in their study differed and included asthma, cerebral palsy, and duodenal atresia. Jarzębicka et al. also investigated this aspect and reported a higher comorbidity rate of 61.7%, which may reflect more extensive diagnostic protocols at their center. Nevertheless, the spectrum of comorbidities in their study including triple A syndrome, celiac disease, seizure disorders, and Down syndrome, was similar to ours [14, 15].

Barium swallow was a key diagnostic tool, with the classic “bird’s beak” sign observed in 61% of cases. Upper endoscopy was performed to support the diagnosis and exclude malignancy. Although esophageal manometry is considered the gold standard for achalasia diagnosis, it is unavailable at our center, which may have contributed to diagnostic delays and a reliance on radiologic and endoscopic findings [8, 17].

Pneumatic dilation (PD) was the first-line therapy for the vast majority of patients (95%), underscoring its availability and feasibility in our setting. However, its long-term efficacy was limited, with 68.4% of patients requiring repeated PD sessions or definitive Heller myotomy (HM). A comparison showed a significantly lower recurrence rate following primary HM (0%) compared to PD (68.4%; p = 0.004). While this difference was statistically significant, these results should be interpreted with caution due to the small cohort size in the HM group and highlights the need for validation in larger, prospective studies. Our findings are consistent with those of similar studies, demonstrating that PD has a higher recurrence rate in the pediatric population than in adults [16, 18, 19]. Younger age was associated with a greater likelihood of needing repeated PD interventions, a relationship also noted in other series [20, 21]. Additionally, differences in outcome definitions across studies represent an important limitation. The absence of a standardized, validated symptom score (e.g., the Eckardt score) reduces the comparability of our results with other reports, and any comparisons of treatment ‘success’ and ‘recurrence’ should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Heller myotomy was performed in patients with persistent symptoms after multiple PD sessions, via both open and laparoscopic approaches. Definitive treatments such as POEM and HM have relatively high long-term success rates, but POEM is not yet available in our hospital. The choice between POEM and HM depends on patient age, local availability, and resources for long-term follow-up [22, 23].

In conclusion, pediatric achalasia remains a challenging condition to diagnose and manage, especially in resource-limited environments. Delayed diagnosis is common, and PD has a high rate of recurrence, particularly in younger patients. Improved access to manometry, earlier recognition of symptoms, and increased availability of definitive treatment options such as POEM could enhance patient outcomes. While international comparisons offer useful context, they should be interpreted cautiously due to substantial differences in healthcare infrastructure, resource availability, and local treatment practices between Iran and other reported cohorts. Future prospective multicenter studies are needed to better define optimal treatment strategies for this rare but impactful pediatric disorder.

Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its retrospective design. First, the absence of high-resolution esophageal manometry, the diagnostic gold standard, likely contributed to diagnostic delays and may have affected diagnostic certainty, reducing the comparability of our cohort with other international series. Second, the absence of a standardized follow-up protocol and validated outcome measures, such as a symptom score, may lead to inconsistency in defining treatment response. Third, the small number of patients who underwent primary Heller myotomy, despite the procedure showing a statistically significant superior outcome, greatly limits the statistical power of this comparison and precludes definitive conclusions about its superiority as a first-line therapy in our setting. Finally, the single-center nature of this study may affect the generalizability of our findings.

Conclusion

This study reports one of the largest single-center series of pediatric achalasia from the Middle East, describing demographics, symptoms, comorbidities, and treatment outcomes over 10 years. Diagnostic delays were common due to nonspecific symptoms and limited access to tools like manometry. Pneumatic dilatation was the main first-line therapy, though younger patients often required repeated interventions. These findings provide valuable region-specific data and may provide useful data to inform management strategies in similar resource-limited settings. To enable definitive comparative effectiveness research, we advocate for the establishment of a multicenter regional registry to standardize diagnostic and therapeutic practices.
