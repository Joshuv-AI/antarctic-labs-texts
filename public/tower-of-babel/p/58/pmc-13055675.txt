High Burden of Cardiovascular Disease and Mental Health Illness Comorbidity in Urban Haiti
Authors: Lily D Yan, Miranda Metz, Rodney Sufra, Reichling St Sauveur, Kelly Celestin, Erline Hilaire, Suzanne Edwidge Marcellin, Sarine Auguste, Anju Ogyu, Catherine Bennett, Serena Koenig, Lindsey Reif, Jessy Dévieux, Marie Marcelle Deschamps, Jean W Pape, Vanessa Rouzier, Margaret L McNairy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople living in low‐income settings facing humanitarian crises are at risk for comorbid cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mental health (MH) illness, yet there are limited population‐based data available.MethodsWe estimated CVD–MH illness comorbidity and associated risk factors using enrollment data from the Haiti Cardiovascular Disease Cohort Study (2019–2021). Adults aged >18 years were recruited using multistage random sampling across Port‐au‐Prince. Prevalent CVD (hypertension, heart failure, stroke, or myocardial infarction) was adjudicated by a physician panel integrating clinical examinations, imaging, and laboratory data. Prevalent MH illness was defined as depression symptoms (Patient Health Questionnaire 9 score >5) or stress (Perceived Stress Score >5). We conducted multivariable Poisson regression to analyze the association between risk factors (age, sex, low income, education, smoking, alcohol consumption) and the outcome of prevalent CVD–MH illness.ResultsAmong 2993 adults in this analysis, the median age was 40 years, 42% were women, and 70% earned an income <1 USD per day. CVD–MH illness comorbidity prevalence was 30% (95% CI, 28–32), with CVD prevalence 34% and MH 85%. In multivariable regression, older age (≥60 versus <40 years; adjusted prevalence ratio, 5.79 [95% CI, 4.57–7.38]), female sex (female versus male sex: adjusted prevalence ratio, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.06–1.42]), and lower education (secondary versus primary education: adjusted prevalence ratio, 1.46 [95% CI, 1.24–1.71]) were significantly associated with CVD–MH illness after adjusting for all other factors.Conclusions CVD–MH illness comorbidity is highly prevalent in urban Haiti, especially among older adults and women. These findings underscore the need for integrated, contextually adapted approaches to address multimorbidity in crisis‐affected low‐ and middle‐income countries.Keywords: cardiovascular disease, Haiti, humanitarian crisis, low‐ and middle‐income country, mental healthSubject Categories: Cardiovascular Disease, Epidemiology, Mental Health, Risk Factors

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) and mental health (MH) illness are 2 of the leading contributors to the global burden of disease, with CVD accounting for 19 million deaths globally and MH causing 98.8 million years lived with disability annually. 1 , 2 There is a growing body of evidence underscoring the complex and bidirectional relationship between CVD and MH. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 Multimorbid CVD–MH illness is associated with higher risk of cardiovascular death and worse mental illness than in either disease alone. 7 , 8 , 9

Poor MH can contribute to CVD through increased autonomic dysregulation, 10 , 11 traditional cardiovascular risk factors, 10 , 12 and medication nonadherence. 13 , 14 , 15 In addition, poor cardiovascular health can contribute to poor MH by reducing quality of life and adding stress from the demands of managing a chronic disease. 5 However, existing studies are almost exclusively from high‐income countries, with very few primary, epidemiological data from low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs).

LMICs bear the greatest CVD and MH illness burdens due to increased poverty‐related social determinants spanning early life adversity, food insecurity, and underresourced health infrastructure. 16 , 17 , 18 More than 80% of CVD deaths and 80% of disability‐adjusted life years from MH illness occur in LMICs. 19 Furthermore, LMICs are more likely to experience humanitarian crises from political unrest or natural disasters resulting in chronic exposure to violence, which magnify these risk factors. 20 , 21 , 22 In conflict‐affected settings, the prevalence of mental disorders is particularly high, with an estimated 22.1% of individuals experiencing depression, anxiety, posttraumatic stress disorder, or other severe psychiatric conditions. 23 Some of this MH burden may be alleviated by increased neighborhood and social cohesion. 24 Furthermore, the CVD burden in LMICs is unique and distinct from high‐income countries, with greater frequency of heart failure compared with ischemic disease, different risk factor profiles including higher levels of untreated hypertension, novel exposures including environmental toxins, and interactions between infectious diseases and CVDs. 25 , 26 , 27 However, population‐based data capturing the comorbidity of CVD–MH illness in these contexts are extremely limited, hampering the development of integrated care strategies tailored to high‐need populations.

Haiti represents a critical setting to explore these intersections and is representative of similar fragile LMIC settings. The country has endured decades of political instability, economic hardship, and recurrent natural disasters, which have eroded public health infrastructure and placed enormous economic, psychological, and physiological strain on its population. 28 , 29 The Haiti Cardiovascular Disease Cohort, a longitudinal cohort of adults in Port‐au‐Prince, Haiti, offers a unique opportunity to investigate the epidemiology of CVD–MH illness comorbidity in an urban, low‐resource, and crisis‐affected setting. This study seeks to fill a critical descriptive gap in the global literature by examining the prevalence and risk factors of CVD–MH illness among adults living in Haiti, including sex‐ and age‐stratified analyses to identify high‐risk subgroups.

Methods

Data Availability

Researchers who provide a methodologically sound proposal may have access to a subset of deidentified participant data, with specific variables based on the proposal. Proposals should be directed to the principal investigator at mcnairy@wustl.edu. To gain access, data requestors will need to sign a data access agreement. Data are available following publications through 3 years after publication and will be provided directly from the principal investigator.

Study Setting and Population

We analyzed cross‐sectional enrollment data from the Haiti Cardiovascular Disease Cohort Study (clinicaltrials.gov NCT03892265), a population‐based prospective study of 3005 adults aged ≥18 years. Participants were enrolled from March 2019 to August 2021 using multistage random sampling from metropolitan Port‐au‐Prince as previously described. 30 Inclusion criteria were adults aged ≥18 years, primary residence in Port‐au‐Prince, and absence of any serious medical condition or cognitive impairment preventing participation. 30 The study was conducted at the Groupe Haitien d’Etude de Sarcome de Kaposi et de Infections Opportunistes located in downtown Port‐au‐Prince. This analysis was restricted to cross‐sectional enrollment data from participants who completed clinical examinations and had complete data on depression and stress score (N=2993; Figure 1).

Flow diagram of participants in the Haiti Cardiovascular Disease Cohort included in the analysis.

Measurements

Sociodemographic enrollment data included age, sex, education, income, employment, and marital status. Age was reported in years and categorized into 3 groups: age <40, 40 to 60, and ≥60 years. Education was classified as primary or lower or secondary or higher. Income was categorized on the basis of self‐report as <1 USD per day, 1 to 10 USD per day, and >10 USD per day. Marital status was categorized as married/living together or single/widowed/divorced/separated.

Health behavior variables were assessed using the World Health Organization’s (WHO) STEPwise Approach to Noncommunicable Disease Risk Factor Surveillance questionnaire. 31 Smoking status was categorized as never/former versus current smoker. Alcohol intake was categorized as low (<1 drink per day) versus moderate or higher (≥1 drink per day). Physical activity was categorized as >150 minutes per week (moderate‐high) versus ≤150 minutes per week (low). Fruit and vegetable intake was categorized as <5 servings per day versus ≥5 servings per day. Food insecurity was assessed using the Food Insecurity Scale and categorized into no/low, moderate, or high (example question: “In the past year, did you or other adults in your household ever cut the size of your meals or skip meals because there wasn’t enough money for food?”). 32

Psychosocial measures included neighborhood cohesion and social support. Neighborhood cohesion was measured with the 5‐item Neighborhood Collective Efficacy Scale (score range, 5–25; Cronbach’s α=0.88) (example question: “People in my neighborhood want to help their neighbors”). Cohesion scores were categorized into tertiles on the basis of their distributions. 33 Social support was assessed using an adapted 11‐item Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support (score range: 11–77; Cronbach’s α=0.87) (example question: “I can talk about my problems with my family”) and categorized as low, moderate, or high. 34

Anthropometric data included height, weight, waist circumference, and blood pressure (BP). Body mass index categories followed WHO standards: underweight (<18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2), and obese (≥30.0 kg/m2). 31 BP was measured by study personnel in accordance with WHO and American Heart Association protocols. 35 , 36 The HEM 907 automatic device (OMRON, Kyoto, Japan) was used, with cuffs appropriately sized to cover at least 80% of the participant’s arm. Measurements were taken after participants had been seated for 5 minutes with feet flat on the floor and arms supported at heart level. Three BP readings were obtained from the left arm at 1‐minute intervals. For the purposes of this analysis, BP was calculated as the average of the second and third readings. 31 , 35 Hypertension was defined as a systolic BP ≥140 mm Hg, diastolic BP ≥90 mm Hg, or current use of antihypertensive medication as defined by the WHO and national Haitian guidelines. 37

Definitions of CVD–MH Illness Comorbidity

Prevalent CVD was defined as hypertension, heart failure, stroke, or myocardial infarction. Heart failure, stroke, and myocardial infarction were adjudicated by a panel of at least 3 physicians using standardized criteria from the American Heart Association, WHO, and other epidemiologic cohorts as previously described. 25 , 38 , 39 , 40 During adjudication, physicians reviewed a combination of reported history, symptoms, physical examination findings, ECG, echocardiogram, and relevant laboratory data. 25 This structured approach was adapted from established cohort studies such as REGARDS (Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke) and CARDIA (Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults), which classify events as definite or probable on the basis of a combination of self‐reported symptoms, clinical signs, imaging, and treatment history. 25 , 41 , 42

MH illness was defined as the presence of stress or depression symptoms based on self‐reported data. Stress was measured using the Perceived Stress Scale‐4 (PSS‐4), a brief 4‐item instrument with responses from 0 (never) to 4 (very often), yielding a total score from 0 to 16 (Cronbach’s α=0.37). Stress severity was categorized as low (<6), moderate (6–10), or high (≥11) using equal cut points similar to prior publications. 33 Depressive symptoms were assessed using the 9‐item Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ‐9), with responses from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day), for a total score of 0 to 27 (Cronbach’s α=0.78). Depression severity was categorized as none (≤5), mild (6–10), moderate (11–15), moderately severe (16–20), or severe (≥21).

To evaluate the prevalence of CVD–MH illness comorbidity, we defined the primary outcome as the presence of any cardiovascular condition (hypertension, heart failure, stroke, or myocardial infarction) and any mental health condition (PHQ‐9 >5 or PSS‐4 >5). We calculated overall, sex‐stratified, and age‐stratified prevalence of CVD–MH illness comorbidity.

Categorical variables were described as proportions, while continuous variables were summarized using medians and interquartile ranges or means±SDs (Table 1).

CVD–MH illness comorbidity prevalence was assessed overall and by sex and age category (<40, 40–60, and >60 years). Comparisons of prevalence between sex and age categories were conducted using Pearson’s χ2 test with Yates’s continuity correction for overall group differences, and when significant, a pairwise proportion test was conducted with Bonferroni adjustment for multiple comparisons. To explore patterns of comorbidity across MH and CVD conditions, we used an UpSet plot to visualize the intersections among stress, depression, hypertension, stroke, heart failure, and myocardial infarction. Traditional Venn diagrams use overlapping circles to show comorbidity across ≥2 sets of diseases, but become difficult to interpret with >3 sets and are often not scaled to reflect the size of intersections. To overcome these limitations, UpSet plots offer a matrix‐based layout to display the relative prevalence of overlapping conditions and allow comparisons across >3 sets in a clean, organized way. 43 There are 3 sections of an UpSet plot: a matrix display with dots and lines showing all possible combinations of conditions (eg, stress alone, stress and hypertension, stress and hypertension and stroke, etc), a horizontal bar chart on the left side tallying up the total number of participants with a given condition either alone or in combination (eg, any stress), and a vertical bar chart on top of the matrix showing the number of participants with each possible combination of conditions.

A multivariable Poisson regression was used to assess the association between sociodemographic and psychosocial factors selected a priori on the basis of prior literature (age, sex, education, income, marital status, social support, neighborhood cohesion, and food insecurity) and the outcome of comorbid CVD–MH illness. 25 , 44 , 45 , 46 Prevalence ratios and 95% CIs based on robust standard errors were reported.

For sample size, we restricted analysis to 2993 participants who had complete data on the outcomes (Figure 1). Of 2993 participants, 66 were missing at least 1 risk factor. In the main analysis, the Poisson regression was conducted on participants with complete data for both risk factors and outcomes. Several sensitivity analyses were performed. First, we accounted for missing data among these 66 participants by using multiple imputation (10 iterations) using all covariates, assuming data were missing at random (mitml R package; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Second, we refined our CVD–MH illness outcome to capture only severe conditions, by excluding hypertension from CVD, and including only perceived stress score >10 and PHQ‐9 >10 for MH illness. Third, to evaluate possible differential associations by sex, 2 separate models were conducted to test for an interaction between sex and age and sex and education with both the interaction term and the main effects included in the model. Fourth, we included a regression model where age is captured as a continuous variable rather than a categorical variable.

Ethics

Written informed consent was obtained, and study procedures were approved by institutional review boards at Groupe Haitien d’Etude de Sarcome de Kaposi et de Infections Opportunistes and Weill Cornell Medicine.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

Among the 2993 participants included in this analysis (Figure 1), the median age was 40 years, 48% were young adults aged <40 years, and 70% earned an income <1 USD per day. Almost everyone consumed <5 servings a day of fruits/vegetables, and there were low levels of smoking or alcohol intake (<4%). Half the cohort (49%) reported engaging in >150 minutes of physical activity per week. A majority (82%) of participants reported high food insecurity. In terms of CVD risk factors, hypertension (27%) and obesity (17%) were the most common.

Compared with women, men had higher levels of secondary education (74% versus 57%), lower levels of obesity (4% versus 26%), higher levels of cohesion (tertile 3: 31% versus 22%), and higher levels of support (high: 70% versus 62%).

CVD–MH Illness Comorbidity

Among all 2993 participants, 30% (n=904) (95% CI, 28–32) had comorbid CVD–MH illness (Table 2), with 904/1009 (90% [95% CI, 88–92]) of participants with any CVD having MH illness. There was a higher prevalence of CVD–MH illness in women (34%) compared with men (25%) (χ2=26.8, P<0.001), including hypertension (32% versus 27%) and heart failure (14% versus 9%). Women also had higher levels of any MH illness (90% versus 78%, P<0.001). Specifically, 17% of women and 6% of men had at least moderate depression (PHQ‐9 >10), while 85% of women and 75% of men had at least moderate perceived stress (PSS >5). More than one‐third (35%) of the population had both depressive symptoms and stress, with substantially higher rates in women (43%) compared with men (24%).

CVD–MH illness comorbidity increased markedly with age: Only 9% of participants aged <40 years were affected, compared with 42% of those aged 40 to 60 years and 69% of participants aged >60 years (χ2=708.2, P<0.001; Figure 2). While CVD prevalence increased dramatically with age categories (10%, 48%, and 74%), MH prevalence was uniformly high (82%, 86%, and 92%). Participants aged >60 years had the highest levels of depressive symptoms (44%) and moderate‐high stress (90%).

In the youngest age group (<40 years), 11% of women had CVD–MH illness comorbidity compared with only 6% of men (Figure 2). Among those aged 40 to 60 years, 47% of women were affected compared with 35% of men. In the oldest age group (>60 years), comorbidity reached 71% among women and 66% among men.

Smaller subsets of participants had combinations of stroke, heart failure, and myocardial infarction, often co‐occurring with both stress and depression.

Factors Associated With CVD–MH Illness in Multivariable Regression

In multivariable Poisson regressions adjusted for covariates outlined previously, older age was strongly associated with higher prevalence of CVD–MH illness comorbidity (Table 3). Compared with individuals aged <40 years, those aged 40 to 60 years had a 4‐fold higher prevalence (adjusted prevalence ratio [PR], 4.02 [95% CI, 3.25–5.01]), and those aged ≥60 years had nearly 6 times the prevalence (PR, 5.79 [95% CI, 4.57–7.38]). Female sex was also associated with increased prevalence (PR, 1.22 [95% CI, 1.06–1.42]), as was living on <1 USD per day (PR, 1.16 [95% CI, 1.00–1.36]). Having a primary education or lower was associated with a higher prevalence of CVD–MH illness comorbidity as well (PR, 1.46 [95% CI, 1.24–1.71]). Marital status, social support, neighborhood cohesion, and food insecurity were not significantly associated with CVD–MH illness comorbidity. When missing data were imputed in a multivariable Poisson regression, similar factors associated with CVD–MH illness were statistically significant (Table S1).

In sensitivity analyses, when the outcome CVD–MH illness was restricted to more severe conditions (myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, high stress, moderate to severe depression; Table S2), age, sex, and income remain significantly associated with severe CVD–MH illness, while education does not. The magnitude of association for sex (PR, 2.45 [95% CI, 1.57–3.82]) and income (PR, 2.65 [95% CI, 1.58–4.43]) is higher than the main analysis. In a model incorporating interaction terms between sex and age (Table S3), sex modifies the relationship between age and CVD–MH illness prevalence. There is a higher prevalence of CVD–MH illness with older ages, but the ratio of CVD–MH illness prevalence in those aged ≥60 years (high) versus <40 years (low) was smaller for women than for men (0.57 [95% CI, 0.39–0.85; Table S2). Taken together with the fact that women have higher CVD–MH illness prevalence than men, this suggests women have a more uniform distribution of high CVD–MH illness prevalence across age categories compared with men.

In a model incorporating an interaction term between sex and education (Table S4), there was a higher prevalence of CVD–MH illness with lower education, but this higher prevalence was again smaller for women than for men (0.76 [95% CI, 0.61–0.95]). Finally, when age was included as a continuous variable instead of categorical, similar associations were seen as the main analysis (Table S5).

Discussion

Our study provides one of the first population‐based estimates of CVD–MH illness comorbidity in an extremely low‐income setting experiencing a humanitarian crisis. We found a strikingly high prevalence of CVD–MH illness comorbidity among Haitian adults, affecting nearly 1 in 3 participants overall and more than two thirds of those age >60 years. We also found a higher prevalence of CVD–MH illness among female participants and those with lower educational attainment.

Our finding that nearly 90% of participants with CVD have either depressive or stress symptoms exceeds levels from high‐income settings. 4 , 5 , 47 , 48 It also exceeds estimates from other LMICs and conflict settings, with 1 meta‐analysis from the WHO estimating the average age‐standardized prevalence of mild depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic stress disorder in these settings as 13%. 23 In another study examining 45 LMICs, the mean perceived stress score was 4.8, and the prevalence of depression was 6.2%. 49 This likely reflects the fact that there are high levels of depressive and stress symptoms among the overall cohort participants (85%), given the ongoing political instability and gang warfare in Port‐au‐Prince. 28 , 29 Depression and anxiety have been shown to increase the risk of incident CVD through behavioral and physiological pathways, including poor medication adherence, unhealthy lifestyle behaviors, systemic inflammation, and dysregulation of the autonomic nervous system. 5 , 50 Conversely, living with chronic heart disease can contribute to the development or worsening of MH symptoms due to functional impairment, chronic stress, and reduced quality of life. 5 In resource‐constrained settings like Haiti, these interconnections may be exacerbated by inadequate access to both MH and cardiovascular care.

We also found that women and older adults have higher levels of stress and depression, which can be attributed to a confluence of biological, psychosocial, and structural factors. Sex modified the relationship between age and education with CVD–MH illness prevalence. Globally, women are nearly twice as likely as men to experience depression, a disparity that emerges in adolescence and persists throughout life. 51 This increased vulnerability to MH disorders is linked to biological sex differences among women as well as heightened exposure to stressors such as poverty, caregiving responsibilities, and sex‐based violence. 52 , 53 Furthermore, women with CVD are more likely to experience comorbid depression and anxiety, which can worsen heart disease outcomes. 54 Similarly, older adults are uniquely at risk for MH illness given loss of their support networks as they age, isolation, and increased frailty. 55 In regressions with interaction terms between sex and age and education, we found that the ratio of CVD–MH illness prevalence within age and education for women were both smaller than the ratio of CVD–MH illness prevalence for men. Taken together with the fact that women have higher CVD–MH illness prevalence than men, this suggests that women have a more uniform distribution of high CVD–MH illness prevalence across age and education categories than men. In Haiti, chronic stressors of gang violence, political instability, and food insecurity may be experienced unequally across sexes and ages. 56 For the other covariates, we did not find any association between social support, neighborhood cohesion, or food insecurity with the outcome of CVD–MH illness prevalence, which is contrary to our hypothesis. This may be due to a few factors. First, the data for food insecurity and social support are unbalanced; most participants reported moderate to high support and food insecurity. Given the relative lack of variation in these 2 variables, we may not have had enough power to detect an association with the outcome. Second, it may be due to limitations of cross‐sectional data, as the risk factors may not have occurred temporally before the outcomes.

In settings like Haiti with underresourced health systems including limited specialists (psychiatrists, psychologists) and limited medications (antidepressants, antianxiolytics), promising interventions to address the MH burden among individuals with comorbid CVD–MH illness in low‐resource settings include both psychosocial therapies and transdiagnostic programmatic approaches. Psychosocial interventions, such as cognitive behavioral therapy, mindfulness‐based stress reduction, community‐based group support, and medications if indicated, have demonstrated efficacy in reducing symptoms of posttraumatic stress disorder, depression, and anxiety in settings of humanitarian crisis. 57 , 58 , 59 However, their application in resource‐constrained settings has remained limited in scope, and further research is needed to develop and evaluate culturally tailored approaches that are feasible. One example of a transdiagnostic program is the WHO’s Mental Health Gap Action Program, which provides a practical, evidence‐based toolkit for the identification and management of common mental disorders by nonspecialist providers. A systematic review of Mental Health Gap Action Program implementation in LMICs found that training improved provider knowledge, attitudes, and confidence, and was associated with better patient engagement and symptom outcomes. 60 Expanding these efforts through programmatic tools like the WHO’s Program Management Plus, which leverages nonspecialist providers to deliver structured psychosocial support in community settings, holds potential for bridging the treatment gap while aligning with existing health infrastructure. 61

Strengths of this analysis include the population‐based Haiti Cardiovascular Disease Cohort Study, which is situated in a highly underresearched setting experiencing an ongoing humanitarian crisis. CVD was rigorously defined using standardized adjudication criteria, comparable to US and global cohorts. Limitations include that MH outcomes were based on self‐reported symptoms of depression and stress, measured using the PHQ‐9 and PSS‐4 rather than clinical diagnosis by an MH professional. While these are widely used and validated tools in global health research, including in low‐resource settings, they may be subject to reporting bias and do not capture the full complexity of mental disorders, with underestimates of prevalence. Still, our methodology is consistent with other large‐scale studies that rely on symptom‐based screening instruments to estimate the population‐level MH burden. Our results also may not be generalizable to rural parts of Haiti. The low Cronbach’s α for PSS‐4 suggests low internal consistency of this questionnaire in Haiti, which may have introduced measurement error into stress estimates. Additionally, as this is a cross‐sectional analysis of baseline data, causal relationships cannot be inferred between sociodemographic risk factors and comorbidity. Future longitudinal analyses will be needed to assess the directionality and persistence of these associations over time.

We found that one third of adult participants from a population‐based cohort in an LMIC had comorbid CVD–MH illness, and almost all participants with CVD had depressive or stress symptoms. Those with the highest prevalence of CVD–MH illness included older age, female sex, low income, and limited education. These findings call for urgent investment in targeted, culturally relevant MH services in low‐resource and crisis‐affected settings.
