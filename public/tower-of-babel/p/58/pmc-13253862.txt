Species-specific dung characteristics and selective grazing jointly affect plant diversity in an experimental grassland community
Authors: Xingzhao Sun, Judith Sitters, Leonardo H. Teixeira, Martin J. Wassen, Harry Olde Venterink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Mammalian herbivores play a pivotal role in nutrient cycling and plant community composition. Their excreta and grazing activity influence plant diversity, but the relative or interactive effects of these influences are unclear. We manipulated dung quantity, quality (three herbivores with varying dung-N:P), and grazing type (bulk, selective, no) in a 3-year experiment. Neither dung quantity nor bulk grazing influenced plant diversity, despite strongly impacting plant productivity and light. Dung’s impact on plant diversity varied among herbivore species but could not be ascribed to nutrients. Effects of selective grazing on plant diversity interacted with dung type (herbivore species), depending on whether it promoted dominance of the consumed plants. Our results highlight that the interactive effects between herbivore species-specific dung characteristics and grazing activities have important indirect effects on plant diversity in terrestrial ecosystems.

Introduction

Mammalian herbivores (hereafter called herbivores) are crucial in shaping terrestrial ecosystems. They significantly influence plant productivity, species composition, and diversity1–3. This influence is multifaceted and operates through the consumption of plants4,5, deposition and spatial distribution of excreta and nutrients in it6–8, trampling activities9, suppression of fires10, and/or distribution of seeds11. Hence, herbivores affect primary production, soil carbon and nutrient storage, and a multitude of other ecosystem functions12–14. Understanding how plant communities respond to different impacts of herbivores is key to developing strategies for sustainable grazing management and ecosystem restoration. This is particularly relevant because of the ongoing losses of wild herbivores from terrestrial ecosystems15. However, the different mechanisms by which herbivores affect plant communities are difficult to disentangle in the field, and therefore their relative importance or how they may interact is largely unknown. Here, we concentrate on three of these main mechanisms: dung quantity, dung quality (dung of different herbivore species varying in C, N and P concentrations and ratios, Fig.1, Supplementary Table 1), and different grazing selectivity (i.e., no, selective, or bulk grazing), as well as the effects of their interactions on the productivity and diversity of an experimental plant community in mesocosms.Fig. 1Conceptual framework and experimental design.Conceptual illustration of the hypothesized influences of herbivore dung quantity, quality, and grazing type on plant community productivity-diversity relationships (a). Dung deposition enriches the soil with nutrients, which are directly available for plant growth or become available after decomposition12,16,17. Plant communities generally respond to increasing herbivore dung quantity with enhanced productivity and aboveground biomass6,18,19 (Fig. 1a; arrows 1 and 2). This response occurs when nutrients are growth-limiting, which is a common condition in natural and semi-natural terrestrial systems20,21, and persists provided that the biomass is not removed by herbivores or fire10,22,23. Increased aboveground biomass can lead to reduced light penetration to the soil, which can in turn reduce species diversity because of competitive exclusion23,24 (Fig. 1a; arrows 3 and 4).

In turn, the quality of dung varies largely among herbivore species. For instance, concentrations of carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P), and ratios among them, vary considerably among herbivore species’ dung due to differences in diet quality, digestive systems, and body size25–29. These differences in dung C:N:P stoichiometry can be large enough to influence plant species competition, community productivity, and diversity. For example, dung with a low N:P ratio promoted N2-fixing legumes in species mixtures and communities25,30. The quality of herbivore dung also varies among species in terms of microbial community composition31, and this variation can also influence plant growth and competitive interactions among plant species32,33. However, the relative importance of the variation in herbivore dung quality on plant community productivity and diversity has been poorly investigated thus far (Fig. 1a; arrows 8 and 9).

Herbivore consumption of plants or plant parts profoundly affects the composition of plant communities5,34,35. The impact of grazing activities on plant communities depends on their form and intensity36,37. Intensive bulk grazing, involving indiscriminate consumption across plant species as exhibited by herbivores such as cattle (Bos taurus) and white-tailed deer (Odocoileus virginianus)38,39, leads to a uniform reduction in aboveground plant biomass (Fig. 1a; arrow 5)40–43. The removal of aboveground biomass enhances light penetration to the soil (Fig. 1a; arrow 6), thereby reducing competitive exclusion for light and increasing plant diversity44. Selective grazers target specific plants of relatively high nutritional content, such as leguminous species35,45,46. Whether this selective grazing promotes or reduces plant species diversity depends on the abundance of the selectively consumed plants, i.e., if dominant species are consumed, it will likely increase plant diversity, but if non-dominant species are consumed, it may decrease it5,47,48 (Fig. 1a; arrow 7). To our knowledge, the interaction between the effects of selective or bulk grazing on plant community composition and the effects of dung deposition has not yet been experimentally investigated.

Here, we present the results of the first three years of a long-term mesocosm experiment in which the effects of dung quantity, quality, different types of grazing, and their interactions on productivity and diversity of an experimental grassland community are investigated (Fig. 1b). The experiment was set up with dung of three European herbivore species: rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus), horse (Equus ferus caballus), and European bison (Bison bonasus). The dung of these herbivores differs in N:P ratio with horse < bison < rabbit, and dung of these species was applied along a gradient of dung quantity (5 levels; Fig. 1b). The dung was added to quartz sand inoculated with fresh soil from the National Park Zuid-Kennemerland in The Netherlands, which is the same coastal dune nature reserve where the dung was collected. In all mesocosms, 16 plants of eight grassland species were initially planted. The species represented four functional groups: grasses, leguminous forbs, non-leguminous forbs, and non-mycorrhizal plants. Three distinct treatments of grazing types were employed: no biomass removal by clipping (no grazing), clipping all plants at 5 cm above the soil surface at the peak of the growing season (bulk grazing), or clipping only the two leguminous forbs (selective grazing).

Our hypotheses followed the predicted patterns of Fig. 1a. Firstly, we expected that increasing dung quantity would promote plant productivity and abundance and, subsequently, reduce light availability and plant diversity24 (hypothesis 1 represented by the arrows 1, 2, 3 and 4). Noteworthy, in our experimental plant communities with a fixed number of plant species, the variation in species diversity will be determined by variation in the evenness of the communities. Secondly, we expected that differences in dung quality through variation among herbivore species would affect plant diversity and we predicted that dung with a low N:P ratio (horse and European bison) would promote productivity and dominance of leguminous species thereby reducing community diversity, whereas dung with a higher N:P ratio (rabbit) would do this to a far lesser extent25,30 (hypothesis 2 represented by arrows 8 and 9). Thirdly, we hypothesized that bulk grazing would increase light availability and plant diversity compared to no grazing44 (hypothesis 3 represented by arrows 6 and 4). Finally, in hypothesis four, we expected that selective grazing on leguminous species would increase community diversity when legumes were dominant (mesocosms with horse and European bison dung—see hypothesis 2) but would decrease diversity when they were not dominant35 (mesocosms with rabbit dung). The experiment started with dung fertilization treatments in early spring 2020, while the grazing treatments were first applied at the peak of the first growing season during the summer of 2020. Hence, the effect of the grazing treatments and their interactions with the dung treatments could only be tested for the second and third years.

Results and discussion

Dung type (herbivore species) is more decisive for plant diversity than dung quantity

The total abundance of plants in the mesocosms (total cover of the community) increased strongly with increasing dung quantity (Fig. 2a). This effect was strongest in the first year (generalized eta-squared, η²G = 0.83 for dung quantity; Table 1) but remained substantial in subsequent years (η²G = 0.74 in both Year 2 and 3; Supplementary Table 3). Subsequently, the increased abundance due to higher quantities of dung resulted in lower light penetration to the soil surface (Fig. 2b, Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3), so far supporting our first hypothesis and previously observed fertilization-biomass-light relationships23,24,44,49. However, the predicted significant reduction in plant community evenness due to lower light was not observed (Fig. 2c, Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3). The proportion of light that reached the soil was generally not below 60% in our experiment, and although Grace et al23. demonstrated that competitive exclusion for light is not restricted to communities with high biomass levels, and light reduction decreases non-linearly with increasing biomass49 and hence, the strongest negative effects on evenness can be expected at higher levels of light reduction (<40–50% penetration to the soil as e.g. shown in herbaceous fen vegetations in the Netherlands) than observed in our experiment50. Moreover, in mesocosms, light can penetrate from the sides, reducing the asymmetric competition effect typically observed under field conditions, where light as a resource is normally unidirectional from above51. Indeed, in some highly productive plots, some low competitive plant species particularly survived at the edges of the mesocosm where light could enter from aside (personal observations). Although there was no significant effect of dung quantity on community evenness, there were significant effects on the relative abundances of individual species (Supplementary Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 4). The relative abundance of two non-leguminous forbs (P. lanceolata and C. jacea) decreased, while it increased for one grass species (H. lanatus) and one leguminous forb species (T. pratense) in at least one year (Supplementary Fig. 1). An increased dominance of some grass species and a decrease in forb species in relation to enhanced nutrient availability is consistent with previous studies52,53. Dung type had a significant effect on plant community evenness, explaining 9–14% of the variance (η²G; Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3). This effect was consistently stronger than that of dung quantity (cf. η²G values in Tables), underscoring its relative importance in this regard. We hypothesized that dung with a lower N:P ratio (horse and European bison with N:P = 5 to 6) would promote growth and dominance of the two N2-fixing legume species more than dung with a higher N:P ratio (rabbit with N:P = 13). This was indeed what we observed for the relative abundances of both T. pratense and L. corniculatus (Fig. 3a), supporting similar findings of two of our previous studies25,30. However, although the legumes were important components of the plant community, particularly in mesocosms that received European bison or horse dung, they were not as dominant as the two grass species (A. capillaris and H. lanatus) (Fig. 3b), and by far not as dominant as in our previous greenhouse study where particularly T. pratense frequently constituted >50% of the plant community25. This difference might be due to the temperature dependency of symbiotic N2-fixation54, which benefits legumes more in heated greenhouse conditions than – as in the present study – in outdoor mesocosms in the temperate zone. One possible explanation for not detecting any reduction in species evenness in response to the fertilization with European bison and horse dung compared to mesocosms fertilized with rabbit dung could be the relatively low abundance of legumes observed under horse and bison dung. Instead, we observed a significantly higher diversity (evenness) in the mesocosms fertilized with horse dung (Fig. 4c, Table 1 and Supplementary Table 2). In these mesocosms also the total plant abundance was lower (Fig. 4a), but the reduction in light was not significantly different compared to mesocosms fertilized with the other two dung types (Fig. 4b). We attribute the differences among dung types to factors other than N:P stoichiometry or nutrient concentrations, as similar N:P ratios in European bison and horse dung had different effects on community diversity, and variations in dung quantity did not affect diversity (cf. evenness in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 3). These other factors could, for instance, be related to dung microbial composition55, which varies among these three herbivore species31, or to dung microbial activity, which influences plant growth, symbiotic associations, and species competition32,33. Bulk grazing strongly reduced the abundance of plants, albeit only in the third year (Fig. 4d and Supplementary Fig. 4a, Table 1), and increased light penetration to the soil (Fig. 4e and Table 1) as we hypothesized. However, the subsequent predicted positive effect of the increased light availability on community diversity (evenness) was not found (Fig. 4f and Table 1). This latter aspect of our results diverges from those of herbivore exclusion experiments under field conditions, where an increase in light due to herbivore consumption was observed to affect plant species diversity44,49. However, since we didn’t observe a decrease in evenness due to light reduction in our experiment (see Supplementary Fig. 5), recovery of evenness could not be expected when the light-reduction effect was removed by means of bulk grazing. Furthermore, the timing of the grazing activity can influence its impact on the plant community36,37. We clipped at the peak of the growing season and only measured its impact at the peak of the next growing season. Hence, we did not observe any diversity (evenness) effects of the bulk grazing on next year’s regrowth, but we did observe a reduced dominance of one of the grass species (H. lanatus) (Fig. 3b). This shift in dominance might initiate changes that could affect plant community diversity in the long term. Of note, if we had clipped earlier in the growing season (e.g., during spring before the biomass peak in summer) the differences between removed vs. unremoved aboveground biomass of the entire vegetation, or of only the legumes, would likely have exerted a stronger and more immediate effect on species dominance during (re)growth. This would in turn have affected community diversity by the peak of that same season. The effects of our clipping treatment at the peak of the growing season were only noted after the winter break and subsequent regrowth of the complete vegetation.

Impact of selective grazing on plant diversity interacts with type of herbivore dung

Selective grazing significantly affected plant community diversity (evenness), depending on the type of herbivore dung applied (Table 1 and Fig. 5). Removing legumes reduced diversity in mesocosms with rabbit dung, but increased it with European bison or horse dung (Fig. 5, comparing evenness in selective grazing with no grazing per dung type). Moreover, when comparing only the selectively grazed mesocosms, the diversity was significantly lower in mesocosms fertilized with rabbit dung than in those fertilized with the other two dung types (Fig. 5). Hence, our results support our fourth hypothesis that the effect of selective grazing on the diversity of plant communities depends on whether dominant or non-dominant species are consumed35. Moreover, we also observed a significant three-order interaction between dung type, quantity, and grazing treatments (Table 1), illustrating that the positive effect of selective grazing on diversity in the mesocosms fertilized with European bison dung occurred at low dung quantity, whereas the negative effect of selective grazing on the diversity in the mesocosms fertilized with rabbit dung occurred at high dung quantity (Supplementary Fig. 4c). The effects of selective grazing on plant community diversity are complex, influenced by whether dominant or non-dominant plant species are consumed and by dung quality and quantity. Notably, selectively clipping the two legume species, i.e., T. pratense and L. corniculatus that were never amongst the most dominant species in our experimental communities, did not significantly alter their relative abundances compared to having no grazing or bulk grazing in the next growing season (Fig. 3b), indicating their tolerance to grazing56,57.Fig. 5Interactive effects of dung types and grazing treatments on plant community evenness at the peak of the growing season.Evenness was calculated using species abundances in years 2 and 3. Our results highlight that the impact of herbivores on plant community diversity is controlled by a combination of the effects of dung quantity, dung quality, and the type of grazing - bulk or selective. Moreover, the effect of selective grazing interacts with the quality and quantity of herbivore dung, as these factors influence which plant species become dominant. These observations, particularly the complex interactions among various herbivore effects, collectively demonstrate that a multifactorial approach is needed to predict herbivore impacts on plant diversity, complementing the trait-based perspective offered by a recent meta-analysis58. Overall, in our experiment, the type of herbivore dung – hence, the herbivore species – played a crucial role in determining plant abundance and particularly in shaping the diversity of the plant community. Moreover, the variation in effects among herbivore species could only partly be attributed to nutrient-related factors, suggesting that apart from nutrient content in the dung, other ecological processes, such as microbiological composition or activity, are driven by herbivore type. This highlights the critical importance of maintaining or restoring diverse herbivore populations for the composition and functioning of plant communities in terrestrial ecosystems, as different herbivore species differentially influence nutrient cycling and plant dominance patterns25,26,30. Hence, nature management organisations should take this aspect of multiple herbivore species into account for maintaining herbivore diversity, but also when mainly aiming at biomass removal by means of herbivores to fight eutrophication or succession to woodland. Moreover, this recommendation may promote sustainable management of livestock systems, since the promotion of grazer diversity could benefit plant diversity and ecosystem resilience under grazing regimes. While the influence of a single herbivore may simplify community dynamics and nutrient cycling, the presence of multiple herbivore species with different grazing preferences and dung characteristics could foster soil quality and species coexistence11,59. Although herbivore introductions often have negative effects on plant communities60, our study shows that such effects can result from multiple interacting factors, thus making it difficult to predict the outcomes of such interactions.

Methods

Dung and soil collection

Dung of European bison, horse, and rabbit were collected at the National Park Zuid-Kennemerland, The Netherlands (52.3961°N, 4.5921°E) on 25 February 2020. The National Park Zuid-Kennemerland is a coastal dune ecosystem on nutrient-poor sandy soils61. We collected fresh dung either directly after witnessing herbivores excreting (horse and European bison) or from the soil in an area with a relatively high density of rabbits (see ref. 31). Hence, the rabbit dung was not as fresh as the dung of the two larger herbivores, but that could not be avoided due to logistical reasons with respect to collecting sufficient amounts of dung required for the experiment. Dung was collected from multiple individuals (at least 5) per species and packed individually. Samples were kept in a cooled box during transportation to the Vrije Universiteit Brussel (VUB) and then stored at 4 °C until further processing. The moisture content (%) and concentrations of total carbon (C), nitrogen (N), and phosphorus (P) in the dung of the three herbivore species were determined for individual samples (see Supplementary Table 1). To assess moisture, a subsample of herbivore dung was subjected to 70 °C oven drying for 48 h. Subsequently, the dried dung was ground using a Retsch Mixer Mill MM300 (Westburg) for nutrient analysis. Total C concentrations were determined using a dry combustion analyzer (LECO CNS-2000). Total N concentrations were analyzed using an elemental analyzer (Thermo EA Flash 1112). Total P concentrations were assessed through a modified combustion and hot HCl extraction procedure62,63.

Immediately after collecting the dung, soil from the top 10 cm layer was also gathered from the same coastal dune area. This sandy soil was sieved in the field, mixed thoroughly, stored in a sealed bucket, and transported to the VUB. Fresh subsamples of this soil (1 L per mesocosm) were added to all experimental mesocosms to inoculate them with soil microbes, including decomposing bacteria and fungi, symbiotic N2-fixing bacteria, and mycorrhizae30.

Mesocosm experiment

In April 2020, experimental plant communities were set up in 90 mesocosms (30 L, 36 cm inside diameter at the top) in the experimental garden of the VUB Etterbeek Campus in Brussels (Fig. 1b). The mesocosms were filled with a mixture of quartz sand (Type M31, Sibelco, Dessel), and 1 L of the fresh soil collected in the National Park Zuid Kennemerland, The Netherlands, in order to inoculate the sand with soil microbes. The quartz sand’s total N and P concentrations were below detection limits. Each mesocosm received a varying quantity of herbivore dung collected in the same National Park as described above, and corresponding to either 12.5, 25, 50, 100 or 200 mg P per mesocosm, either in dung from rabbit, horse, or European bison (see Supplementary Table 2 for supplied dung quantities per dung type). Thereto, dung samples of the various individuals per species were pooled and mixed. The applied dung quantity gradient was based on previous dung experiments25,30,64, and was designed to capture a wide range of potential nutrient inputs to test general ecological principles, rather than to precisely mimic the deposition rates of specific herbivore species in the field. Dung of these three herbivore species was selected for the experiment because they vary in body mass (rabbit 1.5–2.5 kg, horse 400–600 kg, male European bison 800–1000 kg)65, digestive system (rabbit and horse - non ruminant, European bison - ruminant), grazing habit and diet (rabbit—selective mixed feeder, horse - bulk grazer, European bison - selective mixed feeder)25,61; important factors influencing the N:P ratio of the dung27, which indeed varied (particularly between rabbit and the other two herbivores; Fig. 1b). Subsequently, the dung was mixed with sand and soil per mesocosm.

In all mesocosms, 16 plants of eight grassland species (2 plants per species) were planted. The species represented four functional groups: grasses (Holcus lanatus and Agrostis capillaris), leguminous forbs (Trifolium pratense and Lotus corniculatus), non-leguminous forbs (Achillea millefolium and Centaurea jacea), and non-mycorrhizal plants (Carex flava and Rumex acetosella). Having functional traits such as being able to fix atmospheric N2 or having arbuscular mycorrhiza or not, likely affects the interspecific competition under the various dung and grazing treatments, and is also related to the attractiveness for selective herbivores66. All eight species are common in European grasslands and occur in the National Park Zuid Kennemerland, albeit that they aren’t specific to coastal dune ecosystems. Four to six weeks before planting, seeds of the eight plant species were germinated in trays with potting soil in the greenhouse of VUB. Seeds were obtained from a local provider (EcoFlora).

Furthermore, each mesocosm was randomly assigned to one of the three types of grazing treatments to simulate plant consumption: (1) no grazing = no clipping, (2) bulk grazing = clipping of all plant species to 5 cm above the soil, or (3) selective grazing = clipping of the two leguminous species—T. pratense and L. corniculatus—to 5 cm above the soil. The legumes were chosen for the selective grazing treatment because they are often selectively consumed by herbivores, possibly because the leaves are relatively N-rich66. Grazing treatments were performed annually at the peak of the growing season and started in summer (August/September) 2020.

Noteworthy, while the clipping treatments effectively simulate the primary effect of herbivory, which is the removal of plant aboveground biomass via consumption, this simplified method (i.e., clipping) does not mimic other potential impacts associated with the influence of mammalian herbivores on plant communities. Processes such as the deposition of herbivore saliva containing substances that may reflect on plant growth and other secondary responses67–69, but also vegetation trampling, soil compaction, urine excretion and impacts on other soil properties from animal movements and spatial distribution of dung, nutrients and plant propagules in the landscape70 could not be captured by our experimental design. Consequently, our results reflect primarily the effects of aboveground biomass removal combined with dung-related nutrient recycling via microbial activities, and should be interpreted within this context.

All treatments were replicated twice, resulting in 90 mesocosms (3 dung types × 5 dung quantity levels × 3 grazing treatments × 2 replicates). The treatments ‘dung type’, ‘dung quantity’, and the grazing treatments were applied in a full factorial design. On the one hand, this enabled statistical evaluation of main factorial and interactive effects, but on the other hand, it also recognizes that herbivore excretion and plant consumption do not necessarily spatially overlap. Hence, separate factorial and interactive effects of these treatments all take place in grazing ecosystems. The mesocosms were randomly arranged within an outdoor fenced garden on the VUB campus and received regular watering with deionized water in case of insufficient rainfall. The soil between the mesocosms was covered with black plastic foil to restrict plant growth between them. Plants from other species than those planted that appeared in the mesocosms were removed regularly.

For three consecutive years, measurements were conducted after 150 days (during peak plant productivity in August/September 2020), 515 days (August/September 2021), and 880 days (August/September 2022) from the initiation of the experiment. Measurements mainly included the abundance of each plant species and the light penetration to the soil surface per mesocosm. The abundance of the plant species per mesocosm was determined using a modified point intercept method71. A standardized grid of 24 pins was positioned identically across all mesocosms to record contacts with individual species vertically. This non-destructive method provided a reliable estimate of aboveground biomass and abundance without interfering with the subsequent grazing treatments. At approximately solar noon (between 11:00 and 14:00), photosynthetically active radiation (PAR, mmol photons per m2 per s) was measured72,73 using a light meter (Accupar LP80, Decagon, Pullman, USA) in the center of the mesocosms, with each mesocosm having both ground-level and above-canopy PAR measurements. The light penetration to the soil surface was then calculated as the percentage of PAR below compared to above the canopy. Following these measurements, grazing treatments simulated by clipping were conducted annually.

Data analysis

Dung quantity (as mg P) was log-transformed to address assumptions of normality and homogeneity. Per mesocosm, the counts of pin-hits of the point-intercept method were summed per species and transformed by log(x + 1) to represent the absolute abundance and cover per plant species in each mesocosm. Total plant abundance was calculated by summing the abundances of the 8 species, and the relative abundances per species were calculated as percentages of the total abundances. Because the first grazing treatments were carried out at the first year’s peak of the growing season, grazing treatments only took effect in the second and third years. We used a Mixed-design ANOVA using Type III sum of squares to test the effects of dung types, dung quantity, grazing treatments (between-subjects factors), and time (within-subjects factor) on total abundance, light penetration to the soil, and Pielou’s evenness index.
