Reducing the Duration to Reach Full Enteral and Oral Feeding Volumes for Very Preterm and Extremely Preterm Infants
Authors: Yu Ying Mo, Jing Jing Chen, Wan Xiang He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background: Enteral and oral feeding are crucial for infants with a gestational age <32 weeks during hospitalization, with potential for improved outcomes through optimized feeding practices. Purpose: To shorten the time to achieve full enteral and oral feeding volumes in infants with a gestational age <32 weeks. Methods: This pre-post-intervention study focused on patients with a gestational age <32 weeks in Shenzhen City, 44 bed NICU. Quality improvement interventions included workflow enhancements, breastfeeding education, and oral motor strategies. Analysis of feeding times and hospital stays was conducted using SPSS software, alongside a provider survey to project feasibility. Results: Statistical significant improvements were seen in full enteral feeding time for infants under 28 weeks gestation and full oral feeding time for those between 28 and 30 weeks. Length of stay did not show significant differences between period. Providers unanimously found the intervention feasible and acceptable. Implications: Quality improvement interventions can expedite achievement of full feeding volumes in preterm infants, with potential for enhanced feeding outcomes.

Preterm infants are frequently classified by considering the gestational age (GA) at birth.1 Whereas infants born in the extremely preterm (<28 weeks) and very preterm (28+0-31+6 weeks) periods comprise the smallest proportion of births (0.7% in China in 2018), these babies encounter significantly elevated rates of complications associated with prematurity.2 Enteral nutrition and oral feeding are essential for the growth and development of infants <32 weeks’ gestation. Full enteral feeding time refers to the duration required for an infant to receive all of their nutrition through enteral feeding. On the other hand, oral feeding time refers to the duration needed for an infant to transition from receiving nutrition through enteral feeding (via feeding tube) to being able to consume all their nutrition orally. The transition from parenteral nutrition (PN) to independent oral nutrition for preterm infants involves a series of 5 to 7 stages, including total parenteral nutrition, partial parenteral nutrition plus tube feeding, tube feeding, tube feeding plus oral feeding, and ultimately independent oral feeding.3 However, preterm infants often experience feeding and swallowing difficulties due to neurological issues, leading to challenges in generating suction, acquiring food, and producing rhythmic sucking patterns.4 This can result in the need for gavage feeding and limited feeding experiences. To establish enteral and oral feeding for preterm infants, it is essential to implement a standardized feeding regimen (SFR) in order to reduce variability in the initiation and progression of feeds.5

Research indicates that a smaller gestational age correlates with prolonged enteral feeding duration, and delayed achievement of complete oral feeding, which means longer hospital stays and higher medical costs.6 Despite the ability of tube feeding to meet the nutritional needs of preterm infants before oral feeding is achieved, it provides less stimulation to the local and whole digestive tract. Additionally, the indwelling gastric tube can probably lead to gastric abscission, reflux, and other related problems.7 The delayed initiation of enteral and oral feeding can lead to several significant consequences, including growth retardation, prolonged hospitalization, increased medical costs, adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, and early childhood feeding difficulties. These effects highlight the importance of timely and appropriate feeding practices in healthcare settings.8 Therefore, it is advantageous to initiate enteral feeding and oral feeding at the appropriate time for infants <32 weeks’ gestation.

Clinically, healthcare professionals have recognized the impact of enteral and oral feeding on infants <32 weeks’ gestation, and numerous improvement measures have been implemented. However, there remains a lack of operational procedures and a standardized feeding regimen for medical cooperation systems.

PROBLEM STATEMENT

Many preterm infants who undergo a stay in the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) often require PN and an indwelling gastric tube, which can contribute to sucking and swallowing dysfunction. Currently, there is a lack of standard-of-care measures to reduce these rates and improve enteral and oral feeding. By optimizing the enteral feeding protocol and implementing oral motor intervention, it is possible to improve enteral feeding and oral feeding in infants <32 weeks’ gestation. This improvement has the potential to reduce the time for full enteral feeding, and achieve full oral feeding more quickly, shorten the length of hospital stay (LOS), and decrease healthcare costs.

PURPOSE

As part of the Quality Improvement (QI) project, we conducted an evaluation of an evidence-based feeding support program aimed at reducing the duration to reach full enteral volumes and time to reach full oral feedings in infants <32 weeks’ gestation.

PROJECT AIMS

This project focused on 4 objectives to evaluate the use of standardized feeding process for premature infants in the NICU. As part of the 4 objectives, the program underwent evaluation based on the following criteria: (1) duration of time to achieve full enteral feeding volumes; (2) time to reach full oral feedings; (3) length of hospitalization; and (4) a post-interview was conducted to assess the satisfaction levels of clinical providers, in order to ascertain the practicability and acceptance of the program as the standard operating procedure (SOP). The target was set to achieve full enteral feeding within 1 week for very preterm infants and 2 weeks for extremely preterm infants in accordance with guidelines.9

REVIEW OF THE LITERATURE

To support the proposed intervention, a literature review was conducted on feeding support and oral feeding for infants <32 weeks’ gestation published within the previous 5 years. A recent systematic review has determined that initiating enteral feeding within 72 hours are the most effective methods for reducing the risk of neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) in preterm neonates born at or before 32 weeks gestational age.10 Barriers to implementation of early enteral feeding include concerns about feeding intolerance and perceived risks in the presence of NEC, despite insufficient evidence to support these concerns.11 Preterm infants, especially those born very preterm (gestational age <32 weeks), often experience delays in achieving independent oral feeding. A review of the literature indicates that oral motor intervention can enhance feeding performance, promote weight gain, and reduce hospital stay duration in preterm neonates.12-14 Programs for sucking and swallowing intervention has been developed with the aim of promoting the enhancement of oral motor and sensory skills in preterm infants.15 An example of a direct program involves using a gloved finger to gently stroke the intra-oral and peri-oral structures for a specified period of time before feeding. This technique is designed to provide sensory input and promote oral motor skills development.16 In the NICU, however, there is not consistent implementation of this practice for infants <32 weeks’ gestation.

In addition to the initiation of early feeding and oral motor exercises, human milk significantly contributes to enhancing enteral nutrition and facilitating oral feeding in preterm infants. Compared to the use of formula, the utilization of human milk either on its own or in combination with formula resulted in a reduction of length of stay (LOS), infection rates, and an improvement in overall survival for preterm infants.9 Research on feeding processes for infants <32 weeks’ gestation rarely provides guidance for optimizing feeding and oral feeding interventions. Despite clear evidence of the benefits, a combined approach that integrates enteral feeding with oral feeding has not been widely adopted in NICUs. One of the barriers to adoption is the lack of standardized processes for optimizing feeding regimens. Programs that implement successful, proactive feeding strategies offer early guidance and support to parents and families, helping them overcome obstacles related to enteral and oral feeding.17

METHODS

Design

Prior to the intervention, historical “usual practice” and intervention period were utilized in this quality improvement project, “usual practice” refers to the period before the QI program when SOP has not yet been implemented. The defined outcomes of interest included enteral feeding time, oral feeding time, and length of hospital stay. A post-intervention survey to all clinical providers was conducted to assess the feasibility and acceptability of the program.

Setting

The study NICU is situated within a university-affiliated tertiary care center in Shenzhen City and admits approximately 1000 infants annually. The facility is equipped with 18 beds for critically ill and preterm infants, as well as 26 beds for those requiring special care. This quality improvement (QI) program commenced in 2022 and continued through December 2023.

Sample

In this QI program, all infants <32 weeks’ gestation admitted to the NICU were eligible for enrollment. Patients with surgical conditions requiring transfer were excluded. Participants whose parents declined all medical support throughout admission were also excluded. This study utilizes the sample size calculation formula pertinent to cross-sectional studies, and outlines the methodology for determining sample size in single-sample clinical diagnostic tests as follows:

n=(Z1−a/2×p(1−p)σ)2.

The parameters were established as follows: significance level (α) set at 0.05; confidence level (1-α) at 0.95; a 2-sided test was employed; the tolerance error was defined as 0.1; and the sample size consisted of 35 cases.

All clinical providers (nurses, attendings, fellowship-trained physicians) within the unit were invited to participate.

Recruitment

Infants were recruited to the program in the program through face-to-face communication with their parents during hospitalization. The clinical staff were recruited via email, QR code postings throughout the unit, and face-to-face meetings.

The project was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University Shenzhen Hospital. Throughout this QI project, parents and/or caregivers had the right to request modifications to interventions or refuse them at any time.

Intervention/procedures

Topics of the QI project were discussed in a 30-minute information session prior to initiation. The topics covered included nutrition and rehabilitation of enteral and oral feeding for preterm infants, with the team formulating a feeding plan through evidence-based meetings, the evidence supporting the project’s interventions and the process for guiding patient care decisions (see Figure 1). In this process, the enteral nutritional requirements of the patient were evaluated. The target was set to achieve full enteral feeding within 1 week for very preterm infants and 2 weeks for extremely preterm infants in accordance with guidelines.18 The gradual increase in enteral feeding volume was accompanied by a reduction in parenteral nutrition. Once full enteral feeding was achieved, nurses assessed the patient’s oral feeding ability using the Preterm Infant Oral Feeding Readiness Assessment Scale (PIOFRA) for Infants. If the score exceeded 30 points, oral feeding could be attempted; if it fell below 30 points, oral motor intervention could be implemented.19 This oral intervention continued until the patient was able to fully consume orally. Alongside the workflow, we have also implemented relevant enhancements in the domain of breastfeeding. Within 6 hours of birth, all infants’ parents were referred to an experienced breastfeeding specialist nurse as part of the evaluations during the intervention period. A lactation evaluation and breastfeeding education were provided during this appointment, including hand-expressing milk and pumping milk. Prior to this project, NICU nurses did not see parents until skin-care appointments, and breastfeeding education deferred to the Obstetrics department. As part of this project, nurses, the IBCLC (International Board Certified Lactation Consultant) and the patient’s attending physician discussed the feeding strategy throughout their hospitalization.

Instruments/measures & data collection

We employed a self-designed questionnaire to evaluate the practicality and reception of the Enteral Feeding and Oral Feeding Program process. The questionnaire has been meticulously crafted to assess the feasibility, acceptability, and implementation effectiveness of the project, offering 4 response options: strongly agree, agree, disagree, and strongly disagree. The total score is 20.

All data, including the duration to full enteral feeding, the duration to full oral feeding, and the hospital stay, were gathered from the case system by 2 professionally trained researchers.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted to examine the demographic characteristics of the participants including gestational age, gender and birth weight of the preterm infants. Additionally, the age, gender, and occupation of the participants were also analyzed. To determine significance, data from pre- and post-intervention period were compared using IBM SPSS™ Version 27 statistical software program. All tests were 2-tailed, with statistical significance denoted by P < .05. The feasibility and acceptance of the intervention were assessed by analyzing and comparing post-intervention survey results from clinical providers. To calculate and compare between period, t-tests were used for aims 1 to 3 regarding time to reach full enteral feedings, time to achieve full oral feedings and length of hospital stay.

Aim 2: Time to reach full oral feeding volume

The independent t-test revealed that the time to reach full oral feeding volume for pre- and post-intervention period was not statistically significant (P = .827). However, within the group of preterm infants with a gestational age between 28 and 30 weeks, the full oral feeding time showed statistical significance at P = .047 for pre- and post-intervention period, while it was not statistically significant for preterm infants with a gestational age under 28 weeks or over 30 weeks.

Aim 3: Length of hospital stay

LOS for all infants admitted to the program at Shenzhen City Hospital in 2022 was 50.79 days with a minimum stay of 23 days and a maximum stay of 118 days. The median length of stay in 2023 was 55.33 days with a minimum stay of 25 days and a maximum of 110 days. There was a small difference between the pre- and post-intervention period, with an average length of stay of 71.94 ± 18.72 days (range: 28 weeks ≤ GA) before implementation and an average of 81.06 ± 13.97 (range: 28 weeks ≤ GA) after implementation. However, difference between these period was not statistically significant (independent t-test, P = .316).

Aim 4: Feasibility & acceptance

Out of the 34 healthcare providers, 22 participated in the post-implementation survey, representing a participation rate of 64.7%. Those who scored 18 or higher indicated their agreement or strong agreement with the survey questions. The summary score for the post-implementation survey, which evaluated provider acceptance of the QI project methods, was 19.23 (SD 1.37). Amongst all provider groups, RNs ranked highest with a score of 19.4 (SD 1.05). In the survey, none of the staff members expressed disagreement or strong disagreement. The project was deemed feasible and acceptable by all provider groups based on their mean total scores, and all providers agreed or strongly agreed with the project. DISCUSSION

In a unit lacking standardized feeding protocols, this QI project developed an evidence-based feeding support program to promote the oral and enteral feeding of infants <32 weeks’ gestation. While the post-intervention time to reach full enteral feeding volume did not show statistical significance across all period, the results of the QI project indicated that the Enteral Feeding and Oral Feeding Program led to a reduction in the time to reach full enteral feeding volume for infants <32 weeks’ gestation. There was a significant decrease in the time to reach full oral feeding volume for preterm infants with gestational ages between 28 and 30 weeks, as well as a notable decrease in the time to reach full enteral feeding volume for those with gestational ages under 28 weeks. A feasibility survey completed by healthcare staff concluded that the Enteral Feeding and Oral Feeding Program was easy to comprehend and effective in addressing the feeding challenges faced by infants <32 weeks’ gestation.

The findings were not significant between preterm infants with a gestational age of 28 to 30 weeks and those with a gestational age over 30 weeks in terms of the time to reach full oral feeding volume. This lack of significance can be attributed to the small sample size in each period. Additionally, there was no significant difference between preterm infants with a gestational age under 28 weeks and those with a gestational age over 30 weeks in terms of full oral feeding time. The gestational development of infants’ oral feeding skills, including the coordination of sucking, swallowing, and respiration, may account for this phenomenon. It is important to note that infants with a gestational age under 28 weeks often experience respiratory problems, which can result in prolonged periods to reach full oral feeding. As we are aware, the maturation of many components within each oral feeding level occurs at different times and rates due to various factors. Additionally, the lack of significant differences in the length of hospital stay among all period can be attributed to factors such as gestational age and respiratory conditions. However, it is important to note that the program demonstrated high feasibility and acceptability, leading to a noticeable shift in the overall culture of the unit during its implementation, which was crucial to its success.20 Based on evidence of fair-to-high quality, unimodal sensorimotor stimulation protocols involving manual oral stimulation combined with non-nutritive sucking have been shown to decrease the transition time to full oral feeding, enhance feeding efficiency, and reduce the length of hospital stay.11 These findings are partially consistent with the results of this QI project.

LIMITATIONS

In this study, several limitations were identified. Of particular note was the small number of patients in each period, which precluded the determination of significant efficacy. Furthermore, it is worth mentioning that the overall admission rate decreased significantly during the implementation of this project, largely due to declining birth rates in China.19 This limited the number of patients and directly impacted research studies. Moreover, the small patient sample size made it difficult to determine how this program affected all outcomes.

IMPLICATIONS TO PRACTICE

Shifting the culture toward optimizing enteral feeding, oral feeding, or the care of infants <32 weeks’ gestation is essential. Prior to the initiation of this project, formal enteral feeding plans varied among the treatment team. Additionally, nurses utilized different approaches to assess oral feeding readiness and ability. As a result of this project, a SOP was established at this institution and continues to be followed. The success of this project was also dependent on multidisciplinary support. Identified barriers, such as the lack of neonatal rehabilitation therapists within the interdisciplinary team and inadequate training of providers, were addressed early and consistently. This contributed to the success of the project.2 Nurses play a crucial role in implementing evidence-based oral feeding program support pathways in their respective units as they are the largest and most frontline group of providers.

CONCLUSION

Clinical providers widely support the quality-improvement project that established a sustainable feeding support program and oral feeding program incorporating a standardized assessment tool. The ultimate outcome was a cultural shift toward early enteral nutrition optimization and improvement in time to reach full oral feeding. Poor feeding outcomes pose a significant problem for infants <32 weeks’ gestation admitted to the NICU.20 Standardizing feeding support using SOP guidelines ensures adherence to best practices and provides clinical decision support at the bedside. Despite some results not reaching statistical significance, the feasibility and acceptance of this quality improvement project were high.
