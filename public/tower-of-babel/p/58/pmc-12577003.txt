Comparison of the success rate of bone level and tissue level implants placed in autogenous onlay bone block grafted ridges: a retrospective study
Authors: Gulsun Aydogmus, Nurettin Diker, Nur Altiparmak
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe present study aims to compare the success rate of bone level implants and tissue level implants placed in previously reconstructed ridges using autogenous onlay bone block grafts.MethodsThis study included 53 patients presenting alveolar bone defects and treated with autogenous onlay bone block grafts. After 6 months of healing, implant placements were performed in augmented areas. 31 bone level implants and 48 tissue level implants were placed with the standard surgical protocol. Follow-up data (pain, mobility, exudation from peri-implant space, success rate, marginal bone loss) were retrospectively collected after five years of prosthetic loading.ResultsThe overall implant success rate at the 5-year examination was 95,8% for the tissue level implant group and 87% for bone level implant group. No tissue level implants were removed during the follow-up period however, there were four failed implants in the bone level group. Tissue level implants have a significantly higher success rate than bone level implants (p = 0.022). The average marginal bone loss was 1.37 ± 0.60 mm for tissue level implants and 1.59 ± 0.72 mm for bone level implants. No statistically significant differences for the peri-implant bone loss was found between groups (p = 0.263).ConclusionsTissue level implants present better long-term results in terms of success rate, as compared with bone level implants, when placed in reconstructed areas. No significant differences were found between the two types of implants as far as peri-implant bone maintenance is concerned

Background

The use of dental implants for the rehabilitation of missing teeth is widely recognized as a reliable and predictable treatment modality in contemporary clinical practice [1]. Nonetheless, the alveolar bone commonly undergoes resorption following tooth extraction, a process that may be exacerbated by factors such as traumatic extractions, periodontal disease, surgical resections, prolonged use of removable prostheses, or complete lack of prosthetic rehabilitation. In cases where implant-supported restorations are planned in the presence of significant alveolar bone loss, a minimum of 1 mm of surrounding bone is required to achieve sufficient osseointegration and ensure the long-term success of the implant. However, this prerequisite may not be fulfilled in situations involving advanced bone loss. As a result, various reconstructive and regenerative techniques have been developed to enhance both the vertical and horizontal dimensions of the alveolar ridge [2].

Augmentation procedures for the reconstruction of implant sites with inadequate bone volume can be carried out either concurrently with implant placement or as a secondary surgical intervention following a designated healing interval [3]. Among these approaches, autogenous onlay bone block grafts are recognized as one of the most dependable clinical options, owing to their long-standing application and well-documented success in the literature [4]. Autogenous bone block grafts exhibit both osteogenic and osteoinductive properties, contributing to bone regeneration through the delivery of bioactive molecules and cellular elements [5]. Nevertheless, autogenous bone grafts are known to undergo a certain degree of resorption during the healing phase, raising concerns regarding the extent of bone loss that may occur following prosthetic rehabilitation in grafted sites. Despite the clinical relevance of this issue, there is still insufficient evidence in the literature specifically addressing the relationship between implant design and bone loss in regions augmented with autogenous grafts.

Bone level implants are placed with the implant abutment junction at the level of the crestal bone. While this position may facilitate a more favorable prosthetic emergence profile, it also increases the risk of marginal bone loss due to potential bacterial contamination and inflammatory reactions in the crestal area [6]. In contrast, tissue level implants are designed with a smooth transmucosal collar that positions the implant abutment interface above the bone crest, at the mucosal level. This design aims to promote healthier soft tissue integration and may offer improved long term stability in terms of peri implant bone preservation [7]. Identifying the clinical scenarios in which one design demonstrates superior outcomes over the other may aid clinicians in optimizing implant selection within evidence based treatment algorithms, especially in grafted or compromised sites.

Therefore, the present study aims to compare the success rates of tissue level and bone level implants placed in alveolar ridges reconstructed using autogenous onlay bone block grafts.

Methods

This retrospective study included patients who underwent autogenous onlay bone block grafting followed by implant placement due to alveolar bone deficiencies between 2014 and 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Başkent University (Project No: D-KA25/13).

Patients with advanced alveolar ridge atrophy that prevented prosthetically feasible implant placement were selected for inclusion. Following autogenous block bone augmentation, dental implants were placed in the reconstructed sites. Only patients who fulfilled the predefined inclusion criteria were enrolled in the study.

All augmentation procedures in this study were limited to areas involving a maximum of three missing teeth. Grafting was performed using autogenous onlay bone blocks harvested from the mandibular ramus or symphysis regions. In total, 53 patients who underwent grafting procedures were included, comprising 11 vertical, 38 horizontal, and 4 combined vertical and horizontal augmentations. A total of 48 tissue level implants and 31 bone level implants were placed in these grafted sites.

The bone grafts were stabilized with titanium screws and supplemented with particulate xenografts (0.25–1.00 mm, 0.5 mL, Bio-Oss; Gestlich Pharma AG, Wolhusen, Switzerland) and membranes (platelet-rich fibrin membrane). Standard postoperative care included the administration of antibiotics, analgesics, and chlorhexidine mouthwash.

Following a 6-month healing period after grafting, implants were placed according to a standard two-stage surgical protocol. All implants used were Straumann® brand (Straumann, Basel, Switzerland). The choice of implant length, diameter, and implant type (tissue level or bone level) was determined based on the clinical and prosthetic requirements. The implants were allowed a healing period of 4 months in the maxilla and 2 months in the mandible. Subsequently, healing abutments were placed, and cement retained partial fixed dentures were fabricated. Patients were monitored through a 5-year follow up period with clinical evaluations performed at 6-month intervals after prosthetic rehabilitation.

Clinical and radiographic data were retrospectively collected from patient records. Implant success was assessed based on 5-year follow-up findings, considering the presence of pain or tenderness, implant mobility, peri-implant exudation, marginal bone loss, and implant failure.

Marginal bone levels were evaluated using panoramic radiographs, which were analyzed with image processing software (ImageJ v1.8.0, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). For each implant, the distance from the implant neck to the most coronal point of bone-to-implant contact was measured at both mesial and distal aspects. The mean of these two measurements was calculated to determine the bone loss value for each implant. In cases where multiple implants were present within the same edentulous site, the resorption values were averaged to obtain a single value for the site.

To minimize distortion inherent to panoramic radiographs, a calibration process was performed using the actual implant length as a reference. The true bone loss value was calculated according to the following formula:

True bone loss = (True implant length - Radiologic bone loss)/Radiologic implant length.

Statistical analysis

Categorical data of the present study were described with numbers. Success rate was analyzed using Fisher exact test due to number of cells these have excepted frequencies less than 5. Normality of the peri-implant bone loss was checked with the Shapiro-Wilk test. Because of the abnormal distribution of peri-implant bone loss, the Mann-Whitney U test (a nonparametric test) rather than a parametric test was used to compare differences between TL and BL implant groups. The data were analyzed using the SPSS PASW Statistics 26.0. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate significant differences between experimental groups.

Results

A total of 53 patients with alveolar bone defects who underwent augmentation procedures were included in the present study. Of these, 48 tissue level implants were placed in 31 patients, and 31 bone level implants were placed in 22 patients.

At the 5-year follow up, the cumulative success rate of the tissue level implant group was 95.8%, whereas the bone level implant group showed a success rate of 87%. None of the tissue level implants failed during the follow up period. However, four implants in the bone level group failed and were subsequently removed, including two placed in the posterior maxilla and two in the posterior mandible. Statistical analysis revealed a significantly higher success rate in the tissue level implant group compared to the bone level group (p = 0.022). Discussion

This retrospective study presents a 5-year follow up evaluation of tissue level and bone level implants placed in autogenously augmented sites. The findings demonstrated that tissue level implants had a higher overall success rate compared to bone level implants. However, no statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups regarding marginal bone loss. These outcomes contribute valuable insights to the clinical decision making process, particularly in selecting implant types for patients who have undergone autogenous bone grafting with a focus on peri-implant bone preservation.

A retrospective study with a one year follow up conducted in 2023 compared the outcomes of tissue level and bone level implants placed in full arch immediate loading cases. The authors emphasized that both implant types yielded successful clinical outcomes under immediate loading conditions, and the survival rate for tissue level implants was reported as 100% [7]. Similarly, a systematic review by Lago et al. [8] evaluated the clinical performance of tissue level and bone level implants. Their analysis concluded that both implant types demonstrated favorable outcomes, and the choice between them should be guided by case specific factors and clinical indications.

A systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated marginal bone loss around bone level and tissue level implants, a parameter also assessed in the present study. While our results showed no statistically significant difference in marginal bone loss between the two implant types, the metaanalysis, which included 19 studies, reported heterogeneous findings regarding this comparison. It was also suggested that bone level implants incorporating platform switching may offer improved crestal bone preservation [9].

In a separate retrospective study by Shen et al. [10], marginal bone loss of tissue level and bone level implants placed with simultaneous guided bone regeneration in the posterior mandible was compared. The study found no statistically significant difference between the two implant designs, indicating that both achieved comparable marginal bone stability. In addition, a systematic review conducted by Cosola et al. [11] focused specifically on radiographic outcomes associated with bone level and tissue level implants. The analysis included 20 studies encompassing a total of 2933 implants, with follow-up periods ranging from 1 to 5 years. The authors reported that, although some individual studies showed differences, the majority of the included trials found no statistically significant difference in marginal bone loss between the two implant designs. These results are consistent with the findings of the present study, which also demonstrated no significant difference in marginal bone loss between tissue level and bone level implants placed in reconstructed sites.

In the present retrospective study, implant outcomes were comparatively assessed in sites augmented with autogenous onlay bone blocks. When reviewing the literature on implant survival in grafted versus nongrafted bone, a prospective study by Altıparmak et al. [3] reported no statistically significant differences between implants placed in augmented bone and those placed in pristine bone in terms of postoperative pain, peri-implant exudation, implant mobility, implant success, marginal bone loss, or patient satisfaction. In contrast, our study focused exclusively on implants placed in augmented bone areas. Chatelet et al. [12], in a review study, found no significant difference in implant survival rates between sites treated with guided bone regeneration and those reconstructed with autogenous bone block grafts.

Similarly, a systematic review by Fuglsig et al. [13], which included studies with follow up periods exceeding five years, concluded that the long-term survival rate of implants placed in block grafted sites using two stage protocols was consistently above 75%. In our own cohort, which also involved two stage surgeries and a five-year follow up, survival rates were 95.8% for tissue level implants and 87% for bone level implants.

To the best of our knowledge, only the study of Chiapasco et al. [14] has directly compared tissue level and bone level implants in augmented bone sites. In that prospective study, Straumann implants were placed into vertically deficient alveolar ridges reconstructed with autogenous onlay bone block grafts. Although the survival rate was reported as 100% for all implants, bone level implants showed greater peri-implant bone loss compared to tissue level implants. In our retrospective study, all implants were also Straumann brand, and four failures were observed in the bone level implant group. However, no statistically significant difference in marginal bone loss was found between the two groups. The higher amount of bone loss observed in the study by Chiapasco et al. may be attributed to the use of various donor sites for autogenous block grafting, including the iliac crest, ramus, and calvaria. In contrast, the grafts used in the present study were exclusively harvested from the mandibular ramus or symphysis.

The higher success rate observed in the tissue level implant group may be attributed to the transmucosal design of these implants. This design places the implant abutment junction at a distance from the crestal bone, reducing the potential impact of microgaps and subsequent peri-implant inflammation. By minimizing direct microbial exposure at the bone level, tissue level implants may better preserve peri-implant bone tissue [15]. In addition, the smooth transmucosal collar facilitates soft tissue stability, which may further contribute to implant longevity [7].

Although no statistically significant difference was found between the two groups in terms of marginal bone loss, numerically higher values were recorded in the bone level group. This may also be related to the positioning of the implant abutment interface at the crestal bone in bone level designs, potentially increasing susceptibility to microbial irritation.

This study has several limitations. First, its retrospective design limited the ability to control all clinical variables. Additionally, the study included only 53 patients, resulting in a relatively small sample size that may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle differences. Furthermore, as data were obtained from a single clinical center, the generalizability of the findings to broader patient populations is limited. Lastly, bone loss measurements were obtained from panoramic radiographs, which are less sensitive than cone beam computed tomography and may not fully capture fine scale bone changes.

Future research should include prospective randomized studies with larger sample sizes to enhance the statistical power of comparisons. Additionally, investigations evaluating the impact of different graft materials and donor regions would contribute to a deeper understanding of implant performance in augmented sites.

Conclusion

Based on the findings of this study, tissue level implants demonstrated a higher success rate than bone level implants in augmented bone. Although no statistically significant difference was observed in marginal bone loss between the two implant types, the mean bone loss values were numerically higher in the bone level group. These results suggest that tissue level implants may offer more favorable and stable clinical outcomes in sites reconstructed with autogenous onlay bone block grafts harvested from the ramus and symphysis. Therefore, tissue level implants may be considered a preferred option for augmented areas. Both implant types demonstrated acceptable levels of marginal bone loss, supporting their use in clinical practice. The findings of this study can aid clinicians in selecting the appropriate implant type for use in augmented bone sites.
