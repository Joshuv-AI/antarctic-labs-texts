Serum activin A and bone metabolism in patients undergoing hemodialysis
Authors: Yosuke Nakagawa, Hiroo Takahashi, Yuichiro Takahashi, Toru Hyodo, Miho Hida, Takao Suga, Takatoshi Kakuta, Yusuke Tomita, Michio Nakamura, Hirotaka Komaba
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryActivin A, which promotes osteoclast differentiation, increases with declining kidney function, but its role in bone metabolism in kidney failure remains unclear. We found that higher activin A levels were associated with increased bone turnover markers, independent of parathyroid hormone levels.PurposeActivin A, produced by various cells including bone marrow stromal cells, immune cells, and osteoblasts, is thought to promote osteoclast differentiation. Serum activin A levels rise with declining kidney function, but its role in bone metabolism in kidney failure remains incompletely understood.MethodsSerum activin A levels were measured in 35 healthy individuals and 654 patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis. Associations of serum activin A with tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase isoform 5b (TRACP5b), bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BALP), and metacarpal bone mineral density were assessed using both unadjusted and multivariable-adjusted linear regression analyses. Serum activin A levels were also compared between patients with and without a history of fracture, and between those who developed incident fractures during follow-up and those who did not.ResultsThe median serum activin A level in hemodialysis patients was 733 pg/mL (interquartile range (IQR), 591–912), approximately 2.6-fold higher than that of healthy controls (277 pg/mL; IQR, 216–289; P < 0.001). Patients with higher activin A levels had longer dialysis durations, a higher prevalence of polycystic kidney disease, and higher serum phosphorus and alkaline phosphatase levels. Higher activin A levels were significantly associated with increased TRACP5b levels independently of intact parathyroid hormone levels, with a similar trend observed for BALP. Serum activin A levels were not associated with metacarpal bone mineral density, history of fracture, or incident fractures.ConclusionThese findings suggest that in patients with kidney failure, elevated activin A may contribute to high bone turnover, although its ability to predict fracture risk appears limited. Further studies are warranted to investigate its potential as a therapeutic target.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00198-026-07981-z.

Introduction

Patients with chronic kidney disease (CKD) have an increasing risk of fracture as kidney function declines [1, 2], and those undergoing maintenance dialysis have a fracture risk several times higher than that of the general population [3–5]. The major contributor to this increased risk is bone metabolism abnormalities, known collectively as renal osteodystrophy (ROD). Secondary hyperparathyroidism is an important cause of ROD, in which parathyroid hormone (PTH) induces high-turnover bone lesions and cortical bone porosity [6]. However, PTH is not always a reliable indicator of bone turnover [7, 8], and the underlying pathophysiology remains unclear. In this context, activin A has recently attracted attention.

Activin A, a member of the transforming growth factor β (TGF-β) superfamily, is broadly expressed in various cell types, including bone marrow stromal cells, immune cells, and osteoblasts. This molecule has diverse functions in regulating cell differentiation, proliferation, and apoptosis [9]. Activin A also regulates bone metabolism. In murine bone marrow culture studies, activin A enhances osteoclast differentiation [10]. Moreover, in CKD model mice, a ligand trap for the activin receptor type 2A inhibited osteoclast-mediated bone remodeling in a PTH-independent manner and increased bone mass [11, 12]. These findings suggest that activin A may contribute to the pathogenesis of bone metabolism abnormalities in CKD.

Previous studies in CKD patients have reported that serum activin A levels begin to rise in the early stages of CKD and increase as kidney function declines [13–16]. Activin A is a 26–kDa dimeric polypeptide and, based on its molecular weight, falls within the range of middle molecules in the uremic toxin classification. Elevated levels of activin A in kidney failure, which may be explained not only by decreased renal clearance but also by increased production in injured kidneys [15, 16], suggest that activin A may play an important role in bone metabolism in patients with kidney failure. Notably, an observational study of patients across CKD stages G2–5D demonstrated that higher serum activin A levels were associated with increased bone turnover, as evaluated by histomorphometry [13]. However, because that study included patients with a wide range of kidney function, the possibility that kidney function confounded the results cannot be excluded. Furthermore, few studies have examined the relationship between activin A and fracture risk in patients with kidney failure. Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the associations of serum activin A levels with bone turnover markers, metacarpal bone mineral density (BMD), and fracture risk in patients undergoing hemodialysis.

Methods

Study design

This study used data from the Tokai Dialysis Prospective Cohort Study, a prospective cohort study of 654 patients undergoing hemodialysis at 10 facilities in Japan. The study design has been described previously [17]. Briefly, patients were eligible if they were at least 20 years old and had been receiving hemodialysis three times per week for more than 3 months as of June 30, 2012. For the present analysis, we used data from the 3-year follow-up. To compare serum activin A levels between hemodialysis patients and healthy individuals, healthy controls were recruited from prospective living kidney donors. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tokai University School of Medicine, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Data collection

Baseline data, including demographics, height, weight, hemodialysis prescription, vascular access, comorbidities, and medication use, were collected from each facility. Metacarpal BMD was measured by radiographic absorptiometry (RA) in 217 patients across three facilities. In Japan, RA has been used as a routine practice in a subset of hemodialysis facilities, and we have previously reported its clinical utility in hemodialysis patients [18]. Serum samples were collected at baseline, immediately before the first hemodialysis session of the week in hemodialysis patients, or prior to kidney donation in healthy controls. Patients were not required to fast prior to blood collection. Routine biochemical parameters in hemodialysis patients were measured at each dialysis facility, whereas those in healthy controls were measured at Tokai University Hospital. Serum calcium levels were corrected for albumin using Payne’s formula [19]. Serum levels of activin A, intact PTH, tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase isoform 5b (TRACP5b), and bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BALP) for both hemodialysis patients and healthy controls were measured at a central laboratory. Serum activin A levels were measured by sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (R&D Systems, Indianapolis, IN, USA), intact PTH by electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Elecsys PTH; Roche Diagnostics, Basel, Switzerland), TRACP5b by enzyme immunoassay (Osteolinks TRAP-5b; Nittobo Medical, Fukushima, Japan), BALP by chemiluminescence enzyme immunoassay (Access Ostase; Beckman-Coulter, Brea, CA, USA), and sclerostin by sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (Biomedica Medizinprodukte GmbH & Co KG, Vienna, Austria). All abbreviations and units for biochemical indices of bone status, including TRACP5b, BALP, PTH, and sclerostin, were defined and reported in accordance with the recommendations of Lombardi et al. [20].

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics and laboratory values were compared between hemodialysis patients and healthy controls using χ2 tests, Mann-Whitney U tests, or Student’s t-tests, as appropriate. Differences across tertiles of serum activin A were assessed using χ2 tests, Kruskal-Wallis tests, or one-way analysis of variance.

Correlations of serum activin A with TRACP5b and BALP were evaluated using scatter plots and Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients. Associations among intact PTH, activin A, and bone turnover markers were analyzed using multivariable linear regression models. Separate models assessed the associations of intact PTH or activin A with bone turnover markers, followed by models including both variables. Sensitivity analyses stratified by the median intact PTH level were also performed. As an additional sensitivity analysis, the multivariable models were repeated after excluding patients with polycystic kidney disease (PKD). Model 1 was adjusted for age, sex, dialysis duration, body mass index (BMI), diabetes, and PKD. Model 2 was further adjusted for history of fracture, history of parathyroidectomy, hemoglobin, albumin, creatinine, calcium, phosphorus, C-reactive protein, total cholesterol, and the use of calcium carbonate, sevelamer hydrochloride, lanthanum carbonate, active vitamin D, and cinacalcet hydrochloride. To assess multicollinearity among independent variables, variance inflation factors were calculated, and no problematic collinearity was identified. Restricted cubic spline analyses with four knots were performed to examine potential nonlinear associations of serum activin A with TRACP5b and BALP, adjusting for covariates in Model 2. As a supplementary analysis, the association between serum activin A and sclerostin levels was evaluated using correlation analysis and restricted cubic spline models.

The association between serum activin A and metacarpal BMD was analyzed using multivariable linear regression with covariates in Models 1 and 2. Comparisons of activin A levels between patients with and without prior fractures, and between those with and without incident fractures, were performed using analysis of covariance, adjusting for the same covariates, with the exception of the history of fractures in Model 2.

P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 28 (IBM, Tokyo, Japan) and Stata 18 (StataCorp, LP, TX, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among the 654 study participants, the mean age was 65.5 years, 67.0% were male, the median hemodialysis duration was 104 months, and 40.5% had diabetes. The median serum activin A level was 733 pg/mL (interquartile range (IQR), 591–912), approximately 2.6-fold higher than that of healthy individuals (277; IQR, 216–289 pg/mL; P < 0.001) (Table 1). The difference remained significant after adjustment for age, sex, and BMI (P < 0.001). Associations between activin A and bone turnover markers

We examined the associations of serum activin A with TRACP5b and BALP. Scatter plots of TRACP5b and BALP versus activin A levels (Fig. 1a and b) indicated that higher activin A levels correlated with higher TRACP5b (R = 0.20; P < 0.001) and BALP (R = 0.23; P < 0.001). To explore whether activin A is associated with bone metabolism independently of secondary hyperparathyroidism, we analyzed the associations of intact PTH and activin A with bone turnover markers. In separate models, higher intact PTH was associated with higher TRACP5b, and higher activin A was also associated with higher TRACP5b (Table 3). These associations remained significant after multivariate adjustment. When both intact PTH and activin A were included in the same model, each retained similar associations with TRACP5b. Similar findings were obtained for BALP. Excluding patients with PKD did not materially alter the associations between serum activin A and bone turnover markers (data not shown). In sensitivity analyses stratified by the median intact PTH level (157 ng/L), higher serum activin A levels were consistently associated with higher TRACP5b levels in both strata (lower PTH: β = 0.140 (95% CI, 0.028 to 0.252), P = 0.02; higher PTH: β = 0.189 (95% CI, 0.091 to 0.289), P < 0.001). Similar findings were observed for BALP (lower PTH: β = 0.196 (95% CI, 0.083 to 0.309), P = 0.001; higher PTH: β = 0.130 (95% CI, 0.023 to 0.237), P = 0.02). In a supplementary analysis, serum activin A levels were not significantly correlated with sclerostin levels (Supplemental Fig. 1). Association of activin A with bone mineral density and fracture events

Among 217 patients with available metacarpal BMD data, serum activin A levels were not associated with metacarpal BMD in either unadjusted or multivariate adjusted models (Table 4). We then examined associations between activin A and fracture events. At baseline, 59 of 654 patients had a history of fracture. Serum activin A levels did not differ significantly between those with and without a history of fracture (Fig. 2a), and this finding remained unchanged after multivariate adjustment. During the 3-year follow-up, 9 patients experienced clinical fractures. Serum activin A levels were comparable between those with and without incident fractures in both unadjusted and multivariate adjusted models (Fig. 2b). Discussion

In this cohort of 654 patients undergoing maintenance hemodialysis, serum activin A levels were elevated compared with healthy individuals. Elevated serum activin A levels were significantly associated with higher TRACP5b levels, independent of secondary hyperparathyroidism, and a similar trend was observed for BALP. However, serum activin A levels were not associated with metacarpal BMD or fracture events. These findings suggest that, in patients undergoing hemodialysis, activin A may contribute to high bone turnover independently of secondary hyperparathyroidism.

We found that serum activin A levels were markedly elevated in patients with kidney failure undergoing hemodialysis compared with healthy individuals, supporting previous observational findings [13, 16]. Higher serum activin A levels were more frequently observed in patients with a longer dialysis duration, who were considered to have minimal or no residual kidney function. Serum activin A has been reported to increase as kidney function declines [13–16] and to decrease rapidly after kidney transplantation [21], suggesting that decreased renal clearance may partly explain the elevated levels in kidney failure. However, in patients with acute kidney injury, urinary excretion of activin A increases with injury severity [22–24]. Furthermore, animal studies have reported striking upregulation of activin A mRNA in the kidneys of CKD mice [15, 16]. These findings indicate that increased production of activin A in injured kidneys may also contribute to elevated serum levels of activin A in kidney failure. The extent to which activin A production persists in non-functioning kidneys remains an intriguing question that warrants further investigation.

Interestingly, serum activin A levels were markedly elevated in patients with PKD. In a murine model of PKD, activin A expression in the kidney was upregulated even at early disease stages, and inhibition of activin A signaling suppressed cyst enlargement [25]. These findings suggest that the elevated serum activin A levels in PKD patients in this study may be attributable to increased production in cystic kidneys. To our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating increased activin A levels in human PKD patients. However, because this study included only patients undergoing hemodialysis, the relationship between activin A and PKD severity remains unknown. Future longitudinal studies in non-dialysis PKD patients are needed to determine whether activin A contributes to cyst progression.

Few studies have examined the association between activin A and bone metabolism in CKD patients. In an observational study of 104 patients with CKD stages G2–5D, elevated serum activin A levels were associated with an increased bone formation rate, as evaluated by histomorphometry [13]. However, this study did not adjust for PTH, leaving it uncertain whether the associations between activin A and bone histomorphometric parameters were independent of PTH. In the present study, higher activin A levels were strongly associated with higher levels of bone turnover markers in unadjusted analyses, and this association persisted after adjusting for intact PTH. Notably, serum activin A levels were strongly positively associated with TRACP5b, suggesting that activin A may stimulate osteoclast activity independently of PTH. These findings align with previous experimental studies showing that activin A promotes osteoclast differentiation by stimulating receptor activator of nuclear factor κB (RANK) expression in murine bone marrow cultures [10], and that inhibition of activin A signaling by a ligand trap for activin receptor type 2A suppresses osteoclast activity in a PTH-independent manner and increases bone mass in CKD mice [11, 12]. Nevertheless, the observed correlations were modest, suggesting that activin A plays a limited role in the regulation of bone turnover and bone metabolism. In addition, the wider confidence intervals at higher activin A levels likely reflect the smaller number of patients in this range.

Sclerostin is an osteocyte-derived protein that inhibits osteoblast differentiation and mineralization through the Wnt signaling pathway [26] and is elevated in kidney failure [27, 28]. We have previously reported that serum sclerostin levels are associated with lower bone turnover markers in hemodialysis patients [17]. We explored the association between serum activin A and sclerostin levels in a supplementary analysis, but no significant association was observed. This finding suggests that while both factors are involved in the regulation of bone metabolism in patients with kidney failure, they may act through distinct and independent pathways. Specifically, sclerostin primarily reflects alterations in Wnt signaling and osteoblast activity, whereas activin A may preferentially influence bone turnover by affecting osteoclast differentiation and activity.

In contrast to the associations with bone turnover markers, we observed no association between serum activin A levels and metacarpal BMD, fracture history, or incident fractures. In patients undergoing hemodialysis, cortical bone porosity due to secondary hyperparathyroidism is a major determinant of fracture risk, which may reduce the relative impact of activin A on bone metabolism and strength. Moreover, given the limited size of the metacarpal BMD subcohort and the small number of fracture events in this study, the statistical power to detect significant associations was limited. Therefore, these analyses should be considered exploratory, and the absence of significant associations should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect. Larger and longer-term studies are required to address this issue.

Activin A has also attracted attention as a therapeutic target. Inhibin A, a physiological antagonist of activin A, prevented BMD loss in gonadectomized mice [29]. Inhibition of activin A signaling by a ligand trap for the activin receptor type 2A has also been shown to attenuate osteoclast activity independently of PTH, suppress bone remodeling, and increase bone mass in diabetic CKD mice [11]. A randomized controlled trial in healthy postmenopausal women demonstrated that sotatercept, an activin A receptor type IIA IgG1 fusion protein trap, induced a dose-dependent increase in serum BALP and a dose-dependent decrease in serum TRACP5b [30]. Furthermore, a preliminary small-scale randomized controlled trial in patients undergoing hemodialysis showed that sotatercept increased cortical BMD at the femoral neck [31]. Although the mechanism by which sotatercept increases bone mass remains unclear, the findings of the present study and previous studies suggest that sotatercept suppresses osteoclast-mediated bone resorption. Regulation of activin A signaling may thus become a promising strategy for treating bone metabolism abnormalities in CKD-MBD.

This study has several limitations. First, bone histomorphometry was not performed as a direct indicator of bone metabolism. However, the bone turnover markers TRACP5b and BALP used in this study have been shown to reliably reflect bone turnover and are recommended as surrogates for bone turnover in CKD-associated osteoporosis [32, 33]. Second, activin A is widely expressed in various tissues, making it unclear which sources contributed to circulating levels. Examining its local expression in bone or its extracellular concentration around osteoclasts may provide further insight. Third, although we evaluated metacarpal BMD, BMD at the lumbar spine or hip, typically measured by dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, was not assessed. Fourth, residual kidney function was not assessed in this study. It is possible that the capacity for activin A production differs between patients with functioning and non-functioning kidneys. Fifth, the healthy control subgroup was relatively small and differed from the hemodialysis cohort in terms of age and sex distribution. Finally, as this was an observational study, the results should not be interpreted as causal; residual confounding may account for some findings.

Conclusion

Serum activin A levels were markedly elevated in patients undergoing hemodialysis and may contribute to high bone turnover independently of secondary hyperparathyroidism. The strong association between activin A and TRACP5b supports the possibility that activin A promotes bone resorption in CKD-MBD. Further studies are warranted to investigate the potential of activin A as a therapeutic target.
