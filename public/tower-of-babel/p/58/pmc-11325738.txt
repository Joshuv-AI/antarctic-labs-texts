Evaluation of the efficacy of posterior hemivertebrectomy combined with two or more segments fusion
Authors: Shangyu Guo, Yiming Zheng, Zhiqiang Zhang, Dong Fu, Junfeng Wang, Haodong Li, Chuang Qian, Dahui Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough early hemivertebra (HV) resection and short fusion (within 4 segments) have been successful in treating congenital HV, there is limited research comparing the outcomes of the shortest-segment fusion (2 segments) versus 3 or 4 segments, particularly in young children. To evaluate the efficacy of posterior hemivertebrectomy combined with two or more segments fusion in children under the age of 10 years with a solitary simple lower thoracic or lumbar HV (T8-L5).MethodsThis retrospective study included patients under the age of 10 with lower thoracic or lumbar solitary simple HV who underwent hemivertebra resection (HVR) and transpedicular short fusion and were divided into HV ± 1 group (2 segment fusion) and HV ± 2 group (3 or 4-segment fusion). The study recorded preoperative, postoperative (1 week), and the latest follow-up radiographic parameters and complications. The results of the coronal and sagittal planes were analyzed, and the main curve, segmental scoliosis curve, compensatory scoliosis curve, segmental kyphosis curve, and trunk shift were compared.ResultsThe study included 35 patients (15 in the HV ± 1 group and 20 in the HV ± 2 group) with a mean age of 5.26 ± 2.31 years and a mean follow-up of 22.54 months (12–68). The mean preoperative Cobb angle was 32.66° ± 7.339° (HV ± 1) and 29.31°±6.642° (HV ± 2). The final Cobb angle was 10.99°± 7.837° (HV ± 1) and 8.22° ± 4.295° (HV ± 2). The main curve corrected by 72% (HV ± 1), 75% (HV ± 2) postoperatively and 67% (HV ± 1), 72% (HV ± 2) at the final follow-up (P > 0.05). There were no significant differences in the correction of the segmental scoliosis curve, compensatory scoliosis curve, segmental kyphosis curve, and trunk shift between the HV ± 1 and HV ± 2 groups (P > 0.05). The unplanned reoperation rate for HV in the thoracolumbar region (T11-L2) is significantly higher (P = 0.038).ConclusionIn the context of solitary simple lower thoracic or lumbar HV (T8-L5), HV ± 1 segment fusion suffices and yields comparable correction outcomes in the midterm period when compared to HV ± 2. The reoperation rate exhibited a statistically significant increase in the thoracolumbar region.Keywords: Posterior hemivertebrectomy, Segments fusion, Congenital scoliosis, Children

Background

Congenital scoliosis is a result of either a failure of vertebra segmentation, formation, or a combination of both [1]. The estimated prevalence of congenital scoliosis is 1 in 1000 live births based on current studies [2, 3]. The severity and progression of hemivertebra (HV) curvature depend on the rate of deterioration and the extent of spine deformation [1]. Generally, the severity of the spinal deformities caused by HV is determined by the type, site, number, and relationship of the HV. Kawakami et al. proposed a classification system for congenital scoliosis utilizing 3D computed tomography reconstructions, which allows for a comprehensive evaluation of various types of laminae and a more nuanced assessment of severe deformities [4]. Fully segmented HV is most likely to result in significant deformity during development, especially in thoracolumbar vertebrae, which are more prone to deteriorating the spine’s curvature [5, 6]. Surgical intervention is recommended earlier in these cases to ameliorate the progression of deformity and alleviate psychological distress. Early surgical intervention in young children enables normal growth of the spine in the unaffected regions and averts severe local deformities and secondary structural curves [7].

The armamentarium of surgical treatment modalities for spinal deformities encompasses a range of techniques, including in situ posterior spinal fusion, combined anterior and posterior in situ spinal fusion, HVR [8], and guided growth procedures such as growing rods [9], Vertical Expandable Prosthetic Titanium Rib (VEPTR) [10], and Shilla [11] procedures, as reported in the literature. The excision of posterior thoracic or lumbar HV followed by short-segment fusion yields a favorable outcome in terms of deformity correction and stabilization, while exhibiting significantly reduced surgical duration and incidence of complications when compared to alternative surgical interventions [12]. The present study affirms that with the escalating advancements in spinal instrumentation, timely surgical intervention employing posterior HVR and short-segmental fusion has emerged as an optimal surgical modality for addressing progressive congenital scoliosis resulting from HV [13, 14]. Several reports have attested to the satisfactory surgical outcomes achieved by combining HVR with short-segmental fusion [7, 13, 14]. Accompanying the HVR, the use of robust transpedicular instrumentation is necessary and can involve the application of one rod [15, 16], two rods [13], or even three rods [17].

Despite the published successful results concerning this surgical technique, there is a dearth of midterm outcomes in the literature comparing the outcome of HV ± 1 vs. HV ± 2 fusion segments, particularly in very young children. In contemporary surgical treatment, a persistent need exists to bridge the gap between routine clinical practice and the availability of randomized controlled trials (RCTs). This arises due to the continuous advancement of new techniques in the practical world, which often occurs without awaiting the outcomes of RCTs. The main objective of this study is to compare the surgical outcomes between HV ± 1 short-segment fusion and HV ± 2 short-segment fusion with posterior HVR and pedicle screw fixation in children under the age of 10 years with scoliosis caused by a solitary simple thoracic or lumbar HV.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This is a retrospective observational study of exploratory nature in children under the age of 10 years with scoliosis caused by a solitary simple thoracic or lumbar HV who underwent HVR and transpedicular short fusion from Jan. 2015 to Jan. 2022 in our hospital. This study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Children’s Hospital of Fudan University, National Children’s Medical Center (2019 − 184) and this study complies with the provisions of the Helsinki Declaration. Individuals younger than the age of 16, consent to participate had be obtained from their parents or legal guardians.

The inclusion criteria of participants: (1) age less than 10 years at the time of surgery; (2) congenital deformity resulting from a solitary simple thoracic or lumbar HV; (3) surgical intervention through posterior HVR using transpedicular instrumentation; (4) follow-up period of at least 12 months with comprehensive radiographic and clinical data. Patients who were over 10 years old, had undergone prior spinal surgery, exhibited complex congenital spinal deformity, or had syndromic scoliosis were excluded. The patients were divided into two groups, HV ± 1 group (undergoing HV ± 1 segment fusion) and HV ± 2 group (receiving HV ± 2 segment fusion). The study encompassed all 35 patients who provided essential preoperative data, underwent surgery, and furnished one-week postoperative records, as well as the latest follow-up postoperative data. Notably, none of the eligible participants were excluded from the study (Fig. 1).

Data collection

Three seminal time points were considered when collecting radiographic data: preoperative, immediate postoperative, and last follow-up. The data were collected by a single, trained individual and validated by a fellowship-trained pediatric spine surgeon on a random basis. These measures were taken according to radiographic parameters published by Scheer et al [18]. Complete physical and radiographic evaluations were performed before surgery, including standing long cassette anterior-posterior (AP) and lateral X-rays. Computer tomography (CT) scans with reconstruction to evaluate the shape and position of the HV and the anatomy around the pedicles; magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is used to detect spinal cord anomalies. The Cobb method described by Ruf and Harms was used to measure all curve parameters [13].

Surgery technology

In this study, the surgical technique of HVR via a single-stage posterior approach with fusion of adjacent vertebrae was implemented utilizing transpedicular instrumentation [13]. Surgical procedures were performed by the same surgeon team who have vast experience in spine deformity correction. A standard midline incision was performed while the patients were in the prone position, and pedicle screws were inserted into the normal upper and lower vertebrae. Subsequently, a pre-contoured rod was connected to the screws on the convex side, and the posterior HV elements, as well as the adjacent levels, were excised to expose the pedicle and nerve roots above and below. Resection of the posterior elements was executed at the end to ensure protection of the dural sac and cord. Blunt dissection was utilized to expose the anterior HV, and a wedge osteotomy was performed from the convex to the concave side. The anterior vertebral body was then hollowed using a combination of a rongeur, osteotome, curette, and abrasive drilling, and the upper and lower discs, including cartilage endplate, were excised from the bleeding bone. Gradual compression was applied while leaving the concave rod unlocked until the gap was closed. Additional segments were included in the instrumentation if high compressive force was necessary to correct deformity, particularly pronounced kyphosis, to avoid overloading the pedicles and causing pedicle fracture. Precautions were taken to ensure that exiting nerve roots and the dura were not impinged, and decortication of posterior elements was carried out before utilizing autologous bone from the HV for posterolateral fusion. Upon achieving sufficient recovery to ambulate, the patient received a standard custom-fabricated brace. The brace was utilized for at least a duration of 3 months and was discontinued in the absence of radiographic evidence of pseudarthrosis (Fig. 2).

Curve progression evaluation

The present study computed the percentage alteration in main curve from preoperative to postoperative evaluations. The calculation was performed using the following formula: [(preoperative degree of Cobb angle - postoperative degree of Cobb angle)/preoperative degree of Cobb angle] *100%. We use segmental kyphosis measurement to assess sagittal plane balance. We assign a positive sign (+) to thoracic kyphosis and a negative sign (-) to lumbar lordosis. In the realm of orthopedic pathology, the term “curve progression” denotes the emergence of a new curvature that exhibits an increment in the Cobb angle by greater than 20 degrees, with the apical vertebra positioned two or more levels distant from the lower instrumented vertebre. This definition aligns with the criteria utilized for evaluation in this study. The most prevalent diagnostic criteria for “adding-on” entail either a progressive escalation in the number of vertebrae encompassed by the distal curve, or an increase in the first vertebra deviation below the instrumentation from the center sacral vertical line (CSVL) by more than 5 mm, or a rise in the angulation of the first disc below the instrumentation by more than 5 degrees [19].

Statistical analysis strategy

Continuous variables were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and based on normality test, used the independent sample t-test for comparison between two groups. Categorical variables were described as n (%) and compared using chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical tests were two-sided, and statistical significance was determined by a P-value of 0.05 or less using SPSS version 22.0 (Statistical Product and Service Solutions, NY, USA). We employed a linear regression model to compare differences in the primary outcome (follow-up main curve Cobb corrected ratio) variables between the two groups, adjusting for covariates (age and weight).

Results

Patient and surgical characteristics

The patients were divided into two groups, with 15 patients undergoing HV ± 1 segment fusion at mean age of 4.34 years and 20 patients receiving HV ± 2 segment fusion at the mean age of 5.96 year. The patients’ baseline and clinical characteristics, the location and type of HV encountered, the radiographic characteristics were outlined in Tables 1, 2 and 3. Notably, no statistically significant differences were detected between the HV ± 1 and HV ± 2 fusion groups with regards to sex, side of HV, surgical duration, follow-up duration, type of HV, preoperative main curve, preoperative segmental scoliosis, preoperative compensatory curve, and preoperative segmental kyphosis (P > 0.05). Nonetheless, a marked dissimilarity in age, weight, surgical blood loss and location of HV was observed between the two cohorts (P < 0.05).

Preoperative, postoperative, and follow-up radiological features

The location of hemivertebra in HV ± 1 group and HV ± 2 group existed no significantly statistical difference (Table 2).

The follow-up period extended for an average of 25.13 months (range: 12–68 months) in HV ± 1 group and an average of 20.60 months (range: 12–47 months) in HV ± 2 group. Operative time averaged 172.67 min (range: 60–322 min), and the mean volume of intraoperative blood loss was 164 mL (range: 80–400 mL), calculated at average 9.6 mL/kg body weight in the HV ± 1 group. Meanwhile, in the HV ± 2 group, operative time averaged 206.40 min (range: 60–303 min), and the mean volume of intraoperative blood loss was 307.95 mL (range: 130–500 mL), calculated at average 14.1 mL/kg body weight. Consistent with prior methods, the blood loss rate was calculated as the ratio of blood loss in milliliters to total blood volume in milliliters [20]. The mean blood loss rate was 12.8% in HV ± 1 group and 18.8% in HV ± 2 group and the hemoglobin levels remained no statistically signigicant difference. The average hospital day was 7.35 in the HV ± 1 group and 7.89 in the HV ± 2 group.

Radiographic deformities correction

The presentation of corrections made in both the coronal and sagittal planes can be found in Table 3. The total main scoliosis curve demonstrated a reduction from 32.66° (19°–44°) to 10.99° (2°–27°) at the final follow-up in the HV ± 1 group, with a corresponding correction rate of 67%. In the HV ± 2 group, the total main scoliosis curve was corrected from 29.31° (12°–38°) to 8.22° (3°–15°) at the final follow-up, with a correction rate of 72%. During the preoperative to final postoperative follow-up period, no statistically significant correction (P > 0.05) in the main curve and segmental scoliosis was observed between the HV ± 1 and HV ± 2 groups. The compensatory cranial curve improved from 10.19° (1.6°–30.9°) to 6.51° (0.7°–14.4°) postoperatively, while the compensatory caudal curve improved from 10.50° (3.5°–17.9°) to 4.68° (1.7°–9.5°) postoperatively in the HV ± 1 group. The compensatory cranial curve improved from 13.00° (1.4°–29.9°) to 5.44° (1.2°–12.1°) postoperatively, while the compensatory caudal curve improved from 11.88° (1.9°–29.4°) to 4.11° (1°–10.5°) postoperatively in the HV ± 2 group. The correction rates for both groups were also determined. A statistically significant difference was not observed between the HV ± 1 and HV ± 2 groups in compensatory curve correction (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

The study also assessed the sagittal plane correction, focusing on the segmental kyphosis (SK). In the HV ± 1 group, the SK improved from − 3.13° (-19°–15°) to -3.03° (-19°–22°) postoperatively and further improved to 0.18 (-14°–60°) at the final follow-up. In contrast, the SK in the HV ± 2 group improved from 1.05° (-22°–23°) to -1.44 (-21°–25°) at the final follow-up. No statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups in postoperative SK (P > 0.05) (Table 3).

Furthermore, the study evaluated the coronal balance of the two groups. The trunk shift and shoulder balance were assessed preoperatively and at the latest follow-up. The results showed no statistical significance (P > 0.05) in trunk shift between the two groups, with the final follow-up ranging from 0.83 cm to 1.31 cm (Table 3).

Linear regression analyses

The findings of the linear regression analyses exhibited homogeneity (Table 4). Specifically, after adjusting for covariates such as age and weight, there was an absence of statistically significant correlation between groups (HV ± 1 and HV ± 2) and the main outcome, with a P-value exceeding 0.05. The follow up time also has no correlation with the main outcome (P > 0.05).

Discussion

In the context of surgical interventions for scoliosis patients, short-segment fixation typically pertains to the fusion of up to six segments, while long-segment fixation encompasses the fusion of more than six segments [21]. Most of the article reported the fusion level for patients presenting with a single HV delineating the range from HV ± 1 to HV ± 2, with demonstrated notable efficacy in producing favorable outcomes during the course of patient monitoring [22–24]. In certain instances where significant scoliotic or kyphotic deformity is present, fusion endpoints were extended to HV ± 2, in order to provide adequate structural support for the closure of osteotomy gaps or to prevent implant failure. In our study the surgery efficacy shows there was no statistical difference between HV ± 1 and HV ± 2.

The initial posterior approach of HVR case was documented by Royle in 1928 [25]. Shono et al. conducted 12 HVRs via a singular posterior approach in 2001, resulting in a correction rate of 64% for segmental scoliosis after 2 years of surgery [26]. Ruf et al. had already performed posterior-only resection of 12 thoracic or lumbar HV as early as 2003, with a segmental scoliosis correction rate (65.9%) that was comparable to previous studies [7]. For patients with thoracic or lumbar HVe, Bollini et al. reported a mean postoperative improvement of 69.3% [27], while Nakamura et al. demonstrated a 55.1% improvement in three thoracic or lumbar HV cases among five HVe resections [28]. These correction rates are consistent with our findings, with the segmental scoliosis correction rate of 69.2%. It greatly reduces the risk of trunk imbalance.

HV commonly give rise to kyphotic deformity, and the restoration of the sagittal profile is equally crucial to that in the coronal plane. Other studies have reported SK correction rates between 63 and 81.9%, suggesting that posterior HVR and short-segment fusion are dependable in the correction of SK [10, 12, 29–31]. The current research demonstrated a 74.5% improvement in at the final follow-up, indicating a similar degree of correction in comparison to previous studies.

Notwithstanding the favorable outcomes of surgical intervention, the emergence of postoperative complications represents a substantial concern for patients within this cohort. During the investigation, it was noted that in the HV ± 1 group, one patient (6.7%) experienced postoperative curve progression due to incomplete hemivertebra resection. However, no patients (0%) in the HV ± 2 group exhibited curve progression during the follow-up period. In prior studies, Bollini et al. [27]. and Holte et al. [8]. reported instances of curve progression, amounting to 17.6% and 16.2%, respectively, subsequent to hemivertebra resections. While short-segment fixation techniques may correct the deformity and mitigate the influence of neighboring vertebral growth, the potential for postoperative curve progression remains if the selected fusion segments are inadequately short [32]. The implant migration rate in HV ± 1 group was 6.7% in this study. Similarly, Zhang et al [14]. reported an implant failure rate of 8.9%. The higher unplanned reoperation rate in the thoracolumbar hemivertebrae region may be attributed to several factors. These factors could include a larger range of local motion, the younger age of children, and lower bone density, which can make them more susceptible to nail or screw penetration, leading to internal fixation displacement.

The efficacy of simple posterior hemivertebra resection is good, with no significant difference compared to combined AP resection [33]. It has better results than simple hemivertebra blockage [34]. The postoperative complications of HV ± 1 and HV ± 2 was low, with a risk of internal fixation displacement. HV ± 2 can reduce the risk of reoperation due to fixation displacement, but HV ± 1 increases the risk of adding on. Therefore, the timing and surgical approach for hemivertebra treatment should be individualized based on the type, location, and severity of the hemivertebra. Each approach has its own advantages. To date, a consensus has not been reached regarding the optimal surgical timeframe for the management of congenital scoliosis, notwithstanding the fact that the majority of clinicians advocate for early intervention following the first year of life. All of the patients included in this study were under 10 years old with the average of 5.26 years. In cases where patients with CS malformation exhibit an age exceeding seven years, it is plausible that they may still experience favorable clinical outcomes subsequent to surgical intervention [35, 36].

The length of follow-up remains relatively brief, particularly given that the patients were below the age of 10 and thus are still far from reaching skeletal maturity. The potential for late correction loss or implant displacement persists, and the incidence of pedicle fractures at the instrumented level continues to pose a formidable obstacle in very young children undergoing short segmented fusion. Secondly, the present study’s sample size is deemed inadequate, highlighting the need for forthcoming large-scale, multi-center prospective investigations.

Conclusion

Remarkably, in cases of solitary simple thoracic or lumbar hemivertebra, HV ± 1 segment fusion demonstrates comparable correction outcomes to HV ± 2 during the midterm period. The unplanned reoperation rate was significantly higher in HV located in thoracolumbar region.
