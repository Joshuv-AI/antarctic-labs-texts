Utilization of Pediatric En Bloc Kidneys from Donation After Cardio‐Circulatory Determination of Death: 15 Years of Experience
Authors: Lucia De Gregorio, Oswaldo Aguirre, Gianna Mladenova, Luis Fernandez, Raquel Garcia‐Roca
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDespite the efforts by the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) to improve utilization of organs from very small pediatric donors, en bloc kidney grafts are still disproportionately discarded. Although associated with increased technical complications, en bloc kidney transplants achieve excellent graft function and longevity. We present our single‐center experience with en bloc kidney transplants in adult recipients and compare outcomes between donation after cardio‐circulatory death (DCD) and brain death/death by neurologic criteria (BD/DNC) organs.MethodsRetrospective analysis was completed of all en bloc kidney transplants performed at a single‐center academic transplant program, between 2008 and 2023. Two groups were defined according to the type of pediatric donor: DCD or BD/DNC. Demographic data, clinical variables, postoperative complications, and survival outcomes were compared between both groups.ResultsForty‐two adult recipients received pediatric en bloc kidneys, 11 of these from DCD donors. The DGF rate was higher for DCD en bloc kidneys (27% vs. 16% for BD/DNC); however, this difference was not statistically significant. Nearly one third of patients required re‐operation during the index transplant admission (28.6%). Four grafts were lost secondary to thrombosis −4(10%). Urological complications occurred in 9.5% of cases. Nevertheless, technical complications were not statistically different between both groups. Patient and graft survival rates were excellent in both groups. All recipients sustained serum creatinine values below 1 mg/dL beyond the first year post‐transplant.ConclusionAlthough en bloc kidney transplantation of pediatric en bloc kidneys can be associated with greater rates of technical complications, these grafts can lead to excellent graft function and patient longevity. Diligent patient selection and an experienced surgical team are paramount to optimize the outcomes of en bloc kidney transplants.

Introduction

The number of candidates waiting for kidney transplantation continues to outpace the number of transplants performed every year in the United States. The scientific and transplant community continues to improve transplantation rates by expanding the donor pool. Pediatric en bloc kidney transplantation has been successful in achieving this goal. Pediatric donors represent about 5%–7% of all donors for the past few decades [1]. One of the earliest descriptions of pediatric en bloc transplantation dates back to 1972 [2], when two pediatric kidneys were transplanted into an adult recipient while preserving the donor anatomy, including the donor vena cava and aorta. The utilization of pediatric en bloc transplantation has been increasing in numbers, but is associated with increased technical failure risk and urological complications [3]. Donation after cardio‐circulatory death (DCD) is becoming an increasing source of viable organs for transplantation. DCD kidney grafts experience increased rates of delayed graft function (DGF) and early graft loss, but the long‐term results are similar to those kidneys form brain death/death by neurologic criteria (BD/DNC) [4]. Other groups have reported similar outcomes with DCD and BD/DNC en bloc kidneys [5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. The bulk of DCD outcomes data available is based on adult DCD organ donors since pediatric en bloc donation is less common, and significantly less long‐term follow‐up data is available to report, especially with DCD kidneys [10]. With this series of consecutive cases, we aim to present our single‐center experience with pediatric en bloc kidney transplantation for adult recipients from the past 15 years. A comparison between pediatric en bloc kidneys from DCD and BD/DNC donors is performed to understand short‐ and long‐term graft and patient survival.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

This is a retrospective analysis of all adult kidney transplant recipients of en bloc kidneys from pediatric donors from 2008 to 2023, at a single academic, urban transplant program. Donors were stratified in two groups based on the donation type: DCD and BD/DNC. We collected donor demographics, kidney anatomy information, and renal function parameters from the United Network of Organ Sharing (UNOS) database on every donor. Data included donor age in months, gender, race, weight in kilograms, cause of death, kidney donor profile index (KDPI), initial and terminal creatinine, and donor warm ischemia time (dWIT), transplant sequence number and donor organ procurement organization (OPO). Recipient demographic data were obtained from the electronic medical record via retrospective chart review. Data included age at the time of transplant, gender, race, weight in kilograms, height in centimeters, cause of renal disease, date of waitlist qualification, estimated post‐transplant score (EPTS), and calculated panel reactive antibody (CPRA). Surgical technique, urological reconstruction, and postoperative use of anti‐coagulant medications were also collected.

Recipient candidacy for en bloc kidneys has been modified during the 15 years of the database. However, a more standardized approach for en bloc listing criteria has been implemented since 2017. Patients were listed for en bloc kidneys if they met the following: (a) normal range BMI (under 25 kg/m2), (b) renal disease with low probability of recurrence or rejection (to minimize the need for post‐transplant kidney biopsies), and (c) the target arterial anatomy for implantation is suitable for transplantation and without arterial calcifications. For this reason, all recipients considered for en bloc kidney offers had cross‐sectional imaging at the time of evaluation or listing. Postoperative management was likewise standardized and consisted of: (a) aggressive blood pressure control, (b) intravenous anticoagulation on the first 24–48 h with heparin and, (c) early renal sonogram to monitor graft perfusion. The intricacies of en bloc kidney transplantation, including increased risk for technical complications, were fully discussed with the patient by the transplant surgeon in the pre‐operative period. All recipients understood and gave fully informed consent for surgery.

Clinical Outcomes

We collected cold ischemia time (CIT) and warm ischemia time (WIT) for all transplants. DGF was defined as the need for dialysis in the first 7 days after transplant. Additional data collected included creatinine at admission and discharge, creatinine and glomerular filtration rate at 2 weeks, 4 weeks, 1 month, 6 months, 1 year and every year after transplantation, up to 5 years. Among postoperative complications, we documented postoperative bleeding, urological complications, and return to the operating room during the index admission and within 30 days after transplant.

Survival rates were calculated based on patient status information available until the last recorded date in the medical record. Patients who were lost to follow‐up were included in the analysis with their last known status date. Graft status was considered failed when the patient was deceased, resumed maintenance dialysis, the graft was explanted for any reason, or the patient was re‐transplanted. A patient's death with a functioning graft was documented separately for analysis purposes.

The retrospective chart review was approved by the institutional review board at Loyola University Medical Center under protocol LU#217921.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean value and standard deviation (SD) and compared using the ANOVA test. Certain non‐normal variables were analyzed using median and Kruskal–Wallis test. Categorical variables were expressed in proportions (percentages) and compared using Fisher exact test, Chi‐square, and the unpaired t‐test, when appropriate.

Patient and graft survival were plotted using Kaplan–Meyer survival curves and compared by donor type using log rank test. p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

From November 2008 to December 2023, 42 adult patients were transplanted with pediatric en bloc kidneys. Patients were grouped by the donor type: DCD group with 11 patients and BD/DNC group with 31 patients. Incidentally, two patients in the BD/DNC group were recipients of a simultaneous pancreas transplant with the en bloc kidneys.

Recipient Characteristics

Recipient demographic information is summarized in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences between the two groups. Proportionally, recipients were more likely to be female (69%), Hispanic (59.5%), and glomerular diseases were the most common cause of renal disease in both groups (35.7%). Most recipients were on dialysis at the time of transplant, with an average waiting time of 3.5 years. Recipients had a similar mean weight of 58.9 kg and a mean BMI 23.3 kg/m2 in both groups. Patients were mostly not highly sensitized, with a mean CPRA of 11.3%. Recipients of DCD en bloc kidneys had a similar mean EPTS of 23.3, compared to 27.7 for recipients of BD/DNC en bloc kidneys.

Donor Information

Donor characteristics and demographics are summarized in Table 2. There was no statistical difference in donor age between groups, with a median of 8.5 months of age at the time of donation (interquartile range [IQR] of 3.3 to 25.9 months). The majority of donors were Caucasian and male, with a mean weight of 10 kg and SD of 5.6 kg. Organ acceptance sequence was not statistically different in either group. 35.7% of the organs originated from a local OPO, with a sequence of acceptance around the order of 200. Regional organs represented 19% of the cohort, and 45.2% were accepted from national allocation, in a sequence of acceptance over 2000.

Organ Recovery

During the donor organ recovery, both kidneys were procured en bloc with the abdominal aorta and inferior vena cava. The aorta is transected at the level of the superior mesenteric artery, just above the renal artery take‐off on each side. On occasions, when the liver is not procured, the aorta is transected above the celiac axis. The upper vena cava is usually transected just above the implantation of the right renal vein or more cephalad, if the liver is not recovered. On the lower aspect, both the vena cava and the aorta are usually transected at the iliac bifurcation. Upon request to the procurement surgeon, we have received the distal aorta and a portion of the common iliac arteries.

Back‐Table Organ Preparation

The bench preparation of en bloc kidneys consists of ligation of all the lumbar arteries on the posterior aspect of the aorta, ligation of the inferior mesenteric artery, and preservation of the periaortic and pericaval lymphatic tissue with careful ligation of all branches. Both adrenal glands are excised. The upper vena cava is closed with non‐absorbable running sutures just above the insertion of the renal veins. The upper aortic stump is closed using the posterior aortic wall (when sufficient space is available and the take‐off of the right renal artery is away from the stump). Otherwise, a circumferential patch of the lower aorta is cut and used to extend the upper aorta, and its cephalad aspect is closed with a non‐absorbable running suture. Any effort to minimize potential stenosis of the renal arteries is key to successful long‐term graft function. Alternatively, and when available, the donor iliac bifurcation can be used to create a patch used to reconstruct the proximal end of the aorta without stenosing the ostium of the renal arteries. This type of reconstruction is similar to the one proposed by E.J. Wylie in 1975 [11]. However, in the technique described by Wylie, the arterial patch was autogenous and not from a cadaveric donor. Similar reconstruction can be used on the distal vena cava closure with iliac vein bifurcation.

Implantation

The preferred side for en bloc kidney implantation was the right side (39 patients, 92.8%) using the external iliac vessels. One recipient had a previous kidney transplant, and the en bloc kidneys were implanted on the left external iliacs. Additionally, there were two cases of simultaneous pancreas‐kidney transplants, in which the en bloc kidneys were implanted on the left external iliac vessels.

Regarding the management of the ureters, the procurement of the kidneys was usually completed by transecting the ureters at the pelvic inlet. Some procuring surgeons may include, upon request, the dome of the bladder with the trigone and insertion of both ureters. In this series, three techniques were used for the ureteral implantation: ureters are implanted separately to the recipient bladder (13 cases); both ureters are sutured together prior to single implantation to the recipient (20 cases); and lastly, the donor bladder patch with both ureters is implanted into the recipient bladder (9 cases), preserving the native trigone and its anti‐reflux mechanism. Ureteral stent(s) were used in all cases except for two BD/DNC cases, in which the ureters had been implanted separately (see Table 4).

Operative Time and Length of Hospital Stay

CIT was comparable in both groups: mean 19.1 ± SD 7.8 h for DCD cases versus mean 15.1 ± SD 8.5 h for BD/DNC cases (p value 0.176). There were no significant differences in WITs between both groups either (DCD mean WIT 31.7 ± SD 7.7 min vs. BD/DNC mean WIT 33.5 ± SD 5.9 min; p value 0.409). Median length of stay was 4 days, and it was not significantly different between groups (DCD median stay 3 days, IQR [3–6] vs. BD/DNC median stay 4 days, IQR [3–6]; p value 0.385).

Postoperative Management and Complications

Throughout the studied time period, there were numerous variations on postoperative management. In Table 5, we summarize the use of heparin infusion and the incidence of technical complications. In 20 patients (47.6%), systemic anticoagulation was administered immediately in the postoperative period (5 patients in the DCD group and 15 in the BD/DNC group). Heparin dosages ranged from 200 to 300 IU/h, at the surgeon's discretion. Five patients who received systemic anticoagulation (25% of anticoagulated recipients) required re‐exploration for bleeding from the surgical field. Three additional patients required re‐exploration for bleeding despite not being anticoagulated (13.6% of non‐anticoagulated patients). Overall, there was a 19% return to the operating room rate due to bleeding (8 patients). Two of these grafts thrombosed as a consequence of bleeding, both in the BD/DNC group, and both had been immediately started on systemic anticoagulation following the index surgery (see Table 5).

The average rate of return to the operating room was 28.6% (12 patients), and there was no difference between the two donor groups (p value 0.618). Five patients required surgical exploration due to no graft perfusion as evidenced on ultrasound. In two of them, the graft was found non‐viable, and these were explanted (one in the DCD group). In another case, graft salvage was attempted by repositioning of the kidneys and intra‐arterial administration of a fibrinolytic agent. This graft was ultimately explanted due to a lack of blood flow. In one case, the kidneys never gained function, despite adequate sonographic parameters in duplex ultrasounds and normal angiography (BD/DNC donor). There was one instance in which surgical repositioning successfully salvaged the en bloc graft. One patient was taken back to the operating room because the medial kidney compressed the native ureter, causing hydronephrosis. This graft was repositioned, but the medial kidney had to be removed days later due to thrombosis. The remaining kidney lasted 3 years. The overall thrombosis rate was 11.9% (5 cases, 9 kidneys).

The use of antiplatelet aggregation agents was more consistent throughout the study time period. In 38 patients (90.5%), aspirin was initiated within the first 48–72 h post‐transplant. Most patients received aspirin 81 mg (22 patients). In 16 patients, aspirin was started at 325 mg daily and transitioned to a lower dose (81 mg) 3 months after transplant. Half of the patients started on antiaggregant agents continue to be on them to this date (18 patients). The other 18 patients remained on antiaggregant agents for a period of 3 to 6 months, at the surgeons’ discretion. There were only four patients who did not receive aspirin: two of them had graft thrombosis and explant; the other two patients did not experience any technical complications.

Table 4 describes the incidence of ureteral complications by the type of reconstruction used. The urological complication rate of the cohort was 9.5% (4 patients), with no difference found between recipients in each group. Two patients in the BD/DNC group had a urine leak with disruption at the ureteral anastomosis. One had had separate implantation of each of the ureters, and the other had a donor bladder patch including both ureters. Of note, these two patients had a return to the operating room for bleeding, and the ureteral dehiscence happened following the take‐back. One patient had a medial kidney ureteral stenosis 6 months post‐transplant related to an episode of acute rejection. The stenosis resolved after percutaneous nephro‐ureteral stenting without consequences to the graft function (BD/DNC group). One patient developed nephrolithiasis 1.5 years after transplant, with stone impaction within the transplant ureter. This was removed endoscopically by Urology.

Kidney Function

Eight patients required dialysis in the first week after transplant (DGF incidence of 19%). The incidence of DGF in the DCD group was higher (27.3% vs. 16.1% for BD/DNC cases), but this did not reach statistical significance (p value 0.295). The median follow‐up was 4.2 years. The mean creatinine at 2, 4, 6 weeks, 3 and 6 months, and 1 and 3 years were not statistically significant between the groups. All patients experienced a significant improvement of renal function after 6 weeks, reaching nadir at 6 months for recipients of BD/DNC donors, and nadir reached at 1 year for recipients of DCD donors. Both groups demonstrated serum creatinine values below 1 mg/dL beyond the first year post‐transplant (Figure 1).

Simultaneous Pancreas With En Bloc Kidneys

Two patients had simultaneous pancreas and kidney en bloc transplants. In both cases, the pancreas was implanted on the right side, with an arterial implant to the right common iliac artery and systemic drainage to the distal cava. The duodenal segments were anastomosed to the distal jejunum. The en bloc kidneys were implanted on the left external iliac vessels.

One patient had end‐stage kidney disease from type 2 diabetes. He was readmitted 2 months after transplant for orthostatic hypotension and was found to have a non‐occlusive thrombus in the graft's portal vein. This was managed with subcutaneous heparin. The thrombus was not visualized in subsequent ultrasounds. This patient continues to have excellent kidney function (creatinine 0.93 mg/dL), 7 years after transplant. The patient's fasting glucose remains normal, but hemoglobin A1c is mildly elevated (6.2%). The patient had a significant weight gain over the course of postoperative years; however, he remains insulin‐free.

The other patient developed post‐transplant lymphoproliferative disorder (PTLD) within the pancreas graft 4 months after transplant. He received six cycles of chemotherapy and was off immunosuppression (minimal prednisone was maintained). Ultimately, both grafts failed, and the patient resumed hemodialysis 375 days after the transplant. Eventually, the pancreas graft developed a pseudoaneurysm and an arterio‐venous fistula between the common iliac artery and the distal cava. This was treated with a covered stent of the common iliac. Despite this, he did not require pancreatectomy. The patient was lost to follow‐up 3 years later.

Patient Survival

There was no statistical difference in patient survival between the two donor groups (Figure 2). Patient survival was 91% at 1‐, 3‐, and 5‐years for patients transplanted with DCD donors. Only one patient expired due to COVID‐19 infection at 10 months post‐transplant. Patient survival for recipients of BD/DNC donors was 90% at 1‐year, 83% at 3‐ and 5‐years, respectively. One patient developed urosepsis at 3 months and died prior to arrival at the hospital. One patient had anoxic brain injury due to hemorrhagic shock one week after transplant. One patient had a cardiac event within 30 days after transplant. Additionally, there was an accidental death at 3 years post‐transplant and a cardiovascular death at 1.5 years. The patient whose graft was declared PNF at 90 days, died 5 years later of unknown cause.

The death‐censored graft survival was similar between both groups (Figure 3). DCD donor grafts had a death‐censored graft survival of 90% at 1‐, 3‐ and 5‐years. One graft was lost due to graft thrombosis. There was an additional graft loss due to patient death with a functioning graft (secondary to COVID infection). Three grafts were lost in the immediate postoperative course (2 due to thrombosis and one was declared primary non‐function at 90 days). Another graft was lost due to the toxicity of chemotherapy treatment and lack of immunosuppression for PTLD. Discussion

In this article, we summarize our experience with en bloc kidneys over the past 15 years. During those years, there was turnover of the surgical team and, as such, evolution of preferences in perioperative management. This is reflected in the lack of a standardized anticoagulation protocol, and subsequently, this likely influenced our outcomes. In 2017, we implemented stricter criteria for recipient selection, only considering candidates with low BMI, renal disease with low probability of recurrence, and anatomically favorable iliac arteries.

Regarding donor selection and organ acceptance, there is a lack of standardization as well. We do not have data regarding pediatric en bloc kidneys offered to our institution and that were declined. Similarly, we do not have consistent records of organs accepted and later declined upon back‐table evaluation. However, the scope of this manuscript was to analyze our outcomes with pediatric en bloc kidneys for adult recipients, in particular for organs from DCD donors. The decision to recover and offer kidneys en bloc was made by the OPOs and was based on the age and weight of the donor. None of the organs accepted were considered for splitting and transplantation into two recipients. The median age and mean weight of the donors in this series are comparable with those published by other groups [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 12, 13]. Nearly 60% of the donors had a weight below 10 kg, and the median age for the entire series of donors was 8.4 months (minimum age 1 day to maximum age 4.8 years).

Regarding the criteria for accepting organs from DCD, most donors had a short dWIT, with a rapid decline in blood pressure and oxygen saturation. All donors continued to make urine in the hours preceding the donation, and only two of them had an elevation in creatinine above normal levels. As mentioned previously, we do not have data on offers declined based on pre‐donation and recovery parameters. However, we can presume that there was a tendency to accept organs from pediatric donors with relatively short dWIT, preferably with normal kidney function until the time of donation.

With regards to recipient selection, most patients were female and were deliberately selected to have a normal BMI. It is a relatively young cohort, which also reflects recipient selection efforts, targeting candidates with healthy vasculature. This is consistent with reports from another institution, which found that very small pediatric donors (less than 10 kg) were more commonly used for female recipients [8]. There is precedent evidence that low recipient BMI is perhaps the single most important recipient factor to guarantee successful graft outcomes with small en bloc kidney donors [14]. The presence of severe vascular calcifications increases the complexity of the surgical procedure unnecessarily, adding risk to an already technically challenging operation. Increased immunological risk factors for rejection, such as high CPRA or the presence of donor‐specific antibodies, were identified as crucial factors for recipient selection due to the inability to estimate the functional renal mass remaining after a severe rejection episode. Similar concerns exist for recipients with certain glomerular diseases, with a greater risk for early recurrence on the graft. Several of these factors have been validated by other groups [14, 15, 16].

There is significant variation in survival rates among centers, yet our overall survival outcomes are comparable to others [7, 9]. With regards to complications, our rate of thrombosis is comparable to what has been published [13, 17, 18, 19, 20]. White et al. found that the first‐year graft survival for en bloc kidneys from donors smaller than 8 kg was significantly lower than that of other pediatric grafts [12]. One early study compared graft outcomes based on donor age and reported a 10% thrombosis rate among recipients of kidneys from donors under 5 years old, along with a 5% rate of primary non‐function [14]. Recipient factors associated with increased risk of graft failure at 1‐year included African American race, high CPRA, BMI greater than 25 kg/m2, history of prior transplant, and prolonged CIT [14]. Retrospective analysis of recipients of pediatric donors under 20 kg showed a reduction in thrombosis rates of 4.4%. Urological complications were also rare, with only three cases attributed to graft failure, and 19 instances of primary non‐function were reported out of 1516 transplants using donors under 20 kg [21]. Although technical complications have declined compared to earlier experiences, small pediatric en bloc grafts still carry a higher risk of surgical issues, particularly urological complications [22, 23]. In our own series, outcomes were consistent with early data, with a 11.9% thrombosis rate and a 9.5% incidence of urological complications [5, 6, 7].

We had a significantly high rate of return to the operating room (nearly 30%). This was prompted by bleeding or concerns for graft thrombosis or poor perfusion. The high incidence of bleeding was likely related to the use of systemic anticoagulation immediately after surgery. However, there were also cases of bleeding without anticoagulation. On two occasions, bleeding was severe and caused compression of the graft and secondary thrombosis. In two cases of poor perfusion to the graft, repositioning was able to improve the blood flow. However, one of those cases remained with marginal function and was ultimately declared non‐functioning. While our findings suggest that heparin use may increase the risk of bleeding, no definitive conclusions can be drawn regarding its necessity as prophylaxis to definitely prevent early graft thrombosis. Notably, in our series, the incidence of thrombosis appears comparable between recipients of en bloc kidneys who received heparin and those who did not, suggesting that perhaps anticoagulation may not be needed. Despite our findings, there is emerging data supporting the use of heparin prophylaxis in pediatric kidney transplant recipients, particularly in high‐risk cases such as en bloc transplants from small donors [24].

Although our series included a limited number of en bloc pediatric kidney transplants from DCD donors, the experience yields valuable insights into the unique complications associated with this graft type. Specifically, we observed patterns of vascular and urological challenges that differ from those seen in standard pediatric or adult deceased donor transplants. These findings underscore the importance of tailored perioperative management and vigilant post‐transplant surveillance.

Pediatric en bloc kidney transplantation continues to be an underutilized source of grafts that is overlooked despite their excellent outcomes [25]. Recently, the annual utilization of pediatric en bloc kidneys was recorded to be as low as 372 en bloc kidneys. However, the number fluctuates from 184 to 550 en bloc transplants in the last 5 years [26, 27, 28, 29]. The exact annual discard rate of pediatric en bloc kidneys in the U.S. is not consistently published as a standalone figure, but studies suggest it remains notably high, especially for very small donors. One analysis found that up to 36.7% of recovered deceased‐donor kidneys were discarded overall, with pediatric en bloc kidneys contributing disproportionately to the high discard rate [30]. Factors associated with the decreased utilization of pediatric en bloc kidneys are the technical demand, the perceived graft viability, especially for those DCD donors, and the high complication rate associated with their use [18].

Another factor that may be contributing to the continued nationwide underutilization of pediatric en bloc kidneys is the recent shift in allocation practices, which no longer prioritize matching donor and recipient sizes. In our series, over one‐third of transplants were performed using kidneys allocated through the national category after regional offers were declined. These en bloc kidneys were typically transplanted at our center into adult recipients with a BMI under 25 kg/m2, and were accepted through the “open offer” category, allowing us to identify appropriate BMI candidates from our waiting list. The limited regional utilization likely reflects several barriers including: restricted flexibility during organ referrals to local centers when the CIT is short, limited experience or training in handling these grafts, perceived concerns about viability, and a general risk‐averse practice by many centers. The lack of momentum for utilization is further supported. This inertia is confronted by recent data showing that outcomes are more favorable when pediatric en bloc transplants, especially those from donors under 8 kg, are performed at institutions with greater procedural experience [21, 31].

In 2019, UNOS developed a policy to specifically address the allocation of pediatric en bloc kidneys with the goal to improve utilization. The policy was developed separately from dual kidney allocation for adult kidneys into a single recipient due to the differences in donor characteristics. For en bloc allocation, donor weight was used as the criteria to guide the OPOs to offer the kidneys en bloc to a single recipient versus single pediatric kidney allocation to two separate recipients. This new policy designates en bloc kidney allocation as preferred for donors under 18 kg. This decision was influenced by the probability of pediatric organs being utilized based on different transplanting centers practices [32]. The policy also allowed individual transplant centers to split kidneys after en bloc allocation and provided a pathway to reallocate the second kidney.

Despite these recent changes in the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) allocation policy, pediatric en bloc kidneys continue to be underutilized nationwide. Several contributing factors were not fully addressed in the latest policy update. Chief among them is the implementation of the 250 nautical mile fixed‐circle allocation system, which, combined with the operational and logistical challenges brought on by the COVID‐19 pandemic, has significantly reduced the utilization of pediatric donors [33].

In this cohort, 26% of organs came from DCD donors. We observed that overall outcomes are comparable to those of BD/DNC. In larger studies, donation after cardiac death does not constitute a risk factor for kidney graft loss [34]. However, it is important to note that grafts from DCD donors took a longer time to reach nadir function. Ultimately, this does not impact the short‐ and long‐term outcomes. It is particularly noteworthy that initial serum creatinine levels in recipients of pediatric en bloc kidney transplants not only improve steadily over time but also remain remarkably low with long‐term follow‐up, reflecting the exceptional durability and function of these grafts. The improvement of the renal function correlates with the growth of the pediatric graft, as the volume increase occurs in the first 3 months after transplant [34], reaching double to triple the original size by 12 months after transplant. The renal function has been reported to increase 1.8‐fold to six times by the first year [36, 37]. Recently, a 25‐year longitudinal study comparing en bloc kidneys to living donor kidneys graft function conferred similar survival rates. En bloc kidneys showed delayed onset of proteinuria and higher eGFR rates compared to living donor kidney transplants [38].

Our study has several limitations, including the small sample size of the DCD cohort, and the retrospective nature of the study. As mentioned previously, during the study time period, our institution experienced changes in the surgical team, which most likely involved modifications in organ and recipient selection, technique variations and postoperative management. Despite this, our results demonstrate, like others, that en bloc kidney transplants from BD/DNC and DCD are safe, can confer good outcomes and longevous kidney function. We cannot emphasize enough the importance of rigid recipient selection criteria to secure long‐term success rates and minimize complications. Importantly, the data informed the development of a dedicated informed consent protocol for adult recipients of DCD pediatric en bloc kidneys. This consent framework addresses the specific risks inherent to these grafts, including potential for thrombosis, increased take‐back rate, and technical complexity, while also highlighting the long‐term benefits observed in selected recipients. By incorporating these insights into the consent process, we aim to enhance transparency, support shared decision‐making, and ensure that recipients are fully apprised of the unique considerations surrounding this high‐value graft type.

Conclusion

En bloc kidney transplants from pediatric DCD donors offer a promising solution to the ongoing organ shortage, providing viable grafts that can lead to excellent long‐term outcomes. Despite early concerns about size mismatch, vascular complications, and DGF, growing evidence supports the safe and effective use of these organs when transplanted by experienced teams using refined surgical techniques.

Successful programs hinge on meticulous donor selection, standardized procurement protocols, and structured post‐transplant monitoring. As transplant centers continue to embrace innovation, DCD en bloc transplantation not only expands the donor pool but also reinforces the importance of maximizing every opportunity to save lives, especially when the gift comes from the youngest among us.
