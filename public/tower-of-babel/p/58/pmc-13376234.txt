Direct NMR Evidence of Distinct Cation‐CO2 Adducts in Asymmetric Phosphonium‐Based Aprotic N‐Heterocyclic Anion (AHA) Ionic Liquids
Authors: Pranav J Thacker, Louise M Cañada, Jon‐Marc McGregor, Joan F Brennecke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe report direct evidence of distinct cation‐CO2 adduct formation in ionic liquids (ILs) based on aprotic N‐heterocyclic anions (AHAs), using a custom‐designed in situ NMR (iNMR) method under conditions verified to achieve equilibrium speciation. These complexes are in addition to the primary anion‐CO2 carbamate product. 13C and 31P NMR spectra of three triethyloctylphosphonium ([P2228]+) AHA ILs all reveal two distinct carboxylate species, resulting from regioselective CO2 addition at both α‐carbons of the asymmetric cation. The product ratios vary with temperature, and DFT‐predicted pKa values confirm subtle differences in α‐proton acidity. In contrast, a symmetric tetrahexylphosphonium ([P6666]+) analog forms only a single cation‐CO2 adduct, highlighting that multiple‐site reactivity arises from chemical differentiation of α‐positions rather than asymmetry alone. Surprisingly, ammonium and guanidinium‐based AHA ILs previously considered completely inert toward carboxylation also exhibit weak but reproducible cation‐CO2 signals, detected only under undiluted, isotopically enriched conditions. These findings expand the mechanistic understanding of AHA IL‐CO2 chemistry and demonstrate that cation structure, particularly asymmetry and proton acidity, critically governs reactivity. The results provide a framework for designing ILs with tunable CO2 activation profiles for selective capture and release.Keywords: aprotic N‐heterocyclic anions, density functional calculations, ionic liquids, NMR spectroscopy, reaction mechanisms

Introduction

Aprotic N‐heterocyclic anions (AHAs), or azolides, are a class of carbon dioxide (CO2)‐reactive species derived from aromatic nitrogen‐containing heterocycles, offering tunable basicity [1–4], high thermal stability [5], and broad cation compatibility [5–7]. These properties render AHA‐based ionic liquids (ILs) uniquely suited for chemical CO2 capture applications. Unlike aqueous amines, these ILs exhibit reversible, stoichiometric CO2 absorption, enabling defined reaction mechanisms and facile regeneration [1–3]. Their modular design allows independent tuning of thermodynamic and transport properties, facilitating systematic exploration of structure–function relationships. Since our initial development of this class of ILs [8], we have demonstrated reversible, stoichiometric CO2 absorption with a variety of functionalized and nonfunctionalized triazolides, pyrazolides, pyrrolides, imidazolides, and tetrazolides [1, 2, 9–12]. They present a significant advantage over amino acid‐based ILs [8] and ILs with tethered amines [13, 14] because they exhibit little or no viscosity increase upon complexation with CO2. AHA ILs show potential for applications from postcombustion CO2 capture to direct air capture of CO2 [1, 2, 7, 9, 11, 12, 15–36]. Among IL architectures, asymmetric phosphonium cations paired with AHAs have shown particular promise due to their low viscosities and high thermal stabilities, making them ideal candidates for mechanistic CO2 studies [5].

Previously, we established that CO2 reacts with AHA ILs via two primary pathways: (1) direct nucleophilic addition of the anion to CO2, forming a carbamate (AHA‐CO2) [1, 37], and (2) a cation‐mediated pathway in which the AHA deprotonates an α‐carbon on the cation, generating a carbanion (i.e., ylide) that reacts with CO2 to yield a carboxylate (cation‐CO2) [2, 38]. This latter route is cation‐structure dependent, requiring labile α‐protons. A third, hydrolysis‐driven pathway yields bicarbonate when water is present, as the AHA abstracts a proton from water to form hydroxide anion, which rapidly reacts with CO2 [2, 9]. Figure 1 shows the three reaction pathways that are known to occur. Together, these mechanisms define the rich chemistry of CO2 capture in AHA ILs and underscore the need for detailed speciation analysis under well‐controlled, solvent‐free conditions.

Parallel competing reactions of phosphonium AHA ILs with CO2.

Previous efforts [2, 4, 17, 19, 25–28, 30, 31, 33–36, 38, 39] to characterize CO2 reaction products in AHA ILs have primarily employed conventional nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy using naturally abundant 12CO2 and diluted IL samples in deuterated solvents such as dimethyl sulfoxide‐d 6 (DMSO‐d 6). While accessible to most researchers, this approach often suppresses or broadens minor peaks due to dilution and solvent interactions, masking low concentration and transient species. However, quite recently, Philippi and Costa Gomes [40] investigated methyltrioctylphosphonium 1,2,4‐triazolide ([P8881][triaz]) pressurized with CO2 to 10 bar and reported two new peaks in the 31P NMR spectrum. While acknowledging that their NMR measurements were “semi‐quantitative” and providing no additional evidence, they attributed the peaks in the 31P NMR spectrum to “zwitterionic species formed on the octyl and methyl side chains of the phosphonium cation, respectively.” They used an undiluted IL sample contacted with naturally abundant CO2 and a sealed capillary of DMSO‐d 6 inserted in the NMR tube. Philippi and Costa Gomes [40] also reported DFT‐computed proton transfer energies for simplified model cations ([P1111]+ and [P1112]+) to construct a relative basicity scale rationalizing the competition between cation and anion reactivity.

Despite recent progress, there remains no direct, unambiguous experimental evidence quantifying the amounts of the various possible cation‐CO2 adducts in AHA ILs under solvent‐free conditions. The subtle interplay between cation structure, proton acidity, and anion basicity has not been systematically investigated. To address this gap, the present study combines in situ NMR (iNMR) analysis with isotopically enriched CO2 and complementary density functional theory (DFT) calculations to elucidate the mechanistic basis of cation participation in CO2 activation.

Detailed synthesis and purification protocols along with their NMR spectra can be found in Section S1. NMR spectra (1H, 13C, and 31P) were obtained with (CD3)2SO (i.e., dmso‐d 6) using a Bruker 3M 500 MHz instrument to confirm the structure and purity of all AHA ILs. Neat samples were analyzed under a 15 psi helium gas atmosphere. The water content of each IL was measured using a Metrohm 917 Karl Fischer Coulometer prior to experiments and was below 2000 ppm for every IL (Section S2).

Instrumentation and NMR Technique

To definitively investigate the possibility of different cation‐CO2 adduct formations, we designed a custom iNMR setup to capture undistorted quantitative chemical speciation in IL‐CO2 systems. The configuration centers on a high‐pressure rated NMR tube equipped with a Teflon valve (Norell, Inc.; S‐3‐500‐EX1‐HPV‐7), allowing the IL sample to be evacuated and then saturated with 13C‐labeled CO2 gas (ALDRICH; product# 364592). CO2 gas was introduced into the high‐pressure NMR tube through a stainless‐steel manifold equipped with Swagelok fittings and needle valves, allowing controlled evacuation and backfilling of the system (Figure S42). The IL sample was first degassed under vacuum and then exposed to 13CO2 through the manifold until the desired pressure was reached. The sealed tube was maintained under static conditions during saturation. All experiments involving pressurized NMR tubes were conducted using manufacturer‐rated glassware with appropriate shielding and handling precautions to ensure safe operation, even though the working pressure remained close to ambient. Saturation was carried out in a temperature‐controlled sonicator bath (VWR International; 97043‐932) using deionized (DI) water, where sonication accelerates CO2 absorption [41]. After equilibration at a pressure of approx. 1 bar for 14 days, the valve is sealed and the tube is inserted into a standard outer NMR tube containing DMSO‐d 6, which serves solely for field locking and shimming. The spectra were acquired at 298.15 K. The saturation period of 14 days was selected based on continuous pressure monitoring, where the internal pressure stabilized and exhibited only minor fluctuations within ±0.2 psi, indicating that gas uptake had reached a steady state. This behavior was reproducible across all IL systems. As in the system used by Philippi and Costa Gomes [40], the IL sample remains chemically isolated from the deuterated solvent, eliminating dilution artifacts while preserving spectral resolution. This arrangement enables direct, high‐sensitivity, quantitative detection of CO2‐derived species within the IL matrix, offering a more accurate representation of reaction intermediates and products than previously possible. Beyond CO2 capture, the approach could be extended to probe transient or low‐concentration intermediates relevant to electrocatalysis, pharmaceutical transformations, and reactive separations.

To independently verify CO2 saturation and equilibrium conditions, ILs were also equilibrated using a volumetric pressure‐decay apparatus [11] at 333.15 K and 1 bar 12CO2 under continuous stirring. In these experiments, pressure stabilization was achieved within 24–36 h, and systems were maintained for an additional 24 h to ensure equilibrium. Subsequent 31P NMR analysis of these samples showed consistent carboxylate speciation with the iNMR measurements within the NMR integration uncertainty of 0.01. A similar validation was also performed for [P2228][2‐CNPyr] at 298.15 K and 1 bar 12CO2 pressure to confirm equilibrium and reproducibility even at a lower temperature. Detailed spectra and quantitative comparisons are provided in Section S3. Furthermore, to assess any potential redistribution of species upon temperature change, additional experiments were conducted for [P2228][2‐CNPyr] and [P2228][3‐CH3‐5‐CF3Pyra] in which spectra were acquired at 333.15 K following saturation at 333.15 K, as well as after holding the samples at 298.15 K for 1 week prior to reanalysis. Supporting spectra and quantitative analysis are provided in Section S3.

Results and Discussion

To establish a quantitative picture of CO2 speciation in AHA ILs and clarify the influence of cation structure on equilibrium composition, systematic iNMR measurements were conducted under controlled saturation conditions. We began our investigation with a widely studied asymmetric triethyloctylphosphonium cation ([P2228]+), paired with three structurally distinct AHAs, namely, 2‐cyanopyrrolide ([2‐CNPyr]–), 3‐trifluoromethylpyrazolide ([3‐CF3Pyra]–), and 3‐methyl‐5‐trifluoromethylpyrazolide ([3‐CH3‐5‐CF3Pyra]–). These ILs were selected to probe how subtle variations in anion structure influence CO2 reactivity and speciation. Each IL was analyzed using the iNMR method at 298.15 and 333.15 K, enabling high‐resolution tracking of all CO2‐derived products within the neat IL phase using 1H, 13C, and 31P NMR spectroscopy. This approach allowed us to dissect the contributions of cation asymmetry and substituent‐driven electronic effects within the anion to the resulting reaction pathways.

31P NMR spectra for all three [P2228]+‐based AHA ILs are shown in Figure 2 for saturation at 333.15 K and ~1 bar 13CO2 (see Section S1 for full spectra). All samples displayed two upfield‐shifted (2.6–2.8 ppm relative to the dominant peak) phosphonium peaks. This is consistent with the observation by Philippi and Costa Gomes [40] for [P8881][triaz] exposed to (but not necessarily equilibrated with) CO2 at 343.15 K and 10 bar and reflects the formation of two electronically distinct carboxylated phosphonium species. However, we also observe two new peaks in the 31P NMR spectra for our three [P2228]+‐based AHA ILs when saturated with 13CO2 at ~1 bar and a lower temperature, 298.15 K (Figures S6, S15, and S24). Philippi and Costa Gomes [40] did not observe any cation carboxylate peaks in their 31P NMR spectrum for [P8881][triaz] contacted with CO2 at 10 bar and 303.15 K. This is likely due to insufficient contact time between the IL and the CO2. Based on our previous work [39], the amount of cation carboxylate formation for phosphonium ILs with [triaz]– anion should be even greater than the amount formed with [2‐CNPyr]– anion.

To further confirm that the observed speciation reflects equilibrium conditions rather than limitations in gas–liquid mass transfer, we performed an additional validation experiment using an independent volumetric pressure‐decay apparatus under identical conditions (333.15 K and ~1 bar 12CO2) with continuous stirring to enhance mass transfer. In these experiments, CO2 uptake was monitored as a function of time, and saturation was confirmed once no further pressure decay was observed over extended periods, indicating that equilibrium had been reached. To ensure complete equilibration, all systems were maintained under these conditions beyond the point of pressure stabilization. Following saturation, the IL samples were analyzed ex situ by 31P NMR spectroscopy. The values for all the 31P NMR spectra integrations match those obtained using the iNMR methodology within the integration uncertainty of 0.01 (see Table S3 in Section S3).

In addition, high‐temperature iNMR experiments were conducted to evaluate whether differences between saturation and acquisition temperatures influence speciation. The integration of peaks in the spectra for iNMR samples maintained at 333.15 K throughout equilibration and NMR spectra acquisition match those obtained for equilibration at 333.15 K and NMR spectra acquisition at 298.15 K within the NMR integration uncertainty of 0.01 (see Table S3 in Section S3).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the speciation reported here corresponds to equilibrium conditions. They are not subject to error based on insufficient contact time or mass transfer limitations. Likewise, the experimental procedure (i.e., equilibration at 333.15 K followed by acquisition of NMR spectra at 298.15 K within 2–3 h) does not produce any measurement errors or artifacts.

More importantly, in contrast to all previous studies that either show no carboxylate product or resolve only a single cation‐CO2 adduct [4, 9, 15, 38–40], our in situ 13C NMR spectra revealed two distinct carboxylate‐region peaks for all three [P2228]+‐based AHA ILs, as shown in Figure 3 for saturation at 333.15 K and 1 bar (see Section S1 for full spectra). This observation provides the first direct evidence for two distinct cation carboxylation species arising from the asymmetric alkyl environment of a phosphonium cation ([P2228]+ in our case). The nonequivalence of the α‐methylene groups adjacent to the phosphonium center appears to enable regioselective deprotonation, forming two distinct carbanions that each react with CO2. These two carboxylate peaks, observed at 165.7–167.0 ppm, were present at both temperatures studied (see Figures S5, S14, and S23 for spectra of AHA IL saturated at 298.15 K), suggesting that the multiple‐site carboxylate reactivity is intrinsic to the cation’s asymmetric structure. In addition, a peak near 145.5–146.4 ppm was consistently observed and assigned to the carbamate product formed via AHA‐CO2 reaction, further confirmed from previous studies [9, 38, 39]. A broader signal at 159.3–159.5 ppm was attributed to bicarbonate, arising from trace water (<2000 ppm) [9, 26, 31].

Several additional very low‐intensity peaks were observed in the carboxylate region of 13C (167.5–177.0 ppm) and in the upfield 31P (32.3–33.1 ppm) spectra following 13CO2 saturation, as shown in Figures 2 and 3, and more clearly in the full spectra in Figures S8, S9, S17, S18, S23, S24, S26, and S27. While currently unassigned, their correlation across nuclei suggests the formation of other minor or transient cation‐CO2 adducts, possibly arising from alternate conformers or less favorable deprotonation pathways. These species merit future investigation using 2D NMR or advanced computational modeling. Nonetheless, our iNMR setup enables unambiguous detection of all species directly in the neat IL, underscoring the influence of cation asymmetry on electronic distribution and CO2 binding pathways.

The influence of anion structure on cation‐CO2 adduct formation is best estimated from the integrated areas of the 31P NMR signals corresponding to ylide‐containing phosphonium species. We have previously found that carbamate formation (Reaction 1) is rapid, while carboxylate formation (Reaction 2) is kinetically controlled. Even with stirring, as much as 3 days was required for some AHA ILs to approach what appears to be an equilibrium distribution between carbamate and carboxylate [39]. Here, the 1H, 13C, and 31P NMR spectra were taken after 14 days of 13CO2 saturation, enhanced with sonication. Based on the information available, we anticipate that the concentrations of all species should be close to equilibrium. At 333.15 K, [P2228][2‐CNPyr] exhibited 16% conversion to the carboxylated form, while [P2228][3‐CF3Pyra] showed a moderately higher conversion of 22%. Notably, [P2228][3‐CH3‐5‐CF3Pyra] yielded the highest fraction of carboxylated species (40%), indicating enhanced carbanion formation. At 298.15 K the conversions to carboxylate were 2%, 4%, and 20%, respectively, for [P2228][2‐CNPyr], [P2228][3‐CF3Pyra], and [P2228][3‐CH3‐5‐CF3Pyra]. Notably, the reversal in carbamate‐to‐carboxylate ratios between [P2228][3‐CF3Pyra] and [P2228][3‐CH3‐5‐CF3Pyra] suggests that factors beyond intrinsic basicity influence CO2 uptake. While the methyl substituent is expected to increase electron density on the anion, its presence also introduces steric hindrance near the reactive site, which may impede efficient approach and stabilization of CO2 during carbamate formation. This observation highlights that anion structure governs not only thermodynamic driving force but also entropic accessibility, reinforcing the importance of steric effects in controlling CO2 absorption pathways in AHA ILs. These observations underscore the cooperative role of both cation and anion structure, whether through steric or electronic interactions, in governing the extent of carbanion formation and overall CO2 reactivity in AHA ILs.

To further investigate CO2 addition at both the ethyl and octyl α‐carbons of [P2228]+, we quantified the relative intensities of the two carboxylate signals in both 13C and 31P NMR spectra, as tabulated in Table 2. At 298.15 K, the fraction of the integrated intensity area of the minor peak averaged ~0.33 across all ILs and NMR modalities, indicating about 1/3rd formation of one cation‐CO2 adduct and 2/3rd formation of the other. At 333.15 K, these fractions shifted to ~0.09, suggesting a thermodynamic preference for one of the two carboxylate binding orientations. The consistency between 13C and 31P data across temperatures affirms that the observed carboxylate pairs correspond to chemically distinct adducts, with their relative populations reflecting thermally modulated site selectivity. Interestingly, despite the large variation in total carboxylate formation among the three ILs, which ranged from 16% to 40% depending on the AHA structure, the relative distribution of the two adducts remained remarkably consistent across temperature and IL composition. This invariance implies that while anion identity governs the overall extent of ylide formation, the intrinsic asymmetry of the [P2228]+ cation dictates the positional selectivity of CO2 addition with high fidelity.

To probe the origin of multiple distinct carboxylate cation‐CO2 adducts, we synthesized a structurally analogous IL featuring the symmetric tetrahexylphosphonium ([P6666]+) cation paired with [2‐CNPyr]–. Following 13CO2 saturation at 333.15 K for 2 weeks, both 13C and 31P spectra revealed only a single carboxylate signal, consistent with the formation of a single cation‐CO2 adduct, as shown in Figure 4 (see Figures S32 and S33 for full spectra). While this observation is consistent with the equivalence of α‐sites in [P6666]+, it also points to a broader principle that the appearance of multiple carboxylate signals depends on the extent of chemical differentiation between reactive α‐positions rather than symmetry alone. In this context, previous reports [38, 39] on longer‐chain tetraalkylphosphonium systems such as trihexyltetradecylphosphonium ([P66614]+) paired with [2‐CNPyr]– and [3‐CF3Pyra]– anions show a single carboxylate signal despite formal asymmetry, when saturated with naturally abundant CO2 at 333.15 K up to 4 bar CO2 pressure. These results suggest that increasing alkyl chain length might reduce chemical nonequivalence between reactive sites. This behavior may arise from steric shielding and/or electronic homogenization of the α‐environments, leading to effectively indistinguishable cation‐CO2 adducts. Together, these observations indicate that both structural asymmetry and the local chemical environment of α‐carbons govern whether distinct cation‐CO2 adducts are experimentally observable.

For asymmetric tetraalkylphosphonium cations with distinct chemically nonequivalent α sites (like [P2228]+ or [P8881]+), one might be tempted to assign the more intense carboxylate peak (in the 31P and/or 13C NMR spectra) to the more abundant chain. However, this may not be correct since the pKa values are likely different for the α‐protons on the different length chains. To investigate this further, DFT calculations were performed to estimate the chemical shifts in 13C and 31P NMR spectra of carboxylates attached to the α‐carbon of the ethyl chain and octyl chains of [P2228]+ in an aqueous liquid phase, as described in Section S4.2. The conclusion is that the more intense carboxylate peaks (the ones further upfield in the 31P NMR spectra of Figure 2 and the ones further downfield in the 13C NMR spectra of Figure 3) are, in fact, the result of a carboxylate attached to one of the ethyl chains.

In addition, we determined the pKa values of the ethyl and octyl α‐protons on [P2228]+ in both aqueous and DMSO environments, as described in Section S4.3. In aqueous medium, the ethyl α‐proton of the [P2228]+ cation has a pKa of 33.7, compared with 34.1 for the octyl chain. In DMSO, which more closely reflects the lower‐polarity environment of ILs, the ethyl α‐proton (pKa of 28.2) remains slightly more acidic than the octyl (pKa of 29.2). This indicates a slightly greater acidity and therefore a higher likelihood of deprotonation of the ethyl α‐proton. The agreement in relative trends across solvent models supports the assignment of preferential ylide formation at the ethyl position.

We further performed complementary DFT‐based thermochemical analysis of the cation‐CO2 reactions in the gas phase (ε = 1), as well as in models of the solution phase with dielectric constants (ε) of 5, 10, 20, 50, and 78.3553 (water). The influence of dielectric environment on AHA‐CO2 reactions has previously been examined for limited values of ε = 1, 12, and 78, representing gas phase, slightly polar, and aqueous conditions, respectively [42]. However, the effect of solvent polarization on cation‐CO2 reactivity remains unexplored to the best of our knowledge. These ε values were chosen because experimental studies have measured ε of some ILs (excluding hydroxy‐functionalized cations) to be in the range of 6–40 [43–46]. DFT calculations showed that the octyl α‐position forms a more exothermic (i.e., more thermodynamically stable) cation‐CO2 adduct, with an average enthalpy of reaction (ΔH) 7.2 kJ mol−1 lower than that of the ethyl site across all dielectric constants. However, deprotonating the α‐carbon of the octyl chain has a higher energy barrier than deprotonating the α‐carbon of one of the ethyl chains, which is consistent with its lower kinetic accessibility suggested by the pKa. A further important finding was the pronounced sensitivity of cation‐CO2 energetics to dielectric environment, as the cation‐CO2 reaction enthalpies became progressively less favorable with the increase in ε, with ΔH increasing by 47.2 kJ mol−1. On the other hand, anion‐CO2 reaction enthalpies remained nearly constant beyond ε = 5, with only a 2.2 kJ mol−1 change up to ε = 78. These results indicate that medium polarity plays a decisive role in stabilizing or destabilizing cation‐CO2 pathways, while exerting only a minor influence on anion‐CO2 chemistry. These results confirm that the asymmetric [P2228]+ framework supports dual cation‐CO2 reactivity that is robust across structural and environmental variations. Full computational methods, thermodynamic data, and extended analysis are provided in Section S4.

These results underscore that solvent polarization exerts a decisive influence on cation‐CO2 reactivity. The choice of dielectric environment in ab initio simulations can fundamentally alter mechanistic interpretations when modeling or designing ILs as reactive media or as additives in catalytic and electrochemical systems, where local polarity can vary significantly. The computation method used here offers a broadly applicable framework for understanding reactivity in these types of polar and ionic environments.

Previous studies [26, 30, 31, 35], including our own [38], reported no evidence of cation‐CO2 binding for alkyl substituted nitrogen‐based (except for imidazolium‐based) cations like 1‐butyl‐1‐methylpiperidinium ([PI14]+) and triethylheptylammonium ([N2227]+), using standard deuterated solvent‐diluted NMR techniques with naturally abundant CO2. These findings reinforced the long‐held belief that nitrogen‐centered cations do not participate in CO2 activation. However, this conclusion may reflect limitations in sensitivity and resolution, rather than complete intrinsic chemical inertness. Using the iNMR setup and 13C‐enriched CO2, we re‐examined this question with two structurally distinct nitrogen‐based cations, namely, triethylhexylammonium ([N2226]+) and 2,2‐dibutyl‐1,1,3,3‐tetramethylguanidin‐2‐ium ([B2TMG]+), both paired with [2‐CNPyr]–. After saturating both ILs at 333.15 K for 2 weeks, 13C NMR revealed weak but distinct carboxylate signals for both ILs, as shown in Figure 5. These peaks are not visible in the greatly expanded baseline of the pure IL spectra, which are shown in black in Figure 5. The peaks in the red 13CO2‐saturated spectra in Figure 5 align with the chemical shifts of the cation‐CO2 adducts observed in phosphonium systems, suggesting that nitrogen‐based cations can undergo limited carboxylation under controlled conditions. Though significantly less intense than the dominant carbamate signal, these findings challenge the assumption of complete inertness and point to a more nuanced role of cation structure in CO2 chemistry. The presence and intensity of these peaks might also strongly depend on the basicity of the AHA and the temperature of the reaction.

13C NMR spectra of (a) [N2226][2‐CNPyr] and (b) [B2TMG][2‐CNPyr] ILs before (—) and after ( — ) 13CO2 saturation at 333.15 K. The * marked peak is present in the neat IL as well and is suspected to be an extremely dilute impurity. Spectra acquired at 298.15 K.

Interestingly, both [N2226][2‐CNPyr] and [B2TMG][2‐CNPyr] displayed two carboxylate peaks, mirroring dual adduct formation in [P2228]+ and suggesting analogous CO2 reactivity at both alkyl chains, albeit at much lower concentrations than in [P2228]+ (Figure 5). Beyond acidity, the electronic distinction between phosphorus and nitrogen also contributes to the much smaller amounts of carboxylate formed in ammonium than phosphonium systems. Phosphorus can stabilize the ylide‐like intermediate via hypervalent bonding and access to d‐orbitals, while nitrogen is constrained by an octet configuration [47, 48]. This fundamental difference likely destabilizes nitrogen‐based carbanions, rendering the resulting adducts less favorable thermodynamically.

Supporting these observations, DFT‐predicted pKa values in DMSO revealed that the most acidic protons on [N2226]+ (ethyl, 38.1; hexyl, 39.1) and [B2TMG]+ (methyl, 39.8; butyl, 43.4) are significantly less acidic than the ethyl α‐proton on [P2228]+ (28.2), consistent with lower deprotonation propensities for ammoniums. The weak carboxylate signals observed for [N2226]+ and [B2TMG]+ reflect the lower acidity of their α‐protons than those of the ethyl chains on [P2228]+. Quantitatively, the intensity ratios of the carbamate to the most intense carboxylate peak in the 13C NMR spectra at 333.15 K were ~6 for [P2228][2‐CNPyr] and ~7 for [P6666][2‐CNPyr], whereas they were markedly higher for the nitrogen‐centered cations, with values of ~29 for [B2TMG][2‐CNPyr] and ~219 for [N2226][2‐CNPyr]. These values confirm that while nitrogen cations can form carboxylated species, their abundance is orders of magnitude lower than in phosphonium analogs. The significantly suppressed signal intensities suggest a lower thermodynamic driving force for ylide formation and formation of the resulting adducts in nitrogen‐centered ILs. Moreover, DFT‐derived activation energies follow the order [N2226]+ > [B2TMG]+ > [P2228]+. This means that the rate of formation of the carboxylates is slower for [N2226]+ and [B2TMG]+ than [P2228]+, which is also consistent with observed product yields (i.e., less carboxylates for the ammoniums than the phosphoniums). This suggests that the small amount of carboxylation observed in nitrogen‐centered IL cations may be due to both kinetic and thermodynamic limitations.

Conclusion

In summary, we report direct NMR evidence for the formation of distinct cation‐CO2 adducts in asymmetric phosphonium‐based AHA ILs, enabled by high‐resolution iNMR analysis with isotopically enriched CO2. The observed speciation and relative product distributions were verified to represent equilibrium compositions under the applied saturation conditions, ensuring that the reported trends reflect intrinsic reactivity rather than kinetic artifacts. Consistent 13C and 31P spectral patterns across temperature and IL structures confirm that chemical nonequivalence within the [P2228]+ cation supports site‐selective CO2 binding. Observation of only one peak related to carboxylate formation in the 13C and 31P NMR spectra for a symmetric [P6666]+ analog supports the role of chemically distinct α‐environments in enabling multiple adduct formation. DFT‐predicted chemical shifts confirm that the more intense carboxylate peaks are associated with CO2 addition to the ethyl chain. DFT‐predicted acidity values and thermochemistry analysis reinforce the ability of CO2 to react at electronically distinct sites of asymmetric phosphonium cations. Application of the same methodology to nitrogen‐based cations reveals previously undetected, low‐abundance carboxylate formation, revising long‐standing assumptions of chemical inertness. These findings deepen our understanding of IL‐CO2 chemistry and offer a new framework for designing functionalized ILs with tunable CO2 activation profiles. More broadly, the integrated iNMR and DFT approach presented here establishes a broadly applicable platform for mechanistic elucidation in diverse ionic and molecular reaction systems, enabling direct observation of reactive species that typically evade detection in conventional spectroscopic setups.
