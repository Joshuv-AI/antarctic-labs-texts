Factors associated with inequalities in malnutrition among children in Ghana using the 2022 GDHS and WHO HEAT framework
Authors: Eugene Budu, Ebenezer Kwesi Armah-Ansah, Nhyira Owusuaa Gyawu, Rabbi Tweneboah, Kwamena Sekyi-Dickson, Charity Oga-Omenka, Elom Hillary Otchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildhood malnutrition can impair physical and cognitive development, thereby diminishing productivity at adulthood. Current research recognizes the crucial impact of early childhood nutrition on the long-term health and well-being of children. Ghana has achieved notable progress in lowering childhood malnutrition; nevertheless, it remains a major concern. This study advances current understanding on the socioeconomic drivers and magnitude of child malnutrition in the Ghanaian context.MethodsWe used data from 2022 Ghana Demographic and Health Survey to analyze inequalities in malnutrition among children This study was an analytical cross-sectional survey that covered a total weighted sample of 4,935 women aged 15–49. Simple measures of inequality [difference (D), and Ratio (R)], and complex measures of inequality [Population Attributable risk (PAR), and Population Attributable Fraction (PAF)] were performed using the World Health Organization’s Health Equity Assessment Toolkit (WHO’s HEAT) software. We observed age (D = 7.2, UI = 4.0– 10.4, PAF=-67.7, UI=-67.9– -67.4), maternal education (R = 1.8, UI = 1.5–2.1; PAF=-18.8, UI=-18.8–-18.7), place of residence (R = 1.3, UI = 1.1–1.6; PAF=-13.4, UI=-13.4–-13.3), and subnational region (D = 19.1, UI = 13.5–24.8; PAR=-6.9, UI=-10.1–-3.8).ConclusionThis study revealed the existence of inequalities in malnutrition among children in Ghana, which is influenced by child’s age, maternal economic status, maternal education, place of residence, and subnational region. To tackle the issues of child malnutrition in Ghana, it is vital to implement targeted interventions that address age-related disparities and implement social protection programs. Subnational region disparities must be addressed by directing resources to areas vulnerable to malnutrition, such as Northern Ghana, adapting interventions to local needs, and improving infrastructure to facilitate better healthcare and nutrition access in rural communities.Keywords: Children, Ghana, Inequalities, Malnutrition, Stunting, Wasting, Underweight, Overweight

Introduction

Childhood malnutrition can impair physical and cognitive development, thereby diminishing adult productivity [1]. Current research recognizes the crucial impact of early childhood nutrition on the long-term health and well-being of children [2]. People who eats healthy diet tend to be more productive, which may help them break the cycle of poverty in the long run [3]. Conversely, poor nutritional intake may hinder children from reaching their full potential, impairing their physical growth, cognitive development, and academic performance [4]. It also increases their susceptibility to illness, reduces physical capabilities, and contributes to higher risks of morbidity and mortality [5, 6].

While child malnutrition remains a global challenge, it disproportionately affects low- and middle-income countries—where strained public health systems struggle to address the crisis [7, 8]. A 2022 UNICEF report underscores this disparity, revealing that Asia and Africa bear the overwhelming burden of childhood stunting, accounting for nearly 95% of global cases (76.6 million in Asia, 63.1 million in Africa, 5.7 million in Latin America and the Caribbean, 1.4 million in Europe, and 0.7 million in Oceania). Even more alarming is that Sub-Saharan Africa alone contributes approximately 94.7% of Africa’s share, highlighting a severe regional concentration of this public health emergency [9]. Although child survival rates in these regions have shown improvement, sustained developmental progress remains constrained, impeding long-term prosperity [10–12].

Prior studies have underpinned a slew of predictors of child malnutrition, including the role of child age, sex, and birth size [10–13]. Moreover, studies on child malnutrition consistently demonstrate a relationship between household wealth and childhood nutritional status. This relationship is typically measured by increases in household material standards, which are then converted into a wealth index [13–15]. It is argued that children from the wealthiest households are less likely than those from the poorest to be underweight or stunted [8]. Higher rates of stunting and underweight have been associated with children who reside in rural areas than those in urban areas [16, 17]. Also, the level of a mother’s educational attainment is consistently associated with child malnutrition [18–20]. One additional structural factor continuously highlighted as a contributing factor of child malnutrition (particularly in LMICs), is the inaccessibility of healthcare and public health interventions. Research indicates that children from households having difficulties with accessing healthcare services experience significantly elevated rates of childhood malnutrition [7, 13]. Against this backdrop, Ghana emerges as a notable case study in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Ghana stands out as one of the few Sub-Saharan African nations to have made significant progress in combating childhood malnutrition. Between 1993 and 2014, the country halved the prevalence of underweight and wasting among children-meeting the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) targets for these indicators. However, it fell short of its stunting reduction goals, as noted in Ghana’s 2015 MDG final report [21, 22]. Despite some advancements, malnutrition remains a persistent challenge, with pronounced geographic disparities [7]. This uneven burden continues to impede Ghana’s efforts to achieve Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) 2 and 3, underscoring the need for targeted interventions.

Although there have been studies that has measured the prevalence and the factors associated with inequalities in child malnutrition in various geographical areas in Ghana [7, 22, 23], few have quantified inequalities in malnutrition systematically across multiple dimensions using standardized WHO methods. Our study addresses this critical gap by providing a comprehensive analysis of malnutrition disparities along socioeconomic, geographic, and maternal education gradients. By employing rigorous, comparable metrics, we offer policymakers actionable insights to (i) prioritize interventions for high-burden subgroups; (ii) align resource allocation with the uneven distribution of malnutrition risks; and (iii) promote monitoring progress toward SDGs 2 and 3 with equity-sensitive benchmarks.

Methods

Study area

Ghana is a country located in West Africa, bordered to the north by Burkina Faso, to the east by Togo, to the west by Ivory Coast, and to the south by the Gulf of Guinea [24]. Its geographical coordinates are between 07°56′47.5″ N and 001°1.392′ W, covering an area of 238,533 km² [25]. The capital city, Accra, is situated in the Greater Accra region. Ghana is divided into sixteen regions with an average household member of 3.5 and an average fertility rate of 3.9 children. The majority (84%) of the household population has access to at least basic drinking water service, 24% have access to basic sanitation services, and about 61% of women and 74% of men age 15–49 been literate. In 2022, about 17% of children under age 5 were stunted, 6% percent were wasted, 12% were underweight, and 2% were overweight [26].

Study design and source

The study used data from the 2022 GDHS. The GDHS is a nationwide survey that aims to identify consistent trends and changes in demographic indicators, health indicators, and social issues among individuals of all sex and age groups. The GDHS had a cross-sectional design in which participants were chosen using a stratified multi-stage cluster sampling method. A detailed description of the sampling methodology can be found in the GDHS report [26]. This study was an analytical cross-sectional survey that covered a total weighted sample of 4,935 women aged 15–49 in the corresponding GDHS cycles. The 2022 GDHS data was available for direct use using the WHO Health Equality Assessment Toolkit (HEAT) online platform [27]. This study was meticulously crafted, considering the parameters specified in the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [28].

Variables

The dependent variable for the study was malnutrition among children. Malnutrition refers to deficiencies, excesses, or imbalances in a person’s intake of energy and/or nutrients [29]. The study focused on the two main classifications of malnutrition: undernutrition and overweight. Whereas overweight was measured directly, undernutrition was assessed using three key indicators, namely, severe wasting, stunting, and underweight. Each of the malnutrition indicator was treated as a binary variable (Yes/No), where “Yes” indicated the presence of the condition.

Classification was based on WHO Child Growth Standards (WHO-CGS). A child was considered overweight if their weight-for-height exceeded the median by more than two standard deviations (+ 2 SD). Severe wasting was identified if weight-for-height fell below the median by more than three standard deviations (− 3 SD). Stunting was defined as height-for-age below − 2 SD from the median, while underweight referred to weight-for-age below − 2 SD.

The assessment of inequality in malnutrition among children utilized six stratifiers. These stratifiers were selected based on their association with the malnutrition indicators [30, 31] and also their presence in the WHO HEAT software. The stratifiers includes child’s age (0–5 months, 6–11 months, 12–23 months, 24–35 months, 36–47 months, and 48–59 months); economic status of the mother measured by wealth quintile (quintile 1, 2, 3, 4, 5); level of education of the mother (no education, primary education, and secondary/higher education); place of residence of the mother (rural and urban), sex of child (male and female); and sub-national region (Ahafo, Bono, Bono East, Central, Eastern, Greater Accra, North East, Northern, Oti, Savannah, Upper East, Upper West, Volta, Western, and Western North).

Data analysis

The analysis was performed utilizing the online version of the WHO HEAT software [27]. Six equity stratifiers were employed to examine malnutrition among children in Ghana. The factors encompassed are age, economic status, level of education, place of residence, sex of child, and sub-national region. Estimates and confidence intervals (CIs) were computed to assess malnutrition among children, considering the specified stratifiers. Four metrics were utilized to calculate inequality: Difference (D), Population Attributable Risk (PAR), Population Attributable Fraction (PAF), and Ratio (R). Two simple measurements, D and R, are not affected by weight, while two more complex measures, PAR and PAF, are influenced by weight. R and PAF are relative metrics. However, D and PAR are absolute measures. Considering summary measures is grounded in the WHO’s recognition that absolute and relative summary measurements are essential for deriving policy-driven findings. In contrast to simple measurements, complex measures consider the magnitude of categories within a specific population subset. The literature has comprehensively elucidated the World Health Organization’s summary metrics and calculations [32, 33].

Discussion

Ghana has made considerable strides in improving nutritional intake and mitigating the prevalence of undernutrition, along with its numerous adverse health implications, particularly among children. These commitments are evident in recent initiatives designed to alleviate poverty and improve nutrition, including the National Nutrition Policy, the Ghana School Feeding Programme, and the Nutrition-Friendly Schools Initiative (NFSI). However, beneath this narrative of progress lies a troubling issue: malnutrition remains a deeply entrenched issue, marred by stark inequalities. Several recent studies and reports [7, 34] have asserted that a large disparity exists among the prevalence of child malnutrition spatially and amongst marginalized groups. This study adds to the recent discussion of child malnutrition by presenting up-to-date evidence on child inequality in nutritional intake in Ghana.

Our thorough examination of the nationally representative DHS dataset revealed significant disparities in undernutrition and overweight outcomes among children under five, along axes of age, wealth, maternal education, and geography. Specifically, children aged 0–5 months exhibited the highest prevalence of overweight, with an absolute inequality (difference between most-advantaged and most-disadvantaged age groups) of 7.2% points and a relative inequality of 12.3% points compared to infants aged 48–59 months. Similar findings have been reported by [35, 36]. The most cited reason for this finding is childrens’ increased physical inactivity/sedentary behaviour at a tender age. Another plausible explanation for this finding is increased maternal inflammation during pregnancy [37], which increases the likelihood of infant obesity. Conversely, when employing complex inequality measures that account for all age subgroups, older children also faced disproportionately higher risks of stunting, wasting, and underweight. Similar findings have been reported by [38]. Consistent with the findings from [39], the wealth-related analysis revealed negligible disparities in overweight prevalence among under 5 year children. Nonetheless, significant disparities exist in the prevalence of underweight, wasting, and stunting, with children from impoverished households facing far higher risks than those from affluent ones. Maternal education-related inequality accounted for 18.8% of all stunting and 15.5% of all underweight, indicating that children of less-educated mothers bear a larger burden of these forms of undernutrition. This supports the UNICEF conceptual framework of malnutrition, which posits that caregiver knowledge and practices-often channeled through formal education-critically influence feeding behaviors and care-seeking [40]. Similar findings are reported by [41] but largely differ from the findings from [42], who documented that inequality in severe wasting is driven partly by maternal education in Ethiopia. Interestingly, across all indices of inequality measures, we found no differences in the prevalence of malnutrition across the sex of the child. Our findings substantially differ from prior findings from [43].

The regional pattern of malnutrition indicated ongoing clustering in the Northern, North East, and Savannah regions; transitioning from the Northern to the Eastern zone could decrease stunting in children under five by approximately 19.4%. Taken together, these results underscore that, despite national efforts to improve overall child nutrition, substantial subpopulation-specific and region‐specific inequities persist in Ghana. This finding closely aligns with the findings from several related studies that applied other geospatial and econometric modeling approaches to map inequality in Ghana [44]. For instance [44], applied Bayesian spatial interpolation to the 2014 Ghana DHS, demonstrating that Northern part accounts for the highest prevalence and burden of stunting growth in children. In contrast, several other studies [1, 45–47] utilized an econometric decomposition or the multilevel approach to highlighted the role of infrastructure, economic change, socioeconomic, and spatial characteristics, our updated analysis confirms that structural drivers of inequity-particularly in northern Ghana-continue to exert a strong influence.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Estimating inequalities can be a challenging process; nonetheless, utilizing the WHO HEAT software serves as a suitable tool for accurate estimation. The DHS employed a two-stage sampling approach, which guarantees that the data is representative at both regional and national scales. Therefore, we extend the results to encompass the broader population of children in Ghana. The results of this study can play a crucial role in shaping policies and guiding future investigations into malnutrition in Ghana. The combination of simple and complex inequality measures strengthens our findings by offsetting individual methodological limitations. Furthermore, by detailing the results for each subgroup of the equity stratifiers, we offer a reference point for the government to pinpoint areas that require greater focus amidst resource constraints.

Nonetheless, there are some limitations that need to be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design of the DHS precludes any understanding on trends in observed associations between wealth, education, geography, and malnutrition may be confounded by unmeasured factors—such as household food security scales, seasonal food availability, or caregiver feeding beliefs. Second, the HEAT tool is limited to univariate and stratified inequality diagnostics (e.g., concentration indices, D, R, Population Attributable Fraction), but does not permit simultaneous multivariate adjustment. Consequently, we could not disentangle overlapping influences of wealth, maternal education, and urban–rural residence in a single regression framework. Future analyses should employ multilevel or decomposition methods (e.g., Wagstaff decomposition or Blinder–Oaxaca) to quantify the independent contribution of each determinant. Third, our study did not incorporate several important drivers of malnutrition in children that could have yielded deeper insights on the intricacies of spatial variations in malnutrition status of children. For instance, several environmental variables (e.g., rainfall anomalies, soil fertility, or crop yields) may help explain the persistent malnutrition in the northern areas of Ghana. We suggest and promote the idea that future studies continue to incorporate DHS data with high-resolution geospatial layers to further understanding on ecological and environmental determinants of child malnutrition. Similarly, some important sociocultural factors such as religion and ethnicity that may affect malnutrition were not captured in this analysis. Future studies should examine the inequality patterns through these additional dimensions including religion and ethnicity.

Conclusion

This study revealed the existence of inequalities in malnutrition among children in Ghana, which is influenced by child’s age, maternal economic status, maternal education, place of residence, and subnational region. To tackle the issues of child malnutrition in Ghana, it is vital to implement targeted interventions that address age-related disparities and implement social protection programs. Specific child health promoting interventions that could be adopted are as follows:

First, the age-specific overweight inequality prompts that growth monitoring at pediatric and immunization clinics should be augmented with targeted counseling on age-appropriate complementary feeding and early physical activities. The Ghana Health Service may integrate “healthy growth child” modules closely inline with SDG goals into routine well-child-visits, educating caregivers of particularly children aged 12-24months old on preparing nutrient-dense local food. Also, resources should be channeled to the development of structured “Active Play” programs (such as every community child playground development project) to reduce sedentary behaviors amongst children nearing age 5. Second, the regional undernutrition spatial hotspot cluster results revealed in the study hints on the increased need for geographically and culturally tailored interventions. This could include scaling up an already somewhat successful unconditional cash transfer (such as the Livelihood Empowerment Against Poverty [LEAP]) programs. These “cash + care” programs coupled with smallholder farming subsidies for drought-resistant crops, particularly in hotspot zones-could mitigate staggering food insecurity and poverty-driven inequalities. Promoting public health sensitization programs such as mass screening and community management of acute malnutrition in children along the Northern, North East, and Savannah regions would facilitate real-time identification of malnutrition clusters, enhancing efficient resource management (including supplies and healthcare professionals). Lastly, findings on wealth-and-education-related malnutrition provide subjective evidence of the need to implement and intensify literacy and nutritional training models in high-inequality Ghanaian districts, such as parts of the Savannah and Upper West-could yield similar gains. At large, the findings project the need for effective multisectoral coordination among the Ministry of Health, Ministry of Education, and Ministry of Gender, Children and Social Protection to design and implement integrated programs to promote equity, enhance child nutritional uptake, and ensuring that no child is left behind.
