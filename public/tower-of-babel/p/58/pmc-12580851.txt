Sustained remission with eculizumab in refractory lupus nephritis with atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome
Authors: Brady Thomson, Kathleen Nicholls
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryLupus nephritis (LN) is a severe manifestation of systemic lupus erythematosus with approximately 50% of patients having renal involvement. Patients who fail to respond to appropriate induction immunosuppression are considered to have refractory disease. Refractory LN is associated with increased risk of progression to end-stage chronic kidney disease and mortality. Despite the development of modern therapeutics, refractory LN remains challenging in clinical practice and continues to be associated with treatment failure. Complement has a well-described role in the pathogenesis of LN; however, complement inhibition is not routinely used for refractory disease. We present the case of a woman in her 30s with refractory LN who presented with atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome, macrophage activation syndrome and low-severity COVID-19 infection. She was treated with eculizumab with rapid clinical and biochemical improvement. The patient achieved complete remission of her refractory LN after eculizumab and remains in remission 2 years post eculizumab.Keywords: Systemic lupus erythematosus, Proteinurea, Chronic renal failure, Immunology, Biological agents

Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a multisystem autoimmune disease characterised by loss of self-tolerance, production of autoantibodies and immune complex activation of complement.1 Lupus nephritis (LN) is characterised by renal deposition of immune complexes, resulting in renal injury.2 Activation of the complement pathway by these immune complexes can result in atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome, defined by thrombocytopaenia, microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia and microvascular thrombi, causing systemic organ damage, including acute kidney injury.1 Patients with LN who develop atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome have poor outcomes with conventional immunosuppression and thus the use of complement blockade with eculizumab has been described.3 There remain limited studies investigating the response to eculizumab in LN that is refractory to conventional immunosuppressive agents. We report a case whereby eculizumab achieved sustained remission for refractory LN when it was used to treat concomitant atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome in the setting of macrophage activation syndrome and COVID-19 infection. The refractory LN of the patient remains in remission 2 years later with low-dose prednisolone and hydroxychloroquine without any additional immunosuppressive agents.

Case presentation

A woman in her 30s presented with a 2-month history of progressive exertional dyspnoea and fatigue accompanied by drenching night sweats and widespread arthralgias. Two weeks prior to her presentation, she was experiencing severe dyspnoea at rest with intermittent pleuritic chest pain, without any associated cough, palpitations, orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea or haemoptysis. In the days prior to her presentation, she also experienced subjective fevers and rigors due to developing macrophage activation syndrome.

Medical history was significant for SLE diagnosed at age 13 years after presenting with polyarthralgia, alopecia and autoimmune haemolytic anaemia. Other manifestations of SLE include pulmonary arterial hypertension, which resolved with rituximab at age 19 years, and refractory LN class IV. The patient has no obstetric history. Despite multiple immunosuppressive treatments, no long-term sustained remission of her LN was achieved. The patient had proteinuria since diagnosis at age 12 years, apart from a transient resolution of proteinuria with rituximab at age 25 years. On examination, she was normotensive, euvolaemic and afebrile with normal vesicular breath sounds. There were no cardiac murmurs, aphthous ulcers, rashes, lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly.

Investigations

Laboratory tests demonstrated microcytic anaemia (haemoglobin 77 g/L), leucopenia (white blood cells 2.4×109/L and neutrophils 2×109/L) with raised C-reactive protein (21.9 mg/L) and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (50 mm/h). Blood film demonstrated anisocytosis, no schistocytes and neutrophils with mild toxic changes. Creatinine was at elevated (133 µmol/L) and serum albumin was low (13 g/L), with otherwise normal liver function tests. Antinuclear antibodies were positive (1:160) with a speckled pattern, serum C3 levels were low (0.56 g/L), while serum C4 levels (0.22 g/L) and anti-double stranded DNA antibodies (<5 IU/mL) were normal. Of note, ferritin was elevated (17,399 µg/L), as were lactate dehydrogenase (1470 U/L) and triglycerides (9.1 mmol/L). CT pulmonary angiogram demonstrated no pulmonary embolism, no pulmonary infiltrates and no pleural or pericardial effusions. A transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated normal ventricular function, with no evidence of pericardial effusion or pulmonary hypertension. COVID-19 nucleic acid amplification test was positive.

The diagnosis of haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH) was considered; however, the patient did not meet the required diagnostic criteria. Despite hyperferritinaemia, hypertriglyceridaemia and elevated soluble CD25, fibrinogen was normal and organomegaly was absent. Peripheral flow cytometry revealed normal lymphoid populations with 7% natural killer cell activity. Bone marrow aspirate found no evidence of underlying haematological malignancy and the minimal haemophagocytosis was attributed to SLE and COVID-19 infection.

Given the patient did not meet the diagnostic criteria for HLH according to the HLH-2004 criteria and the history and presentation of the patient were consistent with active SLE, macrophage activation syndrome (MAS) was the consensus diagnosis from treating clinicians. The patient had no rash or evidence of end-organ thrombotic injury at this time. She was treated with 3 days of intravenous 1 g methylprednisolone, then continued on 50 mg (1 mg/kg) prednisolone daily. Hyperferritinaemia and hypertriglyceridaemia were both steroid-responsive, further supporting a diagnosis of MAS.

While hyperferritinaemia and hypertriglyceridaemia initially improved, renal function of the patient continued to deteriorate to the need for dialysis. On day 23 of admission, worsening anaemia (67 g/L) and thrombocytopenia (87×109/L), combined with an elevated lactate dehydrogenase (1710 U/L) and new schistocytes on peripheral blood film, raised suspicion of atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome. Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura was excluded with normal ADAMTS13 activity (31.9%). The patient did not undergo a renal biopsy due to anaemia and thrombocytopenia.

Treatment

On day 25 of admission, eculizumab was commenced for atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome. In total, 4 weekly doses of intravenous eculizumab 900 mg were administered. Mycophenolic acid 720 mg two times a day and hydroxychloroquine 200 mg once a day were continued with a slow wean of prednisolone to 7.5 mg once a day. Outcome and follow-up

Nine months post completion of eculizumab, this treatment response was sustained, with significant improvement in creatinine (56 µmol/L), and most notably a resolution of nephrotic range proteinuria (UPCR<30 mg/mmol). haemoglobin (117 g/L) and platelets (411×109 /L) remain normalised, as well as ferritin (42 ug/L), anti-double stranded DNA antibodies (<5 IU/mL), serum C3 levels (0.94 g/L) and serum C4 levels (0.22 g/L). CH-50 was not measured during the treatment course of the patient. Given the patient had been on mycophenolic acid 720 mg two times a day for multiple years prior to her presentation as well as intermittent high-dose corticosteroids throughout her lifetime and never achieved remission of her LN, we propose eculizumab is the primary agent responsible for inducing remission.

Twelve months post completion of eculizumab, the patient presented with new macroscopic haematuria and supra-pubic discomfort. A renal tract ultrasound found an anterior wall bladder mass suspicious for malignancy, presumably secondary to previous cyclophosphamide exposure. Histopathology confirmed urothelial carcinoma with small cell, sarcomatoid and micropapillary variants with extravesical tumour spread without evidence of distal metastases. Mycophenolic acid and hydroxychloroquine were withheld during neoadjuvant chemotherapy with etoposide and carboplatin. She subsequently underwent cystectomy, hysterectomy, pelvic node dissection and formation of ileal conduit and remains under close surveillance.

Two years post eculizumab, LN in the patient remains in sustained remission with stable creatinine (43 µmol/L), low grade proteinuria (UPCR 33 mg/mmol) with normal serum C3, C4 and normal anti-double stranded DNA antibodies (<5 IU/mL). The patient was continued on prednisolone 7.5 mg daily throughout her cancer treatment and recommenced on hydroxychloroquine 200 mg once a day, candesartan 8 mg once a day and spironolactone 25 mg once a day 1 year post completion of chemotherapy. Notably, she has maintained remission of her LN without reintroduction of mycophenolic acid.

Discussion

There are no reported cases we are aware of where eculizumab achieved sustained remission at 2 years for refractory LN. Moreover, there are no previously described cases of concomitant atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome, macrophage activation syndrome and COVID-19 infection.

The exact pathogenesis of macrophage activation syndrome and atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome in SLE remains unclear.4 Macrophage activation syndrome, a hyperinflammatory state characterised by excessive activation of T-lymphocytes and macrophages exhibiting haemophagocytic activity, is often triggered by infective processes, including COVID-19 infection.4 Meanwhile, the presence of immune complexes in LN leads to pathological activation of the complement pathway, overactivation of the terminal membrane attack complex (C5b-9) and subsequent tissue injury.3 5

While significant literature has demonstrated the integral role of complement in the pathogenesis of LN, complement inhibition is not routinely used for refractory disease. Instead, an extensive range of other immunosuppressive agents is used, including corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine, rituximab, calcineurin inhibitors and cyclophosphamide.1 However, inducing clinical remission in LN remains challenging, with between 20% and 70% of cases refractory to conventional treatment.6 In the case presented, the patient had been treated with the aforementioned immunosuppressive agents plus abatacept, a soluble CTLA-4 analogue, without sustained treatment response. Her clinical manifestations reached the severity of patients who undergo anti-19 chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy for SLE and she was being considered for this treatment.7

Eculizumab is a humanised monoclonal antibody which binds to complement protein C5, inhibiting cleavage to C5a and C5b and subsequently blocking formation of the terminal membrane attack complex.8 Eculizumab has been well reported to be an efficacious treatment for thrombotic microangiopathy secondary to LN.3 Multiple studies have shown early intervention with eculizumab leads to improved clinical outcomes and reversal of organ damage in thrombotic microangiopathy.3

Limited literature exists to determine the role for eculizumab as adjunctive therapy in refractory LN. A systematic review by Wright et al found 93% of patients administered eculizumab for thrombotic microangiopathy secondary to LN had resolution of lupus symptoms and recovery of renal function. Eculizumab treatment was successfully stopped in 46% of patients with no relapse of disease in a median follow-up period of 7 months.3 Furthermore, a study to assess the membrane attack complex as a potential biomarker for disease severity in LN found increased C9 deposition in glomerular tissue correlated to more intense disease and treatment-refractory LN.9

While previous studies, including Wright et al have demonstrated that eculizumab successfully treats thrombotic microangiopathy secondary to LN, there are minimal cases where eculizumab also induced remission of previously refractory LN. The effect of eculizumab in this case is further supported by remission sustained at 2 years post eculizumab treatment without the recommencement of mycophenolic acid or other steroid-sparing treatments.

This rare case describes refractory LN with concomitant atypical haemolytic uraemic syndrome, macrophage activation syndrome and COVID-19 infection. It suggests that complement blockade with eculizumab may have a role in the treatment of patients with refractory LN. Additional studies are required to elucidate the role of eculizumab in the management of severe LN.
