Association of Dementia With Total Ankle Arthroplasty and Ankle Arthrodesis Outcomes: A Short Report
Authors: Devon Nixon, Brook I. Martin, Hyunkyu Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Foot and ankle surgeons evaluate older patients with ankle arthritis and dementia. In this report, we analyzed postoperative complications following ankle arthrodesis (AA) and total ankle arthroplasty (TAA), hypothesizing greater risk in patients with dementia.Methods:We conducted a retrospective cohort study using Medicare claims from 2016 to 2021, including beneficiaries aged ≥65 years who underwent TAA or AA. The primary independent variable was dementia, defined using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) dementia component based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes within 90 days before the index procedure. Outcomes included postoperative complications, all-cause readmissions, and mortality within 90 days and 12 months. Multivariable logistic regression models estimated adjusted differences in complication rates by dementia status.Results:Dementia was present in 0.56% of TAA patients (73 of 13 134) and 3.6% of AA patients (308 of 8519). With the numbers available, no significant differences could be detected with dementia in TAA using adjusted multivariable analyses, although 12-month readmissions were higher (P = .046). In contrast, among AA patients, dementia was associated with increased 90-day readmissions (P = .041) and higher 12-month mortality (P = .001), whereas other outcomes were not statistically significant.Conclusion:We found differences in postoperative outcomes, particularly readmissions and mortality, in patients with dementia following AA. Conversely, we found limited differences in patients with and without dementia undergoing TAA, which may reflect limited power to detect modest differences given the smaller number of dementia patients in the TAA cohort. Further research should explore how the severity of cognitive impairment influences decision making in ankle arthritis treatment.Level of Evidence:Level III, retrospective cohort study.

Introduction

Surgical treatment options for ankle arthritis include ankle arthrodesis (AA) and total ankle arthroplasty (TAA). 1 Both operations are commonly performed in older patients. 2 Similarly, in an aging population, the number of adults in the United States who will develop dementia is projected to increase from 514 000 in 2020 to roughly 1 million in 2060. 3 Accordingly, foot and ankle surgeons will increasingly evaluate older patients with both symptomatic ankle arthritis and dementia. There is currently little evidence evaluating the impact of dementia on outcomes following AA or TAA. In this short report, we analyzed postoperative complication rates following AA and TAA in a Medicare cohort, hypothesizing greater risk in patients with dementia than in patients without dementia.

Methods

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using Medicare fee-for-service (FFS) and Medicare Advantage (health maintenance organization [HMO]) claims from 2016 to 2021, including beneficiaries aged ≥65 years who underwent TAA or AA. Surgeries were identified using relevant Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes (TAA: 27700, 27702; AA: 27870, 29899), and the index procedure was defined as the first qualifying TAA or AA during the study period. Patients were required to have continuous enrollment during the 90-day preoperative look-back period to ensure complete capture of comorbidities. Beneficiaries eligible for Medicare through end-stage renal disease (ESRD), or Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI), were excluded.

The primary independent variable was dementia status, defined using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) dementia component based on International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision (ICD-10) diagnosis codes within 90 days prior to the index procedure. Outcomes included postoperative complications, all-cause readmissions, and mortality within 90 days and 12 months following discharge. Continuous postoperative enrollment through the corresponding follow-up period was required for ascertainment of 90-day and 12-month outcomes. Complications were identified using inpatient and part B claims and categorized using previously published ICD-10–based algorithms. 4 Mortality was assessed using the Master Beneficiary Summary File and analyzed separately. Covariates included age (5-year increments), sex, race, obesity, HMO status, and all individual CCI components (excluding dementia) as separate covariates in the primary model. A clinically targeted model including diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, and chronic kidney disease as well as a grouped CCI model were also evaluated as sensitivity analyses.

We compared unadjusted complication rates between patients with and without dementia separately for TAA or AA. Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables and Welch t test for continuous outcomes. Multivariable logistic regression models estimated adjusted differences in complication rates by dementia status, reported as percentage-point (ppt) differences with 95% CIs. Year fixed effects were included, and SEs were clustered at the hospital level. To further address potential selection bias, inverse probability-weighted regression adjustment (IPWRA) was performed as a sensitivity analysis. Additional sensitivity analyses were conducted using alternative comorbidity specifications, including selected clinically relevant comorbidities and grouped CCI models. Post hoc minimum detectable effects were calculated assuming 80% power and a 2-sided α of 0.05. All analyses were conducted in STATA-MP version 19.5 within the CMS Virtual Research Data Center (VRDC).

Results

Dementia was present in 0.56% of TAA patients (73 of 13 134) and 3.6% of AA patients (308 of 8519). In both cohorts, patients with dementia were older and had a higher burden of comorbid conditions than those without dementia (Table 1).

In adjusted multivariable analyses (Table 2) with the numbers available, dementia was not significantly associated with 90-day or 12-month complications following TAA. However, TAA patients with dementia had higher 12-month readmissions (+11.7 ppt; 95% CI 0.2-23.2; P = .046). Among AA patients, dementia was associated with higher 90-day readmissions (+5.0 ppt; P = .041) and higher 12-month mortality (+4.9 ppt; P = .001). Other outcomes were not statistically significant. Results were also similar in direction across alternative model specifications using selected comorbidities and grouped CCI (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2), although statistical significance differed across models. Because baseline event rates vary by outcome, the study’s detectable effect size differs across endpoints. For the 90-day any-complication outcome, the minimum detectable absolute difference (80% power, 2-sided α = 0.05) was approximately 8.2 ppt for TAA and 6.9 ppt for AA, suggesting limited power to detect modest differences across several outcomes, particularly within the smaller TAA dementia subgroup (see Supplementary Table 3).

Discussion

Here, we studied the role of dementia on complications following elective TAA and AA. The TAA and AA cohorts were analyzed as separate clinical populations, and no direct comparison between procedures was intended. Dementia was associated with significantly higher rates of readmission and mortality following AA. In contrast, among TAA patients, no consistent association was observed across outcomes with the numbers available, although higher 12-month readmissions were noted. However, the small number of dementia patients undergoing TAA limits the ability to detect modest differences. Across multiple modeling approaches, the direction of effect was generally consistent, with no clear association observed in TAA and a signal toward increased adverse outcomes in AA. However, the specific outcomes reaching statistical significance varied across models, likely reflecting differences in model specification and limited power in certain subgroups.

Prior studies have analyzed the risk of dementia on elective orthopaedic surgery. Serna et al 5 showed greater risk of periprosthetic fracture, instability, and need for revision in patients with dementia undergoing elective shoulder replacements. Dementia was independently associated with an increased risk of perioperative complications following elective TKA 6 and THA, 7 but other reports highlighted marginal differences. 8 Dementia was associated with increased postoperative complications within 30 days following elective cervical and lumbar spine surgery. 9 Lee et al 10 showed that dementia was a possible risk factor for early TAA failure, but beyond that, no other studies have examined dementia in either TAA or AA.

There are clear limitations of this study. The claims-based definition for dementia likely captures a heterogeneous spectrum of cognitive impairment, and we were unable to assess dementia severity for an individual patient. In addition, dementia was defined using administrative claims and does not allow differentiation by severity or subtype. Although subgroup analyses using specific diagnosis codes may be possible, sample sizes would be small and unlikely to yield reliable estimates. Dementia rates (TAA 0.56%, AA 3.6%) were low, particularly in the TAA cohort, so we may have been underpowered to detect modest differences, particularly in the TAA cohort. But the dementia rates here are similar to elective shoulder, hip, and knee arthroplasties.5-7 Because this study is observational, we cannot make definitive causal inferences about the relationship between dementia and complications following both AA and TAA surgeries. In addition, as with all claims-based analyses, residual confounding from unmeasured clinical factors may influence the observed associations. Administrative data also lack detailed clinical information, which limits risk adjustment and outcome interpretation. Further, our analyses are exploratory and hypothesis-generating but not confirmatory. Although we found associations of higher risk in patients with dementia, these findings cannot be interpreted as causal. Lastly, this study was not designed to compare the risk profiles between AA and TAA. Although our results suggest higher complications in dementia patients for AA, these findings do not necessarily indicate that TAA is safer than AA. Patients with dementia may be preferentially selected for AA rather than TAA, and this potential selection bias may partially explain the higher complication rates observed in the AA cohort.

Conclusion

In this study, we found notable differences in postoperative readmissions and mortality in patients with dementia following AA. In the TAA cohort, we found limited differences in patients with and without dementia, which may in part reflect limited power to detect modest differences given the smaller number of dementia patients in the TAA cohort. Further research should explore how the severity of cognitive impairment influences ankle arthritis treatment.
