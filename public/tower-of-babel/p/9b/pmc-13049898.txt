Sexual behaviours, HIV self-testing awareness, and uptake among Young People (YP) attending a Ghanaian high school
Authors: Selina Sonia Osei, Dorothy Adorkor Sowah, Alberta Yemotsoo Lomotey, Eric Yiadom Boakye, Eric Osei, Victoria Bam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHuman Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) remains a significant global public health problem, with adolescents and young adults being particularly vulnerable to the infection due to risky sexual behaviours, leading to increased rates of HIV transmission. HIV self-testing (HIVST) has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance testing uptake, reduce stigma, facilitate early diagnosis, and reach underserved populations that traditional HIV testing services would otherwise not be able to reach. Globally, approximately 77 countries, including Ghana, have adopted the HIVST strategy to date. However, little is known about its awareness, sexual risky behaviours and non-uptake among Young People (YP), hence this study.MethodThis was a cross-sectional survey among 231 YP attending Donkokrom Senior High School. A stratified sampling method was used to recruit the respondents. Data were collected with a self-administered questionnaire using Computer-Assisted Personal Interviews (CAPI) through the KoboCollect application. Descriptive statistics were used to describe variables, while Binary logistic regression analysis was used to determine predictors of non-uptake of OraQuick HIVST. All data were analysed using Stata version 17.0.ResultsOf the 231 respondents, the majority (61.5%) were in their late teens (aged 16–18 years), with a median age of 18 years (IQR = 16–19). Females constituted 58.4% of the participants; 80 (34.6%) were sexually active, among whom 51(63.8%) had sexual debut before 16 years of age; 17.5% always used a condom during sex; and overall, 29.9% of them were involved in at least one risky sexual behaviour. Regarding the awareness of OralQuick HIVST, 101 (43.7%) participants said they were aware. Among these, 68 (67.3%) had ever received an OralQuick HIVST kit. Forty-seven (69.1%) of these participants used it to self-test for HIV. Overall, the prevalence of OralQuick HIVST uptake in the study population was 20.3% (n = 47). Logistic regression analysis showed that males had higher odds of not taking OraQuick HIVST compared to females (Adjusted Odds Ratio: 2.15; 95% CI: 1.01–4.58).ConclusionNearly one-third of the study’s participants engaged in risky sexual behaviours, yet the non-uptake of HIV self-testing remains high, especially among males, despite the growing awareness of the initiative. These findings highlight the need for targeted education and interventions to address misconceptions and barriers to HIV self-testing, particularly among males.Keywords: Sexual behaviours, HIV Self-testing, Young People, Ghana

Background

HIV remains a significant global public health issue, having claimed approximately 42.3 million lives so far, with 630,000 deaths in 2023 alone. As of 2023, about 39.9 million people were living with HIV (PLHIV), and 1.3 million new infections occurred that year [1]. Africa is disproportionately affected, with 26.0 million PLHIV in 2023 and contributing to about half of global new infections [1]. The young people (10–24 years old) are increasingly impacted, with 360,000 young people aged 15–24 newly infected in 2023, including 140,000 adolescents aged 15–19. In 2019, two out of every seven new global HIV infections were among young people (15–24 years), with sub-Saharan Africa accounting for 82% of these cases [2]. In Ghana, the HIV prevalence rate among adolescents aged 15–19 years is estimated at 1.4% [3]. The UNAIDS 95-95-95 agenda aims to end the epidemic by 2030 by ensuring that 95% of PLHIV know their status, 95% of those diagnosed receive antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 95% of those treated with ART achieve viral suppression [1, 4, 5]. HIV self-testing (HIVST) kits are available, with OraQuick being one of the most widely used and WHO-prequalified oral fluid–based kits. In Ghana, HIVST initiatives have also adopted OraQuick, making it a relevant and accessible option for young people in school settings [6].

According to the Ghana AID Commission [2], the country’s HIV testing coverage rose significantly to 72% by 2022, up from 69% in 2019, demonstrating remarkable progress in the fight against HIV. However, less than 50% of the country’s young people have undergone HIV testing [6, 7], indicating that most young people are unaware of their status. This hampers the achievement of global HIV/AIDS-related targets, including the United Nations Sustainable Development Goals target of ending HIV as a public health threat by 2030 and the UNAIDS 95-95-95 target [8].

HIV self-testing (HIVST), endorsed by the WHO in 2016, is a convenient, safe, and accurate method to reach individuals who might not otherwise test, including key populations, men, and young people. HIVST can be performed reliably by laypersons, with performance comparable to that of trained medical professionals. Many countries have adopted HIVST policies, and their implementation is rapidly expanding [9]. Ghana launched the initiative in July 2023 to augment other innovative strategies, including mobile testing units and community-based testing, allowing individuals to test discreetly and conveniently at home [6].

Despite the introduction of HIVST in Ghana, empirical studies on its awareness and uptake among young people are limited. This gap makes it difficult to assess progress and implement necessary modifications to improve HIVST adoption among this population. This study, therefore, aimed to address the gap by providing empirical evidence on the YPs’ sexual behaviour, awareness, and uptake of HIVST in a Ghanaian high school.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional survey among the young people attending Donkorkrom Agric Senior High School (SHS). In Ghana, the appropriate ages for enrolment into a SHS and graduating is 14 and 17 years, respectively. However, large numbers of students are not often in the appropriate age (MoH) [10] Donkorkrom is the capital town of the Kwahu Afram Plains North District of the Eastern Region, Ghana. Kwahu Afram Plains North District is located in the northernmost part of Ghana’s Eastern Region. It covers an area of approximately 2,570 km², making it the largest district in the region by landmass. The district capital is Donkorkrom. The area is predominantly rural, with scattered settlements and limited infrastructure, and access is often constrained by the Afram River crossing. Agriculture and fishing are the main economic activities, and healthcare and educational facilities are relatively sparse compared to urban centres. These rural characteristics influence young people’s access to health services, including HIV testing. The school is among the four senior high schools in the district. It is a mixed public institution situated on the outskirts of the Donkorkrom township. The school, founded in 1974, with a student population of nearly 500, currently runs six academic programmes, namely Agriculture, Business, Home Economics, Visual Arts, General Arts, and General Science.

Sample size

The minimum sample size required for this study was 219, determined using Yamane’s formula as follows; n= , where n= sample size; N= the population size of 485 students; and e= the margin of error set at 0.05. Adding a 5% non-response rate, the total sample size needed for the study was 230.

Sampling method

The students were stratified into groups based on their class level (SHS 1, SHS 2, and SHS 3), sex (male and female), and programme of study (General Science, General Arts, Home Economics, Visual Arts, and Agric Science). A simple random sampling method was used to select respondents proportionally to the size of the stratum, using the class register as the sampling frame. Students’ names were entered into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, assigned random numbers, and sorted in descending order. The desired number of top rows was then selected to form the sample for each stratum.

Data collection tool and procedure

A pre-tested, self-administered questionnaire designed using Computer-Assisted Personal Interviews (CAPI) through the KoboCollect application was used to the collect data. The questionnaire had three sections. The first section collected socio-demographic data of participants, section two included data on awareness and HIV testing uptake, while the third section focused on collecting sexual practices data. The questionnaire was prepared in the English Language and pretested on 10 students in the same school who were not part of the study population to help identify potential issues related to question clarity, respondent understanding, and overall survey flow, leading to more reliable and valid data collection.

The link of the electronic tool was mounted on computer tablets and handed to the respondents in batches to fill in their responses. Before that, they were introduced to the study’s objectives and how to correctly fill in their responses in the electronic tool. Two research assistants were trained on how to take informed consent from participants and other data collection-related issues to assist the participants in the field to fill in the electronic tool. Each participant used, on average, 30 to 45 min to complete the questionnaire.

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome variable was non-uptake of the HIV self-test among participants, defined as never using OralQuick HIV test kit for self-test preceding the survey. Exposure variables included, demographic factors comprising age, sex, religion, class, programme of study, residential status, current guardian, ownership of a smartphone; a risky sexual behaviour, defined as having respond in affirmative to any one of the following indicators: sexual debut before 16 years of age, currently having multiple sexual partners, engaged in transactional sex, and non-use of a condom during the most recent sexual intercourse. Binary Logistic regression was used to investigate the factors that are associated with non-uptake of the HIV self-test. First, the association of non-uptake of HIV self-test with each potential risk factor collected in the study was examined, ignoring all other variables. Second, to construct a model with risk factors independently associated with non-uptake of HIV self-test, each variable was a candidate for inclusion, provided that, when considered singly, the P-value for the association with non-uptake of HIV self-test was 0.05 or less. Age was modelled as a categorical variable defined as: 0 (13–15 years), 1 (16–18 years), and 2 (19–24 years). All data were analysed using Stata version 17.0 (Stata Corp, College Station, Texas, USA).

Ethical considerations

Ethics approval was given by the Committee of Human Research and Publication Ethics, Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (CHRPE KNUST) (Reference number: CHRPE/AP/485/24). Participants aged 18 years and above provided informed consent directly, while for those under 18 years, one parental or guardian consent and the child’s assent were obtained in accordance with ethical standards. Data obtained from the respondents were kept confidential. Names and other identifiers of participants were not collected; hence, data cannot be linked to any individual participant.

Results

Socio-demographic characteristics of participants

Table 1 presents the socio-demographic characteristics of participants. Of the 231 participants, 142 (61.5%) were in their late teens (aged 16–18 years), with a median age of 18 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 16–19). Females constituted 58.4% of the participants; most (n = 179; 77.5%) of them were identified as Christians, and 111 (48.1%) were in SHS 3. The majority (n = 119; 51.5%), of them were offering General Arts with Visual Arts (n = 35; 15.2%) and General Science n = 33;14.3%), being the next most popular programme of study. Most (150; 64.9%) students resided in boarding houses, and 156 (67.5%) attended public junior high schools (JHS). Over half (n = 138; 59.7%) were living with both parents. During childhood, 150 (64.9%) were cared for by both of their parents. Regarding technology ownership, 138 (59.7%) owned a smartphone, while ownership of a computer or tablet was less common (n = 30; 12.9%).

Sexual behaviours of participants

Out of 231 respondents, 80 (34.6%) were sexually active. Of these, 51 (63.8%) had their first sexual experience before their 16th birthday. Condom use during the last sexual encounter was found among 14 (17.5%) of the sexually active participants, with 11 (13.8%) always using a condom during sex. Decision-making about condom use was typically shared, with 53 (66.3%) reporting that both partners were involved in the decision. Half (n = 40) of the sexually active respondents reported having more than one lifetime sexual partner, and 15 (9.7%) of them currently had multiple sexual partners. The prevalence of transactional sex among the sexually active participants was 17.5% (n = 14); 16 (20%) of them reported ever being diagnosed with Sexually Transmitted Infection (STI); 25 (31.3%) had ever been pregnant or had impregnated someone, with most 11 (44.0%) of these pregnancies ending in self-induced abortion. Overall, 69 (29.9%) of the respondents engaged in one or more risky sexual behaviours, defined as early sexual debut, having multiple sexual partners, engaging in transactional sex, or never and inconsistent condom use (Table 2).

HIV self-testing awareness and uptake among participants

One hundred and one participants (43.7%) were aware of HIVST. Of these, 68 (67.3%) had ever received an OralQuick HIVST kit. Among participants who received the kit, 48 (70.6%) reported that instructions for its use were clearly explained to them, and 38 (79.2%) indicated that they understood the instructions provided. Of the 68 participants who obtained the kit, 47 (69.1%) used it to self-test for HIV, and 38 (80.9%) of those who used the kit reported that it was easy to use. Overall, the prevalence of OralQuick HIVST uptake among the study participants was 20.3% (n = 47) (Table 3).

Factors associated with non-uptake of OralQuick HIVST among participants

Bivariate logistic regression analysis showed that participants who attended mission-owned Junior High Schools (JHS) had lower odds of not taking up OralQuick HIVST (OR: 0.27; P = 0.023) compared to those who attended Public JHS. Males had 2.15 times increased odds of non-uptake of OralQuick HIVST (p = 0.033) compared to females. In the multivariate logistic regression model, male sex remained the only independent risk factor associated with non-uptake of OralQuick HIVST, having 2.21 times higher odds of not self-testing for HIV compared to females (95% CI: 1.01–4.84; P = 0.048). Other covariates, including age, religious affiliation, grade level, program of study, residential status, ownership of electronic devices, family structure, and risky sexual behaviour, were not statistically significantly associated with the non-uptake of OralQuick HIVST (Table 4).

Discussion

This study examined sexual practices, HIVST awareness, and uptake among adolescents and young adults in a Ghanaian high school. The findings have important public health ramifications. Notably, nearly one-third of the participants admitted to at least one risky sexual practice, including transactional sex, having numerous sexual partners, or using condoms inconsistently. This level of risk is alarming and highlights the population’s ongoing vulnerability to HIV and other STIs, despite ongoing national prevention campaigns [3]. The findings are consistent with previous studies in Ghana that have demonstrated that adolescents and young adults frequently engage in high-risk sexual behaviours due to peer pressure, limited access to sexual health education, and socioeconomic factors [11–13]. Gyane et al. [11], in their study in Ghana further underscores the role of social media in influencing adolescent sexual behaviour in Ghana. Their research indicates that exposure to sexual content on social media platforms can shape adolescents’ perceptions and attitudes. With a relatively moderate 44% awareness of HIV self-testing, public health messaging and outreach initiatives appear to have made some headway. The fact that over half of the participants were ignorant of the presence or accessibility of HIVST, however, also indicates a significant disparity. This could be due to a lack of youth-friendly HIVST information dissemination or a failure to adequately integrate self-testing programmes into platforms that young people visit, such as youth centres, schools, or social media [14, 15]. The moderate awareness could also be shaped by persistent myths or misinformation surrounding HIV testing, particularly fears of stigma or privacy breaches [16]. The HIVST awareness level observed in this study is lower than that reported in a study conducted in South Africa [17]. A study among young people in urban areas of southern Ethiopia noted that only 27.9% of the respondents were aware of HIVST [18].

Despite nearly half of the participants being aware of HIVST, only about 20% of them had ever used an HIV self-test, indicating low actual uptake. This disparity between awareness and utilization is in line with trends seen in other low- and middle-income settings, where awareness does not necessarily translate into action due to barriers such as cost, availability, perceived invulnerability to HIV, or fear of a positive result [19–22]. Boakye et al., for instance, emphasized in their Ghanaian study that national policies and programmes supporting HIVST have increased awareness, but uptake remains low due to financial constraints [23].

The low uptake is particularly worrying given the high level of risky sexual behaviour observed, suggesting a missed opportunity to identify HIV infections early and link individuals to care. Previous studies from several Southern African countries also reported a similar finding of low HIVST. For instance, a systematic review [18] reported that university students in South Africa, Nigeria, and Uganda used HIVST at rates of 14.9%, 16.8%, and 19.0%, respectively. Among the reasons that could mitigate the uptake of HIVST services may include coping with a positive test in the absence of counselling and support, physical discomfort, availability, accessibility, and affordability of kits. According to the Ghana HIV and AIDS Network, although the piloting of HIVST in the country was successful, issues related to inadequate counselling, insufficient care linkage, and the logistics of obtaining HIVST kits without jeopardising privacy and anonymity were identified, which could hinder successful implementation [24].

Both formal school policies and prevailing cultural expectations can significantly shape adolescents’ engagement with HIV self-testing. Schools in Ghana maintain strict regulations governing students’ movements, health-seeking behaviour, and interactions with external health services. These policies, though designed to ensure discipline and safeguard student welfare, may inadvertently restrict opportunities for discreet access to HIVST. For instance, it has been documented in other Ghanaian contexts where HIV prevention interventions in schools are constrained by moralistic norms and administrative gatekeeping [25].

Culturally, Donkokrom, like many rural Ghanaian settings, upholds conservative views on adolescent sexuality. Community norms often stigmatize discussions of sexual health, creating fear of judgment among young people who may wish to self-test. Adolescents frequently internalize these norms, leading to hesitancy to request HIVST kits or to be seen engaging in HIV-related activities. Stigma remains one of the strongest barriers to HIV testing in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in smaller communities where anonymity is limited [26]. Consequently, the moral environment of Donkokrom schools and the broader community contributes to limited uptake of HIVST among students.

Gender disparities were also evident in HIVST uptake. Males were more than twice as likely as females not to have taken an HIV self-test. This is consistent with broader literature showing that males often exhibit lower health-seeking behaviours, including for HIV testing, due to cultural norms, masculinity constructs, and fear of stigma [12, 24]. This gender gap highlights the need for gender-sensitive approaches to HIV prevention, including targeted messaging and male-friendly services that can encourage HIVST among young men.

Overall, the findings point to a crucial need for interventions that not only raise awareness but also actively promote the uptake of HIV self-testing among adolescents and young adults. Strategies could include integrating HIVST education into school curricula, expanding community outreach, subsidizing test kits, and leveraging digital platforms to provide information and support. Particular attention should be given to reaching young males and individuals engaging in high-risk sexual behaviours, who may benefit most from increased access to and uptake of HIVST.

Limitations

The study provides valuable insights into sexual behaviours and HIVST uptake among rural in-school adolescents and young adults. However, it may not fully represent all young people in Ghana for many reasons;

Firstly, young people who are currently in school form a substantial proportion of Ghana’s youth, and secondary schools provide a practical setting for studying adolescent sexual behaviour. However, out-of-school youth, who may have higher HIV risk levels, were not represented in the study. Therefore, the findings primarily apply to in-school adolescents and young people. Secondly, the district’s rural nature mirrors the situation of many rural Ghanaian communities, where access to health information and services is limited, sexual and reproductive health education may be inadequate, stigma around HIV remains pronounced. However, nationally, Ghana’s young people include large urban populations with better access to healthcare, technology, and HIV testing options. As a result, the findings may over-represent rural characteristics (limited health access, lower exposure to HIVST interventions) and may not fully reflect young people in urban or peri-urban settings. Thirdly, the school is one of the four SHSs in the district, and its mixed-sex structure and diverse programmes reflect typical SHS environments in Ghana. This diversity improves the representativeness of the sample for rural school-based adolescents. However, the school has a relatively small population (~ 485 students), which may limit the heterogeneity of experiences compared with larger SHSs in cities.

This study may be limited by a potential high risk of social desirability bias in self-reported of HIVST uptake, whereby students may under-report or over-report their behaviours to align with perceived expectations, which might have resulted in under- or overestimation.

Due to these limitations, the findings should be generalized cautiously to the broader population of young people in Ghana, particularly urban youth or those who are out of school.

Conclusion

While HIVST is becoming more widely known among Ghanaian youth, actual uptake remains low, particularly among boys and young men. Risky sexual behaviours such as early sexual debut, inconsistent condom use, and multiple partnerships are common, underscoring the heightened vulnerability of this population to HIV infection. To strengthen HIV prevention efforts, it is important to first identify the informational and structural barriers that limit HIVST uptake among young people. Based on these findings, targeted, youth-friendly, gender-responsive interventions can be designed to close the awareness-to-action gap, thereby supporting progress toward SDG 3 (Good Health and Well‑Being) and the goal of ending the AIDS epidemic by 2030.

Future studies involving multiple schools across rural and urban settings—or including out-of-school youths—would improve representativeness and provide a more comprehensive understanding of HIV self-testing uptake among young people nationwide.
