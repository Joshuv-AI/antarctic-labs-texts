Seven-year surveillance of latent tuberculosis infection using interferon-gamma release assay among health workers at a large tertiary hospital in Thailand
Authors: Naesinee Chaiear, Chatpong Ngamchokwathana, Phanumas Krisorn, Warisa Soonthornvinit, Yingrit Chantarasuk, Arunya Chonlapan, Arpa Surapaitoon, Patimaporn Chanpho, Wipa Reechaipichitkul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite ongoing TB control efforts, health workers (HWs) in Thailand remain at risk for latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI), yet the burden of LTBI in Thai HWs is insufficiently characterized. This study aimed to determine LTBI proportions across preplacement, post-exposure, and annual screening approaches, and to identify factors associated with LTBI among HWs.MethodsThis retrospective analysis (2016–2022) utilized secondary data from an LTBI surveillance program at a large Thai tertiary hospital. HWs underwent LTBI screening with interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs) through three surveillance approaches: preplacement, post-exposure, and annual screening in high-risk units (2021–2022). LTBI was defined as IGRA-positive in the absence of active TB. Demographic and exposure characteristics were recorded; associations were analyzed using chi-square and Mann–Whitney U tests. An exploratory restricted analysis using multivariable logistic regression was also performed to assess between-cohort patterns.ResultsBetween 2016 and 2020, LTBI was detected in 198 of 1,823 HWs (10.9%; 95% CI: 9.5–12.4). Preplacement screening identified LTBI in 96 of 1,230 HWs (7.8%; 95% CI: 6.5–9.5), while post-exposure surveillance detected LTBI in 102 of 706 HWs (14.4%; 95% CI: 12.1–17.2). Annual surveillance (2021–2022) yielded 47 cases among 626 HWs (7.5%; 95% CI: 5.7–9.8). Among HWs diagnosed with LTBI in the preplacement and post-exposure cohorts, 40.9% of LTBI cases initiated TB preventive treatment. In post-exposure surveillance, older age and higher exposure-priority categories were associated with LTBI, although estimates for priority categories were imprecise because of sparse data.ConclusionsLTBI remains an important occupational health concern among HWs, with the highest detection proportion observed during post-exposure surveillance. These findings highlight the ongoing need for targeted, risk-based monitoring and efforts to enhance TB preventive treatment uptake in healthcare settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12995-026-00514-8.

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB) remains a major global health threat, with an estimated 10.8 million new cases and 1.25 million deaths reported worldwide in 2023 [1]. Thailand continues to be classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) as a high TB burden country, with an incidence of 143 per 100,000 population in 2021 [2]. Latent TB infection (LTBI)—an asymptomatic state harboring viable Mycobacterium tuberculosis—constitutes a substantial reservoir for future active disease, particularly within two years post-infection or among immunocompromised individuals. Approximately 25–30% of exposed individuals develop LTBI, with 5–10% progressing to active TB if not treated [3].

Health workers (HWs) are at consistently elevated risk of LTBI due to regular contact with infectious patients, particularly in high-burden settings like Thailand. International studies report LTBI prevalence among HWs ranging from 20% to 40% [4–6], while studies within Thailand have reported even wider variation—ranging from 14.7% to 63% depending on setting and surveillance approach [7, 8]. This occupational reservoir not only threatens workforce health but also contributes to ongoing transmission within health facilities. Despite robust national TB control efforts, data on the true burden and determinants of LTBI among HWs remain limited. Most local surveillance strategies have relied primarily on symptomatic screening or single-point testing, rather than comprehensive, multi-component approaches such as preplacement, post-exposure, and ongoing annual surveillance. The lack of systematic evaluation across these surveillance modalities limits evidence-based policymaking and impedes occupational health protection.

To address these gaps, we conducted a seven-year review of LTBI surveillance among HWs at a large tertiary university hospital in Northeastern Thailand. Specifically, we aimed to: (1) determine the proportion and approach-specific proportions of LTBI across preplacement, post-exposure, and annual screening; and (2) identify factors associated with LTBI status.

Methods

This retrospective descriptive study was conducted at a large tertiary university hospital (~ 1,000 beds) in Northeastern Thailand over two periods: 2016–2020 and 2021–2022.

Study population and samples

The study population consisted of all HWs aged ≥ 18 years who were employed at the study hospital at any time during the surveillance period. HWs were eligible if they had either direct patient contact (e.g., doctors, nurses, allied health staff) or indirect patient contact (e.g., laboratory, housekeeping, or administrative staff). Individuals with evidence of active TB or prior active TB were excluded from this surveillance program and referred to a separate management protocol. The estimated total number of eligible HWs during the study period was approximately 2,700.

Depending on their job assignment and exposure status, eligible HWs were enrolled in one or more of the following surveillance phases: preplacement, post-exposure, and annual screening.

The minimum sample size was calculated using WinPEPI software, based on an expected Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA) positivity rate of 16.0% [7], an allowable error of 1.6%, a 5% significance level, and a 10% margin for anticipated loss, resulting in a required sample size of at least 1,283 HWs.

Surveillance tools and procedures

LTBI surveillance, adapted from the U.S. CDC recommendation [9–10], was implemented through three approaches: (1) preplacement at entry, (2) post-exposure surveillance, and (3) annual screening.

From 2016 to 2020, preplacement screening consisted of IGRA testing at hire for HWs with direct patient or specimen contact (e.g., physicians, nurses, laboratorians, clinical instructors), with chest radiography as indicated to establish baseline status and exclude active TB. By program policy, individuals with prior IGRA positivity and those with active TB were managed clinically and were excluded from subsequent post-exposure or annual surveillance.

Moreover, post-exposure surveillance enrolled HWs with documented occupational exposure to a TB case or infectious specimen, regardless of preplacement participation. Exposure priority was categorized using a structured risk assessment following Sirinapakul et al. and Chaiear [11–12]. Moderate- or high-priority contacts were scheduled for follow-up IGRA and chest radiography after the primary window (8–12 weeks post-exposure) at the earliest feasible visit. Between 2016 and 2020, 1,823 unique HWs participated in preplacement and/or post-exposure LTBI surveillance. Among them, 113 workers participated in both surveillance phases; therefore, the phase-specific groups were not mutually exclusive. For overall reporting, each HW was counted once as a unique individual.

From 2021 to 2022, the surveillance strategy was revised to implement annual LTBI screening for personnel in selected high-risk clinical settings—specifically those working in intensive care units (ICU), intermediate care units (IMC), internal medicine wards, and emergency departments—based on the 2005 U.S. CDC guidelines [10]. This approach was introduced because their work involved frequent occupational exposure to TB cases (typically ≥ 3 incidents per year). A total of 626 HWs participated in this program.

Interferon-gamma release assays (IGRA) were used as the primary diagnostic tool because of their higher specificity than the tuberculin skin test in BCG-vaccinated populations [13, 14]. During 2016 to late-2017, the assay used was QuantiFERON-TB Gold In-Tube (QFT-GIT; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), and from late-2017 onward, QuantiFERON-TB Gold Plus (QFT-Plus; Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was implemented in accordance with routine laboratory practice. These assays measure interferon-gamma responses to Mycobacterium tuberculosis-specific antigens, including ESAT-6 and CFP-10, and have reported pooled sensitivity and specificity of approximately 81–90% and 95–99%, respectively [15, 16]. Whole blood (≥ 6 mL) was collected into lithium heparin tubes at the hospital collection unit and transported to the Department of Microbiology. Tests were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions for the corresponding assay version [17, 18]. For QFT-GIT, blood was transferred into three tubes (Nil, TB antigen, and Mitogen), whereas for QFT-Plus, blood was transferred into four tubes (Nil, TB1, TB2, and Mitogen). Tubes were incubated at 37 °C for 16–24 h, after which they were centrifuged and plasma was harvested for ELISA. For both assays, interferon-gamma concentrations were interpreted after subtraction of the Nil value. For QFT-GIT, results were considered positive when TB antigen minus Nil was ≥ 0.35 IU/mL and ≥ 25% of the Nil value. For QFT-Plus, results were considered positive when either TB1 minus Nil or TB2 minus Nil was ≥ 0.35 IU/mL and ≥ 25% of the Nil value. Indeterminate results were defined according to the manufacturer’s criteria and were scheduled for prompt repeat testing.

Chest radiography was employed in the program to exclude active TB cases. Only individuals lacking clinical symptoms, radiological abnormalities, or laboratory evidence of active infection—as determined by a certified chest physician—were classified as having LTBI in this study.

All operational definitions—including job roles, workplace settings, and exposure-risk categories—are provided in Supplementary Table S1. Briefly, HWs were classified by routine patient contact (direct vs. indirect) and workplace risk level (high vs. low), based on the likelihood of TB exposure. Worksite categories were mutually exclusive. Risk priority following TB exposure was determined using a combination of host, patient, environmental, and procedural factors, with aerosol-generating procedures (AGPs) defined per previous publications [11, 12]. Data collection and statistical analysis

Secondary data—including responses from screening questionnaires, IGRA results, demographic characteristics, BCG vaccination status, exposure details, and surveillance outcomes—were obtained from the Occupational Health and Safety (OH&S) unit in electronic format and entered into Microsoft Excel 2021. Data were double-entered for accuracy. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS v28.0 and OpenEpi. Categorical variables were presented as n (%); continuous variables as mean ± SD or median (IQR). Group comparisons employed chi-square, Fisher’s exact, t-test, or Mann–Whitney U as appropriate. Significance was accepted at p < 0.05. HWs with a history of active TB, previous positive IGRA, or completed LTBI treatment before the study were excluded from analytic cohorts. In addition, because some HWs participated in more than one surveillance phase, overall counts were summarized using unique individuals, whereas phase-specific analyses were performed using the analytic cohort defined for each surveillance phase after applying the predefined exclusion criteria.

To assess the robustness of the observed between-group pattern, we conducted a restricted sensitivity analysis including only HWs who contributed to a single surveillance cohort, thereby reducing overlap across surveillance streams. In this restricted dataset, multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between surveillance cohort and LTBI after adjustment for age, sex, and patient contact category. Because this restriction excluded HWs who participated in multiple surveillance streams, the analysis was considered exploratory and was not used as the primary basis for inference.

Results

Between 2016 and 2020, 1,823 unique HWs participated in preplacement and/or post-exposure surveillance. Of these, 1,230 underwent preplacement surveillance and 706 underwent post-exposure surveillance, with an overlap of 113 HWs between the two phases. The median age of participants was 24 years (IQR, 23–27), and 74.3% were female. The majority were physicians (42.2%) and registered nurses (40.0%). Most participants worked in high-exposure-risk settings (86.3%), with inpatient departments (IPD) and intensive care units (ICU) being the most common sites. Overall, LTBI was identified in 198 of 1,823 unique HWs (10.9%; 95% CI: 9.5–12.4). In the preplacement phase, 1,230 HWs underwent screening, of whom 101 had positive IGRA results. After further evaluation, 3 HWs were diagnosed with active TB and 2 had a history of old TB, leaving 96 HWs classified as having LTBI (7.8%; 95% CI: 6.5–9.5). In the post-exposure phase, 706 HWs were evaluated; 105 had positive IGRA results, of whom 3 had old TB, resulting in 102 LTBI cases (14.4%; 95% CI: 12.1–17.2). LTBI was most frequently identified among registered nurses and those with direct patient contact. Detailed participant and group characteristics are provided in Table 2. Among HWs diagnosed with LTBI in the preplacement and post-exposure cohorts, 81 of 198 (40.9%; 95% CI: 34.3–47.9) initiated TB preventive treatment (TPT). Of those who initiated treatment, 76 (93.8%) completed the prescribed regimen. The vast majority received isoniazid for 6–9 months, whereas only one HW received the 3-month isoniazid-rifampicin regimen. Among HWs who did not initiate TPT, one individual identified through preplacement screening subsequently developed active TB during follow-up.

Over the five-year post-exposure surveillance period, 108 accidental TB exposure incidents were reported, 84.3% of which involved patient contact. No significant differences were observed by sex, BCG scar status, patient contact category, performance of high-risk procedures, or duration of contact between HWs with and without LTBI. However, age was significantly associated with LTBI: the median age in the LTBI group was 39 years (IQR, 30–51) compared to 30 years (IQR, 26–37) in the non-LTBI group (p < 0.001). LTBI was identified in 16.7% of high-priority contacts and 14.6% of moderate-priority contacts, whereas no cases were identified among low-priority contacts. Compared with low-priority contacts, point estimates for high- and moderate-priority contacts were higher (high vs. low: OR = 16.1, 95% CI, 2.48–432.9, p < 0.001; moderate vs. low: OR = 13.7, 95% CI, 2.04–374.2, p = 0.002), although these estimates were imprecise because no LTBI cases were identified in the low-priority group and confidence intervals were wide. Between 2021 and 2022, our facility implemented annual LTBI screening. During this period, 626 HWs participated. Of these, 243 (38.8%) participated only once (66 in 2021 and 177 in 2022). Overall, 47 HWs (7.5%; 95% CI: 5.7–9.8) were identified as having LTBI during the annual screening period. Further details are presented in Table 4.

Of the 37 LTBI cases identified among HWs screened only once, 11 (29.7%) initiated TPT, all with a 9-month isoniazid regimen. Nine completed treatment, one discontinued because of a maculopapular rash, and one was lost to follow-up during the COVID-19 outbreak period.

For the 383 participants who underwent screening in both years, 10 (2.6%; 95% CI: 1.4–4.7) showed IGRA conversion from negative to positive. All 10 HWs with IGRA conversion were female; 6 were registered nurses, 2 were practical nurses, and 2 were hospital housekeeping staff. Most worked in high-risk clinical areas, including the ICU/IMC (n = 7), with the remainder working in the inpatient department (n = 1) and emergency room (n = 2). A history of TB exposure was documented in 7 of the 10 HWs, whereas no identifiable exposure source was found in the remaining 3. Of the 10 HWs with IGRA conversion, 3 initiated TPT, but only 1 completed treatment; the other 2 discontinued treatment because of adverse effects.

Additionally, as an exploratory analysis to further assess the observed differences across surveillance cohorts, a restricted sensitivity analysis was performed among HWs who participated in only one surveillance cohort, thereby reducing overlap across surveillance streams. Using preplacement screening as the reference category, post-exposure surveillance remained associated with higher odds of LTBI detection after adjustment for age, sex, and patient contact category (aOR 1.97, 95% CI 1.42–2.72), whereas annual screening showed lower adjusted odds relative to preplacement screening (aOR 0.58, 95% CI 0.36–0.96) (Supplementary Table S2). However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because comparisons across surveillance cohorts remained descriptive rather than causal.

Discussion

This surveillance program found an overall LTBI proportion of 10.9% among HWs, considerably lower than the global general population estimate of 24.8% reported by the WHO using IGRA [19]. Notably, our findings are similar to those reported among HWs in Italy (13.2%) [20], a low TB burden country, and remain well below proportions reported among HWs in high-burden regions—including India (36.8%) [21], Latin America and the Caribbean (40.9%) [22], Peru (56.2%) [23], and Indonesia (23.6%) [24]. These findings may reflect, at least in part, the effects of infection control measures in this setting.

At preplacement examination, only 7.8% of newly hired HWs—predominantly young adults with minimal prior occupational exposure—were identified with LTBI, likely reflecting baseline prevalence among healthy Thai young adults. This rate is notably lower than the 21.0% regional figure reported in a recent Asian meta-analysis [25]. However, since preplacement employees differ from the general adult Thai population in age and socioeconomic background, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Although the positivity rate was low, preplacement screening remains valuable for early identification of LTBI cases at workforce entry, enabling timely intervention to prevent further TB transmission in the hospital.

By contrast, 14.4% of HWs screened following confirmed contact with active TB cases were diagnosed with LTBI. Although this rate was nearly identical to that observed in minimally exposed staff (14.7%) [7] and lower than previous reports from the same hospital (18.8%) [26], another Thai center (40.0%) [4], and from TST-based studies (38.0%–66.3%) [8, 27], this finding suggests that LTBI remains commonly detected after recognized occupational TB exposure. Post-exposure surveillance may facilitate earlier identification of LTBI among exposed staff and, when linked to timely evaluation and TPT, may contribute to TB prevention in healthcare settings [28]. The effectiveness of such strategies is supported by studies reporting annual reductions in TB incidence by 10.2% [29], a relative risk of 0.35 over five years [16], and lower hazard ratios among high-risk groups (HR 0.76) [30]. In addition, prior studies suggest that targeted surveillance may be cost-effective, particularly when focused on high-risk HWs [31].

Lastly, annual LTBI surveillance among HWs in high-risk units was implemented to optimize resource use and complement post-exposure surveillance in units with frequent TB exposure. In our setting, the overall proportion testing positive during annual surveillance was relatively low (7.5%), and among HWs screened in both annual rounds, the IGRA conversion proportion was only 2.6%, suggesting a relatively limited incremental yield from routine annual testing in this setting. This observation aligns with the 2019 CDC guideline, which recommends prioritizing preplacement and post-exposure testing over routine annual surveillance for all staff [9]. However, this finding should be interpreted in the context of the COVID-19 pandemic, as the annual surveillance period (2021–2022) coincided with major disruptions to healthcare delivery and TB services. WHO reports documented substantial declines in TB case notifications during the pandemic, reflecting disruptions in TB detection and treatment services and suggesting that the epidemiologic context during this period differed from that of routine practice [32]. Accordingly, the relatively low LTBI detection proportion observed during annual surveillance may have been influenced not only by the limited incremental yield of routine annual testing, but also by pandemic-related changes in patient volume, healthcare-seeking behavior, case detection, and opportunities for occupational exposure among HWs. At the same time, intensified respiratory infection prevention and control measures, including expanded masking and respiratory protection, may have reduced nosocomial TB transmission in some settings, whereas service disruption and resource constraints may have had the opposite effect in others [33–35]. In addition, global evidence suggests that LTBI-related preventive services and TB preventive treatment declined during the pandemic [36]. Nevertheless, our findings still support a more targeted, risk-based approach—focusing specifically on HWs with repeated high-risk exposures, such as those frequently performing AGPs on TB patients [37], rather than applying universal screening to all staff with isolated exposures.

Taken together, post-exposure surveillance yielded the highest LTBI detection proportion across the three surveillance cohorts. A similar pattern was observed in the restricted sensitivity analysis, in which post-exposure surveillance showed the strongest association with LTBI after adjustment, followed by preplacement screening. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the three surveillance approaches were implemented for different programmatic purposes and in HW populations with different demographic and occupational characteristics, screening indications, and underlying TB exposure risk. In addition, some HWs contributed to more than one surveillance cohort over time, creating overlap between cohorts and limiting the independence of direct comparisons. Therefore, the observed differences are better understood as descriptive differences in programmatic yield rather than causal differences in surveillance strategy performance.

The relatively low LTBI detection rate observed in our cohort, compared to previous periods and international data, may plausibly reflect strengthened TB control efforts nationwide and at the institutional level. Since 2017, Thailand’s “End TB” strategy has prioritized robust infection prevention and control (IPC) in healthcare settings [2], with occupational TB designated as a key national indicator monitored under the Hospital Accreditation (HA) framework [38]. At the hospital level, tiered TB-IPC programs—including rapid case identification, enhanced ventilation, and respiratory protection [10, 39]—are likely to contribute to occupational risk reduction. While direct causality cannot be established, our findings are consistent with settings where comprehensive IPC measures have been prioritized.

In addition to enhanced TB-IPC and surveillance, effective TPT delivery is integral to occupational TB control in our setting. During most of the study period, isoniazid for nine months (9 H) was the standard regimen; however, TPT uptake remained suboptimal (~ 40%). Although reasons for non-initiation were not systematically recorded, concerns about hepatotoxicity may have contributed, as reported elsewhere [40]. The adoption of a shorter, weekly rifapentine-isoniazid regimen (3HP) in 2022, in accordance with updated guidelines [41], is anticipated to improve adherence rates. TPT has consistently been overseen by chest physicians as part of a multidisciplinary team, aligning with WHO recommendations for coordinated care and long-term follow-up [39].

Given the design of our study, risk factors for LTBI among HWs should be interpreted with caution, as our primary aim was not to establish causal associations. Nevertheless, we observed that increasing age was significantly correlated with the proportion of LTBI in post-exposure cohorts—a finding likely attributable to cumulative occupational exposure and consistent with previous research [7, 22, 26, 42]. In contrast, BCG vaccination showed no significant association with LTBI proportion in our cohort (14.0% vs. 17.3%; OR = 0.78), which may reflect both uncertainty around immunization histories and BCG’s limited efficacy in adults [43]. In the post-exposure analysis, associations across exposure-priority categories (Table 3) were estimated with limited precision because some strata contained few HWs, resulting in wide confidence intervals. These subgroup findings should not be overinterpreted. Nevertheless, the overall post-exposure screening yield remained substantial, supporting the practical value of targeted follow-up after recognized TB exposure. Additionally, although AGPs and prolonged patient contact are recognized contributors to nosocomial TB transmission [44–47], no significant associations were identified in our cohort, possibly due to limitations in surveillance design and the absence of a true low-risk comparison group. During annual screening, LTBI proportion appeared higher in the indirect-patient-contact subgroup than in the direct-patient-contact subgroup. However, this occupational grouping was based on job classification rather than direct measurement of actual TB exposure intensity, and some HWs categorized as indirect-patient contact, such as EMS drivers, may still have substantial close contact with undiagnosed patients, including in enclosed environments. Moreover, the indirect-patient-contact subgroup was small and heterogeneous, which may have produced unstable estimates. Therefore, this finding should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive and requires confirmation in larger studies.

While this study provides important insights into occupational LTBI among HWs, several limitations merit consideration. First, as our main objective was descriptive rather than analytic, the study was not optimally designed to identify or quantify risk factors and may therefore be underpowered for such analyses. This was particularly relevant to the post-exposure component, where surveillance was restricted to moderate- and high-priority contacts because of resource constraints, potentially excluding some lower-risk personnel and limiting comparisons with unexposed controls. As a result, some post-exposure priority strata contained few HWs, yielding wide confidence intervals and limiting the robustness of subgroup comparisons. In addition, exposure classifications were adapted from guidelines developed in low-burden settings; their direct applicability to Thailand’s higher-incidence context remains to be validated. Second, comparisons across surveillance cohorts were limited by heterogeneity in participant characteristics, screening indications, and underlying TB exposure risk, as well as by overlap of some HWs across cohorts. Although a restricted sensitivity analysis was performed among HWs who participated in only one surveillance cohort, this exploratory analysis excluded overlapping participants and may have introduced selection bias. Accordingly, between-cohort differences should be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal. Third, two versions of the QuantiFERON assay (QFT-GIT and QFT-Plus) were used during the study period, which may have slightly affected comparability over time. Finally, the low TPT initiation rate observed in this study highlights an important occupational health gap. However, reasons for non-initiation were not systematically recorded, limiting our ability to better understand barriers to treatment uptake. Future surveillance programs should therefore include structured documentation of TPT initiation, completion, and reasons for non-initiation or discontinuation.

Based on our findings, several strategies are warranted to strengthen TB prevention among HWs. Targeted LTBI screening and prompt initiation of TPT should remain at the core of institutional elimination efforts. Post-exposure surveillance should prioritize individuals with confirmed, significant contact—using clear, context-specific risk criteria. In parallel, a comprehensive approach to TB infection control is essential, incorporating rigorous triage, administrative and environmental measures, and universal adherence to respiratory protection. Routine use of fit-tested N95 respirators during AGPs must be emphasized to minimize occupational transmission.

Future studies should consider revising contact prioritization frameworks and incorporating lower-exposure groups. Future surveillance efforts should also include systematic documentation of TPT initiation, completion, and reasons for non-initiation or discontinuation to better identify barriers to uptake and inform occupational TB prevention strategies. This would allow for more comprehensive risk assessment and generate evidence to inform more effective prevention strategies for TB transmission in healthcare settings.

Conclusions

This seven-year surveillance-based study highlights a persistent LTBI burden among HWs, with LTBI proportions of 7.8% at preplacement, 14.4% in post-exposure surveillance, and 7.5% in annual screening. Older age and higher exposure priority categories were associated with LTBI in post-exposure surveillance, although some subgroup estimates were imprecise because of sparse data. These results support continued investment in TB infection control, targeted screening, and access to preventive therapy for HWs. The low TPT initiation rate also represents an important occupational health finding and highlights the need for future surveillance systems to systematically document treatment initiation, completion, and reasons for non-initiation or discontinuation. Future strategies should focus on refining exposure classification and evaluating LTBI risk in lower-exposure groups. Ultimately, combining early TB case detection, effective treatment, and comprehensive system-level controls remains critical to protecting HWs and minimizing nosocomial transmission.
