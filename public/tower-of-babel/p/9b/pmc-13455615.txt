In memoriam: Professor Shigeto Yamawaki (January 15, 1954–June 8, 2026)—a visionary leader connecting Japan, Asia, and the world
Authors: Kazutaka Ikeda, Gabriella Gobbi, Joseph Zohar, Gregers Wegener, Pierre Blier, Siegfried Kasper, John H Krystal, Anthony Phillips, Hans-Jürgen Möller, Toshikazu Saito, Hiroyuki Uchida, Atsumi Nitta, Go Okada
Published: 2026

A life dedicated to psychiatry, neuroscience, and humanity

With profound sadness, we mark the passing of Professor Shigeto Yamawaki, a distinguished Japanese psychiatrist, neuroscientist, and neuropsychopharmacologist whose career helped shape modern neuropsychopharmacology, biological psychiatry, and translational mental health research in Japan, across Asia, and throughout the international community (Figure 1).

Professor Shigeto Yamawaki (1954–2026). Portrait photograph of Professor Shigeto Yamawaki.

For more than 4 decades, Professor Yamawaki stood at the intersection of clinical psychiatry, neuroscience, psychopharmacology, academic leadership, and international collaboration. He was not only a scholar of exceptional breadth, but also a builder of institutions, a mentor of future leaders, and a visionary who helped position Japan and Asia more prominently within global neuropsychopharmacology.

Hiroshima, peace, and the origins of a humanitarian vision

Professor Yamawaki was born in Hiroshima on January 15, 1954. He graduated from Hiroshima University School of Medicine in 1979 and entered the Department of Neuropsychiatry in the same year. After clinical training at the National Kure Hospital and research experience at Washington University School of Medicine in St. Louis, he returned to Hiroshima University, where he obtained his doctoral degree in 1985 and became Professor of Neuropsychiatry in 1990.

Professor Yamawaki was born and raised in Hiroshima, a city where the meaning of peace can never be abstract. His mother was an atomic-bomb survivor. Although he rarely spoke publicly about this personal background, many who knew him felt that it profoundly influenced his lifelong commitment to alleviating human suffering. For him, psychiatry was never merely a medical discipline. It was part of a broader effort to protect human dignity, promote mental well-being, and contribute to a more peaceful society.

This humanitarian perspective remained visible throughout his career as a clinician, scientist, educator, and academic leader.

Building biological psychiatry and translational neuroscience in Hiroshima

At Hiroshima University, Professor Yamawaki played a pivotal role in establishing one of Japan’s leading centers for biological psychiatry and neuroscience. Yet his scientific philosophy never reflected a narrow reductionism. He believed that psychiatry should be biologically rigorous, psychologically informed, and socially responsible, integrating discoveries across multiple levels of analysis to better understand and alleviate human suffering.

His scientific contributions spanned depression, stress vulnerability, emotional processing, reward systems, sleep disorders, psycho-oncology, liaison psychiatry, and brain function. Throughout his career, he sought to bridge molecular neuroscience, clinical psychiatry, and emerging neurotechnologies, helping to shape a more integrative and translational vision of mental health research.

Among his early influential contributions were studies elucidating the role of brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in mood disorders and psychopharmacology. His group demonstrated that chronic lithium treatment increases BDNF expression in the rat brain, providing important support for neurotrophic mechanisms underlying mood stabilization.1

Professor Yamawaki was also among the pioneers who introduced advanced neuroimaging approaches into Japanese psychiatry. In a landmark collaboration with Kenji Doya and colleagues, his group demonstrated that immediate and future rewards are processed by distinct cortico-basal ganglia circuits, providing important insights into reward processing and decision-making in the human brain.2 This work anticipated later efforts to understand psychiatric disorders through neural circuit dysfunction rather than symptom-based descriptions alone.

Building upon these biological and neuroimaging approaches, he subsequently investigated how environmental stress alters neural plasticity. His group demonstrated that stress induces epigenetic alterations of BDNF regulation in the hippocampus, helping to establish links between environmental stress, neuroplasticity, and depression.3 These findings contributed to emerging concepts regarding the interaction between genes, environment, and vulnerability to mood disorders.

His interest in neuroplasticity continued throughout his career. A particularly influential achievement was the discovery that the BDNF pro-peptide facilitates hippocampal long-term depression and that its actions are altered by the common Val66Met polymorphism, a finding that significantly advanced understanding of synaptic plasticity and vulnerability to psychiatric disorders.4

Even in the later stages of his career, Professor Yamawaki remained intellectually adventurous. His research expanded into biomarker-guided psychiatry, computational neuroscience, and precision medicine. He strongly advocated the development of objective neuroimaging biomarkers capable of transforming psychiatric diagnosis and treatment. At the same time, he continued to engage with fundamental theoretical questions concerning the nature of subjective experience and mental illness. In one of his final international collaborations, he co-authored a conceptual paper with Karl Friston proposing a hierarchical insular network architecture for active interoceptive inference, linking brain function, bodily awareness, and conscious feeling.5

Taken together, these contributions illustrate the remarkable breadth of Professor Yamawaki’s scientific vision. His work evolved from neurotrophic mechanisms and psychopharmacology to neuroimaging, stress biology, neuroplasticity, biomarker discovery, computational psychiatry, and theoretical neuroscience. Across more than 4 decades, he consistently sought to understand mental disorders from molecules and neural circuits to cognition, subjective experience, and human behavior. In doing so, he helped lay foundations for the transition from traditional biological psychiatry toward the emerging era of precision and computational psychiatry.

Transforming neuropsychopharmacology

While Professor Yamawaki’s scientific achievements were substantial, his greatest impact may have been his leadership within neuropsychopharmacology.

He served as Secretary of the Collegium Internationale Neuro-Psychopharmacologicum (CINP), President of the Japanese Society of Neuropsychopharmacology (JSNP), Founding President of the Asian College of Neuropsychopharmacology (AsCNP), President of the International College of Geriatric Psychoneuropharmacology, and ultimately President of CINP from 2014 to 2016 (Figures 2 and 3). He was also elected a Fellow of the American College of Neuropsychopharmacology (ACNP), reflecting the international recognition of his scientific achievements.

These positions reflected not ceremonial recognition but decades of sustained effort to strengthen international collaboration and elevate the visibility of Japanese and Asian neuropsychopharmacology on the global stage. Under his leadership, the 2016 CINP World Congress in Seoul became one of the most successful congresses in the history of the organization.

A visionary for psychiatric drug development

Professor Yamawaki possessed a remarkable vision for the future of psychiatric therapeutics.

At a time when psychotropic drug development had slowed worldwide, he argued that psychiatry required a new scientific foundation based upon neuroscience, biomarkers, neuroimaging, genomics, and translational medicine. Long before “precision psychiatry” became a widely used concept, he advocated the stratification of heterogeneous psychiatric disorders according to biological mechanisms and promoted the development of objective biomarkers capable of guiding diagnosis, treatment selection, and drug development.

Inspired by initiatives within CINP, he became a driving force behind the establishment of Public–Private Partnerships (PPPs) in Japan. Through the CINP–JSNP PPPs Task Force, he sought to bring together academia, industry, and regulatory stakeholders to overcome barriers that had hindered innovation in psychiatric drug development.

He believed that meaningful progress would require not only scientific discovery but also the creation of sustainable ecosystems capable of translating neuroscience into better treatments for patients. Many of the concepts he championed—including biomarker-guided drug development, translational neuroscience, and collaborative innovation frameworks—have since become central themes within contemporary neuropsychopharmacology.

Leadership beyond psychiatry

Professor Yamawaki’s influence extended beyond psychiatry into the broader neuroscientific community.

From 2018 to 2020, he served as Representative of the Federation of Brain Science Societies of Japan. During the COVID-19 pandemic, he helped lead national efforts to address the mental health consequences of the crisis and advocated for evidence-based policies founded upon neuroscience and public health principles.

Throughout these activities, he consistently emphasized the social responsibility of neuroscience and the importance of translating scientific knowledge into benefits for society.

The “small giant”

Many colleagues described Professor Yamawaki as a “small giant.” Though modest in physical stature, he possessed extraordinary energy, determination, foresight, and leadership.

He seemed constantly in motion, always considering the next challenge, the next collaboration, and the next opportunity to advance psychiatry and neuroscience. His enthusiasm was infectious, and his ability to inspire others was exceptional.

Those who knew him well often joked that he resembled a tuna fish that would die if it stopped swimming. Professor Yamawaki himself recognized the humor—and the truth—in this comparison.

Mentor, colleague, and friend

Professor Yamawaki was deeply committed to mentoring younger generations.

Countless psychiatrists, neuroscientists, and neuropsychopharmacologists benefited from his guidance, encouragement, and trust. He actively promoted younger colleagues into international leadership positions, introduced them to global networks, and instilled confidence that Japan and Asia could contribute not only as participants but also as leaders in world psychiatry and neuroscience.

Many of those whom he mentored now occupy prominent academic and leadership positions throughout Japan and internationally. This living network of colleagues and former trainees may represent one of his most enduring legacies.

Courage, resilience, and unwavering commitment

In later life, Professor Yamawaki survived a sudden cardiopulmonary arrest at home thanks to the prompt actions of his family and the outstanding care provided at Hiroshima University Hospital.

Following pacemaker implantation, he would occasionally remark, with characteristic humor, that he had been given a heart that could no longer stop.

Yet what impressed colleagues most was not the story itself but what followed. His physical adversity never diminished his sense of mission. Rather than slowing down, he continued to devote himself tirelessly to science, education, academic leadership, and international collaboration. In the years that followed, he continued to provide leadership to JSNP, AsCNP, CINP, the Federation of Brain Science Societies of Japan, and many other organizations.

Research as hope

Research was, for Professor Yamawaki, not merely an academic pursuit. It was a source of hope—for patients, for families, for young psychiatrists, and for society itself.

He devoted his life to understanding the brain, advancing neuropsychopharmacology, and relieving human suffering. Yet perhaps his greatest achievement was demonstrating that psychiatry can be both scientifically rigorous and profoundly humane.

His legacy lives on in the institutions he strengthened, the societies he led, the scientific discoveries he advanced, and the generations of clinicians and researchers he inspired.

In 2024, he received the CINP Pioneer Award in recognition of his lifelong contributions to neuropsychopharmacology.

A loss beyond measure

We extend our deepest condolences to his family, friends, students, and collaborators.

His absence will be deeply felt in Hiroshima, throughout Japan, across Asia, and within the worldwide neuropsychopharmacology community.

Professor Shigeto Yamawaki was, in every sense, a small giant whose vision, humanity, and leadership transformed the field and touched countless lives.

May his memory continue to inspire future generations of psychiatrists, neuroscientists, and neuropsychopharmacologists.

The international neuropsychopharmacology community has lost one of its most visionary leaders, but his influence will continue through the countless colleagues, trainees, institutions, and scientific initiatives that he inspired.
