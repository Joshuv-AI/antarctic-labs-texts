ICIsAtlas reveals a suppressive NK cell niche in pan-cancer immunotherapy profiles
Authors: Yumo Xie, Jinxin Lin, Haotian Liu, Ying Xiong, Junyi Han, Ziying Huang, Jingrong Weng, Zixiao Wan, Peisi Li, Puning Wang, Xiaoxia Liu, Linping Wu, Qian Cai, Meijin Huang, Yanxin Luo, Xiaolin Wang, Huichuan Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have reshaped the treatment in multiple tumors. However, a substantial proportion of patients exhibit limited responses. The lack of harmonized, large-scale pan-cancer ICI datasets coupled with accessible analysis tools hinders the systematic discovery of response biomarkers and resistance mechanisms. Therefore, we developed a comprehensive resource named ICIsAtlas, which encompassed curated transcriptomic and clinical data from 1,268 ICI-treated patients across eight tumor types, with an accompanying R package implementing a complete workflow for deconvolution and biomarker evaluation. Applying the ICIsAtlas framework, a systematic pan-cancer analysis was performed. We identified the universal signatures that were specific for ICI response, including cooperative interactions among favorable immune cells. In addition, we discovered a competitive cell community and SERPING1 + VEGFA+ Natural Killer (NK) cell-mediated immunosuppressive niche in non-responders, which were further validated with single-cell and multiplex immunohistochemistry data. The ICIsAtlas resource and R package represent a powerful, publicly available platform for hypothesis generation and biomarker discovery, which could be used to develop the next-generation biomarkers and therapeutic targets to improve tumor immunotherapy.Subject terms: Data integration, Cancer microenvironment

Introduction

By exerting anti-tumor effects through augmenting adaptive immunity,1 the immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting cytotoxic T-lymphocyte antigen 4 (CTLA-4), programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) and its ligands, programmed cell death one ligand 1 (PD-L1), have achieved remarkable results in series types of cancer2–4. However, a substantial proportion of patients, estimated at 60–70%, exhibit primary resistance to these therapies, and the detailed molecular and cellular mechanisms underpinning this resistance remain to be fully elucidated2,5. Nonetheless, establishing universally reliable biomarkers to identify possible responders is challenging since many factors, concerning the immune contexture and tumor foreignness, are related to treatment response6,7, while they can vary greatly through different cancer types.

Multiple cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) have been found to be associated with ICIs response, such as tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs)8,9. Based on the density and spatial distribution of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, tumors can be classified into three distinct immune phenotypes: hot (inflamed), altered, and cold (immune-desert)10,11. Hot tumors are characterized by abundant CD8+ T cells within the tumor parenchyma and generally exhibit favorable responses to ICIs12. Conversely, cold tumors lack pre-existing TILs and are typically resistant to ICIs. Whereas altered tumors represent two complex intermediate TIL states, which can be excluded or immunosuppressed, with niches composed of components like cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and tumor-associated macrophages. These components can create an immunosuppressive niche that counteracts anti-tumor immunity and the response to ICIs13.

The advent of high-throughput sequencing has led to the generation of a wealth of transcriptomic data from ICI clinical trials, creating an unprecedented opportunity to investigate the TME at a large scale. These datasets, however, are often disparate, processed using different pipelines, and lack harmonized clinical annotations, making pan-cancer meta-analysis a significant technical hurdle. Despite these advances, intertumoral heterogeneity remains a significant barrier to establishing universal biomarkers. Understanding how comprehensive cell composition determines ICIs response requires large-scale cohort analysis. Recently, computational deconvolution algorithms have provided a solution by inferring high-resolution cell types and states directly from bulk transcriptomic profiles14–18. These methods enable the deep exploration of existing clinical trial data to reveal TME features that were previously hidden in bulk analysis.

Intercellular communication is a core organizing principle of the tumor microenvironment, coordinating immune activation or suppression, and is therefore tightly linked to response and resistance to ICIs. A growing set of computational frameworks is available to infer cell–cell interactions from both bulk19–21 and single-cell transcriptomic data22. Bulk RNA-seq-based approaches can leverage large patient cohorts with clinical outcomes, providing improved statistical power and enabling robust discovery of response-associated interaction patterns at the population level. While single-cell-based methods offer higher cellular resolution and pathway-level directionality, facilitating mechanistic interpretation of how specific cell states may communicate to shape ICIs response.

In order to describe the profiles of the immune microenvironment and their association with the response to ICIs, in this study, we compiled a pan-cancer atlas from 17 studies, encompassing 1268 patients treated with ICIs across eight tumor types. We employed the transcriptomic deconvolution approaches to perform a quantitative analysis of types and states of tumor-infiltrating cells across multiple cancers and developed an R package called ICIsAtlas. This framework was then applied to dissect the cellular and network-level features of ICIs response and resistance, leading to the discovery and validation of a novel SERPING1 + VEGFA + NK cell-mediated immunosuppressive niche in non-responders. This work provides both a valuable community resource and a powerful demonstration of its utility for uncovering new biological insights and therapeutic targets in cancer immunotherapy.

Results

Transcriptomic profile identifies an immune-active tumor microenvironment in ICIs responders

The workflow of this study is summarized in Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1. A meta-dataset was generated by systematically compiling and processing publicly available studies that included pre-treatment bulk tumor transcriptomic profiles and associated clinical response data for patients receiving ICIs. The final atlas comprises 1268 patients spanning eight major cancer types: melanoma, urothelial cancer, clear cell renal cell carcinoma, non-small cell lung cancer, breast cancer, esophageal cancer, colorectal cancer, and pancreatic cancer. Patient response was unified into a binary classification of responders (complete or partial response) and non-responders (stable or progressive disease) based on RECIST criteria or as reported in the original studies. This curated dataset represents a significant resource for the community, providing a harmonized platform for pan-cancer meta-analysis of ICI response. Detailed information for each cohort is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

First, we conducted gene expression analysis to investigate the transcriptomic differences between ICIs responders and non-responders. We applied a random-effect model23 to merge the fold changes and standard errors. This identified 446 common differentially expressed genes (CDEGs) that were universally associated with ICIs response across different tumors (Fig. 2A, C). To highlight the most robust features of ICIs' response, we curated 40 representative genes for Fig. 2A from the top-identified genes (see Supplementary Fig. 2A and Supplementary Table 3A for the full list). These genes were selected based on high sign consistency across 10 cohorts. Responders exhibited significantly higher expression of genes associated with cytotoxic immune cells, such as CD8A, CD8B, GZMA and NKG7, as well as immune checkpoint molecules like IDO1, LAG3, and PDCD1. Conversely, genes in the Notch signaling pathway were downregulated in responders. While most of these top genes reflect immune activation in responders, we found Notch signaling targets HEYL and HEY2 represent the resistance pathway (P < 0.001, Supplementary Table 3B). Similarly, differential single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) revealed 406 common differentially enriched pathways (CDEPs). Notably, both immuno-stimulatory pathways (e.g., T cell receptor signaling) and immunosuppressive pathways (e.g., PD-1 signaling) were more active in responders (Supplementary Table 3B). Together, these findings suggested a pre-existing immune-active TME with a complex regulatory network as a key feature of ICI responders (Fig. 2B, D, and Supplementary Fig. 2B).

Cell atlas reveals favorable cell states and cooperative interactions for ICIs response

Considering the distinct immunological features in the TME between the ICIs responders and non-responders, we sought to identify the infiltrating immune cells that were universally associated with ICIs response. Based on the cell atlas resolved with CIBERSORTx transcriptomic deconvolution, we observed that ICIs responders shared a common feature with abundant M1 macrophages, follicular helper T cells, neutrophils, activated NK cells, CD8 + T cells, and activated CD4+ memory T cells in TME, which were defined as favorable cells (Supplementary Fig. 3A, Supplementary Table 4). In the sensitivity analysis, similar findings were observed in melanoma, urothelial cancer, and renal cell carcinoma, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4). Furthermore, the correlation analysis revealed that more co-occurring immune cells were found in responders, while more mutually exclusive immune cells were found in non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Specifically, the delta correlation (Δrho) reveals that favorable immune cells, such as activated NK cells and M1 Macrophages, exhibit stronger cooperative co-occurrence in responders, while showing a shift toward negative correlation with immunosuppressive components like M2 Macrophages in non-responders (Supplementary Fig. 3C).

Next, we resolved the cell atlas at cell-state resolution using EcoTyper in responders and non-responders. A cell state refers to a transcriptionally defined functional program or phenotype within a specific cell lineage. By modeling digitally purified cell types as mixtures of these discrete transcriptional programs, we can distinguish specific functional subsets that broad cell-type classifications might overlook. We found 22 cell states that were universally associated with ICIs response across different types of tumors (Fig. 3A, Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6), among which 16 were favorable, and six were unfavorable for ICIs response. Overall, we revealed the specific cell states with expressed marker genes that may contribute to ICIs' response or resistance. To assess potential tumor-type heterogeneity, we repeated the meta-analysis stratified by tumor group (melanoma vs carcinoma) for all recovered cell states. Pooled random-effects effect sizes were significantly correlated between melanoma and carcinoma (Pearson R = 0.555, p < 0.001), and most cell states exhibited concordant directions (51/69). All 22 cell states significant in the overall pan-cancer analysis retained consistent directionality in both tumor groups, suggesting broadly shared response-associated programs with tumor-context differences largely reflected in effect magnitude and statistical power. Meta-regression for tumor–group interaction did not identify any cell state with significant effect modification (minimum FDR = 0.962). Robustness was further supported by leave-one-tumor-type-out sensitivity analyses across eight tumor types, where none of the 22 pan-cancer significant states showed sign reversals and effect estimates varied modestly (Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7; Supplementary Tables 7 and 8). Together, these sensitivity analyses support the robustness of our findings across diverse histological contexts.

We found that the favorable and unfavorable cell states were abundant in the proficient-inflamed (pInflam) and deficient-inflamed (dInflam) cell ecotype, which were defined by the Ecotypes 9/10 and 1 that were reported in the previous study15, respectively. The favorable cell states included the exhausted CD8+ T cells (featured by LAG3), central memory CD8+ T cells (featured by BTLA and GZMK), and Tregs (CTLA4+ CD4+ T cell) was favorable for the ICIs response. The unfavorable cell states included pro-tumoral M2 macrophages, cancer-associated fibroblast-1 (CAF-1), pro-angiogenic epithelial cells, and multiple cell states with marker genes that have not been annotated or understood, such as PDPN+ dendrite cells, ANGPTL2+ NID2+ epithelial cells, NTM+ mast cells, and POSTN+ COL10A1+ Fibroblast, which may provide novel markers and targets for cancer immunotherapy.

Finally, we performed the correlation analysis for the 69 cell states in the responders and non-responders, respectively. Favorable cell-state pairs displayed a consistent upward shift in co-association in responders compared with non-responders (83.3% of Favor–Favor pairs showed higher correlation in responders; Fig. 3B–D, Supplementary Fig. 8). In contrast, correlations involving unfavorable states tended to decrease (Favor–Unfavor, P = 0.02; Unfavor–Unfavor, P < 0.001), consistent with greater segregation between opposing cellular programs (Fig. 3B–D; Supplementary Fig. 9, Supplementary Table 9). By utilizing the atlas, this analysis highlights that an abundance of favorable immune cells engaged in cooperative interactions is a universal hallmark of ICI response, and the newly identified cell states with marker genes may serve as novel markers and targets for ICIs.

Dissecting the cell states in tumors with different mutation and neoantigen loads for precision immunotherapy

On the basis of the ICIs response-related landscape of cell states in TME, we sought to assess the association of the response-specific cell states with other universal markers for ICIs response, including TMB, TNL, and MSI status. In both our cohorts and TCGA datasets, we found that the pInflam-CE9 favorable cell states were consistently and significantly associated with higher TMB and neoantigen load in multiple tumors, such as breast and colon cancers, while this association was heterogeneous in the pInflam-CE10 favorable cell states (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. 10), suggesting a relatively TMB-independent immune program. We further evaluated survival in TCGA by the pInflam-CE10 program activity. In TMB-low tumors (≤10 mut/Mb), CE10-high, defined as the upper 50th percentile within each tumor type, was associated with improved survival in tumor-type-stratified Cox models and remained significant after adjustment for continuous TMB (HR = 0.705, p < 0.001; TMB p = 0.646) (Supplementary Fig. 11). Across all tumors, CE10-high remained associated with improved OS after TMB adjustment (HR = 0.747; p = 0.001). In the TMB-high tumor, CE10-high was not significantly associated with OS (HR = 1.46; p = 0.18), and interaction testing suggested potential attenuation of the CE10 association in TMB-high tumors (interaction p = 0.074). Together, these results support that pInflam-CE10 reflects a favorable program that is not simply a surrogate for high TMB. Besides, we found that the favorable cell states were more abundant in MSI-High tumors compared with non-MSI-High tumors (Supplementary Fig. 12A). In the sensitivity analysis, we discovered that TMB-High urothelial, breast, and colorectal tumors had more abundant favorable cell states (Supplementary Fig. 12B). Therefore, these findings may improve TMB-based drugs and marker panels in precision immunotherapy.

Next, to investigate the prognostic value of response-specific cell states, we quantify the superiority of favorable cell states over unfavorable cell states in TME for ICIs response (FavTME). Across 17 ICI-treated cohorts, FavTME was first tested for association with objective response using per-study logistic regression and summarized via random-effects meta-analysis. FavTME was significantly associated with objective response (pooled OR = 1.44, 95% CI 1.26–1.65; p < 0.001) with minimal heterogeneity (I² = 3.2%) (Fig. 4B). Using per-study Cox regression (HR per 1 SD FavTME) and random-effects meta-analysis, FavTME was associated with improved PFS (pooled HR = 0.79, 95% CI 0.70–0.90; p < 0.001; I² = 28.5%; Fig. 4C). For OS, the association was directionally favorable but did not reach statistical significance, with moderate between-study heterogeneity (pooled HR = 0.87, 95% CI, 0.73–1.04; p = 0.126; I² = 53.6%; Fig. 4D). To determine whether FavTME merely reflects a general prognostic marker, we evaluated its association with OS in non-ICI-treated TCGA tumors from the same cancer types. The TCGA cohort was predominantly composed of non-metastatic tumors (3036/3261), whereas most of the ICI-treated cohorts had advanced or metastatic disease. After adjustment for distant metastasis status and stratification by cancer type, FavTME was not significantly associated with OS in TCGA tumors (pooled HR = 0.91, 95% CI 0.81–1.02; p = 0.115; I² = 59.0%; Supplementary Fig. 13). Collectively, these results establish FavTME not as a general prognostic signature, but as a specific biomarker that identifies an immune contexture conducive to a favorable response to ICIs.

Network analysis in ICIsAtlas exposes a competitive cellular community in non-responders

To further explore the role of cell–cell interactions in ICIs' response, we applied the co-occurrence network to characterize the cell–cell interactions in responders and non-responders. We applied the methodology developed by Faust K. et al.24 to determine the co-occurrence threshold and leverage renormalization and bootstrap to mitigate the spurious correlations led by the proportional perturbation. Each cell state was treated as one node, and cell–cell interactions were treated as positive or negative edges.

Overall, favorable and unfavorable cell states could be well distinguished in the co-occurrence networks, and a notable difference in the network topology can be observed between responders and non-responders (Fig. 5A). More active connections were observed among the favorable cell states in responders, whereas more active connections could be found among unfavorable cell states in non-responders. Additionally, more negative interactions were observed in the non-responders, and the unfavorable cells were more engaged in the interactions (Supplementary Tables 10 and 11, Supplementary Fig. 14A, B), indicating the active unfavorable cells and a competitive cell–cell interaction in non-responders.

The CXCL9+ macrophage was the only hub cell in the responders, while four hub cells were identified in the non-responders. Most notably, in the non-responder network, two cell states not significantly associated with response on their own, SERPING1+ NK cells and SIGLEC5+ neutrophils, emerged as critical bridging nodes, connecting the unfavorable cell community to other cell types. The PCA and density plot also revealed that the cell–cell interacting features of unfavorable cells were similar with other cells in non-responders (Fig. 5B). In the network comparison, these bridging cells showed high variance (Dn-score) in their connectivity patterns between the two networks, suggesting they play a pivotal, context-dependent role in shaping the immunosuppressive niche of non-responders (Supplementary Table 12).

Single-cell analysis and validation of molecular targets in SERPING1+ NK cells as a key hub for therapeutic targeting

Given the role of SERPING1+ NK cell in immunosuppressive niche uncovered through the ICIsAtlas, we sought to further validate this finding and dissect its cellular state at the scRNA-seq level from independent cohorts. We found that SERPING1+ NK cells had more interactions with other cells in non-responders compared with responders (Fig. 6A, B, Supplementary Figs. 15A, B and 16).

Further analysis of ligand–receptor (L–R) pairs identified the constant expression of ANXA1 on SERPING1+ NK and its interaction with macrophages, which has been reported to suppress their proinflammatory function25 (Fig. 6C, Supplementary Fig. 15C, D). This finding may partially explain its role in forming an immunosuppressive niche. Moreover, non-responders exhibited more activated pro-angiogenic and immune-exclusion L–R pairs involving SERPING1+ NK, such as VEGFA-VEGFR1 and, a marker of decidual NK cells26, and TGFB1−(TGFBR1 + TGFBR2). We identified significant signaling changes in SERPING1+ NK cells between responders and non-responders, such as VEGF, OX40, KIT, protease-activated receptors (PARs), and ANGPTL (Fig. 6D). Furthermore, we found that SERPING1+ NK cells were a sender of VEGF signal in non-responders (Fig. 6E, F, Supplementary Figs. 15E, F, 18 and 19). scRNA-seq data revealed that VEGFA expression was significantly higher in SERPING1⁺ NK cells from non-responders compared to responders. The non-responder-specific upregulation of VEGFA was most pronounced in the SERPING1⁺ NK subpopulation (Supplementary Fig. 16A–C). To assess the impact of this VEGFA-rich niche, we evaluated the functional states of “receiver” macrophages (CXCL9+ and CCR2+ MMs). In non-responders, both macrophage populations exhibited significantly higher enrichment of angiogenic signatures (p < 0.001), suggesting a functional reprogramming toward a pro-angiogenic, immunosuppressive state (Supplementary Fig. 16D). This was further corroborated by analyzing a pan-cancer NK cell atlas25. Although the frequency of VEGFA-expressing NK cells was lower in this atlas, likely due to the inclusion of cells from peripheral blood and normal tissues (n = 3063). Nevertheless, VEGFA+ subset of SERPING1+ NK cells showed higher levels of suppressive markers (Supplementary Fig. 16E–G). The currently approved drugs are available for most of these targets, which have been reported to be used combined with ICIs as immune adjuvants (Supplementary Table 13)27–32.

Finally, to confirm these findings in patient tissue, multiplex immunohistochemistry was performed on 16 pre-treatment tumor samples from colorectal cancer patients (Fig. 6G, Supplementary Fig. 14G, Supplementary Table 14). This experimental validation confirmed that while the overall density of SERPING1+ NK cells was not different (Fig. 6H), the expression of VEGFA on these cells was significantly higher in non-responders (n = 10) compared to responders (n = 6) (p = 0.011; Fig. 6I). Together, using the ICIsAtlas, we revealed a novel NK cell states and molecular targets in ICIs non-responders, which could be utilized to sensitize immunotherapy.

Discussion

The TME marks the soil of tumors that can decisively influence the treatment outcomes of ICIs11,12. In this study, we developed ICIsAtlas, an R package with a curated dataset designed to systematically analyze the TME in response to ICIs. By applying this new framework, we identified an immune-active transcriptomic profile in ICIs responders. The resolved cell atlas reveals that a pre-treatment TME characterized by enriched favorable cells, along with enhanced positive cell interactions, is a hallmark of ICI responders. In contrast, non-responders exhibited a competitive cell community. Notably, patients with TMB-Low but enriched pInflam-CE10 favorable cells may derive similar benefits from ICIs as TMB-High patients. The ICIsAtlas framework further facilitated the discovery of a SERPING1+ VEGFA+ NK cell-mediated immunosuppressive niche as a key mechanism of ICIs resistance, a finding we subsequently validated in independent cohorts and with scRNA-seq analysis.

Currently, foundational resources such as The Cancer Immunome Atlas (TCIA)33, TIDE34, and massive transcriptomic analyses have provided rich immunogenomic data and indispensable gene-level predictive models for cross-cohort benchmarking35–37. More recently, pan-cancer predictive frameworks, including PredictIO38, VIGex39, and IOSelect40, have further advanced signature-centric modeling by proposing compact classifiers and/or integrated transcriptomic–genomic scores for cross-cohort prediction. While these tools are essential for risk stratification and response prediction, the challenge of mapping tissues with complex TME and multicellular networks across massive clinical cohorts remains. ICIsAtlas is specifically designed to bridge this gap. By providing a harmonized and multi-resolution atlas that profiles 69 functional cell states across >1200 ICI-treated patients, it enables researchers to elevate analysis from single genes to specific multicellular programs, providing the robust statistical power required for clinical endpoint associations and discovery for genes/cells-of-interest. Furthermore, analyzing intercellular communication is critical for understanding TME resistance. While seminal studies have developed specialized algorithms to directly deconvolve cellular crosstalk from bulk tissue19–21, ICIsAtlas employs a distinct topological approach. Rather than proposing a new inference algorithm, our framework leverages the robust co-occurrence network topologies to identify the network rewiring. By identifying the topological bridges exemplified by the dramatic shift of SERPING1+ VEGFA+ NK cells into a central hub connecting disparate immunosuppressive communities in non-responders, we provide a systematic pipeline for discovering network-level biomarkers, which can subsequently be orthogonally validated using established mechanistic single-cell inference and spatial protein assays.

The identification of 16 favorable and 6 unfavorable cell states that are conserved across eight tumor types provides a universal cellular signature of response. Among the favorable cell states, the top one is an exhausted/effective memory CD8+ T cell. This subpopulation represents the dysfunctional T cells driven by antigen recognition in the TME and is an indicator of tumor reactivity41. Hence, it would undergo a proliferative burst after the administration of ICIs42. In contrast, the late-stage differentiated effector CD8+ T cells (LAIR1+ CD8 T) are not significantly associated with response. Besides, consistent with the gene and pathway analysis findings, the Treg cells (CTLA4+ CD4+ T cell) were also a favorable cell state. Elevated infiltration of immune-suppressive Treg cells is associated with chronic self-antigen exposure, as well as disease progression and poor survival in cancer43. In ICIs treatment, as CTLA-4 and PD-1 are highly expressed in Treg cells, their considerable depletion after administration can usually evoke a good ICIs response44. Hence, high infiltration of Treg cells could mark a substantial depletion after ICIs and imply a potential responder. Interestingly, most identified favorable cell states were from the pInflam-CE9 and pInflam-CE10, and the unfavorable cell states were from the dInflam-CE1. They were also reported to be correlated with better and worse survival in the TCGA database15.

Higher TMB is expected to convey a higher number of neoantigens that trigger immune recognition and anti-tumor immune response45,46, and which has been recognized as a universal marker for predicting ICIs response. However, a study demonstrated that TMB and neoantigen load failed to predict the infiltration of CD8+ T cells and ICIs response in certain tumors, which indicates that the association of TMB with ICIs response may depend on the immune cells in the tumor microenvironment47. In line with this finding, we found that the favorable cell states showed a positive correlation with TMB and TNL in some tumors, though this correlation was heterogeneous in others. Moreover, this positive correlation was limited to pInflam-CE9 cell states characterized by IFN-γ signaling, while the pInflam-CE10 favorable cells, characterized by B cell immunity, were not correlated with TMB and neoantigen. One plausible explanation could be that the enriched pInflam-CE10 favorable cells might indicate the abundance of tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs) or their preliminary form, which are characterized by the aggravation of immune cells in non-lymphoid tissues and could boost ICIs response48,49. Our findings suggest that the association between TMB and response to ICIs may depend on the presence of pInflam-CE9 favorable cells in the TME, while pInflam-CE10 favorable cells play a more critical role in mediating ICIs' response for patients with lower TMB.

The TME can be viewed as a network consisting of stromal cells, immune cells, and the extracellular matrix, which contribute to its immunosuppressive nature and the exhaustion of T cells50,51. Network biomarkers could provide a quantifiable and robust form to characterize diseases and elaborate information unavailable from individual biomarkers52. By constructing co-occurrence networks based on the cell-state derived from ICIsAtlas, we found a fundamental difference in network topology between responders and non-responders. In the responder network, favorable cell states showed higher centrality, indicating a cooperative immune community. Strikingly, in the non-responder network, SERPING1+ NK cells emerged as a critical bridging node, functionally connecting otherwise disparate, unfavorable cell communities. Interestingly, like the identified six unfavorable cells, SERPING1+ NK cells were part of the dInflam-CE1 ecotype. While these SERPING1+ NK cells were not independently predictive of ICI response, their dramatic shift in network role points to a significant, context-dependent functional plasticity. This discovery, enabled by the high-resolution cell-state data from ICIsAtlas, highlights a new class of network biomarker that could be targeted to disrupt immunosuppressive TME structures.

NK cells are often referred to as the first responders in anti-cancer immunity53, yet sustained exposure to antigens and tumor cells can render them hyporesponsive and desensitized54,55. Moreover, emerging evidence suggests that NK cells can also adopt immunosuppressive or regulatory roles, suppressing T cell function56,57, regulating macrophages25 and remodeling microenvironment58. Our findings provide a high-resolution view into this duality within the context of ICI resistance. We identified a specific SERPING1+ NK cell state that, while present in all patients, adopts a profoundly different functional profile in non-responders. While these cells constitutively express the immunosuppressive marker ANXA125,59, our analysis revealed the activation of distinct signaling pathways, most notably the VEGF pathway, exclusively in the non-responding TME. By multiplex immunohistochemistry, we confirmed that the level of VEGFA on SERPING1+ NK cells, rather than their infiltrating density, distinguishes ICI responders from non-responders. The expression of VEGFA marks the decidual NK cells, which are involved in vascular formation26, whereas in TME, NK cells expressing VEGFA have been shown to promote tumor growth by enhancing angiogenesis58,60. Our work therefore clarifies a specific mechanism of resistance, providing a strong rationale for the therapeutic combination of ICI and VEGF inhibitors and identifying a rich set of additional non-responder-specific pathways (Supplementary Table 9) as promising future targets.

This study has limitations. The FavTME score, while prognostic, has not yet been developed into a clinical-grade predictive biomarker with a defined cutoff. However, we have summarized the cell markers that can predict survival after ICIs treatments. The TMB and TNL data available for the compiled cohorts were relatively sparse, although analyses in TCGA data showed consistent findings. The value of pInflam-CE10 favorable cell states for predicting ICIs response in TMB-Low tumors should be validated in future studies. Finally, tumor-type-specific behaviors may not be fully captured by the current pan-cancer framework, and future studies using spatially resolved approaches will be needed to directly define VEGFA-mediated TME reprogramming by SERPING1+ NK cells.

In conclusion, we present ICIsAtlas, a tissue-agnostic cell atlas of the TME that includes detailed cell profiles and cell–cell interactions, highlighting potential molecular targets that may influence ICIs treatment outcomes. The application of this framework has led to the identification of an immunosuppressive NK cell state, SERPING1+ NK cells, which play a pivotal role in bridging the gap within the TME cellular network. This compiled dataset and cell atlas can be readily utilized in future studies and validations, with the potential to inform the development of next-generation biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets.

Methods

Dataset search strategy and eligibility criteria

In 2023, we systematically surveyed publicly available ICI-treated cohorts with transcriptomic profiles by querying GEO and PubMed screening references/links from relevant publications. Search terms combined (i) immune checkpoint therapy keywords (“immune checkpoint”, “checkpoint inhibitor”, “PD-1”, “PD-L1”, “CTLA-4”, “ICB”, “ICI”, “nivolumab”, “pembrolizumab”, “atezolizumab”, “durvalumab”, “avelumab”, “ipilimumab”) with (ii) study type (“Expression profiling by array”, “Expression profiling by high throughput sequencing”, “RNA-seq”, “transcriptome”) and (iii) outcome keywords (“response”, “responder”, “non-responder”, “RECIST”, “complete response”, “partial response”, “stable disease”, “progressive disease”, “survival”, “progression-free survival”, “overall survival”). Studies were included if they provided human baseline/pre-treatment tumor bulk transcriptomic data with patient-level response and/or survival endpoints after ICI and sufficient metadata to harmonize outcome definitions and sample annotations. Studies were excluded if they lacked baseline tumor tissue (e.g., blood-only/liquid biopsy), lacked patient-level outcomes, contained only on-treatment/post-treatment sampling, provided only targeted panels not comparable to genome-wide transcriptomes, were inaccessible, or represented duplicate/overlapping cohorts. A summary of included datasets and curated variables is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Tables 1, 2 and 6B.

Study populations and immunotherapy response evaluation

To systematically discover the universal ICIs markers and therapeutic targets for sensitizing ICIs, we generated a compiled dataset that was derived from the previous studies investigating the ICIs with transcriptomic profiling data in various tumors, including melanoma61–66, urothelial cancer67–69, clear cell renal cell carcinoma70–72, non-small cell lung cancer73,74, breast cancer75, esophageal cancer76, colorectal cancer77, and pancreatic cancer77. The evaluation of ICIs treatment outcomes follows the report of the original contributors and the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST)78. Briefly, the response to ICIs was unified, and patients were categorized into responders and non-responders. The responders included the patients with complete and partial responses, while the non-responders included the patients with stable and progressive diseases. A total of 1268 ICI-treated patients were included in this study. The regimens of ICIs comprise anti-PD-1, anti-PD-L1, anti-PD-1/PD-L1, and anti-PD-1 combined with anti-CTLA-4. The details of treatment outcome evaluation and study design for each type of cancer in the compiled dataset were summarized in Supplementary Table 1.

To validate the identified markers, we collected the resected tumor samples prior to ICIs treatment from the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University. The tumor samples were dissected and formalin-fixed. The fixed tissues were then dissected into serial transverse slices, subsequently embedded in paraffin, and processed into sections for staining. All patients provided written informed consent. Ethical approvals were gained from the Institutional Review Board of Sun Yat-sen University (2021ZSLYEC-366).

Transcriptomic analysis in compiled patient cohorts

We generated a unified meta-workflow to identify transcriptomic features consistently associated with ICIs response across tumor types. The workflow takes gene-level expression matrices as input (RNA-seq raw count matrices and two cohorts with author-deposited TPM; normalized microarray matrices). We standardized downstream normalization and statistical testing across cohorts starting from these gene-by-sample matrices. Gene symbols were harmonized to the current HGNC nomenclature prior to analysis. For RNA-seq count cohorts, low-abundance genes were filtered (CPM ≤ 1), followed by TMM normalization (edgeR) and limma-voom transformation; differential expression between responders and non-responders was then tested using limma empirical Bayes moderated t-statistics. For TPM or microarray/pre-normalized cohorts, expression values were log2-transformed and analyzed using limma-trend with the same responder vs. non-responder design. Cohort-specific log2-fold changes and corresponding standard errors were subsequently integrated across cohorts using MetaVolcanoR79 under a random-effects model to account for between-study heterogeneity. Genes were considered significant if the random-effects P-value < 0.05 and the direction of change was consistent in more than half of the included studies. To identify response-associated pathways, we computed single-sample Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (ssGSEA) scores per sample and compared ssGSEA scores between responders and non-responders within each cohort using limma moderated t-statistics; pathway-level effects were then combined across cohorts using the same random-effects meta-analysis framework.

Cell atlas resolved with transcriptomic deconvolution approaches

To quantify the intratumoral abundance of different cell types, we applied the CIBERSORTx14 web portal (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). We employed the “Impute Cell Fractions” module with the LM22 signature matrix and its corresponding source gene expression profile. Input data consisted of TPM-normalized matrices for RNA-seq and pre-normalized matrices for microarray data. Bulk-mode batch correction was enabled to account for platform-specific differences, and quantile normalization was disabled for RNA-seq samples.

For high-resolution cell-state analysis, we applied EcoTyper15 to deconvolute bulk tumor transcriptomic profiles and generate the cell atlas at the cell-state resolution. EcoTyper resolves 69 carcinoma cell states and assigns them to 10 carcinoma ecotypes (CE1–CE10), where each ecotype represents a recurrent multicellular community defined by coordinated co-occurrence of multiple cell states across tumors. We have integrated the deconvolution pipeline from EcoTyper in the function recover_cellstates() in our R package. Using its recovery mode, we quantify the published carcinoma reference cell states and their ecotype (CE1–CE10) community assignments from bulk transcriptomes. The deconvolutional results with a Monte Carlo P-value < 0.05 were considered reliable. The predominant tumor ecosystem subtypes (ecotypes) of each case were also yielded. The detailed information and cell state abundance of 1268 patient samples were provided in Supplementary Table 6.

Identification of favorable and unfavorable cells for ICIs response

The estimated abundance of each cell type was normalized using Z-score transformation across samples within each cohort. The odds ratio (OR) and associated standard error (SE) of each cell in predicting ICIs' response were calculated by Logistic regression. To synthesize these findings, we applied a random-effects model using the R package ‘meta’, which incorporates between-study heterogeneity to yield a conservative pooled estimate. A cell state with a random-effect P-value < 0.05 and pooled OR > 1.0 was defined as favorable, while those with a P-value < 0.05 and OR < 1.0 were considered unfavorable.

FavTME definition and association testing

FavTME was computed from EcoTyper cell-state abundances by summing the abundances of cell states identified as favorable and subtracting the sum of unfavorable cell states (FavTME = Σfavorable−Σunfavorable), where favorable/unfavorable sets were defined a priori from pan-cancer meta-analysis of per-cell-state logistic regression odds ratios with response in ICI-treated cohorts, without cohort-specific optimization. For objective response, within each ICI cohort, we fit a logistic regression model of objective response on within-cohort standardized FavTME (per 1 SD increase), restricting to cohorts with ≥1 responders and ≥1 non-responders. Per-cohort log(OR) estimates were synthesized using random-effects meta-analysis, and heterogeneity was quantified by I². For survival, within each cohort we fit Cox proportional hazards models for PFS or OS using within-cohort standardized FavTME (per 1 SD), and combined per-cohort logHR estimates by random-effects meta-analysis. Kaplan–Meier curves were generated by within-cohort median stratification for visualization.

Co-association patterns between responders and non-responders

To compare co-association patterns between responders and non-responders, we computed Spearman correlations of cell-state abundance profiles separately within each group. We summarized directionality of change by computing, within predefined pair categories (Favor–Favor, Favor–Unfavor, etc.), the fraction of cell-state pairs showing higher correlation in responders than in non-responders. We define the correlation difference as Δrho = rho_R−rho_NR and provide distribution summaries and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Benjamini–Hochberg correction across pair categories.

Analysis for neoantigen load, mutation burden, and microsatellite instability

In the compiled datasets, the neoantigen load (TNL) and the tumor mutation burden (TMB) were acquired and unified from the original studies. In the TCGA cohort, the TNL was acquired from the resource contributed by Thorsson et al. 80. MAF files of called variants of TCGA samples were obtained from the Broad Institute’s Firehose (https://gdac.broadinstitute.org/), the TMB for each sample was calculated using the R package “maftools”. The TMB-High was defined as >10 mut/Mb. For TCGA tumors, tumor-only RNA-seq expression matrices were obtained from the GDC/TCGAbiolinks pipeline and processed with EcoTyper to infer cell-state abundances and ecotype assignments. The microsatellite instability (MSI) data were retrieved from the released data from TCGA81. The predicted MSI data were derived from the study by Hause RJ et al. 82, which applied an MSI-calling software in WES data from the TCGA database to predict MSI status. For survival analyses, a CE10 score was computed for each TCGA sample as the sum of abundances of all cell states annotated to ecotype CE10. Samples were dichotomized into CE10-high versus CE10-low using the median CE10 score within each tumor type. Associations with OS were evaluated using Cox proportional hazards models stratified by tumor type and adjusted for continuous TMB where indicated. Adjusted survival curves were visualized using Cox-model-based standardization.

Cell–cell interactions and network analysis from EcoTyper

We performed the network analysis to further describe the difference in the immune landscape between the responders and non-responders. We used a renormalization and bootstrap approach designed to mitigate the spurious correlations common in compositional data24 to explore cell–cell interactions and build a co-occurrence network in responders and non-responders, respectively. Edges were defined based on a consensus of five statistical methods: Spearman’s correlation, Pearson’s correlation, Mutual Information, Bray–Curtis dissimilarity, and Kullback–Leibler dissimilarity, to generate the threshold for at least 100 positive and negative edges for each method. In the renormalizing process, the P-values for each edge were computed through 500 times of iterations. The P-values were corrected and calculated through bootstrap distributions to filter unstable edges, and the P-values of different methods were merged by the Empirical Brown method to yield the final results.

In hub selection, a log-normal distribution fitting was applied for the centrality values prior to the selection, and a node having a centrality over 85% of the fitted log-normal distribution was recognized as having high centrality83. The hubs of nodes were subsequently identified by selecting nodes with the highest degree, betweenness centrality (represented by node size in Fig. 5), and stress simultaneously. The networks of responders and non-responders were compared and illustrated using the methods developed by Goenawan IH et al. 84. The variance of each node between the two networks was evaluated by calculating the Dn-score developed by Goenawan IH et al. 84. Briefly, it was derived from the normalized squared Euclidean distance from each node to the centroid (mean). A higher Dn-score represents higher variance. Network metrics, such as degree, stress, closeness centrality, and eigenvector centrality, were calculated in Cytoscape (3.8.1)85. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the R package “mixOmics” v6.24.0 based on the network metrics of each node to summarize each cell state’s activity in the cell community. The density plot per principal component was illustrated using the R package “PLSDAbatch” v0.99.1.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis

To validate the findings from the bulk tumor analysis, the single cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) profiles of tumors prior to ICIs treatment in cancer patients were collected and analyzed, including colorectal cancer (CRC)86, head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC)87, and basal/squamous cell carcinoma (BCC and SCC)88. The details of clinical data for each type of cancer were summarized in Supplementary Table 2.

Raw count data were processed with quality control, normalization, scaling, and dimensional reduction using the Seurat v4 R package89 following the methods and criteria provided in each original study. Clustering analysis was performed using the “FindClusters” function, and the clusters were annotated by known markers or the Blueprint-ENCODE reference (SingleR package) according to the clustering methods applied in the original research. The annotated cell clusters were renamed to meet the input requirement of EcoTyper. The annotated cell clusters and corresponding count matrices were processed using the EcoTyper pipeline for scRNA-seq Data. This allowed for the consistent identification of cell states across different single-cell cohorts using default recovery settings.

To evaluate the functional state of receiver cells, we calculated module scores for M1, M2, and angiogenic features in CXCL9+ and CCR2+ macrophages using the AddModuleScore function in Seurat and compared them using the Wilcoxon test. The gene lists utilized for these scores were:

M1: IL1B, TNF, IL6, CD80, CD86, CD38.

M2: IL10, TGFB1, MRC1, CD163, CD274, PDCD1LG2.

Angiogenic: VEGFA, VEGFB, SPP1, FN1, MMP9.

For independent validation of the NK cell states, we retrieved processed data from a pan-cancer NK cell atlas25. We identified the SERPING1⁺ NK state and compared the expression of activating and suppressive markers between VEGFA⁺ and VEGFA⁻ subpopulations within this state.

Cell–cell interactions analysis in scRNA-seq

The cell–cell interactions at the ligand-receptor level were inferred using the R package CellChat v1.5.022. CellChat takes a normalized, log-transformed gene expression matrix and cell-group labels as input and quantifies communication probabilities by integrating expression with a curated ligand–receptor database under a mass action-based modeling framework with permutation testing to identify significant interactions. All ligand–receptor interactions curated in the CellChatDB.human database were included for analysis. For each cohort, we followed the CellChat developer-recommended multi-dataset comparison workflow by constructing one CellChat object per response group, running the standard inference pipeline (identification of overexpressed ligands/receptors and interactions, computation of communication probabilities, pathway-level probability computation, and network aggregation), and then merging group-specific objects for comparative analyses of interaction number/strength and pathway information flow. Besides, each cell state’s incoming and outgoing signal strengths were computed and compared. This pooled strategy was used to derive stable group-level signaling networks for downstream discovery.

Multiplex immunohistochemistry staining and analysis

The as-prepared specimen sections were stained using a six-color multiple fluorescent immunohistochemical staining kit (Absin, abs50014), with dyes 520, 570, 650, and 700, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Antibodies used in the experiment included CD3 (Affinity, DF6594, 1:500 dilution), CD56 (Affinity, DF7832, 1:400 dilution), VEGFA (Affinity, AF5131, 1:200 dilution), and SERPING1 (Proteintech, 66882-1-Ig, 1:150 dilution). Optimal antibody concentrations were determined by single-marker staining on control sections. After primary and HRP-labeled secondary antibody incubation, the sections were incubated with the TSA fluorescent dye working solution. Finally, the nuclei were stained with DAPI, and the sections were sealed.

Three slides from three independent FFPE tumor tissue blocks were stained for each patient. All slides were scanned with the TissueFAX PLUS 7S system (TissueGnostics) at ×20 magnification, using filters for wavelengths 440, 520, 570, 650, and 700 nm. Images were saved in 16-bit format as TissueFAXS files.

Image analysis was conducted using StrataQuest software (TissueGnostics) with the IF 5 Apps module. Multiple 1 × 1 mm² regions of interest (ROIs) were selected per section. A Deep-learning nuclear segmentation was done by the software, and the threshold for each marker was determined automatically. The density, intensity, and percentage of each cell type or marker were calculated and averaged across the ROIs.

Statistics and reproducibility

The correlation coefficients were calculated by Spearman’s rank correlation. The Mann–Whitney U test was utilized to compare two independent groups, and Wilcoxon’s matched-pairs signed-rank test was used for pair-wise comparisons. Survival analysis was performed using the Cox regression model. For network analysis, significant cell–cell interactions were identified using a bootstrap-based permutation test and corrected via the Empirical Brown method. All results with two-sided P-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The statistical analyses were conducted using R software (ver. 4.2.1) and Cytoscape (3.8.1). Details of statistical tests, sample sizes, and replicates are provided in the figure legends. Original code and corresponding package can be found in Zenodo90.
