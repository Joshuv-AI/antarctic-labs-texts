Oncolytic adenovirus in combination with PD-L1-targeted radioimmunotherapy exerts synergistic antitumor effect against pancreatic cancer
Authors: A-Rum Yoon, Seungyoun Kim, Ji Yi, Javeria Zaheer, Hyeongi Kim, Je Hyeon Seo, Jinwoo Hong, Jeongwon Lee, Hyeju Jeong, Joycie Shanmugiah, Ju Hee Kim, In-Wook Kim, Jin Su Kim, Chae-Ok Yun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo date, no radioimmunotherapy (RIT) regimen has been approved by US Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of pancreatic cancers. Highly desmoplastic and immune-desert phenotypes of pancreatic cancer remain two major hurdles that attenuate the efficacy of conventional treatment (radiotherapy and chemotherapy) and immunotherapeutic (immune checkpoint inhibitors and chimeric antigen receptor T cells).MethodTo overcome these hurdles, an oncolytic adenovirus (oAd) co-expressing interleukin-12, granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor, and relaxin (HY-oAd) was investigated in combination with programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-targeted RIT (lutetium-177-labeled atezolizumab (177Lu-aPD-L1)).ResultsHY-oAd treatment was shown to elevate PD-L1 expression level and promoted degradation of extracellular matrix of pancreatic tumors, resulting in increased aPD-L1 or 64Cu-aPD-L1 accumulation in tumor tissues. HY-oAd in combination with either aPD-L1 or 177Lu-aPD-L1 (HY-oAd+aPD-L1 or HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1, respectively) elicited more potent antitumor effect against the pancreatic tumors than respective monotherapy in both subcutaneous and orthotopic pancreatic tumor models. The potent antitumor effect of HY-oAd+aPD-L1 combination therapy was due to superior intratumoral infiltration and activation of dendritic cells and CD4+ or CD8+ T cells over the respective monotherapy.ConclusionCollectively, our findings demonstrate that HY-oAd can enhance intratumoral accumulation of 177Lu-aPD-L1 in a multifaceted manner to elicit synergistic antitumor immune response against desmoplastic and poorly immunogenic pancreatic tumors.Keywords: Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor, Oncolytic virus, Radiotherapy/radioimmunotherapy, Gene therapy

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer is notoriously difficult to treat, and it is the seventh most common cause of cancer-related death with a 5-year survival rate of 5–10%.1 Pancreatic tumors are highly desmoplastic, immunosuppressive, hypoxic, chemoresistant, and metastatic; thus, the standard treatments such as chemotherapy and radiotherapy provide only modest survival benefits to patients.2,5 Even immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), which markedly improved the prognosis of patients with several different types of cancer, have been largely ineffective in improving the therapeutic outcome of patients with pancreatic cancer.5,9 To improve the efficacy of ICI against pancreatic cancer, numerous combination therapy regimens exploring ICI in combination with either standard of care or experimental therapies are actively being pursued in clinical trials and several are showing promising results in phase I or II trials. However, none of these regimens have been validated in a double-blinded and randomized phase III trial to receive worldwide approval by the regulatory bodies.4 5 Therefore, there is still a need for novel therapy that can effectively counteract the unfavorable characteristics of the pancreatic tumor microenvironment (TME) and sensitize poorly immunogenic tumors to immunotherapy.

Radioimmunotherapy (RIT), which uses a radiolabeled monoclonal antibody targeting tumor antigens, is an efficient targeted therapy regimen to induce tumor-specific cell death via radiation. In detail, excellent tumor targeting ability of antibodies enables preferentially elevated accumulation of alpha or beta emitting radioisotopes at the tumor tissues and subsequent induction of antitumor effect on exposure to therapeutic radiation.10 Importantly, radiation-induced cell death has been shown to exert an immunostimulatory effect that can counteract ICI resistance of immunologically “cold” tumors,11 and there is an increasing number of preclinical and clinical evidence showing that synergistic effect can be achieved by combination of radiotherapy with ICI.12 13 Despite these benefits, the therapeutic potential of ICI-based RIT has only been investigated in a few studies,14 15 and the majority of studies have focused on its use as a non-invasive imaging modality to monitor checkpoint expression levels, rather than as a therapeutic intervention.16,20 Based on these backgrounds, the present report aimed to investigate the therapeutic potential of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)-targeted RIT by radiolabeling a clinically approved PD-L1 ICI, atezolizumab (aPD-L1), with lutetium-177 (177Lu). PD-L1 was chosen due to the relatively high prevalence of PD-L1-positive clinical cases and elevated level of PD-L1 being associated with worse survival rate among patients with pancreatic cancer demographic.21,23 However, PD-L1-targeted RIT alone may exert suboptimal therapeutic efficacy in pancreatic cancer due to limited intratumoral penetration and dispersion of ICI owing to highly desmoplastic pancreatic cancer based on the findings of our previous study and others.5 24

To address this challenge, we have developed a hypoxia-responsive and fiber-modified oncolytic adenovirus (oAd) co-expressing interleukin (IL)-12, granulocyte macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), and relaxin (RLX; HY-oAd) and maximize PD-L1-targeted RIT accumulation in tumor tissues through extracellular matrix (ECM) degradation and PD-L1 upregulation in oAd-infected cancer cells. Indeed, our current findings demonstrate that HY-oAd can enhance PD-L1-targeted RIT accumulation in desmoplastic pancreatic tumors via PD-L1 upregulation and ECM degradation, thus resulting in synergistic antitumor efficacy and antitumor immune response against both subcutaneous and orthotopic pancreatic cancer models.

Results

In vitro characterization of HY-oAd

A hypoxia-responsive and fiber-modified oAd co-expressing three therapeutic genes (IL-12, GM-CSF, and RLX), termed HY-oAd, has been generated for this study. The detailed genetic construct of HY-oAd has been provided as online supplemental figure S1A. To evaluate the functionality of HY-oAd in pancreatic cancer cells, therapeutic gene expression level, cancer-specific cell killing effect, and viral replication ability of HY-oAd were analyzed in vitro. As shown in online supplemental figure S1B and S2, infection of human or murine pancreatic cancer cell lines (Mia PaCa-2 or Pan02, respectively) with HY-oAd led to robust and dose-dependent expression of all three therapeutic genes. As shown in online supplemental figure S3, HY-oAd was shown to elicit dose-dependent and superior cancer cell killing effect against both Mia PaCa-2 and Pan02 cells compared with the cognate control oAd expressing no therapeutic genes (control-oAd), suggesting that the high level of therapeutic gene expression enhanced anticancer effect of HY-oAd in vitro. In contrast, neither HY-oAd nor control-oAd elicited observable cell killing effect in human or murine normal fibroblast cell lines (BJ or NIH3T3, respectively), showing that the anticancer effect of HY-oAd occurred preferentially in cancer cells. Lastly, HY-oAd induces a time-dependent and dose-dependent viral replication in both human and mouse pancreatic cancer cell lines (online supplemental figure S4), demonstrating its oncolytic capability.

HY-oAd increases PD-L1 expression level of pancreatic cancer

As oncolytic virus infection has been reported to elevate PD-L1 expression level of cancer cells,25 26 thus we investigated whether HY-oAd infection could elevate PD-L1 expression level of Pan02 cells. As shown in figure 1A,B, HY-oAd infection led to dose-dependent elevation of mPD-L1 messenger RNA (mRNA) expression level of Pan02 cells (*p<0.05 or **p<0.01), which positively correlated with elevated surface expression of mPD-L1 (***p<0.001). Similar trends were also observed in vivo where HY-oAd treatment led to significant elevation of mPD-L1 mRNA and protein expression levels of subcutaneous Pan02 tumors in respect to phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) control group (figure 1C,D; *p<0.05 or **p<0.01), showing that HY-oAd can upregulate mPD-L1 expression of pancreatic cancer at both transcriptional and translational levels.

HY-oAd enhances aPD-L1 binding to pancreatic cancer

To evaluate whether HY-oAd-induced upregulation of PD-L1 expression levels correlates with enhanced binding of aPD-L1, Pan02 cells were infected with different doses of HY-oAd, then atezolizumab binding to the cell surface was analyzed by flow cytometry. As shown in figure 2A, HY-oAd treatment led to a dose-dependent increase in aPD-L1 binding to the surface of Pan02 cells, showing up to 63.3% increase in aPD-L1 bound cell fraction in comparison to PBS control group. Next, we evaluated whether HY-oAd could exert similar effects in vivo by injecting subcutaneous Pan02 tumors with PBS or HY-oAd, then performing immunofluorescence staining with aPD-L1 as the primary antibody. As shown in figure 2B, HY-oAd-treated tumors exhibited higher quantity of aPD-L1 binding compared with PBS-injected tumors, showing that PD-L1 was likely elevated in vivo in Pan02 tumors.

More interestingly, as shown in figure 2C, combination with HY-oAd led to significantly higher accumulation of 64Cu-aPD-L1 in Pan02 tumor tissues compared with 64Cu-aPD-L1 monotherapy at 48–72 hours after the ICI administration (figure 2C; *p<0.05). Additionally, no observable alterations to the liver tissues or abnormalities in serum toxicity markers (total bilirubin, AST, ALT, ALP, total cholesterol, and glucose) were observed (online supplemental figure S5,6), indicating that the accumulation of therapeutic modalities in non-cancerous tissues did not induce observable adverse effects. Together, these results illustrated that HY-oAd upregulates PD-L1 expression of pancreatic tumors to ultimately augment intratumoral accumulation of systemically administered aPD-L1 in a safe manner.

Combination of HY-oAd and aPD-L1 induces inhibition of subcutaneous Pan02 tumor growth

To evaluate the antitumor effect of HY-oAd and aPD-L1 combination therapy, mice bearing subcutaneous Pan02 and KPC tumors were intratumorally treated with 3×1010 viral particle (VP) of HY-oAd (day 0, 2, and 4) and/or intraperitoneally with 0.2 mg/kg of aPD-L1 (day 2, 5, and 8), along with intratumorally administered PBS as negative control. As shown in figure 3A,B, PBS treatment led to rapid growth of the tumors and aPD-L1 failed to exert significant growth inhibition. In contrast, both HY-oAd and HY-oAd+aPD-L1 treatments exerted significant tumor growth inhibition in respect to PBS or aPD-L1 groups (**p<0.01, ***p<0.001 in both Pan02 and KPC tumor models). Importantly, HY-oAd+aPD-L1 treatment exerted significantly more potent antitumor effect than HY-oAd monotherapy (**p<0.01 or ***p<0.001 in both Pan02 and KPC tumor models). A similar trend was observed in the applicable Syrian hamster pancreatic cancer model that was highly permissive to viral replication (online supplemental figure S7). Together, these data demonstrated that the combination of HY-oAd+aPD-L1 can be administered safely without compounding side effects and induce potent anticancer effect against pancreatic cancer.

HY-oAd induces ECM degradation and enhances aPD-L1 accumulation in pancreatic tumors

To investigate the underlying mechanism behind the potent anticancer effect of HY-oAd+aPD-L1 combination therapy, we performed histological and immunofluorescence analysis using Pan02 tumor sections. As shown in figure 4A, both PBS and aPD-L1 treatment led to high levels of ECM accumulation within the tumors with ECM occupying ~30–40% of the entire tumor tissues. In marked contrast, both HY-oAd and HY-oAd+aPD-L1 treatment similarly led to a significant reduction of ECM within tumor tissues compared with PBS or aPD-L1 groups (**p<0.01 or ***p<0.001). Subsequent analysis of aPD-L1 accumulation in Pan02 tumor tissues by immunofluorescence staining revealed that the tumors treated with HY-oAd+aPD-L1 showed significantly elevated aPD-L1 levels in tumor tissues compared with aPD-L1 monotherapy (figure 4B; **p<0.01; 8.3-fold increase). Altogether, these results demonstrated that HY-oAd-induced degradation of tumor ECM enhances aPD-L1 accumulation in desmoplastic pancreatic tumors.

HY-oAd in combination with aPD-L1 improves the infiltration of activated T cells into pancreatic tumor

Cytotoxic CD8+ T cell and T-helper type 1 (Th1) CD4+ T cells are major subsets of immune cell population that can suppress the growth of tumors. In contrast, the regulatory CD4+ T cells (Tregs) exert immunosuppressive functions, like inhibition of cytotoxic T-cell function, in the tumor milieu. Thus, we sought to investigate how the combination therapy affected the immune microenvironment of pancreatic tumor tissues via immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, and immunofluorescence staining.

As shown in figure 5A, aPD-L1 monotherapy and PBS treatment led to similarly low levels of both CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration in Pan02 tumor tissues. In marked contrast, both HY-oAd- and HY-oAd+aPD-L1-treated tumor tissues exhibited significantly higher levels of CD4+ and CD8+ T-cell infiltration in these tissues compared with those treated with PBS or aPD-L1 (***p<0.001). Of note, HY-oAd+aPD-L1 combination therapy induced significantly higher levels of CD8+ T-cell infiltration compared with HY-oAd monotherapy (*p<0.05), whereas the CD4+ T-cell infiltration levels remained similarly high between the two groups, suggesting that the pro-inflammatory response by the combination therapy was more reliant on CD8+ T cells.

More in-depth characterization of the T-cell populations within the pancreatic TME was assessed by flow cytometry analysis. The combination therapy led to a significantly higher percentage of T cells being comprised of activated CD8+ T cells and Th1 CD4+ T cells over immunosuppressive Treg population compared with other treatment groups (figure 5B, online supplemental figure S8: *p<0.05, **p<0.01, or ***p<0.001). Further, significant increases in dendritic cell (CD11c+ DC) and M1/M2 macrophage ratios in the HY-oAd+aPD-L1 group compared with other groups indicate immune activation and polarization by HY-oAd+aPD-L1 (online supplemental figure S8). In line with these results, immunofluorescence staining also revealed that combination therapy led to superior infiltration and activation of CD8+ T cells (CD8 and perforin double positive subsets) in the pancreatic tumor tissues compared with other treatment groups (figure 5C). Activated DC also more efficiently infiltrated in tumor tissue treated with HY-oAd+aPD-L1 (figure 5D). HY-oAd+aPD-L1-treated tumors exhibited significantly higher interferon (IFN)-γ expression than those treated with PBS, aPD-L1, or HY-oAd, showing that combination of HY-oAd with aPD-L1 can enhance Th1 immune responses in tumor tissue (figure 5E). Furthermore, as shown in figure 5F, mice treated with HY-oAd in combination with aPD-L1 showed a dramatic increase in the number of IFN-γ-producing cells compared with controls (PBS, aPD-L1 alone). Of note, it was comparable with HY-oAd monotherapy alone. This result indicates that the immune response was specific to the tumor. Together, these results demonstrated that the combination therapy enhanced the infiltration and activation of pro-inflammatory T-cell subsets within the pancreatic TME to induce robust antitumor immune response.

Combination of HY-oAd with 177Lu-aPD-L1 exerts potent antitumor effect against subcutaneous pancreatic tumors

To investigate the antitumor efficacy of RIT combined with HY-oAd and 177Lu-aPD-L1, subcutaneous Pan02 tumors were established in C57BL/6 mice. When the average tumor volume reached 200 mm3, mice were randomized into six groups and then treated with HY-oAd, aPD-L1, HY-oAd+aPD-L1, 177Lu-aPD-L1, or HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1, along with PBS negative control. As described in figure 6A, 177Lu-aPD-L1 or HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 received only a single dose of 177Lu-aPD-L1 on day 2 followed by aPD-L1 on day 5 and 8. For this experiment, HY-oAd was administered at a 60-fold lower of 5×108 VP/injection than those used in figure 3 to empower this study to more effectively compare the antitumor efficacy of HY-oAd+aPD-L1 versus HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 by attenuating overall efficacy of HY-oAd+aPD-L1. As shown in figure 6B, HY-oAd+aPD-L1 combination therapy induced significantly superior tumor growth inhibition compared with respective monotherapies (*p<0.05 or ***p<0.001). Similarly, both 177Lu-aPD-L1 and HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 treatments also led to significantly superior tumor growth inhibition compared with PBS, aPD-L1, or HY-oAd treatment (***p<0.001). The therapeutic effect of 177Lu-aPD-L1 is attributed to 177Lu, a β-emitter (Emax 498.3 keV; half-life ≈ 6.6 days) with a short tissue penetration range (<3 mm) that enables localized cytotoxic irradiation of the tumor tissues. When labeled to aPD-L1, its β-emission affects not only tumor cells but also all other cell types within the irradiation radius, irrespective of whether they are cancerous or non-cancerous.

Importantly, HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 exerted the most potent antitumor efficacy among all treatment groups (figure 6B: ***p<0.001 vs HY-oAd or HY-oAd-aPD-L1 and *p<0.05 vs 177Lu-aPD-L1). Similar results were obtained via survival rate analysis with HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 treatment resulting in significantly prolonged survival of pancreatic tumor-bearing mice over any other treatment groups (figure 6C: *p<0.05 vs HY-oAd-aPD-L1 or 177Lu-aPD-L1). No significant adverse effects in terms of body weight were detected across the treatment groups, supporting the safety of the therapy (online supplemental figure S9). Together, these findings demonstrated that RIT by 177Lu-aPD-L1 is superior to conventional PD-L1 checkpoint blockade and that the potency can be further augmented by combination with HY-oAd.

Combination radioimmunotherapy using HY-oAd and 177Lu-aPD-L1 exerts potent antitumor effect against orthotopic pancreatic tumors

Orthotopic tumor models are widely accepted to better emulate the clinical progression of cancer than subcutaneous tumor models.25 Thus, the therapeutic efficacy of combination RIT using HY-oAd and 177Lu-aPD-L1 was evaluated in orthotopic pancreatic tumor models established with Pan02 cells stably expressing firefly luciferase. In the PBS-treated mice, rapid disease progression was observed in a time-dependent manner with luminescence also being detected in areas adjacent to pancreas, showing metastatic progression of the disease and rapid termination of mice (figure 7A). Neither HY-oAd nor aPD-L1 monotherapy induced notable tumor growth inhibition compared with PBS control group, thus resulting in similarly rapid termination of all mice by day 33. In contrast, both HY-oAd+aPD-L1 combination therapy and 177Lu-aPD-L1 achieved higher percentage of mice surviving up to day 33 compared with HY-oAd or aPD-L1 monotherapy groups, but all mice eventually succumbed to the disease, and none were alive by day 43 (figure 7B). Importantly, all of the mice from the HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 combination therapy group were alive up to day 43 with notably lower levels of luminescence signal compared with all other treatment groups (*p<0.05 vs HY-oAd+aPD-L1 or 177Lu-aPD-L1). End-point autopsy of three of the surviving mice (#1, #2, and #4) in the combination therapy group from figure 7 revealed that mice #2 and #4 showed no apparent masses or luciferase signals in any examined organs, indicating complete recovery, whereas mouse #1 exhibited a visible tumor-like mass accompanied by luciferase signals in the pancreas and spleen (online supplemental figure S10A). H&E staining of the pancreatic tissues from mice #2 and #4 with no detectable luciferase signals revealed that there was no observable tumor (online supplemental figure S10B), suggesting that the combination of HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 can induce complete regression of orthotopic pancreatic tumors.

As HY-oAd had been administered via a systemic route, unlike the local administration used in previous studies, potential off-target effect of the virus was analyzed. As shown in online supplemental figure S11, 100-fold higher viral genome copies were detected in the tumor tissues compared with the adjacent normal pancreas, suggesting that systemically administered HY-oAd were primarily retained at a high level in tumor tissues. Similarly, immunohistochemical analysis confirmed that strong nuclear staining of Ad E1A, which is critical for viral replication, was exclusively observed in tumor regions. Collectively, these results show that the combination of HY-oAd and PD-L1-targeted RIT is efficacious in controlling the tumor-specific disease progression of highly aggressive pancreatic tumors.

Discussion

Pancreatic cancer is well-known to be refractory to a wide range of cancer therapeutics due to the desmoplastic and immunosuppressive TME.15 24 26,29 Even programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1)/PD-L1 checkpoint blockade, which drastically improved the prognosis and survival of patients with various solid tumor types,30 31 it is largely inefficacious for the treatment of immunologically “cold” pancreatic cancer with low level of intratumoral T-cell infiltration. Furthermore, membranous PD-L1 expression, which can be a predictive biomarker for patients’ response toward PD-(L)1 blockade,32,34 is scarce among pancreatic cancer cases due to absence of T cell-induced inflammatory signaling for the induction of PD-L1 expression.35 36 Currently, only 2% of pancreatic cancer cases with dMMR and MSI-H phenotypes have been reported to benefit from PD-(L)1 blockade with objective response rate of less than 20%.37 38

To overcome many of the obstacles posed by pancreatic cancer and make it susceptible to ICI therapy, a two-pronged approach was investigated in the present study: (1) utilization of oAd capable of reverting desmoplasia, immunosuppression, and lack of PD-L1 expression of pancreatic tumors and (2) simultaneous enhancement to the therapeutic potential of clinically approved PD-L1-targeted ICI, atezolizumab, by labeling it with beta-emitting radioisotope 177Lu for RIT. To this end, our findings demonstrated that low baseline expression level of PD-L1 on the surface of Pan02 tumors could be overcome with HY-oAd, as HY-oAd infection led to upregulation of surface PD-L1 expression level and enhanced uptake of aPD-L1 in Pan02 tumor tissues (figures2 3). This is in line with several reports where oncolytic virus-induced activation of antiviral immune response pathway has been associated with elevated PD-L1 expression of various types of cancer cells.39 40 One potential limitation of this strategy is that reactive upregulation of PD-L1 through type I IFN antiviral immune response pathway within TME on oncolytic virus treatment has been reported to promote adaptive immune resistance to oncolytic virotherapy.41 42 In the present report, the combination of HY-oAd with aPD-L1 should have minimized this potential pitfall of PD-L1-induced resistance against oncolytic virotherapy, as concomitant blockade of PD-(L)1 or type I IFN signaling in combination with oncolytic virotherapy has been shown to mitigate this risk.39 41 42

Despite promising preclinical data of HY-oAd and 177Lu-aPD-L1 combination therapy against murine pancreatic tumor model, a potential limitation of this study must be acknowledged. In general, syngeneic murine tumor models are known to be poorly permissive to human Ad infection and viral replication.43 Still, our findings demonstrated that HY-oAd could replicate in a time-dependent manner to induce dose-dependent and robust cytopathic effect and therapeutic gene expression in both human and mouse cancer cell lines (online supplemental figure S2–4), suggesting that Pan02 was permissive to HY-oAd replication, although at a lower level than the human counterpart. Additionally, HY-oAd had also been modified in the fiber region to include RGD peptide as a targeting motif for αvβ3 and αvβ5 integrin (online supplemental figure S1A) to enhance the virus internalization into mouse cancer cells. Our findings demonstrated that Pan02 mouse pancreatic cancer cells were negative for CAR expression level but positive for both αvβ3 and αvβ5 integrin (online supplemental figure S12), suggesting that HY-oAd could effectively internalize into Pan02 murine cancer cells and replicate over time to induce robust cytopathic effect and transgene expression (online supplemental figure S2–S4). Integrin-dependent internalization mechanism of HY-oAd employed in the present study also has translational relevance, since pancreatic cancer tissues of patients frequently express low levels of CAR while integrin expression levels are high.43 44 These results suggest that our HY-oAd can be functionally evaluated in a murine pancreatic tumor model, and that the viral tropism modification employed in the study could be beneficial toward clinical translation of the technology.

Other than PD-L1 upregulation, HY-oAd infection could also enhance aPD-L1 uptake in the pancreatic tumor tissues (figures1 2) by promoting degradation of tumor ECM (figure 4A) via robust induction of RLX therapeutic gene expression in pancreatic cancer cells (online supplemental figure S2,S3). This assertion is supported by previous works by our group and others demonstrating that degradation of tumor ECM positively correlates with enhanced accumulation of systemically administered cancer therapeutics.2444,48 HY-oAd-induced degradation of tumor ECM has other clinical implications in the scope of cancer immunotherapy, as a dense layer of tumor ECM, which functions as a physical hindrance that prohibits T-cell motility, infiltration into tumor tissues, and its recognition of tumor cells,49,51 is one of the contributors to immunosuppressive phenotype of pancreatic cancer. Indeed, Pan02 tumors in the absence of therapeutic intervention were largely composed of ECM (figure 4A) and devoid of T-cell infiltration (figure 5), thus exhibiting immunologically “cold” phenotype. Under these conditions, Pan02 tumors were refractory to aPD-L1 monotherapy (figures3, 6 7undefined), which is in line with growing clinical evidence that demonstrates minimal intratumoral T-cell infiltration correlates with poor efficacy by ICI therapies.52 53 In contrast, HY-oAd-treated Pan02 tumors with minimal ECM deposition (figure 4A) showed significant T-cell infiltration (figure 5A), suggesting that removal of physical hindrance could promote immune cell recruitment to immunologically “cold” tumors.

HY-oAd also induced high expression level of pro-inflammatory therapeutic genes (scIL-12 and GM-CSF) in both human and mouse pancreatic cancer cells (online supplemental figure S2,S3), which likely contributed to elevated intratumoral infiltration of T cells (CD4+, CD8+) and DCs (CD11c+), as well as their activation (figure 5B–D). These findings are in accordance with our previous findings oAd-induced co-expression of IL-12 and GM-CSF was sufficient to induce robust recruitment and activation of T cells and/or DCs in the tumor tissues; this effect was further augmented by addition of RLX as a third therapeutic gene.24 54 In contrast, aPD-L1 monotherapy failed to induce both recruitment and activation of T cells and DCs in aPD-L1 refractory Pan02 tumors (figure 5) due to dense ECM layer (figure 4A) and low level of surface PD-L1 prohibiting aPD-L1 accumulation in tumor tissues (figures2A 4A) in line with clinical evidence.55 Importantly, HY-oAd+aPD-L1 combination therapy exerted superior intratumoral infiltration and activation of CD8+ T cells and DCs, as well as more efficient polarization of TAM toward pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype (online supplemental figure S8) over respective monotherapies, suggesting that HY-oAd-induced reprogramming of pancreatic TME into a pro-inflammatory and non-fibrotic milieu sensitized pancreatic tumors to PD-L1 blockade.

RIT using radiolabeling of clinically validated therapeutic monoclonal antibodies has been reported to exert superior antitumor effect over the parental antibody with superior safety profile over conventional radiation therapy. In line with these observations, 177Lu-aPD-L1 demonstrated greater antitumor activity than aPD-L1 in aPD-L1–refractory pancreatic tumor models (figures6 7), supporting the concept that tumor-targeted delivery of β-emitting radioisotopes represents a promising strategy to overcome ICI resistance in pancreatic cancer. Consistent with our findings using parental aPD-L1, the therapeutic efficacy of 177Lu-aPD-L1 was further enhanced when combined with HY-oAd, resulting in superior tumor growth inhibition and prolonged survival compared with the parental HY-oAd+aPD-L1 combination (figures6 7). The major advantage of 177Lu-aPD-L1 lies in its ability to integrate the specificity of immune checkpoint targeting with the direct tumoricidal activity of β-emission, thereby overcoming resistance to conventional aPD-L1 therapy. When further combined with HY-oAd, this effect was amplified through synergistic engagement of viral oncolysis, immune activation, and radiation-induced cytotoxicity, producing markedly greater therapeutic benefit compared with non-radiolabeled strategies.

Although the underlying mechanism behind the superior efficacy of HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 over HY-oAd+aPD-L1 or 177Lu-aPD-L1 monotherapy has not been investigated at length, there are several proposed mechanisms behind the synergy of combination RIT. First, several studies have shown that RIT restores not only the cytotoxic activity of T cells inside the irradiated TME, but also reinvigorates antigen-presenting cells,56 which could function in a synergistic manner with HY-oAd that promoted efficient infiltration and activation of T cells and DCs. Second, 177Lu-aPD-L1 was prepared with similar radiolabeling strategies as 64Cu-aPD-L1, which showed superior intratumoral accumulation on combination with HY-oAd (figure 2C) in a similar manner as the parental aPD-L1 (figures2B 4B). Based on these premises, HY-oAd likely improved the potency of 177Lu-aPD-L1 in Pan02 tumors via ECM degradation, upregulation of surface PD-L1, and enhanced immune cell recruitment in the TME.

Due to the desmoplastic TME as well as the clinical reality that tumors are positioned in deep locations, intratumoral drug delivery of pancreatic tumor can be extremely challenging. Our current study demonstrates that HY-oAd efficiently degrades the ECM of tumors and effectively delivers aPD-L1 (figure 4) within the tumor, resulting in a greatly enhanced antitumor effect (figures 3 and 5). Additionally, systemic administration of HY-oAd+aPD-L1 also demonstrated significant therapeutic efficacy (figure 7) with most of the virus being preferentially detected in tumor tissues over the adjacent normal tissues (online supplemental figure S11). These results highlight that the systemic delivery of combination therapy could be a viable approach for the treatment of metastatic pancreatic cancer.

Collectively, this study demonstrated that HY-oAd can sensitize aPD-L1 refractory pancreatic cancer to checkpoint blockade in a multidimensional manner that reprogrammed the unfavorable TME into a more susceptible environment via (1) upregulation of surface PD-L1 expression, (2) degradation of tumor ECM, and (3) conversion of immune milieu from “cold” to “hot”. These changes in TME facilitated both parental and radiolabeled aPD-L1 to accumulate more effectively in pancreatic tumors, ultimately resulting in synergistic induction of antitumor immune response in combination with HY-oAd. Lastly, a proof-of-concept study evaluated the combination RIT using HY-oAd and 177Lu-aPD-L1 was shown to be superior to parental combination therapy (HY-oAd+aPD-L1), suggesting that this strategy can be a promising approach to combat ICI refractory pancreatic tumors with extensive desmoplasia and immunosuppression.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrated that HY-oAd can reprogram the hostile TME of pancreatic tumors in a multifaceted manner to sensitize these tumors to PD-L1 blockade by ICI to elicit synergistic antitumor effect. Radiolabeling of atezolizumab with 177Lu was shown to be an effective strategy to overcome the pancreatic tumors resistance to parental ICI therapy, and the potency of RIT was enhanced by combination with HY-oAd, showing that targeted removal of treatment barriers with HY-oAd can be a promising approach to potentiate clinically approved immunotherapeutics against pancreatic cancer.

Material and methods

Cell lines and culture conditions

Pan02 (murine pancreatic cancer cell) was obtained from National Cancer Institute Division of Cancer Treatment and Diagnosis Tumor Repository (Frederick, Maryland, USA). Mia Paca-2 (human pancreatic cancer cell), NIH3T3 (murine embryonic fibroblast cell), BJ (human fibroblast cell), 293A (human embryonic kidney cell), and A549 (human lung cancer cell) were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, Virginia, USA). HapT1 (Syrian hamster pancreatic carcinoma) was obtained from the Leibniz Institute DSMZ (ACC-222; Braunschweig, Germany). All cell lines were maintained in DMEM (Gibco-BRL, Grand Island, New York, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco-BRL) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37°C in 5% CO2.

Preparation of HY-oAd

The construction and generation of HY-oAd have been described previously.57 Briefly, the E1 region of HY-oAd contains the following genetic modifications (online supplemental figure S1): (1) endogenous E1 promoter was replaced with hypoxia-responsive modified human telomerase reverse transcriptase promoter58 59 to drive Rb binding site-deleted E1A transcription60 (2) E1B 19 kDa gene was deleted,61 and (3) a therapeutic gene cassette containing Rous sarcoma virus promoter driven scmIL-12-T2A linker-mGM-CSF-P2A linker-hRLX gene was inserted behind E1B55 kDa gene. HY-oAd also contains a partial deletion of the E3 region and Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) peptide was inserted into the HI-loop of fiber knob region.61 62 To produce HY-oAd, HY-oAd genome containing plasmid was linearized with PacI then transfected into 293A cells using JetPrime transfection reagent (Polyplus, Illkirch, France) according to manufacturer’s instruction. The construction and generation of hypoxia-responsive and telomerase-responsive control oAd lacking therapeutic transgene (control-oAd) has been described previously.58 All Ads were propagated in A549 cells and purified by CsCl gradient centrifugation. The number of VPs was determined by measuring the optical density at 260 nm, for which an absorbance value of 1 is equivalent to 1.1×1012 VP/mL. Purified viruses were stored at −80°C until use. The particle-to-PFU ratio of the purified adenovirus preparation was 16.2:1, indicating that the majority of particles were infectious.

Quantification of IL-12, GM-CSF, and RLX expression level

Mia Paca-2 and Pan02 cells were plated onto 6-well plates at 2×105 cells per well overnight then infected with HY-oAd at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20, 50, or 100. At 48 hours after infection, the supernatants were harvested. The expression level of mouse IL-12 and mouse GM-CSF secreted into the supernatants was determined by conventional mouse IL-12p70 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) and mouse GM-CSF Duo Set ELISA kit (R&D Systems) according to manufacturer’s instructions. For the assessment of mRNA expression level of RLX, Mia Paca-2 and Pan02 cell lysates were obtained from the same plate as those used for ELISA by treating these cells with RNA iso Plus kit (Takara, Otsu, Japan) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNA was purified from the cell lysates and complementary DNA (cDNA) was obtained using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, California, USA) under the following conditions: 25°C for 10 min, 37°C for 120 min, and 85°C for 5 min. RLX mRNA was amplified by PCR with the following primer set: 5’-CCTGGAGCAAAAGGTCTCTG-3’ as the sense primer and 5’- TCTCAGATAGGGCTGCCTTC-3’ as the antisense primer.

MTT assay

The cancer cell-specific cytotoxicity of HY-oAd was determined by measuring the conversion of the tetrazolium salt 3-(4, 5-dimethylthiazolyl-2)−2,5diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT, Sigma, Livonia, Michigan, USA) to formazan. The cells (mouse: Pan02, NIH3T3, human: Mia Paca-2, BJ) were seeded at 5×103 cells per well in a 96-well plate overnight then treated with HY-oAd or control-oAd at 0–400 MOI (Pan02) or 0–100 MOI (Mia Paca-2, BJ, NIH3T3), along with PBS as negative control. At 48 hours after the infection, 50 µL of MTT in PBS (2 mg/mL) was added to each well then cells were incubated at 37°C for 4 hours. The supernatant was discarded, and the precipitated formazan was dissolved in 200 µL of dimethylsulfoxide (Sigma). Plates were read on a microplate reader at 540 nm. Absorbance of PBS-treated well was considered to be 100% viable. All assays were performed in triplicates.

Flow cytometry analysis

Pan02 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 2×105 cells per well, treated with PBS and HY-oAd (5, 10, and 20 MOI) for 48 hours, dislodged with TrypLE Express Enzyme (Gibco-BRL), and then collected in 5 mL FACS tubes (1×106 cells/tube). For assessment of PD-L1 expression level, the cells were incubated for 20 min in mouse FcR Blocking Reagent (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany) diluted in MACS buffer (0.5% BSA and 2 mM EDTA in PBS), and incubated with mouse PD-L1-PE/Cy7 antibody (10F.9G2, BioLegend, San Diego, California, USA, 1:100) or 10 µg/mL of atezolizumab (aPD-L1; Roche, Grenzacherstrasse, Switzerland) for 1 hour at 4°C.

To evaluate percentage of Pan02 cells bound with aPD-L1, the cells treated with PBS or pre-infected with HY-oAd, as described above, were incubated for 15 min in 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature. After removing the fixation buffer, the cells were incubated with atezolizumab (Roche, 10 µg/mL) for 1 hour at 4°C. After washing, the cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488 (Alexa 488) goat anti-human IgG (Invitrogen, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA, 1:500) at room temperature in the dark for 1 hour.

For assessment of immune cell population in vivo, single cells isolated from tumor or spleen tissues that were harvested from mice harboring subcutaneous Pan02 tumors. Subsequently, the cells were incubated with CD45-APC-Cy7 (30-F11, BioLegend, 1:200), CD3-FITC (17A2, BioLegend, 1:200), CD8-BB700 (53–6.7, BD Biosciences, 1:100), CD4-APC (GK1.5, BioLegend, 1:200), IFN-γ-PE (XMG1.2, BioLegend, 1:100), CD25-PE-Cy7 (PC61, BioLegend, 1:100), CD4-BB700 (GK1.5, BD Biosciences, 1:200), or Foxp3-APC (FJK-16s, e-Bioscience, San Diego, California, USA, 1:100) for 1 hour at 4°C. After washing three times with MACS buffer, the cells were fixed in 1% paraformaldehyde. Antibody signals were detected using flow cytometry (FACSCanto II; BD Biosciences). The fluorescence intensity of all samples were obtained with either flow cytometer (FACSCalibur; BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA or CytoFLEX; Beckman Coulter Life Science, Indianapolis, Indiana, USA).

Quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using TRIzol (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. The tumor tissue from the mice was cut into small pieces and lysed by homogenization in TRIzol. Total RNA was subsequently extracted from the homogenized tissue samples then diluted in RNase-free dH2O (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For the reverse-transcriptase PCR, a SuperScript IV cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to generate cDNA from 5 µg of mRNA following the manufacturer’s instructions. Subsequently, quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed on an ABI qPCR system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) using SYBR Green PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The reaction conditions were: 10 min at 95°C, 50 cycles at 95°C for 15 s, and 60°C for 60 s. The primer sequences for PD-L1 were Forward: TTGGGAAATGGAGGATAAGA and Reverse: GGATGTGCCAGAGGTAGTTCT. The mRNA values were normalized to that for GAPDH. Relative quantity (RQ) values for mRNA expression level were calculated using the equation RQ=2−ΔΔCt.

Western blot

Cell extracts were obtained using 1×Extraction Buffer (Abcam) containing Extraction Enhancer Buffer (Abcam). The tumor tissue was lysed by homogenization in 1×protein Extraction Cell Lysis Buffer (Abcam) containing Extraction Enhancer Buffer (Abcam). Final protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The proteins were electro-transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes and then incubated (4°C overnight) with rabbit anti-PD-L1 (EPR20529, Abcam, 1:1000). The membranes were incubated with secondary goat anti-rabbit IgG (Cell Signaling Technology, 1:2000) as the secondary antibody for 2 hours at room temperature. The protein bands were visualized using the Amersham Imager 600 documentation system (GE Healthcare Life Sciences, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA). Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Animal care

All animal experiments were performed under the institutional guidelines of the respective Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Korea Institute of Radiology and Medical Sciences (KIRAMS) and Hanyang University. 5–6 weeks old C57BL/6 mice were obtained from DBL (Seongnam, Korea) or Shizuoka laboratory center (Shizuoka, Japan). The animals were weighed, caged, housed, and maintained under 12 hours light/dark cycle.

Preparation of radiolabeled aPD-L1 for imaging

The 1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid (DOTA)-conjugated antibody was prepared by conjugating DOTA-N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS)-ester (Macrocyclics, Plano, Texas, USA) to 10 mg of aPD-L1 with a 20-fold molar ratio in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate buffer, pH 8.5. The mixtures were incubated overnight with mild shaking at 4°C, after which the conjugated aPD-L1 were purified from excess DOTA-NHS-ester with a PD-10 size exclusion column (Cytiva, Marlborough, Massachusetts, USA) using 1 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.5. 64CuCl2 was produced by 50 MeV cyclotron irradiation at the KIRAMS, Korea. DOTA-Ab (1 mg) was incubated with 64Cu (111 MBq) in 1 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.5, for 30 min at 40°C. After incubation, the radiochemical purity of 64Cu-aPD-L1 was ≥98% by analysis with thin layer chromatography (iTLC) method.

PET/CT

When the average tumor volume of Panc02 tumor-bearing mice reached 200 mm3, HY-oAd (2.5×1010 VP) was administered on days 0, 2, and 4 via intravenous injection (n=4). After, 64Cu-aPD-L1 (200 µg, 5.55 MBq/100 µL in PBS) was administered on day 4 via intravenous injection. PET/CT images were acquired with an Inveon PET scanner (Siemens, Germany). PET/CT was performed at 24, 48, and 72 hours afterward. The PET data were acquired for 15 min within 350–650 keV and reconstructed using a maximum a priori with 3D reprojection. After the coregistration of CT and PET images, regions of interest were drawn in the tumor using the Inveon research workplace (Siemens, Germany). SUVmax was then calculated using the following formula: SUVmax=(activity in region of interest (ROI) of PET image/injected dose of 64Cu-aPD-L1)/(body weight in grams).

Preparation of radiolabeled aPD-L1 for treatment

S-2-(4-isothiocyanatobenzyl)−1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane tetraacetic acid (p-SCN-Bn-DOTA)-conjugated antibody was prepared by conjugating p-SCN-Bn-DOTA (Macrocyclics) to 10 mg of atezolizumab (aPD-L1, Roche) with a 20-fold molar ratio of atezolizumab to buffer in 0.1 M sodium bicarbonate buffer, pH 8.5. The mixtures were incubated overnight with mild shaking at 4°C, after which the conjugated aPD-L1 were purified from excess p-SCN-Bn-DOTA with an Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter Unit (MWCO 100 kDa, 3000 RPM, 40 min, Millipore, Middlesex County, Massachusetts, USA) using 1 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.5. DOTA-SCN-Ab (1 mg) was incubated with 177Lu (148 MBq, ITG, Munich, Germany) in 1 mM sodium acetate buffer, pH 5.5, for 40 min at 40°C. After incubation, the radiochemical purity of 177Lu-aPD-L1 was ≥98% by analysis with iTLC method.

Evaluation of antitumor effect in subcutaneous tumor model

Tumors were implanted in the abdomens of 5–6 weeks old male C57BL/6 (DBL) mice by subcutaneous injection of Panc02 or KPC cells (5×105 cells). When the average tumor volume reached 100 mm3 animals were randomly assigned to one of four groups to receive PBS, aPD-L1, HY-oAd, or HY-oAd+aPD-L1 (n=6–8). The first day of treatment was designated as day 0. HY-oAd (3×1010 VP) or PBS was administered intratumorally on day 0, 2, and 4. The aPD-L1 (0.2 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally on day 2, 5, and 8.

For the assessment of combined efficacy of HY-oAd and PD-L1-targeted RIT, mice began receiving treatment when the average tumor volume reached 200 mm3. In detail, mice were randomly assigned to one of six groups to receive PBS, aPD-L1, HY-oAd, HY-oAd+aPD-L1, 177Lu-aPD-L1, or HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 (n=7–8). The first day of treatment was designated as day 0. HY-oAd (5×108 VP) was administered intratumorally on day 0, 2, and 4. The aPD-L1 (0.2 mg/kg) was administered intraperitoneally on day 2, 5, and 8. For 177Lu-aPD-L1 or HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 treatment groups, 177Lu-aPD-L1 (11.1 MBq) was administered intraperitoneally once on day 2, which was followed by two intraperitoneal doses of aPD-L1 (0.2 mg/kg) on day 5 and 8. The tumor volume was calculated from measurements of the major axis and minor axis using a caliper and the following equation: tumor volume=(minor axis in mm)2×(major axis in mm)×0.523.

IFN-γ ELISpot assay in splenocytes

After 3 days following the final treatment with PBS, aPD-L1, HY-oAd, and aPD-L1+HY oAd, spleens were collected aseptically from tumor-bearing mice. The splenocytes were isolated by mechanical dissociation through a 70 µm cell strainer (Falcon, Corning, New York, USA), followed by red blood cell removal using RBC Lysis Buffer (BioLegend). Pan02 cells (ATCC) were lysed by sonication to prepare tumor antigens. Splenocytes were co-cultured with Pan02 lysate for 18 hours in complete RPMI-1640 (Gibco-BRL) containing 10% FBS (Gibco) and recombinant mouse IL-2 (R&D Systems) at 37°C, 5% CO₂. IFN-γ secretion was quantified using the Mouse IFN-γ ELISpot Set (BD Biosciences, San Jose, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and spots were counted with an AID ELISpot Reader system (AID GmbH, Strassberg, Germany; V.3.4).

Assessment of antitumor efficacy in an orthotopic Pan02 tumor model

Panc02 cells (2.5×105), which stably express firefly luciferase, were injected into the pancreas of C57BL/6 mice. When the luminescence intensity at the ROI reached 1×107 photons/sec, mice were divided into six groups in order to receive treatment of PBS, aPD-L1 (0.2 mg/kg on day 2, 5, and 8; intraperitoneal injection), HY-oAd (3×1010 VP on day 0, 2, and 4; intraperitoneal injection), HY-oAd+aPD-L1, 177Lu-aPD-L1 (177Lu-aPD-L1 (11.1 MBq) on day 2 then two doses of aPD-L1 (0.2 mg/kg) on day 5 and 8) or HY-oAd+177Lu-aPD-L1 (n =3–4). The beginning of treatment was designated as day 1. Optical and luminescence imaging was carried out every 10 days following the first treatment with an IVIS Spectrum instrument (Xenogen, Alameda, California, USA). Image signals were quantitatively analyzed with IGOR-PRO Living Image software (Xenogen).

Immunofluorescence analysis

Pan02 tumors, treated PBS, HY-oAd (5×108 VP or 3×1010 VP), aPD-L1 (0.2 mg/kg), or HY-oAd+aPD-L1, were harvested 3 days after the final treatment and fixed with 4% formaldehyde overnight at 4°C, embedded in an OCT compound and frozen at −20°C. Transverse 10 µm–thick tumor sections were cut and permeabilized using 0.02% Triton-X solution. The sections were blocked using 10% normal goat serum for 1 hour at room temperature and then processed with rabbit anti-PD-L1 (EPR19759, Abcam, 1:200), hamster anti-CD11c (HL3, BD Biosciences, 1:100), rat anti-CD86 (GL1, BD Biosciences, 1:100), rat anti-CD8a (53–6.7, BD Biosciences, 1:100), and rabbit anti-perforin (EPR8260, Abcam, 1:200) overnight at 4°C. After washing, the sections were incubated with the secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit Alexa 488 (Invitrogen, 1:500), anti-hamster Alexa 568 (Invitrogen, 1:500), anti-rat Alexa 488 (Invitrogen, 1:500), anti-rabbit Alexa 633 (Invitrogen, 1:500) for 1 hour at room temperature.

To assess intratumoral accumulation of aPD-L1 in subcutaneous Pan02 tumor, the sections were blocked using Protein Block Serum-Free solution (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) for 30 min at room temperature and then incubated with Alexa Fluor-488 goat anti-human IgG (Invitrogen, 1:500) for 2 hours at room temperature. The tissue sections were mounted using DAPI mounting solution. Images were then acquired using light microscope (Eclipse Ci; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) and confocal microscope (LSM-880, Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany).

Immunohistochemical and histological analysis

For immunohistochemical and histological analysis, tumor tissues were collected from mice at 10 days post initial Ad injection and embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 5 µm thickness. The sections were blocked using Protein Block Serum-Free solution (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, California, USA) for 30 min at room temperature and incubated with CD4 monoclonal antibody (4SM95, Invitrogen, 1:200) or CD8a monoclonal antibody (4SM15, Invitrogen, 1:200) for overnight at 4°C. After washing, the tumor sections were incubated with Streptavidin-HRP (BD Biosciences, 1:200) for 1 hour at room temperature. And DAB was treated, using Peroxidase/DAB+Detection Kit (Agilent Technologies). In order to assess the degradation of tumor ECM, Masson’s trichrome staining and picrosirius red staining were performed as standard procedure. Images were then acquired using light microscopy (Eclipse Ci, Nikon).

Statistical analyses

The data are presented as mean with SEM and SD. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism V.8.3 (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA). A two-tailed Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (Tukey multiple comparison test) were used. *p<0.05; **p<0.01, ***p<0.001, n.s; not significant.
