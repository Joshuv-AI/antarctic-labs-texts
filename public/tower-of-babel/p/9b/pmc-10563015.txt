Lessons from a successful national sanitation programme: the case of Nyumba ni Choo in Tanzania
Authors: Robert Aunger, Kaposo Mwambuli, Jason Cardosi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractUniversal access to hygienic sanitation is a Sustainable Development Goal for international development. However, many countries are liable to miss this target by the deadline of 2030. As provision and subsidy are prohibitively expensive, promotion is the tactic commonly taken by governments and stakeholders in many countries, even though it is often not effective at generating significant changes in sanitation coverage. A recent 5-year programme used an international consortium made up of organizations’ experts in consumer research, creative communication, programme management and event implementation, media monitoring and programme evaluation, to achieve significant increases in the coverage of improved sanitation facilities throughout Tanzania, using adaptive programming. A number of lessons, outlined here, can be drawn from this experience which are likely to be applicable to promotion efforts in other countries and contexts and which can hopefully help countries to reach their sanitation targets. These lessons include the use of motivation and targeted expertise rather than reliance on training, the use of the theory of change to guide development processes, targeting of high-level government support, collaboration with private sector actors, testing and refreshing of messaging, continuous monitoring of on-ground conditions, use of multiple modes of outreach and branding of all programme outputs.

INTRODUCTION

Access to sanitation facilities is important to a country’s health. However, in many countries, such access is limited (Nhamo et al., 2019; World Health Organization, 2021). Many country governments therefore seek to promote the acquisition of such services, often from households, due to limited funding for the large-scale provision of such services. Small-scale experiments have produced increases in sanitation demand (e.g. Tidwell et al., 2019), but there is scarce evidence that campaigns can produce sustained increases in the large-scale demand for sanitation facilities at the household level (Huda et al., 2012; Cameron et al., 2019) [India’s Swachh Bharat could be considered a significant counter-example to this conclusion, but it was heavily subsidized by the government (Curtis, 2019). Community Led Total Sanitation (CLTS) (Kar & Chambers, 2008) has worked to the extent of reducing open defecation at scale in many countries (Bongartz et al., 2016), with households building themselves basic latrines, but has been less successful at getting people to construct sustainable, hygienic toilets (Perez, 2011; Crocker et al., 2017)]. Furthermore, behavioural interventions with high compliance in large sanitation trials have not produced evidence of better health outcomes, (Cumming et al., 2019) even when combined with hygiene and nutrition interventions to cover other causal pathways to those outcomes (Luby et al., 2018), meaning that health-based arguments for sanitation provision are weakened. This has left the sector in some doubt as to how to move forward (Jones et al., 2013; Heller, 2022).

Recently, however, a promotional programme in Tanzania did manage to generate demand for improved sanitation facilities from households at the national scale (R. Aunger, submitted). How did this programme manage to get households to invest in their own toilets? The Tanzanian government has long been concerned with ensuring that its people have access to safe and hygienic sanitation facilities. In the 1970s, its first national sanitation program, Mtu ni afya, was introduced and produced some positive increase in sanitation coverage (Hall, 1978). Sanitation promotion languished to a degree in subsequent decades, however, such that coverage of improved sanitation facilities remained less than 20% (UNICEF & WHO, 2013). In 2010, the government rejuvenated their efforts with a National Sanitation Campaign, again with some success (World Bank, 2017). In 2016, the second 5-year phase of the Tanzanian Government’s National Sanitation Campaign was initiated. Its objective was to end open defecation in the country by 2025, and increase coverage of improved sanitation, such that all Tanzanians have access to safely managed and hygienic sanitation facilities by 2030. Based on the phase 1 experience with this program, which achieved insufficient change in purchase and use behaviour, the government sought to strengthen local leadership and accountability and to use professionally designed concepts and activities to engage with people across the country.

On behalf of the Government of Tanzania, the Department for International Development/Foreign, Commonwealth and Development Office, the UK government’s international development agency, engaged a consortium of service and technical advisors on behaviour change. Designated Project CLEAR, the consortium supported the government in designing, delivering, and adapting its national sanitation programme activities. The programme’s aim was to help the government achieve significant increases across the country in the coverage of improved sanitation. This was to be achieved without subsidy or infrastructure provision; it was strictly based on demand creation. ‘Improved sanitation’ was taken to mean a facility which has been designed to safely and hygienically separate human excreta from human contact (https://washdata.org/monitoring/sanitation). The resulting program, known as Nyumba ni Choo (which roughly translates as ‘a house is not complete without a proper toilet’), adopted a two-pronged approach to achieve the country’s ambitious goals. First, it used insights from behavioural science and mass communications to spark improved hygiene practices and attitudes among the general population. Simultaneously, it encouraged politicians and government officials to make and uphold commitments to improve sanitation via their own programming activities.

In particular, interventions included pre-scripted mass media programming in the form of TV and radio spots, as well as ‘reality TV’ shows documenting real examples of household and school upgrades of their toilet facilities. Unscripted ‘live’ shows were also conducted on regional radio stations. The slogans used evolved as the programme progressed. In addition, Direct Consumer Contact (DCC) (or ‘roadshow’) events took place around the country, with professional performers, and hosted by a nationally famous celebrity, in which various forms of entertainment were combined with instructive demonstrations and exhortations (directed at both household owners and local government officials) to improve household sanitation coverage in the area. These events were filmed, and excerpts were filmed for later dissemination via bespoke social media channels. Activities directed specifically at government officials included getting explicit commitments early on in the programme during visits by project team members to regular government meetings at the regional level, and visits to local government offices prior to implementing roadshow events in the area.

Internal reports from Project CLEAR and monitoring partners show that, over its 5 years of operation, the programme managed to directly reach over 20 million unique individuals via television and radio broadcasts, which is ~65% of all media consumers in Tanzania (all Project CLEAR documentation can be found at the programme’s website, https://projectclear.com). The average audience member is estimated to have been exposed to campaign messages 126 times (based on repeated media monitoring surveys). Furthermore, the roadshow events were successfully implemented in 21 of Tanzania’s 26 regions, which include 81% of the country’s population. The total audience for these events was about 710,500 individuals. By the programme end, a number of goals had been achieved:

This article is intended to draw a number of generalizable lessons for the water, hygiene and sanitation (WASH) sector from this experience. These can be considered as recommendations for sanitation promotion practices to others who seek similar objectives in relevant circumstances. Subsequent sections detail the lessons learned and draw conclusions about potential future practices for those programs seeking to increase coverage of improved sanitation in other contexts.

Lessons learned

Achieving household-level investment in sanitation at the scale required the Tanzanian programme to exhibit a number of features. Each feature can be linked to the phase of programme development to which it primarily belongs. These phases will themselves be categorized along the lines of the Behaviour Centred Design approach (Aunger, 2016). It argues that programs ideally develop through a sequence of phases, each with a separate function, outcome and type of expertise required. First is the Assess step, where what is known about the behavioural context and its likely determinants is gathered together and a first draft of a theory of change is produced. In the Build step, formative research, often based in the field, helps fill in knowledge gaps to revise the theory of change. Next, a Creative process is introduced, in which insights derived from the earlier steps are used to develop the intervention concept and materials, preferably with the help of creative professionals. In the Deliver step, the intervention is set up and rolled out to the target population, and in the final, Evaluation step, the intervention is appraised both for its outcomes and for its process of implementation, checking actual mechanisms of change against the expectations from the theory of change.

ASSESS PHASE

Targeting of government support

Governmental sponsors requested that Project CLEAR engage in a ‘quick-win’ via some early activity. A professional implementation agency was used to gain the commitment of political figures and development partners through specialized events in which officials professed their support for the new sanitation programme, under the Nipo Tayari (‘I am ready’) campaign slogan. This jump-started the programme by producing political will to see it succeed. Commitments were recorded and amplified on different media platforms, creating a ripple effect that reached other leaders throughout the country. Several district commissioners started their own campaigns to influence the construction and use of improved toilets. For example, in Songwe Region, District Commissioner Joseph Mkude started the Jenga choo na Mkude campaign (‘Build your toilet with Mkude’) project, while in Songwe Region, a Shule ni Choo (‘It’s not a school without toilets’) campaign was started in 2019, calling on the community to build toilets in their local schools.

BUILD PHASE

While this foundation was being laid, the programme simultaneously moved onto the task of getting background research done on sanitation promotion options with the general population.

Use of theory to stay on path

In Behaviour Centred Design, theories of change, which lay out hypothesized mechanisms by which ‘hearts and minds’ can be modified to make the target behaviour more likely, play a central role. Such an exercise can be used to continually evaluate progress with respect to programme goals, as a form of adaptive monitoring and evaluation. However, it should be based on the best possible information to confirm its assumptions.

Use of research to identify key insights

The programme’s success in terms of achievements and adaptability largely depended on the evidence and insights from research studies. All stages of the programme, ranging from initial creation to evaluation, were enriched with research. Particularly important was formative research conducted prior to the onset of the program, which identified a powerful insight. In general, households in Tanzania aspire to be more modern and want to keep up with their neighbours. In particular, it was noted that many rural households were making home improvements, but toilets were not being upgraded. One way to motivate households could then be to get them to include sanitation within their conception of the modern way of life and to consider (outside) toilets as part of the house or compound. The insight can be conveyed as ‘upgrading your toilet tells the world that you are a completely modern person’. This insight was the foundation of the programme theory of change throughout its duration.

Subsequently, a constant stream of quarterly surveys was conducted, measuring everything from sanitation coverage to attitudes and beliefs about COVID-19, to inform message adaptation, given the conditions on the ground at the time.

Few public health programmes engage in sophisticated creative processes to develop their messaging. This programme went through significant and iterative creative developments.

Emphasis on constant creativity/innovation to stimulate behaviour change

In a promotional programme lasting 5 years, messaging cannot remain the same. Certain messages work with certain audiences under certain circumstances. As campaigns progress, they need refreshing, to reach new and old audiences with reinforcing content. These refreshed messages need to be based on proper insights. Refining the programme’s television programming

Television programming underwent major changes over time, following an approach that became increasingly sophisticated as the programme progressed. In the Tumekubamba (‘You’ve been caught!’) show, a dynamic young host found households with nice homes which lacked adequate sanitation. The host then convinced homeowners that they were ‘caught’ in the embarrassing position of having ‘traditional’ toilets rather than modern ones. This was presented as a kind of ‘reality TV’ show. However, the show left the households without having upgraded their toilets. Hence the idea developed into one in which households willing to upgrade their own toilets were identified, under the theme of a ‘makeover’ show, by analogy to existing reality TV shows following people as they upgrade the quality of their homes. In this case, the host would identify households with a choo bora (traditional toilet), get them to declare their willingness to invest in an improved toilet, and then lead them through the engineering and financial aspects of upgrading. This version was known as the Mr. Nogesha Show (‘Mr Fixit’). Finally, the renowned political cartoonist in Tanzania, Masood Kipanya, was enlisted to host a further evolution of this kind of show, called Kipanya Chooni (‘Kipanya in the toilet’), in which he motivated schools and other local institutions, such as football clubs, to improve their sanitation facilities. A number of such shows were broadcast in 2019.

DELIVERY PHASE

An events management company was charged with piloting creative material content, implementing mass media campaigns and supporting the implementation of interpersonal activities. For instance, it was responsible for forming compound-, village- and district-level events, drawing large audiences with engaging behaviour-changing activities. The company also led a public relations/advocacy strategy designed to: (i) publicize the situation on the ground and emphasize the need for improved sanitation; (ii) drive ‘talk-ability’ through the creative use of sanitation ambassadors and (iii) mobilize communities by encouraging them to compete to be part of a national change programme. To achieve these objectives, the programme identified supporting spokespeople; made creative use of digital and social media; radio call-ins; editorials in print and electronic media; press releases in video and print and formed strategic partnerships with private sector companies (for example, getting them to ‘adopt’, or financially support, schools or health facilities).

Use of mass media to achieve reach

This is the standard channel of choice when large-scale dissemination of messages is desired. However, public health programs often don’t follow the practices of private sector marketers, who know the expense of such broadcasts, by doing constant monitoring of delivery, actual reach and coverage through an adaptive mix of national- and regional-scale channels, and a combination of TV, radio and social media. Produced content was even aired free by regional and local radio stations, which are often on the lookout for quality programming.

Direct Consumer Contacts evolved to best motivate change

Mass media can achieve great reach, but each contact tends to be relatively weak in terms of its ability to inspire behaviour change (Noar, 2006; Wakefield et al., 2010). More immediate, personalized and impactful contacts tend to be those which involve active participation, such as attending an exciting event in one’s own neighbourhood (McQuail, 1979). The programme therefore put on many DCC activations (or ‘roadshow’) events around the country.

Programme teams visited local government offices in advance to inform them of plans to verify area sanitation coverage data, of the date and contents of the events themselves and to mobilize participation and commitments. During the early design of Nyumba ni Choo, roadshows were originally planned to be performed in the form of Community-Led Total Sanitation triggering, which focussed on changing behaviour through shame and disgust (Kar & Chambers, 2008). However, this changed during prototyping (April–May 2018), where the use of the more positive modernity concept, which focussed on positive aspiration for change by using celebrities, was started.

A new implementer, Clouds Media Group, was then contracted to perform these revised ground activations. They were the top radio and TV stations among young adults (i.e. the majority of households) and were tuned in by politicians to keep up with the latest trends and topics. Village Executive Officers made commitments during these events to ensure that their villages constructed and used improved toilets. The events were characterized by theme songs and included debates on the meaning of modernity. During this early phase, events reached an average of 200 people.

Between October 2018 and January 2019, roadshow content was updated for use on TV, radio and social media, driving integration. Mrisho Mpoto, an international celebrity, was introduced to facilitate the DCC events, in order to raise the number of participants attending. The activations were also conducted at the ward level, rather than at the village level. Participants could then be drawn from between three and seven villages. The selection of wards prioritized those with low sanitation coverage. In this way, the number of participants for each event increased to an average of 800. To accompany these DCC activations, branded collateral materials like T-shirts, reflector vests, notebooks and bandanas were distributed to attendees so that campaign messages would be taken to areas beyond the event site, especially where media connections were limited.

In February 2019, a flagpole ceremony was introduced in which a Tanzanian flag was raised up the pole only to the level equivalent to the ward’s sanitation coverage percentage. The ceremony was also used as a public symbol of commitment, which leaders found harder to refuse than the previous method of having them sign ready-made commitment papers.

In 2020, the programme and its DCC events were adapted to COVID-19 messaging. The first theme used in this phase was Mikono Safi Tanzania Salama (‘Clean hands, safe Tanzania’). The programme introduced the ‘handwashing challenge’ on social media in which many influential people such as political, religious, cultural and traditional leaders and artists participated. The programme also partnered with Tanzania Police Force to deliver campaign messages, as they are responsible for protecting the community and monitoring border areas, where COVID-19 was most likely to enter the country.

In the second half of 2020, during the Unategemewa phase, the programme was mainly conducted via mass media due to the national presidential elections and the inability of people to gather in large groups. In 2021, a new phase Siri ya ushindi ni kubalansi (‘The secret to victory is balance’) was introduced. In this period, the Project CLEAR Consortium partnered with the Tanzanian Football Federation to conduct activations in the form of the Nyumba ni Choo Cup, a football tournament. Teams were selected from wards with the highest population and wards with sanitation challenges, so that players and coaching staff could serve as effectively as Nyumba ni Choo ambassadors. On-ground activations were now done at the district headquarters level. Football games were used to attract crowds, and the competition attracted larger audiences than any previous platform. In one football match an estimated 5,000 people attended.

Media integration

To achieve both economy and relevance, mentions, testimonials and live content from DCC events were generated to supplement the produced material. This new material could reflect local concerns and issues, as well as culture, and thus maximize the applicability of the message to local audiences. Both pre-produced and ‘on-the-spot’ materials were uploaded to digital media platforms like Facebook, Instagram and Twitter for further dissemination. Some events were live-streamed to enable people in different places to see the campaign in action, and the content produced was shared on a daily basis. The programme also leveraged the increasing use of WhatsApp in Tanzania by initiating groups of government officials in each region where the campaigns were conducted, which helped programme sustainability as these novel channels were used post-events to continue dialogues around sanitation.

Private sector support

Having a programme that is affiliated with private sector companies can prove mutually beneficial, as the missions of both organizations can overlap, enabling synergies in their activities. One private sector partner for Nyumba ni Choo was LIXIL, a Japanese corporation which has licensed the manufacture of SATO toilet pans and other products in Tanzania. Local partners of SATO promoted the product during campaign events as an appealing, low-cost option for households. Similarly, Unilever also provided samples and sold hygiene products during campaign events. The participation of these corporations helped support the campaign’s primary outcome of improved sanitation coverage.

EVALUATION PHASE

Given that the lead partner was a university, and the desire of the government to learn from its activities, evaluation was at the heart of this programme. Adaptive programming requires constant monitoring of implementation activities as well as the consequences of those activities, such as audience reception, appreciation and behavioural consequences. Some of these consequences can be unintended (e.g. negative or defensive responses to messaging). For these reasons, GeoPoll and Ipsos were hired to conduct quarterly SMS surveys across the country.

Evaluation activities were continuous throughout programme delivery. This ‘adaptive M + E’ through regular data dipsticks fed into message tailoring, and thus was instrumental in ensuring that creative outputs stayed relevant and powerful. These evaluations were geared towards understanding the reception of communication messages (output) and how they influenced action (outcome), as well as registering the actual activities of the media companies used. GeoPoll also collected data on other topics such as COVID-19 and vaccine hesitancy attitudes in Tanzania. For example, web-based experiments investigated the appropriate framing of communication messages, finding that positive framing of communication messages is better received than negative messages (unpublished). As a result, Nyumba ni Choo communications were mainly positively framed.

GENERAL LESSONS

We can also identify a number of lessons which reflect the overall experience of the programme.

Adaptive management can increase programme effectiveness

A central feature of this programme was its use of adaptive programming. Adaptive programming can be defined as engaging in practices that enable a programme to respond in real-time to changes in external circumstances and to dynamic information about the efficacy of the programme itself (Valters et al., 2016). This was made possible through the leadership of a consortium composed of members from a wide variety of sectors, including education, and private sector partners, used as ‘plug-and-play’ expert partners, switched in or out as needed by the phase of the programme. The upside of this collaboration was the ability to rely on experts in many of the fields underlying programme success, including consumer research (Build phase), creative communication (Create phase), programme management and event implementation (Deliver phase), media monitoring and programme evaluation (Evaluation phase).

The downside was the need to coordinate and maintain communication among partners located in different countries (which became particularly difficult during the pandemic). Working in an international consortium presents unusual challenges, especially when partners are different kinds of organizations, some of which are subcontracted to other organizations. These challenges include establishing relationships through memoranda of understanding and other contractual arrangements, which need to be both flexible yet accountable, clearly assigning roles and finding effective communication practices—which can be particularly difficult when partners are in different countries, speak different languages and at some points cannot meet in person due to travel restrictions. As the consortium gained a reputation through its activities, many other players sought to cooperate with the programme, so working relationships with external partners also had to be established. We believe that, on balance, the upsides considerably outweighed the downsides in terms of contributing to the programme’s overall success. Ultimately, the advantage of engaging in the more difficult process of adaptive programming is that it is more likely to achieve good outcomes, and at a cost advantage.

Hire experts, don’t train ‘peers’

Many programmes focus on training implementors with the aim of imparting new knowledge. For example, the SHEWA-B programme in Bangladesh was massive (targeting 20 million people), undertaken over 5 years, with many people involved (10,000 community volunteers), so it came at a considerable cost ($100M). It was based on training community members to deliver various WASH messages. It is quite distressing to learn that such a flagship programme, with such resources, had negligible impact on behaviour or health (Huda et al., 2012; Benjamin-Chung et al., 2017). Focussing on training alone as a method for delivering a campaign may not be the most appropriate strategy for the WASH sector. It seems to work for some kinds of behaviour but not others (Webel et al., 2010), and seems to work best for specialized populations, such as prisoners (South et al., 2014) or those at suicide risk (Bowersox et al., 2021).

Nyumba ni Choo was conducted differently. Government health officers—who have professional experience, and are responsible for supervising sanitation in their specific areas—may have knowledge but lack motivation. As a consequence, the programme did not seek to train them but to inspire them. Campaign techniques such as public discussion of sanitation status and giving recognition certificates gave them reason to prioritize sanitation supervision. In some areas, health officers continued to conduct roadshows and football events in their areas even after Nyumba ni Choo had ended.

Training as a programme objective requires someone who may not be predisposed to learning, either motivationally or in terms of personal capacities, to sit through a number of sessions in which someone ostensibly transmits skills to them. They might be paid to sit through these sessions, but then are typically left by the programme to engage in the new, skilled behaviours. However, the skilled behaviours may:

require learning-by-doing, not learning-by-observing;

require learning-by-doing, not learning-by-observing;

depend on acquiring a new role in their organization in order to be charged with the responsibility to produce outcomes based on the newly acquired skill;

depend on acquiring a new role in their organization in order to be charged with the responsibility to produce outcomes based on the newly acquired skill;

depend on having additional funding to do the new things—funding which the programme may lack due to training expenditures (‘crowding out’ of actual outputs).

Compare this to a model where the programme simply concentrates on producing actual outputs (e.g. the payment-by-results model). This is achieved by hiring individuals who already have the necessary capabilities or involving institutions and agencies that specialize in training (e.g. educational institutions), so that training can be more efficient and effective. When it is required, skill development should be outsourced to professionals, not left to programme operatives who may not be skilled trainers themselves.

That is, there was an established slogan, tone, authority and aim shared by a long stream of communications, all linked to the same management and sponsors. Issues of scale

A major problem with many public health programmes is that they are conducted by NGOs and similar agencies or interest groups with limited resources and hence reach. But when public health problems are widespread and have social consequences, such as communicable diseases, then it becomes important to tackle those problems at the largest scale possible. In this case, it was therefore appropriate that Nyumba ni Choo was rolled out nationwide. This was achieved through a combination of official sponsorship by the national government and the use of media outlets on a national scale.

Adoption of a private–public partnership approach where the national government, through its relevant departments, led the coordination and provided the implementation oversight. As a result, Nyumba ni Choo was successfully implemented and became well known to the public in Tanzania, paving the way for it to be used as a brand and platform with great value.

Many of the lessons drawn from the experience with Nyumba ni Choo revolve around the fact that adaptive programming techniques were used throughout the programme’s history. Indeed, the CLEAR Consortium itself, which managed the programme, evolved and adapted from the program’s onset. For example, it introduced the Nipo Tayari (‘I am ready)’ campaign in its first year in response to the Tanzanian government’s request that the programme bridge the gap between design and implementation with some sort of promotional activity. The decision to move creative production in-house to lower overall costs is another clear example of how programme execution changed over time. In the process, it developed the creative capabilities of local talent as well. This managing consortium even grew into an independent company that has survived the original grant-based funding, able to attract its own business.

Programme implementation also had to adapt to changing circumstances in other ways. For example, it initially depended on national media to broadcast all messages. The media monitoring reports showed that the use of national media resulted in high reach, but GeoPoll surveys indicated that the quality of reach was poor. To improve this situation, the decision was made to engage several community radio stations to ensure that messages were tailored to specific areas.

Significantly, the monitoring, evaluation and learning infrastructure that the programme developed over time helped to pivot the programme to respond to COVID-19. When the first case in Tanzania was reported in March 2020, the programme was in place to communicate appropriate COVID-19 behaviours and become an integral part of the Tanzanian government’s response to the pandemic.

Other sanitation programs which have many of these features have also been successful. An example comes from Bangladesh, in which UNICEF collaborated with the national government in the early 1990s to increase coverage of improved sanitation from around 3% to around 25% (Ikin, 1996). This was achieved by a combination of on-ground and mass media activities, together with the introduction of a new kind of inexpensive latrine product. Government officials at the top were involved. Marketing strategies were used: messaging, produced by creative professionals, made reference to good citizenship and good parenting, social status and convenience. There was real-time monitoring of success throughout the programme period. All of these types of features were included in Project CLEAR (without knowing about this earlier example).

CONCLUSION

As the end of the Sustainable Development Goal period approaches, many nations appear likely to fall short of the ambitious goals of this international effort, and in particular the sixth Goal to ‘ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all’, with 2.8 billion people predicted to still lack safely managed sanitation in 2030 (Statistics Division of the Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2022). As a consequence, there is considerable self-reflection in the sanitation sector concerning how to get people to improve their facilities, given the recent failure of large-scale efforts to achieve significant results (Cumming et al., 2019).

The Nyumba ni Choo programme, described here, was quite successful in generating demand for improved sanitation. The long history of this programme, coupled with the intensity and scale of its implementation and the involvement of many partners from multiple sectors, produced many learnings. Media, DCC events (called ‘roadshows’) and targeted government activities all played significant roles in getting households to improve their toilets, in conformity with the programme theory of change. In particular, coverage increased faster in areas where roadshow events had taken place compared to areas where they had not (Mwakalikamo et al., 2023). The government was effective as a central coordinator of partners for national public health efforts when it led from the top and filtered down hierarchies, while also making use of bottom-up insights reflecting the needs, wants and incentives of the public and local officials. However, ranking their relative importance is confounded by cross-channel content and synergies between causal routes. The lessons from this programme are quite generic in terms of implementation practices, and thus can be applied in a wide variety of other contexts to achieve increased demand for sanitation facilities, as well as other public health goals.
