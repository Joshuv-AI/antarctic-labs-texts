Distinct functional networks derived from human induced pluripotent stem cell neuronal activity
Authors: Steve Mehrkanoon, Ben Rollo, Jinchao Gu, Muhammad Shahid Javaid, Ana Antonic-Baker, Terence J. O’Brien, Patrick Kwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We characterised the development of spikes, bursts and functional networks in neurons derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) over a 55-day culture period. Spontaneous neuronal activity was recorded using a multi-electrode array across 20 non-consecutive days. The aim was to track neural maturation by identifying the spatiotemporal patterns of functional connectivity. We identified three distinct activity patterns corresponding to different developmental stages. In the early period (day age 18−23), iPSC-derived neurons exhibited a gradually increasing number of synchronous spikes, firing frequency, network bursts and burst rate, in the mid period (day age 24−28), whereas a fully mature neuronal synchronisation pattern with stable spiking and bursting behaviours were observed, and ultimately iPSC-derived neurons exhibited a decrease in firing rate, number of burst and network bursts for day age 32−55. These patterns exhibited three unique spatial topologies and spectral characteristics: In early period, functional connectivity showed poor neuronal communication with low fluctuation in its temporal dynamics and a 1/f-like spectral distribution. In contrast, a fully mature functional network exhibited strong and broad communication topology, indicating an established iPSC-derived neuronal synchronisation in the mid period, but a decaying network maturation trajectory after day age 32. Staining was also performed on day 21 and day 28 to identify the underlying synaptic mechanisms of functional connectivity obtained from iPSC-derived neuronal population dynamics in MEA. The analysis of Staining showed significant changes in mean volumes of presynaptic and postsynaptic across day 21 and day 28. Our findings suggest that iPSC-derived neurons develop dynamic functional connectivity with distinct communication patterns over time, shedding light on the microscale organisation of neural networks in vitro. This work offers a foundational understanding of in-vitro neuronal network dynamics, which may be valuable for future research into brain function and disorders.

Introduction

The human brain is a complex system, consisting of spatially distributed neuronal populations that communicate via networks of neural circuits to support normal functions1–4.Brain maturation is governed by emergent neural synchronisation in a small cluster or community of neurons5–8. Understanding how neural networks are functionally organised requires direct long-term measurements from human brain, which is invasive and not practical at scale.

To overcome this challenge, cultured neurons derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) are increasingly used as a research tool to understand fundamental cellular processes in the human brain9,10. For research purposes, iPSC-derived neurons have a number of important advantages over the use of brain tissues or primary neurons: 1) For ethical reasons human brain tissues can only be obtained from patients undergoing brain surgery, thus the tissues are likely to be abnormal and their availability is limited, 2) Although brain tissues or primary neurons obtained from rodents may be an alternative, they are genetically distinct from humans and do not fully recapitulate human neuronal functions, and 3) Primary neurons have limited lifespan (few days to weeks), thus posing limitations on experimentation. In contrast, iPSC-derived neurons provide an unlimited supply of cellular material, can be kept for a prolonged period (many months), and can be conveniently generated from a small blood sample.

Recent studies have evaluated the spontaneous biological behaviour of iPSC-derived neurons and their behaviour in response to external stimulation11–15. Further, iPSCs-derived neurons from patients are increasingly used in disease modelling, as they carry the patient’s genetic information once differentiated into a variety of cell lineages, including multiple neural subtypes. These patient-derived neural models provide insights into a broad range of disease-specific neural phenotypes such as abnormal neural morphology14,15 or synaptic transmission16. These iPSC-based models have been used to identify the abnormal behaviour of neurons that carry highly pathogenic gene variants, for instance in children with developmental and epileptic encephalopathies17.

Despite their increasing popularity as a disease modelling tool, the microscopic biological process driving the maturation of nascent neurons toward synchronised neural networks remains poorly understood. This severely hinders the use of cultured neurons in exploring how dynamic functional networks emerge in a small population (typically < 200,000 neurons). The advent of multi-electrode array (MEA) has provided the capacity to record directly from microscale neural network activity18–20. Here, we analysed the MEA recordings from in-vitro iPSC-derived neural activity to characterise the process of developing spikes and network bursts and emergent neural synchronisation based on the analysis of functional connectivity (FC). Two clusters of spikes, each with 2 ms duration, were identified using the K-means of PCA-derived spike features. Distinct spatial topologies of functional connectivity in iPSC-derived neuronal activity

We used the burst analysis results (Fig. 6C-D) to define the day-age intervals consisting of premature and mature iPSC-derived neuronal functional connectivity patterns averaged across 24 MEA wells : Day 18−23, day 24−28, and day 28−55, we estimated the functional connectivity and its modulation frequency. The PCA of day-age-dependent FC array resulted in three spatial topologies and their frequency of variations (i.e. power spectral densirty) over a 5-minute recording on each day age (Fig. 7A-B). Note that the arrangement of electrodes/nodes within neural network correspond to the MEA layout with numerical identifiers (panel A). The three connectivity graphs correspond to different developmental stages:

Day age 18−23: The network showed a single dominant hub (node 12), indicated by the central node with the majority of functional connections (left network), with a gradual decline in power (< 0.2) across increasing frequencies, indicating broad activity under 15 Hz (Fig. 7A, left panel).

Day age 24−28: The network expanded in complexity, displaying four hubs (nodes 31, 24, 13, and 42), suggesting increased connectivity between the iPSC-derived neuronal populations and functional integration Fig. 7A, middle panel, with a peak at a lower frequency range (around 10 Hz with a peak at PSD = 0.29) (Fig. 7B, middle panel). An increase in number of hub nodes in the network indicated an established rhythmic modulation of iPSC-derived neural synchronisation over a 5-minute recording.

Day 32−55: The network reverted to a simpler structure with a single dominant hub (node 34), potentially indicating a pruning or refinement process in the neural connections (Fig. 7A, right panel), with an underdamped trend similar to the earlier stage (around the PSD=0.2), implying reduced structured network oscillatory activity (Fig. 7B, right panel).

Discussion

The coordinated down-regulation of structural markers and volumetric amplification of synaptic puncta support the conclusion that the functional neuronal network maturation is not only occurring but accelerating between day 21 and day 28. This is consistent with changes in the topology and spectral contents of the iPSC-derived functional connectivity (Fig. 7B). We characterised iPSC-derived neuronal population activity using spatiotemporal phase synchronization of MEA signals. The phase-locking value technique identified functional connections between the electrical activities of iPSC-derived neurons using 12 electrodes. Based on burst characteristics such as network burst count and burst rate, we categorised neuronal activity into three developmental intervals: Day 18−23, Day 24−28, and Day 32−55. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was used to reveal the spatial topology of iPSC-derived functional connectivity, resulted in three distinct network states. Over the culture period, the iPSC-derived networks demonstrated three spectral properties that followed a 1/f-like distribution, with only one network showing a strong oscillatory behaviour at 10 Hz. These findings confirm the ability of human iPSC-derived networks to replicate microscale cortical activity dynamics in vitro22,23. The biphasic trend observed in spike rate, burst rate, and network transitions suggests an early phase of increasing synchronous spikes and bursts, followed by a peak in functional connectivity on day 24−28. Beyond day 32, a progressive decline in neuronal synchronisation suggests network dissolution. This dynamic evolution of network structure and function aligns with previous studies, highlighting key transitions in neuronal formation, maturation, and dissolution, which could be driven by synaptic plasticity and connectivity changes24. Furthermore, the Day-age dependent synchronisation index fluctuates over culture period, with some developmental stages (e.g., days 32, 36, and 50) exhibiting higher PLV distributions that exceed the confidence interval threshold, suggesting stronger functional connectivity at those ages. This developmental trend in phase locking may reflect changes in neural synchronisation during maturation23.

Due to the use of opaque MEA plates, the limitation of current study is the absence of correlation between functional readouts and cellular organisation. While it is possible to perform complementary analyses on parallel cultures grown on transparent substrates for imaging and staining (e.g. coverslips or slides), meaningful conclusions drawn from separate cultures (on different substrates) may not reliably reflect the same network characteristics. As such, we based our assessment of stability solely on the functional transitions observed in the MEA recordings. Notably, the shift in activity dynamics after day 18 was highly consistent across wells (N = 24), suggesting a reproducible transition from random activity to functional network stability. We attribute this clear transition to our rigorous assay setup, including precise cell seeding densities and the use of the mitotic inhibitor Ara-C to promote the maturation of defined neuron-to-astrocyte populations.

Previous research on human iPSC-derived neuronal networks using low-density MEAs examined long-term electrophysiological activity and pharmacological responses in co-cultures of neurons and astrocytes24–27. These studies demonstrated the crucial role of astrocytes in network maturation and explored the long-term functional development of iPSC-derived neuronal networks. Our findings are consistent with previous reports, where synchronous spikes and bursts emerged after day 24, with network bursts peaking around day 28. This aligns with synchronised bursting patterns observed in similar studies, where network bursts typically stabilise between 3−4 weeks and increase in complexity24–26. Although burst dynamics vary depending on experimental conditions, our findings indicate that network-wide bursting stabilises at a similar culture age as previously reported studies27–29. Moreover, we observed a decrease in network bursts beyond four weeks, consistent with reports indicating that mature network bursts lose their connectivity patterns over time after 5−6 weeks in culture30,31. The gradual decline in synchrony beyond day 32 suggests that neural cultures undergo a dissolution phase, in which functional network architecture becomes less organised over time. These findings provide further insights into the developmental trajectory of iPSC-derived networks and their potential for modelling neuronal maturation and connectivity changes in vitro.

Our identification of three distinct network states indicates that iPSC-derived neuronal activity undergoes three main phases of spatial connectivity during culture: (1) emergence of spikes and bursts, (2) establishment of synchronised network bursts, and (3) network dissolution. These transitions align with the behaviour of oscillatory networks previously observed in iPSC-derived neuronal cultures23,28,29,32. Spectral fingerprint analysis of these day-age-dependent networks suggests modulation by different frequencies over a 5-minute recording: 10 Hz in the emerging phase (one hub node), 10 Hz in the established phase (four hub nodes), and 10 Hz in the underdamped phase (one hub node). These findings further demonstrate a dynamic phase synchronisation regime in the early stages of iPSC neuronal culture22,32,33. Crucially, variations in spike rate indicate two modes of excitatory spike networks driven by spontaneous excitation and time-locked synchrony34,35. This suggests that premature in vitro neuronal networks require established synaptic connections for maintaining functional synchrony36. Additionally, the slow timescale modulation of fast spiking activity aligns with prior findings on iPSC-derived networks exhibiting electrical modulation37. Since we did not use chemical stimulation to alter neuronal synchrony, observed changes in fast-spiking behavior were solely driven by electrical synchronisation, further supporting the role of endogenous network dynamics in shaping neuronal connectivity37.

Methods

Experimental procedures

The iPSCs were generated by reprogramming human foetal fibroblasts (PCS-201-010, American Type Culture Collection) by the Gene Editing Facility at the Murdoch Children’s Research Institute, Victoria, Australia as previously described38. The iPSCs were maintained on Vitronectin-treated plastic and cultured in E8 media, with routine passaging using the reagents concentration of 0.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mu$$\end{document}g/ml EDTA treatment (all reagents from Thermofisher, USA). The iPSCs were differentiated to cortical neurons using virally delivered doxycycline-induced NGN2 transgenes as previously described39. The NGN2-differentiation method primarily produces excitatory glutamatergic neurons. However, GABA neurons are expressed, although at a lower ratio than observed in vivo40. We implemented synaptic staining to quantify maturation of neuronal neural network on days 21 and 28 independently: DAPI, PSD-95, VGLUT2, Gephyrin, MAP2, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-Tubulin, Synaptophysin, DAP1. This approach allowed for determining cell density (DAPI), network architecture (MAP2, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-Tubulin), synaptic maturation (PSD-95, Gephyrin, VGLUT2, Synaptophysin), and presynaptic vs postsynaptic growth21. To this end, for each sample, 3D image stacks corresponding to DAP1, PSD-95, VGLUT2, and Gephyrin immunostaining were analyzed using ImageJ/Fiji software41. For each image channel, a marker-specific automatic threshold was applied to distinguish puncta from background: DAPI (nuclei) using Otsu, PSD-95 and Gephyrin (postsynaptic markers) using Moments, VGLUT2 (presynaptic marker) using Default, and DAP1 using Otsu. Thresholds were adjusted manually only when necessary to ensure that detected puncta corresponded to visually discernible signal. Thresholded images were converted into binary masks, with pixels above the threshold set to 1 and all others set to 0, enabling subsequent quantification of puncta number and volume. Three-dimensional connected components were identified using 26-connectivity to define individual puncta. For each marker, the mean puncta volume was calculated by multiplying the voxel count of each component by the voxel size. This pipeline was also applied to the Stain 2 condition, which included MAP2 (Moments), \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-Tubulin (Default), Synaptophysin (Moments), and DAP1 (Otsu), allowing analysis of dendritic morphology, cytoskeletal organization, and presynaptic maturation.

Neural data acquisition from human iPSCs/MEA model

The MEA plates were left to stabilise on the head stage for at least 5 minutes prior to electrical recordings. The media was changed 24-hours prior to recording to minimize variation caused by the cell environment. The medium was changed 24 hours before the measurement for all the recordings. The activity was recorded daily at the early stages to capture the emergence of spikes and bursts, and then every second day for the network bursts. We postponed the acquisition of cell neuron activity until 17 days from the first day of cell culture to warrant the transition from early stage of cell neuron development to a spiking-activity stage where dense connections between neurons occur. This delay helped the formation of dense connections between iPSC-derived neurons, and hence minimise the effect of morphological changes and cell movement that could alter the detected electrical signal. Note that structural connectivity pattern (or wiring pattern between neurons) drives the dynamics of interactions between neurons or neuronal synchronisation42. That is, wiring pattern between neurons is central in driving the dynamics of temporal correlation of distributed neuronal activities. Therefore, this study does not eliminate changes in the morphology (or physical connection) between neurons in post early-stage of network development, which can be an inherent limitation of this study. Note also that the emergence of spikes and burst of spikes after Day 18 (the first day of neural activity recording) suggests the establishment of dense connections between iPSC-derived neurons, and hence less degrees of freedom for cell neuron movement and morphological changes. The iPSCs were cultured in a 24-well 12-electrode/well multi-electrode array plates (24W700/100F-288, Multichannel systems, Germany). Neural activity in each well was acquired in 5-minute recordings at 20 kHz using the MEA Multiwell Head stage unit (Multichannel Systems, Germany). During data acquisition plates were kept in an enclosed chamber into which 5% CO2 in air was perfused. The MEA data was acquired from iPSC-derived neurons from cultured day 18 (when independent spontaneous spikes became detectable) until day 55. Recordings were made on 21 non-consecutive days to reduce disruption to neuronal culture.

MEA data pre-processing

For each well, the acquired MEA data were bandpass filtered with a fourth-order Butterworth band-stop filter (48 - 52 Hz) followed by a fourth-order Butterworth filter (100 Hz -3500 Hz). For each well, 12 electrode signals were average referenced.

Spike sorting

To capture and characterise global network-level activity rather than isolate single-unit activity in our MEA data, we aggregated spikes across electrodes to i) capture global synchrony, ii) identify dominant firing patterns representative of collective in vitro activity, iii) reduce dimensionality because per-electrode spike sorting may result in an overwhelming number of clusters − many of which may not be functionally distinct, and iv) overcome low firing rates since some electrodes may record sparse or noisy data. Therefore, simplified clustering from Day age data was implemented to improve reproducibility across batches and experimental conditions, especially when unit-level identity is less critical.

We implemented spike sorting method to identify and cluster the iPSC-derived neurons activity for each culture day; from Day 18 to Day 55. On each day-age, for each electrode data in each well, a local field potential was considered a potential spike if its amplitude crosses the threshold value defined by 4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document}median(\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$|electrode \ data|$$\end{document})/0.6727,43. The shape of each detected spike was next captured by including a 2.5 ms window about the peak of the same detected spike; 0.7 ms to the left-side and 1.8 ms to the right-side of the spike peak. If the same peak value was detected again in that 2.5 ms window, the same spike would be confirmed as a captured spike. The captured spikes from all electrodes were combined to construct well-based spike data profile. For each of the 24 wells, the captured spikes were grouped into different clusters based on their shapes in the spike sorting process27,43. Briefly, for each MEA well, the principal component analysis (PCA) technique was used to extract spike features (i.e., in this case the first two eigenvectors corresponding to the first two largest eigenvalues), followed by the projection of the grouped spikes onto an orthogonal space. The K-means clustering technique was then used to generate two clusters of spike patterns belonging to the two principal components. The accuracy of spike sorting method was tested using the inter-spike-interval (ISI) violation rate where the ratio of number of ISIs less than 1.5 ms (a refractory period) to the number of detected spikes was set to be less than 2%44. Finally, the identified spike clusters in each MEA well were grouped across all 24 wells, and the K-means technique was then used to generate two clusters of spikes across 24 wells on each day-age. The MEA analysis was implemented in MATLAB.

Burst analysis

All detected spikes were further analysed by a 4-step method to identify burst45. The parameters of burst detection were as follows: ISI\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} 50 ms, at least 4 spikes in the burst and 30 ms minimum duration of the burst. Each detected burst was characterised by three measurements; burst duration, total number of bursts during 5 minutes of MEA signal, and inter-burst interval (IBI). For the burst detection in each electrode, we used the following criteria to identify a burst as a train of closely spaced spikes in each MEA signal: Max ISI within a burst \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\le$$\end{document} 50 ms, Min burst duration \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}30 ms, Min number of spikes per burst > 4 spikes. For the network burst characterisation in each well, we detected a time-synchronous bursting activity across 12 electrodes based on the following criteria: Bursts on \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document} 50% of electrodes, time between the first and last burst spike (network burst duration), number of network bursts per minute (network burst frequency). We pooled network bursts and burst rate measurements independently across 24 MEA wells per Day age. Our analysis did extend to pharmacological interrogation of the network using glutamate blockers (i.e NBQV/APV). While we acknowledge that a detailed pharmacological profile, combined with a transcriptomics approach would be helpful for classifying the network’s maturity state, our analysis was focused purely on functional characterisation, without the requirement to incorporate these additional methods. We define a premature network as a low burst rate and low network bursts. The entire burst analysis was implemented in MATLAB.

Construction of functional connectivity in iPSCs-derived neurons

For each day of neural culture, for each of the 24 MEA wells, the FC was computed using phase-locking value (PLV) method, which is the neuronal synchronisation index46–50. A 0.5 s sliding-window with 90% overlapping approach was used to infer dynamic functional connectivity over a 5-minute MEA recording on each day age. For each sliding window, the Hilbert phase of electrode signals was estimated. FC was next computed by the PLV technique. This approach resulted in spatiotemporal functional networks represented by the data matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathbf{X}}\in {\mathbb {R}}^{n\times n\times K}$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n=12$$\end{document} denotes the number of electrodes and K is the number of sliding windows over a 5-minute electrode signals. The Day age FC was then measured by averaging these 24 FCs (i.e., across 24 MEA wells) for subsequent analyses.

Determination of day-age dependent emerging functional connectivity

We define a premature network as a low network bursts and low burst rate. The results of burst analysis were used to identify the day-age intervals consisting of premature and mature iPSC-derived neuronal functional connectivity patterns. Three day-age intervals were defined: the network burst gradually increased in the day-age interval of 18 and 23, then reached its maximum value between the day age 24 and 28, and finally started to decay after the day age 28. Characterisation of spatial topology patterns of functional networks and synchronisation index

For each Day age, we selected the top 25% of the strongest edges in the group-level FC (across 24 MEA wells), as similar in50. A graph theoretical metrics, betweenness centrality, was then used to characterise the topology pattern of the thresholded network. The betweenness centrality of a node is a measure of the node’s importance in a network − quantifying how often the node of interest lies on the shortest paths between other nodes and effectively acting as a hub node48,49. Note that, on each Day age FC, the hub nodes were detected by selecting the top 5% of the betweenness centrality measures. We also determined the Day-age dependent neuronal synchronisation index by averaging the PLV measures across 12 electrodes for each of the 24 wells. We then compared synchronisation indices using the median, 25th percentile and 75th percentile across 24 wells over the culture period.

Statistical analysis

We used surrogate data statistics to test the statistical significance of the results51. To this end, for each day age, the surrogate data were generated by the Fourier phase randomisation of each MEA signal. The surrogate MEA signals were next used by the same analysis pipeline to construct the null distribution of spikes, bursts, network bursts, functional connectivity and PSD of the temporal dynamics of functional connectivity on each day age (1000 realisations). For each measurement of interest such as spikes, bursts, network burst and functional connectivity, the 99% confidence interval, at \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$p<0.01$$\end{document}, was next determined by selecting 995th measurement of ascending-based sorted surrogate data. Note that, a two-tailed null distribution was considered for the determination of 99% confidence interval. Those measurement values such as number of synchronous spikes, spike rate, number of network bursts, burst rate, functional connectivity pattern, synchronisation index and the PSD of the temporal dynamics of functional connectivity.
