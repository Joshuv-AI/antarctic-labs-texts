Longitudinal Research: Differences in Nurse Work Environment Between the COVID‐19 Outbreak and Mitigation Periods
Authors: Ru‐Wen Liao, Chu‐Hsuan Cheng, I‐Shiang Tzeng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimThe coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) outbreak led to a massive influx of patients into hospitals, thus prompting the implementation of various response mechanisms to manage the surge in number of patients. During the mitigation period, hospital response mechanisms ceased and ensued a return to normal settings. However, changing hospital settings can affect nurses' work environments. Therefore, the primary objectives of this study were to investigate the impact of changes during disease outbreaks and the mitigation period on nurses' work environments, and to analyse the relationship between nurses' background variables and work environments.Study DesignA longitudinal research design is employed herein.MethodsTwo surveys were conducted in a hospital: the first during the COVID‐19 outbreak period, when the hospital implemented response mechanisms, and the second during the COVID‐19 mitigation period, when the hospital returned to normalcy. The questionnaire covered the nurses' background variables and work environment.ResultsWhen the COVID‐19 outbreak transitioned to the mitigation period, satisfaction with managerial (p < 0.05) and unit support (p < 0.05) in the workplace decreased. Regarding the relationship between nurses' backgrounds and work environment satisfaction, we found that nurses working in COVID‐19 dedicated units reported higher satisfaction with manager support (p < 0.001), peer support (p < 0.001), unit support (p < 0.05) and intent to stay (p < 0.05) than those in non‐COVID‐19 dedicated units. Additionally, factors such as age, nursing experience, sex, education, clinical teaching/mentoring roles, perceived COVID‐19 infection risk, work shifts, housing problems during the outbreak and experience of discrimination influenced work environment satisfaction.Patient or Public ContributionHospitals need to adjust the transformational steps of their COVID‐19 or emerging infectious disease response mechanisms, or provide sufficient support to these nurses to mitigate the impact on their work environment.

Introduction

The rapid transmission of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) quickly led to a global pandemic (Ciotti et al. 2020). The first outbreak in Taiwan occurred in May 2021. By September 2023, COVID‐19 had infected over 10 million people and resulted in more than 17 thousand deaths in Taiwan (National Center for High‐performance Computing 2023). Since COVID‐19 spread rapidly, medical facilities in many countries have been overwhelmed by the massive need for isolation and treatment of COVID‐19 patients (Ranney et al. 2020; Sen‐Crowe et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2020), greatly impacting medical staff.

Several response mechanisms have been activated in hospitals to cope with the large number of patients affected by the outbreak and prevent nosocomial infections. These mechanisms include temporary assignment of medical staff, support for quarantine efforts, reduction in hospitalisation of nonacute or nonserious patients and implementation of strict precautionary measures to care for patients and prevent COVID‐19 nosocomial infections (Chen et al. 2020; Feng et al. 2020; Said and El‐Shafei 2021; Su et al. 2020).

Nurses who are among the medical staff on the frontlines against COVID‐19 (Chen et al. 2020; Fawaz et al. 2020) also face some of the most stressful situations, especially those who directly care for COVID‐19 patients (Nagel et al. 2022). They experience increased workloads owing to the influx of massive numbers of COVID‐19 patients and strict precautionary measures to prevent nosocomial infections (Hoogendoorn et al. 2021; Nagel et al. 2022; Said and El‐Shafei 2021) and a lack of medical resources (Nagel et al. 2022; Ranney et al. 2020).

Increased work demands on nurses due to COVID‐19 undoubtedly impact workplace satisfaction. Work environment and perceived workload are organisational traits that facilitate professional nursing practices (Lake et al. 2019). A poor or unsatisfactory work environment indicates that the workload exceeds what nurses can handle, or that the workplace lacks support for nursing practice (Cabrita et al. 2022; Teasley et al. 2007), such as work overload and lack of medical resources caused by COVID‐19. This can affect nurses' work performance, quality of medical care (Al Sabei et al. 2020) and patient safety (Moisoglou et al. 2020). Moreover, it can decrease nurses' job satisfaction (Labrague and Santos 2021; Lin et al. 2019), increase their turnover rate and affect the recruitment of new nurses (Al Yahyaei et al. 2022; Cabrita et al. 2022; Phillips 2020). Cox et al. (2006) developed an instrument to assess work environments (Teasley et al. 2007). This instrument measures nurse‐perceived satisfaction with management, units and peer support as well as workload and intent to stay. This instrument can serve as an indicator for improving the work environment.

The work environment may change after the hospital response mechanisms are activated. Paquay et al. (2024) examined these changes in two Belgian hospitals using a mixed methods design. They compared the work environments of medical staff before and after the COVID‐19 outbreak. The findings showed that work satisfaction and the perceived efficiency of services decreased after the outbreak. Interestingly, this study revealed that COVID‐19 units reported higher job satisfaction and greater satisfaction with their work environment than non‐COVID‐19 units, which differs from the findings of other studies. Researchers suggest that this difference could be attributed to the benefits provided by changes in cooperation strategies among various units during the epidemic or that caring for COVID‐19 patients may have reinforced the sense of meaning in the nursing profession.

Conditions during the COVID‐19 pandemic are subject to change. The number of infected patients decreased gradually after the peak of the outbreak (National Center for High‐performance Computing 2023). Hospital response mechanisms were also adopted to mitigate the outbreak. In Taiwan, these mechanisms remained active until the outbreak entered the mitigation phase, when new COVID‐19 cases had decreased to a manageable level, and hospitals began halting partial response mechanisms while resuming the admission and treatment of nonacute, non‐COVID‐19 patients. These changes also affected nurses' work environments. Since the hospital's adjustments were based on medical capacity considerations, and activating or halting response mechanisms required nurses' involvement, the impact on nurses' work environments was difficult to evaluate. Even during periods of low numbers of newly hospitalised COVID‐19 patients, the effects are not easily categorised as strictly positive or negative.

Since nurses are among the frontline personnel fighting emerging infectious diseases, such as COVID‐19, and are also the most in‐demand professionals who are key to maintaining patient safety and clinical care quality, the nursing shortage is an important global problem already (Boniol et al. 2022). Therefore, a comprehensive understanding of the impact of the COVID‐19 trend and the hospital's epidemic response mechanism on nurses' work environments is important, as the work environment is an indicator of the quality of care and nurses' intention to resign. However, much of the research on the effects of the COVID‐19 pandemic on nurses' work environments has relied on cross‐sectional designs. The evolving impact of the changing COVID‐19 conditions and hospitals' epidemic response mechanisms on nurses' work environments remains unclear.

The Study

Aims and Objective

This study investigated the impact of COVID‐19 trends and changes in hospital response mechanisms on nurses' work environments, focusing on the transition from the outbreak period to the mitigation phase. The primary objective of this study was to investigate the impact of changes made during disease outbreaks and mitigation periods on nurses' work environments. The secondary objective was to examine the relationship between nurses' backgrounds and their work environments, especially the differences between COVID‐19 dedicated units and non‐COVID‐19 units, because the nurses in these units had to strictly implement protective care measures against COVID‐19, which may affect the working environment. We hope that our findings offer valuable references for hospital management during outbreaks of emerging infectious diseases, such as diseases similar to COVID‐19, to reduce the impact on nurses' work environments. Most importantly, this study aims to maintain the quality of nursing care and reduce nurse turnover.

Hypotheses

Based on the two objectives of our study, we propose two hypotheses:

Since the impact of COVID‐19 on nurses' work environments was caused by the massive need to care for hospitalised infected patients, leading to nurse overload and a lack of medical resources, the transition to the mitigation period meant that the number of infected patients in hospitals decreased, alleviating overload and resource shortages, and nurses' satisfaction with their work environments should increase. Therefore, the first hypothesis is that ‘nurses' satisfaction with their work environment will increase following the transition into the mitigation phase’.

Methods

Design and Population

The primary objective of this study was to investigate the impact of changes during disease outbreaks and the mitigation phase on nurses' work environments by conducting a longitudinal study with a teaching hospital located in northern Taiwan, implementing outbreak response mechanisms from the first outbreak to mid‐October 2021. Therefore, we administered pre‐ and post‐surveys to all nurses working in the teaching hospital before (22 July 2021) and after (15 November 2021) the hospital's outbreak response mechanisms were halted to assess their impact on the nurses.

Participant Recruitment and Data Collection

In both surveys, the researcher first contacted the head nurse of each unit to obtain permission for recruitment and confirm the number of nurses who matched the inclusion criteria in each unit. The researcher then explained the purpose of the study during unit meetings most frequently attended by nurses and distributed questionnaires along with an incentive reward (approximately 100 New Taiwan dollars) to all participants who wished to participate. For the nurses who did not attend the meetings, we asked the head nurses to inform them. If they were interested in participating, the researcher contacted them and provided them with a questionnaire and an incentive reward. After the participants completed the questionnaire, they returned it to a designated anonymous submission box in their respective work units. Participants could also return the questionnaire directly to the researcher. Questionnaires were collected weekly. If, after 1 month, the number of returned questionnaires from a unit was lower than the number distributed, the researcher asked the head nurse of the unit to remind any nurse who wished to submit their completed questionnaires.

During the questionnaire distribution period, a pre‐survey was conducted between 22 July 2021 and 11 August 2021, while the post‐survey was conducted between 15 November 2021 and 17 December 2021. All completed questionnaires were collected approximately 2 months after the first day of recruitment.

Sample Size

Our target population comprised all registered nurses who had been working for more than 3 months in a teaching hospital located in northern Taiwan. This period allows nurses to complete their newcomer training and officially begin patient care.

According to a hospital report, the total population is approximately 800. Using a sample size calculator with default settings (95% confidence level and 5% margin of error), the required sample size was calculated as at least 260 (Calculator.net, 2025).

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were registered nurses who had been employed at the hospital for more than 3 months.

In the exclusion criteria, first, participants who refused to participate in this study were excluded. Second, participants who refused to participate in the second survey or only participated in the first survey were excluded after analysis of the differences between withdrawal and full participation had been completed. Third, questionnaires with more than half the items left unanswered on at least one of the scales were excluded.

Instrument With Validity and Reliability

This study used a questionnaire survey that included two sections: participants' backgrounds and work environment.

Background

This study collected demographic variables and those that could be affected by COVID‐19, including age, nursing experience, sex, marital status, responsibilities for childcare during the outbreak, education, type of work department, nursing clinical ladder levels, perceived risk of COVID‐19 infection, work shift, clinical teacher/mentoring role, experience caring for COVID‐19 patients during the local outbreak, experience of housing problems during the outbreak and instances of discrimination against family members due to their occupation.

The work departments were divided into non‐COVID‐19 and COVID‐19 dedicated wards. The COVID‐19 dedicated wards included parts of the medical ward, medical intensive care unit and emergency room. These units were converted into wards dedicated to treating COVID‐19 patients during the pandemic.

The nursing clinical ladder levels represent nursing competency levels in Taiwan: N: Newly registered nurses. N1: Registered nurses with more than 1 year of experience. N2: Registered nurses who have been at N1 for more than 1 year and are qualified for critical care. N3+: Registered nurses who have been in N2 for more than 1 year and are qualified for teaching and holistic care.

Work Environment

Work environment was measured using the Taiwan version of the Individual Workload Perception Scale—Revised (T‐IWPS‐R). The IWPS‐R, originally developed by Cox and her colleagues (Teasley et al. 2007), was utilised to assess the work environment of nurses. The Taiwanese version of the scale was translated by Lin et al. (2011), who adjusted the original IWPS‐R from six subscales and 29 items to six subscales and 24 items to better suit the Taiwanese population. These subscales include managerial support, peer support, unit support, workload and intent to stay, all of which are critical factors for nurses (Cox et al. 2006; Lin et al. 2011). Managerial support reflects nurses' satisfaction with the assistance provided by supervisors or managers. Peer support refers to nurses' relationships with colleagues, whereas unit support reflects satisfaction with the resources that their workplace provides to support nursing practices. Workload reflects whether nurses perceive their workload as reasonable and intent to stay captures their intention to remain in their current position (Cox et al. 2006; Lin et al. 2011). Each item in this study was assessed using a 5‐point Likert scale, where respondents indicated the degree of conformity between their own situations and the items. Scores range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with a higher score indicating greater satisfaction with the work environment. The total score is typically the average score for each item. For internal consistency, Cronbach's alpha for the Taiwanese version was 0.88 (Lin et al. 2011), whereas in our study, it reached 0.912 for both surveys (pre‐ and post‐intervention), indicating a high level of internal consistency for this scale.

Data Analysis

The change in scale scores between the pre‐ and post‐surveys was analysed using a paired t‐test. For the relationship between background variables and scale scores, independent t‐tests or one‐way analyses of variance (ANOVA) were employed to evaluate the relationship between categorical variables and scale scores, whereas continuous variables were analysed using Pearson correlation coefficients. Furthermore, the generalised estimating equation (GEE) approach was used, with the covariance structure set to ‘first‐order autoregressive (AR1)’. This structure was applied as a repeated‐measures test to correct for false positive results caused by prior multiple testing (independent t‐test, one‐way ANOVA and correlation coefficient) of variables. Variables that remained significantly correlated with the scores after adjustment were included in the GEE model to construct a time‐dependent framework for adjusting and identifying predictors of scale scores.

In addition, we analysed the differences between withdrawn and fully participating participants. Differences in categorical variables were analysed using the Chi‐squared test, whereas differences in continuous variables were analysed using independent t‐tests.

To handle missing data, missing values in the scales were imputed using the average score of the items from the survey wave. Additionally, missing data regarding the participants' background information were not processed.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by ‘REDACTED’ institutional review board (IRB), with an approved study period from 15 July 2021 to 15 July 2022. The cover page of the questionnaire included an informed consent statement and a yes/no question asking whether the participant wanted to participate. The informed consent statement explains the purpose of this study, what information will be collected (including the number of staff for matching pre‐ and post‐surveys, personal information and scales), how the data will be used, and security measures for the questionnaire data. Voluntary consent was reemphasised during follow‐up reminders. All nurses interested in participating were asked to read an informed consent statement and respond to yes/no questions before completing the questionnaire. Only questionnaires with ‘yes’ responses were included in this study. A ‘no’ response was considered nonparticipation, and the questionnaires were immediately destroyed and not included in the study. Participation in this study was voluntary for all nurses, and they were free to withdraw from the study without providing a reason. The scales used in this study were approved by the respective authors.

Results

A total of 509 nurses participated in the pre‐survey, of whom 209 withdrew prior to the survey. Ultimately, 300 nurses completed the study, exceeding the suggested sample size of 260. Therefore, our results were sufficient. Because the participants were allowed to withdraw without providing a reason, the exact reasons for withdrawal were unknown. However, based on the information provided by the researcher introducing the study, possible reasons included resignation or lack of time to complete the questionnaire. According to the nursing department, approximately 86 nurses resigned from the hospital between August and December.

Examination of Hypothesis Results

H1: Nurses' Satisfaction With Their Work Environment Will Increased Following Transition Into the Mitigation Phase

Our first hypothesis proposed that nurses' work environment satisfaction would increase as the COVID‐19 outbreak transitioned to a mitigation phase. However, our results (Figure 1 and Table 2) do not support this hypothesis. According to the paired t‐test results, satisfaction with manager support and unit support significantly decreased in the post‐survey (manager support: pre = 3.89 ± 0.52, post = 3.83 ± 0.52, p < 0.05; unit support: pre = 3.89 ± 0.57, post = 3.80 ± 0.63, p < 0.05). Even after adjustment using the GEE model, these two subscales still showed significant declines in satisfaction in the post‐survey (manager support: β = −0.10, p < 0.05; unit support: β = −0.10, p < 0.05).

H2: COVID‐19 Care Units (Dedicated COVID‐19 Units) Have Different Levels of Satisfaction With Their Work Environment Compared to Non‐COVID‐19 Units

Our second hypothesis proposed that nurses working in COVID‐19 dedicated wards would have higher satisfaction levels than those in non‐COVID‐19 dedicated wards. Our results supported this hypothesis (Figure 2 and Table 3). In both the pre‐ and post‐surveys, nurses in COVID‐19 dedicated wards reported higher satisfaction with manager support (pre‐survey: nondedicated wards = 3.82 ± 0.48, dedicated wards = 4.10 ± 0.57, p < 0.001; post‐survey: nondedicated wards = 3.77 ± 0.50, dedicated wards = 4.00 ± 0.55, p < 0.001) and peer support (pre‐survey: nondedicated wards = 3.91 ± 0.56, dedicated wards = 4.21 ± 0.55, p < 0.001; post‐survey: nondedicated wards = 3.92 ± 0.54, dedicated wards = 4.08 ± 0.55, p < 0.02). Additionally, in the pre‐survey, nurses in COVID‐19 dedicated wards reported higher satisfaction with unit support (nondedicated wards = 3.84 ± 0.55, dedicated wards = 4.06 ± 0.61, p < 0.05), workload (nondedicated wards = 3.42 ± 0.79, dedicated wards = 3.68 ± 0.83, p < 0.05) and intent to stay (nondedicated wards = 2.98 ± 0.71, dedicated wards = 3.18 ± 0.78, p < 0.05), but these differences were not observed in the post‐survey.

Differences Between Participants Who Fully Participated in the Study and Those Who Withdrew After the Pre‐Survey

These two groups showed no significant differences in most background variables (p > 0.05). However, participants who withdrew had a higher percentage of individuals with no mentoring role (full participation: 58.3%; withdrawal: 68.9%, p < 0.05) and working fixed shifts (full participation: 46.0%; withdrawal: 58.7%, p < 0.05). In addition, differences were observed in the work environment scales (Table 4). Withdrawn participants exhibited significantly lower satisfaction in peer support (withdrawn: 3.86 ± 0.58, fully participating: 3.99 ± 0.57, p < 0.05) and unit support (withdrawn: 3.72 ± 0.58, fully participating: 3.89 ± 0.57, p < 0.05) than those who fully participated. Additionally, although the score difference between manager support and intent to stay was not statistically significant, the p value was close to the critical value (manager support, p = 0.051; intent to stay, p = 0.062).

We identified several variables that may affect nurses' satisfaction with their work environment. Older nurses reported higher satisfaction with their workload and greater intention to stay in their jobs, and male nurses reported greater satisfaction with their workload than did female nurses. Nurses with university education had lower satisfaction with manager, peer and unit support than those with junior college education. Nurses with a clinical teacher/mentoring role had lower satisfaction with their workload than those without. If nurses felt that they were at risk of being infected with COVID‐19, they had lower satisfaction with all work environment subscales and wanted to leave their jobs. Nurses with three shifts had lower satisfaction with managerial support, unit support and workload than those with fixed shifts. Nurses who had to apply for staff quarters had lower satisfaction with manager support and workload, and lower intention to stay in their jobs than those who were unaffected. Finally, if participants' family members had been discriminated against due to their occupation, they would have had lower satisfaction with peer and unit support.

Discussion

This study aimed to assess the impact of changes during disease outbreaks and the mitigation period on nurses' work environments and to explore the relationship between nurses' backgrounds and their work environment. Based on previous relevant research findings, we proposed and examined two hypotheses.

Hypotheses

Hypothesis 1: Since the Number of New COVID‐19 Patients Decreased Significantly During the Mitigation Period, Nurses' Satisfaction With Their Work Environment Likely Increased

However, our results do not support this hypothesis. We found that participants' satisfaction with manager and unit support in the work environment decreased after the COVID‐19 outbreak transitioned to a mitigation period.

Our hypothesis was based on numerous studies reporting that, during the initial stages of the COVID‐19 outbreak, nurses experienced a significant increase in workload, leading to heightened work stress, burnout, intention to leave their positions and emotional distress (Bergman et al. 2021; Nagel et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2023). These challenges arose from a surge in COVID‐19 patient numbers, temporary reassignments, intensified anti‐epidemic regulations, unfamiliarity with COVID‐19 and resource shortages (Bergman et al. 2021; Hoogendoorn et al. 2021; Nagel et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2023). Therefore, as COVID‐19 transitioned into the mitigation period and the number of new COVID‐19 cases decreased to levels that allowed hospitals to consider halting their response mechanisms, nurses' satisfaction with their work environment was expected to increase. However, our results indicate the opposite.

One possible reason for this could be our results. In our study, during the outbreak period, hospital response mechanisms were activated to address COVID‐19 following the guidelines of the Ministry of Health and Welfare (2021). These mechanisms involve reducing nonurgent medical procedures, such as surgeries and hospitalisations. Furthermore, some nonurgent departments were temporarily closed or converted into dedicated COVID‐19 wards. As a result, hospital resources and medical capacity could be more effectively directed towards managing the influx of COVID‐19 patients. During the mitigation period, the response mechanism was paused, and regular medical operations were resumed. However, wards converted into dedicated COVID‐19 wards have not yet returned to their original use, and anti‐epidemic work regulations have not been lifted. Therefore, in nondedicated departments, the allocation of limited resources requires nurses to care for various types of patients while complying with the epidemic prevention regulations, which can complicate the care process. In addition, the risk of nosocomial infections remains high. According to Hoogendoorn et al. (2021), the caregiving process under epidemic prevention regulations can become more complex and increase workload. Nagel et al. (2022) reported that a risky workplace, increased workload and lack of necessary resources indicated a worse work environment and impacted health and well‐being. Therefore, during the mitigation period, when the hospital's response mechanisms were halted, nurses faced limited resources, heavier workloads and riskier workplaces, resulting in decreased satisfaction with the work environment.

Hypothesis 2: COVID‐19 Care Units (Dedicated COVID‐19 Units) May Have Different Levels of Satisfaction With Their Work Environment Compared to Non‐COVID‐19 Units

Our results supported the hypothesis that participants working in COVID‐19 dedicated wards had different levels of work environment satisfaction than those in non‐COVID‐19 dedicated wards.

However, our results contradict some studies that reported frontline nurses experiencing heavy workloads and burnout due to the massive number of COVID‐19 patients, intensified anti‐epidemic regulations, unfamiliarity with COVID‐19 and a lack of resources to combat the disease (Bergman et al. 2021; Hoogendoorn et al. 2021; Nagel et al. 2022).

Conversely, some studies aligned with our findings (Paquay et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2020). Wu et al. (2020) reported that, because supervisors took the COVID‐19 epidemic seriously, nurses in dedicated wards could directly contact decision‐making supervisors and access COVID‐19‐related information more quickly than those in nondedicated wards. Similarly, Paquay et al. (2024) suggested that hospital protocols for non‐COVID‐19 wards are often unclear, causing medical staff in these units to lack COVID‐19‐related information, which increases their concerns about the epidemic. Paquay et al. (2024) suggested that the collective fight against COVID‐19 enhanced cooperation among medical professionals and provided new meaning to their work. This allows medical staff, such as nurses, to support each other in caring for patients, thereby improving job satisfaction to a certain extent. The results of these studies explained our findings, in which the participants working in COVID‐19 dedicated wards had better satisfaction with managers and peer support than those working in non‐COVID‐19 wards.

In addition, we found that when the COVID‐19 outbreak transitioned to the mitigation period, satisfaction with unit support, workload and intent to stay in COVID‐19 dedicated wards was no longer higher than that in non‐COVID‐19 wards. This result may be explained by factors similar to those discussed in Hypothesis 1. As hospitals stopped their response mechanisms, resources were redirected to non‐COVID‐19 wards. Moreover, cooperation among medical professionals may have reverted to previous strategies because of the increasing number of non‐COVID‐19 patients, leaving nurses feeling less supported, with higher workloads and reduced intent to stay.

Differences Between Participants Who Fully Participated in the Study and Those Who Withdrew After the Pre‐Survey

We analysed the differences between withdrawn participants and those who completed the study. We found that participants who withdrew after the first survey had a higher percentage of individuals with no clinical teacher/mentor role, and most worked fixed shifts during the outbreak period. In addition, we found differences in satisfaction with the work environment. Participants who withdrew had lower satisfaction with their peers and unit support. In addition, the differences in satisfaction with managerial support and intent to stay were close to the critical values.

Nurses' satisfaction with their work environment reflects how well the organisation supports them in their nursing practices (Lake et al. 2019). Peer support reflects the relationship between nurses and their colleagues, while unit support indicates how effectively nurses' work units or organisations (e.g., hospitals) provide resources to facilitate nursing practice (Cox et al. 2006; Lin et al. 2011). In our study, participants' satisfaction with both peer and unit support was lower than that of the fully participating participants, and the pre‐survey of our study was conducted during the COVID‐19 outbreak, when the hospitals' response mechanisms were still active. These results indicate that the withdrawn participants were dissatisfied with the hospital changes during the outbreak. Similar to findings in other studies, during the outbreak, nurses faced challenges such as a surge in COVID‐19 patient numbers, temporary reassignments, intensified anti‐epidemic regulations, unfamiliarity with COVID‐19 and resource shortages (Bergman et al. 2021; Nagel et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2023). In addition, strategies for collaborating with different specialists and colleagues have changed (Paquay et al. 2024). Furthermore, during the activation of hospitals' response mechanisms, nonurgent activities, such as on‐the‐job training programmes involving nurse clinical teacher/mentor role training, were postponed. Since the withdrawn participants included a higher percentage of individuals without a clinical teacher/mentor role and most of them worked fixed shifts during the outbreak period, this could be attributed to temporary reassignments and postponement of on‐the‐job training. Consequently, these participants were likely among the most severely affected by the COVID‐19 outbreak.

In the post‐survey, reports from the nursing department and the researcher who introduced the study to the units suggested that some withdrawn participants might have left the study because of resignation or lack of time to complete the questionnaire. Meanwhile, we found that participants who fully participated experienced a decrease in their satisfaction with their work environments. Therefore, the withdrawn participants' satisfaction with their work environment may have also decreased. As these participants already had lower satisfaction, this decline might have worsened their situation, leading them to leave their jobs or find the survey burdensome, ultimately resulting in withdrawal from the study.

This represents a limitation of the present study. The exclusion of these participants may have led to an overestimation of nurses' satisfaction with the work environment in the pre‐survey and an underestimation of the impact of hospitals halting their response mechanisms.

Variables That Affect the Nurses' Work Environment

Our study also analysed the relationship between the work environment and the background of nurses during the outbreak period.

In terms of nurses' age, we found that the older the nurses, the higher their satisfaction with their workload and intention to stay in their work environment. This is consistent with the results of previous studies (Alghamdi 2016; Suliman and Aljezawi 2018). Suliman and Aljezawi (2018) reported that senior nurses are usually in managerial roles that involve less physically demanding work, leading to higher satisfaction with their workload compared to junior nurses. Alghamdi (2016) reported that nurses with more nursing experience or skills perceived a lower workload for the same work content. In our study, age was highly correlated with nursing experience (r = 0.96, p < 0.001). Therefore, older nurses have more nursing experience, leading to higher satisfaction with their work environment.

Based on sex differences, we found that male nurses were more satisfied with their workload than female nurses were. However, most studies have not yielded similar results (Fasoi et al. 2020; Su 2019; Suliman and Aljezawi 2018). The results of this study are consistent with those (Moghadam et al. 2021), who reported that female intensive care unit nurses experienced more work stress than male nurses. This might be because female nurses shoulder more responsibilities at home, which could affect their work. In addition, because the percentage of male nurses was much lower than that of female nurses, the sex differences in workload satisfaction could also be due to uneven sampling, which is a limitation of our study.

Regarding education, we found that nurses who graduated from university had lower satisfaction with managers, peers and unit support than those with junior college degrees. Suliman and Aljezawi (2018) reported that nurses with higher educational degrees had higher expectations of their working environment and aspired to conduct more evidence‐based care practices. However, achieving these aspirations in their actual work is challenging, leading to lower satisfaction with their work environment.

Regarding the clinical teacher/mentor role, we found that nurses in this role were less satisfied with their workload than those who did not. The clinical teacher/mentor role is typically held by nurses who have worked in the hospital for more than 3 years and have received training to become clinical teachers/mentors. Unlike the results reported by Suliman and Aljezawi (2018), nurses with a clinical teacher/mentor role, although they had more nursing experience, did not have a supervisor role. They perform similar work as other nurses but are also responsible for training new nurses, which leads to a higher workload. This can result in lower satisfaction compared to those who do not.

Furthermore, nurses who perceived a high risk of COVID‐19 had lower satisfaction with all sections of the work environment. These results are consistent with those of previous studies (Galehdar et al. 2020; Havaei et al. 2021; Hoogendoorn et al. 2021; Nagel et al. 2022). Galehdar et al. (2020) and Havaei et al. (2021) suggested that nurses who perceive a high risk of COVID‐19 infection believe that support for protective equipment in the workplace is insufficient. Galehdar et al. (2020) reported that nurses with heavy workloads worry about becoming infected because they are too tired, leading to mistakes in epidemic prevention procedures at work and subsequent infections. Hoogendoorn et al. (2021) and Nagel et al. (2022) reported that nurses who worked in unsafe working conditions were worried about being infected with COVID‐19 and potentially transmitting it to their families, which made them consider leaving their jobs. Therefore, nurses who perceive a high risk of COVID‐19 infection may have worse working conditions, leading to low satisfaction with the work environment. This could also explain the results of experiencing housing problems during the outbreak. Nurses with housing problems worried about being infected with COVID‐19 and potentially transmitting it to their families; therefore, they were forced to leave their original houses to protect themselves. Consequently, nurses with housing problems during the COVID‐19 outbreak experienced low job satisfaction in their work environment.

In terms of work shifts, nurses in three‐shift rotations had lower satisfaction with manager support, unit support and workload than those working fixed shifts. These results were consistent with those of previous studies (Min et al. 2021; Moghadam et al. 2021). Moghadam et al. (2021) reported that the workload varies during different working hours, whereas Min et al. (2021) reported that nurses in shift systems experience a heavier burden than those in fixed shifts.

Finally, we found that nurses' family members who were discriminated against due to their occupation had low satisfaction with peer and unit support. Some studies align with our results (Hong et al. 2021; Labrague and Santos 2021). These studies reported that because frontline medical staff have a higher risk of being infected with COVID‐19, the general public may worry that contacting them may lead to COVID‐19 transmission, resulting in discrimination against them. Additionally, their family members are at a high risk of infection and may face discrimination. This situation has been found to contribute to higher intention among nurses to leave their jobs (Hong et al. 2021; Labrague and Santos 2021). Dixon et al. (2022) reported that discrimination can erode the support structures provided by family members or friends and impact their work performance. Therefore, these nurses require more support from their workplace. This could explain our results, as discriminated against nurses may need more support from their workplaces or peers, which could lead to lower satisfaction with peer support.

Implications for Nursing

Our study investigated the impact of the COVID‐19 outbreak entering the mitigation period and hospitals halting their response mechanisms on nurses' satisfaction with their work environment. It also identified variables that could affect nurses' satisfaction with their work environments. In our results, although the number of new COVID‐19 patients decreased during the mitigation period, changes in hospital response mechanisms still had a negative impact on nurses' satisfaction with their work environment. In addition, hospitals have excessively focused their attention and resources on COVID‐19 dedicated wards, making the work environment for nurses in non‐COVID‐19 dedicated wards less satisfying. These negative impacts stem from the hospital's issues with supervisory support and resource allocation after resuming treatment of general patients, and may lead some nurses who are dissatisfied with the hospital's handling of the COVID‐19 outbreak to leave.

Therefore, when facing similar emerging infections in the future, hospitals should first plan how to effectively manage the situation during the outbreak and mitigation periods, ensuring that resources are appropriately allocated to nurses, and addressing resource differences between units. Second, appropriate training should be provided to supervisors and nurses so that when the response mechanisms are switched, cooperation between supervisors and nurses can be smoother and resilience in facing such situations can be improved. Finally, communication and support channels should be provided for nurses during the pandemic so that nurses in need can receive corresponding assistance.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the impact of the COVID‐19 outbreak and the hospitals' response mechanisms may have been underestimated for two reasons. The pre‐survey was conducted during a relatively stable period compared with the early stages of the outbreak, and participants who withdrew from the post‐survey were excluded. This exclusion could potentially lead to an overestimation of satisfaction with the work environment in the pre‐survey and an underestimation of the impact of hospitals halting their response mechanisms. Second, the sex ratio among the participants was extremely uneven, with male participants accounting for only 4% of the total. This disparity may lead to inaccurate inferences regarding sex‐related outcomes. Third, the work environment was measured using a self‐report scale, which may not reflect actual changes in the work environment. Finally, all participants were from the same hospital; therefore, the results may not be applicable to nurses at different hospitals.

Conclusion

We investigated the work environment of nurses during the COVID‐19 outbreak and subsequent COVID‐19 pandemic mitigation period. During the outbreak period, hospitals initiated their COVID‐19 response mechanisms; however, during the mitigation period, these mechanisms ceased and returned to normal settings. After transitioning from the COVID‐19 outbreak to the mitigation period, both managerial and unit support decreased. This implies that the transition from COVID‐19 response mechanisms to normal settings can also build an adversity‐inclined work environment. Therefore, the hospital's process of converting COVID‐19 response mechanisms into a normal setting may require adjustments. The hospital's normal setting during the COVID‐19 pandemic should include a review of resource allocation for each unit, and the hospital should not only provide more support to nurses to improve their work environment but also reduce nurse resignations.
