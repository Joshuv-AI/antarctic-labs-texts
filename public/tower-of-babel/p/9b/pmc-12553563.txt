Plasma IL-6, IL-8 and IL-10 as predictors of response to PD-1 blockade immunochemotherapy in advanced gastric cancer
Authors: Fumeng Yang, Juncun Yao, Xinyue Tong, Xuan Luo, Qian Liu, Wei Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough immunotherapy has demonstrated clinical efficacy in advanced gastric cancer (AGC), its therapeutic benefits are limited to a subset of patients. This study aimed to evaluate circulating cytokines as potential prognostic biomarkers to enhance the precision of clinical decision-making in PD-1 inhibitor-based immunotherapy for AGC. A total of 269 patients with advanced gastric cancer were enrolled according to predefined inclusion criteria. Among them, 117 patients received anti-PD-1 immunotherapy combined with chemotherapy (immunochemotherapy), while 152 received chemotherapy alone. Plasma levels of 12 cytokines were quantified using flow cytometry, and appropriate statistical analyses were performed. Compared with healthy controls, plasma concentrations of IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 were significantly elevated in AGC patients. Furthermore, levels of these cytokines were markedly higher in the immunochemotherapy no response group compared to both the chemotherapy-alone group and response group. Multivariate analysis confirmed that IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 were independent risk factors for poor treatment outcomes in patients receiving immunochemotherapy. Notably, the combined measurement of these three cytokines provided superior diagnostic accuracy compared to individual markers. The present study preliminarily demonstrates that IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 cytokines may serve as potential predictive biomarkers for treatment outcomes in patients with AGC receiving anti-PD-1 immunochemotherapy. These easily accessible blood biomarkers are predictive and rapid and may play a key role in identifying individuals who may benefit from PD-1 blockade immunotherapy.Keywords: Cytokines, Gastric cancer, Immune checkpoint inhibitors, PD-1, Treatment response

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is the fifth most frequently diagnosed malignancy worldwide and the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality [1]. Due to the asymptomatic nature of early disease, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage, where surgical resection alone is often insufficient for long-term control. Current treatment strategies are largely guided by TNM staging, with gastrectomy recommended for resectable tumors and adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy considered based on pathological findings [2].

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) targeting the programmed death-1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) pathway have transformed the therapeutic landscape in various solid tumors, including gastric and gastroesophageal cancers [3, 4]. Agents such as nivolumab, pembrolizumab, and sintilimab have received regulatory approval for use in advanced disease settings [5]. However, clinical responses to PD-1 inhibitors vary widely among patients, emphasizing the urgent need for reliable predictive biomarkers. Although PD-L1 expression is currently the most commonly used indicator of ICI responsiveness, it has well-recognized limitations. In advanced gastric cancer, even among patients with PD-L1-positive tumors, response rates to PD-1 blockade remain suboptimal at approximately 40 to 60 percent [6–8]. Existing monitoring approaches, including serum tumor markers, imaging studies, and tissue-based immune profiling, are inadequate for accurately predicting treatment outcomes [9–11].

Peripheral blood biomarkers are particularly attractive for clinical application due to their minimally invasive nature and suitability for dynamic monitoring [12]. Among these, cytokines have emerged as promising candidates because of their central roles in regulating antitumor immunity and influencing the tumor microenvironment in gastric cancer [13–15]. These signaling molecules modulate key biological processes such as immune cell recruitment, angiogenesis, and metastatic progression through receptor-mediated pathways [16, 17]. Despite their mechanistic relevance, the prognostic value of cytokine levels during PD-1 inhibitor therapy in advanced gastric cancer (AGC) has not been clearly established.

This study aimed to quantitatively assess plasma cytokine profiles using flow cytometry in patients with AGC undergoing PD-1-based immunotherapy. Specifically, we investigated whether cytokine expression patterns could differentiate responders from non-responders, explored the relationship between cytokine levels and clinical outcomes, and identified potential blood-based biomarkers predictive of treatment efficacy in this patient population.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 269 hospitalized patients diagnosed with AGC were recruited from the Second People’s Hospital of Lianyungang and the Affiliated Hospital of Jiangsu University between January 2024 and June 2025. This study followed standardized institutional protocols, with patients assigned to either the experimental group (n = 117) receiving immunochemotherapy (chemotherapy combined with anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody) or the control group (n = 152) receiving chemotherapy alone. The experimental group was further stratified into a response subgroup (n = 77) and a non-response subgroup (n = 40) based on clinical evaluation of treatment efficacy. In parallel, 240 healthy volunteers were recruited during the same period to determine the baseline levels of cytokines.

Participants were enrolled according to strict inclusion criteria, which required the following: (1) a confirmed diagnosis of stage III or IV AGC according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) guidelines; (2) completion of at least one full immunochemotherapy regimen with an expected survival time greater than three months; (3) an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status score between 0 and 2 [18]; (4) laboratory test results within normal limits for hematologic, hepatic, and renal function, and a normal electrocardiogram without signs of organ dysfunction; and (5) ethical approval obtained from the Ethics Committee of the Second People’s Hospital of Lianyungang (Approval No. 2024K013), with written informed consent acquired from all participants prior to study enrollment.

Chemotherapy and immunochemotherapy regimens

Patients were treated with 21-day cycles of immunochemotherapy, consisting of an intravenous infusion of anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibodies (200 mg sintilimab, camrelizumab, or nivolumab) administered on day 1, in combination with one of the following chemotherapy regimens:

Regimen 1: Oxaliplatin at 130 mg/m2 administered intravenously on day 1, combined with capecitabine at 1,000 mg/m2 orally twice daily from days 1 to 14.

Regimen 1: Oxaliplatin at 130 mg/m2 administered intravenously on day 1, combined with capecitabine at 1,000 mg/m2 orally twice daily from days 1 to 14.

Regimen 2: Oxaliplatin at 130 mg/m2 administered intravenously on day 1, combined with tegafur at 40 mg/m2 orally twice daily from days 1 to 14.

Regimen 2: Oxaliplatin at 130 mg/m2 administered intravenously on day 1, combined with tegafur at 40 mg/m2 orally twice daily from days 1 to 14.

Regimen 3: Albumin-bound paclitaxel at 120 mg/m2 administered intravenously on days 1 and 8, combined with tegafur at 40 mg/m2 orally twice daily from days 1 to 14.

Regimen 3: Albumin-bound paclitaxel at 120 mg/m2 administered intravenously on days 1 and 8, combined with tegafur at 40 mg/m2 orally twice daily from days 1 to 14.

Each treatment regimen was repeated every 21 days, unless interrupted due to adverse events or disease progression.

Instruments and reagents

Plasma levels of the following twelve cytokines were measured in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions: interleukin-2 (IL-2), interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-5 (IL-5), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-17A (IL-17A), interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-12p70 (IL-12p70), interferon-γ (IFN-γ), interferon-α2 (IFN-α2), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α).

All measurements were performed using the NovoCyte D2060R flow cytometer (Agilent Technologies China Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) with the matching cytokines detection kit (Lot number: 250205; Qingdao Raisecare Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Qingdao, China).

Sample collection

Fasting venous blood samples (2 mL) were collected from all participants using EDTA-K2 anticoagulant tubes. Within 4 h after collection, plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1000 g for 10 min, immediately aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C. To preserve sample integrity, frozen plasma aliquots were protected from repeated freeze–thaw cycles until batch analysis.

Rationale for cytokine assays

Cytokine quantification was performed using a direct sandwich immunoassay. In this method, capture antibodies coupled to fluorescent microspheres and biotinylated detection antibodies simultaneously bind to the target cytokines present in either samples or calibrators. The resulting immune complexes were subsequently incubated with phycoerythrin-labeled streptavidin. Cytokine concentrations were then determined by measuring the fluorescence intensity of individual microspheres using flow cytometry. Prior to conducting cytokine detection, methodological validation was performed, which included evaluation of precision, accuracy, analytical measurement range, clinical reportable range, and reference intervals. Internal quality control were implemented and met all required standards.

Procedures for detection of plasma cytokines

Plasma cytokine measurements were performed strictly according to the kit instructions and consisted of the following sequential steps:

Sample preparation

Venous blood collected in EDTA-K2 tubes was centrifuged at 1000 g for 10 min. The supernatant plasma was aliquoted into Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until batch analysis.

Calibrator preparation

CAL 1 standard was reconstituted in 300 µL de-ionised water. Cytokine diluent was equilibrated at room temperature for 10 min, after which serial dilutions of CAL 1 were prepared as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Dilution flow chart of calibrators

Reaction assembly

Each flow-cytometry tube received 25 µL reaction buffer II, the detection-antibody cocktail, the vortex-homogenised microsphere suspension and 25 µL of either test sample or calibrator. The contents were mixed thoroughly.

Primary incubation

Tubes were incubated in the dark at 18–25 °C for 2 h on an orbital shaker set to 500 rpm.

Initial wash

Seventy microlitres of wash buffer was added, followed by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min; the supernatant was then discarded.

Reporter conjugation

Seventy microlitres of phycoerythrin-labelled streptavidin solution was added to each pellet, and the mixture was vortexed for 10–15 s.

Secondary incubation

Tubes were incubated again in the dark at 18–25 °C for 30 min while shaking at 500 rpm.

Final wash

Five hundred microlitres of wash buffer was added, followed by centrifugation at 500 g for 5 min and removal of the supernatant.

Resuspension

The microsphere pellet was resuspended in 150–200 µL wash buffer.

Flow-cytometric analysis

After vortexing for 20 s, samples were analysed immediately on the flow cytometer.

Data processing

Instrument settings were optimised according to operating guidelines, and cytokine concentrations were calculated from the corresponding standard curves.

Treatment response assessment

Treatment efficacy was assessed in patients with measurable lesions according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 [19]. A complete response (CR) was defined as the disappearance of all target lesions. A partial response (PR) was defined as a reduction of more than 30% in the sum of the diameters of target lesions. Progressive disease (PD) was identified when there was an increase of more than 20% in the total tumor burden. Stable disease (SD) referred to cases that did not meet the criteria for either PR or PD.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS software, version 19.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was employed to evaluate the distribution patterns of continuous variables. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using appropriate nonparametric methods. For comparisons between two groups, the Mann–Whitney U test was applied, while differences among three or more groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. When significant differences were detected in multiple-group analyses, Dunn’s post hoc test was utilized for subsequent pairwise comparisons. Binary logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors associated with varied responses to immunotherapy in patients with advanced gastric cancer. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive performance of cytokines regarding treatment outcomes in AGC patients undergoing immunotherapy. All tests were two-tailed, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of AGC patients

A total of 269 patients with AGC who met the inclusion criteria were enrolled at the Second People’s Hospital of Lianyungang between January 2024 and June 2025. Of these, 117 patients received immunochemotherapy, and 152 received chemotherapy alone as the control group without PD-1 inhibitor treatment. According to the RECIST version 1.1, the disease control rate (DCR) in the immunochemotherapy group was 65.8 percent. Based on treatment response, this group was further divided into the response group (n = 77) and the no response group (n = 40). In contrast, the chemotherapy-only group showed a DCR of 49 percent (Table 1).

Comparison of plasma cytokines between AGC patients and healthy individuals

Normality assessment using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test revealed non-normal distributions in plasma levels of all twelve cytokines. Compared with healthy controls, AGC patients showed significantly elevated plasma levels of IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 (all P < 0.05; Fig. 1). No statistically significant differences were observed in the remaining cytokines: IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-1β, IL-17A, IL-12p70, IFN-γ, IFN-α2, and TNF-α (all P > 0.05; Fig. 2).

Plasma cytokine profiles in AGC: chemotherapy controls vs. immunochemotherapy (response and no response)

To assess the effect of PD-1 blockade immunotherapy on cytokine profiles, plasma cytokine levels were measured in three patient groups: control group (n = 152), response group (n = 77), and no response group (n = 40). As shown in Table 2 and Fig. 3, plasma levels of IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 were significantly higher in the no response group compared to both the control and response groups (all P < 0.05), while no significant differences were found between the control and response groups. Additionally, plasma levels of IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-1β, IL-17A, IL-12p70, IFN-γ, IFN-α2, and TNF-α showed no significant differences among the groups (all P > 0.05).

Risk factors for predicting response to immunotherapy in AGC patients

To ensure the reliability of the binary logistic regression model, multicollinearity diagnostics were conducted using SPSS 19.0. Twelve cytokines (IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-1β, IL-17A, IL-10, IL-12p70, IFN-γ, IFN-α2, and TNF-α) were included as independent variables, with immunotherapy response (classified as response or no response) as the dependent variable. Regression analysis identified plasma IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 as independent predictors of treatment response, with elevated levels significantly associated with outcomes. IL-6 showed an odds ratio (OR) of 1.135 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.042 to 1.237, P = 0.004), IL-8 showed OR = 1.047 (95% CI: 1.002 to 1.094, P = 0.042), and IL-10 showed OR = 1.651 (95% CI: 1.225 to 2.224, P = 0.001). These findings indicate that increased IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 are significant independent risk factors for predicting immunotherapy response in AGC (Table 3).

Combined predictive value of IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 for immunochemotherapy response in AGC

ROC analysis provides a robust methodological framework for evaluating the diagnostic utility of screening biomarkers, with superior performance indicated by larger area under the curve (AUC) values. In this study, ROC curve analysis was used to assess the predictive value of IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10, both individually and in combination, for immunochemotherapy response in AGC. The biomarkers exhibited varying levels of diagnostic performance, with IL-8 demonstrating the highest discriminative power (AUC = 0.855, 95% CI 0.789–0.920), followed by IL-6 (AUC = 0.832, 95% CI 0.758–0.907) and IL-10 (AUC = 0.799, 95% CI 0.712–0.885). Notably, the combined analysis of all three cytokines resulted in a significantly improved predictive accuracy (AUC = 0.915, 95% CI 0.866–0.965), highlighting their synergistic value in predicting clinical response (Fig. 4).

Discussion

Immune checkpoint blockade has become a pillar of oncology care [20]. Prompt evaluation of therapeutic effect is essential, as timely recognition of responders and nonresponders spares patients unnecessary exposure, toxicity, and financial strain [21]. To meet this need, immune response criteria, irRC, were introduced as endpoints [22]. Yet irRC depends on imaging studies, a method with inherent shortcomings, especially during early assessment and in distinguishing real responses from pseudoprogression [23]. Conventional tools, including radiologic scans and tumour markers, have limited value for rapid surveillance because of delayed tumour shrinkage and atypical progression patterns. These kinetics complicate the early separation of benefit from failure [24]. Recent evidence suggests that traditional criteria can undervalue the activity of checkpoint inhibitors owing to response dynamics and atypical patterns [25]. Although tumour markers offer sensitivity for treatment monitoring, they suffer from restricted specificity and positive predictive value. No accepted test anticipates early benefit from immunotherapy. Consequently, novel predictive biomarkers capable of providing rapid evaluation of anti PD 1 or PD L1 therapy are required. After PD 1 or PD L1 blockade, relief of immune inhibition prompts cytokine release among responders [26]. Cytokine changes mirror antitumor immune activation and, when linked to clinical observations, may yield predictive signals.

Guided by this principle, our study applied radiographic imaging, pathological evaluation, and tumor biomarkers to assess the efficacy of PD-1 blockade. Initial plasma cytokine analysis showed that IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 levels were significantly higher in AGC patients compared to healthy individuals. Patients were subsequently grouped by treatment approach into chemotherapy alone and immunochemotherapy cohorts, with the latter further divided into responders and non-responders based on clinical outcomes. Notably, non-responders displayed significantly elevated plasma IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 levels compared to both chemotherapy controls and responders, while no significant differences were found between the control and responder groups. Supporting evidence from Taube et al. [26] indicated that anti-PD-1 therapy promotes cytokine release and reduces immunosuppression, validating cytokine monitoring as a surrogate marker of immune activation. Similarly, Liu et al. [27] observed that PD-1 antibody treatment stimulates tumor-infiltrating immune cells, leading to increased cytokine expression. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential utility of cytokine levels as indicators of treatment response in AGC patients undergoing immunochemotherapy. Nevertheless, it remains unclear whether these cytokines act as specific biomarkers of PD-1 blockade efficacy or simply reflect nonspecific immune alterations within the tumor microenvironment. Further mechanistic studies are needed to clarify this distinction, particularly those examining cytokine expression within tumor tissues and its correlation with patterns of immune cell infiltration. Such investigations may help delineate whether cytokines function primarily as predictive markers of treatment response or as general prognostic indicators.

Based on these findings, we analyzed the independent risk factors affecting the response of AGC to immunochemotherapy. The results indicated that IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 were independent predictors of treatment outcome in patients receiving immunochemotherapy. Among responders, tumor burden gradually decreased and immune activation diminished, leading to reduced expression levels of IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 in the blood. In contrast, in non-responders, tumor progression persisted and the immune system remained activated, resulting in increased cytokine levels. Therefore, IL-6, IL-8, or IL-10 may serve as independent indicators for assessing the efficacy of PD-1 blockade. Our results are largely consistent with earlier studies [28–31], which have indicated that increased levels of IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 are linked to poorer prognosis and diminished therapeutic efficacy of ICIs. These cytokines may therefore serve as independent biomarkers for predicting clinical outcomes in patients treated with ICIs.

In addition, ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the prognostic diagnostic value of IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 in AGC patients receiving immunochemotherapy. The results showed that IL-8 had superior diagnostic performance compared to IL-6 and IL-10, and the combination of all three further improved predictive accuracy. Liu et al. [32] reported that IL-6 and IL-8 were partially independent and complementary in predicting immunotherapy efficacy in AGC. Compared with their findings, the predictive value of the three cytokines in our study was markedly higher than that of conventional tumor markers including CEA, CA50, CA199, and CA724. The combined assessment of IL-6 and IL-8 showed strong predictive ability for the clinical efficacy of PD-1 monoclonal antibody treatment in AGC. A retrospective study by Ma et al. [33] found that elevated serum IL-6 levels were associated with immune-related adverse events during ICI therapy, and that high IL-6 levels predicted poor outcomes in patients with gastric adenocarcinoma and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma. Lospinoso et al. [34] identified soluble cytokine receptors as contributors to tumor development and chemotherapy resistance in gastric cancer and proposed their use as molecular markers and therapeutic targets. He et al. [35] demonstrated that dysregulated cytokine signaling fosters tumor progression and treatment resistance, and suggested that targeting IL-34 and IL-35 pathways may improve outcomes in GC. Marimuthu et al. [36] examined cytokine interactions with the tumor microenvironment and found that immunosuppressive cytokines such as IL-10 and TGF-β facilitate immune evasion, while pathway inhibition and cytokine-based strategies could help overcome resistance. These findings, together with our results, demonstrate the involvement of cytokines in GC development and progression, particularly in immunotherapy response evaluation, and support their potential as clinically relevant predictive markers.

In a study analyzing serum cytokine levels in 60 NSCLC patients, Liu et al. [37] demonstrated that multiple cytokines (IL-6, IL-5, IL-8, TNF-α, IL-10, IL-4) were significantly associated with prognosis following chemoimmunotherapy. Specifically, IL-6 was identified as an independent risk factor for both PFS and OS, while elevated IL-4 levels potentially indicated improved survival outcomes. Furthermore, research conducted by Laino et al. [31] revealed that high baseline levels of IL-6, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), and C-reactive protein (CRP) were significantly correlated with shorter survival in melanoma patients. This association was consistently observed in both ICI and chemotherapy treatment groups. IL-6 was confirmed as an independent prognostic factor for survival and showed strong correlations with CRP and NLR, suggesting a coordinated systemic inflammatory response. Collectively, these findings, along with the current study, highlight conserved biological patterns across cancer types and support the potential universality of cytokine monitoring in oncology. Furthermore, the study conducted by Silvestris et al. [38] provided the first immunobiological profile of rare pancreatic cancer subtypes and revealed morphological evidence suggesting potential crosstalk between the Notch pathway and the PD-L1/PD-1 axis. This work establishes a theoretical foundation for combining immune checkpoint inhibitors with Notch pathway targeted therapies. Consequently, these findings significantly inform exploratory research on pancreatic sarcomatoid carcinoma, a tumor type that urgently requires biomarkers for evaluating PD-1 or PD-L1 based therapeutic strategies.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this was a cross-sectional investigation in which cytokine levels were measured only once in patients with AGC, without longitudinal monitoring throughout the treatment course. Serial assessments of cytokine dynamics would strengthen the scientific validity and interpretability of the findings. Second, based on the study by Liu et al. [32], which found no significant association between IL-6 or IL-8 and conventional tumor markers such as CEA, CA50, CA199, and CA724, and supported by our own findings indicating that IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 may serve as independent predictors of treatment response, this study focused exclusively on cytokine profiles without incorporating other biomarkers. Future research should consider integrating additional biomarkers to enhance predictive performance. Third, although the current cohort provided valuable insights, the relatively small sample size in subgroup analyses limits the generalizability of the results. Validation in larger and independent cohorts is therefore required. Fourth, variability in treatment regimens may have influenced cytokine levels, emphasizing the need for standardized therapeutic protocols in future studies. Furthermore, complete follow-up data such as progression-free survival, duration of response, and overall survival were not available for some patients due to reasons including treatment discontinuation, transfer to other institutions, or referral. This limited the evaluation of long-term responses to immunotherapy. Despite these limitations, the flow cytometry-based findings from this study offer preliminary evidence supporting the potential utility of cytokine monitoring in patients with AGC receiving immunochemotherapy.

Conclusion

In summary, this study highlights the potential of cytokines as predictive biomarkers for immunochemotherapy efficacy in patients with AGC. Elevated plasma IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 levels were significantly associated with treatment response. Early cytokine monitoring may support timely identification of responders and guide personalized therapy. Further studies are needed to validate these findings and clarify their role in clinical decision-making.
