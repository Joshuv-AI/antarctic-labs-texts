Comparative mitogenomic study of the brown accentor (Prunella fulvescens) and a sympatric relative across an altitudinal gradient
Authors: Wenshu Zhang, Shaobin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Brown Accentor (Prunella fulvescens) is an understudied bird on the Tibetan Plateau, for which life-history and molecular genetic data remain scarce. In this study, we sequenced the complete mitochondrial genome of P. fulvescens and conducted a comparative analysis with two conspecific individuals (one from the similar altitude and one from a lower altitude); we further performed interspecific comparison with its sympatric species Prunella strophiata (a congener) and Carpodacus rubicilloides (a sympatric non-congener). Divergence between high and lower altitude populations of P. fulvescens concentrated in genes associated with oxygen utilization and energy metabolism. As altitude decreased, the number of mutation sites increased (except for Cyt b). ATP8 has the lowest nucleotide and amino acid sequence similarity across both groups. Ka/Ks analysis indicated that ATP6 and COXIII evolve at a faster rate. Sympatric interspecies comparisons revealed that different genes exhibit varying intensities of selective pressure, reflecting their respective functions. Divergence time estimation showed that divergences among the three P. fulvescens populations occurred during the Pleistocene. Collectively, these findings suggest incipient genetic differentiation among P. fulvescens populations at different altitudes, driven by geographic isolation and environmental adaptation. This study provides novel insights into the genetic basis of high-altitude avian adaptive evolution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58073-1.Keywords: Mitochondrial genome, Tibet Plateau, Comparative analysis, Altitudinal variations, Intraspecific and sympatric interspecific variation.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Evolution, Genetics, Zoology

Introduction

Creatures living in high-altitude environments endure selective pressures such as hypoxia, low temperatures, and strong ultraviolet radiation1. To adapt to these extreme environmental conditions, high-altitude species have evolved various adaptive strategies in morphology and physiology, such as increased body weight and size, enhanced metabolic capacity, and genetic specialization2,3. The adaptive evolution of animals in high-altitude environments has long been a focal point of research in evolutionary biology and genetics4. Driven by natural selection, adaptive regulation of energy metabolism and mitochondrial function serves as an adaptive strategy for birds to cope with extreme high-altitude environments5. Mitochondria are the primary sites for energy production in living organisms. Birds inhabiting high-altitude environments consume more energy during flight. Consequently, mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation undergoes specific adaptations in energy metabolism and oxygen utilization to adapt to their living environment6. The mitochondrial genome of birds is a circular double-stranded DNA molecule, which consists of 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 2 ribosomal RNA (rRNA) genes, 22 transfer RNA (tRNA) genes, and a control region (CR)7. PCGs are molecular markers for studying species evolution, and these regions encode respiratory chain complexes that play a crucial role in the electron transport chain8. Gene sequence variation regulates the structure and function of respiratory chain complexes. It improves oxidative phosphorylation efficiency under hypoxia and optimizes energy supply and oxygen utilization. Furthermore, it drives the molecular adaptive evolution of mitochondrial function at high altitude9,10. Mutations in these genes may alter mitochondrial functions, such as respiration, thereby creating critical opportunities for organisms to adapt to high-altitude environments6,11.

The Brown Accentor (Prunella fulvescens) belongs to the genus Prunella, family Prunellidae, order Passeriformes, class Aves. It is a small bird, measuring 13–16 cm in length. It has a brown head and is widely distributed across Eurasian high-mountain regions, including the Himalayas, the Tibet Plateau, and Mongolia12. Its primary habitats consist of alpine grasslands, wilderness, farmlands, and pastures at elevations above 2,000 m, during the breeding season, they ascend to alpine zones above 4,300 m12,13. In China, P. fulvescens is recognized as a common resident bird. Its diet primarily consists of arthropods such as ants, along with other small invertebrates, as well as fruits, seeds, and other plant materials12. This species is permanently distributed in plateau regions, with its fecundity decreasing as altitude increases14, and its population size exhibits significant seasonal fluctuations13.

In this study, we sequenced and compared the complete mitochondrial genomes of P. fulvescens from populations at different altitudes, and further conducted interspecific comparisons with its congeneric species P. strophiata and a sympatric, non-congeneric species C. rubicilloides. By analyzing sequence variations in PCGs both within and between species of P. fulvescens, the study investigated the genetic basis and potential mechanisms underlying differentiation in high-altitude adaptation. It provides molecular level evidence for understanding the plateau adaptation strategies of Prunella species. Additionally, it provides new insights into the genomic structure and phylogenetic relationships of this species.

Materials and methods

Sample collection and DNA extraction

We collected a deceased individual of P. fulvescens (PV890820) of unknown cause in Tianjun County (37°20’ N, 98°49’ E, 3,510 m), Haixi Mongolian and Tibetan Autonomous Prefecture, Qinghai Province, China. Muscle tissue sample was dissected, placed in an EP centrifuge tube, and preserved in 95% ethanol for 48 h. It was then stored at -20 °C for subsequent use. Genomic DNA was extracted from the muscle tissue using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China). The samples of P. fulvescens (PV890820, NC_035747) used in this study were collected by our research team in Tianjun County. Their mitochondrial sequences were jointly analyzed with the P. fulvescens (MN356320) and C. rubicilloides (MH363715) in the NCBI database (Table 1).

Mitochondrial genome sequencing, assembly, annotation, and visualization

DNA sequencing library was prepared from the DNA sample using the Nextera XT DNA Library Preparation Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA). Subsequently, paired-end sequencing was performed on the Illumina NovaSeq 6,000 platform. And the average sequencing depth was 530.74x (Fig. S1). Raw sequencing data were subjected to quality filtering using fastp software (https://github.com/OpenGene/fastp) with the following criteria: the criteria are to remove reads with an N base content exceeding 5%, remove reads with a low-quality (quality value ≤ 5) base number reaching 50%, and remove reads with adapter contamination, resulting in effective data (clean reads) for subsequent analysis. The high-quality sequencing reads were assembled using SPAdes version 3.14.115. The complete mitochondrial genome sequence was annotated using the MITOS web server (http://mitos.bioinf.uni-leipzig.de/index.py), with the NCBI RefSeq mitochondrial genome database and the vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code applied. Subsequent manual alignment and correction were performed to refine gene sequences, locations and lengths. The mitochondrial genome map of P. fulvescens was visualized using the online tool CGView (https://www-simgene-com.atrust.yangtzeu.edu.cn/CGView)16.

Sequence analysis

We analyzed the mitochondrial genome data of the P. fulvescens from Tianjun using the online tool tRNAscan-SE (https://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/)17 for predicting and analyzing the secondary structures of tRNAs. The structures were then refined and visualized using VARNA version 3.9.318. MEGA 11 software19 was used to analyze base composition and codon usage bias. Base composition bias was assessed with the following formulas: AT-skew = (A − T) /(A + T) and GC-skew = (G − C)/(G + C)20. Sequences were aligned using DNAMAN software to verify identity and visualize alignments. The ratio of non-synonymous to synonymous substitution rates (Ka/Ks) for PCGs was calculated with DnaSP 6 software21. BioEdit software22 was used for nucleotide composition analysis and codon usage bias assessment. If the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) value is greater than or less than 1, it indicates a preference for certain codons of the corresponding amino acid or a lower usage frequency; if the RSCU value equals 1, there is no codon usage preference.

Grouping and comparative design

To investigate the differences among P. fulvescens individuals living at different altitudes, we designed two types of comparisons based on altitude variation, (1)H-H Altitude Group, two individuals from the same high-altitude region (Tianjun), aiming to analyze the genetic background under identical altitude conditions. (2)H-L Altitude Group, the sample in this study were compared with P. fulvescens from the Hangai Mountains of Mongolia, aiming to assess potential genetic differentiation caused by altitude differences. Using pairwise sequence alignment, we systematically analyzed the PCGs and non-coding regions of P. fulvescens. The resulting data were further examined to investigate the genetic basis of biological adaptation under different elevation environments.

Phylogenetic analysis and divergence time estimation

To investigate the phylogenetic position of P. fulvescens within the family Prunellidae, this study selected sequences including the mitochondrial genome of the P. fulvescens obtained from our research, along with sequences of 22 other species retrieved from NCBI (Table S2). Using PhyloSuite software23, datasets were extracted from the mitochondrial genomes to obtain nucleotide sequences of the PCGs for the species included in the study. Phylogenetic analysis was based on the concatenated sequences of the 13 PCGs from complete mitochondrial genomes. PCGs are suitable for estimating divergence times because they encode proteins whose sequences are functionally constrained and highly conserved. Alignment and phylogenetic tree construction were performed using MEGA 11 software, which also determined the GTR + G+I model as the best-fit nucleotide substitution model for the sequence data.

Based on existing evidence, fossil calibration points were assigned a normal distribution priors: the divergence time between Gallus and Anas was estimated at approximately 51-99.6 Mya24, the divergence between Phasianidae and Numididae was estimated to be around 50–54 Mya25, the divergence between oscines and suboscines (Acanthisitta chloris as a representative) occurred approximately 27.52-56 Mya26, and the divergence time between Prunellidae and other passerines was estimated to 14.99–17.91 Mya27; Fossil calibration points were set with a normal distribution prior. Divergence times were estimated using BEAST v1.10.528 for MCMC analysis, employing a Yule tree prior and an uncorrelated relaxed clock model. The MCMC parameters included a chain length of 5 × 10⁷ generations, with sampling every 1000 generations. Results were analyzed using Tracer v1.729, with the first 10.00% of samples discarded as burn-in, and the analysis was repeated once. The effective sample size (ESS) of all parameter estimates exceeded 200, indicating reliable results. TreeAnnotator v1.10.5 was employed to construct the maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree, and FigTree v1.4.330 was utilized to visualize the phylogenetic tree with divergence times and 95% highest posterior density (HPD) intervals. All sequences used in this study were downloaded from the NCBI database (Table S2).

Results

Mitochondrial genome structure and composition

The mitochondrial genome of P. fulvescens is a typical circular double-stranded DNA molecule with a total length of 16,838 bp (Fig. 1) It contains 13 PCGs, 22 tRNA genes, 2 rRNA genes (12 S rRNA and 16 S rRNA), and one control region (1,247 bp) located between tRNA-Glu and tRNA-Phe. The ND6 and 8 tRNAs are transcribed on the light strand (L-strand), while the remaining 29 genes are located on the heavy strand (H-strand) (Table S1). The mitochondrial genes are tightly arranged, with a total of 6 overlapping regions ranging from 1 to 10 bp in length. The longest overlap (10 bp) occurs between the ATP6 and ATP8, while the shortest overlap (1 bp) is found in three locations. The mitochondrial genome contains 18 gene spacer regions with a total length of 111 bp, ranging from 1 to 22 bp. The longest spacer (22 bp) is located between the ND1 and tRNA-Ile (Table S1).

The CG view illustrates the mitochondrial genome organization and gene arrangement of P. fulvescens with protein-coding genes (PCGs), transfer RNAs (tRNAs), ribosomal RNAs (rRNAs), and the control region represented in distinct colors.

The mitochondrial genome of P. fulvescens has an A + T content of 54.05% (A: 30.18%, C: 31.08%, G: 14.88%, T: 23.87%), which is significantly higher than its G + C content. The highest A + T content (58.11%) is observed in the tRNAs (Table 2). The AT-skew and GC-skew of the complete mitochondrial genome of P. fulvescens are 0.12 and − 0.35, respectively (Table S3). AT-skew of ND4L (-0.01) and ND6 (-0.52) are negative, while all other genes are positive, indicating a higher proportion of A than T. For the GC-skew values, all are negative except for ND6 (0.58).

PCGs and codon usage

The total length of the 13 PCGs is 11,395 bp, ATP8 is the shortest gene (168 bp), while ND5 is the longest (1,818 bp). The start codon for most PCGs in P. fulvescens is ATG, ND2 and COXI use GTG as their initiation codon, and ND3 uses ATA. The most common stop codon across the mitochondrial genome is TAA. Incomplete stop codons are observed in ND2 (TA-), ND4 (T–), and COXIII (T–) (Table S1). The A + T and G + C content of the PCGs are 53.40% and 46.60%, respectively. Among them, the ATP8 gene has the highest A + T content (56.55%), while ND6 has the lowest (50.10%). The AT-skew for the PCGs is 0.06, and GC-skew is -0.37. 12 PCGs are encoded on the H-strand. All of them except ND4L show significantly positive AT-skew and negative GC-skew. ND6 is encoded on L-strand. It shows the opposite skewing pattern (Table S3).

Analysis of the amino acid composition of the PCGs revealed 3,798 encoded amino acids (Table S4). Excluding stop codons, 34 codons had RSCU values greater than 1, indicating codon preference. Leu exhibited a strong preference for the codon CUA, with an RSCU of 2.01, the highest among all PCGs in this mitochondrial genome. Only two codons, Methionine (AUG) and Tryptophan (UGG) showed no codon usage bias (RSCU = 1). 28 codons had RSCU values less than 1. The stop codons UAA, UAG, AGA, and AGG do not encode amino acids. This non-random pattern of codon usage may be influenced by mutational pressure, natural selection, and translation efficiency optimization, reflecting the evolutionary outcome of gene expression and functional adaptation31,32.

tRNA genes, rRNA genes, and control region

The mitochondrial genome of P. fulvescens contains 22 tRNAs, ranging in length from 66 bp (tRNA-Ser) to 75 bp (tRNA-Leu) (Table S1). Among these, 14 tRNAs are located on H-strand, while the remaining eight (tRNA-Gln, tRNA-Ala, tRNA-Asn, tRNA-Cys, tRNA-Tyr, tRNA-Ser, tRNA-Pro, and tRNA-Glu) are located on L-strand. The total length of all tRNAs is 1,545 bp, with A + T content of 58.11% and G + C content of 41.89%. The AT-skew and GC-skew for the tRNAs are 0.13 and − 0.15, respectively. Prediction of tRNA secondary structures indicates, with the exception of tRNA-Ser (GCT), all tRNAs in the P. fulvescens mitochondrial genome can fold into the typical cloverleaf secondary structure (Fig. S2).

The 12 S rRNA gene is located between tRNA-Phe and tRNA-Val, while the 16 S rRNA gene is situated between tRNA-Val and tRNA-Leu, their lengths are 974 bp and 1,595 bp, respectively (Table S1). Both genes are encoded on the H-strand and contain no spacer regions or overlap regions. The rRNAs exhibit a positive AT-skew (0.22) and a negative GC-skew (-0.12), with an A + T content of 53.72% and a G + C content of 46.29%. The control region is located between tRNA-Glu and tRNA-Phe on the H-strand, with a length of 1,247 bp. The control region exhibits high A (28.23%) and C (30.31%) content. The A + T content is 55.98% (Table 2), with a positive AT-skew (0.01) and a negative GC-skew (-0.38) (Table S3).

Phylogenetic and divergence analysis

To investigate and further clarify the molecular phylogeny, evolutionary relationships of the family Prunellidae, and the divergence history of P. fulvescens, this study constructed a molecular phylogenetic tree based on the nucleotide sequences of the 13 PCGs from Prunellidae birds, and estimated divergence times. Molecular dating results showed that the divergence between the family Prunellidae and other Passeriformes birds occurred around 13.99 Mya, corresponding to the period of recent intense uplift of the Tibet Plateau during the Miocene epoch (Fig. 2)33. The phylogenetic tree shows that the divergence between P. fulvescens and other Prunella can be traced back to approximately 3.50 Mya. The divergence time between two P. fulvescens individuals in the Tianjun H-H altitude group was 0.07 Mya, while the divergence between the Tianjun individual and the individual from the Khangai Mountains occurred earlier, around 0.36 Mya. Both divergence events took place during the Pleistocene epoch. Overall, the estimated divergence times are consistent with previous research findings27,34.

Intraspecific comparative analysis of P. fulvescens

The mitochondrial genomes of the three individuals from different altitudes are all circular DNA molecules but exhibit minor length variations. The total length of the 13 PCGs for the two Tianjun individuals is 11,395 bp, whereas that of the low-altitude sample is 1 bp longer. Except for COXIII (MN356320 uses TA- as a stop codon, compared to T-- in the two Tianjun individuals), the start and stop codons of the other coding genes are identical across all three individuals. We performed statistical analysis of the RSCU values for the PCGs of P. fulvescens. Although these three individuals originate from different altitudes and latitudes, their RSCU values for most amino acids are highly similar (Fig. S3), indicating consistent codon usage preferences in the mitochondrial genomes of P. fulvescens across varying elevations.

The PCGs and non-coding regions of the P. fulvescens living at different altitudes were analyzed using the pairwise sequence alignment method (Table S5). Between two P. fulvescens individuals from the same region and altitude, the sequence similarity of 13 PCGs exceeded 99.00%, and the amino acid similarity exceeded 98.00%. Comparing individuals from H-L altitude group, except for ATP8, ATP6, and ND4, the nucleotide similarity of the remaining genes was greater than 99.00%. Meanwhile, the amino acid similarity was above 99.00% for all genes except ATP8. In contrast, ATP8 showed the lowest sequence and amino acid similarity among all PCGs between the two groups (Table S5). Table S5 shows that the sequence similarity between H-H altitude group individual is 99.83%, while the similarity with the H-L altitude group is 99.19%.

The amino acid similarity for most genes remains consistent between the H-L altitude group and within the H-H altitude group. Genes such as ND1, ND2, COXII, ND3, and ND4L all show a similarity of 100%, indicating that these genes are completely conserved. We counted nucleotide and amino acid variant sites between two groups (Table 3). A comparison among individuals within the H-H altitude group revealed 19 nucleotide variant sites, with ND4 and Cyt b having the most. Only 4 amino acid variation sites were identified. Between the H-L altitude group showed a greater number of nucleotide variants (91 sites), with ND4 contributing the most (16 sites). In addition, 6 amino acid variation sites were identified, with 2 sites each in ATP8 and COXIII. Among these, the genes ATP8, COXIII, and ND5 exhibited non-synonymous mutations in both groups. Among the studied genes, the number of nucleotide and amino acid sequence mutation sites in most genes increased with decreasing altitude.

Although mutations occurred in genes such as ND1, ND2, COXII, these mutations are synonymous and did not lead to corresponding amino acid changes. For example, at the Mongolian sampling site, the COXII gene had a G-A nucleotide substitution at position 230 and a C-T substitution at position 419, while at the Tianjun sampling site, the COXII gene had a G-A substitution at position 509. Mutations at specific sites in the COXI, COXIII, ATP8, ND5, and Cyt b genes resulted in alterations in the corresponding amino acid sequences, indicating non-synonymous mutations (Table S6). For instance, the COXIII gene had two non-synonymous mutations, at the Mongolian sampling site, an A-G nucleotide substitution at position 81 led to a T-A amino acid change, while a G-A nucleotide substitution in the COXIII gene resulted in a V-I amino acid change at the Tianjun sampling site.

The length of the control region was identical at 1247 bp. Comparative analysis of P. fulvescens revealed that similarity was 99.84% among high-altitude individuals and 99.04% in the H-L altitude group. Furthermore, the AT-skew (0.007) and GC-skew (-0.381) in the control region of low-altitude individual were both lower than those in the two high-altitude individuals. Comparison between individuals from H-L altitude group revealed variations in 12 S rRNA and 16 S rRNA, with similarities of 99.59% and 99.81%, respectively. Additionally, differences in 16 S rRNA (98.50%) were also observed in H-H altitude group (Table 2). Alignment analysis of tRNAs showed that no sequence differences were observed except for 6 tRNAs: tRNA-Ile, tRNA-Tyr, tRNA-Thr, tRNA-Ser (UCN), tRNA-His and tRNA-Asn.

We analyzed the selective pressure on the 13 PCGs of P. fulvescens sampled across different altitudes and latitudes. We calculated the Ka/Ks ratios of all PCGs (Table 4), and the results showed that values for all PCGs were less than 1. The genes with the highest Ka/Ks values in the two groups are ATP6 (0.4118) and COXIII (0.7143), respectively.

Most genes exhibited identical AT-skew values; specifically, the AT-skew of ND1, ND6, ATP8 and Cyt b increased with decreasing altitude, whereas that of ATP6 and ND5 showed an opposite trend of decrease with declining altitude (Table S3). The A + T content of the P. fulvescens mitochondrial genome ranges between 54.02% and 54.10%, showing a significant AT bias. Among them, the high-altitude individual NC_035747 has the highest A + T content at 54.10%, while the low-altitude individual has the lowest A + T content among the three. Low-altitude individual has a higher A + T content in the ND2, COXII, COXIII, ND4L, ND5, and ND6 genes among the 13 PCGs compared to high-altitude individuals (Table 2),and the A + T content of COXII and COXIII increases with decreasing altitude. In the high-altitude individual NC_035747, the A + T content of ATP8 reaches 57.14%, which was the highest among all PCGs and significantly higher than that of the other two individuals (both 56.55%), making ATP8 the gene with relatively prominent differences in base composition among all PCGs. The A + T content of the control region also shows altitudinal variation. The control region A + T content of the low altitude individual is 56.07%, slightly higher than that of the two high-altitude individuals (55.97%-55.98%) (Table 2). As a region with a relatively rapid evolutionary rate in the mitochondrial genome, this subtle difference may imply an incipient differentiation trend among populations from different altitudes35.

Sympatric interspecies comparison between P. fulvescens and P. strophiata

A study was conducted on P. fulvescens and P. strophiata both sampled from Tianjun. Their mitochondrial genomes were all circular DNA molecules, the complete mitochondrial genome of P. fulvescens is 16,838 bp, while that of P. strophiata is 16,831 bp. The PCGs and non-coding regions of the two species living at the same altitude were analyzed using pairwise sequence alignment (Table S7). The sequence similarity of all PCGs and the CR exceeded 90.00%, ND2 had the lowest similarity at 91.35%. Amino acid alignment revealed similarities above 95.00% for all genes except ATP8 and ND3, ATP8 showed the lowest similarity (92.00%). The total length of the 13 PCGs in both species was 11,395 bp, and they shared identical start and stop codons. The RSCU values of most amino acids were highly similar (Fig. S4), indicating consistent codon usage preferences between the mitochondrial genomes of P. fulvescens and P. strophiata.

Comparative analysis showed that the two species had identical control region lengths of 1,247 bp. It was found that the AT-skew (0.020) and GC-skew (-0.370) of the control region in P. strophiata were both higher than those in P. fulvescens (Table 5). As a region with a relatively rapid evolutionary rate in the mitochondrial genome, such subtle differences align with its characteristic of relatively high genetic diversity. Comparison between the two individuals revealed certain differences in 12 S rRNA and 16 S rRNA, with similarities of 98.77% and 98.12%, respectively. Alignment results showed that the similarity of all tRNAs exceeded 90.00%. The lowest similarity was observed in tRNA-Glu (91.89%).

Both nucleotide and amino acid sequences of the 13 PCGs were compared. Mutation analysis of the 13 PCGs in P. fulvescens and P. strophiata identified 775 mutation sites (Table 6). Among these, ND5 had the largest number of mutation sites (136), while ATP8 had only 11. The genes with the highest number of nonsynonymous mutations were ND5 (17) and ND2 (16), while the lowest was in ND4L. These results indicate that the genes responsible for the core functions of oxidative phosphorylation (such as ATP8 and the COX series) are generally more conserved, whereas genes constituting Complex I of the respiratory chain (such as ND5 and ND2) may be more prone to accumulating variations and could even be involved in adaptive evolutionary processes, such as adaptation to specific environments like high-altitude or low temperatures36.

The Ka/Ks values of the 13 PCGs in both P. fulvescens and P. strophiata were below 1 (Table S8), indicating that all 13 PCGs are under purifying selection. ATP8 (0.1689) and ND3 (0.1403) showed relatively higher average Ka/Ks ratios. This suggests that ATP8 and ND3 may evolve at a faster rate compared to other PCGs.

The A + T content of the mitochondrial genomes of P. fulvescens and P. strophiata is 54.05% and 54.20%, respectively. The A + T content of PCGs varies minimally between the two species, and the AT-skew and GC-skew values are also similar. This reflects the conserved composition of PCGs (Table 5). However, there were partial differences in the base composition of PCGs. Except for ND6, the A + T% content of all other PCGs in P. strophiata exceeds 50.00%, which is consistent with the pattern observed in P. fulvescens. The A + T content of tRNAs in P. fulvescens reaches 58.11%, slightly higher than the 57.55% observed in P. strophiata. The nucleotide composition and skew characteristics of tRNAs and rRNAs are relatively conserved. However, in the AT-skew and GC-skew, some genes exhibit more notable differences, such as the AT-skew of ATP8 and ND3, the GC-skew of ND6. These variations also reflect differences in the evolutionary pressures acting on distinct genetic elements.

Sympatric interspecific comparison between P. fulvescens and C. rubicilloides (sympatric non-congener)

The mitochondrial genome of C. rubicilloides is 16,805 bp in length, 33 bp shorter than that of P. fulvescens. The A + T content of P. fulvescens (54.05%) is slightly higher than that of C. rubicilloides (53.89%). Among the PCGs, the largest differences are found in COXII and ND4L. Compared with P. fulvescens, C. rubicilloides has a higher A + T content in COXII but a lower A + T content in ND4L (Table S9). The AT-skew values of most genes were positive and similar between the two species, with exceptions in ND1 and control region. P. fulvescens had positive AT-skew and C. rubicilloides had negative AT-skew, while ND4L exhibited the opposite pattern (Table S9).

Comparison between P. fulvescens and C. rubicilloides showed that the nucleotide sequence similarity of PCGs ranged from 78.65% to 89.17% (Table S10). COXI was the most conserved (89.17%), while ND2 showed the greatest variation. As the fastest evolving region in the mitochondrial genome, the control region displayed the lowest similarity (75.63%). ATP6 and COXI displayedshowed the highest amino acid similarities (92.51% and 91.49%, respectively), while ATP8 showed the lowest (63.64%) (Table S10).

Ka/Ks analysis of the PCGs between P. fulvescens and C. rubicilloides showed that all Ka/Ks ratios were less than 1 (Table S11), indicating purifying selection. COXI had the lowest Ka/Ks (0.0243) and the lowest nonsynonymous substitution rate (Ka = 0.0163). In contrast, ATP8 exhibited the highest Ka/Ks (0.1575), with its Ka value (0.1308) being higher than the other genes (Table S11).

Discussion

The mitochondrial genome of P. fulvescens exhibits a typical double-stranded circular molecule (16,838 bp), containing 37 genes (13 PCGs, 22 tRNAs, 2 rRNAs) and a control region. The gene arrangement is highly consistent with other reported avian mitochondrial genomes, reflecting extreme evolutionary conservation in avian mitogenome structure37. The genome displays a significant AT bias. This phenomenon may be related to differences in the efficiency of mitochondrial DNA replication and repair mechanisms, as well as mutation pressure38,39. Among the 13 PCGs, the start codons are predominantly ATG, the stop codons are mainly TAA, and incomplete stop codons (T–, TA-) were identified, which can be completed through polyadenylation40. PCGs also show strong AT preference, with ATP8 having the highest A + T content (56.55%), followed by ND3 (56.13%). All H-strand encoded PCGs (except ND6) display consistent nucleotide skew patterns, aligning with vertebrate mitogenome universality41. The control region is rich in cis-acting elements regulating replication and transcription. Its sequence characteristics and variation patterns may significantly influence the replication efficiency of the mitochondrial genome and the population genetic structure42,43. The nucleotide similarity of this region was lower in H-L altitude group (99.04%), implying that altitudinal differences may have initiated preliminary genetic differentiation within the population.

In this study, we investigated two P. fulvescens populations at the same altitude from Tianjun, China, and one low-altitude population from Mongolia. Divergence time estimates indicate that the two high-altitude individuals from Tianjun diverged at 0.07 Mya, later than the divergence of the Mongolian population. Both divergence events occurred during the Pleistocene epoch. The family Prunellidae diverged from other Passeriformes approximately 13.99 Mya (Miocene), coinciding with the intense late Miocene uplift of the Tibet Plateau. This geological event and subsequent environmental shifts likely drove isolation and differentiation of ancestral species within the family Prunellidae44,45. Consistent with previous studies on high-altitude adaptation of plateau birds, the divergence time and evolutionary patterns in this study match the general differentiation pattern of birds on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau46,47. The divergence time estimates obtained in this study differ from those of previous studies, which may be attributed to the selection of species and fossil calibration points in our analysis.

Analysis revealed that the mitogenome structure of P. fulvescens is highly conserved. This conservation reflected in the consistency of genome length, gene arrangement order, and start and stop codons of most PCGs. In comparisons of PCGs at both the nucleotide and amino acid, large differences were observed. The ATP8 gene demonstrated the lowest sequence similarity in H-L altitude group comparisons and harbored 2 amino acid variant sites. This gene encodes the ATP synthase subunit, which is directly involved in energy metabolism48. Its variation may reflect potential selective pressures from different altitudinal environments (such as variations in oxygen partial pressure and temperature) on the efficiency of energy metabolism49. In addition to ATP8, ND4 and ATP6 also show obvious sequence differences. The nucleotide similarity of ND4 between H-L altitude group is only 98.84%, making it the most variable gene among NADH dehydrogenase subunit genes. Under high-altitude hypoxic conditions, sequence variations of ND subunits lay the molecular foundation for birds to adapt to altitudinal50. Statistics of variation sites show that the numbers of nucleotide and amino acid variation sites between H-L altitude group are higher than those within the H-H altitude group. Altitude difference promotes the accumulation of variations in mitochondrial genes. ATP8, COXIII and ND5 all carried non-synonymous mutations in both groups. Only COXI had non-synonymous mutations between the H-L altitude group. COXI is the core catalytic subunit of mitochondrial Complex IV, responsible for electron transfer and oxygen reduction51. Its sequence variation may reflect the adaptation of P. fulvescens to different oxygen partial pressures at varying altitudes. Nucleotide composition analysis revealed that ATP8 and the control region exhibited variations in A + T content with altitude. The AT-skew and GC-skew values of low altitude individual were lower than those of the two high altitude individuals. The AT-skew of ND1, ND6, ATP8 and Cyt b increased with decreasing altitude, while that of ATP6 and ND5 decreased. The Ka/Ks ratios for all PCGs of P. fulvescens were less than 1, indicating purifying selection. However, the degree of evolutionary constraints varies among different genes. The Ka/Ks values for genes such as ND1, ND2 and COXII are 0, indicating these genes are highly conserved in function. In contrast, COXIII (in the H-L altitude group) and ATP6 (in the H-H altitude group) exhibited relatively higher Ka/Ks values, indicating weaker evolutionary constraints. Variations in a small number of key genes maintain the core stability of mitochondrial energy metabolism, and also enable adaptation to environments at different altitudes52,53.

P. fulvescens and sympatric P. strophiata share similar mitogenome structures but exhibit significant interspecific divergence. Interspecific protein-coding genes (PCGs) contained 775 variable nucleotide sites, far exceeding the 91 intraspecific variable nucleotide sites detected in P. fulvescens. Among them, ND2 and ND4L show the highest variation ,ND2 and ND5 have multiple non-synonymous mutations. Both genes encode NADH dehydrogenase subunits, which play roles in mitochondrial electron transport and energy metabolism54. Ka/Ks analysis indicates that all PCGs genes are under purifying selection (Ka/Ks < 1) between species, reflecting functional constraints on the coding genes55. However, the average Ka/Ks values of ATP8 and ND3 genes are relatively higher. This indicates that ATP8 and ND3 genes may have been subject to weaker evolutionary constraints during the divergence between P. fulvescens and P. rubeculoides. ATP8 participates in ATP synthesis, and ND3 forms respiratory chain Complex I54. Moderate sequence variation can regulate energy metabolic efficiency and improve environmental adaptability. This phenomenon is also observed in Tarsiger indicus, Onychostruthus taczanowskii and Pyrgilauda ruficollis2,56.Nucleotide composition analysis revealed that both species displayed similar A + T content, reflecting compositional constraints on core functional genes among closely related species57. The most distinct differences in A + T content occurred in ND3 and ND6. These differences may be associated with interspecific genetic isolation and ecological adaptation.

The high similarity in mitochondrial genome structure and composition between P. fulvescens and C. rubicilloides conforms to the typical features of avian mitochondrial genomes. The differences observed in COXII, ND4L, and ND6 may reflect specific mutational pressures or divergent selective constraints. The differences in AT-skew values of the ND1, control region, and ND4L genes between the two species suggest that these genes have uexperienced different evolutionary dynamics. COXI showed the lowest Ka/Ks ratio, along with the highest nucleotide and amino acid similarities (89.17% and 91.49%, respectively). COXI plays a key role in the electron transport chain and is highly conserved in function. ATP8 exhibited the highest Ka/Ks ratio (0.1575) among all genes and the lowest amino acid similarity (63.64%), indicating that it is subject to the weakest selective constraints and has accumulated a greater number of nonsynonymous substitutions. In contrast, ND1 showed the highest synonymous substitution rate (Ks = 1.2117) among all genes, but its Ka/Ks ratio was very low (0.0305), and its amino acid similarity was relatively high (89.88%). This indicates that a large number of synonymous mutations have occurred in the ND1 gene between the two species, whereas the protein function is highly conserved.

Conclusions

This study sequenced and comprehensively analyzed the mitochondrial genome of P. fulvescens. At the intraspecific level, small but detectable genetic differentiation has emerged between different altitude populations of P. fulvescens. These differences are mainly concentrated in genes related to energy metabolism. In both groups, ATP8 has the lowest nucleotide and amino acid sequence similarity of all genes. Furthermore, the number of mutation sites and AT-skew values in some PCGs increased with decreasing altitude. In the high-altitude individual NC_035747, the A + T content of ATP8 was the highest among all PCGs and was significantly higher than that in the other two individuals, making it a gene with particularly notable base composition divergence. At the interspecific level, there is a genetic divergence between P. fulvescens and P. strophiata, with variations widely distributed across the entire mitochondrial genome. The average Ka/Ks values of ATP8 and ND3 are relatively high, and the A + T content of the ND3 and ND6 genes shows the most pronounced differences. The genetic differentiation between P. fulvescens and C. rubicilloides (different genus) is more distinct. The average Ka/Ks value of the ATP8 gene is relatively high, and the largest differences in A + T content are observed in the COXII and ND4L genes. By comparing P. fulvescens with sympatric congeneric and sympatric different genera species, these findings offer valuable insights into the evolutionary dynamics of mitochondrial genomes in closely related avian species. In summary, the PCGs related to high-altitude adaptation in the mitochondrial genome respond to the evolutionary processes, intraspecific high-altitude adaptation and interspecific divergence at varying evolutionary rates.

In the intraspecific analysis of P. fulvescens in this study, we found minimal differences in genetic differentiation, which may be attributed to factors such as latitudinal differences offsetting selective pressures. This study has a limitation in that the sampled individuals exhibit a narrow altitudinal range. Therefore, future research should consider the potential impacts of various factors, including physiological (e.g., hemoglobin concentration), biochemical, and increase the sample size and altitudinal span, on species distribution and evolution. This approach will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the adaptability and evolutionary patterns of P. fulvescens in the context of the Tibet Plateau.
