Phylogenetic relationships and divergent evolution in Pueraria sensu stricto (Fabaceae) based on plastid genomes and nrITS sequences
Authors: Yun Zhou, Xiao-Hong Shang, Dong Cheng, Liang Xiao, Sheng Cao, Hua-Bing Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite progress in resolving the polyphyly of Pueraria (Fabaceae) based on multilocus phylogenetic studies, uncertainty remains especially for deep, or backbone relationships among closely related taxa or clades within Pueraria sensu stricto, suggesting a classic case of reticulate evolution.ResultsComparative chloroplast genomic analysis across 13 Pueraria s.l. species and varieties revealed that simple sequence repeats (SSRs) are abundant and predominantly composed of adenine (A) and thymine (T). Complete comparison and sliding window analysis further demonstrated that non-coding regions exhibited greater sequence divergence than the coding regions. Seventeen highly variable loci such as rpoC2 and ycf1 were detected as potential molecular markers for Pueraria s.l. species identification. Phylogenetic analyses of complete plastomes and nrITS sequences revealed Pueraria s.s. as a monophyletic group, characterized by dorsifixed stipules and the absence of canavanine. These traits show multiple independent transitions across legumes, indicating highly dynamic evolution. Cytonuclear discordance, supported by coalescent simulations, species tree methods and split-network, provides strong evidence of introgression and/or hybridization within Pueraria s.s. Specifically, P. montana var. lobata and P. montana var. thomsonii formed a well-supported clade suggesting that they should be treated as a species or species complex, while P. montana var. culaishanensis nested within P. montana var. montana.ConclusionThis study offers new insights into the taxonomy and systematic relationships of Pueraria s.s. by integrating comparative chloroplast genomics, phylogenetic inference, and trait evolution. These results enhance our understanding of the lineages within Pueraria s.s. that have undergone reticulate evolution, and inform future studies on legume systematics and adaptive evolution.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-025-07493-8.

Introduction

Pueraria DC [1]. (1825; Fabaceae), comprising ca. 20 species distributed primarily in tropical and subtropical regions of Asia, is a taxonomically complex genus with several intermediate transitional traits between species and complex evolutionary history [2–5]. Previous phylogenetic studies using plastid markers (e.g., matK, rbcL) and nuclear ITS sequences have provided insights into the relationships within Pueraria, but low phylogenetic resolution has hindered robust relationships among main clades within Pueraria [6–10]. Phylogenetic analyses of Pueraria relied exclusively on DNA sequences, particularly the AS2 and four chloroplast markers, and suggested that several Pueraria species were classified outside the Pueraria s.s. group, namely Haymondia wallichii (DC.) (A) N. Egan (B) Pan bis, Neustanthus phaseoloides (Roxb.) Benth., Toxicopueraria peduncularis (Graham ex Benth.) (A) N. Egan (B) Pan bis and Teyleria stricta (Kurz) (A) N. Egan (B) Pan bis [2, 10]. The presence of canavanine serves as one of the diagnostic characters for the Pueraria s.l., while the Pueraria s.s. exclusively retains taxa with non-canavanine [2]. Furthermore, Pueraria s.s. is characterized by dorsifixed stipules, papery legumes, and non-coiling pods, with species showing considerable variation in floral traits (e.g., corolla color, petal morphology) and vegetative features (e.g., leaf shape, indumentum) [2, 11].

Pueraria s.s. species exhibit remarkable morphological diversity not only at inter-specific, but also intra-specific and individual levels. Traditional taxonomic approaches that rely primarily on quantitative traits (e.g., relative lengths of wing and keel petals) or vegetative traits often underestimate the prevalence of transitional traits, particularly within complex species groups such as the varieties of Pueraria montana (Lour.) Merr., including Pueraria montana var. montana (Lour.) van der Maesen, Pueraria montana var. lobata (Willd.) Maesen & S.M.Almeida ex Sanjappa & Predeep, Pueraria montana var. thomsonii (Benth.) Wiersema ex D. B. Ward, and Pueraria montana var. culaishanensis D. K. Zang. Despite their higher economic value and the resulting extensive molecular phylogenetic research, the former three still have uncertain phylogenetic relationships [3, 9]. Additionally, their names vary in usage among researchers due to phenomena of homonym and unscientific classification, which complicated the species delimitations and phylogenetic relationships [4]. For example, P. thunbergiana Benth., P. lobata (Willd.) Ohwi and P. pseudohirsuta Tang et Wang were treated as the synonym of P. montana var. lobata. Notably, P. montana var. culaishanensis is characterized by its distinct flower coloration and quantitative differences in the relative lengths of bracts and bracteoles comparing with other three varieties, yet remains unresolved at the molecular level [3–5]. Morphological comparisons and phylogenetic analyses may give clearer clue for its taxonomic status, as well as the case in P. omeiensis F. T. Wang Tang ex B. Pan bis, W. B. Yu R. T. Corlett [12]. Pueraria omeiensis has been treated as a synonym of P. montana var. montana and resembles P. montana, P. bouffordii H. Ohashi and P. xyzhuii Ohashi & lokawa. However, both morphological characters and phylogenetic analyses strongly supported P. omeiensis as a distinct species [12]. Previous molecular systematic studies have typically concentrated on genetic diversity, germplasm identification, and functional gene cloning, yet they have not provided sufficient resolution to clarify interspecific relationships within the genus [13–18]. Limited sampling (e.g., the exclusion of P. bouffordii) and low marker resolution have further hindered infrageneric classification and phylogenetic reconstruction of Pueraria s.s.

Morphological plasticity, coupled with widespread distribution and ecological adaptability, suggests rapid diversification and potential reticulate evolution within this group [2–5, 10, 18–20]. Furthermore, frequent hybridization, ancient polyploidy, and rapid radiations may have contributed to the evolutionary complexity of Pueraria s.s., especially the varieties of P. montana [2, 18–20]. Recent genomic studies have highlighted the role of whole-genome duplication (WGD) events in the diversification of legumes, including Pueraria [19, 20]. Notably, Pueraria species share two ancient WGD events with other Fabaceae: one predating the split of core eudicots (ca. 120 Ma) and another shared with Phaseoleae (ca. 59 Ma) [20]. Additionally, P. montana var. thomsonii (commonly known as “Fenge” in Chinese) experienced a lineage-specific WGD ca. 4.8 Mya, suggesting a potential link between genome duplication and morphological diversification [18].

Speciation is the primary driver of biodiversity [21, 22], while evolutionary processes such as hybridization and polyploidization have facilitated the diversification of many lineages [23–26]. Consequently, conflicts among gene trees, particularly plastid-nuclear discordance, exacerbate the challenges in resolving complex phylogenetic relationships [25, 26]. In recent years, phylogenomic analyses integrating diverse genetic backgrounds have not only reconstructed interspecific or intraspecific relationships to elucidate the evolutionary histories of rapidly radiating or reticulately evolving plant lineages [22, 25, 27–32], but also provided novel evolutionary frameworks for taxonomically ambiguous groups lacking diagnostic characters [33–36]. This approach has emerged as a powerful tool for elucidating infrageneric systematics and species diversity in non-model plant genera.

In this study, we utilized plastomes and nrITS sequences to explore the potential roles of hybridization and incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) in generating cytonuclear discordance within the evolutionary history of Pueraria s.s. and its closely related taxa. Here, we aim to resolve the evolutionary relationships and assess the role of reticulate evolution in Pueraria s.s. and its allies by using a comprehensive dataset that integrating plastome sequences and nuclear ribosomal ITS (nrITS) regions. By analyzing 71 plastomes (including 43 newly sequenced accessions) and 80 nrITS sequences, we address the following questions: (1) What are the structural features of the chloroplast genome within Pueraria s.s. species? (2) Does cytonuclear discordance exist in Pueraria s.s., and how can it be interpreted? (3) How have key morphological characters (e.g., stipule attachment) evolved across the phylogeny of Pueraria and related taxa?

Repeat sequence analysis

In 13 Pueraria s.l. species or varieties, the SSR count ranged from 73 to 107 as depicted in Tables S3-S4. P. montana var. lobata exhibited the highest number of SSR loci (107), while N. phaseoloides had the lowest (73). Among the six types, mononucleotide repeats were most frequent, and pentanucleotide and hexanucleotide repeats were relatively infrequent. Pueraria montana var. lobata had the maximum number of single nucleotide repeats (58), whereas the count of single nucleotide repeats in N. phaseoloides was lowest (36). AT/TA repeats were the most prevalent dinucleotide repeats. However, T. peduncularis demonstrated the most diverse SSR types, containing eleven types of repeats.

We identified four long repeats in the chloroplast genomes of 13 Pueraria s.l. species and varieties (Table S5). Results showed that repeat lengths were primarily between 30 and 39 bp. P. mirifica had the highest number of long repeat sequences, while T. peduncularis had the least. The repeat sequence numbers of T. peduncularis and H. wallichii were significantly lower than those of the other eleven species.

Pueraria s.s. is resolved with moderate to strong support based on nrITS sequences (Fig. S4) and coding sequences of plastomes (Fig. S5). Species tree and assessment of hybridization

ASTRAL analyses (Fig. S8) recovered the topology of four major clades in Pueraria s.s. (MBS = 36–51). The accessions of P. montana var. lobata and P. montana var. thomsonii together formed a clade (MBS = 51), while P. montana var. montana, P. bouffordii and P. montana var. culaishanensis also grouped as Clade II (MBS = 36). SVDquartets analyses (Fig. S9) failed to recovered four main clades in Pueraria s.s.. The varieties of P. montana and P. bouffordii formed a group (MBS = 100), while P. edulis and other Pueraria s.s. species clustered another group (MBS = 68). Relationships within these major clades were constant in both analyses, but the position of several species (e.g., the accessions of Pueraria alopecuroides) varied with respect to these two topologies. When the simulated chloroplast gene trees were summarized on the observed chloroplast tree, most clade frequencies were low (< 50%) (Fig. S10).

The incompletely resolved relationships among Pueraria s.s. in the network suggested substantial conflicting signals (Figs. S11-S12). All accessions were sorted into respective clades, and the clades were almost distinct based on plastomes and nrITS sequences, respectively. However, the relationships within the Clade I and Clade II were both less clear based on plastomes and nrITS sequences.

Discussion

Phylogenetic relationships among Pueraria and its closely related taxa

Phylogenetic analyses based on three datasets (COMPLETE, HYBRID-REDUCED, and INGROUP) consistently revealed main clades within Pueraria and supported its polyphyly across all analyses. In the COMPLETE dataset analysis, the chloroplast genomes phylogeny identified four distinct clades within Pueraria s.s. (Fig. S5), while the nrITS phylogeny showed some discrepancies, particularly in the placement of certain accessions such as P. montana var. lobata YG-129 and YG-130, P. montana var. montana GMM-35 and P. montana var. thomsonii FG-JX2 (Fig. S4). Our phylogenetic analyses revealed no significant topological conflict between the INGROUP and COMPLETE datasets, particularly along the critical backbone nodes. This congruence indicates that the inclusion of distantly related legume taxa did not introduce substantial long-branch attraction artifacts. Furthermore, comparative analysis across datasets demonstrated that various molecular markers and taxonomic sampling can profoundly impact both topological resolution and branch support. Owing to the more robust phylogenetic framework for Pueraria s.s. in the HYBRID-REDUCED dataset, we focus our discussion on the relationships among its several lineages.

Our phylogenetic analyses based on CDs of chloroplast genomes and nuclear ribosomal ITS (nrITS) sequences provide new insights into the evolutionary relationships within Pueraria and its relatives, with notable differences and congruence compared to previous studies [2–5, 7–9]. Our chloroplast phylogeny robustly reconstructs Pueraria s.s. as monophyletic and delineates four well-supported clades (I–IV). In contrast, the nrITS tree reveals a more complex evolutionary history, recovering five clades and novel relationships. Clade V, exclusively containing P. omeiensis, emerges as a distinct lineage sister to Clade III. This result provides clearer taxonomic delimitation compared to earlier studies, which, due to limited sampling, were unable to fully resolve these infra-generic relationships [2–5, 7–10]. The accessions of P. montana and its varieties, as well as P. bouffordii, were resolved into two well-supported clades, a finding that significantly advances previous understanding of the phylogenetic relationships within this taxonomic group [2–5]. The phylogenetic relationships among the varieties of P. montana are debated. Pueraria montana has four varieties: P. montana var. montana, P. montana var. lobata, P. montana var. thomsonii, and P. montana var. culaishanensis. Previous research based on morphological traits implies P. montana var. montana might be an independent species, and P. lobata and P. thomsonii could be one species or subspecies [37–41]. Plastome-based analyses support their monophyly [3, 5], further divided into two clades [4]. Yet, phylogenetic analyses based on nrITS indicated that P. montana var. thomsonii should be treated as a variety of P. montana, and P. montana var. lobata should be considered an independent species [7]. These conflicts suggest that ILS, hybridizations or introgressions likely underlie the complex relationships among these closely related taxa (Figs. 4 and 5 ) [2–5, 18–20]. Morphologically, P. montana var. culaishanensis is distinguished from P. montana var. montana by flower color (white vs. purple) and bract length (bracts longer than small bracts in P. montana var. culaishanensis vs. shorter in P. montana var. montana) [42]; yet it shows similar morphology and size of petal shape to P. montana var. lobata and P. montana var. thomsonii [37–41]. These findings suggested that rapid radiations can drive morphological similarity due to ancestral constraint, where similar selective pressures and shared genes shape species remain similar [43, 44]. The detected phylogenetic-morphological mismatch in P. montana var. culaishanensis highlights the roles of reticulate evolution, such as hybridization and introgression [25, 26].

Species tree inferred under ASTRAL supports the monophyly of Pueraria s.s. and resolves four major clades, whereas the SVDquartets analysis failed to consistently resolve relationships within the group, especially among varieties of P. montana. Such incongruence may be attributed to a range of biological and methodological causes [45–49]. Among biological factors, hybridization and incomplete lineage sorting (ILS) are likely particularly relevant in Pueraria given its recent radiative history [2, 4, 18–20]. By contrast, although horizontal gene transfer (HGT) cannot be entirely ruled out, it is generally less common in plants and there is currently no empirical evidence supporting its occurrence in Pueraria. Despite the known occurrence of whole-genome duplication (WGD) in this group (e.g., in P. montana var. thomsonii 4.8 million years ago [18]), its impact on the present phylogenetic discordance is still uncertain. Our results improve resolution yet still reveal phylogenetic ambiguity within the P. montana complex, supporting the hypothesis that recent divergence and hybridization have shaped the evolutionary history of this group [3–5]. Future studies integrating population genomic data, additional nuclear markers (e.g., low-copy genes), and biogeographic modeling will be crucial to disentangle these conflicting signals and test hypotheses of hybridization vs. ILS.

Gene tree and cytonuclear discordance

Chloroplast genomes typically reflect maternal inheritance and historical biogeographic events (e.g., vicariance), while nrITS sequences evolve under more neutral dynamics, enabling detection of recent hybridization or introgression [50]. Thus, the observed cytonuclear discordance based on our data suggests complex evolutionary histories within Pueraria spp., possibly involving ancient hybridization, introgression or incomplete lineage sorting, as has been inferred for species in other genera of Papilionoideae [51, 52].

Notably, the placement of P. montana var. lobata YG-129 and YG-130, P. montana var. montana GMM-35, and P. montana var. thomsonii FG-JX2 was inconsistent between the chloroplast genome phylogeny and the nrITS phylogeny. For example, P. montana var. thomsonii FG-JX2 formed a monophyletic group with P. montana var. lobata in the nrITS tree, despite belonging to a distinct clade in the chloroplast tree (Clade II). These discrepancies align with prior reports of morphological intermediacy and sympatric distributions among P. montana, P. lobata, and P. thomsonii, indicating potential gene flow or hybrid origin [2, 4]. Meanwhile, the cytonuclear discordance further implies potential chloroplast capture from P. bouffordii into hybrid lineages such as P. montana var. culaishanensis × P. montana var. montana, which is also consistent with certain morphological and geographical distribution of P. bouffordii [53].

The phylogenetic placement of P. edulis, a species endemic to southwestern China, reveals notable cytonuclear discordance that underscores the complex evolutionary history within Pueraria. In the nrITS phylogeny (Fig. 5), P. edulis forms a clade with P. omeiensis and is sister to Clade II, whereas P. edulis clustered a monophyletic clade (Clade III) and is sister to Clade I and II in the plastid tree (Fig. 4). However, plastome-based phylogenies show that accessions of P. edulis do not form a monophyletic group but are nested within the varieties of P. montana [5]. This incongruence could be explained by ancient hybridization followed by organellar capture, a phenomenon increasingly reported in other plant groups with complex evolutionary histories [24, 25, 54].

Coalescent simulations further suggested that ILS alone is insufficient to explain the observed discordance, particularly within Clade I and Clade II where hybrids have been identified (Fig. S10), implying a significant role for hybridization [55]. Notably, the chloroplast tree exhibits low bootstrap support for deeper nodes (e.g., within Clade I or Clade II/III split). The lower support values across both gene trees for deeper nodes align with previous studies, suggesting a recent and rapid diversification within Pueraria spp [2]. Meanwhile, split networks revealed that reticulation may complex real phylogenetic relationship among Pueraria s.s. (Figs. S11-S12). Reticulate relationships in the nrITS network (Fig. S12), particularly Clade I, Clade II and Clade IV, likely reflect recent hybridization or ILS in this rapidly diversifying group (c. 5–10 Mya) [2, 18, 20]. The low bootstrap support for deeper nodes in both trees further supports incomplete sorting of ancestral polymorphisms, a common phenomenon in young radiations [50, 56].

In conclusion, the multi-locus approach highlights reticulate evolution in Pueraria, underscoring the necessity of integrative approaches to resolve taxonomic uncertainties in groups with recent radiations and potential hybridization events [56]. Future studies integrating population genomic data, additional nuclear markers (e.g., low-copy genes), and biogeographic modeling will be crucial to disentangle the roles of hybridization, ILS, and historical events in shaping the evolutionary history of this economically important genus.

Evolution processes of major clades

The new and robust phylogenetic relationships based on plastomes among the tribes and genera of the core Phaseoleae or legume plants allowed us to reevaluate the reliability of the characters that were once widely used for systematic consideration (Fig. 6). Morphological analysis revealed a distinct pattern in stipule attachment position among major clades: Pueraria s.s. possess stipules inserted in the axil between the petiole and stem, a character shared with outgroup taxa (e.g., Vigna spp.) (Fig. 6). The basal attachment likely represents the ancestral state, while the axillary position evolved independently within Pueraria s.s. comparing to Neustanthus Benth., Toxicopueraria and Haymondia [2, 11]. Stipules are considered as outgrowths or appendages at the base of petioles [57]. This character not only plays a role in the classification and delimitation in Pueraria s.s., but also correlates with structural modifications in petiole anatomy (e.g., vascular bundle arrangement), suggesting adaptive shifts in leaf attachment mechanisms [58, 59], as well as in Microcos L [60]. or Lamiaceae [61]. Pueraria species are twining herbs or shrubs, so that the type of attachment mechanism determines the extent to which these climbing species mechanically adapt to mountain forests, thickets, and open places. This transition likely occurred early in the diversification of Pueraria s.s., coinciding with ecological niche expansion into forest understories (where axillary stipules may enhance leaf support). Additionally, the dorsifixed stipule has undergone parallel evolution in Vigna and Zornia. Stipules in legumes are often foliaceous and protect the shoot apex at the developing nodes [62–64]. It is likely conserved mechanism that Legume species inhibit SGL1 expression during stipule development through their MtNOOTs [65].

The non-proteinogenic amino acid canavanine, known for its insecticidal properties, has evolved multiple times in Pueraria s.l. and other legumes (e.g., Canavalia ensiformis (L.) DC.) [2]. This pattern likely results from convergent evolution or ancient horizontal gene transfer events during land plant evolution. However, these results may be a clue that the distribution patterns of secondary metabolites in legumes often exhibit discontinuous or non-hierarchical relationships with phylogeny.

The basic structure of the plastomes is relatively conserved across Pueraria species, compared to their morphological characters. Structural alterations caused by inversions, deletions and duplications or loss of chloroplast gene were also detected in some species or lineages in legume, especially in papilionoid legumes [66]. However, our results revealed no obvious expansion or contraction of IR region, indels and inversion in the chloroplast genome among Pueraria s.s. species, even compared to the outgroup species of H. wallichii. Nevertheless, highly variable regions located in certain intergenic spacers or at the junction regions between single-copy (SC) and inverted repeat (IR) regions may serve as useful molecular markers for phylogenetic analysis and species identification (Fig. 2). The nucleotide diversity (Pi) among the varieties of P. montana was extraordinarily low, yet distinct loci could still be discerned for species discrimination (Fig. 3). The results are consistent with those of Zhou et al. [4] and Hai et al. [5], supporting the classification of these taxa as varieties of P. montana [5], while also reflecting the complex evolutionary history that may involve processes such as hybridization or rapid radiation [2, 4]. Moreover, the two genes (rpoC2 and ycf1) and several intergenic spacers (e.g., rps3-rps19) exhibited significant mutations, identifying them as hypervariable regions (Fig. 3). These fragments can serve as potential molecular markers for Pueraria plants [5]. Collinearity analysis indicated no major disruptions in the arrangement of chloroplast gene fragments among Pueraria species, suggesting an absence of significant deletions.

SSRs exhibit variation in copy number among species and typically demonstrate higher mutation rates than neutral DNA regions, making them suitable as genetic markers for assessing population polymorphism in Pueraria [67, 68]. In this study, mononucleotide repeats were the most abundant among the six SSR categories examined. Furthermore, forward and palindromic repeats were the most common types identified across the 13 Pueraria species and varieties, consistent with previous reports [5]. Notably, H. wallichii and T. peduncularis exhibit a significant reduction in both the type and number of long repeat sequences. These characteristics can serve as potential molecular markers for species identification within Pueraria s.l.

Conclusions

This study presents a comprehensive analysis integrating chloroplast genomic structure, phylogenetic relationships, and evolutionary history within Pueraria s.s. and its close relatives. Through extensive sampling and multi-locus phylogenetic approaches, we have refined the phylogenetic framework of Pueraria s.s. and revealed substantial cytonuclear discordance, likely resulting from hybridization and incomplete lineage sorting. Comparative analysis of chloroplast genomes identified conserved structural features along with highly variable regions suitable for DNA barcoding. The combination of dorsifixed stipules and absence of canavanine serves as a morphological and chemical synapomorphy for Pueraria s.s. Our findings provide not only a clarified taxonomic delineation of the genus but also new insights into the genetic and morphological evolution of Pueraria. Future studies incorporating transcriptomic and population genomic data will further elucidate the adaptive evolution and diversification history of this group.

Materials and methods

Taxon sampling, genomic sequencing, and assembly

Twelve fresh leaf samples were collected in the field from Guangxi, Shaanxi, Jiangxi, Sichuan, and Tibet, China (Table S1). Huabing Yan and Xiaohong Shang from Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences identified the plant materials. The plants and voucher specimens were deposited in the Guangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences (GXAAS, Nanning, China) (Table S1). Silica-dried leaf material was sent to the Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co. Ltd. (Tianjin, China) for library preparation and Illumina sequencing on NovaSeq 6000. DNA extraction, library preparation protocols, assembled reference sequences, manually remapped and inspected were previously described by Zhou et al. [4]. The previously published complete plastome sequences of P. montana var. lobata (GenBank nos., OM156439) and P. montana var. thomsonii (GenBank no., OM156452) were used as seed references. Another fifty-nine previously published plastomes were downloaded from the NCBI GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) (Table S1). A representative plastid genome map of Pueraria drawn by OGDRAW [69] is displayed in Fig. 1.

To assemble the nrITS sequences, we downloaded the nrITS sequences of Pueraria and other legume species from GenBank and chose P. montana var. lobata (GenBank accession number KY860925) as a reference. The assembled contigs were mapped to the reference sequences in Geneious v. R9.0.2 [70]. Finally, we obtained 31 nrITS sequences for this analysis, including 12 assembled from the sequencing data generated in this study and 19 assembled from the raw data published by Zhou et al. [4]. Additionally, a total of 49 nrITS sequences of Pueraria and related legume taxa were obtained from NCBI (Table S6).

Comparative genome analysis and identification of the hypervariable regions

We detected the contraction and expansion of the boundaries between SC and IR regions among the chloroplast genomes of 13 Pueraria species or varieties using IRscope online tool (https://irscope.shinyapps.io/irapp/) [71] (Table S1). Global collinearity and potential gene rearrangements were assessed using the Mauve plugin [72] within Geneious v9.0.2. In addition, the nucleotide diversity (π) analysis of 40 plastomes of Pueraria s.l., 36 plastomes of Pueraria s.s., 27 plastomes of the varieties of P. montana (Table S1) was implemented using DnaSP v. 6.12.03 [73] with window length set to 500 bp, and the step size set as 200 bp. Based on previous gene annotation results, this region was precisely located and further analyzed to develop functional molecular markers.

Repeat sequences and SSR analysis

The identification of SSR markers within the Pueraria species was facilitated using the MISA tool [74]. The parameters were set for single nucleotide, dinucleotide, trinucleotides, tetranucleotides, pentanucleotides, and hexanucleotide SSRs at ten, five, four, three, three, and three repeats respectively. The REPuter online utility [75] (https://bibiserv.cebitec.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer/webservice.html) was employed to recognize long repetitive sequences. The parameters were defined as follows: a minimum repeat size of 30 base pairs, and a maximum computed repeat size of 5000 base pairs.

Phylogeny reconstruction

For both plastome and nrITS sequence data, three datasets were analyzed: the COMPLETE dataset that includes all sampled species (71 plastomes and 80 nrITS sequences), the HYBRID-REDUCED dataset (67 palstomes and 76 nrITS sequences) excluding the putative hybrid species, and the INGROUP dataset comprising Pueraria and its closely related taxa (40 plastomes and 53 nrITS sequences). The INGROUP dataset was constructed to test for potential long-branch attraction (LBA) artifacts by reducing the number of distantly related outgroups. This approach helps evaluate the stability of the phylogenetic inference and minimizes the risk of topological bias caused by distantly related taxa [4, 76–78]. Haymondia wallichii (DC.) (A) N. Egan & (B) Pan bis and Neustanthus phaseoloides (Roxb.) Benth. were chosen as the outgroup based on similar morphology and relatedly phylogenetic relationship with Pueraria taxa in our analysis of COMPLETE dataset. Of the protein-coding genes, 75 have intact open reading frames in all 71 plastomes, while three (i.e., rps16, ycf4, infA) are either absent or truncated in at least one species (Table S1). In addition, in case of the gene re-arrangement potentially occurred among the genera in Fabaceae, sequeces matrices (Coding sequences) were extracted and aligned separately, which contained 30 rRNA, 4 tRNA and 75 protein-coding genes (Table S1). Multiple sequence alignment was performed using MAFFT v7.463 online [79] (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/software/) and manually adjusted in Geneious v. R9.0.2 to exclude poorly aligned regions.

The appropriate model for each dataset was determined using jModelTest v2.1.4 [80]. Phylogenetic analyses were inferred using both Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) via the CIPRES Science Gateway server (http://www.phylo.org/) [81]. The ML analysis was performed using RAxML [82] with rapid bootstrapping and 1000 replications, and the GTR-GAMMA model. The best-fit model and calculated generations of BI analysis for both chloroplast sequences and nrITS sequences of three datasets were listed in Table S7. For the BI analysis, two independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) runs with three heated and one cold chain were executed using MrBayes v3.2.7 [83], sampling every 1000 generations. The first 25% of trees were discarded as burn-in and the remaining samples were then summarized and a majority-rule consensus tree was constructed. Two million generations were run for the INGROUP dataset (both chloroplast sequences and nrITS sequences), COMPLETE dataset of nrITS sequences, HYBRID-REDUCED dataset of nrITS sequences, whereas three million generations were run for the other four datasets. The stationarity was considered to be reached when the average standard deviation of split frequencies fell below 0.01.

Species tree and assessment of hybridization

To estimate coalescent-based species trees, we used ASTRAL-II [84] and SVDquartets [85, 86] on the HYBRID-REDUCED dataset of plastid genomes. We extracted each of the 75 protein-coding genes using Geneious v. R9.0.2 and estimated individual locus gene trees using RAxML [82] with a GTR-GAMMA model and 100 replications. The best ML trees were used as input for ASTRAL to generate maximum quartet support species trees with multi-locus bootstrap support values (MBS). The resulting species trees were visualized using FigTree v. 1.4.4 [87].

The coalescent simulations were used to test whether ILS alone could explain plastome and nuclear incongruence in the COMPLETE dataset. Ten thousand plastid species trees were simulated under the coalescent with DENDROPY v.4.1.0 [88] with branch lengths scaled by four to account for organellar inheritance, similar to Morales-Briones et al. [55].

To better visualize and evaluate the phylogenetic signal conflicts among Pueraria s.s., plastomes and nrITS sequences were used to reconstruct split networks, respectively. The neighbor-net al.gorithm based on uncorrected K2P-distances was implemented in SplitsTree v6.1.10 [89], excluding all outgroup taxa to leave species of Pueraria s.s.

Ancestral character-state reconstruction

To reevaluate the reliability of the characters that were once widely used for taxonomic and systematic consideration, ancestral states were reconstructed using the Mesquite v3.81 [90] with the likelihood model to detect potential synapomorphies. In this analysis, a compiled tree including 67 taxa was used as an input tree, which was generated on the basis of the topology of the HYBRID-REDUCED dataset -based BI tree in this study and the knowledge on the phylogenetic relationships of genera from previous studies [2, 4]. Two morphological characters have been used to explore the evolutionary history: stipule (0) basifixed (stipules Attached at or by the base) and (1) dorsifixed (stipules produced below point of insertion); canavanine (0) absent (1) present.
