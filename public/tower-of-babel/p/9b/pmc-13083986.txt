Development of lightweight, environmentally friendly bricks using leather shaving and buffing dust waste
Authors: Moklasur Rahman Mithu, Md. Ariful Islam, Md. Abdul Mottalib, Imdadul Haque, Md. Abdul Goni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study explores the recycling and reuse of leather-shaving and buffing dust waste in brick production, using a new approach to convert waste into valuable materials. Bricks were produced by incorporating varying proportions (0–20%) of leather shaving and buffing dust into a mixture of clay, ash, and river sand. Both fired and non-fired methods were used to prepare bricks. The highest compressive strength was 24.46 MPa for fired bricks, and 19.22 MPa for non-fired bricks made with 10% leather dust, both exceeding the national and international standards. Other properties, including weight losses, area shrinkage, bulk density, and efflorescence, were examined, and the results were found within acceptable limits defined by ASTM and Bangladesh Standards. Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) analysis revealed micro-fibrous structure within composite bricks, demonstrating the lightweight nature of the resulting materials. No oxidation of chromium(III) to more toxic chromium(VI) species after thermal treatment was observed by the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopic analysis. Leaching analysis of metals showed their concentrations within allowable limits, indicating the quality and environmental safety of the synthesized bricks. The novelty of the study is to incorporate leather shaving and buffing dust into brick production, offering an eco-friendly solution for managing tannery waste while enhancing the mechanical properties of the resulting bricks. The current approach revealed a sustainable and environmentally friendly solution for tannery waste management, which not only reduces tannery pollution but also provides a cost-effective alternative to traditional bricks for promoting sustainability in construction industries in developing countries.

Introduction

The demand for environmentally benign construction materials has been increasing dramatically in recent years due to the rapid population growth and industrialization in many least developed countries. Therefore, there is an urgent need for new materials that are environmentally benign, cost-effective, and sustainable for use across different fields. An interesting and significant approach is to utilize waste and convert it into functional materials for various applications, while the process can also reduce environmental pollution. In leather manufacturing processes, about 80–85% solid waste is generated from different steps1,2, which consist of 30–40% of buffing dust, 50–60% of flesh, 2–5% of hair, shaving and splits, 5–7% of skin trimmings3,4. Tannery solid waste, which contains harmful chemicals such as chromium and other toxic substances, can contaminate soil and water, leading to significant environmental degradation and health risks for both aquatic life and humans if not managed properly. Efficient conversion of solid waste into suitable construction materials offers a realistic solution to the challenges of pollution and cost-effective design and construction of traditional buildings and other infrastructure5.

Leather buffing and shaving dust are light micro-fine fibres that are created in the buffing process and shaving operation, respectively, during leather manufacturing. According to recent data, there are about 1.18 × 104 to 3.54 × 104 tons of polishing dust generated annually from different tannery industries in Bangladesh3. It is highly unfortunate that this large volume of buffing & shaving dust waste is not properly handled and managed in many developing countries, such as Bangladesh. These wastes are disposed of without prior treatment, causing severe pollution of the terrestrial environment. Several studies were conducted previously which concentrated on the utilization of solid wastes such as industrial slags6, sludge’s from tannery effluent treatment plants7,8, waste glass and marble sludge9, waste marble powder10, leftover glass waste9,11, recycled glass powder and nano-silica12, arsenic-iron sludge13, waste brick powder and waste ceramic powder14, bentonite–ash–cement composites15 to manufacture bricks materials in order to save environment from pollution. In other studies, rice husk ash and bagasse ash16, paper fibre residue17, agricultural waste materials18, straw19, sisal, jute, and hemp fiber20, waste brick powder and waste ceramic powder14, fly ash (FA) and backside ash21, sugarcane bagasse ash22, liming sludge23, raw buffing dust3, and raw shaving dust14 were incorporated into the clay mixture in the making of brick materials to obtain a sustainable solution for waste management24.

By the way, it is well known that the earthquake force exerted on an infrastructure is directly proportional to a building’s overall weight. The greater the overall weight of an infrastructure, such as a building, the greater the seismic force acting on it. Therefore, a heavy material such as traditional brick cannot be deemed a suitable construction material for earthquake response. Therefore, lighter construction materials are the best choice for building construction and other infrastructure. To the best of our knowledge, there are few studies that have reported on the use of leather waste in the preparation of building materials. The current study introduces the innovative use of leather buffing and shaving dust as a partial substitute for clay in the production of both fired and non-fired bricks. It explores the potential of repurposing leather waste to create lightweight, eco-friendly building materials, offering a sustainable alternative to traditional bricks. The novelty lies in comparing the physico-chemical, mechanical, and environmental performance of fired and unfired bricks made from leather waste, offering valuable insights into their feasibility as a cost-effective, environmentally responsible material in the construction industry.

Materials and methods

Raw materials collection

Clay was collected from six brick factories from different locations in Bangladesh. Buffing dust and shaving dust were collected from the workshop of the Institute of Leather Engineering and Technology, University of Dhaka. Rice husk ash and river sand were brought from Ajanta Rice Mill and Buriganga River, Dhaka, respectively.

Preparation of clay

The clay was initially dried in the air under sunlight for 3 days, and the large pieces were crushed into smaller pieces using a hammer and ball mill. Then the clay was sieved to separate the small pieces of brick, pebbles, crabs, grass, and other unnecessary materials. After that, the cleaned clay was exposed to radical transformation in the air for two weeks to soften. In this way, dry fine clay was prepared and used to make brick materials.

Preparation of buffing-shaving dust

Buffing dust and shaving dust were dried in sunlight for a few days to remove the moisture content, and further cut into smaller pieces and ground into fine powder using a grinding machine. Leather dust was pulverized so that the dust could mix well with the clay particles. In the present study, about 3 kg of shaving dust and 2 kg of chrome-containing leather buffing dust were mixed to prepare 5 kg of chrome leather buffing-shaving dust mixture for producing lightweight bricks.

Blending of raw materials

The dried clay, crushed rice husk ash, and chrome leather buffing-shaving dust were mixed well in different proportions. Rice husk and chrome leather buffing-shaving dust were mixed at a 50:50 (w/w) ratio, with a minimum of 5% and a maximum of 20% waste added to the clay. After weighing, they were mixed and combined properly on a plate, and 10–20% water was added accordingly until the mixture transformed into a plastic form. The mixing process was carried out by initial stirring with hand-held equipment, followed by suitable pressure applied with a mixer machine to ensure all components were well mixed and homogenized.

Moulding of brick

A wooden mould with dimensions (15.8 × 7.8 × 4.4) cm3 was used for moulding the bricks following the stiff mud process. After proper mixing of the clay with water, the resulting mixture was poured into the mould to give the shape of the brick. Then the clay was pressed to get the shape of the whole mould, and then the setting was turned upside down, and the resulting brick was left for drying in the sunlight.

Firing of bricks

After drying the bricks initially in the sunlight and later in the laboratory oven, the bricks became ready for firing. The prepared bricks were fired in the laboratory furnace at a temperature of 900 °C for 3 h (Fig. 1a). The dehydration process occurred between 425 and 750 °C, which released moisture contents where chemically coupled water molecules disintegrated from clay. Then the oxidation process started, and fluxing components were converted to oxides, sulphur was eliminated, all combustible material was consumed, and carbon was removed at about 300–900 °C.

Non-fired bricks

The non-fired brick-making process involved blending raw materials like cement, river sand, clay, ash, and leather dust to create bricks without traditional firing (Fig. 1b). Cement acted as the binding agent while river sand and clay formed the primary structural components. Ash, derived from coal or agricultural waste, and leather dust served as supplementary fillers. These materials were mixed in precise proportions for uniformity, using a concrete mixer. The resulting mixture was shaped into bricks by using the greased moulds. The bricks were then cured in a controlled environment, typically through air drying or water curing at intervals (7, 14, and 28 days). After curing, they were first sun-dried and then dried in an oven before undergoing compressive strength testing.

Characterization techniques

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FT-IR) (Perkin Elmer, USA) analysis was carried out to characterize the structures of products with different functionalities. The elemental composition of cement and admixtures containing cement was analyzed by using Wavelength Dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (WD-XRF) with the Bruker S8 Tiger series model. The Binder Na-borate and powder cement samples were mixed in a weight ratio of 2:4 to produce the pellets required for the XRF equipment. Subsequently, the WD-XRF system was employed to analyze these samples/pellets. The device features a solitary objective (Rh), a 40 KV X-ray tube (50 W). The X-ray diffractogram data were captured using Smart Lab SE’s Multipurpose X-Ray Diffractometer (XRD) equipped with a Nickel (Ni28) K filter and a copper (Cu) tube energy source operating at 2.00 KW (40.00 KV×50.00 mA). The experiment used Cu Kα radiation with a wavelength of 1.5406 Å. The scanning was performed with a step size of 0.01° and a rate of 5 °C/min. The scanning range was 5–80° in two theta (2). The TGA measurement was conducted using the STA-449 instrument from NETZSCH. The temperature increased gradually from 25 to 1000 °C at a heating rate of 10 °C per minute in the presence of a nitrogen atmosphere to avoid oxidation. The DSC measurements were conducted using the STA 449 F5 Jupiter instrument from NETZSCH, with a heating rate of 10 °C/min. Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) quantifies changes in enthalpy during physical or chemical transformations in materials as a function of temperature or time.

Leaching test

Leaching tests were conducted by immersing 50 g of concrete block samples into 500 mL of deionized water within glass beakers, and a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:10 (w/v). The samples were kept at room temperature (23 ± 2 °C) without any disturbance. Aliquots of the leachate were collected at 7, 14, and 28 days, filtered through 0.45 μm membrane filters to remove suspended solids, and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh deionized water. The filtered leachates were analyzed for chromium (Cr) using Atomic Absorption Spectroscopy (AAS).

To ensure reproducibility and minimize experimental error, all measurements were conducted in triplicate, and average results are reported here.

Results and discussion

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) analysis

The FT-IR (ATR) spectra of river sand, cement, leather dust, ash, and brick are shown in Fig. 2. In the FT-IR spectra of the river sand, a weak absorption band around 2350–2360 cm−1, commonly associated with atmospheric or physiosorbed CO2, was observed in the spectra of river sand and some raw constituents. However, the C–H bending resonance at 782 cm⁻¹ remains unchanged before and after the treatment. Several characteristic resonances were observed in the FT-IR spectra of cement, which are at 2356 cm⁻¹ (O=C=O stretching), 1488 cm⁻¹ (C–H bending), 1124 cm⁻¹ (S=O stretching), and 874 cm⁻¹ (out-of-plane C–H bending). Among these resonances, the peaks associated with O=C=O stretching and C–H out-of-plane bending were absent from the corresponding spectra of brick, suggesting thermal decomposition of carbonate-related species and a reduction in organic content under the applied firing conditions. Other resonance bands, such as C–H bending (1488 cm⁻¹) and S=O stretching (1124 cm⁻¹), showed a decrease in their intensities, indicating the partial modification of functional groups in the structures of bricks made in this process. The explanation is based on comparative changes in relative band intensity across the spectra attained under similar measurement conditions; however, no absolute quantification of functional group concentration is disguised from FTIR alone.

XRD analysis

An X-ray diffraction (XRD) study was conducted to investigate the crystalline phases (Fig. 3) of new materials prepared in this study, where multiple distinctive peaks correlate to the material’s crystallographic planes. The diffraction peaks were indexed by comparing their 2θ positions with the standard ICDD/JCPDS database. The intense diffraction peak observed at 2θ = 26.73° can be ascribed primarily to the (101) plane of quartz (SiO2), a leading crystalline phase commonly associated with silica-rich constituents such as clay and ash, as confirmed by the standard JCPDS card no. 46–104526–28. The persistence of quartz as a dominant crystalline phase indicates that the primary mineral framework of the clay-based matrix is retained after processing. The low-angle reflection at 2θ = 5.18° may be linked with residual amorphous or poorly ordered silica-rich phases originating from rice husk ash. The prominent peak detected at 2θ = 32.38° might be related to RHA present in the composite samples, which indicates the presence of a crystalline phase in the respective materials16,29,30. The distinct band at 2θ = 13.18° obtained from the leather dust sample revealed the existence of a specific crystal phase, and the peak detected at 2θ = 34.14° reflects the dominance of quartz-related diffraction in the respective sample. Again, the band at 2θ = 10.21° corresponds to kaolinite, consistent with the mineralogical composition of the clay precursor (JCPDS card no. 14-0164)31. The appearance of a sharp band at 2θ = 26.61° indicated the presence of crystalline quartz and kaolinite phases of clay materials. Furthermore, the relative proportion of crystalline and amorphous components in the clay material was predominantly evidenced by the quartz crystal phase32. The notable signal observed at 2θ = 10.55° indicated the presence of a particular crystal phase in the respective material33. The sharp peak identified at 2θ = 26.89° might be attributed to the presence of leather dust, and clay in the composite materials suggesting distinct diffraction reflections associated with quartz (Fig. 3). These results suggested that the prepared composite materials retained the dominant mineral phases of the clay-based matrix after processing which could be used in various fields such as brick building, tile manufacturing, etc. No attempt was made to quantify crystallinity; the XRD analysis is limited to qualitative phase identification and phase retention following processing.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis

The XPS study was conducted on leather dust, non-fired brick, and fired brick, which provided critical insights into surface characteristics with chemical compositions and the incorporation of leather wastes in brick formation processes.

Various distinct peaks were observed in the spectra of all three experimental samples. A notable Cr 2p₃/₂ peak was found around 576–577 eV. For leather dust, non-fired brick, and fired brick, binding energies were recorded at 576 eV, 576 eV, and 577 eV, respectively. These magnitudes of binding energies are characteristic of trivalent chromium [Cr(III)], which is commonly associated with chromium compounds used in the leather tanning process35. Interestingly, no peaks were detected in the 579–580 eV region, which would correspond to hexavalent chromium [Cr(VI)], indicating the absence of Cr(VI) species in all samples, as shown in Fig. 4. It should be noted that XPS is inherently surface-sensitive, probing only the top few nanometers of the material. So, the absence of Cr(VI) observed by XPS mostly reflects the surface chemistry of the samples rather than the entire bulk composition. Since leaching processes are ruled by surface and near-surface interactions, the surface stabilization of Cr(III) is relevant for assessing environmental safety. These results confirmed the absence of Cr(VI) in the composite samples and suggested that chromium in leather dust remained in its less toxic state, Cr(III) species, after incorporation into bricks. This is directly relevant to the leaching behavior of the bricks, as trivalent chromium exhibits significantly lower solubility and mobility than hexavalent chromium under environmental conditions, thereby reducing the potential for chromium release. This is truly significant from an environmental and health perspective, as Cr(VI) is more toxic and poses serious risks. XPS data from previous studies also confirmed the presence of Cr(III) species in chromium-containing composite materials, as documented in literature by other authors36,37. These research findings evidenced that thermal treatment of leather dust-based brick did not induce any oxidation of Cr(III) to more toxic Cr(VI) species, which is very critical and hazardous regarding environmental and health safety perspectives. Additionally, a notable peak at 400 eV was detected across the spectra of all experimental samples, which corresponds to the N 1 s orbital. The presence of nitrogen in the composite samples could be attributed to the collagen structure inherent to leather, further confirming the incorporation of leather-derived organic components into the brick structure, even after thermal processing. These XPS results evidenced the stability of chromium Cr(III) species and demonstrate the retention of nitrogen in the composite samples, which were obtained from collagen. The XPS results provide strong surface-level evidence for chromium stabilization in a non-toxic form, which, in combination with leaching data, confirms the environmental safety of leather waste incorporation into brick materials.

Thermal analysis

STA analysis

The thermal degradation behavior of the leather dust sample was studied over a temperature range of 30–1030 °C, which is shown in Fig. 5. During the thermal analysis, a multi-step mass loss behavior dominated by the decomposition of organic constituents was observed. An initial mass loss of about 10% at low temperatures is attributed to moisture evaporation, followed by a major mass loss of 45.25% in the intermediate temperature range associated with the thermal degradation of collagenous and organic components29,38,39. The remaining residue represents thermally stable, non-volatile inorganic constituents. An initial weight loss of about 10% was observed at lower temperatures, which might be due to the evaporation of volatile components such as moisture.

TGA and DTGA of clay

The clay samples were thermally treated over a range of temperatures from 76.29 to 800 °C, and exhibited minimal mass loss across the investigated temperature range, indicating high thermal stability, the results are shown in (Fig. 6). Treatment at lower temperatures around 100 °C resulted in a weight loss of 0.5%, indicating possible moisture evaporation6. The weight reduction upon thermal treatment continued and reached at 1.20% observed at 530 °C. The observed weight changes are primarily associated with the removal of physically bound moisture and minor volatile components, while the dominant mineral phases remain stable under the applied thermal conditions. The final residue obtained after the heat treatment was 97.94%, which might contain non-volatile components that were not lost during the thermal analysis22,40.

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) analysis

Field emission scanning electron microscopy (FE-SEM) was used to examine the microstructural features of bricks incorporating 10% leather dust along with ash, clay, river sand, and cement dust. The FE-SEM images (Fig. 8) revealed a heterogeneous microstructure with smooth regions interspersed with irregular pores and micro-voids. These voids are mainly observed at particle boundaries and interfacial zones, suggesting incomplete packing and localized interfacial gaps between leather dust particles and the mineral matrix. These structural features indicate the presence of voids and porosity within the material matrix, which can significantly affect the physicochemical properties as well as the performance of bricks to be used in the construction industry. The presence of interconnected pores contributes to a reduction in bulk density by increasing the overall void fraction within the brick matrix. The observed interfacial gaps and pore connectivity provide a microstructural explanation for the measured compressive strength trends, in which increased porosity and weaker particle–matrix interfaces reduce load-transfer efficiency41. The variations in textures and particle linkage highlighted the complex interactions between the added waste materials and the traditional brick components, as well as underlining the need for further optimization in composite formulations42–44. Overall, the analysis shows that pore formation and interfacial discontinuities introduced by leather dust addition are directly associated with reduced density and compressive strength.

Compressive strength

The compressive strength of bricks or other construction materials refers to their ability to withstand compressive loads. In buffing dust-incorporated bricks, the ratio of buffing dust to clay plays a critical role in determining the compressive strength45–47. The control brick, which contained no leather dust, exhibited a compressive strength of 26.77 MPa. However, the inclusion of buffing dust in the clay mixture increased compressive strength. This enhancement was detected up to an optimal leather dust content, beyond which a gradual drop in compressive strength is expected due to the strength–density trade-off. At higher levels of leather dust, thermal degradation of organic matter during firing results in additional porosity, reducing density and limiting effective load transfer within the brick matrix.

According to Gencel et al., the bricks made from the incorporation of leather dust attained a maximum compressive strength of 24.62 MPa32. This enhancement has likely occurred due to the insertion of finer leather dust particles, which filled up the voids between coarser clay particles, thereby forming stronger and more durable binding, resulting in improved crushing strength that surpasses the standard quality requirements outlined in the Indian Standard (1997)48. The compressive strength observed in the composite bricks produced during the present study was notably higher and exceeded the benchmarks set by various standards, including ASTM C 67−02c, ASTM C 62−08, and BDS 208 (2009)49. All compressive strength tests were conducted in accordance with ASTM C62. Brick specimens were prepared with standard dimensions, and the loading surfaces were leveled prior to testing to ensure uniform stress distribution. Commercially available fired clay bricks typically exhibit compressive strengths of 10–25 MPa, depending on raw materials and firing conditions. The compressive strengths achieved in the present study fall within or exceed this range, indicating the market competitiveness of the proposed leather dust–incorporated bricks.

Compressive strength analysis of clay

The bar chart (Fig. 9) illustrates the compressive strength (in MPa) of clay samples collected from different areas in Bangladesh. Clay collected from Gazipur showed the highest compressive strength at 26.79 MPa, which was much higher than that observed in the clay samples from other areas of the country, such as Tangail (7.85 MPa), Keraniganj (7.75 MPa), and Savar (5.36 MPa). The unusually high compressive strength of Gazipur clay may be attributed to its favorable mineralogical composition, particularly higher silica and alumina contents, which promote vitrification and dense microstructural development during firing. Such mineralogical features are known to improve interparticle bonding and mechanical stability compared to clays with lower flux-forming constituents50. Thus, clay collected from Gazipur was the optimal choice due to its significantly higher compressive strength, which indicated superior load-bearing capacity and durability. This property made this clay ideally suitable for applications in construction industries and engineering sectors where structural stability of materials is critically significant. The development of higher strength in composite materials might probably occur from their mineralogical composition and optimal particle arrangement, which could enhance their performance compared to the clay samples obtained from other areas.

Compressive strength analysis of fired bricks

The compressive strengths of different clay samples with varying compositions, including leather dust, ash, and river sand, Gazipur clay were examined and evaluated accordingly. The first three samples (1, 2, and 3) exhibited the highest compressive strengths as 24.46 MPa, 22.57 MPa, and 24.92 MPa, respectively, which are shown in Table 3. These magnitudes of compressive strengths are significantly higher due to the presence of a larger percentage of Gazipur clay (65–85%) in combination with balanced amounts of leather dust, ash, and river sand. Particularly, in the case of sample 3 with 85% Gazipur clay and minimal additives, the highest strength is due to the dominant presence of high-strength clay and minimal dilution by other components51,52. The superior performance of the first three samples can be attributed to the intrinsic high compressive strength of clay collected from Gazipur, as well as the optimal composition and particle interaction. Sample 3 consists of 10% leather dust with 70% clay, exhibits a comprehensive strength of 23.09 MPa, which is higher than the ASTM C62 standard value of 20.7 MPa49. High clay content (above 60%) promotes strong particle bonding and fewer weak interfaces, while lower additive proportions maintain the integrity of the clay matrix. Conversely, samples with reduced Gazipur clay content (e.g., samples 4–8) showed a marked decline in compressive strength, as the introduction of higher proportions of leather dust, ash, and river sand diluted the structural matrix, which increased pore volume, pore connectivity, and microcracks formed due to the burnout of organic components during firing. These results illustrate that the proportion and quality of Gazipur clay are critical factors that directly influence the compressive strength of the respective materials. It should be noted that the present comparisons are limited to compressive strength values measured under identical laboratory conditions; however, they provide a reliable indication of relative mechanical performance.

Therefore, the results are relevant for practical non-fired masonry applications under standard curing conditions.

Weight loss on ignition

Weight loss might occur from the carbonate decomposition and the silicates’ dihydroxylation, present in the raw materials. Figure 11 shows the results of weight loss upon ignition of the respective samples. As the ratio of waste raised in brick manufacturing processes increases, the weight loss of the blended brick also increases. This might be due to the loss of inorganic and organic substances, which are burned and decomposed in leather buffing-shaving dust, river sand, rice husk, and clay, as reported in ASTM D 431853. It has been observed that the application of only clay in the brick-making processes resulted in composite materials in which a weight drop of 5.56% occurred upon ignition. When 5% waste was added in the brick manufacturing process, the weight loss was found to be 18.60% in the resulting materials. Thereafter, the weight loss of brick was found to be increased linearly with elevation in the percentages of waste additions, as illustrated in ASTM D 7348 (2013)54. It is assumed that the organic contents in the raw brick mixtures are increased when more buffing-shaving dust and rice husk are added to the respective system. As a result, during the ignition process, these organic matters are easily volatilized and released, which causes the loss of weight of the blended bricks. It is noted that all organic matters are volatilized when bricks are burnt below 950 °C.

The highest weight loss was observed as 40.23% in the brick containing 25% leather waste. It should be noted that this value appears higher, indicating that additional factors such as minor mineral breakdown, moisture content, and volatiles may contribute to the total weight loss55. According to ASTM C62-17, the maximum weight loss for bricks should not exceed 15%. For clarity, the ASTM C62-17 standard applies to fired bricks, whereas some literature values mentioned here are included only for comparative reference. Weight loss has a favourable impact on the transportation costs, as these expenses are often determined by the weight of bricks. In addition, lighter bricks may be highly suitable for use in building constructions, which may lower the overall construction costs3,8. It was noticed that the application of a smaller percentage of waste yielded bricks with higher strength, whereas the shape and size of the resulting materials were observed to be decreased. Therefore, a balance between the extent of waste application and the resulting materials’ sizes and shapes can be maintained with careful design and development of new composite materials.

In the present study, two types of bricks were produced, which were fired and non-fired brick materials. Fired bricks were subjected to a high-temperature firing process in a laboratory furnace at 900 °C for three hours. This method enabled the bricks to undergo a series of chemical and physical changes, thereby enhancing their structural integrity and strength. The maximum compressive strength of fired bricks was found as 24.46 MPa, which was attained with 10% leather shaving and buffing dust incorporation. On the other hand, non-fired bricks were made from a mixture of clay, leather dust, ash, and river sand, and were cured by air drying and controlled hydration. The non-fired bricks demonstrated a slightly lower compressive strength of 19.22 MPa with the same percentage of leather dust application. Despite having lower compressive strength, non-fired bricks offer the advantage of being more energy-efficient and environmentally friendly, as they do not require the high energy consumption associated with firing. Additionally, non-fired bricks are more sustainable, as the curing process avoids emissions associated with burning. Both types of bricks exhibited favourable engineering properties, but the choice between fired and non-fired bricks depends on the specific need and requirements of the construction, with fired bricks offering higher strength, whereas non-fired bricks offer a more eco-friendly and cost-effective alternative.

Leaching analysis

The leaching ability values of Pb, Zn, Cu, Ni, and Cd were insignificant regarding their permissible limits (Table 6). On the other hand, the leaching ability value for Cr was 0.0072 solely for the clay brick. For 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% leather dust integrated bricks, leached Cr was measured at 0.070, 0.098, 0.16, 0.29, and 0.45 mg/L, respectively56. The sharp increase in Cr leaching observed between 10% and 15% leather dust content indicates a non-linear release behavior, which may be attributed to saturation of available immobilization sites within the clay–dust matrix or localized heterogeneity in chromium distribution at higher waste loadings. However, the overall concentration of chromium and other metals was observed to be below the corresponding permissible limits.

The leaching tests were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions in which crushed brick samples were exposed to an aqueous extraction solution at a fixed liquid-to-solid ratio and near-neutral to mildly acidic pH, following the relevant USEPA protocols. While the USEPA 1311 method is widely adopted for regulatory screening, its laboratory immersion conditions and limited exposure duration represent a simplified scenario compared to field environments. Among the two leaching tests performed, the USEPA 1311 method showed higher chromium (Cr) leaching from bricks containing 8% or more buffing dust57,58. However, when the buffing dust content was below 8%, the leached Cr level remained within the safe limits with bricks containing less than 6% buffing dust, which may be considered to pose a lower environmental risk under the specific laboratory leaching conditions applied in this study. It should be mentioned that leaching behavior may evolve over time and vary under different environmental conditions, such as changeable pH, wet–dry cycles, and prolonged water contact. The short-duration laboratory leaching tests in this study provide an initial assessment but cannot fully capture the cumulative metal release under prolonged-time conditions. It is noteworthy that the firing temperatures play a critical role, which directly influences the leaching ability of chromium. At elevated temperatures, metals from tannery waste can interact with organic substances to form stable complexes, which further reduce the metals’ leaching16. The sudden decrease in Ni leaching at higher leather dust content, particularly at 25%, may be associated with enhanced encapsulation of nickel within newly formed mineral or organic–inorganic complexes during firing. Additionally, during the brick-firing process, the formation of metal oxides might support the trapping of heavy metals within the composite matrix. Previous research has also indicated that at temperatures above 800 °C, CaCrO4 and MgCrO4 can form crystalline phases, with Cr(VI) reduced to the less toxic Cr(III) form59. The dominant CaCrO4 crystalline phase is water-insoluble, which significantly limits Cr leaching. Although variations exist among the different leaching methods, these findings suggest that buffing-dust-incorporated bricks are a promising, potentially viable alternative for construction applications, provided that waste incorporation levels are carefully optimized and environmental performance is verified through appropriate leaching assessments.

In another perspective, non-fired brick materials are often perceived as having a lower environmental footprint due to the absence of high-temperature processing. However, leaching assessments remain essential to ensure environmental safety, even if the manufacturing process does not involve kiln firing40. The observed chromium leaching trends are consistent with recent findings reported in Environmental Research, which showed that chromium-containing waste-derived construction materials exhibited increased leaching at higher waste loadings but remained below regulatory limits under optimized processing conditions60. However, manufacturers may additionally execute other types of exams, including chemical analysis of the raw materials or physical evaluations of the final product, to ensure that they meet standard safety requirements.

Implications of the study

This study demonstrates that leather shaving and buffing dust, which are waste products from leather industries, can be recycled and managed in a clean environment and solid waste management perspectives, where this waste can effectively be incorporated into the brick-making process to make new composite materials with significant physicochemical and engineering properties. These research findings have several important implications:

Environmental benefits: The research findings provide an eco-friendly and sustainable solution for mitigating the environmental pollution caused by leather waste disposal, which is often irresponsibly discharged without any prior treatment and thus poses severe environmental hazards, such as soil contamination and water pollution. Recycling and reuse of leather waste, when integrated into brick production, offer an eco-friendly and sustainable alternative that can reduce pollution, mitigate health risks, and promote environmentally benign solid waste management. Leaching test analysis data indicated that incorporation of leather waste into brick manufacturing processes does not result in hazardous chromium (Cr) contamination and thus makes the resulting bricks safe for the environment to use in construction industries. It’s still vital to make sure that the substances used in the formation of non-fired bricks are safe and non-poisonous. This new approach of leather waste management could be a key factor in reducing the ecological footprint of both the leather and construction industries.

Economic impact: The study suggests that leather waste can be used as a cost-effective raw material in brick production, which can reduce the need for traditional, more expensive materials such as clay. The utilization of leather waste, especially when it is available from local sources, can decrease the materials costs for brick manufacturers and make the production process more affordable. For tannery industries that generate large quantities of waste, this approach can support the creation of a circular economy, where sold waste is transformed into valuable materials. This approach could bring more profit to the leather industry while supporting the Sustainable Development Goals.

Energy efficiency: The exploration of non-fired bricks introduces the possibility of reducing energy consumption in brick production processes. Non-fired bricks, which are more environmentally friendly due to their lower production energy requirements, can provide significant support to mitigate the carbon footprint of the construction industries, especially in many developing countries like Bangladesh, where energy supply is limited and expensive.

Strength and durability in construction: The incorporation of leather waste into brick structures, especially in the fired version of bricks, has been shown to produce brick materials with significantly higher compressive strength (24.46–24.92 MPa). This ensures that the resulting bricks remain strong and durable for use in construction industries and meet all necessary industry standards for quality and performance. Additionally, the lightweight nature of these bricks, achieved by using leather waste, can result in more efficient construction, bringing benefits in terms of material handling, transportation, and overall building performance, particularly in seismic regions where the reduction of structural weights is crucial to handle and tolerate natural disasters.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that leather industry waste can be effectively transformed from an environmental liability into a high-value construction material by successfully incorporating it into brick manufacturing. Bricks were produced using leather waste at 5–25%, combined with ash, river sand, and clay, in both fired and unfired processes. The incorporation of leather waste resulted in significant weight reduction, with a minimum weight decrease of 17% and a maximum reduction of 40.23%, contributing to lighter construction materials. The highest compressive strength (24.46 MPa) was achieved with bricks containing 10% leather dust, ash, and river sand, mixed with Gazipur clay, satisfying both BDS and ASTM standards and confirming their suitability for structural applications.

Additionally, non-firing, compression-based production offers a low-energy, environmentally friendly alternative to traditional brick manufacturing. Thermal stability, favourable microstructural development, and negligible heavy-metal leaching further validate the safety and durability of the proposed materials. Thermal analysis, SEM-based microstructural observations, and physico-chemical evaluations confirmed good thermal stability, favourable matrix densification, and strong interfacial bonding within the composite bricks. Heavy-metal leaching tests indicated negligible environmental risk, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy showed no peaks in the 579–580 eV range, confirming the absence of Cr(VI) formation and the stability of Cr(III) during firing. By converting industrial residues into structurally reliable building components, this approach reduces pressure on natural clay resources, lowers carbon emissions associated with firing, and mitigates pollution risks. The integration of leather waste into brick manufacturing represents a turning point toward sustainable construction and responsible waste management. The outcomes of this study provide not only a verified research model solution but also a replicable model for industrial symbiosis. Overall, this research presents a validated and scalable model for industrial symbiosis between the leather and construction sectors, supporting sustainable construction practices and responsible waste management.

Limitation: The current study examined only a limited number of materials and processes at the laboratory scale. Further research should be done on a pilot scale before going to industrial-scale production. Determining the scalability and commercial viability of these materials will require evaluating their economic and life-cycle performance. It will also help us understand how economical and sustainable it is to produce them on an industrial scale.
