Gliomas with succinate dehydrogenase deficiency: A case series and brief review of the literature of oncometabolite-driven gliomagenesis
Authors: Madison T Gray, Romain Cayrol, Adriana May, John M Skaugen, Fadi Hayek, Sarah Lapointe, Cynthia E Hawkins, Thomas M Pearce, Drew Pratt, Danica Wiredja, Angus Toland, Daniel F Marker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOncometabolite production plays a key role in the development and progression of isocitrate dehydrogenase-mutant gliomas. The aberrant gain-of-function activity of the mutant isocitrate dehydrogenase protein results in the production of the oncometabolite D-2-hydroxyglutarate, which in turn promotes DNA hypermethylation and gliomagenesis through several mechanisms. Rare gliomas in patients with fumarate hydratase deficiency syndrome share many morphologic and molecular features with isocitrate dehydrogenase-mutant gliomas, with the oncometabolites succinate and 2-succinocysteine similarly thought to promote global DNA hypermethylation. Like isocitrate dehydrogenase and fumarate hydratase, succinate dehydrogenase is also a member of the citric acid cycle that additionally participates in oxidative phosphorylation. While succinate dehydrogenase deficiency has been implicated in familial tumor syndromes, it has not yet been associated with glial neoplasms. Here we report 3 cases of diffuse glioma with succinate dehydrogenase deficiency that show many similarities with isocitrate dehydrogenase-mutant gliomas. We propose that succinate dehydrogenase deficiency with accumulation of the oncometabolite succinate can promote gliomagenesis in a similar manner as seen in isocitrate dehydrogenase-mutant and fumarate hydratase-deficient gliomas, and we discuss the proposed mechanisms that may lead to tumor formation.

INTRODUCTION

The discovery of isocitrate dehydrogenase (IDH) mutations in a subset of adult glioblastomas was a pivotal moment in the molecular era of neuropathology.1 Subsequent work demonstrated that most histologic oligodendrogliomas and lower-grade diffuse astrocytomas carried mutations in IDH1 or IDH2, and these mutations have been used as disease-defining diagnostic criteria since 2016.2,3 Determining IDH1/2 mutational status is now a critical step in the evaluation of infiltrating gliomas, with the presence or absence of these alterations imparting significant prognostic and therapeutic implications.

More recently, rare gliomas with fumarate hydratase (FH) deficiency have been reported.4–8 These tumors appear to be closely related to IDH-mutant gliomas, showing significant molecular overlap with very similar DNA methylation array profiles. While data are limited, FH-deficient gliomas may also have a favorable prognosis when compared to IDH-wildtype diffuse gliomas.

Succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) is an enzyme complex that is closely related to IDH and FH and is another key component of the citric acid cycle. SDH deficiency has been associated with a number of familial tumor syndromes but has not previously been associated with glioma development. Here, we present 3 cases of diffuse glioma with SDH-loss; 1 was associated with a germline SDHB mutation; 1 was sporadic; and 1 had an unknown germline status. All tumors showed genomic alterations and DNA methylation profiles similar to those of IDH-mutant gliomas. Additional analysis of 2 of the cases demonstrated that these tumors show a glioma CpG island methylator phenotype (G-CIMP), like IDH-mutant gliomas. We propose that SDH deficiency can promote gliomagenesis in a manner similar to that of IDH1/2 and FH mutations.

RESULTS

Cases

Patient 1 is a 55-year-old man who presented with visual disturbances. Imaging studies revealed a heterogenous 4.9 × 4.6 × 2.9 cm mass with ring enhancement in the left temporal occipital region (Figure 1A). There was mild surrounding FLAIR abnormality, and the enhancing areas of the lesion showed moderate diffusion restriction. He had no personal neoplastic history, but his family history was significant for increased epithelial malignancies. The mass was resected, revealing a diffusely infiltrative, densely cellular glial neoplasm with high mitotic/proliferative activity, endothelial cell proliferation, and necrosis (Figure 1B and C). The tumor cells were small, ovoid, and monotonous throughout, with minimal cytoplasm. No microcalcifications or microcysts were noted. The tumor was variably immunopositive for GFAP and was immunonegative for Olig2. Initial molecular testing by a targeted brain tumor next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel revealed TERT promoter mutation (C250T), PDGFRA mutation p. C235Y, and 1p/19q codeletion (Figure 1E), as well as additional copy number losses. There was no gain of chromosome 7 or loss of chromosome 10, and the tumor was IDH1/2 and H3F3A wildtype. The tumor was positive for MGMT promoter methylation by allele-specific PCR. DNA methylation array profiling confirmed the MGMT promoter methylation and showed no matching classes by the Heidelberg 12.5 and 12.8 classifiers; the NCI/Bethesda classifiers v1.0 and v2.0 showed moderate to low-score superfamily matches to IDH-glioma (mean score: 0.591) with high class scores to astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, high-grade (mean score: 0.936). UMAP embedding placed the tumor in the astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, high-grade cluster (Figure 1E). Considering these findings, fumarate hydratase staining was performed and was preserved (data not shown). A diagnosis of “high-grade glioma, not elsewhere classified,” was issued. The patient was treated with standard temozolomide chemotherapy and external beam radiation therapy, and did well for 3 years, with only possible slow subclinical recurrence. During this time, a close family member was diagnosed with a pathogenic germline SDHB alteration on a genetic workup of an unrelated epithelial malignancy. Germline testing on our patient confirmed that he also carried this alteration, an early stop gain mutation in the SDHB gene. In light of this new information, his tumor was retested on a broader Oncomine Comprehensive Assay Plus panel, which confirmed the presence of the germline SDHB mutation at 94% allelic frequency in the tumor. SDHB immunohistochemistry confirmed loss of staining in the tumor with appropriate internal positive control staining (Figure 1D). A revised diagnosis of “high-grade glioma with SDHB deficiency, not elsewhere classified,” was issued. The patient continues to do well 3.5 years after diagnosis and has not required additional therapy.

Patient 2 is a previously healthy 51-year-old woman who presented with diplopia secondary to a sixth cranial nerve palsy. Imaging showed a 3 × 2 × 2 cm mass in the pineal region with solid contrast enhancement (Figure 2A). This was associated with aqueductal compression and triventricular hydrocephalus. Spinal imaging and CSF cytology were indicative of leptomeningeal spread. Surgical resection of the supratentorial lesion revealed a mitotically active (>13 mitoses/mm2) infiltrative glial neoplasm (Figure 2B and C), with likely microvascular proliferation and rare areas of necrosis. The tumor cells showed marked nuclear pleomorphism with variable cytoplasmic clearing in a fibrillary background. The tumor showed loss of expression of ATRX but was negative for IDH1 R132H, BRAF V600E, and H3 K27M mutations by immunohistochemistry. Expression of p53 protein was aberrantly increased. An initial targeted NGS panel found a TP53 mutation (p.R248G) but no IDH1/2 alterations. DNA methylation profiling classified the tumor within the methylation classes “diffuse glioma, IDH-mutant family” (DKFZ v12.8, score 0.973) and “astrocytoma, IDH-mutant; high grade” (NCI/Bethesda v2, score 0.981). Copy number analysis demonstrated multiple chromosomal abnormalities including MYC amplification but without CDKN2A/B loss, 1p/19q co-deletion, or obvious loss of segments containing IDH1 or IDH2 (Figure 2E). Consideration was made for a glioma with FH mutation, but FH immunostaining was retained and 2SC expression was not increased (data not shown). Broader NGS testing (Illumina Trusight RNA Pan-Cancer), however, showed a mutation in SDHA (p.R585W, VAF 43%), which has been previously observed in other SDH-related neoplasms. To determine the integrity of the SDH complex, we performed SDHB immunostaining which showed loss of expression in tumor cells with preserved weak internal positive control staining in macrophages (Figure 2D), suggesting a defect in the SDH holoenzyme. The final diagnosis of the tumor was “high-grade glioma with SDH deficiency, not elsewhere classified.”

Patient 3 is a 47-year-old man who initially presented 5 months prior to biopsy with diplopia. On examination, he was found to have restricted upward gaze. He later developed headaches and balance difficulties. Brain MRI initially showed obstructive hydrocephalus and a ventriculoperitoneal shunt was placed. Subsequent MR imaging demonstrated a diffusely infiltrating T2/FLAIR hyperintense lesion extending from the fourth ventricle involving the tectum, pineal region, bilateral thalami, and corpus callosum (Figure 3A). A focal region of contrast enhancement was identified in the fourth ventricle. Clinically, the presumed diagnosis was diffuse midline glioma. Of note, a pineal cyst had been identified on imaging ∼5 years prior to presentation. Approximately 1 week prior to biopsy, the patient presented with increasing confusion, memory loss, and right-sided weakness. Imaging again demonstrated hydrocephalus, now with midline shift, and a shunt revision was performed. The patient then developed seizures, requiring intubation. A biopsy of an infiltrating focus of tumor in the right frontal lobe was performed. The biopsy demonstrated a hypercellular proliferation of cells with irregular nuclear contours, hyperchromatic nuclei, and variable, eosinophilic cytoplasm (Figure 3B and C). Occasional multinucleated cells were also present. Most of the biopsy demonstrated relatively pure tumor although some fragments demonstrating infiltration of background brain parenchyma were identified. Many mitotic figures, including atypical forms, were scattered throughout. No foci of necrosis or microvascular proliferation were present on the sampled tissue. Immunohistochemical staining for Olig2 showed diffuse expression in the neoplastic cells. Staining for ATRX demonstrated loss of expression in the tumor cells. Staining for p53 showed strong, diffuse expression in the majority of tumor cells, consistent with mutant-pattern expression. Immunostains for IDH1 R132H and H3 K27M showed no expression and staining for H3 K27-trimethylation showed retained nuclear expression. Given the immunohistochemical profile, a non-canonical IDH1/2 mutation was suspected. NGS sequencing was performed, demonstrating SDHB p. D74V (VAF 73%), TP53 p. H179R (VAF 79.7%), and PTEN p. R130* (VAF 52.2%) mutations. Variants of uncertain clinical significance were also identified in KRAS and SMARCA4. No gene fusions were detected. Multiple copy number alterations were identified with gains/duplications of 2p, 3q, 19p, 19q, and 20q. DNA methylation profiling matched the tumor to the class “Astrocytoma, IDH-mutant, high-grade” with high confidence on the NCI/Bethesda classifier. Staining for SDHB demonstrated loss of expression in the tumor cells (Figure 3D). Given the molecular features, a diagnosis of “infiltrating glioma, SDHB-mutant, not elsewhere classified” was rendered. Due to tumor progression, the patient was discharged to hospice following the biopsy.

G-CIMP analysis

As further explored in the discussion, IDH-mutant gliomas frequently show a unique pattern of global DNA hypermethylation, referred to as the G-CIMP phenotype.9 As the proposed oncogenic mechanisms are similar between SDH-deficient and IDH-mutant tumors, we explored the possibility that SDH-deficient gliomas would share the G-CIMP phenotype.

Detailed methylation data were available for cases 1 and 2. Analysis of genome-wide DNA methylation data was performed in R using publicly available raw data files (IDAT) from the Genomic Data Commons Data Portal ([GDCP], https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). For index cases and GDCP samples, raw signal intensities from methylation array probes were normalized using the Sesame R package10 with Infinium I probe color channel inference (inferInfiniumIChannel), non-linear dye bias correction (dyeBiasNL), detection p-value calculation using ELBAR (P < .05), and background subtraction (NOOB). For EPICv2 arrays, replicate probes were collapsed to unique probe identifiers. Probes failing detection P-value thresholds or baseline masking criteria were masked and imputed. Samples were filtered based on probe masking proportion (>5%) and bisulfite conversion (GCT score ≥ 2.5). The following probes were removed: probes mapping to X or Y chromosomes, probes where the 30 bp 3′-subsequence is non-unique, probes masked for mapping reasons, probes masked for extension base inconsistent with specified color channel (type-I) or CpG (type-II) based on mapping, probes with a SNP in the extension base that causes a color channel switch from the official annotation, and probes where the 5 bp 3′-subsequence (including extension for type II) overlap with any of the SNPs with global mean allelic frequency >1% (http://zwdzwd.github.io/InfiniumAnnotation).

For clustering, only samples with an estimated purity ≥0.8 and a calibrated score ≥0.9 were retained within IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype glioblastoma methylation classes in the Heidelberg classification system. For the feature set, remaining probes were selected if they overlapped any of the previously reported G-CIMP-related genes (MethylLight marker loci), ie, ANKRD43 (12 probes), HFE (8 probes), MAL (16 probes), LGALS3 (16 probes), FAS (17 probes), RHOF (34 probes), DOCK5 (24 probes).9 Agglomerative hierarchical clustering of M values (log2(β/(1−β))) was performed on the reduced matrix using the Pearson distance metric and ward. D2 linkage method.

Figure 4 shows that both cases 1 and 2 cluster with IDH-mutant tumors within the G-CIMP probeset genes. Rare tumors with concurrent IDH and SDH mutations showed a similar methylation pattern. Tumors lacking either IDH or SDH mutations (ie, IDH-wildtype glioblastomas) did not show this phenotype and clustered separately, as expected.

Heat map of M-values for G-CIMP probeset genes for index cases 1 and 2 and GCDP comparator cases. The index cases (highlighted by an asterisk) cluster with the comparator IDH-mutant tumors, while the IDH-wildtype glioblastomas form a separate hierarchical group.

DISCUSSION

The IDH1 gene encodes a critical component of the cytoplasmic citric acid cycle, a homodimer enzyme that converts isocitrate to α-ketoglutarate, generating NADPH from NADP+ in the process. The IDH2 gene encodes the mitochondrial counterpart. While it was initially surprising that alterations in basic cellular metabolism could drive oncogenesis, the role that IDH mutations play in driving glioma biology has since been well studied. Essentially all IDH1 mutations in gliomas occur at the 132 locus, most commonly as a conversion of arginine to histidine (ie, p. R132H). Dang et al.11 demonstrated that this mutation results in aberrant gain-of-function activity by the mutant IDH enzyme, allowing for the reduction of α-ketoglutarate to D-2-hydroxyglutarate (D-2HG), a strong methylating agent and oncometabolite. D-2HG accumulation leads to global DNA and histone hypermethylation with associated global alteration in gene transcription and the subsequently characterized glioma-CpG island methylator phenotype.9 The aberrant reduction of α-ketoglutarate also depletes cellular NADPH, resulting in increased cellular oxidative stress and HIF1α activation.12,13 Finally, D-2HG inhibits 2-oxoglutarate-dendent oxygenases (2OG) and ten-eleven translocation (TET) enzymes, protein families that play important roles in DNA repair and demethylation, further promoting global DNA and histone hypermethylation.14–18

FH, also a component of the citric acid cycle, is responsible for the conversion of fumarate to malate. Familial FH mutations are associated with the autosomal dominant hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma (HLCRCC) syndrome. Recently, there have been rare reports of FH-deficient gliomas.7,8 These gliomas have DNA methylation array profiles mimicking IDH-mutant gliomas and may show prolonged survival when compared to IDH-wildtype gliomas. Oncogenesis in FH-deficient tumors (including the more recently described FH-deficient gliomas), is thought to progress in a similar manner to IDH-mutant gliomas, with buildup of the oncometabolites succinate and 2-succinocysteine (2-SC), inhibition of TET family enzymes, and subsequent DNA and histone hypermethylation.7,8 Unlike IDH1/2 mutations in gliomas, FH mutations are loss-of-function in nature and do not show aberrant gain-of-function activity; the conversion of fumarate to 2-SC occurs via alternative pathways.19

SDH is a mitochondrial enzyme complex comprised of 4 subunits, SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, and SDHD. This complex is responsible for the oxidation of succinate to fumarate in the citric acid cycle, intermediate between IDH and FH; it also acts as a proton/electron shuttle in oxidative phosphorylation. Pathogenic germline SDH mutations can be found in any of the subunits and these have been associated with a number of different tumor syndromes, including Carney-Stratakis syndrome, hereditary paraganglioma-pheochromocytoma syndrome, and familial SDH-deficient renal cell carcinoma syndrome. In the case of SDH-deficient neoplasms, the oncometabolite is succinate itself; its buildup acts in a similar manner to D-2HG and 2-SC as described above.20,21 Succinate accumulation has been shown to directly inhibit TET enzyme dependent demethylation in non-glial SDH-deficient tumors.22 Indeed, HLCRCC and SDH-deficient tumor syndromes share many common oncologic presentations, including renal cell carcinomas and paragangliomas. SDH-deficiency has not been previously reported as a driving alteration in gliomas.

Mutations in SDH subunits appear to be rare events in human glial tumors. Of the 911 genetically profiled samples in the glioblastoma and lower-grade glioma TCGA Pan-Cancer Atlas study data sets, only 6 tumors carried SDH mutations. Three had mutations in SDHA; 1 had mutation in SDHB; and 2 had mutations in SDHC. These mutations were found in a variety of glioma subtypes, including 2 IDH-wildtype glioblastoma, 2 IDH-mutant astrocytomas, and 1 oligodendroglioma. All mutations were hemizygous or subclonal, suggesting that these are not driver mutations. The status of the SDH enzyme complex was not assessed by immunohistochemistry in those cases. There is also some evidence that loss of SDH function may play a role in glioma progression, even when these alterations are not the primary driving event.23,24

The cases we present here, summarized in Table 1, suggest that in rare instances SDH-deficiency can drive glioma development by a similar mechanism as seen in IDH- and FH-mutant gliomas. Our first case shows a high-grade glial neoplasm with a classic loss-of-function germline mutation in SDHB, with a somatic “second hit” in the form of 1p deletion. The molecular profile otherwise is similar to that of oligodendrogliomas with 1p/19q codeletion and TERT promoter mutation. Despite the aggressive morphologic appearance of the tumor at resection, the patient has done well clinically. Cases 2 and 3 arose in unusual midline locations but otherwise showed molecular features similar to IDH-mutant astrocytoma, with apparent double hit TP53 mutations and ATRX loss by immunohistochemistry. In the second case, the SDHA mutation variant allelic frequency of 43% suggesting only a single allelic hit is interesting and raises the possibility of dominant negative behavior of this mutation or an undetected second hit. Nevertheless, the SDHB immunohistochemistry confirms loss of holoenzyme function. As has been previously described in FH-deficient gliomas, our cases showed DNA methylation array profiles matching those of IDH-gliomas despite the lack of IDH1/2 mutations. The 2 cases for which detailed methylation data were available also showed the G-CIMP phenotype first described in IDH-mutant gliomas.

In conclusion, the cases presented here expand the spectrum of gliomas driven by oncometabolite accumulation secondary to mutations in the citric acid cycle. They reinforce the important role that altered TET family enzyme function and global epigenetic alterations play in gliomagenesis. They also further demonstrate the importance and utility of DNA methylation array classification in identifying unusual and unique tumor entities that may require additional testing for definitive characterization.
