Dihydrobenzofuro[3,2-b]benzofuran: a light-driven molecular switch with fluorescence switching, bistability, and intrinsic chirality
Authors: Fathy Hassan, Nivedha Velmurugan, Jingyun Tan, Yuan-Qiu-Qiang Yi, Yury Torubaev, Síle Nic Chormaic, Akimitsu Narita, Christine Luscombe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Designing photochromic materials with thermally stable bistability, fluorescence switching, and structural responsiveness remains a key challenge in responsive materials. In this study, we introduce photochromic behavior in 4b,9b-dihydrobenzofuro[3,2-b]benzofuran derivatives (DHBs). Guided by bond dissociation energies, we demonstrate that DHB derivatives undergo a reversible, light-induced regioselective ring-opening reaction accompanied by significant changes in structural, electronic, and photophysical properties. Upon UV illumination at 285 nm, the non-conjugated, weakly emissive closed-ring DHB undergoes a regioselective ring-opening to yield a conjugated, hydroxy-functionalized isomer that exhibits strong fluorescence. The reverse ring-closure occurs under 325 nm light, with no observable thermal back reaction, establishing a thermally stable, bistable molecular switch. The rigid, fused structure of DHB possesses intrinsic chirality, as confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction revealing racemic twin crystals with nearly equal enantiopure domains. Enantiomeric separation by chiral HPLC enables the study of its stereochemical and chiroptical properties. Spectroscopic analyses, in both solution and thin films, together with DFT calculations, elucidate photoinduced fluorescence switching behavior, distinct HOMO–LUMO energy levels, and structural transformations between the two isomers. Notably, asymmetric substitution directs the site of bond cleavage, providing a handle to control photochemical reaction pathways. These results highlight the potential of DHB derivatives as a multifunctional photochromic scaffold and open new opportunities for the design of responsive materials with tunable optical and electronic properties.

Introduction

Photochromic materials that undergo reversible structural and property changes under light illumination have attracted significant attention due to their potential applications in sensing, actuation, and responsive materials.1,2 Such systems offer remote, non-disruptive control over molecular function, enabling precise tuning of optical and electronic properties.3–5 They also serve as versatile models for investigating light–matter interactions, excited-state dynamics, and structure–property relationships in organic functional materials.6–8 Their structural tunability enables the tailoring of optical responses through molecular design, while their reversibility provides a route to responsive and adaptive materials.9–11 Multifunctional photoswitches that integrate thermal bistability, fluorescence tunability, and chirality within a single molecular framework are highly attractive for advanced materials.12–16 In particular, operating exclusively via light-driven pathways with no thermal back-reaction is highly desirable to offer the precise control required for applications in optical data storage, logic gates, and molecular electronics.17 Although significant advances have been achieved in extending the lifetimes of metastable photoisomers through kinetic control and rational molecular design, intrinsic bistability remains rare. Fluorinated azobenzenes,18,19 heteroaryl azobenzenes,20,21 and hydrazone-based switches22,23 provide markedly enhanced thermal stabilization of the cis isomer, yet only a limited number of molecular scaffolds, such as diarylethenes,24–26 fulgides and fulgimides,27 and stilbenes,28 intrinsically embed thermodynamic bistability within their core molecular scaffold. Furthermore, combining thermal bistability with tunable fluorescence in photochromic systems remains challenging, as many systems exhibit weak or no emission due to excited-state relaxation through isomerization, leading to nonradiative decay pathways. As such, photoswitchable fluorescence is often achieved by incorporating additional fluorophores.29–32 Finally, introducing chirality further expands the potential to extend the utility of photochromic systems to chiral photonics,33–35 but also presents an additional design challenge, as it can compromise thermal stability36,37 and often requires specific substitution patterns,38–40 including binaphthyl-based motifs.13,41–43

A consequence of the aforementioned issues means that simultaneously integrating thermal bistability, fluorescence tunability, and chirality into single photochromic framework is difficult, typically requiring multistep syntheses.26,44,45 These challenges motivated us to explore alternative molecular scaffolds that could provide multiple photoresponsive functionalities within a structurally simple framework. We previously observed that 4b,9b-dihydrobenzofuro[3,2-b]benzofuran (DHB, Fig. 1a) undergoes ring-opening reactions in the presence of a Pd catalyst and hexafluoro-2-propanol (HFIP) to yield a conjugated 2-(2-benzofuranyl)-phenol (B–OH), suggesting the possibility of selective bond activation within this scaffold.46 Preliminary bond dissociation energy (BDE) analysis indicated that this system could provide access to reversible structural transformations with distinct energetic requirements for ring opening and ring closing, potentially enabling light-responsive yet thermally bistable switching behavior. Based on these observations, we explored the potential of the DHB scaffold for the development of multifunctional photochromic molecular switches.

Previous work on dialkoxydinaphthofuro[3,2-b]furan (DHN, Fig. 1a) demonstrated a photo–thermal cycle in which UV illumination produced a violet dimeric trans-quinonemethide photoproduct (quinone isomer of DHN) that reverted thermally to the parent compound.47,48 In our study, DHB undergoes a regioselective ring-opening under 285 nm illumination to yield a conjugated B–OH, rather than the quinone isomer of DHB (Fig. 1b). The reverse ring-closure from B–OH to DHB occurs exclusively under 325 nm light, with no detectable thermal back reaction, establishing light-exclusive bistability. The structural rigidity and fused architecture of the DHB scaffold give rise to intrinsic chirality, as demonstrated by single-crystal X-ray diffraction analysis revealing racemic twin crystals composed of two nearly equal enantiomeric domains. Enantiomeric separation by chiral HPLC enables the study of its stereochemical and chiroptical behavior under photoirradiation. Furthermore, asymmetric substitution provides control over the directionality of the ring-opening process, offering a molecular design principle for developing tunable and multifunctional responsive materials.

Results and discussion

Synthesis

DHB and its derivatives were synthesized via our recently developed method, based on a single step reaction with benzofurans and aryl iodides through a Heck-type pathway.46 The reaction proceeds under simple stirring of benzofurans with the corresponding hydroxyaryl iodide in the presence of a palladium catalyst. The synthetic details are provided in the SI (Synthesis and Scheme S1).

Photoresponsive behavior

To evaluate whether the DHB scaffold could support light-driven bistable switching, we first examined the BDEs of the benzofuran ring system using DFT calculations (Table S1). The relatively low BDE of the ring-opening C–O bond in DHB (12.1 kcal mol−1) suggested that this bond could undergo selective cleavage upon photoexcitation, enabling the forward ring-opening reaction. In contrast, the substantially higher BDE associated with the reverse ring-closing process in B–OH (54.7 kcal mol−1) suggested that the open-ring form would exhibit high thermal stability. To experimentally investigate this behavior, we next examined the photoresponse of DHB using UV-vis spectroscopy (Fig. 2a). The UV-vis absorption spectrum of DHB in 1,4-dioxane (Fig. S1) exhibits a main absorption band at 285 nm, with two shoulders at 290 nm and 220 nm. The band at 285 nm is attributed to π–π* transition of the fused dihydrobenzofurobenzofuran framework. The presence of a shoulder at 290 nm suggests overlapping contributions from closely spaced excited states of the fused heteroaromatic system.49,50

Irradiation of DHB at 365 or 325 nm results in no measurable spectral response, consistent with the limited overlap between these excitation wavelengths and the DHB absorption band (Fig. S2). In contrast, illumination at 285 nm induced a gradual increase in absorbance at 320 nm, which we attribute to the formation of the ring-opened B–OH (Fig. 2b). This new band reflects the extended π-conjugation introduced upon ring-opening and can be assigned to a lower-energy π–π* transition of the newly formed hydroxy-substituted conjugated structure. Such bathochromic behaviour upon photochemical ring-opening has been observed for chromene/benzopyran systems, where ring opening increases conjugation.51–53 The DHB solution changed from colorless to pale yellow owing to the formation of the conjugated B–OH (Fig. S3). To evaluate solvent effects, we measured the absorption spectrum of DHB in solvents of varying polarity (Fig. S4). In all cases, the spectra displayed the same characteristic absorption band at 285 nm with a shoulder at 290 nm, indicating that the electronic structure of DHB is largely insensitive to the solvent environment. We then examined the photoresponsive behavior in hexane and DMSO in comparison to 1,4-dioxane (Fig. S5). Upon illumination at 285 nm, a gradual increase in absorbance at 320 nm was observed in the two solvents due to the formation of the open-ring B–OH. Notably, the spectral response was more pronounced in polar solvents such as DMSO and moderately polar solvents such as 1,4-dioxane compared to nonpolar solvents such as hexane, reflecting stabilization of the polar hydroxy-substituted open-ring form in higher dielectric media. Because DMSO exhibits strong absorption below 250 nm and leads to partial spectral overlap, 1,4-dioxane was selected for spectroscopic characterization. The reverse ring-closure of B–OH to DHB was observed upon illumination at 325 nm (Fig. 2c). The absorption band at 320 nm gradually decreased with near-complete recovery of the initial spectrum. In contrast, irradiation at 365 nm, where B–OH exhibits minimal absorption, produced no measurable spectral changes (Fig. S6). These results indicate that the back reaction is triggered by excitation within the B–OH absorption band. The photoswitching behavior of DHB was evaluated over five cycles of alternating irradiation at 285 and 325 nm (Fig. 2d). While reversible spectral changes were observed, a gradual decrease in signal intensity indicates the presence of photofatigue under these conditions. Notably, improved stability under N2 during the forward reaction suggests that oxygen influences the subsequent reaction pathways of reactive intermediates generated during the switching process (see the proposed mechanism section). The photoreactions of DHB were further confirmed by 1H-NMR spectroscopy (Fig. 2e). In its initial state, DHB in 1,4-dioxane-d8 displayed four characteristic aromatic resonances at δ = 7.5, 7.2, 6.9, and 6.8 ppm, together with two singlet aliphatic protons at δ = 6.3 ppm (Fig. 2e(i)). Upon UV illumination at 285 nm, new resonances appeared at δ = 8.6 ppm (singlet, OH) and δ = 7.9, 7.5, 7.4, 7.2, 7.0 and 6.9 ppm, which are assigned to the aromatic protons of the open-ring B–OH (Fig. 2e(ii)). The B–OH product was isolated by flash chromatography and fully characterized (see SI, Fig. S7). Integration of the 1H-NMR spectra revealed that irradiation at 285 nm led to DHB : B–OH ratios of approximately 52 : 48 after 1 h (Fig. S8b). Continued irradiation resulted in further enrichment of B–OH, reaching a DHB : B–OH ratio of approximately 47 : 53 after 2 h (Fig. S8c). Time-dependent UV-vis absorption spectra further support this two-stage behavior (Fig. S9). Stage I is characterized by a rapid increase in absorbance during the initial irradiation period, whereas stage II shows a slower but continuous rise upon prolonged illumination. The conversion is initially rapid but becomes progressively slower as the DHB fraction is depleted and accumulated B–OH absorbs at the same wavelength. These results indicate that the observed near-equimolar composition is likely influenced by competing light absorption by the product, rather than a photostationary state being achieved.

B–OH exhibited excellent stability at room temperature when kept in a sealed cuvette and in the dark, with no detectable change in its absorption spectrum after two months (Fig. S10). To further assess the stability of the B–OH, we examined its thermal behavior. Heating the solution to 90 °C did not induce ring closure (Fig. S11). Thermal stability was further confirmed by 1H-NMR spectroscopy after keeping the sample at ambient conditions for two weeks and after heating it to 50, and 90 °C, none of which induced ring closure (Fig. S12). The results from 1H-NMR and UV-vis spectroscopy establish the thermally robust nature of the DHB ⇌ B–OH system. Thus, the B–OH persists indefinitely in the dark, and ring closure can only be driven photochemically. The photoisomerization quantum yields for the forward ring-opening (Φo) and reverse ring-closing (Φc) reactions were determined from the corresponding photochemical reaction rates under 285 and 325 nm irradiation, respectively (see SI for details, Fig. S13 and Table S2).20,54–56 The forward ring-opening reaction from DHB to B–OH exhibited an observed rate constant of (2.6 ± 0.4) × 10−3 s−1 with a Φo of (0.16 ± 0.03)%. The reverse ring-closing reaction from B–OH to DHB showed a rate constant of (2.4 ± 0.8) × 10−3 s−1 and a Φc of (0.19 ± 0.06)%. The lower quantum yields compared with conventional photochromic systems such as diarylethenes and azobenzenes are likely related to the bond-cleavage mechanism (see the proposed mechanism section).57,58

Proposed mechanism

We hypothesize that the ring-opening of DHB is initiated by cleavage of the intracyclic C–O bond upon UV excitation at 285 nm (Fig. 3a). This photodriven bond cleavage generates a radical pair intermediate (C˙ and O˙), which subsequently undergoes intramolecular hydrogen atom transfer (HAT)59 to yield the hydroxy-functionalized open-ring product B–OH. To rationalize the experimentally observed regioselective switching behavior, we analyzed the BDEs associated with the DHB ⇌ B–OH transformation using DFT calculations (Table S1). Although the BDE analysis does not directly describe the excited-state reaction pathway, the calculated bond strengths provide a useful qualitative ground-state framework for understanding the observed regioselectivity. As discussed above, the targeted C–O bond within the DHB scaffold exhibited the lowest BDE (12.1 kcal mol−1), significantly smaller than those of the other bonds analyzed (48.0, 63.2, 121.0, and 64.9 kcal mol−1). This energetically favorable bond cleavage is in agreement with the proposed mechanism and explains the experimentally observed regioselectivity, supporting a photochemical pathway in which C–O bond cleavage yields the stable B–OH. This intermediate can then undergo a HAT process that facilitates reformation of the intracyclic C–O bond, thereby regenerating the closed-ring DHB. From the BDEs analysis of key bonds in B–OH, the –CHC– π-bond exhibited the lowest homolytic BDE (54.7 kcal mol−1), substantially lower than those of the other bonds analyzed (127.3, 68.2, and 125.4 kcal mol−1), identifying it as the most labile linkage under photoexcitation and consistent with its role in initiating the ring-closing process. The relatively high BDE of 54.7 kcal mol−1 also accounts for the remarkable thermal stability of B–OH: while photoexcitation at 325 nm provides sufficient energy to access this cleavage pathway, thermal activation alone is insufficient to trigger the reaction. Consistent with this higher energetic requirement, the ring-closing photoreaction proceeds more slowly than the forward ring-opening reaction and requires substantially longer irradiation times (ca. threefold) to reach comparable conversion.

Because radical intermediates are expected to be sensitive to oxygen, we next examined the influence of the atmospheric environment on the photoresponsive behavior of DHB. During the forward ring-opening reaction at 285 nm, spectra recorded under aerobic conditions exhibit an apparent isosbestic point at 240 nm (Fig. 2b). In contrast, in deoxygenated solvent, this feature becomes less well-defined, accompanied by a gradual increase in absorbance in this region (Fig. S14a). These observations suggest that the forward reaction proceeds through additional transient species whose population is modulated by oxygen. 1H-NMR analysis reveals that irradiation at 285 nm under aerobic conditions generates multiple minor signals (Fig. S15), whereas under nitrogen or in deoxygenated solvent the reaction proceeds more cleanly with fewer detectable byproducts (Fig. S16 and 2e(ii)). This behavior supports the involvement of oxygen in competing pathways, likely through interception or quenching of radical intermediates. In contrast, the reverse ring-closing reaction at 325 nm exhibits the opposite trend. Under nitrogen, deviations from isosbestic behavior are observed (Fig. S14b), and additional NMR signals appear during irradiation (Fig. 2e(iii)), indicating the accumulation of intermediate species. Under aerobic conditions, however, the spectral changes are more consistent with a direct interconversion between B–OH and DHB, with fewer detectable side products. Notably, the additional signals formed under nitrogen disappear upon subsequent irradiation in air, suggesting that these species correspond to reactive intermediates that are further transformed in the presence of oxygen. These results indicate that the presence or absence of isosbestic points does not strictly reflect a simple two-component system, but rather the relative contribution of transient intermediates and side pathways. The atmosphere-dependent behavior supports the involvement of radical species whose lifetimes and reactivity are strongly influenced by oxygen.

The distinct behavior observed for the forward and reverse reactions further support the proposed mechanism that different types of radical intermediates are involved: a C˙/O˙ radical pair in the ring-opening process and carbon-centered radicals in the ring-closing reaction, each exhibiting different sensitivities toward molecular oxygen.60,61 The observed dependence on atmospheric conditions further suggests that radical species contribute to the photofatigue process. These results indicate that photofatigue originates from competing side reactions accompanying the photoisomerization process, which gradually lead to the formation of irreversible byproducts upon repeated irradiation cycles.

Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was performed to gain further insight into the formation of radical intermediates during photoirradiation. Upon cooling the DHB solution in toluene to 223 K, gradual enhancement of an organic-centered radical signal at g ≈ 2.005 was observed under irradiation at both 285 nm (forward ring-opening; Fig. 3b and S17a) and 325 nm (reverse ring-closing; Fig. 3c and S17b). The signal disappeared in the dark upon warming the sample to room temperature, indicating the formation of transient photogenerated radicals with limited lifetime under ambient conditions. To further explore radical involvement, 2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine-1-oxyl (TEMPO) was introduced as a radical scavenger (1 : 1 molar ratio relative to DHB) prior to irradiation. In the presence of TEMPO, the extent of both forward and reverse photoreactions decreased to approximately 25–30% of that observed in its absence (Fig. S18 and S19), consistent with interception of radical intermediates. Additionally, new resonances at δ = 10.3 and 5.7 ppm were observed in the 1H-NMR spectrum, tentatively assigned to a DHB–TEMPO adduct. Further support for radical intermediacy was obtained by LC-MS analysis. Upon irradiation at 285 nm in the presence of TEMPO, a species corresponding to C23H30NO3 [M + H]+ was detected, consistent with formation of a TEMPO–DHB adduct (Fig. 3d). Irradiation of B–OH at 325 nm produced analogous mass spectral features, indicating regeneration of the same trapped species during the reverse process (Fig. 3e). These results provide analytical evidence supporting the proposed radical-mediated forward and reverse photoreactions.

Structural transformation

The photoisomerization of DHB to B–OH is accompanied by a pronounced structural transformation, as revealed by crystallographic and computational analyses (Fig. 4a). In the closed-ring DHB, the dihydrobenzofurobenzofuran structure adopts a bent geometry with an internal angle of 117° (Fig. S20). It should be noted that the closed-ring DHB adopts a single geometrical configuration in the solid state. The rigid fused bicyclic framework precludes the rotational freedom necessary to access an alternative trans-like arrangement, thereby restricting the molecule to a single topology-defined structure. Upon photoinduced ring opening, B–OH becomes essentially planar. This change in geometry is also reflected in the molecular dimensions: the molecular maximum length increases from 9.10 Å in DHB to 10.64 Å in B–OH (Fig. S21).

Alongside geometric changes, the two isomers display different polarity and electronic character. Thin-layer chromatography revealed distinct Rf values (0.56 for DHB vs. 0.34 for B–OH, using hexane/ethyl acetate (90 : 10, v/v) as the eluent), in agreement with increased polarity of the open-ring B–OH (Fig. S22). DFT geometry optimization shows that DHB is nearly nonpolar, with a calculated dipole moment (µ) close to zero, whereas B–OH exhibits a significant dipole moment (µ ≈ 2.7 D) arising from its asymmetric, hydroxy-substituted conjugated framework. Thus, photoisomerization converts a nonpolar closed-ring isomer into a polar open-ring isomer accompanied by substantial reorganization of the π-electronic structure.

To understand the origin of the accompanying spectral changes, TD-DFT calculations were performed on the optimized geometries (Fig. 4b). For DHB, the lowest excited state is essentially dipole-forbidden (f ≈ 0), and the experimentally observed UV absorption mainly arises from allowed transitions to higher-lying excited states, rather than the S1 state (Table S3 and Fig. S23). In contrast, B–OH exhibits an intense S0 → S1 transition at 321 nm with a large oscillator strength (f = 0.96), predominantly characterized by the HOMO → LUMO configuration (Table S4 and Fig. S24). The calculated excitation energies are in good agreement with the experimental UV-vis spectra, reproducing the emergence of the lower-energy absorption band after photoisomerization. These results demonstrate that the structural transformation substantially reorganizes the electronic excited states and generates an intense low-energy allowed transition in the open-ring isomer. The associated light-induced changes in the electronic structure, molecular polarity, and optical response may provide opportunities for future applications in signal amplification, selective recognition, and controlled self-assembly.62

Photoinduced fluorescence switching

The DHB ⇌ B–OH system exhibits OFF/ON fluorescence switching upon alternating light illumination (Fig. 4c). Emission spectra in Fig. 4c were recorded using an excitation wavelength of 285 nm. Spectra recorded at additional excitation wavelengths (325 and 315 nm) produced nearly identical emission maxima (Fig. S25), confirming that the emission profile is independent of excitation wavelength. Minor variations in spectral shape arise from the measurement window (shifted by +10 nm at the start of acquisition). In its closed-ring form, DHB exhibits weak emission with an emission maximum centered at ∼350 nm with a photoluminescence quantum yield (PLQY) of 0.09. Upon irradiation at 285 nm, ring opening to B–OH leads to a pronounced enhancement of fluorescence with a PLQY of 0.21 (Fig. 4d).

Subsequent irradiation at 325 nm regenerates DHB, resulting in near-complete recovery of the initial emission intensity. The fluorescence turn-ON behavior directly reflects the photochromic nature of DHB and its interconversion between non-conjugated and conjugated states. As highlighted in the introduction, many photochromic systems suffer from weak or non-emissive behavior due to excited-state relaxation through isomerization. By contrast, the DHB ⇌ B–OH pair not only integrates thermal bistability but also provides a turn-ON fluorescence output. Changes in fluorescence intensity in solution were studied over five alternating irradiation cycles under ambient conditions. A gradual decrease in signal intensity upon repeated switching indicates the presence of photofatigue, similar to that observed for the absorbance changes (Fig. S26). We further investigated this fluorescence switching in the solid state. Thin films prepared by spin-coating showed uniform morphology, suitable for optical measurements. Both absorption and fluorescence spectra in the film state closely mirrored those observed in solution, confirming that the switching behavior is preserved upon film formation (Fig. S27a and b). The changes in absorbance and fluorescence intensity in the film state were also monitored over five alternating irradiation cycles (Fig. S27c and d). Light illumination had no detectable effect on DHB crystals (Fig. S28). Compared to DHB thin films, this contrast can be rationalized by differences in molecular packing and structural order. In the crystalline state, molecules are locked within a rigid lattice that restricts the conformational changes necessary for photoisomerization, while the bulk nature of the crystal further limits light penetration to only the surface layers. In thin films, looser packing, a higher surface-to-volume ratio, and structural disorder at grain boundaries enable efficient light–matter interactions and measurable photoactivity. These findings highlight the critical role of solid-state organization in controlling the photoisomerization behavior and suggest thin-film processing as an effective approach for exploiting DHBs in optical and optoelectronic applications.

Intrinsic chirality

Single-crystal XRD showed that DHB crystallized from the (R,R)/(S,S) racemic mixture adopts the homochiral space group P212121, yet the crystals are racemic twins composed of two enantiopure domains in an approximately 50 : 50 ratio (Fig. 5a). To validate this at the bulk level, chiral HPLC screening (SI) identified efficient separation on a CHIRALPAK IG column using MeCN at 25 °C (flow rate 1 mL min−1), giving two peaks at 3.7 min (49.41%) and 4.2 min (50.02%) (Fig. 5b). Preparative-scale chiral HPLC using MeCN/MeOH (7 : 3 v/v) as the eluent at 25 °C and a flow rate of 2 mL min−1 enabled the isolation of each enantiomer in enantiopure form, >99% enantiomeric excess (Fig. S29). Notably, enantiopure DHB obtained by preparative chiral HPLC reproducibly crystallized as a distinct enantiopure polymorph in the chiral (non-centrosymmetric) space group C2, indicating enantiomeric-composition-dependent polymorphism: crystals grown from the racemate and the isolated enantiomer solutions adopt different chiral space groups and distinct inter-stack packing arrangements, whereas the primary translational 1D stacking motif remains conserved within each enantiomorphic domain (Fig. S30). Lattice-energy calculations (CE-B3LYP/def2-SVP) show that all three polymorphs lie within a narrow lattice-energy range, differing by only a few kJ mol−1, as commonly observed in polymorphic series. The C2 stacked polymorph ((R,R)-DHB) has Elat = −103 kJ mol−1, very close to P212121 stacked form (CCDC 2533871, −104 kJ mol−1; Δ ≈ 1 kJ mol−1). The zig–zag packed P212121 polymorph (ESEPUQ)63,64 is noticeably lower in energy (−108 kJ mol−1), ∼4–5 kJ mol−1 below the stack-based forms, supporting its assignment as the thermodynamic polymorph, whereas the two stacked structures are kinetically favoured. Optical rotation measurements confirmed the opposite chiralities of the two fractions, with specific rotations of +239° and −235° for the first and second fractions, respectively (Table S5). The nearly equal and opposite magnitudes of the specific rotations are in accordance with the enantiomeric nature of the two compounds, supporting their assignment as mirror-image stereoisomers.

Chiroptical properties

The chiroptical properties of the enantiomerically separated DHB were investigated to elucidate the light-induced modulation of its optical activity. Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of the two fractions obtained by chiral HPLC (Fig. 5c) exhibited perfect mirror-image, confirming their enantiomeric nature. The first-eluted fraction ((R,R)-DHB) displayed a strong positive cotton effect centered at 290 nm (CD ≈ +175 mdeg), accompanied by two negative cotton effects at 270 nm (CD ≈ −200 mdeg) and 230 nm (CD ≈ −185 mdeg). These spectral features closely correlate with the electronic transitions observed in its UV-vis absorption spectrum. The positive cotton effect centered at 290 nm corresponds to the lowest-energy π–π* transition of the fused dihydrobenzofurobenzofuran chromophore. The negative bands around 270 nm and 230 nm arise from higher-energy electronic transitions, which may include contributions from both aromatic π–π and possible n–π* excitations, given the presence of oxygen atoms and the electronic properties of the dihydrobenzofurobenzofuran framework. The second-eluted fraction ((S,S)-DHB) exhibited a mirror-image spectrum of equal intensity with opposite signs, in agreement with the presence of two optically pure enantiomers of DHB. The chiroptical properties was studied for the first-eluted enantiomer ((R,R)-DHB) under alternating UV irradiation. Upon illumination at 285 nm, the CD signal intensities decreased in agreement with the photoinduced ring-opening to B–OH. Specifically, the positive cotton effect at 294 nm decreased from +150 mdeg to +55 mdeg, while the negative bands at 260 nm and 230 nm decreased from −185 mdeg to −80 mdeg and from −140 mdeg to −45 mdeg, respectively. Subsequent irradiation at 325 nm partially restored the initial CD profile, with intensities recovering to +105 mdeg, −120 mdeg, and −95 mdeg, respectively.

Photo induced changes in circular dichroism were then monitored over ten irradiation cycles, revealing that the initially optically pure (R,R)-DHB gradually racemizes to an approximately 1 : 1 enantiomeric mixture by cycle 5 (Fig. S31). During the early switching cycles, a substantial excess of the original (R,R) enantiomer is retained, allowing photoinduced chiroptical modulation to be observed over the first three cycles. This partial recovery of the CD signal upon alternating irradiation arises because 285 nm light converts chiral (R,R)-DHB into the achiral ring-opened B–OH, whereas subsequent 325 nm irradiation induces ring-closing that regenerates both (R,R)- and (S,S)-DHB. Since racemization proceeds progressively rather than instantaneously, the residual enantiomeric excess in the early cycles accounts for the observed partial restoration of CD intensity after each ring-closing event. The large specific rotation values (>±200°) reflect substantial intrinsic chiral asymmetry within the DHB scaffold, suggesting its potential utility in chirality transfer, for example as a chiral dopant. The progressive, light-induced attenuation of optical activity from a highly enantiomerically enriched state represents a form of photoinduced chiral erasure. Such applications include photoerasable chiral encoding, anti-counterfeiting patterns, and related photonic applications that exploit controlled loss of chiral information.65–71

Asymmetric substitutions and regioselectivity

To gain deeper insight into the photoisomerization behavior of asymmetric DHB derivatives, we investigated a series of compounds with different substitutions on the aryl rings (Fig. 6a). The synthetic route followed the same strategy as that for DHB, employing substituted benzofurans and aryl iodides (see SI, Methods and Scheme S1).46 UV-vis spectroscopy of the derivatives revealed systematic substituent-dependent shifts in absorption (Fig. S32), highlighting how electronic effects influence the photoisomerization (see SI for details). In these derivatives, the ring-opening reaction can proceed through cleavage of either bond I or bond II (Fig. 6b and Scheme S2). For NMR analysis, DMSO-d6 were employed, providing clear resolution of the hydroxy protons in the 10–11 ppm region (10.49 ppm for B–OH), distinctly separated from the aromatic signals. Upon illumination at 285 nm, compound 1a (R1 = OMe) produced two distinct hydroxyl isomers (1a–OHI and 1a–OHII), corresponding to cleavage of both bonds (Fig. 6b–d and S33 for the methoxy group). Subsequent illumination at 325 nm regenerated the closed-ring structure. In contrast, compound 1b (R2 = OMe) underwent regioselective bond I cleavage to give a single OH isomer (Fig. 6b–d and S34). Compound 1c (R2 = CHO) underwent bond II cleavage yielded the bond II isomer as the major product (∼91%), with the bond I isomer observed at ∼9% (Fig. 6b–d and S35). Compound 1d (R4 = NO2) underwent selective bond I cleavage (Fig. S36). Compounds 1e (R2 = F) produced two distinct hydroxyl isomers (1e–OHI and 1e–OHII), corresponding to cleavage of both bonds (Fig. S37), whereas 1f (R2,3 = OMe) underwent selective bond I cleavage, to afford a single OH isomer (Fig. S38). To interpret these observations, we calculated BDEs for bond I and bond II using DFT and the results, together with the corresponding experimental OH chemical shifts from 1H-NMR, are summarized in Table S6, Fig. 6b–d and Scheme S2. For DHB, both bonds exhibit identical BDEs (12.1 kcal mol−1) due to molecular symmetry, consistent with formation of a single open-ring product (B–OH, δOH = 10.49 ppm). For 1a, both bonds displayed closely similar BDEs (10.3 and 11.9 kcal mol−1), in agreement with the formation of two open-ring isomers 1a–OHI (δOH = 10.64 ppm) and 1a–OHII (δOH = 10.48 ppm). In contrast, 1b (R2 = OMe) displays a lower BDE for bond I (6.3 vs. 12.4 kcal mol−1), correlating with selective bond I cleavage (δOH = 10.58 ppm). 1c (R2CHO) favors bond II cleavage (12.1 vs. 14.4 kcal mol−1, δOH = 10.29 ppm), while 1d (R4NO2) favors bond I cleavage (11.7 vs. 15.8 kcal mol−1, δOH = 10.38 ppm). For 1e (R2F), the small difference in the calculated BDEs for bond I and bond II (10.2 vs. 11.6 kcal mol−1) is in agreement with the formation of two open-ring isomers, 1e–OHI (δOH = 10.47 ppm) and 1e–OHII (δOH = 10.46 ppm).

Compound 1b (R2 = F) yielded only the bond I isomer, while 1c (R2 = CHO) yielded the bond II isomer as the major product (∼91%), with the bond I isomer observed at ∼9%. In contrast, for 1f (R2,3 = OMe), bond I cleavage is strongly favored, with BDEs of 6.2 and 10.7 kcal mol−1, in agreement with the observed OH chemical shift of 10.62 ppm. The observed regioselectivity can be explained by a general set of principles. (i) Substituents at the para positions (R2,R4) provide strong control because they interact with the aromatic π system by resonance, thereby stabilizing or destabilizing the radical generated upon C–O bond homolysis. (ii) Substituents at the meta positions (R1,R3) have weaker effects, limited to inductive or steric influences, and rarely bias regioselectivity strongly. (iii) Electron-donating groups (e.g. OMe) at para stabilize the adjacent radical through resonance donation, lowering the BDE and enforcing cleavage on that side. (iv) Electron-withdrawing groups (e.g. CHO, NO2) generally destabilize radicals at para by withdrawing electron density, increasing the BDE and promoting cleavage to the opposite bond. (v) Halogens (e.g., F) exhibit mixed inductive and resonance effects, resulting in only a modest influence on regioselectivity.

This framework explains the trends observed theoretically and experimentally. The meta-OMe group in 1a exerts little electronic bias, leading to cleavage at both bonds. Para substitution, OMe at R2 (1b) stabilizes the bond I radical by resonance, lowering its BDE and enforcing exclusive bond I cleavage. CHO at R2 (1c) and NO2 at R4 (1d) destabilize the radical formed upon bond cleavage on their respective sides, thereby shifting the reaction toward the opposite site, favoring bond II cleavage for 1c and bond I cleavage for 1d. F at R2 (1e) does not strongly bias the cleavage pathway, leading to cleavage at both bonds owing to its mixed electronic character. The 2,3-dimethoxy substitution in 1f enforces exclusive bond I cleavage, consistent with the strong donating effect of the para OMe (R2) relative to the weaker meta OMe (R3).

These results establish that para substituents dominate regioselectivity through resonance and inductive stabilization of radical intermediates, whereas meta substituents exert only minor influences. Electron-donating groups at the para position promote bond cleavage on their own side, whereas electron-withdrawing groups generally disfavor local bond cleavage and instead bias the reaction toward the opposite bond. In contrast, halogens such as fluorine have only a weak net directing effect because of their competing inductive and resonance contributions. This substituent-dependent control of regioselectivity provides a clear design principle for tuning the switching behavior of DHB derivatives. BDE values serve as a predictive tool for regioselectivity, with lower BDE correlating directly with the experimentally observed ring-opening pathway. These findings establish that directional control over the ring-opening pathway can be achieved through asymmetric substitution, representing a rare example of chemoselective switching in photochromic systems. While most photochromic materials exhibit symmetric pathways, the DHB derivatives demonstrate the design principle of electron-donating or electron-withdrawing groups at the para positions enables precise control over whether bond I or bond II undergoes cleavage. This strategy not only expands the fundamental understanding of photoisomerization pathways but also offers a powerful tool for engineering smart light-responsive materials with tailored optical, electronic, and chiral properties by molecular design.

Conclusions

In summary, we have demonstrated photochromic behavior in DHB derivatives characterized by light-exclusive bistability, which combines switching between non-conjugated and conjugated states, turn-ON fluorescence, chirality, and structural tunability within a single molecular framework. This light-driven transformation simultaneously alters molecular geometry and electronic distribution, thereby coupling photophysical and electronic responses within a single switching event. Asymmetric substitution enables directional control over bond cleavage during the photoisomerization process, representing a rare example of chemoselectivity in photochromic materials. In this context, the ability to direct C–O bond cleavage regioselectively within the fused DHB framework creates opportunities for multistate photoresponsive systems, in which different ring-opening pathways could generate distinct metastable states with distinguishable optical or electronic properties. Guided by bond dissociation energy analysis and selective bond activation, this work provides insight into substituent-controlled photochromic switching in DHB derivatives and highlights their potential for the future development of multifunctional photochromic systems. Although ring-opening and ring-closing processes are triggered by UV irradiation at 285 and 325 nm, respectively, limiting the direct use of the current system in biological or deep tissue-penetrating environments, UV-responsive photoswitches remain highly relevant for non-biological photonic and materials applications.40,72–78 Given the relatively low photoisomerization quantum yields, future studies will focus on chromophore-based structural design, including π-extension via functionalization at the 2,7- or 3,8-positions and incorporation into polymeric materials, to enhance spectral separation between DHB and B–OH, improve photoconversion to the ring-opened form, and red-shift the ring-closing process. Such approaches have proven effective in transforming classical UV-responsive photoswitches, including azobenzene and diarylethene derivatives, into systems addressable with visible or near-infrared light.79–82 As part of these efforts, our ongoing studies focus on incorporating DHB units into polymer backbones to explore potential applications in responsive materials and photonic systems.
