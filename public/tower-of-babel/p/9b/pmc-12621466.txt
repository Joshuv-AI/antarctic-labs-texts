Duodenocolic fistula after laparoscopic ileocecal resection for ascending colon cancer: A case report
Authors: Noriyuki Sasaki, Shingo Mitomo, Yusuke Matsui, Yugo Ishii, Akira Sasaki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDuodenoileal and duodenocolic fistulas are rare complications often associated with inflammatory bowel disease, cancer, or peptic ulcers. Fistulas that form between the duodenum and a gastrointestinal anastomosis site following colorectal cancer surgery are extremely rare. Here, we report a rare case of duodenocolic fistula following laparoscopic ileocecal resection for ascending colon cancer.Case presentationA 70-year-old man, who underwent laparoscopic ileocecal resection for ascending colon cancer, exhibited no intraoperative injury to the duodenum. On postoperative day (POD) 31, the patient presented with fatigue and increased inflammatory markers. Computed tomography and upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a fistula between the duodenum and the ileocolic anastomosis. Conservative management, including bowel rest, parenteral nutrition, antibiotics, and proton pump inhibitors, resulted in the complete closure of the fistula by POD 70, and the patient was discharged on POD 73.DiscussionTo our knowledge, this is only the second reported case of duodenoileal or duodenocolic fistulas following right-sided colon cancer surgery, and the first to occur during the early postoperative period. We hypothesize that subclinical anastomotic leakage, chronic inflammation, and mechanical contact from the surgical stapler may have contributed to fistula formation. Its small size and early detection likely facilitated the successful conservative outcome.ConclusionAlthough exceedingly rare, duodenocolic fistula may occur following right-sided colectomy. Early identification and appropriate conservative management may result in a favorable outcome without the need for invasive surgical intervention.Keywords: Duodenocolic fistula, Colon cancer, Laparoscopic surgery

Introduction

Duodenoileal and duodenocolic fistulas are secondary complications of inflammatory bowel diseases (such as Crohn's disease), malignant tumors, and peptic ulcer disease [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. However, fistula formation between the duodenum and a gastrointestinal anastomosis site following colorectal cancer surgery has been demonstrated only rarely. This report described the case of a patient who developed such a fistula formation that developed following laparoscopic ileocecal resection for ascending colon cancer. This case was reported based on Surgical Case Report guidelines [5].

Case presentation

A 70-year-old man was admitted to our hospital with a diagnosis of ascending colon cancer. Preoperative colonoscopy revealed a type 0-IIc lesion in the proximal ascending colon, with suspected submucosal invasion. Histopathological examination of the biopsy specimen confirmed a well-differentiated adenocarcinoma. Preoperative computed tomography (CT) indicated no evidence of liver or lung metastases. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed ectopic gastric mucosa in the duodenal bulb; however, no other significant lesions were identified.

The patient underwent laparoscopic ileocecal resection with lymphadenectomy. No intraoperative injury to the duodenum was observed (Fig. 1). A functional end-to-end anastomosis was created extracorporeally using a linear stapler to anastomose the ascending colon and ileum. The stapler intersections were reinforced with absorbable sutures.

Intraoperative findings. No apparent injury to the duodenum was observed (arrow).

On postoperative day (POD) 3, laboratory tests showed an elevated white blood cell count (12,100/μL) and C-reactive protein (CRP) level, (19.11 mg/dL), although, the patient remained asymptomatic, with no fever or abdominal pain. The patient was treated with fasting, intravenous fluids, and antibiotics. Oral intake was resumed on POD 8 after near normalization of both WBC and CRP levels, and the patient was discharged on POD 11.

Histopathological examination of the resected specimen confirmed well-differentiated adenocarcinoma, which was classified as T1bN0M0 (Stage I) according to the TNM Classification of Malignant Tumors, 8th Edition.

On POD 31, the scheduled date for the first follow-up after discharge, the patient presented with fatigue. Laboratory tests revealed an elevated white blood cell count (17,400/μL) and CRP level (3.1 mg/dL). CT demonstrated a fistulous connection between the duodenum and the ileocolic anastomosis (Fig. 2A). Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy indicated reflux of intestinal fluid into the duodenum and a fistula in the duodenal bulb (Fig. 2B). Contrast-enhanced fluoroscopy confirmed a fistula between the duodenum and the ascending colon (Fig. 2C).

The patient was readmitted and treated conservatively with included bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, intravenous antibiotics, and proton pump inhibitors. Intravenous piperacillin/tazobactam (4.5 g every 8 h) was administered for 7 days based on the patient's clinical course and absence of positive culture findings. During total parenteral nutrition (TPN) administration, daily monitoring of electrolytes and blood glucose, as well as weekly liver function tests and C-reactive protein (CRP) measurements, were performed. No catheter-related bloodstream infection, electrolyte imbalance, hepatotoxicity, or metabolic complications such as refeeding syndrome were observed. On POD 52, follow-up upper gastrointestinal endoscopy revealed a reduction in the size of the fistula (Fig. 3A, B), and contrast-enhanced fluoroscopy showed no evidence of leakage between the duodenum and the ileum (Fig. 3C). Oral intake was gradually reintroduced. On POD 70 confirmed complete closure of the fistula (Fig. 4), and the patient was discharged on POD 73.

Endoscopy on postoperative day 70. Complete closure of the duodenocolic fistula secondary to fibrotic scarring was confirmed.

Two years postoperatively, there was no evidence of recurrence of the duodenocolic fistula.

Discussion

Duodenoileal or duodenocolic fistula formation following curative surgery for colorectal cancer is extremely rare. To the best of our knowledge, only one such case has been previously reported. You et al. described a patient who developed a duodenoileal fistula four years after undergoing laparoscopic right hemicolectomy for advanced ascending colorectal cancer [6]. In the case an 8-mm fistulous tract was observed between the duodenal bulb and the ileum, located 15 cm proximal to the ileocolic anastomosis. The patient underwent surgical repair, and fistula formation was attributed to a chronic local infection over a prolonged period.

In contrast, our patient developed a duodenocolic fistula in the early postoperative period. CT imaging revealed erosion of the linear stapler into the duodenum, accompanied by increased perianastomotic fat density. Although no intraoperative injury to the duodenum was observed, we hypothesize that a combination of minor anastomotic leakage, chronic inflammation, and direct contact or pressure from the stapling device contributed to fistula formation.

The patient was managed conservatively with bowel rest, total parenteral nutrition, proton pump inhibitors, and antibiotics. This approach was selected due to the absence of peritoneal signs or systemic instability. The small size of the fistula and the short interval between surgery and diagnosis likely facilitated spontaneous closure without surgical intervention. In previous reports, treatment strategies options for duodenoileal or duodenocolic fistulas have included endoscopic closure with clips and surgical repair [4,7,8]. However, surgical intervention may require resection of the anastomosis, re-anastomosis, or duodenal repair, all of which are invasive. In oncology cases, particularly when recurrence is suspected, through preoperative evaluation is essential to exclude residual or recurrent disease.

A limitation of this report is the lack of histopathological confirmation of the fistula, which might have provided additional insights into the underlying etiology. The accumulation of additional cases with similar features is warranted guide the development of standardized treatment strategies for this rare, but potentially serious complication.

Conclusion

Although extremely rare, duodenoileal or duodenocolic fistula may occur after right-sided colectomy. This case highlights the possibility of early postoperative fistula formation, potentially driven by combined mechanical and inflammatory mechanisms and demonstrates that timely conservative management may be an effective treatment option in carefully selected patients.
