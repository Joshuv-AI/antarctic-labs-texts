Maternal nutrition practices and its implications for child growth and development in Jimma Town a community based cross sectional study
Authors: Buzuneh Tasfa Marine, Yematawork Alemu Haile, Mihiret Genene Zewde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMaternal nutrition is crucial for optimal child growth and development, reducing pregnancy complications like low birth weight and premature birth. Adequate nutrition during pregnancy and breastfeeding helps prevent nutrient deficiencies with long-lasting effects. However, poor maternal nutrition can lead to adverse child growth outcomes, such as intrauterine growth restriction and developmental issues. The main objective of this study was to investigate the factors influencing maternal nutrition practices and its implication for child growth and development in the communities of Jimma Town. A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from April to June 2024 among 423 mothers. Face-to-face interviews were conducted by trained data collectors to gather detailed information on socio-demographic characteristics, maternal health, nutrition, and child growth and development. The multivariate analysis was conducted to control the confounding influence using variables that were eligible for multivariable logistic regression analysis and had a p-value of less than 0.25. In the multivariable logistic regression, factors with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant predictors. This study of 423 mothers with a 100% response rate identified key factors affecting child growth. Education was significant, with adjusted odds ratios (AOR) of 4.02 for primary, 5.44 for secondary, and 2.95 for college education. Casual laborers had a lower AOR of 0.19, while marital status (AOR = 1.14) and income levels between 6,001 and 10,000 (AOR = 2.11) positively impacted outcomes. Important health indicators included no pregnancy support (AOR = 0.27), no antenatal care (AOR = 0.30), and a fetal heartbeat (AOR = 4.02). Nutritional practices such as not consuming calcium-rich foods (AOR = 0.43) and adequate breastfeeding (AOR = 6.10) were linked to better development. Children with a normal BMI (AOR = 2.62) and appropriate birth weight (AOR = 1.16) showed improved growth outcomes, all at a 5% significance level. The analysis reveals that being educated mothers, having husbands employed in NGOs or in private business, being married mothers, having a family monthly income (6001-10,000), having a normal BMI, having a breastfeeding frequency of 6–8 per day, having pregnancy support, increasing the number of meals during pregnancy and lactation, having a balanced diet, having antenatal visits, having tetanus toxoid, having nutritional counseling, and taking different supplements during pregnancy like folic acid, iron, calcium, omega-3, vitamins, and zinc can improve child growth and development. Introduction

Background of the study

Maternal nutrition is a critical determinant of child growth and development, influencing health outcomes from conception through early childhood and having lifelong implications for health1,2. Adequate maternal nutrition during pregnancy and lactation is vital for ensuring optimal fetal development1,2. Nutrients such as proteins, carbohydrates, fats, vitamins, and minerals are essential for organ formation, brain development, and overall growth3,4. The impact of maternal nutrition on birth outcomes is profound. Proper nutrition, including sufficient intake of folic acid, iron, calcium, and other essential nutrients, can help prevent congenital anomalies, preterm birth, and low birth weight5,6. Nutrients consumed by the mother are transferred to the developing fetus through the placenta, serving as the primary source of nourishment for the growing baby. The quality and quantity of nutrients available to the fetus directly influence its growth rate, organ maturation, and overall health7,8.

Globally, undernutrition remains a significant public health issue, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, affecting millions of women and children9,10. Globally, 24% of children under 5 experience stunted growth, while 45% of under-5 deaths are linked to under nutrition11. In Ethiopia, the burden of maternal and child undernutrition is pronounced, with high rates, 38% of children under 5 are stunted, and 7% suffer from wasting12, reflecting a critical public health challenge. Jimma Town, a semi-urban hub in southwestern Ethiopia, mirrors these trends, with 32% stunting prevalence and high rates of maternal anemia (Jimma Health Bureau, 2023), underscoring the urgency of context-specific interventions.

Poor maternal nutrition perpetuates intergenerational cycles of malnutrition. Deficiencies in iron, folate, and protein during pregnancy correlate with low birth weight (LBW), which affects 15% of Ethiopian newborns10, and developmental delays. Postnatally, inadequate breastfeeding practices and maternal micronutrient deficits exacerbate risks of childhood infections and cognitive impairments. In Jimma Town, food insecurity (affecting 28% of households) and reliance on cereal-based diets limit dietary diversity, compounding these risks.

The consequences of poor maternal nutrition are significant for both mothers and their children. In the immediate term, it can lead to low birth weight, preterm birth, and congenital anomalies, posing serious health risks to newborns9. Long-term effects include increased susceptibility to metabolic disorders, such as diabetes, due to epigenetic changes13, and reduced educational attainment and economic productivity in adulthood14. Malnourished mothers are more likely to have stunted children, perpetuating cycles of poverty and undernutrition across generations15.

Globally, the WHO Global Nutrition Targets aim to reduce low birth weight (LBW) by 30% by 2030, while Ethiopia’s National Nutrition Program (NNP) focuses on antenatal supplementation and nutrition education9. However, implementation gaps persist in Jimma Town due to contextual barriers, such as cultural practices that restrict the consumption of nutrient-rich foods during pregnancy, limited access to prenatal care (with only 44% of women attending four or more antenatal visits) (Federal Ministry of Health, 2020), and seasonal food shortages linked to subsistence farming. Additionally, research gaps exist, including a lack of studies evaluating the effectiveness of Ethiopia’s Seqota Declaration, a national initiative aimed at reducing stunting, in semi-urban settings, and limited data on how local dietary patterns, such as reliance on teff and enset, affect maternal nutrient absorption16.

Context-specific factors also play a significant role in influencing maternal nutrition and child outcomes in Jimma Town, Ethiopia. Socioeconomic factors, cultural practices, and local food availability can significantly affect dietary choices and nutritional status. Economic constraints and food insecurity are prevalent issues that hinder optimal maternal nutrition, necessitating targeted interventions tailored to the unique challenges faced by the community15,17. Furthermore, maternal mental health is crucial in this context. Stress, depression, and anxiety can negatively impact dietary choices and nutrient absorption, highlighting the importance of integrated support that addresses both mental health and nutritional needs18,19.

Addressing the global and local burden of undernutrition through continued research and effective interventions is essential for improving maternal and child health outcomes. Innovations in personalized nutrition, community initiatives, and public health policies can enhance maternal nutrition education and support systems, ultimately promoting healthy growth and development for children. By focusing on the multifaceted aspects of maternal nutrition and its implications for child health, this study aims to contribute to a deeper understanding of these critical issues.

Methods

Study design and setting

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from April to June 2024 among mothers with children aged less than two years in Jimma Town, Southwest Ethiopia. Located approximately 345 km southwest of Addis Ababa, Jimma Town has an estimated population of 239,022 people, nearly evenly split between men and women (Central Statistical Agency). Maternal and child health services are provided through public health centers, a general hospital, private clinics, and community health posts, with antenatal care and nutrition counseling primarily delivered at public health centers. The study’s sampling frame was based on household listings within the town’s seventeen administrative kebeles, allowing for inclusion of mothers living in the community regardless of their attendance at health facilities. This community-based approach ensured a representative sample of mothers with children under two years across diverse socioeconomic and cultural settings in Jimma Town.

Study population

The study population comprised all mothers residing in Jimma Town who had children aged less than two years during the data collection period (April to June 2024). These mothers were eligible to participate if they met the inclusion criteria.

Target population

The target population included all mothers with children under two years of age living in Jimma Town, Southwest Ethiopia, who had resided in the town for at least six months prior to the study. This group represents the broader community from which the study sample was drawn and to whom the study findings are intended to be generalized.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Mothers with children under two years of age who had lived in Jimma Town for at least six months prior to the study and who accepted to provide informed consent were included, while those with serious health conditions such as psychiatric disorders, sepsis, gestational hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, or other acute illnesses that could affect their nutritional status or participation in the study were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedures

The sample size was calculated using the single population proportion formula with a 95% confidence interval, a 5% margin of error (d), and an estimated proportion (P) of 50%. To account for potential nonresponse, a 10% contingency was added, resulting in a final sample size of 423 mothers. Jimma Town consists of seventeen kebeles, stratified into five strata based on geographic and administrative divisions. Following WHO Service Availability and Readiness Assessment (SARA) recommendations, five kebeles were randomly selected using a lottery method. Samples were proportionally allocated to each kebele according to population size. Households were selected using systematic random sampling, and individual mothers with children under two years were selected through simple random sampling. Variables of the study

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study was the child’s growth status, classified as poor growth if any z-score (HAZ, WAZ, WHZ) was below − 2 SD or MUAC < 12.5 cm, and good growth if all z-scores were ≥ − 2 SD without clinical signs of malnutrition, based on WHO (2006,2008,2016) Child Growth Standards and Maternal nutrition guidelines criteria20.

Independent variables

The independent variables included a range of socio-demographic factors such as the mother’s age, child’s sex, marital status, educational levels of both mother and husband, family size, religion, mother’s occupation, and monthly household income in Ethiopian Birr. Maternal health and obstetric factors considered were place of delivery, history of diagnosed medical conditions before, during, or after pregnancy, types of medical diagnoses, birth interval, number of antenatal care visits, parity, breastfeeding practices, type of birth, birth weight, and any birth complications. Specific micronutrients important for maternal nutrition during pregnancy, such as folic acid, iron, calcium, omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin D, protein, and zinc, were also assessed. Additionally, maternal nutrition-related behaviors during pregnancy and lactation were examined, including receipt of nutritional counseling, sources of nutritional information, breastfeeding frequency, and family encouragement to increase meal consumption during postpartum, actual meal frequency, and changes in dietary habits during the postpartum period. Maternal BMI during pregnancy was extracted from the antenatal care (ANC) records at the respective health centers where the mothers received care. Child-related variables included anthropometric measurements (weight, height, BMI), monitoring of growth, observed physical changes or concerns, healthcare consultations, and nutritional.

Data collection tools and procedures

Data were collected using pretested, interviewer-administered structured questionnaires complemented by anthropometric measurements of mothers and children. The questionnaire was developed following an extensive review of published literature and validated tools to ensure comprehensive coverage of relevant variables21,22. Face-to-face interviews were conducted postnatally by trained data collectors to gather detailed information on socio-demographic characteristics, maternal health, nutrition, and child growth and development and pregnancy-related data were obtained directly from ANC records at the corresponding health centers where mothers received care. Anthropometric measurements of children were directly collected on-site by trained data collectors in accordance with WHO standards to ensure accuracy and consistency23. The questionnaire was initially prepared in English and subsequently translated into Afan Oromo and Amharic using a rigorous back-translation process to ensure consistency and accuracy. Trained data collectors followed strict protocols to maintain the reliability and validity of data, while supervisors regularly monitored the data collection process to provide ongoing support and ensure adherence to ethical and methodological standards.

Data quality management

The questionnaire was developed based on an extensive review of validated tools relevant to maternal nutrition and child growth. Content validity was ensured through consultation with a multidisciplinary expert panel comprising two senior public health nutritionists, one pediatrician, and one maternal and child health specialist. It was translated into local languages with a rigorous back-translation process for consistency. A pretest was conducted on 5% of the sample. Data collectors were thoroughly trained on study procedures, and supervisors regularly monitored data collection to ensure quality. Completed questionnaires were checked daily for completeness and accuracy, and any issues were promptly addressed. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha, which indicated good internal consistency (α = 0.87).

Data processing and analysis procedures

The collected data were entered using EpiData version 4.6 and then exported to Stata version 17 for further analysis. Data were thoroughly checked for completeness, consistency, and potential outliers before proceeding with analysis. Descriptive statistics, including means, frequencies, and percentage distributions, were computed to summarize the characteristics of the study variables. Bivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the association between each independent variable and the dependent variable. Variables with a P-value less than 0.25 in the bivariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable logistic regression model. Prior to multivariable analysis, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and variables showing high collinearity were excluded. The normality of continuous variables was also evaluated where appropriate. A stepwise backward elimination procedure was applied to develop the final model. The model’s goodness-of-fit was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test, with a P-value of 0.78 indicating a strong fit of the model to the data. Variables with a P-value less than 0.05 in the multivariable logistic regression were considered statistically significant, and adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) were reported to show the strength and direction of associations.

Operational definitions

Maternal nutrition

Refers to the dietary intake and nutritional status of a mother during pregnancy and lactation, which significantly influence both maternal and child health outcomes24.

Breastfeeding practices

Behaviors related to breastfeeding, including initiation, frequency, duration, and techniques that support effective feeding. Maternal BMI and nutritional status can affect breast milk composition and infant growth24.

Child growth

The physical development of a child over time, measured through height, weight, and body proportions. The key micronutrients in breast milk, such as calcium and zinc, to improved infant growth outcomes25.

Dietary intake

The amount and type of food and beverages consumed, providing essential nutrients for health and development. A recent study in Kenya highlights how dietary patterns vary across farming systems, affecting nutritional outcomes26.

Micronutrient supplementation

The provision of essential vitamins and minerals in supplement form to prevent or correct deficiencies. Multiple micronutrient powders have shown promise in improving intake among young children in East Africa27.

Anthropometry

The measurement of physical dimensions (e.g., weight, height, BMI, MUAC) used to assess growth and nutritional status, especially in maternal and child health assessments24.

Result

Socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants

A total of 423 mothers with children under two years participated, with a 100% response rate. Most mothers were aged 20–29 years (44.2%), and a significant portion of mothers, 25.8%, were illiterate, while 36.2% had completed primary education. Poor child growth was highest among uneducated mothers (78%). Nearly half of the mothers were housewives (47.5%), while 24.3% were in business, where the highest rate of poor child growth was observed (56.3%), suggesting that their work may limit their capacity to prioritize nutrition for their children. Most husbands worked as farmers (26.5%) or in government jobs (27.2%), with children of government workers experiencing the poor growth (60.9%). Marital status played a role, with widowed and divorced mothers reporting the highest poor child growth rates (100% and 55.3%, respectively). Muslim (40%) and Orthodox (38.5%) were the dominant religions, but poor growth was most common among mothers in others religions (66.7%). While most families earned 3,000–6,000 ETB/month (42.8%). Larger households (more than six members) also had poor child growth (76.3%) as presented in Table 1.

Antenatal and pregnancy-related factors of mothers with children less than two year

Table 2 outlines key antenatal and pregnancy-related factors associated with child growth outcomes in Jimma Town. Among underweight mothers 62.5% had children with poor growth. In contrast, 57.2% of mothers with normal BMI had children with good growth, suggesting that healthy maternal weight supports better child outcomes. Short birth intervals (≤ 2 years) were associated with poor growth rates (60%). Better outcomes were observed in intervals of 2–3 years (64.2%) good growth, followed by ≥ 4 years (56.7%) good growth, indicating that longer intervals may support improved growth. Of the 309 mothers who attended ANC, 57.6% had children with good growth, compared to 56.1% among those who did not attend, suggesting a positive though modest impact of ANC on child health. Mothers who received dietary counseling had better child growth outcomes 59.2% than those who did not 54.3%, emphasizing the value of nutrition education during pregnancy. Planned pregnancies showed slightly better outcomes 58.7% good growth compared to unplanned ones 55.4%. Mothers with emotional or physical support during pregnancy reported better outcomes 59.8% good growth than those without support. Detection of a fetal heartbeat was associated with better child growth 63.2% good growth versus 47.5% in those where heartbeat was not detected.

Specific micronutrients in maternal nutrition for child growth during pregnancy

The results in Table 3 show the relationship between specific micronutrients in maternal nutrition and child growth in Jimma Town. Among mothers aware of folic acid, 63.4% had children with good growth, compared to 51% among those unaware. Regarding folic acid supplementation, 64.8% of mothers who used supplements had children with good growth, whereas only 43.8% of mothers who did not use supplements had children with good growth. Of the 154 mothers who reported symptoms of iron deficiency, 84 had children with good growth (54.5%), while 70 had children with poor growth (55.5%). Among 269 mothers who did not iron deficiency, 158 had children with good growth (58.7%), while 111 had children with poor growth (41.3%). Regarding iron supplementation, among 184 mothers who took iron supplements, 57.1% had children with good growth, while 42.9% had children with poor growth. In terms of calcium intake, 67.3% of the 211 mothers who consumed calcium had children with good growth, compared to only 47.2% good growth among 212 mothers who did not take calcium. Conversely, poor growth was higher (52.8%) among children of mothers who did not take calcium compared to 32.7% among those who did. Among 248 mothers aware of Omega-3 benefits, 69% had children with good growth, while 31% had children with poor growth. In contrast, among 175 mothers who were unaware, only 40.6% had children with good growth, and a higher 59.4% had children with poor growth. Among 206 mothers who consumed Omega-3, 70.4% had children with good growth, while 29.6% had children with poor growth. In contrast, among 217 mothers who did not consume Omega-3, only 44.7% had children with good growth, and a higher 55.3% had children with poor growth. Among 191 mothers who Vitamin D awareness, 134 had children with good growth (70.2%), while 57 had children with poor growth (29.8%). Among 232 mothers who didn’t, 108 had children with good growth (46.6%), while 124 had children with poor growth (53.4%). Protein and zinc intake among mothers showed a positive association with better child growth outcomes. Among the 164 mothers who consumed protein, 68.3% had children with good growth, compared to only 50.2% among the 259 mothers who did not. Similarly, 61.4% of the 189 mothers who took zinc had children with good growth, while only 53.8% of the 234 mothers who did not take zinc reported the same.

Maternal nutritional related factors during pregnancy and lactation in Jimma town

The result presented in Table 4 reveals several critical aspects of maternal nutrition during pregnancy and lactation in Jimma Town. A majority of the women (60.0%) reported receiving nutritional counseling, which indicates a reasonably good reach of nutrition-related health services. However, the remaining 40.0% did not receive any counseling. Healthcare providers were the primary source of nutritional information for 47.3% of the women, emphasizing the essential role of health workers in maternal education. Additionally, 21.3% of the mothers obtained information from websites, 18.4% from multimedia, and 13.0% from books. Despite the availability of information, only 46.6% of the women actively consulted sources for nutritional advice, while 53.4% did not, indicating a lack of initiative or awareness among many mothers. In terms of breastfeeding practices, 53.7% of mother’s breastfed on demand, while 46.3% followed a specific schedule. Nearly half (47.0%) of the mothers breastfed less than six times per day, while only 23.9% breastfed more than eight times daily, which falls below recommended levels for optimal infant nutrition and growth. Only 44.4% of women reporting encouragement from family members to eat more during pregnancy and lactation. Furthermore, only 46.1% consumed additional meals postpartum, and 42.8% increased their meal frequency. Most mothers (96.3%) consumed three to four meals per day, while only 3.8% reported eating five or more meals. Similarly, dietary adjustments during lactation were made by 49.1% of mothers, while 50.9% did not alter their eating habits. Notably, only 42.1% of women took prenatal supplements. Dietary restrictions were reported by 47.0% of the mothers, which may further limit nutrient intake if not properly managed. Food cravings were experienced by 43.7% of the women, a common occurrence during pregnancy, though not always addressed nutritionally. When asked about maintaining a balanced diet, 52.0% of the mothers believed they did so, while 48.0% did not.

Anthropometric measure of child growth parameter among mothers in Jimma town

The results presented in Table 5 provide a comprehensive overview of various anthropometric measures related to child growth among mothers in Jimma town. Out of 145 male children, 94 (38.8%) had good growth, while 51 (28.2%) were classified as poor. Among 278 female children, 148 (61.2%) had good growth. Of the 48 children classified as underweight, 25 (10.3%) had good growth, while 23 (12.7%) were poor. A significant majority of children, 334, fell into the normal BMI category, with 200 (82.6%) having good growth and 134 (74.0%) classified as poor. Among the 12 overweight children, only 4 (1.7%) had good growth, while 8 (4.4%) were poor. Out of 113 mothers who noticed significant changes in their child’s weight, height, or BMI over time, 55 (22.7%) had good growth, while 58 (32.0%) were poor. Among those who did not notice changes (310 mothers), 187 (77.3%) had good growth, while 123 (68.0%) were poor. Among the 113 mothers who reported family history factors influencing growth, 51 (21.1%) had good growth, while 62 (34.3%) were poor. This indicates that family history may be a contributing factor to poorer growth outcomes. In contrast, 191 (78.9%) of mothers reporting no family history factors had good growth, while 119 (65.7%) were poor.

Factors associated with maternal nutrition on child growth and development in Jimma town

Both bi-variable and multivariable analyses were employed to identify factors associated with maternal nutrition impacting child growth. Variables with a p-value less than 25% in the bi-variable analysis were selected as candidates for the multivariable logistic regression model. The multivariable analysis identified several independent predictors of maternal nutrition influencing child growth, including maternal education status, mother’s occupation, husband’s occupation, marital status, mother’s average monthly income (in ETB), maternal body mass index during pregnancy, smoking and alcohol consumption status of the mother, fetal heartbeat status at admission to the labor ward, tetanus toxoid (TT) vaccination during pregnancy, nutritional counseling, breastfeeding frequency and continuation, complementary feeding practices, prenatal vitamin use, balanced maternal diet, antenatal care visits, intake of folic acid supplements, iron status, consumption of calcium-rich foods, omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin D, protein, zinc supplements, carbohydrate and pulse intake, number of meals consumed per day, child growth consultation, as well as child anthropometric measures including BMI, weight, and height.

The results from Table 7 highlight several key factors influencing maternal nutrition and their impact on child growth in Jimma Town, as demonstrated by Adjusted Odds Ratios (AOR) and statistically significant p-values. Mothers with primary education were approximately 4 times more likely (AOR = 4.02) to have children with good growth compared to illiterate mothers. Those with secondary education showed even higher odds (AOR = 5.44), while mothers with college-level education or above had nearly 3 times greater likelihood (AOR = 2.95) of their children growing well, underscoring the critical role of maternal education in child nutrition. Employment status also played a significant role; mothers working as casual laborers were much less likely to have children with good growth (AOR = 0.19) compared to housewives, likely due to income instability and reduced nutritional access. Furthermore, children of husbands employed in government or NGOs were 3.4 times more likely to experience good growth (AOR = 3.40), and those with husbands in private business occupations had 3.8 times greater odds (AOR = 3.8) of good growth.

Discussion

The findings of this study highlight several critical factors influencing maternal nutrition and, consequently, child growth and development in Jimma Town, Southwest Ethiopia. Notably, maternal education emerged as a statistically significant predictor of child growth, underscoring the vital role educational attainment plays in shaping child health outcomes. Our results indicate that children of educated mothers show better growth compared to those whose mothers are illiterate. This finding aligns with previous studies demonstrating the positive influence of maternal education on child nutritional status and growth28. Educated mothers are more likely to understand nutritional information, access healthcare services, and make informed decisions regarding their children’s diets and overall wellbeing.

Regarding maternal occupation, our findings reveal that children of mothers engaged in casual labor exhibit poorer growth compared to those of housewives. This is consistent with literature indicating that unstable employment and irregular income increase household food insecurity and limit resources for child nutrition27,31. Casual labor, characterized by income instability and time constraints, can negatively affect childcare practices, as noted in similar settings33. These findings align with studies reporting that mothers in insecure or informal work often have less capacity to provide optimal childcare, which contributes to poor child growth outcomes20,28. However, some studies suggest that maternal employment can have a positive impact when it leads to increased household income and access to health services25.

Fathers employed by nongovernment organizations (NGOs) tend to have children with significantly better growth outcomes, likely due to more stable incomes, health benefits, and exposure to health education. This supports previous research linking stable paternal employment to improved child nutrition and health29,30. Similarly, fathers who own private businesses also tend to have children with better growth, possibly due to greater income flexibility, which aligns with findings on the positive impact of paternal economic stability on child health. Marital status also plays a crucial role in child growth. Children of married mothers show better growth outcomes compared to those of single, widowed, or divorced mothers. Marital stability provides emotional, financial, and social support, which enhances maternal capacity to care for children, consistent with prior studies emphasizing the benefits of marital support on child health31,32. Conversely, widowed and divorced mothers often face financial hardships and emotional distress that negatively affect child growth, a finding supported by earlier research identifying widowhood as a risk factor for poor child health outcomes29.

The results indicate that higher household income is strongly associated with better child growth outcomes. Families with greater financial resources can afford more diverse and nutritious diets, improving children’s physical health, cognitive development, and overall wellbeing. Higher income also facilitates access to healthcare services such as vaccinations and preventive care, which are critical for child health33,38. Moreover, better income supports improved living conditions, including adequate housing and sanitation, reducing exposure to illness and fostering growth. Increased income can also enable investment in education, empowering parents with knowledge on nutrition and child care, further supporting child development. These findings align with previous studies highlighting the positive impact of household income on child nutritional status and growth outcomes31,32. However, some research indicates that income alone may not be sufficient to ensure good child growth without concurrent parental knowledge and healthcare access, emphasizing a multifactorial influence on child development31. This suggests that interventions must address not only economic factors but also education and healthcare accessibility to optimize child health outcomes.

The findings indicate a significant relationship between maternal body mass index (BMI) and child growth outcomes in Jimma Town. Mothers with a normal BMI are more likely to have children with good growth compared to underweight mothers, highlighting the importance of maternal health in child development34. Normal BMI is associated with better nutritional status, which is crucial for adequate nutrition during pregnancy and breastfeeding35. Additionally, mothers with a normal BMI experience fewer health complications, enabling better care for their children and promoting improved developmental outcomes36. These mothers are also more likely to initiate and sustain breastfeeding, providing essential nutrition and immune support for infants. Access to healthcare services further supports regular check-ups and health education. These findings are consistent with previous studies emphasizing the importance of maternal nutritional status in child growth and development37,38. For instance, a cohort study by39 found that maternal BMI is strongly associated with infant growth trajectories. However, some literature points out that high maternal BMI (overweight/obesity) can also be linked to adverse child health outcomes, indicating that an optimal BMI range is critical33,40.

The findings regarding maternal nutrition highlight critical associations between folic acid intake and iron deficiency during pregnancy and child growth outcomes in Jimma Town. Specifically, not taking folic acid during pregnancy is linked to lower odds of good child growth, while mothers without iron deficiency have significantly higher odds of their children experiencing good growth. These results emphasize the vital role of maternal nutrition in fetal development and child health41. The association between lack of folic acid intake and reduced child growth underscores the importance of folic acid for DNA synthesis, cell division, and overall fetal development. Adequate folic acid intake is essential for the proper formation of the neural tube, preventing neural tube defects and other developmental issues42. Deficiency in folic acid can lead to growth deficiencies in children, including developmental delays and increased risk of congenital anomalies, which can impair both physical and cognitive growth, affecting long-term health outcomes. These findings support the need for public health initiatives that promote folic acid supplementation among pregnant women. Educating expectant mothers about the importance of folic acid can improve maternal and child health outcomes43.

Similarly, the finding that mothers without iron deficiency have significantly higher odds of their children experiencing good growth highlights the critical role of iron in maternal and child health. Iron is essential for hemoglobin production, which transports oxygen in the blood44. Adequate oxygen delivery is vital for fetal growth and development, especially in the brain and other vital organs. Iron deficiency during pregnancy can adversely affect cognitive development in children, leading to long-term learning and behavioral challenges44. Ensuring sufficient maternal iron levels is crucial for supporting optimal brain development in infants. Furthermore, iron deficiency can cause maternal anemia, increasing risks during pregnancy and childbirth. Healthy mothers are better positioned to provide the necessary care and nutrition for their children’s growth. This finding aligns with previous studies emphasizing the importance of maternal iron status for child health outcomes43,45.

The findings regarding the intake of calcium-rich foods, omega-3 fatty acids, and vitamin D highlight significant associations with child growth outcomes in Jimma Town. Specifically, not consuming calcium-rich foods correlates with lower odds of good child growth, lack of omega-3 intake is associated with reduced odds of good child growth, and not taking vitamin D correlates with lower odds of good child growth46. The association indicating that not consuming calcium-rich foods results in lower odds of good child growth emphasizes several critical points. Calcium is essential for the development of strong bones and teeth, and adequate intake during childhood is crucial for achieving peak bone mass to prevent osteoporosis later in life47. Calcium deficiency can lead to stunted growth and developmental issues, particularly during critical growth phases. This finding aligns with studies that demonstrate the importance of calcium for physical development in children48,49 but contrasts with some reports suggesting that calcium supplementation alone may not significantly impact linear growth if other nutritional needs are unmet50.

The finding that lack of omega-3 intake is associated with significantly lower odds of positive growth highlights the critical role of these essential fatty acids, particularly DHA, in brain development and cognitive function. Omega-3 fatty acids contribute to improved learning, memory, and behavioral outcomes, supporting healthy child growth and emotional well-being. This aligns with research showing positive associations between omega-3 intake and cognitive and physical development in children43,51. However, some studies report mixed results on omega-3 supplementation’s impact on growth metrics, suggesting variability based on dietary context and genetic factors52. The correlation between not taking vitamin D and lower odds of positive growth underscores the vitamin’s role in calcium absorption, bone health, and immune function. Vitamin D deficiency can lead to rickets, characterized by weak bones and stunted growth. This finding is consistent with multiple studies emphasizing vitamin D’s crucial role in child skeletal development and overall growth45,48.

Mothers who did not attend antenatal visits were 70% less likely to have children with good growth outcomes. Antenatal care is crucial for monitoring maternal and fetal health, providing nutritional counseling, and ensuring timely supplementation, all of which support healthy child growth. This finding aligns with previous study53, showing that regular antenatal visits improve birth outcomes and reduce risks of stunting and malnutrition. It highlights the importance of increasing access to and utilization of quality antenatal care to promote better child growth in Jimma Town.

The finding that breastfeeding 6 to 8 times a day is associated with significantly higher odds of good growth compared to breastfeeding less than 6 times a day highlights the critical role of breastfeeding frequency in supporting child growth outcomes. This result underscores the importance of optimal breastfeeding practices during the early stages of a child’s life. Breast milk is rich in essential nutrients, antibodies, and bioactive compounds crucial for healthy growth and development, particularly in the first year of life. The significant increase in positive growth odds associated with higher breastfeeding frequency suggests that more frequent feeding may lead to better weight gain and overall health in infants, which is consistent with findings from49,54 and WHO guidelines emphasizing frequent breastfeeding for optimal infant growth. However, some studies have reported no significant difference in growth outcomes with variations in breastfeeding frequency, especially in settings where complementary feeding practices vary widely38,55. These contradictory findings suggest that while breastfeeding frequency is important, other factors like complementary feeding quality and maternal nutrition also critically influence child growth.

The finding that children with a normal Body Mass Index (BMI) have higher odds of good growth compared to underweight children underscores the critical relationship between weight status and child development. This aligns with multiple studies demonstrating that normal BMI is associated with adequate nutrition, optimal physical and cognitive development, and reduced risk of health complications56. Underweight children are more prone to developmental delays and weakened immunity, consistent with findings by57. Conversely, a few studies argue that BMI alone may not fully capture nutritional status or growth potential, highlighting cases where children with normal BMI still face micronutrient deficiencies or developmental risks58. This contradiction underlines the need to consider comprehensive nutritional assessments alongside BMI.

The finding that child weight at birth is associated with higher odds of good growth underscores the significant role birth weight plays in predicting future growth outcomes. Birth weight is a critical indicator of the health and nutritional status of both the mother and fetus during pregnancy. Adequate maternal nutrition and healthcare directly influence fetal growth, resulting in higher birth weights. Infants born with a normal or higher birth weight are generally more robust and have a better chance of achieving growth milestones compared to those with low birth weight. This finding aligns with studies by59, which emphasize birth weight as a strong predictor of childhood growth and developmental trajectories. Higher birth weight has been linked to various long-term health outcomes, including reduced risk of stunting, developmental delays, and chronic diseases later in life. Conversely, low birth weight predisposes children to health challenges such as metabolic disorders and obesity, particularly if catch-up growth is excessive60. However, some research suggests that in certain contexts, birth weight alone may not fully predict long-term growth outcomes without considering postnatal nutrition and environmental factors39. This suggests the importance of a holistic approach to child growth that considers both prenatal and postnatal influences. The early months and years are crucial for physical and cognitive development, and higher birth weight may support better feeding patterns, nutrient absorption, and overall health, contributing to sustained positive growth during these formative years.

Limitation of the study

The study has a limitations, including potential bias from face-to-face interviews, which may lead participants to underreport negative practices or over report positive behaviors regarding nutrition and health. Additionally, the research was conducted solely in Jimma Town, Ethiopia, limiting the generalizability of findings to other regions with different socio-economic and cultural contexts. Furthermore, the assessment of dietary intake may not fully capture the complexity of maternal nutrition, such as food quality, preparation methods, and nutrient absorption issues.

Conclusion

This study revealed that the majority of children in Jimma Town, exhibit good growth outcomes. Several maternal, paternal, and child-related factors were found to be significantly associated with child growth. These include maternal education, mother’s occupation, husband’s education and occupation, marital status, maternal income, maternal BMI during pregnancy, pregnancy-related factors (fetal heartbeat at admission, tetanus toxoid vaccination, antenatal visits), nutritional counseling, breastfeeding practices, complementary feeding, prenatal vitamin and micronutrient intake (folic acid, iron, calcium, omega-3 fatty acids, vitamin D, zinc), and child characteristics such as sex, BMI, weight, and height.

Higher maternal education and proper nutrition were identified as essential contributors to improved child health outcomes. Conversely, factors such as casual labor employment and lack of nutritional counseling were negatively associated with child growth. Antenatal and pregnancy-related care emerged as critical, with mothers maintaining a normal BMI during pregnancy more likely to have children with better nutritional status. Regular antenatal visits and pregnancy support services play vital roles in enhancing child growth. Nutritional counseling and breastfeeding frequency significantly influence child nutritional status; frequent breastfeeding (6 to 8 times daily) is generally linked with better growth, although some deviations suggest the need for individualized support. Awareness and intake of key micronutrients during pregnancy especially folic acid, iron, and omega-3 fatty acids are intricately tied to child nutritional outcomes, highlighting the need for targeted prenatal education programs. While most children showed adequate development, some still exhibited signs of undernutrition or stunting, emphasizing the need for continued breastfeeding alongside appropriate complementary feeding practices. Child-specific factors such as sex and BMI also affect growth potential, underscoring the necessity of tailored interventions.

To improve nutritional status and dietary practices related to maternal nutrition and child growth, several recommendations are proposed. enhance access to comprehensive prenatal care services, particularly in underserved areas; implement educational programs for pregnant women on balanced diets and provide supplements or food assistance; foster community support systems through peer groups and workshops; establish monitoring systems for child growth from birth to early childhood to identify issues early; advocate for supportive policies like paid maternity leave; promote collaboration among healthcare providers for a holistic health approach; emphasize the importance of frequent breastfeeding (6 to 8 times a day) and encourage initiation within the first hour after birth, continuing for at least two years; monitor birth weights to identify at-risk pregnancies; establish routine check-ups for child growth and nutrition guidance; offer nutritional counseling for children’s dietary needs; and implement programs to monitor BMI and promote healthy weight management through physical activity and balanced diets.
