Primary care attributes among public health service users and perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities: evidences from ELSI-Brazil
Authors: Juliana Lustosa Torres, Gabriela Pérsio Gonçalves, Lucas Leonardo Knupp dos Santos, Maria Fernanda Lima-Costa, Samuel Araújo Gomes da Silva
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe impact of primary care on reducing discrimination is still unknown in Brazil. This study aimed to assess the association between problems in primary care attributes and discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities perceived by adults aged 50 years and over who exclusively use the public health services.MethodsCross-sectional nationally representative study derived from the Brazilian Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSI-Brazil), conducted in 2019-21, summing 6,663 participants who exclusively use the public health system. The outcome was the self-reported perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities. The number of problems in primary care attributes, up to 12, included situations related to primary care attributes (access; continuity of care; communication; and care coordination and resolution). Logistic Regression models were used in the statistical analysis, adjusted for sociodemographic characteristics, prior discrimination experience, municipality size, Family Health Strategy coverage, and participation in outside activities.ResultsPerceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities was associated with experiencing ≥ 5 problems in primary care attributes (OR = 1.52; 95%CI 1.02; 2.28), after accounting for all covariates. We identified statistically significant negative interactions for both prior discrimination experience and Family Health Strategy coverage, where the observed joint effect (OR = 2.41, 95%CI 1.41; 4.10 and OR = 1.56, 95%CI 0.82; 2.97, respectively) were lower than the expected joint effect.ConclusionsThe findings highlight the critical role of high-quality, accessible, and responsive primary care in mitigating perceived inequities, particularly among users of the public health system. Although the Family Health Strategy and prior discrimination experience attenuate this association, municipality size and number of medical appointments are not key factors and further research is needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03378-3.

Background

The Brazilian public health system, known as the Sistema Único de Saúde, SUS, is a universal, comprehensive and equitable publicly funded health system that is primary care-centered. Since the implementation of the National Primary Health Care Policy in 2006, the predominant primary care model in Brazil is the Family Health Strategy [1]. evidenced to perform, on average, five points better than traditional public healthcare model according to the Primary Care Assessment Tool [2]. It emphasizes continuity, comprehensiveness, and a community- and family-orientated approach to care [1, 3]. It is organized through multiprofessional teams, responsible for coordinating care to approximately 3,500 people across health system.

The Family Health Strategy emphasis on community and family orientations is reinforced through the community health worker’ home visits and collaborations with schools and community-based organizations [4]. Therefore, primary care not only reduces health inequalities among its users [5] but also has the potential to influence societal attitudes within the geographically defined areas covered by primary care teams. Considering that Brazil comprises 5,570 municipalities, of which nearly 69% have populations under 20,000 and 23% have up to 5,000 inhabitants, in small municipalities, a single primary care team is often responsible for supporting care of the entire municipality population [6].

Despite of Family Health Strategy promoting equitable healthcare, there are evidences that discrimination at health care level still exists, more frequently against women [7], people with black/brown skin color, those who utilize health services more frequently, individuals with lower educational attainment [7, 8] and those without private health insurance [9]. Discrimination occurs when individuals (i.e., interpersonal interaction) or institutions (i.e., organizational practices or societal discrimination) engage in unfair treatment toward socially subordinated groups, such as sexual orientation minorities, reinforcing societal hierarchies of dominance and subordination [10].

Building upon its commitment to equality, SUS took a significant step forward by implementing the National Comprehensive Health Policy for Lesbians, Gays, Bisexuals, Travestis, and Trans (LGBT) in 2011 [11]. This policy aims to eliminate discrimination and institutional prejudice based on sexual orientation and gender identity, to decrease the heteronormative logic [12, 13], while also improving healthcare access and continuity of care for these populations. Authors such as Herek [14] and Meyer [15] argue that sexual and gender minorities are subjected to multiple forms of social stigmatization, often legitimized by institutional structures that reinforce their marginalization, leading to the deterioration of mental health, known as the minority stress theory.

A nationally representative Brazilian study from the 2013 National Health Survey found that 0.18% of adults reported experiencing discrimination in healthcare services due to their sexual orientation. However, this figure likely underestimates the real prevalence, as the survey did not include direct questions regarding sexual orientation [16]. International studies evidenced heteronormative assumptions, negative responses, ignorance and prejudice from healthcare professionals, and barriers to raising concerns or filing complaints [17]. Additionally, stigma, denial or refusal of care, and even verbal or physical abuse have been reported [18], leading to barriers to healthcare access for sexual minorities and violating human rights [19].

To the best of our knowledge, no study in Brazil has evaluated the impact of primary care quality on reducing discrimination, particularly discrimination based on sexual orientation. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the association between problems in primary care attributes and discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities perceived by adults aged 50 years and over who exclusively use the public health system. We posit that stronger Primary care, characterized by fewer problems in their attributes, may influence societal attitudes reducing discrimination based on sexual orientation.

Methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study utilized data from the wave 2 (2019–2021) of the Brazilian Longitudinal Study of Aging (ELSI-Brazil). ELSI-Brazil is a nationally representative sample of Brazilian population aged 50 years and over. The study is conducted in 70 municipalities the five Brazilian geographic regions [20]. From a total of 9,949 study participants, 7,859 were users of the SUS. Further details can be found elsewhere [20, 21] and in the ELSI-Brazil homepage (https://elsi.cpqrr.fiocruz.br/en/home-english/). ELSI-Brazil was approved by the Fundação Oswaldo Cruz (FIOCRUZ) ethics committee, Minas Gerais, Brazil (protocol number 34649814.3.0000.5091).

Outcome

The dependent variable was the perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation, measured through the question: “In your opinion, there are discrimination in your municipality or place where you live regarding homosexuals/ bisexuals?” (no or yes).

Independent variable

The independent variable was the number of problems in primary care attributes, up to 12, categorized into “0–2 problems”, “3–4 problems” and “≥5 problems”. The participants were asked to rate the frequency (always; most of the time; rarely; or never) that they experienced each of the situations related to primary care attributes (access; continuity of care; communication; and care coordination and resolution). Access encompassed how easy it is to get (1) an appointment, (2) an appointment in 24 h, and (3) an appointment by phone; continuity of care encompassed (4) whether the person is seen by the same doctor, (5) whether the doctor knows what medications the patient is taking, and (6) whether the doctor knows about the patient’s main health problems; communication covered how often (7) does the doctor listen to the patient, (8) does the doctor explain things well, and (9) are visits long enough. Finally, care coordination and resolution covered how often (10) does the doctor usually solve the patient’s health problems, (11) does the doctor speak about specialist visits, and (12) does the doctor help coordinate the patient’s specialist visits. Each situation was considered a problem when the participant answered the frequency of rarely or never [22].

Covariates

Covariates included sociodemographic characteristics, prior discrimination experience, municipality size, Family Health Strategy enrollment, and participation in outside activities.

Sociodemographic characteristics

Sex (female or male); age group (50–59; 60–69; 70–79; ≥80 years); schooling, in complete years (0–1; 2–8; ≥9 years); per capita household income, in quintiles; self-reported race/color (white, brown, or black).

Prior discrimination experience

Self-reported experiences of discrimination were assessed using the Everyday Discrimination Scale (EDS) [23], a widely used instrument in epidemiological studies. The scale includes questions on the frequency of perceiving being treated with less courtesy, receiving poor services in stores/restaurants, receiving poor medical treatment, being threatened/harassed, people acting like the participant are not smart and people being afraid of the participant. Participants who reported experiencing any of these forms of discrimination at least once a month were classified as having prior discrimination experience (yes or no).

Municipality size

Categorized according ELSI-Brazil sampling strata as “small” when having up to 26,700 inhabitants or “medium/large” when having more than to 26,700 inhabitants.

Family health strategy coverage

Measure by the question “Is this household registered with the Family Health Strategy?” (yes or no). The few respondents who answered they did not know if their household was registered with the Family Health Strategy (n = 132) were categorized as having Family Health Strategy coverage if they responded that they had received a community health worker visit anytime in the past two months.

Participation in outside activities

Frequency of religious participation (never, less than once a week, or at least once a week); daily Internet use (yes or no); social outgoing in the past 12 months (yes or no); and number medical appointments in the past 12 months (none, 1–4, or ≥ 5 appointments).

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses were conducted for total sample and stratified by perceived discrimination status. Differences between categorical variables were assessed by Pearson’s chi-square test with the Rao Scott correction. Subsequently, we estimated Odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence internals (95%CI) through Logistic Regression models to examine the association between problems in primary care attributes and perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities.

Considering some contextual covariables of interest for health, we tested interactions between problems in primary care attributes and prior discrimination experience, municipality size, Family Health Strategy coverage, and number of medical appointments in the past 12 months. Using a significance threshold of p < 0.10 to adopt a broader statistically-based approach prior to applying epidemiological concepts of interaction [24], we identified statistically significant interactions for both prior discrimination experience and Family Health Strategy coverage.

Finally, as a sensitivity analysis, we examined the association between problems in primary care attributes and perceived discrimination based on age, race/skin color, and disability in Brazilian municipalities. All three forms of perceived discrimination were assessed using questions structured similarly to that used for discrimination based on sexual orientation. All analyses were performed using STATA software (Stata Corp., College Station, United Station), version 17,0, accounting for sample weights and the complex survey design.

Results

Of the 9,949 participants in wave 2, 7,859 exclusively use the Brazilian public health system and were eligible for this analysis. Among these, 574 participants were excluded because the discrimination section was completed by a proxy respondent, and 622 were excluded due to missing information on perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation. The final analytical sample comprised 6,663 participants. Compared with excluded individuals, the included participants were more likely to be female, younger, have higher socioeconomic position, identify as Black or Brown, and reside in medium/large municipalities (p < 0.05), as shown in Supplemental Table 1. The included participants had a mean age of 62.7 years (standard deviation 9.0).

The prevalence of perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities was 34.7% (IC95% 28.4; 41.6). Participants’ characteristics are shown in Table 1. Variables with statistically significant difference (p < 0.05) between categories of perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities were age group, schooling, prior discrimination experience, daily Internet use, social outgoing in the past 12 months, and number medical appointments in the past 12 months. Individuals who perceived more discrimination based on sexual orientation were those in age group of 50–59 years (38.1%), with ≥ 9 years of schooling (49.3%), those who lived in medium/large municipalities (38.0%), those who had at least one medical appointment in the past 12 months, and those who experienced ≥ 5 problems in primary care attributes (38.7%).

Considering the interactions terms with contextual covariables of interest for health, we identified statistically significant interactions for both prior discrimination experience and Family Health Strategy coverage. Among participants with ≥ 5 reported problems in primary care attributes, the prevalence of perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation was higher among those with a history of prior discrimination experience (46.6%; 95%CI 36.6–56.6%) compared to those without such experience (38.6%; 95%CI 30.7–46.4%) (p = 0.051). Oppositely, perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation was lower among individuals living in household covered by the Family Health Strategy (36.7%; 95%CI 27.7–45.7%) than those without (43.3%; 95%CI 35.1–51.5%) (p = 0.077). No statistically significant interactions were observed for municipality size (p = 0.947) and number of medical appointments in the past 12 months (p = 0.178) (Fig. 1).

For the interaction between prior discrimination experience and reporting ≥ 5 problems in primary care attributes (Fig. 2A), the expected joint effect was 5.09 (represented by the reference line), which was notably higher than the observed joint effect (OR = 2.41, 95%CI 1.41; 4.10), indicating a negative interaction on the multiplicative scale. A similar pattern was observed for Family Health Strategy coverage (Fig. 2B). The independent effect of reporting ≥ 5 problems in primary care attributes was 2.17 (95%CI 1.25; 3.76), while the effect of Family Health Strategy coverage alone was not statistically significant (OR = 1.22, 95%CI 0.65; 2.28). The resulting expected joint effect was 2.61, which again exceeded the observed joint effect (OR = 1.56, 95%CI 0.82; 2.97), suggesting that the presence of Family Health Strategy coverage may attenuate the association between problems in primary care attributes and perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation.

The sensitive analyses, presented in Supplemental Table, examined perceived discrimination in Brazilian municipalities based on age, race/skin color, and disability. In the fully adjusted models, experiencing ≥ 5 problems in primary care attributes was significantly associated with perceived discrimination based on age in Brazilian municipalities (OR = 1.78; 95%CI 1.18; 2.68). However, no statistically significant associations were observed for perceived discrimination based on race/skin color (OR = 1.33; 95%CI 0.87; 2.03) or disability (OR = 1.48; 95%CI 0.98; 2.23).

Discussion

Our findings showed that higher problems in primary care attributes among older individuals who exclusively use the public health system was positively associated with perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities. The strength of this association was homogeneous within groups of municipality size and number of medical appointments. However, the association between more problems in primary care attributes perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities was attenuated by prior discrimination experience and Family Health Strategy coverage. Similar associations were observed for perceived discrimination based on age, but not for discrimination based on race/skin color and disability.

According to guidelines set forth in Brazil’s National Comprehensive Health Policy for LGBT [11], the elimination of discrimination based on sexual orientation and other forms within the SUS is essential to fostering broader societal transformation. Given the SUS’s the primary care-centered model [1, 3], primary care is a strategic and appropriate setting to initiate and implement these anti-discrimination guidelines. Effective primary care is oriented toward population demands, considering their sociocultural and economic contexts, and prioritizes health promotion over disease treatment [25]. Therefore, it is possible to strengthen the representation and participation of sexual minorities in Health Conferences and Councils. Additionally, implementing educational strategies within health services aimed at improving the self-esteem of sexual minorities and reducing discrimination is feasible [11]. This is largely achieved through interprofessional teams that practice shared decision-making focused on users’ needs [26].

The observed association between a higher number of problems in primary care attributes and increased odds of perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation also underscores the role of the health system in either mitigating or reinforcing social inequalities. Discrimination in healthcare extends beyond interpersonal dynamics and perceptions of older individuals; it is deeply embedded in institutional and structural arrangements [10]. These findings are consistent with a broader body of literature demonstrating that discrimination operates through structural and institutional mechanisms across different populations and settings, reinforcing health and social inequalities beyond specific contexts [7–9, 27–29]. When primary care fails to provide accessible, continuous, and person-centered care - particularly in systems designed to promote equity - it may exacerbate perception of exclusion and reinforce experiences of marginalization among sexual minorities [14, 15]. Such experiences are not limited to acts of hostility but may also stem from persistent social stigma and structural barriers within the health system. This may partially explain why a prior personal experience of discrimination enhances the perception of discrimination within the municipality level. In European countries, higher country-level acceptance of sexual minorities is paradoxically the locations where individuals report greater directly experienced discrimination based on sexual orientation, likely reflecting greater awareness and recognition of discriminatory practices against sexual minority populations [30].

In our study, we observed that Family Health Strategy coverage attenuated the association between problems in primary care attributes and perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation. Although this finding does not support causal inference, it may reflect the role of Family Health Strategy in strengthening bond professional-patient relationships and ensuring continuity of care, which are essential components for reducing stigma and discrimination within health settings [31]. In Brazil, the Family Health Strategy represents the cornerstone of primary healthcare system, reaching coverage rates of up to 89.5% of the population in some municipalities by 2019 [32].

A key actor within the Family Health Strategy is the community health worker, whose role is fundamental to identifying and addressing local social needs. Moreover, the primary care team holds responsibility for all residents living within the geographically area covered by health teams [1]. Both general practitioners and nurses as members of the team adopt a generalist approach rather than a specialized one, supporting all residents regardless of age, gender or body organs. This model of integrated care may facilitate stronger connections with sexual minority individuals and community.

Moreover, the Family Health Strategy can play a crucial role in aligning with Ministry of Health to support the implementation of the National Comprehensive Health Policy for LGBT [11], shifting societal attitudes, promoting awareness, and enhancing knowledge about sexual minorities via public policies and community-based educational campaigns aiming at advancing human rights and health equity [12, 33]. and decreasing societal stressors that can lead to mental health deterioration [15]. Further research is needed to explore how specific practices influence experiences of discrimination and to identify effective strategies for strengthening equity in the primary care. Our findings suggest that municipality size and number of medical appointments are not key mechanisms driving the observed associations.

Furthermore, sensitivity analyses reinforce the importance of examining environmental factors - such as discrimination – not only to foster inclusive environments for sexual minorities, but also to promote healthy aging. Given the cross-sectional study design of this study, the direction of the association cannot be stablished. In the opposite direction, a previous meta-analysis [34] demonstrated that educational interventions targeting undergraduate students in health and social science, such as those aimed at improving general knowledge about aging and fostering empathy toward older adults, can improve attitudes in clinical settings, and, consequently, enhance the quality of care.

On the other hand, according to the World Health Organization [35], healthy aging results from the dynamic interaction between individual’s physical and mental capacities and the environments in which they live [35]. Within the environmental domain, attitudes – defined as the observable manifestations of cultural norms, practices, and beliefs that shape individual behavior and permeate all levels of social life – represent a key modifiable factor with significant implications for health outcomes [36]. A previous study revealed that higher country-level sexual minority acceptance contributes to individual well-being among both same-sex and opposite-sex couples, partially mediated by increased levels of trust in people living in the same area and greater engagement in social activities [30].

This study has some limitations. First, the perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation was reported by individuals aged 50 years and over not necessarily identifying as part of a sexual minority. This may have led to variability in perception and underrepresentation of the lived experiences of those most directly affected and it cannot be generalized to perceptions of younger or sexual minority populations. Second, perceived discrimination was used as a proxy of observed discrimination. As such, the findings reflect respondents’ awareness and perception rather than the full problem extent of discriminatory practices. Consequently, the real prevalence of discrimination may be underestimated. Furthermore, discrimination was assessed using a single question focused on interpersonal experiences, which may insufficiently capture broader institutional forms of discrimination. Future research should integrate both types to better account for variation in perception across population subgroups.

Yet, the problems measured in primary care attributes primarily referred to aspects related to physicians, who represent only one member of the primary care team. Moreover, these questions in the ELSI-Brazil study were not specially to assess primary care services. To reduce this limitation, we restricted our analysis to individuals who are exclusive public health system users, where the first point of contact in the health system is predominantly through primary care. Also, we cannot discard the possibility of same source bias, as both perceived discrimination and reported problems in primary care attributes are based on subjective self-report measures.

Regarding potential selection bias, with the exception of age, sociodemographic characteristics of the included participants were systematically associated with a higher perception of discrimination, which may have led to an overestimation of the strength of the observed associations. In addition, the cross-sectional design may further reinforce this bias in the same direction, as survival bias is inherent to this type of study, and reversal causality cannot be ruled out, limiting our ability to accurately determine the direction of the associations. Residual confounding may also be present. Finally, the outcome in our study was relatively prevalent (34.7%), which may result in larger point estimates when using OR compared with Prevalence Ratio (PR). We therefore used Logistic Regression models to allow appropriate estimation and visualization of predicted probabilities in post-estimation analyses. To address this concern, we also estimated models using PR (data not shown), and the interpretation of the 95CI% remained consistent across models, indicating similar conclusions.

Despite these limitations, as advantages we can highlight that this is the first study to examine perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in a nationally representative sample of the older Brazilian population. These findings extend existing evidence from studies conducted with exclusively sexual minority populations.

Conclusion

This study provides novel evidence that problems in primary care are associated with higher perceived discrimination based on sexual orientation in Brazilian municipalities. The findings highlight the critical role of high-quality, accessible, and responsive primary care in mitigating perceived inequities, particularly among users of the public health system. Although the Family Health Strategy appears to attenuate this association, further research is needed to elucidate the specific mechanisms by which primary care model influences perceptions of discrimination, as well as to identify which primary care attributes most effectively contribute to reducing discrimination based on sexual orientation. These results underscore the importance of strengthening equity-oriented practices within primary care to ensure inclusive and respectful, and non-discriminatory health care for all population groups.
