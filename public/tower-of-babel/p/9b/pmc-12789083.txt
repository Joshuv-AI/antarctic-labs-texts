Usable time estimation and suitable battery selection for electric tractor considering agricultural operational load
Authors: Md. Abu Ayub Siddique, Cheol-Woo Yang, Nyunki Chung, Seung-Min Baek, Seung-Yun Baek, Hyeon-Ho Jeon, Yeon-Soo Kim, Wan-Soo Kim, Yong-Joo Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study estimates battery usable time and discharge rates, and selects a suitable battery for a single-motor electric tractor using dynamometer tests and rotary tillage at L-slow, L-fast, and M-slow gear stages. The dynamometer results were validated for rotary tillage to determine a suitable battery for full-day (8 h) operations. In dynamometer tests, battery power consumption for light, medium, and heavy-duty loads was 6.25, 8.76, and 14.51 kW, resulting in usable times of 3.00, 2.33, and 1.25 h, respectively. Rotary tillage results confirmed similar battery consumption trends, validating the dynamometer-based estimation. For continuous 8-hour operations, a 900 Ah battery (64.8 kWh) was satisfied at the gear stage of L-fast for rotary tillage. It was observed that battery mass and volume increased proportionally with energy density. Results confirmed that higher energy storage required a larger and heavier battery pack for full-day operations. The battery energy density of 64.8 kWh (900 Ah) would be suitable with practical weight and space limitations to perform full-day operations. The limitation is that traction efficiency, slip ratio, and effective field capacity were not considered for suitable gear stage selection. However, we believe that this study provides a feasible technique for battery selection in real agricultural scenarios, offering practical insights for electric tractor manufacturers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20517-5.

Introduction

In recent years, the biggest alarming issue in the world is global warming, which is mainly caused by greenhouse gases (GHG). The annual average atmospheric CO2 level has globally reached a record peak of 421.08 ppm (parts per million) in 20231. The increase in CO2 emission of 1.1% has been projected to rise globally to a record high of 37.55 GtCO2 in 20232. The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) stated that one of the largest sources of GHG is the agriculture sector in the USA, which accounted for 11% of the total emissions in 20203. In addition, the air pollution from non-road mobile machinery (NRMM) in Korea has accounted for approximately 22% of CO (carbon monoxide) and 26% of NOx (nitrogen oxides) in 20174. Agricultural tractors mainly use a diesel engine as a power source, resulting in GHG emission5. The fuel is a major source of carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrogen oxide (N2O), and methane (CH4)6. Global agricultural machinery industry7 also predicted that the annual demand for tractors globally has grown to almost 3.92 million units in 2023, and free-tax oil has reached almost 824,935 kL in 20198, which have become main environmental and social issues throughout the world.

Apart from the above issue, the agricultural production cost is another main concern for the farmers. Therefore, farming should be performed with minimum expense. According to the extension report, agricultural production costs continued to rise. In particular, machinery costs increased by over 60% in 2022 compared to 2011. Fuel cost is also at the highest peak over the last 10 years9. In 2018, Reuters (New York/Chicago) reported that the agriculture sector worldwide is seeking profits due to an increase in diesel prices. It was stated that the net farm income declined around 8.3% in 2018 from 2017 and approximately 55% since 2013. The main reason was the fuel expense that might cost up to almost 25% or higher in 201810.

Further, the adaptation of electric agricultural vehicles (EAVs) is drawing global attention to meet global issues such as sustainable agriculture, agri-food supply chain, and environmental challenges. One of the most crucial advantages of EAVs is their compatibility with renewable energy sources, particularly solar energy, which is an abundantly accessible and sustainable energy source for electric farm operations11. The EAVs can be efficiently recharged by solar-powered electricity, resulting in low-carbon and energy-resilient farming systems. Moreover, the emerging concept of agrivoltaics — the co-existence of agricultural production and solar energy generation in the same field — creates a transformative opportunity for sustainable farming12. Agrivoltaic systems not only generate electricity but also provide partial shading for crops, improving water retention and reducing heat stress in certain environments13. The electricity generated from agrivoltaic structures can directly support the changing needs of electric tractors, irrigation systems, and other electric farm machinery, closing the loop toward a renewable and decentralized energy supply within agriculture14. In addition to energy benefits, the EAVs can integrate precision agriculture tools, IoT-based monitoring systems, automated control units, and sensor-driven smart implements15. The electric motors of the EAVs provide more accurate and easily adjustable control of speed, torque, and power output, which is particularly valuable for various field operations16. Furthermore, electric farm equipment can be easily connected to GPS guidance, load sensors, soil monitoring devices, or wireless communication systems, enabling real-time data collection, remote control, and autonomous operations with electric tractors or solar-powered or hybrid agricultural vehicles17.

To address the global issues using the advantages of EAVs, scholars and tractor manufacturing companies have concentrated on the electrification of agricultural machinery because it is expected that electric off-road vehicles can minimize CO2 emissions from production to disposal, accounting for almost 70% of internal combustion engines18. Baek et al.,19,20 developed the simulation model using SimulationX (ver. 4.0, ESI ITI GmbH, Dresden, Germany) and verified the All-Wheel-Drive (AWD) prototype electric tractor for driving operation, and they also evaluated the traction performance of the proposed AWD electric tractor using a traction test vehicle. In their study, the tractive efficiencies of conventional and proposed AWD electric tractors were approximately 84 and 70%, respectively, and the difference between them was 14%. The difference in dynamic ratios between conventional and proposed AWD electric tractors was high due to the self-protection of the motor algorithm. Several researchers in the world are continuously attempting to develop a dual-motor type electric tractor21–23. Their efforts were on the design and optimization of a dual-motor type powertrain for the electric tractor. However, they did not consider the battery performance of single-motor type electric tractors considering agricultural working scenarios.

Electric vehicles require a high-capacity battery that would be able to generate high traction for agricultural operations of the electric tractor. Liew et al.24 studied on estimation of the required battery size for tractor electrification during row crop planting operations. They collected tractor CAN (Controller Area Network) data during corn planting operations to estimate the required energy for the operations, which was utilized to determine the lithium-ion battery size for operating a fully electric tractor to conduct the planting operations. They concluded that approximately 1117 kWh capacity lithium-ion battery was required for operating 14 h of a 16-row planter to plant 68 ha of corn per day at the average tractor speed of 8.6 km/hr. Their results demanded a very high-capacity battery for agricultural operations. However, they did not consider the tillage operations. Brenna et al.25 proposed the feasibility of the electric tractor for heavy-duty operation. They stated that the capacity of the battery must be 660 kWh to conduct plow operation for a whole day. Du et al.26 analyzed the possibility of the electric tractor considering the physical and mechanical properties of the battery, weight, soil pressure, and energy efficiency. They stated that 75 kW of the battery-based electric tractor was suitable for medium and heavy-duty. The battery capacity was 37.5 kWh. Ueka et al.27 also developed a 10-kW class 4WD (4-wheel-drive) prototype electric tractor. They stated that the proposed tractor could conduct the rotary tillage operation of 13 acres continuously at a travel speed of 0.3 m/s with a tillage depth of 10 cm using a single-charged battery, which had a capacity of 105 Wh. Those studies indicated that the battery size and energy density are crucial for full-day operations based on working loads.

It was noticed that the above-discussed researches were not focused on the most important parameters of the electric tractor battery, such as state of charge (SOC), discharge rate, and usable time based on working loads. Most important is to select a suitable battery considering energy density, mass, and volume, considering space limitation and full-day operations of the electric tractor. Therefore, this study focused on the battery performance of a single-motor electric tractor using a Dynamometer test and field experiment (rotary tillage). Therefore, the nobility of this article is to estimate the battery usable time and discharge rate of the electric tractor for various working loads during indoor and real agricultural operational environments. In addition, a suitable battery is selected for full-day operation, considering battery mass and volume with respect to energy density for rotary tillage. This study aims to introduce a technique for estimating the battery usable times of a single-motor electric tractor and suitable battery capacity for full-day agricultural operations, especially for rotary tillage. The specific objectives were to (i) estimate the battery usable times and discharge rates of a single-motor electric tractor for the dynamometer test at various working loads, (ii) evaluate the estimated battery usable times and discharge rates for various gear stages during rotary tillage, and (iii) select a suitable battery considering energy density, mass, and volume for enhancing usable times of a single-motor electric tractor for a whole day (at least 8 h) continuous operations.

Materials and methods

Tractor specifications

A 19-kW electric tractor (e25, Solectrac, California, USA) was used to conduct the indoor and outdoor experiments. The dimensions of the tractor were 2670 (L) × 1380 (W) × 2170 (H) mm. The tractor powertrain is composed of 3 mechanical-type sub-shifts (L, M, and H), PTO with 540 rpm for both light and heavy operations, and 4WD (four-wheel-drive). The tractor’s gross weight is 14,175 N. The tractor specifications are listed in Table 1. The maximum torque of the motor was 84 Nm. The motor characteristics map is shown in Fig. 1. The electric tractor was powered by a Li NMC type battery (EV-008–72 V-300Ah-02, Soundon New Energy Technology Co. Ltd, Hunan, China), which has a total weight of 175 kg. The rated voltage, capacity, and energy of the battery were 72 V, 300 Ah, and 21.6 kWh, respectively, which can continuously run for 8 h based on operational application. The detailed specifications of the battery are listed in Table 2.

Measurement system

Dynamometer system

The axle and PTO performance tests were conducted using the dynamometer test bench, which is shown in Fig. 2. The vehicle speeds were measured for different gear stages (L, M, and H), which was shown in Fig. 2 (a). The vehicle speed measurement test bench was configured with a maximum torque of 9,000 Nm at the maximum rotational speed of 800 rpm using the Dry Braking method. The PTO dynamometer (Schenck, Germany) was a DC motor type, which was configured with a maximum torque of 850 Nm at a maximum rotational speed of 3,000 rpm, and the maximum power was 100 kW. The PTO performance was analyzed for three load levels: Light duty (30% of load), Medium duty (50% of load), and Heavy duty (100% of load or full load). The battery performances (SOC) were measured in real time using a human-machine-interface (HMI) method during the indoor test. The detailed specifications of the dynamometer test bench are listed in Table 3.

Experimental conditions

The battery usable times of a single-motor electric tractor were analyzed for various conditions, which was shown in Fig. 4. In the dynamometer test, the battery performances were analyzed for different load levels: light duty (30% of load), Medium duty (50% of load), and heavy-duty (100% of load). According to the OECD (Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development) code 2, the axle and PTO performances were conducted by measuring the torques and rotational speeds at atmospheric temperature. The axle performances were analyzed for different gear stages (Low, Medium, and High), which are the mechanical range gears of L, M, and H. As the dynamometer test was an ideal condition, the results were validated and evaluated the performance of the battery usable times for real agricultural operations. The power and SOC during the rotary operation were conducted for different gear stages (Low-normal, Low-fast, and Medium-slow). The gear stages from slow to fast were determined by the motor rotational speeds (Slow: 2000 rpm, Normal: 2500 rpm, and Fast: 2800 rpm), which can be selected using the default dialer of the tractor.

Statistical analysis

The battery power and SOC of the electric tractor at various conditions were statistically analyzed for both the dynamometer (Axle and PTO) and field experiment (Asphalt driving and rotary tillage). The statistical analysis, one-way ANOVA, and Duncan’s multiple range test (DMRT) at the significant level of 0.05 were performed to analyze the significance of the battery power, SOC level, discharge rate, and operating time for different conditions. The software used for the analysis was IBM SPSS Statistics (SPSS 25, SPSS Inc., New York, NY, USA).

Performance analysis of the electric tractor using the dynamometer test

Battery performance analysis

Figure 5 shows the battery performance of a 19-kW electric tractor. In this study, the battery power, SOC levels, discharge rates, and usable times were analyzed for various load levels based on operations using a dynamometer test. In the dynamometer test, it was observed that the battery discharge rate was higher with the increase of the working loads. It was also noticed that the minimum required battery capacity was 900 Ah to conduct the rotary tillage for a whole day (8 h) continuously. Discussion

In this study, a 19-kW single-motor electric tractor was used to analyze the battery performance for various working loads using the dynamometer and field experiment. This study focused on the performance of the battery of the tractor using the dynamometer test for load levels of light-duty (30%), medium-duty (50%), and heavy-duty (100%). The estimated battery usable times were evaluated using the rotary tillage at the gear stages of L-normal, L-fast, and M-slow. The results of this study are discussed below:

In the dynamometer test, the lowest and highest battery powers were consumed at light and heavy-duty operations, accounting for 6.25 and 14.51 kW, respectively, as well as the battery usable times were 3.00 and 1.25 h, respectively. For the axle performance analysis, the lowest and highest battery powers were consumed at the L and H gear stages, accounting for approximately 11.02 and 14.16 kW, respectively, but the efficiencies at all gear stages were not significantly different. In addition, the PTO performances were also analyzed in this study because the battery performance of the electric tractor was evaluated for the rotary tillage. The PTO power was calculated at approximately 11.97 kW, while the battery power was consumed at almost 15.36 kW. The PTO efficiency was estimated at around 77%. The dynamometer test was conducted under ideal conditions, such as a constant applied load. However, the agricultural operations occurred fluctuations and uneven loads. Lee et al.33 reported that the engine could suddenly be turned off at low engine torque due to the uneven fluctuations of the working loads. Therefore, the battery performances of the electric tractor for the dynamometer test are required to evaluate using the rotary tillage and to reflect the agricultural operational scenario.In the case of the rotary tillage, the consumptions of battery power were analyzed at the gear stages of L-normal, L-fast, and M-slow, accounting for approximately 9.50, 11.09, and 12.22 kW, respectively. It was noticed that the consumed battery power at the gear stages of L-normal and L-fast was similar to the consumed battery power at the gear stages of Low and Medium during the dynamometer test. The battery usable times at the L-normal and L-fast gear stages were estimated at almost 2.92 and 2.74 h, respectively, which were similar to the battery usable times of light and medium-duty, accounting for 3.00 and 2.33 h, respectively. These results confirmed that the dynamometer test could be feasible and authentic to analyze the battery performance of the electric tractor. Siddique et al.34 estimated the fuel consumption concerning the engine throttle level using the dynamometer test and compared it with the simulation model. They stated that the accuracy was approximately 99%, where the RMSE (root-mean-square-error) and RD (relative deviation) were calculated at approximately 1.89% and 2.54%, respectively. However, the estimated battery usable times were comparatively lower for the rotary tillage operation. It is important to select a suitable battery capacity for the electric tractor for conducting rotary tillage at least 8 h a day. Therefore, the battery discharge rates and estimated usable times were also analyzed for various battery capacities.To select a suitable battery capacity for the electric tractor, the battery discharge rates and usable times were estimated at the gear stages of L-normal, L-fast, and M-slow for the rotary tillage. It was noticed that the estimated usable times for considering continuous operation at L-normal and L-fast were over 8 h a day, whereas the battery discharge rates were almost 0.58 and 0.53%, respectively, except for the gear stages of M-slow when the battery capacity was 900 Ah. It was also noticed that the electric tractor could conduct the rotary tillage continuously for 14.60, 14.21, and 12.40 h at the gear stages of L-normal, L-fast, and M-slow, respectively, when the battery capacity was 1500 Ah, and the battery discharge rates were below 0.35, 0.32, and 0.17% for L-normal, L-fast, and M-slow, respectively. Liew et al., (2023) estimated that an 1117 kWh battery planted corn for 14 h in a day and a 2658 kWh battery could plant the corn for 15 h. Their study confirmed that the estimated battery discharge rates and usable times were reasonable for rotary tillage. In addition, results of the battery mass and volume proved that a higher density of the batteries provides longer battery usable time or operation time. However, it may add heavier weight and wider spaces. Lui et al.35 stated that the size of the lithium-ion battery was increased for higher energy density and faster charging. They noticed that the volumetric energy density increased with battery diameter. However, the gravimetric energy density increased for certain diameters, and further energy dropped with increasing diameter. Therefore, a balance among battery energy density (capacity), mass, and available space in the tractor must be optimized to ensure efficient power management for full-day agricultural operations. However, the vehicle speed at M-slow gear stages was very high for the rotary tillage, accounting for approximately 6.30 km/hr. Kim et al.36 stated that the ground speed of 3.77 km/hr was relatively fast for rotary tillage. They reported that the rotovator just walked on the soil at high ground speed due to a low transmission load. Though the rotary tillage at the gear stages of L-normal was possible, the vehicle speed was comparatively lower, which was around 2.50 km/hr. Therefore, the vehicle speed at L-fast gear stages (2.80 km/hr) would be suggested for rotary tillage using a single-motor electric tractor. Moreover, the battery energy density of 64.8 kWh (900 Ah) would be balanced with practical weight and space limitations to maintain proper traction and field performance for full-day operations, especially for rotary tillage. Conclusions

The objectives of this study were to introduce a technique for estimating the battery usable times of a single-motor electric tractor based on various working loads and suitable battery capacity for full-day agricultural operations, especially for rotary tillage. In the dynamometer test, average battery powers were 6.25, 8.76, and 14.51 kW, respectively, at light, medium, and heavy-duty operations, as well as the battery usable times were 3.00, 2.33, and 1.25 h, respectively. The lowest and highest axle powers consumed at the Low and High gear stages, respectively, but the efficiencies at all gear stages were not significantly different. The PTO efficiency was estimated at around 77%. For the rotary tillage, the battery usable times at the L-normal and L-fast gear stages were estimated at almost 2.92 and 2.74 h, respectively, which were similar to the battery usable times of light and medium-duty estimated using the dynamometer test. These results confirmed that the dynamometer test could be feasible and authentic in analyzing the battery performance of the electric tractor.

It was noticed that the estimated usable times during rotary tillage for considering continuous operation at L-normal and L-fast were over 8 h a day when the battery capacity was 900 Ah, whereas the battery discharge rates were almost 0.58 and 0.53%, respectively, except for the gear stages of M-slow. Battery mass and volume estimation confirmed that higher energy storage required a larger and heavier battery pack for full-day agricultural operations. The battery energy density of 64.8 kWh (900 Ah) at L-fast gear stage would be a balance with practical weight and space limitations to maintain proper working speed and field performance for full-day operations, especially for rotary tillage.

The drawback of this study is that the suitable and economic gear stage was suggested for the rotary tillage operation based on the vehicle speed and energy consumption, but the relevant parameters such as traction efficiency, slip ratio, and effective field capacity were not considered. However, we believe that this study provides a reasonable, feasible, and suitable technique for the estimation of the battery usable times and discharge rates, considering real agricultural working scenarios, especially for the rotary tillage operation using a single-motor electric tractor. The manufacturers of an electric tractor could select a suitable battery for the electric tractor based on energy density, mass, and volume of the battery, which might continuously operate for a whole day (at least 8 h). Most importantly, users could conduct the rotary tillage using the single-motor electric tractor and select a suitable, efficient, and economical gear stage for the rotary tillage.
