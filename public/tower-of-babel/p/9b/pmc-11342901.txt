Incomplete femoral neck fracture with characteristics of atypical femoral fracture: A case report and literature review
Authors: Yasuhiro Higai, Yuji Kanaya, Hiroyoshi Hagiwara, Yuichirou Yano, Takashi Fukushima, Norihiro Akazawa, Takahiro Shimizu, Sueo Nakama, Katsushi Takeshita
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Femoral neck fractures are considered exclusion criteria for atypical femoral fractures; however, femoral neck fractures with atypical femoral fractures have also been reported. Here, we report a case of a femoral neck fracture with features of atypical femoral fractures and a review of the related literature. A 76-year-old woman had been treated with denosumab for five years. The patient had no history of trauma, but complained of pain in the right hip. Radiological examination revealed an incomplete fracture of the right femoral neck. Internal fixation was performed, but fusion was delayed. Bipolar hemiarthroplasty was performed four months after surgery. In a patient with long-term bisphosphonate use, complaints of hip pain could indicate the possibility of an atypical fracture. According to the existing literature, as with atypical femoral fractures, this fracture has a high risk of delayed union; therefore, temporary prosthetic replacement should be considered as a treatment option.

Introduction

Long-term use of bisphosphonates inhibits bone turnover and causes atypical femoral fractures (AFF). According to the diagnostic criteria of the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research (ASBMR), AFF is defined as a fracture occurring from just below the lesser trochanter to the femoral shaft; femoral neck fractures are considered as exclusion criteria [1].

Considering that atypical fractures tend to be concentrated at sites subjected to the maximum tensile load, the lateral cortex of the femoral neck may not withstand this tensile stress resulting in atypical fractures [2]. Recently, femoral neck fractures with characteristics of AFF have been reported [[3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8], [9]]; however, the characteristics of these fractures have not been fully elucidated. Here, we report a femoral neck fracture with features of AFF and present a relevant literature review.

Case presentation

A 76-year-old woman was referred to our hospital with right hip pain persisting for one week. The patient had been treated with denosumab for osteoporosis for the previous five years and had undergone total hip arthroplasty for a left femoral neck fracture two years prior. Plain radiography at another clinic showed no abnormalities and a follow-up was ordered. The patient visited our hospital because of difficulty walking due to pain.

Before admission, the patient could walk independently. Her Japanese Orthopaedic Association (JOA) score was 96/76 (right/left). Plain radiography (Fig. 1), and computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (Fig. 2A and B) revealed an incomplete fracture of the lateral cortex of the right femoral neck. Bone mineral density was 0.840 g/cm2 in the lumbar spine and 0.899 g/cm2 in the right femoral neck. One month post-surgery, a radiograph showed a fracture line on the lateral aspect of the neck extending near the upper border of the lesser trochanter (Fig. 3B). Three months post-surgery, the fracture line had become more obvious (Fig. 3C). The patient could walk using a walker, but experienced weight-bearing pain; the JOA score was 44/74 (right/left). To relieve pain and improve her walking ability, the implant was removed and bipolar hemiarthroplasty was performed four months post-surgery (Fig. 4). Postoperatively, full weight-bearing rehabilitation was allowed. Two weeks post-surgery, the patient could walk with a cane without pain. Discussion

Our patient experienced an incomplete fracture of the femoral neck, albeit with no history of repeated stress or trauma. The fracture line was a transverse fracture localized in the lateral cortex with no comminution at the fracture site and no localized thickening of the bone cortex. Thus, this case met four of the five major ASBMR diagnostic criteria for AFF and the diagnostic criteria for AFF in areas other than the location of occurrence [1]. Atypical femoral neck fracture (AFNF) is an incomplete fracture that occurs in the lateral cortex of the femoral neck with a history of long-term bisphosphonate therapy and no repeated stress or trauma [6].

Therefore, a differential diagnosis of stress fractures is necessary. Stress fractures have historically been categorized into three types: fatigue fractures, insufficiency fractures, and pathological fractures caused by tumors. Fatigue fractures occur mainly in young people who are highly active and exposed to repetitive external forces. Insufficiency fractures can occur spontaneously during routine activities or with mild exercise loads, even without specific trauma, in bones with reduced tensile strength. Stress fractures of the femoral neck are classified into transverse and compression fractures based on radiographic findings. Transverse fractures are common in the elderly and are characterized by a radiolucent line in the lateral cortex of the femoral neck. Compression fractures, common in young people, are highly stable and characterized by localized decreased radiolucency on the medial side of the femoral neck. This case fits the description of an insufficiency fracture in terms of pathogenesis and a transverse fracture in terms of imaging features. Considering the history of long-term bisphosphonate use, this case meets the definition of AFNF [6]. With only case reports in literature, definite criteria distinguishing AFNF from stress fractures are lacking. Oh et al. proposed a new classification of stress fractures that adds atypical fractures as a fourth category, and stated that insufficiency fractures and atypical fractures should be distinguished from each other in terms of different pathologies [10].

In many cases, internal fixation was selected for incomplete fractures at the time of diagnosis [3,4,6,8,9]. Therefore, internal fixation was chosen in this case as well. Canulated cancerous screws (CCS) are commonly used for non-displaced femoral neck fractures because they are inexpensive and minimally invasive. In recent years, implants with superior angular stability, which can provide more rigid fixation, have become available. Bone union with AFF is more difficult to achieve than with normal fractures. It is assumed that AFNF has a mechanism of occurrence similar to that of AFF. Given the tendency to progress to Pauwels type III fractures, achieving bone union can be difficult even with appropriate internal fixation. However, in certain cases, radiographic healing has been achieved despite delayed union after internal fixation [3,5,8,9]. In our case, internal fixation with a three-month follow-up was performed. Bone union was not achieved and hip pain persisted. An additional surgery with bipolar arthroplasty to improve the patient's walking ability yielded satisfactory results. Currently, evidence to determine whether the initial treatment for AFNF should involve internal fixation or prosthetic replacement is insufficient. In general, repeated surgeries can be physically and financially burdensome for elderly patients. Therefore, for AFNF, one-stage prosthetic replacement should be considered as a treatment option, depending on the patient's general condition and activities of daily living.

In conclusion, we encountered a case of a femoral neck fracture with AFF characteristics. Thorough examinations, with cognizance of the possibility of an atypical fracture, are crucial in patients with long-term bisphosphonate use complaining of hip joint pain. Because AFNF is associated with a high risk of delayed union, one-stage prosthetic replacement should also be considered as a treatment option.
