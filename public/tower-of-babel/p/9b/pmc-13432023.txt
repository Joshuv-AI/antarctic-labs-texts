Guidance for Specialised Cancer Nursing of People With Gynaecological Cancers: A Scoping Review
Authors: Natalie Williams, Georgia Halkett, Tracey Moroney, Sharon MacLean, Olivia Cook
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundGynaecological cancer nurses are crucial to the delivery of quality, specialised care to meet the needs of people with these cancers. The extent of evidence‐based guidance to support nursing care is unknown.AimTo identify and describe existing international guidance for specialised gynaecological cancer nursing care.DesignScoping review.MethodsThis scoping review utilised the participants, concept, context framework with narrative synthesis.Data SourcesDatabases including MEDLINE (Ovid), CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCO), Proquest Central (Alumni) and Google Scholar were searched on 4 April 2025 along with relevant websites and reference lists of included sources. Included sources referred to the nurse's role and provided clinical guidelines, practice recommendations or equivalent guidance on how nurses provide specialised care. Sources were limited to those published in English from 2009 to present.ResultsOf 1905 sources identified, 12 met the inclusion criteria. Sources were published by authors in seven countries between 2014 and 2024. Most sources related to one specific gynaecological cancer type and/or one aspect or time point of care. Sources varied in the volume of nurse relevant content. No individual source provided comprehensive nursing guidance across all gynaecological cancer types.ConclusionsInternationally published guidance for specialised gynaecological cancer nursing presents gaps in available evidence for care. We recommend comprehensive guidance resource development to meet clinical nurses' needs and improve translation of evidence‐based nursing practice.Implications for the Profession and/or Patient CareThe current lack of comprehensive published guidance for specialised gynaecological cancer nursing does not meet reported needs of nurses working in clinical practice and may be contributing to variation in nursing care delivery. Introduction

Worldwide estimates indicate that gynaecological cancers were diagnosed in over 1.4 million people in 2024 (Ferlay et al. 2024). Gynaecological cancers represent a collection of tumour types affecting the female reproductive system including cancers of the ovary, fallopian tube/s, peritoneum, uterus, cervix, vulva, vagina and trophoblastic cells (Cancer Australia 2025). As increasing numbers of cancers are diagnosed, the numbers of people living with, or after cancer are also rising (Soerjomataram and Bray 2021; National Cancer Policy Board (U.S.) et al. 2005). Nurses with specialised knowledge and skills are recognised as critical in reducing the burden of cancer. Nurses provide clinical and supportive care throughout diagnosis, treatment, surveillance and beyond, including the continuing impacts of treatment and disease (Yates et al. 2020).

Recommendations suggest people with a gynaecological cancer should have access to a specialist cancer nurse to enhance their care (Cancer Council Victoria and Department of Health Victoria 2020, 2021a, 2021b; Cook et al. 2021; Buckley et al. 2018). People experiencing gynaecological cancers may experience a range of unique consequences requiring specialised nursing care. Examples include, but are not limited to, loss of fertility, early‐onset of menopause, sexual dysfunction, lower leg lymphoedema, bladder and bowel dysfunction, stigma and body image disturbance (Cancer Council Victoria and Department of Health Victoria 2020, 2021a, 2021b).

In Australia, more than 23,000 people are currently living with a gynaecological cancer (Cancer Australia 2025). A recent study reports that Australian gynaecological cancer nurses have called for the development of an evidence‐based nursing resource to guide their practice (Cook et al. 2020) and a programme of work is currently underway to pursue this. A qualitative exploration revealed this guidance resource should be a practical tool to support care delivery and be designed with functionality for use in everyday nursing practice (Williams et al. 2024). The guidance resource should aim to reduce currently recognised variations in care, acknowledge diversity across nursing roles and address barriers faced by nurses (Williams et al. 2024) while remaining adaptable (Cook et al. 2020). Nurses want a resource focused on describing the nurse role and specific guidance on how specialised gynaecological cancer nursing care should be delivered (Williams et al. 2024, 2025).

In Australia, specialised cancer nursing may be provided by specialist nurses, considered as those demonstrating in‐depth knowledge, skills, and expertise in their area of practice (Chief Nursing and Midwifery Officers Australia 2020). Additionally, specialised care is provided across a range of practice settings by cancer nurses (Aranda and Yates 2009) not defined as specialists within their role titles. Collectively, these nurses aim to reduce the burden of disease by addressing the unique physical and psychosocial needs of people with gynaecological cancers, and lead patient advocacy, education, research, workforce capacity and health service administration (Downing et al. 2023).

Guidance resources for other cancer nursing specialties exist in various formats in Australia, such as a model of care for breast cancer nursing (McGrath Foundation 2024), practice framework and competencies standards for the prostate cancer specialist nurse (Sykes 2013) and nursing practice principles for radiation oncology (Radiation Oncology Practice Standards Working Group 2024). The breast cancer nursing model of care example is a resource designed to define and standardise the delivery of breast care nursing across the continuum of care and is presented as an interactive portable document format to be used as a reference guide during care provision (McGrath Foundation 2024). Models of nursing care are defined as including an evidence‐based and person‐centred theoretical basis, nurse‐led organisational work methods and practical methods of care delivery (Clarke et al. 2025). Other guidance resources such as the prostate specialist nurse and radiation oncology examples are presented as static portable document formats to define the scope and recommended practice principles (respectively), rather than explicitly describing the practical application to nursing care (Sykes 2013).

While gynaecological cancer nurses in Australia have access to treatment pathway guidance through Optimal Care Pathways for ovarian, endometrial and cervical cancers (Cancer Council Victoria and Department of Health Victoria 2020, 2021a, 2021b) and point of care treatment protocols (Cancer Institute NSW 2025), no comprehensive guidance resource such as those for breast, prostate or radiation oncology nurses exists for specialised gynaecological cancer nursing in Australia. A small number of international papers on aspects of care relevant to nursing care have been published, such as for menopause, post‐surgical vulvar cancer symptom management and gestational trophoblastic disease (BMS and BGCS 2024; Kobleder et al. 2016; Singh et al. 2023). The extent of other existing guidance for gynaecological cancer nursing was unknown. Considering the current development of a guidance resource for specialised gynaecological cancer nursing in Australia, a scoping review was essential to identify existing evidence, ascertain evidence gaps and provide a basis from which content could be developed.

Aim

The aim of this scoping review was to identify and describe existing international guidance for the specialised nursing care of people with gynaecological cancers. In doing so the review aimed to answer the question ‘What sources of guidance for gynaecological cancer nursing exist?’ addressing the following objectives:

Methods

Design

A scoping review utilising the participants, concept, context framework (Peters et al. 2021) with narrative synthesis was undertaken. A scoping review aims to map the available evidence on a topic in an exploratory manner without being limited by the type of source (Munn et al. 2022) and was chosen as the most appropriate method to comprehensively investigate this concept. A priori protocol was registered with Open Science Framework https://osf.io/ntdzj.

Inclusion Criteria

Participants

Sources were included that identified the role of nurses delivering specialised nursing care for people with a gynaecological cancer. Sources that did not identify nurses within the content were excluded.

Concept

Sources were included if they provided clinical guidance, recommendations for practice or other equivalent guidance (such as algorithms or consensus statements) where the purpose was to explain how specialised gynaecological cancer nursing care is or should be provided. Sources that provided guidance outside of these parameters, for example broadly suggesting how gynaecological cancer nursing care should be delivered or describing a specialised gynaecological cancer nursing intervention but without the provision of practice recommendations, clinical guidance statements or equivalent, were excluded.

Specialised gynaecological cancer nursing was defined as the nursing care of people with a gynaecological cancer. Sources that provided nursing guidance amongst all tumour streams relevant to gynaecological cancers but did not specify gynaecological cancer nursing care were excluded to reduce ambiguity among reviewers.

Context

Sources providing guidance for specialised gynaecological cancer nursing care in any international health care context or setting (i.e., primary or acute care) or by any professional or government body were included. Sources not written in English were excluded.

Search Methods

An initial limited search of MEDLINE (Ovid) was undertaken to identify keywords and index terms, then adapted for each additional database. Following strategy development, with assistance of a health sciences librarian, we searched MEDLINE (Ovid), CINAHL Ultimate (EBSCO), Proquest Central (Alumni) and Google Scholar. Tables S1–S4 demonstrate the search strategies. An advanced Google search and targeted website search (Table S5) was undertaken. Additionally, a keyword search on the website of a key multidisciplinary gynaecologic oncology journal was conducted. This was chosen in the absence of an existing gynaecologic oncology journal exclusively for nursing care. Grey literature search parameters are detailed in Table S6. Sources were initially limited to dates from 1990. The commencement date was chosen to align with the time literature on specialist cancer nursing roles first emerged. This was later refined during full text screening to those published from 2009, the year specialised cancer nursing was formally defined in Australia (Aranda and Yates 2009) and was considered appropriate to maximise relevancy to current practice. The reference lists of included sources were searched for eligible citations.

Types of Sources

This review included sources from both published journals and grey literature. Literature from journals included primary research studies using quantitative, qualitative or mixed methods in addition to systematic, scoping or integrative reviews. Grey literature was limited to sources of guidance including clinical practice guidelines, frameworks, protocols, care pathways, algorithms, models of care or other sources aiming to guide the nursing care of people with a gynaecological cancer found on websites published by professional bodies or associations, both government and non‐government.

Source Selection

All searches were conducted on 4 April 2025. Identified citations were uploaded into EndNote version 20 (EndNote 2013) as an initial repository and then into Covidence Systematic Review Software (2025). Duplicate references were identified and removed. Following a pilot screening test of 20 sources undertaken with four authors to clarify inclusion criteria, all titles and abstracts were screened for eligibility by two or more independent reviewers. After screening, remaining sources were retrieved for full text review by the primary author. Any uncertainties about inclusion were discussed with at least two other authors until a final consensus was reached. Data Extraction and Synthesis

A data extraction table to address the review aims was developed in Microsoft Excel by the primary author (N.W.) and reviewed by the remaining authors. Information was extracted from the full text sources including title, authors, country and year of publication, source type and development details (if available), types of gynaecological cancer addressed, target audience, nursing related topics and key guidance points. The potential existence of other versions of included sources was examined by either reviewing references citing the source through a search on the Web of Science database and/or reviewing the publishing organisation's website to cross‐check the source is the most recent available version. Data were initially extracted by the primary author and reviewed by the remaining authors. Quality appraisal, data synthesis and qualitative analyses are beyond the purpose of a scoping review, and as such, a narrative summary was formulated to address the review questions (Pollock et al. 2023).

Included sources were published within an 11‐year period from August 2014 to October 2024. Seven sources were authored by individuals with clinical and/or university affiliations (Kobleder et al. 2016; Singh et al. 2023; Bakker et al. 2014; Campbell et al. 2019; Long et al. 2016; Cook et al. 2017; Senn et al. 2015), three on behalf of professional societies (Morrison et al. 2021; Reed et al. 2020; Taylor et al. 2024), and the remaining two by Australian government organisations (ACI 2020; Cancer Australia 2020). Eleven sources represented the only and/or latest version available; however, the Bakker et al. (2014) consensus was later modified in Brazil (Matos et al. 2019) with no modification to the nursing relevant guidance.

Type of Guidance

Six sources labelled as guidelines within their title provided variable descriptions, for example ‘guidelines’ (Taylor et al. 2024; Long et al. 2016), ‘evidence based guidelines’ (Kobleder et al. 2016), ‘best practice nursing guidelines’ (Singh et al. 2023) and ‘guidelines: recommendations for practice’ (Morrison et al. 2021; Reed et al. 2020). One source describes the ‘development of an algorithm’ (Senn et al. 2015). One is described as a ‘guide to clinical practice’ (ACI 2020) and another as a resource outlining roles and responsibilities of shared care team members (Cancer Australia 2020). Two sources describe research methods including a systematic review (Cook et al. 2017) and a consensus using the Delphi method (Bakker et al. 2014). One source was published as a special issue article to summarise current recommendations to guide nurses (Campbell et al. 2019).

Methods of Development

Guidance was developed using varied methods. Most guidelines were developed utilising an expert clinician working group, sometimes including a peer review process (Kobleder et al. 2016; Singh et al. 2023; Morrison et al. 2021; Reed et al. 2020; Taylor et al. 2024; ACI 2020; Bakker et al. 2014; Long et al. 2016; Senn et al. 2015). The systematic review by Cook et al. (2017) did not include revision external to authors as is typical for this methodology (Cook et al. 2017). The special article provided little detail on methods used (Campbell et al. 2019) and Cancer Australia (2020) did not provide development details.

Relevance to Nurses

Of the 12 included sources, three indicate nurses exclusively as the intended audience, for example stating ‘nurse’ in the title or being published in a nursing journal (Kobleder et al. 2016; Singh et al. 2023; Campbell et al. 2019). The remaining nine sources are published in journals or websites with the intended audience as multi‐disciplinary health care professionals (such as medical doctors, nurses and allied health professionals) providing gynaecological cancer care and include specific guidance relating to nurses (Morrison et al. 2021; Reed et al. 2020; Taylor et al. 2024; ACI 2020; Cancer Australia 2020; Bakker et al. 2014; Long et al. 2016; Cook et al. 2017; Senn et al. 2015). The volume of content relevant to nurses varies from two to three recommendations for nursing care delivery (Reed et al. 2020; ACI 2020; Cook et al. 2017) to more detailed explanations of the content and manner in which nursing care should be delivered. These included recommendations or guidance points (Taylor et al. 2024; Long et al. 2016), a flowchart (Singh et al. 2023), algorithm (Senn et al. 2015) or tables detailing care at significant time points (Kobleder et al. 2016; Cancer Australia 2020; Bakker et al. 2014) or for specific care needs (Morrison et al. 2021; Campbell et al. 2019). A detailed summary of the guidance relevant to nursing care from each source is provided in Table 1.

Nursing Guidance Content

Gynaecological cancer types addressed include cervical cancer exclusively in two sources (Reed et al. 2020; Long et al. 2016), uterine (or endometrial) cancer exclusively in two sources (Morrison et al. 2021; Cancer Australia 2020), vulva cancer exclusively in two sources (Kobleder et al. 2016; Senn et al. 2015) and gestational trophoblastic disease exclusively in one source (Singh et al. 2023). One source refers collectively to ‘gynaecologic cancer’ in the title but refers to ovarian, cervical, vulvar and endometrial only within the content (Campbell et al. 2019). One source provides guidance on all gynaecological cancers however the recommendations relevant to nurses only relate to vulva cancer (ACI 2020). Three sources refer to all gynaecological cancers either collectively (Cook et al. 2017) or in relation to specific aspect of care, namely radiotherapy treatment (Bakker et al. 2014) or menopause (Taylor et al. 2024). Discussion

The critical role of cancer nurses to deliver specialised care and optimise patient outcomes is undisputed across cancer care broadly (Yates et al. 2020) and in the context of gynaecological cancers (Cancer Council Victoria and Department of Health Victoria 2020, 2021a, 2021b; Buckley et al. 2018; Cook et al. 2019). Considering the extensive incidence of gynaecological cancers globally and the consequential volume of nursing workforce providing care, we suggest these 12 sources of international guidance present significant gaps to support quality, evidence‐based nursing care and meet the needs of people experiencing these cancers.

It is acknowledged that nurses likely seek guidance to address these gaps from other sources independent of guidelines (or equivalent guidance), for example education packages, such as the International Gynecologic Cancer Society nursing certificate (International Gynecologic Cancer Society 2024), professional networking events, and conferences, and from colleagues or mentors. Inconsistency resulting from nurses accessing a variety of education and guidance sources may contribute to the known variation in provided care (Williams et al. 2024).

Other sources of evidence to guide care may be found as published journal articles, such as reports detailing the investigation of gynaecological cancer nursing interventions or publications broadly suggesting ways in which nurses should, could or do provide care. While these publications contribute to building evidence, a lack of distinct recommendations for practice, clinical guidance statements or equivalent may have a limited effect to change practice. While there is no internationally recognised standardised approach to guideline development, they typically include the development of a working party, a literature review, development of recommendations, stakeholder consultation and review, followed by publication and dissemination (De Leo et al. 2023).

Additionally, it is recognised that other guidelines and robust forms of evidence relevant to gynaecological cancer nursing exist but did not meet the inclusion criteria of this review due to the absence of citing the role of the nurse or providing detail on nursing care provision. Notably, in an Australian context, these included the Optimal Care Pathways for ovarian, endometrial and cervical cancers recognised as principal sources of national cancer care guidance (Cancer Council Victoria and Department of Health Victoria 2020, 2021a, 2021b). Similarly, other internationally recognised guidelines for gynaecological cancer care also did not provide detail on nursing roles or care, likely considered outside of the guideline scope (ESGO 2024; Tokunaga et al. 2021; Yamagami et al. 2020). Other sources from Australia that may be considered relevant, but did not meet the inclusion criteria were those detailing aspects of care such as side effect and toxicity management of gynaecological cancers (NSW Government eviQ 2025), palliative care (Palliative Care Australia 2025), care of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people with cancer (Cancer Australia 2024) and education modules, for example caring for lesbian, gay, bisexual, transgender and queer communities (NSW Government eviQ 2025) or the psychosexual care of women with a gynaecological cancer (NSW Government eviQ 2018). These examples similarly either did not cite the role of the nurse and/or provide detail specific to gynaecological cancer nursing.

It is unclear how nurses working outside of specialist gynaecological cancer nursing roles, such as those working in multiple tumour settings or general nursing roles, or nurses newly providing care, access guidance. Previous exploration of Australian nurse perspectives on this concept recommends a comprehensive foundational resource is needed to guide nurses providing care across a variety of health service settings (Williams et al. 2024). It has been suggested that formalised guidance would assist in legitimising nursing roles (Cook et al. 2020) and succession planning, supporting new nurses working in the specialty (Williams et al. 2024).

Considering previous research investigating the needs of gynaecological cancer nurses regarding guidance (Cook et al. 2020; Williams et al. 2024, 2025) and the results of this scoping review, we recommend the development of a guidance resource to synthesise current evidence and address the needs of gynaecological cancer nurses. A dynamic, accessible and interactive resource exemplified in Australian breast cancer nursing as a Model of Care format (McGrath Foundation 2024), is suggested to combine practical care delivery strategies with current evidence and a person‐centred approach (Clarke et al. 2025), considered critical in quality, integrated cancer care (Yates et al. 2020; Krishnasamy et al. 2023). To inform this resource and address the identified gaps in current clinical guidance for nurses, we recommend both consultation with clinical experts and people with lived experience, and additional mapping to broader guidance documents that did not meet the inclusion criteria of this review to ensure resource content adequately addresses all stages of care and gynaecological cancer populations.

When applying existing evidence to practice, a recognition of the context in which it will be implemented is critical to ensure any unique needs of the population are met, particularly groups known to experience inequitable health outcomes. In Australia, these subpopulation groups include Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, those living in geographically isolated regions and other populations with diverse needs known to experience health inequities (Cancer Australia 2023; Australian Government Department of Health 2018). Future research should include the perspectives of people with lived experience, including those experiencing health inequities, and other relevant stakeholders to ensure specialised gynaecological cancer nursing is meeting the needs of these priority populations. With continued investment and reach in cancer nursing across Australia (Dragon 2025), it is important to ensure that nurses are supported to provide evidence‐based care to address the specialised needs of all women and people experiencing gynaecological cancers.

Limitations

This review represents the first to scope and summarise existing published clinical guidance for gynaecological cancer nursing. Utilising a scoping review allowed for inclusion of grey literature and other sources of evidence representing a comprehensive approach and is considered a strength. Given the parameters of a scoping review, a critical appraisal of source quality, credibility and appropriateness was not included. While details of the development process for each source were extracted, this is not intended as an appraisal of the quality of the evidence. Sources were limited to three main databases and Google Scholar and included those available in English only. It is feasible that a broader search may have captured additional information. The targeted website search was limited to those considered likely to include gynaecological cancer clinical guidance or in countries with similar healthcare systems to Australia which may have excluded other existing guidance. This review sought to identify existing sources of guidance on specialised nursing care of people with gynaecological cancers; thereby, the search parameters were limited to those explicitly addressing this topic.

Conclusions

This scoping review identified 12 sources of clinical guidelines, recommendations for practice or equivalent guidance specific to nursing roles and providing guidance on how nurses provide specialised care for people with gynaecological cancers. Considering the incidence of gynaecological cancers worldwide and the volume of the nursing workforce providing care, we consider these sources to present significant gaps to adequately support quality, evidence‐based nursing care. The development of a comprehensive nursing guidance resource with contextual relevance to Australia is recommended. During development, we recommend using the evidence identified in this scoping review as a basis from which to pursue expert consultation and additional mapping of more broadly published recommendations for gynaecological cancer care mapped to the stages of care and needs of priority populations. This would ensure the inclusion of comprehensive content to address the needs of people with gynaecological cancers and improve the quality of care.
