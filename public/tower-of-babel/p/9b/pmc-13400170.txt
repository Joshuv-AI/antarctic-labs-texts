High-dose furmonertinib as first-line treatment for untreated EGFR-mutated advanced NSCLC with central nervous system metastases: A phase 2 trial
Authors: Huan Yan, Wenjuan Jiang, Yi Xiong, Li Liu, Renzhi Zhang, Zhaohui Ruan, Zhe Huang, Jiacheng Dai, Fang Tian, Chunhua Zhou, Zhan Wang, Haiyan Yang, Wei Tao, Hongbo Zheng, Qinqin Xu, Nong Yang, Liang Zeng, Yongchang Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBrain metastases in epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) frequently contribute to treatment failure. This phase II study aims to evaluate furmonertinib (160 mg daily) in 40 untreated patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC and central nervous system (CNS) metastases. At a median follow-up of 18.66 months, the blinded independent central-review-assessed median progression-free survival (mPFS) and intracranial PFS are 16.59 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 12.77–20.41) and 17.83 months (95% CI: 14.16–21.50), respectively. Systemic objective response rate (ORR) is 92.5%, and intracranial ORR is 95.0%, with consistent investigator assessments. Grade ≥3-treatment-related adverse events occur in 15.0% of patients. Exploratory profiling of paired plasma and cerebrospinal fluid reveals significant early molecular response, with high EGFR mutation clearance (85.7% plasma; 66.7% cerebrospinal fluid [CSF]). Furmonertinib shows promising intracranial efficacy with manageable safety as first-line treatment for EGFR-mutated NSCLC with CNS metastases. This study is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05379803).Keywords: furmonertinib, non-small cell lung cancer, EGFR mutation, central nervous system metastases, third-generation EGFR-TKI

Introduction

Non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounts for approximately 80%–85% of all lung cancer cases.1 Epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutations, present in up to 50% of Asian patients with NSCLC,2,3 are associated with an increased risk of central nervous system (CNS) metastasis. Although EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) have significantly improved treatment outcomes, their efficacy in the CNS remains limited due to suboptimal blood-brain barrier penetration.4,5,6 A retrospective analysis reported that 26% of patients had brain metastases at initial diagnosis, underscoring the considerable impact of CNS involvement on quality of life of patients and prognosis.7,8,9

The FLAURA study demonstrated that first-line treatment with the third-generation EGFR TKI osimertinib significantly prolonged survival outcomes in patients with EGFR-mutated NSCLC compared with first-generation EGFR-TKIs. However, subgroup analyses revealed reduced benefit for patients with baseline CNS metastases and no significant overall survival advantage observed in this subgroup.10,11 Similarly, the AURA3 trial, which evaluated patients with acquired T790M mutations, reported shorter progression-free survival (PFS) among those with CNS metastases (8.5 months vs. 4.2 months).12 Despite osimertinib’s improved CNS penetration, intracranial disease progression remains a leading cause of treatment failure. The BLOOM study, which assessed CNS efficacy, reported an intracranial objective response rate (ORR) of 54%; however, over 40% of patients experienced CNS progression during treatment.13 The J-AURA14 and REAL-GO15 studies further corroborated these findings, highlighting persistent CNS progression, particularly in patients with multiple brain metastases or leptomeningeal disease. These data emphasized the limitations of standard-dose osimertinib in achieving durable intracranial control and highlighted the need for optimized therapeutic strategies for EGFR-mutated NSCLC with CNS metastases.

High-dose osimertinib has been explored as a potential strategy to enhance CNS efficacy.16 However, increasing the daily dose has been associated with a higher incidence of grade ≥3 adverse events.17 The AURA first-line cohort revealed no additional efficacy benefit with high-dose osimertinib compared with the conventional dose, likely due to the necessity of dose reductions during treatment, with 60% of patients requiring a reduction to standard dosing within a median of 3.1 months.18

Furmonertinib (AST2818) is a highly penetrant EGFR TKI approved in China for the treatment of advanced NSCLC harboring EGFR-sensitive and T790M mutation.19,20 Preclinical studies have demonstrated blood-brain barrier penetration by both furmonertinib and its primary active metabolite, AST5902, suggesting potential CNS efficacy.21,22 A phase II dose-expansion study indicated that high-dose furmonertinib (160 mg) led to improved intracranial ORR (84.6%) and prolonged intracranial PFS (19.3 months) compared with standard dosing, without a corresponding increase in grade ≥3 adverse events or treatment-related discontinuations.23 These findings suggest a favorable therapeutic window for high-dose administration; however, current evidence remains limited due to small sample sizes and a lack of prospective CNS-specific evaluations.

Given the challenges in achieving optimal CNS control with standard-dose osimertinib and the promising CNS exposure and tolerability profile of high-dose furmonertinib, we conducted a prospective, multicenter, single-arm, phase II study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of high-dose (160 mg) furmonertinib as first-line therapy in patients with EGFR-mutation-positive advanced NSCLC and baseline CNS metastases. Furthermore, this study aims to identify objective biomarkers by characterizing the genomic features of cell-free DNA (cfDNA) in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and peripheral blood and exploring cfDNA-based indicators associated with furmonertinib efficacy.

Results

Patient overview

Between November 2022 and February 2025, a total of 55 patients were screened, of whom 15 did not meet the eligibility criteria (13 due to brain metastases measuring less than 10 mm and 2 who withdrew consent). Consequently, 40 patients were enrolled in the study (Figure 1; Figure S1). All 40 patients received the study treatment and were included in both the full analysis set and CNS evaluable-for-response set. All participants had NSCLC with EGFR mutations and untreated CNS metastases (including brain and/or leptomeningeal metastases). Among them, 17 patients (42.5%) harbored EGFR exon 19 deletions, 22 patients (55.0%) had exon 21 L858R mutations, and 1 patient was detected with both mutations. Baseline patient characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age of the study cohort was 60 years (range: 33–75 years), with a predominance of female patients (n = 26, 65%) and never-smokers (n = 27, 67.5%). The majority of patients (n = 32, 80%) had an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (PS) of 1. All patients had histologically confirmed lung adenocarcinoma and baseline brain metastases. A majority (70%, n = 28) had more than three intracranial lesions, with a median maximum intracranial lesion diameter of 12 mm (interquartile range [IQR]: 10.5–16.0 mm). Intracranial edema was present in 57.5% of patients (n = 23). Additionally, 13 patients (32.5%) had concurrent liver metastases and 23 (57.5%) had bone metastases.

As of the data cutoff (July 29, 2025), 18 patients remained on treatment, while 22 had discontinued therapy. Among those who discontinued, 17 patients experienced disease progression, four patients experienced adverse events, and a patient died unexpectedly (Figure 1).

Survival outcomes

All participants received at least one cycle of study treatment. In the intention-to-treat (ITT) population, the median follow-up time was 18.66 months (95% confidence interval [CI]: 12.53–24.79). As assessed by blinded independent central review (BICR), the median PFS was 16.59 months (95% CI: 12.77–20.41) in the ITT population (Figure 2A). In the CNS evaluable-for-response set, the median intracranial PFS was 17.83 months (95% CI: 14.16–21.50), with 1-year and 2-year intracranial PFS rates of 69.2% (95% CI: 67.6–70.8) and 40.7% (95% CI: 20.5–60.9), respectively (Figure 2B). At the time of data cutoff, the median overall survival (OS) had not yet been reached (Figure 2C).

Investigator-assessed efficacy demonstrated comparable results. The median PFS was 16.59 months (95% CI: 12.77–20.41), and the median intracranial PFS was 17.61 months (95% CI: 14.06–21.16) (Figures S3A and S3B). The systemic ORR was 87.5% (95% CI: 76.8–98.2), with an intracranial ORR of 95.0% (95% CI: 87.9–100.0) (Figures S3C and S3D). The DCR was 100% for both systemic and intracranial disease (Table 2). Among the eight patients who experienced intracranial progression, one also had simultaneous extracranial progression; seven patients had progression at brain parenchymal lesions and one had progression due to leptomeningeal metastases alone.

Safety evaluation

Safety assessments were conducted in all 40 patients (Table 3). Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) were reported in 24 patients (60.0%). Grade 1–2 TRAEs were observed in 18 patients (45.0%), while grade 3–5 TRAEs occurred in six patients (15.0%). The most common grade 1–2 TRAEs included limb or joint pain (20.0%, n = 8), oral ulcers (15.0%, n = 6), diarrhea (15.0%, n = 6), rash (15.0%, n = 6), and diarrhea (15.0%, n = 6). The most frequent grade 3–5 TRAE was interstitial pneumonia (5.0%, n = 2). Four patients discontinued treatment due to adverse events, including two cases of interstitial pneumonia, one case of grade 3 hyperglycemia, and one case of grade 3 rash. No treatment-related deaths were reported. Overall, high-dose furmonertinib demonstrated a tolerable and manageable safety profile.

Biomarker exploration

A total of 20 patients were enrolled for exploratory biomarker analysis. At baseline, 4 patients (20%) were excluded due to quality control (QC) failure, resulting in 16 qualified paired plasma and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) samples. At week-6 post-treatment, QC filtering excluded 1 plasma and 4 CSF samples from baseline-qualified cohort, leaving 15 plasma and 12 CSF evaluable samples. Baseline mutation profiles are presented in Figure 4A. At baseline, EGFR-sensitizing mutations were detected in 93.8% (15/16) of plasma and 56.3% (9/16) of CSF samples, with TP53 co-alterations in 56.3% (9/16) and 12.5% (2/16), respectively. After 6 weeks of treatment, EGFR variant clearance was achieved in 85.7% (12/14) of plasma-positive cases (Figure S4A) and 66.7% (4/6) of CSF-positive cases (Figure S4B). Among cases with persistent mutations, median EGFR variant allele frequency (VAF) declined significantly in plasma (p = 0.001) (Figure S5A) and in CSF (p = 0.036) (Figure S5B), with comparable reductions in TP53 VAF (Figures S5C and S5D).

To further evaluate the clinical relevance of these molecular shifts, we analyzed ctDNA burden (hGE/mL) dynamics stratified by treatment response. In plasma, ctDNA levels declined across all response subgroups (Figure 4B). In CSF, ctDNA dynamics appeared to stratify by response outcome: patients with complete response/partial response (CR/PR) at both first assessment (at week 6) and best intracranial response showed low baseline ctDNA levels with a declining trend, whereas those with stable disease (SD) at both time points demonstrated relatively high baseline levels or an increasing trend (Figure 4C). Notably, at week 6, SD patients harbored higher CSF ctDNA levels compared to CR/PR (p < 0.05; Figure S5E). Baseline plasma chromosomal number instability (CNI) scores were significantly elevated in PR patients compared to SD (p < 0.05; Figure S5F), suggesting that high baseline CNI may identify patients with enhanced sensitivity to furmonertinib. Collectively, these results suggest that ctDNA dynamics in both plasma and CSF, as well as baseline plasma CNI, may reflect early treatment response differences across clinical subgroups.

Discussion

Effective treatment options for NSCLC with CNS involvement remain limited, particularly among those with EGFR mutations. In this prospective phase II trial, high-dose furmonertinib (160 mg daily) was prospectively evaluated as first-line therapy in patients with previously untreated EGFR-mutated NSCLC and CNS metastases. In the full analysis set, the observed median PFS of 16.59 months and intracranial PFS of 17.83 months, along with a systemic ORR of 92.5% and intracranial ORR of 95.0%, indicate that high-dose furmonertinib not only effectively controls systemic disease but also achieves durable CNS responses.

Standard third-generation EGFR TKIs have established a significant benchmark for managing intracranial disease, with trials such as FLAURA,10,24,25 AENEAS, and studies on limertinib reporting intracranial PFS ranging from 15.2 to 15.3 months and intracranial ORRs between 85.7% and 89.0%.26,27,28 Beyond monotherapy, combination strategies have been explored in these patients, positioning them as key comparators. Subgroup analysis of the FLAURA2 trial revealed that adding platinum-based chemotherapy to osimertinib prolonged PFS relative to osimertinib alone (24.9 m vs. 13.8 m).29 However, this advancement is tempered by a substantial toxicity burden: the incidence of grade 3 or higher adverse events was significantly greater in the combination arm (64%) compared to the monotherapy arm (27%). Moreover, while radiotherapy remains effective for rapid local intracranial control, evidence30 demonstrated that integrating brain radiotherapy prior to disease progression on osimertinib improved intracranial response and local control but failed to prolong PFS or OS. Thus, the dilemma of balancing CNS efficacy against systemic toxicity and survival outcomes creates a clear unmet need for a more balanced, potent first-line regimen. Against this background, our study provides the prospective evidence for high-dose furmonertinib (160 mg) specifically as first-line setting in EGFR-mutated NSCLC with CNS-metastases. While the median intracranial PFS observed in our cohort (17.83 months) was numerically shorter than the 20.8 months reported in the CNS subgroup of FURLONG trial (80 mg),24 this difference may be attributable to the significantly higher baseline intracranial disease burden in our population (70% with >3 CNS lesions), an established adverse prognostic factor. Despite the considerable baseline intracranial disease burden observed in this cohort, the intracranial ORR of 95.0% and DCR of 100% suggest that high-dose furmonertinib may provide meaningful intracranial disease control in this patient population.

CNS metastases develop in a substantial proportion of patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC (up to 50%), reflecting both the proclivity of these tumors to seed the brain and the limited blood-brain barrier penetration of earlier generations of EGFR-TKIs.24,31 Consequently, dose doubling represents a streamlined strategy to enhance intracranial drug exposure and optimize CNS control, a concept substantiated by our findings and supported by evidence from high-dose osimertinib (160 mg) and aumolertinib.13,32,33 Furmonertinib likewise exhibits a clear dose-response relationship: intracranial ORR of 85.0% and median intracranial PFS of 19.3 months at 160 mg vs. 65.0% and 11.6 months at 80 mg.23 Our prospective data now provide the clinical evidence that high-dose furmonertinib (160 mg daily) as first-line monotherapy achieves superior intracranial response and durable disease control, with 95.0% ORR and 100% DCR in treatment-naive patients with substantial baseline CNS disease burden.

Importantly, our trial confirms that high-dose furmonertinib maintains a manageable safety profile, avoiding the high rates of dose interruptions (43%) and discontinuations (11%) typically associated with chemo-combination regimens.29 These findings align with previous reports on high-dose osimertinib, including the phase I BLOOM study, which demonstrated no new or unexpected toxicities.13 This safety advantage is critical for preserving performance status and ensuring the feasibility of subsequent therapies. In conclusion, for patients requiring potent CNS control who may be unable or unwilling to tolerate the toxicities of chemotherapy or radiotherapy, the 160 mg furmonertinib regimen, with efficacy and superior tolerability, represents a compelling additional clinical option.

Our exploratory analysis of longitudinal ctDNA dynamics provides preliminary insights, as interpretation is limited by the baseline QC failure rate (20%), additional week-6 QC failures (6.3% for plasma and 25% for CSF), and the resulting small evaluable sample size. Among these, high rates of EGFR-sensitizing mutation clearance were observed after 6 weeks of treatment in both plasma and CSF. These findings are consistent with prior reports.12,13,34 Notably, while plasma hGE levels declined across all subgroups, CSF changes were more in line with intracranial response. This may reflect the limited ability of plasma ctDNA to capture CNS-related disease dynamics, suggesting that CSF may provide a more direct molecular readout. Furthermore, higher baseline plasma CNI scores in PR patients hint at a potential link between genomic instability and furmonertinib sensitivity. These hypothesis-generating findings require further validation in larger cohorts using higher-depth, standardized sequencing.

In conclusion, high-dose furmonertinib demonstrated potent intracranial activity as first-line therapy for EGFR-mutated NSCLC with brain metastases, achieving a median PFS of 16.59 months and a median intracranial PFS of nearly 18 months. These results support the potential of dose-intensified EGFR-TKI therapy in this setting, particularly for patients with substantial intracranial disease burden. Further validation in randomized clinical trials is warranted.

Limitations of the study

This study had several limitations. First, its single-arm, non-randomized design is the primary limitation. Lacking a concurrent control group, the study is susceptible to selection bias and prevents any definitive conclusions regarding comparative efficacy. Specifically, the lack of a direct head-to-head comparison with osimertinib means that the relative efficacy of furmonertinib 160 mg versus osimertinib remains uncertain, and the generalizability of the observed efficacy outcomes may therefore be subject to scrutiny. Second, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. While this sample size was sufficient for the exploration objectives of this phase II study designed to detect an initial efficacy signal, larger, randomized phase III trials with appropriate control arms are necessary to validate these findings. Third, the exploratory analyses for mechanisms of response and resistance were conducted in a subset of patients, often at single time points rather than through longitudinal monitoring. This limits a comprehensive understanding of response durability and the dynamics of acquired resistance. Finally, follow-up is ongoing, and longer-term data are required to fully assess the durability of CNS control and potential overall survival benefits.

Experimental model and study participant details

This study is a multicenter, single-arm, open-label, Phase II clinical trial investigating the efficacy and safety of furmonertinib as a first-line treatment in patients with epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR)-mutated non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and central nervous system (CNS) metastases. The study protocol and all associated documents were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Hunan Cancer Hospital. The trial was conducted in strict adherence to the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legally authorized representatives prior to any study-related procedures.

A total of n = 40 untreated patients with EGFR-mutant NSCLC and CNS metastases were enrolled in this study. The median age of the study cohort was 60 years (range: 33–75 years). The study population demonstrated a predominance of female patients, comprising n = 26 (65.0%) females and n = 14 (35.0%) males. Both male and female patients were recruited to accurately represent the demographic distribution of EGFR-mutated NSCLC in the general patient population.Further detailed information regarding patient inclusion and exclusion criteria, study design, and clinical assessments can be found in the relevant subsections of the Methods.

Method details

Study design and participants

The FORCE study is a multicenter, single-arm, open-label, phase II clinical trial evaluating high-dose furmonertinib (160mg) as a first-line therapy in EGFR-mutated NSCLC with untreated brain metastases. Eligible patients were ≥18 years old, had histologically confirmed metastatic (stage IV) NSCLC, classified according to the 8th edition of the TNM staging system (International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer/American Joint Committee on Cancer), and had not received prior systemic therapy. (Figure S1). At least one measurable CNS metastatic lesion and lung lesion at baseline were required, defined according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) version 1.1 as accurately and reproducibly measurable lesion and not previously treated with radiotherapy or biopsy. Additional inclusion criteria included an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status of 0–1, adequate hematologic and organ function (including cardiac, hepatic, and renal function), and the confirmed EGFR exon 19 deletion or exon 21 L858R mutation via next-generation sequencing (NGS). Pregnant or lactating women were excluded. Full inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in the study protocol (Supplementary Data).

To explore the molecular mechanisms underlying furmonertinib efficacy, matched cerebrospinal fluid and peripheral blood samples were collected from 16 patients at two predefined time points for DNA-based NGS. Sampling occurred at baseline and after six weeks of furmonertinib treatment. The study followed the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hunan Cancer Hospital (Ethics Approval No: 2021-IIT-067). All patients provided written informed consent before study enrollment.

Treatment procedures

All patients received furmonertinib 160 mg orally once daily. Tumor response was assessed according to RECIST v1.1 at weeks 6 and 12, then every 12 weeks until CNS progression, unacceptable toxicity, or investigator-determined lack of clinical benefit. In cases of initial non-CNS progression, investigators could recommend local therapy while continuing study treatment based on clinical benefit and predefined discontinuation criteria. Dose reductions or interruptions were allowed for the management of adverse events.

Radiological tumor assessments were performed by blinded independent central review (BICR) and investigators and documented per RECIST v1.1 during screening, including all measurable and non-measurable lesions. Imaging evaluations were conducted at weeks 6 and 12 post-treatment initiation and subsequently every 12 weeks (±7 days). Consistent imaging modalities were used throughout screening and follow-up periods, including chest computed tomography (CT), abdominal and pelvic CT or ultrasound, brain CT or MRI, and bone scintigraphy (ECT), if clinically indicated. CNS progression follow-up was anticipated to be approximately 28 months. Adverse events occurring from the time of signing the informed consent until at least 30 days after the last dose of study treatment were monitored. For patients who discontinued treatment for reasons other than CNS progression, tumor assessments continued every 12 weeks per RECIST v1.1 until CNS progression was confirmed.

Endpoints and assessments

Tumor response was assessed by both BICR and investigators according to RECIST v1.1 for systemic and intracranial lesions. BICR was performed by two independent radiologists who were blinded to clinical outcomes and investigator assessments; discordant assessments were resolved by a third independent radiologist, whose determination was recorded as the final BICR result. The primary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined as the time from treatment initiation to the first documented disease progression or death from any cause. Secondary endpoints included intracranial PFS, 1-year and 2-year intracranial PFS rates, objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), intracranial ORR, intracranial DCR and safety. Intracranial PFS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to radiographic CNS progression or death. 1-year and 2-year intracranial PFS rates was defined as the proportion of patients remaining free from CNS progression or death at 12 and 24 months, respectively, following treatment initiation. ORR was defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete response (CR) or partial response (PR) as best response. Intracranial ORR was defined as the proportion of patients with measurable CNS lesions who achieved a CR or PR in the CNS. DCR was defined as the percentage of subjects achieving CR, PR, or stable disease (SD). Intracranial DCR was defined as the proportion of patients who achieved CR, PR, or SD in the CNS as their best intracranial response. Safety evaluations considered incidence and severity of treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs), graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (version 5.0). Exploratory endpoints included overall survival (OS) and bioinformatics analyses investigating potential biomarkers associated with treatment response or resistance. OS was defined as the time from treatment initiation to death from any cause, with censoring applied at the last confirmed survival date if the survival status was unknown.

Next-generation sequencing

Circulating cell-free DNA (cfDNA) was extracted from a 3.5–4 mL plasma sample or cerebrospinal fluid sample using a MagMAX Cell-Free DNA Isolation Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Genomic DNA (gDNA) was extracted from the peripheral blood lymphocyte (PBL) samples using a TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China) and TGuide S32 Magnetic Blood Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN), respectively. After quantification using a Qubit dsDNA HS (High Sensitivity) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), 30–300 ng gDNA was sheared into fragments at a 200-bp peak with a Covaris LE220 using the manufacturer recommended settings. An NGS library was constructed using the fragmented gDNA or 10–50 ng cfDNA with the Watchmaker DNA Library Prep Kit (Watchmaker Genomics, USA) and hybridized into a custom-designed panel that covered approximately 2.4 MB of regions in the human genome, spanning 769 cancer-related genes (Table S1). The qualified library was then sequenced using a DNBSEQ-T7 platform in PE100 mode. The average deduped sequencing depth of plasma, CSF and PBLs were 2063×, 535×and 1821× respectively.

Bioinformatics analysis

After data quality control via Trimmomatic (v0.36),35 reference mapping (hg19) by BWA aligner (v0.7.17)36 as well as duplication masking by Picard (v2.23.0),37 processed BAM files were obtained and used for the following analyses. Somatic nucleotide variants (SNVs) and insertions/deletions (InDels) were detected for each paired sample using GATK Mutect238 (version 4.1.9.0). All variants were annotated by using ANNOVAR39 and filtered according to the below criteria: i) driver varinats ≥4 high-quality reads and VAF ≥0.3%, passenger variants ≥6 high-quality reads and VAF ≥1%, and without strand bias; ii) in exonic region and annotated as nonsynonymous variants; iii) allele frequency ≤0.01 in both the Exome Aggregation Consortium (ExAC) database and the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD, MacArthur Lab). CNV was analyzed by CNVkit (v0.9.2)40 and the cutoff value of CNV gain is copy number ≥3 and the cutoff value of CNV loss is copy number ≤1.2. LUMPY(v 0.2.13)41 was used to identify gene fusions. Tumor mutational burden (TMB) for the plasma and CSF samples was quantified as the total number of somatic nonsynonymous variants within the entire covered exome region (sequencing depth >40×), expressed in mutations per megabase (Mb). Quantification and statistical analysis

Continuous variables were assessed for normality. Those following a normal distribution were presented as means ± standard deviations, while non-normally distributed variables were presented as medians with ranges or interquartile ranges (IQR). For independent samples, continuous variables were compared using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test or Student’s t test. For paired continuous data, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test or paired t test was used, as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for independent samples when expected cell frequencies were less than 5, and McNemar’s test for paired categorical data.

Survival endpoints, including PFS, intracranial PFS, and OS, were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) calculated. Follow-up duration was defined as the time from the first dose of study treatment to the date of last known contact or death and was summarized as the median with 95% CIs. Hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs were derived from Cox proportional hazards regression models. All statistical analyses were two-sided, and a P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.2), SPSS (version 26.0), and GraphPad Prism (version 8.0). Data visualization was performed using GraphPad Prism and R software.

In all analyses, n refers to the number of patients unless otherwise specified. Exact values of n for each analysis, along with the specific statistical tests applied, are reported in the corresponding figure legends, figures, and Results section.
