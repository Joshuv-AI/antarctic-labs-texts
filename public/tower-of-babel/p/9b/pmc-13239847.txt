Epithelial‐Myoepithelial Carcinoma of Minor Salivary Glands of the Palate: A Systematic Review
Authors: Nour M. Khattab, Raphaël Seveyrat, Ihsène Taihi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsEpithelial‐myoepithelial carcinoma (EMC) is a very rare malignant neoplasm of the salivary glands. It mainly affects the major salivary glands. Little is known about the specificity of EMC in minor salivary glands (MSGs), which represents a diagnostic challenge.MethodsA systematic review was performed to identify clinical cases and case series of EMC in MSGs. The search was restricted to EMC of the palate to better characterize the diagnosis and management of this entity.ResultsOf 509 initially identified articles, only 21 were included, involving 25 patients. The lesion was mostly located in the hard palate (56%) and was associated in 20% of cases with a pleomorphic adenoma. All patients received surgical resection. P63 and S‐100 were reliable molecular markers. Palatal EMC was associated with the absence of metastasis in 76% of cases.ConclusionOur study summarizes and updates the clinical features of EMC arising from palatal MSGs.Trial RegisterationCRD42024542994.

Introduction

Epithelial‐myoepithelial carcinoma (EMC) is a rare, mostly painless and slowly evolving malignant tumor [1]. It represents less than 1% of all malignant salivary gland tumors [2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. EMC preferentially affects the major salivary glands like the parotid gland (80%) and the submandibular gland (10%), but occasionally involves the minor salivary glands (MSGs) (1%) [7, 8, 9]. It predominantly affects women, and the average age at diagnosis is 60 years old. Histological analysis reveals a biphasic tubular architecture composed of duct‐like structures lined with epithelial cells in the inner layer of the lumen and clear‐staining myoepithelial cells in the surrounding outer layer [10, 11, 12]. Diagnostic confirmation of EMC requires a precise and complete immunohistochemical (IHC) and histological analysis. However, diagnosis remains extremely challenging due to the inherent wide variation in differentiation of both epithelial and myoepithelial cells, which can mimic other salivary gland neoplasms, especially myoepithelial carcinoma or adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC). Indeed, these latter neoplasms can exhibit clear myoepithelial features and resemble EMC in histopathological analysis [10, 12].

Surgical resection is the standard treatment option for EMC. In EMC tumors of the major salivary glands, it is recommended that surgical excision be combined with radiotherapy when the primary tumor size is greater than 4 cm or when the surgical margins are positive [10, 13]. However, the role of radiotherapy in EMC of MSGs is not well‐documented. Similarly, the role of adjuvant chemotherapy in EMC is not well‐defined [10]. According to Cerda et al., chemotherapy should be considered when radiation is delivered at doses of 65 Gy or more in patients with close or positive margins. The extent of tumor resection and inclusion of a safe resection margin during the initial surgery are key factors to reduce tumor recurrence [10, 14]. The 5‐ and 10‐year survival rates of patients with EMC are high, ranging from 80% to 94% and 72% to 90%, respectively. However, recurrence remains common, ranging from 30% to 50% [10].

Given that EMC involving MSGs is extremely rare, studies of this pathology are also scarce. This fact, combined with the clinical and histological similarities shared by EMC and other salivary tumors, complicates not only the clinical diagnosis, but also the anatomopathological diagnosis on biopsy and the management strategy. A definitive diagnosis is often only possible after total excision and complete histological analysis of the tumor [15].

A recent review of EMC describes all maxillofacial and sinonasal EMC, including tumors affecting major salivary glands and MSGs [16]. Here, we focus on EMC of MSGs, and more specifically the palatal location, as it is the most frequent among MSGs malignant tumors as described by Pereira et al., who found that more than two‐thirds of cases reported in the literature were located in the palate [6]. Furthermore, the palatal location of EMC can lead to misdiagnosis as ACC, which is very common in the palate. In addition, the palate is an important anatomic region due to its proximity to the nose and maxillary sinus. The reviews of Li et al. and Lee et al. [2, 12] describe cases of EMC in the nasal region. Therefore, it would be interesting to identify the common clinical and histological features between palatal EMC and sinonasal EMC, due to their anatomical proximity. The presence of common risk factors could also be evaluated. By focusing on EMC in palatal MSGs, we sought to obtain precise and consistent results applicable to all similar cases. It is for these reasons that we decided to exclude EMC cases involving other oronasal locations.

For this, we conducted a systematic review that aimed to better characterize the clinical, histological, diagnostic, and prognostic features of EMC in palatal MSGs. Our secondary objective was to identify possible distinct clinical or histopathological features of palatal EMC. Ultimately, our goal is to improve tumor diagnosis and management by oncological physicians, and to allow earlier screening and more appropriate follow‐up.

Methods

Eligibility Criteria

This systematic review was designed to assess the following question: “What are the clinical, diagnostic, and prognostic features of EMC in palatal MSGs?”

Our search equation was conducted as a PECOS model:

(P): Participants: patients with an MSGs tumor

(E): Exposition: patients with a palatal EMC

(C): Comparison: no control group

(O): Outcomes: clinical characteristics of EMC in MSGs

(S): Study Design: case reports, case series

The review included studies reporting cases of EMC of MSGs of the palate, published in English or French. The reviewed studies included case reports with at least one reported case of palatal EMC. Case series were included, but only the case report(s) of palatal EMC were considered. Case reports with review were included, but only the case report was considered.

The exclusion criteria were: 1.Reviews, in vitro or animal studies;2.Studies where subjects had an EMC at a location other than the palate3.Studies where subjects had another type of MSGs tumor.

Information Sources

We systematically searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, Scopus, and Google Scholar through September 2023. Furthermore, the authors hand‐searched the reference lists of the selected articles for any additional references that might not have been retrieved in the database searches. We also searched the gray literature, but without any further inclusion.

Search Strategy

Given the limited number of articles on EMC of MSGs, and to identify the maximum number of relevant and related articles, the search was conducted without using MeSH terms. Instead, we listed out keywords and their synonyms and conducted free text searching. The search equation used on PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Scopus was the following: “epithelial myoepithelial” AND “carcinoma” AND “palate”. On Google Scholar, considering the few articles found, the search was done with the following equation: « epithelial myoepithelial carcinoma » AND « palate ». All references were managed, and duplicates removed, by using Zotero software (Corporation for Digital Scholarship, Virginia, USA).

Study Selection Process

The final selection of articles, after removing duplicates, followed a two‐phase process. In phase‐one, two researchers (R.S. and I.T.) independently and carefully screened the titles and abstracts of all identified studies. In phase‐two, the same authors read the full‐text articles thoroughly. Disagreements were resolved by involving a third author (N.K.), producing a final decision about the inclusion or exclusion of studies. The results of the screening and selection process are documented in a PRISMA flowchart (Figure 1).

Data Collection Process and Data Items

For each of the included studies, N.K. and R.S. collected the following information: authors’ names, publication year, article type, country, patient description (age and sex), known risk factors (alcohol, smoking habits, medical history), clinical findings (location of the tumor, size, consistency, aspect, metachronous tumor), symptoms, date of first appearance, management (treatment, follow‐up, survival rate, recurrence and metastases).

Risk of Bias in Studies

Risk of bias was assessed as recommended in the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews [17] (see Supporting Information 2).

We assessed if the patient(s) represented the whole experience of the investigator. We also assessed if exposure and outcome were adequately ascertained through the IHC and histological analyses. Alternative causes, challenge phenomena as well as a dose– response effect were not relevant information, because no introduction of medication occurred. We considered a follow‐up duration of 1 year to be sufficient to collect and analyze all clinical features of each case. We considered the clinical description to be sufficient when the author reported the following clinical information about the lesion: the primary lesion, its location, size, and consistency.

In total, five criteria were relevant in our risk of bias evaluation. We used the case reports and case series assessment tool [18] to classify the included articles as having low risk of bias (good total quality assessment: 3–5/5), intermediate risk of bias (fair total quality assessment: 2/5), or high risk of bias (poor total quality assessment 0–1/5).

Statistical Analysis and Synthesis Methods

Descriptive statistics were performed to summarize study and patient characteristics. Continuous variables (e.g., age, tumor size, disease duration) are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD) when approximately normally distributed and as median with interquartile range (IQR) or range otherwise. Categorical variables (e.g., sex, lesion location, recurrence) are presented as counts and percentages with numerators and denominators (n/N).

Given that the included evidence consisted exclusively of case reports and small case series, no comparative quantitative synthesis or meta‐analysis was feasible. Therefore, no effect measure (e.g., risk ratio, odds ratio, mean difference) was calculated. Results were synthesized descriptively using narrative and tabular summaries.

No inferential hypothesis testing (e.g., P values) was conducted due to the absence of comparable control groups and the descriptive nature of the data.

All calculations were performed in Microsoft Excel (version 16.0, Microsoft Corp.).

All statistical terms and abbreviations (e.g., SD, IQR) are defined in the text. No subgroup, sensitivity, or exploratory analyses were performed. Due to clinical and methodological heterogeneity across reports, statistical pooling was not appropriate.

Results

3.1 Study Selection

Our initial database searches identified 509 studies. After eliminating duplicates, 466 studies remained, of which 429 were excluded after title review, and no article was excluded after abstract review, resulting in 37 articles for phase‐two. During this second phase, after reading full‐text articles, 18 studies were excluded, leaving 19 studies for the synthesis. The reference lists of the included articles were scanned, and two additional relevant articles were selected by hand‐searching, and both were included after application of the selection criteria and duplicate checks. Finally, 21 articles were included in the review. Four of the 21 articles each reported two cases of EMC, thereby elevating the number of included clinical cases to 25 (Table 1).

Study Characteristics

A total of 21 articles met the inclusion criteria. Five were case reports with a review [1, 6, 8, 19, 28], 11 were case reports without a review [4, 5, 9, 15, 21, 22, 23, 24, 27, 29, 30], one was a case series with a review [10], and four were case series without a review [20, 25, 26, 31]. The 21 articles were published between 1995 and 2023. The articles originated from 10 countries: Iran [19, 31], Brazil [6, 25], China [20], Japan [4, 9, 10, 22, 24, 27, 28], India [5, 8, 21, 30], Finland [23], Italy [26], Malaysia [29], Morocco [1], France [15]. Results of Individual Studies

Individual study findings are summarized in Table 1. Across the 21 studies, a total of 25 patients were described. Each included article reported patient‐level clinical information, including demographic characteristics, tumor location and size, clinical presentation, histopathological and immunohistochemical findings, treatment modalities, and follow‐up outcomes. Patient age ranged from 28 to 83 years. The most common clinical presentation was a nodule. Tumor size ranged from 1 to 6 cm. Treatment modalities included surgical resection and adjuvant radiotherapy, and recurrence was reported in one case.

All included studies consisted of case reports or small case series describing single or a few patients without comparison groups. Consequently, summary statistics for comparative groups, effect estimates, and measures of precision (e.g., confidence intervals) were not applicable. Results were therefore extracted and presented descriptively for each individual study.

All data were obtained from published full‐text articles, and no additional data sources or author correspondence were required.

Results of Syntheses

Patient Description (Age, Sex, Geographic Origin)

The mean age at diagnosis was 56.6 ± 15.0 years (median 58, IQR 44–68, range 28–83 years). There was a predominance of females, with a female/male ratio of 2:1 (17/25 (68%) women vs. 8/25 (32%) men).

Although the described cases were distributed around the world, the majority were from the Asian continent (8 cases in Japan [4, 9, 10, 22, 24, 27, 28] and 4 in India [5, 8, 21, 30]). The other cases were distributed in Europe (4 cases) [15, 23, 26], Latin America (3 cases) [6, 25], and Africa (1 case) [1]. Figure 2 shows the geographic distribution of the reported cases.

The geographic distribution around the world of included cases.

Risk Factors

Nearly a third of the 25 patients (8/25) had no known cancer risk factor or medical condition [4, 6, 10, 19, 21, 23, 26], 32% (8/25) had no available information about general health or a possible link with cancer risk factors [8, 9, 20, 26, 28, 29, 31], 20% (5/25) presented a previous or associated pleomorphic adenoma (PA) [1, 22, 25, 30], 12% (3/25) presented a previous or synchronous carcinoma [5, 24, 27], and only 4% (1/25) declared using tobacco [15].

Clinical Features (Presentation, Location, Size)

For the EMC clinical presentation, 19/25 (76%) patients have a nodular lesion [1, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 15, 19, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30]. The mass was associated with an ulceration in 9/25 (36%) cases [4, 5, 8, 9, 21, 22, 24, 28, 30]. In 5/25 (20%) cases, the tumor was described as well‐defined [4, 6, 9, 27, 30]. The consistency was soft in 4/25 (16%) cases [1, 9, 19, 27] and hard or firm on palpation in 8/25 (32%) cases [10, 15, 22, 24, 26, 28].

The most common location of the lesion was the hard palate in 14/25 (56%) cases [1, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 15, 19, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26]. The lesion involved the junction between the hard and soft palates in 2/25 (8%) cases [6, 29] and was located in the soft palate in only 1/25 (4%) case [31]. In 6/25 (24%) cases, the exact location in the palate was not provided [20, 25, 27, 28].

The tumor size was noted in 19/25 (76%) cases, with a mean largest diameter of 2.8 ± 1.6 cm (median 2.0, IQR 1.8–3.8, range 1–6 cm) [1, 4, 5, 6, 9, 10, 15, 19, 21, 22, 23, 24, 26, 27, 28, 30, 31].

Symptoms and Appearance

The EMC was asymptomatic in 11/25 (44%) cases [1, 5, 6, 9, 19, 21, 22, 23, 26, 27, 28] and was symptomatic in 8/25 (32%) cases [4, 8, 15, 24, 26, 29, 30, 31]. The symptoms described were intermittent pain, a feeling of pressure, or nasal obstruction. In 6/26 (24%) cases, no information about symptoms was provided [10, 20, 25].

The disease duration, beginning when the symptoms were first noticed and ending at the first hospital visit, was on average greater than 4 years (53.8 months) with a median of 6.0 months (IQR 2–36, range 1–420 months). This information was missing in 8/25 (32%) of the cases [20, 22, 23, 25, 26].

Immunohistochemical (IHC) and Histological Features

Most tumors displayed a biphasic architecture, with inner ductal/cuboidal epithelial cells and outer clear or polygonal myoepithelial cells. Ductal and tubular structures, often accompanied by clear‐cell myoepithelial proliferation, were the predominant histological features in the majority of cases [5, 6, 8, 9, 21, 22, 27, 28, 29, 30]. However, several cases exhibited notable histological particularities. Stromal features were observed in three cases as periodic acid‐Schiff (PAS)‐positive material, oncocytic cells [9]; mucin‐lined lumens, mucicarmine + , PAS‐D+ [21]; and PAS‐positive basement membrane [22]. Infiltrative behavior, including perineural or vascular invasion, was reported in 4 cases [4, 10, 15, 26], while one article noted soft tissue infiltration [24]. Multinodular architecture or lack of encapsulation was observed in the reported cases of Palaniappan, Kawamata, and Chikuda (non‐encapsulated tumor) [8, 27, 28]. Stromal changes, including myxoid [29] or hyalinized periductal stroma [9, 26], were noted. Cellular atypia, pleomorphism, or high mitotic activity were reported by Kusama, Khattab (features of carcinomatous progression), and Munot (bizarre cells, plasmacytoid morphology, capsular infiltration) [9, 15, 30]. Of the 21 articles, 18, including 22 cases performed, IHC analyses by studying epithelial and myoepithelial markers in combination to validate the biphasic differentiation; one further study investigated just a single marker, the myoepithelial marker S‐100 [29].

The most used marker for myoepithelial differentiation was S‐100. It was reported in 13 articles and positive in all 16 included patients; it provided robust cytoplasmic staining for myoepithelial elements [4, 6, 9, 10, 15, 20, 21, 22, 23, 26, 27, 28, 29]. α‐SMA was reported in 13 articles, including 16 patients. The staining was positive in 13 patients [4, 6, 9, 10, 20, 23, 24, 25, 26, 27, 31]. Negative or heterogeneous α‐SMA staining was reported in the remaining 3 patients [8, 15, 26]. α‐SMA focal positivity was noted in one case in selected cell populations [25]. P63 was used as a marker in 10 recent articles and was positive in all 14 included patients, consistently highlighting myoepithelial cell nuclei [8, 10, 15, 19, 20, 25, 26, 27, 28, 31]. Calponin was reported in 8 recent articles, including 11 patients, and demonstrated cytoplasmic positivity of the myoepithelial component in all 11 patients [8, 10, 20, 23, 26, 27, 31]. It was partially positive in one additional case [15].

Epithelial markers frequently included AE1/AE3, CK7, and EMA. Ki‐67 was used in 8 studies to assess proliferative activity. Low to moderate proliferation indices (Ki‐67) were reported by Mahdavi (1%), Angiero (5–10%) and Okuyama (10.6% and 3.8%) [10, 19, 26], and elevated proliferation indices were reported by Kawamata (19.3%), Teppo (up to 30%), Wang (up to 30%) and Sedassari (25–40%) [20, 23, 25, 28].

Markers such as GFAP, CD10, CD117, SOX10, and p40 were sometimes used to complement the core panels.

Treatment

Tumor resection surgery was performed in all 25 included cases and represented the primary treatment modality for palatal EMC. Surgical management ranged from wide local excision to partial or subtotal maxillectomy, depending on tumor extension and suspected bone involvement. In several cases, resection included adjacent structures such as palatal bone or neighboring maxillofacial components to achieve clear margins. Neck dissection was rarely reported [1, 29]. Reconstructive management and prosthetic rehabilitation were inconsistently described; a few authors reported the use of maxillary obturators, prosthetic rehabilitation [15, 27, 28], or local reconstructive techniques following maxillectomy [8, 15]. Postoperative functional outcomes and complications, including oroantral or oronasal communications, were seldom documented. Adjuvant radiotherapy was provided in 6/25 (16%) included cases [1, 23, 25, 29], with delivered doses between 54 and 60 Gy. No chemotherapy was given in any of the included cases.

Recurrence and Follow‐Up

The follow‐up duration ranged from 1 month to 10 years after treatment. Indeed, the follow‐up was less than 2 years in 7/25 (28%) cases [1, 8, 10, 24, 25, 26], between 2 and 5 years in 11/25 (44%) cases [4, 9, 10, 15, 20, 22, 23, 25, 27, 28, 31] and greater than 5 years in 2/25 (8%) cases [6, 20]. The follow‐up duration was not mentioned in 5/25 (20%) cases [5, 19, 21, 29, 30].

The reported survival rate in included cases was 80%. Nineteen patients showed no recurrence or metastases (19/25, 76%) after treatment. One case reported a recurrence of EMC, and this recurrence resulted in death [25]. Another case described a recurrence 7 years after discovery and excision of the primary EMC tumor, followed by a second recurrence 5 years later, which was treated by maxillectomy [5]. Five authors have not reported the survival rate or the follow‐up duration [5, 19, 21, 29, 30]. Important features are presented in Figure 3.

Discussion

EMC is a rare malignant neoplasm of the salivary glands [8]. The objective of this systematic review was to characterize clinical features of EMC arising from palatal MSGs. Our main results indicate that the mean age at diagnosis is about 60 years old, with a female/male ratio of 2:1, as reported for EMC of oral MSG [6]. However, Wockner's review reported a slight male predominance in all maxillofacial and sinonasal locations of EMC [16].

In our review, the majority of reported cases were in Asia (12 cases of 25) [4, 5, 8, 9, 10, 21, 22, 24, 27, 28, 30] and particularly in Japan (8 cases) [4, 9, 10, 22, 24, 27, 28]. Indeed, some authors have described a possible link between environmental exposure to radiation and the occurrence of malignant salivary gland tumors. One example is the high exposure to radioactivity experienced by Japanese survivors of the atomic bombs, which may be associated with the high number of reported cases from this country [32].

Tobacco and alcohol chronic consumption are the main risk factors usually cited in oral oncology [33]. However, a recent study on risk factors for salivary gland cancers in France showed that tobacco use and alcohol consumption were not associated with salivary gland cancer risk [34]. Rather, it showed that a history of head and neck cancer, cervicofacial radiotherapy, and several occupations and industries (e.g., waiter, charworker, electrical and electronic equipment assembler, plumber, electric arc welder, sheet‐metal worker, building painter, and material handling equipment operator) were associated with an increased risk of salivary gland cancer [34].

In the articles reviewed here, risk factor analysis was not deeply addressed in most of them. Indeed, only four cases out of 25 provided information about tobacco consumption [1, 15, 19, 27], and no information about alcohol consumption was reported. This precluded further analysis of potential links between EMC and these risk factors. Medical conditions of the patients were not documented in eight of the included cases. Improved accuracy and completeness in the reporting of this information in further EMC cases is essential to better evaluate links between EMC and these risk factors.

Among the included cases, 20% reported the presence of a previous or simultaneous PA [1, 22, 25, 30]. Furthermore, two cases (8%) suggested initially a PA on histological analysis of the biopsy, before an EMC diagnosis was confirmed by analysis of the resected tumor [10], which may suggest histological similarities between those two pathological entities and underscores the challenge of EMC diagnosis, as reported in the literature [15]. These findings also led us to explore the primary or secondary origin of EMC. Our results are in accordance with the early observations of Eneroth et al. [35] in 1964 as well as more recent findings, reporting that 1.6% to 7.5% of PA develop into a secondary cancer [1, 10]. Further research comparing the aggressiveness of de novo EMC with secondary EMC would be highly appropriate. Indeed, the only mortality observed in our results occurred in a case of secondary EMC that developed from PA [25].

Clinical findings of palatal‐located EMC included in this review are in accordance with those previously described in other locations, that is, in major salivary glands and MSGs. Indeed, EMC is usually described as a painless, slowly growing mass [1, 19], mainly unilateral [5], and may exist as well‐circumscribed, firm masses associated with surface ulceration in MSGs [8, 19, 28].

Determining the mean evolution time of EMC is highly challenging. Our results revealed widely varying disease durations, ranging from 1 month to 35 years at the time of diagnosis. This information was missing in eight of the included cases [20, 22, 23, 25, 26]. This could be explained by the asymptomatic nature of the tumor and its slow progression, which may delay medical consultation and diagnosis. The long duration before diagnosis can also be explained by the slow malignant transformation from a primary benign tumor present during several years, as palatal PA, to a secondary malignant lesion.

In this cohort of 25 cases, a biphasic histological pattern was predominant, though numerous tumors exhibited unique features, including mucinous differentiation, multinodular growth, infiltration, stromal remodeling, or nuclear atypia. IHC plays a critical role in diagnosing EMC, particularly in confirming biphasic differentiation. Indeed, this biphasic composition of epithelial and myoepithelial cells poses difficulties in EMC diagnosis due to the possible similarities with PA and ACC in some cases [10, 15]. A thorough examination of the entire excised tissue is essential to accurately assess the epithelial and myoepithelial components of the tumor [22].

Although these tumors share similar clinical and histological features, it is crucial to establish an accurate diagnosis, as this significantly impacts both the management and prognosis of the condition [15]. Indeed, EMC is a low‐grade malignancy tumor, with a good prognosis and a survival rate > 90%. In contrast, ACC, is classified by the 2022 WHO classification as an intermediate‐ to high‐grade tumor with poorer prognosis [10, 13, 36]. The ACC survival rate at 5 years is 66–92% and only 40–70% at 10 years [37].

In our review, we conducted an in‐depth analysis of potential IHC markers specific to palatal EMC, with the aim of differentiating it from PA and ACC. P63 and S‐100 emerged as the most consistently reliable myoepithelial markers for palatal EMC, showing strong nuclear and cytoplasmic positivity, respectively, across the included studies in this review. This was also confirmed by Seethala et al., who reported that P63 has excellent specificity and sensitivity for labeling myoepithelium, with a positivity rate reaching 100% [38]. Calponin and Ki‐67 have been increasingly utilized in more recent investigations, whereas few reports relied mainly on broad‐spectrum epithelial markers such as pan‐cytokeratins and ɑ‐SMA [9] or only histological analysis [1, 5, 30], with more limited assessment of myoepithelial components. Antibody staining of Calponin, which is a relatively new smooth muscle marker, has been shown to be a specific and fairly sensitive method of detection of myoepithelial differentiation in salivary gland tumors, exhibiting more specificity than other smooth muscle markers of the myoepithelium. Calponin was highlighted as a highly sensitive marker in some series, but other reports indicated variable expression in oral EMC [20] and in salivary gland and upper aerodigestive tract EMC [38]. This variability underscores the importance of using Calponin in combination with P63 and S‐100 rather than as a standalone marker. ɑ‐SMA also showed inconsistent staining, possibly reflecting incomplete myoepithelial differentiation in some tumors. These variations in IHC approach reflect the evolving diagnostic strategies and further highlight the histological heterogeneity of EMC.

We analyzed the IHC available data to gather additional information on distinguishing palatal EMC from PA and ACC. Li et al. reported that EMC shows a higher expression of GFAP, a more prominent basement membrane, and higher PCNA levels [22]. Therefore, a multiparametric IHC panel is essential for accurate diagnosis. This approach enhances diagnostic precision, especially in morphologically ambiguous or low‐grade EMC.

Tumor resection surgery was performed in all our included cases. This surgery is challenging in the palate, due to the proximity of the nasal and sinusal cavities. Clear margins are necessary to avoid recurrences. However, this can lead to unwanted oro‐nasal communication, often associated with nasal regurgitation of food, defective speech, fetid odor, bad taste, and upper respiratory tract and ear infection [39]. Since the last review of the literature on palatal EMC by Kawamata [28] a decade ago, it seems that resection of the tumor is still the treatment of choice, associated with radiotherapy primarily in tumors greater than 4 cm or in cases of positive surgical margins [28]. This accords with our results, in which surgical resection was the principal management in all included cases. Adjuvant radiotherapy was provided in 16% of the included cases (n = 4) [1, 23, 25, 29], with delivered doses between 54 and 60 Gy. In the reported cases of Sounny‐Slitine and Sedassari, the tumor's longest axis was equal to or greater than 4 cm [1, 25]. Furthermore, in the reported case of Najiha, even though the size of the tumor was not precise in the text, we could infer from the CT‐scan of the lesion that the tumor was > 4 cm [29]. There is no consensus on the indication of radiotherapy in the treatment of EMC of MSGs. In major salivary gland tumors, adjuvant radiotherapy is recommended only if the primary tumor size is > 4 cm or in case of positive resection margins [10], as noted above for palatal EMC. Also, the French Rare Head and Neck Cancer Network (REFCOR) does not recommend radiotherapy after a complete surgical resection in stages I and II of low‐grade tumors like EMC [13]. Clear and specific recommendations regarding radiotherapy for every stage of MSGs EMC are necessary to improve the management of this tumor.

The limited description of surgical techniques, reconstructive approaches, and postoperative functional outcomes in most reports reflects the heterogeneous and often incomplete therapeutic reporting in case‐based literature, which restricts detailed comparison of management strategies despite systematic data extraction.

EMC has a good prognosis. In the literature, survival rates are 94% and 90% at 5 and 10 years, respectively. However, its recurrence rate ranges from 30% to 50% [5, 10]. This is in accordance with our findings on palatal EMC. Indeed, the survival rate reached 95% in the included patients, and the recurrence rate was low. Local recurrence resulting in death was reported in only one case [25]. Another included case described a recurrence 7 years after discovery and excision of the primary EMC tumor, followed by a second recurrence 5 years later, which was treated by maxillectomy [5]. However, we note that the follow‐up duration varies widely among the included studies, and only two cases were followed up for more than 5 years after treatment [6, 20]. In five cases, the follow‐up duration was not reported [5, 19, 21, 29, 30]. Therefore, the possibility of non‐reported future recurrences remains.

In our systematic review of 25 cases of palatal EMC, no instances of distant metastasis were reported, and only one case resulted in death. The overall survival rate was therefore 95%. These outcomes suggest a favorable prognosis for EMC located in the palate. In contrast, EMC arising in other anatomical regions, such as the inferior nasal turbinate, appears to have higher metastatic potential. This may be partly attributed to the narrower anatomy of the nasal cavity, which could facilitate earlier tumor spread [40]. Additionally, a broader review of EMC involving both major and minor salivary glands reported a distant metastasis rate of 11%, with the lungs being the most common metastatic site (41%), followed by cervical lymph nodes (24%), brain and bone (12% each), kidney and liver (6% each), and a median metastasis‐free interval of 22 months [16].

Taken together, these findings suggest that tumor location may significantly influence the metastatic behavior and clinical outcome of EMC, reinforcing the need for site‐specific risk assessment and follow‐up strategies.

Limitations

Due to the rarity of EMC and the low number of reported cases of palatal MSGs, our results are limited to just 25 cases, representing a small sample size. Furthermore, the lack of reporting of some clinical, histological, or follow‐up details in the studies resulted in data gaps, lessening the comparative value of those cases. For example, only a few articles reported details about risk factors. Insufficient clinical information could lessen the usefulness of such case reports by researchers or physicians. Given the limited number of reported EMC cases, it is crucial for authors to fully document all future cases in order to enable more rigorous studies. We recommend that future case reports include standardized data on risk factors (e.g., medical history, smoking, alcohol use, prior radiation, high‐risk profession), tumor measurements, symptom duration and quality, IHC, and histopathological analysis.

Additionally, the available literature consisted predominantly of isolated case reports and small case series with heterogeneous reporting, which precluded meaningful statistical pooling or meta‐analytic analyses. Consequently, a descriptive synthesis approach was adopted, as considered more appropriate for rare entities supported by low‐level evidence. To enhance data interpretability, the main findings were summarized using standardized tables and a graphical abstract integrating the principal clinical, pathological, and prognostic trends.

Despite the lack of information about risk factors and poorly described clinical details in some articles, our study provides important clinical, histological, and management information about EMC of MSGs. However, the generalizability of findings to non‐palatal MSGs EMC is limited. Further research on other specific locations of oral EMC is necessary.

Conclusion

Our review could help physicians identify palatal EMC cases more easily. It highlights that palatal EMC affects primarily women, representing approximately two‐thirds of cases, with a mean age of diagnosis around 56.6 years, although palatal EMC can occur at any age. It is important to shed light on the fact that these tumors are often asymptomatic in nearly half of the cases. Clinical diagnosis may be challenging due to the lack of complaints, frequent absence of mucosal ulceration, and the atypical presentation compared to more common oral malignancies such as squamous cell carcinoma. MSGs EMC typically present as large nodules averaging 3 cm, with detection usually starting from a size of 1 cm. This highlights the importance of a thorough oral examination by physicians and dentists, even in young patients who may lack symptoms. Notably, about 20% of patients included in our review had a history of PA, underlining the importance of long‐term surveillance, as malignant transformation may occur even decades later. Histological diagnosis is further complicated by the lack of specific biological markers, although IHC can reveal expression of characteristic epithelial and myoepithelial cell markers. Management is surgical in 100% of cases, often leading to significant postoperative functional impact, particularly in palatal locations. Indeed, surgical excision of palatal lesions has very different consequences compared to other locations, as it can lead to oro‐nasal or oro‐antral communication.

This highlights the critical need for early diagnosis and the development of reliable biological markers to guide detection and treatment planning. Our results will help clinicians to make the appropriate diagnosis and to recognize this entity earlier. Early and precise diagnosis in combination with well‐conducted appropriate treatment, will produce better outcomes in patients.
