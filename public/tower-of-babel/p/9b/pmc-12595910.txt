Reducing misclassification of medical waste: nudging stakeholders to reduce waste misclassification in a novel simulated operating room built environment
Authors: Jonathan J Szeto, Hope Kim, Catherine Z Shen, Cody Chapman, Julia Tchou, Caoimhe C Duffy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe OR generates over 30% of a healthcare facility’s total waste, with more than 60% classified as regulated medical waste (RMW). Municipal waste (MW) is commonly incorrectly classified as RMW, resulting in unnecessary costs and environmental burden. As such, this pilot study aimed to determine if interventions that provide visual cues for waste classification and improve ergonomics in the operating room (OR) can improve waste sorting accuracy.MethodsIn groups of two to four, participants were tasked with quickly solving tangram puzzles while periodically given simulated medical waste to dispose of into either a MW container or a RMW container. Groups first engaged in a control simulation, followed by an intervention simulation where educational signage and the novel adapted waste bin was placed in the OR. Sorting accuracy was assessed, and participants completed a post-participation survey.Results23 participants took part of this pilot study. The proportion of correctly classified waste increased between control (53%) and intervention (88%) conditions, p < 0.01. Cost (n = 21) and sustainability (n = 20) were highly cited reasons why it might be important to correctly dispose of waste. Participants cited location of the containers (n = 13), time issues or OR stress (n = 10), and confusion in waste classification (n = 13) as barriers to accurate waste sorting.ConclusionParticipants had significant improvement in OR waste classification accuracy after the implementation of educational signage and a novel adapted bin system. These strategies can be employed to increase waste sorting accuracy in the OR or be used as an educational tool during trainings to improve staff understanding of proper waste disposal.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13741-025-00623-8.Keywords: OR Waste Management, Sustainability, Regulated Medical Waste

Introduction

The operating room (OR) generates over 30% of a healthcare facility’s total waste, with more than 60% classified as regulated medical waste (RMW) (Blough and Karsh 2021). RMW, as defined under the Bloodborne Pathogen Standard by the Occupational and Safety Health Administration (OSHA), broadly refers to items contaminated with liquid, semi-liquid, or dried blood, or other potentially infectious materials that could release these substances if compressed or handled improperly (1992). RMW is commonly disposed of through incineration, an energy-intensive process that emits approximately 3 kg of carbon dioxide (CO₂) per 1 kg of waste, contributing significantly to greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions (Duong 2023; Wu and Cerceo 2021). With RMW accounting for up to 86% of a hospital’s total waste management expenses, this disposal method is also costly, costing 10 times more to dispose of compared to general municipal waste (MW) (Nussbaum 2008). The Center for Disease Control (CDC) recommends that RMW make up only 3–5% of a facility's total waste (2024). However, in many large hospitals across the United States (US), RMW constitutes approximately three times this recommended percentage, suggesting that over-classification of MW to RMW is common (Hsu et al. 2020; Harding et al. 2021; Hubbard et al. 2017; Sullivan et al. 2023). The specific categorization and interpretation of RMW can vary by state and institution across the US, which can lead to confusion and misclassification of waste by perioperative personnel during operations. For example, at the site of this study, the University of Pennsylvania, items with only a small amount of blood are not considered RMW and can be disposed of with the MW, which has caused great confusion among those in the OR. Past audits characterizing waste in hospitals at other institutions have found that as low as 14.9% of waste disposed of in red bags met the criteria for RMW, highlighting significant opportunities to improve accurate classification of MW and RMW to reduce environmental and financial costs (Hsu et al. 2020).

Accurate classification of MW and RMW may be challenging in the OR due to its high stress, time-pressured environment. The risks of errors or noncompliance, such as misclassification of RMW as MW that may result in health and safety policy/guideline violation and fines, are further compounded by human factors like fatigue or task overload which have been shown to reduce OR performance (Modi et al. 2020). Another challenge in accurately classifying OR waste as MW vs. RMW, is the lack of a clear, standardized definition for RMW and lack of continuous education that may lead to confusion and increased waste misclassification among staff (Guetter et al. 2018). Past initiatives to improve correct waste disposal practices within hospital settings have focused on creating waste disposal guidelines and conducting staff education preoperatively, which has shown success, albeit temporary, in reducing inaccurate waste classification (Healthcare waste. n.d.; Bannour et al. 2024; Mosquera et al. 2014; Denny et al. 2019; Fraifeld et al. 2021). However, there have been few studies looking at intraoperative nudge interventions to improve accuracy of waste classification in OR personnel. Nudging is a technique in behavioral psychology whereas people’s behavior and decisions are subtly influenced by altering how choices are presented or perceived (Mongin and Cozic 2018). Like how nudging can be used to promote healthier behaviors, encourage good financial decisions, and guide consumer choices, we believed nudges could also be used to promote better waste management in the OR. This may similarly compensate for low self-efficacy, whereas OR personnel less confident in their ability to sort properly can be nudged to improve OR waste management without additional cognitive load and decision-making.

In collaboration with the Center for Healthcare Transformation & Innovation (CHTI), a team of design strategy students led by Cody Chapman (CC) conducted an in-depth analysis of waste disposal habits through direct observations, OR shadowing, and stakeholder interviews. This analysis identified key areas for improvement, including the removal of oversized RMW bins and the introduction of a redesigned waste disposal unit to optimize waste sorting. The current OR set up includes two large bins, one with a red bag for RMW and one with a clear, white bag for MW. The CHTI team also identified inconsistencies in understanding waste categorization and terminology, prompting the redesign of bin labels and the creation of targeted educational materials. To address these findings, educational signage and an adapted waste disposal system were collaboratively developed and tested with the CHTI team under the guidance of CC and CCD. The iterative design process incorporated stakeholder feedback to ensure the usability and effectiveness of the intervention, culminating in a solution that improved clarity, functionality, and compliance (see Fig. 1 and Supplemental Figure S.1). We hypothesize that the addition of educational signage and redesigning the ergonomics of the waste disposal systems would improve waste classification accuracy in the OR.

Methods

The aim of this study was to determine if interventions that provide visual cues for waste classification and improve ergonomics in the OR can improve waste sorting accuracy. For this study, the team used the University of Pennsylvania’s definition of RMW, which states items with only a small amount of blood or contaminated material can still be disposed of in the MW container. This simulation-based pilot study was conducted in a surgical OR within a tertiary academic hospital over a two-day period in the summer of 2024. Perioperative medical personnel, including intraoperative nursing, surgeons and anesthesiologists, were prospectively invited to participate in this study during OR downtime. Participants provided written consent before engaging in the simulation. This pilot project was deemed exempt from review by the University of Pennsylvania Institutional Review Board (856,137). This research complied with the American Psychological Association Code of Ethics.

Waste classification OR simulation

The simulation was conducted in an unoccupied OR. Upon entering, participants were divided into teams of two to four and instructed to complete a randomized series of tangram puzzles as quickly as possible. The tangram puzzles were used to simulate cognitive load and time pressure, mimicking the attentional demands experienced in the OR. While not a perfect analog, this allowed us to test waste sorting accuracy under distracted conditions.

Each team participated in two scenarios: (1) a control scenario, where the OR waste disposal setup reflected a standard case, and (2) an intervention scenario, where participants received instructions to review educational signage (Fig. 1) that outlined proper waste sorting into designated bins. The signage was strategically placed on the OR wall, and a novel adapted bin system (Supplemental Figure S.1) was introduced to facilitate correct waste disposal during the intervention. A leaderboard displaying the number of puzzles completed by each group was prominently displayed in the OR to encourage competition and maintain focus on completing the puzzles.

During the puzzle-solving activity, participants were periodically handed pieces of simulated medical waste at set intervals, which varied in item type, contamination status, and saturation levels (Supplemental Table S.1). They were instructed to sort and dispose of these items into designated containers: a MW container (grey) and an RMW container (red) within the OR. The scenario concluded when all waste was disposed of. A flow chart illustrating the sequence of events is included in the supplemental materials (Supplemental Figures S.2).

Outcomes and data analysis

The primary outcome was the percentage of correctly disposed waste, with additional insights gathered from post-participation surveys. Post-participation surveys were paper-based and conducted immediately after the end of the simulation session. Survey questions focused on participants’ prior experience with handling medical waste, personal opinions on medical waste and disposal behavior, and lessons taken away from participating in this simulation. An example of the full survey can be found in the supplemental materials (Supplemental Figure S.3). The frequencies in which each waste item was sorted correctly were calculated and presented as percentages. A χ2 test was used to compare the frequency of correctly sorting the various items between the control and intervention simulations. An inductive thematic approach was used to analyze the survey response data. All survey response data was read first by 3 (JJS, HK, CZS) members of the research team for familiarization and initial codes were generated for various themes among the responses to the open-ended questions (“Have you learned anything new about medical waste disposal during the study?” and “What are your overall thoughts and feedback on the changes introduced during this study to improve medical waste disposal practices?”). The three members then independently read survey answers to group answers and codes into common themes and then convened for an in-depth discussion to refine the identified themes, address any discrepancies, and collaboratively resolve inconsistencies. This process continued until consensus was achieved, ensuring the findings were robust and well-aligned. Each theme was supported by quotes from the response answers and reported as descriptive statistics.

Results

Over 2 days, 23 OR staff, including 8 surgeons, 6 anesthesiologists, 3 radiation technologists, 2 certified registered nurse anesthetists, 1 obstetrics tech, and 1 registered OR nurse, participated in this pilot study. In each simulation, participants were handed 29 pieces of simulated medical waste to sort. Of the 29 items, 8 were considered RMW. Recurring themes in response to the open-ended survey questions are shown in Supplemental Table S.2. Many participants (n = 12) expressed positive feelings toward the intervention, describing it as a “great study” and a “good experiment” with a “great set up” that was “helpful” and “fun.” Participants cited the educational value of the intervention (n = 8), noting the “signs were helpful” and that it was a “great educational resource” for understanding waste management. Participants demonstrated improved understanding of blood contaminated (n = 6) and urine and feces waste (n = 6). They also noted an improved understanding of proper bin usage (n = 2). One participant cited implementation concerns with the challenging in habit change, noting “people might be hard to convince to change their habits.”

Discussion

In this pilot study, the addition of new signage, combined with a redesigned novel waste container system, resulted in a 35% improvement in waste classification accuracy, a significant change after a simple, low-cost intervention. The combination of our qualitative and quantitative findings suggests that the success of the intervention can be attributed to its effective integration and application of targeted education initiatives with choice architecture in the OR built environment.

Ineffective education was one of the most cited reasons for errors in RMW classification by our participants. RMW is a broad term with significant variability in its meaning, and the lack of standardized language when discussing RMW (e.g. terms such as “light blood”) can lead to confusion and sorting error. This likely contributed to the incorrect disposal of blood-contaminated items into RMW regardless of the degree of contamination. Ineffective education may limit the extent to which individuals feel capable of properly sorting waste, reducing their self-efficacy (Ajzen 1991; Valkengoed et al. 2022). These individuals with low self-efficacy may doubt their ability to correctly sort waste, leading to greater hesitation, decreased motivation, or avoidance of the task altogether. Erring on the side of caution is another reason why OR staff may classify MW as RMW resulting in over-classification of MW as RMW. The use of educational signage in our intervention would help mitigate low self-efficacy and fear of misclassification in our participants (Valkengoed et al. 2022). While self-efficacy was not explicitly assessed in our study, it remains an important factor for future research. Overall, our findings highlight the need to standardize language in educational signage to eliminate potential sources of confusion, reduce cognitive overload and enhance self-efficacy in proper waste sorting.

Participants commonly cited a knowledge gap in how waste is processed after it is discarded. Future education should ensure that these knowledge gaps are addressed. Waste audits have also been suggested as a method to streamline disposal and can be used to identify areas of improvement and needed education for staff (Wu and Cerceo 2021). In this study, only 13% of participants had previously received a hands-on workshop for medical waste training and most participants (52%) had positive feelings after the simulation, citing that it was both fun and educational. The simulated waste sorting task employed in this study could serve as an educational tool for other institutions to model. The interactive component of the simulation may also facilitate greater retention of knowledge compared to conventional lectures or self-paced asynchronous modules.

In addition to standardizing education, transforming the built environment of waste disposal can substantially improve waste sorting outcomes. In collaboration with the Penn Innovation Design Center, we redesigned the waste disposal units, including reducing the size of the RMW bin. This intervention led to noticeable improvements in waste-sorting behavior. This reflects an application of “nudging,” a behavioral change approach that promotes behavior by altering choice architecture, or the environment in which individuals make choices (Thaler and Sunstein 2008; Cosic et al. 2018). These “nudges” often target automatic decision-making processes. People often rely on automatic decision-making processes when time constraints, lack of motivation or limited mental resources limit their ability to evaluate options (Valkengoed et al. 2022).

Limitations of this pilot study included its small sample size (n = 23 individuals). The results were also obtained in a simulated OR built environment and therefore may not reflect actual waste classification behavior during surgery in the OR. While the tangram task was included to simulate attentional and cognitive load, additional real-time assessments of behavior in the OR are necessary to confirm the results. Another limitation of the study design is that the novel adapted bin system and educational signage were tested together, making it difficult to tease out the effect of each of the individual component in improving accuracy of MW and RMW classification.

Conclusion

In this pilot study, participants had significant improvement in OR waste classification accuracy in a simulated OR built environment that included the use of educational signage and a novel adapted bin system. Future studies in real-world OR scenarios need to be conducted to fully evaluate the effectiveness of this intervention. However, overall, our approach, to combine education signage and choice architecture in the OR to promote environmentally conscious behavior, enhance compliance with waste management protocols, reduce environmental impact, and reduce health care costs, represents a low-cost scalable solution amenable for adoption in diverse health care settings.
