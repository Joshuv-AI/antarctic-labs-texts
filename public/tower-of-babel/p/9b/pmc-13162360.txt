Multimodal MRI and machine learning for identifying depression in cerebral small vessel disease: a multicenter study
Authors: Guihan Lin, Weiyue Chen, Yongjun Chen, Lei Xu, Ting Zhao, Zufei Wang, Min Xu, Chenying Lu, Minjiang Chen, Shuiwei Xia, Jiansong Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study sought to evaluate the effectiveness of a machine learning (ML) model utilizing multimodal MRI in distinguishing cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) patients with depression (CSVD + D) from those without depression (CSVD − D).Materials and methodsThis retrospective study involved 198 participants from three centers, who were divided into training (n = 113; CSVD + D = 56, CSVD − D = 57), external validation 1 (n = 85; CSVD + D = 31, CSVD − D = 54), and 2 (n = 102; CSVD + D = 39, CSVD − D = 63) cohorts. Structural, functional, and diffusion tensor imaging was used to extract features, which were utilized to construct ML models based on nine ML classifiers. The efficacy of the models was evaluated through the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. SHapley additive explanations (SHAP) analysis provided deep insights into the model’s interpretability.ResultsTwelve features from multimodal MRI were finally identified. The eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) classifier performed the best. Background

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is often associated with neurodegenerative disorders and can intensify their associated cognitive impairments, physical disabilities, and behavioral symptoms [1, 2]. Depression is a common clinical manifestation of CSVD, affecting approximately 10%–39.8% of patients [3–5]. Depression leads to a diminished quality of life, increased disability, elevated suicide risk, and a greater burden on caregivers [6]. It is an important indicator for decline in cognition and the advancement to dementia [7]. Consequently, the prompt identification of CSVD with depression (CSVD + D) is essential.

In clinical practice, depression is diagnosed by a combination of clinical symptoms, neuropsychological evaluations, and imaging examinations [8]. However, its clinical symptoms are highly heterogeneous, and detailed neuropsychological assessments are time-consuming and not feasible for all patients [2]. Indeed, substantial differences in the results of neuropsychological evaluations have been reported due to researcher bias and patient cooperation [9]. MRI is essential for identifying CSVD, with conventional features including white matter hyperintensities and lacunar infarcts being strongly linked to depressive symptoms [10]. Nonetheless, these imaging biomarkers alone are inadequate for identifying depression, as white matter hyperintensities often occur in elderly people. Consequently, further imaging techniques are required to predict and identify the risk of early depression development.

Diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) evaluates the architecture of the deep white matter and offers perspectives on microstructural changes that may be linked to depression [11]. Resting-state functional MRI (fMRI) has also gained widespread acceptance for the noninvasive detection of intrinsic brain functional changes in the early phases of depression [12]. Several studies have reported changes in DTI and fMRI metrics between CSVD + D and CSVD without depression (CSVD − D), primarily in the cingulate cortex, frontal-parietal lobes, temporal lobe, hippocampus, parahippocampal gyrus, thalamus, and amygdala [12–17]. Specifically, several metrics have been proposed to synergistically enhance the understanding of alterations in brain function from many perspectives, yielding more insightful information. Nonetheless, it remains uncertain if integrating multimodal MRI biomarkers offers a more comprehensive insight into CSVD-related depression and how multimodal metrics can be combined and utilized for auxiliary diagnosis in clinical settings.

Machine learning (ML) techniques can proficiently simulate the complex connections among highly dimensional, multidimensional metrics, facilitating the development of prediction models to guide clinical decision-making [18–20]. Motivated by the current application of ML in Alzheimer’s disease along with various neurodegenerative conditions [21–25], this study aimed to develop and evaluate a multimodal MRI-based ML model that integrates structural MRI (sMRI), DTI, and resting-state fMRI to identify depression in patients with CSVD. By leveraging complementary multimodal information, this approach was designed to improve the identification of depressive symptoms within this specific population. Moreover, the incorporation of ML with model interpretability may provide additional insight into imaging features associated with CSVD + D and support objective assessment.

Methods

Participants

This research received approval from the Institutional Review Board of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (approval number: 2024 − 266). This study recruited participants from the outpatient and inpatient divisions of the Fifth Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Center 1), the Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Center 2), and the Second Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Center 3). The inclusion criteria were: (i) aged 50–80 years, (ii) right-handed, and (iii) meeting the neuroimaging criteria for CSVD (Appendix E1). Depression was assessed using the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D17) [26]. CSVD + D refers to patients with HAM-D17 scores between 7 and 17, while CSVD − D refers to those with HAM-D17 scores below 7. The exclusion criteria were: (i) severe anxiety, depression, or other serious psychiatric disorders; (ii) intracranial or extracranial vascular stenosis ≥ 50%; (iii) cognitive impairment; (iv) white matter hyperintensities due to non-vascular causes; (v) cardiogenic brain embolism; and (vi) inability to cooperate with assessments or MRI examinations. Based on these eligibility criteria, the training cohort from Center 1 consisted of 113 patients, 56 with CSVD + D and 57 with CSVD − D; external validation cohort 1 from Center 2 consisted of 85 patients, 31 with CSVD + D and 54 with CSVD − D; and external validation cohort 2 from Center 3 consisted of 102 patients, 39 with CSVD + D and 63 with CSVD − D. Image acquisition

An Philips Ingenia CX 3.0T scanner (Center 1), a GE Discovery 750w 3.0T scanner (Center 2), and a Siemens Magnetom Verio 3.0T (Center 3) were utilized for MRI image acquisition. The patients were directed to keep their eyes closed, maintain stillness, refrain from sleeping and contemplating certain subjects. Prior to image acquisition, a localizer sequence was obtained to align the following scans: structural MRI, fMRI, and DTI. The scanning parameters are detailed in Table S1. Before extracting features for classification, key preprocessing steps were applied to denoise the MRI data. Further details can be found in Appendix E3.

Feature extraction

In the fMRI data, 90 regions of interest (ROIs) were selected according to an anatomy labeling template and the seven brain networks from the Yeo template. To estimate resting-state functional connectivity with temporal resolution, each ROI was regarded as a node for network analysis using Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Thus, the correlation coefficients were transformed into 90 × 90 and 7 × 7 matrices for each subject using Fisher’s z-transformation. Features such as regional homogeneity (ReHo), amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF), node centrality, global efficiency (gE), local efficiency, and degree were calculated. This step was conducted using the RESTPlus toolkit.

In the sMRI data, the same 90 ROIs and seven brain networks were selected, and the regional gray matter volumes within these ROIs were calculated as structural features. This step was conducted using the SPM (version 12) software package.

In the DTI data, the same seven brain networks were selected, and four parameters were calculated as features: fractional anisotropy (FA), mean diffusivity, axial diffusivity, and radial diffusivity. This step was conducted utilizing the PANDA pipeline for the analysis of brain diffusion images.

Feature selection

The features were standardized using z-scores to normalize feature scale and distribution across centers. In addition, we employed the ComBat method to control for variations in MRI protocols in three centers [27] (Appendix E4 and Figure S1). Then, the features underwent selection to mitigate overfitting in the training cohort. Initially, an independent sample t-test was conducted, retaining features with P < 0.05. Subsequently, the retained features were evaluated through least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression. Features with non-zero coefficients were retained, whereas features with zero coefficients were penalized with 0. The feature subset was optimized by modifying the hyperparameter lambda to minimize the mean squared error using ten-fold cross-validation. Lastly, absolute Spearman’s correlation coefficient |r| > 0.8 was employed to eradicate multicollinearity. The aforementioned steps were used to select features for the unimodal analyses. For analyses combining two or three modalities, the features selected for each modality were first merged, and the combined features underwent further selection using the above steps to ensure that the merged feature set contained the most representative features. After feature selection, the resulting feature sets were then directly applied to the validation cohort for model testing.

Model construction

Using features from three modalities, this study used nine ML classifiers for ML model construction, including logistic regression, support vector machine, random forest, linear discriminant analysis, k-nearest neighbors, decision tree, eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), light gradient boosting machine, and multilayer perceptron. The detailed descriptions of these nine ML classifiers were shown in Appendix E5. The Grid Search in Python was utilized to automatically search for the optimal hyperparameter combinations for each classifier in the training cohort (Table S2). Then, models were validated in the validation cohorts. All nine ML models were evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) and other quantitative metrics derived from ROC analysis. After evaluating both fitting and generalization performance, the best-performing model was selected as the optimal ML model. Then, seven models were classified and trained to further identify the most effective prediction model using the optimal ML classifier: three unimodal models (sMRI, fMRI, and DTI), three bimodal models (sMRI + fMRI, sMRI + DTI, and DTI + fMRI), and one multimodal model (sMRI + fMRI + DTI).

Model interpretability and visualization

The importance of features in the most effective prediction model was demonstrated utilizing the SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) method, which could quantify the contribution of each feature [28]. A greater contribution to the model’s prediction is indicated by higher SHAP values. The mapping involved projecting brain areas from the Automated Anatomical Labeling atlas onto a smoothed brain surface utilizing the BrainNet Viewer to see the pertinent brain regions. Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS (version 26.0), considering a two-sided P value of less than 0.05 as statistically significant. The methods used to calculate descriptive statistics, evaluate model predictive performance, and Pearson’s correlation analysis are described in Appendix E6.

Multimodal MRI features

We identified 12 representative features from the multimodal MRI images, which incorporated three modalities (sMRI, fMRI, and DTI). Detailed features between those with CSVD − D and CSVD + D in the training cohort are provided in Table S3. Seven fMRI features were selected. Compared to those with CSVD − D, those with CSVD + D had reduced ALFF values in the left inferior temporal gyrus and lower ReHo values in the right superior occipital gyrus. Moreover, those with CSVD + D had decreased gE in the salience network (SN), fronto-parietal network (FPN), and default mode network (DMN) compared to those with CSVD − D. In contrast, those with CSVD + D had elevated ALFF values in the left superior frontal gyrus and higher ReHo values in the left precuneus than those with CSVD − D. Three DTI features were selected. Those with CSVD + D had lower FA levels in the SN, FPN, and DMN than those with CSVD − D. Two sMRI features were selected. Those with CSVD + D exhibited smaller gray matter volumes in the left hippocampus and greater atrophy in the right lingual gyrus than those with CSVD − D.

Model performance analysis

The classification performance of nine ML models based on the multimodal MRI features is illustrated in Table 2; Fig. 3. The XGBoost model exhibited the highest capability for classification when compared to the other ML models, with AUCs of 0.958, 0.893, and 0.917 and accuracies of 0.929, 0.871, and 0.853 in the training, external validation 1 and 2 cohorts, respectively. In the validation cohorts, it had significantly higher AUCs than the other ML models (DeLong’s test: P < 0.05, Figure S2), and it also exhibited modest improvements and robust performance across other metrics (Fig. 3D − F). Thus, the XGBoost model was selected as the optimal ML model.

Correlation analysis

Table S5 presents the correlations between multimodal MRI features and HAM-D17 scores in the training cohort. The ALFF values in the left superior frontal gyrus and ReHo values in the left precuneus exhibited positive correlations with HAM-D17 scores (R > 0, P < 0.001), with the ALFF value of the left superior frontal gyrus showing a stronger positive correlation (R = 0.630, P < 0.001; Figs. 4A). Other multimodal MRI features were negatively correlated with HAM-D17 scores (R < 0, P < 0.001), with the FA value of the DMN showing the strongest negative correlation (R = − 0.717, P < 0.001; Fig. 4B).

Discussion

In clinical practice, identifying high-risk individuals with depression symptoms among patients with CSVD and distinguishing them from those without depression symptoms is crucial for early intervention and delaying the progression to cognitive impairment or dementia. In this study, we applied a multimodal neuroimaging approach combined with ML to differentiate patients with CSVD + D from those without depression. The XGBoost-based multimodal model achieved AUCs of 0.958, 0.893, and 0.917 in the training, external validation 1 and 2 cohorts, respectively, with classification accuracies of 0.929, 0.871, and 0.853. Moreover, the multimodal model outperformed unimodal and bimodal models, suggesting that integrating structural, diffusion, and functional imaging measures may improve discrimination performance in this population.

According to the fMRI analysis, we identified significant reductions in gE across three key functional networks: SN, FPN, and DMN. This phenomenon is hypothesized to arise from the lower brain network integration in patients with CSVD + D, distinguished by a reduction in the number of nodes in key brain areas and a corresponding decrease in central function [29]. The integration of structural and functional network dynamics is crucial for maintaining normal brain function, as evidenced by the decrease in DTI-based FA values in these networks. In addition, we also observed improved functional compensatory activity in CSVD + D patients, reflected by increased ALFF in the left superior frontal gyrus and higher ReHo in the left precuneus. The sMRI results further revealed a marked reduction in gray matter volume in the left hippocampus and right lingual gyrus among CSVD + D patients. The hippocampus, a fundamental element of the limbic system, is essential for emotional regulation, and its atrophy may serve as a neurobiological foundation for depression [14]. Additionally, the lingual gyrus extends anteriorly to the parahippocampal gyrus and interacts with the thalamus and frontal cortex [30, 31], contributing to emotional perception processes [32]. However, the identification of specific brain regions and their connectivity in CSVD + D remains heterogeneous [33, 34]. Methodological differences and the limited number of studies may contribute to the inconsistencies observed among their findings. In light of this variability, findings based solely on region-specific analyses from a single modality may be limited. By integrating structural, functional, and diffusion features, we conducted a comprehensive evaluation of alterations within the SN, FPN, and DMN, together with related regional changes, thereby providing a more coherent characterization of CSVD + D than isolated regional observations.

Building on the multimodal MRI features identified above, we further compared the performance of different ML models to determine the optimal model for distinguishing CSVD + D and CSVD − D patients. Among the evaluated ML models, the XGBoost model achieved the best performance, yielding AUCs of 0.958, 0.893, and 0.917 in the training and two external validation cohorts, respectively. Previous studies have also demonstrated the effectiveness of the XGBoost algorithm in developing predictive models in medical imaging research [35–37]. As an ensemble learning algorithm based on the gradient boosting framework, XGBoost combines multiple decision trees through a weighted iterative process, which improves predictive accuracy and model stability. Its ability to capture nonlinear relationships makes it well suited for analyzing complex multimodal neuroimaging and clinical data. In addition, the algorithm incorporates regularization and feature selection mechanisms that help reduce the risk of overfitting when handling high-dimensional imaging features [38].

Beyond the choice of classifier, the integration of multimodal neuroimaging features also played an important role in improving model performance. By leveraging complementary structural and functional information provided by multimodal MRI, the multimodal model demonstrated improved classification performance compared with unimodal and bimodal approaches. The sMRI reflects regional gray matter alterations, DTI captures microstructural white matter changes, and fMRI characterizes large-scale brain network activity. Since CSVD involves both structural and functional brain alterations, combining these imaging modalities may provide a more comprehensive representation of disease-related changes than any single modality alone. From a clinical perspective, this integrative approach may contribute to a better understanding of imaging alterations associated with CSVD + D and may assist in identifying patients with subtle or under-recognized depressive symptoms during clinical evaluation. In addition, the SHAP-based interpretation further enhances model transparency by highlighting imaging features that contribute most strongly to the prediction.

SHAP-based interpretation indicated that ALFF in the left superior frontal gyrus was the most influential feature for classification performance. Previous findings by Wang et al. [39] suggest that perceived stress is strongly associated with regional functional activity in the superior frontal gyrus, potentially serving as an intermediary linking ALFF alterations in this region to depressive symptomatology. Kong et al. [40] further observed that reduced psychosocial functioning, including emotional regulation and life satisfaction, is significantly associated with increased activity in the superior frontal gyrus. Another critical feature is the FA in the DMN network, which exhibited a substantial decrease, indicating structural injury to the white matter regions associated with this network. The DMN has been extensively studied and is linked to the onset of depression symptoms, particularly regarding mood and cognitive impairments associated with CSVD [41, 42]. The reduction in FA values suggests a decrease in connectivity and efficiency of information transfer within the DMN, potentially resulting in additional impairments in higher-order functions such as self-reflection, emotional regulation, and social cognition. Based on our findings, we infer that dysfunction in brain networks contributes to the neurobiological mechanisms underlying CSVD-related depression. Moreover, correlation analysis results showed that these key features were also closely related to the severity of depression. These findings suggest that structural and functional network alterations may contribute to the neurobiological basis of CSVD-related depression.

Our research exhibited many limitations. Firstly, being retrospective research, it is inherently vulnerable to selection bias. The relatively limited sample size may constrain statistical power and generalizability. Subsequent research should encompass larger and more diverse patient populations to enhance external validity, and the application of resampling strategies such as bootstrapping may further improve statistical robustness. Secondly, the categorization of brain areas in our study into a relatively limited number of networks reduces sensitivity to subtle regional changes. Future studies might employ other parcellation methodologies to enhance the validation of identifying patients with CSVD + D. Thirdly, our study lacked longitudinal imaging data. Ongoing follow-up studies in CSVD cohorts will be essential for verifying and extending these findings over time. Fourthly, depression status was defined using relatively mild HAM-D17 thresholds, with severe depression excluded, which narrows the clinical phenotype represented in the present model. Inclusion of patients across a broader severity spectrum in the future would strengthen generalizability.

Conclusions

Despite these limitations, the present findings suggest that combining ML with multimodal imaging features may improve the discrimination of depressive symptoms in CSVD. Further studies with larger and prospective cohorts are warranted to determine its potential clinical relevance.
