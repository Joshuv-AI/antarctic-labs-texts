Accessing robust vaccine coformulation stability by single adjuvant detection on a microelectrode
Authors: Azaria A. Wagner, David Perez Herrera, Avery L. Rudder, Caleb Kinsey, David A. Foley, David H. Thompson, Emory M. Payne, Jeffrey E. Dick
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCoformulated vaccines, which combine an antigen with an adjuvant, must maintain stability to ensure efficacy and safety. Conventional analytical tools, such as dynamic light scattering, provide bulk averages that can mask critical nanoscale and microscale changes in particle populations. We present stochastic electrochemical blocking as a complementary, single-particle technique capable of detecting subtle shifts in size and stability within complex vaccine matrices. By quantifying transient current-blocking events at a microelectrode, this method distinguishes heterogeneous populations and monitors formulation changes over time. Applied to both model systems and the licensed SHINGRIX™ vaccine, the approach reveals stability differences invisible to bulk methods, offering a powerful tool for vaccine design, regulatory evaluation, and long-term quality control.

Adjuvanted vaccines play a role in preventing disease, particularly in target populations with reduced immune systems (1). Inclusion of adjuvants such as aluminum salts, (2) emulsions, (3) and liposomes (4, 5) has been shown to promote higher overall efficacy, duration of immune response, and dose-sparing. Accordingly, >75% of all vaccine clinical trials in 2023 contained one or more adjuvants (6)—representing a significant investment in adjuvanted vaccine development.

Many adjuvanted vaccines leverage a two-vial commercial image in which the antigen material is segregated from the adjuvant and the two are mixed immediately before administration. Coformulated vaccines (e.g., antigen and adjuvant in a single vial or prefilled syringe) offer distinct advantages over two-vial formats, such as ease of administration (7, 8). However, development of coformulated vaccines presents significant challenges due to the complexity of the formulation (7, 9, 10). One challenge with the development of coformulated vaccines is utilizing a method to evaluate antigen and adjuvant stability as part of a complex matrix. Antigen stability can be characterized through size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) or Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) approaches, but the presence of a lipid-based adjuvant can interfere with these methods through column fouling in the case of SEC and binding interference in the case of ELISA. Colloidal adjuvant stability is typically measured using dynamic light scattering (DLS), which is strongly affected by interference and is low resolution. Therefore, in coformulated vaccines, an expanded analytical toolbox is necessary to measure antigen and adjuvant stability in these complex matrices.

In addition to component stability, there is an interest in evaluating interactions between adjuvant and antigen in solution (11, 12). Some adjuvant systems, such as liposomes, are proposed to act as antigen delivery systems so an interaction between adjuvant and antigen is critical for an optimized immune response (13). For product development and understanding adjuvant mechanisms of action, new methods for measuring adjuvant–antigen interactions are needed.

In particular, the SHINGRIX™ vaccine is a nonlive, adjuvanted recombinant subunit vaccine designed to prevent shingles (herpes zoster) and its complications in adults—especially those above the age of fifty and immunocompromised individuals. As of 2022, it was available in 45 countries and had grossed £731 million (~$1 billion) (14). One of the key ingredients of SHINGRIX™, the adjuvant AS01B, has previously reported health benefits—demonstrating that this vaccine warrants further study (15). While SHINGRIX™ is currently available in a two-vial format (e.g., separate adjuvant and antigen), a prefilled syringe (e.g., coformulated commercial image) was recently placed under review (16) and accepted by (17) the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA). Based on the approval letter, no significant changes were made to the components of the vaccine formulation based on the previous two-vial image; however, for the two-vial image, the administration of the vaccine must be done within 6 h after reconstitution, whereas the one-vial image has a shelf life of 36 mo at 2 to 8 °C (18, 19). This difference shows that it is important to develop robust techniques that allow us to gain as much insight as we can from these vaccines.

Nanopores are one emerging technique that could be a complementary tool if carefully optimized. Nanopore sensing has been widely applied to detect and characterize single particles, providing information on size, shape, charge, and conformational dynamics with high temporal resolution (20–23). More recently, nanopores have been extensively used to investigate DNA (24), proteins (24, 25), lipid-block copolymers (26), and even virus lipid particles in the Ebola vaccine (27). Though they can be fragile, nanopores do not degrade like the surface of an electrode—making them a viable option for sizing vaccine components. For this study, however, stochastic electrochemical blocking offered clear advantages. Because the size of potential aggregates from interactions in our systems was unknown, nanopore experiments would have required more extensive trial and error to select pore sizes that promote readable signals while avoiding clogging. In contrast, stochastic electrochemical blocking with a microelectrode allows measurement across a broad range of particle sizes without pore-size constraints. We therefore used stochastic electrochemistry as a first-line approach to determine whether interactions occur and to estimate their relative size.

Stochastic electrochemical blocking is a technique that exploits the random, time-resolved interactions between nanoscale particles and a micro- or nanoelectrode surface to determine properties of the particles and their surrounding environment (28–34). When a constant potential is applied to a small electrode in a solution containing particles capable of diffusion—such as biologically relevant cells (35, 36), proteins (37), or virus-like particles (31)—a subset of those particles stochastically adsorb to the electrode surface. If the particle is large enough to partially or fully obstruct electron transfer between the electrode and a redox-active species in solution (e.g., hexacyanoferrate(II)), this results in a measurable drop in the steady-state current. As a general rule for stochastic electrochemical blocking, the radius of the particle adsorbing to the electrode should be around 10% of the radius of the electrode (38). In experiments using high concentrations of redox mediator, this limit can be pushed lower (37). Our group has recently shown that this concept can be used to electrochemically evaluate nanometric motions of insulators on electrode surface, highlighting its unprecedented sensitivity. Thus, for a commercial 5-micron radius electrode and a high concentration of redox mediator, particles can theoretically be sized down to tens of nanometers in radius.

Each current drop event corresponds to a single blocking incident, and the magnitude and duration of these events can provide information about the particle’s size, diffusion behavior, surface interactions, and even deformability. When the particle is much smaller than the electrode measuring the blocking event and assumed to be spherical in nature, a qualitative estimate of the particle’s radius (rp) can be inferred from the radius of the electrode (re), the drop in current caused by the particle adsorbing to the electrode (Δi), and the steady-state current of the electrode (iss) in Eq. 1 (37, 39).[1]rp=reΔiiss.

Admittedly, (Eq. 1) has the drawback that it ignores the influence of heterogeneous current distributions on microelectrodes, or “the edge effect.” The edge effect in electrochemistry with microelectrodes arises because the flux of electroactive species to the electrode surface is not uniform. Diffusion is enhanced at the electrode perimeter because radial diffusion—and not semifinite linear diffusion—dominates, leading to higher local current densities at the edge compared to the center (39–42). In our study, given the analysis is similar between experiments, and the electrode size does not change, the (Eq. 1) approximation can be used robustly to compare across samples.

While the edge effect can be allayed using hemispherical microelectrodes (43), the drawback is present in almost all reported experiments, allowing for a qualitative apples-to-apples comparison of size distributions. The radius of the electrode is typically provided by the manufacturer or measured via in-lab techniques, and the current drop can be determined experimentally. If using a micro- or nano-electrode, the steady state current (iss) can be calculated from the number of electrons being exchanged by the redox mediator (n), Faraday’s constant (F), the diffusion coefficient of the redox mediator (D), the concentration of the redox mediator in solution (C), and the radius of the electrode (re) in Eq. 2 (39).[2]iss=4nFDCre.

This approach holds particular promise for studying complex vaccine formulations, where understanding the nanoscale behavior and interactions of different components is critical for assessing stability, potency, and shelf life. Previously, different aspects of liposomes have been successfully characterized by stochastic electrochemistry—measuring different properties of their contents (44), membrane (45, 46), and size (45, 47). To the best of our knowledge, however, stochastic electrochemical blocking has not been used as a tool to investigate interactions and stability of vaccines. We note that this method, as described, is a size-based method, and thus cannot directly tell a difference in conformation changes or other characteristics about a single particle unless they result in an overall size-change of that particle.

In this manuscript, we have used stochastic electrochemistry to demonstrate interactions between streptavidin functionalized PLGA beads and biotinylated bovine serum albumin (BSA) that were not detectable through traditional DLS methods. This is attributed to the comparatively large ratio of streptavidin functionalized PLGA beads (~1,350 µg per 0.5 mL) to biotinylated BSA (~50 µg per 0.5 mL) in the solution, which is identical to the ratio of AS01B to gE in SHINGRIX™. Previously, Compton and coworkers demonstrated the use of stochastic electrochemistry as a method to compete with DLS and nanoparticle tracking analysis. In their work, silver nanoparticle oxidation was used for the sizing analysis. The present work opens avenues for electrochemically inactive species, such as those one would find in most vaccine formulations (48). Because stochastic electrochemical blocking operates at the single-particle level, it can detect subtle heterogeneities and transient states that bulk techniques average out. We then applied this technique to SHINGRIX™, which contains both an adjuvant (AS01B) and an antigen (gE), and determined that no significant interactions could be seen between AS01B and gE via stochastic electrochemistry. Finally, we showed a comparative study utilizing stochastic electrochemical blocking to help understand the stability of the different SHINGRIX™ formulations over time under different storage conditions—all of which could prove valuable assets to the vaccine and drug development community. This makes stochastic electrochemical blocking a powerful complementary tool for evaluating vaccine integrity, especially after changes in formulation or long-term storage.

Materials and Methods

Chemicals and Reagents.

DiagPol™ streptavidin coated fluorescent poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles (DiO, 120 nm) were purchased from CD Bioparticles (Shirley, NY). Dulbecco’s Phosphate-Buffered Saline (DPBS) (1×) was received from Corning (Corning, NY). Potassium hexacyanoferrate(II) trihydrate (potassium ferrocyanide trihydrate, ≥99%), Pierce™ biotinylated BSA (Biotin-LC-BSA), potassium phosphate (monobasic), sodium phosphate (dibasic anhydrous), and sodium chloride (≥99%) were obtained from Thermo Scientific (Waltham, MA). Zoster Vaccine Recombinant, Adjuvanted SHINGRIX™ was purchased from Myonex (Horsham, PA). Sodium phosphate (monobasic) was purchased from Mallinckrodt (Dublin, Ireland). Potassium phosphate was obtained from Ward’s Science (Rochester, NY). Sucrose and polysorbate 80 were bought from Sigma Aldrich (St. Louis, MO). Potassium chloride (≥99%) was received from VWR (Radnor, PA).

Vaccine Solution Preparation.

SHINGRIX™ is provided as a two vial format with suspended AS01B adjuvant in one and lyophilized gE antigen in the other (49). The first vial (hereafter referred to as the “Adjuvant Vial”) contains the following components necessary for 0.5 mL of aqueous AS01B adjuvant solution: monophosphoryl lipid A (MPL, 50 µg), QS-21 (50 µg), dioleoyl phosphatidylcholine (DOPC, 1 mg), cholesterol (0.25 mg), sodium chloride (NaCl, 4.385 mg), disodium phosphate (Na2HPO4, 0.15 mg), and monopotassium phosphate (KH2PO4, 0.54 mg). MPL, QS-21, DOPC, and cholesterol are the different components of the AS01B liposome. NaCl is a tonicity agent, and both Na2HPO4 and KH2PO4 are buffer agents.

The second vial (hereafter referred to as the Antigen Vial) contains the following components necessary for a gE antigen pellet that can be reconstituted with the Adjuvant Vial: gE (50 µg), sucrose (20 mg), polysorbate 80 (0.08 mg), sodium phosphate monobasic dihydrate (NaH2PO4·2H2O, 0.160 mg), and dipotassium phosphate (K2HPO4, 0.116 mg). gE is the antigen, sucrose is a stabilizer, polysorbate 80 is a known surfactant, and both NaH2PO4·2H2O and K2HPO4 are buffer agents.

For experiments that required reconstituting the Antigen Vial with the Adjuvant Vial, we followed the procedure outlined in the SHINGRIX™ instruction manual. Briefly, the Adjuvant Vial was inverted and the contents were extracted with a syringe—typically resulting in 0.5 to 0.6 mL of solution in the syringe. This extracted solution was then deposited into the Antigen Vial and the contents swirled until fully dissolved. This combined solution of the Adjuvant Vial and the Antigen Vial was then extracted with a syringe and relocated into a 2 mL High-Performance Liquid Chromatography (HPLC) vial for ease of use. For experiments requiring only the Adjuvant Vial, we extracted the contents into a syringe and deposited the solution into a 2 mL HPLC vial.

A couple experiments required the matrix of the Adjuvant Vial (without any AS01B components) added to the Antigen Vial or the matrix of the Antigen Vial (without any gE) added to the Adjuvant Vial. In these cases, we manually measured the required ingredients and added them directly to the proper vial. To all experiments, we added 400 mM hexacyanoferrate(II) trihydrate to the solution to promote stochastic electrochemical blocking measurements. Hexacyanoferrate(II) was added less than 5 min after the reconstitution/preparation of the vials or after permanently removing the vials from their climate controlled environment. We ran the first trial of all experiments less than 5 min after the addition of hexacyanoferrate(II). The remaining three replicates of each experiment did not have a waiting period between each instance. We did not observe a significant change in measurements between replicates when gathering data in this manner.

Positive Control Interaction Solution Preparation.

To simulate the environment of the SHINGRIX™ Adjuvant Vial as closely as possible, the stock streptavidin coated fluorescent PLGA nanoparticles were diluted to 2.5 mg/mL with DPBS (1×). Similarly, to simulate the environment of the SHINGRIX™ Antigen Vial as closely as possible, an appropriate amount of biotinylated BSA was used to result in a final solution concentration of 0.1 mg/mL. For all experiments simulating reconstitution, we followed the procedure outlined in the SHINGRIX™ instruction manual and added 400 mM hexacyanoferrate(II) trihydrate to the solution to promote stochastic electrochemical blocking measurements. Hexacyanoferrate(II) was added less than 5 min after the preparation of the vials. We ran the first trial of each experiment less than 5 min after the addition of hexacyanoferrate(II).

Experimental Setup.

A Pine Research (Durham, NC) potentiostat (WaveDriver 200) was used to make all electrochemical measurements inside of a Faraday cage made of a cardboard box and aluminum foil. All components were properly grounded to a true earth ground. The working electrode was a platinum microelectrode (d = 10.0 μm), purchased from CH Instruments. The reference/counter electrode was a silver/silver chloride (1 M potassium chloride) electrode from CH Instruments.

DLS was performed using a Malvern Panalytical Ltd. (Malvern, United Kingdom) Zetasizer Pro. A quartz cuvette from Malvern Panalytical Ltd. (Malvern, United Kingdom) was used for all measurements.

Amperometric Measurement.

For amperometric experiments, a two-electrode system was used with a WaveDriver200 potentiostat. The working electrode was a platinum microelectrode (d = 10.0 μm) and the reference/counter electrode was silver/silver chloride (1 M potassium chloride) connected to the electrochemical cell by a salt bridge. The salt bridge was a U-shaped glass capillary filled with 3% w/w agarose and 1 M potassium chloride. Cyclic voltammetry was performed on a bulk solution of 10 mM hexacyanoferrate(II) trihydrate in potassium chloride (1 M) to determine the proper oxidation potential for hexacyanoferrate(II), with the potential sweeping from 0.0 to 0.5 V vs. Ag|AgCl and back. The electrode radius was determined to be 5.05 µm using Eq. 2, a steady state current of 12.47 nA (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), one electron, a diffusion coefficient of 6.40·10−10 m2/s, and a concentration of 10.0 mol/m3.

Between each amperogram, the microelectrode was rinsed with water, ethanol, and acetone and manually dried. Between experiments, the microelectrode was polished with thirty figure-8’s on polishing pads covered with deionized water and 0.3 µm, 0.05 µm, and no alumina (in this order). After each polish, the microelectrode was rinsed thoroughly with deionized water and dried. This process was performed before the first experiment, as well, to ensure consistency between experiments. All experiments utilized the same platinum microelectrode.

Blocking transients were characterized as having a baseline of at least ten data points before the sudden drop in current and a baseline of at least five data points after the current drop. For all adjuvant/antigen collision experiments, only the first 20 current drop events around and after the 25-s mark (the point at which we could distinguish the stairstep current drops from the slope of the overall current decay) were considered in calculations to assess and ensure replicability between runs. For all bead/protein collision experiments, only the first 20 current drop events around and after the 1-s mark were considered in calculation for similar reasons. Each condition studied involved four runs for a total of 80 data points per condition. We found that a 5-min duration for each chronoamperogram was more than enough to meet these requirements. All relevant graphs use the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) convention, and each histogram in the following text contains 80 data points. Bin widths of 5 pA (for adjuvant/antigen collision experiments) and 10 pA (for bead/protein collision experiments) were chosen for optimal histogram readability due to the average current drop of the particles being in the tens of picoamps range. All averages reported are followed by their respective SD. These are reported to the nearest picoampere to reflect the precision of the technique.

To see if the histograms followed a normal distribution, the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was performed with 95% confidence in all cases. To compare averages between histograms, an F-test was used to determine if the variances in the datasets were statistically different with 95% confidence and then the appropriate t test was performed with 95% confidence in all cases. When appropriate, the exact probability is listed in the text for t tests.

DLS.

A Zetasizer Pro was used to measure all particle diameter population averages. All measurements were set to have five replicates. Before each experiment, the quartz cuvette was thoroughly soaked in ethanol and allowed to air dry. The volume used for these experiments was approximately 1 mL (as is standard for DLS experiments) with 1 mL of water being added to 10 µL of sample (for adjuvant/antigen collision experiments) and 50 µL of sample (for bead/protein collision experiments) in all cases. The decreased amount of sample volume for the adjuvant/antigen collision experiments was due to having less overall sample per trial than the bead/protein collision experiments. When discussed, all averages reported are followed by their respective SD. These are reported to the nearest nanometer to reflect the precision of the technique.

Results and Discussion

Fig. 1 illustrates the overarching experiment that allows us to quantify the size of individual vaccine particles (like liposomes) using stochastic electrochemical blocking. In our experiments, we biased our platinum electrode at 0.5 V vs. Ag/AgCl to oxidize 400 mM hexacyanoferrate(II) and produced a constant current due to a consistent influx of electrons from the hexacyanoferrate(II) (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1). If particles were present in solution (i.e., liposomes like AS01B) and diffused to the surface of the electrode through the diffusion layer, they blocked a portion of this flux—resulting in a sudden drop in current (Fig. 1B) before returning to a new steady-state current (Fig. 1C). Multiple stochastic electrochemical blocking events cause the overall current to trend toward zero much more quickly than if no blocking events occurred (Fig. 1D). The amount of current blocked (measured via the drop in current) can be correlated to the cross-sectional area of the particle, which can then be converted to a particle diameter if we assume a roughly spherical particle (Eq. 1). Since not all particles were expected to be roughly spherical, however, we listed current drop in place of particle diameter for all histograms in this manuscript as a more accurate comparison between trials.

Schematics (A–C) and a chronoamperometry comparison plot (D) showing (A) unimpeded flux flowing to a platinum electrode resulting in a constant current (Inset), (B) an insulating particle entering the diffusion layer and blocking a portion of the flux from reaching the electrode (Inset), (C) an insulating particle resting on the surface of the electrode resulting in a new, lower constant current (Inset), and (D) a comparison of a 400 mM hexacyanoferrate(II) steady-state curve (black trace) vs. a representative chronoamperogram showing both large and small blocking events (blue trace). The Inset provides a representative small blocking event.

Before applying stochastic electrochemical blocking to evaluate vaccine component interactions and stability, we needed to determine whether the technique could effectively distinguish between systems with low versus high probabilities of interaction. To simulate the former, we probed a solution of streptavidin-functionalized PLGA beads at a concentration similar to that of AS01B in the SHINGRIX™ vaccine with 400 mM hexacyanoferrate(II), which resulted in Fig. 2A (orange plot, n = 80, 250 ± 32 pA). This dataset follows a normal distribution with 95% confidence via the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (SI Appendix, Table S1)—indicating that most datapoints fall evenly around the average.

(A) An overlay of number of particles vs. current drop for streptavidin-functionalized PLGA beads alone (n = 80, 250 ± 32 pA, orange solid columns) and streptavidin functionalized PLGA beads combined with biotinylated BSA (n = 80, 355 ± 369 pA, blue striped columns). (B) An overlay of DLS data [including hexacyanoferrate(II)] for streptavidin functionalized PLGA beads alone (n = 5, 162 ± 1 nm, PDI: 0.05 ± 0.03) and streptavidin functionalized PLGA beads combined with biotinylated BSA (n = 5, 160 ± 1 nm, PDI: 0.06 ± 0.01).

To test a case of high interaction probability, it is known that streptavidin and biotin have a strong interaction (50). Thus, we added biotinylated BSA at a concentration similar to that of the gE protein in the SHINGRIX™ vaccine to the streptavidin-functionalized PLGA beads and hexacyanoferrate(II) solution, which resulted in Fig. 2A (blue plot, n = 80, 355 ± 369 pA). This dataset does not follow a normal distribution (SI Appendix, Table S2) and the averages of this dataset compared to that of the PLGA beads alone are statistically different via a t test with a probability less than 0.001. This is mostly attributed to the larger data points above 350 pA, as when these seventeen points, which are attributed to interactions, are omitted, the average drops significantly (n = 63, 240 ± 36 pA). The truncated histogram, although it appears to have a bimodal distribution, follows a normal distribution according to a Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (SI Appendix, Table S3), and the two averages are no longer statistically significant (P = 0.08), which can be qualitatively confirmed with the overlay in Fig. 2A. Interesting deviations, such as smaller events than the control streptavidin-PLGA case, could provide mechanistic information (such as biotin desorption or conformational changes upon adsorption). We note that the distribution in the streptavidin-functionalized PLGA blocking experiments is relatively narrow. This supports our claim that edge effects are accounted for between experiments, but one could argue that biotinylated BSA binding could increase edge landing, leading to the observed differences. This is a possibility, but we find this unlikely in the more extreme cases—as the current drops span over an order of magnitude (indicating larger particles present in solution). Thus, we have shown that stochastic electrochemical blocking can determine individual interactions between particles of a similar size and concentration to the SHINGRIX™ vaccine components even if the majority of particles measured show no signs of interaction.

Intriguingly, when two DLS measurements were taken under the same conditions (Fig. 2B), they did not show the same trends with their population averages—the averages are practically the same with, perhaps, the combined trial being slightly smaller (the full five trials for each are represented in SI Appendix, Fig. S2). We attribute this to the number of interacting particles present at SHINGRIX™ concentrations being only a fraction of the total number of particles in solution. Thus, DLS would not be able to distinguish whether a small number of interactions are occurring to the same degree that stochastic electrochemical blocking can as a single-particle measurement. This creates a framework that allows for different interaction studies for vaccine research on a particle-by-particle level.

For our next goal, we applied stochastic electrochemical blocking to SHINGRIX™ in order to probe the adjuvant components in their manufactured environment. We performed stochastic electrochemical blocking experiments with AS01B alone (using the Adjuvant Vial from the SHINGRIX™ vaccine) and created the histogram in Fig. 3A (purple plot). With this histogram, we confirmed with the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test that the liposomes follow a normal distribution of particle sizes (SI Appendix, Table S4), which would not have been possible to say with DLS alone. A separate Adjuvant Vial was tested (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) with a t test—showing a statistically insignificant difference (P = 0.69 and P = 0.63, respectively) between the mean of its current drop values (n = 80, 63 ± 8 pA) and size (113 ± 7 nm) the mean of Fig. 3A’s (purple plot) current drop values (63 ± 10 pA) and size (112 ± 9 nm)—indicating that this technique is replicable between vials.

(B) Bar graph comparing electrochemical AS01B diameter data converted from current drop using Eq. 1 (n = 80, 113 ± 9 nm) to DLS AS01B diameter data (n = 5, 111 ± 1 nm). Using Eq. 1, we then calculated the sample average particle diameter and compared it to the population average diameter measured by DLS, shown in Fig. 3B. The theoretical steady state current in Eq. 1 was calculated from Eq. 2 using one electron, a diffusion coefficient of 6.40·10−10 m2/s, a concentration of 400 mol/m3, and an electrode radius of 5.05·10−6 m. The corresponding DLS intensity distribution plot is provided as “Day 0” of SI Appendix, Fig. S4. We first used the DLS population average of 111 nm to estimate the current drop we would see with our calculated steady state current and electrode radius (Fig. 3A), which was ~60 pA. This agreed well with our average of 63 pA. Separately, we note that the SD of the electrochemical calculation in Fig. 3B is larger than that of the DLS measurement (n = 5, 111 ± 1 nm). This difference stems from the DLS value representing the SD across five population averages, whereas the electrochemical method calculates the SD from eighty individual AS01B particles. As a result, the electrochemical approach offers more information via a detailed view of the individual particle size distribution around the mean—rather than the population average variation.

We performed an identical procedure to analyze if there was any difference between AS01B alone and AS01B reconstituted with the gE protein (using the Adjuvant Vial combined with the Antigen Vial from the SHINGRIX™ vaccine). With the histogram in Fig. 3A (n = 80, 57 ± 14 pA, yellow plot), we determined with 95% confidence that the coformulation did not follow a normal distribution of particle sizes (SI Appendix, Table S5). A t test revealed that the averages of Fig. 3A (purple plot) and Fig. 3A (yellow plot) were statistically different with a probability of 0.001. This indicates that we can statistically determine the difference between a formulation of adjuvant alone and adjuvant combined with antigen for SHINGRIX™, which is difficult to do with DLS alone.

To investigate if the vial matrices had any effect on the size of the adjuvant or antigen, we probed how the ingredients of the vaccine behaved when both vials’ matrices were combined but one of the active ingredients (either gE or AS01B) was removed. When AS01B was in the combined matrix (SI Appendix, Fig. S3), it followed a normal distribution and did not have a statistically different (P = 0.17) average (n = 80, 62 ± 9 pA) from the Adjuvant Vial’s average in Fig. 3A (purple plot). When gE was in the combined matrix, it resulted in the histogram of Fig. 3C (n = 80, 693 ± 1,523 pA). The main plot is in nA for ease of visibility, but the Inset is in pA for ease of comparison. This dataset does not follow a normal distribution (SI Appendix, Table S6) and has current drops much larger than expected for a small protein like gE. In addition, no clean DLS data could be obtained due to a “large number fluctuation” error.

It is an interesting contrast from the Adjuvant Vial+Antigen Vial coformulation in Fig. 3A (yellow plot) in that something about the AS01B appears to prevent the gE from aggregating due to the significantly smaller current drops. This qualitative phenomenon would be a great point of research for a future study, since even though we can detect these larger species of gE aggregates, we know they are not adjuvant–antigen interactions. For the remainder of this study, however, we will keep our analysis focused on the Adjuvant Vial and the Adjuvant Vial+Antigen Vial conditions.

Since we established a baseline of the Adjuvant Vial by itself, we used stochastic electrochemical blocking (supplemented with DLS) to study particle sizes and distributions over time in a stability experiment. The first condition we wanted to probe involved four Adjuvant Vials containing AS01B placed in an incubator set at 37 °C and sampled at Day 0, Day 3, Day 7, and Day 14 to see if there was a significant change in size over time as presented in Fig. 4 A–D. We ensured that adding 400 mM hexacyanoferrate(II) did not cause a significant change in DLS population averages via SI Appendix, Figs. S4 and S5, which both follow a similar trend to Fig. 4 A–D (no significant size change over time). All four datasets follow a normal distribution (SI Appendix, Tables S4 and S7–S9). When compared with the original Day 0 (n = 80, 63 ± 10 pA) time point, the Day 3 (n = 80, 65 ± 11 pA), Day 7 (n = 80, 63 ± 12 pA), and Day 14 (n = 80, 62 ± 13 pA) datasets are not statistically significant. This shows that AS01B is stable in its Adjuvant Vial matrix for at least 2 wk without a particle size shift—even at 37 °C. In addition, we note that the variance of Day 14 is the only one that is statistically significant from the Day 0 variance. If we assume a homogenous mixture, this hints that the distribution of particle sizes may be spreading out more around the mean over time—even if that mean is not changing significantly.

A different story emerges when AS01B is reconstituted with gE at 37 °C (Fig. 4 E–H). Supplementary DLS datasets that follow a similar trend can be found in SI Appendix, Fig. S6. All datasets follow a normal distribution except for the Day 14 dataset. While there is no significant increase in particle size on Day 3 (n = 80, 56 ± 15 pA) or Day 7 (n = 80, 60 ± 17 pA) as compared to Day 0 (n = 80, 57 ± 14 pA), the average particle size on Day 14 (n = 80, 91 ± 22 pA) is statistically significant from that of Day 0 (P < 0.001). In addition, Day 7 and Day 14 both have statistically significant variances from Day 0—indicating that the particle sizes are distributing further from the mean at a faster pace than in Fig. 4 A–D. We attribute this to dissociation of the gE protein from AS01B. Another important observation is that both Day 0 and Day 14 histograms do not follow a normal distribution while Day 3 and Day 7 do (SI Appendix, Tables S5 and S10–S12)—likely due to reconstitution of AS01B and gE and destabilization of the coformulation, respectively. With DLS (SI Appendix, Fig. S6), we do see an increase in size on Day 14, but, intriguingly, no increase in polydispersity index—indicating that DLS considers the solution to still be relatively monodisperse. Meanwhile, using stochastic electrochemical blocking, both the size and the variance statistically increase. Thus, we are getting more useful information about the solution from stochastic electrochemical blocking than from DLS. We conclude that the Adjuvant Vial+Antigen Vial condition is not stable according to particle size for up to 2 wk at 37 °C, which is corroborated with the DLS trend in SI Appendix, Fig. S6.

Most vaccines are stored at refrigerator temperatures, so it makes logical sense to test this condition for stability as well. When a 4 °C temperature is used in place of 37 °C to investigate the stability of the coformulation under refrigerator conditions, the instability effect is greatly lessened. Supplementary DLS datasets can be found in SI Appendix, Fig. S7. In Fig. 4 I–L, all datasets follow a normal distribution (SI Appendix, Tables S13–S16). The variances in particle size only differ significantly from Day 0 (n = 80, 59 ± 12 pA) on Day 14 (n = 80, 63 ± 15 pA), much like in Fig. 4 A–D. In addition, none of the means from Day 3 (n = 80, 59 ± 13 pA), Day 7 (n = 80, 59 ± 13 pA), or Day 14 are statistically different from Day 0—with the closest to having a statistical difference being Day 14 (P = 0.06). Thus, we propose that the Adjuvant Vial+Antigen Vial condition is stable according to stochastic electrochemical blocking for up to 2 wk in the refrigerator, which is consistent with the DLS trend in SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and with recent FDA approval (17). A 14-d storage window is highly relevant industrially because it aligns with common shipping and distribution timelines, where products may remain in transit or at distribution hubs for 1 to 2 wk before reaching end users. Demonstrating stability over this period provides confidence that materials can withstand real-world supply chain conditions without requiring special handling or excessively tight turnaround times. We also conclude that our stochastic electrochemical blocking can accurately measure stability through our comparison with all supplemental DLS trials. In a future study, we would like to look into the time points between Day 7 and Day 14 at 37 °C for the AS01B + gE formulation to see if stochastic electrochemical blocking can provide even more data about when the solution starts becoming unstable.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that stochastic electrochemistry provides a powerful, high-resolution complement to DLS for evaluating vaccine particle interactions, stability, and compatibility at the single-particle level. By applying this method to the SHINGRIX™ vaccine, we observed that while the AS01B adjuvant alone remains stable over time, significant size increases and broadening of particle distributions occur when AS01B is reconstituted with the gE antigen and incubated at 37 °C—suggesting destabilization in the combined formulation. This effect is notably reduced at 4 °C, underscoring the importance of temperature in coformulated vaccine stability. These findings highlight the utility of stochastic electrochemical blocking for detecting subtle formulation instabilities on which DLS alone may not be able to properly inform—offering a valuable, stability-indicating tool for future vaccine design and quality control via individual particle sizing. We have demonstrated stochastic electrochemistry’s role in diagnosing colloid stability, a general framework that spans vaccine development, shelf life, and any other colloid of commercial or societal interest.
