Unraveling ‘F’ factor: towards a genetic-clinical framework for the musculoskeletal-heart crosstalk in metabolic aging
Authors: Yunqiao Zhou, Jian Huang, Xinyi Chen, Leqin Xu, Fan Zhang, Chunxiao Bai, Jiju Yang, Fangyang Fan, Yuquan Wang, Bixuan Fang, Tian Wang, Junhao Li, Xiaohong Mu, Jinyu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe rising co-occurrence of cardiometabolic diseases and musculoskeletal degeneration poses a critical challenge to healthy aging, yet the shared biological mechanisms underlying this multimorbidity remain poorly defined. This study aimed to establish an integrative clinical-genetic framework to elucidate the common frailty factor, the ‘F’ factor, that captures the systemic vulnerability linking cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) and musculoskeletal aging.MethodsUtilizing the prospective China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) cohort, we developed and validated novel Frailty-Integrated Indices for CMM risk prediction, evaluated with machine learning models interpreted via SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). Independently, we applied genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic-SEM) to integrate genome-wide association data from six traits—coronary artery disease, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, bone mineral density, frailty, and telomere length—to model a shared latent genetic factor (‘F’ factor). This was followed by multivariate GWAS, fine-mapping, transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS), gene-based analysis, and functional annotation to prioritize causal genes, pathways, and cell types.ResultsClinically, several Frailty-Integrated Indices significantly improved CMM risk prediction, with the optimal model achieving an AUC of 0.727. Genetically, we modeled a significant shared latent genetic factor (‘F’ factor), pinpointing novel risk loci and implicating key genes such as APOE and SLC22A3. These genes were enriched in pathways including cellular senescence and cholesterol metabolism and showed specific expression patterns in developmental brain stages and across multi-organ endothelial cells.ConclusionOur findings provide converging evidence for Musculoskeletal‑Heart crosstalk of metabolic aging and inferred the ‘F’ factor as a genetic correlate of a transdiagnostic state, which links genetic predisposition to metabolic dysregulation, and systemic functional decline. This work provides a multi-level biological characterization of multimorbidity liability, informing early-risk detection and preventive strategies for complex aging-related comorbidities.Graphical abstract This figure has been designed using resources from Flaticon.com and BioGDP.com.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03220-1.

Introduction

Against the backdrop of a continuously deepening global trend of population aging [1], the spectrum of human chronic diseases has undergone a fundamental transformation [2]. The single-disease model is increasingly being replaced by a complex multimorbidity pattern driven by systemic aging, which spans across organs and systems, and has become the primary cause of health loss in the elderly population [3–5]. This state of multimorbidity not only significantly exacerbates individuals’ risks of falls, disability, and premature death but also brings an unbearable dual burden of disease and economic costs to healthcare systems [6]. Crucially, multimorbidity is far from a simple superposition of multiple disease phenotypes at the “phenomenon” level; it involves complex interactions and synergistic deterioration of pathophysiological processes across multiple systems, posing major challenges for precise prevention, diagnosis, and treatment [7]. Therefore, moving beyond traditional single-disease classification systems to uncover the common mechanisms driving multimorbidity from a systemic perspective has become a core scientific issue urgently needing resolution in the fields of aging and chronic disease research.

In this context, cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM)—defined as the co-occurrence of two or more cardiovascular and metabolic diseases (such as hypertension, type 2 diabetes, coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, etc.) in an individual—has emerged as a growing public health challenge, associated with high disability burdens and healthcare costs [8, 9]. Unlike single disease traits, CMM not only represents dysfunction in cardiovascular metabolism but also reflects multisystem-interactive metabolic disorders [10–13]. Its pathophysiological mechanisms involve synergistic dysregulation of organs such as bone, muscle, and heart [14–17]. Recent evidence indicates that CMM can significantly influence the onset and prognosis of chronic diseases across multiple systems [12], particularly in terms of changes in body composition (abdominal obesity) [18], low grip strength [19], and bone loss (fractures) [20]. These emerging findings reflect a systemic imbalance along the Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk in the context of metabolic aging. Deepening the understanding of the comorbid mechanisms along this axis is crucial for advancing the shift from a “single-disease-centered” to a “systemic-health-centered” medical paradigm, as well as for developing precision medical strategies based on this axis.

Building on a systematic cognitive paradigm of disease traits, frailty, as a core geriatric syndrome, has garnered widespread attention due to its strong correlation with CMM [20, 21]. Frailty characterizes a state of multisystem homeostatic imbalance in the human body [22]; it is not only a key aspect of the aging process but has also been proven to be a powerful driver and susceptibility “soil” for the development of various chronic diseases, including CMM, sarcopenia (SP), osteoporosis (OP), and other metabolism-related conditions [23, 24]. Observational studies have repeatedly demonstrated significant bidirectional associations between frailty and components of CMM, together forming a complex, mutually exacerbating disease network [25], with systemic inflammation previously identified as a central hub in this network [26–28]. However, current research paradigms often treat metabolic abnormalities (e.g., insulin resistance, atherosclerosis) and physical aging (e.g., sarcopenia, osteoporosis) as parallel, independent risk dimensions [29–31]. The dynamic interactions between these dimensions in driving the onset and progression of CMM, as well as their shared biological underpinnings, remain a scientific black box that has yet to be thoroughly explored. Therefore, from a “multimorbidity” perspective, identifying high-risk populations for frailty states centered on CMM and elucidating the common genetic pathological mechanisms are key pathways for developing precise prevention and clinical diagnosis and treatment strategies.

The precise identification and prevention of metabolism-related aging disease clusters characterized by CMM, frailty, and somatic metabolic aging (SP, OP) face critical challenges. First, there is the obstacle of identifying common pathways obscured by disease heterogeneity. CMM exhibits high clinical heterogeneity [32, 33], making it exceptionally difficult to identify common, intervenable core biological pathways behind the complex phenotypes. Second, there is a current lack of precise prediction and risk stratification tools capable of integrating multisystem aging indicators [34–36]. Existing models are mostly limited to single-disease risk prediction [34–36]. Although Huo et al. [37] have made positive explorations by combining the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) with the frailty index (FI) and applying it to cardiovascular disease prediction, most current studies still rely on the combination of single indicators and FI. Thus, they fail to effectively quantify the joint effects of core indicators reflecting systemic physiological dysregulation (such as frailty) on CMM risk or to compare the predictive performance differences among various indicators, lacking multi-tool integration and systematic global discovery. Most importantly, there is a disconnect between macro-level clinical phenotypes and micro-level genetic architecture evidence. Although progress has been made in exploring causal relationships between modifiable factors and CMM [38], there is currently a lack of integrated evidence spanning macro clinical and micro omics levels, as well as a deconstruction of the genetic comorbidity mechanisms within CMM itself.

In recent years, systems biology and pan-disease research (e.g., pan-cancer studies) have proposed a new paradigm for understanding the co-occurrence of complex diseases by identifying “common factors” [39–41]. Inspired by this, our study aims to investigate a potential common frailty factor—termed the ‘F’ factor. We conceptually define the ‘F’ factor as a latent shared genetic susceptibility that may underlie the comorbidity of cardiometabolic and somatic metabolic aging. To systematically dissect this factor, we designed and implemented a multi-level analytical framework integrating clinical epidemiology and genetic epidemiology. First, to phenotypically test our hypothesis, we first examined a panel of frailty-integrated indices—combining metabolic (e.g., TyG), musculoskeletal, and inflammatory markers—for their association with CMM. To this end, based on prospective cohort data from the nationally representative China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we developed and validated a series of frailty integration indices. These combine metabolic indices such as the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index and its derivatives [42–44], muscle metabolism-related indices such as pSMI and the sarcopenia index (SI) reflecting somatic health status [45, 46], with the frailty index that comprehensively captures the accumulation of health deficits [47–49], to embody their joint effects. Next, considering the role of chronic systemic inflammation, we included TyG-related indices, frailty measurement indices, sarcopenia-related indices, lipid inflammation indicators, and the Frailty-Integrated Index as candidate features. Using a dual-strategy approach for core feature selection, we identified key indicators with the strongest predictive power for CMM and constructed machine learning models (including seven methods such as logistic regression, random forest, and XGBoost) to achieve risk prediction of CMM in the population. At the level of shared genetic architecture analysis, we applied genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic-SEM) for the first time, integrating genome-wide association study (GWAS) summary data from six traits—coronary artery disease (CAD), type 2 diabetes (T2D), hypertension (HTN), bone mineral density (BMD), frailty, and telomere length (TL)—to model and quantify the shared genetic factor, ‘F’ factor. Finally, through a series of in-depth follow-up analyses (including genomic annotation, gene set enrichment analysis, cell-type-specific analysis, etc.), we systematically revealed the potential causal genes, enriched biological pathways, and associated cell types linked to ‘F’ factor.

Here, we conduct a comprehensive, systematic investigation that integrates deep modeling of clinical phenotypes with cross-omics profiling of genetic architecture. This not only provides novel and powerful tools for the early identification and risk stratification of CMM but, more importantly, reveals from a genetic origin the common pathways driving the onset of CMM and the comorbidity phenomenon characterized by frailty, which underlies the dysregulation of the “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk”. This provides a new theoretical basis for understanding the shared mechanisms of multimorbidity and for future development of intervention strategies targeting common pathways. Methods

Study population

The observational study component of this research utilized a prospective cohort study design based on the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). CHARLS is a nationally representative longitudinal survey encompassing adults aged 45 and older from 28 provinces, 150 counties, and 450 villages across China. The study is conducted by the National School of Development at Peking University. The baseline survey was carried out in 2011–2012 (Wave 1), followed by subsequent follow-up surveys (Wave 2: 2013, Wave 3: 2015, Wave 4: 2018) [51]. The study strictly adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB00001052-11015). All participants provided written informed consent before participation. This study rigorously followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [52]. Further information about CHARLS is available on its official website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en).

The exclusion criteria for this study were as follows: (1) age under 45 years (n = 777); (2) missing CMM diagnosis data at baseline or loss to follow-up (n = 415); (3) diagnosis of CMM at baseline (n = 1721); (4) missing data for any independent variable, including the TyG index, sarcopenia-related indices (pSMI, SI), and the frailty index (n = 9212); (5) missing CMM diagnosis at the 2015 follow-up (n = 1102); and (6) missing data for other baseline covariates used in the analysis (n = 1316). Ultimately, 3165 participants were included in the analysis. A detailed flowchart of participant selection is presented in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Flowchart of study participant inclusion and exclusion. Outcome definition

Based on previous research [8], CMM was defined as the coexistence of two or more cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, and stroke. We determined whether an individual had these conditions based on questions from the questionnaire data: “Has a doctor ever told you that you have hypertension?”, “Have you been diagnosed with diabetes or high blood sugar (including impaired glucose tolerance and elevated fasting blood glucose)?”, “Do you have heart disease (such as myocardial infarction, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart conditions)?”, and “Have you ever experienced a physician-diagnosed stroke (including cerebral infarction and cerebral hemorrhage)?”. The onset time for a new CMM event was defined as the midpoint between the survey wave when it was first reported and the prior wave at which the participant did not meet the CMM criteria [53, 54].

Calculation of TyG-related indices, frailty measurement index, sarcopenia-related indices, lipid inflammation indicators, and frailty-integrated indices

To comprehensively assess metabolic status, inflammation, body composition, and systemic frailty, this study calculated 4 categories of indices and the integrated indices constructed based on them: (1) insulin resistance-related indices, such as TyG index and its derivatives; (2) sarcopenia-related indices, such as the appendicular pSMI; (3) lipid inflammation indicators, such as the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP); and (4) the comprehensive frailty assessment index. Furthermore, to explore potential synergistic effects, we multiplied each of the first three categories of indices by FI to construct the corresponding Frailty-Integrated Indices.

Potential covariates

Covariates encompassed variables from various aspects, including sociodemographic characteristics, anthropometric measurements, and laboratory tests. These included age, sex, marital status, educational level, smoking status, alcohol consumption status, body mass index (BMI), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), and the presence of chronic conditions (such as cancer, rheumatic disease, dyslipidemia, chronic lung disease, liver disease, kidney disease, digestive system diseases and asthma. Educational level was categorized as Primary school or below, High school, and College or above. Marital status was grouped into married and unmarried. Health behavior-related factors included smoking and drinking status, defined as ever/current smoker or drinker (Yes) and never smoker or drinker (No). BMI was calculated as weight (kilograms) divided by height squared (meters squared). Laboratory indicators included C-reactive protein (CRP) and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), which was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) equation [70]. The history of chronic diseases was determined based on self-reported conditions from the questionnaire. Activities of daily living (ADL) were assessed using the Katz Index of Independence in Activities of Daily Living [71]. Functional status was measured using both ADL and Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) [72, 73]. CHARLS asked respondents whether they needed help with any of the six ADLs (dressing, bathing, eating, getting in or out of bed, using the toilet, and controlling urination and defecation) or five IADLs (preparing hot meals, shopping for groceries, making phone calls, taking medications, and managing finances) [74]. Based on previous studies, ADL and IADL were categorized as having no limitations or at least one limitation [75]. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) was defined according to the Age-Associated Cognitive Decline (AACD) criteria. Specifically, MCI involved a total score (sum of Word Recall Test and Mental Status Test scores) more than one standard deviation below the mean for the participant’s age group [76]. Participants aged 60 and above were grouped in five-year intervals, and those meeting the AACD criteria were classified as having MCI.

Machine learning data processing

As the dataset contained 2902 individuals without CMM and 263 individuals with CMM, we employed the SMOTE data balancing technique to achieve a relatively balanced distribution between positive and negative samples [77]. This aimed to enhance the machine learning models’ ability to learn and accurately distinguish between the different classes, thereby avoiding over-prediction of the majority group. To prevent overfitting and assess the generalization ability of the model predictions, we randomly split the samples in each group into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%) [78]. Critically, to prevent data leakage, the SMOTE procedure was applied only to the training data after the train-test split, and the test set was kept completely untouched until the final evaluation. Data processing and model construction were performed based on the training set (characteristics of the training and test sets are shown in Supplementary Table 2).

Core feature variables for model development were selected using a dual-method approach for cross-validation: first, the Boruta algorithm was used for preliminary feature screening [79], followed by Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) combined with a Support Vector Machine (SVM) classifier applied to all features [80]. This method recursively and dynamically removes the least important features based on model performance, ultimately selecting the optimal feature subset for further analysis. The SVM classifier, known for its robust performance in high-dimensional spaces, served as the base model for feature ranking. The RFE algorithm iterates through all possible feature subsets, evaluating model performance via cross-validation until the optimal feature set is identified. Finally, the intersection of features selected by both methods was taken as the core variables for model training. Both the feature selection and the subsequent hyperparameter tuning (described below) were nested within the fivefold cross-validation on the training set to ensure unbiased performance estimation.

We subsequently employed seven machine learning algorithms to predict the risk of CMM: Logistic Regression (LR), Support Vector Machine (SVM), Decision Tree, Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), and Random Forest (RF). The optimal hyperparameter configuration for each model was determined through grid search tuning [81]. On the training set, model training was conducted using a fivefold cross-validation strategy and resampling methods, while the test set was used for final model evaluation. Systematic model performance was assessed on both the test and validation sets. Predictive performance was evaluated using the Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curve (AUC), and model performance was further examined using confusion matrices. In addition to the AUC, we report calibration metrics (calibration curve, Brier score) on test set to provide a comprehensive view of model performance, particularly given the class imbalance. The optimal parameters for each trained model are listed in Supplementary Table 3. The hyperparameter configurations obtained via grid search effectively reduced the risk of overfitting and enhanced the predictive performance of each machine learning model on the dataset [81].

SHAP interpretability analysis

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) is a method rooted in cooperative game theory capable of explaining the output of any machine learning model [82]. By calculating the SHAP value for each predictive variable, its contribution to the model outcome can be characterized. The SHAP value represents the marginal contribution of the corresponding variable to the model’s output.

Data sources for single-input GWAS in genomic-SEM

The single-input GWAS data for our Musculoskeletal-cardiometabolic frailty factor were derived from six GWAS studies related to CMM, the musculoskeletal system, aging, and frailty, including Coronary Artery Disease (CAD), Type 2 Diabetes (T2D), Hypertension (HTN), total Bone Mineral Density (BMD), Frailty, and Telomere Length (TL). All input GWAS studies had ethical approval from their respective institutional review boards, and all participants provided informed consent. The data underwent stringent quality control.

The data for CAD, T2D, HTN, BMD, Frailty, and TL came from the main result data of public GWAS datasets. Specifically, the CAD, T2D, and HTN datasets were constructed using the UK Biobank by the MRC Integrative Epidemiology Unit consortium. The data for BMD, Frailty, and TL were sourced from the MR-Base platform developed by Gibran et al., which aggregates GWAS summary datasets generated by various consortia, supporting systematic causal inference across the human phenome [83]. Quality control for single-input GWAS

Exclusion of low-quality samples: Samples with a missing rate exceeding 5% were excluded [84].

MHC region: Due to the high genetic diversity and structural complexity, particularly the polymorphism of immune-related genes in the Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) region (located on chromosome 6, approximately between positions 25,000,000 and 35,000,000 bp), special handling was applied, typically involving exclusion or careful treatment.

Preparation of summary statistics for the constructed ‘F’ factor:​ When preparing the summary statistics for ‘F’ factor, we used default parameters. We included all autosomal SNPs from the six input GWAS related to ‘F’ factor that passed recommended quality control filters, mapped to the 1000 Genomes Phase 3 EUR panel. SNPs with a minor allele frequency (MAF) < 0.01 were removed (as these SNPs are prone to error due to small sample sizes within genotype clusters and typically have higher standard errors in LD score regression). Additionally, SNPs with an effect size estimate of zero were removed (to avoid affecting matrix reactivity, which is necessary for Genomic SEM), as were SNPs mismatched with the reference panel and those with allele mismatches.

Sample overlap in single-input GWAS

The single-input GWAS included in our analysis originated from different genomic data repositories, and the participants differed across these sources. This means that during our GWAS integration, we accounted for the potential for sample overlap between different cohorts to ensure the accuracy and broad applicability of the results, while also considering the statistical implications of any potential sample overlap.

Genomic-SEM modeling strategy

We used Genomic SEM, implemented in the GenomicSEM R package (v.0.0.5) [85], to perform a genome-wide structural equation modeling analysis on CAD, T2D, HTN, BMD, Frailty, and TL, investigating the broad genetic susceptibility underlying these ‘F’ factor-related traits. Genomic SEM is a newly developed multivariate method capable of fitting multiple latent variable models to explore the underlying structure of traits of interest.

Notably, Genomic SEM is not biased by sample overlap (e.g., the same UK Biobank participants appearing in multiple input GWAS) or imbalances in sample sizes. It also facilitates the identification of genetic variants that influence only a subset rather than all of the complex traits, and thus do not represent broad cross-trait susceptibility.

Genomic SEM is performed in two stages. The first stage estimates the empirical genetic covariance matrix and the corresponding sampling covariance matrix. We prepared the summary statistics for the CAD, T2D, HTN, BMD, Frailty, and TL GWAS for this stage and used the multivariate extension of cross-trait Linkage Disequilibrium Score (LDSC) regression to generate an empirical genetic covariance matrix between the six traits [85, 86], which served as the input for the SEM common factor model. The second stage specifies an SEM model that minimizes the difference between the hypothesized covariance matrix and the empirical covariance matrix calculated in the first stage. Here, our primary objective was to reveal the genetic architecture underlying the six Musculoskeletal-cardiometabolic frailty-related traits; therefore, we tested a single-factor model. Model fit was evaluated using the Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), model χ2, Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Comparative Fit Index (CFI) (see Supplementary Tables 4 and 5). By applying the appropriate common factor SEM specification, the associations of individual autosomal SNPs were incorporated along with the genetic and related sample covariance matrices, generating multi-’F’ factor genome-wide analysis results that reflect the shared covariance among the 4,546,140 input SNPs from the Musculoskeletal-cardiometabolic frailty-related GWAS.

SNP heterogeneity assessment in genomic-SEM

To assess whether the SNP associations for ‘F’ factor were appropriately modeled within the multivariate SEM framework, we calculated the SNP heterogeneity statistic (QSNP). The null hypothesis for the QSNP test is that the SNP associations from the individual phenotype GWAS are statistically mediated through ‘F’ factor. Therefore, a significant QSNP test for ‘F’ factor indicates that the SNP influences the traits through pathways other than the shared genetic mechanism captured by ‘F’ factor model for Musculoskeletal-cardiometabolic frailty. A significance threshold of P < 0.05 was used to evaluate QSNP heterogeneity.

Multi-level evaluation of genomic-SEM

We implemented a multi-level adjustment strategy for the constructed Genomic SEM model. This included, first, adjusting the p values from the correlation tests (using thresholds of P < 5 × 10^(− 16) and P < 5 × 10^(− 12)) to analyze the novel SNP loci identifiable at different significance levels. Secondly, we estimated the genomic control parameters using a two-step LD Score regression approach. The specific steps for the latter were as follows: No SNPs with missing values were removed. No SNPs with an INFO score less than 0.9 were removed. No SNPs with a Minor Allele Frequency (MAF) less than 0.01 were removed. No SNPs with P values outside the valid range were removed. Non-SNP variants or SNPs with ambiguous strand orientation were removed. Partitions with zero variance in LD scores were removed. The two-step estimator was used with a cutoff set to 30.

Defining genomic loci and identifying novel variants

We used FUMA (Functional Mapping and Annotation of Genetic Associations) [87] to identify genomic loci and lead SNPs for ‘F’ factor. These lead SNPs are in linkage disequilibrium (LD) with other SNPs at an r2 threshold of < 0.1 and reached genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10^ − 8). First, we input the summary statistics of the SNPs for Musculoskeletal-cardiometabolic frailty to assess their association strength. We also compared the lead SNPs and loci with those identified in the original univariate GWAS. A locus was considered novel if it was more than 1 Mb away from any locus identified in the univariate GWAS data.

To determine if the 230 lead SNPs for ‘F’ factor had previously known pleiotropic associations, we also consulted the GWAS Catalog for published significant associations (P < 5 × 10^ − 8). Furthermore, based on FUMA’s functionality, we analyzed the risk gene loci for the established ‘F’ factor model, using a significance threshold of P < 5 × 10^ − 8. Additionally, we performed gene-based analysis using MAGMA (Multi-marker Analysis of GenoMic Annotation) on the relevant output files. MAGMA is a tool for post-processing GWAS results, designed to evaluate the association between genes and a phenotype (such as a disease or health trait). It aggregates multiple genetic markers (e.g., SNPs) into a gene-level signal and computes the association of each gene with the phenotype. The aim is to extract gene-function-related information from genome-wide SNP data for analysis at the gene level, using a significance threshold of FDR-adjusted P value < 0.05.

Fine-mapping

To identify the most probable causal variants associated with ‘F’ factor, we used SuSIE (Sum of Single Effects) [88, 89] and FINEMAP [90, 91], both implemented within the R package echolocatoR(v.2.0.3). We set a posterior probability threshold of 0.75 to define credible sets of probable causal variants. Fine-mapping was conducted using the European ancestry reference panel from the 1000 Genomes Project Phase 3 to estimate local linkage disequilibrium (LD) structure, which matches the predominant ancestry of the input GWAS summary statistics. The detailed steps are as follows:

Both SuSIE and FINEMAP are tools for fine-mapping analysis, aiming to pinpoint the most likely causal variants associated with a phenotype. In this step, we used a 250 kb window around each lead SNP and calculated the causal inference probability for each SNP within these regions. Credible Set:​ We set a posterior probability threshold of 0.75; if a variant’s posterior probability exceeded this threshold, it was considered a probable causal variant. Consensus SNPs and Probability Sets:​ echolocatoRdefines a ‘consensus SNP’ as a variant that appears in the results of both SuSIE and FINEMAP. For these consensus SNPs, the tool calculates their average posterior probability, and based on the probability results, determines the average credible set (credibility is set to 1 if the SNP’s posterior probability exceeds 0.75 in both SuSIE and FINEMAP, otherwise 0).

Transcriptome-wide association study (TWAS)

After identifying potential causal variants, we performed a Transcriptome-Wide Association Study (TWAS) [92] to prioritize genes associated with ‘F’ factor based on the relationship between genetically predicted gene expression and the phenotype. We conducted the TWAS using the FUSION method [93], utilizing 37,920 pre-computed expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) features (i.e., gene/tissue pairs) from the GTEx v.8 dataset. These features are used to calculate expression associations across different genes and tissues.

Further analysis of TWAS results:​ ‘F’ factor data contained sufficient variants to analyze 36,149 features (out of the 37,920 eQTL features), indicating high data quality. Genes with a TWAS P value less than 0.05 (significantly associated with Musculoskeletal-cardiometabolic frailty) were carried forward for further analysis.

For these TWAS-significant genes, we further applied the FOCUS method, a fine-mapping method specifically designed for TWAS studies. We used FOCUS to prioritize putative causal genes for ‘F’ factor. The FOCUS method evaluates the causality of gene-phenotype relationships based on the posterior inclusion probability (FOCUS PIP). Combining this with the previous steps, we considered genes that were not only significant in the TWAS but also supported by FOCUS evidence as being more likely to be causal.

Gene set and enrichment analysis

We performed gene enrichment analysis and protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using MAGMA and GeneCards data. The potential relationships between ‘F’ factor’s causal genes and their interacting proteins were investigated using the GeneMANIA platform [94]. Simultaneously, we obtained the latest Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway annotations and Gene Ontology (GO) annotations from the Database for Annotation, Visualization, and Integrated Discovery (DAVID 6.8) to serve as the background [95]. Genes were mapped to these background sets, and enrichment analysis was conducted using the R package clusterProfiler(version 3.21) to explore the enriched pathways and biological functions of genes near the novel loci. The GO annotation analysis included three modules: Cellular Component (CC), Biological Process (BP), and Molecular Function (MF). The resulting P values were adjusted using the default Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) false discovery rate method. An adjusted P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cellular annotation analysis

To identify cell types etiologically relevant to ‘F’ factor, we used CELLECT (Cell Type Expression Specificity integration for complex traits) [96], which integrates single-cell RNA sequencing data. We used the Tabula Muris dataset, which contains transcriptomic data from 100,000 cells from mice (Mus musculus) across 20 organs and tissues. We preprocessed and normalized the single-cell RNA sequencing data from Tabula Muris using CELLEX to calculate the expression specificity score for each gene. Cell type-specific analysis was performed by calling the LDSC software, which classified the cell types. A false discovery rate (FDR) threshold of 0.05 was applied.

Partitioning heritability across genomic regions

The LDSC tool was used to calculate the partitioning of heritability. This method assesses the contribution of different genomic regions (such as genes, enhancers, promoters) to the heritability of a trait by allocating the genetic signal from the GWAS summary statistics to these predefined functional categories. Specifically, LDSC uses weighted LD matrices, allele frequency files, and the summary statistics to estimate the heritability contribution of each region.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (range) or median (interquartile range), and comparisons were made using the Student’s t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages, and comparisons can be made using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

We used Cox proportional hazards regression models to explore the relationship between baseline Frailty-Integrated Index levels and the incidence of CMM, calculating hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The proportional hazards assumption was visually assessed using Schoenfeld residuals prior to analysis. Three models were estimated: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for sociological factors including age, sex, marital status, alcohol use, smoking, and residence; Model 3 included all covariates. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analysis based on the multivariable-adjusted Cox regression was performed to visualize the linear or non-linear relationship between baseline Frailty-Integrated Index levels and CMM risk. Finally, to compare the discriminatory performance of the original indices versus their corresponding Frailty-Integrated Indices for CMM, we performed an analysis visualized using AUC curves.

All statistical analyses in this study were conducted using R version 4.4.2 and Python version 3.12.5 (Python Software Foundation).

Association between frailty-integrated indices and CMM risk

We explored the association between different Frailty-Integrated Indices and CMM risk using the CHARLS sample. Dose–response relationship between frailty-integrated indices and CMM risk

To further investigate the specific dose–response relationship between Frailty-Integrated Indices and CMM risk, we plotted 3-knot restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression curves for different indices against CMM risk, as detailed in Fig. 3. Specifically, TyG-FI, TyG-WC-FI, TyG-WHtR-FI, SI-FI, pSMI-FI, and SHR-FI showed a linear positive correlation with CMM risk (P for nonlinearity > 0.05). Notably, AIP-FI, NHDL-FI, AC-FI, and LCI-FI exhibited a nonlinear relationship with CMM risk (P for nonlinearity < 0.05). To further assess this nonlinear relationship, a two-piece Cox proportional hazards regression model was applied. After comprehensive adjustment for relevant covariates, we identified the inflection point for LCI-FI and CMM risk at 3.068 (P for log-likelihood ratio test < 0.001) (results of threshold effect analysis see Supplementary File 6). When LCI-FI was below 3.068, an increase in LCI-FI was significantly associated with an increased risk of CMM (HR = 1.776, 95% CI = 1.566–2.015). However, when LCI-FI was ≥ 3.068 (HR = 1.001, 95% CI = 0.964–1.039), LCI-FI was not significantly associated with CMM risk. Similarly, NHDL-FI showed a risk inflection point at 13.595. When NHDL-FI was < 13.595, an increase in NHDL-FI was significantly associated with increased CMM risk (HR = 1.103, 95% CI = 1.076–1.131, P < 0.05). Machine learning feature selection

To identify the most relevant predictor variables, we employed a two-step feature selection strategy. First, the Boruta algorithm, a wrapper method based on a random forest classifier, was used to screen candidate variables, retaining 19 features (see Figs. 6A-B). Second, Spearman correlation analysis was performed on the training set variables, and a correlation heatmap was plotted to assess the relationships between predictors and evaluate potential multicollinearity (Fig. 6C). Subsequently, we applied Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE), which recursively and dynamically selected 16 features based on model performance (Fig. 6D). Finally, the intersection of features from both methods yielded 12 predictor variables: FI, pSMI, AIP, AC, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, AIP-FI, AC-FI, CRP, AIP-CRP, SBP, and HbA1c (see Fig. 6E).Fig. 6Core feature selection for the CMM risk prediction model

Genomic structural equation modeling (Genomic-SEM) and statistical metrics

LD Score regression analysis indicated that the heritability contributions (h2) for the six univariate input GWAS (CAD, T2D, HTN, BMD, Frailty, TL) were 10.3%, 11%, 20.4%, 14%, 21.8%, and 9.02%, respectively. The genetic covariances between frailty and the other traits were 0.0322 (CAD), 0.0182 (T2D), -0.0066 (BMD), 0.0495 (HTN), and -0.0151 (TL) (detailed univariate genetic parameters are provided in Supplementary Files 13 and 14; Fig. 8A, B). During model preparation, structural equation modeling analysis was performed. The common factor model for the genetic covariance matrix of the five input GWAS showed good fit with the empirical covariance matrix (Comparative Fit Index [CFI] = 0.986, Standardized Root Mean Square Residual [SRMR] = 0.029) (for detailed model stability assessment, see Supplementary File 4; parameters for the latent factor [F1] and the univariate SEM are in Supplementary File 5). This provides evidence for the existence of a shared genetic factor, ‘F’ factor. Stratified assessment of genomic-SEM GWAS

By extending the SEM to incorporate individual variants, we generated an indirect GWAS estimating the association of 4,546,140 single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with our ‘F’ factor. We identified 327 lead SNPs at a threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻12 and 150 lead SNPs at a more stringent threshold of P < 5 × 10⁻1⁶ (see Supplementary Files 15, 16). The SNPs identified at different P value thresholds were all novel, meaning they differed from the loci identified in the six individual input GWAS used to build ‘F’ factor, highlighting the enhanced discovery power of genomic SEM.

Genomic control assessment of genomic-SEM based on LD score regression

Following the parameter controls described in the methods, 3,601,806 SNPs were removed, resulting in 944,334 valid SNPs retained after considering the regression coefficient. The mean chi-square value for all SNPs was 1.402, the genomic control lambda (λGC) was 1.313, the maximum chi-square value was 141.421, the number of genome-wide significant SNPs was 294, the heterogeneity test was passed (P > 0.05), the total observed-scale heritability (h2) was 0.0119 (SE 0.0006), the ratio of genetic to environmental factors was < 0, the intercept in the regression model was 0.988 (SE 0.0102). These multiple estimates collectively indicate that the observed inflation in the test statistics for the latent factor in the SEM is likely due to polygenic heritability signals rather than population stratification bias or confounding by pervasive pleiotropy.

Causal loci and proximal genes identified by fine-mapping

Additionally, we performed functional annotation of these 17 causal gene loci at the cellular and tissue levels. Among these genes, APOE and ATXN2 were significantly expressed in tissues such as adipose, heart, and nerve. SLC22A3 was significantly expressed in tissues including Artery—Coronary, Artery—Tibial, and Nerve. Other genes did not show significant expression in the corresponding tissues (Fig. 10Q). Protein–protein interaction analysis using GeneMANIA revealed co-expression and genetic interaction networks among these genes (Fig. 10R, Supplementary File 21).

Transcriptomic prediction (TWAS)

We performed a Transcriptome-Wide Association Study (TWAS) using FUSION to identify gene-level associations with ‘F’ factor based on genetically predicted expression. We found 69 genes that survived multiple testing correction (Supplementary File 22), including CDKN2A, CDKN2B, FES, and SLC22A3. Subsequently, we performed further fine-mapping on the Genomic SEM data using FOCUS, which identified 42 genes as potential causal signals for ‘F’ factor (Table 8). To further prioritize these "high-confidence" gene-level associations, we performed an intersection test. This included CDKN2A, CDKN2B, FES, and SLC22A3. The TWAS Z-score for FES was greater than 0, indicating that predicted higher expression of this gene is positively associated with ‘F’ factor, suggesting upregulation might increase risk. Conversely, the TWAS Z-scores for CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and SLC22A3 were less than 0, indicating that predicted lower expression of these genes is associated with ‘F’ factor, suggesting their downregulation might be related to increased risk. Cell-type enrichment analysis

We analyzed ‘F’ factor SEM model at the cellular level. Multi-tissue chromatin state analysis revealed significant enrichment for marks such as H3K4me3 in the Fetal Adrenal Gland and H3K27ac in the Lung (Supplementary File 29). Analysis showed significant enrichment and expression in tissues including Prostate, Myometrium, Cartilage, Chorion, Artery—Tibial, Blood Vessels, and Osteoblasts (Supplementary File 30). Eleven cell types exceeded the significance threshold after multiple testing correction. These included Pancreas pancreatic stellate cell, Heart endothelial cell, Pancreas endothelial cell, Lung lung endothelial cell, Kidney endothelial cell, Fat endothelial cell, Heart myofibroblast cell, and Brain Non-Myeloid brain pericyte (Supplementary File 31).

Partitioning of heritability across genomic regions

Partitioned heritability analysis based on genomic annotations showed that the genome-wide association signals for ‘F’ factor were significantly enriched (FDR P < 0.05) in multiple functional genomic categories (Supplementary File 32). The region around transcription start sites (TSS_Hoffman) showed the highest enrichment fold (Enrichment = 8.99, P = 6.33e-05). Although this category covers only about 1.78% of SNPs, it contributes 16.03% of the heritability, strongly suggesting the pivotal role of regulatory variation in core promoter regions in ‘F’ factor’s genetic architecture.

Notably, the histone mark H3K4me1 (H3K4me1_peaks_Trynka), associated with enhancer activity, covered 16.98% of SNPs and contributed the highest proportion of heritability (51.82%, Enrichment = 3.05). This indicates that the shared genetic basis of ‘F’ factor is significantly enriched in enhancer regions, potentially involving widespread effects on distal gene regulatory networks. Furthermore, other active regulatory marks, such as H3K9ac (Enrichment = 5.82) and H3K4me3 (Enrichment = 5.02), also showed significant enrichment, further supporting the central role of active chromatin states in regulating transcription of "F" factor-related genes.

On the other hand, intronic regions (Intron_UCSC) contributed the largest proportion of heritability (50.83%, Enrichment = 1.31), indicating that a substantial amount of genetic signal resides in non-coding regions, likely acting through mechanisms affecting RNA splicing or functioning as intronic regulatory elements for fine-tuning gene expression. Although the protein-coding regions and their flanks (Coding_UCSC.extend.500) encompassed a limited proportion of SNPs (6.37%), a significant heritability contribution was still detected (Enrichment = 3.15), suggesting that, in addition to regulatory variation, some coding variants with stronger effects might contribute to ‘F’ factor by altering protein function.

Particularly noteworthy was the significant heritability enrichment observed in fetal developmental DNase I hypersensitive sites (FetalDHS_Trynka) (Enrichment = 3.60). This provides genetic support for the hypothesis that ‘F’ factor’s role in metabolic aging may have origins in early developmental programming. In summary, the shared genetic architecture of ‘F’ factor exhibits a multi-layered regulatory pattern, with heritability significantly enriched across multidimensional regulatory elements ranging from transcription start sites, enhancers, developmental open chromatin regions, to gene bodies, systematically revealing the potential molecular basis through which genetic variants influence the skeletal-muscle-heart interaction and metabolic aging processes by modulating gene regulatory networks.

Discussion

Here, this study proposes a “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk” as an integrative framework for metabolic aging. Our clinical findings on frailty-CMM associations and the genetic discovery of a shared ‘F’ factor provide convergent, multi-level support for this concept, offering a new direction for investigating its biological underpinnings.

Specifically, based on CHARLS cohort data, we completed the screening of clinical biomarkers for Cardiometabolic Multimorbidity (CMM) and constructed machine learning models, confirming significant associations between various biomarkers related to metabolism, inflammation, and the musculoskeletal system and the risk of CMM incidence. Furthermore, we developed a series of Frailty-Integrated Indices based on the original biomarkers, which significantly enhanced their clinical diagnostic performance. Subsequently, by performing feature selection and machine learning modeling incorporating both the original indices and the newly developed Frailty-Integrated Indices, we provided an accurate predictive tool for clinical practice, offering new insights for the personalized assessment and prevention of CMM. More importantly, the phenomenon of multimorbidity—the co-occurrence of two or more chronic conditions—is not a random combination of diseases but follows identifiable and classifiable patterns [97]. Therefore, inspired by systems biology and the "pan-disease" research paradigm, we employed Genomic Structural Equation Modeling (Genomic-SEM), constructing and characterizing for the first time a multivariate GWAS model for a shared frailty factor—’F’ factor—underlying CMM and musculoskeletal system decline in the context of metabolic aging. This confirmed significant genetic correlations between CMM components, physical frailty, and aging. Simultaneously, using a multi-dimensional post-GWAS framework, we identified causal genes, cells, tissues, and pathways significantly involved in the multimorbidity process. From a holistic perspective of multimorbidity, we delineated the shared genetic pathological landscape of the “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk” underpinning ‘F’ factor, thereby bridging the gap between clinical cohort studies and genetic susceptibility research.

Clinical cohort highlights the clinical significance of the “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk”

Our exploration of the association between different Frailty-Integrated Indices and CMM risk within the CHARLS sample identified 16 biomarkers, including SI-FI, TyG-FI, and pSMI-FI, that were significantly associated with CMM incidence in the Chinese elderly population. Notably, pSMI-FI showed a significant effect on increasing CMM risk. This finding extends beyond mere biomarker discovery, highlighting the close relationship between musculoskeletal health and cardiometabolic status. Previous research indicates that traits related to muscle loss, including sarcopenia (SP), significantly increase the risk of various metabolic diseases such as insulin resistance (IR), obesity, and chronic inflammation [98], which are established key pathological mechanisms driving CMM development [99]. Clinical evidence shows that loss of muscle mass in community-dwelling elderly individuals is significantly associated with atherosclerosis, vascular calcification, and arterial stiffness [100–102], suggesting SP is a risk factor for coronary artery disease (CAD). Regarding shared mechanisms, the pathogenesis of both involves processes like hormonal changes, immunosenescence, and oxidative stress [103], indicating a bidirectional relationship between SP and CMM regulated by a complex metabolic network. Within this network, chronic inflammation—a shared core pathogenesis for both SP and CAD—is increasingly recognized as a central hub in this bidirectional regulatory network. Emerging evidence shows significantly elevated levels of IL-6 and CRP in individuals with SP [104]. This state of low-grade chronic inflammation significantly increases the risk of cardiovascular diseases like heart failure (HF) and leads to poorer prognoses [105, 106]. Integrating previous research with our findings on biomarker associations, we preliminarily conceptualize this relationship as the “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk”, suggesting a significant association between the musculoskeletal system and cardiometabolic development and progression, sharing not only clinical correlates and pathogenic mechanisms but also potentially a shared genetic landscape. Additionally, we found that AIP-FI, NHDL-FI, AC-FI, and LCI-FI exhibited nonlinear relationships and dose–response effects with CMM risk. These results confirm the central role of chronic inflammation in CMM pathogenesis and underscore the predictive value of lipid-inflammatory indicators for CMM.

Clinical cohort demonstrates performance improvement of frailty-integrated indices over original indices

Previous studies have made valuable explorations into predictive biomarkers for CMM, particularly regarding the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index—a reliable surrogate marker of IR. Initial research identified the relationship between the TyG index and CMM incidence, providing preliminary evidence for its use in CMM monitoring and screening [107]. Subsequently, a series of TyG-derived indices were shown to have more robust clinical utility than the original index [108–110]. Another prospective study involving 374,274 participants from the UK Biobank systematically characterized the link between TyG-related indices and CMM incidence trajectories [111], highlighting the key role of TyG-WHtR and TyG-WC in CMM occurrence, progression, and prevention. These studies collectively confirm the substantial and beneficial clinical value of TyG and its derivative biomarkers for CMM prediction. However, few studies have investigated these indices combined with the frailty index (FI), a marker representing the body’s frail state. One study based on dual Chinese and US cohorts preliminarily confirmed the significant association between TyG-FI and stroke [48]. A global systematic exploration of FI-combined indices is currently lacking.

Addressing this gap, we investigated TyG-related indices, sarcopenia-related indices, lipid-inflammatory indicators, and their corresponding Frailty-Integrated Indices using the longitudinal CHARLS cohort. Using ROC curves and AUC values, we measured the diagnostic value of different indicators for CMM. Results showed that among the original indices without FI adjustment, FI itself demonstrated the best discriminatory ability (AUC: 0.684). Importantly, we found that within the Frailty-Integrated Indices, the discriminatory performance of all indices improved, with TyG-BRI-FI showing the best performance (AUC: 0.721). This demonstrates the performance enhancement achieved by adjusting the original indices with FI, contrasting with previous findings and further emphasizing frailty as the pathogenic basis for CMM. It provides a clinical prediction tool and theoretical reference for moving towards precise CMM prediction within an FI-integrated context. Notably, this study found that the discriminative performance (AUC) of some Frailty-Integrated Indices (e.g., TyG-BRI-FI) surpassed that of the frailty index itself. This finding suggests that while frailty is a strong predictor, its multiplicative integration with specific metabolic/body composition indicators may capture a more specific or higher-risk subgroup of individuals—namely, those in whom metabolic dysregulation and physiological vulnerability co-exist and may exert synergistic effects. The mechanisms underlying this phenomenon require further exploration at the level of interaction analysis.

Machine learning model incorporating musculoskeletal, systemic inflammation, metabolic, and frailty biomarkers achieves CMM risk prediction

After initially identifying significant associations between the aforementioned biomarkers and CMM, we comprehensively included feature variables such as Musculoskeletal indices, systemic inflammation markers, metabolic indicators, and Frailty-Integrated Indices for machine learning model construction. Selecting core feature variables is crucial for robust model performance. Therefore, we employed a multi-stage feature selection strategy, including the Boruta algorithm and Recursive Feature Elimination (RFE) combined with Support Vector Machines (SVM), to ensure feature representativeness. Furthermore, due to the class imbalance between positive and negative outcome samples in our cohort, we applied the SMOTE method during model training to ensure model stability.

Ultimately, we selected 12 core features for model training and validation. After training and evaluating seven machine learning models, the ANN model demonstrated excellent performance in feature selection and model optimization (validation set AUC reached 0.727), and its confusion matrix indicated strong discriminatory ability and robust predictive performance.

To deconstruct the “black box” of the machine learning model for clinical practice, we used SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) to interpret the model’s core features. Results showed that SBP, TyG-BMI, pSMI, AC-FI, TyG-WC, AIP-CRP, and FI played central roles in the model. Consistent with prior related research [112, 113], these findings indicate the significant contribution of blood pressure, metabolism, lipid inflammation, and frailty to the model, highlighting the importance of systemic, metabolic inflammation, and bodily frailty in CMM pathogenesis. Waterfall plots also provided quantitative values for the contribution of variables to individual predictions, aiding in understanding how specific features influence outcomes.

Considering its discrimination, interpretability, and calibration performance together, the clinical translational positioning of the machine learning model developed in this study should be carefully defined. Its core strength lies in integrating multi-system indicators, providing an objective, quantifiable risk assessment tool. SHAP analysis confirms that its driving factors are consistent with known pathophysiology, enhancing its credibility. Calibration analysis further indicates that some models (e.g., LightGBM) can provide relatively reliable risk probabilities. Therefore, this model is most suitable for the initial screening and stratification of CMM risk in elderly populations within community or clinical settings.​ It aims to efficiently identify individuals at moderate-to-high risk who require more detailed examinations (such as intensified metabolic assessment or cardiovascular function testing), thereby optimizing the allocation of preventive healthcare resources.

However, its current limitations must be frankly acknowledged:​ First, the discrimination of the optimal model (ANN) is moderate (AUC ~ 0.73), and its calibration curve shows some deviation in predicted probabilities, meaning it is not sufficient to serve as a standalone diagnostic or treatment decision-making tool.​ Second, the model was evaluated only via internal validation within a single cohort (CHARLS), lacking validation in an independent external cohort; thus, its generalizability awaits further proof. Future work has two clear directions: first, optimizing the accuracy of model output through probability calibration post-processing techniques; and second, validating its efficacy in external cohorts​ and exploring prospective comparisons with simpler clinical rules to ultimately establish its incremental clinical utility.

From clinical cohort to genetic modeling – construction of ‘F’ factor

To further decode the metabolic disease state of the “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk” atop the frail “soil” from a systems medicine perspective, we introduced Genomic-SEM. This emerging GWAS analysis method can model multiple genome-wide datasets, overcoming the limitation of previous GWAS studies focusing on single phenotypes. It represents an important new strategy for elucidating multi-system multimorbidity states in humans. Recent studies have applied Genomic-SEM to various comorbidity domains, including aging [114], adolescence [115], and psychiatric disorders [116]. However, no study has yet revealed the overall genetic structure of metabolic aging from the global perspective of frailty, CMM, and musculoskeletal decline.

Here, we integrated genome-wide data from approximately 2 million individuals. Using a combination of genetic methods—including Genomic-SEM modeling, multivariate post-GWAS analysis, causal gene fine-mapping, Transcriptome-Wide Association Study (TWAS), and chromatin/cell enrichment annotation—we systematically uncovered the shared genetic phenomena and architecture between CAD, T2D, HTN, BMD, Frailty, and TL. By modeling the genetic structure of these six traits, we not only identified multiple novel genetic loci associated with ‘F’ factor but also discovered that its shared genetic signals showed specific expression during a critical stage of fetal brain development and were significantly enriched in developmental enhancers and active transcription start regions. This suggests that genetic factors may exert a foundational influence on the systemic functional decline and metabolic multimorbidity risk of the "Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk" in adulthood by regulating gene expression programs involved in fetal neural and multi-system development—such as pathways involving key genes like APOE, ATXN2, and SLC22A3 in cellular senescence, cholesterol metabolism, and neurotransmitter transport. This study provides integrated evidence for understanding the polygenic genetic architecture of metabolic aging-related multimorbidity in the context of frailty and lays important genetic theoretical insights for the early identification of high-risk populations and the development of intervention strategies for cross-system diseases targeting frailty and “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk” metabolic aging.

Revelation of ‘F’ factor genetic structure and shared genetic factor

Based on the construction and characterization of the Genomic-SEM model, we discovered genetic covariances among CAD, T2D, HTN, BMD, Frailty, and TL, further indicating significant shared genetic liability among the component traits of ‘F’ factor. Crucially, CAD, HTN, and T2D (as components of CMM) and Frailty were identified as core players in the overall genetic structure. We found, consistent with prior knowledge, significant positive genetic correlations among these four core traits. This reflects a common frailty nature among CMM components, corroborating our initial hypothesis. However, the association between frailty and individual diseases is not a simple unidirectional link but rather a complex developmental pattern of bidirectional regulation and synergistic exacerbation leading to a vicious cycle. Previous research has established frailty as a risk factor for adverse cardiovascular events, including myocardial infarction and heart failure [117–119]. A cohort study based on the US elderly population showed that frailty is a significant risk factor for major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients without known coronary heart disease (CHD) [120], strongly demonstrating the necessity of identifying frailty status in CHD patients. Research in T2D provides similar insights. A study by Wu et al. [121] based on a cohort of 18,062 adults from the UK Biobank found that frailty and pre-frailty significantly increase the risk of diabetic microvascular complications (DMC) in T2D patients, filling an evidence gap regarding the association between pre-frailty and DMC risk in T2D. Mechanistically, a genome-wide pleiotropic association study preliminarily revealed the shared genetic basis between T2D and muscle loss/frailty phenotypes, also identifying eight druggable genes like MTOR and PPARG [122], offering references for holistic treatment in comorbid states.

Compared to GWAS data for many single traits like CAD and HTN, ‘F’ factor is more representative of the metabolic aging phenomenon of the "Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk" under frailty. We confirmed significant genetic crosstalk among CAD, T2D, HTN, BMD, Frailty, and TL, indicating that these traits are not isolated entities but exist within a complex network of interactions, underpinned by a intricate shared genetic landscape.

Novel and lead SNPs, risk loci, and their associations with traits

Further analysis of the Genomic-SEM model led to the identification of 7 novel SNP loci, 47 lead SNP loci, and 38 risk gene loci. Among these, the MRAS gene-related variant rs1199337 was further emphasized; this variant has been previously shown to be associated with CAD [123]. Additionally, the other novel SNP loci identified had not been reported before, highlighting the discovery power of Genomic-SEM. The GWAS subtraction method identified a series of significant loci. Notably, we identified the important role of the APOE-related variant rs7412 for ‘F’ factor. In previous studies, APOE, the most well-known genetic risk factor for late-onset Alzheimer’s disease (LOAD), has variants like rs7412 significantly associated with processes such as aging [124], hypertension [125], and cognition [126, 127]. A GWAS study by Guo et al. [128] on European-ancestry participants from the UK Biobank showed rs7412 as a strongly associated genetic variant for BioAgeAccel. Our discovery, combined with prior evidence, underscores the importance of APOE-centered lipid metabolism in metabolic aging-related multimorbidity states. Furthermore, we identified the potential role of the SLC22A3-related variant rs3918286. This gene has been shown to significantly increase T2D susceptibility [129], significantly alter the pharmacokinetics of metformin and glycemic control [130], and be significantly associated with CAD traits [131], further validating our discovery of significant shared genetic loci for CMM.

At the tissue level, annotation results based on FUMA showed that genes associated with ‘F’ factor loci were significantly enriched and expressed during the late mid-prenatal stage of brain development. Although these genes did not show widespread significant expression thresholds in most adult tissues, they showed concentrated enrichment in female reproductive tissues like the uterus, cervix, and fallopian tubes. This suggests a potential neurodevelopmental origin for ‘F’ factor, hinting that its genetic basis might be anchored in an early developmental physiological axis connecting the reproductive system and the central nervous system, such as the developmental programming of the hypothalamic-pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis [132]. The HPG axis not only regulates gonadal function and reproductive cycles but also plays a central role in fetal neurodevelopment [133] and lifelong metabolic regulation [134]. Therefore, our findings suggest that genetic variations associated with ‘F’ factor may exert long-term effects on the homeostasis and aging processes of multiple systems (bone, muscle, heart) in adulthood by interfering with the establishment and function of such early developmental endocrine axes. This hypothesis is strongly supported by the key loci we mapped (e.g., the known aging-associated locus rs7412), providing a novel genetic perspective and mechanistic entry point for understanding the developmental origins of metabolic multimorbidity and systemic aging.

Causal genes identified by multi-method fine-mapping framework and their functional pathways

Using fine-mapping analyses (SuSIE and FINEMAP), we identified and annotated probable causal variants for ‘F’ factor, ultimately mapping 46 causal variant loci and 17 putative causal genes, such as RSRC1 on chromosome 3, PLG and SLC22A3 on chromosome 6, and APOE on chromosome 19. We analyzed the expression patterns of these causal genes across 54 tissues. Consistent with initial findings, results showed significant expression of APOE and SLC22A3 in tissues including coronary arteries, brain, liver, lung, nerves, and the uterus. Additionally, we found that these causal genes interact through co-expression and genetic interactions, suggesting they may form a regulatory network influencing the progression of ‘F’ factor.

APOE, identified as a top candidate for the ‘F’ factor in this study, is a pleiotropic locus with established roles in lipid metabolism and neurobiology. Studies indicate APOE can promote AD progression by modulating the expression profiles of astrocytes and microglia, affecting the CNS immune response [135, 136]. Another autopsy-based AD study, from a translational medicine perspective, achieved AD risk prediction by calculating an APOE-weighted polygenic risk score [137]. This evidence focuses on the important role of APOE and its genetic variants in neurodegenerative disease progression. Although recent advances have been made in areas like human reproductive health [138], vascular injury [139], and cancer immunity [140], the role of this gene in the metabolic aging process characterized by CMM and musculoskeletal decline awaits systematic identification.

SLC22A3 is a candidate gene encoding organic cation transporters (e.g., OCT3) and has been implicated in various metabolic diseases including CAD [131], T2D [141], and cancer [142–144]. In the cardiovascular field, research has confirmed that the rs3088442 variant in SLC22A3 is significantly associated with plasma lipoprotein(a) levels and CAD risk in Chinese populations [131]; another study confirmed a similar effect for the rs2048327 variant in this gene [145]. These studies confirm the gene’s potential research value in cardiometabolism. From a CMM perspective, Li et al. [129] confirmed that SLC22A3 polymorphisms (rs555754 and rs3123636) significantly influence susceptibility to T2DM in the Han Chinese population. Collectively, this evidence provides a preliminary basis for the significance of SLC22A3 in CMM comorbidity mechanisms, though larger-sample systematic studies are needed.

Insights from transcriptome-wide association studies

Using an intersection approach combining TWAS (FUSION) and FOCUS fine-mapping, we identified genes such as CDKN2A, CDKN2B, PSRC1, FES, and SLC22A3 as significantly associated. We found that TWAS Z-scores for PSRC1 and FES were greater than 0, indicating that predicted higher expression of these genes is positively associated with ‘F’ factor, suggesting their upregulation might increase the risk associated with ‘F’ factor. Conversely, TWAS Z-scores for CDKN2A, CDKN2B, and SLC22A3 were less than 0, indicating that their predicted downregulation might increase ‘F’ factor risk. Notably, consistent with our results, PSRC1 plays an important role in cardiometabolism, particularly showing therapeutic potential for CAD. A multi-omics study by Li et al. [146] found that lipid content in HDL particles has a protective effect against atherosclerosis and confirmed the therapeutic potential of PSRC1. Another study, starting from gut microbiota, found that PSRC1 knockout mice had significantly increased levels of TMAO and plaque accumulation [147], highlighting the positive significance of PSRC1 in regulating gut flora and treating atherosclerosis. Interestingly, consistent with our previous findings, PSRC1 has also been shown to significantly reduce inflammatory responses and slow atherosclerosis progression in apoE mice when overexpressed, which not only supports our earlier findings related to APOE but also suggests their co-regulation in CMM progression. CDKN2B has been extensively studied in cancer therapy [148, 149]. In the CMM-related field, a meta-analysis of 17 studies involving 40,979 samples indicated a significant association between the CDKN2B-AS1 gene rs4977574 A/G polymorphism and coronary heart disease [150].

Insights from gene-based analysis, functional annotation, and cell-type enrichment

Gene-based analysis using MAGMA further validated the results from previous steps, with genes like SLC22A3, APOE, and FES identified as significant, further demonstrating the robustness of the research findings. Simultaneously, multi-dimensional enrichment analysis highlighted the potential roles of pathways such as Cellular senescence, Choline metabolism in cancer, AGE-RAGE signaling pathway in diabetic complications, Amoebiasis, and Cholesterol metabolism for ‘F’ factor. Overall, these pathways clarify the importance of cellular senescence and lipid metabolism for CMM and physical frailty. According to existing knowledge, the lipid content of senescent cells follows a dynamic pattern [151], and dyslipidemia significantly regulates cellular senescence. In cardiovascular disease, cellular senescence is a key driver of atherosclerosis fueled by chronic inflammation [152]. Integrating our findings, this lipid metabolism-driven cellular senescence may play an important role in the complex comorbid state characterized by CMM and physical frailty. This mechanism also appears in other inflammation-related metabolic traits like T2D, osteoarthritis (OA), osteoporosis (OP), and cancer [153–155].

Regarding core biological processes, serotonin uptake, vascular process in circulatory system, dopamine uptake, negative regulation of platelet activation, and serotonin transport were highlighted. Specific cellular components like trans-Golgi network transport vesicle, clathrin-coated vesicle, Golgi-associated vesicle, clathrin-coated vesicle membrane, and plasma lipoprotein particle, as well as molecular functions like neurotransmitter transmembrane transporter activity, organic cation transmembrane transporter activity, sodium:chloride symporter activity, quaternary ammonium group transmembrane transporter activity, and phosphate ion binding, were emphasized for ‘F’ factor. Overall, based on the functional annotation of the core genes of ‘F’ factor, we further found significant enrichment in an interconnected network related to neuro-vascular regulation and cell communication. At the biological process level, functions like serotonin and dopamine uptake/transport, vascular processes in the circulatory system, and negative regulation of platelet activation were highlighted, suggesting ‘F’ factor may affect neuro-vascular unit coupling by modulating monoamine neurotransmitter homeostasis and vascular function [156, 157]. Particularly noteworthy, serotonin, a key molecule regulating mood, vascular tone, and platelet aggregation [158–160], could be a hub connecting the nervous, cardiovascular, and metabolic systems. This finding provides key functional genomic evidence for explaining how ‘F’ factor might contribute to systemic musculoskeletal-heart comorbidity by interfering with cell communication and material exchange at the neuro-vascular interface.

Furthermore, results at the cellular level suggested the involvement of Pancreas pancreatic stellate cell, Heart endothelial cell, Pancreas endothelial cell, Lung lung endothelial cell, Kidney endothelial cell, Fat endothelial cell, Heart myofibroblast cell, and Brain Non-Myeloid brain pericyte in ‘F’ factor. We found that its genetic signals are not diffusely distributed but are highly enriched in a "pan-organ vascular-stromal unit" centered on multi-organ endothelial cells and collaborative stromal cells. Specifically, endothelial cells from the heart, pancreas, lung, kidney, and adipose tissue, along with cardiac myofibroblasts, pancreatic stellate cells, and brain non-myeloid pericytes, collectively constitute the key cellular carriers of this factor. The inclusion of brain pericytes is particularly important, suggesting a potential link between the peripheral vascular-stromal axis and the central neurovascular unit. This association provides a hypothetical cellular framework that could be explored to understand the frequent co-occurrence of systemic dysregulation of the “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk” and cognitive disorders. In summary, this study is provides evidence, at cellular resolution, that ‘F’ factor may contribute to trans-system comorbidity during metabolic aging by targeting a common cellular network that maintains homeostasis at multi-organ interfaces.

Genomic elements and risk chromatin identification

Our multi-tissue epigenomics analysis further revealed that the genetic signals of ‘F’ factor showed specific enrichment in active promoter regions (H3K4me3) of the fetal adrenal gland and active enhancer regions (H3K27ac) of lung tissue. This provides critical spatiotemporal and tissue-specific clues. We interpret these enrichments as generating specific, testable hypotheses regarding the mechanisms underlying the “musculoskeletal-heart” metabolic aging axis. The significant enrichment of H3K4me3 in the fetal adrenal gland suggests that the genetic risk embodied by this common factor may partly act by influencing the promoter activity of key genes in adrenal cortical or medullary cells during critical developmental periods (e.g., fetal stage). The adrenal gland, as the terminal effector of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis [161], It is plausible that​ its early programming abnormalities could lead to lifelong alterations in the stress response system, glucocorticoid secretion, and metabolic homeostasis [162, 163], potentially​ laying a​ foundation for the risk of cardiometabolic disease and musculoskeletal aging comorbidity in adulthood. Taken together, these active chromatin marks enriched in specific developmental stages (fetal period) and specific metabolic/endocrine organs (adrenal gland, lung) supports that the shared genetic susceptibility to metabolic aging-related multimorbidity may be rooted in​ developmental processes and adult tissue-specific regulatory networks that govern inter-organ communication. Building on the aforementioned hypothesis, this perspective extends the “single-organ–single-disease” model by implicating epigenetic regulation of developmental and homeostatic networks in the shared etiology of cardiometabolic and musculoskeletal aging.

Challenges and future research directions

Although this study systematically revealed the genetic architecture of ‘F’ factor through integrated multi-omics analysis based on the nationally representative CHARLS prospective cohort, several challenges and limitations remain.

First and foremost, a direct analytical integration between the clinical findings from the CHARLS cohort and the genetic ‘F’ factor was not feasible due to the lack of genotyping data in CHARLS. This precluded a statistical validation (e.g., using a polygenic risk score for the ‘F’ factor) within the same cohort. Moreover, the identified clinical associations are observational and do not establish causation. Consequently, the clinical epidemiological associations (frailty with CMM) and the shared genetic architecture (‘F’ factor) are presented as independent, convergent lines of evidence supporting the “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk” hypothesis. Future studies in cohorts with both deep phenotyping and genomic data are needed to directly bridge this gap. Furthermore, it is important to contextualize the interpretation of the ‘F’ factor itself. This construct is a statistically inferred latent variable representing shared genetic covariance, not a directly measurable biological entity. Thus, its biological meaning remains hypothesis-generating. While downstream multi-omics analyses suggest plausible mechanisms, they prioritize candidates for future study rather than establish causality.

Secondly, limited by the variable scope of the CHARLS database, this study did not include certain key clinical indicators potentially closely related to ‘F’ factor (e.g., dynamic parameters of specific organ function, detailed environmental exposure records, or socio-economic influencing factors), which may affect the comprehensive interpretation of comorbidity mechanisms. Subsequent work could integrate multi-source data (e.g., electronic health records, biobanks) to incorporate more dimensions of biomarkers and social determinants of health, enhancing the biological interpretability and clinical applicability of the model. As a retrospective study, the lack of long-term dynamic follow-up data limits our analysis of the temporal role of ‘F’ factor in disease progression. Furthermore, several limitations of our predictive modeling approach should be noted. First, while the model demonstrates moderate discriminative ability and interpretability, its performance was not formally compared against simpler clinical prediction rules using robust metrics. Second, and more importantly, the model has been validated only internally within the CHARLS cohort; its generalizability requires further testing in independent, external populations. Although we applied the standard midpoint imputation method within the Cox proportional hazards framework, the discrete ascertainment of events remains a methodological consideration.

Thirdly, the GWAS summary data relied upon in this study primarily originate from European ancestry populations, which limits the generalizability and external validity of our findings. Therefore, future cross-ancestry comparative studies are essential to validate the conservation and specificity of these shared genetic signals across populations with different genetic backgrounds. Additionally, although the LDSC framework provides some adjustment, potential sample overlap among the input GWAS (e.g., from shared UK Biobank participants) remains a methodological consideration. Regarding the Genomic-SEM model itself, our study employed a parsimonious single-factor model, which provided clear initial evidence for a shared genetic component. While model fit indices were acceptable and sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of a core ‘cardiometabolic-frailty’ factor, the choice of a single-factor structure represents a simplified representation of what is likely a more complex genetic architecture. Future studies would benefit from employing multi-factor models and incorporating additional musculoskeletal (e.g., grip strength) and metabolic traits to dissect potential genetic sub-structures within the “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk”.

Finally, the analysis primarily focused on genetic factors and did not sufficiently examine how environmental factors (e.g., early life nutrition, physical activity, chronic stress) interact with genetic risk to shape the dysregulation trajectory of the "Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk." Utilizing large prospective cohorts that integrate multi-omics and longitudinal environmental exposure data to reveal gene-environment interaction mechanisms holds promise for identifying new targets for the early warning and precise intervention of comorbidities.

Conclusion

This study, by integrating clinical epidemiological and genetic evidence, systematically revealed the intrinsic connection between cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) and musculoskeletal aging within the process of metabolic aging, and proposed the “Musculoskeletal-Heart crosstalk” as a core framework for understanding their comorbid mechanisms. Based on the CHARLS cohort, the Frailty-Integrated Index we constructed significantly improved CMM risk prediction capability, and we developed an assessment tool with clinical application potential through machine learning models. At the genetic level, using genomic structural equation modeling, we constructed ‘F’ factor, discovering a significant shared genetic basis among CMM, frailty, bone density, and other traits, with effects concentrated in pathways involving genes like APOE and SLC22A3, such as cellular senescence, lipid metabolism, and neurovascular regulation.

This study confirms from both clinical phenotypic and genetic architectural perspectives that "frailty" is a key hub driving the evolution of multi-system multimorbidity. These findings provide new biomarkers and genetic evidence for the early prevention and control of metabolic multimorbidity, and also offer a theoretical basis for shifting the strategy for managing geriatric multimorbidity from a "disease-centered" to a "systemic-health-centered" approach.
