Widely Distributed Pigeon Paramyxovirus Sub‐Genotypes Pose a Risk to Immunocompromised Humans
Authors: Celia Abolnik, Michaela Hayes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionPigeon paramyxovirus type 1 (PPMV‐1) is an antigenic variant of Avian Orthoavulavirus 1 (AOAV‐1) (Newcastle disease virus) with a global distribution that causes lethal infections in pigeon and dove species. AOAV‐1's infecting humans normally cause mild, self‐limiting conjunctivitis, but since 2003, PPMV‐1 has been associated with an increased number of severe and lethal respiratory and neurological infections in immunocompromised persons in the Netherlands, the USA, France, China and Australia.MethodsPPMV‐1's isolated from free‐living pigeons and doves across South Africa from 2012 to 2024 were sequenced using conventional or next generation technologies. Maximum likelihood and time‐scaled phylogenetic analyses were conducted. Cases of human infections with AOAV‐1 were reviewed, and where the genotypes were not previously assigned, the sequence data were re‐analysed for classification purposes.ResultsPPMV‐1 sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.2.1, present in South Africa since at least 2005, continued to circulate in 2021. Sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.2.2, that is widely distributed across Europe, Asia and Australia, was identified in South Africa for the first time, with introduction estimated around September 2017 (95% HPD January 2016–June 2019). Previously unclassified viruses causing lethal human infections in the Netherlands (2003), the USA (2007) and France (2021) were identified as sub‐genotypes VI.2.1.1.2.2, VI.2.1.1.1 and XXI.1.1, respectively.ConclusionsFive recorded AOAV‐1‐associated human fatalities since 1953 were all caused by PPMV‐1. Three out of the five human fatalities, plus one seriously ill survivor, were associated with sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.2.2 viruses, and sub‐genotypes VI.2.1.1.1 and XXI.1.1 each caused one human death. PPMV‐1's, found widely in pigeons and doves, pose a serious health risk to immunocompromised persons.

Introduction

Newcastle disease (ND), a neurological, respiratory and reproductive illness of poultry and other bird species, is caused by the Avian Orthoavulavirus 1 (AOAV‐1), formerly designated as Avian Paramyxovirus Type 1 (APMV‐1), more commonly referred to as Newcastle disease virus (NDV) (Alexander 2000; ICTV 2021). AOAV‐1 is an enveloped, non‐segmented negative‐sense single‐stranded RNA virus with a genome length of ~15.19 kb (Amarasinghe et al. 2019), encoding six structural proteins, namely nucleoprotein, phosphoprotein, matrix (M) protein, fusion (F) protein, hemagglutinin‐neuraminidase and large (L) protein and two non‐structural proteins, V and W (Alexander 2000). AOAV‐1s are classified into three distinct pathotypes based on the clinical disease produced in chickens, and the amino acid motif at the F protein cleavage site, with the latter determining virulence. Lentogenic strains produce mild infection with low or no mortality; mesogenic strains produce an intermediate virulence with respiratory symptoms and may affect egg quality and production; and velogenic strains cause severe nervous and respiratory signs with up to 90% mortality in infected naïve flocks (Kaleta and Baldauf 1988). Humans have become infected with AOAV‐1s through direct contact with high virus titres (e.g., laboratory workers) or by handling infected live birds or carcasses. Mild, self‐limiting conjunctivitis is the most common symptom of AOAV‐1s infection, with influenza‐like symptoms of fever, chills, headache and malaise being rare. There have been no reports of human‐to‐human spread (Capua and Alexander 2004). Since 2003, however, there has been an increasing number of reports of AOAV‐1s causing illness and deaths in immunocompromised persons (Goebel et al. 2007; Kuiken et al. 2018; Winter et al. 2021; Zou et al. 2022; Hurley et al. 2023; Cui et al. 2023).

AOAV‐1s form a single serotype, but substantial genetic diversity has emerged over decades of circulation in susceptible bird populations. Classification systems based on the F gene divide AOAV‐1 into two major genetic groups, Class I and Class II. Class I contains a single genotype with three sub‐genotypes, and Class II contains 20 genotypes, each with multiple sub‐genotypes (Dimitrov et al. 2019). “Pigeon paramyxovirus type 1” (PPMV‐1) is the colloquial term for an antigenic variant of AOAV‐1 with a unique monoclonal antibody binding profile, which is mainly associated with infections of Columbiformes, i.e., species of wild and domesticated pigeons and doves (Aldous et al. 2004). Prior to 1971, no case of natural infection by AOAV‐1 had been observed in pigeons (Vindevogel and Duuchatel 1988), but in 1978 PPMV‐1 emerged in the Middle East, and by the 1980s it had spread via exported pigeons and doves to multiple countries, in a panzootic that continues to present times (Aldous et al. 2004; Annaheim et al. 2022). In non‐immunised pigeons and doves PPMV‐1 is highly virulent, causing clinical signs such as lethargy, loss of appetite, difficulty breathing, greenish watery diarrhoea, vomiting and neurological signs, such as paralysis and torticollis, and death within 72 h (Śmietanka et al. 2014). In cases where PPMV‐1 strains have infected chickens, either through ingesting faeces or in experimental infection studies, they typically only cause mild clinical symptoms (Alexander et al. 1984; Abolnik et al. 2004; Ferreira et al. 2019; Tian et al. 2020; Shan et al. 2021; Annaheim et al. 2022). Older classification systems grouped all PPMV‐1 strains into a single Class II genotype, namely genotype VI, with sub‐genotypes designated as VIa to VIl (Aldous et al. 2004; Diel et al. 2012). An updated classification system increased the cut‐off value of the nucleotide distance from 3% to 5%, consequently splitting PPMV‐1 between genotype VI and a new genotype, XXI, with a common ancestor. Genotype VI currently contains 7 sub‐genotypes, namely VI.2.1.1.1 (formerly VIa and VIn), VI.2.1.1 (formerly VIb), VI.1.2.2.2 (formerly VIh), VI.2.1.1.2.1 (formerly VIj) and VI.2.1.1.2.2 (formerly VIk) (Dimitrov et al. 2019). Genotype XXI contains four sub‐genotypes, namely XII (formerly VIl), XXI.1.1 (formerly VIg), XXI.1.2 (formerly VIm) and XXI.2 (formerly VIi) (Dimitrov et al. 2019). PPMV‐1 continues to persist because a wild reservoir exists, with incidence in dove and pigeon species reported from all continents except Antarctica (Dimitrov et al. 2019). An earlier study in South Africa identified genotype VI viruses belonging to two sub‐genotypes circulating in pigeons and doves in the period 2002 to 2006, with imported racing and ornamental pigeons from Europe or Asia as the likely source (Abolnik et al. 2008). Since then, die‐offs of free‐living doves and pigeons were observed occasionally in South Africa, normally at the beginning of winter. Neurological signs such as lameness and torticollis were typical in the birds before death. In this study AOAV‐1s affecting pigeons in the period 2012–2024 were sequenced and phylogenetically analysed to determine their epidemiological origins.

Materials and Methods

Sampling and Virus Isolation

Fresh carcasses of dead pigeons and doves were submitted by members of the public to the Western Cape Provincial Veterinary Laboratory (WCPVL) in Elsenburg, or Assurecloud (Pty) Veterinary Laboratory in Midrand, or were collected by the study investigators on the University of Pretoria's (UP) Veterinary Faculty's campus at Onderstepoort. An isolate dating back to 2012 from a dead pigeon also collected on the Onderstepoort campus was also included. Virus isolations in 9‐to‐11 day‐old embryonated chicken eggs were performed according to the standard procedure (WOAH 2024) at WCPVL or UP. RNA or total nucleic acids were extracted from allantoic fluid and the pooled organs of individual birds using TRIzol LS reagent (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA), or in a robotic IndiMag extraction system with IndiMag Pathogen Kits (Indical Bioscience, Germany), according to the manufacturers' recommended procedures.

RT‐PCR and Sanger DNA Sequencing

The presence of AOAV‐1 was confirmed in nucleic acid extracts using a real‐time quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT‐PCR) assay that targets the L gene (Fuller et al. 2010), with VetMAX Plus One‐Step qRT‐PCR kits (Applied Biosystems, USA) in an Applied Biosystems Verti 96 well thermal cycler (ThermoFisher, Scientific, USA). A partial region of the genome that spans the F‐M intergenic region, including the F0 cleavage site, was amplified by conventional RT‐PCR (Wang et al. 2001). Following electrophoretic separation in 1.5% agarose gel, the 280 bp amplicons were excised, purified with a QIAquick Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany), and sent to Inqaba Biotech (Pty) Ltd., Pretoria South Africa, for Sanger DNA sequencing. Forward and reverse sequences were checked and assembled into consensus sequences in Chromas Lite v2.6.6 (Technelysium, Australia) and BioEdit v7.7.1 (Hall 1999) software.

Ion Torrent Sequencing and Genome Assembly

Transcriptome libraries were prepared from RNA or total nucleic acid extracts with a Complete Whole Transcriptome Amplification Kit (Sigma‐Aldrich, USA) according to the manufacturer's recommended procedure. The amplification products were purified with a High Pure PCR Product Purification Kit (Roche, Germany) and shipped to the Central Analytical Facility at Stellenbosch University for Ion Torrent NGS with an Ion GeneStudio S5 Prime System (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Sequence reads were imported into CLC Genomics Workbench V11.0.1 for genome assembly using a combination of assembly‐to‐reference and de novo assembly approaches, where the PPMV‐1 strain with accession number JX901110 was used as the scaffold for the former. The PPMV‐1 genome Illumina reads for strain 1902M010210 (Bioproject SRR107144525 SAMN13611976; Winter et al. 2021) were downloaded from GenBank, and the sequence reads for Human/2/Shandong/2021 (Bioproject PRJCA00635 #CRA004864; Zou et al. 2022) were retrieved from the National Genomics Data Centre (https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/) and imported into the CLC Genomics Workbench v24.0.2. The consensus F gene sequences were extracted by assembly‐to‐reference against AOAV‐1 strain with accession number ON986206.

Phylogenetic and tMRCA Analysis

The assembled consensus sequences were imported into BioEdit, and the closest relatives were retrieved using BLAST searches of GenBank. To classify the strains, “Supplemental Dataset S3; Newcastle disease virus class II pilot complete fusion gene dataset for rapid genotype identification of isolates” (Dimitrov et al. 2019) was downloaded an imported into BioEdit. Multiple sequence alignments were prepared in MAFFT (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/server/) and BioEdit. Phylogenetic trees were reconstructed in IQ Tree (http://iqtree.cibiv.univie.ac.at/) using the maximum likelihood method with 1000 bootstrap replicates.

To reconstruct a molecular clock for sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.2.2, full F gene sequences were retrieved by BLAST homology searches and aligned as before. Non‐columbid hosts were excluded from the dataset, and only strains with information for the exact sampling date were retained (n = 20). The time to the most recent common ancestor (tMRCA) of the F gene was inferred from the dated maximum clade credibility (MCC) tree in BEAST v.2 software (Bouckaert et al. 2019). The MCC tree was reconstructed using a Hasegawa–Kishino–Yano (HKY) nucleotide substitution model with a gamma distribution of substitution rates, a Coalescent Bayesian Skyline model and a relaxed lognormal clock. Markov chain Monte Carlo chains of 40 million iterations were performed and assessed with Tracer v1.7.2 (Rambaut et al. 2018) to ensure that an effective sample size (ESS) of > 200 was achieved, with a statistical uncertainty of the nodes reflected in values of the 95% highest posterior density (HPD). The consensus MCC tree with common ancestor heights was summarised in TreeAnnotator v.2.6.6. All phylogenetic trees were visualised in FigTree (v1.4.4) (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/), and are presented as midpoint‐rooted dendograms with ordered nodes.

Results

Eleven PPMV‐1s from feral pigeons and doves, collected in South Africa between 2012 and 2024 (Table 1), were sequenced and phylogenetically classified according to the revised and updated nomenclature system for AOAV‐1. The results showed that sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.2.1, which was present in the country since at least 2005, continued to circulate in 2021 and that this virus shared a recent common ancestor with those detected in neighbouring Namibia since 2016 (Figure 1; Figure S1; Molini et al. 2018). No viruses belonging to sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.1, which were previously detected in South Africa from 2001 to 2006, were detected although the small sample size is a limitation of the study. Five PPMV‐1 strains, from 2020 to 2024, were identified as sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.2.2 (Figure 1; Figure S1). All viruses contained a multiple basic amino acid motif in the F protein cleavage site, which is typical of virulent AOAV‐1s (Table 1). Next, a phylogenetic tree was constructed from the South African PPMVs for which full F gene sequences were available, and their closest available relatives. Three Australian PPMV‐1 viruses, which were also classified as sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.2.2 (Shan et al. 2021), shared a recent common ancestor with the recent South African viruses (Figure 2). The Australian sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.2.2 viruses, which initially caused an outbreak in racing pigeons (Shan et al. 2021), persisted in that country and in 2022 were associated with the death of a 2‐year‐old child with a history of infantile pre‐B cell acute lymphoblastic leukaemia. The child had no known exposure to pigeons and was admitted to hospital with nausea and vomiting, preceded by 3 weeks of upper respiratory tract symptoms. Fever and seizures followed in the 4 days that preceded her death (Hurley et al. 2023). As there were no recent close relatives in GenBank, a time‐scaled MCC tree was constructed from the full F gene sequences of pigeon‐origin VI.2.1.1.2.2 viruses, to determine when this virus was introduced to South Africa, and the tMRCA was consequently dated to September 2017 (95% HPD January 2016–June 2019) (Figure S2).

Discussion

Historically, the South African poultry sector was affected by ND outbreaks caused by exotic virulent (velogenic) strains of AOAV‐1 genotypes VIII or VII (Abolnik 2017; Abolnik et al. 2018). Vaccination against ND using live lentogenic or mesogenic strains has been compulsory in South Africa for decades, but has failed to prevent sporadic outbreaks. It was only after improvements were made in the efficacy of immunisation processes in the hatchery, coinciding with the registration and use of live vectored vaccines since 2013, that sufficient immunity was attained in the national flock for ND to be brought under control (Shahn Bisschop, pers. comm). The last laboratory confirmed case of exotic virulent AOAV‐1 in chickens in South Africa was in 2015 (Abolnik et al. 2018; Marco Romito, pers. comm), and since then, any confirmed cases of virulent AOAV‐1 in South Africa have been limited to pigeons and doves (DALRRD 2025). In the cooler months, it is usual to find moribund or dead pigeons and doves, sometimes in large numbers, that succumbed to PPMV‐1.

In the last survey in South Africa (2001–2006) co‐circulating PPMV‐1 sub‐genotypes VI.2.1.1.2.1 and VI.2.1.1.1 were recorded in columbids (Abolnik et al. 2008), and in this study we determined that a new sub‐genotype, VI.2.1.1.2.2, entered the country around September 2017 and spread within populations of free‐living pigeons and doves of the Western Cape, Gauteng and Mpumalanga provinces. The continued circulation of PPMV‐1s in the region is of great concern to pigeon fanciers and breeders. South Africa is one of the top 10 pigeon racing countries globally, with one of the world's best genetic pools of racing and breeding pigeons that are highly sought after, and international fanciers often send their birds to participate in lucrative South African races (Mostert and Donaldson 2024). PPMV‐1 is also a concern to conservationists, as vulnerable African Ground Hornbills ( Bucorvus leadbeateri ) have succumbed to the infection after scavenging dead doves (Abolnik et al. 2008). Legally imported birds are subject to strict quarantines with RT‐PCR testing to ensure that they are not carriers of live viruses, but illegal trafficking remains a risk for introduction.

ND traditionally had zoonotic importance insofar as it normally causes mild, self‐limiting conjunctivitis in people who have had direct contact with high quantities of live virus through occupational exposure, and only rarely have more severe flu‐like symptoms been recorded (Capua and Alexander 2004). Many studies cite the 2003 case in the Netherlands as the first human fatality associated with AOAV‐1 (Kuiken et al. 2018; Ul‐Rahman et al. 2022; Cui et al. 2023; Hurley et al. 2023), but the first recorded fatality associated with AOAV‐1 was actually in 1953 in the USA, an immune‐compromised 63‐year‐old man with a history of monocytic leukaemia (Moolten et al. 1953). Although the causative genotype is unknown, 10 historical virus isolates from the USA from 1944 to 1947 were all classified as genotype II (Dimitrov et al. 2016). Is AOAV‐1 evolving into a more serious zoonotic threat? In 60 years (1942–2002) dozens of human infections with AOAV‐1 were documented (Table 1), but the aforementioned case in 1953 was the only recorded death. However, from 2003 to 2023 (a 20‐year period), five human fatalities were recorded, all of them attributed to the pigeon‐associated genotypes VI and XXI. Sub‐genotype VI.2.1.1.2.2 specifically is associated with four severe human cases to date, three of which were fatal, and this sub‐genotype that became widespread in many countries across Europe, in Asia and also in Australia (Xie et al. 2020; Shan et al. 2021), has now been confirmed in South Africa.

All AOAV‐1‐associated deaths in humans thus far have occurred in immunocompromised individuals, and this is a particular concern for South Africa, because this country has more people living with human immune‐deficiency virus (HIV) than any other. According to 2023 statistics, approximately 7.8 million people in South Africa are living with HIV, representing about 12.6% of the population. Around 5.9 million people are on antiretroviral therapy, translating to a treatment coverage of 75%, but this indicates that one in four people living with HIV are not receiving treatment. South Africa is also recognised as having one of the highest burdens of TB worldwide, another cause of immunosuppression (HSRC 2023). As global urbanisation increases (Kuiken et al. 2018), including in South Africa where masses are migrating from rural regions to cities in search of employment (Mthiyane et al. 2022), more people will be drawn into close contact with feral pigeons and doves that colonise urban areas, increasing their risk for exposure to PPMV‐1 through infected droppings and windborne dust (Alexander 1988).

More public health awareness is required concerning the zoonotic potential of AOAV‐1, specifically PPMV‐1. We reiterate that AOAV‐1 should be included in the differential diagnosis of cases of human respiratory disease that test negative for more common respiratory pathogens (Goebel et al. 2007; Kuiken et al. 2018), and there is clear value in the use of metagenomic sequencing that enabled the identification of PPMV‐1 in several of the recent human cases (Hurley et al. 2023; Svraka et al. 2010; Zou et al. 2022; Prajna et al. 2022; Winter et al. 2021). Finally, research is required into why and how, of the many AOAV‐1 genotypes, PPMV‐1's specifically have gained virulence for immunocompromised humans.
