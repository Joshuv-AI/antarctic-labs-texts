A Predictive Model for Short-Term Mortality After Status Epilepticus
Authors: Simona Lattanzi, Eugen Trinka, Pilar Bosque-Varela, Giada Giovannini, Giorgi Kuchukhidze, Niccolò Orlandi, Chiara Silvestri, Tiago Lerda-Casaccia, Hari Prasad, Francesco Brigo, Stefano Meletti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesReliable prediction of short-term mortality in status epilepticus (SE) can contribute to guide clinical decisions. Current prognostic systems achieve only acceptable predictive power and show lack of generalizability or poor calibration. We aimed to identify clinical predictors of short-term mortality in patients with nonhypoxic SE and develop a predictive score.MethodsThis was a multicenter, multinational cohort study based on registry data. Participants were consecutive episodes of SE in participants aged 14 years or older from Modena (Italy) (derivation cohort) and in participants aged 18 years or older from Salzburg (Austria) (validation cohort). The predefined outcome was 30-day mortality after the onset of SE. Age, sex, level of consciousness before treatment, semiology of SE, level of disability at baseline before SE, etiology, and treatment refractoriness were assessed. Adjusted regression coefficients of each independent predictor were transformed to produce a points-based risk scoring system.ResultsThe Italian cohort included 689 episodes, and the Austrian cohort comprised 569 episodes of SE. In the derivation cohort, the 30-day mortality rate was 27.3%. The independent risk factors were aged 75 years or older (odds ratio [OR] 5.52, 95% CI 3.45–8.83; p < 0.001), consciousness impairment (stuporous or comatose) before SE treatment (OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.09–2.82; p = 0.020), acute etiology due to primary CNS pathology (OR 2.60, 95% CI 1.60–4.23; p < 0.001), refractoriness to treatment (OR 6.40, 95% CI 3.91–10.46; p < 0.001), and disability before SE onset (OR 3.53, 95% CI 2.22–5.61; p < 0.001); remote etiology was independently associated with a lower likelihood of 30-day mortality (OR 0.28, 95% 0.13–0.61; p = 0.001). An integer-based scoring system termed Age, Consciousness, Aetiology, Refractoriness, Disability (ACARD) was developed by combining these independent predictors. In the validation cohort, the 30-day mortality rate was 11.6%. The area under the curve of the ACARD score was 0.864 (95% CI 0.836–0.891) in the derivation cohort and 0.845 (95% CI 0.801–0.888) in the validation cohort. Calibration plot indicated good fit of predicted and observed data in both cohorts.DiscussionThe ACARD score is a user-friendly tool developed to predict 30-day mortality after nonhypoxic SE. It has the potential to identify participants at high risk of short-term mortality and outperform the performance of other available scoring systems.

Introduction

Status epilepticus (SE) is a frequent neurologic emergency and is associated with high mortality and morbidity.1-3 Reliable outcome prediction may contribute to tailoring individualized medical strategy of patients with SE and improving the counselling of families.4,5

Different scores have been developed to predict in-hospital death in adults with SE. The most widely employed include the Status Epilepticus Severity Score (STESS),6-8 the modified STESS version (mSTESS),9 and the Epidemiology-Based Mortality Score in Status Epilepticus (EMSE).10 Recently, the Seizures, Age, Coma, EEG Evolution (SACE) score has been specifically designed for patients with non-convulsive SE (NCSE).11 So far, there are no scores which would predict the risk of mortality at 30 days from SE onset. This endpoint may represent a more reliable and objective prognostic measure, unrelated to the length of in-hospital stay, which can differ from case to case and be influenced differently across the sites.

Etiology represents one of the most robust risk predictors of mortality after SE, and acute symptomatic etiology has been associated with poor prognosis.12 However, the category of acute symptomatic etiology includes a heterogeneous spectrum of causes, and distinct subgroups have been recently proposed and to differences in the risk of mortality.13

This study aimed (1) to develop a user-friendly risk score for predicting 30-day mortality in adults with SE, incorporating the modified subclassification of acute symptomatic etiologies; (2) to externally validate the newly developed score in an independent cohort; (3) to compare the accuracy of the new scoring system with the accuracy of the STESS, mSTESS, EMSE, and SACE score.

Methods

Study Design, Populations, and Definitions

This was a multicenter, multinational cohort study using data from registries of patients who were diagnosed with SE. We adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines.14

The exploratory cohort consisted of consecutive episodes of SE occurring in participants aged 14 years or older admitted at the Ospedale Civile Baggiovara (Modena, Italy) from September 2015 to December 2023. The validation cohort comprised consecutive episodes of SE diagnosed in participants aged 18 years or older at the Department of Neurology of the Christian Doppler University Hospital, Paracelsus Medical University (Salzburg, Austria) from February 2019 to December 2023.

The definition and classification of semiology and etiology of SE proposed by the International League Against Epilepsy was used.15 Semiology was classified into SE with prominent motor symptoms (including convulsive, myoclonic, focal motor, tonic, and hyperkinetic SE) and without prominent symptoms, that is, NCSE (including NCSE with coma and NCSE without coma).15 The diagnosis of NCSE was performed according to the Salzburg EEG criteria.16,17 Etiology was classified into acute, remote, progressive, defined electroclinical syndrome, and “unknown” (i.e., cryptogenic).15 For the acute etiologies, we adopted the recently proposed 4 subcategories: (1) withdrawal, low levels, or inappropriate prescription of antiseizure medications (ASMs) or sleep deprivation in patients with pre-existing epilepsy (“acute-triggering factors in epilepsy”); (2) acute primary CNS pathology, including cerebrovascular diseases, active CNS infections, or head trauma (“acute-primary CNS”); (3) secondary CNS pathology, including metabolic disturbances (e.g., electrolyte imbalances, glucose imbalance, organ failure, acidosis, renal failure, hepatic encephalopathy), systemic infection, or fever (“acute-secondary CNS”); and (4) drug or alcohol intoxication and withdrawal (“acute-toxic”).13 Participants with SE after hypoxic-anoxic encephalopathy were excluded.

Treatment responsiveness was defined as SE cessation after first‐line therapy with benzodiazepines alone or followed by second‐line treatment with 1 ASM. Refractoriness was defined as persistence of SE despite administration of first‐line therapy with benzodiazepines and 1 second‐line treatment with ASMs. Treatment followed the recommendations of international guidelines at both centres.18,19

The following clinical variables were considered for this study: age, sex, level of consciousness before treatment (i.e., alert or somnolent/confused, stuporous, or comatose),6 semiology of SE, etiology of SE, level of disability at baseline before SE as assessed by the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), treatment responsiveness/refractoriness, alive/death status at 30 days.

Prospective SE registries are maintained at the 2 centers, ensuring that clinical variables are prospectively assessed and recorded in dedicated SE forms by the neurologists or neurointensivists taking care of the individuals with SE. The study outcome was 30-day mortality. Outcome data were obtained from the electronic hospital information systems, which also integrate data from rehabilitation facilities and local death registries, outpatient visits, and telephone calls.

Statistical Analysis

Values were presented as median [interquartile range] and number (percent). Comparisons were made through the Mann-Whitney test or χ2 test. For model development in the derivation cohort, characteristics associated with 30-day mortality in the univariable analysis with a p ≤ 0.05 were entered into the multiple logistic regression model. A backward stepwise selection with variable entry set at p = 0.05 and removal at p = 0.10 was adopted to identify factors independently associated with the outcome.20 Any continuous variable included in the multiple logistic regression model was firstly converted to categorical variable based on the optimal cut-point estimated in the cohort through receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and Youden index. Adjusted regression coefficients of each independent predictor were transformed to produce a points-based risk scoring system: The weighted score of each factor was obtained by dividing each adjusted regression coefficient by the smallest one and rounding it to the nearest integer.20

The predictive performance of the prognostic tool was assessed in the derivation and validation cohorts with discrimination and calibration. Discrimination of the model was measured using the ROC curve. Calibration was assessed with the calibration plot and the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. To increase precision, prediction estimates of 30-day mortality with their 95 CIs were calculated in the combined derivation and validation cohorts.

The discriminative ability of STESS, mSTESS, EMSE, and SACE score in classifying patients for 30-day mortality in the derivation cohort was also assessed by the ROC curve. The equality of the area under the ROC curves was tested using the algorithm suggested by De Long et al.21 Sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated. Given controversial results in the literature, for the STESS, these calculations were performed for the cutoff points ≥3 and ≥4; the cutoff point was ≥5 for the mSTESS, ≥64 for the EMSE and ≥3 for the SACE score. Subgroup analyses by SE semiology (i.e., prominent motor symptoms vs nonconvulsive), younger vs older adult age at SE onset (i.e., younger than 55 years vs 55 years or older), and recurrence of SE (i.e., first-ever vs recurrent) were performed. Data analysis was performed using STATA/IC 13.1 (StataCorp LP, College Station, TX) and MedCalc Software Ltd. (version 23.3.7).

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Modena (556/2018/OSS/AOUMO–RF-2016-02361365) and the Ethics Committee of the Region of Salzburg (415-E/2422).

Data Availability

Completely anonymized data will be available to qualified academic investigators to replicate study results by reasonable request. Data transfer will be regulated by material transfer agreements.

Results

The derivation cohort included 689 episodes of nonhypoxic SE, which occurred in 611 participants. The median age at SE onset was 73 [60–81] years, and 292 (42.4%) episodes occurred in men. The episodes of SE with prominent motor symptoms were 338 (49.1%) and those of NCSE were 351 (50.9%). At 30 days, death was reported in 188 (27.3%) cases. In the multivariable regression analysis, independent risk factors for 30-day mortality were age 75 or older (odds ratio [OR] 5.52, 95% CI 3.45–8.83; p < 0.001), consciousness impairment (stuporous or comatose) before SE treatment (OR 1.76, 95% CI 1.09–2.82; p = 0.020), acute etiology due to primary CNS pathology (OR 2.60, 95% CI 1.60–4.23; p < 0.001), refractoriness to treatment (OR 6.40, 95% CI 3.91–10.46; p < 0.001), and disability (mRS ≥3) before SE onset (OR 3.53, 95% CI 2.22–5.61; p < 0.001); contrariwise, remote etiology was independently associated with a lower likelihood of 30-day mortality (OR 0.28, 95% 0.13–0.61; p = 0.001). By assigning point values to these items, we developed an integer-based scoring system termed Age, Consciousness, Aetiology, Refractoriness, Disability (ACARD) based on the first letters of the 5 included parameters (Table 2). The score can range from a minimum of −2 to a maximum of +11 points, with higher values predicting higher risk of 30-day mortality.

Discussion

We proposed the ACARD score as a user-friendly prognostic tool to predict the risk of 30-day mortality in adults presenting with SE. The model showed good discrimination and calibration and was successfully validated in an external cohort. Main strengths of this score include the use of routinely available predictors, the higher predictive accuracy compared to currently available prognostic tools, and the generalizability to participants with either SE with prominent motor symptoms or NCSE.

The ACARD score incorporates 5 variables, whose association with mortality confirm and strength available evidence.

Age is a well-established determinant of outcome in SE. Older age has been associated with a substantial increase in the risk of mortality.22,23 Categorized either into younger and older than 1 threshold or as strata by decades of life, age was included in prior scores.

Alterations in level of consciousness can be indicative of SE severity, and markedly impaired consciousness such as stupor or coma at SE onset was independently associated with mortality.22,24-26 To develop the ACARD score, we adopted the same categorization of the level of consciousness used by the STESS and distinguished patients into alert or somnolent/confused vs stuporous or comatose.6

Mortality after SE is largely determined by the underlying etiology. Population-based studies reported that 52%–72% of adults with SE had an acute etiology, and outcome in patients with this etiology was consistently reported as poor.22 Acute etiology is however an “umbrella” term encompassing a spectrum of distinct subcategories, which are associated with a different mortality risk. The ACARD score built on previous scoring systems by considering the recently proposed, granular classification of acute SE etiology and identified a higher risk for patients with SE due to acute primary CNS pathology. The lower rate of death in patients with SE due to remote symptomatic etiologies has been already shown, and the lower risk of treatment refractoriness may play a role in these cases.27,28

Although previous studies found significant impact of treatment refractoriness on SE outcome,12,22 treatment response has been largely left outside the current prognostic tools.6-11 The resistance to diazepam has been incorporated in the encephalitis, NCSE, diazepam resistance, imaging features, and tracheal intubation (END-IT) score.29 This score, however, has been specifically developed to predict 3-month unfavorable functional outcome rather than mortality and its generalizability is strongly limited by the characteristics of the derivation cohort.2 While not immediately available at the onset, information on treatment refractoriness can be obtained early during SE. Indeed, the current initial management of SE is based on a stepwise approach, starting with benzodiazepines, followed shortly by a second-line intravenous ASM if the first line proved ineffective. Although different degree of refractoriness up to super-refractoriness can be identified and linked to different prognosis,30 we preferred not to consider these subgroups because of the heterogeneity that may exist in the real-world care for administrating successive ASMs or escalating to general anesthesia when the first parenteral ASM fails to stop SE. Furthermore, the time needed before being able to ascertain these “more advanced” levels of refractoriness would hamper the opportunity of a prognostic tool readily available during the early stages of a SE episode.

Although not specific for patients with SE, the mRS is a global measure of functional status. A substantial literature documents how comorbidities may have a direct impact on the mRS score through the negative effects on physical abilities, cognitive functioning, and overall health status.31,32 Previously existing medical problems may influence the prognosis of SE, and comorbidities represent one of the domains of the EMSE score.10 When the baseline mRS value was incorporated into the STESS, this resulted in a net improvement of its predictive accuracy.9

Currently available scoring systems achieve only acceptable predictive power and show lack of generalizability or signs of poor calibration.5 They are characterized by low PPVs, and no proposed cutoff values displayed a balanced trade-off between specificity and sensitivity.5,33 The STESS was the first score developed to predict in-hospital mortality. It has a simple design and relies on variables that can be assessed at the time of presentation. The application at the bedside, however, is limited because uncertainty exists about which threshold to use to best differentiate survivors from non-survivors, with STESS-3 yielding high sensitivity and STESS-4 high specificity, but without a satisfactory balance of both.5,8 In addition, the STESS was reported to carry the risk of a ceiling effect especially in patients older than 65 years with no history of previous seizures.34 The EMSE is a more comprehensive score and has been shown to have a better predictive value than the STESS.31,35 Owing to its complexity, however, the practicability of the EMSE in clinical routine was questioned.33 Some etiologies, such as autoimmune encephalitis, systemic infection, or neurodegenerative diseases, do not fit into any of the categories in the EMSE.33 Some comorbidities such as endocarditis, paralytic ileus, hemolytic anemia, or graft-vs-host disease are not represented.10 Furthermore, EEG parameters may not be obtainable in some regions where and when EEG is not available, also considering that not only ictal but also nonictal yet potentially, harmful EEG patterns are included in the score.5,10 Of note, the age cut-off in the ACARD score was higher compared to other scales. In this regard, however, the age cut-off used in the STESS and mSTESS was not data-driven but defined a priori.6-9 In the EMSE score, age was categorized by decades instead of 1 single threshold, with increasing score values for any increase in age strata.10 The SACE score also adopted the 75 years cut-off, which was based on the age distribution and statistical data analysis of the derivation cohort.11

The ACARD score showed better discrimination and greater accuracy to predict 30-day mortality after SE than other available scores. The ACARD score yielded a higher PPV than the STESS, mSTESS, and EMSE implying a better ability to correctly identify those participants who have a poor outcome and mitigating one of the most challenging drawbacks of the current predictive systems. The high NPV, which was comparable with that of the EMSE, suggested a good performance of the ACARD score to identify the participants who eventually survive. It is, however, worth to notice that any newly developed score usually outperforms the other competitors in the original derivation study.

One strength of the ACARD score is its generalizability irrespective of SE semiology, as reflected by its similar discriminative ability in both prominent motor SE and NCSE either in the derivation or validation cohort, as well as by its superior performance compared with the STESS, mSTESS, EMSE, and SACE score across both semiology groups. Predicting outcome might be particularly challenging for NCSE. Prognostic scoring systems have been shown to underperform in cohorts of NCSE patients, raising the question whether convulsive SE and NCSE should be evaluated using the same tools.4,11 In the original study describing the STESS, only approximately 12% of patients presented with NCSE.7 Furthermore, a ceiling effect has been shown to be more likely in the case of NCSE.4,11 Similarly, the limited mortality data from NCSE used to develop the EMSE may explain why it performed considerably worse in patients with nonconvulsive than convulsive SE in the original study.10 The SACE score is the only prognostic system exclusively tailored for patients with NCSE. It integrates patient age, history of seizure, and level of consciousness with the Salzburg criterion A2 (ictal morphological, spatial, and temporal evolution) for NCSE diagnosis.11 In the original study, the SACE score yielded a sensitivity of 77%, a specificity of 74%, and an overall accuracy of 76%, performing better than the STESS, mSTESS, and EMSE.11 In this study, the SACE score had similar discrimination ability and overall predictive accuracy compared with the STESS, mSTESS, and EMSE, and it was outperformed by the ACARD score.

The ACARD score has different shortcomings. First, there still exists an imbalance in sensitivity and specificity, although less pronounced than in other scores. Second, the approximate 80% accuracy suggests that it can be useful at group level for controlling disease severity of a cohort, but it can only assist and support clinical reasoning in individual patients. The score still lacks the necessary accuracy to reliably guide treatment decisions in clinical practice and should be interpreted cautiously and only in conjunction with other clinical data. The PPV remains suboptimal, although higher than the values observed with other currently available scores. This indicates that when the score suggests 30-day mortality, a proportion of participants will survive, underscoring the need for caution when considering treatment limitations in individuals identified at high risk to avoid an inappropriate withdrawal of life sustaining care. Third, with up to 13-points and corresponding risk estimates for each value, the ACARD score may seem more complex than other available scores and more difficult to use in routine clinical care; in this regard, however, any component of the ACARD score is easily assessed and routinely and readily available early after the onset of SE. Furthermore, the score can be applied at any time point during a patient's clinical course because its included variables are not time-dependent once refractoriness to treatment has been established. Fourth, biochemical biomarkers, EEG patterns, neuroimaging findings, and the occurrence of complications have not been included. Although additional data might improve outcome prediction, incorporating such variables would require a more extensive diagnostic workup and would make the score less accessible and more difficult to use routinely at SE presentation. In the future, these parameters could be integrated, particularly for the development of a dynamic score that can be applied over time during SE management in an individual patient and help guiding treatment decisions. Fifth, at times, there were deviations from the perfect slope in calibration plots, although they were limited and within the estimated 95% CIs. Finally, the low number of events for some of the values of the score increased the width of the CIs of the prediction estimates; additional studies based on larger sample size would be needed to improve precision.

The development and validation of the ACARD score adhered to established recommendations.36 The assembled population consisted of large, hospital-based cohorts including a broad spectrum of patients treated inside and outside the intensive care unit, supporting the use of this tool in diverse clinical settings. Of note, the predictiveness of the ACARD score remained consistent across the 2 study cohorts. The discrepancy in the baseline characteristics of included patients could explain the lower mortality rate observed in the validation cohort, which included younger patients, a higher prevalence of remote etiologies and a lower prevalence of acute CNS pathologies as the underlying causes of SE episodes compared with the derivation cohort. Nonetheless, the consistency of the ACARD score's performance across populations treated at 2 different institutions in different countries, despite differences in the baseline characteristics and mortality rates, provides additional support for the robustness and generalizability of this score. Some study shortcomings need, however, to be acknowledged. The data collection performed in a real-world setting may have introduced potential sources of bias. In this regard, however, all information related to either the derivation or validation cohorts were collected prospectively through a consistent form over years. In addition, external validation in other geographic regions with differing predominant etiologies, as well as in pediatric populations, is warranted to confirm the generalizability of the study findings. Although no comparisons were provided with other scores like the END-IT score,29 ACD score,37 or Simplified Acute Physiology Score II,38 none have been developed to predict the risk of short-term mortality.

The heterogeneity of SE renders the prognostication of outcome challenging. The ACARD score is a user-friendly tool developed to predict 30-day mortality after SE, and it can outperform the performance of other available scoring systems. Future research may apply the score to specific patient subgroups to determine whether it performs optimally in certain setting and to identify contexts in which its predictive value may be limited.
