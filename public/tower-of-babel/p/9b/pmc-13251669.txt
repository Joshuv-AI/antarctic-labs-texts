Prevalence and antibiotic resistance pattern of Staphylococcus spp. isolated from acne, and disrupting their antimicrobial resistance: unveiling the potential of Ganoderma lucidum extract
Authors: Md. Mainuddin Hossain, Juthi Adhikari, Md. Sharif Khan, Sadia Binta Based, Amit Dutta, Fatama Tous Zohora, Abu Zaffar Shibly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) and Staphylococcus epidermidis (S. epidermidis) are major skin commensals involved in acne pathogenesis and are increasingly associated with antibiotic resistance. This study aimed to determine the prevalence and resistance patterns of isolated Staphylococcus spp., identify associated risk factors, and evaluate the antibacterial activity of Ganoderma lucidum (G. lucidum) extract as a potential natural therapeutic alternative. Acne lesion samples from 135 volunteers were analyzed, yielding 128 S. aureus and 135 S. epidermidis isolates confirmed by biochemical tests. Antibiotic susceptibility was assessed against commonly used drugs. The antibacterial efficacy of G. lucidum extract was tested on Mueller-Hinton agar. Bioinformatics analysis, including molecular docking and ADMET profiling, predicted interactions of G. lucidum bioactives with resistance-related proteins (ermC, mecA, and β-lactamase). Gentamicin and rifampin showed 100% susceptibility in both species. However, erythromycin resistance was 93.3% in S. aureus and 60% in S. epidermidis, while amoxicillin resistance was highest in S. epidermidis (86.7%). Significant risk factors included age, gender, BMI, oily skin, family history, diet, and psychological stress. G. lucidum extract exhibited strong dose-dependent antibacterial activity against both species. Docking and dynamic simulations revealed lucidenic acid D1 strongly and stably binding ermC, while ganoderlactone B and E showed dual binding to mecA and β-lactamase, suggesting inhibition of erythromycin and amoxicillin resistance. ADMET analysis confirmed favorable pharmacokinetics and low toxicity. The study highlights high prevalence of antibiotic-resistant staphylococci in acne and demonstrates the potential of G. lucidum as an effective natural antibacterial and resistance-modifying agent, offering a promising alternative to conventional antibiotics.

Introduction

Acne, also known as Acne vulgaris, is a commonly occurring and persistent skin disease that primarily affects teens throughout puberty and can be characterized by the presence of inflamed or infected sebaceous glands in the skin.1, 2, 3 The frequency of acne varies between nations and age groups; estimates range from 35% to almost 100% of teenagers experiencing acne at some point.4, 5 It affects approximately 70–87% of the world's young adults aged 12–24.1, 6 Both children and adults can experience suffering prior to and following puberty.7 Previous studies showed that the most common bacteria associated with these diseases are Propionibacterium acne, S. aureus, and S. epidermidis.8, 9

S. aureus and S. epidermidis are among the major pathogens in hospital and community infections found as part of the normal skin flora.6, 7, 10 The presence of bacteria can result in various infectious diseases, ranging from minor skin and soft tissue infections like pimples, impetigo, boils, cellulitis, folliculitis, carbuncles, scalded skin syndrome, and open comedones11, 12, 13 to severe conditions such as pneumonia, meningitis, osteomyelitis, endocarditis, toxic shock syndrome, bacteremia, and sepsis.14, 15 The impact of acne can vary based on factors such as the patient's gender, age, BMI, family history, skin type, duration of the condition, and accessibility to therapies.16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 The ensuing manifestation may cause feelings of anxiety, low self-worth, and social exclusion accompanied by shame and sadness. Furthermore, there is a correlation between the exacerbation of acne and the increased prevalence of mental health challenges and suicide ideation.22, 23

A variety of antibiotics, such as tetracycline, erythromycin, gentamicin, rifampicin, clindamycin, amoxicillin, doxycycline, minocycline, cefazolin, and trimethoprim /sulfamethoxazole, are used to treat acne. Among these tropical antibiotics, erythromycin and clindamycin are the ones that are used the most frequently for treating inflammatory acne disorders.11, 24, 25, 26 In recent years, increasing antibiotic resistance among acne-associated bacteria has emerged as a significant clinical concern. Several recent studies have reported high resistance rates to commonly prescribed topical and systemic antibiotics, including erythromycin, clindamycin, and tetracycline, largely attributed to prolonged and indiscriminate antibiotic use in acne management.27, 28, 29 The persistence of resistant staphylococcal strain not only reduces therapeutic efficacy but also facilitates the dissemination of resistance determinants within the skin microbiome and the broader community.28 Consequently, current acne management guidelines emphasize antibiotic stewardship and recommend limiting long-term antibiotic therapy while encouraging the identification of alternative or adjunctive antimicrobial strategies.29 Antibiotic resistance leads to a shortage of efficacious antibiotics for the treatment of bacteria-induced illnesses.30 Antibiotic overuse and poor administration, as well as pharmaceutical companies' lack of medical innovation, have contributed to resistance. Additionally, several countries allow over-the-counter antibiotic purchases.30, 31 The combination of these prevailing circumstances has resulted in natural selection, leading to the proliferation and dissemination of antimicrobial-resistant pathogenic microorganisms.

Despite the urgent need, the development of new antibiotics with novel mechanisms of action has been slow, and many existing drugs are associated with toxicity or diminishing effectiveness. This alarming situation underscores the necessity for alternative and complementary therapeutic strategies to combat drug-resistant Staphylococcus infections.30 Natural products, particularly those derived from medicinal fungi and plants, have gained increasing attention as potential sources of novel antimicrobial agents due to their chemical diversity, multi-target activity, and reduced likelihood of resistance development.32 Therefore, exploring natural bioactive compounds and elucidating their potential antibacterial mechanisms through integrated in-vitro and in-silico approaches represents a rational and timely strategy to address the growing threat of Staphylococcus-associated antimicrobial resistance.

G. lucidum, a medicinal mushroom, has strong antimicrobial properties due to its bioactive compounds like triterpenoids, polysaccharides, and phenolics, showing broad-spectrum activity against bacteria and fungi.33 Notably, the triterpenoids and phenolic compounds present in G. lucidum extracts have demonstrated synergistic effects with conventional antibiotics, significantly reducing minimum inhibitory concentrations and restoring antibiotic susceptibility in resistant strains, including methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA).34, 35 Mechanistically, bioactive triterpenoids from G. lucidum have been associated with disruption of microbial membranes, enhanced leakage of intracellular contents, and generation of reactive oxygen species, contributing to their antibacterial effects.36, 37 These properties provide a scientific basis for investigating G. lucidum not only as an antibacterial extract but also as a potential modulator of resistance-related mechanisms in acne-associated Staphylococcus species. These findings highlight the potential of G. lucidum as a natural antimicrobial agent and an adjuvant to overcome multidrug resistance in clinical pathogens. While previous studies have reported the general antimicrobial properties of medicinal mushrooms, including G. lucidum, against various bacterial pathogens, very limited information is available regarding their role in modulating antibiotic resistance in acne-associated Staphylococcus spp.

The novelty of the present study lies in its integrated approach combining (i) epidemiological profiling of acne patients, (ii) detailed phenotypic antibiotic resistance analysis of clinical S. aureus and S. epidermidis isolates, (iii) in vitro antibacterial evaluation of G. lucidum crude extract, and (iv) molecular docking–based investigation targeting key resistance-associated proteins (ermC, mecA, and beta-lactamase). To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to computationally link bioactive constituents of G. lucidum with specific antibiotic resistance mechanisms relevant to acne-associated Staphylococcus spp.

Furthermore, unlike earlier reports that focus primarily on growth inhibition, our study provides mechanistic insights into how G. lucidum-derived compounds may interact with resistance-related proteins, suggesting a potential resistance-modulating role rather than merely antibacterial activity. This dual phenotypic–in silico strategy strengthens the biological relevance of medicinal mushrooms as alternative or adjunct therapeutic agents against antibiotic-resistant staphylococcal infections associated with acne.

This study explores the occurrence and antibiotic resistance patterns of Staphylococcus species isolated from acne lesions, highlighting the growing concern over antibiotic-resistant strains in acne-related infections. It evaluates the effectiveness of G. lucidum extract in combating these resistant bacteria. The mushroom's bioactive compounds, such as triterpenoids and polysaccharides, may serve as a natural alternative to conventional antibiotics.

Methodology

Sample collection

A total of 135 individuals aged 15 to 25 willingly participated in the study. They were interviewed using a standardized questionnaire, following their written agreement. The acne lesions, including comedones, red papules, and pustules, were sterilized with 70% ethanol. The contents were then extracted and gathered using a sterile cotton swab. The specimens were promptly conveyed to the Laboratory of our department. All isolation and handling were performed under the Biosafety level 2 (BSL-2) condition following WHO and CDC guideline,38 with the approval of Head of the department for the human sample protocol for the collection of specimen sample for the research.

Sample processing and storage

The specimens were introduced into Luria-Bertani (LB) broth and left to incubate at a temperature of 37 °C for one night. Next, the culture was streaked onto a Mannitol Salt Agar (MSA) plate using a sterile loop and then incubated. The white and yellow colonies obtained from the MSA plate were introduced into LB broth and subjected to overnight incubation. The samples cultivated in LB broth were preserved at a temperature of −20 °C with a glycerol concentration of 15% for future utilization.

Primary isolation and morphological study

We used biochemical analysis to look at the colony type, form, size, and other morphological aspects of the colonies that were presumed to be yellow and white on MSA plates.39

Short biochemical analysis

Biochemical tests were utilized to examine the enzymatic activities of microorganisms and aid in the identification of bacterial species by assessing the variations in their biochemical activities and their utilization of diverse carbon sources for energy acquisition. The biochemical reactions of all strains were assessed using the established procedures outlined in the laboratory research manual. The subsequent biochemical assays were conducted: Gram staining, catalase assay, coagulase assay, mannitol fermentation assay, methyl red assay, and oxidase assay.

Gram staining

A newly formed group of microorganisms was transferred onto a clean slide and thoroughly combined with a small droplet of sterile distilled water (sdH2O). After forming a thin layer, the slide was elevated significantly above the Bunsen burner to undergo the heat-fixing procedure. Initially, the smear was saturated with a solution of crystal violet dye for 1 min, followed by rinsing with sdH2O to eliminate any surplus dye. Subsequently, the smear was coated with iodine solution for 1 min and then rinsed with sdH2O. The decolorization procedure involved applying a 95% ethanol solution onto the smear for 10 s. Finally, the smear was washed with distilled water after being stained with a safranin dye solution for 1 to 24 min. The slide was permitted to undergo the process of drying. The chromaticity and morphology of cells were seen at a magnification of 100× using a light microscope with the assistance of immersion oil. Subsequently, the observation was documented.

Catalase test

The catalase test is used to detect the presence of the enzyme catalase in a bacterium. A fresh isolate colony was transferred to a clean slide using a sterilized toothpick, and a drop of 3% hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) was added to the smear. Then, the evolution of oxygen bubbles was observed.2H2O2→2H2O+O2

Coagulase test

The coagulase slide test is used to identify the presence of bound coagulase or clumping factor, which is attached to the cell walls of the bacteria. Bound coagulase reacts with the fibrinogen in plasma, causing the fibrinogen to precipitate. The coagulase test is useful for differentiating potentially pathogenic Staphylococci, such as S. aureus, from other gram-positive, catalase-positive cocci. Two drops of saline water were placed on the slide at each end, and the test sample was inoculated with gentle mixing. A drop of human plasma was added to one of the suspensions and mixed gently. Clumping of the organisms was observed within 10 s.40, 41

Mannitol fermentation

MSA plate and MSA slant were inoculated with the test organism using a sterile wire loop and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h and observed the result. Generally, acid production upon fermentation results in the formation of yellow colour. Those staphylococci that do not ferment mannitol remain unchanged.39

Methyl red test

The methyl red (MR) test is a biochemical assay conducted on bacterial species to identify their capacity to generate stable acidic end products (mixed-acid fermentation) from provided glucose. The unknown microbe was introduced into the fresh and sterile medium using a sterile inoculating loop, while the other broth was left uninoculated as a control. Following incubation at 37 °C for 48 h, the broths were retrieved from the incubator and treated with 5 drops of methyl red reagent. The colour was subsequently observed.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test by disc diffusion test as modified Kirby-Baur method

The antibiotic susceptibility patterns were assessed using the disk diffusion method recommended by the national committee for clinical laboratory standards. This was done on Muller-Hinton agar (MHA), using commercially available antibiotics including amoxicillin (Amx – 20 μg), ceftriaxone (Cef - 30 μg), rifampin (Rif - 5 μg), levofloxacin (Lev – 5 μg), gentamycin (Gen 10 μg), erythromycin (Ery - 15 μg), tetracycline (Tet - 30 μg), and clindamycin (Cln - 2 μg). The specific antibiotics used were obtained from Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK. The methodology followed the guidelines provided by Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) in 2016, as well as the study conducted by Lacy et al. (2004).42 Zone of inhibition diameters were measured and interpreted based on CLSI breakpoints. In brief, a single, well-isolated colony was incubated in 2 ml of Muller-Hinton broth at 37 °C for 4 h to produce a young culture. Dip a sterile swab into the organism's broth culture. A gentle squeeze removes surplus fluid from the swab. A sterile MHA plate is streaked with the test organism swab for a lawn of growth and left to dry for 5 min. Using sterile forceps, antibiotic discs were properly placed on top. After inverting the plates, they were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The isolates' susceptibility was assessed by measuring the zones of inhibition after incubation according to the CLSI.43 Quality control strains, including E. coli ATCC 25922 and S. aureus ATCC 25923, were used to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the results.

Phytochemical retrieval and preparation

The MeFSAT database, Medicinal Fungi Secondary Metabolites and Therapeutics, MeFSAT. MeFSAT: Medicinal Fungi Secondary Metabolites and Therapeutics (https://cb.imsc.res.in/mefsat/home) has so far conducted the search actively by collecting all the available compounds intrinsic to G. lucidum. In total, 269 G. lucidum compounds have been identified. By extraction, these chemical compounds have been saved in the 3D.sdf file format. Next, detailed preparation and optimization of these compounds as ligands have been done, and then, they were converted into. Pdbqt file format for further evaluation purposes.2.

Retrieval and homology modelling of target protein and validation

To investigate the target protein, we examined multiple literature reviews. In this study, the target proteins were selected based on their established roles in antimicrobial resistance (AMR), bacterial survival, and virulence in Staphylococcus species, as supported by published literature. We identified the mecA protein (encoded by mecA gene) linked to amoxicillin resistance in S. epidermidis,44, 45, 46 the beta-lactamase protein (encoded by beta-lactamase gene) connected with amoxicillin resistance in S. aureus,47, 48 and the ermC protein (encoded by ermC gene) related to erythromycin resistance in both S. aureus and S. epidermidis.49, 50, 51

As no experimentally resolved crystal structures were available for mecA (PDB ID: 3JTP) and ermC (PDB ID: 1QAM) protein, three-dimensional (3D) homology models for both proteins were generated using the widely utilized online tool SWISS-MODEL.52, 53 These models were validated by comprehensive evaluation of both protein structures through various quality assessment parameters, using ProSA-web,54 ERRAT,55 and Ramachandran plots.56 To retrieve the crystal structure of beta-lactamase protein (PDB ID: 3BLM), we explored a database named Research Collaboratory for Structural Bioinformatics Protein Data Bank (RCSB PDB). Education and research in basic biology, health, energy, and biotechnology depend on the global Protein Data Bank (PDB) database of 3D structure data for larger biological molecules such as proteins, DNA, and RNA, which has been stored at RCSB PDB in the United States.57, 58 This protein structure was capacitated and depleted through computation using the most recent versions of Discovery Studio 4.5. We removed all of the inhibitors, water molecules, and heteroatoms from beta-lactamase protein through Discovery Studio 4.5. We also used SWISS PDB Viewer 4.1 software59 for energy minimization of these proteins.

Analysis of molecular docking and ADMET

To evaluate the binding affinities between the target proteins (ermC, mecA, and beta-lactamase) and G. lucidum-derived compounds,60 we employed the PyRx virtual screening tool and Autodock Vina, v.1.2.061, 62 for molecular docking. It makes the binding pose clear by displaying every possible orientation and conformation for any specific ligand at the target protein and compound binding site. The substrate-binding pocket that corresponds to the primary protease's active site was identified using a grid box in Autodock after the ligand and substrate had been prepared exactly. The designated grid box at the ermC protein docking site had the following coordinates: Dimensions (Angstrom) of X: 42.3039 Y: 72.1588 Z: 45.0838, with a center of X: 11.3245 Y: 21.3066 Z: 25.5205 Å, for mecA: Dimensions (Angstrom) of X: 112.3807 Y: 57.3262 Z: 104.5609, with a center of X: -7.9737 Y: 45.7520 Z: 44.0317 Å, and for beta-lactamase: Dimensions (Angstrom) of X: 59.4688 Y: 42.8977 Z: 48.5039, with a center of X: 5.6313 Y: -15.163 Z: −19.2456 Å. The conformation with the highest docking energy once molecular docking was complete represented the preeminent conformation.63

The ADMET structure-activity relationship (admetSAR),64 SwissADME,65 Deep-PK,66 and pKCSM67 tools were employed as indispensable web-based servers to study and assess the molecular characteristics, pharmacokinetic properties, drug-likeness, promising biological activity, and toxicity profiles. The medicinal chemistry compatibility of the selected compounds is predicted by the Canonical Simplified Molecular-Input Line-Entry System (SMILES), which is retrieved from the MeFSAT database and utilized by the previously defined web services.

Molecular dynamic simulation

A 100-ns (ns) molecular dynamics (MD) simulation was performed to assess the binding stability of the G. lucidum and erythromycin and amoxicillin resistance gene encoding proteins of S. aureus and S. epidermis complexes.68 On a Linux system, simulations were run using Maestro version 2020.4 with the OPLS4 force field. Evaluating the protein–ligand interactions' fluctuation and structural stability over the course of the simulation was the main goal.69, 70 The TIP3P water model was used to solve the system inside a predetermined orthorhombic periodic boundary box. Na+ and Cl− ions were supplied at a concentration of 0.15 M to attain charge neutrality and replicate physiological circumstances. After that, a 100-ps energy minimization step was applied to the solvated protein–ligand complex in order to relax the system and remove any unfavorable interactions or steric conflicts from the original structure. When the highest force dropped below 1000 kJ·mol−1·nm−1, convergence was reached, guaranteeing appropriate equilibration. The SHAKE method was used to limit all bonds containing hydrogen atoms, allowing the system to be gradually heated to 300 K.71 All bond lengths involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the SHAKE technique, which allowed for the use of bigger integration time steps and improved computational efficiency. The algorithm maintains the system's overall dynamical features while stabilizing the simulation by limiting high-frequency bond vibrations.72, 73 Trajectory snapshots were recorded at 100-ps intervals across the simulation. For the protein–ligand complexes (G. lucidum and erythromycin and amoxicillin resistance gene encoding proteins of S. aureus and S. epidermis complexes), stability was assessed by computing key structural and dynamic parameters, such as the root mean square deviation (RMSD)74 and root mean square fluctuation (RMSF).75

G. lucidum extract extraction and preparation

Fresh fruiting bodies of G. lucidum were collected from natural or cultivated sources and authenticated based on morphological characteristics. The fruiting bodies were thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove surface debris and contaminants, then air-dried under shade for 7–10 days until fully dehydrated. The dried samples were ground into a fine powder using a sterile mechanical grinder and stored in airtight containers at room temperature until extraction. For ethanol extraction, 20 g of powdered G. lucidum were soaked in 200 mL of 95% ethanol in a conical flask. The mixture was kept on a rotary shaker at 150 rpm for 48 h at room temperature to facilitate maximum extraction of bioactive compounds. After incubation, the mixture was filtered using Whatman No.1 filter paper, and the filtrate was collected. The filtrate was then concentrated using a rotary evaporator at 40–45 °C to remove ethanol, yielding a semi-solid crude extract. The crude extract was redissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to obtain a working concentration of (1,6,10,15,20,25 and 50 mg/mL) for antibacterial assays. This prepared G. lucidum extract was used for in vitro antibacterial testing against S. aureus and S. epidermidis isolated from acne lesions. The extraction yield was calculated as the percentage of dry extract obtained relative to the initial dry weight of the raw material. To ensure reproducibility, all extractions were performed in triplicate under identical conditions, and the mean yield ± standard deviation has been included. Furthermore, phytochemical standardization was performed by quantifying total phenolic content using the Folin–Ciocalteu method and/or total triterpenoid content using standard colorimetric assays, expressed as gallic acid equivalents (GAE) and/or ursolic acid equivalents, respectively.

Antibacterial activity of G. lucidum extract against the isolated Staphylococcus strains

Isolates of S. aureus and S. epidermidis from acne were cultured and adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard. MHA plates were prepared and uniformly swabbed with the bacterial suspension. Sterile filter paper discs (6 mm) were impregnated with G. lucidum extract at concentrations of 1, 6, 10, 15, 25, and 50 mg/mL, then placed on the inoculated MHA plates. These concentrations were selected based on preliminary dose–response testing to identify biologically active levels showing measurable inhibition without saturation, and they are within the range of concentrations previously reported for G. lucidum antimicrobial assays (e.g., activity observed at 1–16 mg/mL in broth microdilution studies) and with dose-dependent inhibitory effects observed at up to ∼10 mg/mL in disc assays.36, 76

Erythromycin (15 μg) and amoxicillin (30 μg) discs were used as positive controls. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and the zones of inhibition were measured in millimeters to evaluate antibacterial activity. Lastly, statistical analyses were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's post hoc test, with results reported as mean ± SD and 95% confidence intervals where applicable. This approach allowed evaluation of concentration-dependent antibacterial effects with appropriate statistical rigor.

Results

Socio-demographic measurements

A total of 135 patients were included in the final analysis. The study population was predominantly male (71.1%, n = 96), with 28.9% (n = 39) females. Participants were mainly adolescents and young adults, with most patients aged 15–17 years, followed by those aged 21–23 years and 24–26 years. Based on body mass index classification, 42.2% of participants had a normal BMI, while 31.9% were overweight or obese and 25.9% were underweight. Moreover, the majority of patients had a previous familial history of the disease (79.3%), whereas 20.7% did not. The majority of participants belonged to a low socio-economic background (55.6%), followed by middle (34.1%) and high (10.4%) socio-economic groups.

Inclusion criteria: Patients data were eligible for this study if they were aged 12–26 years and had a clinical diagnosis of acne, with disease severity classified as mild, moderate, or severe based on clinical assessment. Only patients with complete demographic, lifestyle, and clinical records were included in the analysis. Enrollment was independent of antibiotic treatment status at the time of recruitment; however, current antibiotic use was systematically recorded and considered as a distinct subgroup during analysis. Exclusion criteria: Patients were excluded if they were less than 12 years or greater than 26 years, had incomplete demographic, lifestyle, or clinical data, or if acne severity could not be clinically classified due to insufficient or missing documentation. Discussion

The present study aimed to investigate the prevalence of S. aureus and S. epidermidis among individuals with acne and to assess their antibiotic susceptibility profiles. The results showed that gentamicin was the most effective antibiotic, followed by tetracycline, levofloxacin, rifampin, and clindamycin. In contrast, high resistance rates were observed for erythromycin, with approximately 82% resistance in S. aureus and 93% in S. epidermidis. Amoxicillin also exhibited a high resistance rate of 90% in both species. Given these findings, the study further aimed to address the resistance patterns associated with these two antibiotics by exploring G. lucidum extract as a potential alternative therapeutic approach.

Our study revealed that adolescents and young adults, particularly individuals aged 15–23 years, exhibited a higher propensity for developing acne compared with adults. The findings indicated that age, gender, BMI, family history, dietary habits, mental health status, and skin type, particularly oily skin, significantly influenced acne incidence. A higher prevalence of positive family history (79.3%) was observed, which may have a substantial impact on the development of the condition. This observation is consistent with findings reported in previous studies.17, 18, 19, 20, 78

No significant association was observed between acne severity and obesity in this study, which contrasts with earlier reports.20 This discrepancy may be attributed to the relatively small sample size. Socioeconomic status was found to influence acne risk, with individuals from lower-income groups being more susceptible. The high prevalence of oily facial skin (94.8%) and frequent use of cosmetic products (54.8%) may also contribute to acne development, as suggested by several studies,18, 79 although this association has been contradicted by others.80 A large proportion of patients reported frequent consumption of spicy foods (64.4%), oily foods (88.1%), sweets, and chocolate (90.4%), which may have contributed to the increased incidence of acne. Previous studies have reported a strong correlation between acne occurrence and dietary habits.17, 18, 19, 78, 80 Adolescents are particularly susceptible to acne vulgaris due to diets predominantly composed of processed foods that are deficient in essential trace elements, vitamins, and balanced minerals.7 Accumulating evidence suggests that high consumption of processed foods among younger individuals may contribute to acne development. Additionally, a high prevalence of depression (95.6%) was observed in the study population, which is consistent with earlier reports.21, 81, 82 This elevated prevalence of depression may be associated with moderate to severe acne, as suggested by previous research83; however, this factor was not directly assessed in the present study. Notably, the majority of participants (86.7%) had not received any form of treatment, while only 5% had previously undergone antibiotic therapy.

Antimicrobial susceptibility tests revealed the effectiveness of different antibiotics against S. aureus and S. epidermidis isolates. Antibiotic resistance is a major phenotypic trait, particularly in clinical isolates, and it is of significant interest for exploring the characteristics of suspected Staphylococcus spp. The indiscriminate use of antibiotics facilitates the emergence of drug resistance in Bangladesh.84 The current study revealed a highly sensitive profile for gentamicin, with both isolates exhibiting complete susceptibility. This finding suggests that gentamicin could serve as a reliable antibiotic choice for treating infections caused by S. aureus and S. epidermidis, providing a strong likelihood of effective bacterial eradication, which is consistent with findings from previous studies. Similarly, clindamycin was effective against 71% of S. aureus isolates and 67% of S. epidermidis isolates, indicating that it may be a viable treatment option for a substantial proportion of cases; however, the remaining isolates that displayed intermediate susceptibility or resistance may require alternative therapeutic approaches. These findings are consistent with those reported in other studies.9

For levofloxacin, S. epidermidis exhibited a high sensitivity rate of 92%, while S. aureus demonstrated a sensitivity rate of 85%. Rifampin susceptibility was observed in 82% of S. aureus isolates and 92% of S. epidermidis isolates, whereas tetracycline sensitivity was equivalent (87%) in both species. These results suggest that these antibiotics could be considered for treatment in cases where gentamicin and clindamycin are not viable options; however, the presence of resistant isolates necessitates a cautious and individualized approach to antibiotic selection. Similar findings have been reported in other studies.9, 85 Nevertheless, the most concerning aspect of the study was the resistance profile observed. Erythromycin resistance was demonstrated by both bacterial species, with rates of 93% in S. epidermidis and 82% in S. aureus. Additionally, ceftriaxone resistance was detected in 14% of S. aureus isolates and 48% of S. epidermidis isolates, while amoxicillin resistance was observed in 90% of isolates in both species. These findings indicate that increasing resistance to erythromycin and amoxicillin could significantly compromise the effectiveness of treating infectious conditions such as acne. Overall, the observed variability in susceptibility patterns underscores the importance of selecting antibiotics based on species-specific resistance profiles to effectively treat infections and mitigate the development of resistant strains.

In light of this analysis, tetracycline, levofloxacin, gentamicin, and rifampicin were identified as effective antibiotics against both species. Previous research has demonstrated that S. aureus and S. epidermidis frequently exhibit resistance to amoxicillin, ceftriaxone, and erythromycin, which may be attributable to over prescription in primary healthcare settings.86, 87 Excessive antibiotic use creates a natural selection pressure that drives microorganisms to develop resistance.88 Prolonged use of topical or oral antibiotic regimens for acne treatment has been shown to facilitate the transmission of resistance genes to potentially pathogenic microorganisms, including certain Staphylococcus strains, thereby posing significant clinical challenges.89, 90 One study reported that S. epidermidis became completely resistant to erythromycin and partially resistant to clindamycin and tetracycline after twelve weeks of treatment.31 Moreover, another study observed that resistant organisms persisted in both frequency and intensity and did not return to baseline levels until six weeks after discontinuation of topical antibiotic therapy.91 Nevertheless, this issue could be mitigated if healthcare practitioners exercised greater caution when prescribing antibacterial agents. Furthermore, several studies have demonstrated that resistance patterns in staphylococci may change over time.92, 93, 94, 95 The prevalence of antimicrobial resistance within a community can be reduced through the judicious use of multiple effective antibiotics, combined with patient education on appropriate antibiotic use and proper personal hygiene practices.92, 95

G. lucidum extract exhibited dose-dependent antibacterial activity against S. aureus and S. epidermidis, with inhibition zones increasing from negligible at 1 mg/mL to 22 mm and 20 mm at 50 mg/mL, respectively. S. aureus showed slightly higher susceptibility than S. epidermidis, indicating differential sensitivity among staphylococcal species. G. lucidum contains a variety of bioactive compounds, including triterpenoids, polysaccharides, and phenolic compounds, which possess significant antimicrobial activity. These compounds have been shown to inhibit the growth of bacteria and fungi, producing clear zones of inhibition in vitro and thereby confirming their antimicrobial potential. Methanolic extracts of G. lucidum, which are rich in triterpenoids and phenolic compounds, have demonstrated antibacterial effects against pathogens such as Escherichia coli, S. aureus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa, as assessed using agar well-diffusion and disc-diffusion assays.36, 96

The current study demonstrated high levels of antibiotic resistance in acne-associated S. aureus and S. epidermidis, consistent with global trends of increasing resistance in clinical isolates.27, 28, 29 Macrolide and lincosamide resistance, observed here as high rates of erythromycin and clindamycin resistance, can be attributed to the presence of ermC gene, which encode 23S rRNA methyltransferases that modify the ribosomal binding site and confer the MLSB phenotype.27, 28 Beta-lactam resistance, including high resistance to amoxicillin and ceftriaxone, is likely mediated by mecA-encoded PBP2a and beta-lactamase enzymes, which reduce drug binding affinity and hydrolyze beta-lactam rings.36, 37 Similarly, tetracycline resistance may involve efflux pumps (tetK/tetL) or ribosomal protection proteins (tetM/tetO), mechanisms well documented in staphylococci.97

Importantly, molecular docking and dynamic simulation analysis revealed that key bioactive compounds from G. lucidum, including lucidenic acid D1, ganoderlactone B, and ganoderlactone E, demonstrated strong and stable binding affinities to ermC, mecA, and beta-lactamase proteins. These interactions suggest that G. lucidum compounds could potentially inhibit the enzymatic functions that underlie antibiotic resistance, providing a mechanistic explanation for the observed antibacterial activity of the crude extract against resistant Staphylococcus spp. The concentration-dependent inhibition observed in vitro further supports this hypothesis, indicating that these compounds may suppress bacterial growth and modulate resistance-related pathways in a dose-dependent manner. Overall, these findings provide a mechanistic bridge between natural product bioactivity and known staphylococcal resistance pathways, highlighting the potential of G. lucidum as an alternative or adjunctive therapeutic approach in managing acne-associated antibiotic-resistant staphylococci. In this study, the binding sites of the target proteins were identified using a docking-guided binding pattern analysis rather than assuming predefined active sites. These targets are involved in essential bacterial processes, including cell wall biosynthesis, metabolic regulation, and stress response.97, 98, 99 The observed in-vitro antibacterial activity of the crude extract is consistent with these findings, as inhibition of these proteins can suppress bacterial growth and viability. ADMET predictions confirmed favorable pharmacokinetic and safety profiles for these compounds. Lucidenic acid D1, ganoderlactone B, and ganoderlactone E are bioactive lanostane-type triterpenoids isolated from G. lucidum. Together, these findings suggest that lucidenic acid D1, ganoderlactone B, and ganoderlactone E from G. lucidum may not only inhibit bacterial growth in a concentration-dependent manner but may also have the potential to modulate key antibiotic resistance pathways, highlighting their promise as natural therapeutic agents for managing acne-associated staphylococcal infections.

Despite providing valuable insights into the prevalence, antibiotic resistance patterns, and potential natural therapeutics for S. aureus and S. epidermidis in acne, this study has several limitations. First, disc diffusion assay primarily assesses inhibition of bacterial growth, reflecting bacteriostatic activity, and does not directly measure bacterial killing. Determination of bactericidal effects would require additional experiments such as minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) or time-kill assays, which are suggested for future studies. Second, while molecular docking suggested that bioactive compounds such as lucidenic acid D1, ganoderlactone B, and ganoderlactone E could interact with ermC, mecA, and beta-lactamase proteins, genotypic analyses of these resistance genes were not performed, and thus the actual presence, expression, or modulation of these genes in the bacterial isolates remains unconfirmed. Third, S. aureus and S. epidermidis were identified using standard phenotypic and biochemical methods, including colony morphology on Mannitol Salt Agar, Gram staining, and catalase, coagulase, and carbohydrate fermentation tests. Molecular confirmation was not performed and is acknowledged as a limitation. Finally, the study utilized crude extracts rather than purified compounds, which may limit the precision of the observed effects. While computational analyses identified specific bioactive constituents with strong binding affinities to resistance-related proteins, isolating and experimentally validating these individual compounds would provide more accurate and definitive insights. Future research should focus on the incorporation molecular techniques for species confirmation and purification of these bioactive molecules, comprehensive in vivo studies, and clinical trials to confirm their therapeutic potential and safety.

Conclusion

This study provides preliminary evidence that G. lucidum crude extracts exhibit dose-dependent antibacterial activity against acne-associated S. aureus and S. epidermidis. Molecular docking analyses suggest that key bioactive compounds, including lucidenic acid D1, ganoderlactone B, and ganoderlactone E, may interact with resistance-associated proteins such as ermC, mecA, and beta-lactamase, offering a potential molecular basis for the observed antibacterial effects. However, these findings are limited to in vitro assays and in silico predictions, and direct mechanistic validation of resistance modulation was not performed. The study also did not include genotypic analyses of resistance genes, and the use of crude extracts precludes definitive attribution of activity to specific compounds. Overall, while G. lucidum shows promise as a source of natural antibacterial agents, further studies—including in vivo experiments, resistance gene characterization, and evaluation of purified bioactive compounds—are required to substantiate its therapeutic potential against antibiotic-resistant, acne-associated staphylococci.
