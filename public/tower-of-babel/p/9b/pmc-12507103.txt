Molecular epidemiology of pESI-carrying Salmonella Infantis in Korea: insights from a one health framework
Authors: Yeona Kim, Hyeonwoo Cho, Miru Lee, Seung-Hyun Jung, Yeun-Jun Chung, Kwan Soo Ko, Kun Taek Park
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe emergence and global dissemination of Salmonella Infantis carrying the pESI megaplasmid is a growing public health concern owing to its association with multidrug resistance (MDR), enhanced virulence, and increased environmental adaptability. This study investigated the molecular epidemiology and transmission dynamics of pESI-carrying S. Infantis in the poultry production chain and human clinical cases in Korea. A total of 295 Salmonella isolates were collected from poultry sources (farms, slaughterhouses, and retail meats) in 2023, of which 219 (74.2%) were identified as S. Infantis. Additionally, 22 human-origin S. Infantis isolates collected between 2016 and 2023 were included in this study. Among these 241 S. Infantis isolates, 218 from poultry and one from a human case (collected in 2023) were MDR and carried pESIs, most of which carried blaCTX-M-65 and multiple other resistance genes. Comparative whole-genome sequencing revealed a high degree of genetic similarity between poultry and human pESI-carrying S. Infantis isolates, suggesting probable zoonotic transmission through the chicken supply chain. Structural comparison of complete plasmid sequences further revealed close similarity between Korean and US pESIs, suggesting a potential epidemiological link. This study identified the rapid clonal spread of pESI-carrying S. Infantis in the Korean poultry sector and reported the first identification of pESI-carrying S. Infantis in human clinical isolates in Korea. These findings underscore the urgent need for global surveillance, enhanced biosecurity measures in the poultry industry, and stricter regulations to prevent further dissemination of MDR S. Infantis.KEYWORDS: Salmonella Infantis, zoonotic transmission, whole-genome sequencing, one health, pESI

Introduction

Non-typhoidal Salmonella (NTS) is a major global foodborne pathogen responsible for approximately 94 million infections annually, 86% of which are attributed to foodborne transmission [1]. These infections result in approximately 215,000 deaths worldwide, making Salmonella one of the leading causes of foodborne diseases globally [1, 2]. Although most NTS infections manifest as self-limiting gastroenteritis, severe cases, including bacteraemia and meningitis, require antibiotic treatment and can lead to life-threatening complications [3].

Controlling Salmonella remains a major public health challenge owing to its marked adaptability, widespread distribution, and increasing prevalence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [4, 5]. Poultry is a well-established zoonotic reservoir and the primary vehicle for Salmonella transmission to humans, with frequent outbreaks linked to contaminated poultry products [6, 7]. The industrialization of poultry farming and intensification of global trade have contributed to the persistence and widespread dissemination of Salmonella in the food supply chain [1, 4, 6].

A growing concern is the emergence of multidrug resistant (MDR) Salmonella strains, particularly extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing isolates, which significantly complicate treatment options. Resistance to critical antibiotics, such as third-generation cephalosporins and quinolones, poses a serious threat to public health by limiting available therapeutic choices and potentially affecting treatment success, highlighting the urgent need for effective control strategies [7, 8]. Among the numerous serotypes of Salmonella enterica, serovar Infantis has emerged as an important global pathogen, particularly in poultry, and is increasingly implicated in human infections worldwide [1, 9].

A key driver of the global spread of S. Infantis is the MDR emergent S. Infantis (ESI) strains carrying the megaplasmid pESI (plasmid of emerging S. Infantis). This plasmid harbours multiple AMR genes, heavy metal and oxidative stress resistance genes, and other virulence determinants, conferring enhanced adaptability and persistence in diverse environments, particularly on poultry farms [1, 10]. S. Infantis strains carrying pESI were first detected in Israel in 2008 [11] and then subsequently spread in multiple countries [1, 9, 12, 13]. In the USA, pESI-positive S. Infantis was first detected in 2014 and has since become one of the major Salmonella serovars in the poultry industry, accounting for 29% of all Salmonella isolates recovered from retail chickens in 2019 [10]. The increasing contamination of poultry production systems with S. Infantis highlights the heightened risk of transmission to human consumers, as demonstrated in multiple studies worldwide [12, 14–16].

In Korea, Salmonella is the third most commonly reported foodborne pathogen [17], and human salmonellosis is closely associated with the consumption of contaminated chicken and chicken products [18]. Before 2020, the most frequently isolated Salmonella serotypes from poultry in Korea were S. Enteritidis, S. Albany, and S. Virchow, whereas S. Infantis accounted for only 2–3% of poultry-associated Salmonella isolates [19]. However, following the first detection of pESI-carrying S. Infantis in chicken carcasses in 2020, the prevalence of this serotype rapidly increased, replacing other major Salmonella serotypes and becoming the dominant strain in broiler farms, egg packaging facilities, slaughterhouses, and chicken meat [20–23]. This epidemiological shift raises serious concerns regarding the potential zoonotic transmission of MDR S. Infantis to humans, particularly through contaminated poultry products.

Despite these developments, limited data are available on the distribution, molecular characteristics, and genetic relatedness of pESI-positive S. Infantis across the entire poultry production chain in Korea, as well as on its potential link to human infections. Understanding the epidemiology of MDR S. Infantis is crucial for developing targeted surveillance, biosecurity measures, and antimicrobial stewardship programmes to mitigate its spread. Therefore, this study aimed to conduct a comprehensive investigation of S. Infantis isolates from poultry production, processing, and distribution chains, as well as from human clinical cases, using a One Health approach. This approach was practically implemented through: (i) an integrated sampling strategy covering animals and environment in the entire poultry production chain (from farms to retail meat) as well as human clinical cases; (ii) a unified genomic framework to directly compare isolates from these different sources using whole-genome sequencing (WGS); and (iii) an integrated analysis to infer potential poultry-to-human transmission pathways and asses their public health relevance. WGS analysis was used to characterize the molecular features of the isolates and assess their genetic relationships, providing a high-resolution epidemiological framework for understanding the emergence and spread of MDR S. Infantis in Korea.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval and sample collection

The experimental protocol for sample collection from live chickens was reviewed and approved by the Inje University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (approval number: Inje 2023-006).

Samples were collected from five provinces in Korea using a convenience sampling method. The initial sampling design aimed to include three farms, one slaughterhouse, and four retail markets per province. However, participation was restricted due to limited access, resulting in a final total of 1,301 samples collected nationwide in 2023: 708 samples from 15 chicken farms, 241 samples from 3 slaughterhouses, and 200 chicken meat samples and 152 shell eggs from 20 retail markets. To minimize seasonal bias, sampling was conducted across all four seasons throughout the year.

At the farm level, samples were collected from 5 broiler and 10 layer farms. The broiler farms typically raised between 30,000 and 50,000 birds, with samples collected from animals approximately 3 weeks of age. The layer farms housed between 10,000 and 30,000 laying hens, with samples collected from animals between 30 and 40 weeks old. Each farm was independently operated by different owners and attended by different veterinarians. Farm-level samples comprised 150 skin swabs, and 150 cloacal swabs, 48 shell eggs, and 360 environmental samples including farm soils and swabs collected from surfaces of cages/floors, ventilation fans, feed troughs, and drinking troughs. Slaughterhouse samples included chicken carcasses (n = 85), intestinal contents (n = 90), and environmental facility samples (n = 66) collected from surfaces, such as floors, walls, working tables, knives, worker gloves, cutting boards, and the entrance to the packaging room. Chicken meat (n = 200) and shell eggs (n = 152) were purchased from 20 retail markets across different regions of Korea.

Swab methods were used to collect superficial (skin, carcass, and environmental surfaces), intestinal contents, and cloacal samples using sterile cotton-tipped applicators, as follows [24]: (1) for animal skin and carcasses, a swab was swept three to five times along approximately 15 cm on both the ventral and dorsal sides using a single swab; (2) for environmental surfaces, approximately 100 cm² was swabbed following a standardized zig-zag pattern; (3) for intestinal contents, a swab was dipped into the contents three to four times; (4) for cloacal swabs, each swab was gently inserted approximately 1–2 cm into the cloacal opening and rotated to ensure adequate contact with the mucosal surface.

All swab samples were immediately placed into individual sterile collection tubes containing Amies agar gel medium (Copan Diagnostics, Murrieta, CA, USA). Farm soils were collected using sterile specimen cups, while shell eggs collected on-site were placed in individual sterile Whirl-Pak bags. Chicken meat and shell eggs from retail markets were purchased in their original retail packaging. All samples were stored below 4°C in ice-cooled containers and transported to the laboratory within 24 h of sampling.

Isolation, identification, and serotyping of Salmonella spp

All samples were processed according to the Food Code guidelines documented by the Ministry of Food and Drug Safety (MFDS) with minor modifications [25]. Briefly, the tip of each swab stick was placed into 10 mL of buffered peptone water (BPW; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and vigorously vortexed. Whole-body chicken meat was placed in sterile plastic bags with 450 mL of BPW and homogenized using a stomacher, after which 200 mL of the rinsate was transferred to a new sterile bag. Shell egg samples were placed in sterile bags with 450 mL of BPW and homogenized. Soil samples (25 g) were placed in sterile bags with 225 mL of BPW and homogenized using a stomacher. All pre-enrichment broths were incubated at 37°C for 24 h. After the pre-enrichment step, 100 µL of BPW culture was transferred to 10 mL of Rappaport Vassiliadis (RV) broth (BD Biosciences) and incubated at 42°C for 24 h. Thereafter, a loopful of each RV culture was streaked onto CHROMagarTM Salmonella (CHROMagar, Paris, France) and incubated at 37℃ for 24 h. Up to three presumptive isolates were selected and identified using an invA gene-specific TaqMan-based real-time PCR assay as previously described [26]. The serotypes of the Salmonella isolates were determined using a multiplex PCR as previously described [27].

Human clinical isolates

Twenty-two human clinical isolates of S. Infantis, collected between 2016 and 2023, were provided by Dr. Kwan Soo Ko at the Sungkyunkwan University School of Medicine. Detailed information on these isolates is provided in Supplementary Table S1. The use of human isolates in this study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Catholic University of Korea College of Medicine (approval number: MC24SASI0019) and the requirement for informed consent was waived.

Antimicrobial susceptibility test

Antimicrobial susceptibility tests of all S. Infantis isolates (n = 241) were performed using the microbroth dilution method with a SensititreTM KRNV6F kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The 16 antimicrobials tested included amoxicillin/clavulanic acid 2:1 ratio (AUG2), ampicillin (AMP), cefepime (FEP), cefoxitin (FOX), cefotaxime (CTX), ceftazidime (CAZ), chloramphenicol (CHL), ciprofloxacin (CIP), colistin (COL), gentamicin (GEN), meropenem (MERO), nalidixic acid (NAL), streptomycin (STR), sulfisoxazole (FIS), tetracycline (TET), and trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole (SXT). Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was used as a quality control strain. The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) values of the isolates were interpreted according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [28] and National Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring System [29]. MDR isolates were defined as those that were resistant to three or more antimicrobial classes.

WGS analysis

All S. Infantis isolates (n = 241) were subjected to WGS to determine their genetic characteristics and phylogenetic relationships. WGS was performed by ConnectaGen Inc. (Seoul, Korea) following previously described methods [30]. Briefly, the genomic DNA of each S. Infantis isolate were extracted using the iDetect gDNA prep kit for microbes (ConnectaGen Inc., Korea). Library quantification was performed using the TapeStation 4200 instrument (Agilent, CA, USA), and Knowledge Amplification Products and Applications Library Quantification Kit (Roche, Switzerland). Sequencing libraries were generated and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using 150 bp paired-end reads, following the manufacturer’s instruction. The quality of raw sequencing reads was assessed using FastQC (v.0.12.1) and Fastp (v.0.26.0). The read data were trimmed using trimmomatic v.0.39 and de novo assembled with SPAdes v.3.15.4. Assembly qualities were evaluated using QUAST (v.5.3.0). Average genome depth was calculated by mapping to the S. Typhimurium LT2 reference genome (GenBank accession number: GCA_000006945.2) using BWA-MEM (v.0.7.18) and per-base depth was calculated using SAMtools (v.1.20).

The sequence types (STs) of S. Infantis were determined using MLST (https://github.com/tseemann/mlst, software version: 2.23.0, accessed on August 22, 2024). In silico serotyping was conducted using SeqSero2 (software version: 1.2.1, database version: 2021-03-22) [31]. AMR and virulence genes were identified using the NCBI AMRFinderPlus (software version: 3.11.26, database version: 2024-12-18) and Virulence Factor Database (https://www.mgc.ac.cn/VFs/, accessed on January 02, 2025), with a minimum sequence identity threshold of 90% and 50% of coverage, respectively. The plasmid replicons of the isolates were identified using PlasmidFinder (software version: 2.0, database version: 2023-01-18) with ≥ 90% sequence identity and 60% coverage cut-off [32].

Characterization of pESI-associated genetic content in draft genomes

To characterize the genetic features of pESI-like plasmids in S. Infantis, contigs harbouring the IncFIB(pN55391) replicon were extracted from each draft genome using the MOB-recon tool (MOB-suite v.3.0.3) [33]. The presence of pESI was assessed by aligning these contigs against 13 pESI-specific marker genes described by McMillan et al. [34], using BLASTn with identity and coverage thresholds of >95%. Only contigs meeting these criteria were considered indicative of pESI carriage. Additionally, plasmid multilocus sequence typing (pMLST) was conducted for plasmids with the IncI1 replicon using the IncI1 pMLST scheme available at the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (https://www.genomicepidemiology.org/, accessed on December 05, 2024) [35]. OrthoANI analysis was performed to compare sequence identity levels among identified pESI-like plasmids. This approach enabled the detection and comparative profiling of pESI-associated plasmid sequences across all draft genomes, while intentionally avoiding structural inference due to the limitations of incomplete assemblies.

Phylogenetic analysis

Phylogenetic analysis based on the core-genome MLST (cgMLST) and single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) was performed using chewBBACA (v.3.3.1) [36] and Snippy (v.4.6.0) (https://github.com/tseemann/snippy), respectively.

For cgMLST, allele calling was conducted using the Salmonella enterica INNUENDO scheme, comprising 8,558 loci, from which 3,668 loci were extracted at a 95% presence threshold. Clustering was performed based on ≤15 allele differences, following the INNUENDO L1 criteria [37]. A minimum spanning tree (MST) was generated using GrapeTree (v.1.5.0) with the MSTreeV2 algorithm. For SNP-based analysis S. Infantis strain SINFA (GenBank Accession Number: N649235) was used as the reference genome and the recombinant sites were filtered using Gubbins (v.3.3.1) [38]. A maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree was constructed with RAxML-NG (v.1.2.2) [39], using 1,000 bootstrap replications. The population structure was further inferred using the Bayesian clustering algorithm, fastBAPS (v.1.0.8) [40], and all phylogenies were visualized using iTOL (v.6.0).

Comparative analysis of pESIs

A comparative structural analysis was conducted between the pESIs identified in this study and reference plasmids from other countries. Based on cgMLST of all S. Infantis isolates, strain IJCS5-24 was selected as a representative poultry isolate for hybrid assembly, as it was located centrally within the dominant phylogenetic cluster containing 166 isolates. Additionally, the human isolate, SMC2303-022, which also harboured pESI, was selected for comparative purposes. Complete plasmid sequences were generated using hybrid assemblies combining Nanopore GridION long reads (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) and Illumina NovaSeq 6000 short reads (paired-end 2 × 150 bp; Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) as previously described [41]. For Nanopore sequencing, libraries were prepared using the Ligation Sequencing Kit (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK), and sequencing was carried out on the GridION platform using MinKNOW software (default settings). Hybrid assembly of Nanopore and Illumina reads was conducted using Unicycler (v0.4.8). Reference pESI sequences were retrieved from GenBank, including plasmids from the USA (pCVM44454 [accession number: CP016413.1], pN55391 [CP016411.1]) [15], Italy (12037823-11_pESI-CTX-M-1 [OW849779.2]) [7], and Israel (119944_pESI [CP047882.1]) [11]. The structural features of these complete plasmids were compared using Easyfig (v. 2. 2. 2) [41], providing insights into conserved and divergent regions across geographic origins.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Chi-square tests were performed to assess significant differences in the AMR rates of S. Infantis isolates across different sample sources. Statistical significance was defined as a p < 0.05.

The agreement between phenotypic and genotypic antimicrobial susceptibility profiles was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa (κ) statistic. The κ values were interpreted according to established criteria: ≤ 0, no agreement; 0.01–0.20, slight; 0.21–0.40, fair; 0.41–0.60, moderate; 0.61–0.80, substantial; and 0.81–1.00, almost perfect agreement [42].

Results

Distribution of Salmonella in poultry production chain

The overall prevalence of Salmonella spp. in the poultry production chain is shown in Table 1. A total of 295 Salmonella isolates were recovered from 1,301 samples (22.7%). The highest Salmonella contamination rate was observed in retail markets (143/352, 40.6%), followed by slaughterhouses (78/241, 32.4%) and farms (74/708, 10.5%). At the farm level, the prevalence of Salmonella differed between broiler and layer farms. In broiler farms, Salmonella was detected most frequently in skin (22/50, 44.0%) and cloacal (17/50, 34.0%) swab samples. Environmental samples also showed a notable prevalence (25/113, 22.1%), indicating the presence of environmental contamination on broiler farms. In contrast, layer farms had significantly lower contamination rates; Salmonella was detected in only 2.0% of the skin (2/100) and 1.0% of the cloacal swab (1/100) samples. The prevalence in environmental samples on layer farms was also considerably lower (2/247, 0.8%) than that on broiler farms. However, shell egg contamination on layer farms was relatively higher than that in animals and the environment (5/48, 10.4%). At the slaughter stage, Salmonella was most frequently detected in the intestinal contents (48/90, 53.3%), followed by environmental (16/66, 24.2%) and carcass (14/85, 16.5%) samples. In the distribution step, a high proportion of Salmonella contamination was found in chicken meat (70.0%), whereas only 3 out of 152 shell eggs (2.0%) were positive for Salmonella.

Serotype distribution in poultry production chain

The serotype distribution of Salmonella in the poultry production chain is shown in Table 2. A total of 281 Salmonella isolates were classified into 6 distinct serotypes and 14 isolates were non-typable (NT). The most common Salmonella serotype isolated from the poultry production chain was S. Infantis (74.2%), followed by S. Enteritidis (10.5%) and S. Agona (8.8%). S. Infantis was the most prevalent serotype detected across all three sampling stages in farms (48/708, 6.8%), slaughterhouses (74/241, 30.7%), and retail chicken meat (97/200, 48.5%). Within farm samples, S. Infantis was primarily found on broiler farms (47/213, 22.1%), whereas only one isolate (1/495, 0.2%) was recovered from a skin sample from a layer farm. No S. Infantis was detected in farm or retail shell eggs (0/200, 0%). S. Enteritidis, the second most common serotype, was detected only in slaughterhouses (3/241, 1.2%) and chicken meat (28/200, 14.0%). Twenty-six S. Agona isolates were detected in broiler farms (16/213, 7.5%) and on chicken meat (10/200, 5.0%). One S. Mbandaka isolate was detected in a broiler farm environment sample (1/213, 0.5%). Additionally, two S. Thompson isolates were identified in shell egg samples from layer farms (2/495, 0.4%). S. Montevideo was found only in chicken meat samples. Fourteen NT isolates were found on layer farms (7/495, 1.4%) and in slaughterhouses (1/241, 0.4%), chicken meat (3/200, 1.5%), and retail shell eggs (3/152, 2.0%).

Phenotypic antimicrobial resistance profiles of S. Infantis

The AMR profiles of 219 poultry-associated and 22 human clinical S. Infantis isolates are shown in Table 3 and Supplementary Table S1. Among the poultry-associated isolates, the highest resistance was observed against NAL (99.5%), followed by TET (99.1%) and AMP and CTX (98.6%). Nineteen MDR patterns were identified among the 218 MDR isolates (Supplementary Table S2) and the most common phenotype was resistance to AMP-CHL-CTX-FIS-NAL-STR-SXT-TET (p-Pattern-10, 38.1%, 83/218). In contrast, 20 of the 22 human isolates were susceptible to all the tested antimicrobials. Only one human isolate (SMC2303-022) was MDR with MDR p-Pattern-10, which is identical to the most common MDR phenotype of poultry-associated MDR isolates. Of the 219 MDR strains, 217 were resistant to CTX (Table 3 and Supplementary Table S1).

WGS analysis

Genetic characteristics of S. Infantis

A total of 241 S. Infantis isolates were subjected to WGS, and the resulting data were assessed for both read and assembly quality. Assembly statistics showed that the number of contigs per genome ranged from 31 to 90, with N50 values between 137,513 bp and 416,119 bp. The total assembly sizes ranged from 4.56 and 5.02 Mbp, and the average genome coverage depth ranged from 137.6–1,498.1× across all isolates (Supplementary Table S1). MLST analysis revealed that all 241 S. Infantis isolates were found to be ST32, and in silico serotyping confirmed the isolates as S. Infantis (antigenic formula: 7:r:1,5).

Genotypic antimicrobial resistance profile of S. Infantis

Detection of the AMR genes revealed 10 different AMR genes and one point mutation conferring resistance to aminoglycoside: aac(3)-IVa, aadA1, aph(3’)-Ia, aph(4)-Ia, beta-lactam: blaCTX-M-65, phenicol: floR, quinolone: gyrA_D87Y point mutation, qnrB19, sulphonamide: sul1, tetracycline: tet(A), and trimethoprim: dfrA14 (Table 4 and Supplementary Table S3). In poultry-associated isolates, the most frequently detected AMR genes included tet(A) and gyrA_D87Y point mutations (99.5%), followed by aac(3) – IVa, aph(4)-Ia, and blaCTX-M-65 (99.1%). Nine different MDR genotypic profiles were identified, and the most frequent genotypic MDR patterns were MDR g-Pattern-1 and g-Pattern-5 (each 36.5%; Supplementary Table S4). g-Pattern-1 included all identified AMR genes: aac(3)-IVa-aadA1-aph(3’)-Ia-aph(4)-Ia-blaCTX-M-65-dfrA14-floR-gyrA_D87Y-sul1-tet(A). In contrast, g-Pattern-5 was identical to g-Pattern-1 except for the absence of aph(3’)-Ia. Among the human clinical isolates, two harboured AMR genes. SMC2302-022 harboured MDR g-Pattern-1, while I87NH0NE0 carried a plasmid-mediated quinolone resistance (PMQR) gene, qnrB19 (Supplementary S3). Notably, all 84 MDR isolates with MDR p-Pattern-10 harboured either the MDR g-Pattern-1 (n = 44) or g-Pattern-5 (n = 40) AMR genes.

The agreement level between genotypic and phenotypic AMR profiles was assessed using Cohen’s kappa test. Overall, a high level of agreement was observed across most antimicrobials (Supplementary Table S5). For AMP, CTX, and FIS, perfect agreement was observed (κ = 1.00). Almost perfect agreement (κ ≥ 0.81) was noted for CHL, STR, NAL, SXT, and TET. Some discrepancies in this group were associated with likely inactivating mutations in AMR genes. One TET-susceptible isolate carrying the tet(A) gene harboured a point mutation leading to a proline-to-serine substitution at position 148. Likewise, four SXT-susceptible isolates carrying the dfrA14 gene contained an early stop codon, resulting in truncated proteins. One human isolate carrying qnrB19 remained phenotypically susceptible to NAL.

Moderate agreement was observed for GEN (κ = 0.688) and CIP (κ = 0.742). For GEN, 18 isolates carrying aac(3) – IVa remained phenotypically susceptible but showed increased MIC of 4 µg/ mL, whereas all other susceptible isolates lacking the gene had MICs ≤1 µg/mL. In case of CIP, among the 219 pESI-carrying isolates harbouring the gyrA_D87Y point mutation, 208 were categorized as intermediate and 11 as susceptible. These 11 isolates had MICs at the lowest detection limit (≤0.125 µg/mL), making it difficult to assess whether MIC elevation occurred compared to truly susceptible strains lacking the mutation.

Characterization of pESI in S. Infantis

Detection of plasmid replicons of S. Infantis revealed six types of plasmid replicons, including IncFIB(pN55391), IncI1-I(Alpha), IncX4, ColpVC, Col(BS512), and Col(pHAD28) (Supplementary Table S1). IncFIB(pN55391) was detected in all 219 MDR S. Infantis isolates. The IncX4 replicon type was co-detected with IncFIB(pN55391) in four S. Infantis isolates from the same slaughterhouse. The remaining four replicons were each detected in distinct individual isolates. The ColpVC replicon co-existed with IncFIB(pN55391) in IJCS6-15, and Col(BS512) was found together with IncFIB(pN55391) and IncX4 in CSS3-24. IncI1-I(alpha) and Col(pHAD28) were exclusively detected in two different human clinical isolates, both of which were susceptible to all tested antimicrobials.

To characterize pESIs in S. Infantis, the plasmid sequences associated with the IncFIB(pN55391) replicon type were extracted from the draft genomes of all 219 S. Infantis isolates using the MOB-suite tool, and subsequently analysed to characterize their genetic features. All 219 isolates harboured a conjugative plasmid containing all 13 pESI marker genes, confirming the presence of a pESI-like megaplasmid (Supplementary Table S6). pMLST analysis based on the IncI1 scheme revealed that all pESIs were positive for four loci (ardA, trbA, sogS, and pilL) but lacked the replicase gene repI1. Notably, all isolates shared identical allelic profiles: ardA_11, trbA_8, sogS_14, pilL_3. In addition, all pESI plasmids carried the mercury resistance (merR, merT, merP, and merC) and yersiniabactin operons (fyuA, irp1, irp2, ybtA, ybtE, ybtQ, ybtS, ybtT, ybtU, and ybtX). Most isolates also carried the quaternary ammonium compound resistance gene, qacEΔ1, except for four isolates originating from the same retail market. Notably, all detected AMR genes, except for the gyrA_D87Y chromosomal point mutation and PMQR gene, were located on the pESIs.

The average nucleotide identity (ANI) values of the 219 pESI sequences in this study, including the human isolate, ranged from 99.96% to 100% (Supplementary Table S7), indicating that all plasmids were genetically identical or highly homologous at the sequence level. These results confirm the presence of a highly conserved pESI-like plasmid circulating across poultry and human S. Infantis isolates in Korea.

Phylogenetic analysis of S. Infantis

The cgMLST analysis revealed three major clusters (MST Clusters 1–3), which encompassed all pESI-positive S. Infantis isolates except one from slaughterhouse and seven from retail meat (Figure 1). Among them, 166 isolates (75.8%) were grouped into MST Cluster 1 (0-15 allelic differences), which included isolates from diverse sample sources, including 19 from farms, 67 from slaughterhouses, and 79 from retail chicken meat, and one human clinical isolate (SMC2303-022). This cluster also included isolates from all geographic regions, suggesting a widespread clonal dissemination of pESI-positive S. Infantis across the poultry production chain and potentially into the human population.

In contrast, MST Clusters 2 and 3 exhibited more localized patterns. MST Cluster 2 included 32 isolates (0-10 allelic differences), 21 from farms and 11 from meat samples, predominantly from Gangwon Province. MST Cluster 3 (0-4 allelic differences) comprised 13 isolates (8 from farms and 5 from slaughterhouses), all originating from Chungcheong Province. These regional clusters may reflect distinct transmission routes or localized introduction or persistence of novel clones within specific poultry production systems.

All 21 pESI-negative human clinical isolates exhibited substantial genetic divergence from MST Cluster 1, with 117–206 allele differences. Additionally, the pESI-negative slaughterhouse isolate (CSS2-2) was identified as a singleton and showed closer genetic proximity to the human clinical isolates than to the pESI-positive poultry isolates. These findings underscore the distinct genetic separation between pESI-positive and pESI-negative S. Infantis populations.

SNP-based phylogenetic analysis further delineated the evolutionary trajectories of S. Infantis strains harbouring or lacking the pESI megaplasmid. pESI-positive isolates (Clusters 3, 4, and 5) formed distinct clades that were genetically separated from pESI-negative isolates, which were grouped into Clusters 1 and 2 (Figure 2). This phylogenetic partitioning reflects a clear evolutionary divergence between pESI-positive and pESI-negative lineages.

The pESI-negative groups (Clusters 1 and 2) diverged earlier and exhibited greater genetic heterogeneity, with more diffuse branching patterns and higher intra-cluster SNP distances (Cluster 1: median = 122, range = 0–162; Cluster 2: median = 197, range = 7–209). The sole pESI-negative poultry isolate (CSS2-2) clustered with pESI-negative human isolates in Cluster 1, supporting an evolutionary connection independent of the pESI.

In contrast, pESI-positive isolates appeared to have emerged more recently and underwent clonal expansion, forming three compact and genetically homogenous clusters. Cluster 3 consisted of 13 isolates belonging to MST Cluster 3, with a median SNP difference of 5 (range = 0–7). Cluster 4 included 32 isolates from MST Cluster 2, along with six meat isolates previously identified as singletons by cgMLST (median = 16, range = 0–58). Cluster 5 represented the predominant lineage, encompassing all 166 isolates of MST Cluster 1, and two additional isolates from meat and slaughterhouse samples (median = 21, range = 0–39). Notably, the pESI-positive human clinical isolate (SMC2303-022) clustered within Cluster 5, indicating a close phylogenetic relationship.

Collectively, these findings suggest that pESI-positive S. Infantis strains represent a relatively recent introduction into the Korean poultry production system, followed by clonal dissemination and possible zoonotic transmission to humans. The combined cgMLST and SNP analyses highlight the public health significance of this emerging clonal lineage.

Comparative structural analysis of the pESIs from Korean and international isolates

To investigate the potential origin and structural conservation of pESIs in Korean isolates, we compared the complete plasmid sequences of a representative poultry-derived isolate (IJCS5-24) and a human clinical isolate (SMC2303-022) with pESIs from international S. Infantis strains (Figure 3). The comparison between the Korean human and poultry isolates revealed nearly identical plasmid structures (100% query coverage and 99.91% sequence identity), strongly indicating a clonal relationship and suggesting recent transmission.

When compared to international references, plasmid pIJCS5-24 exhibited the highest structural similarity to pESIs from the USA (99% query coverage and 99.87–99.99% sequence identity). In contrast, pESIs from Israel (87% query coverage and 99.98% sequence identity) and Italy (86% query coverage and 99.98% sequence identity,), representing early reported pESIs, shared comparable nucleotide identities but displayed notably lower query coverage. These differences primarily resulted from structural rearrangements, insertions, and deletions in specific plasmid regions, suggesting potential regional or temporal divergence in pESI evolution.

Collectively, these results demonstrate a high degree of structural conservation between Korean and US pESIs, while highlighting distinct structural variations in earlier pESIs from Israel and Italy.

Discussion

Salmonella is a major foodborne pathogen and a leading cause of human salmonellosis worldwide [6]. In the present study, the overall prevalence of Salmonella in the poultry production chain was 22.7%, with a significantly higher contamination rate on broiler farms (30.0%) than that on layer farms (2.0%). This finding aligns with previous reports, suggesting that poor sanitation and management practices in broiler farms contribute to higher contamination levels [43, 44]. The prevalence of Salmonella in slaughterhouses was 32.4%, exceeding the rates previously reported in Korea and China [45, 46]. The high detection rate of Salmonella in intestinal content samples (53.3%) suggests that cross-contamination during slaughter and processing is a major contributor to poultry meat contamination [47]. The final poultry product (chicken meat) had a 70.0% contamination rate, indicating a high risk of transmission to humans through the food supply chain. These findings highlight the urgent need for enhanced biosecurity measures and stricter regulatory interventions to mitigate Salmonella contamination during poultry production.

Among poultry-associated Salmonella, S. Infantis (74.2%) was most frequently detected across all sample sources, except for layer farms and shell egg samples. This widespread distribution suggests that the S. Infantis has established itself as a dominant strain in the Korean poultry industry, mirroring trends similar to those observed in Italy [7, 48], Slovenia [14], Russia [49], and the USA [10, 15, 50]. The worldwide expansion of S. Infantis strains in broiler flocks and poultry meat is of particular concern due to its association with MDR and increased pathogenicity, making it an important global contributor to human salmonellosis [51]. In Korea, data from the Korean Veterinary Antimicrobial Resistance Monitoring System indicate that S. Infantis has emerged as the predominant serotype in broiler farms and slaughterhouses since 2021 [23]. However, previous studies have largely focused on poultry farms and carcasses without providing a comprehensive molecular characterization of these strains. The present study addresses this gap by applying WGS and phylogenomic analyses to investigate the molecular epidemiology and genetic relationships of S. Infantis in South Korea. The inclusion of retail meat samples and 22 S. Infantis isolates from human clinical cases further strengthens the One Health perspective and demonstrates the potential for zoonotic transmission from poultry to humans.

In the current study, 99.5% (218/219) of poultry-associated S. Infantis isolates exhibited MDR phenotypes. The most common MDR profile indicated resistance to eight antimicrobial agents, consistent with previous reports [1, 23]. Notably, one human clinical isolate (SMC2303-022) exhibited an MDR profile identical to that of the poultry-associated isolates, suggesting possible poultry-to-human transmission of an MDR S. Infantis strain. Moreover, 98.6% of poultry-associated S. Infantis isolates were ESBL-producing, CTX resistant, and exhibited MDR phenotypes. The increasing prevalence of ESBL-producing S. Infantis in Korea since 2021 [21, 23] has raised significant public health concerns because ESBL production enhances bacterial resistance to third-generation cephalosporins, which are critical for treating severe salmonellosis.

In a 2018 foodborne disease outbreak in the USA, S. Infantis in raw chicken products was linked to 129 human infections across 32 states, with the outbreak strains exhibiting resistance to multiple antibiotics [52]. Considering this outbreak, the findings of our study emphasize the urgent need for detailed molecular characterization and surveillance of MDR S. Infantis in Korea to prevent potential large-scale outbreaks.

The detection of AMR genes in S. Infantis isolates revealed the presence of multiple resistance genes, most of which corresponded well with the observed phenotypic resistance profiles. For instance, the presence of floR, sul1, and gyrA_D87Y was matched with resistance to phenicols, sulphonamides, and quinolones, respectively. All isolates carrying the blaCTX-M-65 gene exhibited resistance to AMP and CTX, consistent with previous studies [15, 21]. Most isolates harbouring gyrA_D87Y point mutation showed intermediate resistance to CIP, consistent with previous studies showing that this single mutation elevates the MIC but is insufficient to confer full resistance [53, 54]. One human clinical isolate (I87NH0NE0) carrying qnrB19 was phenotypically susceptible to both CIP and NAL, though it exhibited an elevated MIC for CIP. This observation supports prior findings that qnrB19 alone may confer low-level quinolone resistance without exceeding clinical breakpoints [55].

Notably, discrepancies between the AMR genotypes and phenotypes were also observed in some isolates carrying tet(A) and dfrA14. However, the sequence alignment of tet(A) revealed a point mutation at amino acid position 148 (proline to serine substitution) in the susceptible isolates. Similarly, an early stop codon in dfrA14, which is associated with trimethoprim resistance, was found in four isolates that were phenotypically susceptible to the antimicrobial. These findings highlight the need for in-depth functional analyses of AMR determinants in S. Infantis to better predict resistance patterns.

All MDR S. Infantis isolates in the present study carried the pESI megaplasmid with the IncFIB(pN55391) replicon type. The pESI identified in these isolates harboured multiple virulence and stress resistance genes, which enhanced their survivability under various environmental conditions and increased their pathogenicity. The widespread use of antibiotics, disinfectants (e.g. quaternary ammonium compounds), and heavy metals in poultry farms may have promoted the selection and persistence of pESI-positive S. Infantis strains [48]. The global emergence of MDR pESI-carrying S. Infantis clones is a growing public health concern, given their ability to establish themselves in broiler chicken populations and subsequently infect humans [56].

Phylogenetic analysis based on both cgMLST and SNP data revealed a high degree of genetic relatedness among the isolates, regardless of sample type or geographical origin (Figure 1 and 2). This finding suggests a clonal dissemination of pESI-positive S. Infantis throughout the poultry production chain in Korea. Given the widespread contamination of poultry products, such clonal expansion increases the potential risk of human infection in Korea.

Supporting this concern, one S. Infantis human isolate collected in 2023 was found to carry a pESI. To our knowledge, this is the first confirmed report of a pESI-positive S. Infantis strain isolated from a human clinical case in Korea. This strain was clonally grouped within the major poultry-associated cluster (Figure 1), indicating a potential epidemiological link. Furthermore, structural comparison of the complete pESI sequences from this human isolate and representative poultry isolate revealed nearly identical plasmid architectures, including conserved resistance and virulence gene cassettes. This strong genetic and structural similarity further supports a close relationship between poultry and human isolates, potentially indicating recent zoonotic transmission or a shared ancestral origin. A similar pattern was observed during the early emergence of pESI-positive S. Infantis in US. Tate et al. [15] analysed WGS and pESI structures of isolates from human and animal sources collected before 2015, and suggested clonal expansion and potential zoonotic transmission based on small SNP differences (average of 16 SNPs between poultry and human isolates) and highly conserved plasmid structures. Collectively, these findings underscore the emerging zoonotic threat posed by the dissemination of MDR S. Infantis via the poultry production chain and the importance of continuous surveillance and One Health-based interventions.

The pESI was first identified in extended-spectrum cephalosporin-susceptible ESI clones in Israel in 2008 [11]. Since then, several regional variants carrying ESBL genes have been reported worldwide, including a European variant carrying blaCTX-M-1 [7, 13], a Russian variant carrying blaCTX-M-14 [49], and an American variant carrying blaCTX-M-65 [10, 15, 50]. The exact route of introduction of pESI-carrying S. Infantis into Korea remains unclear. However, considering the widespread detection of these strains in farm animals, one plausible route could involve the importation of breeding stocks. The Korean poultry industry relies heavily on specific imported breeds, such as Arbor Acres and Ross, sourced from a limited number of multinational breeding companies primarily based in the US and Europe [57]. Supporting this hypothesis, Korea imported the largest number of breeding chicks from the USA. According to the National Animal and Quarantine Statistics [58], Korea imported 1,368,037 chicks from the USA via 56 reported shipments between 2019 and 2023, a period that coincided with the emergence and spread of pESI-carrying S. Infantis in Korea [23]. In this study, most Korean pESIs carried blaCTX-M-65, the same gene frequently detected in S. Infantis strains from the USA [10, 15, 50]. In addition, structural comparison of complete pESI sequences revealed that the plasmids identified in the current study were more closely related to plasmids from the USA [15] than to those from Italy [7] or Israel [11].

These findings suggest a potential epidemiological link between Korean and USA pESI-positive S. Infantis strains. Global trade in breeding stocks has previously been implicated in the international dissemination of certain Salmonella lineages [59]. Similarly, imports from countries where pESI-positive S. Infantis is prevalent may have contributed to the introduction and spread of these strains. Although other possible introduction rousts, such as importing contaminated feed or human carriers, cannot be ruled out, targeted investigations are urgently needed to clarify the role of imported breeding stocks in the dissemination of MDR S. Infantis in Korea.

This study provides a comprehensive genomic analysis of pESI-carrying S. Infantis in the Korean poultry production chain and its potential zoonotic transmission to humans using a One Health approach. The widespread dissemination of pESI-carrying S. Infantis across all sectors of the poultry production chain may pose a significant risk to public health. While genomic evidence suggested possible epidemiological relatedness between poultry and human isolates, further epidemiological studies are necessary to evaluate the zoonotic potential.

Given the growing public health threat posed by MDR S. Infantis, implementing global WGS-based surveillance, stricter biosecurity measures on poultry farms, and stronger international regulations on breeding stock trade are urgently required. Furthermore, continuous monitoring of pESI-carrying S. Infantis along the poultry production chain is essential to track its ongoing evolution and to enable timely intervention measures.
