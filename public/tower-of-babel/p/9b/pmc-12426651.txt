Medical Therapy for Heart Failure After Mitral Valve Transcatheter Repair
Authors: Matteo Mazzola, Cristina Giannini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Corresponding AuthorKey Words: heart failure, hemodynamics, MitraClip, valvular disease

Secondary mitral regurgitation (SMR) affects approximately one-third of patients with heart failure (HF) and is consistently associated with worse outcomes. Over the past decade, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair of the mitral valve (M-TEER) has emerged as an effective treatment for carefully selected patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and significant SMR. The landmark COAPT (Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip Percutaneous Therapy for Heart Failure Patients With Functional Mitral Regurgitation) trial established M-TEER as a prognostically meaningful intervention when performed with optimized care, a finding recently reinforced by RESHAPE-HF2 (Randomized Investigation of the MitraClip Device in Heart Failure: Second Trial in Patients with Clinically Significant Functional Mitral Regurgitation), which extended clinical benefits to patients with less severe regurgitation.1 Despite these advances, M-TEER should not be viewed as a stand-alone strategy. Implementation of guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) remains the foundation of HFrEF management. Observational data suggest that medical therapy alone may reduce SMR severity in up to 27% of patients referred for valve intervention, particularly those with smaller ventricles and proportionate regurgitation.2 Comprehensive GDMT, typically including a beta-blocker, a renin-angiotensin system inhibitor, and a mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist (MRA), has been independently associated with improved outcomes post-M-TEER. In the Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS)/American College of Cardiology (ACC) Transcatheter Valve Therapy (TVT) Registry, patients on triple GDMT before M-TEER had significantly lower 1-year rates of mortality and HF hospitalization than those receiving incomplete therapy.3 Conversely, M-TEER can facilitate GDMT optimization by improving systemic hemodynamics and renal perfusion. In the EuroSMR Registry, nearly 40% of patients had intensified GDMT within 6 months, associated with reduced mortality and HF hospitalization and predicted by the degree of mitral regurgitation (MR) reduction post–M-TEER.4 Accordingly, in high-risk patients, such as those with right ventricular dysfunction, achieving at least 50% of target GDMT doses after M-TEER correlated with improved survival despite advanced disease.5

In this context, the article in this issue of JACC: Asia by Imamura et al6 adds an important perspective by examining MRA dosing in a real-world Asian population of HFrEF patients undergoing M-TEER for significant SMR. The study included 2,026 patients with SMR and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) <50% from the OCEAN-Mitral (Optimized Catheter Valvular Intervention–Mitral) Registry, a large, multicentre Japanese registry.7 Over a median follow-up of 14 months, use of any MRA dose (≥12.5 mg/d) at discharge was associated with a significant reduction in all-cause mortality and HF hospitalization. The 2-year incidence of the primary outcome was 36% in MRA-treated patients vs 45% in untreated (P < 0.001), with an adjusted HR of 0.83 (95% CI: 0.69-0.99; P = 0.046). No added benefit was observed with higher MRA doses (P = 0.97), suggesting that low-dose therapy may suffice. Post-procedural uptitration also did not improve outcomes, reinforcing the value of early, stable MRA use. Subgroup analyses based on COAPT-like profiles added further insight. In both COAPT-like and COAPT-unlike patients, MRA use was associated with lower event rates (34% vs 42%, P = 0.002; and 40% vs 48%, P = 0.007). However, in COAPT-unlike patients, higher MRA doses were linked to a nonsignificant trend toward worse outcomes. Regarding procedural success, residual moderate or greater MR was associated with worse outcomes in COAPT-like patients (HR: 1.32; 95% CI: 1.04-1.67; P = 0.021), but not in COAPT-unlike (HR: 0.86; 95% CI: 0.64-1.15; P = 0.30), highlighting heterogeneity in treatment response.

The flat dose-response curve raises questions about MRA effectiveness in this population. Age may be a factor: the median age in OCEAN-Mitral was 77 years, vs 72 years in EuroSMR and 73 in the STS/ACC TVT Registry.3,4 Older patients are more susceptible to MRA-related adverse effects such as hyperkalemia and renal dysfunction. A meta-analysis of RALES (Randomized Aldactone Evaluation Study), EMPHASIS-HF (Eplerenone in Mild Patients Hospitalization and Survival Study in Heart Failure), and TOPCAT (Treatment of Preserved Cardiac Function Heart Failure with an Aldosterone Antagonist) found that although MRAs reduce mortality in patients ≥75 years of age, they increase renal risks — an aspect that could attenuate the net clinical benefit at higher doses and contribute to the flat response curve observed in this analysis.8 Body composition may also influence response to MRA. The median body mass index in OCEAN-Mitral was 22.5 kg/m2, lower than EuroSMR and STS/ACC TVT, aligning with broader Asian HFrEF data.9 In EMPHASIS-HF, eplerenone reduced the endpoint by 52% in patients with abdominal obesity vs 23% in non-obese individuals.10 A less prominent “aldosterone gradient” in lean patients may limit benefit from higher MRA doses. Renal dysfunction likely played a role. Compared with EuroSMR and STS/ACC TVT, patients in OCEAN-Mitral had more advanced renal impairment (median estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] 35.4 mL/min/1.73 m2), a known barrier to MRA titration. A pooled analysis of 12,000+ patients from RALES, EMPHASIS-HF, TOPCAT, and EPHESUS (Eplerenone Post—Acute Myocardial Infarction Heart Failure Efficacy and Survival Study) showed that MRAs reduce HF hospitalizations and mortality across a wide eGFR range, although benefit wanes below 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 (HR: 0.96; 95% CI: 0.70-1.32), with 2- to 3-fold increased hyperkalemia risk when eGFR <45 mL/min/1.73 m2 (P for trend = 0.002).11 Thus, the absent dose-response in OCEAN-Mitral may reflect limited benefit and higher risk from MRA escalation in advanced chronic kidney disease, whereas low doses remain effective and safer.

The observational nature of the study from Imamura et al6 imposes limitations. MRA doses were not randomized and only recorded at discharge, with no follow-up on adherence, titration, or side effects. The flat dose-response may reflect both therapeutic inertia and unrecorded changes. A “therapeutic ceiling” may result from clinical caution, particularly in older or renally impaired patients, rather than from a pharmacologic limit. The analysis of COAPT-like and COAPT-unlike profiles may be clinically meaningful, reflecting prognostic patterns seen in prior registries.12,13 However, nearly half of COAPT-unlike patients had hemodynamic instability, a prevalence substantially higher than that observed in the COAPT-unlike cohorts of the Europe-based registries (41%) and the U.S.-based STS/ACC TVT Registry (24%), potentially limiting MRA initiation and escalation.12,13 Evaluation of procedural success was also constrained by a binary classification of residual MR as < moderate or ≥ moderate, without distinguishing 2+, 3+, or 4+. This reduces comparability with studies using more granular MR grading.14 In the STS/ACC TVT Registry, achieving MR ≤ moderate was linked to improved outcomes even in COAPT-ineligible patients.12 EuroSMR data suggest that in patients with advanced left ventricular remodelling or right heart dysfunction—typically COAPT-unlike—achieving MR ≤1+ does not improve survival.14 Taken together, these findings imply that by grouping moderate and severe MR, Imamura et al6 may have obscured prognostic distinctions, particularly in COAPT-unlike patients, where reducing MR to ≤1+ may offer limited benefit. Consequently, the prognostic value of procedural success in this subgroup may have been underestimated.

Despite these limitations, this study offers important clinical implications. It suggests that in elderly, lean HFrEF patients with renal impairment undergoing M-TEER, low-dose MRA therapy may be sufficient. Higher doses may add little and increase adverse event risk. These findings support a more individualized, risk-adapted pharmacologic strategy post–M-TEER, rather than uniform pursuit of maximal dosing. Still, the broader message remains: MRAs are a cornerstone of GDMT, and their underuse is a persistent gap. In the STS/ACC TVT Registry, fewer than 20% of patients undergoing M-TEER were on triple therapy beforehand. Inter-center variability was high, with some sites reporting zero MRA use. Importantly, patients on triple therapy had significantly lower 1-year mortality and HF hospitalization rates.3 Thus, M-TEER should not substitute optimal medical therapy, but rather complement it, prompting renewed efforts toward tailored GDMT implementation and intensification.
