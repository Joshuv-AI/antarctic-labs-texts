A Mixed Methods Feasibility Study of a Clinical Decision Support System Designed to Assess Risk for Aggression and Prevent Aggressive Behaviour
Authors: Maria Ameel, Tiina Talja, Jaakko Varpula, Tessa Maguire, Michael Daffern, Sanna Blomqvist, Tella Lantta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe electronic Dynamic Appraisal of Situational Aggression combined with the Aggression Prevention Protocol (eDASA+APP) is a clinical decision support system designed to prevent aggression, reduce coercive practices and promote proactive nursing interventions. It has previously been tested in forensic psychiatry in Australia. Adaptation and feasibility testing are required before implementation in new clinical and linguistic contexts. The aim of this study was to examine the feasibility of the Finnish version of the eDASA+APP using a mixed methods approach. The study took place in four Finnish mental health inpatient units. Nurses completed an online learning module and used the eDASA+APP for 2 weeks. Quantitative and qualitative data were collected on nursing documentation and feasibility. As a result, the eDASA+APP was perceived as easy to use and helpful in establishing a shared process and terminology for aggression prevention. Documentation of risk assessments and preventive interventions increased. The e‐learning module was considered adequate. One unit caring for older adults found the risk assessment component less practical. The Finnish eDASA+APP appears feasible and beneficial, supporting a larger implementation in units with adult patients. However, the feasibility testing in older adult services is recommended. The Mixed Methods Reporting in Rehabilitation & Health Sciences checklist was followed in reporting this study.

Introduction

Aggression remains a pressing concern in mental health units, frequently leading to use of coercive measures (Doedens et al. 2020; Lenk‐Adusoo et al. 2024). Aggression affects both staff and patients: for staff, exposure to aggression is associated with psychological distress, physical injury, and fear (Lenk‐Adusoo et al. 2024; Renwick et al. 2016); for patients, it contributes to a chaotic and unsafe treatment environment (Lenk‐Adusoo et al. 2024). The American psychological association (APA) defines aggression as ‘behavior aimed at harming others physically or psychologically’ (APA 2018). Coercive measures used in response to aggression have been linked to patients feeling traumatized and therapeutic relationships with staff adversely impacted (Chieze et al. 2019) as well as staff reporting moral distress (Doedens et al. 2020). Aggression in mental health units can be understood as a symptom of mental ill health and the demands of inpatient confinement. Risk of aggression can be assessed and some incidents can be prevented (Griffith et al. 2021; Lenk‐Adusoo et al. 2024; Maguire et al. 2019).

There are several evidence‐based interventions that have been shown to decrease incidents of aggression in mental health settings, including the systematic use of short‐term aggression risk assessment (Abderhalden et al. 2008; Griffith et al. 2021; Maguire et al. 2019; van de Sande et al. 2011) and the use of preventative interventions such as Safewards (Bowers 2014). However, implementing these, as with other evidence‐based nursing interventions, is challenging (Hamari et al. 2023).

One way to support nurses' use of evidence‐based interventions is the integration of novel practice initiatives into clinical decision support systems (CDSS) within electronic health record systems (EHR) (Hamari et al. 2023). CDSSs are health information technology tools that can be combined with evidence‐based assessment and interventions (Chen et al. 2023; Ho et al. 2021). The electronic Dynamic Appraisal of Situational Aggression + Aggression Prevention Protocol (eDASA+APP) is a CDSS, supporting systematic aggression risk assessment and management in inpatient mental health units. It has been shown to decrease the incidence of violence and use of coercive measures as well as increasing proactive preventative nursing interventions in Australian forensic mental health units (Griffith et al. 2021; Maguire et al. 2019). The eDASA+APP combines the DASA (Ogloff and Daffern 2006) with an Aggression Prevention Protocol (APP) to guide nurses' short‐term violence risk assessment and management (Maguire et al. 2019). DASA is a short‐term seven‐item aggression risk assessment measure with strong predictive validity for imminent aggression (Ogloff and Daffern 2006). The APP includes evidence‐based interventions to prevent aggression in a non‐coercive manner. In the original model, each DASA risk band (Low, Moderate and High) specified nursing interventions including one‐to‐one nursing, reassurance, distraction, de‐escalation, limit setting, PRN (pro re nata) medication, and increased observations (Maguire et al. 2019).

Development of the Finnish Version of eDASA+APP

Although DASA has been used in multiple countries outside Australia, the integrated eDASA+APP has not. We used the O'Cathain et al. (2019) co‐design framework to guide adaptation of the eDASA+APP in Finland (eDASA+APP‐FI; FI referring to the Finnish version of the eDASA+APP). This process consisted of six workshops with potential end‐users (nurses and experts by experience), a survey of nurses' and nurse managers' attitudes towards aggression risk assessment (Varpula et al. 2024) and a rapid ethnography regarding patient involvement in electronic documentation and risk assessment (Anttila et al. 2025). This led to adaptations in the original APP interventions as well as adaptation of the training. Additionally, the organization had shared electronic documentation that includes patient participation in the documentation process as a strategic goal. As research demonstrates that the DASA can be rated together with patients (Lantta et al. 2016), we added a goal that eDASA+APP‐FI would be used together with the patient to support the shared documentation process. Some of the adaptations were linguistic and some related to different nursing care practices, reflecting the preferences of Finnish mental health nurses. Following adaptation, feasibility testing is regarded as an essential component of the co‐design process (O'Cathain et al. 2019). To understand different aspects of feasibility, we used Bowen et al.'s (2009) framework to guide both methodology and outcomes of the study. According to Bowen et al. (2009) a feasibility study can provide understanding during the intervention development on how the intervention might work. Bowen et al. (2009) describe different aspects of feasibility defined in eight categories including demand, implementation and integration, acceptability, limited efficacy testing, practicality, adaptation, integration, and expansion. In this study, our focus was on implementation and integration, acceptability, practicality, adaptation, and integration, as demand was essentially discussed in the adaptation process prior to this phase. More specifically, we aimed at assessing the factors supporting and hindering feasibility: integration and implementation in the EHR of the eDASA+APP‐FI and online training.

Methods

Design

The study used a feasibility mixed‐method design with qualitative and quantitative data collected and integrated to help understand different aspects of feasibility (Aschbrenner et al. 2022). The Mixed Methods Reporting in Rehabilitation & Health Sciences (MMR‐RHS) was used as a reporting checklist (Tovin and Wormley 2023).

Study Setting and Participants

We used purposive sampling, commonly used in implementation studies (Palinkas et al. 2015) to gain understanding of the feasibility of the eDASA+APP‐FI in units with different types of levels of aggression. Units with different levels of aggressive incidents (low‐, moderate‐ and high‐prevalence), based on the organization's workplace aggression incident reports, were selected. The selected units included four locked inpatient units for adults, of which one was a unit for older adults with both neurological and psychiatric problems. All units were located in Southern Finland in the cities of Helsinki, Espoo and Vantaa. The yearly variance of workplace violence incidents was 37 in the high‐prevalence unit (Unit B) and 3 in the low‐prevalence unit (Unit A). More detailed information can be found in Supporting Information 2. Prior to this study, three of the units had participated in the co‐design process of the Finnish eDASA+APP version (Anttila et al. 2025).

Training and Feasibility Study

Nurses in the piloting units were instructed to complete an e‐learning course prior to the 2‐week feasibility study. The eDASA+APP‐FI was integrated into the EHR and during the 2‐week feasibility study, nurses were asked to use the eDASA+APP‐FI to assess the risk of aggression daily and to guide aggression prevention activities with all patients daily.

Data Collection and Analysis

Data were collected from November 2023 to February 2024 using three methods: (1) nursing documentation in the EHR of aggression risk assessment and preventative and management interventions for 3 days: 1 month before (November 2023) and during the study (December 2023), (2) survey data on adaptation, practicality and acceptability with the e‐learning course (December 2023) and the eDASA+APP‐FI, after the study period (January to February 2024), and (3) qualitative data from feedback sessions after the study (January 2024). Data collection sheets and questions are described in more detail in Supporting Information 1.

According to Bowen et al. (2009) the integration and implementation can be assessed using a small pre–post design to evaluate deployment of the intervention. First, to better understand integration and implementation of the eDASA+APP‐FI into the EHR, we extracted nursing documentation data from the EHR. In line with best practices for feasibility studies, we used a brief snapshot design, extracting 3 days of documentation before and during the study phase, to explore feasibility and detect early changes in documentation practices, rather than evaluate intervention efficacy (Eldridge et al. 2016). In total, 86 different nurses worked on the pilot units during the 2‐week study period. The units were not notified beforehand of data extraction to ensure their actions were unaffected by the proposed evaluation of nursing documentation. One member of the research team read through all nursing documentation of all patients present at the units during the 3 days (prior and during the study). After that, all documented mentions of aggression risk assessment (unstructured and structured), prevention and management were extracted. The extractions were made by a person with both clinical nursing experience on inpatient mental health units and familiarity with terminology used by nurses when describing aggression and preventative and management interventions. The extractions were read through and verified by two other research group members. The two other research group members independently reviewed the extracted material to ensure that all relevant documentation was included and consistently categorized. Any uncertainties or disagreements were discussed until consensus was reached. The number of risk assessments, prevention and management incidents before and during the study are presented as frequencies and percentages.

Second, two different electronic surveys were used to understand the feasibility of the e‐learning course and the acceptability of the eDASA+APP‐FI. First, to assess the adaptation (feasibility of local adaptations) and practicality (feasibility of the course delivery form) of the eLearning course, an online survey was included in the course platform Moodle. The survey included five questions regarding comprehensiveness of the course, understanding of risk assessment and intention to use the eDASA+APP‐FI. These questions were rated using a Likert scale. Additional feedback was gathered using an open‐ended question. The survey is included in the Supporting Information.

Acceptability of the eDASA+APP‐FI was evaluated through a survey after the study (January to February 2024). We used REDCap electronic data capture tools (Harris et al. 2019) hosted at the [University of Turku]. The survey was sent to nurse managers by email who were asked to forward to all members of nursing staff in the four participating units. The survey comprised nine questions, of which four questions were on a Likert scale, and five were open‐ended questions, following the Bowen et al. (2009) feasibility areas. The questionnaire is included as Supporting Information. Both surveys were analysed using descriptive statistics. A qualitative content analysis (Hsieh and Shannon 2005) was used to inductively analyse the open‐ended questions.

Third, we collected qualitative data to gain understanding of practicality, acceptability and adaptation. This included feedback sessions that were held on the units (n = 3, one session included two units from the same hospital) to understand how the participants experienced acceptability, practicality, local adaptations and integration of the eDASA+APP‐FI. These feedback sessions resembled focus groups; they were facilitated by members of the research team, one taking notes and one moderating the discussion. The themes for discussion included experiences of use, advantages and disadvantages of the eDASA+APP‐FI, and suggestions for improvement. The feedback sessions occurred in the hospitals' premises, during working hours, and they were not recorded. All nurses who were available on the units at the time of the feedback sessions were invited to participate. The number of participants in each group varied between three and eight. In some cases, nurses left and joined the feedback sessions due to clinical work demands.

The notes from the feedback sessions and open‐ended answers from the post‐study survey were analysed using qualitative deductive content analysis (Elo and Kyngäs 2008). In this study, the deductive analysis matrix was based on areas of feasibility as identified by Bowen et al. (2009) acceptability, practicality, adaptation, and integration. The data were first read through several times, then coded, and categorized according to feasibility areas. Two members of the research team reviewed the categorization of text into specific themes, and disagreements were resolved through consensus.

Ethical Considerations

The study received ethical approval from the [Helsinki University Hospital] Regional Committee on Medical Research Ethics (§ 203/2021). Permission to conduct the research was also granted by the hospital authority where the units were based. All study participants were provided with detailed information regarding the study, their role as participants, their rights, and how the data was planned to be processed and stored so that they could make an informed decision about whether to participate. Participants provided informed consent either in written or electronic form, depending on the data collection method. Participation in the study was anonymous as no personal information about the nurse participant or patients was gathered.

Results

Changes in Nursing Documentation Before and During the Feasibility Study

The pre‐study documentation evaluation consisted of nursing documentation of 58 patients (34 men and 24 women) present in the units during the 3 days in November (20–22 November 2023); 28% (n = 16) of patients had their aggression risk assessed, and seven had more than one assessment. A total of 29 aggression risk assessments were documented. All descriptions were based on the nurses' clinical judgement and recorded narratively in the EHR. This included expressions such as, ‘the patient has been very irritable around dinner time’ or ‘the patient has been cooperative. No aggression or delusions’. Notes included descriptions of observed changes in patients' behaviour in 22 entries.

Intervention to prevent and manage aggression risk was documented following 55% (n = 16) of the occasions in which aggression risk was elevated. Interventions included PRN medication (eight cases), de‐escalation (five occasions), directing to own room (used twice) and limit setting (used twice). Additionally, increasing staffing, calling security guards, and seclusion were each mentioned once. In approximately half (n = 13, 45%) of the occasions when the risk of aggression was identified, there was no preventative action documented. Of these, on seven occasions patients' behaviour was described as irritable or that they had raised their voice, shouted or were becoming nervous. On five occasions patients were described as calm or cooperative, and on one occasion a patient was described as calm but was prescribed medication involuntarily.

During the 2‐week eDASA+APP‐FI feasibility study, nursing documentation on patients' aggression risk in the EHR was reviewed between 18 and 20 December 2023. The sample included 64 patients (32 men and 32 women). Aggression risk was assessed in 92% (n = 59) of patients at least once. For five patients, the risk assessment was completed several times. A total of 73 assessments were documented; of these, 89% (n = 65) were done using the eDASA. Various methods of preventing aggression were recorded in 35 (48%) of the assessments, which covered 24 different patients. More than one intervention was documented 18 times. APP interventions were documented after use of the eDASA in 34% (n = 25) of assessments, covering 20 different patients. Additionally, the following interventions, PRN medication and validation, were identified in the free text documentation (both once). In the structured documentation, the personalized day program was recorded 11 times, validation 11 times, de‐escalation eight times, assuring the safety of the environment six times, guiding the use of calm down methods six times, and continuous therapeutic presence three times. Interventions not listed within the APP but which were documented included, under constant observation (without patients' consent), involuntary medication, calling security guards, increased staffing, and reality orientation. No interventions were recorded for 37 of the 59 patients whose risk was assessed with the eDASA. Table 2 describes the documented risk assessment and preventative interventions at the unit level.

User Experiences of the eDASA+APP‐FI and eLearning Course

In total, 71 participants provided feedback for the e‐learning course. Participant's included nurses, medical doctors, nursing students and social workers. Most participants (n = 59, 83.1%) either completely agreed or somewhat agreed that the training course helped them understand the importance of aggression risk assessment in patient care. Similarly, most participants (n = 59, 83.1%) completely agreed, or somewhat agreed, that the course helped them to understand the importance of proactive interventions in aggression risk management. When asked whether the course provided them with sufficient knowledge to use eDASA+APP‐FI to assist risk related decision‐making, 37 (52%) participants somewhat agreed, and 18 (25.4%) completely agreed. Almost all participants agreed (completely agree n = 24, 33.8%; somewhat agree n = 38, 53.5%) that they plan to use eDASA+APP‐FI in their work. Most (n = 53, 74.6%) of the respondents considered their ability to use eDASA+APP to be either good or very good after the course. Some (n = 17, 23.9%) participants remained undecided. One participant considered their ability to use eDASA+APP‐FI to be poor after the course.

Participants provided 58 open‐ended answers as to what was helpful or needed improvement in the course. Of the answers, 43 were positive. The course was regarded as structured, comprehensive, and compact but also adequate in length: ‘This is useful information and helps me a lot in my work’. Positive feedback was given in relation to the study materials, especially the videos and posters describing the preventative interventions: ‘Many useful elements, such as preventative interventions’. Neutral answers (n = 10) included small suggestions regarding the course structure and suggestions to add patient cases.

One participant stated that the course was too short and superficial (n = 1), and three stated that the section regarding the use of the eDASA+APP‐FI in the EHR requires improvement. Two participants commented critically that taking the course on the e‐Learning platform ‘Moving between sections is challenging’. One participant found the course content somewhat elementary for those with long experience in mental health care, suggesting that many topics were already familiar or self‐evident. One answer was positive towards the course and APP interventions but found that the DASA did not serve the purpose with patients experiencing neurological difficulties.

The Feasibility After the Study

Feasibility was studied by the survey and feedback sessions (n = 3), with 3–8 participants per group.

The survey was opened by 29 and completed by 19 of the 86 nurses working in study units at the time of the pilot. Participants regarded eDASA+APP‐FI to be easy to use (88%, n = 17). The online training was regarded as sufficient for using eDASA+APP‐FI (67%, n = 12). Most participants reported that they had not used eDASA+APP‐FI together with the patient (79%, n = 15). Regarding the future use of eDASA+APP‐FI in their units, the participants' responses varied, with approximately a quarter (n = 8) somewhat agreeing that they would continue its use, and one fifth (n = 7) remaining undecided.

Feasibility Experiences

Experiences were captured from the open‐ended questions of the survey and feedback sessions. Nurses described technical factors that hindered the integration of information such as how the eDASA scores appear in different parts of the EHR. Participants identified a need to view the scores for each patient in the unit in one glance to gain an overview of the risk level in the unit. Nurses proposed adding a section to the eDASA+APP‐FI for documenting the patient's own view on risk assessment to support patient participation in risk evaluation.

Practicality for the eDASA+APP‐FI was regarded as high in several situations, such as with new patients or when the patient's condition is unstable. Conversely, the DASA was seen as unsuitable for some patients. For example, nurses felt that patient's personality traits might elevate the risk score, even if the nurse's clinical assessment indicated a low risk. Nurses in the older adults' mental health unit were especially critical of the suitability of the DASA with their patients. ‘The [DASA] scores don't provide sufficient information for our patients’. Additionally, the risk assessment instrument was considered pointless by some nurses, as they rely on intuition and their own clinical judgement.

The local adaptation to the APP was found to be feasible: ‘Interventions are familiar and are used daily’. The APP interventions were recognized as the common way to prevent and manage the patient's risk for aggressive behaviour.

There were themes both hindering and supporting the EHR integration. On the one hand, nurses described eDASA+APP‐FI as easy to use. The eDASA+APP‐FI was easy to locate in the EHR, facilitating information transfer between nurses. The use of the eDASA+APP‐FI was found to standardise assessment of aggression risk, regardless of the nurse's experience level. The eLearning course was quick to complete. Nurses suggested developing the e‐learning course further by adding material such as the importance of documenting the risk and interventions together with the patient. Additionally, nurses suggested that the eLearning module could be part of the orientation phase for new employees to gain a shared understanding of risk assessment and evaluation.

Acceptability was supported by the ease of use and by the standardization of risk assessment and documentation provided through the CDSS. The participants suggested that acceptability could be higher if other professionals such as physicians and psychologists were actively involved in the use of the information gathered with the eDASA+APP‐FI. APP interventions were regarded as highly acceptable. Table 3 describes the feasibility themes. The DASA as a tool to assess aggression was found unsuitable in the unit with older patients suffering from both mental and neurological problems.

Integration of Results

Integrating the results is the final phase in a mixed methods study (Aschbrenner et al. 2022). We analysed the qualitative and quantitative data in parallel and merged it after the analysis (Aschbrenner et al. 2022). Findings from the quantitative data, including nursing documentation and numerical responses of the two surveys (one on online training and one on feasibility), supported the feasibility. The feasibility of the adapted APP interventions and training was high according to these findings. The qualitative data from feedback sessions and open answers showed unit‐level differences in the feasibility of the eDASA and in the preferred frequency of future use of the eDASA+APP‐FI. Additional adaptation needs were identified for the eDASA in units caring for older persons. The practicality of the eDASA+APP‐FI was considered to be highest with new patients and during patient transfer situations. Overall, these findings suggest that minor adjustments to EHR integration to enhance patient participation and multiprofessional use are needed to improve feasibility in addition to unit‐level adaptation before large‐scale implementation.

Discussion

This study explored the feasibility of a novel CDSS, the eDASA+APP‐FI in four Finnish mental health units. Results revealed acceptability, suggesting the eDASA+APP‐FI could support nurses' aggression risk assessments and guide preventative and risk management interventions (Eweida and Ibrahim 2023).

The findings also demonstrated that the eDASA+APP‐FI was easy and quick to use, and its integration into the EHR improved nursing documentation on aggression risk and preventive interventions. We did not identify differences among the use between the units participating in the co‐design process (Anttila et al. 2025), compared with the one unit participating in the study only. This suggests that the modifications/adaptations made were feasible across the organization and that the eLearning course provided sufficient information for staff to use the eDASA+APP‐FI.

Nurses' suggestions for further adaptations made during the feedback sessions were important as they identified challenges related to feasibility. Nurses provided feedback on the eDASA+APP‐FI's clarity, visibility and utility in the EHR. The feedback was incorporated into the development process, making the eDASA+APP‐FI easier to find in the EHR before the larger scale implementation. This highlights the need for end user involvement and feedback during the different stages of CDSS development and implementation (Gu et al. 2024) and the importance of different types of data collection methods to assess the feasibility.

During the study, eDASA risk assessments were conducted on most patients in the units. In addition, significantly more interventions to manage aggressive behaviour were documented. The lack of standardized documentation of nursing interventions in mental health inpatient settings has been noted (Pérez‐Toribio et al. 2024; Myklebust and Bjørkly 2019). The finding that nurses identified the APP interventions, but described lacking the terminology to communicate these, has been noted previously in outpatient settings (Ameel et al. 2019). Standardizing the language of preventative interventions through the APP might help nurses to conceptualize and document their work using standardized terminology, which has been associated with improved quality of documentation (De Groot et al. 2019).

The eLearning training was seen as sufficient, with minor modifications. The online delivery method made it possible for new employees to start the course regardless of time and place; this was regarded as important. This finding highlights the importance of training in the implementation of a CDSS (Chen et al. 2023; Hamari et al. 2023). The finding that some participants thought more training would be needed could indicate that there may be a need to provide the possibility for in‐person training or longer online training as preferences and training needs vary between learners (Amir et al. 2020).

Concerns about the use of the eDASA+APP‐FI were also raised during the feedback sessions. Routine use of eDASA+APP‐FI for all patients was seen as unnecessary. Not all staff members were committed to using the eDASA+APP‐FI, illustrating the challenges and changes that CDSSs bring in the clinical workflow (Sutton et al. 2020). Similarly, documenting the results of the eDASA risk assessment together with the patient is novel and broader uptake of this collaborative clinical practice requires both training and organizational support (Iwuchukwu et al. 2025). Additionally, the importance of multidisciplinary use of the eDASA+APP‐FI and information that the system provides was seen as important. Similar findings of the need for multidisciplinary use of the eDASA+APP have been raised by the original developers of the eDASA+APP (Simmons et al. 2025).

It is important to note that the eDASA+APP‐FI was considered easy to use, even in the unit with older adults, but feedback sessions after the feasibility study revealed problems with the use of the eDASA with this patient group. Participants thought that the items of the eDASA did not capture the unique features of aggression risk among older adults, many of whom had an additional neurological diagnosis. We believe that this has two important implications. First, collecting different types of data, as suggested by Bowen et al. (2009), during and after the study is essential to capture a comprehensive understanding of feasibility. During the feasibility study, all nurses used the intervention daily, but nurses thought the frequency should vary according to the acuity of patients' mental health needs. Acute unit staff suggested frequent use on all patients, whereas sub‐acute units preferred using only at patient transfer situations or in case there is a change in patients' behaviour. This suggests that, besides cultural adaptation, unit‐level adaptation is needed prior to a large‐scale implementation.

Second, there is a need to further develop aggression risk assessment instruments for specific patient groups. There is no previous knowledge of using the DASA with older adult populations. Manifestation of aggression with older people with dementia, for example, is individual and general tools developed for older adult populations may not be reliable (Iaboni et al. 2022). In addition, there may be unmet needs, triggers in the physical environment, and issues with interpersonal interactions (Roberton and Daffern 2022) that may need to be considered when assessing the risk of aggression in older adults.

Strengths and Limitations

The use of different types of data, including official patient documents, surveys and feedback sessions can be considered as a strength of this study; this made triangulation of the results possible. Additionally, use of multiple data points made it possible to assess feasibility and to identify modification needs before larger scale implementation. Another strength is the inclusion of different types of units in the feasibility testing to understand themes supporting and hindering feasibility with different groups of patients.

One limitation is the short time frame of the feasibility study. A lengthier test may have revealed additional issues related to the feasibility. The second limitation is that three of four units participated in the adaptation workshops. Whereas the co‐design framework highlights the importance of active end‐user involvement in the planning of an intervention (O'Cathain et al. 2019), it might have impacted the acceptability of the intervention as the personnel were actively involved in the planning process. We included one unit that only participated in the study. The findings in this unit were similar to other units, suggesting that the adaptations made during the co‐design process were feasible across the units. However, it is important to assess feasibility carefully during the larger implementation process to fully understand how the aDASA+APP‐FI transfers into new units.

Third limitation is the low response rate of the post survey. We received only 19 answers. All together there were 86 nurses working at day or evening shifts during the pilot. These could be seen as potential respondents, leaving the total response rate at 22%. The survey occurred at a time when many nurses were absent due to the Christmas holidays, which might have impacted the response rate. However, the use of feedback sessions and collecting feedback directly from the online training complements these results. Fourth, the conclusion of eDASA not being feasible in the older adult's unit is based on qualitative data only and needs to be confirmed with a statistical evaluation of the predictive validity.

Conclusions

Overall, the eDASA+APP‐FI was found to be feasible for use in general mental health inpatient units in Finland. APP terminology provided nurses a way to structure their work and improved nursing documentation and communication. Adaptations in the Finnish version of the APP and the training were found to be acceptable and only minor modifications were required for the EHR integration. The feedback sessions provided important suggestions for further development, including ways to increase patient participation. The eDASA was found to be unsuitable for assessing the risk of aggression among patients in the older adult wards, suggesting that more research is needed on the assessment of risk for aggressive behaviour with this population. In addition, it is important to continue to study feasibility, using different types of data collection methods during the larger scale implementation to assess the effects that the co‐design process might have had.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

Nurses in mental health units work with patients who present with severe symptoms, including a risk of aggressive behaviour. Implementing evidence‐based practices to reduce this risk involves both structured risk assessment and clear guidance on the use of preventive and management interventions. Establishing a shared terminology to organize, describe, and communicate preventive and management interventions may help nurses conceptualize and document their work more effectively. Evidence‐based clinical decision support systems may require local adaptation, as well as an understanding of the local culture and the needs of different patient groups, followed by feasibility testing before large‐scale implementation.
