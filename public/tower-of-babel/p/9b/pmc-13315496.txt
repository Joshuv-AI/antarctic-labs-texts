Pathway-level proteomics identifies subcellular trafficking dysregulation as a molecular signature in severe traumatic brain injury
Authors: Logan R. Van Nynatten, David Tweddell, Mark Daley, Gediminas Cepinskas, John Basmaji, Marat Slessarev, Douglas D. Fraser
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTraumatic brain injury (TBI) remains a leading cause of death and disability worldwide in intensive care units, yet no targeted neuroprotective therapies exist despite over 300 clinical trials. This therapeutic failure stems partly from biological heterogeneity and the limitations of single-biomarker approaches that cannot capture the multifaceted pathophysiology of secondary brain injury. Systems biology approaches examining dysregulated pathways rather than isolated proteins may reveal candidate endotypes suitable for patient stratification and identify potential therapeutic targets.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory, case–control study, in adult critically-ill patients comparing 10 severe TBI (sTBI) patients (median GCS 5.5) with 10 age-and sex-matched healthy controls. Plasma samples were collected upon admission to the ICU, within 24 h of injury. High-throughput proximity extension assays quantified 1196 plasma proteins, identifying multiple differentially expressed proteins. Gene set enrichment analysis interrogated Reactome pathways and Gene Ontology terms. Protein–protein interaction networks were constructed using the STRING database, and associations between enriched pathways and clinical variables were examined.ResultsWe identified 348 significantly differentially abundant proteins between groups. Gene set enrichment analysis revealed 19 enriched Reactome pathways and 12 Gene Ontology terms, predominantly reflecting immune activation, inflammation, cellular stress responses, and intracellular trafficking. Strikingly, membrane trafficking pathways, including clathrin-mediated endocytosis and Golgi transport, emerged as significantly enriched, representing an underrecognized mechanism in sTBI pathophysiology. Protein–protein interaction analysis identified four functional clusters, with IL6 and IL10 as densely connected hub proteins coordinating inflammatory responses. Membrane trafficking pathways were associated with clinical outcomes including both length of hospital and intensive care unit stay, as well as need for neurosurgical intervention, in sTBI patients.ConclusionsBy profiling the expression of 1196 plasma proteins in parallel, this study provides one of the most comprehensive proteomic characterizations of critically-ill adults with severe traumatic brain injury to date. Systems biology analyses suggest that sTBI may trigger coordinated pathway-level dysregulation extending beyond inflammation to include subcellular trafficking machinery. The association between membrane trafficking pathways and clinical outcomes suggests these may represent a candidate molecular endotype with prognostic relevance. These hypothesis-generating findings support pathway-based approaches for patient stratification and therapeutic targeting in sTBI, though validation in larger cohorts is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00011-026-02287-6.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI), damage to the brain caused by external physical force or impact to the head, continues to harm millions of individuals annually worldwide. TBI accounts for approximately 20% of trauma patients admitted to the intensive care unit, disproportionately conferring morbidity and mortality upon young adults [1]. Despite advances in prehospital [2], medical [3] and surgical management [4] of TBI, numerous patients experience reduced life expectancy, seizures and sleep disorders, neurodegenerative diseases, neuroendocrine disruption and psychiatric comorbidities as a consequence of their injury [5]. Yet, despite decades of research including over 300 clinical trials, no targeted pharmacologic neuroprotective therapies exist to mitigate these devastating consequences [6]. These failed trials are due to the heterogeneity of treatment effect (HTE), a phenomenon in which patients with similar conditions respond differently to the same intervention [7, 8]. When studies enroll patients based only on clinical diagnoses, they mix subgroups who may benefit from treatment with those who won’t benefit or could be harmed. This heterogeneity weakens the treatment effect and can produce statistically insignificant results, even when certain patient subgroups experience substantial benefit.

The failure of neuroprotective drug trials in TBI has been largely attributed to this biological heterogeneity and to the limitations of single-target therapeutic approaches that do not address the multifaceted nature of secondary brain injury. Numerous Phase III trials targeting single pathophysiological mechanisms (including NMDA receptor antagonists, calcium channel blockers, free radical scavengers, and anti-inflammatory agents) have consistently failed to show clinical efficacy despite promising preclinical results [13, 14]. This same limitation is reflected in current biomarker approaches. Blood-based biomarkers such as glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ubiquitin C-terminal hydrolase L1 (UCH-L1), and MAP-2 have been shown to associate with TBI, intracranial lesions on computed tomography (CT), and with the need for neurosurgical intervention [9–11]. However, when considered in isolation, these biomarkers mirror the shortcomings of single-target therapeutics: they capture only narrow aspects of a highly complex and dynamic disease process. Furthermore, biomarkers alone cannot capture the complex, heterogeneous pathophysiology of TBI, which encompasses multiple interacting cascades involving neuroinflammation, excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, blood–brain barrier disruption, and neuronal death [12].

Systems biology approaches offer a paradigm shift by moving beyond isolated protein markers to examine disease-relevant converging pathways as potential therapeutic intervention points, accompanied by downstream biomarkers that enable tracking of drug targeting and correlation with disease mitigation [13, 14]. High-throughput omic platforms coupled with bioinformatic analyses enable identification of brain injury pathways and molecular signatures conserved across different injury contexts, thereby revealing both diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets that reflect the true complexity of TBI pathobiology [15]. Recent expert consensus emphasizes that blood-based biomarker integration into TBI classification systems has the potential to enable a more nuanced approach to patient triage, diagnosis, prognosis, and treatment selection [16]. However, research should focus on characterizing multi-omic pathway-based biomarkers that capture dysregulated biological processes rather than on isolated molecular markers at the transcriptomic, proteomic, or metabolomic levels.

We conducted an exploratory, hypothesis-generating, case–control cohort study, employing an integrative omics strategy combining high-throughput proteomic profiling with comprehensive pathway analysis to systematically characterize the dysregulated pathobiology in sTBI. Given the expense and complexity of high-throughput proteomic assays, early-phase exploratory studies in smaller cohorts play a critical role in identifying high-yield proteins and pathways with large effect sizes, efficiently narrowing the biological search space before investment in larger, resource-intensive validation studies. Our aims were to: (1) use high-throughput proximity extension assays (PEA) to measure the circulating plasma proteome of patients with sTBI and in healthy control subjects; (2) identify proteins distinguishing patients with sTBI from healthy controls; and (3) use pathway enrichment analyses to characterize which cellular pathways are dysregulated in sTBI. By profiling nearly 1200 plasma proteins in parallel, this study represents the most comprehensive proteomic characterization of severe traumatic brain injury to date, enabling pathway-level insights that would be invisible to traditional biomarker approaches. Moreover, by interrogating biological networks rather than only individual proteins, we aimed to assess the feasibility of identifying dysregulated pathways that could serve as candidate molecular endotypes for patient stratification and reveal actionable therapeutic targets for precision medicine approaches in TBI management.

Methods

Study participants and cohort data

This study was reviewed and approved by the Western University, Human Research Ethics Board (HREB #6970). A total of 20 age- and sex- matched subjects were enrolled: 10 sTBI patients and 10 healthy control subjects, similar to prior sTBI cohorts [17–19]. The healthy control subjects without disease were identified from the Translational Research Centre, London, Ontario (HREB #6963); (Directed by Dr. D.Fraser; https://translationalresearchcentre.com/).

Comprehensive demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected for all study participants. All plasma samples were collected upon admission to the ICU, within 24 h of presentation with neurological injury, and prior to any neurosurgical intervention. The four patients who underwent neurosurgical intervention did so following sample collection. None of the enrolled sTBI patients were receiving anticoagulant or immunosuppressive therapy at the time of enrollment, and no patients had pre-existing comorbidities associated with chronic immunosuppression. Inclusion criteria required isolated head injury, with no extracranial trauma identified on primary or secondary survey.

For sTBI patients, demographic variables included age, sex, and pre-existing comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, coronary artery disease, congestive heart failure, chronic kidney disease, obstructive lung disease, malignancy, and neurologic disease), and mechanism of injury. Admission laboratory values were recorded, including complete blood count (leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, hemoglobin, thrombocytes), serum creatinine, coagulation parameters (international normalized ratio [INR], partial thromboplastin time [PTT]), and lactate. Established TBI biomarkers neuron-specific enolase (NSE) and S100 calcium binding protein B (S100B) were measured in both TBI patients and healthy controls (enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays, R&D Systems; Minneapolis, MN, USA). NSE and S100B were previously validated to identify patients with sTBI [17].

Injury severity was assessed using the initial Glasgow Coma Score (GCS) at presentation. Neuroimaging findings from computed tomography (CT) were systematically reviewed and documented, including presence of subarachnoid hemorrhage, subdural hematoma, epidural hematoma, interventricular hemorrhage, intraparenchymal hemorrhage, diffuse axonal injury, midline shift, tonsillar herniation, cerebral contusion, cerebral edema, basal skull fracture, scalp laceration, hydrocephalus, pneumocephalus, and subgaleal hematoma. Clinical interventions were recorded, including medical management, intracranial pressure (ICP) monitoring, and neurosurgical intervention. Outcome variables included ICU length of stay (days), hospital length of stay (days), and in-hospital mortality.

Blood processing

Standard phlebotomy procedures were used to collect blood. Samples were obtained from critically-ill sTBI patients via indwelling venous catheters and immediately placed on ice. Maximum volumes were not exceeded per standard phlebotomy protocols. Following collection, samples were transferred to a negative pressure hood; after centrifugation, isolated plasma was aliquoted in 250 µL increments and frozen at − 80 °C. All samples remained frozen until use to avoid repeated freeze–thaw cycles.

Proximity extension analysis

Plasma samples were thawed and analyzed using proximity extension assay (PEA; O-link Proteomics, Sweden) as previously described [20, 21]. We profiled 1,196 plasma proteins in critically-ill patients with sTBI and in age- and sex-matched healthy controls. Each protein was detected using a pair of antibodies, each coupled to a unique, partially complementary DNA oligonucleotide. The assay proceeded in three steps: (1) antibody–oligonucleotide probes bound to their target antigens; (2) oligonucleotides brought into proximity hybridized and were extended by a DNA polymerase, forming a unique DNA barcode; and (3) this barcode was amplified and quantified by real-time PCR.

Quality control included assessment of immunoassay performance, detection efficiency, and hemolysis. Intra-assay variability was minimized by robotic pipetting and normalization with three internal controls monitoring incubation, extension, and amplification. Each plate also contained external negative controls and plate controls in triplicate to ensure inter-assay precision.

Below-limit-of-detection (LOD) values were handled using Olink’s standard preprocessing pipeline, which imputes LOD values at LOD/√2 and flags them in the output. Proteins with more than 25% of samples below LOD were excluded prior to analysis. No proteins retained in the final analytic dataset had substantial missing values following Olink quality control, and no further imputation was applied. Protein abundance was expressed as normalized protein expression (NPX) values, which are reported on a log2 scale. NPX values were rank-based inverse normal transformed (RINT) prior to differential expression analyses.

Metadata analysis and quality control

Metadata for 20 samples (10 sTBI, 10 healthy controls) included patient ID, group, age, sex, outcome, initial Glasgow Coma Score (GCS), mechanism of injury, and additional clinical variables (reported above). Correlating factors were assessed using pairwise univariate association tests between all variable combinations at an FDR-adjusted significance threshold of P < 0.05. Statistical tests were selected based on data types: Spearman rank correlation for continuous-continuous associations, and appropriate tests for categorical-continuous associations. Resulting P values were transformed as – log10(P) for heatmap visualization.

Proteomic data quality was assessed using four objective criteria: (i) total Euclidean distance to all other samples, (ii) Kolmogorov–Smirnov test statistic, (iii) mean Pearson correlation with other samples, and (iv) Hoeffding’s D statistic. Principal component analysis and kernel density estimates were conducted. Samples failing quality control were excluded from downstream analyses.

Conventional statistics

Cohort demographic and clinical characteristics were collected. Continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), and categorical variables as counts with percentages. Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using Fisher’s exact tests due to the limited sample size. Continuous variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U tests. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism (Version 8.4.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA).

Normalized PEA proteomic data was used to identify proteins that differ significantly between sTBI patients and healthy control subjects. Statistical comparisons were conducted using empirical Bayes moderated t-tests. A statistical threshold of false discovery rate (FDR)-adjusted P < 0.05 was used to define significantly differentially abundant proteins. Volcano plots were generated to visualize these fold changes, whereby a positive log2 (fold change) ≥ 2 was selected to indicate up-regulation in sTBI patients relative to healthy controls, and a negative value indicated the downregulation in sTBI patients relative to controls. Heatmaps showing between-sample pairwise Pearson correlations of normalized data were generated, with Euclidean distance used for clustering.

Gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the Reactome database (package: reactome.db) was implemented using the fgsea R package to determine the cellular pathways that differed between healthy controls and patients with sTBI, based on the proteins measured. Reactome is based on the hierarchical grouping of known reactions (bindings, reactions, modifications, etc.) between proteins. Each analysis was performed by taking a list of proteins, ranked according to a statistic. GSEA evaluates whether proteins involved in each pathway of interest tend to be over-represented at the extremes of the ranked statistic. The minimum gene set size tested was 15, with all pathways below this threshold excluded. The maximum gene set size tested was 500, with all pathways above this threshold excluded. The statistical significance (nominal P value) of the over-representation was evaluated using a method based on an adaptive multi-level split Monte Carlo scheme. For each term tested, the enrichment score (ES), normalized enrichment score (NES), p value and FDR-adjusted p value are reported. A normalized enrichment score (NES) > 0 indicates that the set of pathway-specific proteins is over-represented among up-regulated proteins, while a NES < 0 suggests gene set enrichment among down-regulated proteins.

This analysis was repeated with Gene Ontology (GO) terms (package: GO.db) to identify the biological processes, molecular functions and cellular components that differed between healthy controls and patients with sTBI. Gene Ontologies formally relate biological concepts within three major categories (molecular function, biological processes, and cellular components). Molecular function relates to the molecular-level activities performed by gene products. Biological processes refer to ‘biological programs’ that link multiple molecular activities. Cellular components represent the cellular structure or location in which a gene product functions. Pathways and terms with FDR-adjusted p value < 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Protein–protein interactions

To identify functional relationships and potential regulatory hubs among differentially expressed proteins, protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed using the Search Tool for the Retrieval of Interacting Genes/Proteins (STRING) database (version 12.0, https://string-db.org). The list of significantly differentially abundant proteins (FDR-adjusted P < 0.05) identified from the differential expression analysis (those proteins with adjusted P value < 0.05 and fold change > 3) was used as input for network construction. A high confidence interaction score threshold (≥ 0.7) was applied to ensure reliable interactions while maintaining network comprehensiveness. Disconnected nodes (proteins with no interactions) were excluded from the final visualization.

The resulting network was visualized with nodes representing individual proteins and edges representing interactions, with edge colors indicating the type of evidence supporting each interaction. Functional protein clusters were identified based on known biological pathways and molecular functions using k-means clustering. Hub proteins were defined as nodes with high connectivity across multiple functional clusters, indicating potential central regulatory roles in sTBI pathophysiology.

Clinical variable-pathway association analysis

Association analysis was performed to uncover links between GSEA-identified dysregulated pathways and clinical characteristics. A correlation matrix (C) of clinical variables to gene expression and a gene-pathway association matrix (G), reflecting the GSEA ranks of the leading-edge genes present in each pathway were used to compute the association matrix (A) as: A = C × GT. Here, C is an m × n matrix of m clinical variables by n genes, and G is a p × n matrix of p pathways by n genes. The elements of matrix C are the Pearson correlation coefficients for each (clinical variable, gene) pair, if the Pearson correlation is statistically significant (Pearson p < 0.05); otherwise, the matrix element for that pair is set to zero. Note that when comparing a continuous variable (e.g., gene expression values) with a binary variable (e.g., mortality) the Pearson correlation collapses to the point-biserial correlation. For matrix G, the matrix elements for each (pathway, gene) pair is the GSEA rank value for the gene if the gene is present in the pathway; otherwise, the matrix element is set to zero. In this study, 1,036 biomarkers (gene markers) were evaluated; only 622 unique leading-edge genes were associated with significant pathways for sTBI vs Healthy Control associations with clinical variables. The correlation matrix C used as input to the clinical-feature × pathway association analysis was restricted to statistically significant Pearson correlations (p < 0.05) to limit the contribution of noise correlations. The resulting association matrix A represents a weighted aggregate association score rather than a formal hypothesis test, and therefore does not yield corrected p values in the conventional sense; these associations are interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

sTBI patients exhibited significantly elevated known brain injury biomarkers compared to controls: median NSE was 24.1 ng/mL (IQR: 21.8–28.9) versus 10.5 ng/mL (IQR: 9.3–12.7), P < 0.001; median S100B was 0.19 ng/mL (IQR: 0.14–0.37) versus 0.04 ng/mL (IQR: 0.02–0.05), P < 0.001. These established biomarkers of neuronal injury (NSE) and astrocytic damage (S100B) confirm the biological validity of TBI in our cohort and support confidence that downstream proteomic findings reflect genuine brain injury pathophysiology rather than artifacts.

The cohort demonstrated severe brain injury, with median initial GCS of 5.5 (IQR: 4.0–7.0). All patients received ICU-level medical management including mechanical ventilation with hyperventilation, head of the bed elevation, and administration of hypertonic saline, in addition to routine ICU best practices. 40% of patients received ICP monitoring and 40% required neurosurgical intervention with decompressive craniectomies. Median ICU length of stay was 6.5 days (IQR: 5.0–15.0), median hospital length of stay was 17.5 days (IQR: 7.0–28.0), and ICU mortality was 30%.

Motor vehicle collisions were the predominant mechanism of injury (60%), followed by falls from height (30%) and assault (10%) (Table 2). Neuroimaging revealed diverse intracranial pathology, with cerebral contusion most prevalent (80%), followed by subarachnoid hemorrhage (60%), diffuse axonal injury (60%), and basal skull fracture (60%). Subdural hematoma occurred in 40% of patients. Many patients exhibited multiple concurrent injury types, reflecting the heterogeneous nature of severe sTBI (Table 2). All neuroimaging was performed within 24 h of injury at the time of presentation and was interpreted by trained clinical neuroradiologists.

Clinical and proteomic data distribution

Comprehensive metadata analysis was conducted on 20 samples (10 sTBI patients, 10 healthy controls) to assess potential correlating factors and data quality. Pairwise univariate association tests across all clinical, demographic, and laboratory variables revealed minimal correlations (Fig. 1 A, B). This metadata correlation analysis presents pairwise univariate associations across all clinical, demographic, and laboratory variables in all 20 samples (10 sTBI, 10 healthy controls), intended to characterize within- and between-group variable relationships. A significant association was detected between NSE and S100B, two established TBI biomarkers. Principal component analysis revealed distinct clustering patterns between groups (Fig. 1C). Healthy control samples (blue circles) clustered tightly, indicating consistent proteomic profiles, while sTBI samples (green squares) showed greater dispersion, reflecting increased heterogeneity in the proteomic response to injury. The correlation heatmap showed high intra-group correlations for healthy controls, and moderate correlations among sTBI patients, with lower inter-group correlations reflecting biological differences between groups (Fig. 1D). Hierarchical clustering demonstrated clear group separation.

Differential protein expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed on 1,103 unique protein identifiers to characterize proteins with significantly altered abundance between traumatic brain injury (sTBI) patients and healthy controls. 348 proteins were identified as differentially abundant between the two groups (adjusted FDR P value < 0.05, Supplemental Table 1).

The analysis results are summarized in Fig. 2A, which presents the top 50 differentially expressed proteins ranked by adjusted P value. Fold change values ranged from − 4.14 to 66.397 among the significant proteins. The most statistically significant upregulated proteins included IL6, TNFSF14, NEFL, HGF, and TNFRSF6B. The most statistically significant downregulated proteins included TNFSF11, CRH and SERPINA5. Differential expression was visualized using a volcano plot illustrating the relationship between fold change and statistical significance for all analyzed proteins (Fig. 2B). To visualize the proteomic signatures of sTBI patients compared to healthy controls, proteomic data were visualized with a heatmap using hierarchical clustering of protein expression patterns across samples (Fig. 2C). The heatmap shows distinct clustering of sTBI samples and control samples based on the abundance profiles of differentially expressed proteins, with clear visual separation between the two groups.

Pathway enrichment analysis

To gain insight into the biological pathways and cellular processes affected in traumatic brain injury, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) on the complete ranked list of differentially abundant proteins, comparing sTBI patients to healthy controls. This approach examined whether proteins belonging to specific Reactome pathways or Gene Ontology (GO) terms were disproportionately represented among up- or down-regulated proteins, independent of arbitrary significance thresholds.

We identified 19 significantly enriched Reactome pathways (Table 3). The most prominently enriched pathways were predominantly associated with immune system activation and inflammatory responses (Table 3, Fig. 3A), including “Immune System”, “Innate Immune System”, “Cytokine Signaling in Immune system”, and “Signaling by Interleukins”. All immune-related pathways were over-represented among up-regulated proteins. Visualization of the enriched Reactome pathways revealed that immune-related pathways clustered at higher significance levels with moderate to high enrichment scores (Fig. 3A) with the “Immune System” pathway displaying both the largest gene set size and highest statistical significance.

Protein–protein interactions

To explore functional relationships among differentially expressed proteins, we constructed a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network using the STRING database (Fig. 4). We examined protein–protein interactions among those proteins with at least a threefold change in plasma level (with an adjusted p value < 0.05) in sTBI patients compared to healthy controls to characterize networks of dysregulation among those proteins that were most differentially expressed in patients with sTBI. STRING analysis identified four distinct clusters (Fig. 4) amongst these proteins: a cytokine and inflammation cluster (red), a cluster consisting of intermediate filaments and DNA binding proteins (green) with markers consistent with neuronal injury, a carboxypeptidase and serine protease cluster (yellow) consistent with stress responses in the context of sTBI, and a myoglobin/troponin cluster (blue) possibly consistent with cardiac dysfunction and polytrauma in the context of sTBI.

Enrichment pathway and clinical variable associations

To explore the clinical relevance of dysregulated pathways, we examined associations between significantly enriched Reactome pathways and clinical variables (Fig. 5). Membrane trafficking pathways demonstrated notable associations with clinical outcomes. “Asparagine N-linked Glycosylation” and “Transport to the Golgi and Subsequent Modification” were positively associated with ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, and requirement for neurosurgical intervention. Similarly, “Clathrin-mediated Endocytosis” and “Cargo Recognition for Clathrin-mediated Endocytosis” showed positive associations with hydrocephalus, the established TBI biomarker S100B, and neurosurgical intervention. Bipartite network visualization highlighted these membrane trafficking pathways as hubs connecting multiple clinical variables (Fig. 6). Given the small subgroup sizes underlying certain categorical clinical variables, the corresponding pathway associations should be considered exploratory and interpreted accordingly. These exploratory findings generate the hypothesis that subcellular trafficking dysregulation may have prognostic relevance in sTBI, though validation in larger cohorts is required.

Discussion

This exploratory, hypothesis-generating study combined high-throughput PEA with pathway enrichment analyses to systematically characterize dysregulated pathobiology in severe traumatic brain injury. The scale of this analysis, quantifying over a thousand circulating proteins in a single PEA assay, allowed interrogation of biological systems at a depth not achievable with conventional targeted biomarker strategies. Although limited by sample size, this design is intentional: in high-dimensional, costly proteomic analyses, signals that emerge with large effect sizes despite small cohort sizes are more likely to reflect strong, biologically coherent differences, making them particularly well suited for prioritization and independent validation by future investigators. We identified 348 differentially abundant proteins in sTBI versus controls. Gene set enrichment analysis found 19 enriched Reactome pathways and 12 Gene Ontology terms, mainly reflecting immune activation, membrane trafficking dysregulation, cellular stress responses, and intracellular compartmentalization changes. Protein–protein interaction analysis showed dense cytokine-centered connectivity, consistent with coordinated multi-system responses. sTBI also showed marked enrichment of clathrin-mediated endocytosis and Golgi transport pathways, suggesting disrupted subcellular trafficking after injury as an underrecognized mechanism and candidate molecular endotype and therapeutic target.

Despite the small sample size, our cohort is consistent with severe TBI epidemiology [1, 22], with a median age of 35.5 years, 70% male predominance, and motor vehicle collisions as the main injury mechanism. A median Glasgow Coma Score of 5.5 and 30% ICU mortality are comparable to recent severe TBI cohorts [23–26] and expected mortality ranges (20–40%) for intensive care [26]. Heterogeneous intracranial pathology, including cerebral contusion (80%), subarachnoid hemorrhage (60%), diffuse axonal injury (60%), and subdural hematoma (40%), reflects the multifactorial nature of severe TBI. Elevated NSE and S100B confirm brain injury pathophysiology and support downstream proteomic findings as TBI-specific rather than technical artifacts.

The most significantly differentially expressed proteins indicated inflammatory activation and neuronal injury. IL6 showed a 38.6-fold elevation, the highest upregulated protein in our sTBI cohort, aligning with extensive evidence for its central role in TBI neuroinflammation and its correlation with injury severity and clinical outcomes [27, 28]. Co-upregulation of TNFSF14, HGF, TNFRSF6B, IL10, CCL20, CSF3, and OSM suggests coordinated cytokine responses [29, 30]. Increased NEFL directly reflects axonal injury and is consistent with associations between serum NEFL and TBI severity, brain atrophy, and long-term cognitive outcomes [31, 32]. Upregulation of the cardiac biomarker TNNI3 may reflect polytrauma or neurogenic stress cardiomyopathy due to catecholamine surge [33]. TNFSF14 is a costimulatory molecule for T-cell activation, promoting T-cell proliferation and IFNγ production. In aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage, higher serum TNFSF14 is associated with increased survival [33], whereas in ischemic stroke, elevated TNFSF14 is linked to unfavorable functional outcomes [34]. In the context of TBI, circulating TNFSF14 is predominantly derived from the peripheral immune response, expressed on activated T cells, NK cells, monocytes, and granulocytes as a membrane-bound protein that circulates following metalloprotease cleavage [33, 35]. While CNS transcripts are detectable, parenchymal TNFSF14 expression in brain injury contexts primarily reflects infiltrating immune cells rather than resident microglia or neurons [36]. HGF is a pleiotropic, mesenchyme-derived cytokine originally identified as a hepatocyte mitogen and epithelial motility factor; in the CNS it acts as a neurotrophic and neuroregenerative factor [37]. HGF and its receptor c-Met are expressed by neurons and astrocytes in the brain under normal conditions, with both further upregulated by reactive astrocytes and microglia following CNS injury [38, 39]. Consistent with a CNS origin, HGF mRNA has been shown to increase in both neurons and microglia following experimental TBI, and HGF protein is markedly elevated in the CSF of human TBI patients within hours of injury [40].

Downregulated proteins such as TNFSF11, CRH, and SERPINA5 were less frequent, suggesting that sTBI pathophysiology is driven more by activation of inflammatory and stress pathways than by suppression of cellular processes. TNFSF11 (RANKL), a cytokine involved in osteoclast regulation, also modulates T-cell–dendritic cell interactions and chemokine production in the CNS; RANKL inhibition prevents disease in experimental models [41]. Although TNFSF11 has not been specifically studied in TBI, CRH (the central regulator of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis) drives the stress response via ACTH and cortisol release. TBI often disrupts the HPA axis, with altered cortisol rhythms and corticotropin deficiency in 15–24% of patients; in rats, CRH expression rises rapidly after injury, and CRH receptor antagonists reduce cortical damage by 45%, supporting a role in TBI pathogenesis [42, 43]. SERPINA5 (protein C inhibitor) regulates hemostasis by inhibiting activated protein C, thrombin, factor Xa, and plasminogen activators. Because TBI-related hypoperfusion can activate the protein C pathway [44], SERPINA5 downregulation may contribute to the coagulopathy commonly seen after severe TBI [44].

Beyond these individual markers, pathway enrichment analysis highlighted the broader biological architecture of sTBI pathophysiology. Pathway enrichment analysis revealed immune and inflammatory activation as the dominant biological signature in sTBI. The most enriched Reactome pathways, including “Immune System”, “Innate Immune System”, “Cytokine Signaling”, and “Signaling by Interleukins”, all showed over-representation among upregulated proteins, which may reflect both CNS inflammatory responses and systemic trauma cascades. This aligns with established TBI pathophysiology where neuroinflammation drives secondary brain injury through microglial activation, blood–brain barrier disruption, and peripheral immune cell infiltration [12, 45]. The prominence of immune pathway enrichment underscores that inflammation is a fundamental pathway implicated in sTBI pathophysiology. Moreover, specific pathway enrichment for “Interleukin-4/13 signaling” and “Neutrophil degranulation” may suggest dysregulation of both adaptive and innate immunity [46]. Enrichment of “Post-translational protein modification” and “Metabolism of proteins” may reflect the cellular proteostasis response to TBI-induced stress, wherein proteins undergo aberrant modifications due to oxidative stress and excitotoxicity while cells concurrently activate quality control mechanisms to degrade damaged proteins [47–49]. “Platelet activation, signaling and aggregation” pathway enrichment aligns with the well-documented coagulopathy observed in 10–35% of TBI patients, where tissue factor release and thrombin generation activate platelets, contributing to both hemorrhage progression and microvascular thrombosis [50].

A striking pattern emerged in the enrichment of multiple membrane trafficking pathways, including “Clathrin-mediated endocytosis”, “Cargo recognition for clathrin-mediated endocytosis”, and “Transport to the Golgi and subsequent modification”. Moreover, these dysregulated subcellular trafficking pathways had multiple significant associations with clinical outcomes in sTBI patients, including hospital or ICU length of stay and need for neurosurgical intervention. This coordinated upregulation of vesicular transport machinery could be accounted for by multiple pathophysiological processes in sTBI: disrupted synaptic vesicle cycling and neurotransmitter release following neuronal injury, increased endocytosis of damaged membrane components for degradation, enhanced secretion of inflammatory mediators by activated immune cells, and blood–brain barrier dysfunction with altered transcytosis [51, 52]. Yet this biology remains poorly characterized as a contributor to secondary injury, and largely only investigated in experimental models. It must be acknowledged that these membrane trafficking pathways are not neuron-specific, and plasma proteomics cannot attribute this signal to a particular tissue source; however, the exclusion of extracranial injuries in this cohort lends some biological specificity toward a CNS-driven process.

Early ultrastructural studies demonstrated Golgi and smooth ER dilation in injured neurons [53]. Human cortical specimens with neuronal edema show hypertrophic, vacuolized, and fenestrated Golgi complexes, indicating structural disruption of secretory organelles in vivo [54]. Complementary mechanistic work suggests perturbed vesicle docking and membrane trafficking after repetitive mild TBI [55], while axonal transport failure and ER-stress activation provide convergent evidence that neuronal proteostasis and vesicle transport pathways are strained after injury [56, 57]. Taken together, these observations raise the possibility that patients who exhibit prominent membrane-trafficking dysregulation may represent a biologically coherent and currently unrecognized endotype of sTBI, offering a plausible target for future predictive-enrichment strategies. Indeed, there are ongoing efforts to develop therapeutics that target subcellular trafficking, especially in neurologic diseases [58], which could be investigated in the context of traumatic brain injury.

The protein–protein interaction network revealed four functionally distinct yet interconnected protein clusters reflecting coordinated pathophysiological responses in sTBI. The inflammation and cytokines cluster, anchored by IL6 and IL10, reinforces the central role of neuroinflammation. IL6 (the most upregulated protein in our cohort) acts as a master regulator of inflammatory cascades with dual roles in neuroinflammatory damage and tissue repair. Co-clustering of HGF and EGF with pro-inflammatory cytokines suggests coupling of inflammatory and neurotrophic responses, reflecting compensatory repair mechanisms activated alongside tissue damage [37, 39]. HGF reduces brain inflammation, limits immune cell infiltration, promotes angiogenesis, and reduces scar formation in neurological injury and neurodegeneration [39]. The neural cluster (VIM, NEFL, CHI3L1) likely reflects proteins released from damaged neural tissue: NEFL is an established marker of axonal injury injury [31, 32], while VIM and CHI3L1 reflect astrocytic and glial responses [59–61]. The cardiac cluster (TNNI3, MB) likely reflects neurogenic stress cardiomyopathy from catecholamine surge [62]. A distinct stress response cluster containing pancreatic enzymes SPINK1, CTRC, and CPA2 was unexpected and may indicate systemic polytrauma or stress-induced pancreatic dysfunction, consistent with reports of elevated pancreatic proteins after blunt trauma [63]. SPINK1, a serine protease inhibitor, and CTRC, a serine protease, have not been directly linked to neuronal disease, but growing evidence implicates serine proteases in neuronal development, plasticity, and neurodegeneration [64, 65].

The extensive inter-cluster connectivity illustrates why single-target therapeutic approaches have consistently failed in TBI: disrupting one node in a highly connected network is insufficient when redundant pathways maintain pathological signaling. For example, corticosteroids were used in TBI for over 30 years, yet multiple trials showed no benefit [66–69], and the CRASH trial (stopped early when methylprednisolone significantly increased mortality in 10,008 patients) established that steroids should not be used routinely in TBI, becoming the only Level 1 recommendation in Brain Trauma Foundation guidelines [3, 70]. This failure reflects the dual roles of inflammatory mediators: the same cytokines can promote neuronal death or survival depending on timing and context. IL6, for example, exacerbates secondary injury through pro-inflammatory signaling while simultaneously supporting tissue repair [28, 30]. Corticosteroid trials likely failed by indiscriminately suppressing both pathological and reparative inflammatory responses. A pathway-based approach offers a fundamentally different perspective: rather than globally suppressing inflammation, systems biology can identify specific dysregulated nodes (such as the densely connected IL6 and IL10 hubs) where precise intervention might modulate pathological signaling while preserving reparative functions, with cascading effects across downstream pathways. Critically, pathway activation patterns could enable stratification into molecular endotypes based on network dysregulation rather than individual biomarker levels, ensuring that future therapeutic strategies are applied to the patients most likely to benefit.

Our study has several notable advantages, such as the application of high-throughput proteomics to measure 1,196 proteins, paired with thorough pathway and network analyses for a systematic exploration of sTBI pathobiology. By combining differential protein expression, pathway enrichment evaluations, and analyses of protein–protein interaction networks, we gained complementary insights that highlighted not only specific biomarkers but also disrupted pathways and central hub proteins which may serve as therapeutic targets. Our study sample, marked by severe injuries (median GCS 5.5) and varied intracranial pathologies, parallels populations involved in recent neuroprotective clinical trials, thereby increasing the study’s translational significance. Validation utilizing established biomarkers NSE and S100B supported the detection of genuine brain injury rather than technical errors.

Nevertheless, this study has important limitations. The limited sample size may have reduced statistical confidence and broader applicability, rendering these findings preliminary and more suited for hypothesis generation than for definitive conclusions. The single time-point, cross-sectional design precludes capturing the temporal changes in inflammation or distinguishing between early damaging and later reparative processes. We are unable to determine whether the pathway alterations identified are specific to the CNS or instead reflect systemic responses to trauma, and we also cannot infer causal links to clinical outcomes. In addition, plasma is a peripheral biofluid, and the PEA panel was intended to provide broad proteomic coverage rather than focus on neuro-specific markers; therefore, although blood–brain barrier disruption after TBI can allow brain-derived proteins to enter the circulation, it remains unclear how accurately these circulating signals mirror molecular pathology within the brain parenchyma. As a result, these plasma proteomic data cannot distinguish neuroinflammation from systemic inflammatory activity, meaning that the observed cytokine increases probably represent a combined signal; however, excluding extracranial injury improves specificity for brain-related processes. Future studies that include paired CSF and plasma analyses may better clarify the relative contributions of central and systemic inflammation. Certainly, the lack of a separate validation cohort and absence of mechanistic studies mean that the identified central proteins and pathways await further validation before clinical translation. Moving forward, studies with larger cohorts, multiple time-point sampling, and experimental approaches will be necessary to confirm these pathway enrichment findings and advance precision medicine in TBI. Additionally, future studies should include clinical pupillary responses, hypotension, and hypoxia on presentation, to enable more comprehensive characterization of injury severity and its relationship to molecular signatures. Nevertheless, these findings demonstrate the potential of integrative pathway-based approaches to reveal coordinated biological responses in TBI that transcend individual biomarkers, providing a conceptual framework and candidate molecular signatures to guide future mechanistic and translational investigations.

Conclusions

This exploratory proteomic study demonstrates that severe TBI triggers coordinated pathway-level dysregulation extending beyond isolated biomarker alterations, with 348 differentially abundant proteins converging on pathways reflecting immune activation, cellular stress responses, and subcellular membrane trafficking. Rather than interrogating isolated biomarkers, this study leveraged high-throughput proteomics to survey the circulating proteome at unprecedented scale, capturing nearly 1,200 proteins and enabling a systems-level view of the molecular derangements that accompany severe traumatic brain injury. The emergence of membrane trafficking pathways (clathrin-mediated endocytosis and Golgi transport) as significantly enriched and associated with clinical outcomes represents an underrecognized mechanism that may constitute a candidate molecular endotype in TBI. The extensive interconnectivity observed in protein–protein interaction networks, with IL6 and IL10 as central hub proteins, may partly explain the consistent failure of single-target therapeutic approaches in TBI. While these findings are hypothesis-generating given the small sample size and single time-point design, they warrant validation larger cohorts and may support a paradigm shift from isolated biomarkers toward pathway-based characterization of TBI pathobiology, offering a framework for patient stratification and therapeutic targeting in a disease where progress has remained elusive for decades.
