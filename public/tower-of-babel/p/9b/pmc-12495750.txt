Dual modulation of lipid and glucose metabolism by a nutraceutical combination in patients at cardiometabolic risk: results from a multicenter randomized controlled trial
Authors: Daniela Pacella, Maria Lembo, Valentina Trimarco, Alessia Izzo, Maria Virginia Manzi, Stanislovas S Jankauskas, Imma Forzano, Paola Gallo, Alessandra Spinelli, Carmine Morisco, Stefano Cristiano, Paola Ida Rozza, Eliana Esposito, Felicia Rozza, Lorena Esposito, Sun King B Bear, Giuseppe Giugliano, Gaetano Santulli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent evidence suggests that inhibiting 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), a key enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis, has beneficial effects on lipid metabolism and blood pressure (BP), but detrimental consequences on glycemia. Nutraceuticals (NUTs) containing both Monacolin K (MK) and Morus alba have been shown to be more effective in lowering lipids compared to NUT formulations containing only MK. However, the effects of these NUTs on glucose homeostasis have not been fully determined.MethodsTo evaluate the association between LDL-C-lowering therapy and glycemia in patients receiving NUT combinations with or without Morus alba, we analyzed data from a prospective, randomized, active-treatment controlled trial (NCT02898805), which enrolled 359 patients to compare the effects of a NUT combination containing MK alone (Formulation 1, F1; n = 170) versus one containing MK and Morus alba (Formulation 1, F2; n = 189).ResultsParticipants in the two treatment arms (F1 vs. F2) were comparable in terms of sex, age, metabolic parameters, and BP. After 3 months, both groups experienced significant reductions in LDL-C, fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, and HOMA index. F2 treatment led to a significantly greater reduction in glycemic levels compared to F1 treatment (b = − 16, p < 0.001). Notably, a divergent trend emerged over time: an inverse relationship between LDL-C and glycemic levels was observed in the F1 group, while a significant direct association between LDL-C and glycemic levels was detected in the F2 group (b = 0.06, p = 0.002).ConclusionsTaken together, our findings indicate that the treatment with a NUT combination containing Morus alba simultaneously reduces plasma levels of LDL-C and glucose.Keywords: Nutraceuticals, LDL- cholesterol, Glycemia, Cardiovascular risk, Insulin resistance, Hypertension

Introduction

International guidelines for the treatment of dyslipidemia recommend the use of nutraceuticals (NUTs) for patients with hypercholesterolemia who are at low or moderate cardiovascular risk [1]. This recommendation is supported by randomized trials demonstrating that certain NUT combinations can improve lipid profiles and, consequently, cardiovascular outcomes [2–5]. Among the available NUTs, the most effective in reducing plasma cholesterol concentrations appears to be Monacolin K (MK) [6, 7], the principal active compound in red yeast rice (RYR) [8]. RYR is produced by fermenting white rice with Monascus purpureus [9, 10] and functions by inhibiting 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A reductase (HMGCR), a key enzyme in cholesterol biosynthesis [11–13].

A recent Mendelian randomization study published in Hypertension [14] supports a dual role of HMGCR inhibition, consistent with findings from several observational reports [15–20]: inhibiting HMGCR has a protective action on both lipid metabolism and blood pressure (BP), but a detrimental effect on fasting plasma glucose. Specifically, genetically proxied HMGCR inhibition is negatively associated with systolic and diastolic BP, yet positively associated with fasting glucose levels [14].

Despite the known lipid-lowering efficacy of MK [6, 21], its effects on the relationships between LDL cholesterol (LDL-C), fasting glucose, and BP have not been previously investigated. Furthermore, given that a relatively low proportion of patients reach LDL-C target levels with MK alone [6], NUT combinations incorporating multiple active ingredients have been proposed to engage complementary mechanisms for lipid modulation. In this context, Morus alba extract—an agent with α-glucosidase inhibitory and antioxidant activity—has been shown to enhance the cholesterol-lowering effects of NUT combinations [22–24]. Alpha-glucosidase inhibition reduces postprandial plasma glucose and partially compensates for impaired insulin secretion by interfering with the digestion and absorption of monosaccharides [25]. Therefore, Morus alba may further influence the relationship between LDL-C levels, fasting glucose, and BP when used in combination with MK.

A single-blind, multicenter, randomized, controlled primary prevention trial (Lopiweb) tested the efficacy of a NUT combination containing MK plus Morus alba versus MK alone in 359 adult participants [22]. The primary endpoint demonstrated a significantly higher proportion of individuals reaching the LDL-C target of < 130 mg/dL in the group receiving the combination treatment compared with MK alone (72% vs. 43%, p < 0.0001). Leveraging the comprehensive dataset from this trial, the present post-hoc analysis aims to specifically examine the associations between LDL-C-lowering therapy, BP, and fasting plasma glucose.

Methods

Study design and participants

The study design, rationale, and main findings of the Lopiweb trial have been previously described [22]. Lopiweb aimed at comparing the proportion of subjects achieving normalization of LDL-C plasma levels (defined as LDL-C < 130 mg/dL) with a NUT combination containing MK alone [F1: Policosanol (10 mg), RYR (200 mg; 3 mg MK), Berberine (500 mg), Astaxanthine (0.5 mg), Folic acid (200 mcg) and Coenzyme Q10 (2 mg)] versus a combination containing both MK and Morus alba [F2: Berberine (531 mg), RYR (220 mg; 3.3 mg MK) and Morus alba (200 mg)]. The present report represents a post-hoc analysis of the Lopiweb trial, designed to specifically examining the effects of these two NUT formulations on LDL-C, BP, and fasting plasma glucose.

A total of 31 physicians (including general practitioners and specialists) participated in the trial and were instructed to enroll the first 25 consecutive adult outpatients meeting the eligibility criteria.

Inclusion criteria were: (1) written and signed informed consent; (2) age between 18 and 75 years; (3) mild hypercholesterolemia or metabolic syndrome not requiring statin therapy, or documented statin intolerance.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) use of any lipid-lowering, antihypertensive, or glucose-lowering medications; (2) pregnancy or breastfeeding; (3) a history of cardiovascular diseases such as coronary artery disease, stroke, heart failure, valvular heart disease, or peripheral artery disease; (4) any neurological or psychiatric condition that could interfere with informed consent.

Data collection was completed onsite by participating physicians and submitted to the central data collection center. All participants provided written informed consent, and no subject received any pharmacologic treatment other than the assigned NUT regimen. Although average systolic BP values were occasionally above normal thresholds, enrolled hypertensive patients were classified as having low to moderate global cardiovascular risk; according to the European Society of Hypertension/European Society of Cardiology (ESH/ESC) guidelines [26], such patients do not require pharmacological therapy prior to implementing lifestyle modifications.

Randomization

Participants who met the eligibility criteria at the screening visit entered a 4-week placebo run-in phase. All participants were prescribed a standardized diet, consisting of 1700 kcal/day for men and 1400 kcal/day for women, with a consistent polyphenol content. Only individuals who consumed at least 80% of the placebo tablets during the run-in period were eligible for randomization. These participants were then randomly assigned, in a 1:1 ratio, to receive one tablet per day of either F1 or F2, taken immediately after dinner. Randomization was conducted remotely via the Lopiweb portal by study personnel, using a computer-generated schedule, stratified by general practice, and implemented in variable-sized blocks of two and four. At the end of the run-in phase, baseline measurements were collected, including BP, heart rate, body weight, lipid and glucose profiles, plasma insulin, and HbA1c levels. During the subsequent 3-month treatment period, participants were randomized to receive F1 or F2, while maintaining their prescribed diets. Follow-up assessments were conducted to measure BP, heart rate, body weight, lipid and glucose profiles, insulin, and HbA1c. Participants were blinded to treatment allocation. All tablets—F1, F2, and placebo—were kindly provided by Akademy Pharma (Monza, Italy) for the entire duration of the study.

Measurements and definitions

Fasting plasma glucose, lipid profile, insulin, and HbA1c were measured by standard methods [4]. All lab analyses were centralized. Systolic and diastolic BP were measured by using a standard sphygmomanometer after 5 min in the supine position, according to ESH/ESC guidelines [26].

Outcomes

The primary outcome was to investigate the effects of the two NUT combinations on the relationship between LDL plasma levels and fasting blood glucose concentrations or BP. The sample size of the Lopiweb trial [22] was originally determined for the primary trial endpoint regarding normalization of plasma LDL-C levels. With 359 participants (in the main analysis cohort), our study had at least 80% power to detect difference in primary outcome between the two treatments.

Statistical analysis

Data are reported as mean (SD) or as frequencies (percentage). Adjusted linear mixed-effects regression models were used to analyze the effects of the treatment on the outcomes of interest (i.e. blood glucose and BP) over time considering time (baseline and two follow-up points) as a fixed effect and patient’s ID as a random effect. Additionally, to investigate the moderator effect of the treatment on the relationship between LDL cholesterol and the two outcomes of interests (i.e. blood glucose and BP), and to investigate whether such effect changed over time, two-way and three-way interaction terms were added to distinct models. Only significant interaction terms were then retained in the final models. Where a significant interaction term was found, subgroup analysis was conducted and linear mixed-effects regression models and linear regression models were used to investigate the effect of LDL cholesterol on the outcome separately by treatment or by treatment and time, as appropriate. All analyses were conducted using R statistical software version 4.4.0. A two-tailed p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

A total of 359 patients, with a mean age of 55 ± 11 years (58% female), were included in the analysis: 170 in the F1 group and 189 in the F2 group. Baseline clinical characteristics and metabolic profiles were comparable between the two groups. Participants were evenly matched in terms of sex, age, metabolic parameters, and BP. Table 1 presents baseline values, percent changes observed at the end of follow-up, and the results of linear mixed models for each variable assessed. Both treatment groups showed significant reductions in serum LDL-C, fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, and HOMA index compared to baseline, with greater percentage changes observed in the F2 group. For total cholesterol, LDL-C, triglycerides, fasting glucose, HbA1c, insulin, and HOMA index, statistically significant differences between treatments were found, with the F2 group exhibiting greater reductions, independent of the significant effect of time.

Discussion

This post-hoc analysis of a large randomized clinical trial in healthy adults without previous cardiovascular events yields two main key findings: first, inhibition of the HMGCR pathway by MK does not appear to adversely affect fasting glucose or BP control; second, adding Morus alba to the NUT combination containing MK is associated with a direct correlation between changes in LDL-C and fasting glucose, suggesting simultaneous improvement in lipid and glycemic profiles.

The first observation contrasts with Mendelian randomization studies and meta-analyses of statin trials, which have shown that HMGCR inhibition increases the risk of new-onset diabetes by approximately 10%, typically in individuals with borderline glycemic markers [14, 27]. However, these effects are likely dose-dependent. More intensive statin therapy has been shown to further increase diabetes risk by another 10% [28], while lower-intensity regimens or combination therapies like statins with ezetimibe appear to reduce this risk [29, 30]. Thus, MK may exert a milder inhibition of HMGCR, accounting for the absence of a glycemic penalty and the more modest LDL-C reduction observed.

Even more intriguing is the significant direct correlation between changes in LDL-C and fasting glucose in the F2 group. Both NUT combinations reduced glucose, HbA1c, insulin, and HOMA index, but only in the F1 group did the initial inverse correlation between LDL-C and glucose disappear by the end of follow-up, suggesting differing mechanisms. In the F1 group, the reduction in HOMA index correlated directly with body weight changes, pointing to weight loss–driven insulin sensitivity improvements, consistent with previous observations [31]. Potential mechanisms underlying these findings include reductions in visceral adiposity, which lower pro-inflammatory cytokines that impair insulin signaling, and improvements in lipid metabolism that decrease ectopic fat accumulation in liver and muscle, enhancing insulin receptor function [32, 33]. Weight loss may also increase adiponectin levels, a hormone that promotes glucose uptake in peripheral tissues, while both weight reduction and MK can reduce oxidative stress and support insulin signaling [34]. Additionally, changes in gut microbiota induced by weight loss and MK may produce metabolites that further enhance glucose homeostasis [35–37]. These mechanisms suggest that HOMA index improvements may result from the combined effects of direct metabolic modulation by MK and indirect benefits of weight loss.

F2 treatment led to greater reductions in LDL-C, glucose, HbA1c, and HOMA index than F1. These findings suggest that Morus alba not only potentiates lipid-lowering effects via mechanisms independent of HMGCR inhibition but also improves glycemic control. While the underlying mechanisms remain speculative, Morus alba has been reported to enhance endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) signaling and vascular tone regulation, potentially reducing insulin resistance [38–41]. This aspect is particularly relevant, as cholesterol accumulation in pancreatic β-cells may impair insulin secretion [42–44]—a mechanism supported by both murine models and genetic association studies linking LDL-C–lowering alleles at HMGCR to elevated fasting insulin and BMI [45, 46]. Bioactive compounds in Morus alba, such as flavonoids and polyphenols, can activate the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K)/Akt pathway, leading to phosphorylation and activation of eNOS, which increases nitric oxide (NO) production [47, 48]. NO, in turn, promotes vasodilation, improves blood flow, and enhances insulin delivery to peripheral tissues, thereby reducing insulin resistance. Additionally, Morus alba may reduce oxidative stress by scavenging reactive oxygen species (ROS), preventing NO degradation, and preserving endothelial function [49–55]. The extract also appears to modulate AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) signaling, which enhances glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and improves metabolic flexibility [56–60]. Together, these pathways integrate to improve endothelial health, vascular tone, and insulin sensitivity, providing a molecular basis for the metabolic benefits observed with Morus alba supplementation.

Statins are highly effective cholesterol-lowering drugs that reduce cardiovascular risk [61, 62], but in some individuals they can produce secondary effects that impact quality of life [18, 63, 64]. Common adverse effects include muscle pain or weakness (myalgia and myopathy), fatigue, gastrointestinal discomfort, and elevations of liver enzymes [65]. These side effects can lead to reduced physical activity, frustration, or anxiety about long-term therapy, sometimes causing patients to discontinue treatment despite cardiovascular benefits [66]. Additionally, statins have been associated in certain populations with a higher risk of developing diabetes, which can complicate metabolic health and further affect well-being [67, 68]. Because these effects can diminish adherence and overall quality of life, they underscore the importance of exploring alternative or complementary therapies. NUTs, modified statin formulations, or combination therapies—such as MK from RYR, or statins combined with plant extracts like Morus alba—may provide lipid-lowering benefits while potentially reducing adverse effects [69, 70]. Understanding the mechanisms behind statin intolerance, including genetic susceptibility, mitochondrial dysfunction, or interference with muscle calcium handling, allows researchers and clinicians to develop personalized approaches that preserve cardiovascular protection without compromising daily function. Educating patients about these potential side effects and ongoing research into safer alternatives is crucial. It highlights why investigating new pharmacological formulas or NUT options is not just a scientific endeavor but a practical strategy to maintain adherence, minimize discomfort, and improve overall quality of life for individuals requiring long-term lipid management.

Of note, the inclusion of Morus alba in the F2 formulation uncovered a previously unrecognized direct relationship between LDL-C reduction and fasting glucose improvement, indicating concurrent benefits on both lipid and glycemic profiles. This finding emphasizes the potential of multi-ingredient nutraceuticals to engage complementary metabolic pathways. Our results advance understanding of how these NUT combinations impact cardiometabolic health beyond cholesterol lowering, supporting the use of multi-component therapies to simultaneously modulate lipid and glucose metabolism, and offering insights that could guide future recommendations for NUT use in primary prevention and metabolic optimization. Our study is not exempt from limitations. The relatively young, low-risk population without prior cardiovascular disease limits generalizability to older or higher-risk groups. Follow-up was short, precluding conclusions about long-term risks. Outcomes were limited to surrogate markers (LDL-C, glucose, HbA1c, HOMA-IR, BP) rather than clinical endpoints. Finally, mechanistic explanations remain speculative, as no direct mechanistic data were collected.

Conclusions

Our data demonstrate that a NUT combination containing Morus alba effectively lowers both LDL-C and fasting glucose levels in healthy adults. Given potential regulatory limitations on the use of MK in dietary supplements, alternative NUT formulations, for instance Morus alba and niacin (i.e. LopiGLIK Bio), further investigation for their dual lipid and glycemic benefits.
