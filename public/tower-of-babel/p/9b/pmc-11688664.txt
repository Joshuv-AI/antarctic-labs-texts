Sodium Assists Controlled Synthesis of Cubic Rare-Earth Oxyfluorides Nanocrystals for Information Encryption and Near-Infrared-IIb Bioimaging
Authors: Qinglai Wang, Jie Hu, Yunfei Ying, Peiyuan Wang, Fulin Lin, Yongwei Guo, Yingping Huang, Kaixin Ji, Xing Yang, Siyaqi Li, Xiaolong Liu, Haomiao Zhu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Rare-earth oxyfluoride (REOF) colloidal nanocrystals (NCs) suffer from a low photoluminescence efficiency due to their small size with poor crystallinity and a detrimental surface quenching effect. Herein, we introduce an innovative approach that involves doping sodium ions into REOF NCs to produce monodisperse, size-controllable, well-crystallized, and highly luminescent colloidal REOF core/shell NCs. The Na+ doping allows for successfully synthesizing the cubic REOF NCs with a tunable size from 6 to 30 nm. Further fabrication of the core/shell NCs doped with Na+ results in enhancements up to 1062 (Ho3+), 1140 (Er3+), and 2212 (Tm3+) folds in upconversion luminescence and 17.7 folds (Er3+) in downconversion luminescence compared to that of core/shell NCs without doping Na+ ions. These NCs were subsequently developed into multicolor luminescent inks, demonstrating significant potential application for information security, and used for near-infrared-IIb (NIR-IIb) (1500–1700 nm) in vivo imaging, which exhibits a high-resolution in vivo dynamic imaging capability with a signal-to-noise ratio of 5.28. These results present the way to the controlled synthesis of efficient luminescent cubic LuOF: RE3+/LuOF core/shell NCs, expanding the toolkit of rare-earth doped NCs in diverse applications such as advanced encoding encryption, varied fluorescence imaging, and biomedicine.

Rare-earth (RE3+)-doped luminescent nanocrystals (NCs) have garnered significant interest due to their distinctive optical characteristics, including large Stokes and anti-Stokes shifts, long luminescence lifetimes, narrow band emissions, and superior photostability.1−6 A variety of host materials have been explored for RE3+ doping to facilitate efficient up-conversion (UC) and down-conversion (DC) photoluminescence (PL), such as fluorides,7,8 oxides,9 oxyfluorides,10 sulfides,11 and oxysulfides.12 Among the various substrates, rare-earth oxyfluorides (REOF) are particularly recognized for their exceptional photochemical properties. These properties are derived from fluorides or oxides and include high light transparency, substantial absorbance, UV-excited luminescence, superior luminescence efficiency, robust thermo-chemical stability, and impressive mechanical strength.13,14 Such attributes have sparked significant interest in their potential applications in fields such as optical communications, security systems, multicolor displays, microcavity lasers, nanosensors, and life science and biomedicine.15−19 Specifically, the Y7O6F9: Yb3+, Er3+ bulk phosphor has demonstrated significantly higher brightness than the benchmark β-NaYF4: Yb3+, Er3+ in UC PL.20 Moreover, when compared to the UC NaREF4 NCs,21 REOF exhibits a properly higher lattice density and phonon energy.22,23 These characteristics could potentially enhance its absorptive capacity, suppress UC PL, and boost DC PL,24,25 making it a promising candidate for second near-infrared (NIR-II) emissions (1000–1700 nm).

Despite the promising performance, colloidal REOF NCs have not yet achieved size control at the nanoscale and typically suffer from low PL efficiency due to oxygen vacancies and detrimental surface quenching effects.14,26 This limitation has significantly restricted the application of REOF NCs in advanced technologies. The proliferation of oxygen vacancies restricts the growth of REOF NCs, typically less than 10 nm, into larger crystals and results in suboptimal crystallinity. This deficiency subsequently leads to significant internal and surface quenching, as well as diminished PL efficiency.27,28 Core/shell engineering is recognized as one of the most effective strategies for achieving surface passivation, thereby enhancing the PL efficiency of RE3+-doped NCs.29 While a range of fluoride core/shell structures have been extensively reported for augmenting UC PL and DC PL properties,30,31 their application to REOF has been met with limited success due to the nonuniform and inferior quality of the shell, which fails to provide efficient protection against surface quenching.15,32 Alkali metal ions have demonstrated promising performance in phosphors, perovskites, and rare-earth oxysulfides by either passivating vacancies, balancing charges, or functioning as a flux agent.33−36 Consequently, it is feasible to enhance crystallinity, produce larger NCs, and engineer effective shell protection through alkali metal ions doping.

In this study, we introduced the approach to fabricate monodisperse, size-controllable, well-crystallized, and high-luminescence cubic REOF core/shell NCs by doping sodium ions. We illustrated the successful incorporation of Na+ ions into REOF, demonstrating its impact on phase, morphology, crystal-field, and luminescence properties. Drawing inspiration from the high efficiency of luminescence, we showcased superior performance in multicolor displays, advanced encoding encryption, and high-resolution in vivo fluorescence imaging within the near-infrared-IIb (NIR-IIb) (1500–1700 nm) window.

Results and Discussion

Characterization and Optical Properties of Na+-Doped REOF Core and Core/Shell NCs

We synthesized Na+-doped YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ (core) and YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF (core/shell) NCs using a modified thermal decomposition method. This involved employing sodium oleate (NaOA) as an additive in organic solvents of oleic acid (OA), oleylamine (OAm), and octadecene (ODE). The aim was to facilitate the nucleation and growth of the NCs. The REOF crystal generally exhibits diverse phases including cubic, rhombohedral, hexagonal, tetragonal, and orthorhombic phases with similar X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure S1 and Table S1). We noticed that other phases of REOF are often identified as the cubic phase due to their similar XRD patterns.17,19,32,37 Therefore, a meticulous comparison is essential to accurately identify the phase of the synthesized samples. The cubic phase, in fact, exhibits no diffraction peaks within the 2θ range of 35–45°, enabling its distinction from other phases. Figure 1b illustrates the XRD patterns of the synthesized samples, showing no diffraction peaks between 35 and 45° and indicating a pure cubic phase of YOF (PDF#06-0346) for both core and core/shell NCs. The XRD patterns also demonstrated high crystallinity in the resulting NCs. Interestingly, all diffraction peaks of both core and core–shell NCs shifted to lower angles (Figure S2). The calculated lattice constants for these two NCs were 5.50 and 5.46 Å (Table S2), respectively. These values slightly exceeded the standard of 5.37 Å in YOF, suggesting an expansion of the unit lattice when substituting Y3+ ions (1.019 Å) with larger Na+ ions (1.180 Å). The DC NIR-IIb luminescence of cubic YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF NCs is about 1.2 times brighter than that of α-NaYF4: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/NaLuF4 NCs under 980 nm excitation (Figures 1c and S3). As shown in Figure S4, multiphonon orbit-lattice relaxation were used for boosting nonradiative relaxation of Er3+4I11/2 → 4I13/2 and enhancing the population of Er3+ (4I13/2). Compared with that of α-NaYF4 (298 cm–1),25 the higher maximum phonon energy (490 cm–1)38,39 in YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF led to a higher population of the 4I13/2 state and generated the brighter NIR-IIb luminescence by radiative transition from 4I13/2 to 4I15/2.

The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images in Figures 1d and S5 demonstrated the excellent dispersity of the as-prepared core and core/shell NCs. The mean sizes of the core and core/shell NCs were found to be 22.3 ± 2.1 and 25.3 ± 2.1 nm, respectively, suggesting a shell thickness of 1.5 nm. High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images displayed superior crystallinity (Figure 1e) and distinct lattice fringes (Figure 1f), with observed d-spacings of 0.314 nm attributed to the (111) crystal plane of cubic YOF. This was consistent with the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) diffraction pattern. In contrast to the standard (111) crystal plane (0.310 nm) of cubic YOF, the expanding d-spacings could be attributed to the substitution of Y3+ ions by Na+ ions with a larger radius. High angle annular dark field scanning TEM (HAADF-STEM) images (Figure S5), electron energy loss spectroscopy (EELS) (Figure 1g), and the superposition result of Y3+ and Lu3+ in elemental energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) (Figure 1h) confirmed the formation of a core–shell structure. Further EDS results confirmed a homogeneous distribution of Y, O, F, Yb, and Er in the core and Lu in the shell of YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF NCs. Compositional analyses through EDS, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), and inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) identified the elements of Y3+, Lu3+, O2–, F–, and Na+ and the doped Er3+, Yb3+ ions in core/shell NCs (Tables S3, S4 and Figures S6, S7). If all of the measured F– ions were in the lattice (Table S4), the chemical formula of the complex Na+-doped REOF solid solution would be Na0.16RE0.84O0.32F2.04. To confirm the universality of this synthesis method, we also synthesized REOF (RE3+ = Y3+, La3+-Lu3+) NCs (Table S5 and Figure S8). The variation in the cubic lattice XRD pattern (Figure S9) across the rare-earth series largely aligns with the rare-earth contraction.40,41

Morphological, Structural, and Optical Characterization of LuOF: 20% Yb3+, /Er3+ (LuOF-Na-X) NCs with and without Na+-Doping

To elucidate the impact of Na+-ions on REOF NCs, we synthesized a series of LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ NCs doped with different concentrations of Na+ ions. This was achieved by intentionally introducing NaOA into the reaction environment (see Methods). For simplicity, we refer to the as-synthesized Na+-doped LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ NCs as LuOF-Na-X, where X represents the nominal amount of the NaOA precursor (x = 0, 0.5, 1.0, ..., 3.5 mmol). As shown in Figure 2a, in the absence of NaOA, namely, LuOF-Na-0 NCs, the TEM image reveals an irregular morphology with an average diameter of 4.0 ± 0.6 nm. Upon augmentation of the NaOA content (0.5, 1.0 mmol), the irregular NCs started to evolve into hexagonal cylinder NCs and show two size distributions corresponding to two different shapes of NCs. The pure hexagonal cylinder NCs were obtained after further increasing the amount of NaOA to 1.5 mmol, and the size of the NCs could be tuned from 10 to 30 nm by simply changing the NaOA content. These alterations are also discernible in the diffraction patterns of the LuOF-Na-X NCs. As shown in Figures 2b and S10, there is a peak at 41.3° (2θ) for REOF-Na-0 NCs, and it is gradually unobservable with increment of NaOA content. By calculating the lattice volume (Table S2), we can easily differentiate the tetragonal REOF phase from the others. Indeed, the phase of LuOF-Na-0 NCs is tetragonal REOF, while that of the others is cubic REOF. The slightly distorted XRD diffraction peaks of LuOF-Na-0.5 and LuOF-Na-1.0 were due to the weak XRD diffraction peak signals of the tetragonal phase NCs observed in Figure S10. In addition, the 2θ of LuOF-Na-X (x = 1.5, 2.0, ..., 3.5) shifted to a lower diffraction angle (Figure 2b right), which indicates the successful doping of Na+ into LuOF NCs due to the larger radius of Na+ (1.18 Å) compared to that of Lu3+ (0.97 Å). Consequently, it can be inferred that the introduction of Na+ ions induced the formation of a cubic REOF phase with an enlarged size up to 30 nm and superior crystallinity compared to non-Na+-doped NCs and previously reported REOF NCs (less than 10 nm).14,26

Inspired by these findings, we examined the PL properties of the NCs. A 6.8-fold (Figure S11) PL enhancement was observed in the same size (∼6.1 nm) NCs by Na+ doping. As illustrated in Figure 2c, the UC PL intensity enhancement factor of LuOF-Na-X NCs observed at 660 nm demonstrates a consistent increase from 2.8 to 667.7 times with the escalating sizes compared to that of NCs without doped Na+ ions, which is mainly due to the enlarged size of NCs. Interestingly, LuOF-Na-2.5, LuOF-Na-3.0, and LuOF-Na-3.5 exhibit 491.5, 594.5, and 667.7-fold enhancements in UC PL intensity compared to that of LuOF-Na-0 (Figure 2c), respectively, despite their minor size differences. The lifetimes of these NCs extend from 72.99 to 88.53 μs monitored at 660 nm under excitation at 980 nm (Figure S12a), indicating an approximately 1.21-fold longer lifetime. With the increase of the Na+ doping amount, Table S6 exhibits a change from single exponential decay to double exponential decay. Short-lifetime (τ1) and long-lifetime (τ2) growth evidence defect suppression by Na+ doping. In the general trend, the increase of the long lifetime weight factor and decrease of the short lifetime weight factor are consistent with the size increasing phenomenon, which is attributed to the nano surface effect. Defects disrupt crystallographic site symmetry and will significantly affect the spectral line width of RE3+. Figure 2d exhibits the dependence of full width at half-maximum (fwhm) and lifetime vs Na+ content, and the PL lifetime increases and fwhm decreases with increment of Na+ content, demonstrating the gradual defects decrement in the obtained NCs. It is widely recognized that red PL emission is more susceptible to defect influence than green PL emission (Figure 2e),42−44 and the UC PL intensity of the red to green (R/G) ratio is thus obtained to study the defect in NCs. As shown in Figures 2f and S12b, the increased R/G ratio with an increase in Na+ content correlates with reduced susceptibility to defects. These can also be confirmed by the enhanced crystallinity (Figure 2b), reduced average fwhm of the Er3+4F9/2 emission band (Figure 2d), and decreased oxygen vacancy (Figure 2f). These results suggest that the amplified UC PL intensity of the Er3+ emitter is not solely attributed to the enlarged crystal size, but due to the reduced defect with the increment of Na+ ions. In addition, when the amount of NaOA doping was increased to 4.0–4.5 mmol, the size of NCs further increased but became uneven (Figure S13). This is mainly because Na+ doping reduces the reaction time and excess Na+ causes Ostwald ripening of the synthesized products.45 A weakening of the DC PL was observed, and the DC PL spectra are similar to that of α-NaYF4: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+, as shown in Figure S13e. This may have given rise to a new metastable phase formation; therefore, we did not further increase the doping amount of NaOA.

Crystallographic Site Symmetry of REOF NCs Before and After Na+ Doping

To investigate the local structure of RE3+ dopants in LuOF, we conducted high-resolution PL and excitation spectra measurements on Na+-doped LuOF: 15% Eu3+/LuOF NC (Na-Eu-NCs) and undoped LuOF: 15% Eu3+/LuOF NC (Eu-NCs) samples at a low temperature of 10 K. This was done to circumvent the thermal broadening of spectral bands at room temperature. In theory,46−48 Eu3+ ions are expected to occupy a crystallographic site with point-group symmetry of Oh in Na-Eu-NCs. The electric-dipole emissions from 5D0 to 7F0, 2, and 4 should be strictly prohibited, and only the magnetic–dipole transition 5D0 → 7F1 is permitted and observed in the PL spectrum of Eu3+ (Figure 3a, black line). However, as shown in Figure 3a,b, the number of CF transition lines from 5D0 to 7F0, 7F1, and 7F2 are 2, 3, and ≥4, respectively (Figure 3a), indicating a distortion site symmetry from Oh to low site symmetry, such as C2v, C2, or Cs. In addition, the 5D0 → 7F0 emission is a nondegenerate transition and cannot be split by a CF.49 The bimodal emission spectra of 5D0 → 7F0 emissions (Figure 3a,b) indicate the presence of two sites for Eu3+ ions, corresponding to the double exponential form of the PL decay profiles (Figure 3c), which may be because a significant number of Eu3+ ions are located on the surface of Eu-NCs, while the others are inside the NCs. The excitation spectra of the NCs, monitored at 613 nm are shown in Figure 3d, displayed a similar excitation pattern with an expanded O2––Eu3+ charge transfer band at 254 nm.

Impact of Na+-Doping on the PL Intensity of Core–Shell NCs

After studying the site occupancy of Eu3+ in LuOF NCs, we explored the impact of Na+ doping on the PL intensity of the core–shell NCs. The inert LuOF shell was used to prevent the Er3+-doped YOF cores from surface quenching, thereby significantly augmenting the UC and DC PL efficiency. However, using the conventional synthetic method without Na+ doping, only a small number of NCs formed good core–shell structures (Figure S14d, in the box). 2.47 times and 1.09 times enhancement were observed for these YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF NCs without Na+ doping. The partial dissolution of the core observed through HADDF (Figure S14d, NCs within the ellipse) is one of the reasons for poor luminescence enhancement. A similar phenomenon of partial dissolution of the core was observed in NaYF4.21 Ineffective encapsulation (Figure S14d, NCs within the dashed box) is another cause of poor luminescence enhancement. To directly demonstrate the effect of Na+ doping on shell formation, we coated the Na+-doped YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ core (Figure S5a, 22.3 nm) with LuOF both with and without Na+ doping. The reduction of NC size (Figure S15b, 18.9 nm) suggests that the core partially dissolved, and HADDF images (Figure S15c,d) show no shell layer formed when wrapping the no-Na+-doped LuOF. Meanwhile, a clear shell layer (Figure S5c), size increase (Figure S5d, 25.3 nm), and a 46.1-fold and a 4.3-fold enhancement in the 4F9/2 → 4I15/2 and 4I13/2 → 4I15/2 transitions of Er3+ are observed (Figure S16a) when wrapping the Na+-doped LuOF. These enhancements are considerably higher than the reported 18.5 times for previously engineered YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/YOF NCs.15,32 This can be attributed to suboptimal protection provided by partial coating and irregular shapes. Consequently, introducing Na+ ions into the YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF NCs not only enhances crystallinity but also facilitates superior shell growth. The decay curves of the 4F9/2 and 4I13/2 state increased from 67.29 μs and 5.48 ms for the core-only to 111.25 μs and 6.20 ms for the core/shell NCs (Figure S16b), respectively. Given that lifetime is a crucial property of luminescence, the YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF core/shell NCs demonstrated the longest lifetime (Table S7) compared to the previously reported Yb3+/Er3+ codoped rare earth oxyfluoride NCs. Core–shell NCs with different Na+ doping amounts were synthesized, and their PL properties were characterized, as shown in Figure S17. The PL QY of NCs increased with the increment of Na+ content. The highest PL QY achieved was 3.5 mmol of Na+-doped core–shell NCs, which exhibited a UC PL QY of 0.14% and a DC PL QY of 8.1%. Compared with previously reported NCs (Table S8), these NCs demonstrate a promising DC PL performance.

Generalizability of the Na+-Doping Strategy to Improve the PL Intensity

To assess the generalizability of the Na+ doping strategy in enhancing the PL efficiency of RE3+ ions, we synthesized a series of large-sized and Na+-doped LuOF: RE3+/LuOF (RE3+ = 15% Tb3+, 15% Eu3+, 20% Yb3+/1% Tm3+, 20% Yb3+/2% Ho3+, 20% Yb3+/2% Er3+, 5% Yb3+/15% Nd3+, 20% Yb3+, 3% Nd3+) NCs (Figure S18). These were prepared under similar conditions identical to those used for YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF (see Methods). As shown in the inset image of Figure 4a, bright green and red are observed in Tb3+ and Eu3+ singly doped NCs, respectively, under ultraviolet excitation at 254 nm. The PL emission spectra of these NCs revealed characteristic and sharp emission peaks originating from the 5D4 → 7FJ transitions of Tb3+ at 487 (7F6), 543 (7F5), 584 (7F4), and 623 nm (7F3), and the 5D0 → 7FJ transitions of Eu3+ at 593 (7F1), 610 (7F2), 650 (7F3), and 707 nm (7F4) (Figure 4b). The excitation spectra of the Tb3+-doped NCs (Figure S19) exhibited expanded excitation bands in the UV regions, similar to those of the Eu3+-doped NCs. Furthermore, due to the reduced nonradiative relaxation of RE3+, the integrated PL intensities of Tb3+ and Eu3+ increased by 8.9 and 4.38 times in the Na+-doped core/shell NCs compared to those without Na+-doped core/shell NCs. Additionally, the PL lifetimes extended from 2.31 and 1.42 to 2.88 and 4.05 ms for Tb3+: 5D4 and Eu3+: 5D0, respectively (Figure S20a).

The UC RE3+ emitters are notably susceptible to surface quenching effects.50 Consequently, the Na+-doped core/shell NCs significantly impacts the UC PL properties of RE3+ ions more than their DC PL properties. As shown in the inset image of Figure 4a, Yb3+/Tm3+, Yb3+/Ho3+, and Yb3+/Er3+ codoped LuOF/LuOF NCs exhibit bright blue-purple, green, and red emissions upon excitation with a 980 nm diode laser. This is markedly different from the negligible UC PL observed in non-Na+-doped NCs. Upon 980 nm excitation, several typical upconverted emission bands of Tm3+ at 364 (1D2 → 3H6), 482 (1G4 → 3H6), 650 (1G4 → 3F4), and 798 nm (3F2,3 → 3H6), Ho3+ at 541 (5F4/5S2 → 5I8) and 662 nm (5F5 → 5I8), and Er3+ at 547 (4S3/2 →4I15/2) and 671 nm (4F7/2 → 4I15/2) are observed (Figures 4c and S21). Compared to non-Na+-doped core/shell NCs, the UC PL intensity of Tm3+, Er3+, and Ho3+ in Na+-doped core/shell NCs is significantly enhanced by factors of 2211.71, 1139.82, and 1061.69, respectively. This is paralleled by substantial increases in UC PL lifetimes from 24.16, 5.18, and 8.61 to 344.96, 86.2, and 26.87 μs for Tm3+(1G4), Er3+(4F7/2), and Ho3+(5F4/5S2), respectively (Figures 4c and S20b,c). We also measured the number of photons involved in the UC process of these NCs, and the UC PL intensities as a function of the power density of 980 nm laser diode are plotted in log–log scale in Figure S22. The slopes of different curves (Figure S22b,d,f) were found to be within the range of 1–2, indicating that normal two-photon processes in the UC mechanism of the Yb3+/Tm3+, Yb3+/Ho3+, Yb3+/Er3+ codoped LuOF/LuOF NCs. The Yb3+ emission is very sensitive to surface quenching.51 We synthesized LuOF: 20% Yb3+/LuOF doped with and without Na+ doping, and a 15-fold lifetime increase is observed from 6.09 to 89.71 μs (Figure S20d), which demonstrated that Na+ can synthesize high-quality core–shell structure NCs.

The Na+ doping strategy also promotes the synthesis of NCs with efficient luminescence in the NIR region. Under excitation at 808 or 980 nm, the DC PL spectra (Figure 4d,e) exhibit characteristic emission peaks for Nd3+ at 1060 nm (4F3/2 → 4I11/2, 4F3/2 → 4I13/2) and Er3+ 1532 nm (4I13/2 → 4I15/2). When compared to NCs without Na+ doping, the DC PL intensity of Nd3+ and Yb3+/Er3+ in Na+-doped NCs increases by factors of 11.3 and 4.80, respectively. The lifetimes (Figure S20e,f) rise from 0.048 and 0.107 to 0.338 and 4.80 ms for Nd3+ (4F3/2) and Er3+ (4I13/2), respectively. The slope for the DC PL (Figure S22h) is 0.78 (within the range 0–1), suggesting a one-photon emission process in the DC PL mechanism of as-synthesized NCs. For the 5% Yb3+/15% Nd3+ system (Figure S23), less PL enhancement is mainly attributed to the intense cross-relaxation process: 4F3/2 + 4I15/2 → 4I9/2 + 4I15/2 between Nd3+ and the mismatch between Nd3+(4I3/2) and Yb3+(2F5/2). Consequently, we have achieved efficient and multicolor DC PL and UC PL in REOF core–shell NCs by doping Na+ ions.

Application of the As-Synthesized REOF NCs for Multicolor Display, Information Encryption, and NIR-IIb In Vivo Imaging

Particles with diameters less than 50 nm can be transparently dispersed within a matrix, exhibiting negligible scattering of visible light.52 Consequently, reducing the particle size to the nanoscale could essentially eradicate backscattering loss in field emission displays. As illustrated in Figure 5a, an intense red luminescence of the FJIRSM logo and a green quick-response (QR) code from the homepage of FJIRSM are achieved using LuOF: 15% Eu3+/LuOF NCs (∼10.7 nm) and LuOF: 15% Tb3+/LuOF (∼10.2 nm), respectively, under excitation by ultraviolet (254 nm). In the realm of information encryption and anticounterfeiting, safeguarding against data breaches in everyday life is paramount.53 Transparent REOF NC inks offer an efficacious solution as the most effective camouflage often manifests as no camouflage at all. We prepared three types of multimode luminescent inks composed of LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 1% Tm3+/LuOF (visually blue-purple), LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Ho3+/LuOF (visually yellow), and LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF (visually red) by uniformly dispersing the ligands removed NCs in a ethanol-based polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP 40) solution (referred to as Tm-ink, Ho-ink, and 2Er-ink for simplicity). These three inks were used to create Chinese characters by handwriting. They were invisible by the naked eye in nature light but visible under 980 nm laser diode irradiation (Figure 5b), showing three colors of blue, yellow, and red, corresponding to three inks. To enhance the security of our encryption strategy, we investigated the potential application of NCs for high-level encoding encryption by incorporating Morse code and varying excitation wavelengths to further diversify available features. Thus, we used two kinds of NCs, LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF (2Er-ink) and YbOF: 50% Er3+/LuOF NCs (50Er-ink), as shown in Figure 5c, the 2Er-ink cannot be excited by the 1550 nm laser diode due to its low absorption at 1550 nm, both of two inks can be efficiently excited by 980 nm. By alternately using 2Er-INK and 50Er-INK to write Morse code characters, we were able to decode incorrect information on “dash, dot, dash, dot(C), dot, dash, dot(R), dot, dot, dot, dash(V)” under 980 nm excitation (Figure 5c bottom left), while the correct code “dash, dot, dash, dot(C), dot, dash(A), dot, dot, dot(S)” was decoded under 1550 nm excitation (Figure 5c bottom right). This multilevel information encryption/readout procedure ensures a high level of system security. Given its straightforward decryption process and high level of security, these luminescent inks hold significant potential for practical applications.

Fluorescence probes emitted within the NIR-II window has great potential application in fluorescence imaging due to their reduced autofluorescence, diminished photon scattering, and decreased absorption by tissues and blood.24,25 As illustrated in Figure S24, we have developed a series of innovative REOF (RE3+ = 5% Yb3+/15% Nd3+, 20% Yb3+/2% Ho3+, 20% Yb3+/2% Er3+, and 20% Yb3+/1% Tm3+) NCs with emissions within the NIR-II window. Notably, due to the detection range of commercial detectors being 900–1700 nm, the bright 1532 nm luminescence of Er3+-doped NCs situated in the NIR-IIb region is an ideal probe in vivo imaging. We initially functionalized the hydrophilic, cross-linked amphiphilic 1,2-distearoy-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N- [carboxy-(polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG2000-COOH) phospholipids on LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF NCs (according to the Na+ content used during synthesis and surface ligand, they are abbreviated as Er-OA/PEG-0.0, Er-OA/PEG-1.0, Er-OA/PEG-2.0 and Er-OA/PEG-3.5, see Methods). The FTIR spectra confirmed the successful functionalization of DSPE-PEG2000-COOH on Er3+-doped NCs (Figure S25a). The zeta potential and dynamic light scattering (DLS) of PEG-modified NCs are shown in Figure S25b–d. As shown in Figure S26a, the PL intensity of Er-OA-3.5 is 17.7 times that of Er-OA-0.0. This PL intensity difference was further enlarged after PEG modification (Figure S26b), as the large NCs were less quenched by hydroxyl in water. The hydrophilic functionalized NCs demonstrated stability in aqueous buffers and exhibited zero photobleaching (Figure S26c). A disproportionate enhancement of the down-conversion PL intensity is observed (Figure S26c,d); this may be due to reduced hydroxyl quenching induced by proteins adsorbed onto NCs in plasma. Prior to in vivo fluorescent imaging studies, the potential cytotoxicity of PEG-modified NCs was assessed in 4T1 tumor cells. As depicted in Figure S27, no overt toxicity was observed at concentrations of NCs as high as 800 μg/mL, thereby permitting further in vivo experiments. To ensure the quality of the images, the excitation laser power density was set at 100 mW/cm2. The mice were administrated with different PEG-modified NCs via tail vein injection (40 mg/mL, 150 μL) under anesthesia. A whole-body vascular fluorescence imaging was conducted using the NIR-II in vivo imaging system under excitation by a 980 nm laser diode. Almost no NIR-II signals were detected for Er-PEG-0.0, so bright field images were used instead. Figure 5d demonstrates the significant improvement in imaging performance with the increase in the Na+ doping amount. The cross-sectional intensity distributions of selected blood vessels are compared (Figure 5e), with the signal-to-noise ratio increasing from 0 to 5.28. Figure S28 demonstrates the imaging effects under different excitation powers density and exposure times. The blood circulation half-life of the Er-PEG-3.5 was estimated to be ∼42.8 min (Figure S29). Subsequently, the biodistribution of PEG-modified NCs was assessed in C57BL/6 mice (n = 3). Figure S30 displays that at 24 h posttail vein injection, PEG-modified NCs were primarily found in the liver, spleen, and lung, which are considered the primary excretory organs. Furthermore, hematoxylin and eosin (H & E) staining of major organs at 24 h revealed no significant pathological changes in these organs (Figure S31). In line with this, the blood routine test results (Table S9) for the tested mice indicated good health conditions. Although preliminary, all these findings suggest that our PEG-modified NCs could be promising bioimaging agents for high-contrast cardiovascular bioimaging, even suggesting the potential for further in vivo experiments.

Conclusions

We have proposed a universal strategy for preparing high-crystallinity, size-tunable, efficiently shell-protected, and multicolor cubic LuOF: RE3+/LuOF core/shell NCs via doping Na+ ions. The increased content of Na+ ion led to gradually reduced defects and enhanced crystallinity, as confirmed by XRD patterns and electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR). By means of Eu3+ as the sensitive structural probes, we unraveled that the highest symmetry is C2v, C2, or Cs in the disordered REOF NCs before and after doping Na+ ions. The size of the NCs can be adjusted from 6 to 30 nm, with the highest UC PL enhancement factor of 667.7 for LuOF-Na-3.5 core NCs compared with that of NCs without doping Na+. By creating a core/shell structure, we achieved 46.1- and 3.4-fold enhancement in UC PL and DC PL compared to that of core NCs, respectively, and this enhancement factor is higher than that of NCs without Na+ doping (2.47- and 1.09-fold, respectively). Further fabrication of the core/shell NCs doped with Na+ resulted in enhancements up to 1062 (Ho3+), 1140 (Er3+), and 2212 (Tm3+) folds in UC PL compared to that of NCs without doped Na+ ions and showed excellent performance in information security and high-level encoding encryption. In addition, the LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF NCs demonstrated a bright DC PL at 1550 nm and exhibits superior in vivo dynamic imaging capabilities with a signal-to-noise ratio of 5.28. This study provides an efficient strategy for designing efficient, robust, and multicolor REOF NCs and offers an extended rare-earth-probe toolbox for versatile applications such as high-level encoding encryption, diverse optical imaging, and biomedicine.

Experimental Section

Methods

Materials

1-Octadecene (ODE, 90%), OA (90%), oleylamine (OAm, ≥98% primary amine), sodium oleate (NaOA, 97%), and trifluoroacetic acid (99%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Ethanol (reagent grade), cyclohexane (reagent grade), and rare-earth oxides RE2O3 (99.999%, RE3+ = Y3+, La3+-Lu3+) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. DSPE-PEG2000-COOH (99%) was purchased from Ponsure Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used directly without further purification.

Characterization

TEM was carried on a H-7650 transmission electron microscope with an accelerating voltage of 120 kV. High-resolution TEM imaging was performed on a JEOL-2100F transmission electron microscope with an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The elemental maps and spectra were acquired using a JEM-F200 Microscope, operating at 200 kV. Photoluminescence (PL) measurements were carried out using a spectrometer (FLS980, Edinburgh Instrument, UK) equipped with the external 808, 980, and 1550 nm semiconductor laser excitation sources (Changchun New Industries Optoelectronics Tech. Co., Ltd.). For low temperature measurements, samples were mounted on a closed cycle cryostat (10–350 K, CS202PE-DMX-1AL, Advanced Research Systems). Powder XRD patterns of the samples were recorded using a powder X-ray diffractometer (DMAX 2400 PC, Rigaku, Japan) at a scanning rate of 10°/min in the 2θ range from 5 to 85° with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.154187 nm) at room temperature. XPS was conducted on a Thermo Fisher ESCALAB 250Xi spectrometer using Al-Kα (1486.6 eV). The composition of the NCs was determined by using an inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES, ULTIMA 2). DLS and zeta potential experiments were carried out at a NanoBrook Omni, Brook Haven, USA. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were obtained on a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA). The PL quantum yield (PLQY) was measured using an optical integrating sphere coupled to two fiber optic spectrometers (QE Pro, 190–1100 nm, Ocean Optics and NQ512-1.7, 900–1700 nm, Ocean Optics), and the optical response of the instrument was calibrated with a standard tungsten halogen lamp. The samples were dissolved in cyclohexane and placed in a cuvette with an optical length of 1 mm. A 980 nm semiconductor laser was used as the excitation source.

The absolute internal PLQY is defined as the following function: ηint = (photons emitted/photons absorbed) = {E[sample]/L[blank] – L[sample]}. Here, E[sample] represents the emission intensity, while L[blank] and L[sample] are the intensities of the excitation light in the presence of the cyclohexane and the dissolved sample, respectively.

Synthesis of Nanocrystals (NCs)

Synthesis of Rare-Earth Trifluoroacetates RE(CF3COO)3 (RE3+ = Y3+, and La3+-Lu3+)

RE(CF3COO)3 were prepared by adding 15 mmol of rare-earth oxide (RE3+ = Y3+, La3+-Lu3+) to 20 mL of trifluoroacetic acid and 40 mL of deionized water in a 200 mL round-bottom flask equipped with a reflux condenser. The stirred suspension was heated to 105 °C under an oil bath until the solid oxide was completely dissolved and a clear solution was obtained. After cooling, the solution was filtered and the solvent evaporated with a rotary evaporator at 85 °C for 45 min. The obtained wet powder was dried under a vacuum at 115 °C for 36 h. The dry powder was stored under inert conditions in a glovebox under dry nitrogen.

Synthesis of Cubic Rare-Earth Oxyfluorides REOF (RE3+ = Y3+, La3+-Lu3+) NCs

The REOF NCs were synthesized via a modified thermal decomposition process. Typically, 1 mmol of RE(CF3COO)3 (RE3+ = Y3+, La3+-Lu3+) was mixed with 1 mmol of NaOA, 4 mL of OA, and 5 mL of ODE in a 100 mL three-neck round-bottom flask. Then, the resulting mixture was heated at 120 °C (under Ar flow) for 30 min in order to dissolve trifluoroacetate precursors, during which the turbid solution turned into an optically clear yellow solution. After OAm (6 mL) was added, the vacuum purification consisted in three Ar—vacuum cycles, followed by a dynamic vacuum step for 10 min. Finally, the solution was heated under Ar to 320 °C for 1 h. Then, the heating mantle was removed and the flask was left to cool naturally to room temperature. After cooling, the NCs were precipitated by the addition of ethanol, collected via centrifugation (8000 rpm, 5 min), washed with ethanol two times, and redispersed in cyclohexane. The resulting clear, colorless solution was stored in a tightly closed glass vial.

Synthesis of LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ NCs with Different Contents of NaOA

Raw material usages changed to 1.56 mmol of Lu(CF3COO)3, 0.4 mmol of Yb(CF3COO)3, and 0.04 mmol of Er(CF3COO)3. The content of NaOA is 0, 0.5, 1.0, ..., 3.5 mmol. Organic solutions are still 4 mL of OA, 5 mL of ODE, and 6 mL of OAm. The rest of the synthesis procedure is the same as described for REOF NCs.

Synthesis of Uniform 6–20 nm LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ NCs

The total amount of RE(CF3COO)3, with a specific composition, was increased from 0.5 to 1.0 and 1.5 mmol. The equivalent quantity of NaOA (0.5, 1.0, and 1.5 mmol) was added. The organic solution consists of 4 mL of OA, 5 mL of ODE, and 6 mL of OAm. The rest of the synthesis procedure is the same as described for REOF NCs.

Synthesis of Cubic YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ Core NCs

The synthesis procedure is exactly the same as described for LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ NCs, except that 1.56 mmol of Y(CF3COO)3 was added [instead of Lu(CF3COO)3] and the content of NaOA was fixed at 2 mmol.

Synthesis of Cubic YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF Core/Shell NCs

In a two-step synthetic procedure for cubic YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF, 1 mmol of NaOA and 1 mmol of Lu(CF3COO)3 were added to a mixture of OA (4 mL) and ODE (6 mL) in a three-necked flask at room temperature. The solution was heated to 120 °C for 30 min to dissolve trifluoroacetates precursors. Then, half of the as-prepared core solution (YOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ core NCs) and 6 mL of an OAm were added to the flask. Then, there is a vacuum purification consisting of three Ar—vacuum cycles, followed by a dynamic vacuum step for 10 min. Finally, the solution was heated up under Ar to 320 °C for 1 h. Then, the heating mantle was removed, and the flask was left to cool naturally to room temperature. After cooling, the NCs were precipitated by the addition of ethanol, collected via centrifugation (8000 rpm, 5 min), washed with ethanol for two times, and redispersed in cyclohexane. The resulting clear colorless solution was stored in a tightly closed glass vial.

Synthesis of Cubic NaYF4: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/NaLuF4 Core/Shell NCs

In a typical procedure of synthesizing NaYF4: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ core nanoparticles, 1 mmol of CF3COONa, 0.78 mmol of Y(CF3COO)3, 0.20 mmol of Y(CF3COO)3, and 0.02 mmol of Er(CF3COO)3 were added into a three-necked flask with 6 mL of OA and 6 mL of OM and 10 mL of ODE. The mixture was heated at 120 °C under the vacuum state. After dissolving, the whole solution was heated up to 305 °C and maintained for 120 min under the protection by Argon. Finally, the NCs of NaYF4: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+ core nanoparticles were collected and washed with hexane/ethanol 3 times and then dispersed in 10 mL of hexane for further use.

NaYF4: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/NaLuF4 core/shell NCs were synthesized through an epitaxial seeded growth method. 1 mmol of CF3COONa, 1 mmol of Lu(CF3COO)3, and a certain amount of 5 mL of hexane (core solution) were added to a mixture of OA (10 mL) and ODE (10 mL) in a 50 mL flask. The solution was heated to 100 °C under the vacuum state to get rid of hexane. Then, the solution was heated up to 300 °C and maintained for 1.5 h at Argon atmosphere. Finally, the reacted nanoparticles were precipitated by ethanol and washed by hexane/ethanol, and then the synthesized core–shell structure NCs were dispersed in hexane.

Synthesis of Cubic Rare-Earth Doped LuOF: RE3+/LuOF (RE3+ = 15% Eu3+, 15% Tb3+, 20% Yb3+/1% Tm3+, 20% Yb3+/2% Ho3+, 20% Yb3+/2% Er3+ and 5% Yb3+/15% Nd3+, 20% Yb3+, 3% Nd3+) Core/Shell NCs

The total amount of RE(CF3COO)3, with a specific composition was changed to 1 mmol, and the amount of NaOA was set as 1 mmol. Organic solution is still 4 mL of OA, 5 mL of ODE and 6 mL of OAm. The rest of the synthesis procedure is the same as described for REOF NCs.

Fabrication of the Ligand-Free NCs

The as-prepared NCs were dispersed in acidic ethanol solution (0.1 M) and ultrasonicated for several hours to remove the surface ligands. After the reaction, the NCs were collected by centrifugation at 14,000 rpm for 20 min and redispersed in distilled water.

Fabrication of Two-Dimensional Logo and Quick-Response Codes Doped with Ultraviolet-Excited NCs

First, using an ultraviolet laser engraving machine, we fabricated inverted two-dimensional logo and QR codes on the surface of plastic substrates. Then, the obtained models were cleaned by ultrasound for 5 min. Finally, the LuOF: 15% Eu3+/LuOF and LuOF: 15% Tb3+/LuOF NCs were respectively sprinkled on the surface and wiped out softly.

Fabrication of the Anticounterfeiting Ink

The anticounterfeiting inks were prepared by dispersing 40 mg of ligand-free LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Tm3+/LuOF or LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Ho3+/LuOF or LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF or YbOF: 50% Er3+/LuOF NCs into 4 mL of ethanol, respectively, in which 60 mg of polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP 40) was added as a stabilizer. The solution was sonicated in an ultrasonic bath for 45 min to get homogeneous dispersion.

Phospholipid Decoration of the Downconversion (DC) NCs

For in vivo fluorescence imaging, DC NCs were modified with PEG (DSPE-PEG2000-COOH). According to the literature methods, the synthesized LuOF: 20% Yb3+, 2% Er3+/LuOF NCs (Er-NCs) (40 mg) dispersed in 4 mL of chloroform were mixed with PEG (30 mg/mL) chloroform solution in a round-bottom flask (100 mL). The flask was left open in a fume hood at room temperature to evaporate the chloroform slowly. After completely removing the chloroform by reduced pressure distillation, the obtained mixed film was hydrated with Milli-Q water (10 mL), and the hydrophilic Er-NCs could be dispersed greatly after vigorously sonication. Excess lipids were removed by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 15 min), and the PEG-modified NCs were redispersed in saline solution (0.9% NaCl, 1 mL).

In Vivo Cytotoxicity Assays on Major Organs and Blood
