Magnetoelectric biofeedback for precision-targeted rectocele management: A randomized controlled trial of phenotype-driven pelvic floor neuromodulation
Authors: Hong Zhi Geng, Chen Xu, Jiying Cong, Yuwei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeMagnetoelectric biofeedback therapy (MEBFT) is an emerging intervention for moderate rectocele-associated obstructed defecation syndrome (ODS); however, its efficacy and mechanisms remain unclear. This study compared MEBFT with conventional biofeedback therapy (BFT) and developed a 3D high-resolution anorectal manometry (HR-ARM)-based predictive model for clinical decision-making.MethodsIn this prospective, single-blind, randomized controlled trial, 68 female patients with defecography-confirmed moderate rectocele-associated ODS were treated in the outpatient department of Tianjin Union Medical Center from January 2019 to June 2024). Patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the MEBFT or BFT group. Primary outcomes included 3D HR-ARM parameters, Glazer surface electromyography, and patient-reported outcomes (Cleveland Clinic Constipation and Patient Assessment of Constipation Quality of Life scores) at baseline and 3 months. The secondary outcomes included predictive indicators derived from the Hosmer–Rothman model and treatment response stratification.ResultsMEBFT significantly improved fast-twitch fiber recruitment, anorectal function, and quality of life compared to BFT (all P < 0.01). The Hosmer–Rothman model identified a negative MRP-MTV interaction (synergy index = 0.20), with the R11 phenotype (MRP < 80 mmHg–MTV < 135 cc) predicting an 82.35% response to MEBFT after 3 months of treatment (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.72). Phenotypic stratification-guided management: R11 for MEBFT, R00 (elevated MRP–MTV) for surgical evaluation, and R10/R01 for extended MEBFT with surgical backup.ConclusionsMEBFT demonstrated superior clinical effectiveness, anorectal functional recovery, and quality of life compared with BFT after 3 months of treatment. Introduction

Rectocele, a herniation of the anterior rectal wall into the posterior vagina, is a prevalent pelvic floor disorder [1]. When rectocele size exceeds 15 mm, it can cause obstructed defecation syndrome (ODS), characterized by difficult or incomplete evacuation and reduced bowel frequency [2–4]. The prevalence of rectoceles > 2 cm among symptomatic women ranges from 28 to 42% [5–7].

Defecography is the gold standard for assessing rectocele morphology [1, 8]. Three-dimensional high-resolution anorectal manometry (3D HR-ARM) provides detailed functional evaluation and can predict the response to biofeedback therapy (BFT) in defecation disorders [8]. Glazer surface electromyography (EMG) reflects the electrical activity of the external anal sphincter during straining [9]. One study suggested that rectal mucosal electrical stimulation may enhance the efficacy of BFT [10]. Functional magnetic stimulation is a non-invasive therapeutic approach that utilizes time-varying magnetic fields to induce eddy currents in pelvic floor neuromuscular tissues, thereby triggering neural depolarization and muscle contraction to achieve the goal of activating the pelvic floor muscle group [11].

BFT is recognized as the first-line physical therapy for pelvic floor dysfunction, with an efficacy rate exceeding 70% [12, 13]. However, BFT for ODS associated with rectoceles faces significant challenges: (i) prolonged training cycles and poor patient compliance [13, 14]; (ii) delayed onset and short-lived therapeutic effects, leading to doubts about its long-term efficacy [15]; (iii) lack of a clear efficacy evaluation system for the emerging magnetoelectric biofeedback therapy (MEBFT) [16]; and (iv) absence of a prediction model for ODS associated with moderate rectocele in women (MRW) that incorporates critical indicators for interventional treatment decision-making.

This study aimed to: (i) evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of MEBFT vs. BFT using 3D HR-ARM, EMG, Cleveland Clinic Constipation (CCC) [17] and Patient Assessment of Constipation Quality of Life (PAC-QOL) scores after 3 months of treatment [18]; (ii) develop a Hosmer–Rothman additive model [19] to quantify the impact of predictive critical indicators (3D HR-ARM parameters) and after 3 months of treatment variables (MEBFT vs. BFT) on prognostic outcomes (non-response and response rates); (iii) elucidate the pathophysiological mechanism of MEBFT; and (iv) investigate the clinical utility of risk-stratified phenotypes—based on the interaction between mean resting pressure (MRP) and maximum tolerable volume (MTV)—as critical indicators for distinguishing between surgical and non-surgical management.

Methods

Study design

From January 2019 to June 2024, a single-blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT) was conducted in the outpatient department of Tianjin Union Medical Center, involving 68 patients diagnosed with MRW related to ODS (rectocele with rectal mucosal prolapse, and rectocele with perineal decline) on defecography. This study evaluated anorectal function and clinical efficacy using 3D HR-ARM, EMG, CCC, and PAC-QOL scores before and after 3 months of pelvic floor BFT and MEBFT.

Participant enrolment

Before randomization, all enrolled patients were assessed at baseline. Eligible patients who fulfilled all inclusion criteria and had no exclusion criteria were randomly assigned to either the MEBFT or conventional BFT group.

Randomization

Patients were randomly assigned to MEBFT (n = 34) or BFT (n = 34) groups in a 1:1 ratio using computer-generated block randomization (block size = 4). Allocation concealment was ensured through sealed opaque envelopes. The treating therapist opened envelopes to administer interventions and was consequently unblinded.

Blinding

This trial implemented a single-blind (assessor-blinded) design. While participants and outcome assessors remained blinded, therapists were unblinded due to intervention-specific procedural requirements (e.g., absence of anal electrodes in MEBFT’s magnetic phase).

Sample size calculation

Using G*Power 3.1.9.7 with α = 0.05 and 95% statistical power, 34 patients were required in the experimental group. A total of 68 patients with defecography-confirmed ODS associated with MRW were enrolled in the study.

The protocol adhered to the CONSORT guidelines [20]and the STARD initiative for the accurate reporting of diagnostic accuracy studies [21]. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki [22] and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Tianjin Union Medical Center (2022-B48). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment.

Trial registration was completed retrospectively (ChiCTR2300079173; December 26, 2023) due to administrative oversight during the COVID-19 pandemic. Initial patient enrollment began on January 9, 2019. All primary/secondary outcomes and statistical analysis plans were finalized prior to data analysis and database lock, consistent with ICMJE guidelines for post-hoc registration [23].

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

Diagnosis of functional constipation per Rome IV criteria (2016) [24]; females aged 18–80 years; rectocele severity per Chinese defecography classification for MRW (Grade II: 16–30 mm) [25]; defecography-confirmed perineal descent (2.0–3.5 cm); and rectocele (≤ 3.0 cm) with or without mild-to-moderate rectal mucosal prolapse [25]; patients with no prior biofeedback therapy for rectocele; laxatives could be used for defecation during treatment, and could be continued if ODS symptoms persisted post-treatment.

Exclusion criteria

Pregnancy; chronic systemic disease (cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal); dyssynergic defecation, megacolon, rectocele with rectal intussusception, external rectal prolapse, pelvic floor hernia, or fecal incontinence (confirmed by defecography or 3D HR-ARM); slow-transit constipation (confirmed by colonic transit test); prior biofeedback therapy for rectocele; psychiatric disorders; or noncompliance.

Diagnostic and therapeutic methods

Diagnostic tool

The 3D HR-ARM protocol was performed as previously described [26]. Phenotype cut-off stratification in MEBFT after 3 months of treatment. Based on study-derived thresholds, the MRP was stratified into R0 (MRP ≥ 80 mmHg) and R1 (MRP < 80 mmHg) groups. MTV was stratified into R0 (MTV ≥ 135 cc) and R1 (MTV < 135 cc) groups.

Pelvic floor electromyography Glazer test protocol

The Glazer Pelvic Floor Surface Electromyography Assessment (Glazer Assessment) was developed by Glazer [27]. A pelvic floor electromyoelectric biofeedback instrument (SA9804, Nanjing Vishee Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) was used to collect and measure the pelvic floor electromyoelectric signals during a series of contraction and relaxation movements of the pelvic floor muscles using vaginal or anal electrodes to reflect the function of the pelvic floor muscles, guide rehabilitation training, and evaluate the rehabilitation effects. The test methods were as follows: 1). Baseline test before 60 s: assessed pelvic floor muscle function in the resting state; 2). Five rapid contractions were performed, followed by a 10 s rest before each squeeze to assess pelvic floor fast muscle function; 3). Five sustained squeezes and relaxation (10 s squeeze, 10 s relaxation) were used to assess the function of the fast and slow pelvic floor muscles; 4). a sustained squeeze test for 60 s (relaxation for 10 s before and after the squeeze) to assess the function of the fast and slow pelvic floor muscles; and 5). Baseline rest test after 60 s: Functional recovery of the pelvic floor muscles was assessed.

Intervention protocols

Pelvic floor magnetic stimulation therapy

A Magneuro 60 F magnetic stimulation therapy instrument (Nanjing Vishee Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) was used to treat the patient. The patient sat on the treatment chair, spread his legs, aligned the perineal area with the front of the marked circle on the chair, and remained close to the chair surface. The pelvic organ prolapse program was selected (stimulation frequency, 30–50 Hz; stimulation time, 5 s; intermittent time, 5 s), and the intensity of stimulation was the maximum that the patient could tolerate (20 min). Magnetic stimulation was delivered without an anal electrode placement, and the anus passively received magnetic stimulation.

Pelvic floor electrical stimulation therapy

After BFT treatment, BFT was continued using the same biostimulation feedback instrument (SA9804, Nanjing Vishee Medical Technology Co., Ltd.). In electrical stimulation and biofeedback, a rectal electrode is placed in the anus. An appropriate Kegel training template for the patient was selected from the Kegel-trained template, and the patient was taught to recognize normal and abnormal EMG signals and patterns. The patient was able to master the action elements of squeezing and relaxing the pelvic floor muscles at a stimulation intensity of 30–50 mA. The training was repeated for 15 min each time.

Pelvic floor biofeedback therapy

Applying pelvic floor electrical stimulation and applying the biofeedback instrument (SA9804, Nanjing Vishee Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) before treatment, the patient was asked to find a comfortable and relaxed supine position after the bladder was emptied and the rectal electrode was implanted. A moderate pelvic organ prolapse program was selected (stimulation frequency: 30–50 Hz; stimulation time: 5 s; intermittent time: 5 s). The intensity of stimulation was defined as the patient’s maximum tolerance, and the treatment time was 15 min.

Treatment arms

Nanjing Vishee Medical Technology Co., Ltd. recommends the following protocol: MEBFT Group Phase 1 (magnetic stimulation: five sessions per week for two weeks); Phase 2 (magnetic + electrical stimulation: five sessions per week for two weeks); Phase 3 (Kegel-mode BFT: five sessions per week for two weeks). The cycles were repeated for two weeks phases vs six cycles across 3 months.

Outcome measures and efficacy assessment

Data collection assessments were performed at baseline and after three months of treatment. The measured parameters included 3D HR-ARM results, EMG findings, CCC, and PAC-QOL scores before treatment and after 3 months of treatment. Clinical non-response was defined as a post-treatment reduction in the CCC score (0–30 points) of ≤ 30% [28]. The predicted response rate was defined as the R10 + R01 + R11 phenotypes. The efficacy index was calculated using the following formula: ([pre-treatment score – post-treatment score]/pre-treatment score) × 100 [29].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS software (version 22.0; IBM SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Categorical data are presented as counts or percentages, or both. Continuous data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation.

Group Comparisons:For normally distributed data with homogeneous variances, Paired sample t-tests were used for intragroup (baseline vs 3 months) comparisons, and independent sample t-tests were used for between-group comparisons, when assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance were met. The χ2 test was used to compare categorical data.

Risk Factor Analysis

Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify independent risk factors affecting anorectal function outcomes, and logit-regression for risk-stratified phenotypes in the MEBFT and BFT groups after 3 months of treatment.

Hosmer–Rothman additive model stratification in MEBFT.

Non-response (R00): Concurrent elevation of MRP and MTV.

Partial response (R10/R01): Isolated MRP or MTV dysfunction.

Complete response (R11): Concurrent reduction in the MRP and MTV.

Hosmer–Rothman additive interaction models in MEBFT

These models assessed the specific effects of the MRP and MTV. Relative risks (RRs) were calculated for four exposure categories: R00 (no exposure to either factor), R10 (exposure to factor X alone), R01 (exposure to factor Z alone), and R11 (exposure to factors X and Z). Receiver operating characteristics (ROC) curve analysis

The area under the curve (AUC) of the ROC curves was used to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the interaction parameters. Statistical significance was set at a two-tailed P < 0.05 for all analyses.

Discussion

The randomized controlled trial provides a per-protocol preliminary evidence directly addressing this critical gap through the development and application of a phenotype-driven stratification model based on 3D HR-ARM parameters, validated the clinical efficacy of MEBFT for ODS associated with MRW and identified critical indicators for therapeutic stratification after 3 months treatment, particularly in distinguishing surgical candidates from those suitable for conservative management, an issue that has historically led to either overtreatment or delayed intervention.

MEBFT achieved superior functional outcomes compared with conventional BFT, with a clinical response rate of 82.35%. Magnetic neuromodulation combined with electrical myostimulation enhances fast-twitch muscle fiber recruitment, directly targeting ODS pathophysiology. The study identified two key functional parameters derived from 3D HR-ARM as predictive indicators in the MRP and MTV. Using study-derived thresholds (MRP ≥ 80 mmHg vs. < 80 mmHg; MTV ≥ 135 cc vs. < 135 cc), patients were stratified into distinct phenotypic subgroups (R00/R01/R10/R11). The researchers employed a Hosmer-Rothman additive interaction model to quantify the relationship between these phenotypes and the response to MEBFT therapy. The observed negative interaction between MRP and MTV (synergy index = 0.20) suggests a potential “dual-factor modulation” effect requiring further mechanistic validation.

MEBFT demonstrated a predicted response rate of 85.29% and a clinical response rate of 82.35%, which was significantly higher than those of BFT alone, confirming the synergistic benefits of this multimodal therapy after 3 months of treatment. EMG confirmed enhanced fast-twitch fiber recruitment in the MEBFT group, with larger reductions in CCC and PAC-QOL scores. These results align with previous evidence that multimodal neuromuscular stimulation can improve pelvic floor muscle strength and coordination, directly targeting the underlying pathophysiology of MRW-related ODS [10, 13]. However, variability in disease severity, BFT device types, and protocol parameters may explain the inconsistent outcomes in clinical practice [10–13].

Magnetic stimulation (30–50 Hz) likely activates the deep pelvic floor muscles (e.g., the puborectalis muscle) noninvasively. In contrast, electrical stimulation and biofeedback optimize the voluntary external anal sphincter control. This synergy overcomes the limitations of BFT alone, which relies heavily on patient compliance and often fails to engage hypoactive muscles [15].

Furthermore, the phased therapeutic protocol employed in this study (magnetic stimulation → combined magnetoelectric stimulation → BFT training) may have facilitated progressive neuromuscular adaptation, explaining the high response rate. The pathophysiological mechanism of MEBFT involves three key steps: (ⅰ) starting with deep muscle group activation. A pulsed magnetic field (0.5–2.0 T, 10–50 Hz) penetrates 5–8 cm of tissue, inducing eddy currents via electromagnetic induction [30, 31]. These currents stimulate S2–S4 sacral nerve roots, depolarizing neurons to trigger pelvic floor muscle contraction, enhancing strength and endurance while overcoming surface electrical stimulation’s penetration limitations [32, 33]; (ⅱ) combined with biofeedback electrical stimulation-induced neurocontrol remodeling, through the neural reflex arc, it awakens the brain’s control ability over the pelvic floor muscles, improving muscle coordination [34]; and (iii) synergistic disruption of the BFT “efficacy ceiling,” ultimately enhancing fast-twitch fiber recruitment.

The Hosmer–Rothman additive model revealed a negative MRP-MTV interaction (S = 0.20, RERI = − 7, and API = 94.74%), showing that dual restoration of anorectal dysfunction in the R11 phenotype yielded better outcomes than either parameter alone in a moderate-accuracy phenotype (AUC = 0.72). The MRP reflects internal anal sphincter integrity, whereas the MTV assesses rectal sensory-motor function. Single-parameter assessments of MRW (Youden index: 0.17–0.21) were less precise [35]. Sun et al. demonstrated that the pathogenesis of MRW involves an association between pre-defecation anal sphincter pressure elevation and rectocele magnitude [36]. These findings align with the “dual-factor modulation effect” of MRW-related ODS identified in the current study, characterized by the concurrent elevation of MRP and MTV. Conversely, a negative interaction indicates that the concurrent reduction of MRP and MTV synergistically restores normal defecation dynamics; this pathophysiological paradigm constitutes an “activating mechanism” in anorectal dysfunction. This interaction improves the diagnostic precision, potentially reducing unnecessary surgeries by identifying R11 phenotypes with high specificity (85.30%). The clinical utility of this interaction is further supported by the high concordance of the Hosmer–Rothman model with the observed clinical efficacy outcomes.

Based on the model’s predictions and observed outcomes, the study proposed a phenotype-guided management algorithm: (i) selection of optimal candidates: Patients with the R11 phenotype should receive MEBFT with the implementation of the Geng consolidation protocol (administered interval at 8–10 weeks), as the therapeutic effect of BFT can last for more than three months [28], leveraging its high efficacy (82.35%); (ii) identifying surgical candidates: The R00 phenotype warrants consideration for surgical repair (e.g., transanal rectal resection and meshoplasty) [26], as conservative therapy is unlikely to benefit these patients; and (iii) managing partial responders: R10/R01 phenotypes require defecography to assess concomitant lesions (e.g., mucosal prolapse and enterocele) [26], guiding decisions to continue MEBFT for 3 months, and non-responders after this period should undergo targeted surgery. 3D HR-ARM is central to this workflow, enabling both functional assessment and phenotypic stratification, thereby standardizing the treatment selection.

Limitations

This study had some limitations, including a single-center design with a limited sample size (n = 68), a short duration of follow-up and the exploratory nature of the predictive model. The application of the Hosmer-Rothman additive interaction model alongside phenotype stratification (R00/R01/R10/R11) is noteworthy; however, concerns arise due to the relatively limited sample size (n = 34 per group). The inherent procedural distinctions between interventions posed potential risks to participant blinding despite implemented measures. Assessor blinding remained intact throughout the trial. Consequently, caution must be exercised when interpreting the exploratory findings and drawing conclusions from this study. The exploratory Hosmer-Rothman model suggested a moderate-accuracy phenotype stratification (AUC = 0.72) that may inform future research on individualized management, but requires validation before clinical implementation. The phenotype stratification model represents a promising but preliminary framework for precision management of rectocele-associated ODS. Its clinical adoption awaits prospective validation in larger, diverse cohorts.

Conclusions

The randomized controlled trial demonstrated that MEBFT was significantly superior to conventional BFT in treating ODS associated with MRW, with improvements in clinical effectiveness, anorectal functional recovery, and quality of life after 3 months of treatment. The pathophysiological mechanism underlying MEBFT involves multimodal neuromuscular stimulation, which enhances pelvic floor muscle fiber recruitment and synchronization, thereby improving muscular strength and coordination—key factors in restoring anorectal function. This study represents a significant step towards personalized medicine in MRW management. By utilizing readily available functional assessments (3D HR-ARM) to define phenotypes and developing an exploratory predictive model, it provides clinicians with a tool to: (i) confidently select patients with the R11 phenotype for intensive MEBFT, potentially avoiding unnecessary surgery; (ii) recognize patients with the R00 phenotype early, reducing delays in appropriate surgical referral and avoiding prolonged ineffective conservative therapy; and (iii) guide the duration of conservative therapy and timing of surgical consideration for R10/R01 phenotypes. While further validation in larger cohorts is warranted, this phenotype-driven approach based on the interaction of MRP and MTV offers a promising framework to mitigate the historical issues of overtreatment and delayed intervention in MRW-associated ODS.
