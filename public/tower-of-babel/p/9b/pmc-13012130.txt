A CHKA–PML autophagy checkpoint enables tumors to evade glutamine starvation
Authors: Ruijie Wang, Leixi Cao, Xianwei He, Rick Francis Thorne, Qichen Wu, Caoyuan Ding, Jinjing Zhang, Mengfan Li, Zeyuan Shi, Xinyi Dong, Jing Li, Hongxiang Li, Suhua Gao, Jinming Li, Xu Dong Zhang, Lei Jin, Tianli Fan, Mian Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceCancer cells often encounter chronic glutamine deprivation within poorly perfused tumors, yet the mechanisms enabling their survival remain poorly understood. Here we identify a choline kinase alpha (CHKA)–promyelocytic leukemia (PML)–WD Repeat Domain Phosphoinositide-Interacting Protein 2 (WIPI2) signaling axis that couples glutamine stress to autophagy activation. Under glutamine scarcity, CHKA acquires protein kinase activity to phosphorylate PML, driving its cytoplasmic relocalization and stabilization of WIPI2 through SUMOylation. This mechanism enhances autophagic flux and sustains tumor growth in vitro and in vivo. Our findings redefine CHKA as a glutamine-responsive stress sensor, expand the paradigm of metabolic enzymes with moonlighting kinase functions, and highlight a potential therapeutic vulnerability in glutamine-addicted cancers.

Cancer cells frequently experience nutrient scarcity within the tumor microenvironment, in large part due to their exceptionally high metabolic needs and disorganized tumor vasculature (1). To survive these challenging conditions and sustain their uncontrolled proliferation, cancer cells undergo extensive metabolic reprogramming to activate adaptive mechanisms in response to nutrient stress (2). Among essential nutrients, glutamine is of particular importance in supporting tumor growth by fueling the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, supplying nitrogen for nucleotide and amino acid biosynthesis, and maintaining redox homeostasis via nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate hydrogen and glutathione production (3). Consequently, many cancer types exhibit glutamine addiction and are especially vulnerable to glutamine deprivation (4, 5).

Chronic glutamine stress, which commonly occurs in poorly perfused tumor regions, necessitates coordinated survival strategies (2, 6, 7). Autophagy is a major adaptive response that facilitates intracellular recycling of macromolecules and organelles, providing alternative energy and biosynthetic substrates (8). While basal autophagy can be tumor-suppressive by mitigating oxidative damage and maintaining genome stability, stress-induced autophagy often promotes tumor survival and therapeutic resistance (9–11). Despite growing evidence linking glutamine metabolism to autophagy, the regulatory mechanisms governing this process under chronic glutamine starvation remain poorly understood.

Accumulating studies have revealed that metabolic enzymes are not merely passive participants in biosynthesis but can also function as stress-responsive signaling mediators (12, 13). For instance, the glycolytic enzymes PKM2 and PGK1 have been shown to acquire protein kinase activity and regulate gene expression and autophagy (12, 14, 15). Similarly, PFKP and PGAM1 modulate stress responses through noncatalytic protein–protein interactions (16, 17). These findings highlight the emerging class of “moonlighting” metabolic enzymes that integrate nutrient sensing with signaling control.

Choline kinase alpha (CHKA) is a canonical enzyme in the Kennedy pathway, catalyzing the phosphorylation of choline to phosphocholine for phosphatidylcholine synthesis (18). Beyond this role, CHKA has been implicated in promoting tumor progression, therapy resistance, and the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (19, 20). Moreover, CHKA has recently been found to function as a protein kinase under glucose-limiting conditions, promoting selective autophagy of lipid droplets (21). CHKA has not previously been linked to glutamine metabolism, at least directly, although its involvement in stress adaptation and autophagy supports this possibility. that it may also respond to glutamine deprivation. Akin to the responses of cancer cells to glucose availability, we hypothesized that the metabolic stress response associated with glutamine deprivation could similarly activate the protein kinase-like activity of CHKA.

Indeed, we found that CHKA acts as a glutamine-responsive effector with glutamine limiting conditions serving to activate its noncanonical protein kinase activity. Mechanistically, CHKA phosphorylates the promyelocytic leukemia (PML) protein at tyrosine 339, promoting its cytoplasmic accumulation. Cytoplasmic PML in turn catalyzes SUMOylation of the autophagy initiator WIPI2, preventing HUWE1-mediated ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Stabilized WIPI2 enhances autophagic flux and promotes tumor cell survival under glutamine stress. These findings define a CHKA–PML–WIPI2 axis as a critical metabolic adaptation pathway and highlight CHKA as a potential therapeutic target in glutamine-addicted tumors.

Results

CHKA Is Required for Glutamine Deprivation-Induced Autophagy in Cancer Cells.

To investigate the relationship between CHKA and metabolic stress, we compared 20 pan-cancer cell lines under glutamine (Gln) and glucose (Glc) deprivation, along with serum-free conditions to mimic limited growth factor availability. While glucose withdrawal downregulated CHKA and serum deprivation had no effect, glutamine starvation mostly upregulated CHKA (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S1A), highlighting its selective induction under glutamine stress. We further tested whether this upregulation was mediated by asparagine, a glutamine-derived metabolite. Exogenous asparagine did not affect CHKA expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 B and C) nor did knockdown of asparagine synthetase (ASNS), which catalyzes de novo asparagine synthesis (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Together, these results indicate that CHKA is specifically upregulated in response to glutamine deprivation independently of asparagine availability or synthesis, establishing CHKA as a stress-responsive node selective to glutamine limitation.

To understand CHKA’s role during glutamine starvation, we performed quantitative proteomics in wild-type (WT) and CHKA-knockout (KO) SK-HEP-1 cells. CHKA-KO dysregulated autophagy-related pathways (Fig. 1B), a finding confirmed in SK-HEP-1 and MDA-MB-231 cells where autophagy activation following glutamine starvation, as revealed by increased LC3B-II and reduced P62 expression levels, respectively, was reduced after CHKA knockdown (Fig. 1C). Autophagy defects in response to glutamine depletion were even more marked after CHKA KO, with the reintroduction of WT CHKA but not a kinase dead mutant (CHKAD330A/E332A) restoring autophagy (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 E and F). Consistently, overexpression of CHKAWT but not CHKAD330A/E332A promoted increases in autophagic flux (Fig. 1D). Further assessment using the GFP-LC3 reporter confirmed that CHKA knockdown impaired the appearance of cytoplasmic GFP puncta (Fig. 1 E and F), reflecting impaired autophagosome formation and maturation. Last, transmission electron microscopy analyses confirmed marked reductions in autophagosomes and autolysosomes in CHKA-deficient cells under glutamine depletion (Fig. 1G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that CHKA induction enhances the autophagic response to glutamine deprivation in cancer cells, a function that is dependent on its kinase activity.

To determine which stage of autophagy CHKA regulates, we used the GFP-RFP-LC3 tandem reporter, which distinguishes autophagosomes (yellow puncta, GFP+RFP) from autolysosomes (red puncta, GFP-quenched). CHKA KO did not alter the yellow/red puncta ratio during glutamine starvation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1G), suggesting CHKA acts at autophagosome formation rather than at fusion. Consistently, chloroquine (which blocks autolysosomal degradation) markedly reduced GFP-LC3 puncta in CHKA-knockdown cells under glutamine starvation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1H). CHKA knockdown also decreased LC3B-II and increased p62 accumulation (SI Appendix, Fig. S1I), further supporting a role in autophagosome biogenesis.

From our proteomic data, we identified the key autophagy effector WIPI2 as a CHKA-regulated protein, subsequently validating that its levels were posttranscriptionally downregulated in CHKA-knockdown or KO SK-HEP-1 cells under glutamine starvation (Fig. 1 H–K). However, CHKA and WIPI2 did not interact directly, as shown by coimmunoprecipitation and proximity ligation assays (PLA) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 J and K), pointing to an indirect, posttranscriptional mode of regulation. The kinase-dead CHKAD330A/E332A mutant failed to restore WIPI2 levels in CHKA-KO cells (Fig. 1L), demonstrating that CHKA regulates WIPI2 stability in a kinase activity-dependent manner, consistent with its role in autophagy regulation. We further tested whether these functions depend on CHKA’s canonical role in choline metabolism. However, silencing key enzymes in the Kennedy and choline metabolism pathways (CCT2 and CHPT1, respectively) together with the alternative CHKB isoform did not affect WIPI2 expression or autophagy markers under glutamine replete or depleted conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 L–N). Furthermore, CHKA overexpression did not alter mTOR or AMPK signaling (Fig. 1 M and N), excluding major nutrient-sensing pathways.

Together, these data establish that CHKA promotes WIPI2 stabilization and autophagosome formation under glutamine deprivation through a kinase-dependent mechanism that operates outside canonical nutrient-sensing pathways.

CHKA Promotes Autophagy by Modulating PML Cytoplasmic Localization under Glutamine Deprivation.

To identify the link between CHKA and WIPI2, we mapped the CHKA-protein interactome during glutamine starvation, identifying 10 autophagy-related interactors (22–31) including PML and CCT2 (Fig. 2A). Immunoblotting confirmed CHKA interacted with four of the 10 candidates (Fig. 2B), but among these, only PML knockdown significantly reduced WIPI2 levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A–D). Notably, endogenous CHKA and WIPI2 were recovered within PML immunoprecipitates, suggesting a mutual regulatory complex (Fig. 2C). Additional cotransfection assays with epitope-tagged constructs established that interactions occur between PML and CHKA, and PML and WIPI2, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 E–H). Deletion-mapping showed that the CHKA 375-457 region and the C-terminal domain of PML were critical for their binding (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 I–L). The RB (RING-B box) domain of PML, which harbors its E3 ligase module, was identified as essential for interaction with WIPI2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2M).

To establish the hierarchical relationship between CHKA and PML in autophagy regulation during glutamine deprivation, we performed loss- and gain-of-function experiments. Knockdown of PML suppressed autophagy induction under glutamine deprivation with reductions in LC3B-II levels and p62 accumulation as well as decreases in GFP-LC3 puncta (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 N and O). Conversely, PML overexpression promoted autophagy (SI Appendix, Fig. S2P) while also rescuing autophagic impairment in CHKA knockdown cells (Fig. 2D). However, CHKA overexpression was unable to correct the autophagy defect in PML knockdown cells (Fig. 2E), placing CHKA upstream of PML. In contrast, ectopic WIPI2 overexpression fully restored autophagic flux in PML knockdown cells cultured without glutamine (Fig. 2F), positioning WIPI2 as the principal downstream effector.

We next examined how CHKA regulates PML, which typically localizes to nuclear bodies while CHKA is cytoplasmic. Glutamine starvation enhanced CHKA–PML interactions (Fig. 2G) independently of PML expression levels, which were unchanged by CHKA manipulation (Fig. 2H). We therefore hypothesized that glutamine deprivation facilitates CHKA–PML interaction by altering their subcellular localization. Supporting this, cell fractionation revealed increased cytoplasmic PML upon glutamine depletion (Fig. 2 I and J). This PML redistribution persisted despite Leptomycin B treatment (Fig. 2K), implying glutamine deprivation impairs PML nuclear import rather than enhancing its export. Indeed, CHKA knockdown restored PML nuclear localization under glutamine starvation conditions (Fig. 2L), showing disrupted PML nuclear import coincided with increases in CHKA–PML interactions. Consistently, PLAs detecting CHKA–PML proximity showed increased cytoplasmic foci following glutamine removal (Fig. 2 M and N). Similarly, PLA recorded mainly cytoplasmic interactions between PML and WIPI2 (Fig. 2O).

This work identifies a CHKA–PML–WIPI2 axis that activates autophagy during glutamine deprivation: CHKA recruits PML to the cytoplasm, enabling PML to stabilize WIPI2 and drive autophagosome formation. However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying CHKA-mediated PML trafficking and the PML–WIPI2 functional interaction required further investigation.

CHKA Functions as a Protein Kinase Phosphorylating PML under Glutamine Stress.

Multiple posttranslational modifications (acetylation, SUMOylation, phosphorylation) cooperatively regulate PML nuclear localization and nuclear body assembly (32–34). To explain the cytoplasmic retention of PML, we hypothesized that CHKA binding influences its PTMs. Indeed, under glutamine deprivation, PML showed reduced acetylation and SUMOylation, markedly increased tyrosine phosphorylation but no change in serine/threonine phosphorylation, compared to normal conditions (Fig. 3A). Instructively, PML tyrosine phosphorylation was completely absent in CHKA-KO cells under limiting glutamine conditions (Fig. 3B), whereas overexpression of CHKAWT, but not the kinase-dead CHKAD330/E332A, rescued PML tyrosine phosphorylation in CHKAKO cells (Fig. 3C), confirming the requirement of CHKA’s kinase activity.

CHKA homodimerization is required for its lipid (choline) kinase activity, whereas emerging evidence indicates that CHKA monomers can function as protein kinases (21, 35). The increase in total CHKA protein levels upon glutamine withdrawal was primarily associated with monomers (Fig. 3D) and was accompanied by reduced choline kinase activity (Fig. 3E), consistent with a shift toward its protein kinase function. CHKAKO cells reconstituted with a dimerization-deficient CHKAE97R mutant retained residual choline kinase activity, whereas those expressing the CHKAD330A/E332A mutant were catalytically inert (Fig. 3F). Importantly, ectopic CHKAE97R expression resulted in PML tyrosine hyperphosphorylation, while CHKAD330/E332A failed to phosphorylate PML (Fig. 3G). Furthermore, CHKAE97R enhanced PML phosphorylation irrespective of glutamine availability (Fig. 3H). And consistent with a role for CHKA protein kinase activity in the redistribution of PML under glutamine deprivation, reconstitution of CHKAKO cells with CHKAE97R accentuated increases in the cytoplasmic to nuclear PML ratio over CHKAWT, whereas CHKAD330A/E332A showed no effect on PML localization (Fig. 3I). Taken together, these results demonstrate that CHKA’s protein kinase activity, not its choline kinase activity, drives PML tyrosine phosphorylation and its functional redistribution to the cytoplasm.

In vitro kinase assays with recombinant CHKA confirmed its direct phosphorylation of PML tyrosine residues (Fig. 3J), with reaction specificity validated by ATP-dependent ADP production (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A), thus establishing CHKA’s protein kinase activity. Mass spectrometry identified three candidate phosphotyrosine sites (Y339, Y826, Y849) in PML, with Y339 and Y849 being evolutionarily conserved (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 B and C). Mutational analysis revealed that the PMLY339A mutant but not PMLY849A abolished CHKA-mediated phosphorylation (Fig. 3K). Furthermore, tyrosine phosphorylation was absent in PMLY339A-expressed CHKAKO cells alongside either CHKAWT or CHKAE97R (Fig. 3L). Together, these results demonstrate that CHKA specifically phosphorylates PML at Y339 under glutamine stress.

Cytoplasmic PML Stabilizes WIPI2 by Inhibiting HUWE1-Mediated Proteasomal Degradation under Glutamine Starvation.

Our preliminary investigations showed that WIPI2 protein stability was increased by glutamine starvation (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A). In contrast, PML knockdown reduced WIPI2 stability (Fig. 4A) independently of WIPI2 mRNA levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). Moreover, WIPI2 downregulation upon CHKA or PML knockdown was prevented by the proteasome inhibitor MG132 (Fig. 4B) but not by the lysosome inhibitor chloroquine (SI Appendix, Fig. S4C), indicating that PML promotes WIPI2 degradation via the proteasome.

Although PML contains a canonical E3 ligase domain, it unexpectedly inhibited rather than promoted WIPI2 ubiquitination in cellular assays (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D) and failed to directly ubiquitinate WIPI2 in vitro (SI Appendix, Fig. S4E). Furthermore, the CHKAE97R mutant but not CHKAD330A/E332A stabilized WIPI2 by inhibiting its ubiquitination and extending its half-life, independent of glutamine availability (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 F and G). These findings led us to investigate whether PML might function by competing with or regulating another E3 ligase. Building on previous work identifying HUWE1 as an E3 ubiquitin ligase targeting WIPI2 (36), we found that glutamine deprivation significantly enhanced PML–WIPI2 interactions while concurrently reducing WIPI2–HUWE1 binding (Fig. 4C). Moreover, PML overexpression reduced HUWE1–WIPI2 interactions (Fig. 4D), suggesting that PML stabilizes WIPI2 by competitively interfering with HUWE1-mediated regulatory interactions.

To determine how PML’s subcellular localization regulates the WIPI2–HUWE1 axis during glutamine starvation, we engineered compartment-specific PML mutants: cytoplasmic-targeted NES-PML and nucleus-targeted NLS-PML (SI Appendix, Fig. S4H). In CHKA-knockdown cells, depleted WIPI2 levels were restored by cytoplasmic NES-PML but not nuclear NLS-PML (Fig. 4E), pointing to cytoplasmic PML as the mediator of WIPI2 stabilization. To further test this model, we used UTR-targeting shRNAs to selectively deplete cytoplasmic PML. Under glutamine deprivation, combining nuclear NLS-PML overexpression with cytoplasmic PML depletion (Fig. 4F) impaired autophagy, reduced WIPI2 levels (Fig. 4G), enhanced HUWE1–WIPI2 binding, and decreased WIPI2 stability (Fig. 4 H and I). Conversely, depleting nuclear PML using NES-PML (Fig. 4J) did not affect autophagic flux, HUWE1 interactions, or WIPI2 stability (Fig. 4 K–M).

Thus, cytoplasmic PML is both necessary and sufficient for WIPI2 stabilization during glutamine stress, achieved by competitively inhibiting HUWE1-mediated proteasomal degradation.

Cytoplasmic PML Stabilizes WIPI2 by Mediating Its SUMO3ylation at K281/283.

PML functions as both a SUMOylation scaffold and, via its canonical RING finger domain, a potential E3 ubiquitin ligase. Although direct ubiquitin ligase function remains unverified, these activities may operate sequentially or cooperatively via interactions with partner ligases (37–39). The ability of PML to inhibit WIPI2 ubiquitination led us to speculate that SUMOylation was involved in WIPI2 regulation under glutamine deprivation. Indeed, glutamine withdrawal markedly increased WIPI2 SUMO2/3ylation, particularly a ~100 kDa species suggestive of SUMO chain formation, while SUMO1ylation was undetectable (Fig. 5A). Moreover, the same SUMO2/3 band was evident after ectopic CHKAWT expression, enhanced by the CHKAE97R mutant but absent after CHKAD330A/E332A expression (Fig. 5B). Likewise, PMLWT overexpression promoted WIPI2 SUMO2/3ylation while the phosphomimetic PMLY339D and nonphosphorylatable PMLY339A mutants displayed greatly enhanced and limited activity, respectively (Fig. 5C). Together, these experiments uncover important links between CHKA protein kinase activity, consequent Y339 PML phosphorylation and the facilitation of WIPI2 SUMOylation.

Cotransfection assays using epitope-tagged SUMO paralogs and UBC9 revealed that WIPI2 is preferentially modified by SUMO3 rather than SUMO2, as confirmed by the failure of a catalytically inactive SUMO3 mutant to support SUMOylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 A and B). The requirement for an active E2 conjugase was further demonstrated by the inability of the UBC9C93S mutant to facilitate WIPI2 SUMOylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5C), indicating that this process follows canonical enzymatic mechanisms.

To establish the causal hierarchy within the CHKA–PML–WIPI2 axis, we systematically tested whether PML serves as the direct executor of WIPI2 SUMOylation. Reconstituting the system with WT PML significantly enhanced WIPI2 SUMO3ylation (SI Appendix, Fig. S5D) while more strikingly, the inclusion of the PMLY339D or CHKAE97R mutants led to the hyper-SUMO3ylation of WIPI2 (Fig. 5 D and E). Most conclusively, in vitro SUMOylation assays demonstrated that purified recombinant PML directly catalyzed WIPI2 SUMOylation (Fig. 5F), revealing PML as the direct SUMO ligase for WIPI2 in this pathway.

To map SUMOylation sites on WIPI2, we performed mass spectrometry, which revealed two closely spaced, evolutionarily conserved lysines as the major modification sites (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 E–G). Individually mutating the K281 and K283 sites partially reduced SUMOylation while SUMOylation was largely abolished with a double K281R/K283R mutant (Fig. 5G). Moreover, WIPI2K281R/K283R protein stability was significantly diminished (Fig. 5H), and neither PML nor CHKA overexpression was effective in rescuing reduced WIPI2 SUMOylation levels (Fig. 5I). Instructively, time-course experiments established the temporal sequence of events: glutamine deprivation triggers an initial upregulation of CHKA expression, which is followed by increases in PML tyrosine phosphorylation, and subsequent elevations in WIPI2 SUMOylation and expression (Fig. 5J).

To investigate ubiquitination-SUMOylation crosstalk in WIPI2 regulation, we noted that the K283 SUMO site coincides with a known HUWE1 ubiquitination site [K265 in WIPI2b; (36)], indicating potential competition, though the functional significance of the adjacent K281 site requires further study (SI Appendix, Fig. S5H). Analysis of the WIPI2b-ATG16L1 crystal structure [PDB: 7XFR (40)] revealed that the K281 SUMOylation site is too distant (>49 Å) to influence ubiquitination at K146 (K128) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 I–L). However, K281 lies in close proximity (~18 Å) to a second reported ubiquitination site K237 (K219) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 M and N), suggesting that SUMOylation at K281 could sterically hinder ubiquitin conjugation at the adjacent K219 residue. These observations support a model wherein SUMOylation, promoted by the CHKA–PML axis, protects WIPI2 from HUWE1-mediated degradation through two potential mechanisms: direct site competition at the overlapping K283 site and steric hindrance from the proximal K281 modification. This PML–CHKA–WIPI2 regulatory axis thus enhances WIPI2 stability to sustain autophagy during glutamine deprivation.

The CHKA–PML–WIPI2 Signaling Axis Promotes Tumor Survival under Glutamine Deprivation Stress.

We returned to examine how CHKA signaling aligns with the paradigm of cancer cell growth and survival under glutamine stress. Toward this, we employed in vitro and in vivo models in conjunction with the experimental tools developed in our preceding experiments. We first compared the growth and viability of SK-HEP-1 and MD-MBA-231 cells with CHKA wildtype and KO backgrounds, further integrating ectopic expression of Flag-WIPI2 (Fig. 6 A and B and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A and B). Under normal conditions, CHKAKO cells showed impaired proliferation relative to CHKAWT, which was unaffected by WIPI2 overexpression. Prolonged glutamine deprivation generally suppressed growth across all lines, with the defect in CHKAKO cells remaining pronounced. Notably, ectopic WIPI2 expression selectively rescued the growth impairment in CHKAKO cells under glutamine deprivation and further enhanced the viability of CHKAWT cells. Together these findings support the notion that CHKA promotes cell survival under nutrient stress via WIPI2.

We next tested the functional impact of CHKA and PML signaling mutants on tumor cell growth. Under normal glutamine conditions, overexpression of CHKAWT enhanced proliferation relative to CHKAE97R, while CHKAD330A/E332A had no effect (Fig. 6 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 C and D). Notably, under glutamine deprivation, both CHKAWT and CHKAE97R promoted growth, with CHKAE97R providing a more pronounced survival advantage. PML mutants did not affect proliferation under normal conditions (Fig. 6 E and F and SI Appendix, Fig. S6 E and F), but under glutamine stress, the phosphomimetic PMLY339D significantly enhanced growth and viability over PMLWT, while the phospho-ablative PMLY339A was indistinguishable from controls.

The survival advantage conferred by CHKAWT, CHKAE97R, PMLWT, and PMLY339D, during glutamine deprivation was blocked by the autophagy inhibitor 3-MA (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 G–J), even though WIPI2 induction remained intact. Moreover, 3-MA had no effect on growth under nutrient-replete conditions but selectively reduced viability under glutamine stress (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 K and L). Together, these data indicate that the CHKA–PML–WIPI2 axis promotes tumor cell survival under glutamine deprivation specifically by activating autophagy, rather than through autophagy-independent induction of WIPI2.

To validate the physiological relevance of the CHKA–PML–WIPI2 axis, we established SK-HEP-1 xenograft models, selected for their heightened sensitivity to glutamine deprivation. Consistent with in vitro findings, xenografts from mice fed a glutamine-deficient diet exhibited elevated CHKA and WIPI2 protein levels, accompanied by increased LC3B-II, indicative of enhanced autophagic flux (SI Appendix, Fig. S6M). Notably, CHKA KO attenuated this autophagic response, with reduced LC3B-II levels and obvious P62 accumulation paralleled by decreases in WIPI2 expression in xenografts from mice maintained on glutamine-deficient chow (SI Appendix, Fig. S6N). As anticipated, CHKAKO xenografts were smaller than CHKAWT xenografts while WIPI2 overexpression in CHKAKO cells proved sufficient to restore tumor growth to control levels while also enhancing the growth of CHKAWT xenografts (Fig. 6G and SI Appendix, Fig. S6O).

The in vivo impact of CHKA and PML signaling activity was further evidenced by tumor growth following mutant reconstitution. Tumors expressing CHKAE97R grew more aggressively than those expressing CHKAWT, while CHKAD330A/E332A tumors matched control growth (Fig. 6H and SI Appendix, Fig. S6P), highlighting the specific requirement for CHKA’s protein kinase, not choline kinase, activity under glutamine stress. Similarly, tumors expressing the phosphomimetic PMLY339D, and to a lesser extent PMLWT, exhibited enhanced growth (Fig. 6I and SI Appendix, Fig. S6Q), underscoring the functional importance of PML phosphorylation in vivo.

As a corollary to our investigations, we considered if CHKA impinged on lipid droplet degradation (lipolysis), a key process involved in the autophagy-dependent survival response under nutrient stress (21, 41, 42). Glutamine restriction reduced lipid droplets in WT SK-HEP-1 xenografts but increased them in CHKAKO tumors (SI Appendix, Fig. S6R). Ectopic Flag-WIPI2 expression reduced lipid droplets in both backgrounds, restoring levels in CHKA-KO tumors to baseline WT amounts (Fig. 6J), indicating impaired lipolysis due to defective autophagy in CHKA-deficient cells. Further corroborating these findings, lipid droplet staining in xenografts overexpressing CHKA mutants reflected their growth potential under glutamine restriction: CHKAE97R caused the greatest depletion, followed by CHKAWT, while CHKAD330A/E332A matched control levels (Fig. 6K). Likewise, overexpression of either PMLWT or PMLY339D potently depleted tissue lipid levels with PMLY339A xenografts displaying similar levels to controls (Fig. 6L).

Clinical Implications of the CHKA–PML–WIPI2 Signaling Axis.

Last, we sought to explore the potential clinical relevance of CHKA and its newly identified partners. Bioinformatic interrogation of TCGA pan-cancer data using Kaplan–Meier survival analyses revealed that elevated CHKA mRNA expression was associated with poorer overall survival across several cancer types, including hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) (43), breast cancer (BRCA), adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC), and ovarian cancer (OV) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6S). Nonetheless, as our regulatory model involves PTMs, protein level assessments are more relevant. Accessing the CPTAC database uncovered significant positive correlations between CHKA and WIPI2 protein levels in liver cancer (LIHC, r = 0.1663, P = 0.0362) and adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC, r = 0.6131, P = 0.0001), but not in breast cancer (BRCA, r = 0.02312, P = 0.7989) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6T). Immunoblotting analysis of 35 paired liver cancer samples showed that CHKA was significantly upregulated in tumors compared to adjacent normal tissue, whereas WIPI2 levels remained unchanged (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 U and V). Moreover, CHKA and WIPI2 protein levels were strongly correlated (r = 0.5074, P = 0.0019) (SI Appendix, Fig. S6W), consistent with the fundamental expectation of our model.

Together, these results support a model whereby CHKA acts as a protein kinase under glutamine stress to phosphorylate PML at Y339 and drive its cytoplasmic localization, where it stabilizes WIPI2 and promotes autophagy-dependent tumor survival (Fig. 7).

Discussion

The mechanisms by which tumor cells adapt to glutamine deprivation remain incompletely understood, particularly in terms of how metabolic enzymes integrate nutrient stress signals beyond their canonical functions (13). Here, we identify a previously unrecognized CHKA–PML–WIPI2 signaling axis that links glutamine availability to autophagy activation through posttranslational regulation. Under glutamine starvation, CHKA undergoes a functional switch from a choline kinase to a protein kinase that phosphorylates PML at tyrosine 339. This promotes the cytoplasmic translocation of PML, enabling it to act as a SUMO E3 ligase that modifies WIPI2 at lysines 281 and 283. SUMOylation at these sites prevents WIPI2 from interacting with the E3 ubiquitin ligase HUWE1, blocking its ubiquitin–proteasome-mediated degradation and stabilizing WIPI2 to support autophagosome formation. This pathway operates independently of canonical mTOR or AMPK signaling, establishing a paradigm for glutamine sensing and autophagy regulation.

Our findings expand the landscape of metabolic enzymes with moonlighting kinase activity. Similar to PKM2 and HK2 (44, 45), which phosphorylate substrates to regulate cellular processes beyond glycolysis, CHKA, upon monomerization, exposes a cryptic kinase domain that selectively targets PMLY339. This phosphorylation rewires PML’s function from organizing nuclear bodies to engaging in cytoplasmic signaling, stabilizing WIPI2 through SUMO3ylation. The spatial and functional repurposing of PML illustrates how nutrient stress can reprogram multifunctional proteins to fulfill context-specific roles. Importantly, WIPI2 SUMOylation introduces an additional regulatory layer to autophagy control, complementing the known mTORC1–HUWE1 axis. While mTORC1 promotes WIPI2 degradation via S395 phosphorylation and subsequent HUWE1 recruitment (36), PML-mediated SUMOylation at K281/K283 protects WIPI2 from degradation, revealing a dynamic interplay between ubiquitination and SUMOylation in autophagic regulation.

We observed a striking divergence in CHKA regulation under distinct nutrient stress conditions: glutamine limitation led to CHKA upregulation, whereas glucose deprivation suppressed its expression across most cancer cell lines. These findings argue against a role for CHKA as a general nutrient sensor and instead support its function as a glutamine specific effector that promotes survival under glutamine stress, operating outside canonical nutrient-sensing pathways. Intriguingly, a prior study demonstrated that under glucose limiting conditions, monomeric CHKA translocates to lipid droplets and phosphorylates PLIN2/3 to stimulate lipolysis and support tumor growth (21). These authors did not find that glucose deprivation affected CHKA expression levels, although their experimental conditions involving oleic acid preloading may explain this difference. Regardless, CHKA recruitment to lipid droplets is not mediated by classical trafficking motifs but rather by a posttranslational conformational switch involving AMPK catalyzed phosphorylation and KAT5 acetylation. Together, these context-dependent responses highlight CHKA’s broader role in integrating nutrient cues through both transcriptional and spatial mechanisms to modulate metabolic plasticity and promote tumor cell adaptation.

Therapeutically, the CHKA–PML–WIPI2 axis presents multiple potential intervention points in glutamine-addicted cancers. Compounds that stabilize CHKA’s dimeric conformation may selectively inhibit its protein kinase activity without affecting choline metabolism, while molecules disrupting the PML–WIPI2 interaction may impair SUMOylation-dependent autophagy. However, achieving target specificity remains a challenge, given CHKA’s dual enzymatic functions and PML’s pleiotropic roles. Notably, we found that the CHKA-E97R mutant, which mimics the monomeric kinase-active form, further enhances tumor growth in vivo under glutamine-limiting conditions. This suggests that hyperactivation, rather than inhibition, of this pathway might paradoxically be exploited to sensitize tumors to glutamine-targeting therapies, such as glutaminase inhibitors.

Several important questions emerge from our study. What upstream signals initiate CHKA monomerization in response to glutamine deprivation? Although AMPK-mediated phosphorylation at S279 is known to promote CHKA monomerization (21), other stress signals, such as hypoxia or acidosis, may also converge on this axis. Additionally, does cytoplasmic PML coordinate with specific subcellular compartments during autophagy? Given its roles at mitochondria-associated membranes (MAMs), PML may facilitate lipid mobilization or autophagosome, ER tethering (45). Moreover, how does WIPI2 SUMOylation affect its interaction with PI3P-binding proteins such as ATG16L1? Superresolution imaging or proximity-based proteomics may uncover the spatial dynamics of these complexes. Finally, clinical data from CPTAC and patient samples reveal a strong correlation between CHKA and WIPI2 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma and adrenocortical carcinoma, suggesting their functional cooperation may be exploited for biomarker-guided therapy.

This study redefines CHKA as a metabolic stress sensor that couples glutamine deprivation to autophagy activation through a noncanonical kinase cascade. By uncovering the cytoplasmic function of PML in WIPI2 SUMOylation and its antagonism of HUWE1-mediated degradation, we provide mechanistic insights into how posttranslational modifications orchestrate autophagy under nutrient stress. More broadly, our work highlights the remarkable plasticity of metabolic enzymes and tumor suppressors in adapting to microenvironmental challenges, offering promising avenues for targeted therapies in metabolically vulnerable cancers.

Data Processing and Analysis.

All experiments were independently performed in triplicate. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. Statistical analyses were carried out using Microsoft Excel 2016 and GraphPad Prism 8.
