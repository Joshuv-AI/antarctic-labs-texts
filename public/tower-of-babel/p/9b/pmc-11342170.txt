Protocol for calculating binding free energy of RNA:RNA interactions through molecular dynamics simulations using adaptive biasing force technique
Authors: Takeru Kameda, Daniel K Saha, Sourav Ray, Yuichi Togashi, Katsura Asano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe adaptive biasing force (ABF) technique allows sampling to proceed in a flat free energy surface when performing molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Here, we present a protocol to perform MD simulations using the ABF technique and apply it to calculate the binding free energy of an RNA:RNA interaction. We describe steps for server setup, test running software, and building molecular models. We then detail procedures for running and configuring ABF-MD simulations and analyzing binding free energy and structural change.For complete details on the use and execution of this protocol, please refer to Fujita et al.1 and Kameda et al.2Subject areas: Biophysics, Biotechnology and bioengineering, Computer sciences

Before you begin

RNA lies at the heart of life.3,4,5 Most of the interactions critical for RNA function are mediated by base-pairing interactions between short stretches of complementary RNA sequences, typically of 3 or 4 bases.6,7,8,9 Here we describe a protocol for computing binding free energy of such RNA:RNA interactions through molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, using codon:anticodon interaction within the ribosomal pre-initiation complex (PIC) P-site as an example. In eukaryotic initiation, the start codon selection is strictly monitored by mRNA triplet binding by the anticodon of tRNAiMet and neighboring eukaryotic initiation factors (eIFs).10 In MD simulations, the assessment of the free energy of binding between nucleic acid segments has been challenging due to difficulties in sampling all the possible conformational states, much wider varieties than those assumed for proteins.11 An importance sampling method termed adaptive biasing force (ABF) method can resolve these difficulties. This method applies varying forces to the system, such that sampling can proceed in a flat free energy surface.12 After sampling is complete, the free energy of binding can be computed from the potential of mean force (PMF; defined here as free energy landscape).

To perform MD simulations, we employed a cryo-EM structure of a partial yeast 48S PIC5 (Protein Data Bank [PDB] ID: 3J81) with a total molecular mass of 1,364.52 kDa.13 To reduce computational cost of performing the MD simulations using the ABF method, we built its spherically clipped structure, which has a total molecular mass of 121.79 kDa, by extracting residues within 25 Å radius of N1 atom of tRNAiMet 35-Ade, the middle base of the anticodon, as described previously2 (Table 1; Figure 1A). To evaluate the energetic penalties imposed by placing a non-AUG start codon in the P-site, we replaced the relevant nucleotide of the spherical P-site model with the mismatched nucleotide of the codon. The protocol described here is applicable to any RNA:RNA interaction crucial for the function of RNA-made machinery and its mutagenesis studies.

Timing: minutes to hours

Timing: several minutes to few hours depending on the system

Steps for preparing structure file are shown below.

Note: The structure file contains specific information on how to apply a particular force field to the system. Energy minimization

Timing: minutes to hours depending on the system

In this step, the potential energy of the system is minimized to avoid numerical instability.

Note: When the bases are substituted, the initial conformation of some atoms may be energetically very unstable; hence, attention to the behavior in this energy minimization stage is required. After that, velocities of the atoms are reinitialized by sampling from a Boltzmann distribution based on the specific temperature that is set for the system.

Simulation using the adaptive biasing force (ABF) method

Timing: days to months depending on the system

Here are the steps for running ABF simulation runs.

Note: The adaptive biasing force (ABF) method ensures all different states are sampled, including states that would otherwise not have been sampled.12,18,19 This step is crucial for performing downstream analysis of molecular dynamics results, particularly ones that rely on extensive sampling of the system.

The collective variable-based calculations (Colvars) module in NAMD is used in this step. The definition of each collective variable (CV), with its range and constraints, should be properly written in the configuration file. Note that the configuration must be prepared separately, in the file <distance>.in in addition to the standard configuration file for MD (see step 13). See Figure 2 for examples of triplet base-pairing interactions, as used in1 and2; in this case, to analyze the interactions in codon-anticodon binding in detail, the base-to-base distance at each base-pair is adopted as a CV (i.e., 3 CVs in total). It is also recommended to test run the ABF method for a small system before applying it to the target. Tutorials are available at the developer’s website (https://www.ks.uiuc.edu/Training/Tutorials/namd/ABF/tutorial-abf.pdf). From NAMD version 2.14, the potential of mean force (PMF) for the CV space up to 3-dimension (i.e., defined by 3 CVs) is automatically calculated and output as .pmf file. Expected outcomes

In computational biology, it is crucial to design experiments such that the outcome of computational calculation is evaluated by independent biological/biochemical measurements. In this study, we calculated the binding free energy of anticodon interaction with various triplet start codons computationally and compared it to the binding free energy of anticodon interaction with the authentic AUG start codon. The difference (ΔΔG) was defined as free energy penalty for having a mismatch in the start codon nucleotides. ΔΔG was then compared to initiation frequencies from these codons in human cells. As shown in Figure 5, we obtained strong correlation between the calculated free energy penalties and initiation frequencies. Essentially, the free energy of binding is considered as the log of the ratio of bound versus unbound states. The initiation frequency is proportional to the ratio of mRNA molecules available for initiation versus those that are not,20,21 hence the expected correlation. Once the outcome is verified to be biologically relevant, trajectory (DCD) and potential of mean force (PMF) files obtained from computational simulations will become a useful resource for further in-depth analyses of binding kinetics and structural requirements, as we reported.2 In the general case of RNA biology, the biological relevance should be derived from mutational studies of base-pairing interactions crucial to translational regulation or the function of the RNA-made machinery.

Limitations

The MD runs using the ABF method require running to continue until a desired outcome has been achieved; every bin should be sufficiently sampled to obtain a correct free-energy landscape. Performing MD simulations using ABF is, therefore, computationally demanding, often requiring long computation time even for a system containing relatively small numbers of atoms, and therefore can be limiting for some users. The convergence of the PMF is not always straightforward. Particularly with 3 or more CVs, the free-energy landscape may show many local minima and only slowly converges, which requires multiple long simulation runs and results in high computational costs. Furthermore, the free energy landscape of systems with similar molecules could be quite different. The behavior may depend on molecular details; for example, in our PIC models, the AUG case converged well while the CUG case only slowly progressed.

Working with clipped structures containing only a specific region of the molecules of interest, we are not able to evaluate the contributions of molecules at the edges of the clipped structures as those typically need to be restrained. Moreover, the motion of atoms connected to these restrained atoms are also affected by the restraint, hence the model structure should include sufficiently thick “buffer zone” covering the region of interest. In our case, the mRNA nucleotide corresponding to the Kozak consensus at −3 from the start codon is located at the edge of the clipped structure and thus we cannot evaluate its contribution in this setting.

ABF MD simulation can be drastically accelerated by the use of GPUs, although the use of GPUs for NAMD with some functions is still being improved, and also limited to NVIDIA GPUs with CUDA. When choosing GPUs, generally, the performance of single-precision floating-point (FP32) is most important for MD simulation; some GPUs specially designed for artificial intelligence applications are not very fast in this regard.

Troubleshooting

Problem 1

The run stops because of memory error.

Potential solution

Determine the amount of main memory (random-access memory; RAM) needed for the molecular dynamics runs. Since the amount of memory used by NAMD tends to increase as the run progresses when ABF is on (due to memory leak), it might be a good idea to divide a single run into several segments, especially for long ABF MD simulations. After each segment has been finished, restart the calculation. In a different setting, we also divided a 1,000 ns run into 20 sections, 50 ns each, which typically consumed about 100 GB of memory. It is also useful if the machine time for each job is limited on the shared supercomputer.

Problem 2

An error occurred at some point after the molecular dynamics simulations started.

Potential solution

Error messages in the NAMD log (stdout typically redirected into a log file) help point out the problem. In order to make the best use of resources available, restart the run from the time-step in which the data was last recorded. The ∗.xsc file stores the specific time-step. For ABF runs, the ∗.colvars.state file must also have the data for the exact same time-step, and only then an ABF simulation can be restarted from that time-step.

Problem 3

The runs are taking a very long time.

Potential solution

We recommend using a GPU whenever possible to improve the speed. It might be helpful to do an optimization by increasing the number of cores in the CPU with and without GPU to find the setting that works best for the system and the architecture at which the simulation is performed.

Problem 4

The ΔGbinding computed from the ABF simulation is different from the value determined or inferred from direct biochemical or biological measurements.

Potential solution

One possibility is that molecules could be missing in the structure as key interactions, thus potentially affecting the computed binding free energy (e.g., allostery might be absent). Sample preparation for structure determination using techniques, such as cryo-EM, often requires isolation of the molecules in a certain environment, which could be very different from the physiological environment inside the cell, and thus the determined structure might not be an accurate depiction of the reality. Moreover, we have used a 25 Å spherical region around the codon-anticodon region of interest in our MD simulations (Table 1), which is a rather arbitrary cutoff. The base-pairs might be dislodged from the spherical limit. Thus, it is plausible that choosing a different value or shape for the boundary could potentially lead to a different, more relevant value of the calculated binding free energy. Furthermore, technical issues such as insufficient sampling due to high conformational flexibility and presence of too many unsampled states can always limit the relevance of the outcome from simulations. In order to avoid some of these pitfalls, we recommend examining the simulation trajectories e.g., by looking at their structures using VMD. It is always recommended to run at least several simulation trials; note that, as the MD simulation involves random numbers (to determine the initial velocities etc.), the results are not identical for trials with the same configuration.
