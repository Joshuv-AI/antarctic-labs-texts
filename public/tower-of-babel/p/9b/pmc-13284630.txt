Survival by Detection Pathway in Patients With Lung Cancer—A Nationwide Descriptive Epidemiological Study in Japan
Authors: Ryo Koto, Atsuki Yamamoto, Ikumi Takashima, Chikako Iwao, Jung‐ho Shin, Yuko Hayashi, Hiroshi Kitagawa, Yoshiharu Horie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGreater understanding of the prognosis for patients with lung cancer in Japan according to detection pathway would clarify the effect of population‐based screening programs and the related diagnostic environment. We explored the clinical characteristics and net survival (NS) of patients with lung cancer by detection pathway with Japanese National Cancer Registry (NCR) database of patients registered between 2016 and 2020. Eligible patients were categorized based on detection pathway: Screening, Incidental, and Other groups. We estimated 1, 2, and 3 year NS. Of 565,044 patients with lung cancer in the NCR, 563,244 met the eligibility criteria and were included in this study: 95,081 patients in the Screening (16.9%), 254,569 in the Incidental (45.2%), and 200,850 in the Other groups (35.7%). In the Screening, Incidental, and Other groups, 69.5%, 88.6%, and 83.4% of patients, respectively, were aged ≥ 65 years, and 50.5%, 49.6%, and 14.0% of patients, respectively, had localized disease at diagnosis. The most common initial treatment was surgery in the Screening (62.5%) and Incidental (46.2%) groups, while chemotherapy was most frequently used in the Other group (48.6%). The 3 year NS was 75.2% (95% confidence interval: 74.9%–75.6%) in the Screening group, 56.3% (56.0%–56.6%) in the Incidental group, and 29.4% (29.2%–29.7%) in the Other group. In the subgroup analysis, 3 year NS for localized disease was 96.3% (95.9%–96.7%), 83.9% (83.6%–84.3%), and 76.9% (76.1%–77.6%), respectively, for the Screening, Incidental, and Other groups. Screening may play a key role in early detection and is likely to contribute to survival outcomes.

Introduction

Early detection of lung cancer is critical for improving patient prognosis. A previous study showed that the 5 year survival rate exceeds 75% for patients with lung cancer detected at an early stage [1], and tumor stage (Tumor, Node, and Metastasis stage or clinical stage) is recognized as one of the main factors affecting prognosis [2]. Other characteristics such as age, sex, treatment modality, smoking history, histological type, histological subgroup (non‐small cell lung cancer [NSCLC] and small cell lung cancer [SCLC]) have also been identified as prognostic factors [3, 4]. The favorable prognosis of early stage lung cancer is mainly due to the possibility of receiving curative interventions, such as surgical resection [4].

Lung cancer can be detected through screening, incidental findings such as during follow‐up for other conditions, or presentation with cancer‐related symptoms; screening programs are globally recommended as a method for early detection of lung cancer. The infrastructure of screening programs varies across countries, owing to differences in policy and rollout timing [5]. Large‐scale randomized controlled trials such as the NLST and NELSON trials have demonstrated the effectiveness of lung cancer screening program in reducing lung cancer mortality [6, 7]. Based on these results, the United States introduced low‐dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening for heavy smokers in 2013, and this implementation triggered recommendations for LDCT screening in some European and Asian countries [5, 8, 9]. In Japan, lung cancer screening guidelines recommend chest x‐ray for non‐high risk populations and chest x‐ray and sputum cytology for high‐risk populations [10, 11, 12].

Although a population‐based screening program is recommended at the national level [13], most lung cancer patients are not diagnosed through screening programs in Japan [14]. According to national data from 2021, the most common detection pathway for lung cancer was incidental detection during follow‐up for other diseases (46.6%), followed by visits to the hospital/clinic because of symptoms (34.9%) and screening (16.0%) [14]. One of the reasons a high proportion of lung cancer is detected incidentally in Japan may be that the country has the largest number of accessible CT scanners in the world [15]. Furthermore, the unique Japanese healthcare system, with universal insurance coverage, provides affordable examinations for minor symptoms, which may increase the frequency of incidental detection. Although this unique healthcare system might be expected to find lung cancer incidentally prior to onset of symptoms, a previous study reported that approximately one fourth of patients were diagnosed with advanced‐stage disease, especially stage IV [16]. In addition, the incidentally diagnosed group is associated with better prognoses across all stages compared with diagnosis after the appearance of symptoms [16]. However, these findings were derived from a small sample, single center study conducted two decades ago, and may not be representative of the current healthcare environment. Therefore, there is a need for a larger and more detailed investigation to reflect the subsequent demographics in Japan. In addition, clarifying the characteristics, treatments, and outcomes of patients who were not diagnosed by screening or incidental findings will provide important insights for shaping future health services in Japan.

Therefore, we investigated the clinical characteristics and net survival (NS) of patients with lung cancer according to the following detection pathways: Screening, incidental detection (Incidental), and Other using a nationwide, population‐based cancer registry in Japan (National Cancer Registry; NCR).

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Settings

This was a descriptive epidemiological study using the Japanese NCR, which contains data of patients registered between 2016 and 2020. The date of initial diagnosis of primary lung cancer was defined as the index date, and patients were followed up until December 31, 2020.

The Japanese NCR was established under the Cancer Registry Promotion Act and comprehensively collects and manages data on all patients diagnosed with primary cancer nationwide from January 2016 [13]. Under this Act, hospitals with 20 beds or more have a legislative duty to report all cancer patients. This registry is also linked to national death certificate records, enabling accurate estimation of survival rates [17]. Thus, we considered that this registry is comprehensive, as it includes almost all patients diagnosed with lung cancer in Japan, and used it as the data source for this study.

Study Participants and Settings

Patients with lung cancer were identified using the International Classification of Diseases, 10th revision (ICD‐10) codes C34.0–C34.3, C34.8, and C34.9 [18]. Patients who fulfilled the following criteria were excluded: (1) those identified by death certificates only (DCO), (2) those diagnosed with in situ carcinoma, and (3) those whose cancer was detected by autopsy.

Eligible patients were categorized into one of four groups based on their detection pathway: Screening, Incidental, Other, and Unknown [19]. The Screening group included patients diagnosed with lung cancer through population‐based screening programs conducted by local governments, elderly health checkups, voluntary health checkups, or comprehensive health checkups. The Incidental group included patients diagnosed incidentally during assessment or follow‐up for other diseases. The Other group included patients diagnosed with lung cancer through detection pathways not classified as screening or incidental detection, including, for example, healthcare consultation for subjective symptoms. Patients for whom the detection pathway leading to the diagnosis was unknown were classified in the Unknown group and excluded from all analyses.

Data Source and Patient Characteristics

Our data included 565,044 patients diagnosed with primary lung cancer between 2016 and 2020. Age was defined as the patient's age at diagnosis. Patients were identified as having multiple cancers if more than one cancer was registered at the time of initial diagnosis. Histological types and grades were based on ICD O3 codes. Patients were classified according to the histological subgroup, SCLC and NSCLC. The extent of disease was classified as localized, regional invasion, distant metastasis, or other. Initial treatment was defined as the earliest treatment following diagnosis, and when patients received more than one modality, their multiple selections were recorded. It was categorized into five groups: surgery, radiotherapy, chemoradiotherapy (radiotherapy + systemic therapy), chemotherapy, and other (Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

The outcome was NS. To estimate NS, survival time was calculated from the date of diagnosis to either the date of death or the last confirmed date of survival, multiplied by 30.5 to convert to days. Descriptive statistics were presented as the count and its proportion for categorical variables.

All analyses were conducted for the full analysis set (FAS), which included all patients who met the eligibility criteria except those in the Unknown group. In compliance with the Cancer Registry Promotion Act, data were presented as “Not shown” when the total number of cases was < 10. When the combined data showed that the number of patients was < 10, the results were presented using ranges [13]. NS and its 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using the Pohar–Perme method [20, 21, 22]. The Japanese cohort life table in 2021 was used to standardize the influence of mortality by sex and age in the general population for estimating NS [23]. As a sensitivity analysis, NS was adjusted for lead‐time bias [24] based on assuming a transition from asymptomatic to symptomatic disease rates (λ) of 1.0, 0.5, and 0.3 (only in the Screening group) [25, 26]. Subgroup analyses were also conducted by extent of disease, histological subgroup, and presence of multiple cancers. As the ad hoc analysis, we calculated p values for NS via a log‐rank type test [27]. All reported p values for NS should be interpreted as exploratory because this was an ad hoc analysis and the potential for type I error due to multiplicity was not adjusted. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1.

Results

Patients and Clinical Characteristics by Detection Pathway

Of the 565,044 patients with lung cancer in the Japanese NCR, 563,244 met the eligibility criteria and were included in the FAS. The patients' disposition in each detection pathway was as follows: Screening (n = 95,081, 16.9%), Incidental (n = 254,569, 45.2%), and Other (n = 200,850, 35.7%) (Figure 1).

Table 1 shows the baseline patient characteristics by detection pathway. Regarding age, in the Screening group, approximately 30% of patients were aged < 65 years, with the highest proportion observed in the 70–74 year age range (20.1%). In contrast, in the Incidental and Other groups, the proportion of patients aged < 65 years was under 20%, with peak proportions observed in the 75–79 years for the Incidental group (22.1%) and the 70–74 years for the Other group (19.1%). Regarding the sex distribution, the majority of patients were male across all detection pathways (64.3%, 67.3%, and 69.3% in the Screening, Incidental, and Other groups, respectively). Patients without presence of multiple cancers accounted for 87.1%, 71.9%, and 89.5% of those in the Screening, Incidental, and Other groups, respectively. In terms of extent of disease, 50.5% and 49.6% of patients in the Screening and Incidental groups, respectively, had localized disease, and this was the most prevalent disease extent within these groups. Conversely, in the Other group, 14.0% of patients were diagnosed with localized disease, and distant metastasis was the most prevalent diagnosis (61.0%). Adenocarcinoma was the most common histological type across all detection pathway groups, with the highest prevalence observed in the Screening group (64.7%), followed by the Incidental and Other groups (49.6% and 40.9%, respectively). Regarding initial treatment, surgery was the most common treatment in the Screening (62.5%) and Incidental (46.2%) groups, while chemotherapy was the most common in the Other group (48.6%). Only 15.9% of patients in the Other group underwent surgery as the initial treatment. In terms of the distribution of detection pathway each year between 2016 and 2020, patients identified by Screening ranged from 15.8% to 17.8%; those by Incidental, 45.2% to 47.7%; and Other, 35.9% to 37.1% across all years. Three‐year NS was 75.2% (95% CI: 74.9%–75.6%) in the Screening group, 56.3% (95% CI: 56.0%–56.6%) in the Incidental group, and 29.4% (95% CI: 29.2%–29.7%) in the Other group (Figure 2). As a sensitivity analysis assuming a transition rate (λ) from asymptomatic to symptomatic disease of 0.3, 2 year NS adjusted for lead‐time bias was 65.7% (95% CI: 65.2%–66.1%) in the Screening group. Three‐year NS could not be estimated (Figure S1).

Discussion

In this study, we examined lung cancer survival according to detection pathway using a nationwide, population‐based cancer registry that included over 500,000 patients diagnosed with lung cancer in Japan. This registry, which includes almost all patients with lung cancer in Japan, helps to minimize sampling bias. We described the clinical characteristics and NS according to detection pathway and estimated that the 3 year NS was numerically highest in patients in the Screening group (75.2%), followed by Incidental (56.3%), and then Other (29.4%).

It is known that prognostic factors for lung cancer include Tumor, Node, and Metastasis clinical stage, age, sex, treatment method, smoking history, and histological type [3]. The numerical differences in NS rates according to the detection pathway in our study likely reflect the varying distribution of these prognostic factors. In terms of extent of disease, nearly half of the patients in the Screening and Incidental groups had localized disease, whereas only 14.0% of patients had localized disease in the Other group. Additionally, the prevalence of regional invasion and distant metastasis, known for being associated with a poorer prognosis than localized disease, increased in the order of Screening, Incidental, and Other groups. These distributions in the extent of disease were also reflected in the choice of initial treatment. Surgery was the most common initial treatment in the Screening group. In contrast, the Other group had a higher proportion of chemotherapy, with only 15.9% of patients undergoing surgery. The Incidental group showed a slightly lower rate of surgery than the Screening group. This finding may be because the Incidental group included patients with other complications, which make surgery challenging. This finding further suggests that screening might facilitate curative treatment.

In the subgroup analysis by disease extent, patients diagnosed with localized disease had a relatively high 3 year NS range of 76.9%–96.3% across all pathways. In contrast, patients diagnosed with distant metastasis had a 3 year NS range of 15.9%–36.9%, suggesting the importance of detecting lung cancer at a localized stage for better prognosis. In particular, in patients with localized, the 3 year NS was numerically higher in the Screening group, followed by the Incidental and Other groups. These findings may be explained by the fact that the patients diagnosed with lung cancer via screening tended to be younger than those in the Incidental and Other groups.

In this study, we included both NSCLC and SCLC in the analysis population. Regarding histological subgroup, both subgroups in the Screening group had numerically higher 3 year NS, with numerically lower survival rates for patients in the Incidental and Other groups. Although SCLC is known to have poorer prognosis even at the same extent of disease [28, 29, 30], our findings suggest that patients with aggressive subtypes like SCLC might have better prognoses if they are diagnosed early through population‐based screening programs. This finding highlights the value of population‐based screening programs for both NSCLC and SCLC.

Our findings showed that the prognosis of lung cancer patients with multiple cancers might be favorable. However, previous reports have shown controversial findings regarding the prognosis of patients with multiple cancers [31, 32]. The underlying background of our findings is that patients with multiple cancers tended to participate in cancer screening more frequently [33] and subsequently were more likely to receive medical care.

Our findings align with current lung cancer screening guidelines [11, 12] that promote an increased cancer screening program to reduce cancer mortality and are consistent with a previous study [10] suggesting the effectiveness of the screening program in Japan. These results indicate that detecting lung cancer through a screening program could benefit prognosis, suggesting the need for further increases in screening participation rates to enable earlier detection and better clinical outcomes. The implementation of LDCT, especially for heavy smokers [5, 9] has become a global trend in efforts to improve the detection of lung cancer; however, the adoption of LDCT as a population‐based screening program varies across countries. While Taiwan is the first country in the world to introduce LDCT screening not only for heavy smokers but also for healthy individuals with a family history of lung cancer [34], other countries in Asia and elsewhere have not implemented screening programs, possibly owing to economic limitations [35]. Moreover, some countries in Europe and the United States, where population‐based screening programs have been implemented, have reported challenges such as ensuring equitable access, improving health literacy, and meeting ancillary costs [5, 9, 35], suggesting that the healthcare system and economic situation of each country need to be considered for the implementation of screening programs.

In this, the 3 year NS in the Screening group was numerically more favorable than that reported in the sub analysis of CONCORD‐2 [36] and that of a previous study conducted mainly in Australia and Europe [4]. One of the factors contributing to this is the difference in environments for the lung cancer screening programs. At the time the previous studies were conducted, screening programs had not been implemented in Europe and the United States. The United States introduced screening programs in 2013, while some European countries like the United Kingdom implemented them in 2020 [5]. In contrast, Japan has implemented annual chest x‐ray screening as a population‐based screening program since the 1990s [5], and this is not limited to high‐risk groups like heavy smokers. Furthermore, the Japanese healthcare environment is characterized by universal health coverage [37] and patients' freedom to choose hospitals (including cancer centers) [15], factors that may play an essential role in prognosis. Regarding the effectiveness of x‐ray screening in the general population, the PLCO randomized trial demonstrated that annual x‐ray screening did not reduce lung cancer mortality when compared with usual care [38]. In our study, rather than evaluating screening as an intervention among the general population, we investigated NS among patients already diagnosed with lung cancer, by their detection pathway. Therefore, it could be noted that the research contexts are disparate. Our results suggested that screening could contribute to the improvements in survival in lung cancer patients. Moreover, our findings are supported by the results of previous studies on the effectiveness of x‐ray screening and relevant guidelines [6, 7, 11, 12]. X‐ray screening in Japan is conducted with rigorous image analysis, which includes independent review by two readers and comparative reading, which could have supported the favorable outcomes in our study [11, 12, 39].

This study had several limitations. First, the detection pathways were not validated. In this study, the detection pathways were defined based on NCR data at the time of initial diagnosis of lung cancer. However, findings related to earlier detection, even if they existed, were not always recorded unless they were clearly reported to the NCR. This may have led to under‐ or overestimation of NS. In the future, the reliability of these detection pathways needs to be verified by linking NCR data with electronic health records and administrative claims data. Second, when comparing NS in the Screening group with the Incidental or Other groups, the Screening group may have several residual biases such as length bias, lead time bias, the healthy worker effect, and treatment effects from early interventions for patients. In this study, we adjusted for lead time bias only in the Screening group with this adjustment assuming a mean sojourn time of 1–3 years until the onset of symptomatic lung cancer based on previous reports. While individual sojourn times could not be confirmed in the NCR, the Screening group showed the numerically highest 3 year NS, even under the most conservative assumption of 3‐year sojourn time. However, these residual biases could potentially result in overestimation of NS and caution is needed when interpreting which detection pathway may be most beneficial for prognosis. Third, all patients were not followed up for the full 3 years. This is because this study included patients diagnosed from 2016 to 2020 who were followed up to the end of 2020. If a large number of patients had shorter follow‐up periods, the 95% CIs for the 3‐year NS might be wider than those found if all event‐free patients were fully followed up. However, we consider the potential impact of this limitation to be minimal given the large sample size of this registry.

We revealed the NS of patients with lung cancer by detection pathway using the Japanese NCR. These descriptive results might suggest that increasing current screening participation rates is imperative for early detection and improving prognosis. Furthermore, these findings from Japan, where free access to hospitals after detection and an annual population‐based screening program with rigorous quality control and double reading are implemented, can provide valuable insights for other countries seeking to develop population‐based screening programs. Future research should consider clinical data, such as smoking history and cancer stage, as well as family history and socioeconomic and residential variables, to better understand individual patients' backgrounds and thus improve lung cancer screening participation rates.

In conclusion, we found that the prognosis of lung cancer patients was numerically most favorable in the Screening group, followed by the Incidental and then Other groups. The Screening and Incidental groups contained a higher proportion of patients with localized cancer at diagnosis. These findings suggest that a population‐based screening program may lead to favorable outcomes regardless of disease extent in patients with lung cancer.
