Deciphering novel targets in salivary gland pleomorphic adenoma by integrating plasma proteomics and parotid transcriptomics analyses
Authors: Yuchen Fan, Suning Mao, Guanru Wang, Guile Zhao, Shiyong Zhuang, Fan Yang, Liu Liu, Honglin Li, Chunjie Li, Yubin Cao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background/purposePleomorphic adenoma (PA) is the most common salivary gland benign tumor, with its molecular drivers elusive due to a lack of experimental models. This study aimed to decipher novel targets in PA by systematically integrating plasma protein quantitative trait loci (pQTL)-based Mendelian randomization (MR) with multi-omics profiling of parotid gland tissues.Materials and methodsWe performed two-sample MR using 5450 plasma pQTLs and genome-wide association study summary for benign or broader salivary gland diseases from FinnGen consortium. Bulk RNA-sequencing (RNA-seq) and single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) comparing PA to normal tissue were used for transcriptomic validation. Immunohistochemistry (IHC) was applied for protein-level validation in human PA, adenoid cystic carcinoma (ACC), and murine inflammatory lesions.ResultsMR identified 12 plasma proteins associated with benign salivary gland tumor risk. Transmembrane serine protease 6 (TMPRSS6) was the only protein significantly risk-increasing for both benign and broader salivary gland diseases. Strikingly, mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 4 (MAP2K4) showed opposite MR effects between benign and all-lesion outcomes. Bulk RNA-seq showed limited concordance with MR findings, while scRNA-seq revealed a unique plastic epithelium and partially validated candidates at cellular resolution. Critically, IHC confirmed MAP2K4 protein overexpression specifically in human PA, but not in ACC or inflammatory lesions, while TMPRSS6 was downregulated in established pathologies despite its genetic risk association.ConclusionBy integrating plasma proteome-based causal inference with parotid tissue multi-omics, this study unveils MAP2K4 as a potential PA-specific driver. This integrative framework provides novel, context-specific targets for further functional investigation in salivary gland tumorigenesis.

Introduction

Salivary gland tumors arise from both major and minor salivary glands and comprise a wide spectrum of benign and malignant neoplasms. Among them, pleomorphic adenoma (PA) is the most common benign salivary gland tumor and predominantly affects the parotid gland.1–3 Despite its benign classification, PA is characterized by marked heterogeneity in histological architecture, cellular composition, and clinical behavior, reflecting pronounced epithelial and stromal plasticity.4 Although surgical resection remains the standard treatment, progress in elucidating the molecular drivers of PA and translating such knowledge into targeted therapeutic strategies has been limited.5,6 A major barrier in this field is the scarcity of robust experimental models, including well-characterized cell lines and reliable animal models, which has constrained mechanistic investigation and systematic target discovery in PA.7,8

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) provide a systematic framework for identifying genetic susceptibility factors underlying complex diseases.9,10 Accumulating evidence suggests that targets supported by human genetics are more likely to succeed in drug development.11,12 However, establishing causal relationships between candidate targets and diseases remains challenging using conventional study designs. Traditional observational studies remain susceptible to confounding and reverse causation,13 while randomized clinical trials are often impractical for efficiently screening many candidate targets, particularly in heterogeneous diseases such as PA.14

Mendelian randomization (MR) leverages the random allocation of parental alleles to strengthen causal inference, thereby reducing bias from confounding and reverse causality.15 Conceptually analogous to randomized clinical trials, MR estimates the effect of lifelong, genetically predicted perturbations of an exposure on disease risk, providing a cost-effective approach for causal interrogation using large-scale population data. In the commonly used two-sample MR framework, genetic variants derived from independent GWAS summary statistics are employed as instrumental variables to estimate exposure–outcome associations. 16 For molecular target prioritization, protein quantitative trait loci (pQTLs) has been increasingly adopted as genetically anchored proxies for transcript and protein abundance.17 pQTL-based MR directly interrogates protein-level effects, offering enhanced translational relevance for target discovery. In the PA context, where experimental models are limited, and tissue accessibility is constrained, pQTL-MR provides a scalable strategy to nominate candidate molecular targets grounded in human genetics.18

Nevertheless, growing concerns have been raised regarding the reliability and interpretation of large-scale MR studies. The rapid expansion of two-sample MR using public summary data has been accompanied by issues related to instrument selection, sensitivity analysis, and overinterpretation of results.19 Moreover, because key instrumental-variable assumptions beyond relevance cannot be formally tested, MR findings require careful biological justification. These limitations are particularly relevant for salivary gland tumors, where GWAS outcomes are defined using broad phenotypic categories, and genetically inferred associations do not necessarily translate into detectable or actionable targets without independent tissue-level validation.

Bulk and single-cell transcriptomics provide complementary opportunities to bridge these gaps. Bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) can validate whether genetically prioritized candidates exhibit consistent dysregulation at the tissue level and can contextualize candidates within pathway-level changes.20 Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) further resolves cell-type specificity, clarifies the cellular source and spatial logic of candidate signals, and can reveal tumor heterogeneity that bulk profiling may obscure.21 Therefore, in this study, we integrated pQTL-based MR with bulk and single-cell transcriptomic analyses to prioritize candidate molecular determinants of salivary gland tumor susceptibility, and used PA as a biologically representative disease for downstream tissue- and cell-level transcriptomic validation. By examining the expression patterns of MR-prioritized proteins in PA and resolving their cellular localization, we aimed to bridge genetically inferred causal signals with the unique histological and cellular architecture of PA. Additionally, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) to provide orthogonal evidence supporting tissue-level expression of selected targets in PA and comparative salivary gland lesions.

Materials and methods

Patient samples and data acquisition

The study protocol involving human specimens was approved by the Ethics Committee of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University (WCHSIRB-D-2022-213). Written informed consent was obtained from all patients. Human normal salivary gland tissues, PA, and adenoid cystic carcinomas (ACC) were obtained from the Department of Head and Neck Oncology of West China Hospital of Stomatology, Sichuan University.

All tissues were confirmed by histopathological examination. Fresh tumor tissues from one normal parotid gland sample, two recurrent PA, one carcinoma ex PA sample were collected during surgery. Single-cell suspensions were generated by enzymatic digestion and processed using the 10x Genomics Chromium Single Cell platform.22 ScRNA-seq libraries were constructed and sequenced for all samples as we previously described and deposited in https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE294254.

Bulk RNA-seq data retrieved from the publicly available Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) data set GSE180105 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi?acc=GSE180105), which compromised five PA and five corresponding normal tissues.

Animal model

This animal study was approved by the institutional board of Sichuan University West China Hospital of Stomatology (WCHSIRB-CT-2021-048). Twenty 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice, weighing approximately 20 g, were purchased from Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animals Co. Ltd (Chengdu, China). The mice were weighed and subsequently anesthetized with Avertin (T48402, Sigma–Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA; 0.75 mg/g mouse weight). The mouse model was established by creating a submandibular incision, dissecting the dominant duct, and ligating the ducts as we previously described.23 The inflammatory lesion induced by the duct ligation has been demonstrated in our previous report.24

Genome-wide association study summary data for exposure and outcome data for Mendelian randomization

Two clinically relevant outcomes were included in the present study. The first outcome, benign neoplasm of salivary glands (CD2_BENIGN_SALIVARY), represents a tumor-specific phenotype primarily defined by ICD-10 code D11, which includes benign neoplasms of the major salivary glands. The second outcome, diseases of salivary glands (K11_SALIVARY), corresponds to a broader disease category defined by ICD-10 code K11, encompassing a spectrum of salivary gland disorders such as inflammatory, obstructive, and functional conditions. Both outcomes were ascertained through nationwide health registry data in Finland, with cases identified based on hospital discharge diagnoses and corresponding controls drawn from the same population-based cohort. Data were obtained from the FinnGen consortium (release R13) via the IEU OpenGWAS database as we previously described.25

Genetic instruments for plasma protein levels were obtained from multiple large-scale plasma proteomic pQTL resources, including datasets derived from deCODE genetics, UK Biobank, and Icelandic population studies. Both cis-acting and genome-wide pQTLs were considered. After quality control, harmonization, and removal of duplicated proteins across datasets, a total of 5,450 unique proteins were available for MR analyses. These pQTL resources were originally generated using affinity-based proteomic platforms, as described in previous large-scale studies. All the pQTLs included in our analysis exhibited P values < 5e−6 in the original pQTL study as we previously reported.17

Selection of instrumental variables

As we reported previously, we chose to apply a more lenient threshold of P < 5e−6 to ensure an adequate number of SNPs linked to cathepsins for further analysis.25 In addition, we implemented a clump distance of less than 10,000 kb with r2 < 0.001 to mitigate potential linkage disequilibrium and excluded palindromic SNPs to minimize bias. Furthermore, in adherence to the independence assumption of MR, we carefully examined and excluded SNPs strongly associated with the outcome (P < 5e−8) to ensure the accuracy of our analysis. We determined the strength of each selected SNP by calculating the F-statistic. An F-statistic of 10 or higher provides substantial evidence against weak instrument bias. Conversely, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) with an F-statistic below 10 are considered weak and are recommended for exclusion.

Mendelian randomization analysis and statistical framework

The MR analysis was performed using the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.8) in R as previously described.16,17 Prior to analysis, all SNPs within the extended major histocompatibility complex region were excluded. Exposure (pQTL) and outcome (salivary gland GWAS) data were harmonized to ensure the effect of alleles corresponded to the same strand. For each protein-outcome pair, the primary causal estimate was derived using the inverse-variance weighted method. Sensitivity analyses included the MR-Egger and weighted median methods, which are robust to certain types of pleiotropy. Heterogeneity among instrumental variable estimates was assessed using Cochran’s Q statistic, and directional pleiotropy was evaluated via the MR-Egger intercept test. A false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 0.10 was applied to account for multiple testing across the many proteins analyzed. Proteins meeting this FDR threshold were considered significant candidates for downstream analysis.

Intersection analysis and visualization of Mendelian randomization results

To summarize and compare the MR findings, a comprehensive visualization was employed. Volcano plots were generated using the ggplot2 R package, plotting the −log10(P value) against the log-transformed odds ratio for all tested proteins in each outcome. For proteins meeting the FDR <0.10 threshold, their odds ratios and 95 % confidence intervals were displayed in forest plots. To identify consensus or contradiction of protein signals, the significant protein lists from the MR analyses of benign neoplasms and all lesions were intersected. A Venn diagram, created with the VennDiagram package, illustrated the overlap of positive-effect and negative-effect proteins between the two outcomes. For the proteins found in the intersecting sets, a comparative forest plot was generated to visually align their effect estimates and confidence intervals from both MR analyses side-by-side.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 package (version 1.38.0) in R as previously described.23,26,27 Genes with an absolute log2 fold change greater than 2 and an adjusted P value (Benjamini-Hochberg) less than 0.001 were defined as significantly differentially expressed. A heatmap of the top differentially expressed genes was created using the pheatmap package to visualize global expression patterns.

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes and Gene Ontology pathway enrichment analysis

Functional interpretation of the significant gene list generated from the Bulk RNA-seq analysis and protein list generated from MR analysis was conducted through pathway enrichment analysis. The clusterProfiler package (version 4.4.4) was used to perform Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analyses for biological processes, cellular components, and molecular functions. A P value cutoff of 0.05 and a q-value cutoff of 0.2 were applied to identify significantly enriched terms. The results for upregulated gene sets were analyzed and plotted separately.

Integration of Mendelian randomization and transcriptomic findings

The list of significant genes from the bulk tissue differential expression analysis was cross-referenced with the list of protein-coding genes corresponding to the MR-derived candidate proteins, with a focus on those from the intersection analysis. A custom R script was used to extract expression data for these overlapping genes. Their expression status (upregulated, downregulated, or not significant) in PA tissue was determined based on the transcriptomic analysis thresholds. This integrative dataset was visualized using plots showing the log2 fold change of each overlapping gene.

Single-cell RNA sequencing data processing and quality control

ScRNA-seq data from the four samples were processed using the Seurat toolkit (version 4.1.1) as previously described.28–30 The raw gene-barcode matrices were read into R using the Read10X function. For each sample, a Seurat object was created, and cells were filtered based on three quality metrics. Cells with fewer than 200 detected genes were removed, as were genes detected in fewer than three cells. Cells with a mitochondrial gene content exceeding 25 % were also filtered to eliminate low-viability or apoptotic cells. After individual sample processing, the datasets were merged, and the percentage of mitochondrial reads and the number of detected molecules per cell were regressed during the scaling step to reduce technical confounding.

Single-cell RNA sequencing clustering and cell type annotation

Following normalization and identification of highly variable genes, the integrated dataset was scaled, and principal component analysis was performed. The top 30 principal components were used for downstream clustering and nonlinear dimensional reduction. Cells were clustered using a shared nearest neighbor modularity optimization algorithm with a resolution parameter set to 0.5. The resulting clusters were visualized in two dimensions using uniform manifold approximation and projection. Cell type identities were manually assigned to each cluster based on the expression of canonical marker genes. The final annotated Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) figure and a dotplot depicting the expression of key marker genes across clusters were generated for visualization.

Expression analysis of integrative candidate genes in single-cell RNA sequencing data

The expression profiles of the candidate genes identified from the MR-transcriptome integration were examined within the scRNA-seq dataset. Using the FeaturePlot function in Seurat, the expression pattern of each candidate gene was projected onto the UMAP coordinates to visualize which cell clusters expressed them. To quantitatively compare expression between normal and tumor samples across major cell categories, violin plots were created. These plots displayed the expression distribution of each candidate gene, split by sample type (Normal vs. Tumor), within defined cell categories including epithelia, myeloid cells, lymphoid cells, fibroblasts, and endothelia.

Immunohistochemistry staining

As previously described, fresh specimens collected from patients or mice were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, and embedded in paraffin.24,29,31,32 Paraffin sections were prepared, baked at 65 °C, dewaxed in xylene, and rehydrated through a descending alcohol gradient to distilled water. Antigen retrieval was performed using citrate buffer under high-temperature and high-pressure conditions. After blocking endogenous peroxidase with 3 % hydrogen peroxide for 15 min, sections were incubated with 10 % goat serum for 1 h. The serum was removed, and sections were incubated with primary antibody of mitogen-activated protein kinase kinase 4 (MAP2K4; ab52958; Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and transmembrane serine protease 6 (TMPRSS6; DF13979; Affinity, Suyang, China) at 4 °C overnight. After washing, sections were incubated with secondary antibodies and developed with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine solution. Images were captured for subsequent analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed in R (version 4.5.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). For comparisons between two groups (normal vs. tumor in bulk RNA-seq), P values were derived from the Wald test implemented in DESeq2, followed by Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment. In scRNA-seq analyses, differential expression between conditions within a cell type was assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. For all analyses, a P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with specific adjusted P value thresholds noted in respective method sections. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation where applicable.

Results

Mendelian randomization analysis of protein quantitative trait loci identified risk-increasing or protective proteins for salivary gland benign tumors

Firstly, we performed MR analysis on pQTLs associated with major salivary gland benign tumors to identify statistically significant risk-increasing or protective proteins. This analysis identified a potentially significant relationship between salivary gland benign tumors and 347 proteins (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). Among them, 12 proteins reached the strict standard with an FDR <0.10. AMY2A (odds ratio[OR], 0.87; 95 % confidence interval [CI], 0.84–0.91), ADAM15 (OR, 0.85; 95 % CI, 0.79–0.93), SFTPB (OR, 0.79; 95 % CI, 0.70–0.89), and HEPH (OR, 0.87; 95 % CI, 0.81–0.94) demonstrated a protective effect, whereas elevated expression of the remaining eight proteins, including MAP2K4 (OR, 1.08; 95 % CI, 1.05–1.11), LRRC37A2 (OR, 1.05; 95 % CI, 1.03–1.07), AHSG (OR, 1.12; 95 % CI, 1.06–1.19), TMPRSS6 (OR, 1.06; 95 % CI, 1.03–1.09), ACTN1 (OR, 1.54; 95 % CI, 1.21–1.96), KRT72, (OR, 1.57; 95 % CI, 1.20–2.06) STMN4 (OR, 1.58; 95 % CI, 1.19–2.11), and PVRIG (OR, 1.42; 95 % CI, 1.14–1.77) were associated with an increased risk-increasing of benign tumor development (Fig. 1B). KEGG enrichment analysis indicated that the significant pQTLs might be involved in pathways such as proteoglycans in cancer, ErbB signaling pathway, GnRH signaling pathway, FoxO signaling pathway, and lysosome pathway (Fig. 1C). GO enrichment analysis indicated that these pQTLs participate in the formation of secretory granule lumen, cytoplasmic vesicle lumen, vesicle lumen, neuronal dense core vesicle, vacuolar lumen, neuron apoptotic process, extracellular matrix disassembly, and regulation of calcium ion import (Fig. 1D).

Salivary gland benign tumors and other lesions share several common risk-increasing or protective proteins

Using the similar methodology, we conducted pQTL-based MR analysis for all lesions and identified a potentially significant relationship between all lesions and 298 proteins (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Of the 11 exposures meeting the FDR <0.10 threshold, LRRC37A2 (OR, 0.95; 95 % CI, 0.94–0.97), MAP2K4 (OR, 0.94; 95 % CI, 0.92–0.96), AZU1 (OR, 0.80; 95 % CI, 0.72–0.89), KIAA1324L (OR, 0.89; 95 % CI, 0.84–0.95), MNAT1 (OR, 0.67; 95 % CI, 0.54–0.83), and MTMR6 (OR, 0.77; 95 % CI, 0.66–0.90) exhibited a protective role, while the other six genes including TMPRSS6 (OR, 1.04; 95 % CI, 1.02–1.06), CCL28 (OR, 1.11; 95 % CI, 1.05–1.18), REPIN1 (OR, 1.18; 95 % CI, 1.08–1.29), CLEC4C (OR, 1.09; 95 % CI, 1.04–1.14), and KAL1 (OR, 1.08; 95 % CI, 1.03–1.12) were associated with promoting tumorigenesis (Fig. 2B). As the difference between the two outcomes, “major salivary gland benign neoplasms” and “diseases of salivary glands” is mainly whether including genetic information of malignant tumors or inflammatory lesions, the common pQTLs shared by the two MR analyses might be important for the tumorigenesis of all the salivary gland lesions. To further clarify their functional relevance, we compared the significant pQTLs between benign tumors and all lesions. We found that TMPRSS6, ARHGAP45, CTSF, PDIA3, KIR2DS4, PAIP2B, and CARTPT were risk-increasing proteins for both outcomes (Fig. 2C–D), TENM3, ENPEP, C3, SNRPB2, and AZU1 were protective proteins for both outcomes conversely (Fig. 2E–F). Herein, TMPRSS6 is the only one protein which reached the standard of FDR <0.10 for both outcomes, strongly suggesting its causal effect on salivary gland tumorigenesis.

Several proteins have opposite causal effects on salivary gland benign tumors and broader salivary gland lesions

Surprisingly, although known biological differences exist between benign tumors and all lesions, some proteins displayed opposing causal effects in the two MR analyses. For instance, LRRC372A acted as a risk-increasing genein benign tumors but showed a protective effect on the analysis of all lesions (Figs. 1B and 2B). This inconsistency raised questions regarding certain MR findings. Through cross-over analysis, we identified a total of eight proteins with discordant functional directions in the two MR analyses (Fig. 3A and B). MAP2K4, LRRC37A2, ITPRIPL1, and ANKMY2 were harmful in benign tumors but were protective in all salivary tumors (Fig. 3C), suggesting their potential role in the benign tumorigenesis but not in the carcinogenesis. Conversely, AMY2A, FABP4, CAPN3, and PIR were harmful in all lesions but were protective in benign tumors (Fig. 3B), inferring their potential role in the carcinogenesis but not in the benign tumorigenesis (Fig. 3D). To emphasize, MAP2K4 and LRRC37A2 reached a very strict statistical standard of FDR <0.01, strongly suggesting the reliability of these opposing causal effects. Herein, the FDR values of MAP2K4 were below 0.0001 for both outcomes.

Bulk transcriptomic sequencing of pleomorphic adenoma rarely validated the Mendelian randomization results

To assess the expression level of proteins identified in the MR analyses, we performed differentially expressed gene analysis comparing PA to normal salivary gland tissues for validation by the bulk RNA-seq (Fig. 4A). KEGG enrichment analysis of the differentially expressed genes revealed their primary association with salivary secretion, as well as metabolite transport and absorption (Fig. 4B). GO analysis suggested involvement in establishing cell polarity and membrane transporter activity, among other processes (Fig. 4C). We then tried to validate the expression of aforementioned 20 candidate proteins in PA, including 7 shared risk-increasing proteins, 5 shared protective proteins, 4 risk-increasing proteins exclusive for benign tumors, and 4 protective proteins exclusive for benign tumors. After excluding four proteins (TMPRSS6, KIR2DS4, CARTPT, and AZU1) due to data unavailability, only four of the remaining 16 genes showed statistically significant upregulation (TENM3, FABP4, PAIP2B, AMY2A) (Fig. 4D). Contradictorily, AMY2A and FABP4 were identified as risk-increasing genes in the transcriptomic data, whereas they were protective in the benign tumor MR analysis. TENM3 was protective in both MR analyses but upregulated in the transcriptome data. PAIP2B was the only gene with consistent results, demonstrating a risk-increasing effect on the PA transcriptome, benign tumor MR, and all-tumor MR analyses (Fig. 4E–F). These results indicate substantial discrepancies between RNA-seq and MR analysis, necessitating additional verification.

Single-cell sequencing reveals epithelial heterogeneity in pleomorphic adenoma

To investigate the causes of the inconsistency between the transcriptomic and MR results and to further clarify the functions of the differential genes, we performed scRNA-seq on normal salivary gland and PA tissues. We clustered the cells into diverse cell types including epithelia, myeloid, fibroblast, endothelial, lymphoid, and pericyte populations (Fig. 5A). In PA, the proportion of epithelia decreased from 71.5 % in normal tissue to 40 %, whereas the myeloid cell proportion increased significantly (Fig. 5B). Despite the population ratio change, the epithelial clusters were significantly different in normal and tumor tissues (Fig. 5C). Dotplot analysis showed that, in addition to expressing acinar and ductal-related genes such as AQP5, KRT14, and SOX4, PA epithelial cells aberrantly overexpressed markers typically associated with other lineages, including the neuronal marker SOX10 and TUBB3, the chondrogenic gene ACAN, SOX9, and MIA, osteoblastic marker RUNX2 and ALPL, and also proliferative marker MKI67, TOP2A, PCNA, and STMN1 (Fig. 5D). This suggests substantial differences between PA epithelia and normal glandular epithelia, indicating that bulk transcriptomic analysis of tissues with completely different cell populations may lead to biased results.

Single-cell transcriptome sequencing partially validated the cell-level expression of causal proteins identified in the Mendelian randomization analyses

Based on the scRNA-seq data, we re-evaluated the previously screened 20 proteins and identified 10 proteins detectable in the scRNA-seq (Fig. 6A). Given the cell typespecific nature of gene expression, we further analyzed the expression patterns of these genes across cellular subpopulations. ARHGAP45 was primarily highly expressed in myeloid cells, CTSF expression was elevated in myeloid cells and fibroblasts, PDIA3 showed broad up-regulation across all the cell populations, matching the MR prediction. Results in the MR analyses and scRNA-seq were highly consistent among these three genes. However, PAIP2B, predominantly expressed in epithelial cells, was downregulated, contradicting the MR and bulk transcriptome result. TENM3 was mainly expressed at low levels in fibroblasts, consistent with its protective role in MR analyses. In contrast, the upregulation of ENPEP in fibroblasts, C3 in myeloid cells, and SNRPB2 in epithelial and myeloid cells were all inconsistent with the protective effects predicted by MR. LRRC37A2 was highly expressed in epithelial and myeloid cells, aligning with the benign tumor MR result. MAP2K4 showed widespread up-regulation in epithelial, myeloid, and pericyte cells, also consistent with the benign tumor MR finding (Fig. 6B). ARHGAP45, CTSF, PDIA3, TENM3, LRRC37A2, and MAP2K4 were almost consistent in the MR and single-cell transcriptome findings.

The bulk tissue analysis, which reflects the average transcriptional state of the tumor, showed limited concordance with MR predictions. Only 1 of 16 testable candidate genes (PAIP2B) exhibited fully consistent directionality across MR and bulk RNA-seq. In contrast, single-cell resolution enabled a more nuanced, cell-type-informed assessment. Among the 10 MR candidates detectable in our scRNA-seq atlas, six (ARHGAP45, CTSF, PDIA3, TENM3, LRRC37A2, and MAP2K4) showed expression patterns consistent with their MR-predicted effects, primarily localizing to biologically relevant cell compartments. This higher validation rate within specific cellular contexts suggests that MR signals may often arise from protein expression or release within particular cell types, which can be diluted or masked in bulk tissue averages.

Immunohistochemistry validated the distinct expression patterns of MAP2K4 and TMPRSS6 in salivary gland lesions

To validate the protein expression of key MR candidates, we performed IHC for MAP2K4 and TMPRSS6. Consistent with its MR signal as a risk factor for both benign and all salivary gland tumors, TMPRSS6 protein was highly expressed in the acinar and ductal epithelia of normal human and mouse salivary glands. However, its expression was markedly downregulated in PA and ACC, with minimal detection in ACC (Fig. 7A–C). Furthermore, in a murine model of salivary gland inflammatory lesions, TMPRSS6 expression was also reduced (Fig. 7D–E). These findings indicate that while genetically predicted higher TMPRSS6 levels confer disease risk, its actual protein abundance is decreased within established pathological lesions.

In contrast, the expression pattern of MAP2K4 aligned with its MR-predicted, tumor-type-specific role. IHC revealed that MAP2K4 was highly expressed in the stromal components of human PA, particularly surrounding ductal and vascular structures, which is consistent with its high expression in fibroblasts, endothelia, and pericytes observed in scRNA-seq (Fig. 8A). Its expression was low in normal salivary gland tissue and ACC (Fig. 7B–C). Similarly, in the mouse inflammatory lesion model, MAP2K4 was not upregulated (Fig. 7D–E). Collectively, the IHC results validate that MAP2K4 expression is specifically elevated in the benign tumor microenvironment of PA, whereas TMPRSS6 shows an inverse pattern, being downregulated across developed salivary gland pathologies despite its genetic association with disease risk.

Discussion

This study systematically screened plasma proteins with potential causal relationships to the development of salivary gland benign tumors and all salivary gland tumors using pQTL-based Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses. The study found that TMPRSS6 was the only risk protein that reached the strict significance threshold (FDR <0.10) in both the benign and all-lesion analyses, suggesting a common genetic susceptibility factor for salivary gland tumorigenesis; however, subsequent IHC revealed its expression was downregulated in established tumors, suggesting its role may be more relevant to disease initiation rather than tumor progression. More importantly, this study revealed that a series of proteins may exert opposite causal effects on benign versus malignant tumors. Notably, MAP2K4 and LRRC37A2 were identified as risk factors in benign tumors but appeared as protective factors in the all-tumor analysis, both achieving high statistical significance (FDR <0.01). This strongly indicates their specific involvement in the development of benign tumors rather than malignant transformation or inflammatory conditions. Subsequent transcriptomic validation showed considerable discrepancies between bulk RNA-seq results and MR findings. Through scRNA-seq, we partially validated the expression patterns of key proteins identified by MR (e.g., ARHGAP45, CTSF, PDIA3) at the cellular level. Finally, IHC confirmed that MAP2K4 was highly expressed in salivary gland PA but showed low expression in normal tissue and ACC. This finding perfectly aligns with the MR-derived hypothesis that it plays opposing roles in benign tumors versus all lesions. In summary, this study not only identified potential common pathogenic proteins for salivary gland tumors but also, for the first time, discovered causal proteins with tumor-type-specific, even opposite, effects, providing novel insights into understanding the heterogeneous pathogenesis of benign and malignant salivary gland tumors.

An essential finding of this study was the presence of substantial inconsistency across different sources of evidence. The most striking example is TMPRSS6, which was identified as a robust genetic risk factor in MR analyses, yet IHC revealed its protein expression was highest in normal ductal/acinar epithelia and significantly reduced in PA, ACC, and non-neoplastic inflammatory lesions. This pattern suggests that while genetically predicted higher levels of TMPRSS6 confer increased disease risk, its actual protein abundance decreases within the tumor microenvironment once a lesion is established. This dissociation underscores a critical principle: MR estimates capture lifelong genetic liability influencing disease initiation, which may not correlate with, and can even be inversely related to, protein expression in end-stage tumor tissue. This could occur if the protein plays a role in maintaining normal tissue homeostasis, and its loss is a permissive or secondary event in tumorigenesis. At the MR level, several proteins exhibited consistent causal effects across salivary gland benign tumors and all lesions. For instance, PAIP2B showed a consistent harmful effects in MR analyses of benign tumors and all gland lesions and was supported by bulk transcriptomic data in PA, whereas opposing evidence was retrieved in scRNA-seq; TENM3 emerged as protective in MR analyses but displayed upregulated expression in the bulk transcriptome data and no significant expression difference at the single-cell level, indicating that genetically inferred liability does not necessarily correspond to expression change. MR estimates represent the genetic liability of predicted protein levels on disease risk, which does not necessarily correspond to contemporaneous expression levels within established tumors. Rather than invalidating MR, these discrepancies emphasize the temporal and mechanistic separation between disease risk and tumor-state expression.17

Interestingly, some pQTLs showed opposing casual effects depending on outcome definition. For example, MAP2K4 and LRRC37A2 exhibited opposing MR directions between benign tumors and the broader salivary gland disease outcome, suggesting that differences in outcome definition and disease composition can substantially influence the direction of inferred causal effects; whereas TMPRSS6 represented a consistent genetic risk factor across MR analyses, though its functional expression pattern appears complex, as discussed above. This highlights that a consistent MR signal pinpoints a shared genetic susceptibility factor, which may operate through mechanisms not directly reflected by terminal tissue protein levels. These examples illustrate that consistent MR signals may indicate common or shared susceptibility factors, whereas discordant findings often reflect biological heterogeneity rather than methodological inconsistency. Especially for proteins passing through strict statistical tests like MAP2K4 and LRRC37A2, such discrepancies are more likely driven by differences in phenotype granularity, disease composition, and the underlying biological processes captured by each GWAS, rather than analytical artifacts.

Although MR analyses were performed by regarding salivary gland tumors as a whole, due to data availability, PA was used as a representative and biologically tractable model for downstream validation.33,34 Single-cell data revealed that PA exhibits a cellular architecture that differs fundamentally from normal salivary gland tissue, particularly within epithelial compartments. The presence of epithelial populations expressing markers typically associated with neural, chondrogenic, osteogenic, or proliferative lineages underscores the extreme plasticity of PA and supports the notion that it cannot be simply regarded as a hyperplastic or mildly dysregulated normal gland.35,36 This unique cellular composition provides a plausible explanation for why certain MR-derived signals behave differently in benign tumors versus all lesions. Proteins that appear protective or deleterious in a broad disease context may exert distinct effects depending on epithelial differentiation state, stromal interactions, or immune infiltration. The observation that some MR signals are directionally inconsistent across tumor definitions reinforces the idea that PA represents a distinct biological entity rather than a linear precursor of malignant salivary gland tumors. Moreover, our UMAP clustering revealed a more dispersed epithelial cell distribution compared with the previous study.35 This may be attributed to our inclusion of advanced PA states (recurrent PA and carcinoma ex-PA), which likely exhibit greater cellular heterogeneity and transcriptomic divergence. Differences in sample processing, sequencing depth, and integration algorithms may also contribute to these variations.

Consistent with this view, IHC showed MAP2K4 enrichment in PA stroma around ductal and vascular lumens, with relatively low staining in normal tissue, ACC, and inflammatory lesions in mice, providing evidence that some candidates may exclusively relate to benign lesion biology. In contrast, the IHC pattern of TMPRSS6—high in normal epithelia and low across pathological lesions—while not supporting its role as a tumor progression driver, aligns with its MR signal being interpreted as a marker of genetic susceptibility. Together, the contrasting validation outcomes for MAP2K4 and TMPRSS6 exemplify how orthogonal experimental data can refine the interpretation of MR-derived causal inferences, distinguishing between proteins that may be actively involved in tumor development (like MAP2K4 in PA) from those marking constitutional risk.

This study provides a genetic and transcriptomic landscape of candidate proteins in salivary gland tumorigenesis, yet several intriguing discrepancies and unexplored mechanisms warrant further investigation. First, the conflicting evidence between MR, bulk, and single-cell transcriptomics highlights the need for multi-level validation. For instance, whether MR-identified proteins such as TMPRSS6 or MAP2K4 truly exert causal effects should be examined using multilevel approaches including proteomic profiling of salivary gland tissues, spatial transcriptomics, and in vitro perturbation assays. Second, functional characterization of these candidates is essential to move beyond association. Genetic engineered mouse models, particularly conditional knockout or overexpression systems targeting salivary gland epithelium, could help determine whether these proteins alter gland homeostasis, promote benign hyperplasia, or even drive PA formation. Such models would not only validate causality but also potentially establish the genetically defined animal model of PA. Third, our IHC validation remains preliminary in size. Future studies should include larger, multi-center cohorts of PA and ACC samples to confirm protein expression patterns. Additionally, direct measurement of candidate proteins (e.g., TMPRSS6, MAP2K4) in plasma from PA patients versus controls would be a critical next step to translate our MR-derived hypotheses into clinically applicable biomarkers.

The primary strength of this study lies in the integrative design. By combining proteomic causal inference with transcriptomic and histological validation, we move beyond single-modality analyses and provide a structured approach for evaluating candidate targets in salivary gland tumors. The use of pQTLs as exposures enhances translational relevance by focusing on protein-level effects, and immunohistochemical validation adds robust validation on spatial and cellular context to genetic findings. Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, salivary gland tissue-specific pQTL remains absent whereas plasma pQTL coverage remains incomplete particularly for salivary gland–specific proteins. Consequently, MR inference is inherently constrained by instrument strength and underlying assumptions. Second, some top MR candidates (e.g., TMPRSS6) were not available in bulk and single-cell transcriptome datasets, limiting direct triangulation across evidence layers. Third, tissue-level validation was largely qualitative, and functional consequences of candidate proteins were not directly tested. While PA serves as a useful validation model, additional salivary gland tumor subtypes will need to be examined to assess generalizability. Future studies should integrate colocalization analyses, expanded transcriptomic profiling across tumor entities, and functional perturbation models to determine whether MR-prioritized proteins drive tumor initiation, influence differentiation trajectories, or modulate tumor–stroma interactions. Ultimately, such approaches may enable the development of disease-specific target strategies grounded in genetic causality.

By integrating pQTL-based Mendelian randomization with bulk and single-cell transcriptomic profiling, this study identifies several plasma proteins with putative causal roles in salivary gland lesion development. We demonstrate that genetically predicted TMPRSS6 levels represent a common risk factor for salivary gland lesions, although its tissue expression is downregulated in established lesions, highlighting a disconnect between genetic liability and tumor-stage protein abundance. Meanwhile, MAP2K4 and LRRC37A2 exhibit opposing effects, risk-increasing in benign tumors but protective in all lesions, suggesting tumor-type-specific pathogenic mechanisms. Transcriptomic validation revealed substantial discrepancies between genetic liability and tumor-stage expression, underscoring the importance of contextualizing genetic signals within tissue and cellular environments. Single-cell RNA-seq further illuminated the unique epithelial plasticity of PA, which may explain some of the divergent genetic associations. IHC confirmed MAP2K4 overexpression specifically in PA but not in ACC or inflammatory lesions, supporting its benign tumor-specific role. Collectively, our multi-omics approach not only prioritizes candidate targets for salivary gland tumors but also highlights the biological complexity and heterogeneity underlying different salivary gland pathologies, providing a framework for future mechanistic and therapeutic exploration.
