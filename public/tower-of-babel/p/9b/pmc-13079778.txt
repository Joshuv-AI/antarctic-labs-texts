Automated measurement of hallux valgus angles using a deep learning model: validation and comparison with surgeons of varying expertise
Authors: Qiong Wang, Jun-Hu Wang, Dong-Dong Ji, Yan Zhang, Hong-Mou Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hallux valgus (HV) assessment commonly relies on measuring the hallux valgus angle (HVA) and intermetatarsal angle (IMA) on weight-bearing radiographs. Manual measurement is time-consuming and subject to intra- and inter-observer variability. Deep learning (DL) methods may automate angle measurement with high accuracy, but prior studies often lacked control groups and comparisons across surgeon experience levels. We developed a DL model based on UNet++ with a MobileNetV2 backbone to segment bone axes, followed by a linear regression module to predict HVA and IMA. The dataset comprised 2,468 weight-bearing radiographs (1,920 HV; 548 control) from 2,124 participants. Performance metrics included mean absolute error (MAE), root mean squared error (RMSE), intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), and outlier rates; model results were compared with measurements from two foot and ankle surgeons of differing experience. In the HV group, the model achieved ICCs of 0.902 for HVA and 0.887 for IMA; in the control group, ICCs were 0.821 (HVA) and 0.898 (IMA). For IMA, the model demonstrated lower MAE and fewer outliers than the surgeons (HV outliers 4.6% vs. 6.6–7.9%; control 4.8% vs. 6.0–9.5%). Average measurement time was 0.13 s per radiograph for the model versus 1.8–2.6 min manually. The proposed DL model automates HVA and IMA measurement with accuracy and reliability comparable to experienced surgeons while markedly improving efficiency. The model also showed consistent performance across HV and control radiographs in our cohort, supporting potential clinical application pending external validation.

Introduction

Hallux valgus (HV) is a widespread foot deformity characterized by an outward deviation of the great toe at the first metatarsophalangeal joint. Owing to the low cost and widespread availability of radiography, precise measurement of the hallux valgus angle (HVA) and intermetatarsal angle (IMA) from weight-bearing radiographs remains the primary radiographic assessment for HV and surgical planning1–3. However, manual measurements are time-consuming and suffer from intra- and inter-observer variability.

Artificial intelligence (AI), and in particular deep learning (DL), has shown promise in musculoskeletal radiology for tasks such as automated bone fracture and tumor detection4–6. Existing DL methods for HV angle estimation commonly follow a two-stage workflow: (1) segmentation of bone axes (the first proximal phalanx, first metatarsal, second metatarsal) and (2) calculation of HVA and IMA from the segmented axes using a linear regression module7–9.

Although these approaches have demonstrated satisfactory performance, several important gaps remain. First, many models are not publicly available, limiting independent verification and reproducibility. Second, evaluation has often been restricted to HV radiographs, and the absence of control radiographs limits assessment of performance across the full spectrum encountered in routine practice, including non-HV feet and borderline cases; this may mask spectrum bias and overestimate real-world robustness when models are deployed in mixed clinical populations. Third, prior studies have rarely benchmarked model outputs against surgeons with different levels of experience, making clinical robustness difficult to interpret.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to develop and validate a publicly accessible DL model for automated HVA and IMA measurement on weight-bearing radiographs. Specifically, we aimed to (1) evaluate performance in both HV and control radiographs, and (2) compare accuracy, reliability, and clinically relevant error rates against two foot and ankle surgeons with differing experience to assess clinical robustness and generalizability. By combining public model release with evaluation across both disease and non-disease radiographs and benchmarking across surgeon experience levels, our study provides a reproducible and clinically interpretable reference for future comparative research and translation.

Materials and methods

This cross-sectional study was approved by the institutional review board. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived. A UNet++ segmentation algorithm was employed to segment bone axes (the first proximal phalanx, first metatarsal, and second metatarsal) on radiographs10. Subsequently, a linear regression module was utilized to derive the HVA and IMA from the segmentation results. The overall pipeline of the proposed methodology is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Dataset

This study included 2,468 weight-bearing dorsoplantar radiographs from 2,124 participants, with 548 control cases (502 participants) and 1,920 HV cases (1,622 participants). HV was defined according to clinical and radiographic criteria (HVA ≥ 15°; IMA ≥ 10°), and the control group consisted of weight-bearing radiographs without clinical or radiographic evidence of HV. All radiographs were collected at our institution between January 2015 and March 2025. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) cortical bone alterations, including fractures, prior surgery, or internal fixation; (2) poor image quality (blurred or unannotated images); and (3) age < 18 years.

Participants were partitioned at the participant level to prevent image-level leakage. The test set was held out for final independent evaluation, including comparison between the DL model and surgeons and the inter-/intra-observer agreement analyses. The training set was used to optimize model parameters, and the validation set was used for model selection during development. The resulting sets comprised: test set including 302 HV and 84 control radiographs (total 386); training set including 1,319 HV and 379 control radiographs (total 1,698); and validation set including 299 HV and 85 control radiographs (total 384). To mitigate data imbalance during training, control images were augmented (left–right flip, brightness and contrast adjustments) to produce additional samples, yielding a final augmented set with 1,137 normal radiographs. No augmentation was applied to the validation or test sets. These augmentations were intentionally limited to flipping and photometric perturbations to reflect acquisition variability while avoiding geometric transforms that could alter the underlying angles and confound axis fitting.

Intra-observer and inter-observer agreement between two surgeons using the test set

Two foot and ankle surgeons (Surgeon 1 [S1] and Surgeon 2 [S2] with 3 and 7 years of experience, respectively) annotated bone axes segmentation using LabelMe (version 5.1.0)11, and HVA and IMA were computed from the annotated axes. To assess observer agreement, we randomly selected 100 radiographs (50 HV and 50 control) from the test set. First, S1 and S2 independently annotated all 100 radiographs. Second, after a four-week washout period, both surgeons repeated the annotations on the same subset. Third, all annotations and derived angles were reviewed and approved by a senior foot and ankle surgeon (Surgeon 3 [S3], 20 years of experience). Reference measurements were defined as the surgeon-derived angles after senior review (S3). Finally, inter- and intra-observer reproducibility metrics were computed based on these measurements.

The average annotation time per radiograph for both surgeons was recorded across the test set. The model’s inference time was evaluated on the same test set using a CPU platform (12th Gen Intel® Core™ i5-12600KF).

DL model development and training

The DL model was trained using the training set, and its performance was monitored on the validation set for model selection during development. The segmentation network employed a UNet++ architecture with a MobileNetV2 backbone to balance accuracy and computational efficiency12. The workflow emulates clinical reasoning by first automatically and precisely segmenting the bone axes including the first proximal phalanx, the first metatarsal, and the second metatarsal. Following segmentation, the network outputs a probability map for each bone-axis mask, which was binarized using a threshold of 0.5 to obtain foreground pixels. The (x, y) coordinates of the foreground pixels were then used to fit a single central axis via robust linear regression, where the line parameters were estimated by minimizing the Welsch loss function13. Finally, the HVA and IMA were automatically computed from these axes based on geometric principles.

The UNet++ model was trained with cross-entropy loss function and the Adam optimizer, with a learning rate of 0.0025 and a batch size of 16 for 100 epochs. The entire validation set was used during development for model selection. The input size was set to 384 × 384 pixels. Training was conducted on a system equipped with a 12th Gen Intel® Core™ i5-12600KF CPU (3.70 GHz, 12 threads), 32.0 GB RAM, and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4060 Ti GPU. All experiments were performed on a Linux system (Ubuntu 18.04) with CUDA version 12.6. The model was implemented using Python 3.9.23 and PyTorch version 2.8.0.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation; categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r) was calculated to quantify the linear association, while the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was applied to assess consistency and reliability14–16. In this study, ICC was considered the primary metric of agreement, whereas Pearson’s r reflects correlation. The mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean squared error (RMSE) were employed to assess predictive accuracy for HVA and IMA17–19. Clinical relevance was further assessed by calculating the proportion of outliers, defined as cases in which the absolute difference between predicted and manual measurements exceeded 5° for HVA or 3° for IMA. These thresholds were adopted from prior studies as clinically unacceptable errors and were used to capture disagreement that could be meaningful for clinical interpretation and surgical planning7–9. Bland–Altman plots were constructed to visualize agreement between the DL model and the reference measurements by plotting the mean difference and 95% limits of agreement, allowing potential systematic bias or measurement dispersion to be identified20.

To compare predictive errors, the absolute errors of the DL model and each surgeon were compared on a paired basis using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Discussion

This study developed and validated a DL model for the automated measurement of HVA and IMA from weight-bearing radiographs. The principal findings demonstrate that our model achieved high agreement with manual measurements, performed comparably to foot and ankle surgeons, and exhibited remarkable efficiency. Furthermore, the DL model maintained performance across both HV and control radiographs within our single-center retrospective cohort, suggesting reasonable robustness within mixed HV and control radiographs in our cohort. Most notably, it offered an efficiency advantage, completing measurements in 0.13 s per radiograph, several orders of magnitude faster than manual annotation (1.83–2.58 min).

Previous research on automated HV angle measurement has primarily focused on HV patients, reporting outlier rates of 2%-11% for HVA and 2%-21% for IMA7–9. Our model’s performance is consistent with these reported ranges. To address limitations of previous work, such as the lack of publicly available models and comprehensive validation, we utilized a large cohort including both HV and control cases and benchmarked our model against surgeons of varying experience. Furthermore, we have made our trained model publicly available on [https://drive.google.com/file/d/1sDUfEgD2uZ5JL8BYWk51yQBRZ505drVe/view?usp=sharing] to promote reproducibility, external validation, and clinical translation.

Our findings indicate that the model achieved equal or better performance than the surgeons for IMA in terms of clinically relevant outliers, and it showed competitive (often lower) errors, particularly compared with the less experienced surgeon. In the HV group, it achieved higher ICC (0.887 vs. 0.849/0.872), lower MAE (1.147° vs. 1.373°/1.251°), and fewer outliers (4.6% vs. 7.9%/6.6%). In the control group, it maintained superior reliability (ICC 0.898 vs. 0.771/0.816) with fewer clinically relevant errors. This advantage may result from the fact that the distal shaft of the second metatarsal frequently overlaps with the distal shaft of the first metatarsal on weight-bearing radiographs, making it difficult for surgeons to consistently define the metatarsal axes. Such overlap, combined with variations in bone morphology, frequently introduces subjectivity and interobserver differences. In contrast, the model, trained on a large dataset, can learn to recognize and compensate for these overlapping patterns, providing more stable axis localization and reducing measurement variability.

The DL model achieved HVA measurement performance comparable to experienced surgeons, with an ICC of 0.902 in the HV group, similar to the surgeons’ values (0.896 and 0.908). Importantly, the model maintained robust agreement (ICC: 0.821) in the control group, where inter-observer reliability between the surgeons decreased. This contrast underscores the model’s potential to serve as a reliable and consistent tool for HVA assessment. By minimizing the variability introduced by multiple human observers, the model can help standardize evaluation and improve preoperative planning.

An error case is shown in Fig. 4, where a false-positive segmentation near the distal phalanx led to an incorrect fitted axis and an erroneous HVA. Such failures may occur in radiographs with low contrast, motion blur, overlapping bony contours, or severe deformity, where the boundary between adjacent structures becomes unclear. To reduce similar errors, future work could include more hard cases during training (e.g., severe HV, overlap, low-quality images), apply targeted augmentation that mimics these conditions, and add simple post-processing checks (e.g., expected axis length/orientation ranges and consistency between the three bone axes). In a real-world workflow, the system could also flag cases for quick review when these checks are violated or when the predicted segmentation is uncertain, allowing clinicians to confirm or correct the measurement before use.

The model has three main clinical implications. First, its computational efficiency may improve workflow by allowing near-real-time integration into picture archiving and communication systems, thereby providing automated angle measurements directly on radiographs, standardizing assessments, and reducing clinician workload. Second, the model’s high accuracy and low outlier rate in IMA measurement are clinically relevant, as this parameter plays a key role in surgical planning for first-metatarsal osteotomy21. An automated and consistent tool may help reduce variability near decision thresholds. In particular, when HVA/IMA values are close to common surgical decision thresholds, better repeatability may reduce disagreement between observers and support more consistent treatment decisions. Moreover, in longitudinal follow-up and large cohort studies, automated measurements can standardize reporting across time and readers, facilitating reproducible research workflows. Third, the use of a lightweight network, MobileNetV2, supports potential deployment on portable devices such as smartphones. By making the model publicly accessible, we hope to promote external validation and collaborative improvement, which are necessary for broader clinical integration. Finally, our comparison was designed to reflect routine practice in which radiographic angle measurements may be performed by clinicians with different levels of experience, with more experienced surgeons often reviewing or confirming the results. This provides a practical context for interpreting model performance relative to typical clinical workflows.

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective, single-center design and the exclusion of post-operative, fractured, or pediatric feet limit the generalizability of the current findings to these complex scenarios. Furthermore, to address class imbalance, we applied data augmentation to control radiographs only in the training set, which may improve robustness but could also introduce bias if the augmented images do not fully reflect real-world variation. Therefore, the generalizability of the current model should be further confirmed using external, multi-institutional datasets with a larger number of non-HV radiographs and diverse imaging conditions, including severe deformity, low-quality radiographs, and images acquired using different vendors or equipment.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed and validated a DL-based model for automated measurement of HVA and IMA on weight-bearing radiographs. The model demonstrated accuracy and reliability comparable to experienced surgeons, with consistent performance across both HV and control groups. By reducing observer variability and improving efficiency, the model may facilitate standardized angle reporting for surgical planning and large-scale research; however, its impact on clinical decision-making and outcomes should be assessed in prospective, multi-center studies. Future multi-center validation is warranted to confirm its generalizability and assess its impact on clinical decision-making.
