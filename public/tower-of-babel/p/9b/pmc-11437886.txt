Mitochondrial transplantation attenuates lipopolysaccharide-induced acute respiratory distress syndrome
Authors: Seo-Eun Lee, In-Hyeon Kim, Young Cheol Kang, Yujin Kim, Shin-Hye Yu, Jeong Seon Yeo, Iksun Kwon, Jun Hyeok Lim, Je-Hein Kim, Kyuboem Han, Sung-Hwan Kim, Chun-Hyung Kim
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe mitochondria are essential organelles not only providing cellular energy in the form of ATP, but also regulating the inflammatory response and the cell death program. Mitochondrial dysfunction has been associated with various human diseases, including metabolic syndromes as well as inflammatory and neurodegenerative diseases. Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is an acute pulmonary disorder characterized by uncontrolled alveolar inflammation, apoptotic lung epithelial/endothelial cells, and pulmonary edema. Despite the high mortality of ARDS, an effective pharmacotherapy to treat this disease has not been established yet. Therefore, identifying a novel targeted therapy for ARDS is important. Recently, exogenous mitochondrial transplantation was reported to be beneficial for treating mitochondrial dysfunction. The current study aimed to investigate the therapeutic effect of mitochondrial transplantation on ARDS in vitro and in vivo.MethodsMitochondria were isolated from human stem cells. For in vitro efficacy of mitochondrial transplantation on the inflammation and cell death, murine alveolar macrophages MH-S and human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells HPMECs were exposed to LPS, respectively. The ARDS mice model established by a single intratracheal instillation of LPS was used for in vivo efficacy of intravenously treated mitochondria.ResultsOur results showed that the mitochondria isolated from human stem cells exhibited an anti-inflammatory effect against alveolar macrophages and an anti-apoptotic effect against the alveolar epithelial cells. Furthermore, intravenous mitochondrial treatment was associated with the attenuation of lung injury in the LPS-induced ARDS mice.ConclusionDual effects of mitochondria on anti-inflammation and anti-apoptosis support the potential of mitochondrial transplantation as a novel therapeutic strategy for ARDS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-024-03304-2.

Background

Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) is an acute pulmonary disorder characterized by uncontrolled alveolar inflammation, apoptotic lung epithelial/endothelial cells, and pulmonary edema [1, 2]. In ARDS, dysregulated alveolar inflammation induces lung microvascular barrier disruption by increasing the endothelial and epithelial injuries, ultimately leading to protein-rich edema fluid accumulation [3]. Despite the high mortality and morbidity rates among ARDS patients, there is currently no effective therapy to prevent or ameliorate the excessive inflammation and lung cell damage. Therefore, developing new therapeutic approaches for this devastating disorder based on its pathophysiological mechanism is important.

Mitochondria are indispensable for cell survival by providing ATP via oxidative phosphorylation. They also have a non-bioenergetic role in controlling the metabolism and inflammatory response and are involved in the cell death program. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that the pathogenesis of acute and chronic lung diseases is associated with mitochondrial dysfunction, including reduced mitochondrial biogenesis, impaired oxidative phosphorylation, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS), incomplete mitophagy, and increased apoptosis of lung epithelial cells [4, 5]. Therefore, targeting mitochondrial damage is a promising strategy for alleviating the exacerbation of respiratory diseases.

Recently, mitochondrial transplantation has received attention as an attractive therapeutic strategy in various disease models [6, 7]. Many studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial transplantation rescued the heart, kidney, and liver from ischemia/reperfusion (IR)-induced injury and apoptosis [8–11] and alleviated behavioral and cognitive deficits in an animal model by modulating inflammation and apoptosis [12–14]. In a sepsis animal model, the transplantation of syngeneic and xenogeneic mitochondria improved the survival rate of an LPS-induced sepsis animal by attenuating the inflammation, with suppression mediated by blocking the nuclear factor kappa B (NFκB) signaling [15–17]. Intercellular transfer of the mitochondria between the bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BM-MSCs) and macrophages improved the bacterial clearance in sepsis by increasing the phagocytic activity of macrophages [18]. In an LPS-induced acute lung injury (ALI) mouse model, mitochondrial transfer between BM-MSCs and alveolar epithelial cells protected the animals from developing ALI by increasing the alveolar ATP concentrations and decreasing the surfactant secretion and mortality risk [19]. Although the underlying mechanisms of such benefits are unclear, it has been suggested that the mitochondria may improve the cellular function by providing cellular bioenergetics and regulating the inflammatory response and apoptosis.

The human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cell (UC-MSCs)-derived and platelet-derived mitochondria demonstrated a strong anti-inflammatory effect on human macrophage cells by mediating the blockade of the NFκB signaling pathway [15] and an improvement of cell viability by attenuating the wound-induced apoptosis in dermal fibroblasts [20]. Furthermore, it was demonstrated that intravenously injected mitochondria preferentially migrated to and were taken up by lung tissues of bleomycin-induced idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) mice wherein mitochondria are damaged [21]. Recently, we developed clinical good manufacturing practice (GMP)-grade mitochondria that showed a good safety and efficacy in patients with refractory polymyositis and dermatomyositis in a phase 1/2a clinical study (clinical trial number: NCT04976140). The present study aimed to determine if the transplantation of mitochondria derived from UC-MSCs and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) can be a potential therapeutic approach for ARDS.

Materials and methods

Cell culture and treatment

Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (UC-MSCs) were obtained by primary culture of the umbilical cord from a healthy donor with an informed consent. The study was approved by the Public Institutional Review Board (IRB) designated by the Ministry of Health and Welfare, Republic of Korea (IRB No. P01-202002-31-008). The UC-MSCs were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium Eagle Alpha Modification (α-MEM, Hyclone, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, USA) and 10 ng/mL fibroblast growth factor-2 (FGF-2; CHA Meditech Co, Republic of Korea). Human induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) were kindly provided Dr. Baek Soo Han (Korea Research Institute of Bioscience and Biotechnology). iPSCs were seeded on matrigel-coated (Invitrogen) plates and were fed with Essential 8 Flex Medium (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Murine alveolar macrophages (MH-Ss) purchased from ATCC (Cat no. CRL-2019) were maintained in RPMI-1640 media (RPMI-1640, Welgene, Korea) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and antibiotics (100 units/ml penicillin and 100 mg/ml streptomycin) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Human pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells (HPMECs) were purchased from PromoCell (PromoCell, Germany) and were grown in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium MV (PromoCell, Germany) with 0.5% SupplementMix (PromoCell, Germany) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. For LPS stimulation, the cells were incubated with 100 ng/ml LPS (Sigma-Aldrich) for 24 h followed by the treatment of the mitochondria for 24 h.

Isolation of mitochondria from uMT and iMT

The mitochondria were obtained from the UC-MSCs and iPSCs by differential centrifugation, as previously reported [15] and referred to as uMT and iMT, respectively. Briefly, the cells were disrupted by a nitrogen cavitation and then centrifuged at 2,000 ×g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 12,000×g for 15 min to precipitate the mitochondria. The pellet was suspended in 500-µl SHE buffer (20mM HEPES, 250mM sucrose, 2mM EGTA, pH 7.4) and then centrifuged at 20,000 ×g for 10 min. The final pellet was resuspended in 100-µl SHE buffer and kept at 4 °C. The quantity of mitochondria was determined by a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay.

Labeling of the mitochondria

The mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS) of succinate dehydrogenase complex subunit C was fused with the green fluorescence protein (GFP) gene and red fluorescence protein (dsRED), resulting in MTS-GFP and MTS-dsRED, respectively. Lentiviral expression vectors were constructed by cloning the MTS-dsRED and MTS-GFP genes into a lentiviral vector plasmid with EF1α promoter. For lentivirus production, the HEK293T cells were seeded on 10-cm dishes at 70–80% confluency. The cells were then co-transfected with 4-µg lentiviral expression vector plasmid along with 4-µg pLP1, 4-µg pLP2, and 3-µg pLP/VCVG (Packaging Mix, Invitrogen). The transfection mixtures were prepared in 1-mL Opti-MEM containing DNA and 30-µL of 293 Tran (Origene, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. After transfection, the lentiviral supernatant samples were collected after 24, 48, and 72 h. The collected lentiviral supernatants were concentrated using polyethylene glycol 6000 and suspended in an α-MEM medium. To label the mitochondria with GFP and dsRED, UC-MSCs and iPSCs were seeded into 150-mm plates at 70–80% density and then incubated with lentiviral supernatants and 4-µg/mL polybrene, followed by further incubation for 72 h.

Evaluation of Intracellular ATP and complex IV activity

We seeded 8 × 103 HPMECs and 2.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\:\times\:\:$$\end{document}104 MH-Ss into a 96-well white tissue plate and incubated with uMT or iMT for 24 h. After washing with DPBS, 100 µl of CellTiter-Glo® reagent (Promega, USA) was added to each well. Cells were incubated for 10 min at 37 °C on an orbital shaker to induce cell lysis. The luminescence signal was measured using a SYNERGY HTX multi-mode reader (BioTek, USA). Mitochondrial respiratory chain enzymatic activities of isolated mitochondria were assessed [22]. Complex IV activities were evaluated by preparing a solution of 1 mM cytochrome c in 20 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), which was subsequently reduced by using a very small amount of sodium hydrosulfite transferred from the tip of a pipette. The cytochrome c solution was vigorously mixed to prepare fresh reduced cytochrome c solution before use. Next, 50 µl of 100 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 10 µl of reduced cytochrome c were added to each well.

Animals and Mitochondrial treatment

Six-week-old male BALB/c mice were purchased from Orient Bio (Seongnam-si, Republic of Korea). The animals were randomly distributed to the following four experimental groups (five mice per group): (1) sham, (2) LPS, (3) LPS + uMT; and (4) LPS + iMT. To establish the ARDS mouse model, mice received a single intratracheal instillation of 0.1-mg/kg LPS in 50-µL of saline using an automatic video instillator [23]. The LPS dose was selected based on an earlier study of LPS-induced lung injury in mice [24]. Five micrograms of uMT/head or 5-µg of iMT/head was intravenously injected twice 2 h before and after the LPS treatment. The animals were necropsied at 48 h after the LPS treatment. All animals were euthanized by inhalation of nitrous oxide with 5% isoflurane and humanely sacrificed by exsanguination from the vena cava and aorta.

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid analysis

The bronchoalveolar lavage fluid preparation (BALF) was collected with 0.7-mL cold phosphate- buffered saline in each right lung of the mouse, and the total cells in the BALF were measured using a NucleoCounter (NC-250; Chemometec, Gydevang, Denmark). The mouse TNF-α and IL-6 levels were measured in the BALF using the Quantikine ELISA Kit (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Histopathological examination

The left lung tissues were removed, weighed, fixed in a 10% neutral-buffered formalin solution, and embedded in paraffin. The tissue Sect. (2-µm thick) were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) for histopathological examination. Histopathological analysis was performed under a light microscope at 200× magnification (scale bars represent 100 μm).

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism ver. 8.0.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). For multiple comparisons, a one-way analysis of variance with Dunnet’s post-hoc test was performed.

Results

Migration of exogenous fluorescence-labeled mitochondria into the pulmonary cells

Exogenous mitochondria can be internalized into cells via macropinocytosis [25]. We first examined whether the fluorescence-labeled mitochondria could be transferred to the alveolar macrophages and pulmonary microvascular epithelial cells. To confirm the migration into cells, MTGFP and MTdsRED were isolated from UC-MSCs and iPSCs infected with lentivirus-mediated MTS-GFP and MTS-dsRED, respectively (Fig. 1A) [21]. They were co-incubated with the alveolar macrophages MH-S and HPMEC. The confocal images clearly showed that the fluorescence-labeled mitochondria were successfully internalized into both cells by simple co-incubation (Fig. 1B).

The effect of mitochondrial transplantation on mitochondrial functions

Cellular function was assessed by measuring intracellular ATP and complex IV activity after treating HPMECs and MH-Ss with mitochondria isolated from UC-MSCs and iPSCs (referred to as uMT and iMT, respectively). Cocultures of HPMECs with mitochondria derived from UC-MSCs and iPSCs showed increased levels of ATP production and complex IV activity (Supplementary Fig. 1A and 1B). The similar effect was observed in MH-S cells (Supplementary Fig. 1C and 1D)

Anti-inflammatory effects of mitochondrial transplantation in LPS-induced MH-S cells

To investigate the effect of mitochondrial transplantation on the inflammatory processes in the alveolar macrophages, we measured the cytokine levels of the LPS-stimulated MH-S macrophages, followed by the treatment of uMT and iMT. First, the expressions of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β mRNA were determined by quantitative RT-PCR. As shown in Fig. 2A, increased TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β mRNA levels were observed in the LPS-activated MH-S macrophages. The treatment of LPS-activated MH-S macrophages with uMT and iMT significantly reduced the TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β mRNA expressions. Next, we measured the TNF-α and IL-6 levels of the LPS-treated MH-S macrophages by ELISA. In the absence of LPS stimulation, the treatment of MH-S macrophages with vehicle had no effect on TNF-α and IL-6 production. However, the LPS-stimulated MH-S macrophages significantly increased the secretion of TNF-α and IL-6 (Fig. 2B). Importantly, the treatment of LPS-stimulated MH-S macrophages with uMT and iMT significantly reduced the levels of TNF-α and IL-6 released, respectively (Fig. 2B). Particularly, the secretion of TNF-α and IL-6 was decreased by 73.6% and 39.3%, respectively, by the treatment with uMT, and by 68.2% and 41.1%, respectively, by the administration of iMT. Furthermore, western blot assay clearly showed that the treatment of uMT and iMT decreased the LPS-induced TNF-α and IL-6 protein expressions (Fig. 2C). These results suggested that mitochondrial transplantation could suppress the production of inflammatory cytokines in LPS-stimulated alveolar macrophages.

Next, we examined whether mitochondrial transplantation affects the MH-S macrophage polarization. To investigate this, the MH-S macrophages were treated with LPS before mitochondrial transplantation and then macrophage M1 and M2 polarizations were assessed by measuring the expression of iNOS and ARG-1 as classical M1 and M2 markers, respectively. As shown in Fig. 2D, the expression of the macrophage M1 marker iNOS was significantly upregulated by LPS and alleviated by the administration of both uMT and iMT. Consistent with mRNA expression, the iNOS protein expression was also increased by LPS, and this increase was inhibited by both uMT and iMT treatments (Fig. 2E). The expression of the macrophage M2 marker ARG-1 increased approximately twofold as compared to that of the control by LPS induction, and M2 polarization further significantly increased after the iMT treatment (~ 3.6-fold) but not uMT. Similarly, mitochondrial transplantation showed that the increase in the ARG-1 protein expression was higher in the LPS-treated MH-S macrophages than in the control and LPS groups (Fig. 2D and E). Together, these results suggests that mitochondrial transplantation mitigates M1 macrophage polarization and promotes M2 macrophage polarization at the same time.

Anti-apoptotic effects of mitochondrial transplantation in LPS-induced MH-S cells

Apoptosis of the vascular endothelial cell is a hallmark of ARDS. Given that the mitochondria play a key role in regulating the programed cell death, we investigated whether mitochondrial transplantation modulates the apoptosis of HPMECs exposed to LPS. As expected, LPS-induced cell death in approximately 27% of HPMECs. However, the uMT and iMT treatments significantly reduced HPMEC apoptosis by 10% that was induced by LPS as determined by the results of the flow cytometry assay (Fig. 3A and B). Furthermore, the western blot assay showed that the mitochondria significantly suppressed the expression of pro-apoptotic proteins, including cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-8, cleaved caspase-9, and cytochrome C. Contrarily, a marked increase in Bcl-2, an anti-apoptotic protein, was observed following the administration of mitochondria as compared with the administration of LPS (Fig. 3C). These results suggested that mitochondria transplantation exerts a protective role in HPMEC apoptosis by modulating the expression of pro- and anti-apoptotic genes.

Attenuation of Lung injury by mitochondrial transplantation in LPS-induced ARDS mice

To investigate the in vivo effect of mitochondrial transplantation in LPS-induced ARDS mice model, the histopathological changes in the lung were assessed by performing H&E staining and the lung injury score was measured. As shown in Fig. 4A; Table 1, the lungs in the LPS group showed extensive damages characterized by ALI, including acute inflammation in alveolar, inflammatory cell infiltration, and increased alveolar macrophages. Although the mitochondrial treatment groups also exhibited lung tissue damage, the incidence and severity of histopathological changes were lower in the mitochondrial treatment groups than in the LPS group. We evaluated whether mitochondrial transplantation changed the LPS-induced left lung weight changes. The LPS treatment caused a significant increase in the absolute weights of the left lung as compared with the sham group. However, the iMT treatment significantly alleviated the LPS-induced lung weight increase. Although statistically insignificant, uMT showed the effects similar to those of iMT (Fig. 4B). Next, we assessed the number of total cells and all major leukocytes in the BALF. The total cells and neutrophils in the LPS group were significantly increased as compared with that in the sham group. However, mitochondrial transplantation did not induce any changes in the number of cells, which showed an increase with LPS administration (Fig. 4C and D). No effect of LPS or mitochondrial transplantation was observed on the number of macrophages, monocytes, and eosinophils in the BALF (Fig. 4E). To investigate whether mitochondrial transplantation modulates the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, the inflammatory cytokine levels in the BALF were measured by ELISA (Fig. 4D and E). The TNF-α and IL-6 levels were significantly increased in the LPS group as compared with the sham group. Contrarily, intravenous injection of iMT remarkably attenuated the increase in TNF-α and IL-6 levels induced by LPS. Albeit not statistically significant, the uMT administration tended to reduce the LPS-induced TNF-α and IL-6 productions in the BALF. These results suggested that mitochondrial transplantation protects against LPS-induced lung injury by modulating the inflammatory responses.

(F and G) The expression levels of the TNF-α and IL-6 proteins in the BALF were detected by ELISA. The TNF-α and IL-6 proteins in the BALF were increased in the LPS-treated groups. The iMT treatment significantly mitigated the LPS-induced increase of the TNF-α and IL-6 proteins. Discussion

In the present study, we demonstrated that the mitochondrial transplantation reduced inflammation and lung injury in vitro and in vivo. The results demonstrated that the isolated mitochondria were successfully transferred into the alveolar macrophages and pulmonary endothelial cells (Fig. 1). In the alveolar macrophages, mitochondrial transplantation reduced mRNA expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines and controlled the M1 and M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 2). Additionally, mitochondrial transplantation exerts a protective role in HPMEC apoptosis by modulating the expression of pro- and anti-apoptotic genes (Fig. 3). Lastly, mitochondrial transplantation protects against the LPS-induced lung injury through the modulation of inflammatory responses in the ARDS mouse model (Fig. 4).

ARDS is an acute respiratory illness characterized by an unregulated inflammatory response, necrotic or apoptotic lung epithelial and endothelial cells, and pulmonary edema [1, 2]. Despite the high mortality rate of ARDS, an effective pharmacotherapy to treat the disease has not been established yet. Therefore, identifying a novel targeted therapy for ARDS is urgently needed. Recently, we demonstrated that the administration of mitochondria isolated from the human UC-MSCs and platelets suppressed the inflammatory responses in LPS-induced macrophages in vitro and increased the survival rates of LPS-treated mice in vivo. The anti-inflammatory effect of mitochondrial treatment is mediated by blocking the NFκB signaling pathway through the inhibition of NFκB phosphorylation and nuclear translocation. Furthermore, we developed clinical good manufacturing practice (GMP)-grade mitochondria, which were isolated from the human UC-MSCs. In a human phase 1/2a clinical trial, GMP-grade mitochondrial transplantation showed a good safety and promising efficacy in patients with refractory polymyositis. Based on these encouraging results, the present study investigated whether mitochondrial transplantation would be a potential therapy for the ARDS treatment.

First, we tested whether the mitochondria isolated from the UC-MSCs and iPSCs could be transferred into the alveolar macrophages and pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells. The exogenous mitochondria are internalized into different cells or tissues by micropinocytosis [25] and can be artificially transferred into the cells by co-incubation [26, 27], centrifugation [28], MitoCeption [29], and Mitopunch [30]. We previously showed that the fluorescence-labeled mitochondria could be efficiently transferred to various fibroblast cells by co-incubation [21]. Similarly, the fluorescence-labeled mitochondria were successfully taken up by both macrophages and endothelial cells by co-incubation (Fig. 1), suggesting that the exogenous mitochondria can be completely transferred into alveolar macrophages and endothelial cells.

One of the pathological features of ARDS is the infiltration of inflammatory cells, including alveolar macrophages and neutrophils. A complicated network of pro-inflammatory cytokines secreted from the inflammatory cells triggers and aggravates the ARDS. Given that the mitochondrial transplantation exhibited anti-inflammatory effects in THP-1 macrophages by suppressing the NFκB signaling pathway [15], we hypothesized that mitochondrial transplantation also suppresses the inflammation in the alveolar macrophages induced by LPS treatment. As shown in Fig. 2, mitochondrial transplantation reduced the mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNFα, IL-6, and IL-1b as well as secretion and protein expression of TNFα and IL-6 in the alveolar macrophages, MH-S cells, activated by LPS. Likewise, the post-treatment of mitochondria significantly reduced the expression of TNFα and IL-6 in the BALF of the LPS-treated mice (Fig. 4D and E). Furthermore, the mitochondrial transplantation reduced the M1 macrophage polarization and increased the M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 2D and E). Together, these results suggest that mitochondrial transplantation can alleviate the inflammation by suppressing the hyperactive inflammatory cells in vitro and in vivo. Another pathogenesis of ARDS is characterized by the cell death of the lung epithelial cells. The mitochondria have a critical role in apoptosis by coordinating caspase activation via the release of cytochrome c. Mitochondrial transplantation reduced the IR injury in the heart, lung, kidney, and brain by enhancing cell viability [8, 31–33]. Ma et al. reported that the transplantation of platelet-derived mitochondria alleviated the neuronal apoptosis and cognitive impairment in the db/db mice model [34]. We determined whether mitochondrial transplantation could regulate the LPS-induced apoptosis in human microvascular epithelial cells. As shown in Fig. 3, mitochondrial transplantation attenuated LPS-induced apoptosis by decreasing the proapoptotic genes, including cytochrome C, cleaved caspase-3, cleaved caspase-8, and cleaved caspase-9, and by increasing the anti-apoptotic gene, Bcl-2.

Accumulating evidence has shown that the mitochondrial dysfunction in the lung cells may be involved in the pathophysiological process of ARDS [5]. Impaired ATP synthesis and OXPHOS in the mitochondria induced the necrotic cell death of lung epithelial cells in the lung injury model [35, 36]. Furthermore, the level of ATP5A protein, which is a subunit of the mitochondrial ATP synthase complex, was reduced in the lung epithelial cells of patients with ARDS as compared with that in the normal lung epithelial cells [35]. Islam et al. reported that the mitochondrial transfer from the BM-MSCs to alveolar epithelial cells reduced the mortality of animals in the LPS-induced ALI model [19]. In the in vivo LPS-induced ARDS mouse model, we observed that the intravenously injected mitochondria improved the lung damages and decreased the pro-inflammatory cytokines in the BALF. Although our studies exhibit that mitochondrial transplantation improve the lung injury by suppressing the pro-inflammatory cytokines and inhibiting the apoptosis of endothelial cells, it remains unclear whether mitochondrial transplantation attenuates the mitochondrial dysfunction. In this context, it is necessary to see if mitochondrial transplantation improves oxygen species consumption rate or attenuates the intracellular ROS level.

Experimental animal models—including LPS, live bacteria, acid aspiration, and cecal ligation and puncture (CLP)—have been developed to reproduce acute human ARDS [37]. This study used the LPS-induced mouse model as an ARDS animal model. Although LPS reflects the acute pathophysiology of ARDS by inducing leukocyte accumulation, pulmonary edema, and severe lung tissue inflammation, it doesn’t cause substantial epithelial or endothelial damage nor recapitulates the effects of bacterial infection observed in patients with ARDS [38, 39]. Therefore, a key limitation of this study is that the LPS-induced lung injury model only represented the clinical translation of patients with ARDS. Future studies are warranted to examine the effects of mitochondria in an infectious ARDS animal model. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report demonstrating that the mitochondrial transplantation improves the lung injury in the ARDS animal model, which was induced by intratracheal LPS administration.

Conclusion

Our study findings provide evidence that mitochondria transplantation can be developed as a novel therapeutic for ARDS. Especially, exogenous mitochondria are directly transferable into the alveolar macrophages and pulmonary epithelial cells and reduce lung damage and inflammation. Thus, mitochondrial transplantation has great potential as a candidate treatment for ALI.
