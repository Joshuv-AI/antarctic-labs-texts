Inflammation and day-to-day occurrence of atrial fibrillation
Authors: August Krebs Hessellund, Emilie Katrine Kongebro, Ketil Jørgen Haugan, Lucas Yixi Xing, Oliver Bundgaard Vad, Claus Graff, Daniel Camillo Spona, Jonas Alexander Baadsgaard, Søren Højberg, Axel Brandes, Lars Køber, Ruth Frikke-Schmidt, Jesper Hastrup Svendsen, Søren Zöga Diederichsen

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsInflammation is central in atrial fibrillation (AF) pathophysiology, but the temporal association between inflammatory activity and AF remains uncertain. This study investigated the day-to-day relationship between plasma C-reactive protein (CRP) levels and AF occurrence in continuously monitored individuals.Methods and results Post hoc analysis of the LOOP study randomizing participants with stroke risk factors to implantable loop recorder (ILR) screening (n = 1501) or usual care. ILR raw data were linked to blood samples collected within 1 day. The outcome was AF episodes lasting ≥60 min. Associations were examined using generalized and linear mixed models and as a self-controlled case series (SCCS). ILR and CRP data were available for 1065 participants combining >1.2 million days of heart rhythm monitoring (including ∼40 000 days with AF) with >7000 CRP measurements. CRP was higher during days with AF (geometric mean 8.37 [7.11–9.86] mg/L) vs. non-AF days (4.02 [3.71–4.35] mg/L). Each doubling of CRP was associated with 36% higher odds of AF (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] 1.36 [1.26–1.47]). Compared with CRP ≤3 mg/L, the odds of AF increased for CRP >10–50 and >50 mg/L (aOR 3.50 [2.18–5.60] and 5.75 [3.48–9.50], respectively). In SCCS analyses, CRP >10 mg/L was associated with higher incidence of AF compared to lower levels (incidence rate ratio 2.41 [1.53–3.80]).ConclusionThis exploratory study found a temporal and dose–response relationship between CRP levels and AF occurrence, supporting inflammation as an acute trigger and underscoring the need to evaluate inflammatory markers as potential targets in AF management.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov, identifier: NCT02036450.

Introduction

Atrial fibrillation (AF) affects up to one-third of all individuals throughout a lifetime1 and is associated with serious complications including stroke and heart failure.2,3 Increasing evidence suggests that inflammation is a key contributor to AF pathophysiology.4–9 Several biomarkers have been linked to AF occurrence,10–15 and the prevalence of AF is increased in conditions characterized by chronic inflammation.16–19

With advances in early detection of AF and recognition of AF’s phenotypic heterogeneity,20,21 there is a growing need to clarify the role of inflammation in AF—both as a potential acute trigger and as a chronic driver of AF. C-reactive-protein (CRP) is a well-known biomarker of systemic inflammation,22,23 and increased CRP levels are associated with both postoperative AF24,25 and incidentally detected AF.13,26–30 Prior studies have relied on short-term heart rhythm monitoring or clinically diagnosed AF and infrequent measurements of CRP, limiting the ability to characterize the temporal relationship between inflammation and AF occurrence.

We hypothesized that a higher-resolution dataset would better demonstrate acute inflammation as a potential trigger of AF and chronic inflammation as playing a role in the long-term development and maintenance of AF. This study therefore aimed to investigate the day-to-day within-individual association between CRP levels and AF occurrence in a large dataset of serial blood samples and adjudicated AF episodes detected by long-term implantable loop recorder (ILR) monitoring in participants enrolled in a large, randomized trial.

Methods

Study design

This study was a post hoc analysis of the randomized controlled LOOP study (Atrial Fibrillation Detected by Continuous Electrocardiogram Monitoring Using ILR to Prevent Stroke in High-Risk Individuals), comparing continuous heart rhythm monitoring with blood samples collected throughout the study period. The LOOP study design and main findings have previously been published.31,32 In the study, 6004 persons at high risk of AF and stroke were randomized to receive ILR or usual care in a 1:3 ratio. Eligibility criteria included age 70–90 years and at least one of the following risk factors: hypertension, diabetes, previous stroke, or heart failure, while participants with a history of AF, current malignant disease, or cardiac implantable electronic devices were excluded. The study was conducted at four centres in Denmark from 31 January 2014, to 1 December 2020. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was approved by the Regional Scientific Ethics Committee for the Capital Region of Denmark (H-42013-025).

Participants with ILR were followed with continuous heart rhythm monitoring until end of battery life of the device, device extraction, or death. Arrhythmia episodes were reviewed daily, and incident AF episodes lasting at least 6 min were adjudicated independently by at least two senior cardiologists (K.J.H., S.H., A.B., J.H.S.). Disputes were resolved by majority vote, with the involvement of a third senior cardiologist who was blinded to previous evaluations. Upon the debut of adjudicated AF, subsequent AF episodes with a duration of at least 6 min were included in the final dataset.

Blood test results were obtained from all laboratories having the study participants in their catchment area—i.e. analysing blood samples from all study participants as part of usual care (via the participants’ visits to general practitioners, outpatient clinics, or during hospitalizations). These laboratories are all hospital-based and use high-sensitivity assays for CRP. Each blood test was time- and date-stamped allowing for direct linkage with ILR data collected throughout the study period.

Study outcomes and statistical analysis

The primary outcome of this post hoc analysis was the occurrence of daily AF lasting ≥60 min. Secondary outcomes included daily AF lasting ≥6, ≥240, and ≥480 min. All potential episodes lasting <6 min were not adjudicated as part of the trial were considered not to be AF in this study.

Normally distributed continuous data are presented as mean with standard deviation, while non-normally distributed variables are presented as median with interquartile range. Categorical data are presented as frequencies with percentages. Logistic regression results are presented as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The Pearson’s Chi-squared test and the Wilcoxon rank sum test were used for the comparisons of categorical and continuous data, respectively. Log10 transformation was applied for data visualization, and Log2 transformation was applied for model analysis.

To couple heart rhythm measurements with corresponding CRP measurements, we included ILR data from the day before, the same day, and the day after each blood sample for each study participant. This approach was chosen to analyse temporally relevant ILR data coupled with blood samples while also ensuring an adequate sample size. In case of multiple same-day CRP measurements, the daily CRP mean was used for coupling with ILR data. The day with the longest daily AF duration in the 3-day window around the blood sample was selected and paired with the corresponding CRP measurement. CRP measurements below the lower limit of detection were imputed as half the detection limit (e.g. values reported as <3 mg/L were assigned a value of 1.5 mg/L).33

Data was analysed both at the AF episode level and at the individual level. In initial, descriptive analyses, the distribution of all CRP measurements was visualized in a density plot stratified by AF status (i.e. CRP measurements coupled with AF vs. not coupled with AF). Further, the relationship between CRP levels and AF was visualized using a sliding scale approach. Here, the proportion of blood samples coupled with AF was calculated in windows of log-transformed CRP values of 0.1 (10 mg/L) with 0.05 increments for each subsequent window. Finally, a linear mixed model with a random intercept for each participant was applied to estimate differences in mean CRP values coupled with AF vs. no AF. Due to the skewed distribution, CRP values were log-transformed before analysis. Model results were back-transformed, allowing for interpretation of group differences in geometric mean CRP levels. Model assumptions were assessed by examining residuals using a QQ plot.

In the main analysis, generalized linear mixed-effect regression was applied to explore the within-individual association between CRP levels and the occurrence of AF. Here, CRP was modelled as a continuous variable on the log2 scale. As such, the resulting OR reflect the change in odds of AF occurring in a 1-day window from a CRP sampling for each doubling in CRP concentration, with values above 1 indicating higher odds of AF at elevated CRP levels. The models included a random intercept for each participant to account for between-individual differences and to avoid pseudo-replication. Three different logistic mixed effect models were fitted: an unadjusted model, a model adjusted for sex, age, blood sample count, and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, thyrotoxicosis, valvular disease, heart failure, and previous acute myocardial infarction), and lastly, a model adjusted for sex, age, blood sample count, comorbidities, and log-transformed AF burden. Here, the blood sample count corresponded to the total amount of CRP measurements available for each participant. AF burden was calculated as the percentage of time spent in AF of the total duration of ILR monitoring for each participant.34 Prior to inclusion in the models, AF burden was log-transformed due to skewed distribution, and continuous variables (e.g. age and blood sample count) were standardized. The models were applied for the primary and secondary outcomes, where AF durations below the cutoff were excluded. For the primary outcome, sensitivity analyses were conducted using ILR data from the same day only and the same day and the day after each blood sample.

To further explore a potential dose-dependent relationship, CRP levels were categorized as follows: ≤3, >3–10, >10–50, and >50 mg/L28,35–37 These strata of CRP measurements were then analysed in the three generalized linear mixed models as mentioned above, with CRP ≤3 mg/L as the reference category. The resulting ORs reflect the change in odds of AF in each CRP category relative to this reference.

To characterize the role of chronic inflammation in the development and maintenance of AF, participants were dichotomized according to CRP profile (those who consistently had CRP measurements ≥3 mg/L vs. those with at least one CRP measurement <3 mg/L38). For this analysis, only participants with ≥4 blood samples taken during monitoring were included. AF occurrence and AF burden during the entire monitoring period was then compared between these groups.

Lastly, data were analysed as a self-controlled case series (SCCS)39 where the incidence of AF in the same individual was compared during states of inflammation, defined as CRP values >10 mg/L and states of no inflammation, defined as CRP values ≤10 mg/L. Participants were included in this analysis if they had AF detected and had at least one CRP measurement ≤10 mg/L and at least one >10 mg/L. An incidence rate ratio (IRR) representing the proportion of days with AF at times with inflammation (CRP >10 mg/L) vs. without (CRP ≤10 mg/L) was calculated for each participant, and an average effect was calculated by assigning weight by number of CRP measurements per participant.

Data handling and analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.4.3 (R Foundation), and RStudio, version 2023.10.31.

Results

Study overview

Of the 6004 participants included in the LOOP study, 1501 were randomized to the ILR group and the remaining 4503 were randomized to the control group. A total of 1420 participants in the ILR group underwent ILR implantation, and complete ILR raw datasets detailing the onset and duration of all AF episodes were available for 1410 of them (99%). The ILR dataset included more than 1.6 million days of continuous heart rhythm monitoring in the 1410 participants and included more than 50 000 days with AF lasting at least 6 min. Of the participants with available ILR data, 1065 (76%) had CRP measurements taken during continuous monitoring. Potential role of chronic inflammation

A total of 204 participants had both AF and ≥4 blood samples taken during monitoring. Of these, 164 had at least one CRP measurement <3 mg/L with a total of 2000 measurements (median 8 [6–15] per participant) at median 5 [1.5–32] mg/L, and 40 had only CRP measurements ≥3 mg/L with a total of 681 measurements (median 10 [6–15] per participant at median 37 [13–79] mg/L). Supplementary material online, Figure S3 illustrates the distribution of the overall AF burden in these two groups. Participants who exclusively had CRP ≥3 mg/L exhibited a higher overall AF burden (median 0.28 [0.045–1.05] %) compared with those with at least one measurement ≤3 mg/L (median 0.13 [0.030–1.06] %), and while they had similar number of days with any AF (median 20 [8–133] vs. 20 [4–97] days), they had more days with AF lasting ≥60 min (median 17 [4–56] vs. 8 [2–53] days).

Self-controlled case series analyses

Supplementary material online, Table S3 depicts the baseline characteristics of participants included in the SCCS analysis compared to participants with AF that were not included. A total of 82 participants met the inclusion criteria for the SCCS analysis (AF detected and CRP measurements both ≤10 and >10 mg/L). The IRR of AF within 1 day of a CRP above vs. below 10 mg/L for these patients was 2.41 (1.53–3.80), corresponding to a within-individual 2.4-fold higher incidence of AF when CRP was elevated. Supplementary material online, Figure S4 illustrates the incidence of AF in states of CRP >10 mg/L compared to ≤10 mg/L for each participant. A within-individual significant increase in AF occurrence when CRP was elevated was observed in 51 participants, and here the IRR was 3.15 (1.46–6.83), whereas there was no significant increase during elevated CRP in the remaining 31 participants. The overall AF burden during the monitoring period was lower among participants with increased AF occurrence during elevated CRP (median 0.59 [0.14–2.19] % vs. 1.12 [0.09–3.8] %).

Discussion

We conducted a post hoc analysis of a large randomized controlled trial of individuals with risk factors for AF and stroke, combining more than 1.2 million days of continuous monitoring with more than 7000 plasma CRP measurements. We found a within-individual dose-dependent relationship between elevated CRP levels and increasing AF occurrence on a day-to-day basis. The occurrence of AF increased continuously with increasing CRP. Within the individual, each doubling in CRP corresponded to a 36% increase in daily AF lasting at least 1 h, the effect was stronger for higher CRP values, and the occurrence of AF was more than doubled when CRP was above 10 mg/L vs. below. AF burden was numerically higher in individuals with possible chronic inflammation as indicated by CRP levels consistently elevated ≥3 mg/L.

Inflammation and AF

Inflammation has been suggested as a driver of AF in a wide range of studies,4,6–9,11–14,28,40–42 and several diseases such as rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease, and psoriasis, all characterized by systemic inflammation, have been correlated with long-term risk of developing AF.17–19 Our study adds to the growing body of evidence linking AF and inflammation.

CRP is an acute-phase reactant sensitive to inflammation and tissue damage, released primarily from the liver.22 CRP is frequently measured in clinical practice as a marker of inflammation, typically in response to varying symptomatic presentations. Consequently, we focused our analyses on individuals with several CRP measurements over time. Arguably, the strictest analysis was the SCCS, demonstrating an increased occurrence of AF during CRP above 10 mg/L in most but not all individual participants. We chose a CRP threshold of 10 mg/L to capture AF occurring at CRP levels likely reflecting clinically meaningful inflammation. As AF is a very heterogenous disease with varying phenotypes, acute inflammation may be a trigger only in some patients. Several factors may influence the susceptibility to AF, where transient triggers and more long-term stressors such as chronic inflammation affect the structural properties of the atria. It is also likely that the susceptibility to AF and impact of triggers change over time as structural changes occur. Post-operative AF (POAF) is a frequent complication following cardiothoracic surgery and has been associated with elevated CRP levels,24,25 and surgery-induced systemic and local inflammation represent potential therapeutic targets for POAF prevention. In a prospective study of patients admitted at a non-cardiac intensive care unit, increasing risk of new-onset AF was associated with increasing CRP, suggesting that acute inflammation may more strongly precipitate AF in patients with additional competing risk factors.43 In our study, both blood sampling frequency and CRP levels were higher immediately following new-onset of AF. This may imply a relationship between de novo AF and acute inflammation but should be interpreted with caution, given that the debut of AF may simply impose markers of worsening health, along with the concomitantly increased sample count. On the other hand, the totality of samples analysed appeared to be rather evenly distributed during monitoring. While patients with many measurements of CRP more often had AF diagnosed, they did not have more days with AF or higher AF burden.

Our study is the first to provide data from long-term continuous heart rhythm monitoring coupled with serial CRP measurements in a general population at risk. ILR monitoring offers greater sensitivity for detecting arrhythmia episodes than standard methods (e.g. ECG or Holter) and also identifies subclinical AF,31 which may also have implications for treatment as indicated in a recent meta-analysis of the NOAH-AFNET 644 and ARTESiA45 trials.46 Additionally, subclinical AF with concomitant inflammation might be more underdiagnosed and clinically relevant than subclinical AF under normal conditions due to the already prothrombotic state caused by inflammation,47 emphasizing the need to characterize a potential relationship. While our study did not investigate a potentially causal association between inflammation and AF, a direct link still merits consideration in future studies.29 Inflammation may also represent a key mechanistic link between atrial cardiomyopathy and AF, particularly in the context of ageing. While atrial remodelling increases with age, inflammation could explain why only a subset of individuals with atrial cardiomyopathy develop AF and may represent a potential target for prevention.48 Further, even despite evidence of causality, the association may have implications for awareness about AF in patients with elevated CRP—keeping in mind that CRP is a sensitive and easily obtainable marker for systemic inflammation, and that inflammation likely plays an important role in the long-term development and transient occurrence of AF. Another aspect that may warrant further examination is the possibility of more or less severe phenotypes of AF being linked with acute inflammation rather than chronic inflammation compared to no inflammation. In our study, we saw numerically higher AF burden in patients suggestive of chronic inflammation; however, we did not find a statistically significant difference in AF occurrence in days surrounding CRP measurements ≤3 mg/L vs. >3–10 mg. It remains unclear whether transiently triggered AF differs in nature from more sustained forms, or if it merely represents an early form of AF with limited clinical implications, but with increasing technological possibilities for monitoring (e.g. wearables), there is a need to increase current understanding of AF, as to both prevent overtreating and identify at-risk individuals who would benefit from screening. Characterization of AF patterns in relation to changes in biomarkers over time could contribute to improved phenotyping and prediction of AF progression and clinical outcomes.

Low-grade inflammation and anti-inflammatory treatment in AF

Low-grade inflammation can be characterized as a persistent or chronic, long-term, mild systemic inflammatory condition, which has been associated with cardiovascular diseases including AF41,49 and adverse outcomes in patients with AF.50 Mechanistically, inflammation has been suggested as a driver of atrial fibrosis and electrical disturbances.9,51 Additionally, structural fibrotic remodelling may itself perpetuate inflammatory activity, thereby driving further atrial fibrosis and amplifying arrhythmogenic substrate.52 To assess low-grade inflammation using CRP, a high-sensitivity assay is required, as the accepted thresholds for increased cardiac risk are as low as 2 or 3 mg/L.49,53 The baseline CRP in our study for both the participants who did and did not develop AF throughout monitoring was median 2 [1–4] mg/L. The LOOP Study population comprised older, comorbid individuals recruited from the general population, and some degree low-grade chronic inflammation would be expected in this group.54 We categorized participants who developed AF based on the presence of any CRP measurement under 3 mg/L (in participants with at least four measurements during monitoring). Using this exploratory approach, we did observe a tendency towards a higher overall AF burden in patients with no CRP measurements below 3 mg/L. Anti-inflammatory treatment has also been studied in relation to the prevention of cardiovascular disease. Colchicine has been shown to be beneficial in reducing the incidence of major cardiovascular outcomes, but no difference in AF occurrence was observed in either the LoDoCo2 trial,55 or the COLCOT trial.56 Colchicine has also been suggested as a treatment modality in short term AF prevention post-ablation and post-surgery,57 but results are mixed with some studies showing no effect,58,59 and others showing some efficacy.60,61 While the effect and proper use of anti-inflammatory treatment in AF prevention remains unclear, our study adds to the increasing evidence linking inflammation with AF. As options for targeting specific components of the immune response continue to expand, further research into the potential utility of inflammatory biomarkers and anti-inflammatory treatment in AF would be valuable.

Strengths and limitations

This post hoc study benefits from extensive continuous heart rhythm data collected as part of the LOOP study matched with a large number of blood samples, enabling a direct, temporal linkage between CRP measurements and AF episodes. Continuous heart rhythm monitoring is a sensitive approach for detecting AF, including short paroxysms and asymptomatic episodes,31 whereas other studies have relied on intermittent or short-term ECG monitoring. AF diagnoses were adjudicated, and we further applied a cutoff of 1 h of daily AF as the primary outcome to minimize false positives.62 The blood test results were obtained from all laboratories analysing samples from the study participants taken as part of usual care, which all used high-sensitivity assays. There was no study protocol for blood sampling after baseline, and therefore, indications may vary, and blood sampling is likely biased towards clinical demand such as suspected or confirmed infectious disease or other inflammation. Also, we were unable to ascertain the setting of the blood draw, and as such, we cannot differentiate between CRP being elevated due to acute or chronic inflammation. Equally, we are unable to differentiate between CRP measurements taken as part of inpatient or outpatient care. Furthermore, point-of-care CRP measurements taken in general practices are analysed locally and are therefore not included. Additionally, the observed associations may partly reflect AF occurring in the context of acute illness–related arrhythmogenic factors rather than inflammation per se. However, given the large dataset with several measurements per individual, a within-individual association should still be scientifically valid. The samples were analysed locally and as such we were not able to assess inter- or intra variability amongst lab equipment, though the expected variability is low at approximately 7.5–15% since all laboratories in Denmark must meet quality requirements set by the national accreditation body, which also includes continuous assessment of variability.

The LOOP Study included individuals without known AF but with risk factors for stroke, and while AF was diagnosed in more than one in three participants throughout the study, the overall AF burden was low.63 Whether elevated CRP levels relate to AF onset similarly in patients with clinical AF, where substrate may outweigh inflammation, and in other risk profiles remains unknown. Additionally, the LOOP Study might have been subjected to healthy-user bias, while the eligibility criteria and the fact that participants were of Northern European descent may have introduced additional selection bias, which could influence generalizability. Finally, as a post hoc analysis of a randomized clinical trial, the results should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

Conclusion

In this post hoc analysis of a randomized trial of individuals with stroke risk factors undergoing continuous long-term monitoring for AF, increased CRP levels were associated with increased AF occurrence on a day-to-day basis both on the population level and within the individual. The proportion of days with AF increased continuously with higher CRP levels, and the within-individual occurrence of AF was more than doubled when CRP was above vs. below 10 mg/L. The findings are exploratory but could inspire future investigations of inflammation as a potential modifiable risk factor for AF and the use of inflammatory markers in AF care.
