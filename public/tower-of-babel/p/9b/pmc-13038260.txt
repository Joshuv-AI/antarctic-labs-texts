Basal phosphorylation of SHIP1 by Lyn suppresses proinflammatory signaling in the absence of a phagocytic synapse
Authors: S Erandika Senevirathne, William K Kanagy, Charlotte R Hemmila, Joseph T Greene, Andrew T Rajczewski, Richard T Cattley, Whitney L Swanson, Silvia Toledo Ramos, Myra G Nunez, Siyu Jin, William F Hawse, Tanya S Freedman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicroscale engagement of the hemi-immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif-containing receptor Dectin-1 by fungal particles activates Src-family kinases (SFKs) and Syk, drives second-messenger generation, and induces downstream Erk and Akt signaling and proinflammatory responses in macrophages. To avoid inappropriate activation in the absence of a pathogenic threat, macrophages restrict signaling in response to low-valency ligands. To examine how SFKs regulate this sensitivity threshold, we compared signaling induced by pharmacological SFK activation with signaling triggered by depleted zymosan, a high-valency β-glucan particle that engages Dectin-1 to form a phagocytic synapse. We found that particulate engagement of Dectin-1 protected the inhibitory ITIM-associated phosphatase SHIP1 from phosphorylation by SFKs, allowing robust activation of Erk and Akt and proinflammatory induction. In contrast, receptor-independent SFK activation induced phosphorylation of SHIP1 and failed to amplify signaling downstream of PLCγ2 and PI3K. Although multiple SFKs could phosphorylate SHIP1, Lyn uniquely maintained the basal set-point of SHIP1 phosphorylation, thereby keeping PIP3 levels low and suppressing basal Erk and Akt signaling. This Lyn-dependent regulation was essential for suppressing Akt activation and balancing signaling through the Erk and Akt pathways in the absence of a phagocytic synapse. In contrast, antimicrobial responses to particulate stimuli, including second-messenger signaling, Erk/Akt, and proinflammatory outputs, did not strictly require Lyn expression. These findings highlight the unique role of Lyn in limiting spurious proinflammatory signaling and shed light on a mechanism by which macrophages selectively respond to high-valency particulate ligands that override this basal inhibitory program.

Introduction

Macrophages enforce a stringent threshold for antimicrobial activation, responding robustly to particulate pathogens while remaining quiescent when encountering low-valency, soluble ligands from distant sites of infection. Engagement of macrophage immunoreceptor tyrosine-based activation motif (ITAM)- and hemi-ITAM-coupled receptors (e.g., Fcγ receptor (FcγR) or Dectin-1) by highly multivalent, particulate ligands, such as antibody-opsonized pathogens or fungal-wall β-glucans, activates Src-family kinases (SFKs), which phosphorylate ITAMs that recruit the kinase Syk.1 When Syk binds to phosphorylated ITAMs or hemi-ITAMs, SFKs phosphorylate its interdomain-B linker (including tyrosine (Y)352), relieving autoinhibition and enabling full activation of the Syk kinase domain and transphosphorylation of the activation-loop at Y525 and Y526.2,3 Together, activated SFKs and Syk phosphorylate downstream effectors, including Tec kinases (e.g., Btk) and second messenger–generating enzymes.5–7 Btk-dependent phosphorylation of PLCγ2 at Y1217 and Y759, among other residues, enhances its catalytic activity. PLCγ2 hydrolyzes PI(4,5)P2 to generate inositol trisphosphate, which promotes calcium-ion (Ca2+) release,7 and diacylglycerol, which promote Ras activation and downstream Erk signaling.10 SFK- and Syk-mediated phosphorylation of the PI3K p85/p55 regulatory subunit5,6,11,12 relieves autoinhibition,13 enabling the phosphorylation of PI(4,5)P2 to PIP3, which recruits and allosterically activates PH-domain-containing kinases, such as PDK1, mTORC2, and Akt.14,15 Phosphorylation of Akt by PDK1 and mTORC2 on threonine 308 and serine 473, respectively, activates Akt.16,17 PIP3 also mediates the recruitment and activation of PH-domain-containing proteins upstream of Erk, including Btk and PLCγ2,18,19 thereby serving as a central signaling node that integrates and amplifies inputs into both the Erk and Akt pathways.

As antimicrobial signaling byproducts, such as oxidative bursts, can be toxic to neighboring tissues, macrophages typically induce antimicrobial responses only during a direct encounter with a pathogen, not in response to soluble β-glucans.20 To this end, SFKs also mediate immune suppression by phosphorylating immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibition motifs (ITIMs), which recruit cytosolic inositol phosphatases (e.g., SHIP1) and tyrosine phosphatases (e.g., SHP-1). SFK-mediated phosphorylation of SHIP1 and SHP-1 at Y1020 and Y56421,22 reflect phosphatase activation and membrane recruitment, enabling dephosphorylation of PIP322 and signaling phosphoproteins4,23–25 and dampening Akt and Erk signaling.

Immune cells express distinct SFK repertoires, but defining the function of each kinase remains challenging due to compensation by other family members and secondary cell-specific differences.26–28 Among the SFKs, however, Lyn is distinctive for its dual roles in regulating immune-cell responses. Lyn can promote ITAM-driven proinflammatory signaling in myeloid cells29 and B cells,30 yet it also performs indispensable negative-regulatory functions, as evident from the development of chronic inflammation and autoimmunity in mice lacking Lyn expression.31,32 Lyn knockout (LynKO) mice have increased numbers of autoreactive B cells and proinflammatory myeloid cells and increased production of inflammatory cytokines and autoantibodies33–35; isolated LynKO B cells have increased calcium mobilization31,32 and hyperresponsive Erk signaling.34,36 Crosslinking the LynKO B-cell antigen receptor induces more AktS473 phosphorylation than wild type (WT).36 These signaling abnormalities stem from impaired phosphorylation of ITIMs (e.g., CD22, FcγRIIb) and ITIM-associated phosphatases at steady state and in response to antigen encounter.32,37–39 In addition to Lyn, macrophages express the SFKs Hck, Fgr, and Fyn.27 Previous studies have shown some functional redundancy among Lyn, Hck, and Fgr in FcγR-mediated phagocytosis and proinflammatory signaling,40,41 but the degree of functional overlap within the Src family is not well understood.

SFK activity is controlled by the opposing actions of the SFK-inhibitory kinase Csk and the transmembrane phosphatases CD45 and CD148.8 Although these phosphatases are necessary to dephosphorylate the SFK inhibitory tail, they can also dephosphorylate the SFK activation loop and other membrane-associated signaling proteins,9 necessitating their exclusion from the phagocytic synapse (kinetic segregation) to prolong SFK activation and initiate signaling.1,20,42,43 Although macrophages are generally poorly responsive to soluble β-glucans20,44 or receptor-independent activation of SFKs,44 inflammation-induced basal upregulation of Lyn can sensitize them, a function not necessary for responses to particulate β-glucans.44 Although downstream signaling is blocked, the SFK activation loop, Syk, and other membrane-associated proteins can remain highly tyrosine-phosphorylated,44 suggesting that the rheostatic function of Lyn in setting the cell signaling threshold operates farther downstream than do the inhibitory functions of CD45 and CD148.

To test how basal and induced functions of Lyn interact with the phagocytic synapse to regulate macrophage signaling, we compared signaling triggered by particulate engagement of Dectin-1 with signaling triggered by activation of SFKs in the absence of a phagocytic synapse. To activate SFKs pharmacologically, we used an analog of the kinase inhibitor PP1 (3-IB-PP1) to specifically inhibit an analog-sensitive variant of Csk (CskAS), expressed transgenically on a Csk-null background.44–46 Inhibiting CskAS leads to rapid SFK activation in the absence of a phagocytic synapse.44 We have previously shown that receptor-independent activation of SFKs and some downstream signaling can be achieved by pharmacologically inhibiting Csk, without steric exclusion of CD45 and CD148.44

Although our earlier studies centered a positive-regulatory role for Lyn,44 we now reveal a unique role for Lyn in maintaining a basal pool of phosphorylated SHIP1, which limits accumulation of PIP3 and restricts basal Erk and Akt phosphorylation, suppressing proinflammatory signaling in macrophages. Although other activated SFKs are intrinsically capable of phosphorylating SHIP1, this induced function fails to suppress Akt activation in the absence of a phagocytic synapse, suggesting that the basal regulation of SHIP1 determines a setpoint for future signaling. These findings highlight the pivotal role of Lyn in maintaining macrophage homeostasis and preventing spurious propagation of proinflammatory signaling. The phagocytic synapse protects SHIP1 from further SFK-mediated phosphorylation, enabling amplification of downstream signaling and maintaining Erk/Akt pathway balance, independently of Lyn expression. These findings reinforce a role for Lyn as a steady-state gatekeeper, preventing signal propagation by macrophages in response to soluble stimuli or inappropriate SFK activation in the absence of a direct encounter with a pathogenic cell.

Methods

Mice

All mice were derived on a C57BL/6 background. CskASLynKO(CskKO) and CskASHckKOFgrKO(CskKO) mice were generated by crossing CskAS-transgenic CskKO and nontransgenic Csk+/− parents.35,44 LynAKO and LynBKO mice were used as F1 crosses of LynKO and Lyn[A or B]KO(CRISPR) mice to ensure WT-like expression of the remaining Lyn isoform.35 Animal studies were performed in compliance with the policies of the University of Minnesota, the American Association for Accreditation of Laboratory Animal Care, and National Institutes of Health, under Animal Welfare Assurance A3456-01 and Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee protocol 2209-40372A. All animals were housed in specific-pathogen-free conditions and attended by licensed veterinarians. Genotypes of breeders and experimental animals were performed by Transnetyx.

Preparation of bone-marrow-derived macrophages

Bone-marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were prepared as previously described.44 Bone marrow from 10- to-15-wk-old male or female mice was flushed from the tibiae and femura and subjected to hypotonic erythrocyte lysis. The bone marrow was then plated in non–tissue culture–treated plates (Corning) in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Corning) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (Omega Scientific), 0.11 mg/ml sodium pyruvate (Corning), 2 mM penicillin/streptomycin/L-glutamine (Sigma-Aldrich), and 10% CMG-14-12-cell-conditioned medium as a source of macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF). After 7 d culture, BMDMs were lifted off the plate using 5 mM EDTA and replated at 1 × 106 cells/well in untreated 6-well plates (Corning) in M-CSF-free medium supplemented with 25 U/ml IFN-γ (PeproTech) for inflammatory priming overnight. BMDM purity was confirmed via flow cytometry analysis of surface markers; cell preparations from all genotypes were similarly homogeneous. BMDM genotypes were confirmed by mouse genotyping, immunoblotting, and/or quantitative reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction.

Cell stimulation

BMDMs were treated with 10 µM or the specified concentration of 3-IB-PP1; 10 particles/cell depleted zymosan, prepared in-house by alkali boiling47 and sonication from purchased zymosan (Z4250-1G; Sigma-Aldrich); or 0.8 µg/mL or the specified concentration of the yeast-derived soluble β-glucan Imprime PGG (Patent: US11815435B2; Biothera)48 by pulse spinning and incubation at 37 °C. Signal propagation was suspended by placing the plate on ice and lysing in sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) buffer (128 mM Tris base, 10% glycerol, 4% SDS, 50 mM dithiothreitol, pH 6.8). After scraping cells and incubating 5 min at 37 °C, lysates were collected and sonicated on a Bioruptor (Diagenode) at 50% duty for 3 min to shear DNA. Lysates were then boiled for 15 min and stored at −20 °C.

Immunoblotting and quantification

Samples were separated on a 7% NuPage Tris-Acetate gel (Invitrogen) and then transferred to Immobilon-FL PVDF membrane (EMD Millipore). Revert Total Protein Stain (LI-COR Biosciences) was used to quantify the total protein content in each lane. After destaining, membranes were cut as appropriate and blocked 1 h with TBS Intercept Blocking Buffer (LI-COR). Membranes were then incubated overnight at 4°C with the appropriate primary antibodies, treated 1.5 h with secondary antibody, and visualized using an Odyssey CLx near-infrared imager (LI-COR). Densitometry quantification was performed in ImageStudio (LI-COR), as described previously.49 Antibodies were obtained from Cell Signaling Technologies (cs), Abcam (ab), Santa Cruz (sc), or LI-COR (lc) as follows: rabbit anti-pSykY352 65E4 cs2717, rabbit anti-pPLCγY1217 cs3871, rabbit anti-pSHIP1Y1020 cs3941, rabbit anti-pSHP-1Y564 D11G5 cs8849, rabbit anti- pErk1/2T202/Y204 D13.14.4e cs4370, rabbit anti-pAktS473 193H12 cs4058, rabbit anti-pPI3Kp85/p55Y458/Y199 cs4228, mouse anti-β-Actin 8H10D10 cs3700, mouse anti-LynA+B Lyn01 ab1890, rabbit anti-pSFKY418 (EP503Y) ab40660, rabbit anti-LynA+B 44 sc15, goat anti-Hck M-28 sc1428, donkey anti-mouse IgG 800CW lc925-32212, donkey anti-rabbit IgG 800CW lc925-32213, donkey anti-mouse IgG 680LT lc925-68022, and donkey anti-goat IgG 680LT lc926-32214. Graphing and statistical analyses for this study were performed using Prism software version 10.6.1 (GraphPad Software).

Confocal microscopy and image analysis

BMDMs were IFN-γ-primed on 15-mm glass coverslips (CLS-1763-015; Chemglass Life Sciences) and then treated as indicated at 37 °C followed by 15 min fixation at room temperature (RT) in 4% paraformaldehyde and washing with 10 mM Tris in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), pH 7.4. Samples were washed with PBS and then blocked and permeabilized 10 min at RT with 0.1% Triton X-100 and 2% bovine serum albumin in PBS. Samples were labeled 1 h at RT with rabbit anti-pSykY352 (ab300398) and mouse anti-Lyn (ab1890) in 2% bovine serum albumin in PBS, washed 3 times with PBS, and followed by 60 min RT secondary F(ab′)2 labeling with anti-rabbit-AF647 (111-606-047) or anti-mouse-AF488 (115-546-071, both from Jackson ImmunoResearch) before DAPI staining and mounting in ProLong Gold cs9071. Z-stacks capturing the entire cell volume were collected with z-step 0.3 µm and a 60 nm × 60 nm pixel size and a pinhole of 96 µm, to fit the Nyquist criterion, using a Leica STELLARIS 8 inverted laser scanning confocal microscope with galvonomic scanners and spectral internal HyD-S detectors and a fixed 405 nm violet laser (DAPI/depleted zymosan excitation) and pulsed white-light laser (AF488/AF647 excitation). A Leica Harmonic Compound PL apochromatic CS2 63× oil objective with a correction collar (1.4 NA) was used for imaging. Laser powers were 5.5% for 488 nm and 6% for 647 nm. Two-hybrid detectors collected photons at 505- to 565-nm gain 7% (AF488) and 655- to 723-nm gain 3.5% (AF647). The surface area of pSykY352 clusters above the minimal resolvable surface area (45 nm2) for 3-IB-PP1 and depleted zymosan particles was calculated using Imaris 9.9.1 software.

Immunoprecipitation of Ras-GTP

After treatment with 3-IB-PP1 or depleted zymosan, cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed in buffer provided by the Active Ras Detection kit (cs8821) supplemented with HALT phosphatase and protease inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Equivalent amounts of protein were used for subsequent steps, per the manufacturer’s instructions.

Flow cytometry

A total of 1 × 106 bone marrow cells or BMDMs were stained 1 h at 4 °C with antibody cocktail in flow cytometry buffer: 2% fetal bovine serum and 2 mM EDTA in PBS without Ca2+ or Mg2+. Cells were washed and fixed 20 min at 4 °C with BD Fix/Perm Buffer (Becton Dickinson). Cells were then washed with BD Perm/Wash buffer and stainedovernight at 4 °C for intracellular protein . Cells were washed in flow cytometry buffer and resuspended in 0.5% PFA in PBS for storage and analysis. Data acquisition was performed using an LSRFortessa (BD Biosciences) and analyzed using FlowJo software version 10 (FlowJo). Antibodies were obtained from BD Biosciences (bd), BioLegend (bl), and Tonbo Biosciences (tb) as follows: Ly6C HK1.4 bd755205, CD64 X54-5/7.1 bl139311, B220 RA3-6B2 tb35-0452-U100, Thy1.2 30H12 bl105305, NK1.1 PK136 tb35-5941-U100, Ly6G 1A8 tb65-1276-U100, CD45 30-F11 bl103128, CD11b M1/70 bd563553, F4/80 T45-2342 bd749284, CD11c HL3 bd612796, and Ghost Dye Red 780 tb3-0865-T500, as a viability stain.

Quantification of PIP3 by mass spectrometry

BMDMs were suspended in ice-cold PBS without Ca2+ or Mg2+, and 30 × 106 cells of each genotype were pelleted at 311 g and flash frozen. Lysis buffer (210 µl, 50 mM Tris, 0.1% NP40 alternative, and 5 mM EDTA, pH 9.5), was added to the samples before ultrasonic homogenization (20% power, 3 × 15 s, 5-s pauses). Homogenates were analyzed for protein content via Bradford assay. A 1:1 mixture of aqueous HCl (1 N) and methanol totaling 1 mL was added to the remaining 200 µl of sample, and the mixture was vortexed. Samples were extracted in 500 µl chloroform and vortexed. After 5-min centrifugation at 13,300 g, 400 µl of the bottom organic layer was transferred to a glass autosampler vial (Agilent Technologies) for derivatization. Trimethylsilyl-diazomethane (50 µl) was added to 400 µl sample extract. Samples were vortexed briefly and incubated 10 min at RT before quenching with 10 µl acetic acid. Samples were mixed via pipetting until the yellow color dissipated and then dried under nitrogen and reconstituted in 30 µl 50% isopropanol. For the calibration curve, synthetic PIP3 (64930; Cayman Chemical) was dissolved in methanol to a concentration of 0.1 μg/µl. PIP3 (2 μg in 20 µl) was derivatized, reconstituted, and diluted in 50% isopropanol. Samples were transferred to a 300 µl glass insert in an amber glass autosampler vial and analyzed via liquid chromatography coupled tandem mass spectrometry, using multiple reaction monitoring on an Acquity Premiere UHPLC (Waters) coupled to a QTRAP 6500 (SCIEX). Data were acquired in positive multiple reaction monitoring mode, targeted for PIP3 (1,225.57 → 627.6 mass/charge [m/z]) with declustering potential, collision energy, and collision cell exit potential at 100, 35, and 10, respectively. Chromatographic separation was performed at 55°Cwith an Acquity UPLC Protein BEH C4 column (300 Å, 2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 µM). Mobile phases of separation consisted of 10 mM ammonium acetate in 50% methanol in LC-MS–grade water (A) and 10 mM ammonium acetate in 4:1 isopropanol and methanol (B). Elution started with an isocratic gradient 0 to 1 min, 0% to 50% A; 1 to 7 min, 50% to 2% A; 7 to 9 min, isocratic hold at 2% A; 9 to 10 min, 2% to 50% A; 10 to 15 min to isocratic hold at 50% A, with a flow rate of 0.2 ml/min. Data were imported into Skyline software (v. 23.1.0.455) for analysis.

Dectin-1 binding competition assay

IFN-γ-primed BMDMs were treated with Imprime PGG or depleted zymosan. Following β-glucan treatment, cells were incubated with a 1:10 dilution of anti-mouse Dectin-1-APC antibody (130-102-250; Miltenyi Biotec). Binding was assessed by measuring the decrease in geometric mean fluorescence intensity relative to untreated BMDMs. Values were corrected for background fluorescence, and data were fit in Prism using a nonlinear one-phase decay model: Y = (Y0 − Plateau) * e-KX + Plateau.

RNA extraction

Murine blood was collected into ETDA-containing tubes, and RNA was extracted using RiboPure-Blood kits (AM1928; Life Technologies). Bone marrow was extracted as above and subjected to TRIzol lysis (15596018; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and phase-separated using Chloroform (319988; Sigma-Aldrich). RNA was purified via RNeasy (74534; Qiagen) per manufacturer’s instructions. Synthesis of cDNA was performed using qScript (95047; Quantabio). SYBR green was used to detect Fgr (5′-to-3′ forward: CTCAAGGCCGGACTTCGT, reverse: TGCTTCTCATGTTGCCAGTGTT), Hck (forward: CAGAAGGGCCCTGTGTATGTG, reverse: CCCTGGGATGCCGTACTC), or Cyclophilin (forward: TGCAGGCAAAGACACCAATG, reverse: GTGCTCTCCACCTTCCGT) in 1 µg total RNA.

Quantification of reactive oxygen

IFN-γ-primed BMDMs were detached using 5 mM EDTA and resuspended in DMEM without phenol red, glutamine, or sodium pyruvate (11835030; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cells were loaded 30 min at 37 °C with 10 µM of CMH2DCFDA (C6827; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then washed with plain DMEM, transferred at 1 × 106 cells in 0.1 ml into FACS tubes, and incubated 15 min at 37 °C. Cells were then treated with 10 µM 3-IB-PP1, 10:1 depleted zymosan, or DMEM alone and incubated 1 h at 37 °C. Finally, cells were placed on ice and washed with ice-cold DMEM, resuspended in cold DMEM, and incubated with viability stain SYTOX Blue (S34857; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Flow cytometry and analyses were performed as described previously.

Cytokine array

IFN-γ-primed BMDMs were treated 8 h with 10 µM 3-IB-PP1 or 10:1 depleted zymosan, and supernatants were centrifuged 5 min at 311 g to remove cell debris and applied to a cytokine-array membrane (ARY006; R&D Systems). Membranes were visualized using an Odyssey CLx near-infrared imager following incubation with IRDye 800CW Streptavidin secondary (NC9386176; LiCor). Densitometry quantification was performed by normalizing the raw signal to reference spots and shown relative to medium-only controls.

Results

Particle engagement amplifies signaling downstream of activated SFKs and Syk and restricts phosphorylation of negative-regulatory phosphatases

To probe the role of particle engagement in directing SFK initiated signaling, we compared signaling complexes formed after Dectin-1 ligation by depleted zymosan with those induced by maximal, receptor-independent SFK activation using 3-IB-PP1 (Fig. S1). We visualized BMDMs and sites of active SFK signaling via immunofluorescence, using total Lyn to mark the cell footprint and phosphorylated SykY352 (pSykY352), a direct SFK substrate, to identify areas of active signaling. Untreated controls had little pSykY352 staining (Fig. 1A), whereas cells treated with depleted zymosan formed contiguous spheres of pSykY352 in the phagosome boundary20 surrounding each particle (Fig. 1B). In contrast, treatment with 3-IB-PP1 induced the formation of dispersed pSykY352 puncta (Fig. 1C). Although the larger puncta are most visually striking in the representative image, most were very small in surface area (<1 µm2) compared with the microscale (30 µm2) signaling surface area of the Dectin-1 phagosome (Fig. 1D).

Consistent with our previous qualitative studies,44 pharmacological SFK activation with 3-IB-PP1 induced 8.5-fold more pSykY352 than did treatment with depleted zymosan (Fig. 2A; Fig. S2A). Similarly, the phosphorylation of PLCγ2Y1217 and pPI3Kp85/p55Y458/Y199 was significantly or trending higher (4.5-fold and 11.7-fold, respectively). We have also previously shown that another site, PLCγ2Y759, is also more robustly phosphorylated in response to 3-IB-PP1 than to depleted zymosan.44 Despite the phosphorylation of these key second messenger–generating enzymes, 3-IB-PP1-treated BMDMs had relatively little induction of Ras nucleotide exchange (0.4-fold) and phosphorylation of Erk (0.3-fold) and Akt (0.5-fold) compared with cells treated with depleted zymosan (Fig. 2B; Fig. S2B).

The lack of signal propagation in 3-IB-PP1-treated CskAS macrophages was accompanied by highly induced phosphorylation of the ITIM-associated phosphatases SHIP1Y1020 (Fig. 3A; Fig. S2C) and SHP-1Y564 (Fig. 3B; Fig. S2D). As with positive-regulatory membrane-proximal signaling, pSHIP1Y1020 and pSHP-1Y564 were 8.2-fold and 13.1-fold more highly induced by treatment with 3-IB-PP1 than by depleted zymosan, respectively. This agrees with our previous observation that the ITIM-containing protein Sirpα is highly phosphorylated by SFKs following treatment with 3-IB-PP1 but not with depleted zymosan.44 Together, these results suggest that the phagocytic synapse may block access of activated SFKs to negative-regulatory substrates.

Our data support a model in which ITIM and phosphatase activation create a signaling blockade to prevent membrane-proximal signaling induced by pharmacological SFK activation from progressing to an inflammatory response in macrophages. Although SHP-1 and SHIP1 are both apparently phosphorylated, the persisting phosphorylation of membrane-proximal tyrosine phosphorylation suggests that SHP-1, CD45, and CD148 are not able to suppress early signaling. Therefore the lipid phosphatase SHIP1 may be the dominant impediment to productive signal propagation. In contrast, the phagocytic synapse amplifies modest initiating signals while protecting against SHIP1 phosphorylation above basal levels, thereby preventing inhibitory blockade of downstream propagation.

The SFK Lyn has a primary role in maintaining basal phosphorylation of SHIP1 and restricting signaling to Erk and Akt

Low basal SFK activity tunes the sensitivity of myeloid cells and enables rapid responses through ITAMs, Toll-like receptors, and cytokine receptors.26,50,51 We have shown previously that inflammation-dependent upregulation of Lyn sensitizes macrophages to 3-IB-PP1-induced signaling.44 Because Lyn contributes to both proinflammatory and negative-regulatory pathways,30,52,53 we assessed the contributions of Lyn to basal regulation of macrophages by comparing Lyn-expressing (Lyn+/+) and Lyn knockout (LynKO) BMDMs.

We and others have reported that a full complement of SFK expression is not required for BMDM differentiation,44,54,55 and flow cytometry and RNA sequencing analyses demonstrate that WT and LynKO have comparable expression of macrophage markers (Fig. S3).44,56 Despite this phenotypic similarity, basal phosphorylation of SykY352 was 3-fold lower in LynKO BMDMs than in Lyn+/+ BMDMs (Fig. 4A). Phosphorylation of downstream mediators, however, was elevated, with 1.5-fold higher pErk1/2T202/Y204 (Fig. 4B) and 3-fold higher pAktS473 (Fig. 4C).

Given the importance of membrane phospholipids, including PIP3, for activating the Erk and Akt pathways,57 we posited that a failure to phosphorylate and recruit SHIP1 to the plasma membrane22,58 could lead to basal accumulation of PIP3 in LynKO macrophages, thereby increasing basal signal transduction. Indeed, resting LynKO BMDMs had 2-fold lower pSHIP1Y1020 than Lyn+/+ cells (Fig. 4D), suggesting that loss of Lyn impairs SHIP1 recruitment to the membrane and access to PIP3.

We also tested whether the individual Lyn splice variants, LynA and LynB, contribute differentially to steady-state regulation of BMDMs. Loss of LynA or LynB with expression of the remaining isoform at a physiological level (LynBKO: LynBKOLynA+/− and vice versa)35 rescued basal phosphorylation of SHIP1Y1020 (Fig. 4E) and suppressed basal phosphorylation of ErkT202/Y204 and AktS473 to WT-like levels (Fig. 4F; Fig. S4A). This suggests that LynA and LynB have overlapping roles in promoting SHIP1 recruitment and suppressing basal Erk and Akt signaling in macrophages.

To confirm the link between SHIP1 phosphorylation and downstream suppression of signaling, we tested the abundance of its substrate PIP3 in Lyn+/+ and LynKO BMDMs. Quantitative mass spectrometry, using a standard curve of derivatized, synthetic PIP3 (Fig. S5A), showed that lysates from resting LynKO BMDMs contained 1.6-fold more PIP3 than Lyn-expressing cells (Fig. 4G; Fig. S5B). Therefore, in the macrophage steady state, LynA and LynB both protect against inappropriate signaling by promoting SHIP1 phosphorylation and suppressing production of PIP3, ensuring suppression of the Akt and Erk pathways.

Lyn promotes the Erk pathway and suppresses the Akt pathway during receptor-independent SFK activation

We next interrogated how formation of a phagocytic synapse or pharmacological activation of SFKs alters the requirement for Lyn to induce signaling above basal levels. CskASLynKO BMDMs had 4.1-fold less induction of pSykY352 in response to pharmacological activation (Fig. 5A) and 2.5-fold less induction after particle engagement (Fig. 5B) than Lyn-expressing cells; Lyn knockout had comparable effects on pSykY525/Y526 (Fig. 5C, D).

Despite the steady-state upregulation of pErk1/2 in LynKO cells, further phosphorylation of Erk1/2 following pharmacological SFK activation was limited in the absence of Lyn expression (Fig. 5E), although expression of either LynA or LynB was sufficient to restore Erk activation (Fig. S4B). In contrast, Lyn expression was not necessary for an Erk response to depleted zymosan (Fig. 5F), reflecting Lyn-independent signal amplification downstream of Syk when interacting with a particulate hemi-ITAM engager.

The absence of Erk phosphorylation in 3-IB-PP1-treated LynKO BMDMs mirrored the lack of Erk signaling observed in response to the yeast-derived soluble β-glucan Imprime PGG (Fig. S6A). Residual Erk pathway activation in response to high-dose soluble β-glucans was abrogated by Lyn knockout. The low-level Erk signaling in WT cells treated with Imprime PGG was Dectin-1-dependent, as was the response to depleted zymosan (Fig. S6B). Binding competition studies confirmed that the chosen concentration of soluble β-glucans occupied 80% of Dectin-1 molecules on the BMDM surface (Fig. S6C), suggesting that the failure of the Erk response was not due to low Dectin-1 binding. In contrast, the number of depleted zymosan particles per cell in these experiments (10:1) only occupied about 10% of receptors but still amplified signaling from subtle initiating events. Together, these data highlight the qualitative differences in signaling responses to soluble and particulate Dectin-1 ligands—maximizing receptor occupancy by soluble ligands does not lead to productive proinflammatory signaling through Erk1/2.

In contrast, we found 3-fold more phosphorylation of Akt in 3-IB-PP1–treated LynKO BMDMs than in Lyn+/+ BMDMs (Fig. 5G). Expression of either LynA or LynB alone, however, was sufficient to restore WT-like levels of pAkt (Fig. S4C). Particle engagement through Dectin-1 led to balanced activation of the Erk and Akt pathways in a Lyn-independent manner (Fig. 5H).

Basal levels of pSHIP1, maintained by Lyn, are preserved during particle engagement

Surprisingly, there was no deficit in induced phosphorylation of SHIP1Y1020 in the absence of Lyn that could explain an amplified Akt response after pharmacological SFK activation (Fig. 6A). This suggests that the primary role for Lyn in phosphorylating SHIP1 and suppressing downstream Akt phosphorylation is in homeostasis, not in induced signaling. Despite the ability of other kinases to phosphorylate SHIP1 in the absence of particle engagement, the Lyn-regulated basal state determines the signaling response.

Although steady-state differences in pSHIP1Y1020 were maintained during treatment with depleted zymosan, SHIP1 was protected from further phosphorylation in both the presence and absence of Lyn (Fig. 6B). The combined effects of reduced basal phosphorylation of SHIP1 and its protection from additional phosphorylation may explain how low-level Syk phosphorylation is efficiently amplified within the phagocytic synapse, resulting in robust activation of the Erk and Akt pathways, independently of Lyn. Together, these results suggest that the primary function of Lyn in regulating macrophage hemi-ITAM signaling is at steady state, setting the level of potential pathway induction during later receptor engagement and protecting against activation of SFKs without formation of a phagocytic synapse. Moreover, Lyn supports signal transduction from activated Syk to the Erk pathway (i.e., the Lyn checkpoint)44 but suppresses activation of Akt and maintains pathway balance in the absence of a phagocytic synapse.

Lyn differentially promotes phosphorylation of PLCγ2 and PI3K following pharmacological SFK activation

We next investigated whether the Erk/Akt pathway imbalance in SFK-activated LynKO BMDMs stems from differential activation of PLCγ2 and PI3K, which, respectively, hydrolyze or phosphorylate PI(4,5)P2 to generate second messengers. Pharmacological SFK activation induced half as much pPLCγ2Y1217 in LynKO BMDMs than in Lyn+/+ (Fig. 7A), mirroring the loss of upstream Syk phosphorylation. Expression of LynA or LynB rescued phosphorylation of PLCγ2Y1217 (Fig. S7). Phosphorylation of pPI3Kp85/p55Y458/Y199, in contrast, was not affected by Lyn knockout (Fig. 7B), suggesting that Lyn plays a more central role in Syk/Tec-driven PLCγ2 phosphorylation than in direct phosphorylation of PI3K.5,6,59 This Lyn-independent pPI3Kp85/p55Y458/Y199 in conjunction with elevated steady-state PIP3 and pAktS473 likely underlies the upregulation of the Akt pathway and relative downregulation of the Erk pathway downstream of Syk and PLCγ2.

Lyn, Hck, and Fgr are all required for activation of Erk, but Lyn is the primary suppressor of Akt signaling following 3-IB-PP1 treatment

Because CskAS inhibition induces activation of all the SFKs,44 we investigated the contributions of Hck and Fgr to steady state and 3-IB-PP1-induced signaling in HckKOFgrKO BMDMs (Fig. S8A), which express macrophage surface markers comparably to WT and LynKO (Fig. S3). In contrast to complete Lyn knockout, SykY352 phosphorylation at steady state was not significantly affected by knockout of Hck and Fgr (Fig. 8A), nor was Syk phosphorylation impaired after pharmacological SFK activation. As Lyn expression was not substantially increased in these cells (Fig. S8B), normal steady-state phosphorylation of SHIP1 in HckKOFgrKO BMDMs suggests that Lyn-mediated basal regulation of macrophages is not supplemented by Hck or Fgr activity (Fig. 8B). As in LynKO, HckKOFgrKO BMDMs had no defect in phosphorylation of SHIP1Y1020 during pharmacological SFK activation. Similarly, pAktS473 levels were comparable in Hck/Fgr-expressing and HckKOFgrKO BMDMs, both at steady state and after 3-IB-PP1 treatment (Fig. 8C). Therefore, the expression of Lyn is uniquely required to maintain signal homeostasis and restrict the Akt pathway following SFK activation without receptor engagement.

We then used an array to assess whether the increased AktS473 phosphorylation in 3-IB-PP1-treated LynKO BMDMs was sufficient to drive a transcriptional antimicrobial response. Similar to reactive oxygen, LynKO BMDMs trended toward higher basal secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-12p70, IL-27 (Fig. S9), and TNF (Fig. 9B). Discussion

Signaling outcomes in myeloid cells and lymphocytes are shaped by the valency of ITAM-coupled receptor engagement. Low-valency, nanoscale clustering of Dectin-1 by soluble β-glucans20 or by annexin core domains on apoptotic cells60 fails to trigger downstream signaling or an antimicrobial responses. In contrast, high-valency, microscale engagement of Dectin-1 by particulate β-glucans triggers production of reactive oxygen species and inflammatory cytokines.20,60 A similar principle operates in B cells, in which soluble antigens generate strong membrane-proximal signals but fail, unlike multivalent particulate antigens, to mobilize calcium or activate Erk. This impairment likely reflects ITIM-phosphatase activation or lack of PLCγ2 access to PI(4,5)P2, together limiting second-messenger production and downstream signal amplification.61

Here we show that pharmacological SFK activation in macrophages generates dispersed, nanoscale clusters of pSykY352, which cannot induce downstream signaling comparable to the continuous, microscale signalosome of the phagocytic synapse. Strong membrane-proximal signaling fails to propagate efficiently downstream of PLCγ2 and PI3K, mirroring the downstream signaling defect observed with soluble antigens in B cells61 and in 3-IB-PP1–treated CskAS thymocytes and B cells.46,57 This impaired signal amplification is associated with phosphorylation of the ITIM-associated phosphatases SHIP1 and SHP-1, consistent with our previous observation that 3-IB-PP1, but not depleted zymosan, induces phosphorylation of the ITIM-containing receptor SIRPα.44 Other ITIM-containing receptors (e.g., FcγRIIb, PIR-B, MICL, and DCIR)62–64 are likely also phosphorylated, collectively providing abundant docking sites for inhibitory phosphatases and adaptors. In contrast, during particulate Dectin-1 engagement, ITIM-associated phosphatases are shielded from SFK-mediated phosphorylation, implying their spatial exclusion from the phagocytic synapse and mirroring the physical segregation of ITIM-containing receptors observed in B cells and antibody-ligated macrophages.65,66 This selective activation, in conjunction with kinetic segregation of transmembrane phosphatases,42,43 likely helps macrophages distinguish soluble from particulate microbial ligands.

Despite phosphorylation of the tyrosine phosphatase SHP-1 following 3-IB-PP1 treatment, we did not observe bulk dephosphorylation of its putative targets, such as Syk,23 PLCγ2,24 or PI3K,25 suggesting that the negative-regulatory function of the inositol phosphatase SHIP1 may dominate, as it does in 3-IB-PP1-treated B cells.57 B cells and macrophages, then, share a common regulatory mechanism to limit Erk and Akt activation in response to SFK disinhibition or receptor binding by low-valency ligands.

Our data reveal a dominant role for Lyn in suppressing proinflammatory signaling in resting macrophages, by promoting SHIP1 phosphorylation and restricting Erk and Akt phosphorylation. However, receptor-independent activation of SFKs in macrophages shows that Lyn has distinct positive and negative regulatory roles. Intriguingly, the combined functions of Lyn, Hck, and Fgr promote Erk activation during pharmacological SFK activation and treatment with soluble β-glucans. In contrast, the net function of Lyn, but not of Hck and Fgr, suppresses the Akt pathway, despite the intrinsic ability of all of them to phosphorylate SHIP1. Interestingly, we did not observe hyperphosphorylation of PI3K59 or impaired SHIP1 phosphorylation in LynKO BMDMs following pan-SFK activation that could explain the heightened Akt response. This suggests that the steady-state levels of pSHIP1 and pAkt are also important rheostats, determining the potential of subsequent, induced signaling. Particulate β-glucans override this suppressive regulatory role of Lyn and restore the balance of Erk and Akt signaling. Interestingly, in the absence of Erk and other pathway activation, hyperactivated Akt is unable to induce an inflammatory functional response, suggesting an additional fail-safe mechanism limiting proinflammatory signaling.

As previously reported in B cells,57 macrophages,40,41,44 and mast cells,67 Lyn transduced signals through ITAMs, Syk, and PLCγ2, but Hck and Fgr seem to activate a parallel pathway to promote Erk phosphorylation. This could occur through RhoGTPases or integrin-mediated signaling68–70 in adherent macrophages. In the absence of a phagocytic synapse, however, the roles of individual Src family members are not interchangeable,41 and a complex combination of their total dose and unique roles drives the Erk response downstream.

Finally, our results suggest that tyrosine dephosphorylation of membrane-proximal signaling proteins, either by the transmembrane phosphatases CD45 and CD148 or the ITIM-associated phosphatase SHP-1, is not necessary to suppress signaling in the absence of a phagocytic synapse. Rather, the basal setpoint of SHIP1 phosphorylation and exclusion from the phagocytic synapse enable the coupling of upstream tyrosine phosphorylation with second-messenger generation and the Ras-Erk and Akt pathways. Lyn and SHIP1 therefore act in the basal state, maintaining homeostasis and preventing proinflammatory activation. Thus, the formation of a phagocytic synapse allows bypass of these negative-regulatory functions and amplifies and balances Erk and Akt pathways.
