Confronting corporate power: addressing commercial determinants of health and the rising non-communicable disease epidemic in South-East Asia
Authors: Shalini Bassi, Saurabh Kumar Gupta, Priyobrat Rajkhowa, Robert Marten, Suvajee Good, Monika Arora
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe South-East Asia Region (SEAR) faces a growing burden of non-communicable diseases (NCDs), shaped in part by complex commercial determinants of health (CDoH). This analysis considers how aggressive marketing, policy interference and addictive product design by the tobacco, alcohol and ultra-processed food (UPF) industries contribute to this burden. SEAR’s distinctive demographic, cultural and economic conditions create both vulnerabilities and opportunities. Rapid urbanisation, high population density, rising disposable incomes and uneven policy enforcement create environments in which commercial actors can expand market reach and influence consumption patterns. These industries frequently target youth and lower socioeconomic groups through tailored marketing, sponsorships, digital engagement and strategic product placement. Cultural norms further shape consumption in SEAR, including the longstanding use of smokeless tobacco, socially embedded alcohol consumption in several countries and the growing incorporation of UPFs into daily diets. These patterns are strengthened by expanding digital and e-commerce ecosystems that increase exposure, accessibility and the normalisation of health-harming products across diverse populations. Despite challenges, the region can address CDoH by adapting evidence-based strategies like marketing restrictions, excise taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages and safeguards against industry interference to local policies.Strengthening regulatory frameworks, enforcing comprehensive marketing restrictions and adopting WHO ‘best buy’ interventions are critical steps. In parallel, international cooperation and capacity building in low- and middle-income countries are essential, as is the engagement of civil society and academia to enhance accountability and support effective policy implementation. Collectively, these strategies can help SEAR accelerate progress in reducing NCD risks and improving population health.

Introduction

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) represent a major global health challenge; they are responsible for approximately 75% of annual deaths globally, corresponding to roughly 43 million lives lost.1 In the South-East Asia Region (SEAR), 55% of all deaths, about 9.5 million are due to NCDs, with approximately half of these deaths occurring prematurely before 70 years.2 While NCDs are a global concern, their impact is particularly pronounced in SEAR, affecting individuals at a younger age compared with the global average.1 This early disease burden results in SEAR countries losing people to NCDs during their most productive years, negatively impacting regional economies.3 Diabetes affects approximately 90 million adults (7.4% of adult population), including 77 million in India, where prevalence is 9.7%; chronic obstructive pulmonary disease affects an estimated 78 million people (13.6%), while cardiovascular diseases cause nearly four million deaths annually, accounting for roughly one-quarter of total mortality within SEAR.46 The region also experiences about 1.8 million new cancer cases each year, with a lifetime incidence risk of 11.7% and approximately 1.1 million annual cancer deaths, representing 7.8% of global cancer mortality.7 SEAR countries such as India additionally face significant economic burdens from NCDs, with an estimated cost of $4.58 trillion between 2012 and 2030 due to NCDs and mental health conditions.8

NCDs in the SEAR are shaped by multiple commercial forces that influence population exposure to health risks.9 Empirical evidence demonstrates that many such contributors, specifically alcohol, tobacco and ultra-processed foods (UPFs), are often designed to be highly addictive and hyper-palatable, overriding an individual’s ability to limit consumption.10 11 Other commercial sectors, particularly fossil fuel-driven air pollution and plastic-intensive production and packaging systems, also make significant contributions to NCD burden and health inequities in the region.12 However, this analysis focuses on commercial practices linked to tobacco, alcohol and UPF systems, including marketing, pricing, product design and policy influence, because these sectors have direct and well-established links to major NCD pathways and are supported by substantial SEAR-specific evidence.1 13 The intentional commercial design of these products makes their consumption a function of corporate practice rather than just an individual choice. These factors do not occur in isolation but are significantly shaped and exacerbated by underlying socioeconomic, environmental and political factors.14 The Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) Agenda 2030 explicitly recognised NCDs as a significant challenge to sustainable development.15 Countries have committed to an ambitious SDG target of reducing the burden of premature mortality from NCDs by one-third by 2030 through comprehensive prevention and treatment strategies (SDG target 3.4).15 Achieving this ambitious target requires addressing the complex web of factors contributing to NCD risk, most importantly, the powerful influence of commercial actors.

Commercial entities shape global, national and local markets, policies and consumer behaviour, often prioritising profit over public health outcomes.16 This challenge is further compounded by the aggressive marketing tactics and substantial political influence of industries that produce and promote products contributing to NCD risk.17 There is a growing understanding of these interconnected challenges as Commercial Determinants of Health (CDoH).18

CDoH are defined as ‘strategies and approaches used by the private sector to promote products and choices that are detrimental to health’ within the realm of goods and services.16 While commercial activities can positively contribute to health (eg, by providing essential medicines and healthy food options), they often promote unhealthy products, shape social norms and influence government policies.

Within SEAR, rapid urbanisation, burgeoning e-commerce platforms, sustained economic growth and digital growth and penetration amplify national and transnational industry influence.19 These industries aggressively penetrate markets in economically transitioning countries by producing and promoting ultra-affordable, single-serving sachet products that exploit weak regulations, while simultaneously using targeted strategies that take advantage of vulnerabilities among youth and lower socioeconomic groups.20 The pathways through which commercial actors influence health can be broadly categorised into three levels: downstream practices that directly affect individuals, meso-level commercial practices that influence communities and upstream systemic influence where commercial entities operate (macro level), often in ways that serve their interests.21 This creates a cyclical relationship, enabling commercial actors to entrench their power and posing significant challenges for the design and implementation of effective public health policy. This regional specificity demands targeted CDoH analysis.

Unveiling the CDoH in SEAR

The concept of CDoH is primarily driven and shaped by perspectives from high-income countries, frequently overlooking unique local contexts, products and commercial practices in SEAR.11 Palm oil is widely used in processed foods in Europe and the United Kingdom, and regulatory attention in those settings has focused primarily on environmental sustainability and sourcing, rather than on its nutritional role in UPFs.22 In contrast, in SEAR, palm oil is a domestically produced, low-cost commodity that underpins large segments of the UPF supply, enabling the mass production of inexpensive, shelf-stable products for widespread consumption.23 Its integration into national agricultural economies, combined with weaker nutrition-focused regulation and aggressive market expansion by food manufacturers, makes palm oil a commercially strategic input that directly shapes dietary exposure at the population scale.24 This is a critical omission, as transnational food and beverage corporations (TFBCs) extensively use palm oil in UPFs due to its low cost, making UPFs highly affordable and accessible.25 The core public health concern is that palm oil is high in saturated fats, and its mass-market use is driven primarily by commercial profit motives and supply chain control, rather than health considerations.26 This analysis manuscript highlights a distinctive pathway through which commercial determinants influence diet-related NCD risk in SEAR. Another distinctive commercial adaptation in SEAR is the deliberate expansion of the sachet economy, characterised by TFBC’s widespread use of single-use plastic packaging to sell ultra-processed products at very low unit prices.27 This strategy has been particularly prominent in SEAR, where it targets low-income consumers through informal retail systems, increases consumption frequency and generates substantial plastic waste in contexts with limited regulatory and waste-management capacity, compounding both health and environmental harms.28 This oversight reflects how CDoH frameworks, shaped primarily by high-income country experiences, can overlook region-specific commercial practices and market dynamics in SEAR, thereby reducing the visibility of policy-relevant issues at national and local levels.

Considering SEAR’s large, diverse population, cultural variety and diverse health system capabilities, we aim to bridge the relative underrepresentation of the region in the CDoH literature by examining how region-specific commercial practices influence NCD risk factors.19 For instance, the widespread use of smokeless tobacco, deeply rooted in cultural practices in SEAR countries, significantly contributes to the rise of oral cancers.29 For an example: the consumption of areca nut, which contains a human carcinogen especially in its fermented form, is deeply rooted in the culture of Bangladesh, Bhutan, India, Myanmar and Sri Lanka, contributing to high rates of oral and oesophageal cancers.30 While traditionally consumed for its social and cultural significance, industrial food systems and tobacco companies exploit this cultural acceptance and consumer familiarity by incorporating areca nut into commercially manufactured, mass-marketed products like gutkha and paan masala, (commercially manufactured mixtures of areca nut, flavouring agents, sweeteners and tobacco) accelerating consumption through low-cost production and profit-driven promotion.31 Addressing CDoH is essential for NCD prevention, and analysis from the SEAR context helps to identify region-specific policy gaps and regulatory priorities. We seek to examine the complex role of CDoH in the NCD epidemic in SEAR, understand the mechanisms through which they operate, analyse industry strategies and offer practical recommendations for policymakers and public health practitioners.

Intersections of commercial and social determinants: advancing health equity in South-East Asia’s NCD crisis

CDoH are intrinsically linked to the broader Social Determinants of Health (SDoH) framework, with commercial activities often exacerbating existing social inequities and undermining health equity.32 These determinants operate within a broader context of economic structures, social policies and political landscapes, all of which are significantly influenced by commercial actors. Addressing CDoH is not just a public health imperative, but also an economic necessity.33

In SEAR, the interaction between CDoH and SDoH creates unique challenges for NCD prevention.32 A combination of rapidly growing markets, high population density, increasing disposable incomes and relatively weak policy enforcement differentiates SEAR from other regions.9 Transnational corporations aggressively penetrate markets in countries where regulatory frameworks are fragmented or weakly enforced. TFBCs invest heavily in logistics and cold chains to ensure UPFs and drinks are widely available even in remote areas, and the use of inexpensive monocrops (such as palm oil, sugarcane and rice or corn) enables production at low cost, making them affordable and attractive to low-income consumers.20 25 Multi-national tobacco companies have aggressively marketed flavoured cigarettes and smokeless tobacco products in India and Indonesia and exploited regulatory gaps such as weak advertising restrictions and enforcement challenges.34 35 This enables deeper market penetration and sustained consumption despite public health efforts.36 Additionally, policy incoherence, with trade and industry often outweighing public health priorities, further strengthens the influence of commercial actors.37 These examples highlight the urgent need for critical evaluation of industry influence and transparent disclosure of funding sources to safeguard health policy and reduce the growing threats posed by commercial determinants.

Commercial actors often customise their marketing strategies and product placement to exploit existing social vulnerabilities, further marginalising disadvantaged groups and exacerbating health inequities. Furthermore, CDoH influences are seen through widespread product design favouring taste over health, targeted marketing normalising unhealthy behaviours and active policy interference restricting access to healthier options, creating an obesogenic environment and weakening public health regulations.38 Regulatory measures, such as restricting harmful marketing, levying a tax on unhealthy products and promoting healthier choices, can lead to significant health and economic benefits.39 Without targeted actions that tackle CDoH, progress towards achieving the SDG goal of reducing premature mortality from NCDs by one-third by 2030 could be significantly constrained.

The tobacco industry has exploited wide-ranging, assertive marketing and political strategies (table 1). These strategies include using surrogate advertising to bypass direct restrictions, sponsoring events specifically aimed at youth, and implementing comprehensive corporate social responsibility (CSR) programmes to build goodwill and secure influence over policy. Tobacco companies take advantage of television and digital media platforms for promotion and target demographic groups such as youth and women with product innovations like flavoured cigarettes, notably kreteks in Indonesia. On the political front, the tobacco industry frequently engages in litigation to oppose public health measures like pictorial health warnings in countries like India and Nepal. Moreover, they emphasise their economic significance, such as employment generation and tax contributions, to position themselves as legitimate stakeholders in policy discussions, particularly in countries like Indonesia, framing their activities as crucial to countries’ and peoples’ economic prosperity. CSR activities, such as the tobacco industry’s substantial COVID-19 relief donations in India, are well-known tactics to gain access to policymakers and improve the industry’s public image.

Similarly, alcohol producers in the region have used multifaceted strategies to expand their market and influence regulatory environments (table 2). The industry innovates with products such as flavoured ready-to-drink and low-alcohol beverages to appeal to younger and health-conscious consumers, while heavily investing in digital and social media marketing, including influencer campaigns that effectively reach youth despite advertising restrictions. Sponsorship of high-profile sports and cultural events, like major cricket tournaments in India, alongside emotional lifestyle branding and celebrity endorsements, fosters strong consumer loyalty. CSR initiatives are used to enhance companies’ public image while subtly normalising alcohol consumption. Politically, the industry frames alcohol use as a matter of personal responsibility and emphasises its economic contribution, wielding lobbying power to delay stricter advertising regulations and tax increases, particularly in India. This dual approach combines aggressive market expansion with sustained efforts to undermine regulatory control.

The way forward

This evidence presented here underscores not only the critical role of CDoH but also their accelerating influence over the past decade, driven by profound shifts in digital marketing, e-commerce expansion and increasingly sophisticated strategies that did not exist twenty years ago. Evidence from WHO and UNICEF shows a rapid rise in algorithm-driven digital marketing of tobacco, alcohol and UPFs in Asia since the 2010s.40 Analyses from Southeast Asia Tobacco Control Alliance 2023 and recent Lancet reviews document the growth of sachetised products, deeper corporate market penetration and persistent industry interference.41 42 These developments illustrate how the CDoH landscape in SEAR has intensified rather than remained static, amplifying NCD risks and widening health inequities. This creates an environment where commercial interests continue to supersede public health objectives, exacerbating existing social and health inequities.

Momentum in this area is reflected in regional consultations, including the 2023 South-East Asia Regional Office meeting, which contributed to refining regional guidance on the CDoH. These efforts have led to concrete advancements, including the draft Intervention Framework model and the Global Report on CDoH, which provide actionable guidance tailored for regional contexts.43 Ahead of the fourth United Nations General Assembly, youth-led initiatives such as the Kigali Youth Declaration emphasised the urgent need to address CDoH and stressed that health governance must remain free from industry interference.44 45 According to the 2021 WHO NCD Capacity Survey, SEAR demonstrates the strongest policy readiness globally, with 91% of countries having operational, multisectoral, integrated NCD policies, significantly higher than any other WHO region.46 Despite this progress in awareness and guidance, a significant gap persists between normative frameworks and their effective implementation.

Translating global and regional recommendations into robust, implementable context-specific policies and actions remains a central challenge. To move from discussion to meaningful action, SEAR governments must prioritise: strengthening regulatory frameworks; adopting evidence-based interventions, such as WHO ‘Best Buys’ including taxation on harmful products, comprehensive marketing bans and mandating clear front-of-pack labelling.44 Equally important is the establishment of transparent policy-making processes with strong safeguards against conflicts of interest and ensuring public disclosure mechanisms. Strengthening conflict-of-interest safeguards is essential to protect health policy from commercial influence.47 Key measures include mandatory disclosure of lobbying activities and meetings with industry, restrictions on revolving-door practices for former officials and codes of conduct limiting non-essential engagement with health-harming industries. In parallel, it is important to strengthen health system responses through public counter-marketing campaigns, investment in health literacy and community engagement strategies that empower individuals to make healthier choices.44 These combined efforts are essential not only for reducing NCD risk factors but also for protecting public health policies from undue commercial influence. The 2023 UN High-Level Meeting on Universal Health Coverage concluded with a political declaration that, while recognising NCDs, notably lacked explicit, binding commitments to regulate harmful commercial practices and counter industry interference.48 This outcome has set a low benchmark for the 2025 United Nations General Assembly and underscores the urgent need to push for more decisive action. Building on the commitments made and lessons learnt, the post-High-Level Meeting period offers a strategic opportunity to address persistent implementation gaps in NCD responses.49

International partners, including UN agencies and development organisations, play a crucial role in supporting these efforts. This involves explicitly integrating CDoH into global health frameworks and political declarations, enhancing inter-agency coordination, and investing in the capacity of low- and middle-income countries to monitor, evaluate and counter harmful commercial influences. Funding independent research and surveillance systems to track corporate political activities and health impacts to ensure accountability. Civil society and academia are essential partners in this effort. Maintaining ongoing research, translating evidence into strong advocacy, forming coalitions and amplifying the voices of affected communities, especially youth, are critical strategies for advancing health-equity-focused policies.

Conclusion

Failure to address the CDoH, particularly the inadequate implementation of existing high-level political commitments, undermines progress towards achieving NCD targets, advancing health equity and meeting the SDGs. The widespread availability and promotion of unhealthy commodities, driven by powerful commercial interests prioritising profit, fundamentally conflict with public health goals.

The policy measures must go beyond mere recognition of risk factors to establish enforceable accountability frameworks that confront harmful commercial practices and protect policy spaces from undue industry influence. The WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (FCTC), and specifically FCTC Article 5.3, limiting interactions with the tobacco industry, offers valuable lessons, demonstrating how limiting corporate political and economic influence can support significant public health gains.50 Adapting such principles to other health-harming industries could serve as a model for further strengthening regional policy responses. Building on foundational work by WHO and accumulating evidence, stakeholders across SEAR must mobilise the necessary political will and resources to prioritise human and planetary health over commercial profits, promoting resilient and equitable societies capable of confronting commercial harms.

Equally critical is strengthening the regional research and policy agenda. Local researchers across SEAR need to partner with policymakers, providing rigorous, context-specific evidence illuminating the scale, nature and impact of CDoH in the region. Strengthening research capacity will enable tailored interventions, including the development of regional NCD targets that track CDoH metrics, establishing a SEAR-specific conflict-of-interest registry and creating an independent regional watchdog funded by non-industry sources. Such initiatives help to ensure that responses to NCDs are both scientifically grounded and politically feasible. Despite persistent challenges, SEAR has the potential to pursue transformative reforms by leveraging health sector investments towards proactive, system-wide approaches that address the root commercial drivers of NCDs, rather than relying primarily on reactive, curative responses.
