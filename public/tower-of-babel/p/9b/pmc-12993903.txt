Impact of antiplatelet therapy on the hemostatic efficacy of platelet-targeted FVIII gene therapy in hemophilia A mice
Authors: Hongyin Yu, Jocelyn A Schroeder, Jeremy G Mattson, Chunyan Gao, Qizhen Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Platelet aggregation is essential for the efficacy of platelet-FVIII gene therapy and is affected by antiplatelet agents.•Platelet-FVIII retains better hemostatic function than plasma-FVIII in clopidogrel-treated mice, supporting its clinical potential.

Introduction

Hemophilia A (HA) is an X-linked genetic bleeding disorder caused by a deficiency of factor VIII (FVIII), affecting ∼1 in 5000 male births.1 With life expectancy in patients with HA increasing due to improved care, especially curative gene therapy, cardiovascular comorbidities that require antiplatelet therapy, such as atherosclerotic disease and atrial fibrillation, may become more common. Consequently, clinical decisions frequently depend on individualized risk-benefit evaluations conducted by multidisciplinary teams.2, 3, 4

Recent advances in gene therapy, using adeno-associated virus-mediated, liver-targeted FVIII gene therapy, have received the US Food and Drug Administration approval to deliver a functional FVIII transgene to hepatocytes.5, 6, 7 Although adeno-associated virus liver gene therapy has shown therapeutic FVIII activity and reduced bleeding in adult patients with severe HA, some concerns remain.8, 9, 10, 11 These include vector-associated hepatotoxicity and the need for immunosuppressive regimens, both of which pose safety concerns.12 The long-term durability of FVIII expression remains uncertain, with some patients experiencing a gradual decline in FVIII levels.10,13 Additionally, patients with preexisting high-titer FVIII inhibitors are excluded from liver-targeted gene therapy, because hepatocyte-secreted FVIII is subject to immune surveillance in circulation.

To overcome these challenges, we developed a platelet-targeted FVIII (2bF8) gene therapy approach, in which FVIII is expressed under the control of the platelet-specific αIIb promoter and stored in platelet α-granules.14,15 This strategy offers several unique advantages: FVIII is sequestered intracellularly, protecting it from circulating inhibitors, and is released only at sites of vascular injury during platelet activation, enabling site-specific coagulation.15, 16, 17 Furthermore, expression is restricted to the megakaryocyte lineage, minimizing off-target effects and reducing systemic antigen exposure. Preclinical studies have demonstrated that platelet-derived FVIII corrects the bleeding phenotype in HA mice, even in the presence of high-titer inhibitors.15, 16, 17 A phase 1 clinical trial is currently underway to evaluate this approach in patients with severe HA and has shown efficacy.18

Because platelet-FVIII function depends on platelet activation and aggregation to deliver FVIII at injury sites, a key question is how antiplatelet drugs affect the efficacy of platelet-targeted gene therapy. This is crucial because patients with hemophilia live longer with advanced treatment and face cardiovascular problems needing such therapy. In this study, we evaluated the impact of aspirin, clopidogrel, and an αIIbβ3-blocking antibody (Leo.H4) on platelet-FVIII function in HA mice with bleeding models and coagulation tests. Our findings from this study provide mechanistic insights into the dependence of platelet-FVIII on platelet aggregation and have important implications for optimizing gene therapy strategies in medically complex patients.

Methods

The details of antibodies and reagents, as well as the methods and statistics used in this study, are provided in the supplemental Methods.

Mice

All animal studies were conducted following protocols approved by the institutional animal care and use committee at the Medical College of Wisconsin. FVIII-deficient (FVIIInull) mice used in this study had a targeted deletion of exon 17 of the FVIII gene.19 Two lines (LV17 and LV18) of 2bF8 transgenic mice in an FVIIInull background were used, expressing FVIII in platelets under the αIIb promoter, generated by lentiviral transgenesis as described in our previous reports.20, 21, 22 Wild-type (WT) mice were used as plasma-derived FVIII controls.

Antiplatelet drug administration

Three antiplatelet antagonists were evaluated for their impacts on platelet-derived FVIII in HA mice vs plasma-derived FVIII in WT animals: (1) clopidogrel, a P2Y12 receptor antagonist23; (2) aspirin, a cyclooxygenase (COX) enzyme inhibitor, and (3) the anti-αIIbβ3 antibody, Leo.H4. For clopidogrel treatment,24,25 mice received 20 mg/kg by intraperitoneal injection or vehicle control; experiments started 5 hours later. For aspirin, 2 doses (10 mg/kg per day) or vehicle control were administered via gavage; experiments were conducted 4 hours after the second dose. The Leo.H4 antibody dose, optimized at 20 μg/109 platelets, was added to whole blood for in vitro rotational thromboelastometry (ROTEM) analysis and native whole-blood thrombin generation assay (nWB-TGA) or injected IV into mice for in vivo bleeding model studies, with injury models performed 5 minutes later. Immunoglobulin G (IgG) served as a control.

In vitro assessment of the impact of antiplatelet antagonists on platelet-derived FVIII

FVIII levels in 2bF8 mice were measured with a chromogenic assay on platelet lysates, as previously reported.16 The effects of platelet antagonists on the hemostatic function of platelet-derived FVIII were assessed using ROTEM and nWB-TGA as described in our previous reports.20,22

In vivo assessment of the impact of antiplatelet antagonists on the clinical efficacy of platelet-derived FVIII in HA mice

Four in vivo injury models were used to evaluate the influence of antiplatelet antagonists on platelet-derived FVIII: (1) lateral tail vein transection (TVT)26, 27, 28; (2) tail tip transection (TTT)26,27; (3) FeCl3-induced carotid artery injury29; and (4) 6-hour tail-bleeding test.30, 31, 32, 33

Results

Platelet-FVIII expression and the hemostatic properties in 2bF8 mice

The level of platelet-FVIII in the LV17 line was 6.89 ± 0.65 mU/108 platelets, and that in the LV18 line was 12.32 ± 0.73 mU/108 platelets (Figure 1A). Platelet counts and integrin αIIbβ3 complex expression in 2bF8 mice were similar to those in WT and FVIIInull mice (supplemental Figure 1A-B). As shown in Figure 1B, parameters of hemostatic properties in 2bF8 mice measured by ROTEM, including clotting time, clot formation time, and maximum clot firmness, were comparable to those in WT mice. nWB-TGA analysis showed that the thrombin generation rate in 2bF8 mice was lower than that in WT mice, but there were no significant differences in lag time, peak time, or endogenous thrombin potential (ETP) between these 2 groups (Figure 1C).

Assessment of the impact of αIIbβ3 blockade on the hemostatic properties and clinical efficacy of 2bF8 gene therapy

To investigate the impact of antiplatelet antagonism on platelet-FVIII gene therapy, we first used the Leo.H4 antibody,34 which specifically blocks the binding of the activated integrin αIIbβ3 complex to fibrinogen, for both in vitro and in vivo studies. The inhibition of Leo.H4 on platelet aggregation was titrated using light transmission aggregometry, as shown in supplemental Figure 2A. One microgram of the Leo.H4 antibody fully inhibited platelet aggregation of 5 × 107 platelets from WT mice in 250-μL modified Tyrode buffer. This effect was seen in 2bF8Tg and FVIIInull mice (supplemental Figure 2B-C). The optimal dose (20 μg/109 platelets) was used in subsequent studies. ROTEM analysis showed significant impairment across all parameters in both 2bF8 and WT mice with Leo.H4, compared with IgG control (Figure 2A,C). Interestingly, nWB-TGA analysis revealed that peak thrombin and ETP were significantly increased in the 2bF8 group in the presence of Leo.H4, compared with the IgG isotype control, whereas no differences were observed in the other parameters. In contrast, Leo.H4 affected the lag time but not the other parameters measured by nWB-TGA in WT mice (Figure 2B,D). Of note, platelets could still be activated even in the presence of the Leo.H4 antibody, as shown by P-selectin staining, but JON-A, which stains activated αIIbβ3, was blocked due to competition with Leo.H4 (supplemental Figure 3).

We subsequently used in vivo injury models to evaluate how αIIbβ3 blockade affects the clinical efficacy of platelet-derived FVIII in correcting the hemophilia phenotype. In the TVT model, 2bF8 mice (LV17 or LV18) and WT mice treated with the Leo.H4 antibody had longer primary and rechallenge bleeding times than those treated with IgG control. (Figure 3A). Furthermore, both primary and rechallenge blood losses were significantly greater in Leo.H4-treated 2bF8 mice than in mice receiving IgG isotype control (Figure 3B). Total bleeding time and blood loss were significantly longer and greater, respectively, than in the IgG control group throughout the test (Figure 3C-D). There were no significant differences in bleeding time or blood loss between 2bF8 and WT mice, regardless of treatment with Leo.H4 or IgG. However, 4 of 12 Leo.H4-treated LV17-2bF8 mice died during the rechallenge bleeding test; and these data were excluded from the statistical analysis because those animals lost a large amount of blood during the primary 10-minute bleeding test and died shortly afterward during the rechallenge bleeding test.

In the TTT model, Leo.H4-infused 2bF8 mice bled continuously for 10 minutes during both bleeding tests, unlike IgG controls, which stopped within 2 minutes. Blood loss was also significantly higher in the Leo.H4 group (Figure 3E-H). Total bleeding time and blood loss were significantly longer and greater than in the IgG control group throughout the test (Figure 3G-H). WT animals were similarly significantly impaired as 2bF8 mice in the TTT injury model test after Leo.H4 treatment, with no statistically significant differences between the 2 groups, regardless of primary bleeding time, rechallenge bleeding time, total bleeding time, or blood loss (Figure 3E-H).

In the FeCl3-induced carotid artery injury model, as shown in Figure 4, without the Leo.H4 antibody, all 2bF8 and WT mice experienced occlusion within 20 minutes. However, the vessels of all 2bF8 and WT mice treated with the Leo.H4 antibody remained open and did not occlude during the 30-minute observation period. Consistent with this observation, occlusion time was significantly extended in both 2bF8 and WT mice treated with the Leo.H4 antibody compared to those treated with IgG (Figure 4B-C). The blood flow in the 2bF8 group was significantly higher than in the WT group after Leo.H4 treatment (Figure 4C), suggesting that Leo.H4 may affect platelet-derived FVIII more than plasma-derived FVIII in the FeCl3-induced carotid artery injury model.

Collectively, these data show that blocking integrin αIIbβ3, as seen with Leo.H4, significantly reduced the clinical effectiveness of platelet-derived FVIII in 2bF8 mice, regardless of whether LV17 or LV18 was used. Because LV18 has nearly twice the platelet-FVIII expression of LV17, we used mice with higher expression (LV18 line) for the remaining tests in our study.

Assessment of the impact of clopidogrel on the efficacy of platelet-derived FVIII in 2bF8 mice

We then investigated the impact of clopidogrel, a common antiplatelet medication,35 on the clinical efficacy of platelet-derived FVIII in LV18-2bF8 mice. As shown in supplemental Figure 4, there were no significant differences in ROTEM or nWB-TGA analyses for 2bF8 mice after clopidogrel treatment compared to the vehicle control group. In the TVT injury model, 2bF8 mice treated with clopidogrel exhibited significantly longer primary and rechallenge bleeding times than vehicle controls. WT mice treated with clopidogrel also demonstrated longer bleeding times than those given vehicle controls (Figure 5AI). The primary blood loss in 2bF8 and WT mice treated with clopidogrel (55.78 ± 34.25 μL and 33.84 ± 44.96 μL) was significantly higher than in vehicle controls (1.55 ± 1.44 μL and 2.74 ± 1.46 μL), but this difference was only seen in WT mice during the rechallenge test (Figure 5AII). Total bleeding time and blood loss were greater in clopidogrel-treated groups than in vehicle control groups, in both 2bF8 and WT mice. (Figure 5AIII-IV).

In the TTT injury model, 2bF8 and WT mice treated with clopidogrel showed significantly longer primary and rechallenge bleeding times than vehicle controls. Seven of 8 2bF8 mice and all WT mice bled throughout the tests. Blood loss in clopidogrel-treated mice was also higher during the tests (2bF8 mice, 84.1 ± 68.17 μL and 55.39 ± 47.19 μL; WT mice, 56.74 ± 49.16 μL and 46.12 ± 37.9 μL) than in controls (2bF8 mice, 6.4 ± 6.19 μL and 1.72 ± 1.07 μL; WT mice, 3.5 ± 4.67 μL and 1.2 ± 0.98 μL; Figure 5BI-II), with increased total bleeding time and blood loss (Figure 5BIII-IV). There were no significant differences between the 2bF8 and WT groups treated with clopidogrel or vehicle control in the TVT and TTT injury models with respect to primary, rechallenge, or total bleeding time and blood loss (Figure 5A-B).

We conducted a 6-hour tail-bleeding test with a 1.6-mm diameter template (∼1.5 cm in length), fully transecting the central tail artery and 2 lateral veins, causing more severe injury than TVT (single lateral vein) or TTT (4-mm plexus removal), to further assess clopidogrel's effect on platelet-FVIII–mediated hemostasis. The bleeding times in the clopidogrel-treated 2bF8 and WT groups were significantly longer than in the vehicle-treated control groups but still significantly shorter than that in the FVIIInull group (Figure 5CI). After the tail-bleeding test, the percentage of remaining hemoglobin in clopidogrel-treated 2bF8 mice and WT mice was 57.06% ± 6.14% and 43.99% ±11.38%, respectively, which were significantly lower than those in vehicle control-treated mice (81.86% ± 12.17% and 95.13% ± 3.21%, respectively) but significantly higher than those in FVIIInull mice under the same test (34.60% ± 5.91%). Notably, the remaining hemoglobin level in clopidogrel-treated 2bF8 mice was significantly higher than in clopidogrel-treated WT mice (Figure 5CII). This suggests that platelet-FVIII in 2bF8 mice can more effectively prevent bleeding after clopidogrel treatment than plasma-FVIII in WT mice and that it can still maintain some clinical effectiveness. We also used a FeCl3-induced carotid artery injury model to assess the impact of clopidogrel on 2bF8 mice. As shown in Figure 6A, blood flow fluctuated in clopidogrel-treated 2bF8 and WT mice, indicating clot instability. The occlusion time was prolonged in treated mice compared to controls, regardless of whether they were 2bF8 or WT mice (Figure 6B). Blood flow was higher in clopidogrel-treated 2bF8 mice than in WT mice (Figure 6C).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that clopidogrel affects the clinical efficacy of platelet-derived FVIII in both tail and carotid injury models, but platelet-FVIII still shows some efficacy in 2bF8 mice treated with clopidogrel.

Assessment of the impact of aspirin on the efficacy of platelet-derived FVIII in 2bF8 mice

Lastly, we evaluated aspirin, the oldest nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug and the most used antiplatelet medication, for its effects on hemostatic properties in 2bF8 mice. Similar to clopidogrel, we found that hemostatic parameters, as determined by ROTEM and nWB-TGA analyses, remained unaffected after aspirin treatment (supplemental Figure 5). In the TVT injury model, 2bF8 mice treated with aspirin had longer bleeding times during rechallenge testing than controls, but primary bleeding times did not differ significantly (Figure 7AI). After aspirin treatment, the rechallenge bleeding time and total bleeding time in the 2bF8 group were significantly longer than those in the WT group; however, there were no significant differences in primary bleeding time between the 2 groups, regardless of whether they received aspirin or vehicle treatment (Figure 7AI,III). In terms of blood loss, there were no statistically significant differences between the aspirin- and vehicle-treated groups, whether 2bF8 or WT, during the primary or rechallenge tests, or in total blood loss throughout the entire period of the bleeding tests (Figure 7AII,IV).

In the TTT injury model, when 2bF8 and WT mice were treated with aspirin, all mice bled throughout the entire duration of the primary and rechallenge tests, significantly longer than vehicle-treated mice (Figure 7BI). Furthermore, the volumes of primary and rechallenge blood loss in 2bF8 (235.9 ± 111.1 μL and 112.8 ± 60.3 μL, respectively) and WT mice (216.7 ± 82.8 μL and 137 ± 67.9 μL, respectively) were also significantly greater than that observed in vehicle-treated 2bF8 (82.3 ± 155.6 μL and 8.7 ± 9.8 μL, respectively) and WT mice (23.4 ± 28.9 μL and 3.1 ± 1.7 μL, respectively; Figure 7BII). Total bleeding time and blood loss were significantly different in the aspirin-treated group compared to the vehicle control group in both 2bF8 and WT mice, but there were no statistically significant differences in bleeding time or blood loss between the 2bF8 and WT groups upon aspirin treatment or vehicle control, regardless of whether it was during the primary or rechallenge bleeding test or throughout the entire test (Figure 7BIII-IV). We also performed the 6-hour tail-bleeding test and found no significant differences in bleeding time or remaining hemoglobin between the aspirin-treated and vehicle control groups, regardless of whether 2bF8 or WT mice were tested (Figure 7CI-II).

Together, these data demonstrate that aspirin treatment affects the bleeding phenotype in 2bF8 mice as determined by the TTT injury model but has a mild influence on the TVT and 6-hour tail-bleeding tests.

Discussion

The premise of our platelet-targeted FVIII gene therapy for HA is that FVIII will be stored in platelet α-granules together with von Willebrand factor and delivered to the site of injury when and where it is needed.15,36,37 Our study highlights the critical role of platelet aggregation in the efficacy of platelet-derived FVIII gene therapy, providing important insights into how commonly used antiplatelet agents may affect its therapeutic performance. Using in vivo injury models and in vitro hemostasis assays, we systematically evaluated the functional consequences of αIIbβ3 blockade, clopidogrel, and aspirin on platelet-derived FVIII hemostatic function in HA mice.

Our in vivo data show that all 3 antiplatelet agents significantly impaired platelet-FVIII–mediated bleeding control, with increased blood loss and longer bleeding times observed in both the TTT and TVT injury models, to varying degrees. Among the agents tested, Leo.H4, an antibody that directly blocks the αIIbβ3 integrin complex, had the most profound impact, effectively abrogating platelet-FVIII–mediated clot formation. The αIIbβ3 integrin acts as a receptor for multiple ligands38 and is essential for platelet-platelet interactions and thrombus stability at the site of vessel injury.27 Its blockade prevents the αIIbβ3 receptor from binding to ligands, thus stopping platelet aggregation even when other platelet activation pathways are still active. ETP was retained in the whole blood of 2bF8 mice even when treated with Leo.H4. Interestingly, our studies show that Leo.H4 treatment increased peak thrombin and ETP in 2bF8 but not in WT whole blood, as determined by nWB-TGA, indicating that FVIII was released from activated 2bF8 platelets during the ex vivo nWB-TGA test. However, when the αIIbβ3 receptor is blocked by the antagonist Leo.H4 antibody, its binding to ligands such as von Willebrand factor and fibrinogen is impaired. In this situation, although platelets can still adhere to collagen, they are unable to accumulate and aggregate.27 As a result, platelet-FVIII stored in α-granules cannot effectively gather in vivo at injury sites, leading to uncontrolled bleeding despite the presence of FVIII.

Clopidogrel inhibits ADP-mediated platelet activation and amplification aggregation.39 Although less potent than Leo.H4 in disrupting platelet function, clopidogrel significantly impaired hemostasis in HA mice with platelet-targeted FVIII expression. These mice exhibited prolonged bleeding and significant blood loss despite therapeutic levels of platelet-derived FVIII. This finding suggests that the function of platelet-derived FVIII depends not only on initial platelet adhesion but also on full activation and recruitment of additional platelets to consolidate the hemostatic plug. Aspirin, in contrast, irreversibly inhibits COX-1, thereby blocking thromboxane A2 (TxA2) synthesis and impairing platelet activation through a separate pathway.40, 41, 42 Although aspirin treatment also increased bleeding and diminished platelet-FVIII efficacy, its effects were milder than those observed with Leo.H4 and clopidogrel. This difference may reflect aspirin’s more selective impact on platelet recruitment, whereas pathways mediated by ADP and thrombin remain functional. Aspirin was not evaluated in the FeCl3 model due to limited animal availability, but results from the TVT and tail-bleeding assays support its relatively mild impact on platelet-FVIII efficacy. Nevertheless, the additive or synergistic effects of aspirin with other agents or comorbid conditions may still pose a concern for patients treated with platelet-targeted FVIII gene therapy.

In addition to the overall impairment of platelet-FVIII efficacy by antiplatelet agents, our findings reveal model-dependent differences in the impact of aspirin on hemostasis. In the TVT model, aspirin-treated 2bF8 mice exhibited a significantly longer rechallenge bleeding time than vehicle-treated controls, whereas primary bleeding times and blood loss were not significantly different between treatment groups. This suggests that aspirin exerts a mild, delayed impact on hemostasis in this injury setting, potentially due to its inhibition of TxA2-mediated platelet activation during reexposure to vascular injury. In contrast, the TTT model demonstrated a more profound impairment of platelet-FVIII efficacy after aspirin treatment. Aspirin-treated 2bF8 and WT mice bled continuously throughout the primary and rechallenge tests, with significantly increased bleeding duration and blood loss compared to vehicle-treated mice. The TTT model, which produces a mixed microvascular injury involving small arterioles, venules, capillaries, and connective tissue in the richly vascularized tail tip,43 may more accurately reflect the cumulative effects of platelet activation, recruitment, and thrombus propagation. The differential impact observed between these 2 models likely reflects the relative dependence on secondary platelet activation pathways in achieving durable hemostasis. In the TTT model, aspirin’s inhibition of COX-1 and TxA2 generation more directly impairs platelet-mediated thrombus consolidation, which is critical for halting bleeding in a high-shear microvascular context.44,45 These results emphasize the importance of injury model choice in evaluating hemostatic therapies and indicate that platelet-FVIII’s effectiveness may be more affected by aspirin in diffuse microvascular injury than in localized venous disruption.

Interestingly, in vitro analysis using ROTEM and nWB-TGA failed to detect significant differences in hemostatic parameters between drug-treated groups (including clopidogrel and aspirin) and control groups. This apparent discrepancy between in vitro and in vivo findings highlights a key limitation of global coagulation assays in capturing the complex interplay between platelet activation, aggregation, and spatially restricted FVIII delivery. ROTEM and nWB-TGA measure coagulation potential in a static environment and do not account for dynamic platelet behavior, such as platelet accumulation, vascular injury response, or the spatial targeting of FVIII that occurs with platelet-FVIII. Although nWB-TGA showed thrombin generation in 2bF8 mice, similar to WT mice, clopidogrel and aspirin did not alter the curves. This aligns with red blood cell–driven thrombin generation in WB-TGA46 and its relative insensitivity to platelet activation.22 Therefore, the effects of antiplatelet agents on platelet-FVIII efficacy are better shown in vivo, in which platelet activation at injury sites is necessary.

Despite the inhibitory effects of antiplatelet agents, a particularly notable finding was that platelet-derived FVIII still maintained some efficacy and outperformed plasma-derived FVIII in clopidogrel-treated mice, even though platelet-FVIII activity levels were only 24% of WT plasma-FVIII. Additionally, platelet-FVIII retains some efficacy despite reduced platelet release due to antiplatelet treatment. This 6-hour tail-bleeding assay showed that clopidogrel- and aspirin-treated 2bF8 mice bleed less than FVIIInull mice, with the central artery and lateral veins fully transected. This indicates that delivering FVIII through platelet α-granules is more effective for hemostasis than systemic FVIII, especially in challenging cases. Localized platelet-FVIII at injury sites may improve FVIII activity within a near-physiological microenvironment, reducing inhibitor exposure. This benefits patients with FVIII inhibitors, who often do not respond well to traditional therapies.

The potential clinical benefits of platelet-specific FVIII gene therapy are considerable.36,37,47 Targeting FVIII to megakaryocytes and storing it in platelet α-granules eliminates the need for frequent infusions and provides protection even in the presence of inhibitors.15, 16, 17 Controlled FVIII release at injury sites aids hemostasis and reduces off-target thrombosis. Platelet-targeted gene therapy induces immune tolerance through natural platelet mechanisms, thereby ensuring effective delivery of the therapeutic.30,48, 49, 50, 51, 52, 53 These attributes offer a safer, more durable option for individuals with severe HA, including those with a history of inhibitor development.18 However, the findings from this study also highlight the importance of caution when using antiplatelet medications in patients undergoing or receiving platelet-targeted gene therapy. As this therapy advances, it is important to assess how platelet-inhibiting comedications affect its effectiveness. Approaches such as optimizing FVIII levels, temporarily discontinuing antiplatelet drugs during periods of increased bleeding risk, or selecting agents with minimal effect on FVIII should be considered.

There are some limitations to consider when interpreting our findings. First, although our mouse models recapitulate key aspects of human hemophilia and platelet function, there may be species-specific differences in drug metabolism and platelet biology that limit direct extrapolation to human patients. Second, we evaluated each antiplatelet agent in isolation, whereas many patients, particularly those with cardiovascular disease, are treated with dual or triple antiplatelet regimens. Additionally, although we focused on acute hemostatic responses, longer-term studies are needed to evaluate whether platelet gene therapy can maintain efficacy under chronic antiplatelet use. From a clinical perspective, our data have several important implications. First, patients undergoing platelet-targeted gene therapy should be carefully evaluated for concurrent antiplatelet use, and temporary adjustment of therapy may be warranted during periods of heightened bleeding risk. Second, platelet-FVIII’s ability to outperform plasma-FVIII in clopidogrel-treated settings suggests its potential to offer superior protection even in medically complex patients, including those with cardiovascular comorbidities. These findings emphasize the need for patient-specific therapy planning, especially as gene therapy becomes more common in treating aging patients with hemophilia likely to receive antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs.

In summary, our data suggest that platelet aggregation is vital for the success of platelet-specific FVIII gene therapy, and common antiplatelet drugs can impair its function in vivo, even if standard in vitro coagulation tests appear normal. Notably, platelet-FVIII has an advantage over plasma-FVIII in certain drug-treated settings, supporting its development as a therapeutic option for HA, particularly in patients with inhibitors or complicating conditions. Future research should examine how platelet-FVIII interacts with medications affecting platelet activity to establish safe guidelines for patients with hemophilia.
