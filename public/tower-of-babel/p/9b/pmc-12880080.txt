Multi-trajectories in different domains of social supports and subjective motoric cognitive risk syndrome: a 16-year group-based multi-trajectory analysis
Authors: Hui-Min Chuang, Lin-Chieh Meng, Chih-Kuang Liang, Fei-Yuan Hsiao, Liang-Kung Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe aim of this study was to examine the longitudinal relationships between the trajectories of distinct subtypes of various domains of social supports and risk of subjective motoric cognitive risk (MCR) syndrome.DesignLongitudinal cohort study.Setting and participants2,279 participants in the Taiwan Longitudinal Study on Aging (TLSA) between 1999 and 2011.MethodA group-based multi-trajectory modeling (GBMTM) was implemented to identify distinct trajectory subtypes within various social support domains, encompassing social networks, emotional support, instrumental support, as well as working and economic status. Logistic regression models were then utilized to evaluate the associations between these trajectory subtypes and the risk of subjective MCR.ResultsAmong 2,279 participants, GBMTM identified four distinct trajectory subtypes: "low social support" (n = 371), "medium social support " (n = 862), "high social support" (n = 292), and "high social support with employment" (n = 754). The incidence rates of subjective MCR for these groups were 9.4%, 9.0%, 4.1%, and 0.8%, respectively. After adjusting for age, sex, education level, and comorbidities, both “low social support” (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 4.07, 95% CI [1.60–10.34]) and “medium social support” (aOR 3.10, 95% CI [1.26−7.66]) were significantly associated with an increased risk of subjective MCR compared to the "high social support with employment" group.Conclusions and implicationsThe current study demonstrates that social support significantly reduces the risk of subjective MCR, with lower support levels correlating to higher risk, necessitating further intervention studies to confirm the link between social support and risk of subjective MCR.Keywords: Social supports, Motoric cognitive risk (MCR) syndrome, Group-based multi-trajectory modeling (GBMTM), Community-dwelling adults

Introduction

Aging, characterized by impaired homeostasis, reduced physiological reserve, declined physical and cognitive functions, and increased multimorbidity, intertwines with social vulnerability, escalating the risk of adverse health outcomes. The World Health Organization defines healthy aging as maintaining functional ability for well-being in later life, prioritizing the preservation of physical and cognitive function to prevent disability and dementia. Motoric Cognitive Risk Syndrome (MCR) is characterized by the simultaneous presence of both motor and cognitive impairments in older adults [1], which represents a transitional state between normal aging and dementia. Individuals with MCR, exhibiting slow gait along with subjective cognitive declines [2], may face an increased likelihood of adverse health events like falls and dementia, positioning MCR as a potential early indicator for healthy aging [3,4]. Identifying MCR in older adults is thus crucial for implementing preventive interventions to mitigate the progression of cognitive impairment and enhance overall well-being in the aging population [5,6].

Research underscores the crucial role of social support, including emotional, instrumental, and relational aid from various networks, in shaping cognitive function among older adults, as indicated by current evidence [[7], [8], [9]]. The link between social support and cognitive function is manifested through stress reduction, mitigation of depressive symptoms, and mental stimulation from regular social interactions [10,11], all of which are key determinants of cognitive activities like communication, problem-solving, and knowledge exchange. Despite extensive efforts went into the investigation regarding the association between social support and cognitive function, most studies have inherent limitations. First, most existing studies have focused on the cognitive function or dementia only [12], but not MCR. While considering the interaction of social determinants and cognitive functions, existing studies suggest that the co-occurrence of mobility and cognitive impairments in older adults can synergistically contribute to adverse outcomes, with both aspects being susceptible to social vulnerability [[13], [14], [15]]. Second, while many studies focus solely on a single domain of social determinants like socioeconomic status [12,16], it is essential to recognize that social support is a broader, multifaceted concept. Third, the focus of most existing studies on domains of social support was based on an one-time measurement, thereby overlooking the time-varying properties inherent in several domains of social support.

To date, no prior study has utilized a multi-domain approach with repeated measurements of social support to examine its association with MCR development. Hence, our study uses a nationally cohort sample to examine the relationship between trajectories across various domains of social support and the risk of subjective MCR.

Materials and methods

Data source

The data for this study were derived from the Taiwan Longitudinal Study on Ageing (TLSA), a nationwide survey initiated by the Health Promotion Administration and overseen by the Health and Welfare Data Science Center, Ministry of Health and Welfare. The TLSA is a comprehensive survey that has been initiated since 1989, with subsequent follow-ups conducted at intervals of 3–4 years. Data collection was facilitated through in-person household interviews, encompassing assessments of demographic characteristics, household details, and evaluations of physical, functional, and mental health statuses. Further information regarding the TLSA can be accessed via the official website of the Taiwan Health Promotion Administration, as well as previous scholarly investigations [[17], [18], [19]]. This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of our institution (Registration number, 202204025RINA).

Study participants

The study comprised individuals aged 60 years or older from the eighth wave (outcome wave) of the TLSA survey conducted in 2015. As the primary objective was to investigate the longitudinal impact of distinct trajectories in different domains of social support on the risk of subjective MCR, participants with incomplete information of subjective MCR during the outcome wave (n = 332) or had a history of subjective MCR before the outcome wave were excluded (n = 31). The fourth (1999) to the seventh (2011) waves (trajectory period) of the TLSA survey were utilized to establish the multi-trajectory of social support. Therefore, individuals with missing data for any domain of social support during the trajectory period (n = 354) were excluded from the analysis (Fig. 1B).

Study design and study population.

Motoric cognitive risk syndrome

MCR refers to a condition characterized by the presence of subjective cognitive complaints and slow gait in individuals without dementia or mobility disability. In this study, an modified version of MCR was employed to assess cognitive and motoric impairments. Subjective cognitive complaints were determined using a specific question: "Do you perceive a decline in your cognitive abilities compared to your last assessment?" Respondents who expressed strong agreement or agreement with this statement were classified as having subjective cognitive decline.

In traditional MCR assessments, slow gait is typically quantified based on objective measurements of walking speed. However, in this study, we adopted a subjective approach to define slow gait. Specifically, participants were asked to report their perceived difficulty in completing a distance of 200–300 meters. Those who reported finding it very difficult or being unable to complete the designated distance were classified as exhibiting slowness. To identify individuals with subjective MCR, we considered the co-occurrence of subjective cognitive decline and subjective motoric complains [20]. Participants who manifested both subjective cognitive decline and slowness were categorized as having subjective MCR.

Measurements of social support

A group-based multi-trajectory modeling (GBMTM) was employed to identify distinct subtypes of trajectory in five domains of social support, including social network, emotional support, instrumental support, as well as working status and economic status (Table 1). Social network was assessed using five items, which included marital status, regular contact with children on a weekly basis, regular contact with non-child family members on a weekly basis, regular contact with friends on a weekly basis, and participation in social activities. Each item was assigned a score of 1, and the total score ranged from 0 to 5, representing the level of social network.

Emotional support was defined using two items: "Is there any family or any relatives or friends available to provide attentive listening and support in times of need?" and "To what extent are you content with the degree of consideration exhibited by your family or friends regarding your emotional or psychological well-being?" A response of "strongly agree" or "agree" to the first item, and “very satisfied” or “satisfied” to the second item were scored as receiving emotional support. Each item was assigned a score of 1, with a maximum score of 2 for emotional support.

Instrumental support was measured through two items: "When you are sick, can you rely on your family or relatives/friends for assistance?" and "When you need to go out, such as seeing a doctor, shopping, or meeting friends, and require someone's help, do you have someone to assist you?" A response of "strongly agree" to "agree" for the first item and a positive response to the second item were considered as receiving instrumental support. Each item was assigned a score of 1, with a maximum score of 2 for instrumental support.

Regarding the working status, participants who currently had full-time or part-time job were assigned a score of 1, while those did not were assigned a score of 0. Lastly, economic status was determined based on subjective perception on their financial situation. The question "Do you and your spouse have enough money in managing monthly expenditures?" was used to define economic status. A response of “strongly agree” or “agree” was considered as having good economic condition, and the participant was assigned a score of 1.

Covariates

The seventh (2011) wave of the TLSA survey was assigned as the baseline period to retrieve demographic information, including age, sex, education level, and comorbidities. Comorbidities were documented according to self-reported diagnoses told by a doctor, including hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, stroke, cancer, liver or kidney disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD).

Statistical analysis

Group-based multi-trajectory modelling to identify social support patterns

We applied the group-based multi-trajectory model (GBMTM) to identify different clusters of individuals displaying comparable patterns of social support over time. This data-driven approach allowed us to capture the dynamic changes and transitions within social support domains. Each of the five components reflecting the social support situation was considered as a time-varying variable. The working status was treated as a binary variable, while remaining variables were viewed as continuous variables. To select the most suitable model, we conducted a comprehensive analysis following established procedures [15,21,22]. We systematically evaluated the optimal number of trajectories and shapes by utilizing the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) value, which enabled the comparison of models with varying number of groups and trajectory shapes. Our inclusion criteria ensured that trajectory groups comprised more than 5% of participants and had an average posterior probability (AvePP) value exceeding 0.7. The model with BIC value closest to zero was selected as optimal choice for our cohort, as it suggested a model that fits well without overfitting.

Baseline characteristics of different social support patterns

Descriptive statistics were employed to analyze the demographic and clinical characteristics of all participants. Continuous variables in both the text and tables were summarized as mean values with standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as percentages.

The association between social support trajectories and risk of subjective MCR

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was employed to examine the association between social support patterns and the risk of subjective MCR, and the adjusted odds ratios (aORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software, version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Results

Subtypes of social support trajectories

Among 2,996 individuals included from the eighth wave (2011) of the TLSA survey, a final cohort of 2,279 participants was selected for analysis. The inclusion and exclusion details are provided in Fig. 1. The GBMTM was employed, and model selection was optimized following established procedures. Based on the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) values (five-trajectory model = −30,312; four-trajectory model = −29,476; three-trajectory model = −28,997), the five-trajectory model was superior to models with four or fewer trajectories. Despite meeting the criteria for model selection, the differences between the groups in the five-trajectory model were clinically insignificant, with minimal observed variations. Consequently, we proceeded the four-trajectory model for subsequent analyses, and each participant was assigned to specific trajectories based on their highest posterior probabilities of membership.

The four identified groups were labeled as follows: “low social support” (n = 371, 16%), “medium social support” (n = 862, 38%), “high social support” (n = 292, 13%), and “high social support with employment” (n = 754, 33%) (Fig. 2). The mean posterior probabilities of group membership for each trajectory (0.88, 0.92, 0.90, and 0.95) indicated strong consistency and similarity within the low social support, medium social support, high social support, and high social support with employment groups. The “low social support” group exhibited lower scores across all five domains of social support. The “medium social support” and “high social support” groups showed similar trajectories in terms of emotional support, instrumental support, working status, and economic status, with only social network scores being higher in the “high social support” group. The “high social support with employment” group was characterized by the predominant feature of being employed.

Trajectories of social support between 1997 and 2009.

A detailed analysis of the individual component of social support within the four identified trajectories further revealed that being not married or cohabited, lack of regularly contact with friends, and lack of participation in social activities were three key components contribute to vulnerability of social network domain in “low social support” and “medium social support” groups (Supplement 1 and 2). For emotional support and instrumental support, all trajectory groups, except for the “low social support” group, exhibited high score in these two domains. For example, the proportion of participants being scored “1” in wave 2011 for the question of emotional support domain “If any family or relatives/friends can listen to you when you need?” were 84.9%, 88.0%, and 86.5% in the “medium social support”, “high social support”, and “high social support with employment” groups, respectively. Interestingly, those in the “medium social support” and “high social support” groups had the lowest proportion of full or part-time employment rates, which were even lower than those in the “low social support.” For example, in wave 2011, the proportion of full or part-time employment rates were only 1.7% and 3.2% for the “medium social support” and “high social support” groups, as compared to the 20.94% in the “low social support” group. Noteworthy, the proportion of full or part-time employment rates decreased dramatically in these two groups as well. In the “medium social support” group, the rates declined from 23.0% in wave 1997 to 1.7% in wave 2011 while in the “high social support” group, the rate declined from 36.7% in wave 1997 to 3.2% in wave 2011. Even so, the “low social support” group reported highest proportion of vulnerability in the economic status domain. Only 38.02% of those in the “low social support” group being scored “1” in wave 2011 for the question “Do you and your spouse have enough money or experience difficulties in managing monthly expenditures?” However, over 80% of participants in other three trajectory groups were scored “1” in the same question.

Baseline characteristics

The baseline characteristics of each trajectory group were shown in Table 2. The “high social support with employment” group were obviously younger than the other trajectory groups, with 37.9% of them aged 60−64. Men were the dominant gender (70.2%) in the “high social support with employment” group while women were the dominant gender in other trajectory groups. Approximately 20% of the participants in the “low social support” and “medium social support” groups were illiterate. Compared to other trajectory groups, participants in the “high social support with employment” group had fewer comorbidities, in terms of individual diseases such as hypertension as well as the number of comorbid conditions.

Risk of subjective MCR

The incidence of subjective MCR among these groups was as follows: 9.4% in the low social support group, 9.0% in the medium social support group, 4.1% in the high social support group and 0.8% in the high social support with employment group (Table 3). The results of logistic regression models are also presented in Table 3. After adjusting for age, sex, education level, and comorbid conditions, both the low social support group (adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 4.07, 95% conﬁdence interval (CI) 1.60−10.34) and the medium social support (aOR 3.10, 95%CI 1.26−7.66) showed a higher risk of subjective MCR compared to the high social support with employment group.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study stands as the first to employ a multidimensional approach to capture social support, utilizing a nationally representative sample observed over a 16-year period with multiple measurements of social support. Through a data-driven approach, we have elucidated the role of social support in the development of subjective MCR, a contribution not previously reported in the literature. The current study analyzed a cohort of 2,279 participants from the eighth wave of the TLSA survey and identified four distinct groups based on social support patterns: "low social support" (16% of participants), "medium social support " (38%), "high social support" (13%), and "high social support with employment" (33%). The incidence of subjective MCR varied among these groups: 9.4% in the low social support group, 9.0% in the medium social support group, 4.1% in the high social support group, and 0.8% in the high social support with employment group. After adjusting for age, sex, education level, and comorbidities, the study found that the low social support group (aOR: 4.07, 95% CI 1.60−10.34) and the medium social support group (aOR: 3.10, 95% CI 1.26−7.66) were at significantly higher risk for incident subjective MCR, compared to the high social support with employment group. Utilizing a data-driven methodology, we discerned longitudinal patterns of social support, acknowledging its potential for temporal variation and modification. We distinctly identified four categories of temporal social support status and observed their differential risks associated with the subsequent development of subjective MCR. The study's findings substantiated the elevated risk of subjective MCR development in older individuals with diminished social support.

An epidemiological study reported the prevalence of MCR of 6.4% among older Japanese population, with diabetes, depressive symptoms, falls, obesity, and physical inactivity identified as significant risk factors without the sex-specific association [23]. On the other hand, in a study of 3,251 older adults in China, loneliness was found to be associated with an increased risk of MCR in women, but not in men. No significant association was found between social isolation and MCR in either gender [24]. A previous study found that certain personality traits, such as higher neuroticism, extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness, are associated with the risk of MCR [25]. Besides, a higher sense of purpose in life was found to be associated with a 33% lower concurrent risk and a 26% lower longitudinal risk of MCR, which highlights the importance of personal and social determinants in the development of MCR [26]. Increased tangible and overall social support were found to decrease the risk of MCR by 30% and 33%, respectively, in a cohort study of 506 older adults followed for 2.5 years [27]. While this study also utilized multiple dimensions of social support to investigate its influence on the risk of MCR development, our research broadens this scope by incorporating a multitude of measures in a data-driven approach over an extended follow-up period of 16 years, thereby reinforcing the validity of the association.

The interrelationship between social determinants and health of older people is bi-directional. The onset of a physical disability in adulthood significantly influences an individual's social determinants of health, such as income [28], which in turn profoundly impacts the physical and mental health of older individuals. Analogous to MCR, the Physio-Cognitive Decline Syndrome (PCDS) has shown its potential for promoting healthy longevity through its extensive epidemiological evidence and demonstrated reversibility, and incorporating social determinants as modifiable risks could further enhance this pursuit [[29], [30], [31]]. Although numerous biological factors have been identified as predisposing factors of PCDS [[32], [33], [34]], the interplay between these biological factors and social determinants could potentially alter the progression of the disease. A prior study found that a higher state of social vulnerability was associated with increased all-cause mortality in middle-aged and older adults, and this association was moderated by ApoE genotypes, but not 5-HTTLPR genotypes, highlighting the importance of gene-environment interactions in the aging process [35]. In addition, sex-specific associations between social isolation and loneliness have been reported, with social isolation linked to a higher risk of loneliness in women but not in men, and demographic factors like marital status and self-reported health status having protective effects against depressive symptoms [7]. Contrary to the study from China that reported potential vulnerability of women in developing MCR due to loneliness, not social isolation [24], the current study did not find significant sex differences in the relationship between social support and incident MCR. A potential mitigating factor in the sex-specific associations could be the role of employment, as it has shown significant protective effects against MCR in aging. However, in this study, employment beyond the age of 65 was predominantly observed in males. Future studies are warranted to explore the role of employment in later life for healthy aging.

Despite the rigorous efforts invested in this study, it is important to acknowledge certain limitations. First, the MCR identified in this study, which incorporates subjective cognitive decline within its operational definition [20], is a construct measurable within the TLSA survey. Future studies may consider the application of sophisticated neuropsychological assessments necessary for defining PCDS and investigate the impact of social support on this dual impairment trajectory [36]. Second, the status of social support was self-reported, which could potentially introduce reporting bias, but this is a common issue in most previous studies. Third, while we excluded participants who had MCR before the outcome wave to mitigate reverse causality, there remains a possibility that social factors, such as social supports, observed during the study period were influenced by the development of MCR. Future research should employ longitudinal designs incorporating time-lagged analyses to elucidate the bidirectional effects between MCR and social factors. Lastly, to ensure complete data for all participants at the outcome wave, those who died during the study period were not included. This may result in a healthier cohort, potentially limiting the generalizability of our results to a broader population. However, focusing on a healthier cohort enables a precise examination of the impact of social support on the development of MCR in individuals who are more likely to benefit from interventions, offering valuable insights for preventive strategies.

Conclusions

In summary, the current study, which utilized a nationally representative cohort over a 16-year period, revealed that older individuals with low or medium social support faced a significantly increased risk for subjective MCR. Conversely, those with high social support, particularly high social support coupled with employment, demonstrated a reduced likelihood of developing subjective MCR. Given that social support is a potentially modifiable risk factor, it is imperative to incorporate social determinants in intervention studies aimed at preventing subjective MCR and subsequent adverse outcomes, thereby promoting healthy longevity.
