Prevalence and characteristics of temporomandibular joint disc displacement based on Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) in pre-clinical diagnosis: A retrospective study
Authors: Bidisha Sarmah, Komal Smriti, Prajna P Nayak, Ramprasad Vasthare, Manohara A Bhat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionTemporomandibular joint (TMJ) is a complex synovial joint commonly affected by temporomandibular disorders (TMDs), with disc displacement being the most prevalent subtype. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the gold standard for TMJ evaluation, offering superior soft tissue contrast and non-invasive assessment of disc position and morphology.Aim and objectivesTo evaluate the diagnostic value of MRI in the preclinical detection of TMJ disc displacement, determine the prevalence of unilateral and bilateral displacement patterns and compare disc position and TMJ anatomical variables between normal and abnormal disc positions in the closed-mouth position.MethodologyThis retrospective cross-sectional study analyzed 214 MRI scans of patients aged 13–70 years using high-quality sagittal closed-mouth TMJ sections. Disc thickness, Disc area and Articular eminence inclination (AEI) were assessed by a TMD-trained specialist. Descriptive statistics, Chi-square and Mann-Whitney U tests were applied, with significance set at p < 0.05.ResultsAfter excluding poor-quality scans, 214 participants (428 TMJs; mean age 45.8 years; 61.2% male) were analyzed. Abnormal disc position was present in 61.7% of TMJs, mainly anterior displacement (57.5%) and was associated with increased disc thickness, reduced disc area and flatter AEI (p < 0.001). Symptomatic individuals exhibited thicker discs and reduced AEI (p < 0.05), with no gender differences. Abnormal disc position showed a significant association with TMJ symptoms (p = 0.049).ConclusionMRI can characterize TMJ disc position and morphology on closed-mouth sagittal sections and frequently identified anterior displacement even in asymptomatic individuals, highlighting its value in early and preclinical diagnosis.Keywords: Temporomandibular joint disc, Magnetic resonance imaging, Anterior disc displacement, Osteoarthritis, Temporomandibular disorders

Introduction

The temporomandibular joint (TMJ) is considered to be one of the most intricate and distinctive joints in the human body. It acts like a synovial joint, with lower part moving in a circle and upper part moving back and forth between the disc and the condyle.1

Temporomandibular joint disorder (TMD) is an umbrella term that includes a wide range of clinical symptoms, such as pain in the TMJ, limited mouth opening, headaches, clicking or popping sounds, teeth clenching, bruxism, crepitus, and tenderness of the masticatory muscles.2 The condyle serves as the principal growth centre of the mandible and is fundamental to jaw development. Various factors, such as condylar inflammation, occlusal patterns, aging processes, and orthodontic treatments, have been reported to influence its growth and morphology.3 Temporomandibular joint disc displacement (TMJ-DD) is a common TMD characterized by an abnormal alignment of articular disc with mandibular condyle and temporal bone. It is a significant factor in non-dental orofacial pain and is categorized as a musculoskeletal disorder characterized by condyle-disc complex disruptions, articular surface incompatibility and related inflammatory alterations.4 TMD affects approximately 5-12% of adults and are reported more frequently in females, particularly those aged 20-40 years.5,6 Approximately one-third of the population reports TMD symptoms, while 3.6-7.0% experience symptoms severe enough to require treatment.6,7

Amongst the various types of TMDs, disc dislocation is the most common, with an incidence rate ranging from 41% to as high as 89%. Disc dislocation is defined as the disturbance of the normal relationship between the disc and condyle, usually in an anterior direction, due to occlusal dysfunction, trauma, stress, sleep disorders, and systemic/local factors. Disc dislocation alters the biomechanics of the TMJ, leading to an increase in condylar loading and affecting condylar growth.1,8

Detection of disc displacement is a crucial step for diagnosing TMD and for developing a treatment plan. MRI, arthrography, computed tomography (CT) and cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) are among the advanced imaging modalities used in the evaluation of TMD. Of these, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is one of the commonly used, non-invasive, pain-free diagnostic tool for the TMJ. 6,7,9 It gives a direct visualization of TMJ disc morphology, position and joint effusion and is considered the gold standard for evaluating bone and soft tissues, including bone marrow oedema, synovitis and disc displacement. 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13 At present, MRI is recommended as a second-line assessment of internal derangements and is advocated in academic guidelines for clinical evaluations. 14, 15, 16, 17.

The purpose of this retrospective study was to evaluate the usefulness of MRI in preclinical diagnosis of disc displacement in TMJ and to establish the prevalence of different types of disc displacements among patients. Hence, to evaluate the use of MRI compared to conventional techniques, to create a more objective and reproducible tool for TMDs. The objectives were to evaluate patients for disc positions concerning the condylar head and mandibular fossa in a closed mouth position; to determine the prevalence of disc positioning as unilateral/bilateral disc displacement among patients presenting for head and neck MRI scans and to determine TMJ anatomical variables among patients with normal and abnormal disc positions.

Materials and methods

Study design

A retrospective, cross-sectional, record-based study was conducted using archived MRI records from 1st January 2021 to 30th September 2025. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. Permission was obtained from the Medical Superintendent to access the medical MRI records of the patients after removing personal identifiers.

The study included participants aged 13-70 years and evaluated high-quality MRI scans of the right and left TMJs, necessary to delineate anatomical structures of the joint and assess articular disc position in the closed-mouth state. Written patient/guardian consent for future research use of MRI images had been obtained before imaging. Sagittal closed-mouth images were used to compare joints with normal disc-condyle and disc-fossa relationships with those showing disc displacement. Scans with motion artifacts, poor anatomical visualization, incomplete sequences, or sections other than sagittal closed-mouth sections were excluded. Patients with a history of condylar fracture, condylar cysts or tumours associated with the condyle were also excluded.

As this was a retrospective census study, no formal a priori sample-size calculation was performed. All eligible MRI records during the defined study period were included to maximize use of the available dataset. A post-hoc sample size estimation based on an expected prevalence of TMJ disc displacement of 60%, a 95% confidence level and precision of 7% calculated a minimum required sample of 189 participants. The final sample of 214 participants exceeded this requirement, suggesting adequate precision for prevalence estimation. Of 220 records screened, 6 were excluded because of incomplete or poor-quality images, resulting in 214 participants (428 TMJs) for analysis. The sample was considered appropriate for descriptive prevalence estimates and bivariate comparisons; however, the small symptomatic subgroup (n = 28) limited statistical power for subgroup analyses and precluded reliable multivariable adjustment.

Data collection

As this is a retrospective study, all eligible cases during the study period were included. Sociodemographic data (age, gender and clinical symptom status) were collected using a structured proforma. MRI was performed on a 3.0-T SIGNA MRI scanner (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) using a 12-channel head coil. Archived high-resolution T1-weighted turbo-spin-echo 3D images and proton-density-weighted sagittal closed-mouth images were reviewed. The recorded imaging parameters were TR/TE 3500/100 ms, matrix size 256 x 182, field of view 120 × 120 mm, in-plane resolution 0.80 mm, approximate acquisition time 4 min, and sagittal slice interval/thickness 3 mm.

For this retrospective analysis, only closed-mouth sagittal sections from the archived MRI datasets were selected. Patients had been scanned in the supine position without anaesthesia and closed-mouth sections were chosen because they were consistently available with adequate quality across all eligible records. Dynamic/open-mouth sections were not analyzed because they were not consistently available in a standardized form across the retrospective dataset; therefore, the study was limited to static assessment of the disc-condyle relationship and could not determine disc reduction during mandibular movement. MRI data were reviewed in a Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) viewer, and all diagnostically adequate sagittal sections spanning each TMJ were screened, with the midsagittal closed-mouth section used for primary assessment of disc position.18

Disc position was categorized as normal, unilateral, or bilateral displacement and further classified by direction (anterior, posterior, or normal). TMJ morphological parameters evaluated included right and left disc thickness, disc area (cm2), and articular eminence inclination (AEI, degrees), derived from MRI images. These measures enabled assessment of the prevalence, direction, and anatomical correlates of disc displacement and their associations with demographic and clinical variables.

Data analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp). Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. Sociodemographic characteristics were presented as frequencies and percentages. The Chi-square test and Mann-Whitney U test were applied to assess associations between gender and disc displacement characteristics, compare symptomatic and asymptomatic participants and evaluate differences in TMJ anatomical variables between normal and abnormal disc positions.

Results

A final study sample (N = 214) after excluding 6 incomplete or poor-quality MRI cases during data cleaning. These participants underwent MRI evaluation of the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) to assess disc positioning in the closed-mouth position using sagittal sections. The mean age group among the study sample was 45.79 ± 15.02 years. Males comprised 61.2% (n = 131) and females 38.8% (n = 83) of the sample (Fig. 1).

Table 1 summarizes the demographic and clinical characteristics where most participants were males (61.2%) and asymptomatic (86.9%), while females constituted 38.8% (n = 83). Only 13.1% (n = 28) presented with clinical symptoms. MRI evaluation showed normal disc position in 38.3% (n = 82) of participants, whereas 36.4% (n = 78) had unilateral and 25.2% (n = 54) had bilateral disc displacement, reflecting the study objective of assessing prevalence. Overall, abnormal disc positioning was observed in 61.7% (n = 132), indicating a high burden of internal derangement. Anterior disc displacement was the most common pattern (57.5%, n = 123), while posterior displacement was rare (4.2%, n = 9). Table 2 compares disc displacement characteristics between symptomatic and asymptomatic participants using the Chi-square test. Differences in unilateral versus bilateral displacement and in the direction of displacement were not statistically significant (p = 0.125; p = 0.126, respectively), although bilateral and anterior displacements were more frequent among symptomatic individuals. In contrast, a significant difference was observed in disc positioning (χ2 = 3.888, p = 0.049), with abnormal placements in more than three fourths of symptomatic participants compared with 59.1% of asymptomatic participants. This means that TMJ symptoms were associated with a higher likelihood of abnormal disc position in this sample, but the strength of the association was modest. These findings indicate a significant association between abnormal disc position and TMJ symptoms, despite no significant differences in displacement type or direction. Graph 1 illustrates the comparison of articular eminence inclination (AEI) degrees between male and female participants. The visual trend suggests that males generally have a higher AEI than females.

When TMJ anatomical measurements were compared between normal and abnormal disc positions, participants with abnormal disc positions showed greater mean disc thickness on both right and left sides (0.239 cm and 0.238 cm respectively) than those with normally positioned (0.189 cm and 0.190 cm respectively), along with greater variability. The area under the disc was 1.4 ± 0/09 sq cm in normal discs but was markedly reduced in abnormal discs (mean = 0.422 sq cm). In contrast, articular eminence inclination was substantially higher in the normal group (mean = 45.5°) compared with the abnormal group (mean = 32.7°). Disc thickness on both right and left sides showed significantly higher mean ranks in the abnormal group compared with the normal group, indicating thicker discs in abnormal positions. Conversely, area under the disc and articular eminence inclination were substantially higher in the normal group, reflecting larger disc area and a steeper eminence. All differences were statistically significant (p ≤ 0.001). Overall, abnormal disc positioning was associated with increased disc thickness, reduced disc area and a flatter articular eminence inclination (Table 3).

Discussion

In the present MRI-based study, sample consisted of 214 subjects (mean age 45.8 ± 15.0 years, 61.2% male and 38.8% female). More than half of the total images presented abnormal positioning of the disc, with 61.7% being abnormal, where, most were displaced anteriorly (57.5%) and only 4.2% displaced posteriorly. The high prevalence of abnormal disc positions aligns with earlier MRI studies reporting disc displacement in both symptomatic and asymptomatic populations by Tasaki et al. and Serindere et al.8,17 Clinically, this finding emphasizes that disc displacement on MRI should not be interpreted in isolation. A proportion of asymptomatic individuals may show disc displacement; therefore, MRI findings should be correlated with clinical symptoms, examination findings and the indication for imaging before treatment decisions are made.

The study highlighted that symptomatic individuals exhibited significantly increased disc thickness bilaterally and reduced articular eminence inclination (AEI) compared to asymptomatic participants. The disc area did not differ significantly between symptomatic and asymptomatic patients, as noted in previous studies by Jerele et al.19

Abnormal position of discs was more common among symptomatic than asymptomatic individuals (78.6% vs. 59.1%, p = 0.049), which is in agreement with Orhan et al.4 who stated that “TMJ disorders can be present even among individuals without clinical symptoms and can be considered incidental findings.” But the strength of association is modest and should be interpreted cautiously as, only 28 participants were symptomatic. Our results suggest a trend that symptomatic patients are more likely to show disc displacement on MRI, but it does not establish causation.

This study further demonstrated that abnormal disc position, whether unilateral, bilateral, anterior or posterior, was found to affect the thickness of the disc, the area under the disc and the AEI values when assessing the position of the disc relative to the condylar head and mandibular fossa in the closed mouth position using the sagittal section views, as previously found in the studies by Zieliński et al.20 and Litko-Rola et al.15

The findings demonstrate the usefulness of MRI in identifying preclinical TMJ disc displacement, even in asymptomatic individuals. Anatomical parameters such as disc thickness and AEI were found to be associated with TMD and may have diagnostic relevance; however, their predictive value for identifying individuals at risk of developing TMD cannot be established due to the cross-sectional nature of the study. These findings support the inclusion of MRI in diagnostic procedures where early detection is considered relevant. These findings are in agreement with previous literature findings by Takahara et al.13 and Alqhtani et.21

The strengths of the study include the use of MRI, assessment of both TMJs, inclusion of a reasonable retrospective sample, and comparison of imaging findings between symptomatic and asymptomatic participants. The findings may be useful for describing the prevalence and imaging characteristics of TMJ disc displacement in a clinical MRI cohort.

This study has a few limitations. Its retrospective cross-sectional design limits causal inference and may introduce selection bias because the sample comprised patients undergoing MRI for clinical indications. In addition, all MRI scans were interpreted by a single TMD-trained examiner, and intra-observer or inter-observer reliability assessments were not performed; therefore, the reproducibility of the imaging measurements and disc position classifications could not be formally evaluated. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causality.

Symptom status was derived from existing records and was not assessed using a fully standardized symptom classification, which may have resulted in misclassification. No formal sample size calculation was performed and the symptomatic subgroup was relatively small. Important potential confounders such as bruxism, trauma history, osteoarthritis and MRI indications were not consistently available in the retrospective records; therefore, multivariable analyses could not be carried out to adjust for the above factors. Also, a relatively small symptomatic subgroup limited the stability of multivariable modelling.

Classification of disc position was based on static sagittal closed-mouth MRI, and dynamic imaging was not available. Consequently, the study could evaluate only static disc-condyle relationships and could not distinguish anterior disc displacement with reduction from anterior disc displacement without reduction. Hence, we recommend that future prospective studies should incorporate standardized dynamic MRI protocols.

Conclusion

In this retrospective MRI-based study, anterior disc displacement was the most common pattern of TMJ disc displacement and was observed even among asymptomatic individuals. The thickness of the disc and the angle of the articular eminence could possibly function as imaging characteristics of TMD; however, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, they should not be interpreted as predictors of individuals at risk for developing TMD. Abnormal disc position was more common in individuals suffering from TMD and was associated with a large difference in disc thickness and inclination of articular eminence, while disc area did not differ significantly between the two groups. These findings reinforce the diagnostic value of MRI in both clinical and preclinical settings and highlight the need for early recognition of morphological alterations that may precede symptom onset.

Considering the diagnostic capabilities of MRI in determining preclinical disc displacements, MRI could be considered in selected risk groups to aid in early intervention. It is recommended that future studies be conducted to establish a cause-and-effect relationship between disc morphology, AEI, and symptom development. The inclusion of dynamic MRI would offer further functional insights into disc-condyle relationships. Standardization of parameters and subgroups will enhance comparability between studies, thereby aiding in the integration of MRI findings into decision-making pathways.
