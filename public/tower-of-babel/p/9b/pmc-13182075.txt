Development and internal validation of a parsimonious clinical score for multiple organ dysfunction syndrome following acute poisoning: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Shuhao Ye, Jiujiu Gui, Pinxuan Zhou, Yuheng Shi, Hu Tang, Donghan Zhang, Zhengkun Bao, Yahui Tang, Zhongqiu Lu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAcute poisoning is a frequent cause of emergency department admission and may rapidly progress to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS); however, existing scoring systems were not specifically developed for early MODS prediction in acute poisoning. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a parsimonious clinical score for early MODS prediction at emergency admission.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 362 patients with acute poisoning admitted between January 1, 2023, and June 1, 2025, and grouped them according to MODS occurrence during hospitalization. MODS was defined using the maximum daily SOFA score during hospitalization. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression followed by multivariable logistic regression was used to develop the predictive model, with internal bootstrap validation. A simplified points-based score (P-GNLS) was derived from the final model. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis and decision curve analysis (DCA) were used to assess its predictive performance and compare it with existing scoring systems.ResultsAmong the 362 patients, 76 (20.99%) developed MODS. In-hospital mortality was significantly higher in the MODS group than in the non-MODS group (39.47% vs. 1.40%, p < 0.001) and increased with the number of dysfunctional organs. The LASSO–logistic regression model identified pesticide poisoning, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score, neutrophil count (NEUT), blood lactate (Lac), and serum creatinine (SCr) as variables independently associated with MODS. These variables were used to construct the P-GNLS score (0–38 points). The P-GNLS score showed good calibration (Hosmer–Lemeshow χ²=10.7551, p = 0.2160) and discrimination (AUC = 0.972), and demonstrated greater net benefit than the comparator scores across clinically relevant threshold probabilities in decision curve analysis.ConclusionsPatients with MODS following acute poisoning had substantially higher in-hospital mortality. In this single-center retrospective cohort, the P-GNLS score showed good discrimination and calibration for early prediction of MODS at emergency admission. Background

Acute poisoning remains a major public health and clinical challenge worldwide. Despite advances in toxicological management and organ support, a substantial proportion of patients with acute poisoning progress to multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), particularly in severe cases. Among critically ill patients admitted to the ICU for acute toxicity, organ failure was present in 48.5% of patients [1]. In a hospitalized cohort of organophosphate poisoning, 23.5% of patients met criteria for MODS [2], and studies of moderate-to-severe paraquat poisoning have likewise reported substantial MODS burdens [3]. These data suggest that the burden of MODS following acute poisoning is clinically meaningful, although reported frequencies vary by toxicant and clinical setting. Therefore, the early identification of patients at high risk for MODS is crucial for the timely escalation of monitoring, organ-support interventions, and resource allocation in the Emergency Department [4, 5].

The Poisoning Severity Score (PSS) is a disease-specific scoring system used in toxicology [6]. In addition, several general critical illness scores, such as the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II), and Modified Early Warning Score (MEWS), can be used to assess patients with acute poisoning [7–10]. However, these tools primarily grade overall severity or predict mortality rather than specifically estimating the subsequent risk of MODS. In the setting of acute poisoning, they may also be suboptimal for early MODS prediction, may be cumbersome to calculate at the bedside, and do not incorporate poisoning-specific information. To our knowledge, no widely accepted scoring system is tailored to acute poisoning, uses simple variables available at emergency admission, and specifically predicts subsequent MODS.

Therefore, we aimed to develop and internally validate a parsimonious, poisoning-specific risk score based on routinely available admission variables to enable early prediction of MODS following acute poisoning.

Methods

Study design and selection of study patients

This retrospective cohort study included patients with acute poisoning who were admitted to the Emergency Department (ED) and Emergency Intensive Care Unit (EICU) of the First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University between January 1, 2023, and June 1, 2025. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age between 16 and 90 years; and (2) a clear history of toxic exposure. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of significant pre-existing organ dysfunction (e.g., severe cardiac, pulmonary, hepatic, or renal disease); (2) MODS confirmed upon admission; (3) admission to the hospital beyond 48 h after poisoning; (4) a hospital stay of less than 12 h; and (5) critically incomplete clinical data (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of study enrollment. ED, emergency department; EICU, emergency intensive care unit; Non-MODS, non–multiple organ dysfunction syndrome; MODS, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome

Upon admission, patients received routine emergency management according to institutional practice and standard toxicological principles [11, 12]. This included prompt removal of residual toxins and a comprehensive assessment of vital signs to stabilize any immediately life-threatening conditions. After initial laboratory and diagnostic evaluations, an individualized treatment plan was formulated, focusing on preventing further absorption of the toxicant, enhancing the elimination of absorbed substances, and administering specific antidotes as early as possible. Supportive care, complication management, and organ function support were provided as clinically indicated.

Patients who met the inclusion criteria were assessed using the SOFA score to quantify the degree of organ dysfunction [13, 14]. According to MODS occurrence during hospitalization, patients were categorized into MODS and non-MODS groups (Fig. 1). MODS was defined as a maximum daily SOFA score of ≥ 2 points in at least two organ systems during hospitalization [13, 14]. The SOFA score evaluates six organ systems (respiratory, hepatic, coagulation, cardiovascular, neurological, and renal), each scored from 0 to 4, yielding a total score of 0–24. For outcome analysis, patients were additionally stratified into survivor and in-hospital death groups.

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Approval No. KY2025-R125). The Institutional Review Board granted a written informed consent waiver due to the retrospective design of the study.

Data collection

Clinical data for all eligible patients were systematically collected and categorized as follows:

(a) Demographics and history: sex, age, body mass index (BMI), medical history, and pre-admission treatments. (b) Poisoning characteristics: category of causative toxic agent and route of exposure. (c) Vital signs and laboratory parameters on admission: vital signs, white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count (NEUT), monocyte count (MONO), lymphocyte count (LYM), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), platelet count (PLT), PaO2/FiO2 ratio (P/F ratio), blood lactate (Lac), blood glucose (GLU), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), serum creatinine (SCr), C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and high-sensitivity troponin T (hs-TnT). (d) Severity scores: Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), PSS, SOFA, APACHE II, and MEWS were calculated on admission. (e) Clinical management and outcomes: treatments administered during hospitalization, including gastric lavage, mechanical ventilation, blood purification, and vasoactive drug use, were recorded. The length of stay in the EICU and hospital outcomes were also recorded.

To enhance the practicality of early emergency assessment, causative toxic agents were grouped into four broad categories according to the documented exposure history and medical records. Detailed representative toxic agents are provided in Additional file 1.

Blood purification was defined as extracorporeal detoxification or organ-support therapy administered during hospitalization. In our cohort, the techniques used for this purpose included hemoperfusion, continuous renal replacement therapy, intermittent hemodialysis, and plasma exchange.

Vasoactive drug use was defined as administration of vasopressor, inotropic, or other hemodynamic support agents during hospitalization. In our cohort, the agents used for this purpose included norepinephrine, metaraminol, dopamine, epinephrine, and isoproterenol.

Statistical analyses

Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed continuous variables are expressed as medians and interquartile ranges and were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables are presented as numbers (percentages) and were compared using the χ² or Fisher’s exact tests.

All clinically plausible admission variables were entered into a least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression model to handle multicollinearity and reduce overfitting. Predictors retained by LASSO were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to develop the final predictive model. To address model stability and sample size adequacy for the final multivariable model, we evaluated the events-per-variable (EPV) ratio and performed bootstrap internal validation with 500 resamples to assess potential overfitting and optimism.

The simplified regression equation approach proposed by Sullivan et al. [15] was subsequently applied to develop the risk scoring system. Calibration of the scoring system was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was used to evaluate discrimination, and decision curve analysis (DCA) was used to assess clinical utility. For DCA, all scoring systems were expressed on a common predicted-probability scale using cohort-specific univariable logistic models [16, 17]. Statistical significance was defined as a two-sided p < 0.05. Data processing and visualization were performed using Python (v3.11.2).

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 362 patients with acute poisoning were included in this study. Of these, 76 patients (20.99%) developed MODS, whereas 286 (79.01%) did not. Analysis of toxic agents revealed that pesticide-related exposures were more common in the MODS group than in the non-MODS group (67.11% vs. 38.81%, p < 0.001), whereas pharmaceutical-agent exposures were more frequent in the non-MODS group (43.01% vs. 22.37%, p < 0.05). On admission, the MODS group had significantly higher PSS, SOFA, APACHE II, and MEWS scores than the non-MODS group (all p < 0.001). A substantially higher proportion of patients in the MODS group received blood purification (96.05% vs. 67.13%, p < 0.001), mechanical ventilation (63.16% vs. 16.78%, p < 0.001), and vasoactive agent support (65.79% vs. 2.10%, p < 0.001) than those in the non-MODS group (these in-hospital interventions were reported to characterize illness severity and resource utilization, and were not included as candidate predictors in model development). Similarly, the outcomes differed markedly between the two groups. The in-hospital mortality rate was significantly higher in the MODS group (39.47% vs. 1.40%, p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Among the 76 patients who developed MODS, 30 (39.47%) died during hospitalization. Comparison of baseline laboratory indices

Significant differences in several baseline laboratory indices were observed between the MODS and non-MODS groups on admission. Markers that were significantly higher in the MODS group included indicators of inflammation and infection (WBC, NEUT, MONO, CRP, and PCT) and metabolic stress (Lac and GLU). In addition, markers reflecting organ dysfunction or injury, including BUN, SCr, ALT, AST, NT-proBNP, and hs-TnT, were also significantly higher in the MODS group (all p < 0.001). Conversely, the P/F ratio, reflecting impaired gas exchange, was significantly lower in the MODS group (p < 0.05). In contrast, there were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of LYM, RBC, hemoglobin (HGB), or PLT (all p > 0.05) (Table 3).

Development of the risk scoring system for MODS caused by acute poisoning

All clinically plausible admission clinical and laboratory variables were entered into the LASSO logistic regression model. LASSO identified eight candidate predictors at λ.1se = 0.034 (Fig. 2). This model applies strict coefficient shrinkage, effectively balancing bias and variance while reducing the number of variables [18, 19]. Subsequent multivariable logistic regression retained five predictors in the final model: pesticide poisoning, GCS score, NEUT, Lac, and SCr ( all p < 0.05; Table 4). The EPV ratio was 15.2 (76 events/5 variables), which is generally considered acceptable to limit overfitting in logistic regression [20]. To address potential predictor–outcome component overlap, we performed sensitivity analyses by refitting the model after excluding predictors that contribute to the SOFA-based endpoint. Model discrimination remained acceptable after the exclusion of these variables. After bootstrap correction (B = 500), the optimism-corrected AUC was 0.962 for the model excluding SCr, 0.941 for the model excluding GCS, and 0.934 for the model excluding both GCS and SCr. Bootstrap and optimism-corrected ROC curves are shown in Additional file 2.

Based on the final multivariable logistic regression model, we constructed a quantitative risk score for MODS following acute poisoning by simplifying the regression coefficients into a points-based system. This scoring system was termed the P-GNLS score, derived from the initials of the five included variables: pesticide poisoning (P), Glasgow Coma Scale (G, GCS), neutrophil count (N, NEUT), blood lactate (L, Lac), and serum creatinine (S, SCr). Each variable was stratified according to commonly used clinical cut-off values and assigned a corresponding point value, yielding a total score ranging from 0 to 38 points (Table 5). The Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated good calibration (χ²=10.7551, p = 0.2160). The detailed process of P-GNLS score construction is provided in Additional file 3.

Performance evaluation of the P-GNLS score

Upon admission, the PSS, SOFA, APACHE II, MEWS, and P-GNLS scores were all significantly higher in the MODS group than in the non-MODS group (all p < 0.001; Table 6). The P-GNLS score demonstrated better overall discriminative performance for MODS, with an AUC of 0.972 (95% CI: 0.956–0.988), outperforming PSS (0.853, 95% CI: 0.803–0.903), SOFA (0.823, 95% CI: 0.763–0.884), APACHE II (0.910, 95% CI: 0.870–0.949), and MEWS scores (0.804, 95% CI: 0.740–0.868) (all p < 0.05; Fig. 5; Table 7). Across clinically relevant threshold probabilities, the P-GNLS score provided greater net benefit than the comparator scores (Fig. 6).

Discussion

MODS is a severe complication of acute poisoning and is characterized by simultaneous or sequential dysfunction of at least two organ systems [23]. Given the high mortality rate associated with MODS and the absence of a widely used bedside score for its early prediction in acute poisoning, we developed the P-GNLS score based on pesticide poisoning, GCS, NEUT, Lac, and SCr as a practical tool for early risk stratification at emergency admission.

In this retrospective cohort study of 362 patients with acute poisoning, 20.99% developed MODS and experienced markedly higher mortality than those without MODS. Patients in the MODS group also had higher admission severity scores (PSS, SOFA, APACHE II, and MEWS) and more frequently required organ support interventions during hospitalization. In addition, mortality increased markedly with the number of dysfunctional organs, consistent with previous evidence [24], highlighting the importance of early recognition and risk stratification.

Although acute poisoning involves a broad spectrum of toxic agents, pesticide poisoning has consistently been associated with more severe clinical outcomes in many regions [25, 26]. In our cohort, 67.11% of MODS cases were attributed to pesticide exposure, a proportion significantly higher than that observed for other toxicant categories. Multivariable analysis further showed that pesticide poisoning was independently associated with MODS. This finding suggests that patients with pesticide poisoning may require closer early monitoring and prompt risk stratification in the emergency setting.

In multivariable analysis, in addition to pesticide poisoning, lower GCS score, higher NEUT count, elevated lactate, and increased SCr were independently associated with MODS following acute poisoning. These variables likely capture several key pathophysiological domains of early deterioration, including neurological dysfunction, systemic inflammation, tissue hypoperfusion, and renal injury. GCS is a widely used standardized measure of neurological status and may objectively quantify the level of consciousness impairment in acute poisoning [27, 28]. Neutrophils are major effector cells in the acute inflammatory response [29], and an increased admission NEUT count may indicate early inflammatory activation and immune dysregulation. Lactate is a well-established marker of impaired tissue perfusion and oxygen metabolism, and hyperlactatemia is not only a marker of shock and tissue hypoxia but is also strongly associated with the development of MODS [30]. Similarly, elevated SCr may reflect toxin-related renal injury through direct nephrotoxicity, hypoperfusion, or secondary inflammatory mechanisms [31, 32]. Acute kidney injury not only independently increases the mortality risk but also triggers water–electrolyte disturbances, acid–base imbalances, and potentially remote organ damage, creating a vicious circle that promotes MODS development [33, 34].

Based on the final model predictors, we used the simplified regression equation model proposed by Sullivan et al. [15] to develop the P-GNLS score. In this cohort, the P-GNLS score showed higher discriminative performance and greater net benefit than the comparator scores. Although the PSS is one of the few standardized methods for classifying poisoning severity, its bedside application may be limited by subjectivity and a relatively cumbersome categorization process [35]. Generic critical illness scores, such as SOFA, APACHE II, and MEWS, are also applied in this context; however, APACHE II requires 12 physiological parameters, along with age and chronic health evaluation, making data collection demanding and may limit its use in early assessment [8]. Although previous studies suggest that the SOFA score and MEWS are more practical in acute poisoning [9, 10], their performance for predicting MODS in acute poisoning may still be improved.

To facilitate clinical implementation, we further categorized the P-GNLS score into four risk strata with progressively increasing observed MODS risk, and the resulting four-category grouping retained good discriminative performance (AUROC = 0.964). This stratification may support ED triage and targeted monitoring of patients at higher risk.

Notably, Han et al. [36] developed a new Poisoning Mortality Score (new-PMS) based on a large national ED cohort in Korea, which showed strong performance for predicting in-hospital mortality. In contrast, the P-GNLS score was designed to predict MODS, an organ-based endpoint closely linked to clinical deterioration and resource use. It also uses fewer admission variables, which may improve bedside practicality.

Overall, the P-GNLS score provides a concise, admission-based approach for estimating MODS risk after acute poisoning; however, its transportability and clinical impact should be further evaluated in external cohorts and implementation studies.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design may have introduced selection bias and limits generalizability, particularly because the toxic-agent spectrum in our cohort may differ from that in other settings. Second, key determinants of poisoning severity, such as the exact exposure dose, formulation, and duration, were not fully captured in our dataset. Third, the model was derived from variables recorded at admission and did not incorporate dynamic monitoring or treatment-response data, which may further refine risk prediction. Fourth, although sensitivity analyses supported the robustness of the model, some overlap between predictors and the SOFA-based endpoint cannot be completely excluded. Finally, the model underwent internal bootstrap validation only and requires external validation before broader clinical application.

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective cohort, pesticide poisoning, GCS score, NEUT, Lac, and SCr were independently associated with subsequent MODS following acute poisoning. The P-GNLS score, developed from these factors, showed good internal discrimination and calibration and may provide a simple and practical tool for early risk stratification at ED admission. However, its generalizability and impact on clinical decision-making should be evaluated in external cohorts before routine use.
