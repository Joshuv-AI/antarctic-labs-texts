Complete revascularization with percutaneous coronary intervention for acute coronary syndromes presenting with multivessel disease
Authors: Rohin K. Reddy, Hannah Bernstein, James P. Howard, Yousif Ahmad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose of reviewComplete revascularization (CR) by percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in acute coronary syndromes with multivessel coronary artery disease (CAD) was previously contraindicated in the absence of cardiogenic shock or high-risk ischemia. Over the last decade, CR has been a focus of recent clinical investigation and practice evolution due to high-quality evidence supporting hard cardiovascular outcome benefit, contributing to a reversal in international guidelines. This review provides concise syntheses of contemporary and emerging randomized evidence underpinning current strategies and unresolved questions regarding patient selection, timing of CR and guidance modalities for the identification and treatment of nonculprit lesions.Recent findingsThe randomized evidence base supporting CR with PCI for acute coronary syndromes (ACS) and multivessel CAD has increased recently with large-scale trials comparing immediate versus staged CR and physiology-guided versus angiography-guided CR, including reports of longer-term comparative outcomes. Enough events have recently accrued to enable demonstration of all-cause mortality benefits with CR.SummaryContemporary randomized data increasingly support CR with PCI in haemodynamically stable patients. However, ACS type, timing of intervention and method of evaluation still necessitate individualized shared clinical decision-making, and further trials are required to validate the optimal PCI strategies by which to achieve CR in the correct populations.

INTRODUCTION

Complete revascularization (CR) by percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) in the setting of acute coronary syndromes (ACS) with multivessel coronary artery disease (CAD) was previously contraindicated in the absence of cardiogenic shock or high-risk ischemia. Over the last decade, CR has been the focus of renewed investigation and practice evolution due to high-quality evidence from multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) supporting hard cardiovascular outcome benefits, contributing to reversals in international guidelines. This review provides a concise synthesis of contemporary and emerging randomized evidence underpinning current strategies and unresolved questions regarding patient selection, timing of CR and guidance modalities for identification and treatment of nonculprit lesions.

THE EVOLUTION OF THE EVIDENCE BASE FOR STEMI

Guideline recommendations regarding CR for ST-elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) have largely reversed in the last decade. The adoption of CR into mainstream practice for STEMI offers a valuable case study on the importance of generating large-scale randomized evidence to inform clinical practice in areas of critical importance to global population health, such as ischaemic heart disease [1].

European guidelines in 2012 stated in a Class IIa, Level of Evidence (LoE) B recommendation that unless patients were in cardiogenic shock, had multiple critical (≥90% diameter) stenoses, angiographic signs of instability or continuous ischemia unresponsive to medical therapy, primary PCI in ACS with multivessel CAD should be limited to the culprit vessel [2]. The 2013 joint North American guidelines gave similar Class IIa, LoE B recommendations that CR was reasonable with intermediate- or high-risk findings on noninvasive testing, provided it was separate from the primary PCI [3].

The first major RCT was Preventive Angioplasty in Myocardial Infarction (PRAMI) [4], terminated early for benefit. In 465 participants with STEMI and multivessel CAD, compared with culprit-only revascularization (COR), CR reduced major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) by 65% over mean follow-up of 23 months [4]. The Third Danish Study of Optimal Acute Treatment of Patients with ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction–Primary PCI in Multivessel Disease (DANAMI-3–PRIMULTI) trial enrolled 627 participants, demonstrating fractional flow reserve (FFR)-guided CR reduced MACE by 54% [5]. In 2025, 10-year outcomes from DANAMI-3–PRIMULTI remained durable and incidence of the primary endpoint was 25% lower with FFR-guided CR, although this was driven by repeat revascularization [6▪]. In 2017, the Comparison Between FFR Guided Revascularization Versus Conventional Strategy in Acute STEMI Patients With MVD (CO6MPARE-ACUTE) [7] trial also showed that FFR-guided CR reduced MACE by 65% in 885 randomized patients. These trials all challenged conventional dogma that CR should be avoided in stable STEMI patients.

Also in 2017, data from the Culprit Lesion Only PCI versus Multivessel PCI in Cardiogenic Shock (CULPRIT SHOCK) trial [8] inverted common practice regarding CR in STEMI with cardiogenic shock [2]. In this multicentre RCT, 706 patients with multivessel CAD, MI and cardiogenic shock were randomized to CR or COR. At 30 days, risk of the composite primary endpoint of death or renal-replacement therapy was 17% lower in those assigned to COR, with a 16% reduction in death [8]. These results were consistent across all prespecified subgroups, including those that conceptually represent appropriate targets for immediate CR, such as occluded right coronary culprit lesions with high-grade proximal left anterior descending or other subtotal lesions with TIMI grade I or II flow [9].

The Complete Revascularization with Multivessel PCI for Myocardial Infarction (COMPLETE) trial [10] assigned 4041 participants to angiography-guided CR or COR in STEMI. At median three-year follow-up, both co-primary outcomes of cardiovascular death or MI and cardiovascular death, MI or ischemia-driven revascularization were reduced by 26% and 49%, respectively [10]. A recent systematic and network meta-analysis including 24 trials that randomized 16 371 patients examined CR strategies in ACS, and reported a 15% reduction in all-cause death with CR compared with COR in patients, alongside reductions in cardiovascular death, MACE and repeat revascularization [11▪▪]. This evolution and maturation of randomized evidence led to 2023 European ACS guidelines providing a Class I, LoE A recommendation for CR in hemodynamically stable patients with STEMI and multivessel CAD at index PCI or within 45 days [12]. In line with these, the recent 2025 joint North American guidelines also provide Class I, LoE A recommendations for CR in STEMI incorporating treatment of the significantly stenosed nonculprit arteries (≥70% for nonleft main disease and ≥50% for left main disease) to reduce death or MI and improve quality-of-life related to angina [13▪▪]. The 2023 European guidelines reversed previous recommendations for cardiogenic shock, providing a Class I, LoE B recommendation for COR at index PCI [12], and the 2025 North American guidelines provide a Class III, LoE B-R recommendation to avoid routine nonculprit revascularization in shock [13▪▪]. The Revascularization Strategy of Multivessel Disease for Patients with Acute Myocardial Infarction Complicated by Cardiogenic Shock Undergoing Veno-arterial Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenator (RESCUE-SHOCK; NCT05527717) trial is recruiting 560 participants with ACS and advanced shock requiring VA-ECMO to CR or COR, which will add further granularity to current recommendations for hemodynamically unstable ACS. However, open questions in haemodynamically stable ACS persist, which will form the focus of the rest of this review.

COMPLETE REVASCULARIZATION IN STEMI VERSUS NSTE-ACS

Despite the relevance and prognostic importance of multivessel CAD in NSTE-ACS, guideline recommendations are weaker for CR in NSTE-ACS compared with STEMI due to absence of dedicated RCTs. The 2023 European guidelines give Class IIa, LoE C recommendations for CR in stable NSTE-ACS with multivessel CAD [12], and the 2025 North American guidelines provide stronger Class I, LoE B-R recommendations for CR to reduce MACE in those not intended for bypass grafting or with left main stem stenoses [13▪▪].

A meta-analysis of 22 observational studies totalling 182 798 participants found in analyses restricted to propensity-score matched groups (n = 11 372) CR reduced the odds of death by 28%, MACE by 35% and MI by 60% through mean follow-up of 3 years [14]. These results are caveated by stemming from non-randomized comparisons and therefore should be viewed as hypothesis-generating only. Heterogeneity amongst constituent studies was high and in observational analyses there is a high possibility that operators select patients for emergent CR who are deemed more stable, better candidates for longer procedures or have less complex coronary anatomy. Seemingly improved outcomes with CR may therefore simply represent confounding by indication.

Recently the Functional Assessment in Elderly MI Patients with Multivessel Disease (FIRE) trial compared physiology-guided CR to COR for MACE at one year in 1445 patients ≥75 years of age [15]. CR reduced the MACE by 27% amongst 35.2% STEMI and 64.8% NSTE-ACS cases [15]. Importantly, in secondary analyses of FIRE, there was no evidence supporting differential treatment effects between ACS types (Pinteraction = 0.17) [16▪], providing more robust evidence that outcomes may be similar, albeit within the framework of a hypothesis-generating secondary analysis of an RCT [17].

Unlike in STEMI, a crucial challenge in NSTE-ACS is correct identification of culprit lesions. A large angiographic analysis reported 14% of NSTE-ACS cases had multiple culprit lesions, with the identification of a single culprit in <50% of patients [18]. Similarly, compared with patients presenting with STEMI, those with NSTE-ACS are older, more comorbid, and often have complex multivessel CAD that is more typically due to plaque erosion, rather than acute rupture [19–23]. Finally, NSTE-ACS patients are more likely to present with type two MI or have subendocardial ischemia [17]. Extrapolating benefits of CR demonstrated in STEMI without further evidence requires caution.

Establishing the exact role of CR in NSTE-ACS with multivessel CAD therefore remains a critical evidence gap. Ongoing randomized trials will inform future practice in this area [24,25▪▪]. The largest is Complete revascularization versus culprit-lesion only PCI in patients with NSTEMI (COMPLETE-NSTEMI), enrolling 3390 NSTE-ACS patients with multivessel CAD across ≥65 sites in Germany and Austria and testing a MACE primary endpoint [25▪▪]. All revascularization will be angiography-guided. This is the first sufficiently powered trial to test the impact of CR overall versus COR in NSTE-ACS patients. IMMEDIATE VERSUS STAGED COMPLETE REVASCULARIZATION

An early trial randomly assigned 263 participants with STEMI and multivessel CAD to COR, immediate CR, or staged CR. Both immediate CR and staged CR were superior to COR for MACE [26]. In COMPLETE, staged CR was performed at the discretion of operators, provided nonculprit PCI was performed within 45 days of the index culprit revascularization [10]. A substudy of COMPLETE further stratified analyses according to timing of nonculprit PCI, which was either during index hospitalization (median one day) or postdischarge (median 23 days), demonstrating that benefits of CR were consistent irrespective of the investigator-determined timing of nonculprit revascularization. Intuitively, immediate CR appears attractive due to the potential for lower risk of subsequent MI, reduced length of hospital stays and overall reduction in resource use. Questions remain surrounding efficacy and safety, related to increased procedural duration with concomitantly higher risks of hemodynamic instability and ischemia in multiple vascular beds, in addition to potential renal injury.

The Impact of Different Treatment in Multivessel Non ST Elevation Myocardial Infarction Patients (SMILE) trial was the first to directly compare an immediate versus staged CR strategy in ACS, enrolling 584 participants with NSTE-ACS to CR at index PCI, or later during the index hospitalization [27]. Those assigned to index PCI CR experienced 45% lower rates of MACE at one-year, although this was largely driven by 48% less target vessel revascularization. The Percutaneous Complete Revascularization Strategies Using Sirolimus Eluting Biodegradable Polymer Coated Stents in Patients Presenting With Acute Coronary Syndrome and Multivessel Disease (BIOVASC) trial was the first large randomized trial (n = 1525) to test the hypothesis that immediate CR at index PCI was noninferior to a staged approach within 6 weeks for all ACS (~40% STEMI) [28]. At 30 days, immediate CR was superior to staged CR and reduced the primary endpoint of MACE by 62% (Psuperiority = 0.0007). At one-year, superiority was not maintained (Psuperiority = 0.17), although the prespecified noninferiority margin was met (Pnon-inferiority = 0.001). Subsequently, Multivessel Immediate versus Staged Revascularization in Acute Myocardial Infarction (MULTISTARS AMI) randomized 840 participants with STEMI to immediate CR, or culprit PCI versus nonculprit revascularization within 19–45 days postprocedure [29]. Immediate CR was superior on MACE at one year, driven by nonfatal MI and unplanned revascularizations [29].

A recent network meta-analysis facilitated inclusion of 11 361 participants in 20 RCTs comparing immediate CR, staged CR and COR. Overall, when combining direct and indirect data, immediate CR ranked higher for mortality, MI and MACE [11▪▪]. To conclusively provide evidence of superiority, large randomized strategy trials powered for cardiovascular death or spontaneous MI comparing optimal timing are required so that ascertainment bias in periprocedural MI evaluation with immediate CR is minimized. In clinical practice, decisions must balance logistical considerations, renal function, patient comorbidities, and institutional expertise. Two ongoing parallel trials are Optimal Timing of Fractional Flow Reserve-Guided Complete Revascularization for Non-Infarct-Related Artery in ST-Segment Elevation Myocardial Infarction with Multivessel Disease (OPTION-STEMI) reporting in 2025 [30▪], and OPTION-NSTEMI (NCT04968808), which will compare FFR-guided immediate CR with staged CR during the same admission.

PHYSIOLOGY VERSUS ANGIOGRAPHY

Application of FFR during ACS is subject to debate, with some studies suggesting that hyperemia in the distal vasculature is impaired during states of infarction, secondary to coronary microvascular dysfunction [31,32]. More contemporary data suggest FFR may still be useful in ACS [33–35], although outcome data are conflicting.

The aforementioned DANAMI-3–PRIMULTI [5] and COMPARE-ACUTE [7] trials were foundational trials in CR, demonstrating superiority of an FFR-guided strategy versus COR on MACE endpoints, driven by revascularization, whilst COMPLETE showed superiority of angiography-guided CR on the harder composite of cardiovascular death or MI [10]. It was unclear whether there was a relative benefit of one guidance modality over another, or whether the benefit was all driven by complete revascularization per se. The Flow Evaluation to Guide Revascularization in Multivessel ST-Elevation Myocardial Infarction (FLOWER-MI) trial was the first to directly compare FFR-guided CR with angiography-guided CR, enrolling 1171 patients with STEMI and multivessel CAD [29]. CR was encouraged at index PCI, but could also be performed 5 days after the primary procedure, prior to discharge. Only 4% of patients had CR at index PCI, suggesting physiology measurements are challenging in emergent settings. Due to a lower than expected event rate, statistical power for the primary endpoint was diminished and MACE at one year was similar between FFR-guided CR and angiography-guided CR [29]. Recently, three-year data demonstrated similar results [36].

The Fractional Flow Reserve versus Angiography-Guided Strategy for Management of Non-Infarction Related Artery Stenosis in Patients With Acute Myocardial Infarction (FRAME-AMI) trial randomized 562 patients with ACS (47.2% STEMI) to FFR-guided versus angiography-guided CR [37]. At median follow-up of 3.5 years, FFR-guided PCI reduced MACE by 57%, mostly driven by reductions in death and MI. A potential reason for these divergent results include the FRAME-AMI trial being terminated early, with attendant risks of an overestimated treatment effect.

While FLOWER-MI and FRAME-AMI were underpowered for death and MI, FFR-Guidance for Complete Nonculprit Revascularization (FULL REVASC) was designed to overcome this limitation, aiming to randomly assign 4052 STEMI or high-risk NSTE-ACS participants to FFR-guided CR at index PCI or COR [38▪]. Unfortunately, following publication of COMPLETE in 2019 [10], enrolment was halted owing to feasibility and ethical concerns, and the steering committee extended follow-up in order to aid event accrual and preserve power. At median 4.8 years, MACE was similar between FFR-guided PCI and COR. Failure to include enough patients with high-grade nonculprit lesions and three-vessel CAD who may derive greater benefit from CR [39] may be an explanation for the neutral long-term results of this trial, but lack of utility of FFR-guidance is ultimately concordant with FLOWER-MI [29] and FRAME-AMI [37].

In summary, despite multiple randomized trials, whether FFR-guided CR in ACS is superior to angiography-guidance remains unclear. This may be due to outcomes being related to plaque morphology rather than the hemodynamic significance of an epicardial stenosis: in ACS around 50% are lipid-rich thin-capped fibroatheromas with a high propensity for plaque rupture due to their greater underlying inflammatory milieu [40,41]. If FFR encourages deferral of lesions with high-risk characteristics, it follows that FFR could be associated with worse outcomes. The Physiology-guided versus Angiography-guided Nonculprit Lesion Complete Revascularization Strategies (COMPLETE-2) trial is comparing physiology-guidance with angiography-guided CR in an international trial of over 5100 patients with ACS followed for median of 3 years (NCT05701358). COMPLETE-2 will hierarchically test whether a physiology-guided strategy is first noninferior to angiography-guidance for efficacy and superior for safety. If noninferiority for efficacy is established, then superiority will subsequently be evaluated.

CONCLUSION

Over the past decade, randomized trials have firmly established the benefit of CR in STEMI to reduce MACE. Conversely, CR in cardiogenic shock appears harmful on current evidence. In NSTE-ACS alone, evidence supporting CR remains largely limited to observational studies, though data from trials including mixed ACS populations suggest benefit. The optimal timing of CR remains unknown, highlighting the need for further large-scale trials to definitively answer this question. Furthermore, the value of physiology-guided CR versus angiographic guidance also remains unresolved, and this may relate to plaque biology having more of an impact on clinical outcomes than the hemodynamic significance of nonculprit stenoses.

In conclusion, CR improves outcomes in STEMI with multivessel CAD, and evolving evidence is refining its role in NSTE-ACS. Optimal timing and guidance strategy remain open questions. Ongoing trials such as COMPLETE-NSTEMI and COMPLETE-2 are poised to clarify these uncertainties and guide evidence-based management in this high-risk population.
