Hyaluronic Acid-Modified Redox-Responsive Gambogic Acid Prodrug Micelles for Targeted Therapy of Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer
Authors: Zhongcheng Ke, Tao Luo, Xiaoling Cheng, Xuwen Chai, Zitong Zhu, Zhusheng Huang, Zhen Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeGambogic acid (GA) shows potent inhibitory activity against various malignancies. However, achieving precise targeted delivery and spatiotemporal control of drug release at tumor sites remains a significant challenge. This study aimed to develop a hyaluronic acid (HA)-modified, redox-responsive drug delivery system based on a methoxy polyethylene glycol-linked GA prodrug (mPEG-ss-GA).MethodsThe amphiphilic prodrug, mPEG-ss-GA, was synthesized by conjugating GA to methoxy polyethylene glycol (mPEG) via a disulfide linkage. Subsequently, it spontaneously self-assembled with HA in an aqueous medium to form HA-modified mPEG-ss-GA micelles (HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M). The physicochemical properties of the micelles, including particle size, zeta potential, morphology, and in vitro release profiles, were systematically characterized. Cellular uptake in human non-small cell lung cancer cells (A549) was visualized using fluorescence microscopy. Furthermore, the in vitro pro-apoptotic effects were quantified using an Annexin V–propidium iodide (PI) binding assay. Finally, the in vivo anti-tumor efficacy was evaluated in a subcutaneous xenograft tumor model.ResultsThe HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M micelles exhibited an average particle size of 251 nm with a zeta potential of −17.36 mV. The system demonstrated glutathione (GSH)-triggered drug release, with release kinetics that aligned well with the Higuchi model. In vitro studies revealed that HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M significantly enhanced cellular uptake, induced apoptosis, and suppressed cell migration. In vivo experiments showed that HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M achieved a tumor volume inhibition rate of 54.09%, compared to 29.56% for free GA.ConclusionHA/mPEG-ss-GA-M is a promising drug delivery system for targeted non-small cell lung cancer therapy and offers precise and efficient treatment options.

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the most common malignancies worldwide and remains the leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality. Its incidence and mortality continue to increase globally, with nearly 2.5 million new cases and more than 1.8 million deaths reported in 2022.1 According to the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), the 5-year overall survival rate in patients with lung cancer is only 17.7%. Pathologically, lung cancer is broadly classified as small cell (SCLC) or non-small cell (NSCLC) subtypes, with NSCLC accounting for about 85% of cases.2

Gambogic acid (GA) is a bioactive natural compound with potent antitumor activity against a range of malignancies, including lung cancer,3,4 colorectal cancer,5,6 and osteosarcoma.7,8 Nevertheless, its clinical translation is substantially hindered by low aqueous solubility and poor oral bioavailability. To address these challenges, various injectable formulations, such as micelles, liposomes, and hydrogels have been developed to enhance GA solubility, prolong systemic circulation, and facilitate tumor accumulation via the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect.9–11 However, relying exclusively on the EPR effect for passive accumulation is often insufficient due to high interstitial fluid pressure and dense stromal barriers, which impede deep nanoparticle penetration. Meanwhile, these delivery systems are often limited by low drug loading, limited stability, and aggregation during storage due to weak noncovalent interactions between GA and excipients.

Nanoprodrugs, formed by covalently conjugating drug molecules to polymers, offer a promising alternative. Owing to their intrinsic amphiphilicity, they self-assemble into nanomicelles with high drug loading and stability.12,13 Typically, these systems utilize acid-labile or redox-sensitive linkages (eg, ester or disulfide bonds) to trigger drug release.14,15 Although previous studies have reported on acid-responsive GA nanoprodrugs,16,17 the mildly acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) often leads to suboptimal drug release. Furthermore, while HA-modified nanoparticles successfully achieve active targeting, they typically lack a stimuli-responsive release behavior, resulting in delayed or incomplete drug release.

To overcome these limitations, the TME provides endogenous stimuli such as glutathione (GSH), which is overexpressed in tumor tissues. GSH-responsive disulfide bonds can be cleaved to selectively activate nanoprodrugs,18,19 offering a synergistic effect with the acidic TME.20 In parallel with the active mediation of cellular uptake by CD44 receptors, the incorporation of hyaluronic acid (HA) can significantly enhance internalization efficiency.21,22 In the context of NSCLC, CD44 is significantly upregulated in tumor tissues compared to normal lung parenchyma,23,24 correlating with advanced stage and poor prognosis. Notably, the A549 cell line inherently expresses high levels of CD44, rendering it a highly suitable model for evaluating the targeting efficiency of CD44-directed nanomedicines.

In this study, a redox-responsive disulfide-bridged polymeric prodrug of GA was constructed, enabling the formation of self-assembled nanomicelles with favorable physicochemical characteristics and surface functionalization via HA. This system exhibited CD44 receptor-associated cellular uptake and GSH-triggered drug release, demonstrating pronounced antitumor activity against A549 cells. Specifically, it effectively blocked cell proliferation and migration, activated apoptosis in vitro, and inhibited tumor progression in vivo, highlighting its therapeutic potential.

Materials and Methods

Materials

GA (purity > 99.5%) was obtained from Jingzhu Tech (Nanjing, China); N,N-Diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA), methoxypolyethylene glycol amine (mPEG-NH2, MW 2000Da), 2-(7-azobenzotriazole)-N, N, N′, N′-tetramethylurea hexafluorophosphate (HATU) and cysteamine were acquired from McLean BioTech (Shanghai, China); Succinimidyl 3-(2-pyridyldisulfide)-propionate (SPDP), glutathione (GSH) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China); Annexin V-FITC/PI cell apoptosis detection kit was provided by Beyotime (Shanghai, China); 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) was bought from Solarbio (Shanghai, China), Coumarin-6 (C6) were purchased from Aladdin (Liguria, Italy).

Synthesis of the GA Polymeric Prodrug

The synthesize of GA-cysteamine amide (GA-SH) was performed following a previously established protocol,25,26 with minor modifications (Figure 1). GA (0.1 mmol), HATU (0.12 mmol), and DIPEA (0.2 mmol) were co-dissolved in anhydrous DMF (50 mL) and stirred at room temperature for 2 h to activate the carboxyl group. Subsequently, cysteamine (0.1 mmol) was added to the mixture. The reaction was stirred at 25°C in the dark for 24 h under a nitrogen atmosphere. Upon completion, the reaction mixture was diluted with water and extracted with dichloromethane. The organic layer was washed sequentially with 1 M HCl and saturated aqueous NaHCO3, dried over anhydrous MgSO4, and concentrated under reduced pressure. The crude residue was purified by silica gel column chromatography to afford the target product GA-SH as a pale yellow solid. Synthesis of mPEG-ss-GA.

SPDP (0.1 mmol), mPEG-NH2 (0.1 mmol), and DIPEA (0.1 mmol) were dissolved in 50 mL of anhydrous DMF and stirred at 25°C for 2 h. Subsequently, glycine (1.0 mmol) was added to quench unreacted active esters. The reaction mixture was dialyzed (MWCO 1000) against deionized water to remove small-molecule impurities and solvent, followed by lyophilization to afford mPEG-SPDP as a white solid.

mPEG-SPDP (0.1 mmol) and GA-SH (0.12 mmol) were dissolved in 50 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 7.4) under a nitrogen atmosphere. The mixture was stirred at room temperature for 4 h in the dark to facilitate the thiol-disulfide exchange reaction. The resulting solution was purified by dialysis (MWCO 1000) against deionized water to remove the unreacted reagents. Finally, the solution was lyophilized using a LGJ-10 vacuum freeze drying machine (Songyuan, China) to afford the mPEG-ss-GA conjugate as a solid.

HPLC Analysis of GA Polymeric Prodrug

Quantitative GA, mPEG-GA prodrug was dissolved in methanol and subsequently passed through a 0.45-μm nylon syringe filter. High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) was performed on an LC-16 system (Shimadzu, Japan) using an Agilent TC-C18 column (250 × 4.6 mm, 5 μm) at 35 °C. The mobile phase consisted of methanol and 0.1% aqueous acetic acid (92:8, v/v), delivered at 1.0 mL/min. A 10 μL sample was injected, and detection was carried out at 360 nm.

FT-IR Analysis of GA Polymeric Prodrug

A small amount of each sample (GA, PEG-NH2, and mPEG-ss-GA) was mixed with dry potassium bromide and homogenized in an agate mortar. The mixture was then compressed into transparent sheets using a mold. Infrared spectra were acquired with a Nicolet 380 infrared spectrometer (Nicolet, USA), and characteristic peaks were analyzed accordingly.

Hydrogen Spectrum Analysis of GA Polymeric Prodrug

Appropriate amounts of GA, PEG-NH2, and mPEG-ss-GA solid samples were dissolved in appropriate amounts of deuterated reagent, and the hydrogen spectra of each sample were measured using a 500 MHz DRX nuclear magnetic resonance instrument (Bruker, USA) to analyze chemical shifts, peak shapes, etc.

Determination of GA Loading in Polymeric Prodrug

The GA loading in mPEG-ss-GA was determined using UV spectrophotometry. To this end, 2.7 mg of mPEG-ss-GA was dissolved in 10 mL methanol to yield a sample solution containing mPEG-ss-GA at 270 μg/mL. The solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm nylon syringe filter, and analyzed at 360 nm using a UV-1901 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Lengguang, China), the GA loading in mPEG-ss-GA was subsequently calculated.

Preparation of Hyaluronic Acid-Modified GA Prodrug Micelles

GA-M was successfully prepared via the thin film hydration method.27,28 Briefly, mPEG-ss-GA (20 mg) and HA (4 mg) were dissolved in 50 mL of absolute ethanol. The solvent was removed under reduced pressure using an RV 10 rotary flash evaporator (IKA, Germany) to form a thin film. The film was subsequently hydrated with 40 mL of distilled water at 45°C and agitated at 40 rpm for 30 min to obtain a homogeneous dispersion. Finally, the dispersion was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane, yielding 38.2 mL of HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M micelles with a filtration recovery rate of 95.4%.

Characterization of Hyaluronic Acid-Modified GA Prodrug Micelles

Particle Size, Zeta Potential, and Morphology

Hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of PEG-NH2, mPEG-ss-GA-M were analyzed using dynamic light scattering (Nano ZS, Malvern Panalytical, UK) after filtration of undiluted samples through a 0.45 μm membrane.

For morphological observation, mPEG-ss-GA-M was diluted in deionized water to a final GA concentration of 50 μg/mL. A small aliquot of the diluted sample was deposited onto a carbon-coated copper grid, air-dried, negatively stained, and subsequently examined using an H-7650 transmission electron microscope (TEM, Hitachi, Japan).

In vitro Drug Release Profiles

To study the GSH sensitivity of the HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M, in vitro drug release properties were measured by dialysis method.29,30 The in vitro release was conducted in PBS (containing 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfonate) under four different condition: pH 5.0, pH 7.4, pH 5.0 with 10 mM GSH, and pH 7.4 with 10 mM GSH, respectively. Briefly, HA/mPEG-ss-GA (equivalent to 5.0 mg of GA) was dispersed in 10 mL of the corresponding release medium, sealed in a dialysis bag (MWCO: 1000 Da), and the bag was then immersed in 100 mL of the same medium and incubated at 37°C in a thermostatically controlled shaking water bath. At scheduled time points, 2.0 mL of the external medium was withdrawn and immediately replaced with an equal volume of fresh pre-warmed release medium. The GA concentration in the collected samples was quantified using the UV spectrophotometric method described above. Drug release data were analyzed using zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, and Korsmeyer–Peppas models to probe the release mechanism.

In vitro Evaluation of Hyaluronic Acid-Modified GA Micelles

In vitro Cytotoxicity

An MTT assay was conducted to evaluate the cytotoxicity of free GA and HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M on A549 cells.31,32 Briefly, A549 cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 5 × 103 cells/well and incubated overnight. The cells were assigned to three groups: control, GA treated, and HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M treated. The control group received 100 μL of complete culture medium, while the treatment groups were exposed to 100 μL of GA or HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M solutions at varying concentrations. After 24 h of incubation, the culture media were aspirated and replaced with 100 μL of fresh medium containing MTT (0.5 mg/mL). After a further 4 h of incubation, the intracellular formazan crystals formed were solubilized with 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), and the absorbance at 570 nm was recorded following 10 min of gentle agitation. Cell viability (CV, %) was determined as the ratio of the sample absorbance to that of the untreated control. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) was defined as the GA concentration required to reduce cell proliferation by 50% relative to the control.

Cell Apoptosis Assay

The effect of GA, HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M to induce apoptosis on A549 cells were assessed via an annexin V–propidium iodide (PI) binding assay.33,34 A549 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 2×105 cells/well and treated for 24 h. The culture medium was then replaced with 2 mL of fresh medium supplemented with either GA or HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M (equivalent to 3.0 μM GA). Subsequently, cells were harvested and stained with Annexin V-FITC and PI for 30 min in the dark. Flow cytometry analysis was performed by collecting 10,000 events using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA), and the gating strategy involved selecting the viable cell population based on forward scatter, side scatter to exclude debris and cell aggregates.

In vitro Migration Assay

A wound healing test was performed to assess the impact of GA and HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M on the migratory ability of A549 cells.35,36 Briefly, A549 cells were seeded at a density of 2×105 cells/well and cultured until a confluent monolayer was formed. A straight-line scratch was gently introduced into the monolayer using a sterile tip. After washing thoroughly with PBS, the cells were incubated with either GA or HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M (both at a GA-equivalent concentration of 3.0 μM). Images of the wound region were recorded at 0 h, 24 h post-scratch using an IX81 inverted light microscope (Olympus, Japan). Cell migration capacity was assessed by measuring the distance between the two edges of wound at 24 h.

In vitro Cellular Uptake Assay

C6 was used as a fluorescent probe to evaluate cellular internalization via confocal laser scanning microscopy.37,38 C6-loaded HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M (C6/HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M) was prepared using the same protocol as for HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M. A cellular uptake study was conducted on A549 cells to investigate the effect of HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M. Briefly, the cells were then divided into free GA, C6/HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M groups, and incubated with C6, C6/HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M (1 µM of C6 concentration) at 37°C for 4 h. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS, stained with DAPI for 45 min and then observed using a LSM800 laser confocal microscopy (Zeiss, German), where green fluorescence (from C6) indicated drug localization, and blue fluorescence (from DAPI) marked the cell nuclei, enabling a comparative evaluation of cellular uptake efficiency between free C6 and C6/HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M.

In vivo Studies Using Mouse Model

Male nude mice (22 ± 2 g) were obtained from Cavens Lab Animal Co., Ltd. (Changzhou, China, SCXK2011-0003). The animals were maintained in a controlled environment with a temperature of 25 ± 0.5°C, relative humidity of 45 ± 5%, and a 12-h light/12-h dark cycle. All animal studies were carried out in accordance with the protocols (No 250929001) issued by the Animal Care and Use Committee of Huangshan University.

When tumor volumes reached approximately 50 mm3, A549 tumor-bearing mice were randomized into four groups and received intravenous injections every 3 days for a total of five doses as follows: saline (control), cisplatin (4 mg/kg), free GA or HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M administered at an equivalent GA dose of 10 mg/kg. Body weight and tumor dimensions (length and width) were recorded every 3 days. Tumor volume (TV) was calculated as follows: TV = length × width2/2.

After the final dose, mice were euthanized via cervical dislocation and tumors were harvested, weighed, and imaged. Relative tumor volume (RTV) was calculated as: RTV=Vt/V0, where Vt represents the tumor volume at a given time point, such as V0 denotes the tumor size on day 0.

Furthermore, tumor volume inhibition (TVI), tumor weight inhibition (TWI) were calculated as follows: TVI=1-Vdrug(15d)/Vcontrol(15d), TWI=1-Wdrug(15d)/Wcontrol(15d), where Vdrug(15d), Wdrug(15d) represents the mean tumor volume and weight, respectively, in the drug-treated group on day 15, such as Vcontrol (15d), Wcontrol (15d) denotes the tumor volume, weight of saline group at 15th day.

Data Statistics

All experiments were performed in triplicates. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS 23.0 software. Intergroup variations were assessed through one-way analysis of variance, and statistical significance was defined as p<0.05.

In contrast, the chromatogram of the mPEG-ss-GA exhibited a peak at 4.1 min, while no peak was observed at 7.1 min, indicating complete consumption of GA in the reaction. The shorter retention time suggests that mPEG-ss-GA has a higher polarity than GA, which can be attributed to the conjugation of water-soluble polyethylene glycol group. Additionally, the broader peak shape of mPEG-ss-GA is consistent with the polydisperse nature typical of polymeric compounds. FT-IR

Infrared spectroscopy is commonly employed to characterize functional groups and chemical bonds present in each sample. As shown in Figure 2B, the spectrum of GA exhibited two characteristic peaks: a carbonyl stretching vibration of the carboxyl group at 1730 cm−1 and a hydroxyl stretching vibration at 3431 cm−1. In contrast, PEG-NH2 displayed a dominant N-H stretching vibration at 3408 cm−1. In the spectrum of the mPEG-ss-GA, no distinct amino peak was observed. Notably, the carbonyl stretching vibration shifted significantly from 1730 cm−1 (in GA) to 1630 cm−1, which was attributed to the amide C=O stretching. Additionally, a weak absorption peak observed at 555 cm−1 corresponded to the disulfide bond (-s-s-) stretching vibration. These spectral changes inferred the formation of amide bonds via the reaction between the carboxylic acid of GA and the amino groups of cysteamine, and verify the presence of disulfide linkages in the mPEG-ss-GA.

1H-NMR

The 1H NMR was employed to assess the structure of mPEG-ss-GA (400 MHz, DMSO). The 1H NMR analysis of GA displayed characteristic signals at 12.99 (s, 1H), 8.58 (s, 1H), 6.55–7.39 (m, 2H), 5.89 (m, 2H), 5.01–5.61 (m, 2H), 2.50–3.51 (m, 12H), 1.18–1.61 (s, 24H). For mPEG-NH2, the spectrum showed a dominant singlet at δ 3.50 ppm corresponding to the ethylene glycol units and a multiplet at δ 2.29 ppm assigned to the methylene protons adjacent to the terminal amino group. In the spectrum of the mPEG-ss-GA conjugate, the characteristic signals of the GA skeleton, including the aromatic protons (δ 6.55–7.39 ppm) and olefinic protons (δ 5.89 ppm and 5.01–5.61 ppm), were clearly observed, confirming the presence of GA. Meanwhile, the resonance signal of the PEG backbone remained at δ 3.50 ppm. Notably, the signal at δ 2.29 ppm (attributed to -CH2-NH2) attenuated in the conjugate spectrum, indicating the consumption of the amino group. Furthermore, the methylene protons of the polyethylene glycol chain in mPEG-ss-GA appeared slightly broadened compared to the mPEG-NH2, attributed to the increased molecular weight and restricted molecular motion upon conjugation. These results collectively confirm the successful synthesis of the mPEG-ss-GA conjugate.

GA Loading

The standard curve equation was established by linear regression over a concentration range of 5.75 μ g/mL to 46 μ g/mL, with GA concentration (C) on the x-axis and absorbance (A) on the y-axis. The resulting equation was A=0.0244 C+0.0141 (r2=0.9991). Based on this calibration, the GA content in 2.7 mg of the mPEG-ss-GA-M was 0.211 mg. The GA loading in GA-PEG was calculated to be 178.1 mg/g, demonstrating the promising loading efficiency of this nano-prodrug system for GA.

Particle Size, Zeta Potential, Morphology

As illustrated in Figure 2C, particles in the PEG-NH2 solution had a hydrodynamic diameter of approximately 18 nm and a positive zeta potential of about +11 mV, whereas those in HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M had a size of about 251 nm and a negative zeta potential of approximately −17.36 mV. The marked differences in both zeta potential and particle size indicate the successful connection of PEG-NH2 with GA. Due to the integration of GA, the particle charge undergoes a reversal from positive to negative, and remains above 10 mV, which confers strong electrostatic repulsion, thereby inhibiting particle aggregation and enhancing system stability.

As shown in Figure 2D, the nanoparticles exhibited a spherical shape with a relatively uniform size distribution. The observed particle diameter was approximately 200–300 nm, consistent with the hydrodynamic diameter measured by dynamic light scattering. These results confirm the successful self-assembly of the mPEG-ss-GA with HA into stable nanomicelles.

In vitro Release Profile

The redox-responsive drug release profile of HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M was assessed using a dialysis-based approach. As illustrated in Figure 3, under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), only a negligible amount of GA (8.20%) was released from HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M over 48 h, indicating its high stability. In contrast, in GSH-containing medium (10 mM GSH), the cumulative release of GA reached 36.67% at 12 h and up to 60.47% by 48 h, confirming the GSH -triggered drug release capability of the system. Cellular Uptake

Cellular uptake of C6 and C6/HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M in A549 cells was assessed, with representative images shown in Figure 4B. Intracellular C6 accumulation appears as green fluorescence, while nuclei were counterstained blue with DAPI. After 4 h of incubation, only faint cytoplasmic green fluorescence was observed in A549 cells incubated with free C6. In contrast, those exposed to C6/HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M exhibited a markedly stronger green fluorescence signal. These findings indicate that HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M significantly enhances cellular internalization of C6, effectively overcoming the limited passive targeting efficiency of EPR effect caused by high interstitial fluid pressure and a dense matrix barrier.41,42 Although blocking assays have not been conducted in this study, the enhanced uptake is attributed to the specific interaction between HA and CD44-associated receptors, which has been well-documented in previous literature. Future work will focus on competitive inhibition experiments to further validate this specific uptake pathway.

Pro-Apoptotic Effect of GA on Cells Apoptosis

During apoptosis, cell nuclei undergo characteristic morphological changes, including progressive chromatin condensation, nuclear fragmentation, and eventual degradation. To assess the pro-apoptotic effect of HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M, an Annexin V/PI double-staining assay was performed on A549 cells. As demonstrated in Figure 4C, the apoptosis rate in the HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M treated group (16.5%) was markedly higher than that in the free GA group (10.6%, p<0.05). These results indicate that HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M exerts a stronger pro-apoptotic effect on A549 cells than free GA, suggesting an enhanced capacity to induce apoptosis, potentially through interference with the cell cycle.43,44

Inhibitory Effect of GA on Cells Migration

The inhibitory effects of GA and HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M on A549 cells migration were studied using a scratch wound healing assay. As illustrated in Figure 4D, the blank group exhibited 43.3% wound closure after 24 hours, whereas both GA and HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M significantly suppressed cell migration. Specifically, the wound closure rate in the HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M treatment group was markedly reduced to 12.2%, compared with 20.6% in the free GA group, demonstrating that HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M exhibits a superior anti-migratory effect.

In vivo Anti-Tumor Efficacy

A subcutaneous xenograft mouse model was employed to assess the in vivo antitumor efficacy of HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M. As illustrated in Figure 4E and F, the RTV in the free GA-treated group remained consistently smaller than that in the saline-treated group throughout the treatment period. Notably, the HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M group exhibited greater tumor growth inhibition than the free GA group and showed antitumor activity comparable to that of the cisplatin-treated group, with no significant difference between them. On the day 15, the TVI rates were 29.56% for free GA and 54.09% for HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M, while the corresponding TWI rates were 26.83% and 52.44%, respectively. These findings demonstrate that HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M exerts a significantly enhanced antiproliferative effect against A549 tumors in vivo (p<0.05).

The superior therapeutic performance of HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M is primarily attributed to its ability to efficiently accumulate in target tumor tissue through the EPR effect45,46 and HA-associated active targeting, which promotes effective cellular uptake and subsequent redox-triggered rapid, complete drug release.

Despite these promising results, it should be noted that this in vivo study is exploratory with a relatively short observation period, which limits the definitive conclusions regarding long-term therapeutic benefits. Future studies with extended monitoring are needed to fully evaluate the sustained efficacy and safety.

Prodrugs linked by disulfide bonds exhibit good stability in systemic circulation, effectively preventing premature drug leakage and minimizing off-target toxicity. Complementing this stability, the hydrophilic mPEG shell provides a steric barrier that prolongs blood circulation time, evading rapid clearance by the mononuclear phagocyte system. Additionally, the strong binding affinity of HA for CD44 receptors on NSCLC cells significantly enhances cellular uptake efficiency. Collectively, these features ensure that GA is predominantly released via GSH-mediated cleavage specifically within the tumor microenvironment.

Despite the promising anti-tumor efficacy demonstrated, several challenges remain. Although the functionalized polymer achieves precise stimulus-responsive behavior, the multi-step synthesis route is complex, which may hinder large-scale reproducibility and requires further process optimization. Moreover, while the current design addresses stability and uptake, further research is needed to investigate its potential effect on P-glycoprotein (P-gp) efflux inhibition, which could be crucial for overcoming multidrug resistance in NSCLC.

Conclusion

A redox-responsive polymeric prodrug of GA is developed and shows to spontaneously self-assemble with HA into stable nanomicelles (HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M) in aqueous media. The HA/mPEG-ss-GA-M demonstrates robust antitumor efficacy. The distinct advantage of this system lies in its dual capacity for active tumor targeting and GSH-triggered intracellular drug release, thereby maximizing therapeutic outcomes while minimizing systemic toxicity. Despite these promising preclinical results, large-scale manufacturing consistency, as well as the long-term efficacy and safety of this strategy in overcoming multidrug resistance warrant further investigation. Furthermore, additional pharmacokinetic or biodistribution studies are needed to validate the proposed mechanism of tumor accumulation.

Overall, this work provides a compelling paradigm for intelligent delivery systems for hydrophobic anticancer agents in NSCLC therapy.
