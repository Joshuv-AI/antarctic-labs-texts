A novel humanized mouse model exhibits neurobehavioral impairments and recapitulates key neuropathological features of infantile Tay-Sachs disease
Authors: Lujaina Elbakr, Georgiana Forguson, Hong Anh Truong, Joshua E. Hung, Wing Suen Chan, Dong-Kyu Kim, Reid A. Brewer, Sydney Steiman, Toan Q. Nguyen, Ajoy Vincent, Evgueni A. Ivakine
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Tay-Sachs disease (TSD) is a fatal lysosomal storage disorder caused by mutations in the HEXA gene which impair B-hexosaminidase A activity and result in the toxic accumulation of GM2 gangliosides. Here, we report the generation of a novel mouse model that harbors a partially humanized Hexa gene carrying c.1278insTATC, the most prevalent TSD-causing variant, and a Neu3 deficiency to circumvent a murine bypass pathway. Upon characterization, this model reflects key pathological features of TSD including significant GM2 deposition in the central nervous system (CNS), prominent astrogliosis, neuroinflammation, and exhibit progressive neurobehavioral impairments. Retinal characterization revealed widespread GM2 accumulation leading to structural changes detectable by optical tomography coherence and fundus imaging, highlighting the significance of retinal involvement in TSD. Taken together, these findings establish this model as a valuable tool for elucidating TSD pathophysiology and provides a platform for evaluating targeted therapeutic strategies in a genetically accurate and clinically relevant context.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03783-6.

Introduction

Tay-Sachs Disease (TSD) is a severe, progressive neurodegenerative disorder caused by biallelic pathogenic variants in HEXA, resulting in the deficiency of the lysosomal enzyme known as β-N-acetylhexosaminidase A (HexA). It functions as a heterodimer composed of an α-subunit that is encoded by HEXA and a β-subunit encoded by HEXB [1]. HexA plays a critical role in maintaining cellular homeostasis as the only isozyme capable of catalyzing the hydrolysis of the terminal N-acetyl galactosamine residue from GM2 gangliosides, thereby converting GM2 to GM3 [2]. HexA enzyme deficiency halts a sequential glycosphingolipid degradation pathway, which ultimately leads to a toxic accumulation of GM2 gangliosides in affected cells [1].

Gangliosides are complex sialic acids containing glycosphingolipids that are ubiquitously present in various cell types with high enrichment in the central nervous system (CNS), where they localize to the plasma membrane [3]. Ganglioside homeostasis in the brain is critical for various processes that underlie neuronal development and functions such as myelination, synaptogenesis and cell-signalling modulation, all of which when disrupted, can lead to degeneration and severe pathology [3]. In TSD, pathological GM2 accumulation damages neurons and drives progressive neurodegeneration [4]. Infantile TSD typically manifests between 3 and 6 months of age and is characterized by a progressive neurological decline, including hypotonia, dysphagia, seizures, and a gradual reduction of motor cerebral functions, ultimately leading to early mortality [4, 5]. With a disease incidence of about 1 in 100,000 live births and a poor prognosis, TSD currently has no curative options, and treatment is largely confined to symptom management, highlighting an urgent need for in vivo models to support the development of targeted therapies [5, 6].

To develop an infantile TSD mouse model, we generated a mouse model harbouring the most common TSD-causing variant (c.1278insTATC), a 4-base pair (bp) insertion found in exon 11 of Hexa. The c.1278insTATC variant is particularly enriched in Louisiana Cajun and Ashkenazi Jewish communities with carrier frequencies of approximately 1 in 27 compared to ~ 1 in 300 in the general population [7–9]. Due to the divergence between mouse and human sequences at the Hexa locus, we first performed partial humanization of the region where the sequence transitioned from GTA AAG to ATA TCC. Mice with this partial humanized Hexa portion serve as our baseline control and are referred to as the VK-IS colony (Fig. 1). The 4 bp duplication causes a frameshift that introduces a premature stop codon, resulting in mRNA targeted for degradation and ultimately resulting in the absence of a functional protein required for GM2 catabolism [10, 11]. Previous studies have revealed that the disruption of Hexa in murine models does not fully recapitulate the severe neurodegenerative phenotype typically observed in patients with TSD [12, 13]. Rather, mice with a disrupted Hexa locus display a relatively mild phenotype as they exhibit normal motor coordination, exploratory behaviour, and only minimal GM2 deposition limited to the posterior horn of the spinal cord until at least one year of age [12]. Recent studies have revealed a metabolic bypass that allows HexA-deficient mice to catabolize GM2, ultimately limiting the toxic accumulation in the lysosomes of neurons [13]. Sialidases catalyze the hydrolysis of sialic acid residues from glycolipids and glycoproteins. Plasma membrane associated sialidase (Neu3) is primarily responsible for the metabolic bypass observed in mice as it cleaves a sialic acid residue from GM2, converting it to asialo GM2 (GA2) [13]. GA2 can then be recognized and degraded by a functional β-hexosaminidase B (HexB) [13]. Therefore, to generate our infantile TSD mouse model, we crossed mice harbouring the 4 bp microduplication variant in the Hexa locus with mice that have a Neu3 deficiency. Although a range of TSD mouse models have been previously developed, this study presents the first model incorporating the most common TSD causing variant. The Hexa-/- mouse accumulates GM2 ganglioside but fails to develop severe neurodegeneration due to compensatory GM2 catabolism mediated by Neu3 [12, 13]. To overcome this limitation, the Hexa-/- Neu3-/- mouse model was generated and has been shown to exhibit features of TSD, however, the resulting phenotype is driven by complete gene disruption rather than a clinically relevant variant [13] Additional late-onset TSD (LOTS) models, such as c.805G > A Neu3-/- variant mice have been valuable for modeling and examining variant-specific disease variability, however they do not fully address the genetic and pathological features of infantile TSD [14, 15]. In contrast, we generated a novel mouse model using CRISPR/Cas9 genome editing to introduce the most prevalent variant causing infantile TSD. This model enables the study of disease pathophysiology and potential therapeutics, in a genetically accurate and clinically relevant context. In this study, we characterized a TSD mouse model harboring the humanized HEXA c.1278insTATC variant to investigate the consequences of GM2 accumulation, with a particular focus on retinal involvement. We performed retinal assessments, including fundus imaging and optical coherence tomography (OCT), to evaluate structural changes. These studies were complemented by analyses of neuroinflammation and behavior throughout disease progression.

Materials & methods

Animals

All TSD mouse models have been generated at The Centre for Phenogenomics (TCP; Toronto, Ontario, Canada) following their standard protocol. Briefly, to generate mice harboring a partially humanized Hexa gene and a 4-bp insertion, a guide RNA (gRNA) targeting exon 11 in the proximity to the variant site along with Cas9 and a DNA template containing the desired mutation were electroporated into fertilized zygotes of C57BL/6 N mice and then transferred into pseudo-pregnant females. Founder mice were screened by PCR and Sanger sequencing (Hexa ex 11 seq F and Hexa ex12 short R) for the desired variant and subsequently crossed with C57BL/6 N to establish germline transmission. To generate Neu3 null mice, a trans-exonic deletion strategy was designed to remove most of the coding region. This deletion spans just over 10,000 base pairs and includes the majority of exon 2 and the entire coding sequence of exon 3. Positive mice were then crossed with Neu3-/- mice to generate both the VK-IS Neu3−/− and c.1278insTATC Neu3 -/- colonies.

Animal husbandry

All mice were maintained TCP under standardized housing conditions (12-hour light/dark cycle, unrestricted access to food and water). All procedures adhered to the Animals for Research Act of Ontario and the guidelines of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the TCP Animal Care Committee. For genotyping analysis, ear samples were biopsied, and DNA was extracted using Qiagen DNAeasy Blood and Tissue Kit. DNA was then amplified using DreamTaq polymerase (Thermo Fisher Scientific). To genotype the Hexa locus, the DNA was purified using Qiagen PCR Purification Kit and Sanger sequenced at The Centre for Applied Genomics (TCAG) at the Hospital for Sick Children. To genotype the Neu3 locus, PCR was performed using primer sets (Neu3 Ex2 F, Neu3 Ex2 R, Neu3 Ex 3 F, Neu3 Ex3 R) that flank the deletion junction and determine zygosity of the deletion (Fig S2).

Functional tests

Locomotor activity was evaluated using the VersaMax Animal Activity Monitoring System (AccuScan Instruments). Mice were individually placed in the center of a transparent Plexiglas arena (41.25 × 41.25 × 31.25 cm) illuminated at 200 lx. Activity in the central and peripheral zones was recorded for a total duration of 20 min per animal. Motor coordination and balance were evaluated using the Rota-Rod NG apparatus (model 46750, Ugo Basile). Mice were tested under the ramp protocol, in which the speed accelerated from 5 RPM to 40 RPM over a period of 300 s. Each mouse underwent three consecutive trials, with adequate rest periods between trials to prevent fatigue. The average latency to fall across trials was used for analysis.

Brain histology

Mice were transcardially perfused at 22 weeks of age with ice cold PBS (Wisent 311 − 011 CL). under isoflurane anesthesia. For all studies, brains were removed and hemisected along the midline. Brain halves were either frozen for molecular and biochemical analysis or fixed in 10% neutral-buffered formalin (Sigma-Aldrich HT501128) for immunohistochemical analysis. Following overnight fixation, tissues were dehydrated in 30% sucrose overnight at 4 °C. Brain tissues designated for GM2 immunofluorescent staining were embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (Tissue-Tek), rapidly frozen, and sectioned at 20 μm thickness using a cryostat (Thermo Scientific HM525). For all other immunostaining analyses, brain tissues were embedded in paraffin at TCP following their standard protocol. Sections were cut at 7 μm thickness using a microtome.

Retinal histology

Eyeballs were immediately collected after cervical dislocation. A 25-gauge needle (Precision Glide Needle BD 305125) was used to gently puncture the center of the cornea, and small cross-shaped incisions were made to facilitate fixative penetration. Tissue was then placed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Thermo Scientific 043368.9 M) and rotated for 30 min at room temperature (RT). After initial fixation, the vitreous body and lens were carefully removed, and the tissue was returned to 4% PFA for an additional 15 min under rotation at room temperature. Surrounding tissues were trimmed away, and the eyecups were transferred to 30% sucrose in PBS and rotated for 30 min at room temperature for cryoprotection. Tissue was then embedded in a 1:1 mixture of 30% sucrose and OCT compound, rapidly frozen, and sectioned at a thickness of 7 μm.

Protein isolation and western blotting

Mouse tissues were homogenized in 400 µL of RIPA buffer [50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, supplemented with Halt™ Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (100X; Thermo Fisher Scientific)] and lysed using a MagNA Lyser (Roche). An additional 400 µL of RIPA double-detergent buffer (2% deoxycholate, 2% NP-40, and 2% Triton X-100 in RIPA homogenizing buffer) was added, and the lysates were incubated for 45 min at 4 °C, followed by centrifugation at 14,000 g for 10 min. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Proteins were separated on 4–12% Bis-Tris gels and transferred onto membranes using an iBlot 2 transfer apparatus (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Membranes were blocked with 5% milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 h at RT, then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against GFAP (GFAP; Merck Millipore 1:1000),Calbindin D-28 K (Proteintech, 1:1000) and GAPDH (Invitrogen, 1:5000). After washing, membranes were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies. Gel images were captured using the Bio-Rad GelDoc Imaging System according to the manufacturer’s instructions Membranes were not cut. Stripping and reprobing for GAPDH was done for the blot in Fig. 4d, using a commercial stripping buffer (Thermo Scientific, 62299) following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Fluorescent imaging

Whole slide scans were acquired on a Pannoramic 250 Flash III slide scanner (3DHistech) using a 40 × 0.95 NA air objective (Zeiss). Slide scanning was used for Figs. 2b, 3a and 4a, c and f, snapshots were acquired using Caseviewer software version 2.3 (3DHISTECH). Confocal images in Fig. 4g 5c were acquired using a Leica Stellaris 5 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems) using HyD detectors in combination with a 63X oil (Fig. 4g) and 40X water (Figs. 4g and 5c) immersion objective, controlled by LASX image acquisition software.

Image analysis

Dendritic thickness was quantified manually using the “measure length” function in Caseviewer software version 2.3 (3DHISTECH). 5–6 measurements were taken per lobe, lobes III, IV, VII and IX were measured between 1 and 2 sections, three animals per condition (InsTATC Neu3 −/−, VK-IS Neu3−/− and WT). GM2 and GFAP quantification in brain regions was performed using HALO image analysis platform (Indica Labs). The HALO HighPlex FL v4.2.14 algorithm was used to identify and quantify GM2-postive and GFAP-positive cytoplasms, only cells that colocalized with nuclear DAPI were included. One image analysis algorithm was optimized across all brain images, using Random Forest (object colocalization). % GFAP-positive area was calculated using Fiji by ImageJ following previously established protocols [16].

Immunofluorescence

Slides were washed with 1X PBS (3 × 1 min washes) and a hydrophobic barrier was applied around the tissue using a PAP pen (Diagnostic Biosystems Elite PAP Pen, NC0822416). Tissue was then permeabilized using 0.1% Triton-X 100 (Sigma-Aldrich, T9284-1 L) in PBS for 10 min followed by blocking with 5% Bovine Serum Albumin (Sigma-Aldrich, A7906-50G) in 0.1% Triton-X 100 in PBS for 1 h. Slides were washed with 1X PBS (3 × 5 min washes) and primary antibodies were applied and left to incubate overnight at 4 C. Primary antibodies and dilutions used are as follows: anti-Glial Fibrillary Acidic Protein (GFAP, MAB360, Merck Millipore 1:1000), Calbindin-D28 (14479-1-AP, Proteintech, 1:500); (Invitrogen, PA5-143561, 1:500), inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor type-I (IP3R1, Invitrogen, PA1901,1:300), GM2 (Tokyo Chemical Industry, A2576, 1:250),neurofilament medium protein (NF-M, Invitrogen, 13–0700, 1:1000) and neurofilament light protein (NF-L, Invitrogen, MA5-42752, 1:500). Secondary antibody probes Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A-11008) and Alexa Fluor 555 (A-31570) were applied to slides and incubated for 2 h before PBS washing (3 × 5 min washes). DAPI (Thermo Fisher, D3571), diluted in PBS, was applied to slides and incubated for 10 min prior to slide mounting with ProLong Gold (Thermo Fisher, P36934).

Fluorometric HexA assay

A synthetic substrate known as 4-methylumbelliferyl 6-sulfo-2-acetamido-2-deoxy-β-d-glucopyranoside (MUGS) that releases 4-Methylumbelliferone (MU) fluorophore upon hydrolysis was used to assess HexA activity in tissue lysates. The anionic sulfated group of MUGS mimics the negatively charged Neuraminic (NANA) residue on the native HexA substrate, GM2, therefore conferring high substrate specificity and making this assay suitable for quantifying relative HexA activity. Protein concentrations were determined using a Pierce™ BCA assay following the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23227 ). Briefly, 5 µg of tissue lysate was diluted in citrate phosphate buffer (pH 4.5) and incubated with 3.2 mM MUGS substrate (Toronto Research Chemicals) at 37 °C for 1 h. Reactions were stopped with 100 mM 2-amino-2-methylpropanol (Sigma-Aldrich) and fluorescence was measured (Ex: 360 nm, Em: 450 nm) using Synergy Neo2 microplate reader (BioTek). Parallel reactions with MUG substrate were used to normalize for total protein content. The MUGS/MUG ratio was multiplied by 100 to yield an unscaled HexA function score.

RNA extraction and sequencing

Mouse cerebella and cortices were isolated following cervical dislocation and collected in 1.4 mm Zirconium Beads tubes (OPS Diagnostics) and homogenized using a MagNA Lyser (Roche Diagnostic). RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Total RNA quality was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100 with an RNA Nano chip, and concentrations were determined with the Qubit RNA HS Assay (Thermo Scientific). Library Preparation, sequencing and analyses were performed by The Centre for Applied Genomics using the Illumina Novaseq S4 flowcell PE2 × 150 bp, which generates 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw transcript reads were aligned to the GRCm39/M33 reference genome, the quality of the data was assessed using FastQC v.0.11.5, and adapters were trimmed using Trim Galore. Raw trimmed reads were then aligned to reference genome using STAR aligner v.2.6.0c and then processed to extract raw read counts for genes using htseq-count v.0.6.1p2. Exploratory analysis was performed using R v.3.6.1 and differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2 and edgeR package v.3.28.1.

Functional gene enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment of gene expression changes was assessed using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) following the Broad Institute method. Mouse-ortholog gene sets from Molecular Signature Database were used, including Hallmark (mh.all.v2025.1.Mm.symbols.gmt) and Gene Ontology categories: Biological Process, Cellular Component, and Molecular Function (m5.all.v2025.1.Mm.gmt). GSEA was performed using gene set permutations to assess enrichment significance. Multiple testing correction was applied using the False Discovery Rate (FDR) q-value provided by GSEA, and gene sets with FDR q-values below 0.05 were considered significantly enriched.

Optical coherence tomography and fundoscopy

Fundus and optical coherence tomography imaging were performed by TCP personnel following institutional standard operating procedures. Briefly, mice were anesthetised using intraperitoneal injection of ketamine (100 mg/mL) and xylazine (20 mg/mL) at a volume of 0.01 mL/g body weight. Pupils were dilated using 1% (w/v) tropicamide ophthalmic solution (Mydriacyl),. fundus and optical coherence tomography images were captured (Phoenix, Micron-5) Three photographs of each eye were acquired with focal planes centered on (1) blood vessels, (2) intervascular spaces, and (3) the retina. Additional photographs were obtained if abnormalities were observed. Following imaging, anesthesia was reversed with an intraperitoneal injection of atipamezole (Antisedan; 0.1 mg/mL, 0.01 mL/g + 0.05 mL).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 10 was used to analyze and plot experimental data. Comparisons between groups were performed using unpaired two-tailed t-tests, one-way ANOVA, or two-way ANOVA, as appropriate. For both one-way and two-way ANOVA, Tukey’s multiple comparisons test was applied. The threshold for statistical significance was p < 0.05. Data is reported as mean ± SD.

Results

CRISPR/Cas9-mediated generation of a mouse model harboring the c.1278insTATC variant and Neu3 deletion

To generate mice harboring a partially humanized Hexa gene and a 4-bp insertion, a guide RNA (gRNA) targeting exon 11 in the proximity to the mutation site, along with Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 and a DNA template containing the desired variant were electroporated into fertilized zygotes of C57BL/6 N mice and then implanted into pseudo-pregnant females (Fig. 1a). To generate Neu3 null mice, a trans-exonic deletion strategy was designed to remove most of the coding region using 2 gRNAs targeting exon 3 of Neu3 gene (Fig. 1b). This deletion spans just over 10,000 base pairs and includes the majority of exon 2 and the entire coding sequence of exon 3. Founder mice were screened by PCR and Sanger sequenced for the presence of the desired variant and subsequently crossed with C57BL/6 N to establish germline transmission. To prevent a murine metabolic bypass mechanism that mitigates disease manifestation, positive InsTATC and VK-IS mice were crossed with Neu3 null mice to generate both the VK-IS Neu3−/− and InsTATC Neu3−/− colonies to recapitulate the GM2 accumulation observed in TSD patients (Fig. 1b).

InsTATC Neu3−/− mice lack HexA activity and significantly accumulate GM2 ganglioside in the brain

To enable accurate modeling of the c.1278insTATC variant, we first sought to humanize the corresponding region of exon 11 within the murine Hexa, due to sequence differences between the two species. It was critical to confirm that the partial humanization of the Hexa sequence did not compromise HexA’s ability to function and effectively catabolize GM2. We performed a fluorometric enzymatic assay across multiple tissues (cortex, cerebellum, liver and kidney). Overall, VK-IS Neu3−/− mice exhibited HexA function levels comparable to WT across all tested tissues (Fig. 2a). These findings indicate that the sequence modification introduced to humanize the mice did not impair HexA function. In contrast, HexA function scores in TSD mutants were reduced by more than 10-fold in the cortex and cerebellum compared to the WT mice (Fig. 2a). Similarly, in liver and kidney tissue, HexA activity was nearly undetectable in InsTATC Neu3−/−(Fig. 2a). Upon confirmation that HexA is not functional in InsTATC Neu3−/− mice, we next examined GM2 accumulation in the brain. To evaluate GM2 accumulation, we performed immunofluorescence staining for GM2 on brain sections from WT, VK-IS Neu3−/− and InsTATC Neu3−/− mice. GM2 accumulation was not observed in WT or VK-IS Neu3−/− brains, while InsTATC Neu3−/− brains showed widespread and pronounced GM2 accumulation across the cortex, hippocampus and cerebellum (Fig. 2b). InsTATC Neu3−/− mice exhibited a significant increase in GM2-positive cells compared to both control groups, consistent with impaired GM2 clearance due to HexA dysfunction. The profound reduction in HexA activity and extensive GM2 accumulation in this model reflects the enzymatic deficit typically associated with this mutation, making it a valuable model for studying disease mechanisms and evaluating therapeutic strategies.

InsTATC Neu3−/− mice exhibit reduced survival and progressive motor dysfunction

To assess overall health and disease progression in the TSD mouse model, we conducted Kaplan-Meier survival analysis. InsTATC Neu3−/− mice exhibited a significantly shortened median survival of 167 days, whereas no reduction in lifespan was observed in WT, VK-IS Neu3−/−, or Neu3−/− mice (Fig. 2c). Notably, survival endpoints of InsTATC Neu3−/− mice were typically reached due to humane intervention criteria rather than spontaneous death, including signs such as severe weight loss, ataxia, lethargy, hunched posture, and reduced mobility. To further evaluate motor function and assess anxiety-related behaviour, we performed rotarod and open field testing at 11 weeks of age. InsTATC Neu3−/− mice demonstrated significant impairments in motor coordination and balance, as they exhibited the shortest latency to fall in comparison to WT and VK-IS controls (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the open field test revealed pronounced locomotor impairments and a marked reduction in explorative behaviour. Specifically, TSD mice displayed a significant reduction in movement time, total distance traveled, ambulatory episode average velocity, and exhibited prolonged rest times (Fig. 2e). InsTATC Neu3−/− mice also demonstrated behavioral signs of anxiety, as indicated by a strong preference to remain in the periphery of the open field arena (Fig. 2f). InsTATC Neu3−/− mice demonstrated consistently low body weight starting at 15 weeks as well as a markedly hunched posture upon X-ray imaging (Fig. 2g, h). InsTATC Neu3−/− mice begin to exhibit hindlimb clasping upon tail suspension at approximately 19 weeks of age, which became increasingly more pronounced as they approach endpoint (Fig. 2i). These findings suggest that motor deficits emerge early in disease progression and likely contribute to the progressive functional decline preceding end-stage disease.

GM2 accumulation in InsTATC Neu3−/− mice induces astrogliosis

The accumulation of GM2 gangliosides is known to lead to neuronal dysfunction and degeneration, which in turn triggers secondary neuroinflammatory processes [4, 17]. To evaluate neuroinflammatory responses associated with GM2 deposition in InsTATC Neu3−/− mice, we assessed astrogliosis by examining expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), a well-established marker of astrocyte activation [18–21]. Under normal physiological conditions, GFAP is expressed at low levels in non-reactive astrocytes [18–20]. However, in response to CNS injury, astrocytes undergo hypertrophy and proliferate, often resulting in astrogliosis [18–20]. In WT and VK-IS Neu3−/− brain sections, GFAP staining was minimal and did not appear to constitute reactive gliosis, (Fig. 3a). In contrast, InsTATC Neu3−/− brains exhibited significant astrogliosis, as shown by a prominent increase in GFAP-positive cell bodies in the thalamus (Fig. 3b), and a significant increase in GFAP protein expression in the cerebellum (Fig. 3c).The hippocampus and cortex regions in InsTATC Neu3−/− brains showed a lesser, nonsignificant increase in GFAP-positive cells compared to WT and VK-IS Neu3−/− (Fig. 3b). These findings suggest that HexA deficiency and the resulting GM2 storage in the brain triggers a localized neuroinflammatory response characterized by astrocyte activation.

InsTATC Neu3−/− mice exhibit abnormal purkinje cell morphology and axonal protein aggregation

Purkinje cells (PCs) are large, inhibitory neurons located in the cerebellum that serve as the sole output of the cerebellar cortex and therefore play a critical role in regulating motor function [22–25]. PC depletion is a commonly reported feature of TSD contributing to motor dysfunction and cerebellar pathology and previously reported in Hexa−/−Neu3−/− mice [13]. Cerebellar PCs were identified using Calbindin-D28 staining, and indeed some InsTATC Neu3−/− cerebella appeared to show less Calbindin-D28-positive PCs (Fig. 4c). Similarly, InsTATC Neu3−/− cerebella showed a significant reduction in Calbindin-D28 protein expression (Fig. 4d) as well as mRNA expression (Fig. 4e) In order to confirm the loss of PCs, a second marker, inositol 1,4,5-triphosphate receptor type 1 (IP3R1), was stained for. We observed that the IP3R1-positive InsTATC Neu3−/− PCs are in fact retained but exhibit morphological features of dysfunction, notably dendritic thickening, when compared to VK-IS Neu3−/− and WT controls (Fig. 4a, b). Specifically, despite no observed loss in IP3R1-positive PCs, some InsTATC Neu3−/− cerebella appeared to show less Calbindin-D28-positive PCs (Fig. 4c). Together, this may indicate a loss of calcium buffering protein expression despite PC survival in InsTATC Neu3−/−.

Furthermore, Calbindin-D28 staining revealed the presence of focal axonal swellings within the granular layer and white matter tracts of InsTATC Neu3−/− cerebella (Fig. 4c). These swellings were notably absent in both WT and VK-IS Neu3−/− controls (Fig. 4c). Further staining against neurofilament-M (NFM) also revealed the presence of large aggregates in the molecular layer and white matter tracts of InsTATC Neu3−/− cerebella (Fig. 4f). Interestingly, co-staining with Neurofilament-light (NFL) and NFM yielded a partial overlap between Calbindin-D28-positive swellings and NFL and NFM-positive aggregates. Specifically, dendritic swellings appeared positive for only Calbindin-D28 while axonal aggregates were partially triple-positive for Calbindin-D28, NFL and NFM, but sometimes only positive for NFL/M (Fig. 4g).

Together, these findings indicate that while PCs are retained in our TSD mouse model, they do undergo structural and molecular alterations consistent with neuronal dysfunction. This, alongside abnormal neurofilament aggregation, could potentially contribute to motor dysfunction despite the absence of PC loss.

Progressive retinal GM2 deposition and astrogliosis in InsTATC Neu3 -/- mice

Fundoscopy imaging at 10 weeks of age revealed diffuse punctate retinal deposits and optical coherence tomography showed structural changes in InsTATC Neu3−/− retinas, which were absent in WT and VK-IS Neu3−/− controls (Fig. 5a). Given the regional variability of the retinal nerve fibre layer (RNFL), the overall combined thickness from the RNFL to the inner plexiform layer (IPL) was measured as a surrogate marker for inner retinal structure. InsTATC Neu3−/− mice displayed a mean inner retinal layer thickness of approximately 80 μm, compared with 58 µM in VK-IS Neu3−/− mice and 62 μm in WT mice, indicating a significant thickening in the TSD mouse model (Fig. 5b). At 22 weeks of age, immunohistochemical analysis revealed a pronounced increase in GFAP-positive signal intensity within the retina of InsTATC Neu3−/− mice, indicative of Müller cell activation (Fig. 5c). In these mice, GFAP-positive Müller cell processes extended through multiple retinal layers, suggesting widespread glial remodeling. Together, these findings demonstrate progressive retinal alterations in TSD mice, characterized by RNFL thickening and subsequent glial remodelling.

Discussion

Robust preclinical models of infantile TSD are lacking, with few models available that recapitulate the full spectrum of pathological features observed in patients [12, 13]. Previous studies have revealed that disruption of HexA in murine models fails to elicit the severe neurodegenerative phenotype that is typically associated with TSD [12]. Later studies have shown that Hexa KO mice alone do not fully recapitulate TSD pathology due to a murine bypass mechanism that mitigates GM2 accumulation [13]. To address this, we introduced a Neu3 deletion in our mouse model to enable a more faithful modeling of the disease. Here, we report the generation and phenotypic characterization of an infantile TSD mouse model that was generated using CRISPR/Cas9 technology to install a predominant insertion mutation in the Hexa gene. To our knowledge, this is the first mouse model to precisely replicate this clinically relevant variant that exhibits key histological, biochemical, and behavioural features of the disease. The InsTATC Neu3−/− model represents a crucial tool for investigating the pathophysiology of infantile TSD, elucidating the underlying disease mechanisms, and creating opportunities for the development of novel targeted therapeutics development. This model exhibits phenotypic features similar to those reported in Hexa -/- Neu3-/- mice while also revealing early retinal pathology as well as previously unreported proteinopathy elements, providing a comprehensive characterization of important TSD features.

InsTATC Neu3-/- mice exhibit a shortened lifespan of approximately 167 days and experience significant weight loss as they approach this endpoint. Importantly, they typically reach survival endpoints due to humane intervention criteria including severe weight loss, ataxia, lethargy, and reduced mobility. By comparison, Hexa-/- mice do not exhibit neurological deficits and can survive up to 1 year [26]. Hexa-/- Neu3 -/- have a markedly shortened lifespan of ~ 80 days, although it remains unclear whether this reflects spontaneous mortality or humane intervention [13, 17]. Previously reported LOTS models carrying the Hexa c.805G > A mutation show variable survival of approximately ~ 155–189 days and have been reported to reach humane endpoints primarily due to weight loss, presumably reflecting a milder, late-onset phenotype of this variant relative to infantile TSD [14, 15]. The observed difference in survival of our model relative to Hexa-/- Neu3 -/- may potentially be attributable to differences in genetic background, as Hexa-/- mice were maintained on a 129/Sv background whereas InsTATC Neu3 -/- mice are on a C57BL/6 N background. Overall, the lifespan of InsTATC Neu3-/- mice is consistent with a severe infantile phenotype. In comparison to Hexa -/- Neu3 -/- mice, they show pronounced GM2 accumulation in the brain and a complete loss of HexA activity. Behavioral analyses revealed severe motor and coordination deficits. These impairments are detectable at 11 weeks in rotarod and open field tests, and by 18 weeks significant ataxia prevented further motor function assessment. Additionally, hindlimb clasping is consistently observed as the mice near 22 weeks. Histological analysis demonstrated reactive astrocytes across most brain regions, most prominently in the thalamus and cerebellum, but also present to a lesser degree in the cortex and hippocampus. Widespread glial activation, as well as an increased expression of proinflammatory cytokines, is a well-described feature of late-stage TSD in both humans and animal models. Most characterizations of the inflammatory response in murine TSD have focused on end-stage (< 20 weeks) disease, but behavioural testing does indicate that some motor deficits are present at 2.5 months and increase in severity over time [13, 14, 27]. This is in accordance with human cases of infantile TSD, as first symptoms are thought to occur at 5 months of age and intensify progressively [28]. Similarly, we report motor symptoms as early as 11 weeks and transcriptional changes occurring at 6 weeks. In the retina, GM2 deposition is evident across multiple layers and is detectable in retinal imaging as early as 10 weeks demonstrating that retinal pathology emerges early and progresses alongside CNS deficits. Bulk RNA sequencing of the cerebellum and cortex in 6-week-old mice revealed early transcriptional changes occurring at a pre-symptomatic age. GSEA analysis of cortical and cerebellar tissues identified a significant upregulation of pathways associated with respiratory burst, glial cell activation, and pyroptotic inflammatory response, suggesting enrichment of innate immune activation and oxidative stress related pathways at a pre-symptomatic stage (Fig S1). In the cerebellum, PC pathology was evident as dendrites appeared significantly thickened and axons contained aggregates. Unlike the PC loss reported in the previous Hexa−/−Neu3−/− model, we primarily observed a reduction in Calbindin-D28 staining and expression while the PCs themselves were preserved [13]. Calbindin, a calcium-binding protein highly expressed in PC’s, plays a critical role in regulating intracellular calcium levels and modulating neuronal signalling required for proper PC output [29]. Previous studies have shown that mice lacking calbindin in PC’s exhibited pronounced impairments in sensory processing and motor coordination [29]. Specifically, conditional deletion of calbindin in PC’s has been previously shown to alter the amplitude and timing of short bursts of calcium signalling triggered by parallel and climbing fiber stimulation [29]. Parallel fibers deliver excitatory inputs that depend on precise temporal summation, whereas climbing fibers provide all-or-none bursts that generate complex spikes, with both forms of input relying on finely tuned calcium regulation to sustain balanced PC firing [29–31]. As the sole output of the cerebellar cortex, PCs must precisely integrate excitatory parallel and climbing fiber inputs to regulate their firing activity and impaired calcium signalling disrupts this process, leading to irregular PC output to the deep cerebellar nuclei (DCN) [23, 25, 29]. Given our findings, we hypothesize that this disruption in calcium homeostasis is likely to compromise the precise regulation of motor control, potentially contributing to the progressive motor coordination deficiencies observed in the InsTATC Neu3−/− mice. While it has previously been speculated that motor impairment in TSD models is due to progressive PC loss, our data indicates a potential association between disrupted PC signaling and motor impairment [13].

To further assess PC health, we examined IPR3R1 staining in the cerebellum as IP3R1 is an endoplasmic reticulum calcium channel that releases calcium upon binding IP3 [32].While staining for Calbindin suggested PC depletion in InsTATC Neu3−/− mice, IP3R1 labeling indicated that the cells remained intact, highlighting the limitation of solely relying on calbindin as a marker for PC’s. Moreover, although previous studies of murine TSD have indicated a causal relationship between neuronal loss and symptom progression, this finding demonstrates the possibility of end-stage disease in the absence of cell loss [13, 17].

Although still present, PCs appear to exhibit morphological abnormalities, most notably swollen and enlarged dendrites. Furthermore, calbindin staining revealed the presence of axonal swellings within the granular layer and white matter tracts of InsTATC Neu3−/− cerebella. These axonal swellings have been previously associated with neurodegenerative diseases including and Parkinson’s disease, multiple sclerosis, and Huntington’s disease [33–35]. Previous studies have shown that PCs with axonal swellings are less prone to exhibiting failures in propagation of action potentials relative to PC’s that do not have these swellings [36]. Moreover, mice with a higher density of axonal swellings performed better in cerebellar related learning tasks including the rotarod and Erasmus ladder tasks [36]. These swellings may potentially represent a compensation mechanism that aims to counteract PC axonal dysfunction in our InsTATC Neu3−/− mouse model. Together, these observations suggest that dendritic swelling and torpedo formation may represent adaptive responses aimed at preserving PC function, although further investigation is needed to elucidate their precise causes and consequences in TSD which were not addressed in this study. In addition to Calbindin-D28-positive swellings, Neurofilament (NFM/NFL) staining showed the presence of aggregates within the molecular layer and white matter tracts of InsTATC Neu3−/− cerebella. These axonal aggregates were found to partially overlap with Calbindin-D28-positive swellings, indicating that distinct protein aggregates contribute to TSD pathology, and NFM/NFL aggregates are not limited to PC axonal swellings. Neurofilament has been found to be elevated in the cerebrospinal fluid of GM2 disorder patients, although the precise mechanism underlying this increase in NF levels has not been thoroughly addressed [37]. The InsTATC Neu3−/− mouse line is therefore a model amenable to the study of proteinopathy-like TSD features. Such studies might address whether NFs, which have not been characterized in a TSD-specific context, could present a therapeutic target for symptom management. InsTATC Neu3−/− mice exhibit progressive weight loss beginning at 18 weeks of age, coinciding with the onset of the ataxic behaviour. We speculate that this weight loss results from difficulty accessing food due to their pronounced motor impairments. From 18 weeks onwards, rotarod assessment could not be conducted as the mice were unable to remain on the rod, highlighting the early onset and severity of motor deficits in this model compared with Hexa⁻/⁻ Neu3⁻/⁻ mice, where motor impairments were first detected at 19 weeks [13]. In addition, open field testing revealed stress-related behaviors, consistent with previous observations in Hexa−/−Neu3−/− mice [17]. Neither male nor female mice have been observed to produce offspring when crossed with WT mice, indicating a possible reproductive impairment which is consistent with what has been previously reported in Hexa-/- Neu3-/- mice [13]. InsTATC Neu3-/- mice exhibit a shortened lifespan of approximately 22 weeks, highlighting the severe effect of disease on physiological and behavioral functions, consistent with the rapid progression and early mortality reported in infantile TSD patients.

Retinal abnormalities are among the earliest and most visible clinical signs of TSD, yet the molecular mechanisms driving these changes remain poorly understood [38, 39]. Importantly, retinal phenotyping has not been thoroughly explored in preclinical models of TSD, and it is unclear which retinal cell populations are most susceptible to pathological changes resulting from GM2 accumulation. The cherry-red spot, a common feature of TSD, arises from GM2 accumulation in retinal ganglion cells (RGCs) surrounding the fovea, which lacks RGC’s and therefore appears red in contrast to the GM2-laden surrounding area [38, 40, 41]. Notably, TSD patients experience early-onset visual difficulties and eventual blindness. While the cherry-red spot is a useful diagnostic feature of human TSD, it is only observable in approximately 50% of patients and therefore the interrogation of additional visual features of TSD could be diagnostically relevant. We observed punctate retinal deposits likely representing GM2 accumulation in the fundus images of 10-week-old InsTATC Neu3−/− that were absent in WT and VK-IS Neu3−/− retinas. As expected, the absence of a macula in mice prevented the formation of a cherry red spot, highlighting an important anatomical difference between mice and human disease [42]. Moreover, optical coherence tomography imaging demonstrated significant thickening of the inner retina including the RNFL, indicative of GM2 deposition and remodeling consistent with observations in patients with TSD [40].The RNFL, a hallmark feature for assessing axonal health, is primarily composed of RGC axons which converge at the optic disc and form the optic nerve [43, 44]. While RNFL thinning is often associated with neurodegeneration, thickening has also been associated with diseases such as retinitis pigmentosa (RP) and autosomal recessive spastic ataxia of Charlevoix-Saguenay (ARSACS) [45–47]. We speculate that thickening of the RNFL may be a feature of pronounced GM2 deposition and remodeling within inner retina. Retinal GM2 accumulation is also accompanied by GFAP upregulation in Müller cells indicating reactive gliosis as a response to the pronounced deposition [48]. Upregulation of GFAP in Müller cells is a hallmark of retinal gliosis and reflects activation in response to injury, suggesting that GM2 storage triggers a glial response that could potentially impact retinal function [48, 49]. Collectively, these findings show an early-onset and pronounced retinal pathology in our TSD mouse model, which precedes motor symptom onset. These retinal features of TSD could therefore serve as effective markers of disease progression and could provide a robust platform for assessing the efficacy of future treatments.

Conclusion

In summary, this study establishes the first mouse model harbouring a prominent TSD causing mutation, c.1278insTATC. Compared with the previously reported Hexa -/- Neu3-/- model, InsTATC Neu3 -/- mice exhibit several distinguishing features. Median survival is 167 days, compared with 80 days in double knockout mice. In contrast to Hexa-/- Neu3-/- mice which were reported to exhibit PC loss, we observed reduced Calbindin-D28 labeling and expression with preserved PC’s demonstrating morphological dysfunction. Both models exhibit extensive astrogliosis and stress-related behaviors, and neither male nor female mice produce offspring, consistent with reported reproductive impairment. While Hexa-/- Neu3-/- mice showed only mild motor impairment at 10 weeks that progressively worsened at 15 and 19 weeks, we were able to detect a strong motor phenotype as early as 11 weeks, with severe ataxia by 18 weeks that prevented further rotarod testing. Moreover, Hexa -/- Neu3-/- mice did not exhibit anatomical alterations in retinal layers whereas InsTATC Neu3-/- display significant RNFL thickening as early as 10 weeks. This model faithfully recapitulates key features of TSD, including early-onset neurological deficits, behavioral changes, and retinal pathology, providing a novel and valuable tool to investigate disease pathophysiology. Importantly, it enables the study of both disease mechanisms and therapeutic responses in a genetically accurate and clinically relevant context.
