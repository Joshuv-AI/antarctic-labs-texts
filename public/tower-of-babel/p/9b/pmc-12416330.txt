CPPD-related basilar impression with vertebral artery dissection and subsequent subarachnoid hemorrhage: illustrative case
Authors: Sara Smeets, Thomas Daenekindt, Frank Weyns, Christophe Oosterbos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUNDCalcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) deposition disease at the craniocervical junction (CCJ) typically presents with a retro-odontoid pseudotumor. Here, the authors report a case of CPPD-induced basilar impression, causing vertebral artery (VA) dissection and hemorrhage.OBSERVATIONSA 65-year-old male presented with worsening chronic cervicalgia, occipital headaches, and unstable tandem gait. CT revealed basilar impression with severe atlantoaxial erosion and instability, absent on imaging 8 months prior. MRI showed high-grade CCJ stenosis without myelopathy, due to CPPD. Before treatment could be initiated, the patient was suddenly found unresponsive. CT showed posterior fossa hemorrhage due to VA dissection, with secondary obstructive hydrocephalus. An external ventricular drain was placed, followed by coil VA embolization. After halo vest placement, CCJ alignment was restored and the patient underwent occipitocervical fusion. Following prolonged rehabilitation, he regained independent living without significant neurological sequelae.LESSONSThis is the first reported case of VA dissection with posterior fossa hemorrhage secondary to basilar impression due to CPPD. Inflammatory arthritides of the CCJ may cause rapidly progressive atlantoaxial erosion, predisposing one to unstable basilar impression and VA dissection. Awareness of this risk is crucial in the surgical management of inflammatory basilar impression, where preoperative vascular imaging may be warranted in selected cases. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE25332 Keywords: basilar impression, calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate, CPPD, vertebral artery dissection, fossa posterior hemorrhage, case reportABBREVIATIONS: CCJ = craniocervical junction, CPPD = calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate, PICA = posterior inferior cerebellar artery, RA = rheumatoid arthritis, VA = vertebral artery

Inflammatory arthritides at the craniocervical junction (CCJ), such as calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate (CPPD) crystal deposition disease and rheumatoid arthritis (RA), can lead to progressive deformity, instability, and myelopathy.1 RA is a well-recognized cause of basilar impression (or cranial settling),2 whereas CPPD is classically associated with retro-odontoid pseudotumor formation and crowned dens syndrome. CPPD or “pseudogout” is a crystalline arthropathy resulting from calcium pyrophosphate deposition in articular and periarticular structures. Although it predominantly affects peripheral joints, CPPD can involve the CCJ, where it is characterized by calcifications within the alar and/or transverse ligaments, occasionally causing stenosis and myelopathy.1,3,4

CPPD at the CCJ typically causes chronic cervicalgia and stiffness as a result of the chronic inflammation.1,4,5 However, these symptoms can also have an acute onset and may be accompanied by fever and elevated inflammatory markers in the blood, as seen in crowned dens syndrome, an acute manifestation of CPPD at the CCJ.1,5–7 In some cases, this may even be the initial manifestation of the disease without prior peripheral arthritis.5,6 While mild forms of basilar impression secondary to CPPD have been reported,8,9 it is generally less pronounced than that seen in RA.

In RA, vertebral artery (VA) involvement is uncommon but can rarely lead to dissection and secondary stroke.10 To date, VA dissection in CPPD-related basilar impression has not been reported. Here, we present the first reported case of severe CPPD-induced basilar impression, resulting in VA dissection and posterior fossa hemorrhage. This case emphasizes the need for early recognition and vascular assessment in surgical planning of CPPD disease at the CCJ.

Illustrative Case

Initial Presentation

A 65-year-old male consulted the emergency department with exacerbation of chronic cervicalgia and occipital headaches for several weeks. The patient reported that his “neck was getting shorter.” His medical history included chronic left-sided hemicorporeal pain syndrome and chronic cervicalgia without prior rheumatological or musculoskeletal disease. Clinical examination showed mild left hemiparesis (Medical Research Council grade 4+/5), general hyporeflexia, and a slightly unstable tandem gait.

CT revealed basilar impression with severe atlantoaxial erosion (cranial settling), absent on imaging 8 months prior. The tip of the odontoid process extended 9.5 mm above Chamberlain’s line, and there was marked erosive destruction of the odontoid accompanied by subchondral cyst formation (Fig. 1). Laboratory analyses revealed a mildly elevated white blood cell count (11.3 × 109/L) and normal C-reactive protein level (3.3 mg/L). The patient had no fever. An underlying inflammatory arthritis of the CCJ was suspected, and the patient was admitted for further investigations and treatment.

Further Investigations

MRI showed high-grade CCJ stenosis without myelopathy (Fig. 2A). Dynamic flexion-extension imaging revealed severe atlantoaxial instability with pronounced worsening of basilar impression and spinal canal stenosis in flexion (Fig. 2B andC). The patient had no history of rheumatological disease, including peripheral arthritis, and serological markers for RA were negative. The presence of calcifications within the alar ligaments and at the insertions of the transverse ligament on CT imaging strongly suggested CPPD (Fig. 2D). This diagnosis was further supported by the presence of subchondral bone cysts in the odontoid process and atlantoaxial joints, the odontoid erosion, and the scattered calcifications within the annuli fibrosi of the subaxial cervical spine.1,4,5,11 In addition, the bilateral masses extending anteriorly and laterally from the atlantoaxial joints demonstrated typical imaging features of a CPPD-related pseudotumor, such as scattered multiple small calcifications on CT and hypointensity on both T1- and T2-weighted MRI sequences (Fig. 2E).4,11,12 Although CPPD pseudotumors are typically located in the retro-odontoid region, they can rarely originate more laterally at the level of the C1–2 lateral masses.4 In summary, the diagnosis of CPPD was established following multidisciplinary evaluation, based on the presence of characteristic imaging features for CPPD, in conjunction with the absence of systemic inflammatory disease and peripheral joint involvement, as well as negative serological markers, thereby effectively excluding alternative inflammatory CCJ arthritides such as RA.

B and C: Dynamic flexion-extension CT image revealing severe atlantoaxial instability with pronounced worsening of basilar impression and spinal canal stenosis in flexion (C) compared with extension (B). D: Calcifications in the alar ligaments and at the insertions of the transverse ligament and a CPPD-related mass or pseudotumor are characteristic of CPPD at the CCJ. Treatment was planned in two stages, with halo traction as the first step to restore CCJ alignment, followed by occipitocervical fusion.

Acute Deterioration and Management

Prior to treatment, the patient deteriorated to an unresponsive state with stertorous breathing (Glasgow Coma Scale score 5/15). Urgent cranial CT and CT angiography showed extensive subarachnoid and intraventricular hemorrhage in the posterior fossa due to dissection of the V3 segment of the VA at the level of the CCJ, with secondary obstructive hydrocephalus (Fig. 3A– D). An external ventricular drain was urgently placed, followed by angiography, which showed complete occlusion of the right VA, with retrograde perfusion of the posterior inferior cerebellar artery (PICA) via the left VA (Fig. 4A). To prevent rebleeding during planned treatment to realign the CCJ, an endovascular coil embolization of the V3 segment of the right VA was successfully performed (Fig. 4B). MRI confirmed the absence of cerebral ischemia. Additionally, the patient developed a severe aspiration pneumonia necessitating long-term intubation for respiratory support.

D: CT angiogram revealing VA dissection in the V3 segment at the level of the CCJ (arrow) as the underlying cause.

Angiograms obtained after the patient’s sudden deterioration, revealing a dissection of the right VA in the V3 segment, with spontaneous complete occlusion of its caudal portion. A: The right PICA originates immediately cranial to the dissection and is retrogradely perfused, along with the V3 and V4 segments of the right VA, via the left VA. CCJ alignment was restored after manual traction using intraoperative neuromonitoring (transcranial motor evoked potentials and transcranial somatosensory evoked potentials) and halo jacket placement (Fig. 5A). The patient subsequently underwent occipitocervical fusion with occipital plating and C2–5 pedicle screws (Fig. 5B). Given the severe craniocervical instability, cervical pedicle screws were preferred over lateral mass screws due to their longer bone trajectory with superior biomechanical strength. Preoperative CT confirmed adequate pedicle diameter for safe screw placement using intraoperative neuronavigation. Spineart Perla cervical pedicle screws and occipital plate were used for fixation. Persistent hydrocephalus necessitated ventriculoperitoneal shunt placement. The patient developed severe critical illness myopathy during his extended stay at the intensive care unit for respiratory complications with prolonged intubation and sedation. Given the absence of peripheral CPPD involvement, there was no indication for concurrent medical management of the CPPD itself, with nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs only being transiently administered before the acute deterioration. After rehabilitation, the patient resumed independent living with nearly complete relief of his chronic occipitocervical pain and without neurological sequelae. Radiographs 1 year postoperatively showed stable alignment (Fig. 5C and D), and CT scans demonstrated significant regression of the erosive changes in both the odontoid process and atlantoaxial joints, with resolution of the calcified pseudotumors.

A: After halo vest placement, CCJ alignment was restored. B: The patient subsequently underwent occipitocervical fusion with occipital plating and C2–5 pedicle screws as a definitive stabilization procedure. C and D: Lateral (C) and anteroposterior (D) cervical radiographs 1 year postoperatively, demonstrating stable alignment. Because of severe craniocervical instability, cervical pedicle screw fixation was selected over lateral mass screws to ensure optimal biomechanical stability. All instrumentation was provided by Spineart. No screw was placed in the left C2 vertebra, as both the pedicle and lateral mass were extensively eroded by the CPPD, precluding secure fixation. Similarly, no screw was placed in the right C3 lateral mass due to a fracture. A transverse connector was added to improve construct stability, and autologous bone graft was applied along the facet joints to promote bony fusion. The coils in the right VA and the ventriculoperitoneal shunt are also visible on the radiographs.

Discussion

Observations

To our knowledge, this report is the first to document VA dissection with subsequent posterior fossa hemorrhage secondary to basilar impression induced by CPPD at the CCJ.

Although mild vertical migration of the odontoid process has been described in CPPD,8,9 this is the first report of severe basilar impression in CPPD with the tip of the odontoid protruding through the foramen magnum—a phenomenon commonly observed in RA.2 Moreover, in this case, the CPPD-related pseudotumor extended anteriorly and laterally from the C1–2 lateral masses, a much rarer presentation than the classic retro-odontoid location.4

VA dissection has not previously been reported in association with CPPD at the CCJ, despite being a well-documented but infrequent complication in RA, where it correlates with the degree of vertical odontoid subluxation.13 It is also a rare but known phenomenon in congenital basilar invagination, posing a risk for recurrent strokes.14 Additionally, VA dissection has been described in a patient with ankylosing spondylitis at the CCJ, complicated with a bilateral VA dissection following halo traction to realign the basilar impression.15

Several underlying mechanisms for VA dissection in basilar impression, whether or not associated with RA, have been proposed, with mechanical factors playing a key role. Erosion by osteophytes may gradually weaken the arterial wall over time.10 Abnormal biomechanical forces associated with movement restriction of the VA, such as stretching, kinking, or compression of the VA between the C1 and C2 vertebrae or between the odontoid process and the foramen magnum, may also predispose the VA to dissection.10,14–16

Considering these underlying mechanisms, it is not unlikely that VA dissection may occur in the context of CPPD disease at the CCJ, particularly when accompanied by basilar impression.

Lastly, subarachnoid posterior fossa hemorrhage due to VA dissection in the setting of CCJ instability is exceedingly rare, with only one previously reported fatal case in RA.16 Our case is the first that illustrates the potential for catastrophic vascular complications in CPPD-induced basilar impression.

Lessons

This case demonstrates that CPPD disease at the CCJ, while typically presenting with a retro-odontoid pseudotumor or crowned dens syndrome, may also present with rapidly progressive basilar impression, akin to that seen in RA. This may be the result of severe atlantoaxial erosion, especially when the CPPD pseudotumor is located more laterally at the level of the C1–2 lateral masses instead of at the typical retro-odontoid location. Abnormal mechanical forces on the VA in basilar impression, whether it is congenital or secondary to an inflammatory arthritis at the CCJ, may predispose the vessel wall to dissection.10,14–16 In rare cases, this may result in a life-threatening posterior fossa hemorrhage.

Given these observations, it is imperative that clinicians are aware of potential VA dissection when managing inflammatory arthritides of the CCJ associated with basilar impression. In cases with suspected vascular involvement, preoperative vascular imaging using digital subtraction angiography or MR angiography may be warranted to improve preoperative surgical risk assessment and evaluate the patency of the circle of Willis.13

Although this report is limited to a single case, it expands the spectrum of CPPD-related complications at the CCJ and emphasizes the need for preoperative vascular assessment to mitigate the risk of devastating vascular events.
