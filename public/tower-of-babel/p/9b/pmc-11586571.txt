Comparison of the pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics of apixaban and rivaroxaban in dogs
Authors: Alex M. Lynch, Laura K. Ruterbories, Yao Zhu, Frank Fialkiewicz, Mark G. Papich, Marjory B. Brooks, Robert Goggs
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComparative pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) of apixaban and rivaroxaban have not been studied in dogs and the propensity of these drugs to cause hypercoagulability after discontinuation is unknown.HypothesisCompare the PK/PD of clinical dosing regimens of PO apixaban and rivaroxaban administered repeatedly to healthy dogs and assess the effect of abrupt drug discontinuation on coagulation.AnimalsSix University‐owned, purpose‐bred, middle‐aged, mixed‐breed dogs (4 male, 2 female).MethodsDogs were given apixaban or rivaroxaban PO at 0.5 mg/kg q12h for 7 days with a 14‐day washout period between drugs. Plasma drug concentrations were quantitated, and anticoagulant effects were measured using clotting times, calibrated anti‐Xa bioactivity assays, and measurements of thrombin generation. The potential for rebound hypercoagulability was assessed by measuring D‐dimers, thrombin‐antithrombin (TAT) complexes, and antithrombin activity after drug discontinuation.ResultsPlasma drug concentrations and anti‐Xa bioactivities were closely correlated for both drugs, but drug concentrations varied considerably among dogs, despite consistent dose regimens. Thrombin generation variables were significantly correlated with the anti‐Xa bioactivity of both drugs and no significant differences in the effects of apixaban and rivaroxaban on thrombin generation were observed. Drug discontinuation had no effect on D‐dimer concentrations. The concentration of TAT complexes decreased after apixaban discontinuation and did not change after rivaroxaban discontinuation.Conclusions and Clinical ImportanceRepeated PO administration of apixaban or rivaroxaban to healthy dogs produced comparable anticoagulant effects measured by inhibition of thrombin formation. Rebound hypercoagulability after drug discontinuation was not observed and weaning of these drugs in clinical patients might not be necessary.

INTRODUCTION

Thrombosis contributes to morbidity and mortality in dogs with various diseases, including immune‐mediated hemolytic anemia, pancreatitis, protein‐losing disorders, and cancer. 1 , 2 The deleterious effects of thrombosis result from clot propagation, 3 embolization, 4 amplification of inflammation, and direct end‐organ damage. 5 In humans, direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are now preferred to warfarin because of better efficacy, greater safety, and no requirement for frequent monitoring. 6 , 7 Unlike heparin anticoagulants, DOACs do not require parenteral administration. 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Consequently, there is growing interest in the use of these drugs in veterinary medicine. The biological effects and pharmacokinetics of the direct Xa (FXa) inhibitors apixaban 12 , 13 , 14 and rivaroxaban 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 have been characterized in dogs. 14 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 , 27 Additionally, the clinical use of rivaroxaban in dogs has been reported for a cumulative total of 43 cases. 15 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 28 , 29 To date, only preclinical reports of the use of apixaban are available, described in 13 dogs. 12 , 13 Consensus guidelines on antithrombotic use in dogs have been published, but the strength of evidence cited was generally weak and many knowledge gaps remain. 30

Individual DOAC treatment regimens for thrombotic syndromes in humans differ, and the drugs are not used interchangeably because of distinct efficacy and bleeding risk profiles. 31 Meta‐analysis of clinical trials of anticoagulants for venous thromboembolism suggests a lower risk of major bleeding and recurrent thromboembolism with apixaban than other DOACs. 32 , 33 The comparative pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics (PK/PD) of apixaban and rivaroxaban after repeated administration have not been studied in dogs. This information will be critical to inform the development of effective veterinary treatment regimens. Safety concerns exist in humans for increased risk of thrombosis after abrupt discontinuation of rivaroxaban, 34 , 35 but the propensity of the DOACs to cause a similar phenomenon in dogs is unknown.

We aimed to compare the PK/PD of clinical dosing regimens of PO apixaban and rivaroxaban administered repeatedly to healthy dogs. A secondary aim was to determine if abrupt DOAC discontinuation caused rebound hypercoagulability detected using a panel of coagulation assays. It was hypothesized that both drugs would attain stable FXa inhibitory bioactivity after repeated PO administration, that apixaban would induce more potent FXa inhibition than rivaroxaban at the same dosage, and that abrupt DOAC discontinuation would cause rebound hypercoagulability.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Six University‐owned, purpose‐bred mixed‐breed dogs (4 male, 2 female) were used. The median age was 3.5 years (range, 1‐7), median body weight was 17.4 kg (range, 8.0‐29.5) with a median body condition score of 5/9 (range, 4‐5). Dogs were deemed healthy based on normal physical examination findings, CBCs, and serum biochemistry profiles. Dogs were individually housed in the North Carolina State University Laboratory Animal Resources facility, where a commercial maintenance diet (Science Diet Adult, Chicken and Barley, Hill's Pet Nutrition, Topeka, KS) was provided twice daily, with water ad libitum. The local Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Protocol number 23‐167) approved the study.

Drug preparation and dosing

Dogs were given apixaban (0.5 mg/kg PO q12h) for 7 consecutive days (14 doses). After a 14‐day washout period, dogs were given rivaroxaban (0.5 mg/kg PO q12h). Dogs received proprietary whole tablet formulations of apixaban (Eliquis, Bristol‐Myers Squibb, New York, NY) and rivaroxaban (Xarelto, Janssen Pharmaceuticals, Titusville, NJ) to avoid any impact of compounding on drug absorption and in accordance with US Food and Drug Administration guidance. 36 Tablet size limitations precluded administration of exactly 0.5 mg/kg to each dog and thus “as administered” doses were calculated. Drugs were administered by hand pilling before feeding.

Blood collection and sample handling

Blood samples were obtained by direct venipuncture of cephalic, saphenous, or jugular veins by experienced operators using 21G needles and collected directly into evacuated plastic blood tubes containing 3.2% sodium citrate, K2‐EDTA or no additive (S‐Monovette, Sarstedt, Newton, NC). Venipuncture difficulty was recorded using a previously reported scale. 37 A score of 0 represented successful venipuncture with 1 attempt without readjustments of needle position and constant blood flow. A score of 1 represented successful venipuncture with 1 attempt, but with slight readjustment or nearly constant blood flow. A score of 2 was assigned if venipuncture was performed with 1 or 2 attempts, with multiple needle readjustments or moderate interruptions in blood flow. A score of 3 was assigned if numerous attempts at venipuncture or substantial needle repositioning were performed or if a marked interruption of blood flow occurred. Venipuncture sites were closely monitored for bleeding and pressure bandages were applied if necessary.

Blood samples were collected for determination of plasma drug concentration, anti‐Xa bioactivity (aXa), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), fibrinogen concentration, antithrombin activity (AT), and thrombin generation (TG) at baseline (Day 0) and then at 8 subsequent time points over the course of each study phase. Specifically, blood was obtained 4 hours after DOAC administration (corresponding to predicted peak drug concentration) on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 of drug administration, and then 6, 12, 24, and 36 hours after discontinuation. Additional blood samples also were collected at baseline, and 12, 24, and 36 hours after drug discontinuation for measurement of D‐dimers and thrombin‐antithrombin (TAT) complexes to assess for evidence of hypercoagulability. Citrated whole blood samples were centrifuged for 10 minutes at 3500g, 24°C within 30 minutes of collection. Platelet‐poor citrate plasma was separated, transferred into storage cryovials (VWR International, Radnor, PA), and stored at −80°C pending batch analysis. The maximum storage duration was 7 months.

Plasma drug concentration quantitation

Plasma samples were analyzed for apixaban and rivaroxaban by high‐pressure liquid chromatography paired with mass spectrometry detection (LC‐MS) using a validated method developed at the North Carolina State University (NCSU) Clinical Pharmacology Laboratory based on procedures described in a review of analyses of these compounds. 38 The laboratory uses the International Council for Harmonization validation guidelines for analytical methods, 39 and the relevant United States Pharmacopeia guidelines. 40

Samples were thawed at room temperature, followed by liquid phase extraction of apixaban or rivaroxaban from 500 μL of plasma, and reconstitution with a mixture of 60/40 (water/acetonitrile). Samples were injected into the LC‐MS system consisting of a quaternary solvent delivery system at a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min (1260 Solvent Delivery System, Agilent Technologies, Wilmington, DE), an autosampler (1260 Autosampler, Agilent Technologies) and a mass spectrometer (61060A LC/MSD iQ, Agilent Technologies). The mass spectrometer was run in single auto acquire ion monitoring mode, with positive ionization and a mass/charge (m/z) of 460.2 for apixaban and 436.1 for rivaroxaban. Chromatograms were integrated using dedicated software (OpenLAB CDS, Agilent Technologies). A reverse‐phase 4.6 mm × 15 cm C8 column (Zorbax RX‐C8 4.6 × 15; 5 μm) kept at a constant 40°C was used. The mobile phase consisted of 40% acetonitrile and 60% of 0.1% formic acid in water for apixaban and 45% acetonitrile and 55% of 0.1% formic acid in water for rivaroxaban. A fresh mobile phase was prepared, filtered (0.45 μm), and degassed for each run. Calibration curve samples and quality control (QC) samples were prepared by fortifying blank (control) citrated dog plasma with standard solutions of either apixaban or rivaroxaban. Standard spiking solutions were prepared by dissolving relevant analytical reference standards (United States Pharmacopeia) in pure methanol for apixaban and 50% methanol/50% acetonitrile for rivaroxaban. The reference solutions of each drug were used to make spiking solutions to fortify blank (control) citrated dog plasma. Additional standard solution dilutions were made in 40% acetonitrile/60% water. Spiking solutions were stable for 8 weeks at 4°C. The incurred citrated plasma samples were quantified using a 7‐point calibration curve ranging from 10 to 1000 ng/mL and included a blank (0 ng/mL). The limit of quantitation for the assay was 10 ng/mL, determined by the lowest point on the linear calibration curve that met established signal‐to‐noise ratio criteria and calibration curve linearity. Samples with concentrations below the limit of detection were recorded as zero. Fresh calibration standards and QC samples were prepared daily. Incurred samples, calibration samples, and QC samples were prepared by pipetting 500 μL of plasma into clean microcentrifuge tubes and adding 1 mL of acetonitrile. After vortexing, samples were centrifuged for 5 minutes, 1 mL of supernatant was harvested and dried in an evaporator under an airflow of 20 psi at 40°C for 25 minutes. The dry residues were reconstituted with 250 μL of a 60/40 (water/acetonitrile) mixture and loaded into syringeless vials (Whatman UniPrep syringeless filters, pore size 0.2 μm, Cytiva, Marlborough, MA) for injection. Drug recovery from plasma was approximately 100% for apixaban and approximately 80% for rivaroxaban.

Coagulation analyses

All coagulation assays were performed on citrated platelet‐poor plasma samples at a reference laboratory (Comparative Coagulation Laboratory, Cornell University). Samples were thawed at 37°C immediately before analysis. Assays were performed using an automated coagulation analyzer (STA Compact Max, Diagnostica Stago, Parsippany, NJ). The aXa bioactivities of apixaban and rivaroxaban were measured using an assay configured using a bovine FXa reagent and a chromogenic FXa substrate (STA Liquid anti‐Xa, Diagnostica Stago). Results were expressed as ng/mL of aXa bioactivity, based on drug‐specific calibration standards (STA Apixaban Calibrator; STA Rivaroxaban Calibrator, Diagnostica Stago). A mechanical endpoint detection method was used to measure prothrombin time (PT) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) using reagents designed for use in humans (Thromboplastin LI, Helena Diagnostics and Actin FS, Siemens Healthcare respectively). Plasma AT activity was measured using a chromogenic substrate kit (Stachrom AT III, Diagnostica Stago) calibrated with a dog plasma standard assigned a value of 100% activity. Clottable fibrinogen was measured by the Clauss method (STA Fibrinogen, Diagnostica Stago). Plasma D‐dimer concentrations were measured with a quantitative, immunoturbidimetric assay (HemosIL D‐dimer, Werfen, Bedford, MA) using D‐dimer calibration standards designed for use in humans (HemosIL D‐dimer Calibrator, Werfen).

Thrombin generation was measured by the calibrated automated thrombogram method using a dedicated spectrofluorimeter (Thrombinoscope, Diagnostica Stago). Samples were activated using the manufacturer's thromboplastin reagent containing 1 pM tissue factor (PPP‐reagent low, Diagnostica Stago). Replicate assays were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions in microtiter plates using reaction mixtures containing 80 μL test plasma (diluted 1 : 2 in imidazole‐buffered saline) combined with 20 μL of thromboplastin reagent and 20 μL of a fluorogenic substrate/calcium trigger reagent (FluCa buffer, Diagnostica Stago). The thrombin generation (TG) measurements for each sample were calibrated against reactions run in parallel that contained the test plasma and a thrombin‐α2‐macroglobulin complex reagent (Thrombin calibrator, Diagnostica Stago). The variables lag time (minutes), peak thrombin (nM), time to peak (minutes), and overall endogenous thrombin potential (ETP, representing the area under the curve) were calculated using dedicated software (Thrombinoscope, Diagnostica Stago).

TAT complexes were measured by ELISA (Enzygnost TAT Micro, Siemens Healthcare, Marburg, Germany). Hypercoagulability was defined a priori as decreased antithrombin with concurrently increased TAT complexes, fibrinogen, and D‐dimers.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using commercial software (Prism 10.2.3, GraphPad Software, Boston, MA). Comparisons of continuous variables over time were performed using the Friedman test with Dunn's post‐hoc correction except for the rivaroxaban drug concentrations and aXa values where 1 data point was missing. Mixed‐effects analyses with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test were employed for those data. Regression modeling (linear and nonlinear) was used to assess the relationship between plasma DOAC concentrations and aXa bioactivity. Alpha was set at 0.05.

Pharmacokinetic modeling was performed at the NCSU Clinical Pharmacology Laboratory using commercial software (Phoenix WinNonlin, Certara, Princeton, NJ). Noncompartmental analysis was used because only 1 sample was available for measurement on days 1, 3, 5, and 7. Drug concentrations in samples collected 6, 12, 24, and 36 hours after the last dose were used to estimate the terminal half‐life (t ½). To assess PD, plasma concentrations were plotted against aXa bioactivity. Visual inspection of the relationships informed the PK/PD models tested, and the relationship between plasma drug concentrations and aXa bioactivity was used to estimate the maximal concentration effect (E max) and the concentration required for 50% of the maximal effect (EC50).

RESULTS

Apixaban dosing, venipuncture, and blood tests

The 6 dogs received a median apixaban dosage of 0.51 mg/kg (range, 0.45‐0.63) PO q12h. The median venipuncture score across all dogs and all time points was 0 (range, 0‐2) with 2/56 episodes scoring 2. Serum biochemistry and CBC data from baseline and 24 hours after drug discontinuation (Data S1) were normal. Occasional individual analyte concentrations were outside of laboratory reference intervals, but no consistent abnormalities in any dog at either time were observed. Pairwise comparisons between values for individual analytes between baseline and 24 hours after discontinuation identified significant changes in glucose, phosphorus, and magnesium concentrations and aspartate aminotransferase and creatine kinase activities. Most results remained within reference intervals and none of the differences between times were considered clinically relevant. No changes in CBC variables were observed.

Apixaban PK/PD

Plasma apixaban concentrations increased from pretreatment baseline to a median maximum of 738 ng/mL (interquartile range [IQR], 479‐964) on Day 5. The corresponding median peak apixaban aXa bioactivity of 620 ng/mL (IQR, 246‐703) also was achieved on Day 5. Apixaban aXa activities throughout the study were positively correlated with plasma apixaban concentrations (R 2, 0.833; P < .0001; Figure 1). Considerable variation in plasma drug concentrations was observed within and between dogs, with consequent variation in aXa activities. In general, the aXa activities of all dogs appeared to plateau from Day 3 to 7. Upon drug discontinuation, the plasma drug concentrations and the corresponding aXa activities returned to baseline within 24 hours. Pharmacokinetic analysis of plasma apixaban concentrations showed a mean C max of 821.6 ± 295.8 ng/mL and terminal t ½ after the last dose of 7.6 ± 0.7 hours. The estimated E max and EC50 for apixaban were 967 and 448 ng/mL, respectively. The model fit the data well and these derived values match those on the curve (Data S2).

Apixaban administration significantly prolonged the PT relative to baseline on Days 5 and 7, and prolonged the aPTT on Days 1, 3, and 5 (Figure 2). Although the clotting times of some dogs were above the reference interval on some days, the group median values for both PT and aPTT remained within respective reference intervals throughout the study. Clotting times for both tests were significantly correlated with plasma drug concentrations and aXa bioactivity (Data S3). Fibrinogen concentrations were normal at all time points during apixaban treatment and no significant differences were observed between any time points. Antithrombin activities decreased significantly between baseline and 12 and 36 hours after discontinuation, but values remained within the reference interval at all time points (Data S3). Concentrations of TAT complexes significantly decreased between baseline and 12, 24, and 36 hours post‐discontinuation. No changes in D‐dimer concentration were observed between baseline and any time point after drug discontinuation (Data S3). Compared with baseline, apixaban administration significantly decreased peak thrombin and ETP, and increased lag time and time to peak from Day 3 until discontinuation (Figure 3). All TG variables were significantly correlated with apixaban aXa bioactivity (Data S3).

Rivaroxaban dosing, venipuncture, and bloodwork

The 6 dogs received a median rivaroxaban dosage of 0.51 mg/kg (range, 0.45‐0.59) PO q12h. The median venipuncture score across all dogs and all time points was 0 (range, 0‐2), with 2/54 episodes scoring 2. Serum biochemistry and CBC data from baseline and 24 hours after drug discontinuation (Data S1) were normal. Occasional individual analyte concentrations were outside laboratory reference intervals, but no consistent abnormalities were observed in any dog at either time. Pairwise comparisons between values for individual analytes between baseline and 24 hours after discontinuation identified a significant increase in blood glucose concentration, and a significant decrease in serum bicarbonate concentration. All results remained within reference intervals, and none of the differences between times were considered clinically relevant. Mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration, reticulocyte percentage, mean platelet volume, and plateletcrit significantly decreased between baseline and 24 hours post‐discontinuation, but erythrocyte counts, hemoglobin concentrations, absolute reticulocyte counts, and platelet counts were unchanged. As such, none of the differences between times were considered clinically relevant.

Rivaroxaban PK/PD

Plasma rivaroxaban concentrations increased from baseline to a median maximum of 161 ng/mL (IQR, 113‐169) on Day 3 (Figure 4). The aXa activities of rivaroxaban were positively correlated to the plasma drug concentrations (R 2, 0.958, P < .0001, Figure 4). Consequently, the median peak rivaroxaban aXa bioactivity 136 ng/mL (IQR, 100‐147) also was achieved on Day 3. As with apixaban, considerable variation in plasma drug concentrations was observed within and between dogs. A stable plateau of drug concentration and aXa bioactivity was less apparent for rivaroxaban than for apixaban, predominantly because of decreased drug concentrations for 2 of 6 dogs on Day 5 compared with results on Days 3 and 7. As with apixaban, plasma drug concentrations and aXa bioactivity returned to baseline within 24 hours after drug discontinuation. Pharmacokinetic analysis of plasma rivaroxaban concentrations showed a mean C max of 161 ± 58 ng/mL and a terminal t ½ after the last dose of 4.0 ± 2.1 hours. The estimated E max and EC50 for rivaroxaban were 213 and 111 ng/mL, respectively. For rivaroxaban, the curve did not plateau and hence a maximal effect was not attained. As such, the E max and EC50 are higher than shown on the plot. A linear relationship therefore was assumed, and the E max and EC50 values were taken directly from the data (Data S2).

Rivaroxaban administration significantly prolonged the PT relative to baseline on Days 1, 3, and 7, and prolonged the aPTT on Days 1, 3, 5, and 7 (Figure 5). The median PT was above the reference interval throughout the treatment period but normalized rapidly after drug discontinuation. The median aPTT was above the reference interval on Days 1 and 7. Clotting times were significantly correlated with plasma drug concentrations and aXa bioactivity (Data S4). DISCUSSION

We compared the PK/PD of PO apixaban and rivaroxaban administered twice daily at 0.5 mg/kg for 7 days to 6 healthy middle‐aged mixed‐breed dogs. Both drugs demonstrated aXa bioactivity that peaked after 3 to 5 days of treatment, albeit with variations within and between dogs in drug concentration and corresponding bioactivity. For both drugs, aXa bioactivity was not detectable within 24 hours of discontinuation. Both drugs had similar inhibitory effects on the onset, rate, and maximal extent of thrombin formation when administered at the same dosage. No evidence of a rebound hypercoagulable state was detected after the abrupt discontinuation of either drug. Neither drug affected CBC or serum biochemistry variables after administration for 7 days, and no adverse effects or evidence of bleeding were observed.

Strong correlations between the plasma concentrations of apixaban and rivaroxaban and the aXa activities measured by calibrated chromogenic assays were observed. This observation is consistent with the findings of other studies of DOACs in dogs and other species, 14 , 41 , 42 and confirms that measures of drug bioactivity in calibrated aXa assays correspond to circulating drug concentrations. Factor Xa inhibitory bioactivity was detected within 1 day of initiating both drugs in all dogs, with significant differences from pretreatment baseline after 1 day of apixaban and 3 days of rivaroxaban. On average, aXa bioactivity plateaued after 3 days for both drugs. The substantial variation in drug concentrations within and between dogs was surprising but is consistent with other reports of these drugs in this species. 14 , 19 We used proprietary tablets to mimic clinical practice and eliminate potential influence from compounding procedures or gelatin capsule formulations. Dogs were given DOAC tablets before eating and each dog was visually confirmed to have swallowed the tablets after every dose. Moreover, prior studies of rivaroxaban suggest that drug absorption is unaffected by the presence of food in the gastrointestinal tract. 43 Based on prior studies, blood samples were collected 4 hours after dosing to estimate peak plasma concentrations. 14 , 43 However, in individual dogs, the timing of peak plasma concentrations likely varied and some dogs may have achieved peak anticoagulant effect before or after blood sample collection thereby introducing an additional source of variation in our data. We used identical drug dosing regimens for both DOACs to eliminate 1 source of variation that could influence comparisons of the 2 drugs. Once daily dosing has been used in several studies of rivaroxaban in dogs, 24 , 44 , 45 and most dogs in a recent case series received the drug once daily. 19 The short half‐life of rivaroxaban in dogs, however, indicates that twice daily dosing might provide a more durable anticoagulant effect, consistent with a previous report. 24 Notably, twice daily dosing of rivaroxaban in humans decreases variation in plasma drug concentrations. 46 We did not compare once‐daily with twice‐daily dosing in our study, but this comparison warrants investigation in treatment trials to optimize rivaroxaban usage in dogs. The observed variability in plasma drug concentrations suggests that some monitoring of aXa activities in dogs receiving these DOACs might be warranted, particularly in dogs with poor clinical response. A previous study of dogs with confirmed or suspected thrombosis and receiving rivaroxaban treatment found no relationship between the initial rivaroxaban dose and the aXa bioactivity measured 2 to 4 hours after dosing. 19 In that study, dogs had various underlying diseases, and received a range of dosages and a variable duration of treatment before sampling. The variation observed in that study also could have resulted from concurrent medication administration, interindividual differences in absorption and bioavailability, and the effects of tablet sizes and tablet splitting on the dosage absorbed or administered. To eliminate some of these sources of variation, we used healthy dogs not receiving any other medications, and only whole tablets were administered. Several tablet sizes were used to achieve consistent dosages, but doing so likely did not impact our data because they are bioequivalent between sizes. The combined results of ours and previous studies suggest that the bioavailability of apixaban and rivaroxaban is less consistent in dogs than in humans.

Both DOACs significantly prolonged clotting time test results in the absence of any evidence of bleeding. With apixaban, the observed clotting time changes were minor, and median values remained within reference intervals throughout treatment. In contrast, during rivaroxaban treatment, many dogs had prolonged clotting times, and median values were above the reference interval. Approximately 20% of PT values were >10% prolonged, and 10% were >25% prolonged relative to the upper reference interval bound. These differential effects of apixaban and rivaroxaban on clotting time test results have been described in humans, 47 , 48 with rivaroxaban inducing more clotting time prolongation than apixaban. The sensitivity of these tests to these drugs varies greatly, depending on reagent composition, and the use of different coagulation testing instruments that employ distinct endpoint detection methods (eg, photo‐optical compared with mechanical endpoint detection). 49 , 50 , 51 Previous studies in healthy dogs observed variable prolongation of clotting times after treatment with either drug or distinct relationships among drug concentration, aXa bioactivity, and clotting times. 13 , 14 , 24 , 45 , 52 , 53 A study of 10 dogs given rivaroxaban at a dosage of 1 mg/kg/day found no effect on a whole blood point‐of‐care PT assay. 44 A second study using a different assay methodology observed a significant prolongation of PT for 8 dogs given rivaroxaban at 2 mg/kg/day. 24 A third study of 9 dogs given rivaroxaban at 1.5 mg/kg/day observed significant PT prolongation after 2 days of treatment using the same assay we employed. 45 The effects of apixaban on aPTT and PT in dogs also have varied among studies. The PT was prolonged at 4, 6, and 8 hours after PO administration of 0.2 mg/kg apixaban, 14 but the aPTT was not affected by PO apixaban in that study. In contrast, in a separate study using different clotting time tests, 0.5 mg/kg apixaban prolonged PT at 3 hours, but not at 8 hours, whereas 1 mg/kg prolonged PT and aPTT at 3 hours but not at 8 hours. 13 The observed differences among studies, assays, drug dosages, and subjects, illustrate the practical difficulties of using aPTT or PT to monitor DOAC treatment. Anti‐Xa bioactivity assays are recommended for situations requiring therapeutic monitoring in human medicine. 54 , 55

The aXa assays represent the bioactivity of the DOACs based on the inhibition of an FXa reagent, rather than the animal's endogenous FXa. The clotting time test endpoints reflect the formation of a threshold level of thrombin sufficient to generate a fibrin clot. Thus, clotting time assays are influenced by the sample fibrinogen content and are only sensitive to substantial deficiencies or dysfunction of clotting factors. 56 We therefore employed a TG assay to better gauge the PD of the DOACs. The TG assay system measures endogenous FXa function through its ability to participate in prothrombin cleavage as a constituent of the prothrombinase complex. The TG assays are dynamic measures of the amount of thrombin generated over time and are well suited for monitoring anticoagulant intensity. 24 , 57 , 58 , 59 In humans, TG assays are preferred to aXa or clotting times for the prediction of bleeding or thrombosis risk in individuals. 60 In our study, apixaban and rivaroxaban significantly delayed and inhibited thrombin formation and the TG variables correlated strongly with plasma drug concentrations. No differences in the degree of inhibition of onset, rate, or maximal extent of thrombin formation were identified between the 2 drugs, indicating that apixaban and rivaroxaban produce comparable anticoagulant effects when administered at the same dosage.

Throughout the administration of both drugs, fibrinogen concentrations, and AT activities remained within the reference intervals and no clinically relevant changes were observed. Significant decreases in AT activity occurred at 12 and 36 hours after discontinuation of apixaban compared with baseline, but all results remained >85%. Moreover, no evidence of procoagulant imbalance was observed because D‐dimer concentrations did not increase and TAT complex concentrations decreased. No significant changes were detected in fibrinogen, AT, TAT complexes, or D‐dimers during or after rivaroxaban administration. As such, there is no indication of rebound hypercoagulability after abrupt discontinuation of these drugs in healthy dogs and it might not be necessary to wean clinical patients from these drugs. However, it is unknown if the risk of rebound thrombosis differs in clinical patients with underlying diseases.

Our study had some limitations. Only 1 dosage of each drug was evaluated in a small number of healthy dogs. A clinically relevant dose was administered to a diverse group of middle‐aged mixed‐breed dogs, but these dogs might not reflect the population treated with these medications in clinical practice. Attempts were made to control as many potential sources of variation from drug administration, sample processing, and assay performance as possible to enhance comparison of the 2 drugs. As such, our results might represent optimal conditions and hence be less generalizable to clinical practice. Notably, no individual dog was consistently the cause of variation within or between drugs. A sparse sampling population PK approach 61 in clinical patients receiving these drugs will be needed to determine the extent and source of interindividual variability among sick dogs receiving these drugs. Our study attempted to measure peak concentrations, but trough concentrations that might indicate drug bioactivity duration were not assessed. However, trough concentrations can be estimated from the observed terminal t ½. Dogs were dosed for 7 days to ensure that steady‐state conditions were reached. The average t ½ was 7.6 and 4 hours for apixaban and rivaroxaban, respectively, and steady state is reached after ~5 half‐lives, making it likely this goal was achieved. The PD of these drugs was studied using in vitro assays, but in vivo assessments of anticoagulant efficacy were not performed. As such, we cannot determine if the inhibition of thrombin formation observed is relevant for therapeutic or prophylactic anticoagulation in clinical patients. In vivo thrombotic model systems such as venous stasis, 62 or pulmonary embolism 63 , 64 , 65 could be used to assess the prophylactic or therapeutic effect of the dosages studied here, but ultimately, clinical trials involving dogs with naturally occurring diseases will be needed to determine if they provide real‐world benefits.

In our study, both drugs were administered at a dosage of 0.5 mg/kg PO q12h. This dosage translated to an apixaban C max of 822 ng/mL and to a rivaroxaban C max of 161 ng/mL. For comparison, maximum peak concentrations observed for humans taking apixaban range from approximately 150 to 575 ng/mL. 66 For rivaroxaban, maximum peak concentrations vary by indication (eg, venous thromboembolism prevention approximately 200 ng/mL; deep vein thrombosis treatment and stroke prevention in the context of atrial fibrillation, 360 ng/mL). 67 , 68 , 69 Comparable studies remain to be performed in dogs and cats and thus it is uncertain what plasma concentration is required for thromboprophylaxis or treatment of in situ thrombosis. This requirement also may vary by individual animal and underlying thrombotic risk profile.

In summary, repeated PO administration of 0.5 mg/kg q12h of apixaban or rivaroxaban to healthy dogs produced aXa bioactivity results strongly correlated with the corresponding plasma drug concentrations. Both drugs resulted in comparable inhibition of thrombin formation. Considerable inter‐ and intraindividual variation occurred in plasma drug concentrations and resulting anticoagulant effects. This variation, despite consistent dosing, suggests that drug monitoring should be included in clinical trials and considered in dogs receiving these drugs. Calibrated aXa assays measure circulating drug bioactivity, whereas TG assays provide PD information regarding anticoagulant intensity over time. Because of their variable sensitivities, clotting time tests are less useful for therapeutic drug monitoring but could provide information on bleeding risk associated with drug overdose. We found no indication that rebound hypercoagulability occurred after drug discontinuation, and gradual dosage reduction before the cessation of treatment might be unnecessary. Our findings in healthy dogs provide the basis for designing future treatment trials comparing the clinical effects of apixaban and rivaroxaban anticoagulant treatment in dogs with thrombotic disorders.
