H2O2-responsive M2 macrophage membrane-camouflaged biomimetic nanosystems to dual-targeted delivery tanshinone IIA for enhanced anti-atherosclerosis
Authors: Yubin Liang, Jialin Huang, Guangping Wu, Ling Yu, Keer Zhang, Peipei Chen, Xu Zou, Minzhou Zhang, Judun Zheng, Yuhui Liao, Shuai Mao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAtherosclerosis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by pathological vascular alterations, including endothelial cell dysfunction and apoptosis, macrophage infiltration, lipid deposition within the intima. Due to this complex pathophysiology, current therapeutic approaches lack effective systems for the precise delivery of antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agents to the affected sites.ResultsHerein we developed a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive M2 macrophage membrane-camouflaged biomimetic drug delivery nanosystem (MM@TIIA-NPs) to dual-targeted delivery tanshinone IIA for enhanced anti-atherosclerosis. The dual-targeting capability arises from the combination of M2 macrophage membrane-mediated biological targeting, which promotes preferential accumulation at inflammatory atherosclerotic plaques while reducing reticuloendothelial system clearance, and H2O2-responsive microenvironmental targeting, which enables site-specific drug release within the oxidative plaque microenvironment. Following plaque accumulation, MM@TIIA-NPs would be internalized by the macrophages and vascular smooth muscle cells, leading to the reduced inflammatory responses and protection against apoptosis triggered by oxidative stress. Furthermore, these nanoparticles impeded foam cell development by limiting the absorption of oxidized low-density lipoproteins. In an established atherosclerotic zebrafish model, MM@TIIA-NPs demonstrated notable inhibition of lipid deposition, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses, while restoring endothelial function. Similarly, the biocompatible MM@TIIA-NPs can preferentially accumulate in atherosclerotic lesions, resulting in significant reduction in necrotic core size, decreased macrophage infiltration and plaques stabilization in vivo studies conducted in ApoE−/− mice.ConclusionIn conclusion, MM@TIIA-NPs represent a promising therapeutic strategy for atherosclerosis management.Graphical Abstract Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-026-04638-1.

Introduction

Atherosclerosis is a major underlying cause of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular conditions, including heart attack and stroke; thus, it remains a dominant global health threat [1]. Its development begins with endothelial dysfunction, which promotes the deposition of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (oxLDL) within the intima, excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and chronic inflammation [2]. Macrophages, central players in this process, differentiate into pro-inflammatory M1 or anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes depending on microenvironmental cues [3]. The predominance of M1 macrophages amplifies inflammation, foam cell formation, and plaque instability, whereas M2 macrophages exert anti-inflammatory and reparative effects that may favor plaque regression [4]. Consequently, therapeutic strategies that suppress oxidative stress and inflammation while promoting vascular homeostasis hold great promise for atherosclerosis management [5].

Current pharmacological interventions, such as statins and proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PSK9) inhibitors, primarily reduce circulating cholesterol but achieve only partial reductions in cardiovascular events and are often accompanied by adverse effects [6]. In contrast, natural compounds like tanshinone IIA (TIIA), derived from Salvia miltiorrhiza, have shown potent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity, especially in facilitating macrophage polarization toward the M2 phenotype [7]. Despite the potent efficacy of chemical drugs, natural products such as TIIA are considered an ideal choice for atherosclerosis therapy due to their low toxicity, well-established clinical safety profile, suitability for long-term use, and fewer side effects. However, both chemotherapeutic agents and phytochemicals face significant challenges in systemic administration, including poor bioavailability, nonspecific distribution, and inadequate accumulation at atherosclerotic lesions [8]. Addressing the limitations of current drug delivery systems is crucial, and innovative targeted delivery systems are essential for overcoming these challenges.

Nanotechnology has emerged as a powerful strategy to address these challenges, enabling controlled release, prolonged circulation, and targeted delivery to diseased tissues [9]. Metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) and polymer-based nanocarriers have been explored for antioxidant and anti-inflammatory therapies, with responsive polymers designed to release drugs under pathological conditions such as elevated ROS [10]. However, conventional nanoparticles are rapidly recognized and cleared by the reticuloendothelial system (RES), limiting their clinical translation [11]. To overcome this limitation, biomimetic nanocarriers coated with natural cell membranes have gained attention for their ability to evade immune clearance and actively home to target tissues [12]. For example, red blood cells, platelets, and macrophage membrane-coated nanoparticles have demonstrated enhanced circulation and targeted delivery in inflammatory and infectious disease models [13]. Such systems represent an attractive platform for developing effective anti-atherosclerotic therapies [14].

Herein, we report the design of a hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-responsive biomimetic nanosystem (MM@TIIA-NPs) that integrates an ROS-sensitive nanoparticle core loaded with TIIA and camouflaged with M2 macrophage membranes. This system achieves enhanced therapeutic efficacy through a synergistic dual-targeting strategy: (1) biological spatial targeting, where the M2 macrophage membrane facilitates active homing to inflamed vasculature and evasion of RES clearance; and (2) microenvironmental stimuli-responsive targeting, where the H2O2-sensitive core ensures on-demand TIIA release specifically within the oxidative plaque environment. These nanoparticles accumulate preferentially in atherosclerotic lesions, where they attenuate inflammation, inhibit foam cell formation, reduce oxidative stress, and stabilize plaques. Preclinical evaluations in zebrafish and ApoE−/− mouse models demonstrated that MM@TIIA-NPs significantly improved endothelial function, reduced lipid deposition, and suppressed macrophage infiltration, highlighting their potential as a novel therapeutic strategy for atherosclerosis (Fig. 1).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and evaluation of ROS-responsive MM@TIIA-NPs

The ROS-responsive oxidation-sensitive chitosan oligosaccharide (Oxi-COS) was synthesized by grafting the hydrophobic small molecule (imidazole carbamate) onto the backbone of naturally hydrophilic COS [15]. The conjugation of Oxi-COS was verified by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 1a), confirming the successful integration of the phenylboronic acid pinacol ester moieties. Subsequently, nanoparticles loaded with the hydrophobic compound TIIA (TIIA-NPs) were generated via a simple self-assembly process (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 1b). We first verified the successful synthesis of the products by FTIR (Fig. 2b). The FTIR spectrum of CDI-PBAP retained the methyl stretching vibration peak of the PBAP structure at approximately ~ 2900 cm⁻¹. Meanwhile, a new strong absorption peak appeared at approximately ~ 1760 cm⁻¹, which was attributed to the carbonyl (C = O) stretching vibration of the carbamate intermediate formed after CDI activation, indicating that CDI had successfully reacted with the hydroxyl group of PBAP to generate CDI-PBAP. In the spectrum of Oxi-COS, the carbonyl peak of CDI-PBAP at 1760 cm⁻¹ completely disappeared, while a new absorption peak emerged at 1705 cm⁻¹. This red shift (from 1760 cm⁻¹ to 1705 cm⁻¹) was attributed to the carbonyl stretching vibration of the newly formed carbamate bond (-NH-CO-O-), confirming the covalent conjugation between CDI-PBAP and the amino group of COS, and thus the successful synthesis of Oxi-COS. The drug encapsulation efficiency (DEE) of the TIIA-NPs was 96.5% with a drug loading content (DLC) of 2%. As depicted in Fig. 2c and d, when exposed to ROS-mimicking conditions (1.00 mM H2O2), the nanoparticles underwent degradation, releasing TIIA in a controlled manner. Cumulative drug release studies (Fig. 2d) revealed that in ROS-rich environments, drug release reached approximately 60% within 24 h, in contrast to 18% under non-ROS conditions, confirming the ROS-responsive release mechanism. This sensitivity to ROS is attributed to the oxidation of the phenylboronic ester groups. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that the TIIA-NPs were spherical in structure and displayed a homogenous size profile, averaging approximately 150 nm in diameter, with no evidence of agglomeration (Fig. 2e). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) revealed that the average diameter of TIIA-NPs was 116 nm, while the particle size of MM was 160 nm, and the MM@TIIA-NPs exhibited a slight increase in particle size, the increased diameter indicated the successful coating of the macrophage membranes (~ 5–10 nm in thickness) on the surface of the TIIA-NPs (Fig. 2f). Additionally, the average particle distribution coefficients (PDI) were 0.178 ± 0.021 in the TIIA-NPs group, 0.186 ± 0.059 in the MM group, and 0.202 ± 0.039 in the MM@TIIA-NPs group (Supplementary Fig. 1c). To assess the size stability of the three NPs, they were suspended in saline and FBS and stored at room temperature for 15 days. As demonstrated in Supplementary Fig. 1d, the sizes of the MM@TIIA-NPs remained almost constant, indicating that the coating of cell membranes effectively enhanced the size stability of the NPs.

To enhance the targeting specificity, the nanoparticles were further coated with activated M2 macrophage membranes to form MM@TIIA-NPs (Fig. 2a). TEM showed a core-shell structure with an approximately 10 nm-thick membrane surrounding the nanoparticle core (Fig. 2e). MM@TIIA-NPs showed reduced negative zeta potential relative to free TIIA-NPs, reflecting the surface properties of macrophages (Fig. 2g). To verify that MM@TIIA-NPs respond to endogenous stimuli rather than just exogenous H2O2 solutions, we simulated the plaque microenvironment using an oxLDL-induced macrophage model. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 2., RAW264.7 cells stimulated with oxLDL exhibited a significant increase in intracellular fluorescence, indicating that endogenous ROS successfully triggered the oxidative cleavage of the aryl boronic ester linker (Supplementary Fig. 2). Moreover, western blot analysis identified key macrophage markers such as TLR4 (LPS receptor), integrin β1, CCR2 (a receptor for MCP-1 in atherosclerotic plaques), and SRA (oxLDL receptor), which validated the successful functionalization and retention of macrophage membrane proteins on the MM@TIIA-NPs (Fig. 2h, i).

In vitro biocompatibility and cellular uptake

The cytotoxicity of the MM@TIIA-NPs was evaluated across three key cell types involved in atherosclerosis: human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs), and RAW264.7 macrophages. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 3, cell viability rates exceeded 85% even at the highest tested concentration (50 µg ml− 1), confirming their excellent biocompatibility and potential safety in therapeutic applications. Macrophages of the RES undergo phagocytosis and clear foreign substances in vivo [16]. Avoiding phagocytosis and clearance is essential to enhance the efficacy of drug delivery systems. The cellular uptake of TIIA-NPs and MM@TIIA-NPs by RAW264.7 macrophages was investigated using confocal microscopy and flow cytometry. Confocal microscopy revealed strong red fluorescence signals in cells treated with Cy5.5-labeled TIIA-NPs, indicating substantial intracellular uptake. In contrast, macrophages treated with the MM@TIIA-NPs exhibited a significantly reduced red fluorescence intensity, demonstrating the immune evasion capability conferred by the M2 macrophage membrane coating (Fig. 3a). Quantitative flow cytometry confirmed these findings. The TIIA-NPs exhibited a time-dependent increase in macrophage internalization, with fluorescence intensity increasing over the incubation period. However, the MM@TIIA-NPs exhibited significantly attenuated uptake at all time points, validating their reduced clearance by macrophages (Fig. 3c).

Given that macrophages reside within the inflammatory microenvironment of atherosclerotic plaques, we investigated whether inflammatory stimuli would alter the phagocytic response to TIIA-NPs and MM@TIIA-NPs. RAW264.7 cells were exposed to interferon-γ (IFN-γ) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) for 12 h to mimic an inflamed microenvironment. Quantitative flow cytometry analysis revealed that inflammatory stimulation significantly enhanced the macrophage internalization of TIIA-NPs, as evidenced by an increase in fluorescence intensity (Fig.3 b, c and Supplementary Fig. 4). In comparison, the MM@TIIA-NPs exhibited limited changes in uptake under inflammatory conditions, further supporting their immune evasion properties (Fig. 3b, c). Furthermore, we evaluated the cellular uptake characteristics of MM@TIIA-NPs in both VSMCs and HUVECs using quantitative flow cytometry. Both cell types showed significantly stronger phagocytosis of MM@TIIA-NP/Cy5.5 than TIIA-NP/Cy5.5 (Fig. 3 d, e). Moreover, in the inflammatory microenvironment, both HUVECs and VSMCs showed significantly enhanced recruitment of MM@TIIA-NP/Cy5.5 (red) relative to that of TIIA-NP/Cy5.5 (Fig. 3 d, e). This finding demonstrated that the M2 macrophage membrane enhanced the ability of MM@TIIA-NPs to penetrate and specifically target both HUVECs and VSMCs within atherosclerotic plaques, thereby improving the accumulation of TIIA at the lesion site.

Antioxidation, anti-apoptosis, and anti-inflammatory properties of MM@TIIA-NPs

ROS play vital roles in the regulation of multiple functions within vascular cells. However, when their production exceeds the body’s antioxidant capacity of the body, they can lead to oxidative stress, which triggers an inflammatory cycle that further amplifies oxidative stress [17, 18]. The next series of experiments focused on whether MM@TIIA-NPs could inhibit ROS generation in macrophages. RAW264.7 cells, when treated with 100 ng ml− 1 LPS and 100 IU ml− 1 IFN-γ, exhibited significantly elevated ROS levels. Upon treatment with MM@TIIA-NPs, the mean fluorescent signals of ROS decreased, indicating the effect of these nanoparticles on attenuating ROS production (Fig. 4a, b). Flow cytometry analysis confirmed that the MM@TIIA-NPs effectively suppressed intracellular ROS levels in LPS/IFN-γ-stimulated macrophages (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Fig. 4).

Moreover, pre-treatment with MM@TIIA-NPs effectively inhibited the apoptosis of RAW264.7 cells triggered by H2O2 (Fig. 4d, Supplementary Fig. 5). Excessive ROS levels can lead to the oxidation of DNA, lipids and proteins [19]. Therefore, to further investigate the protective role of MM@TIIA-NPs, we measured the intracellular concentrations of 8-hydroxy-2’-deoxyguanosine (8-OHdG) and 8-isoprostaglandin F2α (8-iso-PGF2α) as indicators of ROS-induced oxidative damage. Consistent with previous research, exposure to H2O2 led to a marked elevation in the levels of both 8-OHdG and 8-iso-PGF2α in RAW264.7 cells. However, pre-treatment with either TIIA or TIIA-NPs significantly reduced the levels of these biomarkers (Fig. 4e, f). Notably, MM@TIIA-NPs exhibited a more robust protective effect than did free TIIA or TIIA-NPs, highlighting their potential as a superior therapeutic agent to mitigate ROS-induced oxidative damage (Fig. 4e, f). Collectively, these findings indicate that MM@TIIA-NPs can safeguard macrophages from oxidative injury, thereby reducing cell apoptosis by mitigating intracellular ROS production.

The next phase of our study assessed whether MM@TIIA-NPs could attenuate inflammatory responses. Upon stimulating RAW264.7 cells with LPS/IFN-γ, a significant increase in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), was observed (Fig. 4g, h,i). When macrophages were pre-treated with MM@TIIA-NPs for 2 h, there was a significant decrease was observed in the levels of these factors. In contrast, treatment with an equivalent dose of TIIA resulted in a minimal impact on TNF-α, MCP-1 and IL-1β. Previous research has suggested that intracellular ROS can initiate the activation of various signal transduction pathways, thereby modulating the inflammatory injury in atherosclerosis [20]. Also, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 6, MM@TIIA-NPs significantly reduced the expression of iNOS and CD86 (M1 markers) while upregulating CD206 and Arg-1 (M2 markers), confirming a successful M1-to-M2 phenotypic shift. Our results demonstrate that MM@TIIA-NPs can alleviate inflammation within macrophages by mitigating intracellular ROS production, underscoring their potential therapeutic efficacy in mitigating the inflammatory responses associated with atherosclerosis.

Suppression of oxLDL uptake and foam cell development by MM@TIIA-NPs

The accumulation of foam cells in the vascular intimais a pivotal event in the development of atherosclerosis in blood vessels [21]. This process is largely driven by the internalization of oxLDL by macrophages and VSMCs [22]. To explore this process, we evaluated how MM@TIIA-NPs treatment influences oxLDL uptake in RAW264.7 macrophages and VSMCs (Fig. 5a, b). After 3 h of incubation with DiI-labeled oxLDL, RAW264.7 cells exhibited prominent red fluorescence in the cytoplasm (Fig. 5a). Pre-treatment with TIIA led to a modest reduction in the intracellular uptake of DiI-oxLDL, whereas pre-incubation with MM@TIIA-NPs significantly reduced the fluorescent signals from DiI-oxLDL in RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. 5a, d). The reduced cellular uptake and enhanced efficacy of MM@TIIA-NPs reflect a dual-functional advantage where ‘self’ markers enable immune evasion from non-specific phagocytosis (Fig. 3a), while the M2 membrane acts as an extracellular interceptor using native scavenger receptors to sequester oxLDL.

Similarly, in VSMCs, pre-treatment with MM@TIIA-NPs demonstrated a clear inhibitory effect on oxLDL uptake (Fig. 5b, e). This fluorescence-based observation was further validated by flow cytometric analysis, which exhibited a substantial reduction in the intracellular presence of DiI-oxLDL in the MM@TIIA-NPs-treated group compared with that in the TIIA-NPs or TIIA groups (Supplementary Fig. 7). These findings highlight the superior efficacy of MM@TIIA-NPs in inhibiting oxLDL uptake by both RAW264.7 macrophages and VSMCs, suggesting a promising approach for atherosclerosis intervention.

The impact of MM@TIIA-NPs on the development of foam cells was then further evaluated. Upon stimulation with oxLDL, macrophages exhibited significant intracellular lipid accumulation and prominent foam cell formation, as indicated by Oil Red O (ORO) staining (Fig.5c, top panel). In contrast, pre-treating with MM@TIIA-NPs resulted in suppressed foam cell formation. This was corroborated by the quantitative analysis of intracellular ORO, which revealed a notable reduction in foam cell formation following MM@TIIA-NPs treatment (Fig. 5f). Similarly, the VSMCs treated with MM@TIIA-NPs exhibited a marked reduction in oxLDL-induced foam cell formation (Fig. 5c, bottom panel; Fig. 5g). These results are consistent with the observed reduction in oxLDL uptake and suggest that MM@TIIA-NPs can mitigate foam cell formation, which is a critical step in atherosclerosis development.

To further evaluate the endothelial-protective effects of MM@TIIA-NPs, we performed a series of biochemical and functional assays in HUVECs. ELISA results showed that oxLDL stimulation markedly increased the secretion of VCAM-1 and ICAM-1, whereas MM@TIIA-NPs significantly suppressed their release, indicating attenuation of endothelial inflammation (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). In addition, MM@TIIA-NPs restored the oxLDL-induced reduction of eNOS expression (Supplementary Fig. 8c), suggesting recovery of endothelial homeostasis. Functionally, oxLDL severely impaired tube formation ability in HUVECs, while MM@TIIA-NP pretreatment preserved angiogenic capacity, as demonstrated by increased tube length and branch points (Supplementary Fig. 8e). Together, these findings indicate that MM@TIIA-NPs protect endothelial cells from inflammatory activation and preserve their physiological function.

Therapeutic effects of MM@TIIA-NPs in the zebrafish model of atherosclerosis

The zebrafish model has been widely recognized as an ideal system for studying atherosclerosis owing to its genetic tractability and suitability for live imaging [23]. Disorders of lipid metabolism in the blood vessels are significant risk factors for atherosclerosis [24]. Therefore, we employed the wild-type AB-line and albino zebrafish to establish an atherosclerosis model to assess the effects of MM@TIIA-NPs on the lipid levels in zebrafish blood vessels (Fig. 6a). Considerable accumulation of triglycerides and cholesterol was observed near the blood vessel tail in the model group relative to that in the control group (Fig. 6b, c). Furthermore, administration of both TIIA and TIIA-NPs significantly decreased lipid deposits in zebrafish. Importantly, the most significant difference was found between the MM@TIIA-NPs and model groups (Fig. 6b and c). Consistently, the LDL cholesterol (LDL-C) level considerably increased in the model group compared with that in the control group, but this increase was markedly counteracted by MM@TIIA-NPs (Supplementary Fig. 9a).

To evaluate the impact of MM@TIIA-NPs on inflammatory responses in atherosclerosis, transgenic zebrafish lines Tg(mpeg1:EGFP) and Tg(mpx:EGFP) were employed to assess macrophage and neutrophil recruitment in blood vessels (Fig. 6a). The atherosclerotic model group exhibited pronounced infiltration of both macrophages and neutrophils in the blood vessels. Importantly, treatment with MM@TIIA-NPs significantly reduced the infiltration of inflammatory cells (Fig. 6d, Supplementary Fig. 9b).

Under normal physiological conditions in healthy mammalian blood vessels, endothelial cells form a uniform layer aligned in the direction of laminar blood flow [25]. However, when lipid deposits accumulate in the intima and trigger endothelial cell activation, the endothelial layer thickens and blood flow slows, as observed in the early lesions of hypercholesterolemic mice. In the zebrafish with atherosclerosis, an increase in the thickness of the endothelial cell layer was observed in the fundus vasculature (Fig. 6e). Notably, treatment with MM@TIIA-NPs resulted in a dramatic reduction in the thickness of the endothelial layer in the fundus blood vessels compared to that in the model group in Tg(fli-1:EGFP) zebrafish (Fig. 6e).

To assess the impact of MM@TIIA-NPs on oxidative stress in a zebrafish atherosclerosis model, we investigated the ROS activity. The findings indicated that the ROS levels were considerably elevated in the model group compared with that in the control group (Fig. 6f). However, treatment with MM@TIIA-NPs resulted in a marked reduction in the ROS levels (Fig. 6f).

Hemodynamic factors play an increasingly recognized role in the pathophysiology of atherosclerosis [26]. Low and oscillating wall shear stress that change directions are associated with early stages of atherosclerosis. Our results showed that treatment with MM@TIIA-NPs led to an increase in blood flow velocity, further highlighting its potential to improve the hemodynamic conditions in atherosclerotic blood vessels (Fig. 6g).

Therapeutic effects of MM@TIIA-NPs injection in ApoE−/−mice

Building on promising results obtained from the previous experiments, we evaluated the therapeutic impact of MM@TIIA-NPs in a mouse model of atherosclerosis. Atherosclerotic plaque formation was observed to initiate after 1 month of a high-fat diet in ApoE−/− mice, with notable plaque accumulation evident after 3 months. For preventive therapy, we started administering MM@TIIA-NPs after 1 month of the high-fat diet and continued for an additional 2 months. The mice were allocated randomly to various treatment groups, each receiving distinct formulations along with the Western diet for 2 months (Fig. 7a). The formulations were administered by intravenous injection once weekly throughout the treatment period.

To identify the optimal time for in vivo plaque targeting studies, we evaluated the pharmacokinetics of Cy5.5-labeled MM@TIIA-NPs (Cy5.5/MM-NPs) and Cy5.5-labeled TIIA-NPs (Cy5.5/NPs) through the use of an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). Blood samples were collected at specified time intervals after the intravenous administration of these formulations. The fluorescence intensities significantly diminished 24 h post-administration of the Cy5.5/NPs, with almost complete clearance after 48 h (Fig. 7b, c). Conversely, the Cy5.5/MM-NPs demonstrated a prolonged circulation time compared with that of the Cy5.5/NPs. Consequently, 48 h after injection, the aortae from all the treated groups were harvested and examined for fluorescence. Aortae from the Cy5.5/MM-NP group exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity than those from the Cy5.5/NP group, indicating superior accumulation in aortic tissue (Fig. 7d, e and Supplementary Fig. 10).

After the 2-month treatment period, the aortas were collected and subjected to ORO staining to assess the plaque burden. The analysis revealed a substantial increase in the ORO-positive areas in the model group. In contrast, mice treated with MM@TIIA-NPs displayed a marked reduction in ORO-positive areas. Quantitative assessment of the plaque areas demonstrated a noteworthy reduction in the MM@TIIA-NPs group, which surpassed the results observed in both the TIIA and TIIA-NPs groups (Fig. 7f, g). Overall, the findings indicated that intravenous administration of MM@TIIA-NPs effectively curtailed the development of atherosclerosis, outperforming the results achieved by TIIA-NPs and TIIA treatments. In addition, MM@TIIA-NPs had minimal effect on the body weight changes in the treated mice (Fig. 7h). Collectively, these data provide strong evidence that MM@TIIA-NPs exhibit superior therapeutic effects against atherosclerosis compared with those of TIIA-NPs or TIIA.

MM@TIIA-NPs ameliorated atherosclerotic plaque regression in ApoE−/− Mice

Subsequent analyses focused on the composition of atherosclerotic plaques after the MM@TIIA-NPs treatment. Specifically, histochemical evaluations were performed on aortic sinus sections (Fig. 8a). Immunofluorescence and H&E staining revealed notable differences in plaque composition between the groups. In the model group, the plaques were predominantly composed of lipid-rich necrotic cores (Fig. 8a, b). Remarkably, the necrotic core area assessed by TUNNEL was reduced in the MM@TIIA-NPs-treated groups compared with that in the model group, suggesting improved plaque stability (Supplementary Fig. 11). Further examination revealed that treatment with MM@TIIA-NPs led to a marked reduction in macrophage infiltration (immunostaining with anti-CD68 antibodies) and suppressed the expression of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9) and MMP-2 within the plaques (Fig. 8a, c,d, e). These changes are indicative of enhanced plaque stability because larger necrotic cores, greater macrophage infiltration, and elevated MMP expression are typically associated with unstable plaques.

Additionally, Masson’s trichrome staining revealed increased collagen content surrounding the plaques in the MM@TIIA-NPs-treated groups (Fig. 8a, f). Augmented collagen deposition contributes to the formation of a thicker fibrous cap, a hallmark of stable plaques. Staining results also demonstrated MM@TIIA-NPs increased accumulation of VSMCs within the plaques (Fig. 8a, g). VSMC proliferation is considered beneficial during atherogenesis, and the presence of collagen and VSMCs in plaques is associated with enhanced stability. Collectively, these findings, including reduced necrotic core size, decreased macrophage infiltration, lower MMP-9 expression, increased collagen content, and enhanced VSMC accumulation, strongly suggest that MM@TIIA-NPs treatment significantly stabilizes atherosclerotic plaques. Furthermore, we observed a marked reduction in the levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines in both aortic root sections and serum (Fig. 8a, h, i, Supplementary Fig. 12) of ApoE−/− mice that received MM@TIIA-NPs. In contrast, the mice from model group exhibited elevated cytokine levels. These findings further support that MM@TIIA-NPs effectively attenuate systemic inflammation and inflammation within atherosclerotic plaques.

Assessment of safety following the MM@TIIA-NPs treatment in vivo

To evaluate the safety of the MM@TIIA-NPs, we conducted comprehensive assessments of the potential adverse effects following a 2-month treatment. Blood count analyses revealed no abnormalities in red blood cell, white blood cell, or platelet counts in MM@TIIA-NPs-treated mice, suggesting the absence of hematological toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 13). Additionally, clinical biochemical analyses of hepatic and renal function showed no significant changes, indicating that MM@TIIA-NPs treatment did not induce organ dysfunction. Lipid profile assessments demonstrated that the MM@TIIA-NPs treatment led to a reduction in total cholesterol and triglyceride levels compared with those in the model group. To further investigate potential organ toxicity, we conducted histological examinations of major organs, including the liver, spleen, lung, heart, kidney, and brain (Supplementary Fig. 14). No discernible histopathological abnormalities or injuries were detected in the MM@TIIA-NPs-treated groups, suggesting that the treatment was well-tolerated and did not cause significant organ damage. The results of these comprehensive safety evaluations demonstrated that the intravenous administration of MM@TIIA-NPs at the investigated dosage was well-tolerated and safe. The lack of adverse effects on hematological parameters, hepatic and renal functions, lipid profiles, and the absence of observable organ injuries underscores the favorable safety profile of MM@TIIA-NPs as a therapeutic option for atherosclerosis.

Elevated cholesterol levels in the bloodstream are well established as significant contributors to lipid accumulation within arterial walls, leading to vascular inflammation and the onset of atherosclerosis, a primary etiological factor in cardiovascular events such as myocardial infarctions and cerebral vascular accidents [27]. The stability of atherosclerotic plaques within arteries plays a crucial role in determining whether the condition remains asymptomatic or progresses to a more severe state. Unstable plaques are prone to rupture, causing blood vessel blockages and potentially damaging vital organs [28]. Conversely, stable plaques are characterized by a thick fibrous cap composed primarily of SMCs and extracellular matrix components. These protect the plaque from rupture by acting as a barrier between blood-clotting factors and thrombogenic molecules in the plaque. However, as atherosclerosis advances, the fibrous cap weakens owing to degradation by MMPs, increasing the risk of plaque rupture and thrombosis.

Our research focused on developing an innovative drug delivery platform, where ROS-sensitive TIIA-loaded nanoparticles were enveloped in membranes derived from M2 macrophages. In contrast to conventional strategies such as PEGylation or antibody modification, this M2 macrophage membrane coating allows nanoparticles to evade elimination by the RES and enhances their accumulation at sites of inflammation [29]. This approach utilizes inherent cellular characteristics for functionalization, enabling allowing precise targeting without relying on specific ligand modifications or complex bioconjugation processes. Once TIIA-loaded NPs reach the inflammatory sites, they are designed to release their cargo in response to elevated ROS levels, providing a potent anti-inflammatory effect. Furthermore, M2 macrophage membrane-coated NPs can bind to and remove pro-inflammatory factors, such as TLR4, CD36, and CCR2, which are key players in the development of atherosclerosis [30]. This dual action of targeted drug delivery and sequestration of inflammatory molecules resulted in enhanced therapeutic outcomes in both zebrafish and mouse models of atherosclerosis.

In our preliminary study, we used RAW264.7 cells as a model for macrophages. However, future research could benefit from utilizing bone marrow-derived macrophages that more closely mimic plaque-associated macrophages, thereby optimizing biomimetic drug delivery platforms for precise targeting of atherosclerotic lesions.

Our in vitro study demonstrated that MM@TIIA-NPs were efficiently internalized by macrophages, endothelial cells, and VSMCs, leading to the elimination of intracellular ROS, inhibition of inflammatory injury and apoptosis. The MM@TIIA-NPs also markedly reduced the internalization of oxLDL, effectively preventing foam cell formation. This may be related to the suppression of oxidative stress and inflammatory signaling. Elevated intracellular ROS levels are known to enhance the expression of scavenger receptors such as CD36, Scavenger Receptor Class A type 1 (SR-A1), and Lectin-like oxidized LDL receptor-1 (LOX-1), thereby promoting oxLDL internalization and foam cell formation. By efficiently scavenging intracellular ROS, MM@TIIA-NPs may attenuate ROS-dependent inflammatory responses and reduce scavenger receptor activity, ultimately limiting oxLDL uptake. Furthermore, the macrophage membrane coating may contribute to modulating macrophage activation and reducing inflammatory stimuli, thereby disrupting the feed-forward cycle between oxidative stress and lipid accumulation. These mechanisms collectively contribute to the inhibition of foam cell formation observed in this study.

In vivo studies involving the intravenous administration of MM@TIIA-NPs demonstrated the passive targeting of atherosclerotic plaques, resulting in significant delays in the progression of atherosclerosis. MM@TIIA-NPs led to a decrease in the size of the atherosclerotic lesions in both the longitudinal and latitudinal arteries. Notably, MM@TIIA-NPs contributed to the enhancement of plaque stability by augmenting collagen and α-SMA+ VSMC contents while suppressing CD68 + macrophage infiltration and MMP-9/MMP-2 levels. Furthermore, TIIA released from MM@TIIA-NPs scavenged pro-inflammatory cytokines, as evidenced by the reduced levels of these molecules. Overall, MM@TIIA-NPs showed promise in attenuating plaque development in ApoE-/- mice.

Although many current experimental animal studies on atherosclerosis rely on genetically modified mice and a high-fat diet to induce extreme hypercholesterolemia and rapid development of atherosclerosis, a drawback of using mice is the inability to conduct real-time microscopic examination of atherosclerotic lesions. Therefore, we opted to use zebrafish for the dynamic observations of MM@TIIA-NPs in the processes of vascular lipid accumulation and inflammation in live organisms. Our results demonstrated that MM@TIIA-NPs not only enhanced lipid metabolism and reduced accumulation but also diminished the infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils into the vascular wall, inhibited lipoprotein oxidation, and safeguarded vascular endothelial cells.

Previous studies have shown that targeted drug delivery to plaque tissues can enhance anti-inflammatory effects more effectively than traditional drug administration methods [31]. Here, we sought to harness the anti-inflammatory properties of TIIA, an active ingredient in Chinese medicines, using an M2 macrophage biomimetic delivery platform. The M2 macrophage membrane targets and captures inflammatory cytokines, reducing inflammation. The novel approach involving the coating of an M2 macrophage membrane with TIIA-loaded nanoparticles that respond to reactive oxygen species, holds promise for treating atherosclerosis and other inflammatory disorders. Furthermore, this biomimetic delivery strategy has the potential to be adapted to other immune cells, such as neutrophils, T cells, and B cells, allowing the development of multiple cell membrane-coated nanoparticles for targeted and synergistic therapy [32]. By engineering NPs to respond to specific microenvironments, such as pH, glutathione, enzymes, light, or glucose, we can more effectively control the release of therapeutic agents [33]. The combination of an immune cell membrane coating with stimuli-responsive nanoparticles provides numerous advantages in treating inflammatory diseases [34].

Despite the promising therapeutic effects observed in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the in vitro experiments were primarily conducted using the RAW264.7 macrophage cell line, which may not fully recapitulate the behavior of primary macrophages or the heterogeneity of macrophage populations present in atherosclerotic plaques. Future studies using primary macrophages or bone marrow-derived macrophages would further strengthen the mechanistic conclusions. Second, although zebrafish and ApoE−/− mouse models are widely used for investigating atherosclerosis, these models cannot completely reproduce the complexity and chronic progression of human atherosclerotic disease. Therefore, additional validation in more advanced preclinical models will be necessary to confirm the therapeutic efficacy and safety of this system. Third, biomimetic nanocarriers based on cell membrane coatings may exhibit variability in membrane composition, protein content, and batch-to-batch reproducibility, which could influence targeting performance and drug delivery efficiency. Standardization of membrane preparation, characterization, and large-scale manufacturing will therefore be important considerations for future translational development.

Conclusions

MM@TIIA-NPs demonstrate potential as an anti-atherosclerotic therapy that deserves further investigation. The in vivo efficacy can be enhanced by optimizing plaque targeting through surface modification with active targeting agents, thereby maximizing its therapeutic potential. Moreover, integration of additional pharmacological agents commonly used in the treatment of atherosclerosis into this platform offers the potential for combination therapy, further improving its clinical applicability.

Methods

Preparation of ROS-responsive NPs and TIIA-NPs

4-hydroxyphenyl boronic acid pinacol ester (5 mmol) and carbonyldiimidazole (CDI, 25 mmol) were combined with dry dichloromethane (15 mL) in a flame-dried flask. The crude product was washed with water (3 × 20 mL), saturated NaCl (10 mL), and dried over MgSO4. COS (5 mmol) was dissolved in anhydrous DMSO (10 mL) and DMAP (10 mmol), followed by the addition of CDI-activated boronic ester (10 mmol). After overnight shaking, the product (Oxi-COS) was purified by dialysis in deionized water and confirmed using NMR.

For nanoparticle formation, Oxi-COS (30 mg) was dissolved in 300 mL deionized water and stirred for 1 h. Particle morphology and size were assessed by TEM and DLS. TIIA-NPs were prepared by co-dissolving Oxi-COS and TIIA (90:10, w/w) in DMSO. Release profiles were evaluated in H2O2 solutions, with 2 mL of medium sampled and replaced at each time point for HPLC analysis.

Synthesis of MM@TIIA-NPs

M2 macrophage membranes were prepared using the sucrose hypotonic method [35]. Briefly, M2 macrophages were centrifuged and resuspended in a TM buffer containing 1% PMSF for 2 h at 4 °C. The cell suspension was mixed with 1 M sucrose and centrifuged twice at 2000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected after further centrifugation at 3000 × g and the cell membranes were washed twice with TM buffer with 0.25 M of sucrose for purification. To construct MM@TIIA-NPs, macrophage membranes were fused with TIIA-NPs cores via co-extrusion through 200 nm polycarbonate filters, facilitating uniform membrane coating. The hydrodynamic size and zeta potential of nanoparticles were measured via DLS (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern). Membrane coating was validated via transmission electron microscopy (JEM-1400 PLUS 120-kV, Japan). FTIR spectroscopy was measured using an INVENIO R Fourier infrared spectrometer (Bruker, Germany).

ROS-responsive drug release

The in vitro release of TIIA from TIIA-NPs were investigated in the presence of varying concentrations of H2O2 (0, 0.2 mM, 0.5 mM, and 1 mM). A 5 mg sample of TIIA-NPs was weighed and dispersed in 2 mL of PBS solution; this dispersion was then enclosed within a dialysis bag (1000 Da) and immersed in 25 mL of PBS buffer solution containing the specified concentrations of H2O2. The system was maintained at 37 °C under gentle agitation. At various time intervals (0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60, 70, and 80 h), a 2 mL aliquot of the external buffer solution was withdrawn for high-performance liquid chromatography analysis, and 2 mL of fresh buffer solution was replenished to the system; the cumulative release rate was subsequently calculated. The results of the release experiments represent the average of three independent trials.

Cytotoxicity studies of the MM@TIIA-NPs

RAW264.7, VSMCs, and HUVECs were incubated with varying concentrations of MM@TIIA-NPs (0, 5, 25, and 50 µg ml-1), and their viability was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay .

Cellular uptake of the MM@TIIA-NPs

RAW264.7 cells were cultured at 2 × 105 cells/well, whereas VSMCs were seeded at 3 × 105 cells/well with fresh media containing Cy5.5/MM@TIIA-NPs. Cellular uptake was analyzed using confocal microscopy and flow cytometry (FACS Calibur, BD Biosciences).

Intracellular ROS detection

RAW264.7 cells were pre-treated with MM@TIIA-NPs for 2 h, followed by stimulation with LPS (100 ng ml-1) and IFN-γ (100 IU ml-1). Cells were then treated with 2′, 7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) in fresh medium for 30 min. After washing, intracellular ROS levels were measured by flow cytometry (FACS Calibur, BD Biosciences) [36].

Effect of MM@TIIA-NPs on foam cell

RAW264.7 cells were pre-treated with LPS (100 ng ml-1) and IFN-γ (100 IU ml-1) for 24 h and further, incubated with MM@TIIA-NPs for 2 h. Subsequently, oxLDL (50 µg ml-1) was added for an additional 48 h. After washing and fixation, the sections were stained with ORO.

Animal experiments

All animal care and experimental procedures were conducted in strict compliance with the guidelines outlined in the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. The protocols and procedures employed in these experiments were thoroughly scrutinized and approved (IACUC-2024-7903-01) by the Animal Ethics Committee of Guangzhou University of Chinese Medicine, which confirmed compliance with the highest standards of animal welfare and scientific rigor. Following a week acclimatization period to ensure their adaptation to the laboratory environment and minimize stress, male C57BL/6 mice aged 6–8 weeks and male ApoE−/− mice were subjected to a high-fat diet regimen spanning 3 months. Following the initial month, the mice were categorized into four groups and distinct treatments were administered over an additional 2-month period.

The zebrafish utilized in this study were meticulously cared for in a temperature (28 °C), and pH (6.5–8.5)-controlled multi-tank re-circulating water system under a 14 h light:10 h darkness cycle conducive to their health and well-being. Several zebrafish lines were employed, including the AB strain, as well as transgenic lines, such as Tg(fli-1:EGFP), Tg(mpeg1:EGFP), Tg(mpx: EGFP), and albino. Atherosclerosis was induced in zebrafish by subjecting them to a diet high in cholesterol (10%) and glucose (3%).

Quantifying lipid levels in zebrafish

Atherosclerosis models were developed using wild-type AB-line and albino zebrafish. Following the modeling phase, zebrafish were exposed to a solution of 0.03% ethyl 3-aminobenzoate methanesulfonateand subsequently subjected to ORO staining to assess the lipid content. Commercial assay kits (Jiancheng, Nanjing, China) were used to quantitatively measure LDL cholesterol levels in zebrafish samples.

Quantifying oxidative stress in zebrafish

Wild-type AB-line zebrafish were used to model atherosclerosis and ROS levels were quantitatively measured using commercial CM-H2DCFDA assay kits (Invitrogen, USA).

Quantifying inflammation in zebrafish

Transgenic zebrafish lines, including Tg(mpeg1:EGFP) and Tg(mpx:EGFP), were used to study the infiltration of macrophages and neutrophils into zebrafish blood vessels. After specific feeding and treatment protocols, fluorescence microscopy was used to analyze the macrophage and neutrophil levels, providing insights into the inflammatory response [37].

Measurement of endothelial cell layer thickness

The thickness of the endothelial cell layer inTg(fli1:EGFP) zebrafish was measured by capturing optical sections through the ocular vasculature. The GFP fluorescence from the endothelial cell layer was detected in the green channel to visualize the endothelial structure.

Quantification of Atherosclerotic Plaques in ApoE-/-Mice

Following the administration of various treatments, the aorta from ApoE−/− mice were collected and perfused for 50 min to adequately fix and preserve the tissue. After fixation, the sections were subjected to the same ORO staining protocol to ensure comprehensive visualization and assessment of lipid deposition within these crucial areas. Subsequent analysis of the plaque area was performed using ImageJ software, facilitating precise measurement and quantification of the identified plaques or lipid deposits within the stained Sect [38].

Histology and immunohistochemistry in ApoE-/-mice

The aortic sinus sections, with thicknesses ranging from 4 to 6 μm, were obtained from tissues. Subsequently, the sections were subjected to separate staining protocols [39]. Furthermore, these sections underwent an incubation process involving specific antibodies targeting CD68 (DF7518, Affinity, USA), matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9, AF5228, Affinity, USA), MMP-2 (AF5330, Affinity, USA), and α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA, AF1032, Affinity, USA). Semi-quantitative analysis of the histological images was performed using sophisticated ImageJ software, with consistent threshold settings applied across all images within the same experiment. Regions of interest were manually defined based on identical criteria, and background subtraction was performed prior to measurement.

Biochemistry analyses in ApoE-/- mice

Blood samples were analyzed for standard blood parameters using a Chemaray 800 analyzer (Rayto Life & Analytical Sciences Co., China).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error (SE) and were analyzed using SPSS 26. Data collection and image quantification were performed in a blinded manner to minimize bias. For experiments with multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, while a two-tailed, unpaired t-test was used for comparisons between two groups. Statistical significance was set as P < 0.05.
