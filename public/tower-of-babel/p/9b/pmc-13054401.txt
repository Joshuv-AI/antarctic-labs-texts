Change in movement disorder specialist attitudes to genetic testing after implementation of PD GENEration
Authors: Jesse Wang, Vanessa Ibrahim, Julian Agin-Liebes, Martha Nance, Margaret E. Caulfield, Anna Naito, Kamalini Ghosh Galvelis, James C. Beck, Roy N. Alcalay, Anne-Marie Wills
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeDespite advances in recent years, genetic testing for Parkinson disease (PD) is still underutilized in clinical practice. A 2019 questionnaire of movement disorder specialists found low rates of genetic testing in PD and barriers such as cost and insurance coverage. Since that time, PD GENEration, as well as several international programs, have broadly increased access to genetic testing and counseling for people with PD. A repeat survey sent out in 2024 examined how attitudes to genetic testing for PD in clinical practice have changed over the past 5 years.MethodsBetween October 2024-January 2025, 621 movement disorders specialists from the Parkinson’s Study Group (PSG) were invited by email to complete a questionnaire assessing knowledge, attitudes, and barriers to genetic testing in PD based on the 2019 survey.ResultsIn total, 119 PSG clinicians from the United States and Canada responded to the questionnaire. When compared with results from 2019, in 2024 both PD GENEration-affiliated and nonaffilated survey respondents reported fewer barriers to genetic testing both at the clinician and patient level.Conclusion2024 survey respondents report greater comfort with ordering and returning genetic test results compared with 2019, concurrent with the launch of PD GENEration, ROPAD, and GP2.

Introduction

The landscape of genetic testing in Parkinson disease (PD) is rapidly evolving.1 Knowledge of the genetics underlying Parkinson’s disease has grown substantially since the identification of disease-causing and high-risk variants in genes such as α-synuclein (SNCA), leucine-rich repeat kinase-2 (LRRK2), or glucocerebrosidase (GBA1).2,3 These discoveries have provided insight into the biological mechanisms behind Parkinson disease and are now enabling the development of therapies targeted specifically to patients with relevant genetic variants. With expanding access and growing recognition in the value of personalized medicine, genetic testing is becoming increasingly incorporated into everyday clinical practice.

In 2019, Alcalay et al surveyed 178 movement disorders specialists and experienced clinical trialists who were part of the Parkinson Study Group (PSG), representing 131 sites in the United States and 15 in Canada to assess their knowledge and attitudes about genetic testing for PD.4 They found that 41% of respondents had not referred any PD patients for genetic testing in the prior year, and 87% of respondents had ordered 10 or fewer genetic tests in the prior year. The main barriers reported by respondents were lack of insurance coverage for genetic testing and the impression that genetic results would not change therapy. This was despite the fact that a high percentage (83%) of people with Parkinson disease (PWP) are interested in receiving genetic testing and genetic counseling according to a 2018 survey.5, 6, 7

Since the survey by Alcalay et al was published, a number of events have occurred that have impacted access to Parkinson disease genetic testing. First, the Parkinson’s Foundation launched the PD GENEration study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifiers: NCT04057794 and NCT04994015) in August 2019.8, 9, 10, 11 The study, initially only in the United States but later expanded to other countries, provides testing and return of results via neurologists or genetic counselors for the 7 most common PD-related genes by a CLIA-certified laboratory at no cost to participants, without reliance on patient insurance. In addition to this 7-gene panel, in 2024 testing was expanded to include genome sequencing and an additional panel of 21 genes was added to the protocol. In the 5 years since the launch of PD GENEration, over 25,000 individuals with Parkinson disease have enrolled in the study through referral by clinicians, referral by clinical trial sites participating in PD GENEration, and through online self-referral supported via online advertising and social media.12 From this study, approximately 13% of all participants were identified to have a PD-relevant variant, and 9% of participants were found to have a variant, even in the absence of high-risk factors such as ancestry, age of onset, or family history.9 Globally, the Rostock International Parkinson’s Disease Study (ROPAD) study was also launched in 2019 (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT03866603) and has enrolled 12,580 participants mostly in Europe but also in the United States and Latin America.13,14 In the ROPAD study, participants underwent an initial screen for LRRK2 and GBA1 variants, followed by sequencing of a panel of 50 genes with 14.5% of participants found to have a positive PD-relevant genetic test. Additionally, the Global Parkinson’s Genetics Program (GP2) also began in 2019, with 108 currently active cohorts in the United States sharing DNA with collaborators around the world. although many of these research genetic cohorts began before 2019 and do not include return of results to participants.15,16

Lastly, the number of precision medicine clinical trials increased from just 1 GBA1-PD-targeted therapy trial active in the United States in 2019 to 5 in 2024. The Sanofi MOVES-PD trial17 (NCT02906020) was the sole precision medicine clinical trial active in the United States in 2019. Active trials for PD in 2024 include BIAL ACTIVATE (NCT05819359),18 Prevail PROPEL (NCT04127578),19 Denali BEACON (NCT06602193),20 Neuron 23 NEULARK (NCT06680830),21 and Biogen REASON (NCT03976349).22

We hypothesized that the increased access to genetic testing through PD GENEration and, to some extent, ROPAD and GP2 would affect North American clinicians’ attitudes toward genetic testing in PD. We therefore resurveyed a large group of movement disorders clinicians in North America and conducted a cross-sectional comparison of their answers to the 2019 survey results. Because PD GENEration recruits participants from many PSG sites, for the 2024 survey respondents we separately analyzed answers of sites that were involved in PD GENEration from those that were not to evaluate the impact of PD GENEration, ROPAD, and GP2 on the greater Parkinson’s community.

Materials and Methods

Participants

The PSG (Parkinson’s Study Group) is the largest Parkinson disease clinical trial site network in North America, comprising over 155 credentialed sites across the United States and Canada. To be credentialed as a member of the PSG, investigators must spend a significant portion of their practice treating PD and conducting PD related research. Most PSG investigators have had fellowship training in movement disorders.

The survey protocol was approved by the Advarra Institutional Review Board (IRB). Given the low-risk nature of the protocol, acceptance of the invitation and an electronic consent were accepted as a waiver for paper consent.

A total of 621 clinician members of the PSG were invited to take part in an anonymous online survey (see Supplemental Files). Invitations to the anonymous online survey was sent 3 times between October 15, 2024 and January 6, 2025. Participants who chose to include their email contact information were provided with a $25 Amazon gift certificate.

Questionnaire

Questions from the 2024 survey fell into the following categories:(1)basic demographics of the participants (gender, age, and location)(2)questions about the clinician and their patient populations(3)questions regarding current practice, accessibility, and barriers of genetic counseling (including involvement with PD GENEration)(4)general knowledge questions concerning PD genetics, including 2 case scenarios focused on GBA1 and LRRK2-related PD.

The questionnaire repeated many of the questions that were sent in the survey in 2019. Several new questions were added to assess PD GENEration involvement and its effectiveness in improving comfort with and access to genetic testing in Parkinson disease. The full questionnaire can be viewed in the Supplemental Files.

Statistical analysis

Because many of the surveyed respondents were affiliated with clinical sites involved in PD GENEration, data from PD GENEration and non-PD GENEration sites were analyzed separately. PD GENEration affiliates were defined as sites who reported that they were either involved as sites in PD GENEration or referred participants to the online enrollment center (“referral sites”). Because very few PSG sites were involved in the ROPAD study, sites were not separately analyzed. The original data from the 2019 survey were available and used to compare with the 2024 survey results. Descriptive statistics (percentages, means, and SD) were used to visually compare the responses across groups, and statistical significance between groups were calculated either by ANOVA, Chi-square analysis, or Fisher’s exact test. All statistical analyses were conducted using R [Version 4.4.2, 2024-10-31].

Results

Of the 621 total clinicians invited to take part in the anonymous online survey, 119 participants responded, and 113 (18.2%) completed the full questionnaire, which was lower than the 178 out of 374 (47.6%) PSG investigators who responded in 2019. Twenty-two participants stated that they recalled responding to the 2019 survey. Because both surveys were anonymous, we were unable to match responses between the 2.

Of the 119 responders, 108 were movement disorder specialists, 106 were PSG members, 74 reported practicing in an academic medical center, 67 were active in clinical trials for Parkinson disease, and 83 were involved with PD GENEration either as recruiting sites (58) or referral sites (25). In total, 11 PSG sites were involved in the ROPAD study. Of the participants who chose to share their demographic data, there were 56 male respondents and 56 female respondents. All clinicians surveyed practice in the United States or Canada (9) and reported working in clinics with a median PD population of 300 patients, (ranging from 4 to 2000).

Distributions for genetic testing for research only (without return of results), and DNA banking did not significantly change between 2019 and 2024 (Chi-square, P = .446). Respondents reporting more than 10 patients undergoing direct-to-consumer testing (eg, 23andMe) remained stable at 20.0% for non-PD GENEration and 10.8% for PD GENEration sites compared with 14.6% in 2019 (data not shown). In total, 16.8% of 2024 clinicians reported more than 10 patients declining to a genetic test in the prior year across all sites compared with 3.8% in 2019.

As access to genetic testing has improved overall, knowledge of the presence of pathogenic variants has also increased among survey respondents. The number of clinicians reporting that they provided care to more than 10 patients with known genetic variants increased from 5.5% for LRRK2 and 4.9% for GBA1 in 2019 to 12.6% and 18.4%, respectively. The total number of known LRRK2 gene heterozygote patients reported remained the same at 490 (although this was a higher total number per respondent), and GBA1 heterozygotes increased from 402 in 2019 to 674 in 2024. Providing care for patients with known PRKN, PINK1, SNCA, PARK7, and VPS35 pathogenic variants has also become more prevalent. Although 2% of clinicians previously reported providing for more than 2 patients with these pathogenic variants, this increased to 21.8% of respondents in 2024, with 235 cases of these such patients reported overall. Other rare genetic variants associated with Parkinsonism reported included TWNK and ATP13A2 among others (9 total with 2 or fewer reported for each).

Access to genetic counseling

In a situation in which genetic testing would be ordered, a similar proportion (59.3%, compared with 61.2% in 2019) reported they would either involve a genetic counselor or recommend a genetic counseling referral before genetic testing for PD. A similar 20.3% of respondents (compared with 21.9% in 2019) reported they would provide the genetic counseling themselves. A majority (76%) reported having access to services of a genetic counselor within their institution or practice, which was similar to results from the 2019 survey (70%). An increase of 9.2% reported using genetic counseling via telephone or telemedicine compared with 4.5% in 2019.

Barriers to genetic testing

Respondents were then asked to grade the same obstacles from the 2019 survey that prevented them from ordering genetic tests on a scale from 0 to 100 (Figure 2). The most prevalent obstacles remained lack of insurance coverage/cost and the opinion that test results would not change their approach to therapy. However, all of the perceived barriers were significantly lower (P < .05) in 2024 compared with 2019 in both PD GENEration recruiting/referral and nonaffiliated sites, including insurance barriers and concerns about potential repercussions for the patient, such as impact of test results on insurance, employability or family members. Case scenario 1 responses by group

Case scenario 1 asked about a patient with a single GBA1 L444P (L444P/L483P) variant. When asked whether the participant had GBA1-associated PD (correct answer), 52.4% of participants in 2019 replied in the affirmative, compared with 76.8% of participants in 2024 in PD GENEration affiliated sites and 63.3% of participants in nonaffiliated sites (overall χ2 P value = .013, df = 4). None of the other knowledge questions differed significantly (P < .05) in the distribution of responses between 2019 and 2024 (Table 1); however, the percentage of these correct responses in the 2019 survey were already comparable to the 2024 results, ranging from 73.8% to 82.1%.

Case scenario 2 asked about a participant with the G2019S LRRK2 genetic variant. More respondents in 2024 answered the following false statement correctly “this patient is more likely to have cognitive impairment sooner than a patient with idiopathic PD” (Supplemental Table 1, P = .0006) than in 2019, regardless of PD GENEration affiliation. With subsequent questions, there was no change in the comparable accuracy of responses from 2019 (ranging from 61.4% to 91.7% correct).

Discussion

Compared with 2019, clinicians responding to the 2024 survey showed a marked reduction in perceived barriers to genetic testing and an improvement in both perceived access to testing and clinicians’ willingness to send genetic tests. Reported volume for genetic testing for research with return of results has increased, particularly at sites participating in PD GENEration. We were surprised to find that even within the sites that were not affiliated with PD GENEration, the perceived barriers to genetic testing have reduced compared with respondents from 2019, suggesting that the effect of free genetic testing across even global programs such as ROPAD and GP2 has had a broader impact on the Parkinson’s community.

Limitations of our study include the fact that the survey was sent only to PSG members who are already experts in Parkinson disease and therefore is not representative of all clinicians in North America or even all neurologists. However, the survey was designed to obtain and compare similar results between 2019 and 2024 to determine the effects of PD GENEration in a controlled manner. Another limitation was the limited information on the effect of ROPAD on PSG sites. Because only 11 PSG sites are involved in ROPAD, we did not separate out this group. A third limitation is the fact that a smaller number of participants completed the 2024 survey compared with 2019. It is unclear whether this could have biased our results either positively or negatively.

Although there remains a clear need for continuing education around genetics, genetic testing, and genetic counseling, as has been provided by PD GENEration, PSG, and GP2,8,16,23 it is evident that the clinician community is responding to education and access and increasing their knowledge and utility of genetic testing. We therefore conclude that attitudes toward genetic testing are changing within the Parkinson’s community as a result of increased access to genetic testing through research protocols such as PD GENEration, thereby setting the stage for the advent of precision medicine trials for genetic forms of PD.
