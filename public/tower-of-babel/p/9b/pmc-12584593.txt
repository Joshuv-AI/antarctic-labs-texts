A novel sex-associated genomic region in Catostomus fish species
Authors: Cassandre B Pyne, Jillian N Campbell, S Eryn McFarlane, Elizabeth G Mandeville
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGenomic regions that influence sex are hypothesized to play a key role in evolutionary diversification, as sex determination mechanisms may promote or impede reproductive isolation between closely related species. Remarkably, these mechanisms are almost completely unknown in many taxa, especially in clades like fish with extremely variable sex determination. In Catostomus fishes, contemporary hybridization is extensive but variable, and factors influencing hybridization dynamics are not fully understood. We used genotyping-by-sequencing data from three Catostomus species (bluehead, white, and flannelmouth suckers) to identify regions of the genome associated with sex using a genome-wide association study. We identified a genomic region in bluehead suckers from Colorado on chromosome 4 that significantly associates with sex and is suggestive of a sex-determining region. This region is not significant in the other species studied here, or in a divergent lineage of bluehead suckers, implying that either the sex-determining region of the genome differs in these species and populations, or that technical limitations precluded identification of sex determination. These results provide the first description of sex determination systems in Catostomus species and suggest that further investigation of sex determination in the Catostomidae genus is needed to better characterize evolutionary turnover of sex determination in this species-rich group.

Introduction

Genetic mechanisms of sex determination in gonochoristic species are variable and can range from large heteromorphic sex chromosomes, where sex chromosomes are morphologically differentiated, to multiple sex-associated loci (Mank et al. 2006) represented by unlinked genes across the genome or localized regions with many sex-determining genes (Kobayashi et al. 2013; Heule et al. 2014). Previously, sex determination was described using “master” sex-determining regions, where master sex-determining genes control sexual differentiation by acting as a switch that leads to either the male or female phenotype (Yano et al. 2012; Heule et al. 2014). However, as more studies use genome-wide data to interrogate sex determination systems, there is increasing evidence for polygenic sex determination, where sex is controlled by multiple, possibly interacting alleles at different loci in one individual (Kosswig 1964; Vandeputte et al. 2007; Schartl et al. 2023). Recent work suggests that sex determination is more complex and polygenic than originally thought (Tree of Sex Consortium 2014; Heule et al. 2014; Roberts et al. 2016; Faggion et al. 2019; Adhikari et al. 2021; Curzon et al. 2023; Kitano et al. 2024).

Sex determination systems are particularly variable in fish. The genetic elements that control sex determination in fish are less conserved than in some other vertebrate clades like mammals and birds, suggesting multiple evolutionary transitions between sex-determining systems in fish (Heule et al. 2014). Both genetic and environmental sex determination have been documented in fish; however, evidence is accumulating that sex determination systems that rely on multiple genetic elements, potentially with environmental influence, are more frequent than initially recorded (Heule et al. 2014). The genetic architecture of sex determination in fish can vary beyond heteromorphic sex chromosomes. For example, sex can be determined with just one locus or gene such as SRY or dmrt1b, or multiple loci such as sar3, sar5, and sar16 in zebrafish (Anderson et al. 2012). Polygenic sex determination is seen in Nile tilapia (Oreochromis niloticus), channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus), and Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) (Baroiller et al. 2009; Eisbrenner et al. 2014; Kijas et al. 2018; Bao et al. 2019). Additionally, in sea bass, both genetic factors and temperature influence the sex of the individual (Palaiokostas et al. 2015). Each of these variables, as well as spatial and geographic variation can drive variation in sex determination.

Many fish species do not have a sex determination system that relies on a known sex-determining gene, which makes the mechanisms of sex determination even more difficult to detect (Palaiokostas et al. 2015; Faggion et al. 2019). The lack of a phylogenetically widespread shared sex-determining gene for fishes leaves a large gap in knowledge on the underlying genetics and mechanisms of sex determination, particularly for nonmodel fish taxa. Not only are the mechanistic pathways and specific genes involved in sex determination mostly unknown, but overall systems are undescribed in many teleost fish species (Mank et al. 2006), making the identification of genetic mechanisms of sex determination challenging. Different sex determination systems can also cause variation in genome-wide patterns of loci involved in sex determination. For example, loci present or linked to those in homomorphic sex chromosomes are present in the genomes of both sexes and can have different expression in each sex (Yang et al. 2024). In contrast, loci that are present exclusively in one sex usually correspond to heteromorphic sex chromosomes or smaller sex-specific regions (Pratlong et al. 2017). We can begin to make inferences about sex determination by investigating patterns of sex-associated loci across the genome.

Genera and species with unknown sex determination systems, such as Catostomus fishes, are of particular interest as there is little known about any underlying genomic regions associated with sex. Catostomus fishes (“suckers”) are a genus of freshwater fishes that are widespread through North America. Catostomus fishes are historical tetraploids, as they diversified following an allopolyploid genome duplication event approximately 50 million years ago at the base of the Catostomidae family (Hubbs 1955; Uyeno and Smith 1972; Bagley et al. 2018; Krabbenhoft et al. 2021), leading to the species richness and diversity seen in the Catostomus genus. In total, there are 28 species in Catostomus including both native and introduced species to the Colorado River Basin (Dowling et al. 2016). Most species in the Catostomidae family have approximately 100 chromosomes, with an absence of well-differentiated sex chromosomes (Uyeno and Smith 1972). Additionally, suckers do not display obvious sexual dimorphism; thus, identifying males and females sublethally can only be achieved during the spawning season.

Catostomus fishes hybridize, both historically and contemporarily (Hubbs et al. 1943; Mandeville et al. 2017; Bangs et al. 2018), and hybridization can occur both between historically sympatric native species and between native and introduced species. Hybridization is incredibly variable and can be human-induced through the introduction of species such as the white sucker (Mandeville et al. 2015, 2017). Hybridization in Catostomus fishes in the Upper Colorado River basin primarily involves three species, bluehead (Catostomus discobolus), flannelmouth (Catostomus latipinnis), and white sucker (Catostomus commersonii). Some species of Catostomus suckers are of conservation concern, such as the bluehead and flannelmouth sucker (Cooke et al. 2005), so part of the motivation for this study is understanding how sex-biased processes might play out in hybridization. Hybridization outcomes vary from F1 hybrids only to extensive backcrossing depending on the location or species pair. For example, hybridization between the flannelmouth and white sucker can produce later-generation hybridization including backcrossing to both parental species, whereas bluehead × white hybrids sampled as adults may be mostly F1 hybrids (Mandeville et al. 2017). Additionally, flannelmouth × white hybrids may have asymmetrical backcrossing depending on the location, where some locations have hybrids backcrossing to only one parental species (Mandeville et al. 2017).

Understanding how the sex of an individual is determined genetically can shed light on the role of sex determination loci in reproductive isolation, as mismatching sex-determining systems can lead to strong postzygotic isolation (Haldane 1922; Coyne and Orr 2004; Kitano and Peichel 2012; Lima 2014). There is a possibility that sex determination incompatibilities may play a role in the variation in reproductive isolation across hybrid crosses in Catostomus fishes. Although explicitly testing this hypothesis is outside of the scope of this study, we hope that providing the first description of sex determination mechanisms in Catostomus species will lay the foundation for future work. Sex determination is mostly undescribed in Catostomus suckers, although karyotyping studies provide evidence against the existence of heteromorphic, strongly differentiated sex chromosomes (Uyeno and Smith 1972). Other fish in the Cypriniformes order with known sex determination systems show some form of genetic sex determination including male and female heterogamety (Ashman et al. 2014). Therefore, there is likely a genetic basis of sex determination in suckers (Tree of Sex Consortium 2014). The potential for sex determination to be a driver for reproductive isolation in Catostomus suckers is an intriguing possibility and makes these fish an interesting clade in which to investigate the basis of sex determination.

In this study, we investigated regions of the genome that associate with sex in bluehead suckers (Catostomus discobolus), in addition to a smaller sample of flannelmouth suckers (Catostomus latipinnis), white suckers (Catostomus commersonii), and bluehead × white sucker hybrids. Searching for these loci allows us to begin to determine to what extent sex is controlled by genetic factors. We used genomic data from Catostomus species and their hybrids to identify genomic regions that were significantly associated with sex. A secondary aim of this study was to determine to what extent the genetic basis of sex determination is variable across Catostomus sucker species and locations. Due to the high evolutionary turnover of sex-determining mechanisms in fish (Pennell et al. 2018), all species (or even populations of the same species) of Catostomus suckers may not share the exact same genetic architecture, including individual loci, that underlies sex determination. If genetic basis of sex determination differs across species, it may explain some of the variability in hybridization outcomes across species pairs in Catostomus fishes.

Materials and methods

Sampling

We used existing genomic data from fish collected for a previous sequencing effort as well as new data generated for this study. The data are from Catostomus sucker fin clip samples (predominantly from the upper lobe of the caudal fin) from sampling in 2015, 2019, 2021, and 2022 in the Gunnison River basin (Colorado, USA), Jackson Lake (Wyoming, USA), and small streams in Southern Ontario (Canada). Fish were collected by collaborators under permits from the states of Colorado and Wyoming, as well as from the University of Guelph and Ontario Ministry of Natural Resources. Fish sampled in Colorado were collected by Colorado Parks and Wildlife field crew personnel. These fish were sampled at a Resistance Board Weir apparatus as part of another project (Campbell 2024). Spawning adult fish moving upstream were captured by the weir and a random subset of fin clips were taken from adult fish. The total data set consists of 533 individuals of known sex and includes three species, C. discobolus (bluehead sucker, n = 248), C. latipinnis (flannelmouth sucker, n = 129), and C. commersonii (white sucker, n = 106) as well as their hybrids (n = 50) (Table 1, Supplementary Table S1 for a breakdown by species and location). Each individual’s species and sex were determined by experienced field personnel by visual inspection during spawning season or by dissection. For the samples sexed by field personnel in Colorado, individuals were assigned based on gamete production. Therefore, individuals were assigned male if sperm was observed and female if eggs were observed (after manipulation by field personnel). Gonad data were not gathered at the field sites in Colorado or Wyoming, as sampling was sublethal and fish were subsequently released. For the samples sexed by dissection in Ontario, additional information about stomach contents and gonads was recorded. However, there was no record of abnormal or intersex gonads (although intersex gonads have been previously identified in catostomids; Smith et al. 2018). Previous studies suggest that rates of species misidentification are low when phenotypes are assessed by experienced field personnel (Mandeville et al. 2017).

During data collection, 34% of individuals were phenotypically identified as “probable female” or “probable male” rather than either males or females. The “probable male” individuals were those that had tubercles present on their fins, but had not yet produced milt. Usual conclusive sex phenotyping in the field assigns an individual as male if both tubercles and milt are present. The individuals that were not yet in spawning condition were marked as “probable females” if neither of these male-specific traits present, nor eggs. Expert field personnel familiar with spawning time in this system assigned sex to all individuals used in this study. To evaluate how likely it was that individuals designated “probable females” were female, we used GEMMA’s LMM and BSLMM (see Supplementary Material for more information).

Sample preparation and sequencing

We completed DNA extractions on the fin clips using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Qiagen, Inc) and quantified the samples with a Nanodrop spectrophotometer. The extracted DNA was used to create a genomic library for DNA sequencing. Specifically, we used reduced-representation genotyping-by-sequencing (GBS) methods, where we used restriction enzymes “EcoRI” and “MseI” to digest genomic DNA into fragments (Parchman et al. 2011). Barcodes and identifying adaptor sequences were ligated to each DNA fragment and amplified by PCR. After library preparation, we used a PippinPrep (D-Mark Biosciences) to size-select DNA fragments with lengths between 250 and 350 base pairs. A total of six prepared libraries were then sent to the University of Texas Genome Sequencing and Analysis Facility for high-throughput DNA sequencing on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 and Toronto’s The Centre for Applied Genomics at The Hospital for Sick Children for sequencing on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000. The samples from the 2015 sampling, including all three species and hybrids, were sequenced in Texas, whereas the samples from the 2019, 2021, and 2022 sampling were sequenced in Toronto. Colorado samples from 2021 and 2022 were sequenced on four separate runs of an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S1 flowcell. As our data set was sequenced on two different platforms, we investigated batch effects. There was no apparent batch effect between the HiSeq 2500 and NovaSeq 6000 (Supplementary Fig. S1).

A new flannelmouth sucker (C. latipinnis) reference genome was used, which improved on an artificial reference genome used in a previous study (Mandeville et al. 2017). The male individual was sampled by Wyoming Game and Fish from Little Sandy Creek, near Farson, Wyoming. The high-quality reference genome was produced at Dovetail Genomics using a draft assembly from a previous mate-pair library sequencing effort, in addition to new data from Hi-C and Chicago libraries. Assembly was completed by Dovetail Genomics using the HiRise pipeline. The reference genome has a contig N50 of 14.69 kb and scaffold N50 of 37.5 Mb. It is assembled into nearly chromosome-level scaffolds (NCBI ID: SAMN30494180). Because of the high quality of the reference genome, some of our figures show only the 50 largest scaffolds, which likely correspond to the known haploid chromosome number of 50 for this species (2n = 100; Uyeno and Smith 1972).

Variant calling and filtering

After sequencing, the resulting .fastq files were parsed to separate out individual fish with differing identifying barcode sequences using a custom perl script (this script and all others used for analysis are available here: https://github.com/pynec). Reads for each individual fish were then aligned to the flannelmouth sucker reference genome (NCBI ID: SAMN30494180) using bwa (version 0.7.17) (Li and Durbin 2009). This resulted in binary alignment map files that were then used for variant calling using samtools (version 1.12) and bcftools (version 1.11) (Li et al. 2009; Li 2011).

We initially filtered variants on the whole data set in order to use entropy to determine the ancestry of the hybrids and sort individuals by species (Gompert et al. 2014; Shastry et al. 2021). We used vcftools (Danecek et al. 2011) to filter variants, where we filtered to retain loci that were sequenced in more than 50% of individuals, had a minimum quality of 20 and a minor allele count of at least 3. We also removed individuals with more than 60% missing data. Later stages of filtering screened for a minor allele frequency of 0.05 or greater. In addition, potential paralogous loci were detected using a custom R script that identifies loci with unusually high coverage (exceeding the 0.99 quantile for coverage across all loci) or with heterozygote allele depth ratios deviating substantially from 1:1, as suggested by McKinney et al. (2017), and we removed these potential paralogous loci using vcftools (Danecek et al. 2011). We then used entropy, which estimates genetic parameters and individual ancestry using a Bayesian model and Markov Chain Monte Carlo. Sequence data are incorporated into the model using genotype likelihoods (Shastry et al. 2021), which were estimated using bcftools. To determine the optimal value of k (number of genetic clusters), we ran entropy for k = 1 to k = 6, corresponding to the maximum possible number of Catostomus species that could be included in our samples (Mandeville et al. 2017). To assess genetic structure in the data, genotypes and associated likelihoods were used in a principal components analysis (prcomp in R). Linear discriminant analysis was applied to the results in R (R Core Team 2022) to create starting values of q (in entropy, admixture proportion). The linear discriminant analysis grouped the data by k-means clusters determined by the PCA results (Shastry et al. 2021).

After we confirmed phenotypic identification of species using entropy, we split the data set by species (bluehead, white, flannelmouth, and bluehead × white hybrids) and conducted filtering as outlined above. We additionally split the data by species just within Colorado as a majority of samples came from Colorado. We used nonrandom starting values for q because starting in a better parameter space (as informed by the data) speeds model convergence (Shastry et al. 2021). We ran three replicates for each species set (k = 1). Each replicate included 50,000 MCMC (Markov Chain Monte Carlo) steps, including 25,000 burn-in discarded values, and we kept every 25th value. Entropy’s output is genotype probability and ancestry estimates, which allows for grouping of individuals into genetic clusters. We then searched the genome for regions that are linked to sex.

Genome-wide association study

We used a genome-wide association study (GWAS) to detect associations between regions of the genome and sex in both sexes. GEMMA, a single-locus software, was used to fit a linear mixed model (LMM), where the effect sizes of SNPs are assumed to be from a normal distribution (Zhou and Stephens 2012). GEMMA only allows loci that have been genotyped in at least 95% of the individuals; therefore, genotypes were imputed using the genotype likelihoods from the output of entropy in a custom R script (Shastry et al. 2021). Prior to conducting the association analysis, GEMMA calculates a relatedness matrix to account for population structure. The random effects are expected to be structured by population and species; therefore, including this in the association analysis leads to more reliable results.

To detect loci that are associated with sex and are present in both sexes, we ran GEMMA on the species- and hybrid-specific data sets (bluehead, white, flannelmouth, bluehead × white) to determine differences in sex-linked loci across species. SNPs were then visualized using Manhattan plots with the qqman package in R (version 0.1.8) (Turner 2018). SNP P-values were adjusted using the negative log function, and the significance threshold was corrected using the Bonferroni correction.

In addition to the LMM in GEMMA, we also used the Bayesian sparse linear mixed model (BSLMM). This model uses Markov Chain Monte Carlo to estimate the proportion of phenotypic variance explained (PVE) by the genotypes (Zhou et al. 2013). BSLMMs are beneficial to use in addition to LMMs because they assume that each variant falls under two distributions and do not assume nonzero SNP effects a priori. In the first distribution, all variants are assumed to contribute to a normal distribution of effects, as is the case in LMMs (Zhou et al. 2013). In the second (the “sparse” matrix), a small number of variants are assumed to have an effect, as is the case in Bayesian variable selection regression models (BVSR) (Guan and Stephens 2011; Zhou et al. 2013). The two models used complement each other because they use different assumptions about how explanatory power is weighted across SNPs. This model was run using the same input as described above. We ran BSLMM with 25 million iterations and a burn-in of 10 million iterations. Samples were taken every 100 iterations. White suckers and bluehead × white hybrids, however, did not converge with these parameters; therefore, the model run conditions were adjusted to ensure convergence. Colorado white suckers converged with 100 million iterations and a burn-in of 20 million, with samples taken every 100 iterations. Bluehead × white hybrids converged with parameters of 3 million iterations with a burn-in of 1 million, with samples taken every 100 iterations. BSLMM was run three times for each data set to ensure convergence. MCMC distributions of the phenotypic variation (PVE) were used to assess convergence of the model. Results were visualized in R using a GEMMA workshop example (Soria-Carrasco 2019). Significance was quantified using posterior inclusion probability (“PIP”), which indicates the proportion of iterations in which the SNP is included in the sparse matrix, suggesting a nonzero effect size.

Discriminant analysis of principal components

We also identified candidate sex-associated loci using DAPC (discriminant analysis of principal components, Jombart 2008), implemented through the adegenet package in R (Jombart et al. 2010) and following analyses from Junker et al. (2020) and Meuser et al. (2022). This approach uses a principal components analysis to identify major axes of genetic variation, then uses discriminant analysis with sex as the grouping variable to identify sex-associated loci. We completed analyses separately for bluehead and white suckers. For each data set, we used a randomization procedure to generate a statistical threshold for SNP loading, and identified loci that exceeded the 99.5% quantile as sex-associated for bluehead suckers and 99.9% for white suckers. The threshold for white suckers is higher than bluehead suckers to account for geographic variability in white sucker samples and larger number of SNPs in the data set.

Additional analyses

To complement the results of the GWAS and DAPC, we also investigated FST and linkage disequilibrium for significant loci from the GWAS. Using VCFtools, we calculated the Weir and Cockerham FST to assess genetic differentiation between the sexes (Weir and Cockerham 1984; Danecek et al. 2011). Significant outliers were detected using Rosner’s test. Additionally, we calculated pairwise r2 (squared correlation coefficient) to estimate linkage disequilibrium between significant SNPs using VCFtools (Danecek et al. 2011).

RADSex was also used as a complementary technique to identify sex-specific loci in bluehead suckers and Colorado bluehead suckers (Feron et al. 2020). This workflow takes .fastq files of demultiplexed reads and a phenotype file with individuals and sex as input. RADSex will then determine which monomorphic markers are significantly associated with sex through a presence-absence scan and Pearson’s chi-squared test (Feron et al. 2020). Thus, it is able to identify both homomorphic and heteromorphic sex chromosomes and sex-determining regions (Feron et al. 2020). Output was visualized with the sgtr R package (Version 1.1.4) (Feron et al. 2020).

Results

DNA sequencing resulted in approximately 4.6 × 1013 raw reads. The average number of raw reads per individual was 1,855,051 and the mean number of assembled reads was 1,494,436, leading to an average of 81% of reads aligning to the flannelmouth sucker reference genome. Flannelmouth suckers had an average alignment of 53% but a median of 69%. Bluehead suckers had an average rate of 79% and white suckers had an average alignment rate of 76%. From the aligned reads, variants were identified. We removed 123 individuals with more than 60% missing data and removed 10,793 potentially paralogous loci. This resulted in 24,726 SNPs for 406 individuals that were kept after variant calling and filtering. After running entropy, we confirmed that there are three genetic clusters, corresponding to the three species in the data (Supplementary Fig. S2). Using the DIC (deviance information criterion) values, the k = 3 model was best supported with the lowest DIC value, which matches the three phenotypically identified species (Supplementary Fig. S3). In addition, using a principal components analysis, we confirmed that individuals of the same species cluster together along the first three principal components (Supplementary Fig. S4). The three species and their hybrids formed three distinct genetic clusters across the principal components. PC1 accounts for 77.7% of the variation and separates out white and bluehead suckers; PC2 accounts for 21.2% of variation and separates out flannelmouth suckers from the other two species. Including “probable females” as females led to qualitatively similar results as excluding “probable females” suggesting that these individuals are most likely female, as initially identified by expert field personnel (Supplementary Fig. S5). For the following analyses, probable females were included as females.

The data were grouped by bluehead suckers, white suckers, flannelmouth, and bluehead × white hybrids. Filtering resulted in 16,908 loci for 194 bluehead sucker individuals. These data had a mean coverage of 11.97 reads per locus per individual. For white suckers, there were 97,477 loci for 84 individuals with a mean coverage of 12.07 reads per locus per individual. After filtering, 83 flannelmouth individuals remained with 43,121 loci with a mean coverage of 19.95 reads per locus per individual. Lastly, filtering of bluehead × white hybrids resulted in 126,557 loci for 48 individuals with a mean coverage of 5.58 reads per locus per individual.

When analyzing each parental species within Colorado, bluehead suckers were left with 164 individuals, white suckers had 60 individuals, and flannelmouth had 79 individuals. Bluehead suckers had 22,015 loci with a mean coverage of 11.26 reads per locus per individual. White suckers had 93,470 loci with a mean coverage of 17.77 reads per locus per individual. Lastly, flannelmouth suckers had 26,080 loci with a mean coverage of 27.73 reads per locus per individual.

Geographic variation in sex-associated loci in bluehead suckers

We identified sex-associated loci in Colorado bluehead suckers. Specifically, we found two sex-associated SNPs on chromosome 4 that were supported by the LMM in GEMMA (Fig. 1c, P = 10−20, P = 10−19). These two sex-associated loci form a haplotype that may participate in sex determination in bluehead suckers. The two sex-associated loci are located closely together on chromosome 4, at base pair positions, 27210425 and 27210426. The r2 value between the two loci is 0.965, where the average along chromosome 4 is 0.019, suggesting that these two loci are linked. Additionally, these two loci are the most divergent between the sexes, with an FST of 0.31. One of these SNPs was also supported by the Bayesian sparse LMM (Fig. 1d, PIP = 0.906). Estimates reported from BSLMM are from the first run. All three runs of BSLMM converged and consistently demonstrated the same well-supported locus (Supplementary Table S2). The average proportion of variance explained by all SNPs in the BSLMM model is 0.56547, whereas the proportion explained by the sparse matrix was 0.797369. Additionally, only an average of 3.7 ± 0.0066 SNPs are expected to be involved in sex in Colorado blueheads based on the BSLMM model. Neither of the two significant loci identified by GEMMA, however, were identified by DAPC with a SNP loading statistical threshold of 0.995. A total of 97 loci were identified across 41 chromosomes in Colorado bluehead suckers, with only four located on chromosome 4. This does indicate that a small part of chromosome 4 is involved in sex determination, as it was identified by all four methods (LMM, BSLMM, DAPC, FST; Fig. 2). No sex-linked loci were identified using RadSex. We report P-values and beta estimates for the Colorado bluehead suckers in Supplementary Tables S2 and S3. A clear signature of sex chromosomes or large sex-determining regions is genotypic variation between sexes. When searching for XY sex chromosomes, for example, all females are homozygous and all males are heterozygous. Of the 71 available genotyped Colorado bluehead females, we found that 89% of females were homozygous for the same genotype in the sex-linked haplotype. Of the 48 available male genotypes, 80% were heterozygous for the same genotype. It is important to note that not all individuals were confidently genotyped, which may be due to phenotypic misidentifications or the complexity of sex determination in suckers. We also estimated the proportion of variance of these loci using the proportion of PVE by VSNP (VSNP = 2pqa2), where p and q are the major and minor allele frequencies and a is the effect size (Falconer and Mackay 1996). The proportion of variance for the two significant loci on chromosome 4 was 0.15 and 0.53, respectively, which is consistent with the number of SNPs, 3.7, estimated to be involved in sex in Colorado blueheads.

In comparison to Colorado bluehead suckers, Wyoming blueheads and the full bluehead data set did not have any SNPs significantly associated with sex using either GWAS method (Fig. 1a and b). The SNPs in both of these populations not being significant may indicate that there is a spurious relationship in Colorado blueheads. In order to rule out this possibility, we estimated FST between the Colorado and Wyoming blueheads. Genome-wide FST was 0.0295, whereas FST at one of the loci significantly associated with sex was 0.2733. This indicates that the significant locus in Colorado is more diverged than the rest of the genome. In contrast to the GWAS results, DAPC did detect two significant sex-associated loci on chromosome 4 for all blueheads; however, neither were the same loci in the sex-associated haplotype found in Colorado bluehead suckers. Using a SNP loading statistical threshold of 0.995, 44 sex-associated loci were identified across 26 chromosomes in bluehead suckers with DAPC. Two sex-associated loci were found on chromosome 4.

Unclear genetic basis of sex determination in Catostomus suckers

Unlike bluehead suckers, the genetic mechanisms of sex determination remain unclear across other species studied here. Intriguingly, when combining all individuals from each species in one analysis, the pattern of sex-associated loci did not reflect the result from any other species. After site and individual filtering, a total of 406 individuals from bluehead, white, flannelmouth, and bluehead × white suckers were left for the GWAS analysis. Chromosomes 10 and 22 were found to be sex-associated with both the LMM and all BSLMM runs for all individuals (Fig. 3a and b). The proportion of variance from BSLMM was 0.481 with 4 SNPs expected to be involved. Using DAPC, SNPs were only detected from chromosome 10, rather than both chromosomes 10 and 22 (Supplementary Fig. S8). However, flannelmouth and bluehead × white hybrids did not have any significant loci in the LMM or any of the BSLMM runs (Supplementary Fig. S6). There are many confounding variables when combining all samples; therefore these signals may not be true sex-linked regions.

Analyses point to some genetic control of sex determination in white suckers, although the genetic architecture remains unclear, as different loci were significant with different analyses. In contrast to bluehead suckers, the LMM detected 21 sex-associated SNPs across 14 of the 50 largest chromosomes in white suckers, indicating a more complex architecture of sex determination (LMM, Fig. 3c, Supplementary Table S5). Using DAPC, 153 SNPs across 48 chromosomes were identified as sex-associated in white suckers (Supplementary Fig. S7). A SNP loading statistical threshold of 0.999 was used. This was higher than the threshold used for bluehead suckers to account for the larger number of SNPs and geographic variability. No sex-associated SNPs were detected in white suckers using the BSLMM GWAS method or RadSex (Fig. 3d). Additionally, there were four FST outliers in white suckers based on Rosner’s test, located on chromosomes 13 and 24. Chromosomes 13 and 24 were identified by all three methods (LMM, DAPC, FST; Fig. 4). Additionally, a total of four SNPs on these chromosomes were the same across three methods. Discussion

Despite the ubiquity of genetic sex determination and gonochorism in nature, the genetic architecture of sex determination is poorly characterized in many taxa, particularly in groups like fish where there is high evolutionary turnover of sex determination systems (Tree of Sex Consortium 2014; Pennell et al. 2018). This study investigated the genetic basis of sex determination in three species and one hybrid of Catostomus fishes using a GWAS. We present evidence of sex-linked loci in Catostomus fishes, but the exact genetic architecture remains uncertain, and it is unclear how conserved sex determination is across Catostomus species. We have found compelling evidence of a sex-associated region on chromosome 4 that explains some variation in sex in Colorado bluehead suckers. Different loci are implicated in white suckers, although it is unclear how robust this finding would be to analyses with larger sample sizes. From these results it appears unlikely that all species of Catostomus suckers share the same sex-linked loci on chromosome 4; however, it is still possible that they share other sex-determining loci, such as those detected on chromosomes 10 and 22 in the full analysis. Our study suggests that sex determination may vary across closely related taxa. If there is indeed variation in sex determination mechanisms, an interesting goal for future work might be to investigate the degree to which the genetic architecture of sex determination is shared across geographic regions within a species and among species, which could potentially inhibit or facilitate hybridization in Catostomus fishes.

Evidence of a sex-associated region in bluehead suckers

We found a significant region linked to sex on chromosome 4 in Colorado bluehead suckers that is suggestive of a homomorphic sex chromosome. Loci on this chromosome were significant in both the LMM and BSLMM, with large effect sizes, meaning that an individual that is homozygous for one of the significant SNPs on chromosome 4 in bluehead suckers would be more likely to be female than male. Previous work on Catostomidae karyotypes, which includes bluehead suckers, indicates the absence of well-differentiated sex chromosomes (Uyeno and Smith 1972). Therefore, it is likely that sex determination in suckers is controlled by homomorphic sex chromosomes or a small sex-linked region. Beyond the previous karyotype data, our results concur that it is unlikely that there are heteromorphic sex chromosomes because a region as large and differentiated as a heteromorphic sex chromosome would have been detected with RADSex (Jombart et al. 2010). Teleost fish have a wide variety of sex determination mechanisms (Mank et al. 2006; Tree of Sex Consortium 2014); however, it is common to have a single sex-determining region control sexual differentiation (Charlesworth and Mank 2010; Kongsstovu et al. 2020). Further, autosomal sex determination is thought to be ancestral to well-differentiated sex chromosomes (Traut and Winking 2001; Mank et al. 2006). Therefore, it is likely that the two significant loci are involved in sex determination or linked to sex-determining loci in that region of chromosome 4.

The sex-associated region on chromosome 4 could be part of a homomorphic sex chromosome or has recently changed genomic location, as we found no evidence of heteromorphic sex chromosomes. Sex chromosomes usually evolve from homomorphic to heteromorphic chromosomes, suggesting that young sex chromosomes would most likely be differentiated (Charlesworth et al. 2005; Stock et al. 2011; Furman et al. 2020). The identified region on chromosome 4 only consists of two sex-associated variants; therefore, a small and young homomorphic sex chromosome system could be present (Ogata et al. 2021). In this case, the sex determination region is either young and not diverged, or there has been a change recently in the location of the sex-determining region (Charlesworth and Mank 2010), which would be consistent with chromosome 4 being associated with sex in Colorado bluehead suckers, but not a geographically separated population of blueheads in Wyoming. These populations are from different major river systems (Colorado River vs. Columbia River basin) and are quite divergent genetically; a previous analysis showed that FST between the northwest Wyoming bluehead populations and maximally different Upper Colorado River basin populations was 0.17 (Mandeville, unpublished data). However, based on these data, it is difficult to pinpoint the age and development of possible sex chromosomes.

Assessing the likelihood of a polygenic architecture in white suckers

We found multiple chromosomes with significantly sex-associated loci in white suckers, potentially indicating a polygenic basis of sex determination (Fig. 3c). However, the smaller sample size of white sucker individuals (n = 84) undermines our confidence in a polygenic architecture to some extent, so caution is warranted in the interpretation of these analyses. BSLMMs are better suited than LMMs for small data sets (Lee and Song 2004; Baldwin and Fellingham 2013), as BSLMMs are Bayesian and sparse and thus estimate parameters from data, rather than defining them a priori. These informative parameters and distributions are a better fit to data than those defined a priori (Zhou et al. 2013; van de Schoot et al. 2015). Thus, the results from the BSLMM model provide more sensitive interpretations, even with a small sample size. Because the BSLMM did not detect any sex-associated loci for white suckers, this may indicate that the polygenic architecture we detect here with the LMM may not hold with a larger sample size. It is also possible that there may be one specific sex-determining region in white suckers (similar to the region on chromosome 4 in Colorado bluehead suckers) that cannot be detected due to the sample size. This is an area that merits further work, as larger sample sizes of white suckers and flannelmouth suckers can help clarify whether these three species, which experience extensive contemporary hybridization, have a conserved or variable basis of sex determination.

Implications of variation in sex-determining mechanisms in Catostomus sucker species

The sex-associated loci identified on chromosome 4 in Colorado bluehead suckers are potentially part of (or adjacent to) a homomorphic sex chromosome. However, these loci were not significantly sex-associated in flannelmouth, white, or bluehead × white hybrids, suggesting that bluehead suckers may have a different genetic basis of sex determination. Despite potential limitations associated with the sample size of white and flannelmouth suckers, it is unlikely that chromosome 4 is sex-associated in either species as no significant loci were identified in flannelmouth suckers and chromosome 4 did not have a high PIP or effect size in any of the three BSLMM runs. Other SNPs from chromosome 4 were identified by DAPC and LMM in white suckers, but neither contained SNPs that were significant in bluehead suckers. Therefore, based on the data presented here, it is unlikely that chromosome 4 plays a consistent role in sex determination among all Catostomus sucker species. Differing sex determination systems could therefore contribute to reproductive isolation between species, although this is outside of the scope of this study. Bluehead and white suckers do hybridize in many geographic locations, suggesting that if they do have different forms of sex determination, this difference is insufficient to fully prevent admixture and they are genetically compatible on some level, but at the same time few hybrids beyond the first generation have been observed in adult suckers for this cross (Mandeville et al. 2017), which might be indicative of incompatibilities or low fitness of the first-generation hybrids. A previous study also suggests asymmetry in ancestry of parents, where white sucker females and bluehead sucker males produce the vast majority of bluehead × white hybrid offspring (Song 2013). It is also unknown whether sex ratios in hybrid individuals are typically 1:1, with relatively equivalent numbers of males and females, or whether hybrids are sex-skewed. It is possible that other factors contribute to reproductive isolation as well, including pre- or postzygotic factors and genetic or environmental interactions.

Intriguingly, no sex-linked loci were detected in the data set consisting of all bluehead suckers (Colorado and Wyoming) or with just Wyoming bluehead suckers, suggesting geographic variation in sex-linked loci in blueheads. Although rare, genetics of sex determination can vary within a species, especially in taxa with evolutionarily labile sex determination such as fish (Faggion et al. 2019; Beaudry et al. 2020). Variation in the underlying mechanisms of sex determination may influence reproductive isolation between and among species (Ser et al. 2010; Dufresnes et al. 2020; Kottler et al. 2020), as sex chromosomes can play a role in the formation and maintenance of postzygotic isolation. Catostomus suckers have variable levels of reproductive isolation both between species and geographically (Mandeville et al. 2015, 2017). Adult bluehead × white hybrids are most often first-generation hybrids, with occasional but limited backcrosses identified in adult fish when compared with flannelmouth × white hybrids that have extensive spatial variability in hybridization, with more common backcrossing and later-generation hybridization (Mandeville et al. 2017). The observed differences in the loci contributing to sex between white and bluehead suckers as well as the potential geographic variation in sex determination in blueheads may partially explain the variation in hybridization. In particular, the absence of chromosome 4 as a sex-associated region in white suckers may play a role in the limited backcrosses observed in some previous studies of sucker hybridization (Mandeville et al. 2015, 2017).

Limitations

Certain aspects of our data set may have limited inference in this study. In particular, despite sampling thousands of loci genome-wide, GBS typically only samples about 1%–2% of the genome, which may bias our ability to detect a sex-linked region if the causal loci were not directly sampled, as is likely with this sort of reduced-representation data set (Lowry et al. 2017). Some or all of the SNPs that were sex-associated in our data set may not be within the causal locus, but instead might be linked to causal loci. Additionally, the depth of sequencing may impact the detection of sex-associated loci as coverage was 3–5 reads per locus per individual on average (Palmer et al. 2019). One additional caveat is that it is possible that a female heterogametic (ZW) system with heteromorphic sex chromosomes would be missed because the fish sampled for the reference genome was male, so sequenced genome fragments from a W-like chromosome would not align to our reference genome. However, because we did detect a signal, it is unlikely that we would miss the whole length of differentiated sex chromosomes aligning to our reference. This potential for effects of reference genome is not unique to our study, but rather a consideration that must be taken into account when selecting a reference genome for species where sex determination is unknown. However, regardless of these limitations, our data support the conclusion that genetic factors are most likely linked to sex determination, and that loci on chromosome 4 are likely sex-associated in bluehead suckers.

Although evidence supports at least partial genetic control of sex determination in suckers, we cannot discount the potential for a contributing environmental factor. This is especially the case in white suckers, where we found multiple sex-associated loci. In fish without differentiated sex chromosomes, it is often the case that sexual differentiation occurs later in development, which can allow for more environmental influence (Mank et al. 2006). It has also been shown that the reproductive responses of white suckers can change in response to lake acidity (Trippel and Harvey 1987). In particular, sexual maturity was found to be later in white suckers in acid lakes in comparison to lakes with more neutral pH, offering a hint that environmental factors may play a role. Only a small fraction of Cypriniformes have been shown to have fully environmental sex determination (Ashman et al. 2014), but closer study of sex determination in other fish lineages has revealed examples of a polygenic basis of sex determination that may interact with environmental variables like temperature (as in European sea bass; Palaiokostas et al. 2015). We therefore cannot rule out environmental contributions to sex determination in Catostomus fishes, and further study should investigate potential environmental factors.

Conclusion and future directions

In this study, we identified a probable genetic basis of sex determination in Catostomus fishes. We identified loci potentially located on homomorphic sex chromosomes on chromosome 4 in Colorado bluehead suckers, which may play a role in the level of reproductive isolation with white suckers. Although our current results suggest multiple sex-associated loci in white suckers, the identity of the causal loci and regions of the genome remains unclear, and it is possible that sample size limited our statistical power and therefore the scope of our inferences. From these data, it also appears that the genetic basis of sex determination might vary geographically in bluehead suckers as well. Although the full role of sex determination systems in reproductive isolation remains unclear, we can speculate that sex determination might exert some influence on the level of reproductive isolation seen between different Catostomus sucker species. Additional work with these species, including bolstering sample sizes, considering additional species, and sequencing a larger fraction of the genome, will reveal more fully the extent to which sex determination loci might affect current hybridization dynamics, and could illuminate what role sex determination has played in evolutionary diversification of the Catostomidae.
