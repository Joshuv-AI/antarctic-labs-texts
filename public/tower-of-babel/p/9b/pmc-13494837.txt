A maternal Polycomb imprint is maintained in early Drosophila development through lower-order methylation states
Authors: Eleanor A Degen, Natalie Gonzaga-Saavedra, Shelby A Blythe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYPolycomb group (PcG) proteins regulate embryonic epigenetic states by facilitating mono-, di-, and trimethylation of H3 lysine 27 (H3K27me1/2/3). For the zygote to inherit a maternal H3K27 methylation state, PcG modifications established during oogenesis must survive until zygotic genome activation. Whether parental PcG states persist through Drosophila embryogenesis remains unclear. Here, we combine stochastic modeling with in vivo experiments to define the fate of maternal H3K27 methylation. We find that the inherited PcG state consists of broad, non-canonical H3K27me2/3 domains and that H3K27me3 is not maintained through cleavage divisions but lost and later re-established within canonical PcG domains. In contrast, H3K27me2 persists in cis on maternal chromatin. Modeling and genetic analyses indicate that this retention is consistent with Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2) allostery-dependent maintenance rather than non-specific, nucleation-independent methylation. These findings suggest that the early embryo inherits a maternal H3K27 landscape that persists primarily as H3K27me2 and is later remodeled into canonical H3K27me3 domains.

INTRODUCTION

Multicellular organisms employ Polycomb group (PcG) proteins, which function in complexes including Polycomb repressive complex 1 (PRC1) and Polycomb repressive complex 2 (PRC2), to maintain patterns of gene silencing.1–3 PRC2 methylates histone H3 at lysine 27 (H3K27), and both di- and tri-methylated H3K27 (H3K27me2 and H3K27me3) contribute to negative regulation of gene expression.1–6 During development, PRC2 directs establishment of H3K27me3 at canonical PcG targets, resulting in stable, heritable gene silencing across cell divisions.1–3,7,8 Additionally, PRC2 deposits broad, non-canonical domains of H3K27me2 that attenuate gene expression and restrict enhancer function.4,6 These chromatin states are generally thought to be established as part of the zygotic developmental program. However, embryos also inherit parental chromatin states,9–16 raising the possibility that zygotic H3K27 methylation patterns may be influenced by epigenetic information transmitted through the gametes. A key question, therefore, is whether inherited parental H3K27 methylation states persist long enough to influence chromatin organization in the early embryo or are instead remodeled over early embryogenesis.

A major challenge for the maintenance of parental PcG states is the rapid succession of cleavage divisions that follows fertilization. In Drosophila, H3K27me3 decorates maternal chromatin and is absent from paternal chromatin at the time of pronuclear fusion.15 The embryo then undergoes thirteen rapid nuclear divisions, each lasting only ~10 min, before cell cycle lengthening and large-scale zygotic genome activation (ZGA) at nuclear cycle 14 (NC14).17,18 Because restoration of H3K27me3 is thought to occur over much longer timescales,5,19–23 these unusually short cleavage cycles pose a substantial challenge to the maintenance of an inherited maternal H3K27me3 state. On this topic, prior studies have reached conflicting conclusions about whether the maternal H3K27me3 imprint survives early Drosophila embryogenesis,15,24–29 leaving unresolved whether any parental H3K27 methylation state persists through the cleavage divisions.

Parental inheritance of H3K27 methylation has been characterized more extensively in mouse preimplantation embryos, where broad maternal H3K27me2/3 domains persist after fertilization and contribute to parent-of-origin differences in epigenetic regulation.11,13,16,30 Early mouse cleavage divisions proceed over several days, in marked contrast to the rapid early divisions of Drosophila embryos. In mouse, maintenance of maternal H3K27me3 depends in part on PRC1-mediated histone H2A ubiquitylation (H2Aub).13 However, H2Aub is not detectable on chromatin through most of the cleavage divisions in Drosophila and is instead established de novo at ZGA.26 These differences suggest that parental H3K27 methylation states in Drosophila may be erased during the rapid cleavage divisions, or if they persist, may do so through a mechanism distinct from that described in mouse.

Across systems, maintenance of H3K27 methylation can occur through at least two distinct modes of PRC2 activity. At canonical PcG targets, H3K27me3 domains are maintained through nucleation of PRC2 at genomic targets followed by spreading across neighboring nucleosomes.2,3,5,19,20,22,31,32 Prior H3K27me2/3 states within canonical PcG targets allosterically stimulate PRC2 to enable restoration of methylation on new histones incorporated after DNA replication.33–36 In contrast, broad non-canonical domains of H3K27me2 may arise through a less sequence-specific mode of PRC2 activity termed “hit-and-run,” in which transient chromatin interactions promote lower order methylation outside canonical PcG domains.2,4–6,19,37 However, the sharp distinction between canonical H3K27me3 domains and non-canonical H3K27me2 domains observed in many biological contexts is less apparent in both Drosophila female germline stem cells and mouse oocytes and early embryos, where these marks can coexist in broader non-canonical domains.30,38 This raises the possibility that early embryos inherit an H3K27 methylation landscape whose organization and maintenance differ from those described in more differentiated cells.

Here, we investigate the kinetics of maternal H3K27 methylation states in Drosophila embryos through computational modeling and in vivo experiments. We adapt a prior stochastic modeling framework28 by incorporating in vivo measurements of catalytic and nucleating components of PRC2 activity and by fitting the model to measurements of H3K27 methylation at NC14.26,39–41 The model recapitulates the loss of maternal H3K27me3 during the cleavage divisions and its re-establishment at ZGA, while also simulating persistence of maternal H3K27me2 in cis through a mechanism dependent on PRC2 allosteric simulation. We experimentally validate this prediction, finding that broad maternal H3K27me2 persists through the early cleavage divisions, whereas maternal H3K27me3 is remodeled over early embryogenesis. Furthermore, we experimentally validate the model’s prediction that parental asymmetries in H3K27 methylation equalize shortly after ZGA. These findings show that H3K27me2, but not H3K27me3, persists as an inherited H3K27 methylation state in the early Drosophila embryo.

RESULTS

Estimation of PRC2 catalytic output in early embryos

At fertilization, the maternal but not paternal pronucleus is decorated with H3K27me3.15 However, during the rapid cell cycles of the cleavage stage, these maternally supplied modifications are not maintained at high levels.15,26 At ZGA, H3K27me3 is rapidly re-established and is readily detectable within canonical PcG domains (Figure 1A).15,25–27,29 A prior modeling approach predicted the observed loss of maternal H3K27me3 but did not simulate the observed rapid re-establishment of H3K27me3 at ZGA.28 We hypothesized that modeling of H3K27me3 in early embryos could be improved by better accounting for the specific conditions of early embryogenesis. In early development, critical components of the PcG system are not constitutively present in nuclei. Importantly, a host of nucleating factors (Pleiohomeotic [Pho], GAGA-factor, and Combgap) show limited nuclear localization during the early cleavage divisions.26 Additionally, the constitutively nuclear histone methyltransferase subunit of PRC2, enhancer of zeste (E(z)), undergoes measured changes in nuclear concentration during nuclear divisions.26 We therefore sought to develop a revised modeling framework that accounts for our prior measurements of factor concentrations and catalytic output.

To estimate catalytic output, we quantified de novo H3K27me3 accumulation measured by chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing (ChIP-seq) across a time course spanning NC13 through late NC14, by re-analysis of a previously published dataset.26 During this time, H3K27me3 is established de novo and spreads bidirectionally within canonical PcG domains from sites of nucleation known as Polycomb response elements (PREs) (Figure 1A).26 Because the dynamics of nucleation and spreading are less evident at closely spaced PREs, we chose to focus on a set of isolated PREs to characterize the H3K27 methylation levels associated with a single PRE over time (Figures 1B–1D). We defined an isolated PRE to be a major E(z) peak within a PcG domain that is ≥ 27 kbp from a neighboring peak (STAR Methods). Plotting 99th percentile values of H3K27me3 at isolated PREs, we find that H3K27me3 increases roughly linearly over time (Figures 1B and 1D). These dynamics reflect the upgrading of lower order methylation states, as H3K27me1 is progressively depleted at isolated PREs as H3K27me3 emerges (Figure 1C). We next implemented the measured methylation states of isolated PREs as empirical benchmarks for a revised PRC2 modeling framework.

A revised stochastic approach for modeling H3K27 methylation dynamics

To computationally model H3K27 methylation across cell divisions, we adapted a prior framework to simulate E(z) activity across an array of nucleosomes representing a canonical PcG target.28 In our model, we incorporated allosteric regulation of E(z) by existing H3K27me2/3, as well as nucleation of E(z) activity by a DNA-binding factor (Figure 2A).36,42 We assumed in our initial implementation that non-allosteric, nucleation-independent E(z) activity would contribute minimally to chromatin modifications within canonical PcG domains. We drew upon prior live imaging and quantitative mass spectroscopy measurements to model E(z) and Pho nuclear concentration dynamics over early development (Figure 2B and Data S1).26,39 We assumed that Pho is a representative nucleating factor in the early embryo. With these concentration measurements coupled with dissociation constant estimates (Data S1),40,41 we estimated the probability that E(z), the nucleator (in this case, Pho), and E(z) + the nucleator associate with a PcG target across developmental time (Data S1). Finally, in line with prior work, we modeled the effect of DNA replication on the nucleosome array by fully demethylating each nucleosome with a probability of 0.5 once per nuclear cycle (Data S1).20,28

Our model allows E(z) to methylate H3K27 in the nucleosome array only if either allostery or nucleation occurs (Figure 2A and Data S1). For allosteric stimulation of E(z), at least one of the modeled histones neighboring a nucleosome of interest must be H3K27me2/3, and E(z) must be associated with the modeled PcG domain. To estimate the allosteric stimulation parameters of the model, we drew upon prior in vitro reaction rate measurements36 to calculate the degree of allosteric stimulation provided by H3K27me2/3 (Data S1). For nucleation, an allostery-driven reaction must not have occurred and both E(z) and Pho must be simultaneously associated with the PcG domain. In tests, the order of implementation of allostery and nucleation did not have a significant impact on model predictions (Data S1). To calibrate the model to in vivo measurements of methylation outputs,26 we performed a parameter sweep with the model’s one free parameter: the baseline methylation reaction probability, V. We varied V from slightly less than the value used in Lundkvist et al.,28 V = 4 × 10−4, to V = 1.2 × 10−3 in steps of V = 1 × 10−5 and evaluated the model’s performance at these values (Data S1). We find that the model best fits both the H3K27me3 and H3K27me1 ChIP-seq time-course data when V = 7.35 × 10−4, 1.18× the baseline methylation probability of Lundkvist et al. (Data S1).28 We therefore used V = 7.35 × 10−4 in all simulations presented in this work.

We find that, following parameter fitting to the in vivo data, our model predicts observed H3K27me3 dynamics at isolated PREs (Figures 2C and 2C′; Data S1). Simulated PcG domains begin NC14 with nearly no H3K27me3 and acquire the mark linearly over the nuclear cycle, consistent with in vivo observations (Figures 2C, 2C′, and 1B).15,25–27,29 In addition to allowing the model to reproduce the rapid NC14 accumulation of H3K27me3 measured in vivo,26 a feature not captured by the original parameterization of Lundkvist et al.,28 our model predicts the observed dilution of maternal H3K27me3 during the cleavage divisions (Figure 2C), consistent with prior work.26,28 Regardless of the initial H3K27me3 levels of the chromatin array, the model predicts depletion of nearly all maternally supplied H3K27me3 by NC7 (Data S1). Together, our model recapitulates key features of in vivo H3K27 methylation dynamics at PcG domains in early embryos.

The model additionally makes predictions about two features of the system that have not been subject to extensive prior study: 1) the behavior of lower order H3K27 methylation states during early Drosophila development and (2) potential asymmetries between maternal and paternal chromatin states. We performed simulations of maternal and paternal states by varying the initial H3K27 conditions of the model, as maternal but not paternal chromosomes begin development with H3K27me3.15 We assumed that the paternal pronucleus lacks lower order H3K27 methylation in addition to H3K27me3. The model predicts that, unlike H3K27me3, H3K27me1/2 persists on maternal chromatin throughout cleavage stages (Figure 2C). In contrast, the model predicts that H3K27 methylation does not accumulate on paternal chromosomes until NC10, when nucleating factors such as Pho first localize to nuclei (Figures 2B and 2C′). Our simulations point to a dependence of paternal chromosomes on the activity of nucleating factors for the de novo establishment of H3K27 methylation. In contrast, our model predicts that maternal chromosomes maintain lower order methylation states in cis through allosteric regulation of E(z), independently of nucleation. The model predicts that parent-of-origin asymmetries persist for all methylation states through the cleavage stage and then begin to resolve at ZGA, with H3K27me3 equalizing on maternal and paternal alleles by late NC14 (Figures 2C and 2D; Data S1). The key feature of the model underlying these predicted parental asymmetries is the limited availability of nucleating factors and the assumption that there is no non-allosteric, nucleation-independent E(z) activity within canonical PcG domains. Because nucleation-independent activity is thought to contribute to lower order H3K27 methylation within non-canonical PRC2 targets, we asked if we included non-allosteric, nucleation-independent methylation in our model, what effect this would have on simulated outputs (Figures 2E and S1; Data S1). The main effect of modeling non-allosteric, nucleation-independent E(z) activity is that simulated paternal chromatin immediately begins gaining methylation during the cleavage divisions, attenuating any expected parent-of-origin asymmetry in lower order methylation (Figures 2E and S1). Given these predictions, we investigated H3K27me2 dynamics experimentally in early Drosophila embryos.

H3K27me2 is propagated on maternal chromosomes from the beginning of development

Our model predicts that maternal chromosomes maintain lower order H3K27 methylation throughout the earliest nuclear divisions, while paternally derived chromosomes do not (Figure 2C). We tested this prediction by immunostaining H3K27me2 in early wild-type embryos and in haploid embryos containing either solely maternal or paternal chromatin (Figure 3). Our wild-type measurements reveal extensive maintenance of an H3K27me2-modified state (Figure 3A). Staining H3K27me2 in E(z) mutant embryos yields no measurable signal, confirming that the signal detected by this approach is specific to E(z) activity (Figure 3B). Like -me3, H3K27me2 staining shortly after fertilization decorates only a portion of chromatin in each nucleus (Figure 3A, NC2).15,26 Unlike -me3, however, H3K27me2 staining remains detectable throughout the cleavage divisions (Figure 3A, NC6 and NC13; Figure 3C) before increasing in intensity at NC14 (Figure 3A, NC14). We noted that, in all pre-ZGA specimens, the H3K27me2 signal marked only a subregion of the nucleus, similar to H3K27me2/3 staining near the time of fertilization (Figures 3A and S2A).15,26 To better visualize this, we identified pre-NC14 embryos at mitotic prophase and generated high-resolution images. In all cases, the high-resolution H3K27me2 signal shows a punctate distribution on only a subset (approximately 50%) of DAPI-positive material (Figure 3D, wild type). We hypothesized that the partial, E(z)-dependent staining pattern reflects active maintenance of the H3K27me2 modification on maternal chromatin.

To test this hypothesis, we stained haploid embryos. We first stained gynogenetic haploid embryos produced by sesame mutant mothers (FlyBase: hira). These embryos develop with nuclei that only contain maternally derived chromatin, due to a failure of the male pronucleus to decondense at fertilization.43 In contrast to patterns in wild-type embryos, H3K27me2 staining covers nearly all the chromatin mass in sesame mutant nuclei (Figure 3D, sesame). We also tested whether wild-type paternal chromosomes lack H3K27me2 by staining androgenetic haploid embryos produced by ɑTubulin67C mutant mothers. ɑTubulin67C mutants contain nuclei with only paternal chromatin, due to the failure of meiosis to complete in the egg.44 In ɑTubulin67C mutants, nuclei lack detectable H3K27me2 (Figure 3D, ɑTubulin67C). Our haploid experiments provide strong evidence that H3K27me2 is propagated through the cleavage divisions solely on maternal chromatin.

We next tested whether H3K27me2 propagation depends on PRC2 activity by staining embryos produced from homozygous extra sex combs (esc) mutant mothers.39,45 Esc is a core PRC2 subunit responsible for allosteric stimulation of E(z) activity.36 Loss of function for the mammalian ortholog of Esc (EED) results in loss of the majority of genomic H3K27me2 in a manner that depends both on H3K27 binding and allosteric activity.19,34,36 At pronuclear fusion, H3K27me2 is observed in one of two pronuclei in both wild-type and esc mutant embryos (Figure 3E). However, unlike wild-type embryos, esc mutants lack H3K27me2 in cleavage-stage nuclei (Figure 3E). Thus, although esc mutant embryos begin development with parentally derived H3K27me2, that state is not effectively maintained over cleavage divisions in the absence of esc function. Similarly, although E(z)61 mutant embryos begin development with an intact maternal PcG state, they fail to maintain this information over the cleavage divisions (Figures 3B and S2B). These findings indicate that the maintenance of parental H3K27me2 requires PRC2 function and are consistent with a role for Esc-dependent allosteric stimulation. Because null esc allelic combinations also reduce expression of other PRC2 subunits,39 reduced PRC2 abundance could in principle contribute to this phenotype, and the genetic result alone does not specifically isolate the role of allostery. To address this, we repeated our simulations across a range of E(z) concentrations and quantified H3K27me2 levels expected at NC8. When E(z) concentration in the model is reduced by the 9-fold reported for esc mutants, maternal H3K27me2 levels are expected to be reduced by 2.8-fold (Figure S2C). We therefore infer that reduced PRC2 abundance alone is unlikely to explain the near-complete loss of cleavage-stage H3K27me2 in esc mutants, supporting a specific role for Esc-dependent allosteric stimulation in maintenance of the maternal state.

We next measured H3K27me2, -me3, and total H3 using ChIP-seq, comparing early cleavage-stage embryos (NC1–10) with NC14 embryos. At both time points, H3K27me2 occupies broad genomic territories (Figures 4A and S3). In contrast, while H3K27me3 accumulates to high levels within canonical PcG domains at NC14, at NC1–10, H3K27me3 is found at relatively lower levels and in broader, non-canonical genomic territories (Figures 4A, 4B, and S3). We interpret the NC1–10 measurements to represent a mixture of the maternally inherited H3K27me2/3 states and the states maintained on early cleavage-stage chromatin. Given our modeling and immunostaining observations, we hypothesize that H3K27me2 is maintained primarily on maternal chromatin during NC1–10 and that this asymmetry equalizes by NC14. To assign ChIPped DNA to a parent of origin, we performed experiments on polymorphic embryos produced from a cross between y w; Rpa70-EGFP females and OregonR males. Based on resequencing of parental stocks, 0.16% of bases within canonical PcG domains (n = 11,927) are represented by homozygous polymorphisms (SNPs) in only one parent. We counted the frequency with which such informative SNPs were recovered in each of our ChIP-seq samples (Figure 4C, see STAR Methods). Whereas we observe that total H3 is recovered with the expected allelic frequency of ~50% at both time points, both H3K27me2 and -me3 are strongly enriched for recovery of maternal SNPs at NC1–10 (85% H3K27me2, 86% H3K27me3, mean over two biological replicates, Figure 4C). By NC14, the proportion of maternally associated SNPs for both modifications approaches 50% (56% H3K27me2, 57% H3K27me3, Figure 4C). These parent-of-origin asymmetries are not limited to canonical PcG domains and reflect a genome-wide trend where both H3K27me2/3 modifications associate primarily with maternal chromatin during early cleavage stages (Figure S3). These results confirm our computational model’s predictions that parental asymmetries in H3K27me2 persist during the cleavage divisions and that these asymmetries resolve by late NC14.

Finally, we measured the co-occurrence of H3K27me2 and -me3 over NC1–10 and to what extent these domains change by NC14. Prior observations in Drosophila female germline stem cells as well as in mouse oocytes and early embryos have observed co-occurrence of H3K27me2/3 over broad, non-canonical domains.30,38 To characterize co-occurrence of these modifications in early Drosophila embryos, we used ChromHMM46 to call genome-wide presence/absence of these modifications in 2 kb bins. Similar to observations in mouse embryonic stem cell cultures and early embryos, H3K27me2 is found at both NC1–10 and NC14 to occupy ~61% of the genome (62% at NC1–10, 60% at NC14, Figure 4D).4,30 At NC1–10, H3K27me3 co-occupies the majority of H3K27me2-modified regions (57% genomic coverage, 98% overlap with H3K27me2, Figures 4D and 4E). However, in contrast to H3K27me2, which is maintained over equivalent genomic regions from NC1–10 to NC14, H3K27me3 coverage is lost over 74% of its maternal domains when re-established biparentally at NC14 within canonical PcG domains (16% genomic coverage, Figures 4D and 4E). These observations are consistent with a mechanism where, while H3K27me2 is maintained, H3K27me3 states are reprogrammed over the course of early development.

DISCUSSION

Here, we addressed whether parental H3K27 methylation states persist in the Drosophila embryo or are instead remodeled during early development. Our results suggest a mechanism where (1) maternal H3K27me2 and -me3 are provided in broad, non-canonical domains; (2) these domains are maintained on maternal homologs at the level of H3K27me2 throughout the cleavage divisions primarily in cis; (3) following loss of maternally supplied H3K27me3 during the cleavage divisions, H3K27me3 is established de novo at ZGA within canonical PcG domains on both homologs; and finally (4) parental asymmetries in H3K27 methylation states equalize shortly after ZGA. These findings support a mechanism where the early embryo inherits a developmentally immature PcG landscape that is later remodeled into the canonical PcG domain architecture of the zygotic genome.

The maternal-paternal asymmetries in H3K27 methylation states that persist through the cleavage divisions suggest that the maintenance of maternal H3K27me2 likely does not depend on a trans-acting nucleation system acting equivalently on maternal and paternal chromatin. In contrast to Drosophila embryos, mouse embryos both maintain maternal and establish de novo H3K27 methylation through PRC1-mediated H2Aub, which is read by Jarid2 and AEBP to recruit and stimulate PRC2.13,30 A similar mechanism has been reported for de novo H3K27me3 establishment in zebrafish.47 Drosophila differs from these systems in that H2Aub is undetectable on cleavage-stage chromatin and is established shortly before large-scale ZGA.26 These observations indicate that early maintenance of maternal H3K27me2 in Drosophila is unlikely to rely on the H2Aub/Jarid2/AEBP-dependent nucleation mechanisms described in vertebrates, although they leave open the possibility that such mechanisms contribute at ZGA to de novo establishment of H3K27 methylation.

Rather, our results support a nucleation-independent mechanism for maintenance of maternal H3K27me2. Broad, non-canonical H3K27me2 domains often occur in genomic regions lacking focused peaks of PRC2 components and have therefore been proposed to arise through a transient “hit-and-run” mode of PRC2 activity, which can operate in principle with and without allosteric enhancement.2,4–6,19,28,37 However, our simulations indicate that a purely non-allosteric hit-and-run mechanism would likely promote more extensive methylation of paternal chromatin during the cleavage stages than we observe in vivo. Our results instead suggest that maintenance of H3K27me2 is informed by the prior chromatin modification state, which our computational model captures through allosteric stimulation of E(z) by pre-existing H3K27me2/3. Consistent with this interpretation, esc mutants fail to maintain maternal H3K27me2 through the cleavage divisions. Although Esc binds H3K27me2 less strongly and stimulates E(z) less effectively than H3K27me3,36 these results suggest that Esc-dependent allostery is sufficient to support maintenance of the maternal state. Additional H3K27me2-binding factors may also contribute,35 and it will be important to determine whether such factors act with PRC2 to stabilize broad maternal H3K27me2 domains.

Compared with H3K27me3, the role of H3K27me2 in gene regulation remains less understood. Nonetheless, loss of PRC2 function causes widespread gene derepression, including in genes located within non-canonical PcG domains, consistent with H3K27me2 attenuating transcription across broad genomic regions.4,6 It is therefore possible that early embryonic H3K27me2 contributes to zygotic transcriptome regulation. Aligning with this idea, our work suggests that an early derepression phenotype previously attributed to loss of H3K27me3 may instead reflect failure to maintain broad H3K27me2.15 Additionally, the persistence of broad maternal H3K27me2 in the early embryo raises the possibility that these domains reflect continued maintenance of a distinct germline-associated chromatin state. This is consistent with prior observations in Drosophila where, like in mouse, germline cells exhibit broad non-canonical H3K27me2/3 domains.13,16,30,38 The potential impact of parental domain inheritance has been dampened by observations that maternal esc mutants can be rescued to adult viability by zygotic esc+ expression,45 which argues that parental PcG-state inheritance is not absolutely required for gross embryonic patterning. However, maternal H3K27me2 may nonetheless tune early transcriptional output. This possibility may be especially relevant for the germline: pole cells (germline precursor cells) must suppress zygotic transcription while somatic lineages undergo ZGA,48–50 raising the possibility that efficient maintenance of broad non-canonical H3K27me2 is important for preserving germline identity. It will be of particular interest to compare the zygotic epigenomes of somatic and germline lineages to determine whether pole cells preferentially retain these broad non-canonical H3K27 states after ZGA.

Limitations of the study

Our experimental validation of the model relies on antibodies and immunodetection. As such, we cannot completely rule out the presence of low but biologically relevant quantities of H3K27 methylation on paternal chromatin. Additionally, while our modeling supports a scenario where non-allosteric, nucleation-independent PRC2 activity has little impact on the H3K27 methyl-state in early Drosophila development, our results do not rule out that such an activity could contribute to biological systems where establishment and maintenance of these states unfold over significantly longer timescales.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL DETAILS

Drosophila stocks

The wild type stock used in this study is w1118 as it serves as the genetic background for gene edited and transgenic lines in our laboratory.

E(z)61 mutant embryos were produced by culturing homozygous male and female E(z)61 adults at 29°C overnight prior to embryo collection. E(z)61/TM3 was a kind gift of Richard Jones, and is a temperature-sensitive loss-of-function mutant allele that is expressed at the restrictive temperature of 29°C.56 For these experiments, E(z)61 adults are reared at the permissive temperature (18°C) until the evening before embryo collection. Following an overnight incubation at the restrictive temperature (29°C), embryos are collected for immunostaining at the restrictive temperature. To prevent the occurrence of expected secondary effects on oogenesis due to prolonged loss of E(z) function,38 embryos were only collected from adults reared at the restrictive temperature for 24 h or less. This approach results in embryos derived from oocytes that developed almost entirely under permissive conditions and that were only shifted to the restrictive temperature shortly before egg activation/fertilization.

sesame mutant embryos were produced by collecting eggs laid by homozygous sesame mutant mothers crossed to male siblings. The sesame185b mutant stock was originally provided by Benjamin Loppin to the Princeton Stock Collection. esc mutant embryos were collected from esc2/6 mutant mothers crossed to mutant fathers. esc2 and esc6 stocks were a kind gift of Jürg Müller. Mutant cuticle phenotypes and lethality rates were measured prior to further experimentation to confirm that stocks bred true.

Androgenetic haploid embryos were collected from alphaTubulin67C3/Δ mothers produced from a cross between alphaTubulin67C3/TM3 and Df(3L)BSC576/TM3 (Bloomington Drosophila Stock Center). The paternal zygotic genotype was w1118. Although prior reports44 indicated that ~10% of alphaTubulin67C3/Δ embryos should survive to cellular blastoderm stages albeit with aberrant morphology, in our collections, only ~1% of embryos had close to normal cleavage stage morphology. In all the morphologically normal cleavage stage embryos we observed, blastoderm nuclei lacked any detectable H3K27me2. In many of the abnormal (defective) embryos, we observed large, atypical chromatin masses that were either intensely positive or negative for H3K27me2. We interpreted this based on the prior report, which specifies that a subset of these masses represent abortive maternal meiotic products caused by the reduced function of maternal alpha tubulin.44

For the polymorphic ChIP experiments, y w; RpA70-EGFP females51 were crossed to OregonR males. OregonR was a kind gift of Robert Holmgren.

METHOD DETAILS

Immunostaining

Fixation

Embryos were collected from flies of a genotype of interest (w1118, E(z)61, sesame185b, ɑTubulin67C3/Δ, or esc2/6) after a laying period of 2–6 h on an apple-juice agar plate. A heat-methanol fixation process was then performed as reported previously (Gonzaga-Saavedra et al., 2025). Embryos were dechorionated for 1 min in 4% Sodium Hypochlorite and then washed with deionized water. Following dechorionation, embryos were immersed in 5 mL of boiling 0.3% Triton X-100 + 0.4% (w/v) NaCl in a 20 mL scintillation vial for 10–20 s. The vial with the embryos was then placed on ice to cool for ≥15 min. Once ice-cold, the salt solution was removed (leaving the embryos). 5 mL methanol and 5 mL heptane were then added and the vial vortexed for 30 s to help the embryos dissociate (“pop”) from their vitelline membranes. The popped embryos that fell to the bottom of the vial were collected and washed 3x in methanol. The embryos were then stored at −20°C for ≥24 h.

Immunostaining

Fixed embryos were rehydrated in PBT (1x phosphate buffered saline +0.1% Triton X-100 + 1% Bovine Serum Albumin) and then blocked for 1 h in blocking buffer (1x phosphate buffered saline +0.1% Triton X-100 + 5% Bovine Serum Albumin) rocking at room temperature. Embryos were then incubated in blocking buffer and primary antibody rocking overnight at 4°C. To stain H3K27me3, the anti-H3K27me3 antibody (Diagenode C15410195) was diluted 1:500 in blocking buffer. To stain H3K27me2, the anti-H3K27me2 antibody (Cell Signaling #9728) was diluted 1:1000 in blocking buffer. The specificities of the anti-H3K27me3 and me2 antibodies were previously tested using the methodology used here57 and reported here: https://chromatinantibodies.com/. Staining of H3K27me1 was not feasible, as the available antibodies are incompatible with the immunostaining protocol. After overnight incubation, the primary antibody solution was removed and embryos washed in PBT. Following the washes, embryos were incubated in blocking buffer and secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit coupled to Alexa 546 nm, 1:500 dilution) rocking for 2 h at room temperature. The secondary antibody solution was then discarded and embryos rinsed in PTw (1x phosphate buffered saline +0.1% Tween 20). Embryos were then stained with 1 μg/mL DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole; Invitrogen D1306) in PTw rocking for 1 h at room temperature. The DAPI solution was replaced with PTw and the embryos stored at 4°C.

Imaging

Slides were prepared for imaging by mounting embryos in Prolong Gold Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen P36934) and overlaying them with a glass coverslip. Images were collected as reported previously26 on a Leica SP8 WLL Confocal Microscope using a 63x/1.3 NA glycerol-immersion objective. To create the presented time course images, staged embryos were imaged with a 100 Hz scan rate at 512 × 512 pixels,12-bit resolution, and 2x zoom. Z-stacks were imaged with a 1 μm z-step size and positioned such that the first and last slices of the stacks corresponded to the top and bottom of the nuclei. To create the wild type NC8 and sesame NC9 high-resolution images, embryos were imaged with a 100 Hz scan rate at 1024 × 1024 pixels, 12-bit resolution, and 4x zoom. In this case, z-stacks were imaged with a 0.33 μm z-step size and positioned such that the first and last slices of the stacks corresponded to the top and bottom of the nuclei. A 557 nm laser was used to excite Alexa 546 nm conjugated secondary antibodies. A 405 nm laser was used to excite DAPI. Images were collected with a by-line sequential scan of the lasers.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

Four replicates of the ChIP-seq experiment comparing occupancy of H3, H3K27me2, and H3K27me3 in either NC1-10 or late NC14 embryos were performed in total. To distinguish maternal and paternal association of each ChIP target, female y w; Rpa70-EGFP flies were crossed to male OregonR flies. Two replicates of this ChIP were performed. The remaining two replicates were from a cross between y w; Rpa70-EGFP males and females. Rpa70-EGFP is a bright nuclear marker that facilitates staging of fixed embryos by nuclear density.51 Additionally, whole genome resequencing was performed for both y w; Rpa70-EGFP and OregonR to identify parental single nucleotide polymorphisms.

Fixation

Embryos were collected from small population cages, dechorionated in 4% Sodium Hypochlorite (Clorox), washed extensively in deionized water, and fixed for 15 min in 6 mL Heptane, 2 mL PBS +0.5% Triton X-100, and 180 μL 20% Formaldehyde. Fixation was quenched with a two minute incubation in PBS +0.5% Triton X-100 + 125 mM Glycine followed by three washes in ice-cold PBS +0.5% Triton X-100.

Embryo sorting

Fixed embryos were transferred to 35 mm petri dishes lined with 1% agarose and sorted under PBS +0.5% Triton X-100. Rpa70-EGFP epifluorescence was observed using a Leica M165 FC stereomicroscope. For NC1-10 collections, interphase embryos with nuclear density ranging from NC1 to NC10 were collected. For NC14 collections, late NC14 embryos (up to and including cephalic furrow formation, but prior to ventral furrow formation) were collected. To match approximate nuclear density between stages, 150 NC1-10 and 10 NC14 embryos were used per ChIP. Sorted embryos were frozen at −80°C until the beginning of the ChIP procedure. Under these conditions, sufficient embryos for a full ChIP replicate could be collected in one day or less.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation

ChIP-seq was performed as described previously.26 Briefly, sorted fixed embryos were thawed and resuspended in RIPA buffer + protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma P8340) (RIPA: 150 mM NaCl, 1% Igepal-CA630, 0.5% Sodium Deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM Tris pH7.4). Sufficient embryos for three ChIPs were pooled together (450 NC1-10 and 30 NC14 embryos) in a final volume of 700 μL. Pooled embryo samples were subjected to four 15-s rounds of sonication at 20% output (Branson Sonifier 450, ¼-inch microtip horn) to yield sheared chromatin. Aliquots of sheared chromatin corresponding to 150 NC1-10 embryos or 10 NC14 embryos were distributed to Eppendorf DNA Lo-Bind tubes (Eppendorf 022431021) and brought up to 650 μL final volume with fresh RIPA buffer + protease inhibitors. Sheared chromatin aliquots were incubated with primary antibodies (1 μg each per ChIP) overnight at 4°C on a nutator. For each replicate, total H3 (Mouse monoclonal 1B1B2, Cell Signaling Technology 14269), H3K27me2 (D18C8 rabbit monoclonal, Cell Signaling Technology 9728), and H3K27me3 (Rabbit Polyclonal, Diagenode C15410195) was ChIPped from both NC1-10 and NC14 samples.

The following morning, 15 μL of Protein G Dynabeads (Fisher 10004D) pre-blocked overnight in PBS +5% Bovine Serum Albumin +0.2 mg/mL Yeast tRNA (Sigma 10109223001) was added and incubated for an additional hour. Bead-antibody complexes were immobilized on a magnetic separation stand and washed 2x with 10 mM Tris pH 7.5 + 5 mM MgCl2. Bead-antibody complexes were subjected to tagmentation by addition of 20 μL of 1x Illumina Tn5 reaction buffer and 1 μL of Tn5 transposase (Illumina 20034210) and incubation at 37°C with 1000 RPM shaking on an Eppendorf Thermomixer. Bead-antibody complexes were then washed 2 × 5 min in ice-cold Wash Buffer I (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 0.1% SDS, 1% Triton X-100, 2mM EDTA, 150 mM HCl) on a nutator at room temperature. This was followed by 2 × 5 min washes in ice-cold Wash Buffer II (Wash Buffer I with 500 mM NaCl), and 1 × 5 min wash in TE (10 mM Tris, pH 8.0, 1 mM EDTA). Immunoprecipitated and tagmented DNA was eluted from beads and crosslink-reversed with a four-hour incubation at 65°C with 1200 RPM shaking in 50 μL of Elution Buffer + Proteinase K (50 mM Tris pH8.0, 10 mM EDTA, 1% SDS +1 mg/mL Proteinase K). DNA was cleaned up using a Zymo ChIP DNA Clean and Concentrator kit (D5205), following the manufacturer’s instructions, and eluting in 22.5 μL.

Libraries were amplified and barcoded by performing PCR (50 μL) containing 22.5 μL of ChIP DNA, 2.5 μL of 25 mM Dual-unique barcoded primers, and 25 μL of NEBNext 2x Hi-Fi PCR Mastermix (New England Biolabs M0541). Primers were as described previously.58,59 To determine the number of total PCR cycles, a 5 μL aliquot of the initial PCR solution was subjected to a QPCR side reaction by addition of 5 μL of NEBNext Hi-Fi PCR Mastermix and 5 μL of SYBR-Primer mix (1.8x SYBR Green I (Invitrogen S7563) + 1.25 mM Dual-unique barcoded primer mix). The main PCR (45 μL) solution was stored at 4°C while the side reaction was subjected to one 5 min cycle at 72°C and 30 s at 98°C and 25 cycles of 10 s at 98°C, 30 s at 63°C, and 1 min at 72°C with imaging of SYBR Green fluorescence at the end of each extension stage using an Applied Biosystems Quantstudio 3 QPCR system. The optimal number of PCR cycles was determined by dividing the plateau QPCR fluorescence value by 3 and rounding up to the nearest whole cycle number. The main PCR was then completed by performing the determined number of cycles using the same cycling parameters as described above.

Amplified libraries were cleaned up by 1.8x Ampure (Beckman Coulter A63880) and eluted in 15 μL Qiagen Buffer EB. Concentrations were measured using Qubit 1x dsDNA High Sensitivity fluorometry (Fisher Q33230) and fragment size distributions were determined with TapeStation D5000 High Sensitivity reagents (Agilent 5067–5592). Equimolar concentrations of fragments sized between 100 and 700 bp were pooled together and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated using an Illumina NovaSeq (Admera Health).

Whole genome resequencing

Genomic DNA from male flies was extracted using the Zymo Quick-DNA microprep kit (D3020) following the manufacturer’s instructions for tissue samples without Proteinase K treatment, eluting in 12 μL to yield ~10 ng/μL final concentration. DNA libraries were made in 10 μL tagmentation reactions containing 1 μL Illumina Tn5, 1 μL of DNA in 1x Illumina Tagmentation Buffer, which were incubated for 10 min in a 55°C water bath. Reactions were stopped by addition of 2 μL of 0.2% SDS solution and further incubation for 5 min in a 55°C water bath. DNA libraries were cleaned up using the Zymo ChIP DNA Clean and Concentrator kit as described above. Libraries were amplified by performing 8 cycles of PCR as described above for the main PCR reactions using appropriate unique dual indexing primer sets. PCR reactions were cleaned up by 1.8x Ampure and were quantified by Qubit and TapeStation as described above. Libraries were pooled with ChIP-seq libraries and were mapped as described above.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

Modeling

The structure of the modeling framework is described in detail in Data S1: Modeling Supplement.

Sequencing data analysis

Sequencing read mapping

Sequencing reads were trimmed using FastP (0.11.552) and mapped to the Drosophila melanogaster reference genome assembly (dm6) using bowtie2 (2.4.153). Default parameters were used for mapping except that mapped paired-end reads were limited to 2000 bp lengths using the -x 2000 parameter. PCR duplicates were marked using Picard MarkDuplicates (2.21.4, https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/) and bigwig coverage plots of deduplicated mapped reads with map quality scores greater than 20 were generated using deeptools (3.5.6 54). Reads mapping to the mitochondrial genome were used as an internal spike-in control to account for differences in starting DNA content between NC1-10 and NC14 samples as described previously.51

Parental SNP quantification

Parent-of-origin analysis was performed by quantifying maternal and paternal allele representation at informative SNPs within NC14 Polycomb Domains.26 First, whole-genome resequencing data from the two parental strains were jointly analyzed using bcftools mpi-leup and bcftools call (1.10.155) to identify high-confidence biallelic SNPs in the regions of interest. Informative SNPs were defined as sites at which the two parents carried opposing homozygous genotypes and each parent met minimum depth (≥10 reads) and quality (base call and mapping quality ≥20) thresholds. This identified 11927 informative SNPs (0.16% of bases) across the set of Polycomb Domains that could be quantified for parent-of-origin asymmetries. This observed frequency of SNPs is similar to independent measurements of genome-wide polymorphism between OregonR and w[1118] (~0.25–0.16%60). For each ChIP-seq library, read counts for maternal and paternal alleles were then obtained at each informative SNP position using bcftools mpileup, with filters on mapping quality and base quality (≥20). Maternal and paternal counts were then summed across all the informative SNPs for each library, and maternal and paternal fractions were calculated from these totals to generate per-sample estimates of parental SNP frequency.

H3K27me2 and -me3 state calling

To identify broad coverage tracts of either K27 modified state, we used ChromHMM46 to call a two-state model that resolved presence or absence of an individual modification over consecutive bins of the genome at each of the measured timepoints. ChromHMM output was visually evaluated to confirm concordance with observed H3K27me2 and -me3 occupancy at both NC1-10 and NC14. Through this approach, a 2000 bp bin size was sufficient to call both -me2 and -me3 states. Additionally, the -me3 state was effectively called using default threshold parameters, but a poisson p-value threshold of 0.1 was required to accurately call -me2 states. For the analysis in Figure 4, we further merged separate -me2 or -me3 states that were separated by ≤ 8100 bp.

Isolated PRE analysis

To assess activity of single PREs, we drew upon prior ChIP-seq measurements of the genome-wide distributions of E(z), H3K27me3, and H3K27me1 (from GEO: GSE299311).26 E(z) peaks were chosen for analysis if they occupy an H3K27me3 domain, if they are in the top 90% of E(z) peaks in terms of max signal, and if they are more than 27 kilobases (or 150 nucleosomes, at 180 bp/nucleosome) away from neighboring E(z) peaks. We selected the H3K27me3 and H3K27me1 profiles at NC14-early, NC14-mid, and NC14-late time points over 27-kilobase segments on either side of the chosen E(z) peaks. By selecting H3K27 methylation regions this way, we isolated the activities of single PREs on Polycomb dynamics; when E(z) peaks are spaced sufficiently far apart (by eye we approximate 27 kilobases apart), H3K27me3 builds up at approximately the same rate on either side of each peak.
