Pathologically Confirmed Subcutaneous Fat and Lymphovascular Invasion of a Hypopigmented Area in Extramammary Paget Disease
Authors: Gyoo Huh, Ga-Young Lee, Yumin Chung, Seung-Wan Chae, Young-Jun Choi
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Extramammary Paget disease (EMPD) is a rare adenocarcinoma that usually occurs in areas of the body that are rich in apocrine sweat glands. Great depth of tumor invasion is a well-known risk factor for worse prognosis. Paget cells usually are limited to the epidermis, whereas invasive EMPD, which infiltrates the dermis, is relatively rare. It is even rarer for the tumor to spread beyond the dermis. Only 3.1% of patients with EMPD of the penis and scrotum have exhibited infiltration of the subcutaneous fat layer. We report a case of a 62-year-old male with EMPD that invaded the subcutaneous fat layer. He presented with a several-year history of a slowly expanding erythematous plaque with the hypopigmented area on the left penoscrotum. One month before presentation, the patient had undergone punch biopsy at another hospital and diagnosed with EMPD. He had no personal history of urogenital cancers. The patient was treated with Mohs micrographic surgery, and negative margins were achieved after four stages. The histopathologic findings revealed Paget cells scattered throughout the epidermis. At the hypopigmented area, Paget cells extended to the subcutaneous fat layer with lymphovascular invasion. There was no evidence of recurrence at seven months postoperatively. Herein, we describe a case of hypopigmented EMPD that infiltrated the subcutaneous layer, which rarely has been reported in Korea.

INTRODUCTION

Extramammary Paget disease (EMPD) is a rare intraepithelial adenocarcinoma that usually occurs in areas of the body that are rich in apocrine sweat glands. The perianal region in males and the vulva in females are the most commonly affected sites1. EMPD can be sorted into primary EMPD, which arises in the absence of underlying malignancy, and secondary EMPD, which develops due to pagetoid spread from an adjacent adnexal carcinoma or internal malignancy. Primary EMPD is thought to begin as an intraepithelial carcinoma that can advance by invading the dermis and metastasizing via lymphatic spread2. Great depth of tumor invasion and lymphovascular invasion indicate poor prognosis3.

The incidence of invasive EMPD is relatively rare. Kang et al.4 reported that the ratio of in situ to invasive EMPD was 2.9:1. According to Yang et al.5, only 3.1% of patients with EMPD of the penis or scrotum have demonstrated infiltration of the subcutaneous layer beyond the dermis. Herein, we describe a rare case of hypopigmented EMPD that infiltrated the subcutaneous layer.

CASE REPORT

A 62-year-old Korean male presented with an erythematous plaque in his left penoscrotal area that had been slowly expanding for the past several years. One month before presentation, the patient underwent punch biopsy at another hospital and was diagnosed with EMPD. He reported pain and irritation but denied other systemic symptoms. He had no personal history of visceral cancers, including urogenital cancer. Physical examination revealed an ill-defined, 9.7×7.7-cm sized erythematous plaque with erosion and hypopigmentation in the left penoscrotal area (Fig. 1A). Multiple scouting biopsies were performed to determine the initial Mohs margin. A preoperative whole-body positron emission tomography-computed tomography scan revealed no regional lymph node (LN) metastasis.

The patient was treated with Mohs micrographic surgery (MMS) starting with 5-mm margins based on the results of preoperative multiple scouting biopsies, and negative margins were acquired after four stages. The post-Mohs defect was reconstructed using an abdominal rotation flap and a split-thickness skin graft. Histopathological findings revealed a proliferation of large cells with abundant, vacuolated cytoplasm scattered throughout the epidermis (Fig. 2A). At the some part of the hypopigmented area (Fig. 1A; arrow), Paget cells infiltrated the septa of the subcutaneous fat layer at 6-mm deep from the dermoepidermal junction (Fig. 2B). Lymphovascular invasion was also observed (Fig. 2C). Contrastingly, there was no evidence of the invasion of subcutaneous fat or lymphovascular tissue at the erythematous area (Fig. 3). The patient declined a sentinel LN biopsy, so we recommended a complete physical examination, age-appropriate cancer screening, and periodic computed tomographic imaging. There was no evidence of recurrence at seven months postoperatively, and he continues to undergo regular checkups (Fig. 1B).

DISCUSSION

EMPD typically appears as an intraepithelial carcinoma and has a favorable prognosis6. However, once it progresses to an invasive tumor, it metastasizes primarily through lymphatic channels and leads to poor prognosis7. Previous studies have reported varying incidence rates of invasive EMPD ranging from 14% to 33%8. In a Korean study of patients with penile or scrotal EMPD, there was a lower frequency (3.1%) of patients with subcutaneous tissue involvement compared to that of the dermis (21.9%)3.

Minimal invasion was defined as the appearance of Paget cells to a depth <1 mm below the basement membrane9. Lymphovascular invasion and LN metastasis, two of the most important poor prognostic factors, were significantly more frequent in patients with invasive EMPD, which invades to a depth >1 mm, compared to those with minimal invasion10. Reticular dermis or subcutaneous tissue invasion is an independent risk factor for sentinel LN positivity11. In addition, great depth of invasion is associated with shorter survival12.

Histological patterns can be classified as either a nodular/glandular pattern or a micronodular pattern10. The nodular/glandular pattern was described as proliferating Paget cells in large or medium-sized nests and/or glands, whereas tumor cells are distributed in small nests and/or as individual cells in a micronodular pattern. The nodular/glandular pattern and the presence of mitosis were significantly more common in cases with dermal invasion >1 mm in comparison to those with minimal invasion10. Paget cells might begin as small nests in early stromal invasion and then progress to large-sized nests as depth of invasion increases.

In our case, depth of invasion was 6 mm, and lymphovascular invasion was present with a predominantly nodular/glandular pattern. The patient underwent four Mohs stages, which was more than the average number (2.62) in Asian EMPD patients treated with MMS8. As more extensive procedures can be more technically difficult and disfiguring for patients, the lowest required stage is typically recommended. In our case, a higher MMS stage could be attributed to invasion of the subcutaneous tissue. Early detection of this type of invasion is critical for considering the difference in prognosis between in situ and invasive disease10.

Ohara et al.7 proposed an EMPD-specific TNM staging system in 2016. Patients with tumor thickness >4 mm demonstrated worse survival than did those with tumor thickness ≤4 mm and a primary tumor status of T1 or T2. Our case showed >4 mm thickness of the tumor with no sign of regional LN or distant organ metastasis and was categorized as Stage II. Patients in this stage show a 3.2% rate of tumor-related death7.

The incidence of hypopigmented areas in EMPD patients ranges from 10% to 30%. Hypopigmentation was observed at a higher rate than expected in a Korean EMPD population (67.7%)8. Histopathologically, Paget cells of hypopigmented areas mostly proliferate in the lower epidermis. A decreased number of basal melanocytes and reduced epidermal melanization were demonstrated in this type of lesion13. Hypopigmentation has a negative relationship with surgical outcome. A higher recurrence rate and an increase in number of MMS stages has been demonstrated for such hypopigmented lesions. This pigmentary change can be regarded as an alarming sign for subclinical extension8. However, such an area could be mistaken for acquired disorders that demonstrate hypopigmentation, including post-inflammatory hypopigmentation. Thus, pathological confirmation is recommended in hypopigmented lesions of EMPD13. In our case, infiltration of the subcutaneous fat layer and lymphovascular invasion were seen in a clinically hypopigmented lesion. As far as we know, no previous research has investigated the association between the hypopigmentation and the depth of invasion in EMPD. One of the possible hypothesis for the deeper invasion at the hypopigmented area relates to tumor immunology. The recruitment of CD8+ T cells was correlated with poor prognosis of EMPD in the previous study14. These cells had an exhausted phenotype which is correlated with incompetent cytotoxic effect to tumor cells. CD8+ T cells may also target adjacent melanocytes as well, similar to the pathophysiology of vitiligo8. It is possible that the poor cytotoxic effect of the exhausted CD8+ T cell could lead to more extensive infiltration of the tumor cells and at the same time CD8+ T cell could attack melanocytes to induce the hypopigmented lesion. Interestingly, during surgery, tumor cells in the excised hypopigmented area continued to be found infiltrated into the subcutis, so additional Mohs stages were performed until an acceptable margin was achieved.

MMS or wide local excision (WLE) is recommended in the anogenital region; MMS offers a more favorable survival outcome than does WLE15. However, all surgical procedures are complicated by high local recurrence rates that range from 30% to 60%3. In addition, in situ disease has a lower recurrence rate (35%) than invasive disease (67%)3. The difficulties in treatment are likely due to the multifocality, uneven margins, and clinically obscure extensions of the disease16. Given the high recurrence rates, regular postoperative surveillance for early detection is recommended.

To our knowledge, there is a paucity of reports on hypopigmented EMPD that has infiltrated the subcutaneous fat layer. Our case supports the negative impact of hypopigmentation in EMPD that has been seen in previous reports.
