Effects of exposure time and voxel size on measured alveolar bone thickness in cone-beam computed tomography: An ex vivo animal study using a pig model
Authors: Graham Garvey, Werner Harumiti Shintaku, Wanda I Claro, Anastasios Karydis, Chenhao Zhao, Ayman Al Dayeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study assessed the effects of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) voxel size and exposure settings on the accuracy of alveolar bone thickness (BT) measurements.Materials and MethodsSeven pig mandibles were scanned using 6 CBCT settings: 200, 400, and 600 µm voxels, each with an ultra-low dose (ULD) and a standard dose (SD) setting. BT was measured at 3 locations on 1 anterior and 1 posterior tooth. These teeth were then scanned using micro-computed tomography (micro-CT), and BT was measured at the same locations. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and paired t-test were used to evaluate agreement between CBCT and micro-CT measurements. The discrepancy between CBCT and micro-CT measurements was calculated, and repeated-measures analysis of variance (RMANOVA) was used to evaluate the effects of the 6 CBCT settings on BT measurement accuracy.ResultsThe lowest discrepancy between micro-CT and CBCT was observed with the 600 µm ULD (0.29±0.39 mm), followed by the 200 µm SD (0.41±0.72 mm). When BT was less than 2 mm, the lowest discrepancy occurred with the 200 µm SD (0.29±0.33 mm), followed by the 600 µm ULD (0.32±0.25 mm). The ICC between CBCT and micro-CT decreased as BT decreased. RMANOVA indicated no significant effect of CBCT settings on measurement accuracy.ConclusionBT measured with the 600 µm ULD was comparable to that obtained with the 200 µm SD. The discrepancy between CBCT and micro-CT was notable, especially when BT was less than 2 mm.Keywords: Cone-Beam Computed Tomography, Alveolar Process, X-Ray Microtomography, Observer Variation

Introduction

Since the introduction of cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), clinicians have sought to determine its optimal uses and limitations in dentistry. CBCT images comprise isotropic voxels that can be viewed equally in 3 orthogonal planes, allowing clinicians to observe and analyze structures from multiple perspectives. One emerging application of CBCT in orthodontics is in measuring the buccal and lingual thickness of the alveolar bone surrounding anterior teeth,1,2 as this dimension cannot be assessed using traditional 2-dimensional (2D) panoramic and cephalometric X-rays. Despite the increased use of CBCT in orthodontics, its accuracy in assessing bone thickness remains a topic of debate.3 While some authors have suggested that CBCT can provide an accurate estimate of alveolar bone thickness,1 others have reported significant variability and a tendency toward either underestimation4 or overestimation1 of alveolar bone thickness. This variability can be attributed to the limited spatial resolution when CBCT volumes are captured using low radiation doses. Spatial resolution is defined as the capacity of an imaging modality to distinguish small structures that lie close together.5

Measuring buccolingual alveolar bone thickness on CBCT is clinically relevant due to concerns about moving teeth outside their alveolar housing.6 Several parameters can affect the CBCT depiction of alveolar bone, including field of view (FOV), voxel size, artifacts, CBCT software, and scanner type. Understanding the effects of these parameters on the representation of alveolar bone can assist clinicians in determining the appropriate CBCT settings for various clinical scenarios.

Most orthodontic treatments involve growing patients. X-ray photons are known to disrupt rapidly dividing cells, which are particularly susceptible to damage and progression toward a diseased state.7 The “as low as reasonably achievable” (ALARA) principle emphasizes the importance of using the lowest diagnostic radiation dose feasible in radiographic examinations, including CBCT. Planmeca’s (Helsinki, Finland) ultra-low dose (ULD) protocol claims to reduce the radiation dose of a CBCT examination by lowering the milliamperage (mA) and exposure time while using algorithms to maintain image quality.8,9 Combining ULD with increased voxel dimensions can further reduce radiation dose. Few studies have investigated the accuracy of the ULD protocol in rendering alveolar bone dimensions. Additionally, limited research has assessed the impact of combining ULD with a larger voxel on the accurate depiction of alveolar bone dimensions.

Ludlow and Walker,10 using anthropomorphic child and adult phantoms, examined changes in radiation dosimetry across different protocols on the i-CAT Next Generation CBCT unit (Imaging Sciences International, Hatfield, PA, USA). Using a ULD approach, the i-CAT QuickScan+ protocol (16 cm×13 cm FOV, 180°, 160 frames, 90 kVp, 3 mA, 2 s) yielded a dose of 11–18 µSv, which was significantly lower than higher-dose protocols, including standard (16 cm×13 cm FOV, 360°, 300 frames, 120 kVp, 5 mA, 3.7 s) and QuickScan (16 cm×13 cm, 180°, 160 frames, 120 kVp, 5 mA, 2 s). They described the changes in image quality as modest and suggested selecting the CBCT protocol based on the diagnostic information required. ULD was recommended for reconstructed cephalometric and panoramic images.11

The Planmeca system offers a range of protocols and radiation doses (Table 1). For example, at a 600 µm voxel size, the system provides a standard low-dose setting (600 µm, 10 mA, 18 s) with a radiation exposure of 671 mGy/cm2, as well as a ULD setting (600 µm, 5 mA, 9 s) with an exposure of 168 mGy/cm2. To the authors’ knowledge, no studies have investigated the quality of bone rendering using the ULD setting.

Micro-computed tomography (micro-CT) is the gold standard for assessing 3-dimensional (3D) bone morphology and dimensions.12,13,14 Micro-CT provides near-anatomic measurements of alveolar bone thickness and was therefore used as the anatomic reference for the CBCT measurements in this research.

The purpose of this study was to assess the effects of CBCT voxel size and exposure time on the accuracy of buccal bone thickness measurements. The null hypothesis was that CBCT exposure time and voxel size do not affect the accuracy of the measured alveolar bone thickness.

Materials and Methods

Subjects

This study utilized 7 pig mandibles. The pig heads were sourced postmortem from a local abattoir. A priori sample size calculation (G*Power, University of Düsseldorf, Düsseldorf, Germany)15 indicated that a sample of 10 teeth would provide greater than 80% power (within-subjects analysis of variance [ANOVA], F-test) to detect differences between groups, assuming a large Cohen effect size of 0.4 and a correlation of 0.5 among repeated measures (different CBCT settings). A conservative non-sphericity correction of 0.75 was applied. To account for measurement error and overall variability, 14 teeth were included: 7 anterior and 7 posterior. Soft tissue was not removed. The mandibles were mounted on a customized holder that allowed each mandible to be repeatedly exposed. One anterior and one posterior tooth were selected in each mandible. Vertical and horizontal grooves intersecting to form a “crosshair” were drilled on the teeth using a 0.5 mm disc (Fig. 1) to provide a reference point for measurement and orientation.

Imaging acquisition and processing

The ProMax 3D Classic CT (Planmeca) was used to perform 6 CBCT scans on each mandible: 3 acquisitions with ULD and 3 with a standard dose (SD) protocol (Table 1). The FOV was set to 16 cm×10.2 cm for all scans.

Volumes were exported as Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine (DICOM) files and analyzed using Romexis (Planmeca) on a MacBook Pro (Apple, Cupertino, CA, USA) with a 13.3-inch display (2560×1600 pixels) in a room with subdued, dim ambient light. All images were given the same orientation in Romexis. Each cross-sectional image of the selected teeth was magnified to fill the screen before measurement, and no adjustments to image contrast or density were made. All values were measured in millimeters (mm). Bone thickness was measured at the same 3 points along the root of each selected tooth. Point 1 was identified as the first location where bone could be seen in the first CBCT acquisition analyzed. The distance from that point to the reference plane (passing through the crosshair) was measured and used for all subsequent CBCT acquisitions and micro-CT images for that tooth to ensure that bone thickness was measured at the same location. Points 2 and 3 were established by drawing perpendicular lines to the reference line at 2 and 4 mm apical to point 1, respectively (Fig. 1). A total of 252 points were measured, 42 for each of the 6 CBCT acquisition settings. The same examiner repeated these measurements on the CBCT images after a minimum 24-hour washout period. All measurements were performed in the same room under the same lighting conditions.

Following the CBCT scans, the teeth and associated bone were sectioned and processed for micro-CT scanning. Samples were placed in 10% neutral buffered formalin and then transferred to 70% ethanol 24 hours before micro-CT scanning. Micro-CT scans were performed using a 100HE cabinet micro-CT scanner (SCANCO Medical, Brüttisellen, Switzerland) with the following settings: a voxel size of 49 µm, 177 µA, and an FOV of 5.0×4.4 cm. This voxel size was selected to balance scanning/computing time and anatomic accuracy. Bouxsein et al.16 recommended a maximum ratio of 2 : 1 between bone thickness and acceptable voxel dimensions for micro-CT. As the alveolar bone thickness in this study was expected to be greater than 1 mm, a voxel dimension of 49 µm was chosen. The micro-CT scans were exported as DICOM files and imported into Romexis. Using the 3D rendering feature and the “crosshair” configuration of the drilled groove for orientation, bone thickness was measured at the same locations as the corresponding CBCT measurements. A total of 42 micro-CT measurements were made; in 12 of these sites, the bone thickness was ≤2 mm. The same examiner repeated the measurements on the micro-CT images after a minimum 24-hour washout period.

Statistical analysis

The intra-examiner error between the first and second measurements was assessed using the Dahlberg formula17 and the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

The following descriptive statistics were reported: the mean absolute difference between the CBCT and micro-CT measurements, the range (minimum-maximum), and the 95% confidence interval for the difference.

To test the null hypothesis and evaluate the effects of CBCT settings (combined voxel dimension and exposure time) on measurement error relative to micro-CT, the bone thickness measured on each CBCT image was subtracted from the corresponding micro-CT measurement. Because micro-CT provides a near-anatomic reference, this difference was considered the CBCT measurement error. As CBCT can underestimate or overestimate bone thickness, the absolute value of the difference was used for this analysis The effect of the 6 CBCT settings on measurement error was tested using repeated-measures within-factors ANOVA, followed by post hoc analysis.

A paired t-test was used to compare bone thickness among all pairs of modalities/settings (micro-CT and the 6 CBCT settings). Given the 21 pairwise comparisons, the significance threshold was adjusted using the Bonferroni correction to α=0.05/21=0.0024. Additionally, to determine the accuracy of each CBCT setting relative to micro-CT, ICCs were calculated using a 2-way mixed-effects model. Both single-measure and average-measure ICCs were reported, as single-measure ICCs evaluate accuracy on an individual point basis rather than the group average. The 95% confidence intervals for the ICCs were also reported.

Results

Good reliability was observed between the initial and repeated measurements. Dahlberg error values (Table 2) ranged from 0.21 to 0.50 mm. The lowest error occurred with micro-CT measurements, whereas the highest was seen with the 600 µm SD CBCT images, a pattern reflected in the reliability analysis. The absolute ICC was highest for micro-CT and lowest for the 600 µm SD CBCT (Table 2). Due to the high agreement between the initial and repeated measurements, values were averaged.

At the most occlusal point (point 1), the lowest discrepancy between micro-CT and CBCT was found with the 600 µm ULD setting, followed by 200 µm SD, 400 µm SD, 200 µm ULD, 400 µm ULD, and 600 µm SD. A similar pattern—the lowest discrepancy with 600 µm ULD—was observed at point 2 (mid-root). At the most apical point (point 3), comparably minimal discrepancies were observed with 200 µm ULD, 600 µm ULD, and 200 µm SD.

The ICC agreement between CBCT and micro-CT measurements is shown in Table 3 and followed a trend similar to the absolute differences. Across the 3 points, higher ICCs were generally noted for 600 µm ULD, followed by 200 µm SD and 400 µm SD. No significant differences were detected between CBCT and micro-CT measurements at any point (P>0.05, paired t-test). Although measurements obtained with different CBCT settings varied relative to micro-CT values, they displayed no consistent tendency toward overestimation or underestimation, indicating that discrepancies were about equally likely in either direction.

To test the effects of CBCT settings (voxel size and exposure time) on the accuracy of measured bone thickness relative to micro-CT, the measurement from each CBCT setting was subtracted from the corresponding micro-CT measurement to derive the CBCT measurement error. Because CBCT can overestimate or underestimate micro-CT values, the absolute difference was used for analysis. Absolute differences were compared using 1-way repeated-measures ANOVA (within-factors; 6 levels). The Mauchly test indicated a violation of sphericity, so Greenhouse-Geisser (GG) and Huynh-Feldt (HF) corrections were applied. ANOVA revealed a significant effect of CBCT setting on measurement error (P<0.05 for both GG and HF corrections). Post hoc analysis demonstrated a significantly higher error for the 600 µm SD combination compared with 600 µm ULD (P<0.05).

Because the alveolar bone surrounding human anterior teeth is usually thin, sites with bone thickness less than 2 mm (n=12, as measured by micro-CT) were further analyzed (Table 4). In this subset, the lowest discrepancy between CBCT and micro-CT occurred with 200 µm SD, followed by 600 µm ULD, 400 µm SD, 200 µm ULD, 600 µm SD, and 400 µm ULD. Notably, at bone thickness less than 2 mm, an overall tendency to overestimate bone thickness was observed across the 6 CBCT settings. Although the errors were numerically small, on average they differed by approximately 25% from the micro-CT values.

Discussion

Moving teeth outside their bony housing can cause adverse effects such as periodontal damage, gingival recession, fenestrations, or root resorption.6,18 Consequently, many clinicians use CBCT to evaluate alveolar bone thickness and to measure changes during orthodontic treatment.19,20,21 Several studies have employed CBCT to measure changes in alveolar bone during treatments that may move teeth unfavorably relative to their supporting bone, including maxillary expansion6,22 and lower incisor proclination.23,24 However, the reliability of CBCT for assessing alveolar bone thickness remains a matter of debate.1,2,3,13

No clear guidelines exist regarding specific CBCT exposure parameters to produce diagnostic images of buccal bone thickness. Many studies have compared voxel sizes and exposure times to assess their impact on image resolution. In this study, the reliability of 6 CBCT settings for measuring buccal alveolar bone was evaluated in a pig model. The gold standard for anatomic thickness was the bone thickness measured by 49 µm micro-CT. The null hypothesis—that CBCT settings do not affect measurement error relative to micro-CT—was rejected. Post hoc analysis indicated that this difference was driven by the lower accuracy of the 600 µm, 18-second exposure compared with the 600 µm ULD. In several respects, the descriptive statistics suggested that the combination of the largest voxel size and the shortest exposure time (600 µm ULD) produced accuracy comparable to or greater than that of higher-exposure, smaller-voxel settings. The present study is not alone in affirming the reliability of lower-dose protocols. Ruetters et al.25 used pig mandibles without soft tissue that were imaged at high, standard, and low doses. They concluded that lower-dose CBCT can be used to accurately detect bone thickness. Cook et al.26 used an i-CAT scanner to image cadaver heads with 2 combinations of exposure time and voxel size: long scan (26.9 s, 200 µm voxel size) and short scan (4.8 s, 300 µm voxel size). Based on these scans, they measured bone height and thickness, comparing these measurements with those taken with a digital caliper. The researchers found sufficient similarity between the long and short scans to recommend the larger voxel and shorter exposure. Similar findings were reported2 using 4 cadaver heads scanned with low- and high-dose CBCT to measure buccal bone thickness in the mandibular anterior region. While the present study supports the accuracy of the ULD protocol, this was true only for the 600 µm voxel. Measurements from the 200 µm ULD and 400 µm ULD settings showed lower accuracy than their SD counterparts and the 600 µm ULD, as evidenced by greater variability, lower ICCs, and a wider overall error range.

Although the present results did not reveal a statistically significant difference between bone thickness measured on CBCT and micro-CT, these findings require careful interpretation. The apparent lack of difference can be attributed to the possibility that CBCT can either overestimate or underestimate measurements, rendering aggregate statistical comparisons potentially misleading. For example, at 600 µm ULD, the CBCT measurements at the most occlusal point overestimated micro-CT in 7 teeth and underestimated it in 7, with a range from -0.82 to +0.63 mm. Another example involved areas with bone thickness of less than 2 mm: approximately 50% of the CBCT measurements showed about 25% discrepancy from micro-CT, and ~16% overestimated bone thickness by >40%. Thus, although statistically nonsignificant, these errors may be clinically important and should be considered when using CBCT to assess alveolar bone thickness. The present findings are broadly comparable to those reported by Wood et al.1 (2013), who measured buccal bone thickness in pig heads using an i-CAT CBCT scanner (Imaging Sciences International) with 2 exposure/voxel combinations (200 µm, 8.9 s; 400 µm, 26.9 s). They found that measurements from the larger voxel size (400 µm) generally fell within 1 mm of physical measurements (digital caliper) and exceeded 1 mm only 10% of the time. In the present study, at 400 µm, the error exceeded 1 mm in 4 of 42 measurements.

Ballrick et al.27 reported that in phantom scans with voxel sizes of 300 µm and 400 µm, the spatial resolution averaged 0.7 mm, indicating that smaller distances may be poorly distinguished. They suggested that larger voxels and shorter exposure times increase partial volume averaging (PVA), leading to CBCT measurement errors that can affect the display of alveolar bone. PVA occurs when a voxel lies at the interface of 2 materials with different radiodensities. This causes the voxel to display the average of the 2 densities, introducing an error in volume construction3 and thus not representing the true tissue boundary.28 For example, because structures such as cementum can blend with the cortical plate, PVA may make bone appear thicker.1 Theoretically, larger voxel sizes increase the likelihood of PVA. This was also reflected in the present results for the standard exposure mode, where error was lowest at 200 µm, followed by 400 µm and 600 µm. Interestingly, under the ULD mode, error was lowest with 600 µm ULD compared with 400 µm ULD and 200 µm ULD. In fact, across the 3 measured points, 600 µm ULD performed comparably to 200 µm SD.

A previous study found that when bone thickness exceeds the voxel size, CBCT tends to overestimate thickness, whereas when bone is thinner than or close to the voxel size, it tends to be underestimated.29 This observation was supported by Wood et al.,1 who suggested that thin bone is often indistinguishable from cementum and is therefore less visible and difficult to measure. Although not statistically significant, the present results do not align with this pattern. In the current study, when bone thickness was less than 2 mm, CBCT exhibited a clear tendency to overestimate bone thickness (Table 4).

The present findings suggest that the 600 µm ULD protocol may depict alveolar bone thickness with accuracy comparable to 200 µm SD. However, these results should be interpreted cautiously due to several limitations. Post hoc power analysis indicated an achieved power of ~68–75%, suggesting the study may have been underpowered to detect smaller yet clinically meaningful between-group differences; larger samples may clarify such effects. The repeatability of image orientation and measurement across CBCT settings and micro-CT could also have introduced error. Although the vertical and horizontal grooves aided orientation and measurement, their relatively large and non-standardized dimensions may have led to measurement error. The high correlation between first and second measurements suggests this error was minimal, but future work should employ more reproducible reference planes to reduce variability. Another limitation is that reliability (in the form of ICC) was assessed using a single examiner with only 2 intra-examiner repetitions per site; additional repetitions would likely yield more precise reliability estimates for the comparisons of CBCT to micro-CT. Additionally, the inclusion of a second examiner could mitigate potential observer bias and strengthen the reliability assessments. Nonetheless, the 95% confidence interval for the intra-examiner ICC was narrow, indicating minimal discrepancy between the first and second measurements. Future studies should incorporate more intra- and interexaminer repetitions to further improve reliability within CBCT and relative to micro-CT. The generalizability of this study is also limited: given substantial variation among CBCT machines, software, and reconstruction algorithms, these results primarily apply to the system used here. Investigations using different systems and settings are needed to broaden generalizability. Lastly, this study employed an animal model. Although porcine teeth are commonly used in CBCT research, differences in bone density and thickness from human tissues may influence the results.

In conclusion, within the limitations of this study, a statistically significant difference in bone measurements was observed among the 6 CBCT exposure settings. CBCT acquired at 600 µm with an 18-second exposure displayed lower accuracy than the other CBCT settings. The 600 µm ULD setting was comparable to the 200 µm SD when compared with micro-CT measurements. When alveolar bone thickness was less than 2 mm, approximately 16% of CBCT measurements overestimated thickness by more than 40%. Such discrepancies should be considered when CBCT is used to assess alveolar bone thickness. Future studies with larger sample sizes, more repeated measurements, and more standardized protocols are needed to better characterize the CBCT depiction of alveolar bone.
