Spontaneous perineural hematoma of a lumbar nerve root presenting as radiculopathy successfully treated with needle aspiration: illustrative case

Spinal hemorrhagic disorders are relatively uncommon; however, conditions such as ligamentum flavum hematoma, spinal epidural hematoma, subdural hematoma, and subarachnoid hemorrhage are occasionally encountered in clinical practice. These lesions arise within the spinal canal and may result from idiopathic, vascular, or iatrogenic causes. In contrast, hematomas occurring outside the spinal canal, particularly around the spinal nerve root, are exceedingly rare. To date, reported cases have been limited to a single case associated with a nerve root block procedure1 and 9 cases of Tarlov cyst hemorrhage.2–10

In this report, we describe a case of a spontaneous hematoma adjacent to the L5 nerve root in the extraforaminal region, resulting in acute L5 radiculopathy. The lesion was successfully treated with fluoroscopy-guided needle aspiration.

Illustrative Case

A 35-year-old female fitness instructor with no significant past medical history, including no history of hematological disorders or anticoagulant use, presented with right buttock pain. She had experienced mild symptoms 3 months prior, which resolved spontaneously within 1 month. However, the pain recurred several days prior and progressively worsened, with right lower limb pain and numbness developing the night before presentation.

Neurological examination revealed weakness of the right extensor hallucis longus (EHL) muscle with a manual muscle testing (MMT) grade of 3/5. The straight leg raise test was negative. Lumbar radiographic imaging and MRI demonstrated Meyerding grade 1 spondylolisthesis at L4–5 with degenerative changes of the right facet joint (Fig. 1). MRI also revealed a septated cystic lesion in the right L5–S1 extraforaminal region, posterior to the L5 nerve root. The lesion was hypointense on T1-weighted imaging and hyperintense on T2-weighted imaging (Fig. 2A and B). Three-dimensional MR myelography using a fast imaging employing steady-state acquisition (FIESTA) sequence further demonstrated no communication between the lesion and the intrathecal space (Fig. 3).

Given the presence of motor weakness and the cystic appearance of the lesion, fluoroscopy-guided needle aspiration was performed. Approximately 0.5 mL of dark red viscous fluid resembling a liquefied hematoma was aspirated (Fig. 4 left). The patient’s pain resolved immediately following the procedure, and follow-up MRI obtained 6 days later demonstrated a reduction in lesion size (Fig. 2C and D). Motor function of the right EHL muscle recovered completely.

Aspirated fluid obtained during fluoroscopy-guided needle aspiration. Left: Dark red viscous fluid obtained during the first procedure. Right:Xanthochromic fluid obtained during the second procedure at recurrence.

However, the patient experienced recurrence of right buttock and lower limb pain 10 days after the procedure and returned 17 days after the initial aspiration. Neurological examination revealed worsening weakness of the right EHL muscle (MMT grade 2/5), and MRI demonstrated a recurrent lesion (Fig. 2E and F). A second fluoroscopy-guided aspiration was performed, yielding 0.5 mL of xanthochromic fluid (Fig. 4 right). Symptoms again resolved immediately, and motor function fully recovered within several days. No recurrence was observed during a 3-year follow-up period. Serial MRI at 15 days, 1 year, and 3 years after the second procedure confirmed complete resolution without recurrence (Fig. 2G–J).

Discussion

Observations

Perineural hematoma adjacent to a lumbosacral nerve root has been reported in noniatrogenic settings only in the context of Tarlov cyst hemorrhage. Reported mechanisms include subarachnoid hemorrhage,2,5,6 abnormal vessels,8 and idiopathic cases.3,4,7,9,10 Tarlov cysts are characterized by communication with the intrathecal space,11 and in hemorrhagic cases, their contents have been described as bloody CSF.10 In contrast, the lesion in the present case demonstrated no communication with the intrathecal space and contained dark red liquefied hematoma material. Furthermore, Tarlov cysts typically contain nerve fibers within the cyst cavity, whereas the lesion in this case was located external to the nerve root. These findings suggest that the present case represents a distinct pathological entity rather than a variant of Tarlov cyst hemorrhage.

Each nerve root of the cauda equina arises from the dural sac and is enveloped by a nerve root cuff, which represents a dural extension surrounding the proximal nerve root.12 The nerve root cuff consists of an inner dural layer and an outer adipose layer.13 In the present case, imaging findings suggested that the hematoma was located external to the dural layer, most likely within the adipose tissue of the nerve root cuff. Intraneural hematomas have been reported in peripheral nerves, including the median, ulnar, sciatic, femoral, peroneal, and sural nerves.14 These hematomas occur within the nerve sheath. The sheath is composed of the paraneurium, epineurium, perineurium, and endoneurium.12 The epineurium is continuous with the dura mater, and the present lesion may be analogous to a subparaneurial hematoma, located external to the epineurium. Because only needle aspiration was performed and no resection or histopathological examination was available, the exact origin and pathological nature of the hematoma could not be definitively confirmed. In addition, vascular imaging studies such as CTA or MRA were not performed, and therefore an underlying vascular lesion such as an extraforaminal arteriovenous fistula could not be completely excluded.

The patient had experienced mild symptoms 3 months prior to presentation, and the aspirated material was a dark red liquefied hematoma. MRI findings demonstrated T1 hypointensity and T2 hyperintensity, consistent with a chronic-stage hematoma. Although no definite history of trauma was identified, the patient was a fitness instructor with frequent physical activity and had underlying L4–5 spondylolisthesis. These factors may have subjected the nerve root to repetitive mechanical stress, resulting in a minor initial hemorrhage. Similar to intracranial chronic subdural hematoma, minor or even unrecognized trauma may trigger a chronic inflammatory process that promotes progressive enlargement of the lesion rather than simple persistence of the original bleed. In chronic subdural hematoma, ongoing expansion is thought to be driven by membrane formation, fragile neovascularization, repeated microhemorrhage, and fluid exudation from permeable capillaries.15 At recurrence, the aspirated fluid was xanthochromic, indicating hemoglobin degradation and further supporting the chronic nature of the lesion, a feature also commonly observed in intracranial chronic subdural hematoma.

Because motor weakness was present, early intervention was considered necessary to prevent irreversible neurological deficits. Compared with surgical decompression, needle aspiration is less invasive and can be performed rapidly under local anesthesia. In this case, the cystic appearance of the lesion on MRI suggested a liquefied component, making it amenable to aspiration. Previous reports have demonstrated successful treatment of an intraneural hematoma of the median nerve with ultrasound-guided aspiration16 and of Tarlov cyst hemorrhage with CT-guided aspiration.2,10 Although the initial aspiration resulted in immediate symptom relief, the patient experienced recurrence within 10 days. This may have reflected persistent activity within the hematoma cavity, analogous to the mechanism proposed for chronic subdural hematoma, rather than simple incomplete evacuation. Following the second aspiration, no recurrence was observed during long-term follow-up, suggesting that repeat aspiration may be an effective strategy in selected cases.

Lessons

This case presented with acute unilateral lower limb radicular pain, initially suggestive of lumbar disc herniation. However, MRI revealed an atypical cystic lesion adjacent to the nerve root. Perineural hematoma should be considered in the differential diagnosis of acute radiculopathy. Fluoroscopy-guided needle aspiration may offer a minimally invasive and effective treatment option, particularly in the presence of motor weakness. Early MRI evaluation is important for accurate diagnosis and timely management.
