Background

Pericardial window creation is a commonly performed procedure for the management of recurrent pericardial effusion and cardiac tamponade. Although generally considered safe, complications may occur, including rare instances of diaphragmatic hernia [1]. This complication is thought to arise from disruption or weakening of the diaphragm, particularly with the subxiphoid approach, creating a communication between the peritoneal and thoracic cavities [2, 5].

Most reported cases present in the early postoperative period with nonspecific thoracoabdominal symptoms. Delayed presentations are rare, and cases complicated by intrathoracic closed-loop obstruction are exceptionally uncommon [3]. Given the risk of strangulation and bowel ischemia, prompt recognition is critical. We report a case of delayed intrathoracic closed-loop small bowel obstruction occurring two years after a subxiphoid pericardial window.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old man with a history of recurrent pericardial effusion secondary to traumatic injury sustained in a motor vehicle collision four years earlier, treated with a subxiphoid pericardial window two years prior, presented to the emergency department, with a 2-day history of anorexia, nausea, vomiting, abdominal distension, and severe epigastric pain. On examination, the patient was hemodynamically stable. Abdominal examination revealed distension, tympany, and tenderness in the epigastrium without signs of peritonitis.

Computed tomography of the abdomen and pelvis demonstrated herniation of small bowel loops into the thoracic cavity through an anterior diaphragmatic defect, initially described radiologically as a Morgagni hernia, with findings consistent with a closed-loop obstruction (Figs. 1, 2 and 3).

The patient was taken urgently to the operating room for exploratory laparotomy where we encountered the previous subxiphoid surgical scar. Intraoperatively, small bowel loops were identified herniating through an anterior diaphragmatic defect into the thoracic cavity. The bowel was viable, with no evidence of ischemia or necrosis. The herniated bowel was reduced into the abdominal cavity and an anterior iatrogenic diaphragmatic defect measuring 4 × 5 cm was identified. After complete dissection of the hernia sac, a 7.5 × 7.5 cm Gore-Tex mesh was placed for reinforcement and secured with interrupted 2 − 0 Prolene sutures (Fig. 4). The repair was tension-free with adequate hemostasis. No drain was placed given the clean operative field and satisfactory hemostasis. The fascia was closed with 2 − 0 PDS, and the skin was closed with staples.

The patient had an initially uneventful postoperative recovery, with resumption of diet on postoperative day (POD) 3, and was discharged home on POD 4. He was subsequently readmitted on POD 8 with acute kidney injury secondary to dehydration, which resolved with intravenous fluid resuscitation; he remained hospitalized for one day and tolerated his diet well. He was later readmitted on POD 12 with epigastric pain, anorexia, and early satiety. Computed tomography demonstrated gastric wall thickening, and upper endoscopy revealed gastric, duodenal, and esophageal ulcers, which were managed medically with proton pump inhibitor therapy. Follow-up computed tomography performed one week after repair demonstrated an intact diaphragmatic repair with no evidence of recurrent herniation (Figs. 5 and 6). At one-year clinical follow-up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no evidence of hernia recurrence.

Discussion

Diaphragmatic hernia following pericardial window is a rare iatrogenic complication, with only isolated case reports and small case series available in the literature [1, 3]. Most reported cases present within the early postoperative period or within several months of surgery, whereas delayed presentations beyond one year are particularly uncommon. Compared with previously reported cases, our patient developed an intrathoracic closed-loop small bowel obstruction two years after the index operation performed for recurrent pericardial effusion following a traumatic event, making this presentation exceptionally rare [4]. The underlying mechanism is likely multifactorial, involving iatrogenic weakening or unrecognized injury to the diaphragm during the subxiphoid approach, combined with chronically increased intra-abdominal pressure leading to progressive enlargement of the defect over time [2, 5]. Eventually, this may allow gradual herniation of abdominal viscera into the thoracic cavity, with potential for acute obstructive complications once incarceration occurs.

A focused literature review was performed using PubMed, MEDLINE, and Google Scholar with the search terms “pericardial window,” “pericardioperitoneal window,” “diaphragmatic hernia,” “intrapericardial diaphragmatic hernia,” and “pericardial hernia.” This identified a limited number of published cases (approximately seven reported cases, depending on inclusion criteria and terminology). Overall, the literature consists primarily of isolated case reports, underscoring the rarity of this complication. Most cases present within weeks to months after the index procedure, likely due to early herniation through an incompletely sealed or enlarged defect, whereas delayed presentations beyond one year are uncommon and sparsely described. The present case is therefore notable for its unusually late presentation at two years postoperatively and for presenting with intrathoracic closed-loop small bowel obstruction, highlighting the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion even in delayed postoperative presentations [1–7].

Intrathoracic closed-loop obstruction represents a particularly severe manifestation, as it carries a high risk of strangulation, ischemia, and perforation if not promptly recognized [4]. Clinical presentation may be nonspecific, often mimicking typical small bowel obstruction, which can delay diagnosis. Computed tomography remains the imaging modality of choice, allowing accurate identification of the diaphragmatic defect, herniated contents, and signs of obstruction or ischemia [6].

Management is surgical and should not be delayed. Operative principles are consistent with established management of diaphragmatic hernias and include reduction of herniated contents, assessment of bowel viability, and definitive closure of the diaphragmatic defect [1, 3, 8]. Repair techniques include primary suture closure, mesh reinforcement, or a combination of both. Primary repair may be appropriate for small, acute defects with minimal tension; however, it may be associated with higher recurrence risk in chronic defects due to tissue attenuation and tension at the repair site. In contrast, prosthetic mesh reinforcement provides a tension-free repair and is generally preferred in larger or chronic defects. Reported cases of intrapericardial and diaphragmatic hernias after pericardial window have successfully utilized both synthetic and composite mesh reinforcement with good short-term outcomes and no early recurrence [2, 8]. Expanded polytetrafluoroethylene (ePTFE, Gore-Tex) mesh offers durable reinforcement with low recurrence rates and is particularly useful in contaminated or complex surgical fields, although potential risks include adhesion formation and infection. Biologic meshes may reduce infection risk in contaminated cases but are associated with higher long-term recurrence due to gradual resorption and reduced tensile strength over time [4, 7].

In the present case, mesh reinforcement with Gore-Tex was selected due to the chronic nature of the defect and the need for a durable, tension-free repair. Although long-term outcome data for this specific complication are limited due to its rarity, available reports suggest favorable outcomes when early diagnosis and definitive surgical repair are achieved [8, 9]. Compared with previously reported cases, our case is notable for both the significantly delayed presentation and the presence of closed-loop obstruction without bowel compromise, likely reflecting timely diagnosis and urgent operative intervention.

Conclusion

Delayed diaphragmatic hernia following subxiphoid pericardial window is a rare but serious complication. Intrathoracic closed-loop obstruction should be considered in patients presenting with obstructive symptoms even years after the initial procedure. Early recognition and prompt surgical management are essential to prevent life-threatening complications.
