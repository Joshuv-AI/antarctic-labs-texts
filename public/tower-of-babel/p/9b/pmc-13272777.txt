Optimizing the heating performance of Trombe walls with ribbed surfaces using three-dimensional CFD analysis
Authors: Jamal-Eddine Salhi, Tarik Zarrouk, Supriya Jain, Mohamed Barboucha, Md Irfanul Haque Siddiqui, Shahid Parvez, Geleta Afessa Moreda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImproving the thermal efficiency of Trombe walls is crucial for optimizing passive solar heating systems. Despite extensive research, the effect of ribbed surfaces on the thermo-hydrodynamic behavior has not been fully explored. This study aims to address this gap by performing a three-dimensional CFD analysis to investigate the combined effects of rib density (Nr = 3, 5, 7, 9) and rib geometry (rectangular, pentagonal, hexagonal, octagonal, triangular, and semi-circular) on heat transfer and fluid dynamics within Trombe walls. The simulations were conducted using ANSYS Fluent (2020 R1) under laminar flow conditions (Re = 600–1600), with boundary conditions including a constant solar flux of 748 W/m2, an ambient temperature of 293.75 K, and an external wind speed of 1 m/s. The model was validated against experimental data correlations for the Nusselt number (Nu) and friction factor (f), with deviations of less than 6%. The results show that ribbing significantly enhances heat transfer by disturbing the boundary layer and promoting vortex formation. The optimal configuration (Nr = 5) increases the Nusselt number by 68.3% at Re = 1600 (from 12.41 to 20.88). Triangular ribs provide the highest thermal enhancement, nearly doubling Nu (a 99.4% increase, from 12.41 to 24.75), but incur significant friction penalties, with f rising by 169.4% (from 0.0489 to 0.1317). In contrast, semi-circular ribs strike a balance, yielding a thermal enhancement factor (TEF) of 1.51 (+ 50.6%) and increasing heat flux by 12.7%. These findings demonstrate that Nr = 5 with the appropriate rib geometry can optimize Trombe wall efficiency, making it suitable for nearly zero-energy buildings (nZEBs).Keywords: Trombe wall, Passive solar heating, Heat transfer enhancement, Rib geometry, Thermal performance, Friction factor, Nusselt number, Poiseuille numberSubject terms: Energy science and technology, Engineering, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Trombe walls, passive solar heating devices, harness solar heat by storing it in thermal mass, which is then released into the building. Although widely used in the design of energy-efficient buildings, their performance depends on several factors, including the wall’s geometric configuration, the material used, and the management of the air circulating within the channels. In recent years, research has shown that optimizing these parameters, particularly through CFD simulations and the integration of innovative materials such as thermal fins, ribbed surfaces, and phase change materials (PCMs), can significantly improve the thermal efficiency of Trombe walls.

As shown by, Ghriss et al.1 demonstrated that integrating V-shaped fins increased thermal efficiency by about 45% by improving natural convection and air circulation within the system. Thermal performance of Trombe wall with fins2 quantified the impact of integrating metallic fins into the air cavity, showing an improvement in convective heat transfer. Fidaros3 used CFD simulations to examine different geometric configurations of Trombe walls, revealing that the width of the air channels and the thickness of the walls directly affect the system’s thermal performance. El Zein4 explored the effect of humidity coupled with temperature in a transient CFD model, highlighting the importance of these parameters for long-term thermal regulation in buildings. Charqui et al.5 showed that optimization under transient conditions increased thermal efficiency by 30% compared to conventional designs.

In addition to geometric configurations, the integration of ribbed surfaces in the air space of Trombe walls has shown significant improvements. Bezir et al.6 conducted a CFD analysis on Trombe walls, introducing artificial roughness to enhance convective heat transfer. Their results showed a significant improvement in heat absorption, demonstrating that surface modifications can optimize the natural convection process, thus enhancing the thermal performance of Trombe walls. This approach offers a promising method for increasing energy efficiency in passive solar systems. Qin et al.7 conducted experimental and numerical investigations on the use of double-layer phase change walls (PCWs) for enhanced thermal regulation in summer climates. Their study demonstrated that integrating PCMs in Trombe walls significantly reduced internal temperature fluctuations by improving heat storage and release. The findings highlight the effectiveness of PCWs in passive cooling, offering a sustainable and energy-efficient solution for buildings in hot climates by reducing the reliance on mechanical cooling systems. Bevilacqua et al.8 developed a multi-physics model for optimizing the Trombe wall system for winter use. Their study showed that by carefully adjusting geometric and physical parameters, the optimized Trombe wall significantly improved solar heat absorption and energy storage, leading to reduced heating demands in cold climates. The results underscore the Trombe wall’s potential as a reliable and energy-efficient solution for sustainable building design.

Guan et al.9 investigated an active-passive heat storage wall system incorporating PCMs in a Chinese solar greenhouse. Their study demonstrated that PCMs effectively stored solar energy during the day and released it at night, stabilizing the indoor temperature and reducing the need for external heating. The research confirms the system’s potential for enhancing thermal regulation and energy efficiency, making it an ideal solution for sustainable greenhouse design and passive solar buildings. Madsen et al.10 found that slightly rough surfaces increased heat transfer, but excessive roughness generated turbulence, thus reducing overall efficiency. Bulmez et al.11 proposed a new Trombe wall concept, optimized using CFD simulations to enhance thermal efficiency and solar energy absorption. Their study demonstrated that adjusting the channel configuration and material properties improved heat transfer and solar energy capture, making the new design an effective solution for energy-efficient building systems. Zamora12 reviewed the fundamentals of natural convection in solar chimneys and proposed thermohydraulic optimization strategies to enhance their performance. The study emphasized the importance of factors such as chimney height and air gap size in improving the efficiency of solar chimneys for natural ventilation and energy savings. The research also highlighted the potential of combining solar chimneys with other systems for better thermal regulation in sustainable buildings.

The optimization of thermal fins has also been a key strategy for improving natural convection. Qi, Wang, and Wang13 studied the effect of a finned Trombe wall on the indoor thermal environment in cold regions. Their research demonstrated that integrating fins into the Trombe wall improved heat transfer and solar energy storage, resulting in better thermal comfort and reduced reliance on active heating systems. This solution is particularly effective in cold climates, offering a sustainable approach to energy-efficient building design. Briga-Sá et al.14 explored the influence of air vent management on Trombe wall temperature fluctuations through experimental analysis under real climate conditions. Their study found that optimizing air vent placement and ventilation control could significantly enhance the thermal performance of Trombe walls, reducing temperature fluctuations and improving thermal comfort. This research highlights the importance of air vent management for maximizing energy efficiency in passive solar systems. Xu et al.15 assessed the performance of a novel PCM-equipped Trombe wall design for improving natural ventilation and thermal regulation in buildings. Their study found that integrating PCMs in the Trombe wall not only enhanced thermal comfort but also reduced the need for active HVAC systems, offering a sustainable solution for energy-efficient buildings. The results underscore the importance of PCMs in energy-saving applications and natural ventilation strategies.

The Optimization of geometric parameters, introduction of new materials such as PCMs, and CFD simulations enable the creation of more efficient Trombe walls to be used in sustainable buildings. Guan et al.16 used the multi-objective optimization of the geometrical layout of atriums in almost zero-energy buildings (nZEBs) in terms of energy consumption, carbon emissions, and cost reduction. They found that, optimal atrium geometry can maximize energy efficiency, increase natural lighting, ventilation, and also be cost-effective and hence this contributes to success in designing a sustainable building. The study of Żelazna et al.17 put the PCMs incorporated in Trombe walls under the prism of the life cycle. In their work, they proved that PCMs can enhance the thermal storage and temperature regulation which results in substantial energy savings and carbon footprint decreases throughout the life of the building. The integration is a cost effective and sustainable integration towards decreasing heating and cooling requirements in energy efficient buildings. Ibrahim, Algburi and Ahmed18 have conducted a literature review on the performance improvement of PV Trombe walls, a hybrid construction that combines photovoltaic panel and Trombe walls. It was noted in the study that such integration has enhanced thermal as well as electricity production and hence it is an effective solution in the production of energy-efficient buildings. The reduce heating requirements of the structures by maximizing the design and operational schemes through panel location and cooling system can translate to substantial saving of heating needs by PV Trombe walls, which also produces solar power.

Recent publications present the picture that new materials, advanced simulation tools and optimization of geometries leading to the optimization of thermal performance as well as the energy footprint of buildings are the reasons why the Trombe wall systems are progressive upgraded. One such interesting analysis was provided by Bilgen and Jeldres19 who studied the optimization of the Trombe solar collector and offered some interesting solutions of incorporating it into the low-energy building strategies. Imessad and Belhamel20 evaluated the performance of the different Trombe wall systems in relation to their thermal performance and determined that there were geometric designs that could save on energy in a significant way.

A number of studies21, 22 had raised the significance of the choice of wall material in Trombe wall functioning. In a study done in Turkey23, Trombe walls constructed using concrete, brick and autoclaved aerated concrete (AAC) were studied on solar heat gains as a result of various surface finishes. The findings revealed that concrete, brick and AAC utilized annual solar a rate of 26.9 to 9.7, 20.5 to 7.1, and 13.0 to 4.3 respectively, based on the finish. Concrete configurations were always the most efficient in terms of solar captivity with brick coming in the second position and AAC last because of its lower volumetric heat retention even though it contained high insulation qualities.

Other factors that influence the performance of Trombe walls include the nature of glazing. Glazing also regulates the solar heat gain by allowing the shortwave solar radiation to enter the glazing and limiting the longwave thermal radiation. It has two critical parameters to its performance, which are Solar Heat Gain Coefficient (SHGC) and U-value. The literature indicated that the use of Low-E double glazing instead of single glazing can save 41.33% of the solar wall energy in winter24–28.

New developments are also intended to enhance the performance of Trombe wall using creative solutions. The incorporation of photovoltaic (PV) modules on the wall surface is one of the promising methods29–32. This allows electricity production and thermal optimization of the PV panels behind the panels to improve system performance during winter and prevent over- heating during summer. Also, optimization of ventilators is a major component of Trombe wall. According to the recent studies, the velocity of airflow is highly dependent on wall height and thermal gradients33. An optimal aspect ratio of air gap has been noted by other researchers to be 0.0534, and Kaya et al.35 suggest that the opening to wall surface ratio should be 8% in order to achieve the highest amount of energy efficiency.

Despite significant advancements in Trombe wall systems, limited research has explored the influence of ribbed surfaces on their thermo-hydrodynamic behavior. Previous studies have mainly focused on general heat transfer improvements, but the specific effects of varying rib density and rib geometry on thermal behavior and fluid flow have not been thoroughly investigated. While some studies have explored the use of ribs for heat transfer enhancement, the relationship between rib geometry and hydraulic resistance remains inadequately addressed.

This study aims to fill this gap by investigating the combined effects of rib density and rib geometry on the thermo-hydrodynamic performance of Trombe wall systems. The novelty of this work lies in its detailed evaluation of four different rib densities (Nr = 3, 5, 7, 9) and six rib geometries (rectangular, pentagonal, hexagonal, octagonal, triangular, and semi-circular). By analyzing the impact of these design parameters on heat transfer, pressure losses, and overall system efficiency, this research provides new insights into optimizing Trombe wall configurations for enhanced thermal performance and minimal flow resistance. These findings offer valuable design guidelines for improving passive solar heating systems, particularly in nearly zero-energy buildings (nZEBs).

Problem description

Physical model

Trombe walls are among the best-known examples of how to use passive strategies in buildings to utilize solar energy for heating. It operates based on a simple, yet very thermally-efficient way to collect and use solar energy. The process starts when incident solar energy hits the transparent glazing of the Trombe wall and is absorbed by a solid wall. The wall has been painted black so as to absorb the maximum amount of energy possible (see Fig. 1). The wall serves as an energy storage device, collecting energy as sensible heat during the day and slowly releasing the collected energy from the wall, through the vertical space or air-gap created between the wall and the glazing. When the warm air rises along the heated wall and comes into contact with it, there is a gradual increase in the air temperature which allows the heat being transferred from the wall to be transferred towards the indoor environment. The system works by having the solar energy collected by the wall combined with the convection/conduction heat transfers occurring inside the air-space of the air-gap, without needing the assistance of complicated mechanical systems.

Schematic representation of the thermo-hydrodynamic functioning of a Trombe wall.

As illustrated in Fig. 1, the thermal cycle can be summarized in three stages: (i) absorption of solar radiation through the glazing and storage within the wall, (ii) heat transfer from the wall to the air circulating in the channel, and (iii) release of the gained energy into the occupied space. This mechanism enhances the overall energy efficiency of the building and optimizes the use of passive solar gains while reducing reliance on conventional heating systems.

The schematic highlights the entire construction of the system which comprise: a massive wall that serves as a thermal storage medium, a layer of external glazing which could be coupled with an absorptive coating, and an intermediate cavity which is an air filled space where heat exchange occurs between a warm wall surface and the circulating air.

Governing equations

The ANSYS Fluent (2020 R1) numerical simulations were conducted on the basis of the resolution of the basic conservation equations of mass, momentum, and energy. These equations of state are the theoretical basis of the computational fluid dynamics (CFD) and give the physical structure upon which to rigorously model the thermo-hydrodynamic behavior of the Trombe wall.

Air in this study was considered to be an incompressible fluid but this assumption was justified by the fact that the Mach number (Ma ≪ 0.3) is low and the range of temperature changes in the study is moderate. Its thermophysical data (density, viscosity, specific heat and thermal conductivity) were assessed at the inlet by using temperature-dependent polynomials correlations and maintained constant throughout the computational domain to maintain numerical stability. The simulations were carried out at a laminar forced convection regime (Re = 600–1600), and the natural convection was ignored and Boussinesq equation was not employed.

The three forms of heat transfer were however also incorporated in a simplistic yet consistent manner. Conduction was solved directly in the solid regions (storage wall and glazing), convection was modeled in the air cavity, and radiation was modeled by applying a constant solar heat flux (I = 748 W/m2) on the absorber surface, and the glazing was modeled as adiabatic with some trapping of the radiation. It is a methodology that separates the effect of the rib geometry on the thermo-hydrodynamic behavior of the Trombe wall at the expense of providing a reasonable trade-off in physical realism and computational efficiency.

Boundary conditions and numerical model

It is important to note that while the use of a constant inlet velocity in our model does not fully replicate the natural convection phenomena typically observed in passive Trombe walls, this approach was chosen for both methodological and practical reasons. This strategy enables the precise isolation of the impact of rib configurations on heat transfer and hydrodynamic performance by controlling airflow variables without the complex influence of natural convection. In the laminar flow regime studied (with Reynolds numbers ranging from 600 to 1600), the effects of forced convection are significantly more pronounced than those of natural convection. Therefore, assuming a constant inlet velocity offers a reasonable approximation for this analysis. This simplification also allows for a more focused investigation of the impact of rib geometries on the thermal performance of the Trombe wall (similar works45, 48).

The boundary conditions were established to reproduce the operating principles of the Trombe wall while maintaining numerical stability and robustness. The operating parameters were set as follows: an incident solar flux of I = 748 W/m², an ambient air temperature of T = 293.75 K, and an external wind speed of U = 1 m/s45. At the inlet (bottom opening, h₁), a velocity boundary condition was imposed, corresponding to Reynolds numbers in the range Re = 600–160048. The thermo-physical properties of air were pre-processed from temperature-dependent polynomial correlations, ensuring that the inflowing fluid reproduced realistic atmospheric conditions. At the outlet (top opening, h₂), a pressure-outlet condition with zero gauge pressure was applied, which enabled buoyancy-driven circulation while satisfying global mass balance.

For the walls, the absorber surface was maintained at a uniform constant temperature, representing the solar heating of a massive wall with a dark coating. The glazing layer was modeled as adiabatic with partial radiative trapping, reflecting the greenhouse effect mechanism. All remaining walls were treated as adiabatic and subjected to the no-slip condition, in order to isolate the influence of the absorber and the internal ribs on flow and heat transfer. Although these boundary conditions represent a simplified version of real building environments where fluctuating wind, variable outdoor temperature, and the full solar spectrum play significant roles they are consistent with established CFD studies on Trombe walls. Such an approach allows the effect of rib configurations to be examined under controlled and reproducible conditions, thus providing a rigorous framework for assessing thermo-hydrodynamic performance.

In order to guarantee numerical stability, the smooth walls (i.e., without ribs) at Re = 600 were checked against mesh independence. The domain was separated into three zones: glazing, fluid cavity and solid wall as depicted in Fig. 6. In the present work, a structured grid was used to achieve a trade-off between accuracy and numerical cost, and local mesh refinement in regions of importance especially near walls and ribs for improving thermal gradients and flow features resolution.

Numerical discretization of the Trombe wall system highlighting mesh independence analysis.

The results of the mesh independence study are shown in Fig. 7, with the variations of the maximum velocity (Umax), and the friction factor (f) as functions of the number of elements used to discretize the flow field at Re = 600. It can be seen from the data presented in the figure, that these two quantities experience considerable variations when the mesh is first refined and then stabilize. Therefore, based on the observed mesh dependence it can be concluded that additional refinements of the mesh will not provide any improvement in the accuracy of the numerical prediction, but rather increase the time required to compute the solutions. Therefore, a mesh size of 1, 221,675 elements was selected to balance the need for high spatial resolution to capture the thermo-hydrodynamic gradient in the flow field, with an acceptable level of computational expense.

In summary, the mesh dependence study provides a solid methodological basis for the analysis of the present work, providing assurance that the numerical predictions obtained are stable, consistent, and reliable, enhancing the confidence in the use of the CFD methodology used in this investigation.

Validation of the model

Under the laminar flow regime, the heat transfer in a Trombe wall is usually calculated by use of Eq. (11), which is a common reference equation used in the description of convective exchange in these configurations. This expression, which has been experimentally confirmed by a variety of authors41–44, has been confirmed as a useful and precise approximation, especially in the Reynolds number range 600–1600 where it gives results that are consistent with the experimental results. It can therefore be used as a very effective instrument in the prediction of the Nusselt number and the measure of the convective heat transfer that takes place inside the vertical air channel of the Trombe wall.

Simultaneously, the hydrodynamic behaviour of laminar flow in the channel is modelled using Hagen-Poiseuille equation which is given as Eq. (12). This legislation has a direct proportional relationship between the pressure drop and the mean velocity of fluid in a duct and as such, it is possible to determine the friction factor (f), which is one important parameter used to evaluate the aerodynamic and energy performance of this system. This relationship is valid provided that the Reynolds number does not exceed the transition number to turbulence such that the applicability of the linear assumptions supporting the model is still preserved.

A rigorous and consistent analysis of the coupled thermo-hydrodynamic processes in a Trombe wall is made possible by the combination of these two formulations (the one that governs heat transfer and the other one that describes the pressure losses). This theoretical framework on which experimental validation is supported offers a useful foundation to the design, assessment and optimization of passive solar heating systems, with a balanced approach to both thermal and hydrodynamic restraint.

Results and discussion

The thermo-hydrodynamics of Trombe walls are characterized into two different stages that can be used as a way to systematically evaluate their performance. Stage one will be the determination of the rib quantity for the base-line Model A (a rectangular shaped Trombe wall), where the rib quantity has been determined at four levels (Nr = 3, 5, 7 and 9) in order to determine the ideal rib quantity that allows for maximum convective heat transfer while also minimizing flow resistance. Results from this stage will provide information about how best to balance the amount of turbulent flow that occurs by using ribs to promote convection versus how much resistance there is to fluid flow through the ribs in order to find the optimal rib arrangement.

The second phase is when the probing is detracted to the influence of rib geometry. The best rib density that has been identified is tested on six different designs (Models 1–6). This comparison study points out how varying shapes and arrangements change the organization of flows, the intensity of mixing, and the interactions between the walls and fluids. This step allows calculating how different geometry options would affect the overall performance by measuring the effect of each option on the heat transfer efficiency and pressure losses.

Effect of Rib Number (Nr) on thermo-hydrodynamic performance

This paper has investigated the effect of rib density (Nr) on the thermo-hydrodynamic properties of Trombe wall, that is, the heat transfer and pressure losses. As distinctly shown by the outcomes provided in Fig. 9-a and b, the methods of conduction heat transfer enhancement through the use of ribs is highly useful. This improvement is most notable when Nr = 5 is turned out to be the most productive.

Effect of the number of vertical ribs (Nr) on the Trombe wall thermal performance: (a) average Nusselt number; (b) normalized Nu/Nu₀ ratio.

The Nusselt number (Nu) of the smooth channel at Re = 600 is 7.257, and the Nr = 5 setting of the channel has Nuis 10.794; an increase of 48.7. The improvement is also experienced at Re = 1600 where Nu = 12.412 smooth has been upgraded to Nu = 20.880, Nr = 5 hence an improvement rate of 68.2. This trend has been caused by the creation of the vortex structures which result in the enhancement of the mixing of the fluid hence increasing the heat exchanged between the air and the wall under heating. Ribs provide turbulence which increases the surface of the heat exchange and increases the rate of heat transfer which leads to a great improvement in efficiency.

Nevertheless, beyond Nr = 5, a saturation effect on the thermal performance takes place. Such configurations as Nr = 7 (Nu = 18.137, + 46.1%) and Nr = 9 (Nu = 18.603, + 50.0%) are still better than the smooth channel, but the improvement is less pronounced. This loss in heat transfer rate could also be attributed to excessive blockage of the flow that heightens the pressure losses as well as the reduction in the natural movement of air. In fact, the existence of excessive number of ribs generates larger recirculation zones, which can no longer play an effective role in heat transfer and augment hydraulic resistance. This is observed in the findings in Fig. 9-b, where normalized Nusselt number ratio (Nu/Nu0) attains 1.682 (+ 68.2%) at Re 1600, when Nr = 5, but is capped to 1.461 (+ 46.1%) when Nr = 7 and 1.498 (+ 50.0%) when Nr = 9. This verifies that adding more than Nr = 5 ribs does not proportionally add to the thermal gains with proportional increase in the friction.

Altogether, the Nr = 5 configuration provides the most optimal trade-off between thermal performance and control of hydraulic losses. This design offers a great deal of thermal advantage without too many flow complexities and is therefore the best choice in terms of the efficiency of the Trombe wall systems. Beyond this value, extra thermal advantages are countered by far too much pressure loss, and it is necessary to have a fine tune adjustment of the rib density to each unique application.

Figure 10 shows the correlation between Colburn factor (j) and Reynolds number (Re) of different Trombe wall configurations, such as the smooth model, the ribbed model (Nr = 3, 5, 7, and 9). The j-factor is an important performance parameter because it includes heat transfer gain and weight of the penalties due to friction of the ribs, which provide an overall coverage of thermo-hydraulic performance of the system.

We find that the j-factor with ribs is greatly enhanced relative to that of the smooth channel, which confirms the fact that the turbulence and the secondary flows produced by the ribs enhance effective convective heat transfer. Due to the disruption of the airflow by the ribs, the thermal boundary layer is broken, hence, increasing heat exchange between the air and the heated wall. The result is an increase of the thermal performance of the ribbed configurations.

But we also find a tendency to the general diminution of the j-factor with the increasing Reynolds numbers (Re) of all configurations, and this is consistent with the usual behavior of laminar-to-transitional flow regimes. The higher the value of Re, the more inertial the flow and the less significant the viscous effects (related to the j-factor) will be, which significantly reduces the increase in the thermal efficiency. This reduction is common in the passage of flows, between laminar and turbulent flow where the flow processes are less dependent on the strengthening of frictional heating.

Out of all the configurations, the Nr = 5 design is the most preferable. This arrangement at Re = 600 gives a j = 0.02017 which is 48.7% better than the smooth channel (j = 0.01356). At Re = 1600, the gain is even higher, and the value of j = 0.01469, and the gain of j is 68.2. These results confirm the Nr = 5 setting as an efficient solution, which creates a good balance between thermal augmentation and a sensible flow resistance. The enhanced turbulence enhances the convectional heat transfer and flow resistance is manageable.

Contrarily, the j-factor plateau (or even minorly deteriorated) is observed in higher rib densities (Nr = 7 and 9). The cause of this trend may be explained by the overblocking of the flow passage resulting in the creation of the stagnant recirculation pockets. Such recirculation areas minimize the regeneration of warmed air on the absorber wall and restrict the net thermal value. The more the ribs one has, the less favorable the trade-off between thermal gains and flow resistance and finally the efficiency of the system.

In general, the Nr = 5 design proves to be the most practical option, and it will provide significant improvements in energy performance with a healthy natural circulation in the air cavity. This is the most appropriate design as it offers optimal balance between heat transfer and pressure losses, thus being the best design to be used in the optimization of Trombe wall systems in both thermal performance and hydraulic efficiency.

Variation of the friction factor (f) and normalized ratio (f/fo) with Re for different rib densities (Nr).

In every instance, the friction factor reduces with rising Reynolds number which is typical of weakly transitional and laminar flows. The level of viscous dissipation is diluted relative to the inertial effects in these flow regimes resulting in a decrease in the resistance due to friction. But upon introduction of ribs; there is a marked increase in the friction factor compared to the smooth reference channel because of the added form drag, as well as the shear stresses due to the disruptive action of the ribs on the flow. The flow is forced by the introduction of ribs, a factor that creates turbulence and consequently enhances the heat transfer, but also causes the resistance of flow to increase.

The configuration with Nr = 5 has the best ratio, as far as the trade-off of thermal enhancement and the losses of friction is concerned. The friction factor of a smooth channel at Re, 600, is f = 0.1067; the friction factor of the same channel with Nr = 5 is f = 0.1871, which is 75.4% higher. At Re = 1600 the value is even greater and the friction factor of the smooth channel equals f = 0.0488 whereas the five-rib channel equals f = 0.1025, which is by 110% higher. This underscores the great influence of the ribs in the resistance to flow that in spite of aiding in heat transfer also causes a hydraulic penalty that should be taken into account when designing a system.

On the other hand, the friction factor increases significantly when the rib density is increased (Nr = 7 and 9), which results in the plateau and even the slight decline in the overall performance. An illustration is at Re = 600, seven ribbons are used, we find f = 0.2005 an 87.9 per cent increase over that of the smooth channel, and the nine ribbons show even less resistance. Such configurations with increased rib densities form too much obstruction to the flow producing stagnant recirculation areas which lower the renewal of heated air at the absorber wall. Consequently, the thermal advantage is not directly proportional to the increment in friction resulting in diminishing thermal performance returns.

The trend is also confirmed in Fig. 11-b showing the normalized ratio f/f 0. The nine rib design demonstrates a doubling of the resistance to friction over the smooth case at Re = 600, where f/f 0 = 2.022 (+ 102.2%). More ribs, however, beyond Nr = 5, the penalty due to friction increases disproportionately to an equilibrium in the normalized ratio. This implies that the five-rib (Nr = 5) design is the most efficient design, which will maximum balance thermal enhancement and a reasonable flow resistance.

Finally, the 5-rib (Nr = 5) is the most balanced structure that provides both high thermal performance and efficient flow dynamics. Adding further density to the ribs (Nr = 7 and 9) has too much in the way of hydraulic penalties to justify the added thermal gain. This shows that it is very significant to perfectly balance the density of ribs so as to have the most efficient design of Trombe walls that would give adequate thermal enhancement and controllable flow resistance.

Figure 12 shows a plot of the Poiseuille number (Po) versus Reynolds number (Re) at various Trombe wall niches, such as the smooth reference channel and those ribbed with Nr = 3, 5, 7 and 9. A major indicator of the hydraulic resistance to flow is the Poiseuille number that is the product of the friction factor (f) and the Reynolds number (Re) (Po = f x Re). It gives a quantitative determination of flow penalty of the internal obstacles, e.g. ribs, of the Trombe wall cavity.

Variation of the Poiseuille number (Po) with Re for different rib densities (Nr).

The results show that the Poiseuille number is always greater when ribbed structures are used than when using the smooth channel. This means that the resistance to flow is augmented by the flow which is caused by the addition of ribs and which forms and creates more friction. The Po of smooth channel is 64.01 at Re = 600 and the Po of five rib (Nr = 5) is 112.28, which is 75.4% higher. In the case of the nine-rib (Nr = 9) structure, Po is again boosted, by 102.2 per cent. These findings reveal that whereas the ribs are required to improve the heat transfer, it also raises the hydraulic resistance because of the additional friction and flow disruptions.

With a higher Reynolds number, the values of Po start converging with each other meaning that with higher inertial regimes, the relative effect of the ribs becomes insignificant. The Poiseuille number increases once more at Re = 1600 in all configurations, with the five-rib configuration increasing to 163.98 (+ 110.0%), and the nine-rib configuration increasing to 159.06 (+ 103.5%). At greater Reynolds numbers, the hydraulic losses are still considerable as the ribs still interfere with the flow creating pressure losses.

The outcomes of this study support the fact that rib density directly and quantifiably influences the aerodynamic resistance of the Trombe wall. Although adding ribs enhances the heat transfer rate due to improvement in fluid mixing and turbulence, the Poiseuille number ratio is also increasing relative to it, which means that flow resistance is increasing. The five-rib design (Nr = 5) appears to be the most acceptable solution, as it provides a considerable improvement in the heat transfer with only moderate hydraulic penalties.

On the contrary, the Poiseuille number keeps growing without matching thermal benefits as the density of the ribs increases to Nr = 7 and 9. The added ribs result in more flow impediment, which forms non-renewable recirculation areas diminishing the renewal of the heated air inside the absorber wall. This will lead to a reduction in the thermal benefits which will eventually reduce the total system energy efficiency. Such results emphasize the need to achieve the right balance between ensuring that the heat transfer is as high as possible and that the hydraulic resistance is as low as possible.

Finally, the design with Nr = 5 provides the highest compromise to make sure that there is a great thermal gain at a reasonable flow resistance. The increase in rib densities (Nr = 7 and 9) only adds minimal thermal enhancement and increases hydraulic losses, which ultimately causes the decrease in the global performance of the Trombe wall. It shows that to maximize the design of Trombe walls it is necessary to pay close attention to both thermal and hydraulic limitations and design a wall that will optimize both of these features to reach maximum efficiency with the wall.

The triangular ribs have the largest normalized friction factor (f/f 0 = 3.07) at Re = 600, which means that the frictional resistance is over three times higher than the frictional resistance of the smooth channel. The pentagonal and hexagonal ones come next, with normalized ratios of 2.30 and 2.21 respectively, and the rectangular and semi-circular designs, with penalties of 2.02 and 1.75 respectively. With increase in Reynolds number, friction penalties become less penalty in all the configurations but trend is always the same way. With Re = 1600, f/f 0 equals 2.97, and pentagonal and hexagonal ribs are between 2.45 and 2.55, and rectangular and semi-circular ribs are between 2.00 and 2.15. It means that the more inertial the flow is, the lesser the effects of the ribs on the friction, although the total resistance is still greater than in the case of the smooth channel.

In general, the results of Fig. 18(a) and (b) underline that the rib geometry plays a very important role in determining the resistance to flow in the Trombe wall system. The geometries with the greatest hydraulic penalties are the angular rib geometries, which include the triangular, pentagonal, and hexagonal ribs because they have sharp angles and higher disruptive flow. The rectangular and semi-circular ribs, on the other hand, offer a more reasonable trade-off in which the additional losses of friction are moderate, but they still help in enhancing the convective heat transfer. These findings indicate that the rib geometry is an important factor to consider in the achievement of the optimal thermal enhancement and flow resistance.

The bifold evaluation of the absolute friction factor (f) and the normalized friction factor (f/f0) shows that although angular geometries have a better heat transfer, they also have an increase in the flow resistance. Smoother geometries, such as rectangular and semi-circular ribs, have moderate penalties in friction and also improve convectional heat transfer, so may be more useful in designs where the cost of energy reduction associated with flow resistance is paramount. Hence, Trombe walls design should be unique with regard to thermal performance and hydraulic efficiency with the most effective geometry being determined by different applications as well as the necessity to prioritize both.

The development of the Poiseuille number (Po = f × Re) with Reynolds number (Re) in the six ribbed configurations as compared to the smooth reference channel is presented in Fig. 19. The Poiseuille number is the aggregate of the influence of friction and inertial forces on the flow which gives a complete evaluation on the hydodynamic losses of the internal ribs.

Variation of the Poiseuille number (Po) with Reynolds number (Re) for the six rib geometries (Models 1–6).

In the case of the smooth channel, Po rises in a moderate way with Reynolds number between Po = 64.01 at Re = 600 to Po = 78.18 at Re = 1600. This is similar to the laminar flowing, whereby the effect of the viscosity is predominant, and the Po increment is relatively slow. Conversely, the ribbed designs exhibit very high Po values which means that there is a greater resistance to flow caused by the extra friction and shedding of vortices created by the ribs. The fact that Po has risen above the ribbed designs is a positive pointer to the additional hydraulic penalty, which is created by the interruption of the flow by the ribs.

The triangular ribbed structure (M5) at Re = 600 produces an optimum hydraulic penalty (Po = 196.23) 206% higher than the smooth channel. The pentagonal (M2) and hexagonal (M3) designs are next with Po = 147.42 (+ 130%) and Po = 141.31 (+ 121), respectively. Intermediate penalties are brought about by the existence of the octagonal (M4) and semi-circular (M6) ribs with Po = 140.41 (+ 119%) and Po = 129.49 (+ 102%). The rib structure is the most penalized (M1) producing Po = 112.29 (75%).

Reynolds number changes these rankings of the rib geometries little, as the number of the rib geometry increases, but the distance between the configurations reduces. At Re = 1600 the triangular (M5) ribs again produce the maximum Po = 232.10 with + 197% increase, then the pentagonal (M2) ribs then with Po = 199.57 with + 156% increase and then the hexagonal (M3) ribs yielding Po = 196.91 with + 152% increase. Po = 201.75 (+ 158) is caused by the octagonal (M4) arrangement, whereas Po = 174.66 (+ 123) and Po = 163.98 (+ 110 ) are caused by the semi-circular (M6) and the rectangular (M1) rib arrangement, respectively.

These findings affirm that rib geometry is influential in the defining the resistance to flow in Trombe wall system. These angular geometries, triangular, pentagonal, hexagonal, and octagonal ribs produce the highest hydraulic penalties as the disturbance of the flow, vortex generation and recirculated areas are increased. Smoother geometries (including M1, M6) are also less of an obstacle to flow, and lower Po values are obtained, a more balanced trade-off between thermal enhancement and hydraulic resistance.

The results are clear that although triangular rib design (M5) has the highest thermal performance in the context of heat transfer enhancement, it is also associated with the highest hydraulic costs. This shows the need to take into consideration the thermo-hydraulic trade-offs during optimization of Trombe wall systems. In order to get the greatest overall efficiency, one needs to trade off the thermal performance of the rib geometry against the flow resistance. Smoother rib geometries, though having an inferior thermal improvement, can offer a more desirable balance by reducing the energy costs of flow resistance.

Finally, the rib geometry is a determining factor to the hydrodynamic resistance, and the angular shape produces the most severe penalties. The majority of thermal performance, as observed in the triangular design is associated with the greatest hydraulic costs. Trombe wall systems therefore need to be optimised with due consideration on thermal gains and hydraulic penalties in order to come up with an efficient design.

Figure 20 shows the velocity contours of the smooth reference channel and the six ribbed configurations (M1-M6) at Re = 1000, and this has an effect on the internal flow structure and, eventually, efficiency of convective heat transfer. The velocity distribution in the smooth channel is much stratified: the centre of the flow has the greatest velocity, with long low velocity streaks at the glazing and the absorber wall. Large, high density boundary layers and low entrainment rates, as a result of no perturbations, inhibit convectional heat exchange drastically.

Velocity contours for the smooth and ribbed Trombe wall configurations (Re = 1000).

The appearance of ribs radically changes this behavior, periodically breaking the boundary layer, and forming localized recirculation zones at each end of the rib. In the case of the rectangular ribs (M1), the effects are moderate: the obstacles induce the destabilization of shear-layers and small vortices, but the recirculation areas are not large, which causes the partial homogenization of the velocity field. Having stiffer angles and more edges than triangular, pentagonal (M2) and hexagonal (M3) ribs enhance the separation of flows and result in a diversity of coherent vortices. These vortices result in enhanced lateral diffusion, decreased stagnation in the region of glazing and a relatively homogeneous velocity field in the cavity.

The octagonal ribs (M4) give a more even velocity distribution (symmetric) compared to the angular ones and generate some recirculation. Nevertheless, the recirculation causing the octagonal ribs seems to be a weaker one, and it means that the enhanced mixing and the enhanced hydraulic properties might be trade-offs. That is, octagonal shaped ribs are an intermediate solution with good rather than best improvements, but which balance hydraulic and thermal enhancements.

Lastly, the most mildly affected geometry of all those analyzed is semi-circular ribs (M6). The rounded form creates a smooth deflection of the flow and minimizes stagnation as compared to smooth channel. The vortices caused by the semi-circular ribs, however, are not as strong as the vortices caused by the sharp-edged geometries.

Taken together, these observations indicate the strong impact of rib geometry on the organization of flow. Angular geometries, especially the triangular ribs (M5) are the most promising in terms of developing the strong recirculating flows and, hence, the high turbulence rates. These unstable situations are directly connected with an increase in the rate of convective heat transfer. Smoother geometries (i.e. M4 and M6) have less severe perturbations to the flow, and may be helpful when the design objectives include fewer hydraulic penalties but lower thermal performance.

This discussion, which is founded on employing streamlines and vorticity magnitude, demonstrates the influence of the rib geometries on the flow regime. Angular designs especially M5 create high vorticity, which is equivalent to strong turbulence and enhanced heat transfer. The less vorticose and therefore less turbulent and smoother geometries (M4 and M6) have a lower hydraulic losses in any case, so that smoother flow and less thermal gains are less important in designs where low hydraulic losses are more vital than high thermal gains.

Improvement in other rib geometries is also significant albeit less than that. The conclusions drawn by these findings are that the ribbed designs have a great impact on the performance of the Trombe wall since it promotes the turbulence and the discontinuation of the thermal boundary layer, which enhances the volume of convective heat transfer. The triangular geometry (M5) always offers the best j-factor among the whole range of Reynolds numbers, and by nearly a factor of 2 better than the smooth channel.

The findings stress the importance of rib geometry on the optimization of the performance of Trombe wall systems. Angular designs, which include the triangular, pentagonal, and hexagonal ribs, are the designs that have the highest thermal advantage since they amplify the action of disturbances in the flows and this leads to an increased heat transfer. Nonetheless, there are also increased hydraulic penalties associated with these angular geometries that have greater resistance to flows. The more moderate improvement in thermal performance with a minimum of the frictional losses due to flow resistance is achieved by smoother geometries as the rectangular and semi-circular ribs.

Finally, although the designs of angular ribs (and triangular arrangement in particular) offer the best thermal performance, it should be closely balanced with the related hydraulic costs. The rib means that it should consider both thermal enhancement and flow resistance when designing the best rib to use in constructing Trombe walls which will be the most efficient.

Triangular ribs are not as useful in generating excellent turbulence at these lower inertial conditions resulting in a disproportionate rise in flow resistance compared to the thermal gains.

The ranking of the rib geometries varies with increase in Reynolds number. The rib designs (hexagonal (M3)) and pentagonal (M2)) are more efficient at the Re ranging between 800 and 1200. Finally, rectangular ribs (M1) have the most balanced trade-off at low Reynolds numbers since they have moderate turbulence and comparatively low penalties in friction. But, in fact, triangular rib configuration (M5) is the most successful as the Reynolds number is high and shows maximum overall thermal enhancement. These findings demonstrate the importance of aligning rib geometry with the operating flow regime triangular ribs are best in high inertial regimes and are therefore the best solution to Trombe wall applications that require thermohydraulic efficiency to be considered at higher Reynolds numbers.

Conclusion

This study numerically explored the thermo-hydrodynamic performance of ribbed Trombe walls using three-dimensional CFD simulations, examining the dual influence of rib density (Nr = 3, 5, 7,9) and rib geometry (rectangular, pentagonal, hexagonal, octagonal triangular, and semi-circular). The results provide a detailed picture of how internal obstacles alter flow structures, vortex formation, pressure losses, and convective heat transfer mechanisms.

The introduction of ribs significantly strengthens heat transfer by disturbing the boundary layer, promoting vortex formation, and enhancing fluid mixing. The optimum effect is reached with Nr = 5. At Re = 600, the average Nusselt number increases from 7.26 (smooth channel) to 10.79 (+ 48.6%), while at Re = 1600, Nu rises from 12.41 to 20.88 (+ 68.3%). Beyond this threshold, additional ribs merely thicken the recirculation zones and elevate hydraulic resistance without providing proportional gains.
