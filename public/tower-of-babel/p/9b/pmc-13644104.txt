A single-center retrospective study of a decade-long experience in managing pediatric lymphatic malformation
Authors: Yiran Meng, Jialu Wang, Yanni Wang, Wenyuan Shi, Jie Li, Xuexi Zhang, Ge Zhang, Qiaoyin Liu, Zhiyong Liu, Yanzhen Li, Nian Sun, Shengcai Wang, Xin Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and objectivesLymphatic malformations (LMs) are rare pediatric conditions that present variable symptoms depending on lesion size and anatomical location, necessitating diverse treatment approaches. This study aims to characterize the clinical features and prognosis of LMs across different anatomical sites and to compare the therapeutic efficacy of various treatment modalities.Materials and methodsA retrospective analysis was performed on 478 pediatric patients diagnosed with LMs at Beijing Children’s Hospital between January 2014 and December 2024. Data regarding patient demographics, clinical characteristics, treatment modalities, and therapeutic outcomes were extracted and analyzed.ResultsThe head and neck region was the most involved site (45.0%). Macrocysts were predominantly located in the pelvic/abdominal, mixed cysts in the head and neck, and microcysts in the trunk/extremities (p < 0.01). The macrocystic subtype demonstrated the most favorable response across all therapeutic modalities (p < 0.05). Furthermore, no statistically significant difference in effective rates was observed between surgery and sclerotherapy.ConclusionsSubtype distribution demonstrated distinct anatomic predilections. Surgery and sclerotherapy both achieve high effective rates when selected according to appropriate clinical indications. Macrocystic LMs appeared to have the best therapeutic outcomes.

What is known

• LMs is a low-flow vascular anomaly predominantly presenting in neonates, frequently leading to cosmetic disfigurement or functional impairment. Morphologically, it is categorized into macrocystic, microcystic, and mixed subtypes, with surgery and sclerotherapy serving as the primary therapeutic modalities.

What is new

• In this study, we found that each subtype of lymphatic malformation has certain anatomic preferences. Regardless of the treatment modality, the macrocystic type showed the best therapeutic response.

Introduction

Lymphatic malformations (LMs) are rare congenital low-flow vascular anomalies, with an estimated incidence ranging from 1 in 6,000 to 1 in 16,000 live births [1, 2]. Over 90% of cases present in children under 2 years old [2, 3]. These malformations may develop anywhere in the body, particularly in lymphatic-rich regions. The head and neck represent the most common site, followed by extremities and trunk, while pelvic/abdominal involvement is relatively uncommon [4, 5]. The International Society for the Study of Vascular Anomalies (ISSVA) classifies LMs into three types: macrocystic, microcystic, and mixed [6]. Primary manifestations include asymptomatic masses, pain, and dyspnea. Lesion volume typically expands proportionally with somatic growth in children and may undergo sudden enlargement secondary to intracystic hemorrhage or infection, potentially leading to impaired organ function, airway obstruction, cosmetic deformity, or even mortality. Based on symptom severity, treatment options range from conservative observation to multimodal therapeutic approaches [7–10].

To date, standardized diagnostic and therapeutic guidelines for lymphatic malformations remain lacking. However, current clinical practice offers relatively well-established therapeutic approaches [4, 7, 11]. Conventional modalities primarily include surgical resection and sclerotherapy. Surgery is applicable to LMs across all anatomical sites, demonstrating an overall efficacy rate of approximately 90%, with most patients achieving definitive resolution following a single procedure [12–16]. In recent years, sclerotherapy has emerged as a mainstream therapeutic modality for LMs. Compared to surgery, it offers advantages of minimal invasiveness and reduced hospitalization. Reported overall efficacy exceeds 80%, with particularly favorable treatment responses observed in superficial macrocystic LMs of the head and neck [17–19]. Meanwhile, oral pharmacotherapy has gained significant recognition for managing LMs, including the mTOR inhibitor sirolimus and the more recently employed PIK3CA inhibitor alpelisib [20–23]. Nevertheless, data on pediatric patients undergoing treatment for LMs are largely confined to single-center case series. Furthermore, these studies often focus on only one treatment modality or a single location of LMs and are limited by small sample sizes. To our knowledge, the present study represents the largest single-center retrospective cohort to date. We aim to comprehensively characterize the clinical features of pediatric LMs and evaluate the comparative efficacy and rationale for modality selection between surgical intervention and sclerotherapy.

Materials and methods

Patients

We collected patients diagnosed with LMs at Beijing Children’s Hospital from January 2014 to December 2024. The inclusion criteria were age at diagnosis < 18 years; radiologically confirmed common LMs meeting the latest ISSVA 2018 classification criteria and clinically assessed as LMs by physicians, in the absence of other diseases or concurrent vascular malformations; and a minimum follow-up duration of ≥ 12 months after treatment. Exclusion criteria comprised: insufficient pre- and post-treatment imaging data; concurrent treatment for other conditions during LMs therapy; patients whose parents refused any form of active intervention (surgery or sclerotherapy). However, to illustrate the natural history and potential life-threatening complications of severe LMs, data from fatal cases who received no active treatment were collected and descriptively reported in a separate section. Based on the maximum cystic diameter measured on imaging examinations, lesions were classified as macrocystic LMs (diameter ≥ 2 cm), microcystic LMs (diameter < 2 cm), or mixed LMs (presence of both cyst types).

Treatment

Since the treatment of LMs with sirolimus in our center is limited to small-cohort clinical studies conducted in the past five years, the treatment modalities included in this study are restricted to surgery and sclerotherapy. The surgical or sclerotherapy treatments discussed in this study are all initial treatment for patients with LMs. For most superficial LMs, sclerotherapy is the recommended modality of treatment. Sclerotherapy is usually performed under ultrasound guidance, with the injection of sclerosing agents (e.g. bleomycin, polidocanol) into the cysts. Conversely, surgical intervention is indicated in the following scenarios: (1) LMs with symptoms (e.g. pain, physical limitations/disability, volvulus); (2) lesions located in anatomical sites unsuitable for percutaneous sclerotherapy; (3) microcystic LMs or lesions with multiple intralesional septations rendering them unsuitable for sclerotherapy. The primary objective of surgery is complete resection, however, for extensive lesions where total excision is unfeasible, the goal shifts to partial resection (debulking) for symptom palliation. Ultimately, the specific treatment modality is determined through comprehensive clinical evaluation combined with parental preference.

Data collection and follow-up

Clinical data for pediatric patients were collected from electronic medical records. This included patient sex, age, lesion location and size, clinical symptoms, initial treatment modality (surgery, sclerotherapy), number of hospitalizations, response to treatment and clinical information for deceased patients. LMs volume was quantified using MRI volumetry, with ultrasound volume estimation employed when MRI was unavailable. Follow-up assessments were conducted using two distinct methods. Radiological follow-up was performed at standardized intervals to objectively measure lesion volume reduction and assess primary treatment efficacy. Additionally, long-term follow-up was conducted via telephone interviews. The telephone follow-up protocol aimed to collect long-term survival status, identify any late clinical recurrences, and determine the therapy administered upon recurrence outside our institution. The cutoff date for data collection was December 31, 2024.

Outcome assessment

All patients underwent imaging examinations at the time of diagnosis, and the measured lesion volume was defined as the pre-treatment volume. To objectively assess primary treatment efficacy, structured radiological follow-up imaging was routinely scheduled at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months, and 12 months after the initial treatment. However, if patients presented with recurrent symptoms or lesion re-expansion, an immediate imaging examination was conducted, and the results were considered the final imaging measurement. The volume reduction rate (VRR) was used to evaluate treatment response and was calculated as follows: VRR = [(pre-treatment volume − volume at the final imaging examination) / pre-treatment volume] × 100%. Treatment outcomes were defined as follows: (1) Cured: Complete disappearance of the lesion on imaging and without recurrence; (2) Good: VRR ≥ 50% without recurrence; (3) Poor: VRR < 50%; (4) Recurrence: Lesion re-expansion confirmed by follow-up imaging compared to its smallest recorded volume. This includes lesion re-expansion identified during scheduled radiological follow-up, as well as late recurrences first detected by symptoms and subsequently confirmed by imaging during long-term surveillance. In the outcome analysis, “Cured” and “Good” results were categorized as “Effective”, while “Poor” and “Recurrence” results were defined as “Ineffective”.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables, including sex, site of involvement, LMs subtype, and therapeutic efficacy, were reported as frequencies and percentages and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables, including age, baseline lesion volume, and volume reduction rate, were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR). The association between LMs subtype and anatomical location was assessed using Pearson’s chi-square test. When the overall chi-square test was significant, post-hoc analysis based on adjusted standardized residuals was performed to identify cell-specific overrepresentation. Bonferroni correction was applied for multiple comparisons. Results with a p-value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Clinical characteristics of the study cohort

A total of 542 patients evaluated for LMs were initially identified. Among the 542 initially identified patients, 64 were excluded, including 47 with insufficient imaging data, 11 with other concurrent treatments, and 6 fatal cases without any active treatment. Consequently, a final primary cohort of 478 patients who underwent active treatment was included for efficacy analysis. The median follow-up duration for the entire cohort was 58.5 (32.5, 80.3) months. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics of the participants are summarized in Table 1, and representative pretreatment MR images of macrocystic, microcystic, and mixed lymphatic malformations are shown in Fig. 1. There were 288 (60.3%) males and 190 (39.7%) females, yielding a male-to-female ratio of 1.51. The median age at diagnosis was 24 (0, 56.5) months. A total of 120 patients (25.1%) were diagnosed via prenatal screening. The head and neck region was the most common site of LMs, followed by the pelvic/abdominal, trunk/extremities, and mediastinum. At presentation, 294 (61.5%) patients had an asymptomatic mass. Complications included asphyxia, pain, and intestinal obstruction, among others. The distribution of LMs subtypes differed significantly across anatomical locations (p < 0.01). Post-hoc analysis showed that macrocystic LMs was significantly associated with pelvic/abdominal lesions, mixed LMs with head and neck lesions, and microcystic LMs with trunk/extremity lesions (Table 2).

Surgical treatment outcomes

A total of 279 patients underwent surgical resection as the initial treatment. The anatomical distribution of lesions was head and neck (n = 55, 19.7%), trunk/extremities (n = 46, 16.5%), mediastinum (n = 23, 8.2%), and pelvic/abdominal (n = 155, 55.6%). Among all surgical cases, lesion subtypes comprised: macrocystic LMs (n = 125, 44.8%), mixed LMs (n = 104, 37.3%), and microcystic LMs (n = 50, 17.9%). Recurrence occurred in 30 patients (10.8%). Of these, 19/30 (63.3%) were partial resections. The effective rate for macrocystic LMs is significantly higher than that for microcystic and mixed LMs (Fig. 2). The median number of hospitalizations was 1 (1, 1). Drains were placed at the operative site in 171 patients (61.3%), with a median duration of placement of 4.0 (3.0, 6.0) days.

Sclerotherapy treatment outcomes

A total of 199 patients received sclerotherapy as initial treatment. Lesion distribution was head and neck (n = 160, 80.4%), trunk/extremities (n = 39, 19.6%). Lesion subtypes comprised: macrocystic LMs (n = 47, 23.6%), mixed LMs (n = 119, 59.8%), and microcystic LMs (n = 33, 16.6%). The median number of hospitalizations was 2 (1, 2). Eight patients underwent five or more sclerotherapy sessions. Bleomycin was the most used sclerosant, accounting for 180/199 (90.5%) of all sclerosing agents administered. Other alternative agents included polidocanol (n = 9, 4.5%) and pingyangmycin (n = 10, 5.0%). The overall response rate was 82.8% for bleomycin, 55.6% for polidocanol, and 50% for pingyangmycin. Given the substantial differences in sample size among the three groups, formal statistical comparison was not feasible. The median VRR was 93.4% (77.3%, 98.7%). A total of 43 patients (21.6%) had ineffective treatment, including 33/43 (76.7%) with recurrent cases and 10/43 (23.3%) with poor treatment response. For ineffective cases, 20 cases (46.5%) underwent surgical resection, and 23 cases (53.5%) received sclerotherapy. Regarding the salvage treatment outcomes of these 43 non-responsive cases, the overall response rate reached 95% in the surgical group. In comparison, the repeated sclerotherapy group exhibited a response rate of 60.8%, with a median VRR of 58% (22%, 79%). Surgical salvage achieved a significantly higher complete resolution rate compared to repeated sclerotherapy (p < 0.05). Macrocystic lymphatic malformations also showed the highest effective rate (Fig. 3).

Analysis of treatment efficacy by anatomical site

Given the observed differences in subtype distribution across anatomical locations, treatment efficacy was analyzed separately by site. Among patients with head/neck LMs, treatment cohorts comprised surgery (n = 55) and sclerotherapy (n = 160). No statistically significant difference in effective rate was observed between the surgery and sclerotherapy groups (Fig. 4a). Patients with trunk/extremity LMs received surgery (n = 46) and sclerotherapy (n = 39). No statistically significant difference in effective rate was found between the surgery and sclerotherapy groups (Fig. 4b). Although there was no statistically significant difference in the effective rate between surgery and sclerotherapy, interpretation of comparable efficacy should be made cautiously due to obvious selection bias and different outcome definitions.

Fatal cases

Six patients died. The fatal cohort consisted of 2 males and 4 females with a median age of 3.5 months (range 0.8–10 months). Lesions were located in the parapharyngeal space (2 cases), retropharyngeal space (2 cases), carotid space (1 case), and perirenal region (1 case). All 5 patients with head and neck lesions presented with airway compression caused by cervical masses and secondary respiratory distress on admission; the child with abdominal disease presented with abdominal distension and jaundice. Intralesional hemorrhage was radiologically confirmed in all patients upon admission. The causes of death were pneumonia and respiratory failure due to airway compression in the head and neck cases, and hemorrhagic shock following lesion rupture in the abdominal case. The guardians of all deceased children declined active therapeutic interventions due to concerns about poor prognosis and opted for palliative conservative treatment.

Discussion

This retrospective study analyzed a cohort of 478 pediatric patients with LMs treated at our institution between 2014 and 2024, representing the largest single-center retrospective LMs cohort to date. Consistent with prior studies, half of patients developed symptoms before 2 years of age, and over one-quarter were diagnosed prenatally via ultrasound. Notably, the male-to-female ratio of patients with lymphatic malformations in our study was 1.51, which is higher than the sex ratio of the 0 to18-year-old population in China (male: female = 1.04). This may suggest a certain male predilection in the incidence of lymphatic malformations. However, this observation is inconsistent with previous studies, and we plan to conduct a nationwide multicenter study to explore the authenticity of this finding.

The head and neck region was the most common site of involvement, aligning with established literature, followed by the pelvic/abdominal. However, compared with previous studies, our cohort demonstrated a higher proportion of abdominopelvic lesions [2, 3]. This discrepancy may be attributed to our institution’s status as a national pediatric medical center, which frequently receives referrals for rare and complex cases. Distinct distributions of LMs subtypes were observed across anatomical locations: macrocystic LMs predominated in the pelvic/abdominal region, mixed LMs was most frequent in the head and neck, and microcystic LMs showed higher prevalence in the trunk/extremities. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report a site-specific predilection for different LMs morphological subtypes. Nevertheless, given the inherent limitations of a single-center design, it remains to be determined whether this observation represents a universal characteristic of LMs. The underlying reasons for this site-specific subtype distribution remain unclear and warrant further investigation. We hypothesize it may relate to regional tissue architecture. For instance, the expansive pelvic/abdominal may permit less restricted cystic expansion, facilitating macrocyst formation. Conversely, the denser subcutaneous tissues of the trunk and extremities may restrict cyst expansion, thereby contributing to microcystic morphology. Previous research suggests LMs subtypes may correlate with the timing of PIK3CA mutations—mutations in early gestation potentially leading to macrocystic LMs, while those in late gestation or postnatally favoring microcystic forms [24–26]. Transcriptomic differences between macrocystic and microcystic LMs have also been identified. While these findings do not directly explain the site-specific subtype distribution, they highlight the potential utility of molecular biological approaches to investigate differences across locations and subtypes [27].

Current management strategies for LMs encompass conservative observation, surgical resection, and sclerotherapy. Although spontaneous regression has been documented, most lesions expand proportionally with somatic growth. Furthermore, rapid expansion due to hemorrhage or infection can cause severe organ dysfunction or death, necessitating active intervention for most patients [4, 5, 7, 12].

Surgical resection is a conventional therapeutic modality for LMs. Its primary advantage is immediate volume reduction, offering rapid symptom relief for patients with compression, pain, or risks of volvulus/bowel obstruction [16]. Surgical intervention demonstrates favorable overall efficacy, with over 90% of patients remaining recurrence-free during follow-up periods ranging from 6 months to 10 years postoperatively. These outcomes are highly consistent with previously reported findings [14, 28]. Recurrence predominantly occurs in cases with incomplete resection during the initial surgery, suggesting complete excision is a critical factor influencing recurrence rates.

In recent years, sclerotherapy has emerged as a primary treatment modality. Its minimally invasive nature, procedural simplicity, lack of surgical scarring, and reduced risk of neurovascular injury have established it as the mainstay for head and neck LMs at our center. Most children achieved satisfactory outcomes after only two sclerotherapy sessions. Sclerotherapy is the preferred treatment for most superficial LMs [8, 29]. However, although there was no statistically significant difference in efficacy rate between surgery and sclerotherapy, several factors may have influenced the comparison of therapeutic effects. In our cohort, surgery was prioritized for complex, microcystic, or deep lesions, whereas sclerotherapy was primarily used for superficial and macrocystic LMs. Mediastinal and pelvic/abdominal LMs were managed surgically because these lesions are usually deep-seated, making precise puncture and intralesional injections technically challenging and increasing the potential risk of sclerosant leakage and related complications. In addition, patients with lesions in these locations often presented with obvious clinical symptoms, for which surgery could provide more rapid symptom relief and prevent further deterioration. By contrast, most head and neck or trunk/extremity lesions were superficial and asymptomatic, making sclerotherapy a less invasive, technically simpler, and more acceptable option for parents. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as demonstrating comparable efficacy when applied to their respective appropriate indications.

For patients with recurrent or residual lesions, as well as those with persistent symptoms and an inadequate response to previous treatments, our center adopts a relatively aggressive intervention strategy. Surgical resection is generally recommended when anatomically feasible, while sclerotherapy serves as an important second-line alternative for patients whose parents decline surgical resection. Active surveillance is only considered for asymptomatic, clinically stable individuals for whom the risks of intervention outweigh the benefits.

We report six LMs-related deaths. All deceased patients were under one year of age and presented emergently with radiologically confirmed intralesional hemorrhage. Five head/neck patients presented with respiratory failure where airway compression precluded safe intubation. We noted that these children had a history of dyspnea for at least 2 days before admission, but delayed diagnosis and intervention led to further symptom progression, resulting in severe secondary complications upon presentation to our hospital. Emergent aspiration decompression was proposed to temporarily reduce mass effect, but parents declined intervention due to perceived poor prognosis. One abdominal LMs patient developed acute hypotension during evaluation; diagnostic paracentesis revealed massive hemoperitoneum indicative of lesion rupture. Urgent surgery was recommended following parental counseling, but all interventions were declined. These cases underscore that while LMs is benign, rapid expansion secondary to hemorrhage or infection can be life-threatening, particularly for lesions in the head/neck, mediastinum, or pelvic/abdominal cavity. Vigilant monitoring and prompt intervention are crucial when rapid enlargement occurs to prevent catastrophic outcomes [7–9].

In summary, for pediatric LMs, the median age at initial diagnosis is 24 months, with a predominance of male patients over females, and the most common site of disease involvement is the head and neck region. Different subtypes exhibit distinct anatomical predilections: macrocystic lesions predominantly occur in the pelvic/abdominal regions, mixed-type lesions are most found in the head and neck, and microcystic lesions tend to arise in the trunk/extremities. Lesion location, subtype, and clinical presentation influence treatment selection and outcomes. Surgery and sclerotherapy both achieve high effective rates when selected according to appropriate clinical indications. For most superficial LMs, sclerotherapy is the first-line treatment option, unless the patient presents with symptoms or is unsuitable for sclerotherapy. Macrocystic LMs consistently maintains the best treatment response.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design and variable follow-up intervals may introduce inherent biases. Second, incomplete records of previous treatments at external facilities could influence outcome interpretations. Third, significant selection bias exists regarding treatment allocation, as the choice between surgery and sclerotherapy was dictated by lesion type and location. Lastly, the lack of standardized clinical photographic archives precludes the visual comparison of cosmetic outcomes. Future prospective, multicenter trials are required to corroborate these findings.

Conclusion

This study reports a decade of pediatric lymphatic malformation management experience at Beijing Children’s Hospital. Anatomic distribution varied significantly by subtype: abdominopelvic dominance in macrocystic LMs, trunk/extremity predominance in microcystic variants, and head/neck preponderance in mixed lesions. Regardless of treatment modality, macrocystic LMs consistently demonstrated superior treatment response. Surgery and sclerotherapy both achieve high effective rates when selected according to appropriate clinical indications. LMs cases complicated by airway compromise or imminent cyst rupture risk necessitate expedited, individualized therapeutic strategies based on multidisciplinary risk stratification. These findings provide valuable clinical data to inform the development of standardized management guidelines for LMs.
