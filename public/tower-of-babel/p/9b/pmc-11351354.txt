Interprofessional education interventions for healthcare professionals to improve patient safety: a scoping review
Authors: Yan Jiang, Yan Cai, Xue Zhang, Cong Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundPatient safety incidents, such as adverse events and medical errors, are often caused by ineffective communication and collaboration. Interprofessional education is an effective method for promoting collaborative competencies and has attracted great attention in the context of patient safety. However, the effectiveness of interprofessional education interventions on patient safety remains unclear. This scoping review aimed to synthesize existing studies that focused on improving patient safety through interprofessional education interventions for healthcare professionals.MethodsSix databases, including Medline (via PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL (via EBSCO), Scopus and Web of Science, were last searched on 20 December 2023. The search records were independently screened by two researchers. The Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Tool for Quasi-Experimental Studies was used for quality appraisal. The data were extracted by two researchers and cross-checked. Finally, a narrative synthesis was performed. The protocol for this scoping review was not registered.ResultsThirteen quasi-experimental studies with moderate methodological quality were included. The results revealed that the characteristics of current interprofessional education interventions were diverse, with a strong interest in simulation-based learning strategies and face-to-face delivery methods. Several studies did not assess the reduction in patient safety incidents involving adverse events or medical errors, relying instead on the improvements in healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitude or practice related to patient safety issues. Less than half of the studies examined team performance, based primarily on the self-evaluation of healthcare professionals and observer-based evaluation. There is a gap in applying newer tools such as peer evaluation and team-based objective structured clinical evaluation.ConclusionAdditional evidence on interprofessional education interventions for improving patient safety is needed by further research, especially randomized controlled trials. Facilitating simulation-based interprofessional education, collecting more objective outcomes of patient safety and selecting suitable tools to evaluate teamwork performance may be the focus of future studies.KEYWORDS: Interprofessional education, patient safety, collaborative practice, healthcare professionals, scoping review

Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) points out that one out of ten patients suffers harm caused by adverse events and almost half of them are preventable in healthcare [1]. These adverse events were associated with prolonged stays in the intensive care unit and a heightened likelihood of in-hospital mortality [2]. The resulting patient harm could reduce global economic growth by 0.7% per year, with indirect harm costs amounting to trillions of dollars per year worldwide [1]. One of the common causes of adverse events is medical errors [3,4]. For example, the number of medication errors in England exceeds 237 million every year [5]. Unsafe care is an important cause of adverse events and medical errors. Interprofessional teamwork and effective collaborative practices among healthcare professionals are important guarantees for providing patients with safe and high-quality care [6–8]. It has been estimated that about 70% of adverse events are due to a lack of communication and collaboration [9]. Miscommunication between healthcare professionals accounts for 80% of all serious medical mistakes worldwide [10].

Interprofessional education is an important and prioritized method to build collaboration competencies and promote effective collaboration among interprofessional teams, which involves engaging participants from two or more professions in either educational or work-based settings to learn team-based skills, acquire knowledge or skills in a specific area, and improve attitudes towards interprofessional learning and collaboration [11–13]. In medical settings, interprofessional education interventions can effectively improve the ability of healthcare professionals to work as a team, respect and appreciate each other’s unique and complementary roles in the healthcare team, and foster shared values of collaboration [14,15], which ultimately enables effective communication with patients and contributes to a safer healthcare environment [16]. Due to the alignment between the goals of interprofessional education interventions and the current global priorities for safe and patient-centered healthcare, interprofessional education in the context of patient safety has received a great deal of attention [12]. For instance, Bolore et al. [17] conducted interprofessional simulation training for physicians, nurses and nursing assistants to improve their safety management abilities of patients with rapid clinical deterioration. An interprofessional education of medication safety program for physicians, nurses and pharmacists in Irajpour et al.’s study was used to reduce the number of medication errors in multiple stages of the medication process [18].

Although several systematic reviews have summarized and evaluated the effectiveness of interprofessional education interventions for healthcare professionals and students in recent years [11,12,19–22], none have focused on the application of interprofessional education in improving patient safety. For example, the latest by Shuyi et al. [12] assessed the effectiveness of interprofessional education on educational outcomes based on the modified Kirkpatrick’s Model among nursing and medical professionals and students, but most of the included studies lacked evaluation of related patient safety outcomes. Therefore, the status of research on interprofessional education interventions for patient safety is still unclear. It is necessary to summarize the research progress in this field. However, published reviews have revealed that related outcomes of patient safety were scarcely reported in studies investigating the impact of interprofessional education among undergraduates from nursing and medical disciplines [19–21]. Paying attention to studies about interprofessional education interventions for healthcare professionals working in a real clinical setting is particularly important, which include the current workforce and advanced learners like residents [11,12].

Given the above, the purpose of this scoping review was to identify and synthesize the present status of interprofessional education interventions for healthcare professionals to improve patient safety. The specific research questions in our scoping review were as follows: (1) What are the characteristics of interprofessional education interventions for patient safety? (2) What outcomes related to patient safety are of concern in current studies? (3) How is teamwork performance evaluated? The results of this review are essential since they will determine what is known about interprofessional education interventions for improving patient safety and provide a basis for further research.

Methods

The present scoping review was carried out in accordance with the framework and principles put forth by Arksey and O’Malley [23] and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Reviewer’s Manual for scoping reviews [24]. A scoping review serves to delineate key concepts in existing studies through a comprehensive examination of the literature, and is commonly employed to address concerns and identify gaps in knowledge and practice within a specific research domain [25]. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) was utilized [26]. The protocol for this scoping review was not registered.

Search strategy

A preliminary search was conducted in PubMed to ascertain pertinent search terms related to ‘healthcare professionals’, ‘interprofessional education’ and ‘patient safety’ in the title and abstract. Subsequently, a search strategy incorporating subject words and text words was devised primarily for Medline (via PubMed) and then adjusted for the remaining five databases. The ultimate search terms and strategy were formulated based on the Population-Concept-Context framework and subjected to review by a methodologist possessing substantial expertise in evidence-based nursing. A systematic search of the literature was carried out across six databases, including Medline (via PubMed), Embase, Cochrane Library, CINAHL (via EBSCO), Scopus and Web of Science. The search was conducted on 30 August 2023 without imposing any restrictions on the initial publication date. And this search was repeated on 20 December 2023 to identify any relevant literature published after the initial search date. Additionally, the first author manually examined the reference lists of related systematic reviews to identify potential articles. There was no gray literature search. Gray literature refers to a report that is not published in a traditional commercial publication such as government reports, conference proceedings, dissertations and unpublished clinical trials [27]. The specific search terms and strategy employed for each database can be found in Supplementary Table S1.

Inclusion criteria

The primary inclusion criteria were determined based on the JBI Population-Concept-Context framework [24]. We included studies that met the following criteria. (1) Population: healthcare professionals working in any type of medical institution. (2) Concept: any interprofessional education interventions focused on improving patient safety. The outcomes must be clearly related to patient safety, including healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitude or practice about improving patient safety, adverse events (e.g., infection rate, fall rate, mortality) or medical errors (e.g., medication errors). (3) Context: studies carried out in all types of medical institutions irrespective of clinical setting. (4) Study design: intervention studies utilizing randomized controlled trials or quasi-experimental designs. (5) Publishing language: Studies published in English were eligible.

Studies meeting any of the following criteria were excluded: (1) addressing irrelevant topics; (2) lacking full-text availability; (3) duplicate or secondary publications on the same sample; (4) classified as technical reports, guidelines, letters, editorials, protocols, or reviews; and (5) nonpeer-reviewed articles such as government reports and conference abstracts.

Study selection

Prior to the screening process, all the search results were imported and consolidated utilizing EndNote X9. After automated elimination of duplicate studies, the titles and abstracts of the remaining studies were evaluated by two independent researchers adhering to the predetermined inclusion criteria. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion, and any unresolved conflicts were taken to a third researcher for further resolution. Studies that satisfied the inclusion criteria were then subjected to full-text screening. The methodology used in the full-text study screening process was consistent with that used for screening the title and abstract.

Methodological quality assessment

The JBI Critical Appraisal Tool for Quasi-Experimental Studies [24]was applied to assess the methodological quality of each quasi-experimental study included in the scoping review. Comprising nine items, this tool facilitated the rating of each checklist item as ‘Yes’, ‘No’, ‘Unclear’ or ‘Not Applicable’. The appraisal process was performed independently by two researchers, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with a third researcher. To ensure consistency in the appraisal and domains assessed, a pilot test involving three studies was conducted. The methodological quality of each study was assessed by calculating the percentage of items that met the criteria, with a score of 100% indicating the highest methodological quality (see Supplementary Table S2). A study with a checklist score of at least 60% was deemed to have a low risk of bias [12]. It should be noted that evaluating the methodological quality of the included studies is not obligatory in scope reviews [24,28], but this action can help us judge the distribution of high-quality evidence across interprofessional education interventions and provide more precise direction for future research.

Data extraction and synthesis

We created a preliminary data extraction form in Excel through group discussion and improved it by randomly selecting three studies for testing. Then two researchers jointly conducted the data extraction process, with each researcher’s extracted content cross-checked. The data extraction form encompassed the author, year of publication, the country where the study was conducted, study design, population, setting, specific department, sample size, percentage of individuals who were trained, characteristics and implementation components of the interventions (such as aim, delivery method, learning strategy, theory framework, detailed description, provider, frequency and duration), comparator, attrition rate, outcomes and directions of effect.

To get a broader and more comprehensive view of the data from the included articles, a narrative synthesis of the articles was carried out. The analysis was conducted by the first author and the research team achieved consensus on all facets of the narrative synthesis, resolving any disagreements through discussion and data review. The results were presented using appropriate tables.

Results

Search results of included studies

A comprehensive search across six databases and reference lists of relevant systematic reviews yielded a total of 1577 records. After removing 679 duplicates, 845 records were excluded by title and abstract, and 43 records were eliminated on the basis of full-text screening. Ultimately, this scoping review included thirteen studies. The screening process is depicted in Figure 1.

Flow diagram of studies selecting.

Characteristics of the included studies

The included studies were published between 2001 and 2023. The studies were conducted in seven countries, including the United States (n = 6), Switzerland (n = 2), Australia, Denmark, England, Iran and Malaysia (n = 1 from each country). All studies included in this review were quasi-experimental studies, of which nine studies were controlled before-and-after design and four studies were non-randomized concurrent controlled design. Except for one study conducted in a multiple setting involving hospitals, mental health service providers and individual general practices [29], the other studies were conducted in hospital settings. Four studies were conducted in multiple departments [30–33], and four and two studies involved emergency [30,33–35] and intensive care unit settings [18,36], respectively. The healthcare professionals recruited in six studies [18,29,30,34,37,38] included not only doctors and nurses, but also other roles, such as radiation technologists [37], faculty [30], clinical pharmacists [18] and allied health practitioners [38]. Excluding two studies for which the sample size was not reported, the study sample sizes ranged from 28 to 1058 (median 61, interquartile range 50–116). The percentage of individuals receiving training in an environment ranged from 50% to 100% in six studies that reported this data [18,30,31,34,35,38]. Six studies reported sample loss during the follow-up process, with sample attrition rates ranging from 3.27% to 50% [17,29,32,33,38,39]. Critical appraisal results

In terms of the methodological quality of the included studies, the percentage score of items that met the criteria varied from 75% to 88.9%, indicating a low risk of bias for the included studies. The main methodological deficiencies of the included studies were found in the comparability of treatments other than the intervention of interest, the absence of an independent control group and the completeness of follow-up (see Supplementary Figure S1). Specifically, three non-randomized concurrent controlled studies did not report whether the control group received similar treatment except for the intervention of interest [32,34,36], four studies lacked sufficient information to determine whether follow-up was complete [30,31,36,37], and six studies failed to offer details about the strategies used to address incomplete follow up [17,29,32,33,38,39]. More quality assessment results were presented in Supplementary Table S2.

Synthesis of results

Characteristics and implementation components of interprofessional education interventions

The interprofessional education interventions were designed to advance the understanding or management of a patient safety issue in six studies [18,29,31,33,36,37], to advance the understanding of collaboration in three studies [30,32,34], and to advance both aspects simultaneously in four studies [17,35,38,39]. In addition to two studies that did not report on delivery methods for interprofessional education [29,34], the remaining 10 studies used a face-to-face method. For example, Slater et al. [29] only reported that online learning modules and Team Web site were employed for interprofessional team learning, but the delivery method of its workshops was unclear. Most studies used simulation-based learning strategies [17,30–33,35,37,39], especially Bursiek et al. [39] and Saraswathy et al. [33] reported that high-fidelity simulation was involved. Other four studies adopted clinical-based learning strategies [18,29,34,36] and one study employed case-based learning strategies [38]. Theoretical frameworks, such as TeamSTEPPS Framework [30,32], Hospital Culture of Patient Safety Framework [39], Harden’s ‘ten steps’ principle for planning curriculum [31] and Smith’s principles of patient safety [35], were applied during interprofessional education interventions in nine studies.

In addition, the specific implementation process of interprofessional education interventions varied among studies. For example, the interprofessional simulation training by Bursiek et al. [39] started with a briefing, followed by simulation training for two scenarios that involved interprofessional teamwork and patient falls, and a debriefing session was required after each scenario. The interprofessional simulation training in the study by Ackerman et al. [37] was conducted after an interactive lecture. No studies have reported the training of intervention providers. The total duration of interprofessional education intervention ranged from 30 minutes to 20 weeks, and the training in four studies was performed only once [17,30–32]. Six studies and three studies did not report on the frequency [29,31,33–35,38] or total duration of intervention [34,35,37], respectively. The range of group sizes in five studies was two to ten, and eight studies did not report on their group size [29,31,34,36–39]. Related outcomes of patient safety

The existing studies of interprofessional education interventions have mainly concentrated on two themes related to patient safety outcomes, which included improving healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitude or practice about patient safety issues (n = 8) [17,18,29,30,33,35,37,40] and decreasing the rate of patient safety incidents involving adverse events (n = 6) [17,18,30,31,39,40] and medical errors (n = 2) [32,36]. There were different subtopics of the healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitude or practice about patient safety issues in the current studies. The main examples contained healthcare professionals’ patient safety culture [29,32], knowledge of local anaesthetic systemic toxicity signs, symptoms, management strategies and priorities [37], staff awareness of patients at risk on general wards [31], knowledge, attitude and practice of hospital-acquired infection control [33]. Adverse events mainly included patient fall rates [32,38,39], mortality [30,31,36], complication rates [30], infection rates [36] and incidence of pressure ulcers [32]. For medical errors, the study of Irajpour et al. [18] focused on reducing medication errors, and the study of Morey et al. [34] involved any clinical errors.

All the studies measured patient safety outcomes at multiple time points before and after the intervention, with three studies reporting follow-up at 3 months [17,32,33]. In terms of the effect of interprofessional education intervention on patient safety outcomes, only three studies found no statistical difference in the effect of the intervention [30–32]. The results of Slater et al. [29] showed that interprofessional education intervention was effective only in improving some dimensions of patient safety culture. Among the ten studies that concluded that interprofessional education was effective at improving patient safety outcomes, three studies described changes in outcome indicators but did not conduct statistical testing [29,37,38]. Evaluation of teamwork performance

As shown in Table 2, among the seven studies that explicitly identified the purpose of interprofessional education intervention to promote interprofessional collaboration [17,30,32,34,35,38,39], five studies reported on assessments of teamwork performance [17,30,32,34,39]. Teamwork performance in three studies was evaluated using a self-completed scale by healthcare professionals receiving interprofessional education intervention, including two subscales (working in teams with other healthcare professionals, and communicating effectively) of the Health Professional Education in Patient Safety Survey [17], the Mayo High Performance Teamwork Scale [39] and the TeamSTEPPS-Teamwork Perceptions Questionnaire [32]. The remaining two studies evaluated teamwork performance using observational tools. The Trauma Team Performance Observation Tool was utilized by trained evaluators to assess the performance of teams during each trauma resuscitation event in Capella et al.’s study [30]. And Morey et al. [34] adopted the Team Dimensions Rating Form to measure the quality of team behaviors, in which multidisciplinary team members were observed and scored by physician instructors and nurse instructors. Except for Kim’s study [32], four other studies found that interprofessional education intervention had a positive effect on improving team collaboration.

Discussion

The current scoping review provided a comprehensive summary of the concerns and gaps in intervention studies about interprofessional education for healthcare professionals to improve patient safety. Our findings indicated that interprofessional education interventions were primarily designed to advance the understanding or management of patient safety issues and to advance the understanding of collaboration. Although interprofessional education interventions involved in existing studies presented a variety of characteristics, face-to-face delivery methods and simulation-based learning strategies were widely used. The patient safety outcomes of interprofessional education interventions mainly included healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitude or practice about patient safety issues, and the incidence of adverse events and medical errors. Less than half of the studies assessed teamwork performance using mainly healthcare professionals’ self-evaluation and independent observers’ evaluation.

This review showed that the current forms and content of interprofessional education interventions for improving patient safety were flexible. This may be because priority patient safety issues vary depending on the specific clinical context [41,42]. In terms of the purpose of the intervention, although the original intention of interprofessional education was to improve cooperation ability, we found that six studies did not take improving teamwork ability as the purpose of their interventions [18,29,31,33,36,37]. This may lead to the neglect of the characteristics of different professional roles in the interprofessional education curriculum during the intervention, which may affect the training effect. Despite the rapid development of online learning [43], almost all of the included studies used a face-to-face delivery mode to implement training. This approach may provide the opportunity for direct dialogue and fulfill the complex needs related to social emotions in interprofessional education situations [44]. Moreover, simulation-based learning strategies were the most popular among these interprofessional education interventions. In simulation scenarios, interdisciplinary healthcare professionals receiving training can learn and get to know each other to improve teamwork and care quality in an environment that is risk-free and error-tolerant similar to clinical settings [45]. A variety of advanced simulated technologies were proven to be effective, including simulated avatars, mannequins, and virtual reality applications [46]. Besides, clinical-based learning strategies were also important, in which professionals discussed and addressed real-world safety issues [34,36]. Finally, it was reassuring that most interprofessional education interventions were designed and implemented under the guidance of relevant theoretical frameworks, and these systematic approaches made the interventions more scientific and the results more explainable [47]. TeamSTEPPS, for example, was an evidence-based resource designed to enhance communication and teamwork among healthcare professionals [48].

Most studies in our review found that interprofessional education intervention had a positive effect on improving diverse outcomes related to patient safety, primarily including healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitude or practice about patient safety issues, adverse events and medical errors. We defined the above outcome measures as those that were highly relevant to patient safety. According to Brasaite et al. [49], a key strategy for promoting a safe environment for patients was measuring and improving healthcare providers’ safety knowledge, attitude and practice. Reducing adverse events and medical errors is central to improving patient safety [50]. It was different from other reviews on the effectiveness of interprofessional education interventions, which have focused on educational outcomes [11,12] or extensive outcomes of healthcare quality [16]. Furthermore, there were differences in the evaluation of healthcare professionals’ knowledge, attitude or practice about patient safety. Some studies concentrated on promoting their ability to address specific patient safety issues, such as local anaesthetic systemic toxicity [37], hospital-acquired infection control [33] and safe sedation procedures in the emergency department [35]. While other studies focused on improving patient safety capabilities in general, often assessed by broad surveys of patient safety culture or patient safety knowledge [17,29,32]. And more than a third of the studies did not assess the impact of interprofessional education intervention on more objective outcomes regarding patient safety, involving adverse events or medical errors. These safety outcomes can easily be evaluated using routine medical data within a sufficient follow-up period [51–53]. However, there was no definitive conclusion with regard to the effect duration of interprofessional education interventions on these patient safety outcomes. For instance, Morey et al. [34] reported that their intervention had a lasting positive effect in large part over the eight months following training. The study of Fuhrmann et al. [31] did not detect the intervention effect of interprofessional education on reducing 30-day and 180-day mortality. Finally, the findings of existing studies indicated that interprofessional education interventions for patient safety may not consistently yield satisfactory outcomes, despite the acceptable methodological quality of the included studies. The possible reason might be that the main purpose of interprofessional education is to teach teamwork skills and develop collaboration competencies. Without a large-scale training participant and training that is specific to a specific safety issue, the impact may be diminished, especially on actual safety events.

Additionally, teamwork performance evaluation was described as evaluating the cognitions, attitudes and behaviors of team members based on clearly operationalized criteria through standardized tools [54,55]. This review found that more than half of the studies did not evaluate teamwork performance after the interprofessional education intervention. This may affect the interpretation of the mechanisms by which interprofessional education interventions improve patient safety, in which improvements in teamwork ability and performance were regarded as significant mediating factors [14,15,56]. For example, when three studies reported improvements in teamwork performance, the patient safety outcomes they focused on also improved [17,34,39]. Here, we also identified several interesting findings. Capella et al. [30] found that the complication rate and mortality of patients did not decrease with improvements in teamwork performance. A study by Kim [32] did not detect the effectiveness of interprofessional education in improving teamwork performance or patient safety outcomes. This could be due to the varying quality of interprofessional education interventions across studies. Moreover, in terms of the methods used to evaluate teamwork performance, research showed that both self-evaluation of healthcare professionals and observer-based evaluation were reliable [57], and that the choice of methods was determined by accessibility and resource availability. Some emerging tools to appraise teamwork performance, such as peer evaluation of team performance and group- or team-objective structured clinical evaluation [58,59], are still not sufficiently applied in team performance evaluation of interprofessional education events incorporating the topic of patient safety.

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive scoping review to summarize the existing intervention studies about interprofessional education interventions for healthcare professionals to improve patient safety. The findings of our review offer some insight into the research status of interprofessional education interventions towards patient safety. We also evaluated the methodological quality of the included studies, which was conducive to better understanding the quality of intervention studies in this area, in order to provide a reference and basis for future high-quality research. There are also limitations in this scoping review. First, despite our efforts to search the most pertinent databases and reference lists of important reviews, only English-language articles were included and gray literature was excluded. There is a possibility that some relevant studies may have been missed. However, there were fifteen studies included in a newly published systematic review involving interprofessional education interventions for medical and nursing professionals and students [12], while we identified thirteen intervention studies about patient safety. And no decisions or recommendations for the effectiveness of the results were made in this review. Hence, this impact seems to be bearable. Second, the concepts of patient safety and quality of care were often confused in related research [60]. We focused only on outcomes that were closely related to patient safety, which may also be inconsistent with the definitions of patient safety outcomes in some studies.

Implications for future research

To advance future research, three recommendations based on current research findings are made. First, the focus of future research is likely to be on simulation-based interprofessional education, and interprofessional educational interventions aimed at improving patient safety should be designed with a clear purpose of enhancing collaborative capacity, while also selecting appropriate theoretical frameworks. Second, more high-quality interventional studies are needed to examine the effect of interprofessional education interventions on patient safety, especially randomized controlled trials. And future research could apply patient safety indicators objectively measured in conjunction with assessments of the knowledge, attitudes and practices of healthcare professionals, and evaluate the effect duration of interprofessional education intervention for patient safety. Third, future research should select appropriate tools to evaluate teamwork performance in order to better explore and explain the internal relationships between interprofessional education interventions and related outcomes of patient safety.

Conclusions

In this scoping review, we identified thirteen intervention studies and summarized the current state of knowledge about interprofessional education intervention for healthcare professionals to improve patient safety. The results showed that the characteristics of the current interprofessional education interventions were diversified, and there was high interest in simulation-based learning strategies and face-to-face delivery methods in these interprofessional education interventions. Some studies lacked assessments of more objective outcomes of patient safety involving adverse events or medical errors. Fewer than half of the studies examined teamwork performance, mainly relying on the self-evaluation of healthcare professionals and observer-based evaluation. Overall, more high-quality intervention studies on this topic are needed, especially those with a randomized controlled trial design. The areas of focus and improvement in future research may include facilitating simulation-based interprofessional education intervention, increasing the observation of more objective outcomes of patient safety and selecting appropriate tools to assess teamwork performance.
