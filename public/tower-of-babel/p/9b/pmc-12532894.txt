Equitable mosaic: the ideal for medical pluralism
Authors: Lakshmi K Josyula
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This commentary is a reflection, through a health policy and systems lens, on the medically pluralistic workforce, particularly in low- and middle-income countries that have and utilise numerous systems of medicine, indigenous and adopted. It analyses and distinguishes integration and pluralism, and examines the interaction, and the more frequently observed lack thereof, among different systems of medicine. It highlights the implicit and express hierarchies among the different systems of medicine; the epistemic injustices, wrongs, and resultant harms to different systems of medicine, their practitioners, and the populations that could benefit from them; concomitant inequities in the administration and functioning of the workforce; and the gaps in coordination among diverse disciplines in health care. The commentary underscores the imperative for thoughtful and equitable administration of pluralistic health systems, including emic and etic enquiry, sensitisation, and participatory action, to accomplish the diverse goals of health systems, the health workforce, and the population.

Every society has in its health repertoire several systems of medicine, indigenous, imported, improvised, or imposed. This de facto medical pluralism has made the journey to de jure medical pluralism in many regions of the world [1], albeit with gaps in comprehensiveness and equity. Some countries’ health systems officially endorse and administer only one system of medicine; others support multiple systems in a hierarchy; and yet others govern multiple systems in no official hierarchy, but often tacit inequality [2, 3].

Health systems are understood to be complex adaptive entities, social systems embedded in social and political contexts, and shaped by human values, individual and collective [4]. A foundational framework in health policy and systems research describes health systems as constituted of ‘hardware’ and ‘software’: [5, 6] Health system hardware refers to the concrete components of health systems, such as service delivery structures, finance, medical products, information systems, organisation of human resources, and governance systems. Software refers to subtle but significant components, such as ideas, interests, values, norms, relationships, and power, which underpin and shape the decisions made on policy and practice, the day-to-day running of health systems, and the prospects for the future of health systems, particularly the promotion or culling of certain services and modalities [5]. Some researchers subdivide health systems software into tangible elements such as committees and protocols, and intangible elements such as power relations and social hierarchies [7, 8].

This commentary critically examines medical pluralism from a health policy and systems research perspective, specifically in terms of the interactions among the hardware and software elements of the health system and their interplay in the implementation of medically pluralistic practice. It considers the experiences and evidence of bilateral influences and impacts of health system software and hardware on the establishment of medically pluralistic practice, and calls for greater focus and action on the values [4] underlying policy and practice decisions in medical pluralism, specifically equity in the formulation of policy, promotion of research, and implementation of practice.

Many systems of medicine in many hierarchies

Allopathy (Western medicine or biomedicine) is the mainstream system of medicine in most of the world, and exerts hegemony over myriad traditional, complementary, and alternative systems of medicine (TCAM), and local health traditions (LHT), as demonstrated in allopathy’s more favoured position in health system governance, funding, and promotion, and the higher status of its practitioners in society [2, 9–13]. Nevertheless, non-allopathic systems of medicine, indigenous or adopted, whether formal and codified, or informal and transmitted organically, are part of all cultures.

The co-existence of diverse systems of medicine is the reality of the past, present, and future. However, pluralistic health systems seldom view different systems of medicine with an egalitarian lens. Allopathy usually occupies the mainstream, enjoying labels such as scientific, modern, and evidence-based; TCAM systems often receive tokenistic consideration, or are constrained to field challenges to their credibility in contests that use criteria unsuited to their tenets and modalities; and local health traditions are painted with the same brush as ethnic arts and crafts that at best supplement disease prevention and health promotion efforts, and at worst confound robust healthcare, and so are frequently overlooked as systems of health. Further, numerous non-allopathic systems of medicine are denied their individuality, too often confused for one another, believed to have similar or the same world views and conceptions of human health, and accorded support, if any, in an inequitable manner.

With successive waves of geopolitical movements, related especially to colonisation and commerce, systems of medicine have spread, with cuisines, languages, and arts, to places distant from their origins, and sometimes assimilated local influences, been edited or expanded, and developed into transformed versions in their new homes. Over time, the tenuousness of their moorings in the context and philosophy of their origin; the fallouts of vexed political, economic, and administrative processes; [14, 15] and the influence of societal trends, could fray the fabric of these bodies of knowledge. The erosion of diverse systems of medicine poses the threat of losses of cultural wealth in addition to the more discernible forfeitures of health markers and population indices of well-being.

While there is a consciousness among governments and transnational entities that diverse systems of medicine should be conserved alongside allopathy, particularly traditional systems with centuries of empirical evidence if not laboratory testing to support their use, the translation of this consciousness to policies and practice for a medically pluralistic society is inadequate.

Pluralistic health systems—the salad–smoothie spectrum

The history of the establishment of so-called pluralistic health systems [2, 9] is too often one of the exercise and contestations of unequal political power, philosophical hegemonies, the predilections of influential individuals at critical junctures, and tokenism displayed in the inclusion without equity of non-allopathic systems of medicine.

There is diversity even in conceptions of the coming together of diverse systems of medicine. Boon and colleagues [16] developed a framework to understand and describe the spectrum of coexistence of CAM with conventional allopathic medicine in patient care delivered by teams of professionals, with inverse variation in the degree of autonomy and application of the philosophies of the different systems of medicine with the level of collaboration and collective decision-making within the therapeutic team. At one end of the spectrum is ‘parallel practice’, i.e., discrete applications of different systems of medicine, and at the other end what is called ‘integrative practice’, i.e., a blend of different systems of medicine. These two poles are (i) pluralism that can be analogized to a salad bowl with each component maintaining its distinct character and juxtaposed with other components; and (ii) integration that can be analogized to a smoothie with all its components blended and ceding their distinct characters to the character of the resultant that they all contribute to.

Pluralistic health systems in the different countries of the world show this diversity: [2] Examples are China that has three systems—Traditional Chinese Medicine, allopathic, and integrated; Korea that has two— traditional Korean and allopathic, with allopathic having higher power; Bhutan with traditional and allopathic practice, pharmacy, and research established at all levels of healthcare facilities with guidelines for cross-referral and collaboration; Cuba with all systems endorsed by the government being taught to and practised by all medical graduates, i.e., every doctor receiving education in all the systems supported by the government; and India with the allopathic system governed by the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, and seven systems of TCAM being governed by a parallel ministry, the Ministry of AYUSH (for the administration of Ayurveda, Yoga, Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, and Homoeopathy) that was initially a department in the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.

Medical pluralism may be implemented as a motley collection of practitioners trained in different systems of medicine practising a hybridised system. Such a hybridised system, which results from reductive pruning of various systems of medicine to particular medications or therapeutic techniques without regard to the tenets underpinning them [17] hurts the integrity of individual systems of medicine in the effort to integrate them. Sometimes the choice of combination of therapeutic techniques and materials may be left to a practitioner trained, usually partially, in more than one system of medicine, to practise what is deprecatingly referred to as ‘mixopathy’ [18]. The tools of a few systems of medicine may be selected to be used in parallel or in series, with unfortunate and utter neglect of the philosophy and conceptualisation of human health and disease that motivates those tools. And when the desired health outcomes are not achieved with such ill-judged application, the blame is customarily placed squarely at the door of the systems of medicine that are non-mainstream, and susceptible to labels such as ‘unscientific’, and unsupported by rigorous studies, therefore not ‘evidence-based’. Too often, the coming together of multiple systems of medicine is the force-fitting of disparate elements into a combination as a solution to particular population health concerns, such as anaemia or outbreaks of certain diseases. In contexts that do not attempt to develop a single system of medicine from multiple systems, the different systems of medicine are often reported to languish in uneasy, unproductive co-existence [10–12] with the health system unable to harness the diversity of expertise among the practitioners to achieve population health goals, and the non-mainstream systems of medicine faced with decimation.

Systems of medicine—bodies of knowledge and praxis

The different systems have their unique origins, histories, and philosophies, and modalities, techniques, and therapeutic tools that may be called upon in disease prevention, health protection, maximising functioning, and treating and managing disease. Evaluating or attempting to use particular aspects of a system of medicine in a reductive manner without consideration of the tenets underlying the prescription may be successful by fluke, often simply ineffective, and sometimes actually harmful [17].

The philosophies of different systems of medicine may align with one another, such as with Siddha and Ayurveda; not obviously clash with one another, such as with chiropractic and naturopathy; or be opposed to one another, such as with homoeopathy and allopathy. Nevertheless, the simultaneous practice of different systems of medicine can be governed without the need for the philosophies of the systems of medicine to be uniform, as long as a basic framework of autonomy and equity is adhered to. In fact, attempts to force-fit systems of medicine together lead to the trimming off, and thereby loss, of problematic or controversial, often quintessential, elements of each system, in order to yield a wieldy, compound system that is purported to feature all the best aspects of the component systems of medicine.

Systems of medicine are ways of perceiving and interacting with the world to optimise human health. They may be regarded and treated as different languages and different religious faiths co-existing in space are—distinct paths, with individual world-views, to the same destination. The therapeutic techniques in the diverse systems of medicine need to be interpreted in accordance with these world views, rather than forced into academic dockets dictated by the conventions of allopathic medicine.

Epistemic hegemonies and discrimination

Even well-intentioned examinations of different systems of medicine with a view to useful harnessing of their benefits for the public weal can fall short of its ideals: The relative strengths and shortfalls of any body of knowledge can be truly understood only by a combination of emic and etic inquiry, or both the insider and outsider views. The absence of the emic view, and, in fact, the overwhelming presence of the dominant allopathic philosophy even in the presence of an emic view, lead to defaulting to the dominant framework to explicate a non-mainstream system, an exercise that is always doomed to inadequacy, and often to misinterpretation and gaps in information, with potentially disastrous ramifications for the non-mainstream systems of medicine. The sensemaking tools of allopathic medicine may be entirely unsuited to, or inadequate for TCAM, with the unfortunate outcome that the non-mainstream systems come up short in laying out facts acceptable as evidence. An instance of the scrutiny of evidence to support a system of medicine is in the review of the literature: If only large, controlled studies (preferably randomised) involving biochemical and pathological laboratory testing, published in particular academic journals, qualify as high-quality evidence, it is all too easy to declare that there is little to no evidence of TCAM systems, notwithstanding the centuries and generations of experience of the value of various systems. Practitioners of TCAM systems who succumb to the pressure to prove their systems using the instruments of allopathic medicine per force distort their system’s approach to health and disease, hamstring their treatment in the process, and unsurprisingly seldom come out successful in demonstrating their system’s success in restoring health. The basic demonstration of equity in medical pluralism is to evaluate each system in line with its philosophies and using its own standards. In line with this approach, the need to transcend epistemic confines and convene transdisciplinary, participatory research and evaluation teams in service of population health is great [19].

Epistemic exclusion and injustice, both testimonial and hermeneutical (interpretive) as described by Fricker [14], and explored further by Bhakuni and Abimbola [15], are the causes and the consequences of an iniquitous approach to plural medical systems. Lower consequence and credibility are attributed to disadvantaged groups (non-mainstream systems and their practitioners), thereby trapping them, their knowledge, and their power in a subordinate position in the scheme of systems of medicine. Simultaneously, an excess of credibility may be granted to certain bodies of knowledge owing to powerful advocates, exacerbating the gulf between the underserved dominated and the undeserved dominant groups. Today’s world, with its multiplicity of health and disease conditions, exposures, and triggers; old, new, and resurgent communicable and non-communicable diseases and injuries; and array of systems of medicine to address these conditions, necessitates awareness of the various systems of medicine in practice—awareness among practitioners to base medical advice and referral on, and awareness in the general population to seek healthcare recourse. Such awareness is essential to the establishment of a pluralistic health system which assures equity for diverse medical practitioners, and access to patients. Epistemic wrongs, and harms arising from them, are based in prejudice against particular systems of medicine and their practitioners, and generated by the exercise of social power by particular agents, as well as structural power dispersed in social systems [20]. Proactive and concerted action is essential to dismantle the epistemic wrongs that abound in the arena of medically pluralistic health systems, to facilitate emic and etic enquiry, sensitization of knowers within and outside each body of knowledge, development of knowledge repositories, and orientation to the optimal techniques of examination and evaluation of each system of medicine.

Towards a thriving pluralistic health system

At present, the goals envisioned in the establishment of a pluralistic health system fall far short of accomplishment, with direct implications for three of the six building blocks of health systems, in the World Health Organization framework: [21] the health workforce, service delivery, and leadership and governance. There is a growing realisation among public health researchers and practitioners of the critical importance of inclusivity, equity, and participatory planning and action to ensure impact, acceptability, and sustainability of health interventions. For the edifice of a pluralistic health system to be true to its stated goals—to achieve population health outcomes, to place within the reach of people the array of systems of medicine to harness for the realisation of their health and well-being aspirations, to provide practitioners of different systems of medicine a platform to exercise their expertise and contribute to public good—and for the individual epistemic entities to flourish, the foundational requirements are respect and trust among proponents of the diverse systems of medicine, distinct bodies of knowledge and philosophies that developed in varied contexts and different points in history. Essential to this are the exercise of intellectual openness and mutual awareness, ideally through formal education involving sensitisation to (without extending to training in) all the systems of medicine in practice in each context. The information context of policymakers, implementers, medical practitioners, and persons seeking healthcare needs to be improved, for which power relations need to be reconfigured to be egalitarian. An equitable, productive, vibrant pluralistic health system should also not place on particular non-mainstream systems of medicine the onus of proving themselves by the criteria developed for other systems. The contributions and the strengths of the diverse systems of medicine, many of which are ways of life rather than narrow protocols of healthcare, need to be appreciated. The systems of medicine need to be treated as dynamic, interacting social systems, an equitable mosaic rather than a homogenised composite.
