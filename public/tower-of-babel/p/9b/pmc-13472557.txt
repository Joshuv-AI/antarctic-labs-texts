Audit and Evaluation of the Effect of Fresh Frozen Plasma and Adjunct Pharmacological Agents (Vitamin K1, Prothrombin Complex Concentrate, Tranexamic Acid, and Recombinant Factor VIIa) on Pre- and Post- Transfusion INR in Patients with Coagulopathy: A Prospective Study
Authors: Grazy Chinnamuthu, Gowtham Ganapathy, P Padmavathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Coagulopathy is frequently encountered in patients with liver disease, trauma, anticoagulant therapy, and perioperative bleeding. Fresh Frozen Plasma (FFP) is widely used to correct coagulation abnormalities associated with elevated International Normalized Ratio (INR). However, plasma alone may not always provide optimal correction. Adjunct pharmacological agents such as vitamin K1 (phytonadione), prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC), tranexamic acid (TXA), and Recombinant Factor VIIa (rFVIIa) are increasingly used to improve coagulation outcomes.Objective:To evaluate the effectiveness of FFP transfusion and the role of adjunct pharmacological therapy in correcting elevated INR values.Materials and Methods:A prospective observational study was conducted in a tertiary care teaching hospital over a period of 1 year. Adult patients (≥18 years) receiving FFP transfusion for elevated INR (>1.5) due to bleeding or prophylaxis before invasive procedures were included (n = 120). Pre-transfusion INR values were compared with post-transfusion INR measured within 6–12 hours. Data on concomitant administration of vitamin K1, PCC, TXA, and rFVIIa were recorded. Exclusion criteria included congenital coagulation disorders, incomplete records, and out-of-hospital transfusions. Statistical analysis was performed using paired t-test and ANOVA for subgroup comparisons (P < 0.05 is significant).Results:The mean pre-transfusion INR was 3.15 ± 1.20, decreasing significantly to 1.96 ± 0.82 post-transfusion (P < 0.001). Subgroup analysis showed greater INR reduction with adjuncts: FFP + PCC (1.78 ± 0.60), FFP + vitamin K1 (1.32 ± 0.52), FFP + rFVIIa (1.65 ± 0.55), and FFP + TXA (1.05 ± 0.48) compared to FFP alone (0.80 ± 0.45; P < 0.01). Minor transfusion reactions occurred in 3.3% of patients.Conclusion:FFP significantly reduces INR in coagulopathy, with adjuncts like vitamin K1 and PCC enhancing correction. Rational integration of blood components and pharmacology optimizes outcomes and transfusion practices.

INTRODUCTION

Coagulopathy remains a critical challenge in patients with liver disease, trauma, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), and anticoagulant therapy. The international normalized ratio (INR) serves as a key marker for extrinsic pathway dysfunction and guides therapeutic interventions.[1]

Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) replenishes multiple coagulation factors (II, V, VII, VIII, IX, X, XI, XIII) and fibrinogen, making it a cornerstone for managing elevated INR before procedures or during hemorrhage.[2] Common etiologies include warfarin-induced anticoagulation, where reversal often combines FFP with targeted agents.

Emerging adjuncts include:

Despite benefits, FFP risks include allergic reactions, transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), and transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO). Auditing practices and evaluating adjuncts are vital to minimize overuse and improve efficacy.

This study assesses FFP’s impact on INR and the additive value of adjuncts in a real-world cohort.

AIM OF THE STUDY

The primary objectives of this study were to evaluate the effectiveness of fresh frozen plasma (FFP) transfusion in correcting elevated INR levels, to compare pre- and post-transfusion INR values, to assess the additional impact of adjunct pharmacological agents (vitamin K1, prothrombin complex concentrate [PCC], tranexamic acid [TXA], and recombinant factor VIIa [rFVIIa]) on INR correction, and to audit the rational and appropriate use of plasma transfusion in clinical practice.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This prospective observational study was conducted in the Department of Transfusion Medicine at a tertiary care teaching hospital over a 12-month period. The study included 120 adult patients (≥18 years) with elevated INR (>1.5) who received fresh frozen plasma (FFP) transfusion for active bleeding or prophylaxis before invasive procedures. Patients with congenital coagulation disorders, incomplete laboratory records, or transfusions administered outside the hospital were excluded.

Data collected included patient demographics (age and gender), clinical indication for FFP transfusion, baseline and post-transfusion INR values (measured 6–12 h after transfusion), number of FFP units transfused (typically 10–15 mL/kg), concomitant administration of adjunct pharmacological agents (vitamin K1, PCC, tranexamic acid, or recombinant factor VIIa), and any observed transfusion reactions.

PHARMACOLOGICAL THERAPY AND DOSAGE

In this study, adjunct pharmacological agents were administered alongside fresh frozen plasma (FFP) as per clinical indication. Vitamin K1 (phytonadione) 5–10 mg IV was used for warfarin reversal to enhance the synthesis of vitamin K-dependent factors (II, VII, IX, X). Prothrombin complex concentrate (PCC) 25–50 IU/kg IV provided rapid delivery of factors II, VII, IX, and X for anticoagulant reversal. Tranexamic acid (TXA) 1 g IV every 8 h was given as an antifibrinolytic to inhibit plasminogen activation in trauma and surgical bleeding. Recombinant factor VIIa (rFVIIa) 90 μg/kg IV was reserved for severe or refractory bleeding to enhance thrombin generation. FFP was transfused at 10–15 mL/kg to replace multiple clotting factors in patients with coagulopathy and elevated INR.

Statistical analysis

Data analyzed via SPSS v. 25. Continuous variables: mean ± SD. Paired t-test for INR changes; ANOVA for adjunct comparisons. P <0.05 is significant.

RESULTS

A total of 120 adult patients were enrolled in the study, with a mean age of 52.1 ± 14.8 years. The cohort comprised 72 males (60%) and 48 females (40%).

Indications for Plasma Transfusion Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) was transfused for various clinical indications [Table 1]. The most common indication was liver disease (38 patients, 31.7%), followed by preoperative correction of coagulopathy (30 patients, 25.0%), anticoagulant reversal (22 patients, 18.3%), trauma (18 patients, 15.0%), and other miscellaneous causes (12 patients, 10.0%).

Effect of Adjunct Pharmacological Therapy Subgroup analysis revealed that adjunct pharmacological agents enhanced the degree of INR correction compared to FFP alone [Table 3]. The mean INR reduction was 0.80 ± 0.45 in the FFP-alone group (n = 45). Greater reductions were observed with FFP combined with vitamin K1 (1.32 ± 0.52, n = 30, P < 0.01), PCC (1.78 ± 0.60, n = 25, P < 0.001), TXA (1.05 ± 0.48, n = 15, P < 0.05), and rFVIIa (1.65 ± 0.55, n = 5, P < 0.01). Overall, the combination of PCC with FFP produced the greatest INR correction (one-way ANOVA: F =12.45, P < 0.001).

Transfusion reactions

Minor transfusion reactions were observed in four patients (3.3%), including two allergic reactions (1.7%) and two febrile non-hemolytic reactions (1.7%). The remaining 116 patients (96.7%) experienced no reactions. No severe complications such as transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) or transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO) were reported.

DISCUSSION

FFP effectively lowered INR (mean reduction 1.19), aligning with Stanworth et al. who reported similar corrections in coagulopathy.[1] Adjuncts amplified effects: PCC’s rapid factor delivery outperformed others (Levy et al.), while vitamin K1 aided sustained synthesis (Franchini and Lippi).[34] TXA stabilized clots in trauma (Shakur et al.,), and rFVIIa was efficacious in select severe cases despite limited use.[5]

Integration reduced FFP volumes (mean 2.1 units/patient vs. historical 3+), curbing risks (Tinmouth et al.).[67] Limitations: Observational design; small rFVIIa subgroup. Future RCTs could validate in specific etiologies.

CONCLUSION

FFP corrects INR in coagulopathy, enhanced by vitamin K1 and PCC. Multimodal therapy optimizes hemostasis, safety, and resource use.
