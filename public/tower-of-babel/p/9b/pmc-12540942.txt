A novel method for selective adsorption of toxic H₂S gas using core–shell hybrid Nano adsorbent MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H₂S
Authors: Hassan Asilian Mahabadi, Rohadin Moradirad, Seyed Jamaleddin Shahtaheri, Alimorad Rashidi, Saeed Fakhraie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHydrogen sulfide (H2S) presents a significant challenge in the gas industry due to its high toxicity, corrosiveness, and environmental impacts through the formation of SO2 and acid rain during combustion. This study introduces a novel core–shell hybrid nanosorbent, MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S, which is specifically designed for the efficient adsorption of H2S through adsorption processes. The difference is that MIPs are selectively designed polymers with molecular cavities for specific target recognition, while NIPs are unprinted control polymers that exhibit non-specific binding. This research systematically investigated the optimization of operating parameters affecting adsorption efficiency through thirty carefully designed experiments. The Central Composite Design (CCD) method was utilized in the experiments. The key variables evaluated included adsorbent dosage (0.1–1 g), temperature (25–80 °C), H2S concentration (10–1000 ppm), and flow rate (40–100 mL/min). The molar adsorption capacity was determined using the Soave–Redlich–Kwong equation, while comprehensive material characterization was performed using FTIR, XRD, FE-SEM, and BET techniques. Statistical analysis revealed that the adsorption process followed a quadratic model, with temperature and adsorbent dosage as the primary variables affecting the performance. MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S exhibited superior adsorption efficiency (94.3%) and capacity (11 mg/g) compared to MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S (9.9%, 5.97 mg/g), which was attributed to the enhanced site pattern compatibility. Equilibrium adsorption studies confirmed the compliance with pseudo-second-order kinetic models and the Langmuir isotherm. The synthesized nanosorbent exhibited exceptional selectivity for H2S over competing gases such as CO2 and CH4 in mixed gas environments, demonstrating remarkable reliability and stability. This innovative hybrid material, combining the strengths of molecularly imprinted polymers and metal–organic frameworks in a core–shell structure, offers a promising solution for efficiently removing H2S from industrial gas streams, with potential applications across the energy sector where cleaner gas processing is required.Keywords: Optimization, Nanosorbent, MIL-101(Cr) @MIPs@H2S, Isotherm, Selectivity, Soave–Redlich–Kwong equationSubject terms: Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

As the world’s energy demands increase, natural gas is becoming the most practical fossil fuel choice. It is widely available, cost-effective, and has relatively low emissions. However, both natural gas and biogas contain significant impurities1–3 that, when combusted, produce environmental pollution and release harmful gases like NOx, SOx, and H2S4,5. H2S, with a toxicity limit of 10 ppm, poses a serious danger to humans due to its corrosive and acidic nature, especially for carbon steel used in oil and gas industry pipelines6,7. Therefore, there is an urgent need for efficient H2S removal or adsorption techniques8,9. Various methods such as adsorption, chemical conversion, and catalytic membranes have been employed to meet global environmental regulations10. Adsorbents are commonly used in processes like hydrogen purification, oxygen and nitrogen production, and air drying through adsorption11. However, existing traditional adsorbents lack the necessary properties to effectively eliminate gases, necessitating the development of new adsorbents with superior adsorption capabilities, cost efficiency, and selectivity12. This study focuses on a hybrid nanoadsorbent specifically designed to adsorb H2S gas efficiently. We have concentrated on molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) within metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), which have gained attention in recent years13. By creating porous hybrid nonadsorbent MIPs@MOFs in core–shell structure, we have enhanced thermal and water compatibility, increased adsorption rates, and improved stability due to their extensive surface areas. The specific surface area obtained is substantial14. MOFs find applications in chemical sensing, catalysis, drug delivery, and gas storage15. The proposed sorbent combines the strengths of MIPs and MOFs, providing specific recognition sites through molecular template polymers, abundant pores, a large surface area on MOFs, and easy separation, leading to rapid transfer rates and reduced extraction time16. In the field of H2S gas adsorption, the combination of the MIL-101(Cr) metal–organic framework with molecularly imprinted polymers (MIPs) and non-imprinted polymers (NIPs) has been developed as core–shell structures17. The core consists of MIL-101(Cr) in these structures, and the shell is a polymer17. In the “MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S” samples, the polymer shell is molecularly imprinted with H2S template molecules, creating specific and selective recognition sites for H2S adsorption. In contrast, in the “MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S” samples, the polymer shell is made without a template, lacks these specific sites, and is usually used as a control sample for performance comparison18. While various methodologies have been developed across different domains, the use of MIPs has emerged as a practical approach19,20. Some processes that involve breaking down H2S into H2 and S21 may not be economically viable for hydrogen production. Hence, it is crucial to reduce the presence of H2S particles in gases and pipelines to produce cleaner, more adaptable products19,20. Materials like silica nanoparticles, carbon nanotubes, and multi-walled magnetic nanoparticles have been used22,23 Although they have been used, MOFs outperform MIPs cores due to their massive surface area, uniform porosity, selectivity, and exceptional adsorption capacity24,25. The removal of H2S pollution from gas mixtures and streams21 is essential for creating cleaner and more versatile products. Optimizing key parameters is crucial for enhancing extraction efficiency and increasing the effectiveness of MIPs. Developing hybrid adsorbents allows for faster equilibration with analytes, a better surface-to-volume ratio, and improved binding efficiency and capacity through reduced sorbent usage26–29. Alternative methods like response surface methodology (RSM)30 have been used to study variable interactions and optimize parameters to reduce energy consumption and costs31. This study aims to enhance the operational parameters of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S gas adsorption, utilizing RSM as a statistical tool for optimization32

Synthesis of MIL-101(Cr) and MIL-101(Cr) @MIPs/NIPs@H2S

Synthesis of the adsorbent MIL-101(Cr)

An adsorbent was created using a hydrothermal method. 5 mmol of terephthalic acid and chromium nitrate were dissolved in 20 ml of deionized water at room temperature. The mixture was stirred for 15 min in a stainless steel autoclave and then heated to 218 °C for 18 h. Following this, the solution was cooled to room temperature and subsequently washed with acetone,deionized water, and methanol to eliminate any unreacted material. Subsequently, 20 mL of DMF solution was introduced to the powder, and the solution was maintained at 70 °C for 24 h to eliminate any remaining unreacted terephthalic acid. After three rinses with pure ethanol, the powders were dried at 100 °C overnight33,34.

Synthesis of MIL-101(Cr) @ MIPs/NIPs@H2S Composite

The synthesis of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S composites involves combining molecular imprinting polymerization with the hydrothermally synthesized MIL-101(Cr) metal–organic framework, which provides a high surface area (up to 3800 m2/g) and unsaturated chromium sites that enhance polymer adhesion and maintain structural integrity35,36. Initially, the template molecule (H2O) is dissolved in a DMF/toluene mixture, and methacrylic acid (MAA) is added to form a stable pre-polymerization complex through hydrogen bonding and dipole interactions, critical for imprinting efficiency35. MIL-101(Cr) is then introduced, followed by ethylene glycol dimethacrylate (EGDMA) as a cross-linker, which forms a rigid three-dimensional polymer network around the MOF core; the cross-linker amount is optimized to balance binding capacity and pore accessibility37. Azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN) initiates free radical polymerization under an inert atmosphere at 70 °C for 24 h to prevent oxygen inhibition and ensure polymer formation. After polymerization, Soxhlet extraction with acetic acid/methanol (20:80 v/v) removes the template molecules efficiently (> 95%), exposing selective binding cavities and minimizing nonspecific sites. The resulting core–shell MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@ H2S composite retains the crystalline MIL-101(Cr) structure with a 50–100 nm thick MIPs layer that exhibits high selectivity for water molecules and mechanical stability suitable for multiple reuse cycles. This synthesis approach optimizes monomer-to-template ratios and solvent conditions to maintain complex stability without dissolving the MOF core, enabling precise molecular recognition application38,39. Furthermore, MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S can be synthesized without adding H2O molecules. MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S resembles a molecular polymer40 See Fig. 1. Given H2S’s considerable instability and toxicity, H2O was selected as an alternative template for preparing MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S through a distinct molecular manipulation strategy41,42. This choice was made because H2O possesses the same molecular kinetic diameter and spatial structure as H2S43.

Experimental design and gas adsorption measurements

This study aimed to optimize the adsorption process of hydrogen sulfide gas using hybrid nanosorbents, specifically MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S and MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S, through a well-structured experimental design. In the initial stage, we identified the key variables that affect the adsorption process, including the amount of adsorbent (g), adsorption temperature (°C), initial H2S concentration (ppm), and gas flow rate (ml/min). Our goal was to achieve the adsorbent’s highest adsorption capacity and efficiency by optimizing these parameters.

Central composite design (CCD) in response surface methodology (RSM) was utilized to explore the interaction effects of variables and to identify the optimal conditions44,45. In this regard, 30 experiments with appropriate coverage of variable levels (low, medium, and high levels) were randomly planned to avoid systematic errors. A probability value (p) with 95% confidence is utilized to validate or invalidate the model parameters. Operation

Parameter

The hydrothermal method synthesized the MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S adsorbent using molecularly imprinted polymers in the preparation section. The fixed bed reactor was equipped with a quartz tube (inner diameter 0.8 cm, length 40 cm), and a certain amount of adsorbent was placed in the reactor with a bed height of 3 cm. The gas mixture with controlled concentration (10–1000 ppm H2S) and specific flow rate (40–100 mL/min) was introduced into the reactor. The temperature and pressure conditions were controlled by a heat jacket and a vacuum pump, respectively, and the adsorbent bed was vacuumed at 120 °C for 4 h before each experiment to achieve complete activation. The adsorption capacity was measured using a volumetric method and a KECO gas analyzer. The system was calibrated with helium gas before each series of experiments, and the dead volume was determined. The adsorbed gas was calculated by determining the difference between the initial and equilibrium pressures, using the Soave–Redlich–Kwong (SRK) equation of state. The exhaust gas H2S is absorbed using a NaOH solution to prevent environmental contamination. Results and discussion

Characterization

XRD patterns

The XRD patterns of the MIL-101(Cr) adsorber from 2θ to 80θ matched those in previous studies50,51, confirming its cubic structure52. All diffraction peaks aligned with calculated values, verifying successful synthesis of MIL-101(Cr)53,54. Peaks at 2θ values of 5.25, 5.88, 8.42, 9.06, and 16.50 corresponded to crystallographic planes55. No peaks indicated other structures or amorphous phases, affirming well-synthesized MIL-101(Cr) with a regular structure akin to Fig. 3, maintaining crystallinity56.

XRD patterns of the structure adsorbents.

The XRD patterns of the MIL-101(Cr) @ MIPS/NIPs@H2S adsorbers were also analyzed. These results were consistent with prior studies43,57. A comparison of the MIL-101(Cr) pattern with the MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S pattern revealed no significant changes in the phase structure of MIL-101(Cr) post polymerization. The XRD pattern of the MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S particles closely resembled that of MIL-101(Cr), indicating successful coating. Thus, MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S does not alter the crystalline phase of MIL-101(Cr). Following the creation of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S, the peaks were largely diminished due to the amorphous nature of the polymer layer on the composite surface, likely the MIPS layer. The patterns of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S exhibit similar diffraction patterns as MIL-101(Cr), affirming that the presence of MIPs/NIPs does not disturb the crystal structure of MIL-101(Cr). However, it is worth noting that the peak intensity of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S is relatively lower compared to that of MIL-101(Cr) due to the incorporation of the metal–organic framework in the polymeric structure14,58–63.

FT-IR spectra

The FT-IR spectroscopic analysis results for the synthesized chromium MIL-101(Cr) spectrum are in line with similar studies, both in peak shape and position50–55. The successful bonding of metal clusters to H2BDC carboxyl groups is supported by the Cr–O stretching vibration at 567 cm−1, as shown in Fig. 5. The interaction between chromium ion carboxylate groups and terephthalic acid was confirmed by two moderately intense adsorption bands at 1567 cm−1 and around 1600 cm−1. This may be attributed to the presence of a monosubstituted benzene ring and strong stretching vibration51,70. Most peaks fell within the range of 600 to 1600 cm−171. FT-IR analysis was conducted to assess the chemical composition of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S before and after removing the template molecule. All adsorptions exhibited an O–H stretch at about 3606 cm−1 in the FT-IR spectrum. A prominent peak at 1729 cm−1 was identified and linked to C = O stretching vibrations in the aldehyde/ester structure (RCHO-RCOOR), possibly originating from stretching vibrations of MAA or EGDMA. Technical terms like MAA and EGDMA are explained upon first use. The MIL-101(Cr) @MIPs/NIPs@H2S spectrum displays a slight peak at around 2972 cm−1 due to C-H stretching vibration, suggesting that MIL-101(Cr) has influenced the polymer spectrum to some extent. The MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S curve indicates the vanishing of the 1593, 1382, and 660 cm−1 bands, which are distinctive vibrations of MIL-101(Cr). An O-C-O framework vibrational flexibility band is observed at 1636 cm−1, confirming the presence of the MIL-101(Cr) structure and successful integration between the cores of MIL-101(Cr) and MIPs. When the shell was thin, no noticeable difference in the spectra was observed between MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S and MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S42,72. There was no significant distinction in the FT-IR between the NIPs and MIPs, and a new, faint peak at about 2969 cm−1 in the MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S spectrum showed no substantial difference from that of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S14,58–63.

Optimizing the functional parameters and analyzing the adsorption capacity

The statistical predictions were strongly correlated with the experimental findings. This method was also used to evaluate the linear effects of independent and dependent variables. The synthesized MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S exhibited high porosity, favorable physical and chemical traits, and chemical stability76, along with a high mass transfer rate and selective adsorption affinity. Adsorption studies

The analysis of the isotherm data demonstrated the interaction between the MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S adsorbent and the adsorbate. Understanding the mechanism of this interaction and how the concentration affects it is essential, and this is where equilibrium isotherm data play a crucial role. The three isotherm models most widely used for this purpose are the Freundlich, Langmuir, and Temkin models78,79–81. In this study, the equilibrium adsorption of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S, at different H2S concentrations (200–1000 ppm) was obtained at 2 bar pressure by adding an optimal adsorbent dose (0.247 g) and temperature (35.11°C). The experiments were conducted for 40 min to achieve equilibrium. The correlation between the experimental data and different isotherms was analyzed. The Langmuir equation had a better fit, and model brushes for MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S adsorption (R2= 0.9943) and MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S adsorption (R2 = 0.9831) were obtained. Therefore, it can be concluded that adsorption monolayer adsorption occurs, which means that the adsorbing materials have the same affinity and adsorption energy for the target molecules over the entire surface during the adsorption process. These results are in line with those obtained from mesoporous and microporous adsorbents57,60. Furthermore, the dimensionless factor RL expresses the degree of desirability and shape of the isotherms, as shown in the diagrams in Fig. 8 and Tables 8 and 9. When the value of RL is between 0 and 1 the isotherm is favorable. An RL greater than 1, is unfavorable, but if the RL is 1, the isotherm is linear. When RL is 0, the isotherm is unfavorable and irreversible (87, 88). For the MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S adsorbent, the value of RL is 0.3, while for the MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S adsorbent, it is 0.12. These values indicate an optimal adsorption isotherm at equilibrium between the gas and solid phases and an active adsorption site on the polymer surface. These models help analyze experimental data because when model molecules occupy the sites, they cannot adsorb other substances43. The data showed that the equilibrium adsorption of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S and MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S followed a monolayer model, and the identified adsorption sites were uniformly distributed on the surface of the substance57.

Regeneration studies of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S adsorbents

The stability of adsorbents can be measured by their regeneration performance, which is crucial for cost reduction and reuse evaluation. The H2S adsorption regeneration efficiency of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs/NIPs@H2S adsorbents was studied over ten consecutive adsorption‒desorption cycles. The desorption formula was used to calculate the desorption efficiency for each cycle. After each cycle, the samples were regenerated under a vacuum of N2 gas at 10 bar pressure at 120 °C for four hours. The H2S desorption capacity showed no visible change after ten cycles (Fig. 10). In the tenth experiment, the reduction capacity of the MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S adsorbent decreased by only 1% compared to that in the first experiment, while the reduction capacity of the MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S adsorbent decreased by 9.21%. The average regeneration efficiency for H2S was close to 98.92% of the initial value, indicating a significant adsorption efficiency, similar to other research in this field55,86,87. Repeated adsorption and desorption can reduce the adsorption rate by destroying some identified sites, making them suitable for industrial-scale cyclic adsorption processes. The first cycle caused the most significant decrease in the adsorption capacity for H2S gas, indicating that the micropores were significantly affected after the first adsorption test. However, these adsorbents have excellent stability and broad application potential55,57,68. H2S molecules interact with active adsorption sites and saturate a significant portion of the open metal sites during initial gas adsorption, resulting in poor cyclic behavior. Due to its corrosive nature, H2S is irreversibly confined between H2S molecules and open metal sites88. Finally, compared with the MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S adsorbent, the MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S nano adsorbent can not only be used as a promising adsorbent with high regeneration for long-term adsorption–desorption operation but also has high stability due to the gaseous nature of H2S.

In this study, the data were standard, and the polynomial equations of the data in the nanoadsorbents indicated the successful fit of the data. The LOF test associated with the quadratic model of data fit is not significant, indicating good data fit. After adsorption and reduction, the FTIR and XRD patterns of the MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs/MIPs@H2S nanoadsorbents were not significantly different from those of the amorphous and crystalline tissues. Thus, MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs/MIPs@H2S had a stable physical structure for multiple adsorption and desorption cycles. Therefore, it can be assumed that monolayer adsorption occurs, and the adsorbents have the same affinity and adsorption energy for the molecule. The adsorption kinetics and modeling of the optimized nanoadsorbents, based on the results of pseudo-second-order theory, show the best agreement with the experimental data at all concentrations. The ability of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S to adsorb H2S gas was significantly greater than that of MIL-101(Cr)@MIPs@H2S to adsorb H2S gas, and the selectivity of MIL-101(Cr)@NIPs@H2S for IFH2S was also greater than that for other gases.
