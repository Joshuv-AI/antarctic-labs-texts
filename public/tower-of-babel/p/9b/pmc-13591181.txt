Return to Community Living and Functional Ability Following Inpatient Rehabilitation for Hip Fracture in Patients Aged ≥65 Years: A Retrospective Descriptive Observational Study
Authors: Hitomi Oishi, Motoyuki Horii, Maki Asada, Norikazu Hishikawa, Suzuyo Ohashi, Koshiro Sawada, Yasuo Mikami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Most older patients returned home after hip fracture rehabilitation.•Three-quarters of patients living alone returned home after discharge.•Many patients returned home despite limited daily living ability.•Lower functional ability was common even among those discharged home.

As the Japanese population ages,1 the number of hip fractures continues to increase, with many affected patients aged ≥75 years, including those aged ≥85 years.2 Most hip fractures in older adults are caused by low-energy falls, as reported in previous studies.3 Hip fractures in older adults are associated with substantial morbidity,3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 including long-term functional decline and loss of independence, as well as increased mortality.9,10

In Japan, the proportion of people aged ≥65 years living alone has been steadily increasing.11 Older adults often require intensive rehabilitation after surgery for a hip fracture to regain the ability to live safely in the community.

Specialized postacute inpatient rehabilitation wards (often referred to as convalescent rehabilitation wards) provide structured, multidisciplinary rehabilitation programs aimed at improving activities of daily living (ADL) and facilitating return to the community.

To date, few studies have investigated the proportion of patients with hip fractures who return to their original homes following inpatient rehabilitation or examined the distribution of their functional status at discharge, as assessed by the Functional Independence Measure (FIM) score.12 FIM assesses functional independence across motor and cognitive domains and consists of 18 items (13 motor and 5 cognitive), including self-care, transfers, and locomotion for motor function, and communication and social cognition for cognitive function. Understanding these patterns may help inform the development of postdischarge support strategies.

Materials and methods

We included patients aged ≥65 years who sustained a hip fracture between January 1, 2016, and December 31, 2022; underwent surgery in an acute care hospital; and were subsequently transferred to our specialized postacute inpatient rehabilitation ward (hereafter referred to as a convalescent rehabilitation ward).

Only those who had been living at home (with family or alone) in the community before the injury were included; in patients who sustained the fracture during hospitalization for other medical conditions in an acute care hospital, the residence before admission was considered the prefracture residence. Patients who were hospitalized in long-term or chronic care hospitals at the time of fracture, as well as those living in institutional settings such as service-enhanced senior housing or fee-based nursing homes, were excluded.

Discharge destinations were categorized as follows for both those who had lived with family and those who had lived alone before the injury: return to living with family, return to living alone, transfer to a residential facility,13 admission to a long-term care hospital ward, or transfer to an acute care hospital. Facilities included service-enhanced senior housing, health care facilities for the elderly (Roken), fee-based nursing homes, group homes for persons with dementia, and special nursing homes. Long-term care medical facilities (Kaigo Iryouin) were classified as long-term care hospital wards. Patients who were transferred to acute care hospitals were excluded from the analysis.

We investigated the number of cases and the mean age (range) by sex according to the preinjury living situation. For patients who were discharged home (ie, not to a long-term care facility, group home, or other institutional setting), we also examined the number of cases and the mean age (range) by sex separately for those who had lived with family and those who had lived alone. In addition, among patients who had been living at home before the injury, we compared the proportions of discharge destinations (home vs long-term care facility or long-term care hospital ward) between those who had lived with family and those who had lived alone using a chi-square test.

To compare the levels of ADL ability among patients discharged to different destinations, we also recorded each patient’s motor and cognitive FIM scores at discharge and plotted these scores according to discharge destination.

In addition, the total FIM scores of patients discharged to institutions or long-term care hospital wards were analyzed separately for those who had lived alone before the injury and those who had cohabitants.

Ethics approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Rakuwakai Otowa Rehabilitation Hospital (RAKUOTORI-RIN-19-001). In accordance with the approved protocol, the study was conducted using an opt-out approach by posting information about the research on the hospital’s website, thereby ensuring that patients had the opportunity to decline participation.

Results

During the study period, 676 patients aged ≥65 years were admitted to our convalescent rehabilitation ward after undergoing hip fracture surgery (108 men and 568 women). The mean age was 83.5 years (±7.76 SD), and the oldest patient was 102 years old.

Twenty-six patients (9 men and 17 women) were transferred to acute care hospitals and were therefore excluded from further analysis. Discussion

In Japan’s health care system, convalescent rehabilitation wards play a central role in postacute care, following hip fracture surgery. These wards focus on improving ADL and facilitating discharge to home or community settings.

The “home discharge rate” from these wards is frequently reported publicly because of its relevance to insurance-based facility certification. However, this figure also includes patients who had been living at home before hospitalization but were discharged to long-term care facilities, such as nursing homes. Therefore, accurately inferring the actual home discharge rates of older patients with hip fracture from these reports is challenging.

In this study, discharge destinations other than home were classified as either residential facilities or long-term care hospital wards. Although there are many types of facilities,13 they are institutionally categorized as either long-term residential care facilities or facilities aiming to return home. Health care facilities for the elderly (Roken) are designed to support return to home; however, in practice, many residents transition to long-term residential care,14 so these were categorized as residential facilities. Long-term care medical facilities (Kaigo Iryouin), introduced in 2018, function similarly to long-term care hospital wards and were therefore grouped accordingly. Patients who were unexpectedly transferred back to acute care hospitals because of complications such as new trauma or illness were excluded from the analysis because their discharge destinations were diverse and could not always be confirmed in the available records.

Our findings emphasize that discharge to the original home should not be interpreted as the sole indicator of successful rehabilitation. For patients with hip fractures, accurately assessing premorbid ADL is often difficult, making it challenging to evaluate rehabilitation outcomes with precision. Furthermore, it is not always evident whether the observed decline in ADL performance primarily results from the fracture itself or reflects preexisting functional impairment that may have contributed to the injury. When the latter factor plays a major role, discharge to an alternative living arrangement, such as a care facility, may be more appropriate than returning to the original home. The selection of discharge destination is also influenced by regional factors, including the accessibility of long-term care services. Consequently, it is essential to develop discharge plans tailored to each patient’s needs and living environment.

This study was conducted in a convalescent rehabilitation ward located in Yamashina Ward, Kyoto City, Japan. Although Kyoto is one of Japan’s major urban centers, Yamashina lies on its eastern periphery and combines both urban and semirural characteristics. The area has a relatively high aging rate (31.7% in 2021, compared with the national average of 28.8%) and a markedly higher proportion of older adults living alone (34.9% vs 19.0% nationwide).15,16 Community-based care resources, including visiting nurse stations and home-helper agencies, are relatively well developed in this region. Therefore, our findings may reflect outcomes within a setting that possesses comparatively strong community care infrastructure.

Among the 364 patients who had lived with family, 317 (87.1%) returned to their homes (including 1 patient who lived alone after discharge). Those admitted to facilities mostly had total FIM scores below 90 (fig 2). Among those who returned home, 59 patients (18.1%) had total FIM scores <80 (fig 1).

According to the Uniform Data System for Medical Rehabilitation,12 a total FIM score of ≥80 is generally consistent with the ability of family members to provide care at home. Therefore, patients with total FIM scores <80 likely require more assistance than family caregivers could typically offer. Even among those with FIM scores between 80 and 100, however, adequate family caregiving may not always be feasible in Japan, where 63.5% of caregiver pairs are aged ≥65 years and 35.7% are aged ≥75 years.11 In this context, strengthening social support systems for both patients and family caregivers is essential to sustain home-based care, especially for cohabiting patients with low levels of ADL.

Among patients who had lived alone before the injury, 136 of 179 (75.3%) were discharged to live alone. Of the remaining patients who were discharged to a facility, most had total FIM scores below approximately 100 (fig 2). However, even among those who returned to living alone, 36 patients (26.3%) had total FIM scores below 100. According to the Uniform Data System for Medical Rehabilitation,12 a total FIM score between 100 and 110 indicates independence in personal care, suggesting that individuals with scores below 100 generally require some degree of care support. Therefore, our findings indicate that a considerable proportion of patients who returned to living alone may have required some level of caregiving assistance.

Only 7 patients who had lived alone began cohabiting after discharge, and 5 of them had FIM scores ≥110. This finding suggests that family cohabitation after injury is uncommon, even when functional status declines. This rarity likely reflects the prevailing social and demographic conditions in contemporary Japan. Increasing nuclear family structures and dual-income households, limited housing space in urban areas, and concerns about caregiver burden may all contribute to the difficulty of resuming cohabitation with older relatives following a major decline in functional ability. Furthermore, the relatively widespread availability of long-term care facilities and community-based residential options—such as nursing homes, group homes, and assisted-living residences—likely provides practical alternatives to family cohabitation, particularly in urban and suburban environments.

These findings highlight the importance of community-based integrated care systems, such as those promoted by the Ministry of Health, Labor and Welfare in Japan, which aim to enable older adults to continue living in their communities with peace of mind.17 Close coordination among medical, nursing, and welfare services may be particularly important for patients who return home despite ongoing functional limitations. Individualized discharge planning and early coordination with community-based support services may facilitate a safer transition from inpatient rehabilitation to community living. Although the effectiveness of such approaches was not evaluated in the present study, they may help support sustainable community living for older adults with care needs.

Implications for practice and future research

The findings of this study have several implications for clinical practice and future research.

First, discharge to home should not be interpreted as the sole indicator of successful rehabilitation. A considerable proportion of patients returned home despite discharge FIM scores below levels generally associated with independent living or manageable caregiver burden. These patients may benefit from proactive discharge planning and early coordination of community-based support services, including home care assistance, visiting nursing services, and long-term care resources.

Second, rehabilitation programs for older adults with hip fractures should incorporate not only functional recovery but also assessment of postdischarge support needs. Particular attention should be given to patients with lower discharge FIM scores, as sustainable community living may depend on the availability of both formal care services and informal caregiver support.

Third, future research would benefit from more standardized documentation of factors relevant to community reintegration, including prefracture functional status, living arrangements, caregiver availability, postdischarge service utilization, and discharge planning processes such as multidisciplinary discharge conferences and pre-discharge home assessments. More consistent collection and sharing of such information may help identify determinants of successful return to community living and facilitate comparisons across health care systems.

Finally, prospective longitudinal studies are needed to examine whether patients discharged home are able to maintain community living over time and to clarify which rehabilitation strategies and support services contribute most effectively to long-term independence and quality of life.

International considerations

The median rehabilitation length of stay in the present study was substantially longer than that reported in many Western countries, where postacute rehabilitation stays after hip fracture are often considerably shorter. Indeed, international comparison data indicate that the combined length of stay for acute care and inpatient rehabilitation in many Western nations ranges from ∼20 to 45 days,18 whereas inpatient rehabilitation stays in Japan are generally longer. Differences in health care systems, reimbursement structures, and the availability of postacute care services may influence discharge timing and functional outcomes. For example, countries with shorter rehabilitation stays may rely more heavily on community-based rehabilitation, home health services, or long-term care facilities after discharge.

Therefore, caution is warranted when comparing discharge destinations and functional status across countries. The relatively high rate of return to home observed in the present study may partly reflect the characteristics of Japan’s convalescent rehabilitation system, which combines relatively long inpatient rehabilitation with multidisciplinary discharge planning and coordination of community care services. This integrated approach may support community reintegration after hip fracture; however, further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify whether these favorable discharge outcomes are maintained over time.

Study limitations

In this retrospective study, preinjury ADL status was not evaluated using standardized and objective measures such as the FIM. In routine clinical practice, information regarding preinjury functional status is comprehensively collected from patients, their families, and available medical or care records. However, because these data were not recorded using standardized or quantifiable measures, they could not be analyzed in this study. Therefore, we were unable to accurately assess the effectiveness of convalescent rehabilitation based on changes from preinjury functional status. In addition, postdischarge outcomes, including the sustainability of independent living at home and the type of care services provided, were not examined. The characteristics of cohabitants were also not available.

Despite these limitations, this study uniquely focused on patients with hip fracture—one of the major conditions treated in convalescent rehabilitation wards—and analyzed their rates of returning home in relation to discharge FIM scores. Because hip fractures generally do not result in overt neurological deficits, our findings may also provide insights into the broader population of older adults with general impairments in ADL.

Conclusion

A high proportion of older adults returned to their original homes after rehabilitation for hip fracture. Among those who had lived with family, 86.8% resumed living with family, and an additional 0.3% returned to the same home but began living alone, resulting in 87.1% returning to their original residence. Similarly, those who had lived alone before the fracture, 76.0% resumed living alone, whereas 3.9% were discharged to live with family members, totaling 79.9% who returned to a home setting.

However, not all of these individuals achieved sufficient functional independence. Specifically, 18.1% of those who returned to live with family had scores <80, and 26.3% of patients who returned to living alone had total FIM scores <100.

These findings indicate that even among patients discharged home, some—particularly those with lower FIM scores—likely required support beyond what family caregivers alone could provide, including formal or community-based services, to sustain daily living. To promote sustainable community living after hip fracture, especially for patients with lower FIM scores, coordinated postdischarge support systems are essential. Such systems should not only address the care needs of patients but also provide adequate social and practical support for family caregivers within the framework of community-based integrated care.
