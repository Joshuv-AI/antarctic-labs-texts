Atypical Presentations of Coronary Microvascular Dysfunction in Patients After COVID
Authors: Nisarg Ray, Ahan Kayastha, Krunal Shukla, Michael Massoomi, Ellen C Keeley, Natalie Bracewell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoronary microvascular dysfunction is a recognized cause of angina without obstructive coronary disease, yet routine tests are often nondiagnostic. In this case series, we describe 3 previously active adults with persistent chest pain after SARS-CoV-2 infection who fulfilled the World Health Organization criteria for postacute sequelae. All 3 patients underwent invasive coronary angiography with coronary reactivity testing with coronary flow reserve (CFR). In all patients, the epicardial coronary arteries were angiographically normal, however CFR was significantly impaired (<2.0). Medical management was tailored and guideline-concordant, including the use of calcium-channel blockers, beta-blockers, long-acting nitrates, and graded exercise, with some improvement in symptoms. We contend that coronary microvascular dysfunction may be the etiology of post-COVID chest pain. An earlier CFR assessment may shorten diagnostic delays and improve quality of life.Key words: cardiovascular disease, chest pain, complication, coronary angiography, coronary circulation, echocardiography, exercise

Coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) is an increasingly recognized cause of myocardial ischemia in the absence of epicardial coronary disease. CMD, first described in 1988, is defined as any structural or functional abnormality at the level of the coronary microcirculation.1,2 The coronary microcirculation comprises a network of high-resistance prearterioles, arterioles, and capillaries, and their dysfunction can cause ischemia in the presence of nonobstructive lesions of the epicardial coronary arteries through several mechanisms including endothelial dysfunction, abnormal vascular smooth muscle reactivity, and luminal narrowing and perivascular fibrosis of the microvasculature.2

CMD can result in adverse clinical outcomes, including myocardial ischemia and infarction, nonischemic cardiomyopathy (eg, stress cardiomyopathy), and heart failure resulting in increased morbidity and mortality.1,3,4 While CMD is associated with the traditional risk factors of macrovascular disease (tobacco use, advanced age, hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia), a growing body of evidence suggests that it may occur in younger populations without these risk factors.5,6

We present 3 patients who had coronary flow reserve (CFR) testing consistent with CMD but without significant macrovascular risk factors. All 3 of these patients had SARS-CoV-2 infection preceding the onset of symptoms. Coronary reactivity testing was performed using the Abbott Coroflow system, with intravenous adenosine as the hyperemic agent. Provocative testing for coronary vasospasm was performed with intracoronary acetylcholine according to standard protocol.3 A summary of the characteristics of each case is available in Table 1.

Case 1

A 32-year-old man who was a former National Collegiate Athletic Association Division I football player with elevated lipoprotein(a) presented to the emergency department with 1 hour of severe left parasternal chest pain radiating to the left arm and shoulder with dyspnea. His evaluation was negative for acute coronary syndrome, and he was referred to the cardiology clinic for further evaluation.

The patient reported progressively frequent episodes of sharp substernal chest pain and dyspnea associated with palpitations. He was unable to perform his usual level of physical activity and began to experience symptoms at rest. Noninvasive work-up with exercise electrocadiographic stress testing was unremarkable, transthoracic echocardiography was normal, and a 72-hour event monitor demonstrated rare premature atrial and ventricular contractions. Given persistent symptom burden with unrevealing noninvasive work-up, he was referred for coronary angiography with coronary reactivity testing. His study revealed normal epicardial vessels, no evidence of acetylcholine-induced spasm, and a CFR of 1.3, consistent with CMD.

Further history revealed that he had experienced SARS-CoV-2 infection twice before symptom onset: the first time in 2021 and the second in 2022. Chest pain and fatigue began 1 to 2 months after the second infection. The patient was started on low-dose aspirin, high-intensity statin, lisinopril, and was prescribed a graded exercise regimen. Given continued symptoms, he was referred for a cardiopulmonary exercise test, which revealed decreased exercise capacity with Vo2 max of 79.7% (normal: >84%). He was referred to our institution's long COVID clinic and was started on low-dose amitriptyline. Upon follow-up, chest pain frequency and dyspnea had decreased, and he had resumed his weightlifting, yoga, and walking regimen without limiting symptoms. Time from initial presentation to diagnosis was 14 months.

Case 2

A 65-year-old woman with Sjogren's disease, small vessel disease of the brain, and hyperlipidemia presented to the emergency room with intermittent, stabbing, left-sided chest pain associated with dyspnea. Her work-up was negative for acute coronary syndrome, and she was referred to the cardiology clinic. Additional history in the clinic revealed that these symptoms started in 2020 after a SARS-CoV-2 infection. Cardiac testing including transthoracic echocardiography, event monitor, and coronary computed tomography angiography were all unrevealing. Over the next 4 years, she continued to be symptomatic. She was referred for coronary angiography with coronary reactivity testing, which revealed angiographically normal epicardial coronaries and a CFR of 1.9, consistent with CMD. She was prescribed amlodipine and sublingual nitroglycerin as needed and was referred to the long COVID clinic. Since then, she has had improvement in her symptoms and an increase in activity level. Time from initial presentation to diagnosis was 5 years and 10 months.

Case 3

A 61-year-old man with hyperlipidemia and hypertension presented to the cardiology clinic given 6 months of substernal chest pressure and dyspnea with swimming. Initial vital signs and examination were unremarkable. Electrocardiogram demonstrated sinus rhythm with nonspecific intraventricular conduction delay. He previously had a calcium score of 1,181 (> 95th percentile for age and gender). Owing to persistent symptom burden limiting exertion with swimming, he was referred for coronary angiography with coronary reactivity testing, which revealed mild luminal irregularities and a CFR of 1.2. Notably, he was diagnosed with SARS-CoV-2 infection in July 2023, after which he developed these symptoms.

The patient was medically managed on low-dose metoprolol tartrate and isosorbide mononitrate, in addition to low-dose aspirin and rosuvastatin. He has experienced an improvement in symptoms. Time from presentation to diagnosis was 10 months.

Discussion

We present 3 patients who were found to have CMD upon coronary reactivity testing after SARS-CoV-2 infection. All 3 fulfilled World Health Organization criteria for postacute sequelae of SARS-CoV-2 infection (“long COVID”), presenting with ongoing fatigue, dyspnea, and episodic chest discomfort for >3 months after their index infection. These cases highlight growing evidence linking long COVID to systemic endothelial inflammation, microthrombotic phenomena, and impaired coronary flow reserve, thereby providing a plausible biological explanation for their microvascular angina.7

Although CMD occurs in up to 30% to 50% of patients with angina and nonobstructive coronaries, it remains a challenging diagnosis.8 Standard noninvasive work-up does not typically detect CMD. Advanced noninvasive methods, such as positron emission tomography quantification of CFR and stress cardiac magnetic resonance imaging evaluation of myocardial perfusion reserve, can identify microvascular ischemia, but these modalities are not universally accessible. Coronary angiography with coronary reactivity testing can facilitate identification of microvascular flow abnormalities and coronary vasospasm. However, the diagnosis is frequently delayed owing to low clinical suspicion, limited availability of noninvasive modalities such as positron emission tomography and magnetic resonance imaging, and the risk involved with invasive procedures.

In our series, the median time to diagnosis was 14 months, reflecting a delayed focus on microvascular dysfunction. We contend that the CMD diagnosis would have been made sooner had early provocative testing been considered. Given the overlapping clinical phenotype of long COVID and CMD, earlier consideration of CFR assessment in the post-COVID population might shorten these diagnostic timelines.

Recent advanced imaging data demonstrate that individuals hospitalized with COVID-19 have a significantly lower coronary flow-velocity reserve compared with matched controls, indicating a higher propensity for CMD after SARS-CoV-2 infection.8 Evidence suggests that early invasive testing may improve diagnosis and quality of life in symptomatic patients without obstructive coronary artery disease, as demonstrated in the CorMicA (Coronary Microvascular Angina) trial.7,9 While the CorMicA trial did not demonstrate differences in major adverse cardiovascular events at 6 months, the WISE (Women's Ischemia Syndrome Evaluation) study reported that in women with suspected ischemia who underwent invasive testing, coronary reactivity testing (specifically, CFR) was a strong predictor of major adverse outcomes (death, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, and hospitalization for heart failure).10

Conclusions

Our case series highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for CMD in patients after SARS-CoV-2 infection with recurrent chest pain and no evidence of epicardial coronary artery disease. Earlier diagnosis and appropriate treatment for CMD in these patients may improve clinical outcomes.
