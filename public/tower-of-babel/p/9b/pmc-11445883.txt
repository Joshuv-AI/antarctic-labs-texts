The impact of the implementation of the two-dose varicella vaccine immunization strategy in Quzhou: A retrospective birth cohort study
Authors: Tingcui Wen, Quanjun Fang, Canya Fu, Canjie Zheng, Jie Pan, Biyun Zheng, Wenjie Xu, Zhiying Yin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaricella is a vaccine-preventable disease caused by the varicella zoster virus (VZV), but the varicella incidence among children has increased in recent years. This was a retrospective birth cohort study based on the Zhejiang Provincial Immune Information System (ZJIIS) and the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention (CISDCP) in Quzhou. A total of 1,291 clinically diagnosed varicella cases born from 2009 to 2014 were collected during 2009–2023, which were analyzed the impact of changes in vaccination strategy on the incidence of varicella based on the Cox-proportional hazards model. It was observed that the onset age of varicella shifted to the older age group and later to 9–11 years. After the change to the two-dose varicella vaccination strategy, the population affected by varicella was concentrated among students and received more than one dose of live attenuated varicella vaccine (VarV). Based on the Coxproportional hazards model and adjusting for all covariates, the risk of varicella infection in children decreased after the introduction of the two-dose varicella vaccination strategy (HR = 0.04, 95% CI: 0.03–0.05). Meanwhile, the Kaplan–Meier curves also showed that the hazards were lower after the change in vaccination strategy. It is recommended that two doses of VarV should be included in the national immunization schedule and that full vaccination should be completed approximately four years after the first dose.

Introduction

Varicella is an infectious disease caused by VZV and characterized by a widespread blistering rash and mild systemic symptoms. The World Health Organization (WHO) conservatively estimated that there were at least 140.0 million cases of varicella infection worldwide each year, resulting in 4.2 million cases of severe complications requiring hospitalization and 4,200 deaths.1 The number of varicella cases continues to rise, leading to an increase in medical service fees and other indirect costs.2 A total of 3.0 million varicella cases were reported in Zhejiang province during 2016–2019, with an average annual incidence of 55.05 per 100,000 population.3

The VarV is the most economical and effective way to prevent varicella, reducing the incidence and mortality of varicella while being cost-effective. VarV was licensed in China in 1998 as a self funded vaccine, and it was recommended that children receive the first dose of VarV at one year of age.4 A previous study has shown that the performance of one-dose VarV in healthy children aged 1–12 years in China was satisfactory, with a vaccine effectiveness (VE) of 75.0% [95%CI: 68.0%–80.0%].5 However, in some economically developed cities, such as Jiangsu and Beijing, outbreaks of varicella in schools are still common, with a high prevalence of VarV.6,7 Research has shown that compared to one-dose VarV, the geometric mean antibody concentration in children after receiving a second dose of VarV increased approximately 10-fold.8 A meta-analysis estimated the pooled one-dose VE and two-dose VE against all varicella to be 81.0% (95% CI: 78.0%–84.0%) and 92.0% (95% CI: 88.0%–95.0%), respectively.9 Studies from several countries, including Germany, the United States, Italy, and Canada, have also shown that two-dose VarV vaccination is effective in reducing the incidence of varicella.10,11 Due to the high incidence of varicella outbreaks and the greater effectiveness of the two-dose strategy compared with the one-dose strategy, many countries recommend the use of two-dose VarV and are generally expanding its use.12 As of 2022, 28 countries or regions worldwide have included the VarV in their national immunization programs (NIPs),1 with the United States being the first to include two-dose VarV in their immunization plans (2006).11

The WHO recommends that in countries with a heavy burden of varicella and sufficient resources to ensure vaccination rates of ≥ 80.0%, the routine immunization with VarV should be considered, and a disease surveillance system should be established before inclusion to assess the disease burden of varicella.1 Although varicella has not yet been included in the scope of China’s statutory infectious disease reporting, provinces have gradually begun to report varicella cases in the China Infectious Disease Surveillance Information System since 2005. The annual average reported incidence rate of varicella increased from 23.04 per 100,000 population in 2005–2015 to 55.05 per 100,000 population in 2016–2019, the incidence rate increased from 3.17 per 100,000 population in 2005 to 70.14 per 100,000 population in 2019 in China.13,14 Besides, the sudden public health emergencies caused by the varicella epidemic have also had serious social impacts.15 Hunan Province reported 725 school public health emergencies, with varicella accounting for the second largest proportion.16 With the increase in varicella incidence rate, whether to include VarV in NIPs has been a momentous issue for immunization program professionals.

Efforts to reduce costs and raise vaccine awareness, especially in low-income families, or to incorporate VarV in the routine schedule, may increase vaccination rates and reduce morbidity and mortality from varicella.17 Using the Halloran model and a 50 year time frame, the cost-effectiveness of VarV administration in China showed that, based on non-vaccination, the cost-effectiveness of one-dose VarV (18 months) was 1:4.19, and the cost-effectiveness ratio of the combination strategy of routine immunization and booster immunization (2–11 years old) is 1:3.57.18 Many countries around the world, such as the UK and Italy, have found that incorporating VarV into NIPs has good cost-effectiveness. In Mexico, a study compared the economic benefits of four different vaccination strategies and found that vaccination with onedose and two-dose VarV avoided 20,570,722 and 23,029,751 cases, respectively, during a 20-year follow-up, saving Mexican society $53.16 million and $34.41 million annually.19 In Switzerland, considering the high incidence of HZ in the elderly population, they studied the cost–benefit analysis of the inclusion of different doses of VarV vaccination after birth and the inclusion of herpes zoster virus vaccine (HZV) vaccination in the NIPs for people over 65 years of age. The results showed that the introduction of VarV vaccination in Sweden was cost-effective, while vaccination of HZV in the elderly was not cost-effective at present.20 In addition, the simulation prediction analysis was also carried out for countries that have not yet included VarV in NIPs. In Norway, the study predicted the economic benefit model of six twodose VarV vaccination strategies. The conclusion showed that universal VarV vaccination across the country was expected to reduce the incidence rate of natural varicella to 48–59 per 100,000 population, and the number of diagnosed cases, inpatients, and deaths of varicella could be reduced by 75.0–85.0%, 67.0–79.0%, and 75.0–79.0%, respectively.21

In summary, global research has confirmed that including VarV in NIPs has good cost-effectiveness, and the cost benefit of two doses of vaccination is greater. It is recommended that national policies adopt the inclusion of two-dose VarV in NIPs. Consequently, some regions in China have recommended the use of two-dose VarV in 2012 to further control varicella.22 Subsequently, in July 2014, the Zhejiang Provincial Center for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) issued the “Zhejiang Province varicella attenuated live vaccine vaccination guidance,” proposing the two-dose VarV immunization strategy. Quzhou also began implementing the two-dose VarV immunization program, with the first dose at 15 months and the second dose at 3 years of age.

By incorporating the VarV into the immunization program, timely vaccination of eligible children can be effectively promoted, rapidly increasing the vaccination rate. According to the CDC in the United States, the vaccination rate for children born in 2019–2020 who received at least one-dose VarV was 91.1%.23 Through large-scale VarV vaccination not only has the resistance of susceptible individuals been effectively enhanced but also an immune barrier has been developed in specific populations. After 25 years of varicella vaccine immunization program in the United States (1995–2019), the hospitalization and mortality rates of varicella among the population under 50 years old have decreased by 94.0% and 97.0%, respectively, preventing over 10,500 hospitalizations and 100 deaths annually.24 One-dose universal varicella vaccination (UVV) was introduced in the Argentinian NIPs in July 2015. In 2015, the national varicella vaccination coverage rate in Argentina was 44.8%. From 2016 to 2019, the national varicella vaccination coverage rates increased to 74.4%, 76.8%, 81.0%, and 77.6%, respectively.25 Furthermore, significant declines in incidence, reaching reductions of 83.9% and 69.1% in the target and overall populations, and decreasing trends in mortality rate from 0.4 to 0.2 per 1,000,000 population were observed, respectively.25 The regression analysis of the epidemic characteristics of varicella in Minhang District, Shanghai from 2013 to 2022 also showed that the inclusion of VarV in the immunization program from 2018 to 2022 can reduce an average of 2,421 cases annually.26 Qingdao, as one of the earliest cities administrated free two-dose VarV for preschool children in 2016 in China. After implementing freely two-dose VarV in 2016, the coverage rates of one-dose VarV for eligible children increased from 86.7% to 97.8% after 4 years, and the coverage rates of two-dose VarV from 42.1% to 85.2%, respectively. On the contrary, the average annual incidence rate of children aged 1–6 years has dropped from 122.58 per 100,000 population to 92.96 per 100,000 population, and further dropped to 26.74 per 100,000 population in 2020.27

According to the Zhejiang Provincial Immunization Planning Information Management System, Quzhou has implemented the two-dose VarV immunization strategy since 2014, with vaccine coverage rates ranging from 49.6% to 63.3% among varicella cases born between 2009 and 2014. The purpose of this study was to evaluate the impact of changes in vaccination strategy on the incidence of varicella based on the Cox-proportional hazards model and to propose some measures to control varicella in Quzhou, China.

Material and methods

Setting

Quzhou is a prefecture-level city located in Zhejiang Province in eastern China, with an area of 8844 square kilometers and a population of approximately 2.29 million in 6 districts. The Zhejiang Provincial Immunization Information System (ZJIIS) was implemented in Quzhou in 2005 and recorded immunization information for children under 15 years old. Hence, the ZJIIS data include children’s demographic information and historical immunization data.

Case definition

Clinical cases were diagnosed on the basis of clinical symptoms with acute onset of generalized maculopapular or vesicular rash (oval, 3–5 mm in size, thin-walled and easily erupted, surrounded by redness) without other known causes.28,29

Data sources and collection

The basic information of varicella cases with disease onset between 1 January 2009 and 31 December 2023 was extracted from the China Information System for Disease Control and Prevention (CISDCP). Basic information of varicella cases of the children born from 2009 to 2014, including name, gender, age, date of birth, population classification, address, and date of disease onset. In addition, we obtained their information on varicella vaccinations by ZJIIS, including the current address of the vaccinee and the date dose of vaccination.

A total of 1,554 varicella cases born from 1 January 2009 to 31 December 2014 were reported through the CISDCP in Quzhou during 2009–2023. Among them, 16 cases who had received one dose of VarV within 42 days before the onset were excluded, 83 cases were duplicated, and 164 cases immune history was unavailable. Ultimately, 1,291 varicella cases were included in this study.

Statistical analysis

A simple life table method was used to calculate person-year, which stipulated that individuals entering the cohort in the observed year will be calculated as 1/2 person-year and those who are lost to follow-up or have an end result will also be calculated as 1/2 person-year. Assume that the onset of varicella is an end result. Formula to calculate person-year using the simple life table method isLx=Ix12+Nx−Dx−Wx Ix+1=Ix+Nx−Dx−Wx

Lx is the number of years of exposure at time X, Ix is the number of observation at the beginning of time X, Nx is the number of entry into the queue at time X, Dx is the number of end results at time X, and Wx is the number of lost follow-up at time X.

Descriptive statistics such as rate and proportion were used to analyze the characteristics of the subjects. The Chi-squared and Cochran’s and Mantel–Haenszel (CMH) tests were used to compare count data between cases, and the ANOVA test was used to compare the month of disease onset between different doses of VarV. Further, the Kaplan–Meier method was used to estimate the cumulative risk probability of varicella infection based on factors such as gender, year of birth and district, and differences were evaluated using the log-rank test. Cox-proportional hazards regression was used to calculate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 17.0(SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). A p < .001 was considered statistically significant.

We built three Cox proportional regression models to assess the association between change in vaccination strategy and varicella incidence. Model 1 was not adjusted for covariates, model 2 was adjusted for gender and year of birth. Finally, model 3 was further adjusted for district, VarV doses, and group classification.

To enhance the robustness of our findings, we conducted several sensitivity analyses. Due to changes in the varicella immunization strategy in 2014, we performed analyses excluding individuals born in 2014. Besides, to minimize the potential bias caused by the COVID-19 pandemic, we reconducted the primary analyses after reviewing data up to 31 December 2019. Finally, to ensure accurate causality, we also performed interaction tests for gender, year of birth, district, and VarV doses.

Ethical considerations

Subjects were anonymized by deleting their personal informations (such as name, address, and contact information) and were exempted from informed consent by the Ethics Committee of the Quzhou Center for Disease Control and Prevention (QZCDC).

Results

The incidence of varicella

In the 2009–2014 birth cohorts, the cumulative incidence of varicella was 198.12, 108.01, 103.64, 73.62, 47.12, and 49.00 per 100,000 population, respectively. The varicella incidence in different birth cohorts peaked in 2019 and then showed a declining trend. The annual incidence rate of varicella in the 2009–2011 birth cohorts was highest at the age of 9–11 years, while the annual incidence rate of varicella in other birth cohorts was evenly distributed (Figure 1). Discussion

Recently, there have been frequent outbreaks of varicella have occurred with high VarV coverage, and breakthrough cases have received widespread attention. After the adjustment of the two-dose VarV immunization strategy in China, the varicella incidence decreased. However, the overall epidemic trend increased from 2013 to 2019, which may be related to the increased sensitivity of varicella epidemic surveillance and reflected the public health issues caused by varicella that cannot be ignored.30 In this study, we analyzed the changes in the risk of varicella infection before and after adjusting the immunization strategy and its interfering factors based on the Cox-proportional hazards model.

The results of the present study showed that, apart from birth cohort factors, the varicella incidence was 1.92 times higher after the change in immunization strategy than before. There was a short-term increase in varicella cases in children under 5 years old shortly after the implementation of the two-dose VarV schedule, and many unvaccinated or one-dose VarV vaccinated individuals born between 2009 and 2014 (just before the introduction of the two-dose VarV schedule) remain susceptible into adolescence. Similarly, the US Department of Health actively monitored the varicella outbreaks in schools from 2012 to 2015 and found that the majority of infections occurred in unvaccinated or one-dose VarV vaccinated children.31 In addition, our research results observed that varicella cases mainly occurred between the ages of 9–11 years, with the age of onset shifting backward, which was well consistent with the published literature.32,33 The implementation of the two-dose VarV immunization strategy may be the main explanation.

The outbreak of varicella is always associated with high concentrations of individuals, and human congregation areas are of great importance for the spread of varicella.34 With the continuous development of socio-economic and urbanization, high population density and high population mobility have led to outbreaks of infectious diseases such as varicella in large cities.35 The results of this cohort study are consistent with the above studies, which revealed that the varicella incidence in urban areas is higher than that in rural areas. For the prevention of varicella among children in urban areas with high population mobility and density, it is recommended to strengthen self-protection and receive a second dose to prevent the spread of varicella.

Varicella is an acute respiratory infectious disease, which is mainly susceptible to infection in collective institutions such as childcare centers and primary and secondary schools.36 In our study, after changing the immunization strategy, students accounted for 75.4% of the cases and became the main group infected with varicella. Ningbo, China, also reported that 51.6% of varicella cases occurred among students, while 18.7% occurred among preschool children, indicating that the changes in the age of onset may be caused by two-dose VarV.37 Among the 1,291 varicella cases, the cases without VarV, with one-dose VarV, and with two-dose VarV accounted for 44.1%, 35.1%, and 20.8%, respectively. The average time from birth to the onset of varicella was 98.65 ± 2.08 (95%CI: 100.73–96.57) months for one-dose VarV and 117.46 ± 2.29 (95%CI: 115.17–119.75) months for two-dose VarV. It proves that two-dose VarV can change the epidemic characteristics of varicella, and the peak age of onset is shifted backward.38 The historical experience of many years of immunization programs implemented in the United States is also worth our reference for the phenomenon of increasing the age of onset. In 2005–2006, 10 years after the United States included varicella in the immunization program, there was a decline in the incidence rate but an upward shift in the age of incidence. In 2013–2014, nearly 20 years after the inclusion of varicella, the incidence rate continued to decline and the upward shift in the age of incidence was improved.39 This study suggests that continuing to administer two-dose VarV will improve the occurrence of varicella outbreaks in primary and secondary schools.

To analyze the interactions between different factors, this study estimated the cumulative hazard of varicella infection. The Kaplan–Meier curves showed that the onset after the change in immunization strategy, born in 2009, living in rural areas, and vaccinated with two-dose VarV were detected to have lower hazards. Children born in 2009 have a lower risk of infection, which may be due to the longest interval between two doses of VarV compared to other cohorts, with an average of 4.80 years, suggesting that the vaccination interval may affect immune persistence. Hu P et al. found that the protective effect of children who received one-dose VarV decreased significantly to 60.8% after 4–6 years.40 These results indicated that the long-term protective effect of one-dose VarV is weak, and a second dose is required for booster immunization. After the change of varicella vaccine strategy, the vaccination rate of ≥ 1 dose exceeded 65.0%, and the proportion of immunization blank population is relatively low; vaccination with VarV forms an immune barrier in the general population, protecting the immune blank population and reducing the gap between them and the vaccinated population. An observational study in the United States recruited over 10,000 children to receive one-dose VarV which doubled the risk of breakthrough cases after 5 years and increased the risk of breakthrough cases by 6 times after 9 years.41 Although this study suggests immune decline, the initial immune failure in children after receiving one dose of varicella vaccine may also be another important reason.42,43 A cross-sectional survey of varicella antibodies in healthy populations conducted in Beijing in 2012 found that among 165 children aged 1–9 who had received one dose of varicella vaccine, the 1–3 year old group, 4–6 year old group, and 7–9 year old group all showed a decrease in antibody levels 2–3 years after vaccination.44 The above was the main reason why the majority of cases after the strategy change occurred in vaccinated individuals.

Shortening the interval between two-dose VarV can improve vaccine efficacy, significantly reducing breakthrough varicella and outbreaks in preschool children.45 Numerous epidemic studies have shown that two-dose VarV provides better protection against varicella than one-dose VarV.46-48 In a comparison of vaccine efficacy among children and adolescents in Qatar, one-dose VarV showed a 56% reduction in varicella infection compared to unvaccinated, while two-dose VarV reduced the risk by 86%.47 Since 1995, the United States has been widely vaccinated against varicella, reducing hospitalization and mortality rates by 94.0% and 97.0%, respectively, for people under 50 years old.24 The one-dose VarV program (1995–2006) reduced the incidence rate and serious consequences of varicella by 71.0%–90.0%, while the two-dose VarV improved the effectiveness of VarV against varicella by at least 10.0%.49 In addition, a prospective randomized clinical trial found that the vaccine efficacy of two-dose VarV was 98.0%, and the risk of infection was three-fold lower than that of one-dose VarV.50 In our study, we also found that after adjusting for two-dose VarV immunization strategy, children had a lower risk of varicella infection, which was similar to these studies. It has been sufficiently demonstrated that the two-dose VarV’s immunization strategy for varicella can effectively reduce the varicella incidence and alleviate the disease burden.

In our study, we found that the majority of cases after the strategy change occurred in vaccinated individuals. A study of varicella outbreak in a highly-vaccinated school population in Beijing, China, further confirmed that children who received two-dose VarV may be infected with varicella, and the moderate coverage may not provide sufficient population immunity to prevent the outbreak.51 This investigation suggested that the lack of isolation of the varicella cases may have been the main factor contributing to the outbreak. It significantly emphasizes the importance for timely isolation in preventing outbreaks in two-dose era, even though cases of breakthrough varicella are likely less contagious than unvaccinated cases. Besides, according to the recommendations of the WHO, increasing and maintaining the two-dose VarV coverage rates as high as 80% or above may be more helpful in preventing varicella outbreaks or further reducing the duration and scale of outbreaks.1 Not surprisingly, this also reminds us not to rely solely on two-dose VarV to prevent and control varicella outbreaks, traditional epidemic control measures are still important.

Studies have found that vaccination with VarV can not only reduce varicella infection in children but also affect the clinical manifestations of children with varicella.52 In a study of varicella cases among children in Tianjin, China, it was found that among children without a history of VarV, those who received one or two doses of VarV had severe rash rates of 51.6%, 43.2%, and 5.2%, hospitalization rates of 7.5%, 2.9%, and 1.6%, and average medical expenses of 284 RMB, 218 RMB, and 200 RMB, respectively.53 The investigation on the economic burden of varicella cases among school-age children in Shenzhen, China, also showed that the economic burden of vaccinated one-dose VarV was higher than that of unvaccinated children.54 It can be concluded that vaccination can alleviate some clinical symptoms of varicella and reduce diagnosis and treatment costs. Moreover, an increase in the number of immunization doses can reduce the fever rate and rash range of varicella cases. At present, there is a lack of systematic and comprehensive comparative studies on the clinical manifestations of children infected with varicella who have not been vaccinated or have received one or two doses of the vaccine in Quzhou. Besides, economic analysis showed that outpatient surgery and medication costs for children aged 0–3 with varicella and complex patients are relatively high. Since the inclusion of VarV in NIPs for children, the hospitalization rate and medical expenses have significantly decreased by 15.0%.55 Our study lacks data on hospitalization rates and medical expenses before and after immunization strategy changes and only conducted population-level analysis. Further economic benefit analysis is needed in the future.

To sum up, our study had the following limitations. Firstly, CISDCP is a passive surveillance system with a certain underreporting rate, and the varicella incidence may be underestimated. Second, all varicella cases were diagnosed clinically without laboratory diagnosis, the use of VarV may lead to unclear clinical features in some cases, which may result in clinical diagnostic omissions or errors. Third, due to the migration of children between different regions, their immunization history cases cannot be queried, which may lead to some information errors. In addition, this study adopted a retrospective approach, which may result in incomplete data collection, non inclusion in the study, and potentially inaccurate data, leading to selection bias and information bias. The small portion of children’s VarV vaccination information comes from parental recollection, which may lead to recall bias and a shortage of vaccination doses, resulting in a possibility of underestimation of vaccination rate. In addition, China has been reporting varicella cases in its infectious disease surveillance information system since 2005, but the infectious disease surveillance systems don’t typically have high notification efficiency (~30%).56 As for varicella, this is because many patients have never sought care or report; and unless complications occur, secondary cases in the family rarely receive treatment or report. At the beginning of the varicella season, medical staff tend to report cases, but actual reporting rate declines once the medical staff believes that the varicella season has begun. Due to these reasons, the lower overall case reporting rate may affect mortality and severity. If only severe cases are reported, the overall burden is unknown. Furthermore, although we considered numerous confounders, residual confounding, such as changes in disease surveillance, reporting, or health-care utilization, as well as the clinical severity of varicella cases before and after the change in strategy, may still have interfered. Finally, due to the lack of funding and cost data for the varicella vaccine, only population-level analyses were performed, and the cost-effectiveness analyses of the two-dose VarV immunization strategy cannot be conducted. As it is an important aspect of the inclusion of the VarV immunization strategy, this analysis will be needed in future studies.

Conclusion

After the implementation of the immunization strategy in 2014, the risk of varicella infection decreases, the peak age has shifted back, and students have become the main population of varicella infection. It is recommended that the two-dose VarV should be included in the national immunization schedule and the booster vaccine should be given approximately four years after the first dose.
