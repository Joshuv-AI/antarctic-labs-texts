Co‐Designing a Virtual Reality Intervention for Adolescents With Psychosis
Authors: Guri Holgersen, Anita Barsnes, Oda Bergum Pedersen, Tine Nordgreen, Irene Bircow Elgen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimInvolving patients and clinicians in creating digital mental health treatments can ensure engaging, acceptable, and feasible interventions. Co‐design is particularly important when developing treatments for young people suffering from severe mental health disorders. The purposes of this paper are to (1) provide a detailed example of a co‐design procedure; and (2) offer guidance and recommendations to other researchers and mental health professionals who are seeking to co‐design digital treatments for adolescents with severe mental health disorders.MethodsThe co‐design procedure was structured into three phases which served as a framework: Phase 1 Identify and defining the problem; Phase 2 Creating the intervention; Phase 3 Initial testing. During the three phases we used self‐administered questionnaires, qualitative interviews, focus group interviews, standardised clinical interviews, and workshops.ResultsA total of 62 participants were involved in the co‐design procedure. The participants were involved in phases depending on aim and need. The co‐design procedure led to the development of a VR‐assisted CBTp treatment for adolescents with psychosis to be tested in future clinical trials. This paper highlights the importance of involving adolescents, clinical expertise, and software developers in planning and development of this intervention.ConclusionsThis co‐designing procedure yields support to researchers and mental health professionals aiming to co‐design digital treatments for adolescents with severe mental health disorders.Keywords: adolescent, co‐designing, cognitive behavioural therapy, psychosis, virtual reality

Introduction

The use of virtual reality (VR) technology in the treatment of mental health disorders has grown exponentially the last decade (Li et al. 2017; Schloss et al. 2025). VR is used to treat a variety of conditions including depression, anxiety, autism spectrum disorders, and psychosis (Park et al. 2019; Schloss et al. 2025). For people experiencing psychosis, VR has been successfully used in treating various symptoms including paranoid ideations, hallucinations, and cognitive and social deficits (Bisso et al. 2020; Rus‐Calafell et al. 2018). Several studies show that VR is feasible to treat social functioning in psychosis (Riches et al. 2021). The VR technology enables users to become fully immersed in a simulated world, offering a safe and effective means of training life skills and simulating real‐world experiences (Li et al. 2017; Schloss et al. 2025). For adolescents, VR provides an opportunity to confront challenges and fears within the peer social context in a less threatening setting with fewer social risks (Parrish et al. 2016; Schloss et al. 2025).

Despite the widespread availability of using technology in treatments there are several barriers to implementation in routine care (Bucci et al. 2018; Torous et al. 2018; Vis et al. 2018). Some of the most frequent barriers are lack of acceptance and engagement by patients and clinicians (Taylor et al. 2021; Topooco et al. 2017; Vis et al. 2018). A potential solution is to involve the stakeholders in the conception, design and testing of the intervention (Birckhead et al. 2019; Goodwin et al. 2016; Mummah et al. 2016).

Co‐designing an intervention is defined as a process where the potential users and stakeholders are actively involved across the entire development of the intervention, helping to ensure the intervention meets the user's needs and preferences (Thabrew et al. 2018). This requires a shift from the traditional expert‐led development work (Hodson et al. 2019), where people with mental health disorders are first involved once development is completed (Bucci et al. 2019). Co‐designing digital interventions is particularly important when developing treatments for people with severe mental health disorders, as they often can have challenges (e.g., cognitive impairment) that may affect the way they can engage in novel interventions (Ben‐Zeev et al. 2013). It is also important to involve children and adolescents in the co‐designing process as designing digital treatment for them require special developmental and age‐appropriate considerations (Bevan Jones et al. 2020; Thabrew et al. 2018). Additional to the importance of co‐designing intervention (Bucci et al. 2018; Halldorsson et al. 2021) it is also recommended that the development process is published in order to allow transparency (Halldorsson et al. 2021).

Although there is a range of approaches to intervention development (O'Cathain et al. 2019), detailed descriptions of the development are rarely seen (Ben‐Zeev et al. 2013). There is especially a notable lack of detailed reporting on co‐design processes involving children and adolescents (Bevan Jones et al. 2020; McGovern et al. 2025). Thus, there is an urgent need to understand how co‐design is managed, as well as establishing a practical framework to ensure young stakeholders are meaningfully involved and empowered throughout the process (McGovern et al. 2025). Reporting and evaluation of the co‐design processes themselves are needed to identify areas of potential improvement and to determine the impact of research co‐design (Peters et al. 2024). The purposes of this paper are to (1) provide a detailed example of a co‐design procedure; and (2) offer guidance and recommendations to other researchers and mental health professionals who are seeking to co‐design digital treatments for adolescents with severe mental health disorders.

Materials and Methods

The co‐design procedure presented in this paper was conducted at Haukeland University Hospital in Bergen, Norway between 2019 and 2024. The project was reviewed and approved by the Norwegian Regional Committees for Medical and Health Research Ethics (REK 95428).

Participants

Adolescents, mental health professionals, researchers and software developers were invited to participate in the co‐design process:

Adolescents

Adolescents aged 13–18 years with lived experience of psychosis were recruited to provide insights into treatment needs and challenges. Tailoring the intervention based on their inputs may ensure a more relevant, engaging, and usable treatment. The adolescents were recruited from a specialised early intervention in psychosis outpatient unit. To maintain their vulnerability, they were required to have been engaged in treatment for at least 4 months, allowing sufficient time to develop a mental health crisis plan which ensures safety during and after participating in the co‐design process. In addition, researchers and software developers met with the adolescents in familiar surroundings at the outpatient clinic, and the adolescents were given the opportunity to have a parent or therapist present. In addition, they had to sign consent forms to participate, with those aged 13–15 the project was required to provide additional parental consent.

Adolescents (13–18 years) not receiving treatment for a mental health disorder were recruited to provide insight into adolescents' real‐world settings, ensuring the VR scenarios align with communication styles, preferences, and cultural context. This may ensure the intervention is more likely to be adopted and sustained. These participants were recruited through friends and colleagues from the research team.

Voice actors aged 13–18 years were also involved in the co‐design process aiming to give personality, emotion, and depth to the virtual characters. These adolescents were recruited through an add on Facebook.

Mental Health Professionals

Mental health professionals working at the specialised outpatient early intervention unit were also participating in the co‐design process. The unit consists of an interprofessional team employing clinical psychologists, specialised mental health nurses, clinical social workers, and psychiatrists. These participants were involved to ensure that the VR treatment was not only clinically sound but also ethically and practically effective. Their involvement may also increase the likelihood that they will trust and recommend the treatment to their patients. In addition, mental health professionals working with other adolescent psychiatric disorders were recruited. They were involved to ensure that the treatment aligns with more general clinical experiences.

Research Team

Researcher were involved in the co‐design process to navigate ethical considerations, ensure that the VR‐treatment was grounded in evidence‐based practises, and translate insights from stakeholders into structured and testable components. The researchers had clinical experience, and the co‐design process was led by the researcher aiming to balance authentic user input with clinical and scientific validity.

Software Developer

Software engineers from the hospital's own ICT department were recruited to develop the VR application. Bachelor and master students at the Western Norway University of Applied Sciences were recruited to assist the development.

Procedures

There is a range of approaches to involve stakeholders in the co‐development of technologies, but these methods need to be tailored according to the specific users, the digital technology and setting (Bevan Jones et al. 2020). Due to lack of an appropriate framework for co‐designing VR treatment for adolescents with psychosis, we utilised a blended approach. Based on literature search, supplemented by recommendations from established experts, we chose to tailor the co‐design procedure based on inputs from The Person‐Based Approach for developing health interventions (Yardley et al. 2015), Participatory design of evidence‐based online youths mental health interventions (Hagen et al. 2012) and Recommendations for methodology for clinical VR‐trials in health care (Birckhead et al. 2019). We chose approaches where interventions are developed through direct involvement from the stakeholders combined with insights from the existing evidence base (Hagen et al. 2012; Yardley et al. 2015). Guidance on best practises for VR design in health care was chosen aiming to ensure scientific rigour in the development and evaluation of the VR application (Birckhead et al. 2019).

Based on inputs from these approaches we chose to structure the co‐design procedure into three phases which served as a framework (Table 1). First, we aimed to identify and define the problem the VR‐intervention should solve. The second phase dealt with creating the VR‐intervention and the third phase was about initial testing. During the three phases we used literature search, self‐administered questionnaires, qualitative interviews, focus group interview, standardised clinical interviews, and co‐design workshops. The methods of involvement varied depending on the context and the questions needed to be answered. Decisions regarding which stakeholders to involve and at what stage were made by the research team. The involvement of adolescents with lived experience of psychosis was discussed with the adolescent's therapist and a mental health crisis plan was used to moderate the involvement.

Results

Participants

A total of 62 participants were involved in the co‐design: 18 adolescents with lived experience of psychosis, 15 mental health professionals, 17 adolescents without psychotic disorders, 3 software engineers, 4 computer students and 5 researchers. The participants were involved in phases depending on aim and need.

Phase 1—Identify and Defining the Problem

In Phase 1 we identified the conceptual framework ensuring that each decision was grounded in theory and aligned with the overall therapeutic goals. Based on the needs of key stakeholders, we identified the areas of focus and determined how to frame the intervention to be meaningful for adolescents with psychosis.

The Conceptual Framework

The conceptual framework for developing a VR‐intervention for adolescents with psychosis was identified through a close collaboration between the research team and the mental health professionals working at the specialised outpatient early intervention unit. An initial theory on how the VR‐intervention should be framed was based on clinical experiences; adolescents' social functioning was impacted not solely by psychotic symptoms, but also by reduced confidence in their social abilities. The existing evidence base revealed a lack of efficient treatments targeting social recovery (Devoe et al. 2019; Hodgekins et al. 2015) and promising results from VR‐studies (Bisso et al. 2020; Riches et al. 2021; Rus‐Calafell et al. 2018). Literature search confirmed the clinical assumptions (Vaskinn et al. 2015) and revealed an evidence gap regarding VR‐treatments targeting adolescents with early onset psychosis (Bisso et al. 2020; Halldorsson et al. 2021; Valmaggia et al. 2016). The initial hypothesis was that a VR‐intervention focusing on exposure‐based social training would improve adolescents' social self‐efficacy and additionally reduce psychotic symptoms, increase social functioning, and improve quality of life (Pot‐Kolder et al. 2018; Rus‐Calafell et al. 2018; Vaskinn et al. 2015).

Initiated by the mental health professionals, it was decided that the first edition of the intervention should be VR‐assisted treatment. A VR‐assisted treatment would give the therapist the opportunities to tailor the scenario according to the adolescents' needs. The request was to have the opportunity to choose between different social reactions according to the patient's function and level of anxiety. In line with guidelines, Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Psychosis (CBTp) would serve as the theoretical framework (Helsedirektoratet 2013; NICE 2014).

Defining the Need

Next step was to understand what adolescents with lived experience of psychosis perceived as a problem regarding social situations. To ensure that we obtained the information necessary to address our research question we created a self‐report questionnaire. The questionnaire was based on two pre‐existing forms: The Mobility Inventory for Agoraphobia (Chambless et al. 1985) and Revised Self‐Efficacy Scale (McDermott 1995). The adolescents were asked to indicate how often 15 social situations would create discomfort using a 5 points Likert scale ranging from 1 = Never to 5 = Always (Bradburn et al. 2004). Adolescents were asked by their therapist to fill out the questionnaire and given the opportunity to submit anonymously. A total of 21 adolescents were invited to respond to the questionnaire and 11 submitted their answers. The following situations had the highest score: Start a conversation with a stranger; Introduce yourself to a stranger; Ask a stranger a question; Go on a date; Small talk; Talking to a group of people. The answers were converted into the following three VR‐scenarios: “Talking to strangers”, “Talking to a group of peers” and “Go on a date”.

After defining the areas of focus, we needed to understand how the social situations should be framed to be meaningful for adolescents with psychosis. First, adolescents with lived experience of psychosis were asked to elaborate how the selected social situations would create distress. We conducted five individual interviews. The adolescents talked about own experiences but also reflected on how in general these situations would create distress for adolescents. E.g., on input from an adolescent on the scenario “Talking to a group of peers”: “I don't like all the attention. Especially walking up to a group is scarry”. Secondly, a focus group with five mental health professionals was conducted aiming to gather broader insights regarding the selected social scenarios. Based on clinical experience they shared opinions and thoughts on how the social situations should be framed. E.g., on input from a mental health professional on the scenario “Talking to a group of peers”: “They should have a prepared topic to discuss or a question to ask”. All answers were transcribed and used when creating the intervention (phase 2).

Additionally, a qualitative study was conducted aiming to investigate the acceptability of VR‐assisted CBTp amongst adolescents with psychosis (Holgersen et al. 2023). The study concluded that VR could be an acceptable intervention given the adolescents' comfort with technology and the opportunity to confront their fears in less threatening virtual social settings with fewer social risks (Holgersen et al. 2023).

Phase 1 helped us to identify key issues, needs, and challenges the intervention had to address by exploring the existing evidence‐base and the stakeholders' experiences and perspectives. Phase 2—Creating the Intervention

Phase 2 was about designing and developing initial prototypes of VR environments and scenarios that replicate situations where adolescents with psychosis might encounter difficulties.

Designing the VR‐Scenarios

Several co‐design workshops with software developers and the research team were conducted to jointly design the VR‐scenarios. Based on the results from phase 1, the social situations were made multifaceted to include more challenging situations and were to be situated on a light rail (talk to a stranger), in a schoolyard (talk to a group of peers), and in a café (go on a date). Manuscripts for each VR‐scenario were written consisting of detailed descriptions of the scene, participants, expressions, reactions, and surroundings.

To confirm, adjust and modify the language the manuscripts were given to five adolescents not suffering from a psychotic disorder. They were given eight questions and asked to give written feedback. Examples of questions: “If you were in one of these conversations, would you talk/ask in the way written here? If not, in what way would you have spoken?” Example from feedback given by a 15‐year‐old boy on the schoolyard scenario: “It can be like this in a schoolyard. We talk about these things. The problem often is misunderstandings; that one person does not fully understand what the other means”.

Software Development

The system was developed in accordance with design principles for developing e‐health interventions that are user‐friendly for persons with severe mental illness. For the developers to consider the cognitive needs of the users, lecturers on adolescence and psychosis were given by the research team.

Adolescent voice actors were involved in the development of the audio software. Before recording the voices, the actors were asked to test and give feedback on a prototype of the VR‐scenario. They were also encouraged to change the scripts to make the conversations more natural.

Phase 3—Initial Testing

Phase 3 is ongoing. We are conducting a usability study aiming to initially explore whether virtual social situations can be used as a treatment modality for adolescents suffering from a psychotic disorder. Research questions are: Does the social virtual situations create simulator sickness? Do the social virtual situations increase psychotic symptoms?

Participants and Procedure

The following inclusion criteria are applied: (a) age between 13 and 18 years, (b) diagnosed with a psychotic disorder (World Health 1992), and (c) competent to consent. Exclusion criteria are (a) epilepsy or other neurological disease, (b) experiencing frequent motion sickness/nausea/vomiting, (c) IQ < 75 and (d) ongoing substance abuse. The participants are given randomised paths through the virtual reality environments. This is to ensure that distress emerging is not linked to the order the participant is testing the VR‐scenarios. Data are being collected pre‐test, after each VR‐scenario and post‐test, using self‐report questionnaires, standardised clinical interviews, and usability questions.

Measures

The self‐report questionnaire developed in phase 1 and Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) (Steer and Beck 1997) are used to evaluate the participants' degree of discomfort and anxiety before testing the VR‐scenarios. The Social Self‐Efficacy Scale (SSES) (Muris 2001) is assessed post‐testing to do a primarily assessment on the correlation between lack of confidence in the ability to master social situations and the degree of discomfort. The 6‐items version of the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS‐6) (Østergaard et al. 2016) is used pre and post to evaluate if the social virtual situations increase psychotic symptoms. Whilst the Simulator Sickness Questionnaire (SSQ) (Kennedy et al. 1993) is used to measure if the social virtual situations create simulator sickness and the Gatineau Presence Questionnaire (GPQ) (Laforest et al. 2016) to measure the feeling of presence experienced in the virtual environment. Post‐testing will also include an end‐to‐end user experience assessment to provide insight into any changes that need to be made to the workflow and to investigate tolerability.

Discussion

This paper provides detailed examples of the decision‐making process, methods, and findings that occurred when co‐designing a VR‐assisted CBTp treatment for adolescents with psychosis. This paper highlights the importance of involving adolescents, clinical expertise, and software developers in planning and development of this intervention.

We experienced that it was especially important to involve adolescents with lived experience of psychosis when we identified and defined the social difficulties the VR‐intervention should aim to solve. Clinicians' experiences from treatment were that the adolescents' main social difficulties were related to friends and family. Contrary to what we were expecting, the adolescents' answers revealed that social situations involving strangers had the highest score. Another interesting result from the co‐design was that the adolescents' contributions to the manuscript were shaped by typical youth conversations rather than being influenced by psychotic ideations. Further, to involve clinical expertise emphasised some important aspects. Especially the clinicians' views on the therapists' involvement; Preferring to control the virtual situations themself. This could be due to lack of VR‐experiences, uncertainty regarding the therapeutic effect without their guidance, or concerns regarding the patient‐groups' vulnerability. These results highlight how a co‐design procedure can ensure that the core ingredients in the intervention reflect the real needs, experiences, and insights of those who will use it. By exploring these perspectives, we have more likely developed an engaging, feasible and efficient intervention (Bevan Jones et al. 2020; Bucci et al. 2019; Yardley et al. 2015).

Further, the involvement of the software developer in all phases of the co‐design procedure was important to create consistency and underlines the value of interprofessional collaboration. Combining technology, psychology, and research is not just about merging different theoretical approaches; it is also about team members contributing diverse skills, knowledge, and perspectives from their respective fields. Team collaboration is emphasised as fundamental for the collective design of a VR treatment (Birckhead et al. 2019). The interprofessional collaboration enabled a feedback loop that continuously informed the design process and addressed emerging problems. This required, amongst other things, a common understanding when communicating across roles and professions (McLaney et al. 2022). The co‐design procedure demonstrates how a logic model can serve as a dynamic communication tool within an interprofessional team. The logic model was used to navigate towards a shared understanding and provided the team with opportunities to evaluate and make necessary adjustments throughout the process. The positive experiences associated with using a logic model are consistent with recommendations outlined in several established frameworks (Peters et al. 2024; Skivington et al. 2021; Yardley et al. 2015).

The co‐design procedure encountered several challenges that need to be addressed. One of the major barriers has been securing funds and resources as the project expanded. This aliens with other co‐designing projects experiences (Bevan Jones et al. 2020; Realpe et al. 2020) and a potential solution is being clear from the outset about the justification and timescale for this rigorous approach (Bevan Jones et al. 2020). Another challenge was balancing the empowerment of adolescents to influence the development whilst simultaneously being aware of their vulnerability and the potential risk of an increase in psychotic symptoms. A plan was developed to secure common guidelines, but the most important measure was facilitating predictability and feeling safe. This was done by meeting the adolescents at their outpatient clinic and giving them the opportunity to have a parent or their therapist present. A systematic review examining the use of co‐design with young people in digital mental health interventions highlights a lack of detailed reporting on how power dynamics are managed within these studies (McGovern et al. 2025). Thus, a strength in this co‐design project is the reporting on how power dynamics can be addressed when working with adolescents with psychosis. Lastly, this project lacks a systematic evaluation of the process itself making it difficult to determine the impact of research co‐design. Since we did not systematically evaluate the co‐design process, we do not know whether it was the involvement of stakeholders at a particular phase or the continuous involvement across all phases of design that offers benefits to design research. Unfortunately, this has been largely overlooked in previous research (McGovern et al. 2025; Peters et al. 2024). Future works should incorporate a more structured evaluation of the co‐design process as recommended by frameworks like the Co‐design Evaluation Framework (Peters et al. 2024). Although research on the involvement of adolescents within the co‐design process is limited (McGovern et al. 2025), describing the procedure and reporting findings from the co‐design that informed the intervention may offer guidance to others developing digital treatments for this population.

Conclusions

This co‐designing procedure yields support to researchers and mental health professionals aiming to co‐design digital treatments for adolescents with severe mental health disorders.
