Global monthly CMIP6-downscaled high-resolution (1 km) near-surface air temperature dataset (1950–2100)
Authors: Xuewen Lei, Qingyan Meng, Ming Luo, Linlin Zhang, Mijia Yin, Longfei Liu, Qikang Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTemperature projections from general circulation models (GCMs), serving as an important approach of understanding future global warming, are essential for developing adaptation and mitigation strategies of climate change. However, the coarse spatial resolutions (~1–3°) limit their effectiveness at fine-scale (e.g., intra-urban) research. Here, we produced MoCHAT, a global monthly CMIP6-downscaled high-resolution (1 km) near-surface air temperature dataset. We utilized delta downscaling method to generate MoCHAT based on NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 and WorldClim. MoCHAT encompasses mean, maximum, and minimum air temperature of 16 GCMs. It covers both the historical period (1950–2014) and future scenarios (2015–2100) under three Shared Socioeconomic Pathways (SSPs) scenarios (SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, and SSP5-8.5). Validation with meteorological station observations and existing high-resolution climatic datasets showed that the mean absolute errors for these variables range from 1.60 to 2.38 K and overall biases below 2.0 K. With sufficiently long span and fine resolution, MoCHAT breaks through data resolution limitations and provides solid support for global fine-scale heat risk research.Subject terms: Projection and prediction, Climate sciences

Background & Summary

Global warming is posing enormous challenges to natural ecosystems and human societies, causing more frequent heat extremes and threatening tolerance limits of human, animals, and plants1–3. Synergistic effects of global warming and rapid urbanization lead to persistent higher temperatures in urban areas than others, known as Urban Heat Island (UHI) effect4,5. UHI effect results in greater heat stress for urban populations6–8, and these adverse effects are projected to further exacerbate in the future9–11. High-resolution (~1 km) temperature data are crucial for assessing climate impacts studies (e.g., heat-related mortality12, agriculture yields13 and biodiversity changes14). Recent advancements in high-resolution global temperature datasets have provided valuable insights15–18, but their relatively short temporal coverage limits their ability to fully characterize temperature changes from past to future. By contrast, general circulation models (GCMs), simulating climate scenarios over extended time scales, are the primary tools for global warming impact assessment11,19,20. However, the coarse resolution of raw GCM outputs (~1–3°) hampers their ability to represent fine-scale temperature variation.

Several global- or regional-scale downscaled datasets (Table 1) derived from the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) have been developed, such as NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 (0.25°, global)21, Bias-corrected CMIP6 dataset (1.25°, global)22, CLIMEA-BCUD (0.1°, East Asia)23 and BASD-CMIP6-PE (0.1°, Peruvian and Ecuadorian river basins)24. However, these downscaled datasets with spatial resolution of 0.1–1° are still too coarse to provide precise spatial details and variations for inner-city research25–28. CHELSA V2.129 and WorldClim30 provide high-resolution (1 km) temperature projections in future scenarios, but their temporal resolutions (e.g., 20-year monthly average) are insufficient to capture long-term seasonal trend. Generally, a global-scale, long-term and high-spatiotemporal-resolution temperature dataset is still scarce.

To improve spatial resolution of temperature data from GCMs, recent years have witnessed various downscaling methods, which typically fall into two categories: dynamical and statistical31–33. Dynamical downscaling, employing Regional Climate Model (RCM) nesting within GCM34, can generate a fine-resolution (<50 km) temperature dataset. However, due to complex physical simulation processes, RCMs are computationally expensive making them difficult to create high-resolution, long-term, and global-scale data35. Alternatively, statistical downscaling method provides equivalent solution but with much less computational demand36. These methods derive statistical relationships between predictors and predictand (i.e., coarse GCMs and fine-grained observations) and construct high-resolution outputs. Moreover, the downscaled outputs often have higher accuracy than original data with comparison to weather station data37. Among the statistical downscaling methods, delta downscaling method is computationally efficient and relatively robust towards the inherent bias of the models, making it well suited for downscaling global temperature dataset at high resolution29,38,39. It has been widely used to generate global multi-year average temperature dataset29,30 or regional monthly temperature dataset (e.g., China)37,40, which have proven invaluable in fields such as macroecology over the last decade25,41–44.

In this study, we utilized delta downscaling method to produce a global monthly near-surface air temperature projection dataset (MoCHAT), with high resolution (~1 km) from 1950 to 2100. Our MoCHAT dataset encompasses three variables, i.e., mean temperature (tas), maximum temperature (tasmax) and minimum temperature (tasmin). The coarse-resolution data are derived from 16 distinct GCMs of NEX-GDDP-CMIP6. We conducted a comprehensive accuracy assessment to quantify the advantages of MoCHAT in comparison with the original data and other existing downscaled datasets. Furthermore, we investigated the spatiotemporal patterns of MoCHAT and highlighted its potential applications in thermal environment at finer spatiotemporal scale in the future. MoCHAT is expected to provide precise and practical information for policymakers and urban plannings to mitigate and prevent heat stress. Finally, we discussed the limitations in this study.

Methods

Data source

Coarse-resolution GCM data (0.25°) were obtained from the NASA Earth Exchange Global Daily Downscaled Projections, CMIP6 (NEX-GDDP-CMIP6, available at 10.7917/OFSG3345)21. This product was produced by a daily variant of the monthly bias correction/spatial disaggregation method, which excels in describing the climatology and surpassing the performance of raw GCM simulations. NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 provides seamless daily climate projections over global land for 35 GCMs of historical period and future scenarios. It improves CMIP6 simulations from coarse-scale to regional-to-local scales with an unchanged long-term trend. This process does not introduce new information beyond what is already present in the original CMIP6 data, but it offers a more detailed representation of regional climate variables for impact studies45–48. However, this downscaled dataset with spatial resolution of 0.25° is still too coarse to provide precise spatial details for inner-city research25–28. In this study, we conducted further downscaling on NEX-GDDP-CMIP6. We focused on three temperature variables, i.e., mean surface air temperature (tas), maximum surface air temperature (tasmax), and minimum surface air temperature (tasmin), across both the historical period (1950–2014) and future scenarios (2015–2100). To account for a wide range of potential greenhouse gas emission changes in the future, from low to very high greenhouse gas emissions, we chose three SSP scenarios, i.e., SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.549,50. We selected 16 GCMs based on their availability of requisite data for the target variables and timesteps (Table 2). Other GCMs from the NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 dataset did not provide the necessary data for the target variables or the required timesteps, and therefore, they could not be included. Specifically, we used a single member from each model for a fair comparison, typically the first member ‘r1i1p1r1’.

Historical monthly climatology averages (1970–2000) from WorldClim version 2.1 dataset were utilized as the baseline data for delta downscaling (available at https://www.worldclim.org/data/worldclim21.html)30. WorldClim, with high resolution (~1 km) and high global cross-validation correlations for temperature (>0.99), was produced by observation data from between 9000 and 60,000 weather stations over 1970–2000 worldwide. Weather station observations were interpolated by thin-plate splines algorithm with variant covariates (i.e., maximum and minimum land surface temperature, cloud cover, elevation and distance to the coast), which can effectively improve estimates of temperature data.

Both meteorological-observed data and high-resolution gridded dataset were used in accuracy assessment. The meteorological-observed daily near-surface air temperatures in historical period (1950–2014) comes from the Global Surface Summary of the Day (GSOD, available at https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/metadata/landing-page/bin/iso?id=gov.noaa.ncdc:C00516), containing over 9000 weather stations’ data globally51,52. We computed monthly temperature time series for each station, discarding daily observations for months with fewer than 15 days. Additionally, if the total number of valid days of a station falls below 180 in a calendar year, the entire station is omitted (spatial distribution of GSOD stations see Supplementary Fig. 1). We conducted a spatial performance comparison between our downscaled results and the Climatologies at High resolution for the Earth’s Land Surface Areas version 2.1 (CHELSA V2.1) dataset (available at https://chelsa-climate.org/downloads/)29. The CHELSA V2.1 consists of a monthly temperature dataset with high-resolution (~1-km) over 1980–2019, by employing CHELSA temperature downscaling algorithm to downscale ERA-Interim temperature. CHELSA V2.1 exhibits accuracy comparable to other temperature datasets (e.g., CRU53, 0.5°), while offering a higher spatial resolution. It should be noted that, existing high-resolution global temperature datasets derived from satellite data typically cover a relatively short temporal coverage (2001–2020)15,16, making them unsuitable for our accuracy assessment.

Delta downscaling process

These changes are then integrated with high-resolution reference data to generate downscaled outputs40,54. Accuracy evaluation

We employed four statistic metrics to evaluate the performance of downscaled dataset, including the Pearson’s correlation coefficient (Cor), the mean absolute error (MAE), the root mean square error (RMSE) and bias55. Correlation coefficient quantifies the relationship between downscaled and observed values. MAE measures the average absolute error between downscaled and observed values, while RMSE represents the square root of the average squared difference between them. Data Records

The MoCHAT dataset is available at the National Tibetan Plateau / Third Pole Environment Data Center (10.11888/Atmos.tpdc.301876, https://cstr.cn/18406.11.Atmos.tpdc.301876)56. It encompasses three temperature variables (i.e. tas, tasmax, and tasmin) from 16 diverse GCMs and their multi-model ensemble (for users’ convenience). The temporal ranges include the historical period (1950–2014) and three future emission scenarios (2015–2100), i.e., SSP1-2.6, SSP2-4.5, SSP5-8.5. This dataset is stored in NetCDF format that can be easily read by different programming languages such as R and Python. The filename includes the variable short name, temporal resolution, the respective model used, the respective model variant used, the climate scenario, spatial resolution, and the respective time variable of data, such as “tas_month_ACCESS-CM2_r1i1p1f1_historical_1 km_2001_01.nc”, parameters of the separate files are listed in Table 3.

Technical Validation

Overall accuracy assessment

To identify the downscaled performance, we implemented assessment for original NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 and MoCHAT datasets from 1950 to 2014 by comparing them with GSOD data (Fig. 2). Results show that MoCHAT performs well for all the variables (tas, tasmax and tasmin, hereafter). MAE of all GCMs for all the variables ranges from 1.60 to 2.17 K, 1.79 to 2.38 K and 1.73 to 2.29 K, respectively, while RMSE ranges from 2.34 to 3.06 K, 2.53 to 3.27 K, and 2.52 to 3.25 K. Additionally, we found that MoCHAT present higher accuracy than NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 for all variables, evidenced by lower MAE and RMSE, and a stronger correlation with observations. The multi-model ensemble (MME) created by assigning equal weights from all 16 GCMs performs the best for all variables with the lowest MAE and RMSE, and have the highest correlation coefficient, which is consistent with previous studies57. These results collectively denote that our downscaled results have high quality, reflecting effectiveness of downscaling process.

Accuracy assessment of three temperature variables of NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 and MoCHAT during 1950–2014. Results from all GCMs are evaluated against GSOD data. (a) tas: mean temperature, (b) tasmax: maximum temperature, (c) tasmin: minimum temperature.

Our analysis of the probability density functions (PDFs) reveals that both NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 and MoCHAT exhibit similar monthly distributions of GSOD data for tas, tasmax and tasmin (Fig. 3, Supplementary Figs. 2, 3). This similarity can be attributed to the fact that NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 has already undergone rigorous bias correction through the BCSD method before our downscaling processing. The BCSD method applied to NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 includes quantile mapping that adjusts the raw GCM outputs to match the statistical distribution of observational data. As a result, NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 effectively captures the PDFs of monthly temperatures. Our subsequent downscaling processes based on delta downscaling method preserves these well-calibrated statistical properties while enhancing spatial resolutions. This demonstrates that our downscaling process does not introduce additional distortions to the statistical properties of the bias-adjusted data.

Probability density of tas of NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 and MoCHAT in comparison with observations during 1950–2014. The continuous lines are probability density for all MoCHAT models (gray), MME of MoCHAT (blue) and MME of NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 (black). Red dotted line is probability density for GSOD data.

Accuracy assessment in different continents and climate regions

We comprehensively evaluated the annual accuracy of our downscaled data across six continents and four Köppen-Geiger climate regions (including equatorial, arid, temperate, and snow). Our results demonstrate that MoCHAT consistently surpasses NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 for all temperature variables across different regions, as evidenced by significantly lower MAE and RMSE values (Figs. 4, 5, Supplementary Figs. 4–13). Both datasets display distinct regional accuracy variations, with North America, Europe, and Asia exhibiting higher errors compared to other continents across all GCMs, where MAE and RMSE values of MoCHAT above 1.52 K and 2.77 K, respectively. These differences in accuracy may be attributed to the variations in climate backgrounds and topography of these continents. For instance, South America, which is dominated by tropical climate and covered with about 60% of its land below 300 m, exhibits the smallest spatial variations in temperature. In contrast, other continents (e.g., North America) contain diverse dominant climate background types and geomorphic types, contributing to greater spatial variability of temperature. The climate-type based assessment further reveals clear accuracy variations, with equatorial regions achieving the best performance (MAE of MoCHAT < 1.1 K) and snow-dominated regions showing the poorest results (MAE of MoCHAT > 2.5 K). These findings align with well-documented climate modeling challenges in regions with strong seasonal amplitude and complex cryosphere-atmosphere interactions58–60. In contrast, low-latitude regions exhibit more stable temperatures with minimal intra-annual variability, consequently yielding higher simulation accuracy. These systematic regional variations underscore the critical importance of accounting for geographical and climatic characteristics when assessing and interpreting climate model outputs.

Accuracy assessment in different seasons

Accuracy assessments in different seasons were conducted to reveal the capability of MoCHAT in depicting temperature seasonal fluctuations. In general, during winter months (December-January-February, DJF), RMSE and MAE are notably higher, yet they decrease as the weather warms, reaching their lowest in summer (June-July-August, JJA) for all the variables from all GCMs (Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 14). In contrast, correlation coefficients for all variables are relatively lower in summer months but increasing during other seasons. These seasonal variations highlight differences between continents (Supplementary Figs. 15–20). For example, Africa exhibited less pronounced seasonal variations (Supplementary Fig. 15), attributed to the weaker seasonal fluctuations of temperature in tropical areas. In South America, higher RMSE values were observed in summer compared to winter (Supplementary Fig. 19), indicating more homogeneous spatial variations of temperatures in winter than in summer.

Spatial distribution of biases

We also assessed spatial biases by comparing CHELSA V2.1 dataset with our MoCHAT from 1980 to 2014. MoCHAT performs well for all variables. tas and tasmin exhibit bias below 0.5 K and 1.0 K, respectively, for most GCMs. For tasmax, the average bias shows slightly higher, but still below 1.5 K (Fig. 7, Supplementary Figs. 21, 22). While there is a widespread overestimation for tas and tasmax and underestimation for tasmin for almost all GCMs, these biases are still reasonable in comparisons with state-of-the-art downscaled products (e.g., CHELSA-CMIP561) (Fig. 8, Supplementary Figs. 23, 24). Downscaled GCMs, such as ACCESS-ESM1-5 and EC-Earth3, show significant warm biases for all variables in most regions, particularly in Europe and northwest Asia. Other models exhibit varying degrees of overestimation or underestimation of annual temperatures across different land regions, though with smaller biases. In high-elevation regions, such as Greenland, the Tibetan Plateau, the Andes, and the Rocky Mountains, biases for all the variables across all GCMs surpass 4 K, indicating that elevation significantly affects downscaled accuracy. This might be a consequence of the inherent limitations posed by the coarse resolution of these GCMs, which inadequately capture the atmospheric circulation patterns over high-elevation narrow area62. The examination of average biases across global land and individual continents underscores the influence of land cover’s spatial heterogeneity on downscaled biases.

Spatiotemporal patterns

Taking the MME of tas, tasmax and tasmin, respectively in July 2010 as examples, the temperatures decrease from ~30° to higher latitudes (Fig. 9). Higher-elevation areas such as the Tibetan Plateau and Greenland exhibit lower temperature. Generally, the spatial variations of MoCHAT are consistent with the existing studies16,61. Comparison with two existing temperature datasets CHELSA V2.1 and ERA5-Land63 reveal that all three datasets exhibit consistent spatial patterns in topographically complex regions like Beijing (with its pronounced elevation gradients from mountains to plains) and Adelaide (featuring coastal-inland transitions) (Fig. 9f,g). This agreement suggests that the delta downscaling method for MoCHAT effectively preserves climatic signals while adding local topographic details. However, we observed underestimations by MoCHAT in relatively flat regions like Paris, particularly in rural areas (Fig. 9e). This bias likely stems from the integration of WorlClim data in our downscaling process. WorldClim integrates land surface temperatures, elevation, distance to the coast and cloud cover as interpolation variables. While this can generally improve estimates of temperature data in areas with sharp elevation changes, it also possibly tends to amplify local temperature contrasts in homogeneous landscapes due to their reliance on environmental lapse rate. Therefore, in flat terrains (e.g., Paris), this can lead to overcorrection of microclimate variations, whereas in areas with strong elevation gradients (e.g., Beijing or Adelaide), the topographic signal dominates and improves accuracy.

Spatial pattern of temperatures in July, 2010 based on the MME. Black polygons are the urban extents of 2010 extracted by using impervious area data82 and a surrounding buffer. ERA5-Land available at https://cds.climate.copernicus.eu/datasets/reanalysis-era5-land?tab=download.

The temporal evolutions of anomalies for all the variables under three scenarios indicate that the changes of warming trend in urban centers are consistent with the changes projected for global in previous studies64 (Supplementary Fig. 25). Before the 2050 s, warming trends of the three scenarios are similar to those of historical period, with only minor differences. However, differences in temperature changes among the three scenarios significantly increase after the 2050 s. The high-emission scenario (SSP5-8.5) exhibits the largest increasing trend, with a rate of 0.54 K/decade, 0.55 K/decade and 0.53 K/decade for tas, tasmax and tasmin. Persistent increase in annual mean temperature can also be found under the low- and mid-emission scenarios (SSP1-2.6 and SSP2-4.5), with rates ranging from 0.07–0.08 K/decade and 0.23–0.24 K/decade, respectively. Furthermore, we estimated variations of temperature and UHI intensity inside individual cities under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5. Here, we took Paris, one of the most densely populated cities in the world and characterized by predominantly flat topography, as an example. UHI intensity (UHII) is defined as the difference of tas between urban center and rural background. We found that the spatial patterns of temperature under SSP2-4.5 and SSP5-8.5 are generally consistent with those in historical period (Fig. 9e). The warming rate at rural background is slightly higher than that in urban center and suburban area (Fig. 10), which results in the UHII exhibiting a downward trend in the future, with the largest decreasing rate of −0.015 K/decade under SSP5-8.5 scenario. This may be attributed to the static urban extent in 2020 adopted to calculate future UHI intensity. That’s to say, some rural areas urbanized in the future are still treated as rural, which can potentially induce overestimated warming level in rural area and underestimation of total UHI intensity. Therefore, we encouraged users to use updated urban boundary when using our MoCHAT dataset in future UHI research.

Comparison with Kriging interpolation for delta change

To evaluate the influence of interpolation techniques on MoCHAT, we conducted a comparative analysis of bilinear interpolation and ordinary kriging method for processing 1-km delta changes across two topographically distinct metropolitan areas, i.e., Beijing (North China Plain) and Chongqing (Sichuan Basin) (Fig. 11). Our results demonstrate that while bilinear interpolation produces visible stratification due to its piecewise linear approximation, these do not significantly impact the downscaling results when integrated with WorldClim baseline climatology. Both interpolation approaches yield highly consistent temperature probability distributions and spatial patterns, while clearly capturing the characteristic temperature variations between plains and mountainous terrain. These findings align with Peng et al. who revealed that combining bilinear interpolation with delta downscaling method performs better than other interpolation methods, such as bicubic and nearest-neighbor interpolation, for downscaling temperature datasets37 and confirmed that the delta downscaling method demonstrates robust convergence across interpolation methods when combined with high-quality observational baselines.

Comparison with simply bilinear interpolated results

To evaluate the effectiveness of delta downscaling method, we also conducted a comparison between MoCHAT and directly bilinear interpolation from NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 (Bilinear-1 km, hereafter) (Fig. 12). As shown in the Rocky Mountains, the Alps and Hengduan Mountains, Bilinear-1 km fails to capture fine-scale topographic and climatic variations, producing overly smoothed patterns. In contrast, MoCHAT preserves local texture information, aligning closely with high-resolution reference data (CHELSA V2.1). Moreover, compared to GSOD observational data, MoCHAT achieves superior accuracy than Bilinear-1 km across all metrics, with RMSE and MAE less than 2.0 K and 1.3 K, respectively. These results demonstrate that the delta downscaling method effectively enhances spatial resolution while maintaining local temperature variations, outperforming a simple interpolation from 0.25-degree to 1-km resolution.

Comparison with existing downscaled datasets

Given the availability of existing downscaled datasets, we compared MoCHAT with CLIMATE-BCUD (0.1°, East Asia)23 and BCCAQ-CMIP6 (0.25°, global)35 across various scales, including the subcontinental scale (East Asia), city scale (the Greater Bay Area, GBA and Changsha-Zhuzhou-Xiangtan, CZT) (Fig. 13). The tas in July 2014 was selected as an example from the above three datasets. Generally, MoCHAT, CLIMATE-BCUD, and BCCAQ-CMIP6 depict analogous spatial distribution patterns on the intercontinental scale. Nevertheless, MoCHAT exhibits marked advantages, excelling in delineating intricate variations in tas distribution at city scale. Additionally, we also made a comparison with GSOD weather station records. Results show that CLIMATE-BCUD and BCCAQ-CMIP6 generally underestimate tas, while MoCHAT is in good agreement with the observations. Except for CLIMATE-BCUD and BCCAQ-CMIP6, other existing downscaled projections either have high resolution but are limited to the national or continental scale, or they have a large scale (global) but insufficient temporal resolution (multi-years’ averages) (Table 1). Therefore, our MoCHAT dataset offers advantages in both spatiotemporal resolution (1 km and monthly) and global coverage, thereby providing fundamental support for fine-scale urban climate studies under future scenarios.

Uncertainty and limitations

The annual extreme temperature projections derived from MoCHAT are presented in Fig. 14. The results reveal that the extreme temperature variations of MoCHAT generally align with observations, although some biases remain. Compared with the observations, tasmax increased by approximately 2.0 K, while tasmin decreased by about 0.5 K. Previous studies found delta downscaling method tends to underestimate minimum temperatures and overestimate maximum temperatures, which mainly attributed to the sensitivity of extreme temperatures to the selection of GCMs and downscaling techniques, as well as the fluctuations in climate variability signals during data processing37,38,65. In addition, the effects of extreme temperatures are partially mitigated after monthly mean processing65. However, given that the overall accuracy of MoCHAT is satisfied, with MAE ranging from 1.60 to 2.17 K (see Fig. 2), we believe that the dataset is reliable but should be used more cautiously when applying in investigating extreme temperatures.

In addition, although MoCHAT dataset exhibits relatively high accuracy across most global regions, there are still some regions observed with poor accuracy. These regions are evidenced with high elevation and complex terrain (e.g., Tibetan Plateau). We attribute this to the limited meteorological stations used to produce the gridded WorldClim dataset in these areas and the systematic bias in GCM outputs over mountainous regions66–68. WorldClim and NEX-GDDP-CMIP6 have implemented bias correction methods to improve accuracy of temperature estimation. For example, WorldClim has taken elevation and satellite-observed land surface temperature as covariates to improve accuracy of temperature estimation at high elevations and complex terrain locations. However, the impact of topography on temperature projection still cannot be effectively eliminated. Therefore, a more high-quality historical dataset and more useful bias correction method would further improve the accuracy of downscaled temperature projections.

Moreover, delta downscaling method only considers linear variations of temperature. However, nonlinear interactions among climatic factors may also influence temperature variations. Such as solar radiation, cloud covers, and winds, have inevitable impacts on temperature48,69–71. This limits its applicability in regions where the correlation between large-scale temperature patterns and local observation is weak34. Nowadays, there are some statistical methods to describe nonlinear relationship between temperature with local climatic observations and capture complex and abstract characteristics from climate data, i.e., machine learning72,73 and deep learning23,74. However, these methods require large computational demand, climate observations and relative covariates, making it hard to produce global-scale, long-term, high-resolution datasets. Thus, delta downscaling method with less resource and computational demand is still an efficient approach for global temperature dataset downscaling. Additionally, downscaled dataset typically exhibits higher accuracy than original dataset because of its ability to mitigate topographical effects75.

Furthermore, a significant limitation of the delta downscaling method is its inability to apply it in daily data. The delta downscaling approach assumes that climate variables follow a normal distribution. However, daily temperatures often exhibit skewed distributions due to the presence of extreme events (e.g., heatwaves), which deviate from normality. Thus, this method particularly relies on historical monthly climatological averages, leading to monthly outputs, rather than daily ones. As a result, the method is primarily suited for downscaling monthly data. Future research may explore the use of more efficient approaches (e.g., weather classification76,77 and weather generators78,79) to produce datasets with both high spatial and temporal resolution. Despite the aforementioned limitations, we believe our MoCHAT dataset holds extraordinary significance for research focusing on fine-scale future heat risk in the globe. With sufficiently long span and fine resolution, MoCHAT breaks through the data resolution limitations and provides solid support for understanding and mitigating the impacts of climate change on heat-related risks.

Usage Notes

MoCHAT includes three temperature variables from 16 GCM models with high resolution (~1 km), covering both historical periods and three future scenarios, demonstrating broad applicability. MoCHAT can support temperature assessments at fine scales (e.g., city level) from past to future, monitor urban heat island effects, and provide data support for urban heat mitigation strategies. However, as the uncertainty and limitations emphasized above, we advise users to be mindful of the uncertainties inherent in our downscaling processes and not interpret our dataset as precise temperature predictions for particular regions. Instead, MoCHAT should be regarded as high-resolution and bias-corrected temperature projections, which is a compromise between coarse-resolution GCM outputs and fine-scale climate research.
