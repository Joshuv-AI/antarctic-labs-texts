The Impact of Interventional Radiology Educational Webinars during the Coronavirus Disease 2019 Pandemic and Beyond―The Asia Pacific Society of Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiology (APSCVIR) Experience
Authors: Dorothea Akousua Amoh, Bien Soo Tan, Joemel Francisco, Deborah Halim, Yasuaki Arai, Andrew Holden, Luke Toh, Rickie Cheng, Alfred Bingchao Tan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose: To describe a series of webinars by the Asia Pacific Society of Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiology as a substitute for educational outreach activities during the coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic and beyond.Material and Methods: A retrospective review was performed for 10 webinars organized by Asia Pacific Society of Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiology between July 2021 and February 2024. The target audience was interventional radiology physicians. After each virtual session, the recordings of these sessions were posted online and made accessible via a link on the Asia Pacific Society of Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiology website. Link access to these recorded seminars was also emailed to participants. Descriptive statistics were used for the analysis of the quantitative data.Results: A total of 10 sessions were conducted. There was a median of 258 registered participants, a median attendance of 126, a total attendance of 1,302, and a registration-to-participation conversion percentage of 54%. The majority of attendees (median ≥94%) were Asia Pacific Society of Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiology members. Two-thirds of participants attended for an hour or more (70.7%). The top three videos with the highest viewership and watch time were on arterial treatment to tough hepatocellular carcinoma (633 views, 87.4 hours watched), non-oncological embolization (350 views, 48.9 hours watched), and lower limb angioplasty (334 views, 58.4 hours watched). The two most attended and most viewed webinars were arterial treatment of hepatocellular carcinoma and non-oncological embolization.Conclusions: Use of webinars for interventional radiology education in the Asia Pacific region was well-received and demonstrates the viability of virtual interventional radiology educational programs to reach underserved communities where interventional radiology has room to grow.

Introduction

The coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic brought significant disruptions to medical education globally [1]. As a result of travel restrictions, social distancing measures, and suspension of clinical meetings, traditional educational formats such as in-person workshops, conferences, and hands-on procedural training abruptly came to a halt [2, 3]. The impact of these disruptions was particularly challenging for interventional radiology (IR), a specialty that relies heavily on procedural skills and real-time demonstrations [3].

The Asia Pacific Society of Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiology (APSCVIR) is the regional society representing IR in the Asia Pacific region. One of its objectives is to support continuing education and training of doctors in each country through appropriate forums or refresher courses [4]. As part of efforts to achieve this, APSCVIR initiated outreach programs for IR training, which included in-person workshops and proctorship for hands-on training [5]. The advent of the COVID-19 pandemic hampered efforts to realize the APSCVIR Outreach Program objective. Many medical professional organizations, including APSCVIR, cancelled their scheduled annual conferences [6].

The need for continued professional development and knowledge exchange remained critical despite these challenges. The APSCVIR Outreach Program pivoted to a virtual platform in December 2020 [5]. This study aims to describe and analyze a series of webinars organized by APSCVIR since the COVID-19 pandemic.

Material and Methods

From December 2020 to February 2024, a total of 14 virtual IR seminars were organized. Ten of these sessions were solely organized by APSCVIR, whereas four were organized in collaboration with the Japanese Society of Interventional Radiology under the umbrella of the Seminar for Interventional Radiology in Asia Pacific. A retrospective review was performed for the 10 webinars solely organized by APSCVIR.

Webinars took place on Saturday afternoons between 1,400 and 1,600 hours (Singapore time) using a virtual online platform (Zoom Video Communications Inc., San Jose, CA, USA). The target audience was IR physicians, particularly those in training or early in their IR career. They were invited to participate via email, e-flyers on social media platforms, short text messaging, and word of mouth. Participation was free. After each virtual session, the recordings of these sessions were posted online and made accessible via a link on the APSCVIR website (https://www.apscvir.com/outreach-programme/). Link access to these recorded seminars was also emailed to participants.

The themes and topics of the webinar were selected by the APSCVIR Outreach Standing Committee. Topic selection was guided by the regional disease burden (e.g., hepatocellular carcinoma, dialysis access), evolving areas of IR practice, and the availability of faculty experts. The presenters were given suggested topics to ensure coverage of clinically relevant material, with an emphasis on content suitable for early-career interventional radiologists while incorporating advanced topics where appropriate.

For this analysis, registration was defined as the number of Zoom registration form sign-ups; attendance as registrants who logged in to the live stream, participation duration as time spent in the live session categorized into <1 hour, 1-2 hours, and full 2 hours, as extracted from Zoom participant reports; and views and watch time as post-even analytics obtained from the APSCVIR platform. Attendees were a subset of the registered participants, and attendance was expressed as an absolute count as well as a registration-to-attendance conversion rate.

The main outcome measure was to assess the level of participation, which was defined as webinar registration, live attendance numbers, and attendance duration. The secondary outcome measure was to evaluate the interest level in the online video resources, based on view counts and watch time.

Descriptive statistics were used for the analysis of quantitative data.

Results

A total of 10 webinar sessions were conducted, each focusing on a different theme in IR. The webinar themes were: 1) Masterclass in Lower Limb Angioplasty; 2) Embolization; 3) Embolization Part 2; 4) Dialysis Access Interventions; 5) Arterial Treatment to Tough HCC; 6) Management of Vascular Malformations; 7) Urological Interventions; 8) Venous Interventions; 9) IR Management of Aortic Disease; and 10) Pediatric IR. There was a median of six presenters per session with six subtopics (Table 1). In six of these webinars, there was faculty support from the Cardiovascular and Interventional Radiological Society of Europe, the Society of Interventional Radiology, and the Society for Pediatric Interventional Radiology.

A median of 258 (interquartile range [IQR] 109-332) registrations were recorded for the webinars, with a median attendance of 126 (IQR 117-188), giving a total attendance of 1,302. The registration-to-participation conversion percentage was 54% (Figure 1). The highest number of attendees were from Indonesia, 17.4% (227/1,302), Pakistan, 14.3% (186/1,302), and India, 11.1% (145/1,302) (Figure 2).

There was variation in attendee participation time per webinar. Of the 10 webinars, six had the most attendees participating for the entire duration, while the remaining four had the majority participating for between one and two hours. Overall, participants' attendance duration was 29.3% (381/1,302) for less than one hour, 32.3% (420/1,302) for between one and two hours, and 38.5% (501/1,302) for the full two-hour duration (Figure 3). When all 10 webinars were uploaded for viewing, the top three videos with the highest viewership and watch time were Arterial Treatment to Tough HCC (633 views, 87.4 hours watched), Embolization 2 (350 views, 48.9 hours watched), and Masterclass in Lower Limb Angioplasty (334 views, 58.4 hours watched) (Figure 4).

The two webinars that were most attended as well as most viewed when uploaded were Arterial Treatment to Tough HCC and Embolization 2 (Figure 5).

Discussion

This study sought to examine the level of engagement in the live webinars and the degree of interest in the online video resources regarding IR educational webinars by APSCVIR during the COVID-19 pandemic and beyond. Overall, there was a good level of interest in the organized webinars, with wide participation from various countries.

We observed the highest interest in topics relating to HCC and embolization. This interest in HCC is likely due to the high prevalence of HCC in the region. Asia accounts for 72.5% of the world's cases of liver cancer [7].

The discrepancy between registered and attending participants may stem from several factors. Firstly, personal commitments such as family obligations and social activities may take precedence over professional webinars on weekends. In one study involving a total of 326 physicians that assessed physicians' attitude among different medical specialties towards the sudden shift of traditional face-to-face meetings into webinars, the most commonly reported factor (87.1%) for declining to attend was the inconvenient timing of the meeting [8]. Furthermore, in the same study, respondents reported disturbance of work/life balance, as most webinars occur during weekends or after working hours, as part of the several reasons for their dissatisfaction with webinars [8]. In contrast, another study noted that physicians preferred to attend a weekend webinar (62.8%) and in the evening (52.8%) [9]. However, some studies have shown that the best time for a webinar is mid-week, with a strong preference for Tuesday and Wednesday [8, 10].

Secondly, registered participants may forget to attend if they do not receive timely reminders. A recommendation of two reminder emails for a webinar event has been suggested, the first sent the day before the event, and the second an hour before the event starts [10, 11].

Thirdly, participants may prefer to attend Continuing Medical Education (CME)-accredited webinars rather than webinars without CME accreditation. The opportunity to earn CME points has increasingly become a strong incentive for webinar attendance, particularly in regions where health workers are required to earn a specific number of CME credits to maintain their certification or license. In a previous study among physicians, CME certification was the second most common factor determining webinar attendance (50.3%) [8]. The webinars by APSCVIR were not CME-accredited.

Fourthly, passive registration behavior might also be one of the possible factors. For example, if participants know that the webinar will be recorded and made available later, they might register but opt to watch it at a more convenient time instead of attending live. To address this, APSCVIR has provided the recorded material on their website to be accessed freely by those who did not attend live.

Obvious differences were also noted with respect to attendee numbers as well as participation time per webinar in this study. We did not obtain participant feedback to understand factors that could have influenced this observation. However, Ismail et al. in their study on physicians' attitude towards webinars and online education during the COVID-19 pandemic, listed the scientific content, timing of the meeting, CME certification, speaker's name and experience, annual international conferences, personal factors, and relations with the inviting party as the most commonly reported factors influencing attendance [8]. The level of engagement during webinars may also have had an effect on attendee participation time per webinar. This is because webinars that lack engaging delivery or interactive elements may lead to a decline in interest and early exit from the sessions [12]. Another factor that may have also impacted attendee participation time per webinar could be the content relevance of the webinars. Attendees may prioritize segments of the webinar that align with their interests or needs and may exit the webinars once the relevant segment concludes.

The webinar on HCC emerged as the most viewed, with the highest watch time among the uploaded recordings on the APSCVIR website, which were freely accessible to those who did not attend live. The fact that this same topic also had the highest attendance during the live webinars reflects the clinical relevance of liver tumor embolization in the management of HCC, particularly given the high incidence of this condition in the Asia Pacific region. This underscores the growing demand for specialized knowledge in managing liver tumors worldwide [13].

Previous systematic reviews have evaluated the evidence regarding the benefits of online learning for continuing education for healthcare professionals. While some studies found that e-learning intervention not only provided a satisfactory learning experience for healthcare professionals but also achieved comparable learning outcomes compared to face-to-face teaching [14, 15], other studies reported difficulty in making that comparison, and in some cases concluded that e-learning had a negligible effect on health professionals' knowledge [15, 16]. Our study did not obtain information regarding the effectiveness of these webinars, and this should be considered in the future.

Due to the retrospective nature of this study, we did not capture data on the preferences of the webinar attendees, which might have provided useful guidance to enable organizers to tailor these webinars to suit the preferences and expectations of participants. A survey by Odayappan et al. in India involving 509 ophthalmologists and 91 physicians from other specialties assessed their perspectives on the role of webinars in medical education during the COVID-19 pandemic. There was a preference to attend a weekend webinar (62.8%), in the evening (52.8%), not extending more than two hours (97.7%), by ≤3 reputed national speakers (89.8%), each given 15 to 30 minutes for their presentation, and the topics being clinical or surgical skill demonstration (47.5%) or recent advances (39.5%) [9]. There was also an equal preference for watching a webinar live or recorded (50.7% vs. 49.3%) in the same study [9]. The above observations support the need to place emphasis on the participants' preferences going forward.

Despite the obvious advantages of webinars, such as the convenience of a virtual platform for learning, the ability to reach a wide audience, and often the option for future reference, webinars alone, however, cannot fully replace face-to-face teaching due to the lack of hands-on opportunities, social interaction, and the so-called “webinar fatigue” [17]. In their publication titled “Webinar fatigue: fallout of COVID-19,” Sharma et al. mentioned that the increased use of online/web meetings with the advent of COVID-19 leads to “screen-fatigue, physical and mental exhaustion, and ultimately digital burnout” [17]. In addition, other disadvantages of online education worth mentioning include technical difficulties, internet connection problems, less communication with attendees, and increased distraction during webinars compared to traditional face-to-face learning [8, 18]. In light of the limitations of a fully virtual IR education outreach program, APSCVIR transitioned to a hybrid system following the end of the pandemic. This approach leverages webinars as a useful tool while complementing them with traditional in-person IR outreach training programs.

The findings from our study could guide strategic planning processes to enhance subsequent engagement across both live and uploaded online sessions. To reduce the gap between registered and attending participants in the future, interventions such as timely reminders before webinars may help improve attendee numbers. Furthermore, participants may be engaged after the webinar through surveys and additional learning resources. They can also be encouraged to provide feedback and share their experience with the organizers, which can be incorporated into future program planning to guide topic selection. Incorporating short replays of webinar videos on the APSCVIR website gives a glimpse of the content covered in the video and may encourage and direct visitors to watch full videos online. There is still a need for long-term follow-up to gain valuable insights into audience engagement and preferences. Future studies should evaluate the satisfaction of participants and the effectiveness of these sessions to obtain useful feedback for continuous improvement of these webinars.

Conclusion

The use of webinars for IR education in the Asia Pacific region was well received. Our experience demonstrates the viability and ability of virtual IR educational programs to reach underserved communities where IR has room to grow. Since online learning has now come to stay with the advent of COVID-19, webinars in IR should be seen as an invaluable adjunct to the traditional face-to-face teaching as we move forward. This study's findings may help institutions and organizations to create more effective webinars for IR education. Further studies on the perspective of participants will be useful in defining future structure and platforms for IR online education.
