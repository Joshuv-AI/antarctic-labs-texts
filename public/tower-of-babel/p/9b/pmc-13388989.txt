Decoding the regulatory genome: single-cell four-omics integration
Authors: Hakjin Kim, Jongwon Byun, Taeho Kwon
Published: 2026

In a recent study published in Nature, Chen et al. introduced CHARM (single-cell assay for Chromatin conformation, Histone modification, chromatin Accessibility, and RNA expression Multi-omics profiling), a platform that simultaneously captures four regulatory modalities within the same nucleus.1 This integrated strategy provides a comprehensive framework for dissecting how multiple layers of epigenetic regulation converge to control gene expression at single-cell resolution.

Gene regulation in eukaryotic cells is governed by a complex interplay of molecular and spatial mechanisms. Chromatin accessibility determines whether regulatory elements such as promoters and enhancers are available for transcription factor binding. Histone modifications define chromatin states that either promote or repress transcription. In parallel, the three-dimensional organization of the genome establishes spatial proximity between distal regulatory elements and their target genes. Although each of these regulatory layers has been extensively studied, understanding how they operate together within the same cell has remained a major challenge.2

Previous technologies have provided valuable insights into individual modalities. ATAC-seq profiles chromatin accessibility, CUT&Tag captures histone modifications, and Hi-C reveals three-dimensional genome architecture. Recent single-cell platforms such as ChAIR and scHiCAR jointly profile chromatin accessibility, RNA, and 3D contacts, but their 3D contact capture is anchored at accessible chromatin or candidate cis-regulatory elements, introducing structural bias into chromatin architecture reconstruction.3,4 Moreover, regulatory modalities not captured by these platforms, such as histone modifications, require separate profiling and computational integration for broader cross-modality analysis. CHARM is distinguished from previous platforms by adding histone modification as a fourth same-cell modality and by using restriction-enzyme-based Hi-C, enabling a less biased reconstruction of chromatin architecture. CHARM addresses this gap by capturing four key regulatory modalities simultaneously within the same nucleus. This approach preserves the native relationships between chromatin structure, epigenetic state, and transcriptional output. The method builds on previous multi-omics strategies and integrates chromatin labeling, proximity ligation, and reverse transcription into a unified workflow. Application of CHARM to mouse embryonic stem cells demonstrated robust performance across all modalities. The platform detected large numbers of chromatin interactions, accessibility fragments, histone modification signals, and RNA transcripts per cell. Importantly, aggregated CHARM data showed strong agreement with previously established datasets, confirming its reliability.1 At specific genomic loci, CHARM captured coordinated regulatory features. For example, at the Sox2 locus, chromatin accessibility was observed at both promoter and enhancer regions, accompanied by a chromatin loop connecting these elements. In contrast, the Hoxd gene cluster displayed strong enrichment of H3K27me3, forming a repressive chromatin domain. These observations illustrate how active and repressive regulatory states are organized across the genome.

CHARM captures chromatin accessibility, H3K27me3, and three-dimensional contacts within the same nucleus, enabling direct comparison of how distinct regulatory layers are restored after DNA replication. Analysis of single-cell trajectories revealed distinct patterns of epigenetic regulation during the cell cycle. Chromatin accessibility exhibited strong dependence on DNA replication timing. Accessibility was lowest during mitosis and restored progressively in a replication-timing-dependent manner that closely followed the replication program.1 In contrast, H3K27me3 re-establishment displayed a different behavior. This repressive histone mark was restored more uniformly and showed limited dependence on replication timing. These findings indicate that active and repressive chromatin states are governed by fundamentally different regulatory mechanisms.

Mechanistically, the study provides important insights into how epigenetic memory is maintained.5 Three-dimensional chromatin organization emerged as a critical factor influencing the restoration of histone modifications.1 Regions that were spatially proximal to H3K27me3-enriched domains exhibited more efficient recovery of repressive marks. This observation suggests that spatial genome architecture contributes to the propagation of epigenetic states across cell division.5 Such a mechanism provides a potential explanation for how cells maintain stable transcriptional programs despite ongoing DNA replication and chromatin remodeling.

The integration of single-cell Hi-C with simultaneously profiled chromatin accessibility and H3K27me3 within the same nucleus enables reconstruction of three-dimensional regulatory hubs that emerge only from joint multi-modal profiling. Reconstruction of single-cell three-dimensional genome structures revealed that accessible chromatin regions form discrete spatial clusters within the nucleus. These clusters are enriched for super-enhancers and transcriptional co-activators, indicating that they represent functional hubs of gene regulation. Genes located within these clusters tend to exhibit higher transcriptional activity, supporting the concept that spatial clustering facilitates efficient transcription.

Further analysis showed that these clusters are not restricted to contiguous genomic regions. Instead, they frequently include regulatory elements from multiple distant loci, indicating long-range coordination within the nucleus. In addition, accessible chromatin and H3K27me3-marked regions exhibited partial spatial co-localization. This finding reflects the presence of bivalent chromatin, which contains both active and repressive features and plays a key role in maintaining developmental genes in a poised state. Together, these results highlight the importance of spatial organization in coordinating regulatory activity.

Applied to mouse cortical tissue, CHARM resolves enhancer–promoter linkages specific to each cell type by simultaneously profiling chromatin loops, H3K27ac, accessibility, and transcripts in individual cells. Integrated analysis of multiple modalities enabled the identification of distinct cell types based on their regulatory profiles.1 Notably, gene expression patterns were closely associated with specific epigenetic configurations. The Gad2 locus exemplifies this: a small set of distal elements forms long-range chromatin loops with the Gad2 promoter and is broadly shared across inhibitory subtypes, whereas a larger set drives Gad2 activation specifically in Ndnf/Lamp5 interneurons. Both layers show increased chromatin accessibility and H3K27ac enrichment at the linked elements, revealing a nested signature undetectable by lower-modality approaches. To systematically map regulatory interactions, the authors first identified 89,076 candidate distal linkages¹ through multi-modality correlation analysis, defining a broad pool that does not, on its own, distinguish causal enhancer–promoter pairs from co-varying loci. Many of these candidates bypass nearby genes to interact with distant targets through chromatin looping, underscoring the role of three-dimensional genome organization in long-range regulation. The study also introduces an interpretable machine learning framework to quantify the contribution of different regulatory modalities to gene expression. Models incorporating chromatin accessibility, H3K27ac, and chromatin interactions showed improved predictive performance compared to single-modality models. Among individual modalities, H3K27ac exhibited the strongest predictive power, whereas chromatin interactions alone showed lower predictive accuracy. However, combining all modalities resulted in the highest performance, indicating that transcription is governed by the coordinated action of multiple regulatory layers.

To refine this pool into causally supported pairs, the authors applied an interpretable machine-learning framework based on Shapley value decomposition, yielding a high-confidence set of 7146 cell-type-specific enhancer–promoter linkages across 16 cortical populations, with Ndnf/Lamp5 interneurons accounting for the largest number of subtype-specific linkages, consistent with the Gad2 example above. Distinguishing this causally supported set from the broader correlation-based pool provides a quantitative framework for linking regulatory elements to expression and interpreting noncoding genetic variants.

Collectively, these findings establish CHARM as a robust platform for dissecting multi-layered gene regulation at single-cell resolution, integrating accessibility, histone modifications, and three-dimensional organization within the same cell. As multi-omics data grow in dimensionality, advanced computational strategies will be essential for interpreting high-dimensional biology and characterizing gene regulatory networks with greater precision.
