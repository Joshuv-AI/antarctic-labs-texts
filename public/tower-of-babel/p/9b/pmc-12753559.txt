Sex Categories
Authors: Aivars Cīrulis
Published: 2025

Introduction

There is a heated ongoing debate about sex and gender categories. In the last decades, some scholars have begun to argue—often on dubious scientific grounds—that sex is a spectrum or even does not exist, while treating self-reported gender as the only relevant category (Sullivan & Todd, 2023). Meanwhile, others insist on strict binary categories in all aspects. However, despite the binary nature of biological sex, scientific rigor and fairer treatment frequently justify the implementation of an additional category in both research and different policies. In this piece, I first outline the evolution of binary sex, and then turn to what I call “aspects of sex”: core biological dimensions in which karyotypes, cells, and organs show bimodal, sex-related variation. These aspects are not “sex” itself, but partially independent traits that can sometimes be misaligned, as in the case of intersex and transsexual individuals.

The Evolution of Two Reproductive Strategies (Sexes)

Sex is a process that mixes genetic material from different individuals. In eukaryotes, this process involves meiosis, which is thought to have originated at least 1.5 billion years ago (Beukeboom & Perrin, 2014), coupled with syngamy (the fusion of gametes) (Rhen & Crews, 2007). Subsequently, anisogamy—the evolution of two distinct gamete types, large eggs and small sperm—arose, giving rise to two reproductive strategies. From this point, we can meaningfully speak of the evolution of two sexes, male and female (Bachtrog et al., 2014). These two reproductive strategies can be combined within a single individual, in which case the species is hermaphroditic, or they can be partitioned into separate male and female individuals, referred to as gonochorists, as is the case in humans.

Phenotypic Sex (Genital Sex)

Phenotypic sex refers to the external and internal anatomical and physiological traits associated with sex, including genital morphology, reproductive ducts, and secondary sexual characteristics. These traits arise from the interaction of chromosomal, gonadal, and hormonal factors. In biomedicine, sex usually refers to this phenotypic level: the sex of a child is recorded based on external genitalia. In some cases, this assignment can be discordant with the underlying gonadal (or chromosomal) sex. Individuals with atypical or ambiguous genitalia are often described as intersex, or as having disorders or differences of sex development (DSD) (Sax, 2002). Thus, in humans, phenotypic sex is best characterized as bimodal, while biological sex remains binary, since an individual can potentially be either a mother or a father, but not both.

Genotypic Sex (XY vs. XX)

Genotypic sex refers to the complement of sex chromosomes inherited, which in mammals is typically XX for females and XY for males. The presence of a Y chromosome—more specifically, an active SRY gene—directs the bipotential gonadal ridges to develop into a testis. In contrast, its absence leads to ovarian development in humans. Thus, genotypic sex is an upstream determinant of gonadal and phenotypic sex (Cīrulis et al., 2022). However, variations in sex chromosome composition—such as XXY, X0, XX/XY mosaics, or mutations in sex-determining genes—can result in a mismatch between genotypic, gonadal, and phenotypic sex. This demonstrates that although the core sex-determining pathway operates as a binary switch (SRY on/off; Y present/absent), chromosomal configurations themselves exhibit a bimodal distribution with rare intermediate or atypical karyotypes—an essential consideration in genomic research.

Cell Sex (Tissue Sex)

The sex of cells is inherently determined by the sex chromosome complement (XX or XY) and the established gene regulatory networks. This cell-autonomous mechanism can generate phenotypic differences that occur independently of sex hormones (Beyer et al., 1992; Carruth et al., 2002). Unlike phenotypic sex, which is defined at the level of the whole organism, cell sex is a cell-autonomous property. Every genome-containing cell in the body has a sex (XX, XY, or aneuploid combinations, while tissues can exhibit sex chromosome mosaicism). In mammals, XX and XY cells differ in the dosage of X- and Y-linked genes, X-chromosome inactivation patterns, and in the regulation of hormone receptors and signaling pathways. These differences can influence cellular behavior—such as proliferation, differentiation, metabolism, and disease susceptibility—even in the same hormonal environment. Cells and tissues are not generic; they are biologically male or female. Because one-third of our genes behave differently based on sex (Aguet et al., 2020), scientists must consider the sex of the cells they use, even when studying isolated tissues outside the body. Importantly, cellular sex can contribute to graft rejection in humans (Vogt et al., 2000).

Brain Sex

Brain sex refers to sex-related patterns in brain structure, function, and gene expression that arise from the combined effects of sex chromosomes, gonadal hormones, other biochemical factors, and experience. During sensitive developmental windows (prenatal and early postnatal), sex steroids (e.g., androgens and estrogens) organize neural circuits, and later hormone exposure can activate sex-biased behaviors and physiology during puberty. Moreover, as sexual differentiation of genitals takes place in the first half of pregnancy, sexual differentiation of the brain occurs in the second half, when the gonads have already formed and secrete sex hormones, such as testosterone, which masculinizes the brain. This two-step process of sexual differentiation in the body can explain the opportunity of mismatch between the phenotypic sex and brain sex, possibly explaining homosexuality and transsexuality. In general, males and females often differ in average size, activity, or receptor expression within specific brain regions, and these variations are linked to traits such as stress responsiveness, cognitive function, and susceptibility to various neuropsychiatric conditions (Frigerio et al., 2021; Swaab et al., 2022). The concept of "brain sex" serves as a functional heuristic for the bimodal distribution of sex-differentiated neural traits, which are more accurately conceptualized as a multifactorial mosaic rather than a rigid binary. One study showed that the machine learning approach of gray matter MRI scans can differentiate sex correctly 93% of the time (Anderson et al., 2019).

Moving Forward

Even though sex, defined by gamete type, is binary, many sex-associated phenotypes show bimodal rather than perfectly dichotomous distributions. In evolutionary biology, it is already common to recognize three reproductive categories when males, females, and hermaphrodites coexist. By analogy, in human biology, medicine, and law, it can be helpful to introduce a third analytical or legal category in cases where there are substantial incongruences between sex-related traits—for example, in some intersex conditions and in the lived realities of many trans people.

Recognizing a third category in these contexts can make our work more straightforward, rather than more complicated. It provides a principled approach to handling outliers not in strictly binary phenotypic classifications, particularly in genomics, endocrinology, and neuroscience, as well as in the study of sex and gender in the social sciences. It also offers a potential way forward in currently polarized debates within feminism: a framework in which trans and intersex people can be explicitly protected without erasing women as a sex class or abandoning the long-standing biological definition of sex as a binary based on reproductive role. That binary, properly understood at the level of gametes, has always been compatible with—and able to explain—the existence of variation, including intersex traits and the experiences of trans people.
