Efficient Ambient Electrosynthesis of Ammonia over a Vanadium-Doped MnO2 Catalyst
Authors: Lanxiang Huang, Xi Jiang, Renchuan Deng, Qin Wang, Ting Long, Yuan Liang, Yang Li, Qiang Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ammonia serves as an ideal hydrogen energy carrier due to its safe transportability and potential as a renewable, zero-carbon fuel. The electrochemical nitrogen reduction reaction (eNRR) in aqueous electrolytes under ambient conditions, powered by renewable energy sources, represents a sustainable pathway. To maximize process efficiency, catalyst design must prioritize selective N2 adsorption while effectively suppressing the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER). Building upon theoretical and experimental evidence that transition metals with unoccupied d-orbitals facilitate N2 adsorption and inert bond activation, we developed vanadium-doped manganese oxide electrocatalysts for ambient-condition ammonia synthesis. The optimized V-MnO2(0.2) catalyst exhibits exceptional performance, delivering an NH3 yield rate of 14.5 μg h– 1 mgcat – 1 and a Faradaic efficiency of 17.3% at −0.4 V vs RHE (28% at −0.3 V). This work provides fundamental insights into electronic structure modulation via transition metal doping, active site engineering for selective N2 adsorption, and design principles for noble-metal-free NRR catalysts.

Introduction

Ammonia serves as an ideal hydrogen energy carrier due to its high energy density (4.32 kWh/L), ease of safe transportation, and potential as a renewable, carbon-free fuel. However, the conventional Haber–Bosch process for ammonia production presents significant sustainability challenges: (i) energy intensity, operating under extreme conditions (400–500 °C, 150–300 atm) and accounting for approximately 2% of global energy consumption annually; (ii) carbon footprint, relying on hydrogen produced via steam methane reforming (SMR) and generating ∼320 million tons of CO2 emissions per year. These limitations highlight the urgent need to develop alternative ammonia synthesis pathways that are energy-efficient, environmentally benign, and compatible with renewable energy sources. The development of such green ammonia synthesis technologies represents a critical step toward sustainable economic and ecological development in the postfossil fuel era.

The electrochemical nitrogen reduction reaction (eNRR) offers a sustainable alternative for ammonia synthesis by enabling N2-to-NH3 conversion in aqueous electrolytes under ambient conditions (25 °C, 1 atm) using renewable electricity from solar or wind power. This approach presents several fundamental advantages over conventional Haber–Bosch synthesis: (i) thermodynamic benefits, overcoming equilibrium limitations of traditional NH3 synthesis (ΔG° = +33.3 kJ mol–1 at 25 °C) and decoupling reaction kinetics from temperature/pressure constraints; (ii) environmental advantages, eliminating CO2 emissions associated with steam methane reforming and enabling direct integration with intermittent renewable energy sources. −

The close thermodynamic potentials of these reactions (ΔE = 57.7 mV) create significant selectivity challenges, making catalyst design crucial for practical implementation. This fundamental understanding has driven extensive research into developing efficient electrocatalysts that can suppress the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER), enhance N2 adsorption and activation, and facilitate proton–electron transfer processes. ,

To achieve efficient electrochemical nitrogen reduction, the catalyst design must prioritize selective N2 adsorption over competitive hydrogen evolution. Transition metal-based catalysts are particularly promising due to their unoccupied d-orbitals, which facilitate strong N2 chemisorption via π-backdonation, effective weakening of the NN triple bond (bond energy: 941 kJ mol–1), and stabilization of key NRR intermediates (*N2H and *NNH). Manganese-based catalysts have emerged as particularly effective, as demonstrated by both experimental and theoretical studies. − Notable examples include MnO2 nanosheets in 0.1 M HCl with a NH3 yield rate of 34.12 μg h– 1 mgcat – 1 , Mn3O4 nanocubes in 0.1 M Na2SO4 with a NH3 yield rate of 11.6 μg h– 1 mgcat – 1 and FE of 3.0%, and MnO-carbon composite in 0.1 M Na2SO4 with a low FE of 1.5%. These studies reveal two critical trends: (i) acidic electrolytes generally enhance NH3 yield rates; (ii) the manganese oxidation state significantly impacts Faradaic efficiency. The performance variation underscores the importance of precise control over the catalyst crystalline phase, surface oxidation state, and electrolyte proton availability.

In this study, the V-MnO2(0.2) composite was utilized as an electrocatalyst for ammonia synthesis under ambient conditions and demonstrated remarkable catalytic performance. Specifically, it achieved an NH3 yield rate of 14.5 μg h– 1 mgcat – 1 and a satisfactory Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 17.3% at an applied potential of −0.4 V (vs RHE) under the same conditions. The catalyst exhibits exceptional stability, durability, and selectivity, which can be attributed to the optimal vanadium doping-induced electronic structure modulation, increased density of active sites from morphological control, and improved charge transfer kinetics. This straightforward yet adaptable approach may serve as a valuable source of inspiration for researchers seeking to rationally design highly efficient NRR electrocatalysts.

Experimental Section

Materials Preparation

The manganese–vanadium oxide composites were prepared through a hydrothermal method using manganese sulfate monohydrate (MnSO4·H2O) and potassium permanganate (KMnO4) as manganese precursors, with ammonium metavanadate (NH4VO3) serving as the vanadium source. In a typical synthesis: an aqueous solution was prepared by dissolving 1.8120 g (12 mmol) of MnSO4·H2O in deionized water under continuous stirring (initial pH ≈ 6). Separately, 1.2640 g (8 mmol) of KMnO4 and predetermined quantities of NH4VO3 were dissolved in DI water. The two solutions were combined, resulting in an immediate pH reduction to approximately 1. The homogeneous mixture was subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 100 °C for 12 h in a Teflon-lined autoclave. After natural cooling to ambient temperature (final pH ≈ 1), the resulting precipitate underwent multiple DI water washes and then vacuum drying at 60 °C for 12 h. Finally, grinding was performed to obtain fine powders. For comparative studies, vanadium doping concentrations were systematically varied to achieve V/Mn molar ratios of 0.06, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.3, with the resulting samples designated as pure MnO2 (undoped control), V-MnO2(0.06), V-MnO2(0.15), V-MnO2(0.2), and V-MnO2(0.3), respectively.

Materials Characterization

The synthesized samples underwent comprehensive structural and chemical analyses through multiple advanced characterization techniques. The crystal structure was examined using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD, Rigaku DX-600X diffractometer) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), operating at 40 kV and 30 mA. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were performed on a Thermo Scientific NEXSA system equipped with a monochromatic Al–Kα X-ray source (1486.6 eV) to determine surface elemental states and chemical bonding environments. Microstructural evaluation was conducted using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FEI Inspect F50 SEM), transmission electron microscopy (JEOL JEM2100 TEM), and high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) for atomic-scale imaging. The vanadium and manganese contents were precisely determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (Agilent 730 ICP-OES) following acid digestion of samples. The oxygen vacancy information on the material was collected using an electron spin resonance spectrometer (EPR, Bruker EMX Plus) at 77K.

Electrochemical Tests

Electrochemical characterization was carried out in a membrane-separated (Nafion 117) two-compartment cell under a N2 atmosphere. The working electrode was prepared by coating a homogeneous catalyst ink (containing 5% Nafion in ethanol/water) onto carbon cloth substrates (1 cm2), yielding a catalyst loading of 0.5 mg/cm2. A conventional three-electrode configuration was employed, using platinum foil as the counter electrode and saturated Hg/Hg2SO4 as the reference. The electrolyte was continuously purged with high-purity N2 for 30 min prior to measurements to eliminate dissolved oxygen. All reported potentials were normalized to the reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) (eq ). E(vsRHE)=E(vsHg/Hg2SO4)+0.656+0.059×pH 1

Linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) measurements were performed at a scan rate of 5 mV s– 1, followed by chronoamperometry tests at various negative potentials. The cathodic chamber was continuously purged with high-purity nitrogen during the experiments. Additionally, cyclic voltammetry (CV) scans were recorded between 0.35 and 0.45 V at scan rates of 20, 40, 60, 80, and 100 mV s– 1. All of the above tests were performed by an electrochemical workstation (DongHua Analytical, DH7000).

Determination of NH3

The ammonia (NH3) yield from the electrocatalytic N2 reduction reaction was quantified using the indophenol blue method. , For analysis, 4 mL of the postreaction electrolyte was combined with 500 μL of sodium salicylate solution (0.4 M C7H5NaO3, 0.32 M NaOH), 50 μL of sodium hypochlorite solution (0.05 M NaClO, 0.75 M NaOH), and 50 μL of sodium nitroprusside solution (1 wt %). The mixture was stored in the dark for 2 h before measuring its absorbance via UV-visible spectrophotometry. A calibration curve was generated using standard NH4Cl solutions (0–2 μg mL– 1). The NH3 production rate was calculated using eq : rNH3=(cNH3V)/(tm) 2 where c NH3 is the ammonia concentration (μg mL– 1), V is the electrolyte volume (mL), t is the reaction duration (h), and m is the catalyst mass (mg).

Determination of N2H4

Hydrazine was quantified using the Watt–Chrisp method. , A colorimetric reagent was prepared by dissolving p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde (0.998 g) in ethanol (50 mL). For analysis, 2 mL of the electrolyte was mixed with 2 mL of the reagent and allowed to react for 20 min. The absorbance of the resulting solution was measured at 455 nm by using a UV-visible spectrophotometer. A calibration curve was constructed using standard hydrazine solutions (0–1 μg mL– 1).

FE of NH3 Production

The Faradaic efficiency of ammonia synthesis represents the fraction of the total electrical charge utilized for NH3 production, calculated using eq : FE=3FnNH3/Q 3 where F denotes the Faradaic constant (96,485 C mol– 1), n NH3 is the moles of ammonia generated, and Q is the total charge passed during electrolysis (C).

Results and Discussion

Morphology and Structure

The XRD pattern of undoped MnO2 matched perfectly with α-MnO2 (PDF #44-0141), confirming a pure phase with characteristic [1 × 1] and [2 × 2] tunnel structures (Figure ). All observed peaks (12.6° (110), 17.9° (200), etc.) indexed well to the tetragonal structure. With NH4VO3 doping, peak intensities decreased, particularly for V-MnO2(0.15/0.2/0.3), suggesting reduced crystallinity and a smaller particle size. Lattice distortion and local disorder induced by V doping are the primary causes of the weakening of diffraction peaks. The ionic radius difference between V5 + and Mn4 + leads to lattice distortion, disrupts long-range order, and reduces the intensity of the diffraction peaks. A high V content may further induce local structural disorder (e.g., distortion of V–O–Mn bond angles), resulting in the formation of amorphous phase domains. No impurity peaks were detected, confirming the incorporation of vanadium into the MnO2 lattice rather than the formation of secondary phases. Rietveld refinement revealed lattice expansion (a = b = 9.8341 Å, and V = 275.46 Å3 for V-MnO2(0.06) vs 9.8175 Å, 274.89 Å3 for pure α-MnO2), attributed to V5 + (58 pm) substitution for Mn4 + (53 pm). This structural expansion enhances electrochemical activity by facilitating ion transport.

XRD patterns of pure MnO2, V-MnO2(0.06), V-MnO2(0.15), V-MnO2(0.2), and V-MnO2(0.3).

SEM analysis revealed significant morphological changes induced by vanadium doping in α-MnO2. As shown in Figure a1,2, pristine α-MnO2 exhibits a well-defined nanorod morphology with lengths exceeding 500 nm. HRTEM imaging (Figure a3) demonstrates excellent crystallinity, showing clear lattice fringes with a spacing of 0.48 nm, corresponding to the (200) crystal plane along the nanorod growth direction.

SEM, TEM, and HRTEM micromorphology of pure α-MnO2 (a1–3), V-MnO2(0.06) (b1–3), V-MnO2(0.15) (c1–3), V-MnO2(0.2) (d1–3), and V-MnO2(0.3) (e1–3).

Vanadium incorporation dramatically altered the microstructure. For low doping concentrations (V-MnO2(0.06)), the nanorod morphology was preserved but with progressively shorter lengths (Figure b1,2). HRTEM analysis revealed that while the (200) growth direction was maintained, the interplanar spacing increased to 0.53 nm (Figure b3). This lattice expansion, accompanied by observable crystal defects, provides direct evidence of successful vanadium incorporation into the α-MnO2 framework, consistent with the XRD results. These structural modifications increased interlayer spacing and introduced defects that are expected to facilitate ion diffusion and enhance electrochemical kinetics. −

At higher doping levels (V-MnO2(0.15), V-MnO2(0.2), and V-MnO2(0.3)), more pronounced morphological transformations occurred, evolving into nanoparticle clusters and nanosheet assemblies. The dominant exposed crystal planes shifted to (301) and (211) with corresponding spacings of 0.22 and 0.26 nm, respectively. These samples showed significantly reduced crystallinity, as evidenced by blurred lattice fringes and increased amorphous regions, in agreement with XRD observations. Notably, the absence of vanadium oxide lattice patterns in HRTEM images confirms the homogeneous distribution of vanadium within the MnO2 matrix without secondary phase formation (Figure S1). Among the three samples analyzed, V-MnO2(0.2) exhibits the smallest particle size. Interestingly, V-MnO2(0.3) shows relatively larger particles, despite the higher doping concentration. This apparent contradiction can be explained by considering the following mechanism: the increased vanadium doping content leads to the formation of smaller primary nanoparticles. These reduced primary particle dimensions result in higher surface energy, which in turn promotes more extensive secondary agglomeration during synthesis. Consequently, the final observed particle size appears larger due to this enhanced aggregation behavior.

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis reveals the oxidation states of vanadium and manganese in the doped materials. As shown in Figure a, the V 2p3/2 peaks for all samples exhibit a binding energy (BE) of about 517.0 eV, confirming the predominant presence of V5 + species. Meanwhile, the XPS peak position shifts toward the high-energy region first from 0.06 to 0.2 and then toward the low-energy region as the content increases from 0.2 to 0.3. The electronic structure of V5 + ions differs from that of MnO2. Doping alters the electronic structure of the material. At low doping concentrations, the electrons of V ions may interact with those of MnO2 to form new energy levels, thereby increasing the binding energy. At high doping concentrations, these new energy levels may overlap or interact, leading to a redistribution of the electronic structure, which subsequently reduces the binding energy of V ions. The Mn oxidation state was determined through the analysis of the Mn 3s splitting peaks (Figure b). The energy separation (ΔE) between these characteristic peaks shows a progressive increase with the vanadium doping concentration. The AOS values decrease systematically from 3.669 (pure MnO2) to 3.303 (V-MnO2(0.3)), indicating the coexistence of Mn4 + and Mn3 + in all samples, with Mn4 + remaining the dominant species, either an increasing proportion of Mn3 + with higher vanadium doping or the creation of manganese vacancies as the vanadium content increases. This trend suggests that vanadium incorporation induces electronic structure modifications in the manganese oxide framework, potentially through charge compensation mechanisms.

The O 1s XPS spectra (Figure c) exhibit two distinct components that can be deconvoluted into lattice oxygen (O l a t, ∼530 eV) corresponding to oxygen atoms in coordinatively saturated environments (O sat) bonded to metal cations and a higher-binding-energy component (∼531–532 eV) comprising both adsorbed oxygen species and oxygen vacancies (O v a c), representing coordinatively unsaturated oxygen environments (O unsat). Quantitative analysis reveals a systematic decrease in the O sat/O T ratio (where O T = O sat + O unsat) with increasing vanadium doping concentration (Table ), demonstrating that vanadium incorporation promotes oxygen vacancy formation. This trend shows a clear doping-dependent relationship: a higher vanadium content leads to a greater oxygen vacancy concentration.

The specific oxygen vacancy was detected by EPR measurements, as shown in Figure d. The catalysts all show a pair of peaks with a symmetric distribution consistent with the oxygen vacancy signal at g = 2.003, indicating that the electrons are trapped at oxygen vacancies. The comparison suggests that V-MnO2 samples contain more oxygen vacancies compared with pure MnO2, and the concentration of oxygen vacancies increases with vanadium doping concentration, which is consistent with the above XPS analysis.

This phenomenon can be understood through charge compensation mechanisms: when V5 + substitutes for Mn4 + in the MnO2 lattice, charge neutrality requires the creation of manganese vacancies or reduction of Mn4 + to Mn3 + (as evidenced by decreasing Mn AOS values). The resulting lower Mn oxidation states (more Mn3 +) increase the lability of oxygen atoms in the [MnO6] octahedra, facilitating oxygen release and vacancy formation. Concurrent loss of manganese and oxygen atoms induces structural distortion, manifested by elongation and weakening of the remaining Mn–O bonds and further generation of oxygen vacancies. These structural modifications have significant implications for ion transportation. In ideal spinel structures, ion diffusion is inherently restricted by high electrostatic barriers. The introduced Mn and O vacancies effectively (i) reduce electrostatic repulsion against ions, (ii) create lower-energy diffusion pathways, and (iii) enhance ion mobility within the host structure.

As summarized in Table , the specific surface area exhibits a positive correlation with the vanadium doping concentration, in good agreement with the morphological evolution observed by SEM and TEM. When the V/Mn molar ratio reaches 0.15, 0.2, and 0.3, the original nanorod morphology undergoes complete transformation into smaller nanoparticles and two-dimensional nanosheets, resulting in a significantly enhanced surface area. Notably, among the three doped samples, V-MnO2(0.2) exhibits the largest specific surface area, consistent with the morphological characterization by SEM and TEM. This enhanced surface area provides several catalytic advantages: (i) increased exposure of active sites for catalytic reactions, (ii) improved accessibility of reactants to the catalyst surface, and (iii) enhanced mass transport properties during the catalytic process.

ICP-OES analysis confirms the doping efficiency with measured V/Mn ratios closely matching the theoretical stoichiometric values (Table ). This near-quantitative incorporation indicates that vanadium species from the vanadate precursor are effectively and completely doped into the MnO2 lattice during the synthesis process.

The electrochemical nitrogen reduction reaction (eNRR) is a surface-dependent process, making the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) a critical indicator of catalytic performance. To assess this, we estimated the double-layer capacitance (Cdl) via cyclic voltammetry (CV) measurements in a non-Faradaic potential window (0.3–0.45 V vs RHE) (Figure a–e). For undoped MnO2 and lightly doped V-MnO2(0.06), the Cdl values were nearly identical (0.42 and 0.43 mF cm–2, respectively), consistent with their similar nanorod morphologies and limited specific surface area. However, at higher vanadium doping levels (V-MnO2(0.15), V-MnO2(0.2), and V-MnO2(0.3)), Cdl increased significantly to 0.52, 0.68, and 0.67 mF cm–2, respectively. Notably, the Cdl values for V-MnO2(0.2) and V-MnO2(0.3) were comparable, though the former exhibited a marginally higher ECSA. This trend aligns with SEM, TEM, and BET data, where higher doping levels led to nanoparticle/nanosheet formation and increased specific surface area. Given its optimal Cdl and structural properties, V-MnO2(0.2) was selected for further eNRR performance testing as the most promising catalyst.

Electrocatalytic Performance

The NRR activity of V-MnO2(0.2) was first evaluated through linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) in N2-saturated H2SO4 solutions with different pH values (pH = 1, 2, 3, and 4). As shown in Figure S2a, the lower the pH value, the higher the current density. As the pH value increases, the current density gradually decreases. When the pH are 3 and 4, the current density reaches a minimal value and the two LSV curves nearly overlap. This indicates that when the pH value rises to a certain level, the changes in the hydrogen ion concentration have almost negligible effects on the NRR reaction. To optimize the reaction conditions, we conducted a systematic potential-dependent study from −0.2 to −0.6 V (vs RHE). Quantitative analysis via the indophenol blue method (Figure S3) reveals a volcano-type relationship between applied potential and ammonia yield at all pH values, with absorption intensity peaking at −0.4 V (Figure S2b–e). The ammonia yield–Faradaic efficiency (FE) curve calculated from the absorbance and current density-time curves (Figure S1f) at −0.4 V is shown in Figure S2g. When the pH is 1, the ammonia yield reaches 20.6 μg h–1 mgcat –1, but the FE is only 5.3%. The low FE indicates that a significant portion of the charge is consumed by the hydrogen evolution side reaction, resulting in substantial energy waste. When the pH is 3 or 4, the FE increases to 30%, but the ammonia yield drops to approximately 8 μg h–1 mgcat –1, which is too low to meet industrial production efficiency requirements. When the pH is 2, the ammonia yield and FE are 14.5 μg h–1 mgcat –1 and 17.3%, respectively. Both parameters fall within reasonable ranges, making pH = 2 the optimal acidity condition.

Figure a presents the LSV curves both in N2- and Ar-saturated H2SO4 solutions (pH = 2). While both curves display similar trends, the N2-saturated system exhibits significantly higher current densities at equivalent potentials, confirming enhanced catalytic activity for nitrogen reduction. Figure b presents the corresponding chronoamperometric profiles, while UV–vis analysis of postreaction electrolytes (Figure c) verifies successful NH3 generation, reaching an ammonia yield optimal production rate of 14.5 μg h–1 mgcat –1 at −0.4 V (Figure d). Notably, FE demonstrates a monotonic decrease with increasing overpotential; −0.3 V shows the highest FE (28%) with an ammonia production rate of 12.5 μg h–1 mgcat –1. As the overpotential increased to −0.4 V, FE increased to 17.3%. At −0.4 V, the reaction rate is enhanced at the expense of some selectivity, resulting in a higher overall NH3 yield. At lower overpotentials (e.g., −0.3 V), the competition from HER is reduced, but the slower reaction kinetics lead to a decrease in the production rate per unit time. Taking into account the yield and FE value comprehensively, −0.4 V was adopted as the optimal potential in the subsequent experiments. Control experiments using the Watt–Chrisp method confirmed minimal hydrazine generation (<0.8 μg h–1 mgcat –1 across all potentials) (Figures S4 and S5), indicating high selectivity for the desired NH3 product over potential N2H4 byproducts.

To unambiguously verify the nitrogen source in the produced ammonia, we performed isotopic labeling experiments using both 14N2 and 15N2 gas feeds. As shown in Figure e, the 1H NMR spectra exhibit distinct fingerprint patterns for each isotope: characteristic triplet peaks (J = 52 Hz) for 14NH4 + when using 14N2 feed gas and corresponding doublet peaks (J = 73 Hz) for 15NH4 + with 15N2 feed gas. This clear isotopic signature provides definitive evidence that the nitrogen atoms in the generated ammonia originate exclusively from the molecular nitrogen feedstock rather than any potential nitrogen-containing contaminants. − The observed coupling constants and peak patterns match precisely with literature values for ammonium ions containing these nitrogen isotopes, confirming the electrocatalytic N2 reduction pathway.

To confirm that N2 serves as the primary nitrogen source for the ammonia synthesis reaction, a series of comparative experiments were carried out: (i) The V-MnO2(0.2) catalyst was subjected to electrolysis at the open-circuit voltage in a nitrogen (N2)-saturated electrolyte for a duration of 2 h. (ii) The carbon paper (CP) electrode was electrolyzed at a potential of −0.4 V in an N2-saturated electrolyte for the same 2 h period. (iii) The V-MnO2(0.2) catalyst underwent electrolysis at −0.4 V in an argon (Ar)-saturated electrolyte for 2 h. The UV–vis absorption spectra of the electrolytes obtained from these three experiments after treatment with the indophenol indicator are depicted in Figure a. The results reveal that only trace amounts of NH3 were detected in all three cases compared to the performance of the V-MnO2(0.2) catalyst. This finding underscores that the electrocatalytic nitrogen reduction reaction (NRR) only occurs under an N2-saturated atmosphere and at a specific applied voltage.

In addition to catalytic activity, the durability and stability of catalysts are crucial parameters for evaluating their performance. Figure b (Figure S6) illustrates the NH3 yield rates and corresponding Faradaic efficiencies (FEs) of the V-MnO2(0.2) catalyst for 10 2 h cycles in N2-saturated electrolytes. Notably, the NH3 yield rates remained relatively consistent, with minor fluctuations, and the FEs remained nearly stable throughout these cycles. As shown in Tables S1 and S2, the relative standard deviations (RSDs) of NH3 yield rate and FE are 6 and 5% respectively, indicating good repeatability. The electrocatalytic performance of V-MnO2(0.2) was systematically evaluated through alternating 2 h cycles in N2- and Ar-saturated electrolytes (Figure c and Figure S7). Under a N2 atmosphere, the catalyst demonstrated stable ammonia production with a consistent yield rate of 13 μg h– 1 mgcat – 1 and maintained an FE of ∼12%. In stark contrast, when operated in an Ar atmosphere, the system showed negligible ammonia generation (∼4 μg h– 1 mgcat – 1), resulting in a minimal FE of 2% that falls within the experimental background level. This marked difference in catalytic activity between N2 and Ar environments provides compelling evidence for the material’s authentic electrocatalytic nitrogen reduction capability under ambient conditions. Furthermore, after a prolonged 100 h cycle (Figure d), there was no significant change in the current density of the catalysis at an applied potential of −0.4 V. The XRD diffraction patterns of the catalyst before and after cycling are shown in Figure S8a. Since the catalyst is attached to the carbon cloth, the diffraction peaks of carbon can be clearly observed, while the characteristic peaks of V-MnO2 are relatively weak. The XRD patterns before and after cycling remain consistent, showing almost no noticeable changes. From the microscopic morphology images (Figure S8b,c), it can be observed that after prolonged cycling, the catalyst no longer maintains its initial nanoparticle morphology but instead forms flake-like structures adhered to the carbon cloth fibers.

The ammonia synthesis performance of vanadium-doped manganese dioxide was benchmarked against reported electrocatalysts under ambient conditions, as summarized in Table . Key observations include that V-MnO2 achieves a competitive NH3 yield rate of 14.5 μg h– 1 mgcat – 1, outperforming most catalysts in the surveyed literature. The Faradaic efficiency (17.3%) demonstrates superior selectivity compared to the listed electrocatalysts under comparable reaction conditions. Defect engineering strategies have been demonstrated as effective for modulating catalyst properties. , High-valent vanadium doping induces the formation of manganese vacancies, which further promotes the generation of oxygen vacancies (V O). These dual vacancies (Mn and O vacancies) can effectively regulate the bandgap states and surface electronic properties of metal oxides, thereby significantly influencing the physicochemical characteristics of the catalyst. , This synergistic effect facilitates the capture and activation of inert N2 molecules, ultimately enhancing nitrogen reduction reaction (NRR) performance.

Conclusions

The V-MnO2(0.2) material demonstrates exceptional electrocatalytic performance for ambient-condition ammonia synthesis, achieving a high NH3 yield rate of 14.5 μg h– 1 mgcat – 1 and a remarkable Faradaic efficiency of 17.3% at −0.4 V vs RHE. Key advantages of this catalyst system include outstanding operational stability with negligible performance degradation over extended testing and excellent selectivity for NRR against competing hydrogen evolution. Comparative analysis reveals that these performance metrics surpass most previously reported NRR electrocatalysts operating under similar ambient conditions. The success of this simple yet effective doping strategy provides valuable insights for rational design of transition metal oxide catalysts, optimization of the electronic structure for NRR applications, and development of efficient noble-metal-free electrocatalysts. This work establishes a promising direction for sustainable ammonia production through electrochemical means.
