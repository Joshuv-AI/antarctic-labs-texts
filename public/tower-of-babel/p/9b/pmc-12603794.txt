Cerebrospinal fluid proteins in relation to longitudinal cognitive trajectories
Authors: Bo‐Hyun Kim, Sang Won Seo, JunPyo Kim, Kwangsik Nho, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative#
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe investigated longitudinal cognitive trajectories in relation to cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) proteomics in Alzheimer's disease (AD).METHODSDifferential protein abundance analysis of proteomics (SomaScan 7K) data was performed for composite scores for domain‐specific cognitive functions (memory [MEM], executive function [EF], and language [LAN]) and their longitudinal changes in AD. BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the most common neurodegenerative disease, characterized by senile plaques and neurofibrillary tangles 1 composed of amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau proteins, two of the main hallmarks of AD. 2 , 3 In cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), Aβ42 reflects cortical amyloid plaque formation, while phosphorylated tau (p‐tau) represents pathological tau hyperphosphorylation. These CSF protein biomarkers have been extensively studied for their ability to reflect the pathological characteristics of AD. 4 However, these core CSF proteins represent only a small fraction of the complex biological mechanisms underlying the disease. Therefore, it is necessary to identify a diverse range of AD biomarkers to reflect these underlying pathological changes.

Along with Aβ and tau proteins, cognitive decline is a typical feature of AD. Cognitive dysfunction reflects the pattern of pathological changes in AD, as neuropathological changes in the cerebral cortex and limbic system lead to deficits in learning, memory (MEM), language (LAN), and visuospatial skills. 5 Several cognitive composite scores have been developed, 6 and these measures can sensitively detect subtle cognitive decline during the preclinical phase of AD. In addition, the association between cognitive decline and Aβ and tau has been reported, along with the relationship between cognitive composite scores and these pathological markers. 7 , 8

Most demented elderly individuals exhibit more than one hallmark pathology of cognitive decline. 9 The overlapping pathologies undermine the current diagnostic framework for dementia and highlight the need for understanding the pathophysiological mechanisms. Thus, omics approaches have been emphasized to discover diverse AD biomarkers. Moreover, advancements in large‐scale sequencing technologies have led to a significant increase in the number of human AD proteomic studies in recent years. Proteins are the optimal markers for understanding diseases as they are most proximal to phenotypic changes. Recent studies have investigated protein alterations across different brain regions in AD, identifying differentially abundant proteins and their associated biological pathways. 10 , 11 These findings provide important new insights into AD pathogenesis and highlight potential drug targets and biomarkers for the disease. In addition, network proteomics analysis has emerged as a valuable tool for organizing complex proteomic data into groups or “modules” of co‐expressed proteins, which reflect various biological functions linked to AD. 12 , 13

Protein concentrations in CSF can reflect ongoing pathophysiological processes in the brain and have been widely studied to examine the molecular complexity of neurodegenerative diseases. These studies have identified proteins with altered abundance related to neuronal death, tau phosphorylation, immune system and inflammation responses, carbohydrate metabolism, phospholipid processing, and synaptic regulation. 14 , 15

This study aimed to delineate the biological processes underlying longitudinal cognitive trajectories in AD using CSF proteomics and longitudinal domain‐specific composite scores (MEM, executive function [EF], and LAN) for cognitive function. We conducted a comprehensive analysis of CSF proteome data. First, we analyzed the protein abundance of SOMAscan 7K proteomics data of participants across the full spectrum of AD in relation to both cross‐sectional cognitive performance and longitudinal cognitive trajectories. Second, we performed machine learning analysis to develop a prediction model for cognitive trajectories to identify individuals at higher risk for faster cognitive decline. Finally, we performed weighted co‐abundance network analysis and functional enrichment analysis to further explore protein modules and their associated biological pathways linked to longitudinal, domain‐specific cognitive trajectories.

METHODS

Study participants

This study used data from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) cohort. ADNI is a longitudinal study with > 50 sites across the United States and Canada launched as a public–private partnership in 2004, and details have been described previously. 16 , 17 In this study, a total of 728 participants were included in the cross‐sectional analysis, comprising 173 cognitively normal (CN) older adults, 403 individuals with mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and 152 with AD. All participants had CSF proteomics data available at baseline and domain‐specific composite scores (MEM, EF, and LAN) for cognitive function. For the longitudinal analysis, 697 participants with follow‐up cognitive assessments were included, consisting of 170 CN, 398 MCI, and 129 AD individuals.

CSF proteomics

CSF proteomics data were downloaded from the ADNI database (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/). CSF proteomic profiling was conducted using the SOMAscan 7K platform, measuring the abundance of ≈ 7000 proteins. Normalization and quality control (QC) procedures were performed in advance to address plate effects and technical variability. 18 , 19 The raw data were initially divided into two matrices: one containing experimental samples and another for non‐sample controls, including buffers, calibrators, and QC samples. SomaLogic performed the initial data normalization using hybridization controls for intra‐plate adjustments and median signals to correct for inter‐plate variability. 20 Additionally, an external reference was used for further normalization to account for biological variation. Aptamer‐level QC was applied based on protocols from previous studies. 18 , 19 Aptamers were removed if they failed either of two criteria: (1) if the maximum absolute difference between the aptamer scale factor and the median scale factor of any plate was ≥ 0.5, or (2) if the median cross‐plate coefficient of variation (CV) was ≥ 0.15. Next, the interquartile range (IQR) was calculated for each aptamer based on log‐10 transformed aptamer levels. Aptamer values that exceeded 1.5 times the IQR were replaced with missing values (NA). Aptamers with a call rate < 65% (i.e., detected in < 65% of samples) were excluded, and the same threshold was applied to filter out low‐quality samples. The call rate was then recalculated, and a stricter threshold of 85% was applied. Additionally, analytes targeting non‐human proteins were removed. After filtering aptamers, the sample call rate was recalculated, and a final threshold of 85% was applied at the sample level. The analysis included 728 samples and 7008 aptamers targeting 6360 unique proteins passed the final QC.

Neuropsychological data

Cognitive composite scores were downloaded from the ADNI database. Comprehensive neuropsychological evaluations were used to calculate composite measures for MEM, EF, and LAN. 21 , 22 , 23 Briefly, composite scores for memory included the Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test, Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), Logical Memory I‐II, and Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale Cognitive Subscale (ADAS‐Cog) Test. Composite scores for EF included Digit Span Backwards, Category Fluency, Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale‐Revised (WAIS‐R), Digit Symbol Substitution, Trail Making Tests A and B, and the Clock Drawing Test. Composite scores for language included Category Fluency, ADAS‐Cog, MMSE, Boston Naming Test, and the Montreal Cognitive Assessment.

Differential protein abundance analysis

The associations between the abundances of 7008 CSF aptamers (6360 proteins) and cross‐sectional cognitive composite scores—including MEM, EF, and LAN—were assessed using linear regression, with adjustments for age, sex, and education as covariates, while the association between longitudinal cognitive changes and protein abundances was analyzed using a linear mixed‐effects model with the same covariates. CSF protein levels were log10 transformed, and longitudinal cognitive scores over a 5‐year period were used in the analysis. The p values were adjusted for multiple comparisons using the false discovery rate (FDR), and proteins with an FDR p value < 0.05 were identified as differentially abundant.

Functional enrichment analysis

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially abundant proteins was performed using Gene Ontology (GO) terms via Metascape. 24 Proteins with increased and decreased abundances were analyzed separately for each cognitive domain score. The results of functional enrichment analysis were obtained by the online database Metascape, and GO terms that met the criteria of p value < 0.01, a minimum count of 10, and an enrichment factor > 1.5 were considered significant. The enrichment network was visualized using Cytoscape v3.9.1, 25 with clustering and annotation of significant GO terms performed using the AutoAnnotate app 26 implemented in Cytoscape.

Machine learning analysis for a prediction model for cognitive trajectories

To evaluate the potential of the identified proteins in predicting longitudinal cognitive changes, we developed a prediction model using the identified proteins as input features. All protein abundance values used as input features for the machine learning model were normalized, and missing values were median‐imputed. The longitudinal cognitive changes were calculated using a linear mixed‐effects model, with age, sex, education, and baseline cognition as covariates. Individual changes of cognitive domain scores were estimated using random slopes derived from the linear mixed‐effects model. Participants were grouped into faster and slower decline patterns using k‐means clustering on individual slopes and intercepts derived from longitudinal trajectories.

Machine learning algorithms, including logistic regression, naïve Bayes, elastic net, support vector machine (SVM), decision tree, random forest, extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost), and light gradient boosting (LightGBM), were trained using 5‐fold cross‐validation. These models were implemented using the corresponding R packages: glm for logistic regression, e1071 for naïve Bayes and SVM, glmnet for elastic net, rpart for decision tree, randomForest for random forest, xgboost for XGBoost, and lightgbm for LightGBM. To evaluate the performance of a prediction model for cognitive trajectories, logistic regression with APOE ε4 status as an input feature was used as a base model (Model 1). In addition, three prediction models of the identified proteins (Model 2) for domain‐specific composite scores were constructed using different sets of input protein features, respectively. In the prediction models (Model 2), we calculated the importance of input features based on the absolute coefficient values derived from elastic net regression.

Weighted protein co‐abundance network analysis

Network‐based co‐abundance network modules of CSF proteomics data were identified using the weighted gene correlation network analysis (WGCNA) package in R. 12 Out of the 7008 aptamers obtained from 728 participants, aptamers within the lower quantile (Q1) with the lowest standard deviations were excluded to filter out non‐variable proteins, leaving a total of 5256 aptamers, which were used to construct the network. The WGCNA blockwiseModules function was run with a soft‐threshold power of 9 and a minimum module size of 30. The biweight midcorrelation (bicor) was used to compute similarity, and a signed network type was used to calculate network adjacency.

Network modules in the protein co‐abundance network were identified by summarizing the module eigenprotein (ME) as the first principal component of each module. The relationships between module MEs and cognitive scores were analyzed using linear regression models for cross‐sectional cognitive scores and linear mixed‐effect models for longitudinal changes in cognitive scores. Age, sex, and education were considered covariates. Results were reported as p values after adjusting for multiple comparisons using the FDR correction through the Benjamini–Hochberg (BH) procedure. Modules with FDR‐adjusted p values < 0.05 were considered significant. Fisher exact test (FET) was used to assess cell type enrichment for each module's proteins using a merged brain cell marker list and the script from https://github.com/edammer/cellTypeFET.

Functional enrichment analysis of module proteins

Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using proteins from modules associated with cognitive scores using Metascape. 24 Enrichment analysis was based on GO terms, with a minimum protein overlap of 10; enrichment terms with p value < 0.01 were considered significant. The enrichment network was visualized using Cytoscape v3.9.1, 25 and clustering and annotation of significant GO terms were performed using the AutoAnnotate app 26 within Cytoscape.

Construction of protein network and identification of hub proteins

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks of module proteins were constructed using the STRING database 27 (https://string‐db.org/). Interactions with a confidence score > 0.7 were used to construct PPI network and maximal clique centrality (MCC) was calculated using cytoHubba to identify hub proteins. 28 In this study, the top 20 proteins ranked by MCC scores were considered hub proteins for each module.

RESULTS

Study participant characteristics

A total of 728 participants from the ADNI cohort, all of whom had both CSF proteomics data at baseline and longitudinal domain‐specific composite scores for cognitive function, were included in this study. For the cross‐sectional analysis, the study participants consisted of 173 CN individuals, 403 individuals with MCI, and 152 patients with AD, as summarized in Table S1 in supporting information, which also includes demographic information and cognitive scores such as MEM, EF, and LAN. For the longitudinal analysis, a total of 697 participants were included, comprising 170 CN individuals, 398 with MCI, and 129 with AD. Detailed demographic and cognitive information for the longitudinal dataset are presented in Table S2 in supporting information.

Proteins related to cross‐sectional domain‐specific composite scores and their longitudinal trajectories

Differential protein analysis (DPA) was performed using linear regression models and linear mixed‐effects models to identify proteins associated with domain‐specific composite scores at baseline and their longitudinal changes. A total of 690 aptamers were identified as significantly associated with composite scores for MEM at baseline. Among these, 226 aptamers exhibited increased abundance levels in individuals with higher MEM scores, while 464 aptamers exhibited decreased abundance levels in those with higher MEM scores (Figure 1A; Tables S3–S5 in supporting information). Proteins with increased abundance levels included ATE1 (arginyltransferase 1), S100A13 (S100 calcium‐binding protein A13), ATG7 (autophagy related 7), TBCA (tublin folding cofactor A), and HYKK (hydroxylysine kinase), whereas proteins with decreased abundance levels included YWHAG (14‐3‐3 gamma), NEFH (neurofilament heavy chain), YWHAZ (14‐3‐3 zeta), YWHAB (14‐3‐3 beta), and TCEAL3 (transcription elongation factor A like 3; Figure 1A; Table S3). A total of 578 aptamers were significantly associated with baseline composite scores for EF. Of these, 366 showed increased abundance and 212 showed decreased abundance in individuals with higher EF scores (Figure 1B; Table S4). Proteins with increased abundance included NPTX2 (neuronal pentrazin‐2), NPTXR (neuronal pentraxin receptor), CCK (cholecystokinin), and DCUN1D1 (defective in cullin neddylation 1 domain containing 1), while proteins with decreased abundance included YWHAG, NEFH, GPI (glucose‐6‐phosphate isomerase), MAPRE3 (microtubule associated protein RP/EB family member 3), and YWHAB. A total of 526 aptamers were significantly associated with baseline composite scores for LAN. Of these, 216 aptamers showed increased abundance, and 310 aptamers showed decreased abundance in individuals with higher LAN scores (Figure 1C; Table S5). Proteins with increased abundance included NPTX2, NPTXR, LRFN2 (leucine‐rich repeat and fibronectin type III domain containing 2), and IRF1 (interferon regulatory factor 1), while proteins with decreased protein levels included YWHAG, NEFH, GPI, MMP10 (matrix metallopeptidase 10), and YWHAB.

Functional enrichment analysis of differentially abundant proteins

To investigate the biological functions of differentially abundant proteins, we performed pathway enrichment analysis with GO terms. The enrichment analysis was conducted separately for increased and decreased proteins for each cognitive domain at baseline. The increased proteins for MEM were enriched in 264 pathways related to the neuronal cell body, regulation of transferase activity, and the glutamatergic synapse axon (Figure 1D), while the decreased proteins were enriched in 162 pathways related to the neuronal cell body, regulation of transferase activity, and the glutamatergic synapse axon (Figure 1G). Similarly, the increased proteins for EF were enriched in 244 pathways related to nervous system development, regulation of neuron differentiation, and cell adhesion recognition (Figure 1E), while the decreased proteins were enriched in 127 pathways related to regulation of vesicle mediated transport, ubiquitin protein ligase activity, and protein catabolic process (Figure 1H). Additionally, the increased proteins for LAN were enriched in 122 pathways related to regulation of synapses and axons, receptor regulator signaling, and regulation of cell activation (Figure 1F), while the decreased proteins were enriched in 133 pathways related to amino acid modification, regulation of cytoskeleton organization, and extracellular matrix (Figure 1I).

For longitudinal changes of cognitive domains, the increased proteins for MEM were enriched in 270 pathways related to nervous system development, transmembrane receptor proteins, and regulation of epithelial cells (Figure 3D), while the decreased proteins were enriched in 163 pathways related to cytoskeleton organization and projection, protein catabolic processes, and peptidyl amino acids (Figure 3G). Similarly, proteins with increased abundance related to EF were enriched in 141 pathways, including those related to projection neuron development, receptor kinase activity, and signaling receptor function (Figure 3E). In contrast, proteins with decreased abundance were enriched in 94 pathways related to axons, glutamatergic synapses, and peptidyl‐amino acid modification (Figure 3H). Additionally, the increased proteins for LAN were enriched in 100 pathways related to cell adhesion synapses, neuronal cell bodies, and sensory organ development (Figure 3F), while the decreased proteins were enriched in 120 pathways related to regulation of synaptic organization, protein catabolic processes, and peptidyl amino acid modification (Figure 3I).

Machine learning analysis to predict longitudinal trajectories in domain‐specific composite scores

Machine learning models were trained to evaluate the prediction performance of protein signatures for longitudinal cognitive trajectories. Model 1 was trained using logistic regression with APOE genotypes as an input feature. Model 2 was trained using eight different machine learning algorithms, incorporating APOE and proteins identified from DPA analysis as input features. We trained machine learning models by increasing numbers of the top‐ranked proteins, ranging from the top 10 to top 50 proteins identified from the DPA analysis (Tables S15–S17 in supporting information). Among these, elastic net regression demonstrated consistently high average area under the curve (AUC) values across different feature sets. Based on these results, we selected the elastic net model and compared the prediction performance of Model 1 and Model 2 (Figure S2 in supporting information). For longitudinal trajectory of composite scores for MEM, Model 1 achieved an AUC of 0.63 and Model 2 achieved an AUC of 0.76. Consistent with the findings in the MEM prediction model, the EF and LAN prediction models (Model 2) also demonstrated improved performance when protein features were included, compared to the only APOE model (Model 1; Figure S2A–C). Subsequently, we evaluated feature importance based on the absolute coefficient values derived from elastic net regression to construct the final prediction model (Figure S2D–F). We constructed our final model using the proteins with a feature importance (FI) score > 0.4. FI analysis revealed that nine proteins, including YWHAB, YWHAG, and YWHAZ, had importance values > 0.4 in the MEM prediction model; four proteins, YWHAG, YWHAE, NPTX2, and GARS1 (glycyl‐tRNA synthetase 1), in the EF model; and four proteins, YWHAZ, YWHAG, NPTX2, and MAPRE3, in the LAN model. The final prediction model for MEM achieved an AUC of 0.79, representing 27.4% improvement in AUC compared to Model 1. Similarly, the final prediction model for EF achieved an AUC of 0.75, representing 33.9% improvement. The final prediction model for LAN achieved an AUC of 0.76, showing a 35.7% improvement (Figure 4). The performance of the final models using proteins with feature importance > 0.4, as well as the models using the top 10 proteins for each cognitive domain, is shown in Figure S3 in supporting information.

Protein co‐abundance network modules and their pathways associated with domain‐specific composite scores for cognitive functions

To ensure a scale‐free network, scale independence and mean connectivity were assessed using the gradient method with power values ranging from 1 to 20, with a soft‐thresholding power of β = 9 selected (Figure S4 in supporting information). After the construction of the scale‐free network, the modules were detected using a hierarchical clustering dendrogram (Figure S5 in supporting information). As a result, 4488 of the 5256 aptamers were clustered into 15 modules based on abundance correlations, while 768 unclustered aptamers were excluded from further analyses. These modules reflect various biological functions related to brain cell types and ontologies. The module associations with cognitive domain scores were examined (Figure 5). Among the 15 modules, four modules (ME4, ME5, ME14, and ME15) were significantly associated with cross‐sectional domain‐specific composite scores for cognitive scores, while five modules (ME4, ME6, ME7, ME14, and ME15) showed significant associations with longitudinal changes in these scores. The ME15 module showed the strongest association with cross‐sectional cognitive performance, including MEM (FDR p = 6 × 10−10), EF (FDR p = 2 × 10−5), and LAN (FDR p = 3 × 10−3; Figure 5A). It also showed significant associations with longitudinal changes in MEM (FDR p = 9 × 10−10), EF (FDR p = 9 × 10−4), and LAN (FDR p = 2 × 10−5; Figure 5B). The ME4 module showed associations with both cross‐sectional and longitudinal changes in MEM, EF, and LAN (Figure 5; FDR p < 0.05). ME5 and ME14 exhibited associations with cross‐sectional EF (Figure 5A), while ME6 showed association with longitudinal changes in MEM (Figure 5B). Additionally, ME7 and ME14 showed associations with longitudinal changes in EF (Figure 5B).

As shown in Figure 5C, the largest module, ME1, showed strong enrichment for neuronal proteins (e.g., ENO2, B4GALT6, and MRAP2), along with additional contributions from oligodendrocyte, endothelial, and astrocytic proteins. ME2 was primarily enriched with microglial markers (e.g., STAT1, STAT6, and AGA), while ME3 displayed strong enrichment for neuronal proteins (e.g., SATB2, PIAS4, and ADGRG2). ME13 included proteins predominantly expressed in endothelial (e.g., COL28A1, FLNA, and CD93) and astrocytic cell types (e.g., CNN1, TAGLN, and EFHD1). ME5 was enriched with oligodendrocyte proteins (e.g., SLITRK3, LINGO3, and GALNT13) and also contained proteins expressed by neurons (e.g., NEGR1, FLRT3, and CA10), microglia (e.g., IRF1, TMEM106A, and PLOD3), and astrocytes (e.g., NR3C2, CNTFR, and ADGRB3). Similarly, ME6 was enriched for microglial proteins (e.g., ELMO1, NEURL1, and IL1R2). However, ME4, ME14, and ME15 were not enriched for any of the five main brain cell types.

Hub proteins in network modules based on the interaction networks

The interaction networks in each network module were constructed, and hub proteins within a network were determined by the MCC score. The ME4 module consists of 263 protein nodes and 411 edges, with the top 20 hub proteins ranked by MCC values, including HSP90AA1 (heat shock protein 90 alpha family class A member 1), HSP90AB1 (heat shock protein 90 alpha family class B member 1), and HSPA8 (heat shock protein family A [Hsp70] member 8; Figure 6A). Similarly, the ME5 module comprises 227 nodes and 146 edges, with hub proteins including EGFR (epidermal growth factor receptor), FGF17 (fibroblast growth factor 17), and HGF (hepatocyte growth factor; Figure 6B). The ME6 module contains 205 nodes and 372 edges, with hub proteins including AHSG (alpha 2‐HS glycoprotein), ALB (albumin), and SERPINA1 (serpin family A member; Figure 6C). Last, the ME7 module consists of 206 nodes and 47 edges, with hub proteins including CTLA4 (cytotoxic T‐lymphocyte associated protein 4), INS (insulin), and TIGIT (T cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains; Figure 6D). Proteins in the ME14 and ME15 modules showed no interactions with a confidence score > 0.7. Functional enrichment analysis of network module proteins

To investigate the biological functions of the network module proteins that were significantly associated with composite scores for domain‐specific cognitive functions, we performed GO pathway analysis. The 274 proteins in ME4 were enriched in 200 pathways related to the regulation of binding proteins, protein catabolic processes, aminoacyl‐tRNA ligase activity, focal adhesion, and cadherin binding (Figure 7A). Similarly, 248 proteins in ME5 were enriched in 179 pathways related to projection neuron development, inflammatory response, and axonal processes (Figure 7B). In ME6, 237 proteins were enriched in 119 pathways related to cholesterol homeostasis, immune‐regulating signaling, and calcium ion binding (Figure 7C). Likewise, 210 proteins in ME7 were enriched in 99 pathways related to immune effector regulation, histone‐modifying activity, and plasma membrane regulation (Figure 7D). However, no significant GO terms were enriched for the proteins in the ME14 and ME15 modules with a minimum protein overlap threshold of 10. DISCUSSION

In this study, we analyzed CSF proteomics data to investigate the relationships with cross‐sectional and longitudinal changes in domain‐specific cognitive functions. Longitudinal DPA analysis identified 854 proteins with altered abundance associated with longitudinal changes of composite scores for MEM over time, 477 with longitudinal changes of composite scores for EF over time, and 420 with longitudinal changes of composite scores for LAN over time. These longitudinal findings highlight protein changes that are closely linked to progressive cognitive decline in AD. In addition, cross‐sectional DPA analyses identified 690 proteins with altered abundance associated with composite scores for MEM, 578 with composite scores for EF, and 526 with composite scores for LAN. Furthermore, machine learning models incorporating differentially abundant proteins improved prediction performance, particularly for longitudinal cognitive trajectories. Network analyses further revealed four co‐abundance network modules associated with cognitive domain scores and five modules specifically associated with longitudinal changes in cognitive domain scores. These modules were enriched for biological processes including the regulation of protein binding, cell development, and immune regulatory signaling. Together, these results suggest potential CSF biomarkers for AD progression and provide new insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying longitudinal domain‐specific cognitive decline in AD.

In the DPA analyses, several differentially abundant proteins, including YWHAG, S100A13, and NPTX2, were associated with longitudinal cognitive trajectories. The 14‐3‐3 protein family (e.g., YWHAG, YWHAZ, and YWHAB) is involved in signal transduction and neuronal plasticity and has been linked to AD pathology and cognitive decline, particularly through associations with tau pathology. 29 , 30 , 31 S100A13 plays a role in neuroinflammation and is influenced by APOE ε4. 32 NPTX2, a marker of inhibitory circuit dysfunction, is reduced in the AD cortex and CSF, correlating with cognitive performance and hippocampal volume, and its CSF ratio to total tau (t‐tau) is a strong predictor of cognitive decline. 33 , 34 NPTXR, related to synaptic plasticity and neuronal communication, is associated with positron emission tomography Aβ and CSF biomarkers such as Aβ42, t‐tau, and p‐tau. 35

Among them, S100A13, ATE1, YWHAG, and NEFH were consistently associated with all cognitive domains, suggesting their potential as shared molecular markers of cognitive decline in AD. ATE1 is involved in post‐translational modification and has been shown to regulate G‐protein signaling via RGS7 (regulator of G‐protein signaling 7), suggesting a potential involvement in AD. 36 NEFH (neurofilament heavy chain), a key structural protein in neurons, has been associated with AD and interacts with 14‐3‐3 proteins in the postsynaptic cytoskeleton. 37 , 38 These shared proteins with increased abundance are involved in axonal and synaptic development, whereas those with decreased abundance are associated with the modulation of chemical synaptic transmission and neuron‐to‐neuron signaling, which are pathways known to be implicated in the pathophysiology of AD.

A subset of proteins demonstrated cognitive domain‐specific characteristics, indicating their potential involvement in distinct molecular mechanisms underlying specific cognitive domain scores. SPACA3 and ADPRH were specific to MEM, EXTL2 and CRH to EF, and SSR2 and AREG to LAN. ADPRH regulates cellular signaling and stress responses by removing ADP‐ribose from arginine residues on proteins. 39 EXTL2 is a glycosyltransferase involved in heparan sulfate biosynthesis; heparan sulfate proteoglycans contribute to synaptogenesis and synaptic plasticity, and their interaction with Aβ promotes plaque formation, 40 leading to neuronal damage and cognitive decline. Mutations in HS biosynthetic genes, including EXTL2, have been linked to neurodevelopmental disorders and cognitive deficits. 41 CRH confers neuroprotection in the hippocampus by activating CRH receptor type 1, reducing oxidative stress‐induced neuronal death, suppressing nuclear factor kappa beta, and promoting the release of non‐amyloidogenic soluble amyloid precursor protein, thus contributing to cognitive preservation and reducing AD pathology. 42 AREG is a growth factor in the EGF (epidermal growth factor) family that promotes cell growth and repair. In mouse models, BMAL1‐mediated transcriptional activation of AREG has been shown to alleviate cognitive dysfunction. 43 SSR2 is a glycosylated ER membrane protein, and its gene has been reported to be overexpressed in the hippocampus of AD brains. 44 Specific proteins with increased abundance associated with MEM decline are involved in the regulation of cell recognition, a process essential for maintaining brain homeostasis. Disruption of cell recognition, particularly through microglial dysfunction, has been implicated in the progression of AD. 45

Functional enrichment analysis of the ME4 module, which includes hub proteins such as HSP90AA1, HSP90AB1, and HSPA8, revealed significant enrichment in pathways related to the protein binding. HSP90 proteins contribute to the prevention of protein misfolding and aggregation, key pathological features of AD, and are involved in the regulation of tau metabolism and Aβ processing. 46 , 47 Proteins in the ME5 module, including the hub proteins EGFR, FGF17, and HGF, are enriched in biological pathways associated with cell development and axonal function. They also show the strongest enrichment in oligodendrocytes, suggesting a potential role in oligodendrocyte‐related neurobiology. EGFR contributes to neural stem cell maintenance, astrocyte and oligodendrocyte maturation, and neurite outgrowth, and has been linked to Aβ pathology and cognitive function. 48 , 49 FGF17, highly expressed in the CSF, plasma, and cortex, plays a key role in cell proliferation and early neurogenesis and is associated with MEM and cognition. 50 , 51 HGF, involved in neuronal development and survival, is elevated in the CSF of AD patients and has been associated with cognition and white matter damage. 52 , 53 The ME6 module, comprising hub proteins such as AHSG, ALB, and SERPINA1, is enriched in pathways associated with the inflammatory response and immune regulation, and its constituent proteins are predominantly enriched in microglia, suggesting a potential role in microglia‐mediated neuroinflammation. AHSG, abundant in the cortical plate, has been identified as a serum and CSF biomarker in AD 54 and is reduced in the cortex, hippocampus, and thalamus in AD mouse models. 55 ALB, the most abundant CSF protein, binds to Aβ, inhibiting its aggregation and reducing hyperphosphorylated tau. 56 , 57 SERPINA1 is overexpressed in the CSF and brain of AD patients and is implicated in neurodegeneration and neuroinflammation. 58 , 59 Proteins in ME7, including hub proteins CTLA4, INS, and TIGIT, are enriched in pathways related to immune effector regulation and hematopoiesis. CTLA4 is a key negative regulator of T‐cell activation via the B7‐CD28/CTLA4 pathway, potentially contributing to neurodegeneration in dementia. 60 INS regulates glucose metabolism, and insulin resistance can promote neuroinflammation, synaptic dysfunction, and Aβ and tau accumulation, leading to cognitive impairment. 61 , 62 TIGIT is an immunoregulatory protein elevated in the peripheral blood of healthy elderly individuals 63 and, along with CTLA4, may play a role in neuroinflammation in AD. 60 , 64 Given these immunomodulatory roles, TIGIT may serve as a novel risk marker for AD.

The machine learning models for predicting cognitive trajectories demonstrated improved predictive performance when protein features were incorporated, with up to a 30.9% increase in performance compared to the model using APOE only. Several proteins exhibited greater contributions to the prediction models, including YWHAB, YWHAG, and YWHAZ for MEM; YWHAG, NPTX2, YWHAE, and GARS1 for EF; and YWHAZ, YWHAG, NPTX2, and MAPRE3 for LAN. GARS1 is a key enzyme involved in protein synthesis, a process that may be potentially implicated in the pathogenesis of neurodegenerative diseases such as tauopathies. 65 Together, these results underscore the relevance of protein features in advancing the precision of cognitive trajectory modeling.

Our study has some limitations that should be considered. First, the sample size is modest; thus, replication in larger independent cohorts is needed to validate the findings, but availability of datasets with both CSF proteomic profiles and longitudinal cognitive data remains limited. Second, although we examined associations between proteomic data using both cross‐sectional and longitudinal cognitive domain scores, causal relationships cannot be inferred from these analyses. Further studies using longitudinal proteomic data with causal inference methods are needed to clarify the temporal links between protein changes and cognitive decline. Third, the data used in this study were predominantly derived from individuals of Caucasian ancestry, limiting our ability to assess population‐specific associations. Future studies including more diverse populations are needed to ensure generalizability and to identify potential population‐specific effects.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, we investigated the relationship between CSF protein abundances and cognitive domain scores, with a particular focus on longitudinal cognitive trajectories, to identify protein changes associated with AD. Through DPA and network analyses, we identified proteins and co‐expressed modules whose altered abundance was significantly associated with longitudinal changes in MEM, EF, and LAN. Functional analyses revealed that these proteins are involved in diverse biological pathways implicated in AD. Furthermore, we demonstrated that differentially abundant proteins improved the performance of machine learning models in forecasting future cognitive decline. These findings highlight the potential of CSF proteomics in capturing dynamic disease processes and provide valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying progressive cognitive decline in AD.
