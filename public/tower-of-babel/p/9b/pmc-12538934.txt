Impact of digoxin versus beta-blocker in patients with coexistent atrial fibrillation and heart failure: a target trial emulation
Authors: Lin Liu, An-Ping Cai, Jia-Yi Huang, Wen-Li Gu, Ran Guo, Jing-Nan Zhang, Hao-Chen Xuan, Yap-Hang Chan, Ai-min Xu, Bernard M. Y. Cheung, Gregory Y. H. Lip, Kai-Hang Yiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare the impact of digoxin versus beta-blocker on adverse clinical outcomes in patients with coexisting atrial fibrillation (AF) and heart failure (HF).MethodsThis study employed a target trial emulation with a clone-censor-weight approach to analyze data from 28,377 patients diagnosed with both AF and HF in the Clinical Data Analysis and Reporting System (CDARS) in Hong Kong between January 1, 2005, and December 31, 2017. Patients were followed up for up to 3 years or until the occurrence of clinical outcomes. The exposures were digoxin (N = 5351) versus beta-blocker (N = 7655) within a 90-day grace period. Absolute risks (ARs), risk differences, and risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using weighted pooled logistic regression adjusted for demographic characteristics, comorbidities, and medication use. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality, while secondary outcomes included cardiovascular (CV) mortality, heart failure hospitalization, acute ischemic stroke, acute myocardial infarction, and pacemaker implantation.ResultsOver 3 years, digoxin was associated with a significantly higher risk of all-cause mortality (AR: 51.2% vs. 42.2%; RR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.17 to 1.26), CV mortality (AR: 25.1% vs. 21.0%; RR: 1.20; 95% CI: 1.11 to 1.29), and heart failure hospitalization (AR: 29.0% vs. 26.4%; RR: 1.10; 95% CI: 1.04 to 1.16). No significant differences were observed for acute ischemic stroke (AR: 4.3% vs. 4.3%; RR: 1.00; 95% CI: 0.85 to 1.18), acute myocardial infarction (AR: 4.6% vs. 4.3%; RR: 1.04; 95% CI: 0.88 to 1.23), or pacemaker implantation (AR: 1.0% vs. 1.3%; RR: 0.74; 95% CI: 0.54 to 1.01).ConclusionsIn patients with coexisting AF and HF, digoxin was associated with significantly higher risks of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and heart failure hospitalization compared to beta-blocker.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-025-04408-0.

Background

Atrial fibrillation (AF) and heart failure (HF) are two prevalent cardiovascular disorders that are increasingly impacting global health, contributing significantly to economic burdens, morbidity, and mortality [1, 2]. These conditions frequently coexist, not only due to shared risk factors but also because each can predispose the other [3]. Independently, AF and HF escalate the risk of adverse health outcomes; together, they exacerbate conditions more severely than either condition alone, emphasizing the need for effective clinical management of this patient population [4, 5].

Heart rate control is a cornerstone of therapy for patients with either AF or HF [6, 7]. Treatment options for pharmacological rate control are typically limited to beta-blockers, digoxin, or a combination of both in patients with coexistent AF and HF. Beta-blockers have been shown to significantly improve survival and reduce hospital admissions in patients with HF in sinus rhythm; however, these benefits are not as evident in patients with HF and sustained AF [8–10]. The efficacy and safety of digoxin remain contentious, with studies reporting both negative and neutral effects in patients with AF [11, 12]. A meta-analysis indicated no increased mortality in patients with both AF and HF, yet an increase was noted in patients with AF without HF [13].

However, direct comparisons between digoxin and beta-blockers in this demographic are scarce. Recent findings from the Rate Control Therapy Evaluation in Permanent Atrial Fibrillation (RATE-AF) study revealed no significant differences in quality of life at 6 months between patients treated with digoxin or bisoprolol [14]. Similarly, another observational study found no difference in all-cause mortality and adverse cardiovascular events between the two treatments [15].

The limitations posed by small sample sizes and short follow-up durations in these studies underscore the urgent need for future investigations with larger study populations and prolonged monitoring periods to thoroughly evaluate the efficacy of digoxin versus beta-blockers in patients with AF and HF. To address these limitations by utilizing a territory-wide dataset and applying the target trial emulation framework, our study aims to compare the long-term adverse events associated with digoxin versus beta-blockers in patients with coexistent AF and HF.

Methods

Data source

We utilized the Clinical Data Analysis Reporting System (CDARS), a comprehensive, territory-wide database established by the Hong Kong Hospital Authority, for this study. As the primary provider of public healthcare services in Hong Kong, the hospital authority delivers more than 80% of inpatient care across a population of 7.5 million [16]. Since 1993, CDARS has been prospectively gathering a wide range of patient information, including demographic details, diagnoses, drug prescriptions, procedures, laboratory tests, and hospital visit records. Previous studies have validated the high accuracy of CDARS data coding, particularly diagnostic data identified through the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD-9) [17–19]. For privacy compliance, patients’ names and Hong Kong identification numbers were anonymized, with unique reference numbers assigned. The study received approval from the institutional review board of the University of Hong Kong and the West Cluster of the Hong Kong Hospital Authority (IRB Reference No. UW25-323).

Study design and eligibility criteria

We conducted a population-based cohort study using a target trial emulation (TTE) framework and the clone-censor-weight (CCW) approach [20–22] to compare the effects of digoxin versus beta-blockers on adverse clinical outcomes among patients with concurrent AF (ICD-9: 427.3) and HF (ICD-9: 402, 404, 425, 428). The emulation of the target trial is detailed in Additional file 1: Table S1 [20–25]. Eligible participants were those aged over 18 who had been diagnosed with both AF and HF in the CDARS from January 1, 2005, to December 31, 2017. We identified all individuals diagnosed with AF and HF from inpatient and outpatient records within the CDARS spanning from January 1, 1993, to December 31, 2017. To accurately capture new-onset concurrent cases, we implemented a minimum of 12-year washout period, excluding patients with any AF diagnosis in HF patients before January 1, 2005, or vice versa. The enrolment date was defined as either the first date of an AF diagnosis in HF patients, the first date of an HF diagnosis in AF patients, or the date of concurrent diagnosis. Exclusion criteria included prescriptions for beta-blockers (bisoprolol, metoprolol, carvedilol, propranolol), digoxin, amiodarone, diltiazem, verapamil within 180 days before the enrolment date, and any history of pacemaker implantation (n = 1526). After these exclusions, a total of 28,377 participants were deemed eligible. The flow chart of the study population is depicted in Additional file 1: Fig. S1.

Treatment strategies

The treatment comparison was between digoxin and beta-blocker in participants. Digoxin group was defined as the administration of any dose of digoxin within a 90-day grace period following the enrolment date. Similarly, patients receiving any dose of beta-blockers during the grace period were categorized under beta-blocker group. Patients deviated from the assigned treatment (switched from one drug to the other or did not start either digoxin or beta-blocker) during the grace period were artificially censored in the study (Additional file 1: Fig. S2).

Covariates

Covariates assessed at the enrolment date included age, sex, the temporal relationship of AF and HF (AF before HF, HF before AF, concurrent AF and HF), year of cohort entry (2005–2009, 2010–2013, 2014–2017), and CHA2DS2-VASc score. Comorbidities such as anemia, cancer, coronary heart disease (CHD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic liver disease (CLD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, dyslipidemia, hypertension, myocardial infarction (MI), obesity, peripheral vascular disease (PVD), rheumatic arthritis (RA), ischemic stroke, and hemorrhagic stroke were identified using respective ICD-9 codes recorded at or before the enrolment date. Medications considered included acarbose, angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors (ACEIs), angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), aspirin, calcium channel blockers (CCBs), dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitors (DPP-4i), insulin, loop diuretics, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs), metformin, oral anticoagulants (OACs), statins, sulfonylureas, and thiazolidinediones within 180 days before or at the enrolment date. Detailed definitions of covariates are provided in Additional file 1: Table S2. And the directed acyclic graph of treatment and clinical outcomes were described in Additional file 1: Fig. S3.

Study outcomes

The study design diagram is illustrated in Additional file 1: Fig. S4. All patients were followed from their enrolment date until the occurrence of a study outcome or until 3-year post-enrolment. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality. The secondary outcomes were cardiovascular mortality, heart failure hospitalization, acute ischemic stroke, acute myocardial infarction, and pacemaker implantation, with the detailed definitions of secondary outcomes provided in Additional file 1: Table S3.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics of eligible participants were presented using means ± standard deviation (SD) or median [interquartile range Q1, Q3] for continuous variables and frequencies (percentages) for categorical variables.

To emulate a target trial, we implemented a cloning, censoring, and weighting approach with a 90-day grace period for treatment screening. We generated a dataset with two copies of each eligible individual (cloning) and designated each replicate to one of the treatment strategies at the enrolment date. We then monitored adherence to the assigned treatment strategies; replicates whose treatment deviated were censored at the point of deviation, ensuring adherence to the assigned treatment plan. To correct for potential selection bias introduced by censoring, individual was assigned an inverse probability weight. These weights were calculated using a pooled logistic model that included censor time (time from index date to the date deviating from the assigned treatment, modelled as linear and quadratic terms) and all baseline covariates. Weights were truncated at the 1st and 99th percentiles to mitigate the influence of outliers, creating two pseudo-populations where treatment was independent of measured factors. Standardized mean difference (SMD) was used to evaluate the difference in patient characteristics between two treatment groups before and after weighting. Details of the clone-censor-weight approach are further elaborated in the Additional file 1: Supplementary Methods.

The cumulative incidence of all-cause mortality was estimated using weighted pooled logistic regression. This model incorporated an indicator for treatment strategy, event time (linear and quadratic terms), and their interactions to accommodate nonproportional hazards, with all baseline covariates adjusted to standardize cumulative incidence. We derived 3-year absolute risks, risk differences (beta-blocker group as reference), and risk ratios (RRs, beta-blocker group as reference), along with pointwise 95% confidence intervals (CIs), using a nonparametric bootstrap of 300 samples.

We also performed subgroup analyses for all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and heart failure hospitalization to test the robustness and consistency of our results. Subgroups included age (< 80 years vs. ≥ 80 years; < 90 years vs. ≥ 90 years), sex (women vs. men), temporal relation of AF with HF (AF before HF vs. concurrent AF and HF vs. HF before AF), baseline CKD (yes vs. no), and CHA2DS2-VASc score (< 5 points vs. ≥ 5 points). The CCW approach mentioned above was applied to all subgroups.

We conducted several sensitivity analyses. First, we varied the grace period from 90 days to 60 and 120 days, respectively. Second, we compared the results using untruncated weights. Third, we estimated the cumulative incidence, absolute risks, risk differences, and risk ratios for both 1-year and 5-year outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline characteristics

We identified 28,377 eligible individuals with coexistent AF and HF (mean age: 80.0 years; 44.8% male, Table 1). The distribution of AF before HF, concurrent AF and HF, and HF before AF was 44.0%, 25.2%, and 30.8%, respectively. The median CHA2DS2-VASc score was 5 points (interquartile range: 4–6 points). After censoring for treatment deviation during the grace period, 5351 individuals remained in the digoxin group, and 7655 remained in the beta-blocker group. The characteristics for each treatment strategy at the end of the grace period, both before and after weighting, are shown in Table 2. Inverse probability weighting demonstrated the balance of covariates (SMD < 0.1), except for hypertension, ARB, and CCB (SMD: 0.11, 0.11, and 0.13, respectively). The duration of the treatment strategy within the 90-day grace period is shown in Additional file 1: Table S4, with 90.0% and 90.7% of individuals maintaining treatment for ≥ 60 days in the beta-blocker and digoxin groups, respectively. Discussion

Using real-world data to emulate a target trial, our study demonstrated that digoxin is associated with significantly higher risks of all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, and heart failure hospitalization compared to beta-blockers in patients with coexistent AF and HF. These findings were consistent across various subgroups, including age, sex, temporal relation of AF with HF, baseline CKD, and baseline CHA2DS2-VASc score.

Few studies have directly compared the effectiveness of digoxin and beta-blockers in patients with coexistent AF and HF. For instance, the RATE-AF trial [14], a well-conducted randomized controlled trial (RCT) involving 160 patients with permanent AF and HF symptoms, found no statistically significant differences in quality of life at 6 months between those treated with low-dose digoxin and those treated with bisoprolol. Secondary analyses of the trial reported numerically lower adverse events, including deaths, in the digoxin arm compared to bisoprolol. Similarly, an observational study of 540 matched patients using similar inclusion criteria to the RATE-AF trial found no significant differences in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, or major clinical events between the two treatments [15].

Comparable results were also observed in the European Society of Cardiology–European Heart Rhythm Association (ESC–EHRA) EURObservational Research Programme Atrial Fibrillation (EORP-AF) General Long-Term Registry, which showed a neutral effect of digoxin compared to beta-blocker [adjusted hazard ratio (HR), 1.45 (95% CI, 0.79–2.65)] in heart failure subgroup [26]. In contrast, our study found that digoxin group had a 21% higher risk of all-cause mortality, a 20% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality, and an 11% higher risk of hospitalization heart failure compared to beta-blocker group during 3 years of follow-up.

Several factors may contribute to our unique results. First, the primary outcome of RATE-AF trial was 6- and 12-month quality of life, with a sample size of 160 patients. The trial was not designed to statistically compare hard clinical outcomes (e.g., mortality, adverse cardiovascular events) [27]. Our study substantially expanded the sample size and prolonged the follow-up period to 3 years, enabling a comprehensive comparison of the two treatment strategies. Second, the ESC–EHRA study compared digoxin and beta-blocker use in heart failure subgroups, with baseline comorbidities which were significantly unbalanced between the treatment strategies, and participants receiving beta-blockers had a higher prevalence of CKD, hypertension, and coronary artery disease [26]. Our study weighted the treatment strategies using a series of covariates to ensure balance. Third, although the 540-patient observational study performed 1:1 matching of baseline characteristics between the two groups [15], the authors did not address the potential for immortal time bias, which can lead to misestimation of outcome event rates in one or both groups [28]. To eliminate immortal time bias and emulate a target trial, our study conducted a CCW approach using grace periods of 60, 90, and 120 days, respectively [21]. Lastly, our study included patients at the first recorded diagnosis of coexistent AF and HF, regardless of the temporal relationship, whereas previous studies did not report the timing of AF or HF diagnoses or their duration. The co-occurrence of AF and HF is associated with a significantly worse prognosis than either condition alone, marking a new stage of disease burden [27, 29, 30]. To reassess clinical practices for this population, we further excluded patients with previous digoxin or beta-blocker use and specifically compared the effectiveness of digoxin versus beta-blockers as primary therapy.

It is also worth noting that racial differences may contribute to the inconsistencies between our findings and those of previous studies, especially since differences in clinical outcomes such as stroke and bleeding have been described between Asians and non-Asians [31, 32]. Prior studies included a small proportion of Asian participants, who are known to have greater sensitivity than White individuals to the effects of propranolol on heart rate [33].

Our study showed no significant difference in acute ischemic stroke or acute myocardial infarction. These findings are highly consistent with a Denmark national cohort study [10], which reported a neutral effect of beta-blockers on thromboembolic events (including ischemic stroke, systemic embolism, pulmonary embolism, and myocardial infarction; HR, 1.02 [95% CI, 0.93–1.12]) in AF patients with prevalent HF. In addition, we found that the digoxin group tended to have a lower 3-year risk of pacemaker implantation (RR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.54–1.01]), which aligns with the RATE-AF trial, where pacemaker implantation was numerically lower in the digoxin group compared to the bisoprolol group (0 vs. 2). However, when we adjusted the follow-up period to 1 year and 5 years, nonsignificant effects were also observed (RR, 0.84 [95% CI, 0.60–1.14] and RR, 0.80 [95% CI, 0.60–1.06], respectively). These findings suggest that the observed differences might not be statistically significant and could represent random variation rather than a true effect of the medication.

Beta-blockers are a cornerstone of HF therapy, with their benefits in patients with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) strongly supported by large RCTs [34–36]. As a result, they are given a Class IA recommendation in both European and American guidelines. Beta-blockers are also recommended (Class IB) for AF management due to their ability to slow conduction through the atrioventricular node [7, 37]. However, this recommendation is not based on large, randomized outcome trials. In a patient-level meta-analysis of landmark RCTs including individuals with HFrEF, beta-blockers significantly reduced all-cause mortality in patients with sinus rhythm but not in those with AF at baseline [8]. This discrepancy may stem from the lack of benefit associated with slower heart rates in AF compared to sinus rhythm [38], and a low heart rate in patients with AF may be an expression of an underlying conduction disorder, which may be associated with impaired outcome itself [39]. Although additional prospective studies are required to elucidate this relationship further, beta-blockers are generally considered safe for patients with AF and HF [10] and are supported by a Class IIa recommendation.

The mechanism of action of digoxin includes neurohormonal (antiadrenergic or vagal) effects, electrophysiological effects (prolongation of the atrioventricular node refractory period), cellular effects (inhibition of the sodium–potassium ATPase pump), and resultant hemodynamic changes [16]. Digoxin is recommended as a Class IIb therapy for symptomatic HFrEF in sinus rhythm, based on evidence from the Digitalis Investigation Group (DIG) study [40], which showed that digoxin reduces hospitalization rates and symptom burden but has a neutral effect on mortality. For AF patients, digoxin is recommended as a Class IB therapy for heart rate control, similar to beta-blockers, though previous studies have reported conflicting results regarding its impact on mortality [41, 42]. A 2015 systematic review and meta-analysis suggested a neutral effect of digoxin on mortality [11], whereas a 2023 umbrella review indicated that digoxin use is associated with a moderately increased risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality [43], making a declined trend in digoxin prescription [44].

Our study provides direct evidence comparing digoxin and beta-blockers in real-world settings, supporting beta-blockers as the preferred treatment in patients with coexistent AF and HF. However, our study could not prove digoxin itself increased mortality risk compared to nonusers or placebo. Instead, its effectiveness appears neutral or less pronounced than that of beta-blockers [45]. Additionally, beta-blockers plus digoxin therapy may also be a potential treatment option; however, this combination was not included in our study. It was also challenging to determine whether patients switched from one drug to another or added one drug to another using electronic prescription records alone. Consequently, patients with prescriptions for both digoxin and beta-blockers during the grace period were censored from both treatment strategies, leaving only individuals on monotherapy in the cohort. Further randomized studies are warranted to clarify the effectiveness of different treatment strategies.

Strengths and limitation

One of the main strengths of our study is its large sample size and extended follow-up period, a feature that sets it apart from previous research. Further, the use of a territory-wide, well-validated electronic healthcare database (CDARS), which includes comprehensive records of diagnoses, hospitalizations, and drug dispensations. This robust dataset minimizes common biases, such as recall bias, often present in conventional observational studies. Second, we employed the TTE framework with a CCW approach, a method designed to estimate the causal effect of treatment and its duration using observational data. This approach, widely applied in studies utilizing large electronic health records, effectively reduces the risk of bias, accounting for time-dependent confounders, immortal time bias, and strengthens causal inference [25, 46].

Despite these strengths, several limitations should be noted. First, although the TTE framework mitigates bias, the observational nature of the data means treatment assignment was not randomized, leaving the possibility of residual confounding. Second, the grace period used in our study was exploratory, as no prior evidence supports a specific duration. The grace period is necessary because heart rate control therapy is not always initiated immediately after an AF and HF diagnosis, and treatment strategies may evolve during the early stages of care. We used a 90-day grace period in the main analysis and conducted sensitivity analyses with 60- and 120-day grace periods, which yielded consistent results. Third, we were unable to differentiate between patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) due to the lack of left ventricular ejection fraction and other echocardiographic parameters in the database. This is a notable limitation since beta-blockers are only recommended for HFrEF patients [47]. The inclusion criteria concerning the type of HF was also not specified in the RATE-AF study, as it was challenging to accurately assess systolic function in patients with AF [14]. In the meantime, we could not stratify patients by AF patterns (e.g., paroxysmal, persistent, chronic, or post-operative); however, the treatment was fully determined by physicians in a real-world setting, this ensures a degree of homogeneity in the study population. In addition, we weighted the population from different groups by a series of baseline characteristics, which showed a good balance. Fourth, we could not evaluate dynamic treatment interventions, especially those occurring beyond the grace period, which may be clinically relevant. However, our study was designed to emulate a hypothetical scenario in which patients remain on their initial treatment (a static strategy), and we tested alternative grace periods to ensure robustness of the findings. Lastly, lifestyle factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption were not routinely recorded in CDARS. Nevertheless, we accounted for important confounding variables that may partially reflect these risk factors, such as COPD and chronic liver disease [18].

Conclusions

In this large, population-based cohort of patients with coexistent AF and HF, digoxin was associated with higher risks of all-cause, cardiovascular mortality and heart failure hospitalization compared to beta-blockers. Our findings contribute valuable evidence to the discussion on pharmacological heart rate control options in patients with coexistent AF and HF, supporting the preferential use of beta-blockers in this patient population. Further randomized controlled trials will be needed to confirm these findings.
