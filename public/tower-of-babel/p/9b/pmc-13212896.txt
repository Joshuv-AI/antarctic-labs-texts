Maternal obesity disrupts epigenetic reprogramming via peroxisomal-dependent phospholipid-methyl uncoupling during zygotic genome activation
Authors: Xiao-Guohui Zhang, Lin-Li Yang, Xie Feng, Yu-Wei Zhang, Lei Guo, Sun-Xing Huang, Tie-Gang Meng, Pei-Ying Li, Lei-Ning Chen, Rui-Bao Su, Jun-Yu Ma, Xiao-Yan Fan, Jia-Li Su, Yue-Fan Wang, Lan-Fang Luo, Qing-Yuan Sun, Shyh-Chang Ng, Shi-Ming Luo, Xiang-Hong Ou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Maternal obesity is known to cause systemic lipid dysregulation, yet its effect on lipid reprogramming during the maternal-to-zygotic transition (MZT) remains unknown. Here we show that obesity disrupts very-long-chain fatty acids (VLCFAs) storage in oocytes and downregulates PEX13, impairing peroxisomal protein import in 2-cell embryos. Normally, peroxisomal β-oxidation converts oocyte-stored VLCFAs into medium- and long-chain fatty acids, which fuel triglyceride synthesis and drive phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) methylation to phosphatidylcholine (PC). This metabolic flux consumes methyl donors to facilitate H3K4me3 erasure and zygotic genome activation (ZGA). In obese mice, VLCFAs deficiency and PEX13 dysfunction lead to metabolic-epigenetic uncoupling, depleting lipid droplets and sustaining H3K4me3, thereby suppressing ZGA and blastocyst development. Importantly, supplying long-chain fatty acids or overexpressing PEMT restore the phospholipid-methyl cycle, rescuing epigenetic reprogramming and embryonic development. Our findings establish peroxisomal β-oxidation as a metabolic-epigenetic nexus essential for MZT and reveal a phospholipid-methyl coupling mechanism underlying obesity-associated embry development, offering novel therapeutic entry points to improve fertility in metabolic disorders.

Introduction

Obesity has emerged as a global public health crisis1–3, with maternal obesity representing a leading cause of infertility and adverse reproductive outcomes4–6. Exposure to metabolic dysfunction during critical developmental windows such as oogenesis and early embryogenesis can disrupt offspring development and elevate long-term disease risk7,8. Embryo transfer in murine study confirms that these defects often originate from oocyte-specific impairments, including meiotic defects, mitochondrial dysfunction, and oxidative stress, rather than the uterine environment9–11. The oocytes rely on maternally stored factors, which govern essential processes like epigenetic reprogramming, mRNA degradation, and ZGA12. Disruption of above components, through altered metabolic conditions or environmental stress, can lead to early embryo arrest13. While oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction have been extensively implicated in obesity-associated developmental arrest10,14,15, the impact of maternal obesity on endogenous lipid reprogramming and its underlying mechanisms during the MZT remains poorly understood. Notably, the role of dysregulated lipid metabolism in epigenetic remodeling during the MZT remains largely unexplored.

The oocyte-to-embryo transition (OET) defines a critical window of developmental plasticity, during which the early embryo relies exclusively on maternally inherited organelles and cytoplasmic resources12,16. This transition is characterized by extensive epigenomic reprogramming, including the erasure of maternal epigenetic marks such as H3K4me3 and the initiation of ZGA17–19. Importantly, the MZT is accompanied by comprehensive metabolic remodeling involving carbohydrates, amino acids, and lipids. Metabolic intermediates derived from these pathways not only supply energy and biosynthetic precursors but also directly influence epigenetic reprogramming, thereby functionally linking metabolic changes with the regulation of chromatin status and gene expression during this critical window20–22.

Eukaryotic cells must reprogram metabolism and gene expression in response to a dynamic nutrient environment23. Metabolic regulation is indispensable throughout mammalian embryogenesis, serving not only bioenergetic and biosynthetic functions but also guiding precise developmental transitions22. Recent studies have illuminated the interdependence of metabolic and epigenetic remodeling during this process24. Pyruvate is essential pre-ZGA, maintaining redox balance via LDH-mediated lactate conversion and NAD⁺ regeneration, while also providing acetyl-CoA and α-KG for epigenetic regulation during ZGA20. Lactate, derived from pyruvate, accumulates in the nucleus and promotes lactylation to facilitate ZGA, its inhibition causes embryonic developmental arrest, highlighting its epigenetic role25,26. Glucose gains importance later, promoting trophectoderm specification through HBP-driven O-GlcNAcylation of YAP and TFAP2C, and supporting redox and nucleotide synthesis via PPP27. α-Ketoglutarate acts as a cofactor for epigenetic demethylases and contributes to redox balance through reduction to 2-HG under stress, linking tricarboxylic acid cycle activity to early embryogenesis28. Concurrently, lipid metabolism provides structural components and energy, critically influencing membrane fluidity, cellular polarization, and blastocyst formation21,29. While the impact of maternal and metabolic disorders on oocyte quality and offspring metabolic health is increasingly recognized8,30. The specific metabolic pathways that orchestrate lipid-mediated epigenetic reprogramming during the MZT, and how these are disrupted in the context of maternal obesity, remain largely elusive.

Lipids have emerged as key regulators of cell fate decisions31,32. Lipid metabolism plays a pivotal role in early embryonic development21, serving not only as an energy source but also as a critical regulator of membrane remodeling, signaling, and epigenetic modifications29. Recent metabolomics studies revealed dynamic lipids remodeling during oocyte maturation and pre-implantation development33. For example, reduced polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) levels correlate with oocyte maturation competency34, while the serine-glycine one-carbon network actively modulates epigenetic marks in mature oocytes23,35,36. Furthermore, recent lipidomic studies have revealed dynamic changes in lipid species, particularly phospholipids and unsaturated fatty acids (unSFAs), during preimplantation stages, with notable accumulation and mobilization of lipid droplets facilitating blastocyst formation and implantation21,37. Key enzymes such as stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) modulate membrane fluidity and apical protein trafficking, thereby influencing cell polarity and lineage specification33.

Peroxisomal function as intracellular sensors for fatty acids and play a central role in lipid homeostasis by catalyzing the β-oxidation of VLCFAs(C ≥ 22) into medium- and long-chain fatty acids (MCFAs, C6-12 /LCFAs, C14-20), thereby supplying substrates for membrane biosynthesis and signaling pathways38,39. The import of peroxisomal matrix proteins is mediated by the cytosolic receptor Peroxisomal biogenesis factor 5 (PEX5), which recognizes proteins bearing a C-terminal peroxisomal targeting signal 1 (PTS1, e.g., SKL motif)40. Peroxisomal biogenesis factor 13 (PEX13), a key docking component on the peroxisomal membrane, facilitates the translocation of PEX5–cargo complexes into the matrix and is essential for protein import41. Concurrently, ABCD3 (ATP binding cassette subfamily D member 3, also named PMP70, Peroxisomal Membrane Proteins), a peroxisomal membrane transporter, imports VLCFA-CoAs into peroxisomes for β-oxidation42. The rate-limiting enzyme of peroxisomal β-oxidation, acyl-CoA oxidase 1 (ACOX1), is imported via PEX5 and catalyzes the first step of fatty acid β-oxidation within the matrix43,44. Thus, the functional integration of ABCD3 (substrate import), PEX5 (protein import), and ACOX1 (metabolic catalysis) ensure efficient peroxisomal β-oxidation45–47. Genetic ablation of Acox1 results in poor health and infertility in offspring, underscoring the indispensability of peroxisomal function48. However, the role of peroxisomes in lipid remodeling and epigenetic reprogramming remain uncharacterized during the OET.

Chronic hyperlipidemia due to maternal obesity disrupts lipid homeostasis in mouse oocytes, leading to accumulation of cytotoxic lipid intermediates49,50. Fatty acids serve as phospholipid precursors, supporting PC synthesis via the Kennedy pathway (de novo synthesis pathway) or PE methylation (Lands cycle)—a process that consumes S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and thereby links lipid metabolism to epigenetic regulation51. Impaired PE methylation elevates SAM levels and causes aberrant histone hypermethylation52. However, it remains unknown whether maternal obesity affects peroxisomal β-oxidation and whether lipid remodeling during the MZT contributes to epigenetic reprogramming. Understanding how maternal metabolic status influences peroxisomal lipid metabolism and its crosstalk with epigenetics remains a fundamental biological question.

Here, using a high-fat diet (HFD)-induced mouse model, we demonstrate that peroxisomal dysfunction impairs the β-oxidation of VLCFAs (C ≥ 22), leading to a subsequently decreased lipid droplet formation, and disrupting the metabolic link between phospholipids and the methyl pool. Strikingly, we observed conserved defects in human oocytes and embryos from obese individuals, evidenced by down-regulated PEX13 expression and a concomitant failure to import ACOX1 and SKL-tagged proteins into peroxisomes. Functional rescue experiments revealed that LCFAs supplementation or phosphatidylethanolamine N-methyltransferase (PEMT) overexpression can promote H3K4me3 erasure, enable ZGA and rescue embryonic development. These results demonstrate that maternal obesity disrupts peroxisome-dependent VLCFAs catabolism, leading to metabolic-epigenetic uncoupling and embryonic developmental arrest. Our findings establish peroxisomal β-oxidation as a metabolic-epigenetic nexus critical for MZT. Moreover, our findings highlight histones as a metabolic methyl pool, directly linking lipid-derived metabolic fluxes to epigenetic modifications and revealing a mechanistic basis for obesity-associated embryonic defects.

Results

Maternal obesity impairs peroxisomal protein import and β-oxidation, compromising lipid remodeling during MZT

Maternal obesity induces systemic lipid dysregulation, yet its impact on endogenous lipid reprogramming in early embryogenesis is poorly understood. To address this question, we generated maternally obese mouse model using HFD (Fig. 1a, SFig 1a,b), and found that embryos from these obese mice exhibited partial developmental arrest at the 2-cell stage (Fig. 1b,c). The 2-cell embryo stage is a critical period for OET and ZGA in mice12. Maternal obesity compromises both the quality and quantity of oocytes and embryos11,13. Micro-metabolomics facilitated the investigation of oocyte-to-embryo developmental dynamics by requiring minimal sample input. We first evaluated the reliability of micro-metabolomics (with a per-sample input of 10 cells) using three cell groups (10, 100, and 1000 cells) with 10-fold incremental cell numbers (SFig 1c,d), and then profiled metabolites across germinal vesicle (GV) oocytes, metaphase II (MII) oocytes, zygotes and 2-cell embryos from normal diet (ND) and HFD mice (Fig. 1d). The sparse partial least squares-discriminant analysis (sPLS-DA) uncovered developmentally programmed metabolic remodeling during the OET by micro- and trace-metabolomics analysis (Fig. 1e, SFig. 1e). And at each cell development stage, the HFD group and the ND group had obvious separation trend in micro-metabolomics (Fig. 1e). The GV and MII oocytes displayed prominent accumulation of VLCFAs, including saturated (octacosanoic acid (C28:0), tetracosanoic acid (C24:0)), and monounsaturated VLCFAs (nervonic acid (C24:1), erucic acid (C22:1)). Strikingly, this storage profile pattern underwent a dramatic shift at the 2-cell embryo stage, characterized by a significant reduction in the aforementioned VLCFAs and a concurrent rise in saturated and monounsaturated MCFAs and LCFAs (e.g., palmitic acid (C16:0) and myristic acid (C14:0)) (Fig. 1f, SFig. 1f,g). Palmitic acid is a key fatty acid sitting at the crossroads of fatty acid β-oxidation (FAO) and de novo lipogenesis, for phospholipids, triglycerides (TAG), and thus constitutes an important step in lipid remodeling53. This metabolic transition implied that active peroxisomal β-oxidation occurred during the OET, which shortens maternal VLCFAs into MCFAs and LCFAs. In contrast, the 2-cell embryo exhibited a sharp increase in specific polyunsaturated VLCFAs, such as docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C22:6) and docosapentaenoic acid (DPA, C22:5) (Fig. 1f, SFig 1f,g). Previous studies have shown that DHA and DPA were synthesized by oxidating the precursors (tetracosahexaenoic acid (C24:6) and tetracosapentaenoic acid (C24:5)) via peroxisomes β-oxidizing54,55. Importantly, we also observed a substantial elevation in TAG from oocytes to 2-cell embryos (SFig 1h,i), indicating active lipid storage and restructuring during this transition. These results indicated that peroxisomal β-oxidation may be essential for physiological lipid remodeling at 2-cell embryo stage, as demonstrated by shortening maternal VLCFAs into MCFAs and LCFAs, facilitating TAG accumulation and supporting subsequent embryonic development.

While peroxisomal β-oxidation is essential for physiological lipid remodeling during the OET, maternal obesity severely disrupts this process. In HFD-exposed mice, we observed the following metabolic alterations: (i) impaired storage of VLCFAs in oocytes, reflected by reduced levels of C28:0, C24:5, C24:1, C24:0, and C22:1; (ii) defective peroxisomal β-oxidation of VLCFAs in 2-cell embryos, resulting in significant accumulation of C24:1, C24:0, and C22:1; and (iii) dysregulated synthesis of polyunsaturated VLCFAs and LCFAs, including DHA and DPA. Notably, arachidonic acid (ARA, C20:4) was markedly elevated in HFD 2-cell embryos (Fig. 1g, h), suggesting potential compensatory shifts in lipid metabolism.

To investigate the mechanism underlying impaired β-oxidation, we examined peroxisomal protein import. Importantly, transcriptomic analyses revealed significantly reduced expression of Pex13 in GV, MII oocytes as well as 2-cell embryos in HFD mice (Fig. 1i). Consistent with these findings, an mCherry-SKL reporter analysis demonstrated substantial PTS1 import deficiency in 2-cell embryos of HFD group in mice, as indicated by diminished peroxisomal fluorescence and enhanced cytoplasmic signal, reflecting widespread disruption of matrix protein trafficking (Fig. 1j,k). Peroxisomal matrix proteins, including the key β-oxidation enzyme ACOX1 depend on the PTS1 (SKL motif) for organellar localization. Consistent with this import defect, although Acox1 mRNA levels remained unchanged in HFD mouse oocytes and 2-cell embryos (SFig 1j), peroxisomal import of ACOX1 was significantly impaired, leading to reduced intra-peroxisomal ACOX1 protein accumulation (Fig. 1l,m). We next asked whether similar defects occur in human oocytes and embryos from obese patients. Strikingly, RT-qPCR analysis confirmed significant down-regulation of PEX13 expresion in GV and MII oocytes from obese women (Fig. 1n,o). Similarly, evaluation of 8-cell embryos from obese patients revealed substantial impairment of peroxisomal protein import, as demonstrated by both mislocalization of the EGFP-SKL reporter (Fig. 1p,q) and defective peroxisomal import of ACOX1 (Fig. 1r,s). Given that ACOX1 catalyzes the initial and rate-limiting step of peroxisomal β-oxidation56, its functional deficiency provided a mechanistic basis for the observed VLCFAs accumulation and metabolic dysregulation. This import failure, therefore, directly compromised peroxisomal β-oxidation capacity during the MZT.

Together, these results demonstrated that maternal obesity disrupted peroxisomal lipid metabolism by down-regulating PEX13 expression in oocytes, which impaired the protein import machinery, led to mislocalization of enzymes such as ACOX1, and ultimately caused functional deficiency in early embryos.

Peroxisome-mediated β-oxidation drives lipid remodeling essential for 2-cell embryo development

Previous studies have established that the knockout of Acox1 leads to growth retardation and infertility48, thus providing strong evidence for the crucial role of Acox1 in fertility-related processes. To elucidate the precise functional role of peroxisomal β-oxidation during oocyte maturation or embryonic development, we first knocked down Acox1 expression in GV oocytes via siRNA-mediated gene silencing (siAcox1), and simultaneously established a negative control (NC) group. Interestingly, this did not affect meiotic progression or polar body extrusion (SFig 1l,m). We collected in vivo-matured MII oocytes and microinjected them with siAcox1, performed zona pellucida drilling to optimize fertilization conditions. Fertilization occurred in the siAcox1 group, while subsequent embryonic development was consistently arrested at the 1- to 2-cell stage (SFig 1 n,o). And we also specifically knocked down Acox1 expression in mouse zygotes, and this intervention resulted in embryo developmental arrest at the late 2-cell stage (Fig. 2d-g, SFig. 1p,q). These findings suggest that peroxisomal FAO plays a stage-specific and indispensable role during the 2-cell embryonic transition rather than oocyte maturation. Unlike global Acox1 knockout approaches, which fails to eliminate maternally inherited Acox1 transcripts, our siRNA-mediated maternal knockdown strategy achieved near-complete depletion of both maternal and zygotic ACOX1 protein during the OET. This discrepancy explains why prior knockout studies missed the critical requirement for peroxisomal β-oxidation at the 2-cell stage; residual maternal ACOX1 protein (from heterozygous mothers retaining one intact Acox1 allele) can sustain early development, masking the essential function of peroxisomal β-oxidation in 2-cell embryos. Peroxisomes and mitochondria are the main organelles involved in fatty acid metabolism through β-oxidation38. To directly assess the contribution of mitochondrial FAO, we targeted Cpt1a (carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1), the rate-limiting enzyme for mitochondrial fatty acid import. Knockdown of Cpt1a expression in zygotes did not impair embryonic development to the blastocyst stage (SFig 1r,s).

Additionally, to independently validate the functional significance of peroxisomal FAO in early development, we further knocked down expression of Pex5 and Abcd3 via siRNA in zygotes, respectively. Consistent with Acox1 knockdown, siRNA-mediated depletion of Pex5 and Abcd3 similarly arrested embryonic development at the 2-cell stage (SFig 2a-h). Collectively, transcriptomic profiling, IF, and siRNA-mediated knockdown experiments converged to demonstrate that peroxisome-mediated FAO is functionally indispensable for 2-cell embryo development.

To elucidate the precise role of peroxisomal β-oxidation in lipid reprogramming during the OET, we performed integrated metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses in siAcox1 2-cell embryos. The sPLS-DA analysis of the micro- and trace- metabolomics data revealed distinct metabolic profiles between the siAcox1 and control group (SFig 2i,j). Micro-metabolomics identified selective accumulation of saturated, monounsaturated VLFCAs (e.g., C24:0, C22:1) and polyunsaturated LCFAs (e.g., C20:4, C20:5, C18:3). Conversely, essential saturated LCFAs such as C14:0 and C16:0 were reduced (Fig. 2h) in siAcox1 2-cell embryos. These findings aligned with earlier reports that palmitic acid (C16:0) depletion impairs de novo lipogenesis53,57. Consistent with these metabolic alterations, siAcox1 2-cell embryos showed a significant decrease in TAG and phospholipids by Nile Red staining (Fig. 2i-k). Trace metabolomics further validated these trends, revealing a partial reduction in TAG species (Fig. 2l). Together, these results indicated that peroxisomal β-oxidation ablation disrupted VLCFAs catabolism, compromised lipid droplet storage during the 2-cell stage.

In summary, peroxisomal β-oxidation served as a metabolic hub that channels carbon flux from oocyte-stored lipids through the catabolism of VLCFAs. Peroxisome-mediated FAO was responsible for regulating the lipid remodelling in 2-cell embryos, funneling carbons from VLCFAs stored in oocyte lipid droplets into phospholipid and TAG biosynthesis, with consequences for embryonic development to the blastocyst stage.

Peroxisome-mediated β-oxidation regulates phospholipid remodeling at the 2-cell embryo stage

Given the well-established link between fatty acid metabolism and phospholipid synthesis58, we investigated whether peroxisomal β-oxidation contributes to phospholipid remodeling during early embryogenesis. Transcriptomic profiling of 2-cell embryos revealed coordinated expression up-regulation of key genes involved in: (i) de novo PC synthesis (via Pcyt1, Chk, Chpt1); and (ii) PE and lyso-PE (lysophospholipid-PE) methylation and acyl remodeling for PC production (via Pemt, Lpcat2, Lpcat3) (Fig. 3a). To mechanistically dissect the specific role of peroxisomal β-oxidation in phospholipid homeostasis in early development, we analyzed the siAcox1 2-cell embryos. Trace metabolomics revealed that siAcox1 treatment disrupted phospholipid remodeling through two distinct mechanisms: (i) impaired methylation of PE and PE-O via the Lands cycle (PE methylation pathway), which resulted in partial depletion of PC and PC-O (plasmalogen phosphatidylcholines) through the Lands pathway; and (ii) secondary phosphatidylserine (PS) loss downstream of PE depletion (Fig. 3d). Furthermore, these findings were corroborated by reduced PE-specific fluorescence staining (NBD-PE) (Fig. 3e,f) and decreased Pemt expression (P < 0.01) in siAcox1 2-cell embryos (Fig. 3g). Trace metabolomic analysis showed a specific decrease in PE species containing C16 (saturated/monounsaturated fatty acids, SFAs/MUFAs) acyl chains (SFig 2q). Additionally, choline as the initial substrate for de novo PC synthesis, was substantially consumed, and intermediate metabolites in the de novo synthesis pathway accumulated. Simultaneously, SAM/SAH ratios were increased, indicating accumulated methyl pool (Fig. 3h,i, SFig 2r). This metabolic shift suggested that upon suppression of the Lands cycle, embryos might upregulate PC synthesis to maintain basal membrane PC integrity, which was a compensatory response that nonetheless proved insufficient for sustaining development.

In summary, peroxisome-mediated β-oxidation played a pivotal role in phospholipid remodeling at the 2-cell stage by processing maternal VLCFAs into LCFAs, which supported lipid droplet formation and phospholipid-methyl coupling. When this process was compromised, embryos appeared to prioritize the preservation of PC membrane integrity by lipid remodeling—a temporary adaptation that was insufficient for continued developmental progression.

Peroxisomal lipid remodeling governs methyl pool for zygotic genome activation

Metabolic-epigenetic coupling is pivotal for cell fate determination59,60. Meanwhile, compared to the ND group, metabolomic analyses revealed a downward trend in PE and PE-O levels in the HFD group (Fig. 5d, SFig 4a), suggesting an impaired capacity to synthesize PC via the PE methylation pathway. This defect was likely attributable to the reduced availability of PE and PE-O as essential methyl pool. Both LCFAs supplementation and Pemt overexpression partially rescued developmental arrest in siAcox1 embryos, improving blastocyst formation rates (Fig. 6b,c. SFig 4c,d). In addition, we also excluded the influence of sodium hydroxide as a solvent on this results. Microinjection of the sodium hydroxide solution (NaOH) alone had no effect on embryo development and did not rescue the developmental arrest in siAcox1 group (SFig 4e,f). NBD-PE staining further confirmed increased PE abundance in the Pemt-rescue group (Fig. 6 f.g).

Notably, Pemt overexpression increased SAM consumption, reflecting active PE methylation, and reduced the accumulation of de novo PC synthesis intermediates—including choline, CDP-choline, and phosphatidylcholine, indicating a shift away from compensatory de novo synthesis when the Lands cycle was reactivated (Fig. 6h, i). Both rescue strategies also reduced H3K4me3 levels (Fig. 6j, k) and improved transcriptional activation (Fig. 6l, m), supporting restored epigenetic reprogramming and ZGA. These findings further validated our earlier hypothesis: stimulating PE methylation-driven PC synthesis suppressed the de novo PC biosynthesis pathway, thereby maintaining PC homeostasis and modulating methyl donor metabolism in embryos (Fig. 6n).

To establish clinical relevance, we then extended the rescue strategies to embryos from HFD-fed mice, which exhibited identical metabolic signatures depleted saturated and monounsaturated LCFAs and MCFAs, paradoxically elevated ARA (Fig. 1h, 7a). Remarkably, microinjection of either the two LCFA species or Pemt mRNA into HFD zygotes achieved substantial rescue of H3K4me3 erasure, ZGA competence, and blastocyst development capacity in 2-cell embryos (Fig. 7b-g). Most importantly, the LCFAs intervention significantly improved live birth rates(Fig. 7h,i), demonstrating potential therapeutic translation.

Collectively, these findings demonstrated that precision restoration of metabolic homeostasis achieved through either direct metabolic supplementation or targeted enzymatic enhancement can effectively reverse epigenetic reprogramming defects and restore embryonic development potential in both peroxisome-deficient and maternal obesity contexts.

Discussion

Maternal obesity is a well-established risk factor for impaired embryonic development and metabolic infertility11,62,63, yet its specific impact on metabolic reprogramming during the MZT has remained largely unknown. In the present study, we demonstrate that maternal obesity downregulates the expression of PEX13 in oocytes, impairing peroxisomal protein import. This disruption leads to mislocalization of ACOX1—a key enzyme in peroxisomal β-oxidation and compromises the shortening of VLCFAs into LCFAs. Consequently, lipid homeostasis is disrupted, resulting in reduced TAG synthesis and defective phospholipid remodeling. Specifically, the conversion of PE to PC via methylation is impaired, leading to elevated SAM/SAH ratios and aberrant H3K4me3 erasure. This metabolic-epigenetic uncoupling ultimately disrupts ZGA and causes embryo developmental arrest at the 2-cell stage. Our work identifies peroxisomal β-oxidation as a fundamental metabolic-epigenetic coordinator during the MZT and reveals a novel mechanism through which maternal obesity disrupts embryonic development via impaired peroxisomal protein import and lipid-methyl coupling.

In this study, we demonstrate that maternal obesity impairs peroxisomal function through two complementary defects: (i) diminished storage of VLCFAs in oocytes, and (ii) disruption of PTS1-mediated protein import in early embryos. Specifically, we provide the first evidence that maternal obesity downregulates the expression of PEX13, a key regulator of peroxisomal protein import, in both oocytes and embryos of humans and mice. PEX13 serves as a membrane-anchored docking receptor for PTS1-tagged proteins41,64, and its reduction likely compromises the import of peroxisomal matrix enzymes, including ACOX165,66. Although the upstream mechanisms linking obesity to reduced PEX13 expression remain to be elucidated, obesity-related metabolic disturbances such as lipotoxicity or oxidative stress may contribute to their dysregulation. While the connection between obesity and PEX13 suppression is still limited, broader literature supports that systemic metabolic dysfunction can disrupt organellar gene expression and protein homeostasis67,68. Collectively, these deficits lead to mislocalization of ACOX1 and severe impairment of peroxisomal β-oxidation, thereby disrupting lipid remodeling and downstream epigenetic events during the MZT. Our findings establish a novel mechanism through which maternal obesity compromises embryonic development via peroxisomal dysfunction.

The contributions of peroxisomes to lipid metabolism and their unique regulatory roles in early embryonic development remain poorly characterized. Lipids are traditionally viewed as energy substrates, serving as the fundamental building blocks of cellular and organelle membranes, and as signaling molecules69,70. The remodeling of fatty acyl chains in lipids can significantly influence various biological functions in growing cells58,59. From oocytes to embryos, TAG increases suggest a post-fertilization shift toward lipid energy storage and biomembrane remodeling to support embryo cleavage71. Therefore, peroxisomes mediate the shortening of oocyte-derived VLCFAs into various LCFAs accumulation, which in turn supports TAG biosynthesis, with peroxisomes serving as metabolic sensors that integrate lipid homeostasis during the OET. In contrast to our findings in early embryogenesis, Lu et al. demonstrated that hepatic ACOX1 loss altered systemic metabolism via ω−3 VLCFAs, improving insulin sensitivity and adipose remodeling43, whereas its global deletion caused severe hepatic pathology72. This work identified heplatic peroxisomal β-oxidation as a key regulator of metabolic homeostasis, suggesting ACOX1 manipulation as a therapeutic strategy for obesity disorders.

Our comprehensive metabolomic profiling reveals a fundamental principle of embryonic metabolism: methyl donors, particularly SAM, are strategically stockpiled in oocytes, while PC synthesis intermediates-including choline, phosphocholine, and glycerophosphocholine-undergo dramatic depletion during the 2-cell embryo stage. This metabolic shift suggests active phospholipid remodeling essential for post-fertilization developmental competence. Previous studies have shown that methylation of the phospholipid PE is the major SAM-consuming methyl pool reaction which drives sulfur metabolism52. Defective PE methylation for PC synthesis elevates the SAM/SAH ratio and disrupts methylation balance, leading to hypermethylation of histones52. In mice, maternal H3K4me3 exhibits precisely orchestrated dynamic remodeling: initial establishment in GV oocytes followed by systematic erasure in late 2-cell embryos19. Notably, we demonstrate that impaired peroxisomal β-oxidation, whether through maternal obesity or Acox1 knockdown—reduces methyl donor consumption by suppressing PE methylation, thereby disrupting timely H3K4me3 erasure and compromising ZGA in late 2-cell embryos. Collectively, our findings reveal that peroxisomal β-oxidation is essential for normal MZT progression, where it converts VLCFAs into LCFAs to fuel TAG synthesis and drive PE methylation to PC conversion. This metabolic flux acts as a methyl pool that consumes SAM and facilitates timely H3K4me3 erasure. These findings uncover a critical coupling between peroxisomal lipid metabolism and methylation-dependent epigenetic reprogramming during early embryonic development.

To rescue this defect, we employed two strategies: (i) microinjection of SFAs to promote TAG synthesis and phospholipid remodeling; and (ii) overexpression of Pemt to enhance PE methylation and consume excess methyl donors. Both interventions successfully overcame the 2-cell developmental arrest induced by siAcox1 knockdown or maternal obesity-exposured embryos, accompanied by improved H3K4me3 erasure and restored transcriptional activity. This study is the first to identify peroxisomal lipid metabolism as a key mediator linking maternal obesity to epigenetic dysregulation and developmental failure in the early embryos. Notably, the fatty acid injection approach is non-genetic, highlighting its potential for clinical translation. This strategy offers a promising therapeutic avenue for improving reproductive outcomes in obese patients.

In summary, our study reveals that maternal obesity downregulates the peroxisomal import receptor Pex13 in oocytes, leading to impaired PTS1-mediated protein import and disrupted peroxisomal function in 2-cell embryos. We demonstrate that peroxisomal FAO is essential for lipid homeostasis during the MTZ, facilitating the conversion of VLCFAs into LCFAs that support TAG synthesis and enable coupling between phospholipid metabolism and methyl donor availability. This metabolic coupling is essential for epigenetic reprogramming, including histone demethylation and ZGA. Although our rescue strategies show compelling therapeutic potential, further clinical validation is essential before translational application. Future studies should clarify the direct mechanistic links between obesity and Pex13 suppression in oocytes and evaluate whether restoring peroxisomal β-oxidation can improve embryogenesis and fertility outcomes in obese infertile women undergoing assisted reproductive technologies.

Method

Human ethics statement

Human oocyte and embryo specimens were obtained from patients undergoing assisted reproductive technology (ART) at our institution. Subjects were rigorously selected into two groups: an obese group (BMI ≥ 30) and a normal-weight control group (BMI 19-22), matched for age. All obese patients exhibited concomitant hyperlipidemia, glucose intolerance, and insulin resistance. Patients with ovulation disorders, chromosomal abnormalities, or other causes of infertility related to ovarian dysfunction were excluded. Sources of donated specimens were as follows: GV oocytes and MII oocytes were donated by patients following informed consent and were exclusively unfertilized oocytes that failed to fertilize after insemination. 8-cell stage embryos were generously donated by patients who had successfully delivered two children and no longer wished to use their cryopreserved supernumerary embryos. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital (Approval No.2024-KY-KZ-102). All participants provided written informed consent prior to donation.

Animals

In this study, all C57BL/6 J mice were purchased from Zhiyuan Biotechnology Corporation (Guangzhou, China) and treated according to the guidelines of the Animal Experiment Standard, and the Ethnic Committee of Animal Use of Guangdong Second Provincial General Hospital approved the study (2022-DW-KZ-018-02, 2023-DW-KZ-037-01).

HFD-induced obese mice

After one week of adaptive feeding, female C57BL/6 J mice aged 4 weeks were randomly divided into two groups, and fed with HFD (D12492, 60% Kcal, Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd) and ND (D12450, 10% Kcal, Beijing HFK Bioscience Co., Ltd) for 8 weeks. The standard for successful modeling is that the weight of the HFD group is 20% higher than the average of the ND group.

Oocyte and zygote collection and culture

The GV oocytes were collected from superovulated 4-week old female mice treated with 7.5 IU of pregnant mare serum gonadotropin (PMSG, Ningbo Animal Hormone Factory). The GV oocyte were collected by M2 medium (Sigma Aldrich, M7167) containing 2.5 μM Milrinone (MCE, HY-14252) to inhibit spontaneous GVBD. After microinjection, oocytes were washed and cultured in M2 medium at the incubator (37 °C, 5% CO2) for in vitro maturation. Zygotes were collected from superovulated female mice treated with 7.5 IU of PMSG, followed by 7.5 IU hCG injection 46 h later, and crossed to fertile males. Zygotes were collected at 18 h post-hCG injection and cultured in KSOM medium (MR-121-D, Merck) in the incubator (37°C, 5%CO2).

In vitro fertilization

MII oocytes were collected from superovulated female mice treated with 7.5 IU of PMSG, followed by 7.5 IU hCG injection 46 h later, and collected at 12 h post-hCG injection. Sperm was obtained from dissected epididymis of C57BL/6 mice aged 10 weeks, and capacitated for 1 h in HTF fertilization medium (M1135, Nanjing Aibei Biotechnology). After 3 h of microinjection, MII oocytes were performed zona pellucida drilling to optimize fertilization conditions and fertilizated.

Micro-metabolomics analysis

Oocytes or early embryos were washed three times in 0.2% BSA/PBS and snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for 15 min, stored at −80 °C refrigerator temporarily, and were used for analysis within one week. The metabolite extraction protocol was based on previously published methods73. Briefly, sample masses and volumes were measured and combined with the same volume of 80% (v/v) methanol-water at −78 °C. Lysates were centrifuged at 5000 × g for 15 min, and the supernatants were collected for LC-MS analysis. The Acquity I-class ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) system coupled in line with a Xevo G2-XS high-resolution quadrupole-time of flight hybrid mass spectrometer (Waters) was used for untargeted metabolite profiling. Reversed phase chromatographic separation was performed using a T3 column (Acquity UPLC HSS T3, 2.1 × 100 mm in length, 1.7 µm particle size), with the following solvents: mobile phase A (100% water) and B (100% acetonitrile) both containing 0.1% formic acid, and the following elution gradient was used: 0 min 95% A; 8.5 min 50% A; 12 min 2% A; 16 min 95% A. The flow rate was 0.4 ml/min. The injection volume was 2 µl. The mass spectrometer was used in positive and negative ESI mode for data acquisition using UPLC/MSE, which allowed both precursor and product ion data to be acquired in one injection. Typical source conditions for maximum intensity of precursor ions were as follows: capillary voltage, 2.5 kV in positive mode and 2.0 kV in negative mode; sample cone, 40 V; source temperature, 120 °C; desolvation temperature, 20 °C; cone gas flow rate, 30 L/h; desolvation gas (N2) flow rate, 800 L/h. All analyses were performed using the lockspray, which ensured accuracy and reproducibility. Leucine-enkephalin (5 ng/ml) was used as the lockmass generating a reference ion (positive mode: m/z 556.2771; negative mode: m/z 554.2615) and introduced by a lockspray at 10 μl/min for accurate mass acquisition. MS data was acquired using MSE, which has two separate scans with independent collision energies. Scan 1 (low energy): 50-1200 mass-scan range; 0.2 s scan time; 6 eV collision energy; scan 2 (high energy): 50-1200 mass-scan range; 0.2 s scan time; collision energy ramp of 10-40 eV. Acquiring MS data in this manner allowed for profiling of precursor ions and fragment ions. MS data processing was conducted using the Progenesis QI software (Nonlinear Dynamics, Waters). Raw data was imported into Progenesis QI for peak alignment and peak picking. Peak annotation was first performed by matching experimental m/z, retention time and high-resolution MS/MS spectra to an in-house library of analytical-grade standards. Remaining peaks were identified by matching experimental m/z and fragmentation spectra to publicly available databases including HMDB 5.0 (http://www.hmdb.ca/) and MassBank (http://www.massbank.jp/). Briefly, metabolic feature tables from Progenesis QI were matched to fragmentation spectra with an m/z error tolerance and a retention time window of ± 15 ppm and ± 20 s, respectively. We used the Metabolomics Standards Initiative (MSI) level of confidence to grade metabolite annotation confidence (level 1-level 4). The data were analyzed using the following pipeline: Data preprocessing was performed using the software package MetaX74. Briefly, low quality ions were filtered out, data imputation was performed using the k-nearest neighbor method, and data normalization was performed using the pqn method. Further analysis was conducted based on Metaboanalyst (6.0). After calculating the average of biological replicate data, perform Z-score conversion and then visualize it using a heatmap (Supplementary Data 1).

Trace metabolomics analyses

Lipids were extracted from cells using a modified version of the Bligh and Dyer’s method as described previously75. Briefly, cells were homogenized in 750 µL of chloroform: methanol: MilliQ H2O (3:6:1) (v/v/v). The homogenate was then incubated at 1500 rpm for 1 h at 4 °C. At the end of the incubation, 350 µL of deionized water and 250 µL of chloroform were added to induce phase separation. The samples were then centrifuged and the lower organic phase containing lipids was extracted into a clean tube. Lipid extraction was repeated once by adding 450 µL of chloroform to the remaining aqueous phase, and the lipid extracts were pooled into a single tube and dried in the SpeedVac under OH mode. Samples were stored at −80°C until further analysis.

Lipidomic analyses were conducted at LipidALL Technologies using an ExionLC-AE coupled with Sciex QTRAP 7500 PLUS as reported previously76. Separation of individual lipid classes of polar lipids by normal phase (NP)-HPLC was carried out using a TUP-HB silica column (i.d. 150 × 2.1 mm, 3 µm) with the following conditions: mobile phase A (chloroform: methanol:ammonium hydroxide, 89.5:10:0.5) and mobile phase B (chloroform:methanol:ammonium hydroxide:water, 55:39:0.5:5.5). MRM transitions were set up for comparative analysis of various polar lipids. Individual lipid species were quantified by referencing to spiked internal standards. d9-PC32:0(16:0/16:0), d9-PC36:1p(18:0p/18:1), d7-PE33:1(15:0/18:1), d9-PE36:1p(18:0p/18:1), d31-PS(d31-16:0/18:1), d7-PA33:1(15:0/18:1), d7-PG33:1(15:0/18:1), d7-PI33:1(15:0/18:1), C17-SL, Cer d18:1/15:0-d7, C12:0 Cer-1-P, d9-SM d18:1/18:1, C8-GluCer, C8-GalCer, d3-LacCer d18:1/16:0, Gb3 d18:1/17:0, d7-LPC18:1, d7-LPE18:1, C17-LPI, C17-LPA, C17-LPS, C17-LPG, d17:1 Sph, were obtained from Avanti Polar Lipids. GM3-d18:1/18:0-d3 was purchased from Matreya LLC.

Glycerol lipids, including diacylglycerols (DAG) and triacylglycerols (TAG) were quantified using a modified version of reverse phase HPLC/MRM77. Separation of neutral lipids were achieved on a Phenomenex Kinetex-C18 column (i.d. 4.6 × 100 mm, 2.6 µm) using an isocratic mobile phase containing chloroform:methanol: 0.1 M ammonium acetate 100:100:4 (v/v/v) at a flow rate of 300 µL for 10 min. Levels of short-, medium-, and long-chain TAGs were calculated by referencing to spiked internal standards of TAG(14:0)3-d5, TAG(16:0)3-d5 and TAG(18:0)3-d5 obtained from CDN isotopes, respectively. DAGs were quantified using d5-DAG17:0/17:0 and d5-DAG18:1/18:1 as internal standards (Avanti Polar Lipids).

Free cholesterols and cholesteryl esters were analysed under atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (APCI) mode on a Jasper HPLC coupled to Sciex 4500 MD as described previously, using d6-cholesterol and d6-C18:0 cholesteryl ester (CE) (CDN isotopes) as internal standards78. Amino acids were analysed at LipidALL Technologies. In brief, amino acids were extracted with methanol: water (8:2, v/v) at 4 °C for 30 min at 1500 rpm. At the end of the incubation, samples were centrifuged at 4 °C for 5 min at 12,000 rpm. Clean supernatant was transferred to a new tube and dried in a SpeedVac under OH mode with no heating. The metabolite extract was reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile in water containing isotope-labelled standards for quantification of amino acids, which included d2-glycine, d3-serine, d7-proline, d8-valine, d3-4-hydroxyproline, d10-isoleucine, d10-leucine, d3-aspartic acid, d9-lysine, d5-glutamine, d5-glutamic acid, d3-methionine, d5-histidine, d8-pheylalanine, d7-arginine, d7-tyrosine, d8-tryptophan, d11-bataine, d9-L-carnitine, d13- choline, 13C2 taurine and N-benzoyl-d5-glycine purchased from Cambridge Isotope Laboratories. Amino acids were separated on a Waters Acquity UPLC HSS-T3 column (3 × 100 mm, 1.8 µm) using water containing 0.1% formic acid as mobile phase A and acetonitrile as mobile phase B, and analysed on a Shimadzu 30AD-QTRAP 6500+ as described previously (Supplementary Data 2).

RNA-Seq and data analysis

Oocyte and early embryo samples were sent to Annoroad Gene Technology Co., Ltd (Beijing, China). The resulting libraries were sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq platform using a paired-end 150 bp (PE150) strategy. Raw sequencing reads were processed with standard quality control to obtain clean reads. The clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Gene expression levels were estimated. Differential expression analysis between groups was performed using DESeq2 with genes having a |log2Ratio | ≥ 1 and an adjusted p-value (q-value) <0.05 considered significantly differentially expressed. After calculating the average of biological replicate data (FPKM value), perform Z-score conversion and then visualize it using a heatmap.

CUT&Tag and data analysis

CUT&Tag was performed by using the Hyperactive ®Universal CUT&Tag Assay Kit for Illumina (TD903; Vazyme). Embryos were incubated with 10 μl pre-washed ConA beads, followed by 0.5 μg antibody mixture for 12 h at 4 °C. After washing three times with dig-wash buffer, 150 μl dig-wash buffer with Images were drawn by using ondary antibody added and the mixture incubated at room temperature for 1 h. After washing three times with dig-wash buffer, 2 μl pA/G–Tnp and 98 μl dig-300 buffer were added, and the samples were incubated at room temperature for 1 h and then washed three times with dig-wash buffer. Then 50 μl of tagmentation buffer was added and the samples were incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The reaction was stopped with 10 μl 0.5 M ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid. After extraction of DNA with IDNA extract beads, PCR was performed to amplify the libraries under the following cycling conditions: 72 °C for 3 min, 95 °C for 3 min, 20 cycles of 98 °C for 10 s, and 60 °C for 5 s, followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 1 min and holding at 4 °C. Post-PCR clean-up was performed by adding a 100 μl DNA Clean Beads (N411;VazymeBiotech), and libraries were incubated with beads at room temperature for 10 min, washed gently in 80% ethanol, and eluted in 22 μl water. All libraries were sequenced by using the Illumina NovaSeq 4000 platform according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

The original sequencing data in FASTQ format were obtained by sequencing NovaS4. Low-quality reads were filtered out using Bowtie2 and compared with MM10. SAMtools was used to remove data with low comparison rates, and Picard was used to remove PCR repeats. To visualize the H3K4me3 signal in the IGV Genome Browser, the genome was divided into 50 nucleotide Windows and the RPKM for each window was calculated using DeepTools’ bam Coverage function.

Preparations of siRNAs

To inactivate target genes, we use the Website address: http://biodev.extra.cea.fr/DSIR/DSIR.html to design the siRNA target sequence. siRNAs were provided in Supplementary Table 1, and the random sequence was used as a negative control. The templates were synthesized (Suzhou Genwiz Biotechnology), and T7 High Yield RNA Transcription Kit (TR101, Vazyme Biotechnology) was used to transcribe the corresponding siRNA and VAHTS RNA Clean Beads (N412, Vazyme Biotechnology) to purify siRNA.

mRNA synthesis

For construction of the plasmids, target gene coding sequence was cloned into pcDNA3.1 with an EGFP/mCherry tag (ClonExpress MultiS One Step Cloning Kit, C113, Vazyme). The target gene fragments were amplified by PCR (2XPhanta Max Master Mix, P515, Vazyme), and in vitro transcription of capped mRNA was performed using the HiScribe T7 ARCA mRNA Kit (E2065S, NEB, USA). Subsequently, the HyperScribe Poly(A) Tailing Kit (K1053, APExBIO, USA) was used to add poly (A) tails. The synthesized mRNA was purified using VAHTS RNA Clean Beads (N412, Vazyme). For SKL sequences, the fragment was directly synthesized and clones it into pcDNA3.1 with an EGFP/mCherry tag. The primer was provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR)

Oocytes or embryos were amplified by using a Single Cell Sequence Specific Amplification Kit (P621-01, Vazyme) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For the RT-qPCR analysis, we used AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Q111-02, Vazyme) and a Lightcycler II (Roche). 2−ΔΔct method was used for analyzing relative expression between groups in Graphpad Prism 9.0 software. All measurements were performed in triplicate for each experiment. Primer information was provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence

Oocytes or embryos were fixed in 4% PFA (E672002-0001, Sangon Biotech) at room temperature, and permeabilized by 0.5% Triton-X100 (A600198, Sangon Biotech) at room temperature. then incubated in blocking solution (P0260, Beyotime) at room temperature after three washes in washes buffer (0.1% Tween-20, 0.01% Triton X-100 in PBS). Then, the oocytes or embryos were incubated at 4°C in primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution overnight with following concentrations: ACOX1 (ab184032, Abcam); PMP70 (ab109448, Abcam); PEX5 (HPA039259,Merck); H3K4me3 (9751S, Cell Signaling Technology); H3K9me3 (13969T, Cell Signaling Technology); H3K27me3 (K003334P, Solarbio); H3K27ac (K005114P, Solarbio); H3K9ac (K005116P, Solarbio). Then oocytes or embryos were washed three times in washes buffer and incubated at room temperature with a secondary antibody (AFluor 488, A0423/A0428, Beyotime; CoraLite594, SA00013-3/SA00013-4, Proteintech). followed by fixed on the slide using an anti-fluorescence quenched sealing solution (P0131, Beyotime).

A high-speed confocal imaging system (Dragonfly Andor Technology) with a Z-scan model was used to collect the immunofluorescence labelling results. The distance between each two layers was 1 μm. Raw image files and 3D object volumes were analyzed by Fiji software, and the single layer of the Z-scan images was displayed in this study.

Western blot

Oocytes or embryos were lysed in 10 μl of RIPA buffer (89900, Thermo Fisher Scientific) containing 1 mM PMSF (ST505, Biyotime) in a pre-cooled 0.2 mL centrifuge tube after washing in DPBS for three times, then 5×SDS-PAGE Sample Buffer (Kangrun Biotech) was added, and finally the samples were heated at 95°C for 10 min and frozen at −80 °C for further use. Samples were incubated with primary antibodies (ACOX1 (ab184032, Abcam), H3K4me3 (9751S, Cell Signaling Technology), α-tubulin (S0B0645, Starter) for 12 h at 4 °C, then transferred to secondary antibodies (anti-rabbit IgG, HRP-linked antibody (7074, Cell Signaling Technology, and anti-mouse IgG, HRP-linked antibody (7076, Cell Signaling Technology), respectively for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were measured using SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (34094, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with ChemiDoc XRS+ (1708265, BioRad). The images were analyzed by Image Lab software.

Nile red staining

We used 0.1%PVP (PVP360, Merck)/PBS to dilute Nile red (N1142, Thermo Fisher Scientific) to be 10 µg/ml. then oocytes or embryos were transferred to a liquid drop with 30 μl 0.1% PVP/PBS containing the dye and stained in an incubator at 37 °C for 5 min. All samples were washed three times with 0.1% PVP/PBS and used immediately for fluorescence imaging (Dragonfly, Andor Technology). In order to minimize fluorescence quenching, it was necessary to avoid light during the whole process. To quantify the TAG and phospholipids content, we used a wavelength of 488 nm to qualify the TAG content and a wavelength of 600 nm was used to qualify phospholipids. To provide better visualization, phospholipids fluorescence was adjusted from the original purple fluorescence to the pseudo-colour red fluorescence.

PE staining

Following collection, embryos were immediately fixed and permeabilized in 0.5% Triton-X100/paraformaldehyde solution for 20 min at room temperature. After three washes with PBS containing 0.1% Tween-20 and 0.01% Triton X-100, samples were stained with 0.025 mM NBD-PE (HY-D1683, MCE) for 15 min at room temperature. Finally, specimens were mounted on slides using anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium for microscopic observation.

EU incorporation

We used Cell-Light EU Apollo567 RNA Imaging Kit to capture the expression of newly transcribed RNAs, based on linking 5-ethynyluridine (EU)-labelled RNAs and biotin using the click reaction (C10316-1, RIBOBIO). Embryos were incubated in EU droplet for 2 h for incorporation into nascent transcripts, following which they were fixed in paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 30 min and permeabilized in 0.5% Trinton X-100/PBS at room temperature for 30 min. The click reaction buffer was freshly prepared, and the embryos were incubated in it at room temperature for 30 min, protected from light, and then embryos were washed in washing solution (0.1% Tween-20, 0.01% Triton X-100 in PBS) four times, 10 min each, at room temperature, followed by fixing on the slide using an anti-fluorescence quenched sealing solution. The data were collected and analyzed as in immunofluorescence.

Microinjection of siRNAs and mRNAs

We injected siRNAs and mRNA at a concentration of 700 ng/μl or 300 ng/μl, respectively, and an injection volume of 5–10 pl into per cell, the microinjection details were described before79. Zygotes were cultured in KSOM medium and recorded the development of the early embryos at the 2-cell- (32 h), the 4-cell- (54 h), and the blastocyst- (96 h) embryo stage.

Microinjection of fatty acids

For embryonic development rescue experiments, palmitic acid (A600497, Sangon Biotech) and myristic acid (A600931, Sangon Biotech) were reconstituted in 10 mM NaOH, heated to 70 °C until they were dissolved. The working concentration after dilution by RNase-free H2O was 18.3 μM, and a volume of 5–10 pl was injected per embry.

Embryo transfer

The zygotes from ND group, HFD group and HFD + FA group were collected and cultured in KSOM medium until 2-cell embryo stage. Ten weeks old ICR female mice in natural estrus cycle were selected as as pseudo pregnant mice, and they were caged with sterilized male mice one night in advance. The 2-cell embryos were transferred into the oviducts of ICR pseudo pregnant mice, with 8–10 2-cell embryos transferred into each pseudo pregnant mouse’s unilateral oviduct.
