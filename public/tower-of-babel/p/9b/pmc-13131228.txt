Development of structural damage in the spine in patients with PsA: findings from a longitudinal cohort study
Authors: Fadi Kharouf, Pankti Mehta, Virginia Carrizo Abarza, Shangyi Gao, Daniel Pereira, Ngai Chow, Dafna D Gladman, Vinod Chandran, Denis Poddubnyy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThis study aimed to examine associations between disease activity/severity markers across multiple PsA domains and structural damage development in the spine in a large longitudinal cohort.MethodsWe analysed patients from a prospective longitudinal PsA cohort and evaluated structural damage in the spine by assessing the development of new syndesmophytes (a binary outcome) on radiographs of the cervical and lumbar spine. We used Cox regression to examine the association between syndesmophyte development and disease activity and severity variables, including nail involvement, PASI, swollen joint count, SPARCC enthesitis score, dactylitis, cDAPSA, modified Steinbrocker score, radiographic sacroiliitis score (range 0–8), BASDAI, ESR and CRP. Separate multivariable models were conducted and adjusted for age, sex, PsA duration, BMI, smoking status, decade of observation, HLA-B*27, HLA-A*29, NSAID use and, where applicable, radiographic sacroiliitis score and b/tsDMARD use.ResultsAmong 1584 PsA patients (median age 45), 218 (13.8%) had syndesmophytes at baseline (clinic entry). Median follow-up was 8.5 years [IQR: 4.4–14.3]. Among 922 patients without baseline syndesmophytes who underwent repeat radiographs, 166 (18.0%) developed new syndesmophytes, with a median time to development of 5.8 years [IQR: 2.4–9.9]. The incidence of new syndesmophytes appeared to decline in more recent calendar years. In multivariable models, higher ESR (HR 1.02, 95% CI 1.01–1.03) and higher radiographic sacroiliitis score (HR 1.19, 95% CI 1.07–1.32) were associated with syndesmophyte development.ConclusionSyndesmophytes developed in approximately one-fifth of patients with PsA during long-term follow-up. Systemic inflammation and radiographic sacroiliitis are major, independent predictors of spinal structural damage.

Introduction

PsA is a complex inflammatory disease that can complicate skin psoriasis and is characterized by a broad spectrum of musculoskeletal manifestations, including both peripheral joint and axial skeleton involvement [1, 2]. Axial disease, manifesting as spondylitis (spondyloarthritis) and/or sacroiliitis, affects ∼25–70% of patients with PsA and is associated with greater disease burden, often necessitating treatment with biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs (b/tsDMARDs) [3–5].

In contrast to the extensive literature on structural damage in the spine in axial spondyloarthritis (axSpA), particularly in AS, research specifically focused on axial PsA (axPsA) remains limited [1, 3, 6]. Much of the current knowledge has been derived from studies involving mixed axSpA and PsA populations, making it difficult to clearly define the distinct clinical and imaging features of axPsA. In this context, the AXIS study [7], a large, multinational, cross-sectional initiative aiming to systematically characterize axial involvement in PsA, develop classification criteria and establish a unified nomenclature, is expected to significantly advance the understanding in this field, with complementary insights anticipated from real-world observational cohorts [8]. Importantly, identification of predictors of development and progression of axial disease in PsA remains one of the most significant and unresolved knowledge gaps. To help address this, we aimed to investigate associations between markers of disease activity and severity across multiple PsA domains and the development of structural damage in the spine, using data from a large longitudinal cohort.

Patients and methods

Setting

This study was conducted at the Gladman Krembil Psoriatic Arthritis Program (formerly the University of Toronto Psoriatic Arthritis Clinic), a long-standing, prospective observational cohort where individuals with PsA are systematically recruited and followed over time. Eligibility for inclusion requires a clinical diagnosis of inflammatory musculoskeletal disease consistent with PsA; >99% of participants meet the ClASsification criteria for Psoriatic Arthritis (CASPAR) [9].

Upon recruitment and at regular intervals of 6–12 months, participants undergo comprehensive evaluations following a standardized protocol. These assessments include a detailed medical history, physical examination and laboratory investigations. Radiographs of peripheral joints and the axial skeleton, including the cervical (lateral view) and lumbar (lateral and antero-posterior views) spine, as well as the sacroiliac joints, are obtained at baseline and biennially thereafter. Standardized radiographic views of the spine, sacroiliac joints and peripheral joints have been used since the cohort was established. Although minor technical changes (e.g. equipment upgrades) may have occurred over time, the imaging views and anatomical coverage have remained consistent.

Peripheral joint radiographs are scored using the modified Steinbrocker method [10], while sacroiliac joint involvement is assessed according to the modified New York criteria [11]. Structural damage in the spine was assessed based on the presence of syndesmophytes. All radiographs were evaluated in dedicated consensus reading sessions by at least two experienced rheumatologists. In cases of disagreement, discrepancies were resolved through discussion involving a musculoskeletal radiologist until consensus was achieved. Radiographic outcomes were recorded only after agreement among the readers.

All clinical and imaging data are entered into a secure, web-based database. The reliability and validity of both the clinical assessments and radiographic scoring methods employed in this cohort have been previously established, demonstrating consistency and minimal bias in disease severity and follow-up assessments [10, 11].

Patient selection

Data for this study were obtained from patients enrolled in the cohort between 1 January 1978 and 30 April 2025.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the program’s database at baseline and at subsequent 6–12-month intervals. Key variables included demographic information such as age, sex, ethnicity, age at onset of psoriasis and PsA, disease duration, BMI and smoking status. Peripheral musculoskeletal disease activity was evaluated using the 66-swollen joint count (SJC), the Spondyloarthritis Research Consortium of Canada (SPARCC) Enthesitis Index [12], the presence of dactylitis and the clinical Disease Activity Index for Psoriatic Arthritis (cDAPSA) score [13]. Axial musculoskeletal disease activity was evaluated using the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI) [14]. Skin disease severity was evaluated using the Psoriasis Area and Severity Index (PASI) [15], alongside assessment of nail involvement. Radiographic data were collected biennially and included the presence of erosions, the modified Steinbrocker score, radiographic sacroiliitis score (calculated as the sum of grades from both sacroiliac joints, ranging from 0 to 8) and the presence of syndesmophytes. Axial disease (axPsA) was radiographically defined by the presence of syndesmophytes or sacroiliitis meeting the modified New York criteria. Laboratory data included CRP, ESR and HLA, HLA-B*27 and HLA-A*29, both of which were included due to their known associations with axial damage progression: HLA-B*27 with a positive association and HLA-A*29 with a negative association [16]. Patient-reported physical function was assessed using the Health Assessment Questionnaire (HAQ) disability index [17]. Additionally, information on current treatments was recorded, encompassing NSAIDs; conventional synthetic DMARDs (csDMARDs) such as MTX, SSZ and LEF; tsDMARDs including apremilast, tofacitinib and upadacitinib and bDMARDs, including tumour necrosis factor (TNF) inhibitors, IL-12/23 inhibitors, IL-17 inhibitors and IL-23 inhibitors.

Ethical approval

This research study was approved by the University Health Network Research Ethics Board (REB Study#: 08–0630). All individuals have provided written informed consent for this research study and its publication.

Outcomes

Development of structural damage in the spine was defined as the development of at least one new syndesmophyte (binary change from absence to presence).

Statistical analysis

We summarized baseline descriptive statistics by reporting continuous variables as means with S.D. or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and categorical variables as counts and percentages (%).

To investigate factors associated with syndesmophyte development, we applied Cox proportional hazards models. We began with univariable analyses followed by multivariable models adjusting for potential confounders. Each disease activity or severity marker was examined individually as a separate exposure in multivariable Cox models. The evaluated exposures included the 66-SJC, SPARCC Enthesitis Index, presence of dactylitis, cDAPSA, nail involvement, PASI, modified Steinbrocker score, BASDAI, ESR and CRP.

In multivariable models, we adjusted for a predefined set of key confounders including age, sex, PsA duration, BMI, smoking status, decade of observation (with 1978–1988 as the reference group, and the subsequent decades 1989–1999, 2000–2010 and 2011–2025 as categorical covariates), radiographic sacroiliitis score, HLA-B*27, HLA-A*29, NSAID use and use of b/tsDMARDs. Correlated variables (e.g. ESR and CRP or SJC and cDAPSA) were therefore evaluated in separate models rather than jointly to avoid collinearity.

All covariates, with the exception of sex, HLA status and decade of observation, were treated as time-varying. The Cox models were specified at the patient level, with time-to-event defined as time to first development of a syndesmophyte. Each patient contributed a single risk interval until the occurrence of an event or censoring.

Radiographic spinal damage-related variables were imputed using last observation carried forward, whereas observations with missing disease activity variables were excluded. Given the sufficient sample size, alternative imputation methods or model assumptions were not considered.

To assess the robustness of our findings, we conducted a multivariable sensitivity analysis stratified by observation period (year 2000 and after). In addition, to evaluate the association between syndesmophyte presence and physical function, we performed a separate sensitivity analysis using univariable and multivariable generalized estimating equations (GEEs), with the HAQ disability index as the outcome. The multivariable model was adjusted for age, sex, PsA duration, BMI, calendar decade, radiographic sacroiliitis score, HLA-B*27 status, NSAID use and use of b/tsDMARDs. Covariates were treated as time-dependent where appropriate.

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.3 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R version 4.2.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

The study cohort included 1584 individuals with PsA. The median age was 45.0 years [IQR: 34.3–54.7], with 55.5% being male and 85.6% identifying as White (Table 1). The median PsA duration at baseline was 2.9 years [IQR: 0.7–8.8], and the median BMI was 27.6 kg/m2 [IQR: 24.3–32.2]. Comorbidities were common, including hypertension (14.7%), hyperlipidaemia (7.9%) and diabetes mellitus (6.4%).

In total, 5505 spinal radiographs were evaluated among the 1584 patients. Of these, 1074 patients had two or more spinal radiographs available for longitudinal assessment. The median interval between consecutive spinal radiographs was 2.6 years [IQR: 2.0–4.0].

Disease features at baseline included a median PASI of 1.2 [IQR: 0.0–3.9], a median SJC of 2.0 [IQR: 0.0–5.0] and dactylitis in 27.0% of the cohort. Radiographically, 52.7% of patients had erosive disease, 22.7% had radiographic sacroiliitis, 13.8% had syndesmophytes and 28.2% had axPsA at baseline. HLA-B*27 was positive in 16.9% of tested individuals. At baseline, 34.3% of patients were using csDMARDs, 8.6% were receiving biologic DMARDs and 0.5% were on tsDMARDs.

Factors associated with syndesmophyte development

Over a median follow-up of 8.5 years [IQR: 4.4–14.3], 166 of 922 patients (18.0%) without baseline syndesmophytes developed them, with a median time to development of 5.8 years [IQR: 2.4–9.9]. Figure 1 illustrates syndesmophyte-free survival using Kaplan–Meier analysis. Over the same follow-up period, 30.1% of patients without axial disease at baseline progressed to axial disease, with a median time to development of 6.4 years [IQR: 2.8–10.9]. Additionally, 22.7% of patients progressed from no sacroiliitis to sacroiliitis with a median time to progression of 6.2 years [IQR: 3.0–11.6].

Kaplan–Meier survival analysis depicting the probability of remaining syndesmophyte-free over time

In univariable Cox models (Table 2), several factors were significantly associated with the development of syndesmophytes. These included male sex, longer duration of PsA, higher BMI, the presence of nail disease and dactylitis. A higher SPARCC enthesitis score was associated with a lower risk of syndesmophyte development. Other significant associations included higher modified Steinbrocker scores, higher radiographic sacroiliitis scores, HLA-B*27 positivity and elevated ESR levels. Additionally, the use of b/tsDMARDs was associated with a reduced risk of developing syndesmophytes. Due to limited sample sizes within specific b/tsDMARD subclasses, subclass analyses were restricted to TNF inhibitors and IL-17 inhibitors. TNF inhibitor use was significantly associated with a reduced risk of syndesmophyte development, whereas IL-17 inhibitor use demonstrated a similar directional effect but did not reach statistical significance, likely reflecting limited sample size and reduced statistical power within this subgroup.

In a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients observed from the year 2000 onward, the results were similar to the primary analysis (Supplementary Table S1), showing consistent associations of higher ESR and radiographic sacroiliitis scores with syndesmophyte development.

Association between syndesmophytes and physical function

In GEE analyses with the HAQ disability index as the outcome, the presence of syndesmophytes was significantly associated with higher HAQ scores in univariable analysis (estimate 0.11, 95% CI 0.01–0.20). In the adjusted multivariable model, the association was similar in magnitude (estimate 0.09, 95% CI −0.0004 to 0.19) but did not reach statistical significance.

Discussion

In this longitudinal observational study, 13.8% of patients had syndesmophytes at clinic entry. Among those without baseline syndesmophytes, 18% developed them during long-term follow-up, with a median time to development of 5.8 years (IQR: 2.4–9.9). Over the same follow-up period, 30.1% of patients without axial disease at baseline progressed to axial disease, with a median time to development of 6.4 years [IQR: 2.8–10.9]. Additionally, 22.7% of patients progressed from no sacroiliitis to sacroiliitis with a median time to progression of 6.1 years [IQR: 3.0–11.6]. Notably, the progression of radiographic spinal damage has declined in more recent years, likely due to advances in clinical management and the expanded use of effective therapies. Higher ESR and radiographic sacroiliitis scores predicted new syndesmophyte formation, with consistent findings in patients observed from 2000 onward. Syndesmophyte presence was also associated with worse physical function, although this association was attenuated after adjustment. Universally accepted classification or diagnostic criteria for axPsA are currently lacking; nevertheless, it remains a key domain and a common manifestation of PsA, affecting ∼25–70% of patients [3, 5, 18]. When discussing axPsA, it is important to understand that while it shares some features with axSpA, such as chronic back pain, systemic inflammation and overlapping treatment options, it differs significantly in demographics, clinical manifestations and imaging characteristics [3, 19, 20]. AxSpA predominantly affects younger males, has a high prevalence of HLA-B*27 (∼70–80%) and is more commonly associated with extra-musculoskeletal manifestations, including uveitis and inflammatory bowel disease [3, 19, 20]. It typically presents with symmetrical sacroiliitis, marginal syndesmophytes and bone marrow oedema, and does not respond to IL-12/23 inhibitors [3, 6, 19]. In contrast, axPsA affects both sexes equally, tends to have a later onset and is associated with a lower prevalence of HLA-B*27 (∼10–40%) [3, 6, 19]. Psoriasis with nail involvement is common, peripheral musculoskeletal symptoms are more prominent and mood disorders such as depression are more frequently observed [1, 19, 21]. Imaging often reveals isolated spinal involvement, asymmetrical or unilateral sacroiliitis, non-marginal syndesmophytes, paravertebral ossifications and potential ligament-centric inflammation [3, 19, 22]. Despite these distinctions, both conditions demonstrate structural changes on radiographic imaging and share markers of systemic inflammation [3, 6, 19, 20].

In our study, elevated markers of systemic inflammation, specifically ESR, were associated with the development of new syndesmophytes, consistent with their known association with peripheral damage [23, 24]. This finding may reflect the central role of persistent inflammation in PsA, likely originating from peripheral sites such as the skin, which can eventually extend to other anatomical structures, including peripheral joints and the axial skeleton [25, 26]. Additionally, our results show that a higher radiographic sacroiliitis score predicts syndesmophyte formation, suggesting that inflammation in the sacroiliac joints, an anatomically and functionally connected axial compartment, may actively drive structural changes in the spine.

Chandran et al., over a decade ago, identified elevated ESR as a risk factor for the development of axPsA [27]. Remarkably, the relationship between higher radiographic sacroiliitis scores, elevated CRP levels and axPsA structural changes has been reported in a recent Belgian multicentre PsA cohort study [8]. These observations are consistent with findings in axSpA, where inflammatory markers are recognized risk factors for radiographic progression, and sacroiliac joint inflammation and damage are closely associated with the development of spinal structural abnormalities [28–32].

The wide range of axPsA prevalence reported in the literature (25–70%) [3] likely reflects not only the absence of standardized classification criteria but also differences in disease duration and the timing of evaluation. Longer follow-up and progressive disease activity generally lead to higher observed prevalence rates [3]. In our cohort, for example, while 28.2% of patients met axPsA criteria at baseline, this increased to 43.6% over a median follow-up of 8.0 years [IQR: 1.8–15.6]. Similarly, Chandran et al.’s study [27] reported that among 156 PsA patients without axPsA at baseline, 15% developed incident axPsA over 10 years. Their definition included bilateral sacroiliitis ≥grade 2 or unilateral sacroiliitis ≥grade 3, along with inflammatory neck or back pain or limited spinal mobility [27]. These findings reinforce the notion that effective and sustained control of systemic inflammation and/or sacroiliitis throughout the disease course may help prevent the onset and progression of spinal damage in PsA. Supporting this, we observed a reduction in new syndesmophyte development over the past 15 years, likely reflecting improved care and the increased availability of treatment options.

The strengths of our study include a large and diverse patient population, longitudinal data collection and the evaluation of multiple surrogates of disease activity and severity with robust statistical adjustments. However, several limitations must be acknowledged. The single-centre, observational design may affect the generalizability of our findings. Importantly, due to the historical nature of the cohort, we assessed syndesmophyte development dichotomously rather than as a continuous variable. For the same reason, we could not use the modified Stoke Ankylosing Spondylitis Spinal Score (mSASSS) as a reliable outcome, despite it being a well-accepted score of spinal damage [33]. Additionally, radiographs were evaluated by consensus rather than through independent, blinded readings, which may introduce potential bias. Moreover, the absence of advanced imaging modalities—such as ultrasonography, CT and MRI, which offer greater sensitivity for detecting peripheral and axial inflammation in PsA [34–36]—may have resulted in underdetection of key features, including enthesitis and non-radiographic sacroiliitis, potentially influencing the effect estimates.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that radiographic spinal progression affects nearly one in five PsA patients over long-term follow-up, with a slowing trend in recent decades likely reflecting advances in care. Systemic inflammation, as indicated by elevated ESR and radiographic sacroiliitis, is independently associated with new syndesmophyte formation in PsA. These findings help define a clinical profile that drives axial disease progression and, importantly, suggest that b/tsDMARDs may prevent long-term spinal damage. Ongoing research, including results from the anticipated AIXS study [7] will further clarify and validate these observations.
