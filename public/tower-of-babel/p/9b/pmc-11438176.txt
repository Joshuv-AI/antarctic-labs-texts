Anxiety and sleep mediate the effect of food insecurity on depression in single parents in Ghana
Authors: Obed Jones Owusu-Sarpong, Kabila Abass, Solomon Osei Tutu, Razak M. Gyasi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundData on the association between food insecurity and depression in single parents in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are limited, and no study has reported the serial mediation effects of psychosocial factors in this association. This study examines the extent to which anxiety and sleep serially explain the food insecurity and depression link among single parents in Ghana.MethodsData on 627 single parents were obtained through a multi-stage stratified sampling technique. Food insecurity was assessed using the Food and Agriculture Organization Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES), and depression was assessed using the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10). Multivariable OLS models and bootstrapping serial mediation analyses were performed to evaluate the hypothesized associations.ResultsThe mean age (SD) was 45.0 (14.7) years; 67.3% females. After full adjustment, food insecurity was significantly associated with increases in anxiety symptoms (β = 0.61, 95%CI = 0.476 − 0.737), sleep problems (β = 0.04, 95%CI = 0.02 − 0.07), and depression (β = 0.24, 95%CI = 0.12 − 0.36). Food insecurity indirectly related to depression via anxiety (β = 0.35, 95%CI = 0.26-0.44) representing 55.8%, sleep (β = 0.03, 95%CI = 0.0032-0.0575) suggesting 4.0%, and anxiety→sleep (β = 0.013, 95%CI = 0.0024-0.0265) yielding 2.0% of the total effect.ConclusionsFood insecurity was positively associated with depression. This association was partially and serially explained by generalized anxiety and sleep problems. Efforts to address depression among single parents should consider interventions for food insecurity and psychosocial problems, particularly in LMICs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20087-7.

Introduction

Food security is defined as having physical and financial access to enough safe, nutritious food to meet dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life [1]. However, despite the global efforts to achieve the SDGs “1” (No Poverty), “2” (Ending hunger), and “3” (improving health) [2], hunger remains a critical challenge, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [3]. Severe food insecurity affected over 258 million people in 58 countries and territories in 2022, up from 193 million people in 53 countries and territories in 2021 [4]. In 2022, more than 137 million Africans were severely food insecure [5]. There are, however, regional differences in the prevalence of food insecurity, with sub-Saharan Africa suffering the brunt of these differences [3]. According to the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) and the Ministry of Food and Agriculture (MoFA) 2020 Comprehensive Food Security and Vulnerability Analysis (CFSVA) report, 11.7% of Ghana’s population, or 3.6 million people, were food insecure [6].

The literature shows that hunger is strongly linked to poor well-being, such as chronic diseases [7], poor psychological health [8–10], and low quality of life [11]. Vulnerable populations like single parents are more susceptible to the association of food insecurity and depression [9, 12]. Studies exploring single parenting in Ghana are incredibly important, given the rapid increase in the number of single parents in the country [13]. This rise is linked to higher rates of divorce/separation and widowhood, resulting in vulnerabilities such as poor socioeconomic conditions and declining living standards due to a possible lack of resource-sharing among multi-parent households [12, 13]. Single parents are also more likely to experience psychological and emotional disorders, heightened susceptibility to health challenges, and difficulties in meeting healthcare costs [13].

Recent studies have found a link between food insecurity and mental/psychological disorders like depressive symptoms [8–10]. The American Psychiatric Association defines depression as a sustained period of sadness that disrupts daily activities. Anybody can experience depression, and it can affect people of all ages and backgrounds. A significant number of individuals with depression also suffer from other mental health issues [14].

Depression is an indication of psychological disorder, according to current studies on food insecurity and health. For example, a study conducted by Gyasi et al. [8] in Ghana found that psychological distress among older adults is influenced by food insecurity. Additionally, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Pourmotabbed et al. [15] revealed that food insecurity raises the incidence of depression by 34% in persons under 65 and by 75% in older adults 65 years and over. There is, however, a dearth of data on single parents in sub-Saharan Africa because the majority of the studies that relate food poverty to inferior health are undertaken among older individuals and other population groups [16].

The association of food insecurity with depression may be explained by anxiety [8, 17, 18] and sleep problems [16, 19, 20]. Studies on the association of food insecurity with depression among single parents are limited. Therefore, it is very necessary to examine the direct association of food insecurity with depression among single parents, as well as the degree to which anxiety disorders and sleep problems may explain this association in the context of sub-Saharan Africa. This may help global and state agencies take pragmatic measures to deal with depression among food-insecure single parents. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between food insecurity and depressive symptoms among single parents in the Atwima Kwanwoma district, Ghana. The study further aimed to explore how much of the food insecurity (FI)-depression (D) link may be mediated by anxiety (A): (FI→A→D), sleep problems (SP): (FI→SP→D), and if anxiety (A) is associated with sleep problems (SP): (A → SP), (i.e., food insecurity leading to anxiety, anxiety leading to sleep problems, and sleep problems leading to depression). See Fig. 1 for the study’s hypothesized serial multiple mediation model (serial mediation, in this case, is when the mediators are linked in a chain where the effect of the independent variable on the outcome variable is mediated by one mediator, which is, in turn, mediated by another mediator, unlike parallel mediation where multiple mediators operate simultaneously and independently in explaining the relationship between the independent variable and the outcome variable) of the association of food insecurity with depression via anxiety symptoms and sleep problems as potential mediating variables.

Methods

The survey

Multi-stage stratified sampling was used in this study. Atwima Kwanwoma District was purposively selected based on anecdotal evidence, which suggests that the population of single parents is high in the district. Furthermore, the Ghana Statistical Service (GSS) 2020 Comprehensive Food Security and Vulnerability Analysis (CFSVA) report reveals that by regional differences, the Ashanti region’s food insecurity situation of 6.2% (369,096) is among the first three highest food insecurity prevalence in the country [6], and therefore, Atwima Kwanwoma, by extension, is included. The district was unofficially divided into Atwima and Kwanwoma, and the various communities were stratified into urban and rural based on their socioeconomic features and general development levels like economic activity levels, nature of roads, health, and educational infrastructure [21]. A simple random sampling technique was employed to select 12 communities, 4 urban and 8 rural, out of the total of 64 communities in the district [22]. Names of all the respective communities were written on paper, considering that some communities are in Atwima and others are in Kwanwoma. This is an unofficial division of the district. Naturally, the district is divided into East and West belts.

By the fishbowl selection method, a total of 6 communities (4 rural and 2 urban) were chosen from Atwima, and another 6 communities (4 rural and 2 urban) were chosen from Kwanwoma, for a total of 6 communities, resulting in a grand total of 12 communities (4 urban and 8 rural). The basis for selecting more rural communities (8 communities) than urban communities (4 communities) is that the urban communities are more populated than the rural communities. Since the prevalence of food insecurity among single parents was unknown, the suggested prevalence of 50% [23] used for an unknown population, which translates to 0.5, was used as a sampling frame for determining the number of single parents in the district. Based on the sampling frame, Fisher’s sample size estimation formula was employed to determine the overall sample size for the study [24].

A face-to-face interviewer-administered questionnaire was developed by the researchers to reach out to the respondents. The questionnaire guide for the study has been included as Supplementary File 2. The data were collected within three months, from June 2023 to August 2023. Ethical clearance was sought from the appropriate body, and written informed consent was sought from the respondents.

Measures

Depression

In this investigation, depression was assessed using the Centre for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10) [26]. Ten questions concerning the respondents’ emotions or actions during the previous week were posed. “I was bothered by things that usually do not bother me,” “I had trouble keeping my mind on current activities,” and other such statements are examples. A four-point rating system was used to record each item: 1 for rarely, 2 for little or some of the time, 3 for occasionally or for a significant length of time, and 4 for most of the time. The scores of these items were combined, which varied from 10 to 40. Higher scores here correspond to higher degrees of depressive symptoms. The CES-D-10 among adults in Africa has been demonstrated in previous studies [27]. The variable achieved a Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.739 in this study.

Food insecurity

Food insecurity was assessed with the Food and Agriculture Organization Food Insecurity Experience Scale (FIES), which consists of eight items measuring different experiences of food insecurity over the past 12 months [28]. The items include “During the last 12 months, was there a time when you were worried you would not have enough food to eat because of a lack of money or other resources?” “…you were unable to eat healthy/nutritious food because of a lack of money or other resources?” “. you ate only a few kinds of food because of a lack of money or other resources?” etc. Each item was scored on a dichotomous no = 0 or yes = 1 scale. A total score ranged from 0 to 8, with higher scores indicating greater levels of food insecurity. Food insecurity was categorized into 3 types (no food insecurity, moderate food insecurity, and severe food insecurity). The variable achieved a Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.754.

Anxiety

GAD-7 included questions on anxiety symptoms, and the summary score then indicated the severity of anxiety [29]. An example of the question is, “How often have you been bothered by the following over the past two weeks, feeling nervous, anxious, or on edge?” It has been established that the GAD-7 possesses psychometric qualities [30]. A four-point rating system was used for each item: 1 for not at all, 2 for a few days, 3 for more than half the days, and 4 for almost every day. The total score varied from 7 to 28, with a greater score indicating a higher level of anxiety in the respondent. Cronbach’s Alpha for the variable came out as 0.836.

Sleep problems

Two sub-domains were used to assess sleep disorders: nocturnal sleep troubles and daytime sleep problems. The study employed a fatigue-related questionnaire to measure sleep problems at night. The findings indicate a moderate-to-high sensitivity for detecting cases of obstructive sleep apnea in the general population that are clinically significant [31]. The respondents were asked, “Overall, in the last 30 days, how much of an issue did you have with sleeping, e.g., falling asleep, waking up during the night, or waking up too early in the morning?” Daytime sleep concerns were measured using the following question: “Overall, in the last 30 days, how much of a problem did you have due to not feeling rested and refreshed during the day (for example, feeling tired, not having energy)?” Five response options were available for these questions: none, mild, moderate, severe, and extreme (coded from 1 to 5). These items have been used in prior SSA data investigations [32]. An increasing score indicated more issues with daytime and nighttime sleep, leading to a latent poor sleep quality (PSQ) score. A range below 7 h is poor sleep quality [16, 33]. For analysis, a continuous variable was created as the two questions were merged to form sleep problems. This variable comprising just two questions achieved a small Cronbach’s Alpha of 0.532.

Covariates

The analytic models were adjusted with demographic, socio-economic, lifestyle, and health-related variables selected from previously published literature [34, 35]. Age (in years), residential status (rural/urban), educational attainment (number of years spent in school), work status (unemployed/employed), and income levels (in Ghana Cedis) are among the socio-economic and demographic characteristics. Physical activity participation was added as a control variable, as determined by the International Physical Activity Questionnaire. Additional inclusions were current smoking (no = 0/yes = 1) and alcohol intake (no = 0/yes = 1). Self-rated health status, categorized from 0 to 4 on a five-point scale from poor to excellent, was one of the health-related factors. A list of disorders, including hypertension, diabetes, heart disease, stroke, cancer, renal disease, chronic respiratory diseases, asthma, arthritis/rheumatism, and ulcers, was used to evaluate chronic physical problems.

Statistical analyses

The statistical analysis was undertaken using SPSS v25 (SPSS, Inc., IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). First, descriptive statistics were calculated to describe the sample characteristics. We estimated means and standard deviation for the continuous variables and counts and percentages for the categorical variables. Second, zero-order correlations (Pearson’s r) were performed between depression and food insecurity, anxiety, and sleep problems to evaluate the bivariate associations in these key variables.

We evaluated the hypothesized serial mediation path models using the PROCESS macro plug-in [36], where food insecurity was the independent variable, severity of anxiety, and sleep problems were the mediators, and depression was treated as the outcome variable. The mediation equation of the association of food insecurity with depression is given as C = c’ + [a×b]. Therefore, as seen in Fig. 1, food insecurity, which is the independent variable, is associated with depression (dependent variable) through anxiety and sleep problems (mediating variables). This is \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:a\times\:b$$\end{document}a×b (indirect effect). Food insecurity is associated with depression directly, and this is represented by c’ (direct effect) in the equation. The addition of c’ to the product of a and b is equal to the total effect, represented by C in the equation. Paths a1, a2, b1, b2, c, and c′, denote path coefficients. Food insecurity is positively associated with depression (total effect, c). There is a significant specific indirect association of food insecurity with depression via anxiety (indirect effect, a1b1). Food insecurity has a significant, specific indirect association with depression via sleep problems (indirect effect a2b2). Food insecurity has a significant cascading or serial mediation effect on depression via anxiety and sleep problems (serial indirect effect a1d21b2).

We conducted a non-parametric bootstrapping procedure to compute confidence intervals (CIs) around the indirect effect. The final estimate of the indirect effect is represented by the mean indirect effect computed across 5,000 bootstrap samples. This estimation method involves random and repeated sub-sampling, with greater statistical power than a traditional Baron and Kenny’s casual mediation estimation approach [37]. The models were adjusted for age, sex, residential type, income, smoking, alcohol intake, physical activity, social networks, and chronic conditions. The statistical significance of the indirect effects was examined based on bias-corrected confidence intervals derived from 5000 bootstrap samples [36]. The level of statistical significance was set at P value < 0.05 for the indirect effect if its 95% bootstrap CI does not overlap zero.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 627 individuals were included in the current study. The characteristics of this analytic sample are shown in Table 1. The average age was 45.0 (SD = 14.66) years, ranging from 14 to 85 years, and 67.3% were females. The majority of the sample lived in rural communities (54.5%), were gainfully employed (71.8%), and neither smoked (97.4%) or consumed alcohol (92.5%). On average, every participant had schooled for 9.5 (SD = 4.0) years, while the mean monthly income was approximately 1938.28 Ghana Cedis (USD$ 140) (SD = 904.46). Moreover, the mean physical activity, social network, and number of chronic physical conditions scores were 330.79 (SD = 420.83), 8.32 (SD = 6.44), and 1.07 (SD = 1.35), respectively.

Correlations

Zero-order Pearson’s correlation matrix is presented in Table 2. The results indicate that the depression score was significantly related to all critical independent and potential mediating variables. Thus, depression was strongly and positively correlated with food insecurity (r = .490, p < .001), anxiety (r = .705, p < .001), and sleep problems (r = .238, p < .001). Also, food insecurity positively correlated with both anxiety (r = .515, p < .001) and sleep problems (r = .178, p < .001), while anxiety correlated positively with sleep problems (r = .237, p < .001). The significant correlations between depression, food insecurity, anxiety, and sleep problems provide a firm ground for statistical assumptions for further multiple serial mediation modeling of the indirect association of food insecurity with depression in this sample.

Serial mediation analysis

Figure 2 presents the unstandardized regression coefficients in each hypothesized pathway. Indeed, all hypothesized path models were statistically significant even after a robust adjustment for several potential covariates. The results found that food insecurity was significantly associated with increases in anxiety (β = 0.607, 95%CI = 0.476 − 0.737), sleep problems (β = 0.042, 95%CI = 0.016 − 0.067), as well as depression (β = 0.238, 95%CI = 0.116 − 0.360). Anxiety was positively associated with sleep problems (β = 0.035, 95%CI = 0.021 − 0.050) and depression (β = 0.572, 95%CI = 0.502 − 0.642). Further, sleep problems were significantly related to an increase in depression (β = 0.593, 95%CI = 0.214 − 0.973). The direct effect of food insecurity on depression remained significant after including the mediators. This indicates that anxiety and sleep problems partially mediated the association between food insecurity and depression. Bootstrapping estimates

Table 3 provides details of the direct, indirect, and total effects of the link between food insecurity and depression among single parents. The bootstrap-derived 95% CI estimation procedure with 5000 bootstrap samples did not cross zero for any estimate. Thus, a significant mediating effect of food insecurity on depression through anxiety and sleep problems has been observed after controlling for several covariates. The three mediation pathways include (1) anxiety (β = 0.347, BootSE = 0.046, 95%CI = 0.2598-0.4431) representing 55.8% of the total effect, (2) sleep problems (β = 0.025, BootSE = 0.014, 95%CI = 0.0032-0.0575) suggesting 4.0% of the total effect, and (3) anxiety →sleep problems (β = 0.013, BootSE = 0.006, 95%CI = 0.0024-0.0265) yielding 2.0% of the total effect. The total indirect or mediating effect of food insecurity on depression among single parents in this study was 61.75%. Discussion

Main findings

The current study investigated the direct and indirect associations between food insecurity and depression among Ghanaian individuals without marital partners. The analysis found that food insecurity was independently associated with depression in a dose-response manner in the sub-Saharan African context, where research on this topic is limited (β = 0.623, 95%CI = 0.4844 − 0.7605). Another novel finding was that this association is partially and serially mediated through three crucial pathways: Thus, food insecurity is indirectly associated with depression through anxiety, sleep problems, and anxiety → sleep problems. To our knowledge, this is the first study to evaluate the link between food insecurity and depression among large, representative single parents in LMICs and the first to report the serial mediation effect of anxiety and sleep in this association. There is currently no specific or targeted support for single parents. This is the reason why this study is very crucial. The social intervention programs currently available are for the poorest of the poor in the general population. A typical example is the Livelihood Empowerment against Poverty (LEAP), where an amount ranging from GHC64 to GHC106 (USD4 to USD7), depending on the number of qualified people in a beneficiary household, is given to the poor every month in Ghana. This intervention is for a selected few in the country. There will be the need for targeted interventions to address food insecurity and improve mental and psychosocial health outcomes of single parents, particularly in the LMICs context.

Interpretation of findings

The current study resonates with previous works, suggesting a significant association between food insecurity and depression [36]. For instance, a cross-sectional, population-based study among men and women in rural Uganda by Perkins et al. [38] found a positive association between severe symptoms of depression and food insecurity. Sweetland et al.’s [39] study in rural Nigeria, Uganda, and Ghana also revealed a stronger correlation between food insecurity and mental health, especially for women and older persons. An autoregressive cross-lagged model analysis by Wu et al. [40] revealed that household food insecurity was linked to maternal depression. Despite the numerous research linking food insecurity to depression, research on food insecurity and depression among single parents is limited in LMICs, including Africa. The current study further builds on extant works on the potential underlying mechanisms in the food insecurity-depression link.

Several mechanistic pathways may explain the food insecurity-depression association. For instance, in our analysis, anxiety explained 55.8% of the total effect of food insecurity on depression. Several previous studies have looked into and reported a positive association between food insecurity and anxiety. For example, Whittle et al.’s [41] study on anxiety in American women living with or at risk from HIV argued that there is a positive association between food insecurity and anxiety. Furthermore, Wolfson et al. [9] found that the COVID-19 pandemic had a severe effect on low-income food insecure individuals’ mental health in the US. The intake of food with poor nutritious value may lead to anxiety, particularly among vulnerable people like single parents. Moreover, the stressful events associated with hunger and the pressure to imagine where to obtain food the next moment may plunge one into anxiety [10]. The chronic worry and stress associated with not having enough food may lead to feelings of helplessness, inadequacy, and uncertainty about the future, all of which may contribute to heightened anxiety [42]. This persistent anxiety, in turn, may increase the risk of developing depression in single parents. For example, the American Psychiatric Association [14], has indicated that prolonged periods of anxiety may lead to depression. Indeed, anxiety and depression are known to be of the same origin [17], and a global survey reported that 45.7% of people with depression have had a history of anxiety disorder [18]. Thus, anxiety and depression may originate in the same area of the brain called the amygdala, which generates emotional responses to environmental stimuli [43]. Indeed, people who are anxious are more likely to be depressed due, at least in part, to the escalation of mood symptoms and disruption in the balance of activity in the emotional centers of the brain [44].

In this study, sleep problems also explained approximately 4% of the total effect of food insecurity on depression. Several studies have argued that food insecurity is associated with poor sleep quality. For example, there is a correlation between sleep disorders and food insecurity [33]. A study in the US by Nagata et al. [45] supported the notion that young food-insecure adults also tend to have worse mental health and sleep quality. Furthermore, higher rates of household food insecurity led to higher levels of poor sleep quality in middle-aged and older adults in Ghana [16]. Food insecurity may lead to disordered dietary patterns, which may potentially reduce the energy required to initiate sleep or interfere with sleep quality [16]. Moreover, poor nutritious food due to food insecurity may cause gastroesophageal reflux, in which stomach acid frequently flows back toward and irritates the esophagus [19]. Again, single parents facing food insecurity may employ coping mechanisms such as working multiple jobs or taking on additional responsibilities to make ends meet. These efforts to address food insecurity may result in increased stress, leading to sleep problems, particularly among vulnerable people, including single parents [46]. Indeed, people who experience sleep problems are more likely to report depressive symptoms via abnormal development of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, which may further result in long-term alterations in neuropeptide and neurotransmitter synthesis in the central nervous system [47]. Again, sleep problems often result in fatigue and low energy levels, which may impact a person’s ability to cope with daily stressors and responsibilities. For instance, about 60–90% of patients with major depression suffer from sleep problems [48], while depression may be reduced with the treatment of sleep problems [49].

Another novel finding of this study is that the association between food insecurity and depression was serially mediated by anxiety → sleep problems, but to a lesser extent. Thus, food insecurity may lead to anxiety; anxiety may also lead to sleep problems, and sleep problems may consequently lead to depression [10, 15, 16]. Literature shows that sleep problems are much more common among individuals with anxiety disorders [50]. Again, individuals with anxiety disorders often complain of sleep problems [51]. As earlier established by literature, the anxiety-sleep problem is a mechanistic pathway in the association of food insecurity with depression. Thus, heightened anxiety resulting from food insecurity may make it difficult for single parents to relax and fall asleep. Persistent worry and hypervigilance may lead to fatigue, muscle tension, and disrupted sleep patterns. These physical symptoms may contribute to a general sense of malaise and low energy, which are common features of depression [52].

Implications of the study

The government and non-governmental organizations should consider targeted support programs to address food insecurity among single parents. This could include initiatives such as food assistance programs, economic empowerment programs, and access to affordable childcare to alleviate the financial burden. Again, policymakers should consider integrating mental health services within food insecurity alleviation programs. This could involve training community health workers to identify and support individuals experiencing anxiety, sleep problems, and depression related to food insecurity. Furthermore, efforts to create sustainable employment opportunities for single parents can significantly reduce food insecurity and improve mental well-being. Policies promoting job training, flexible work arrangements, and employment support services can be beneficial. In practice, practitioners working with single parents should take a holistic approach to addressing their needs. This may involve collaborating with social workers, nutritionists, and mental health professionals to provide comprehensive support that addresses the immediate food insecurity and the associated mental health challenges. Again, professionals working with single parents should prioritize education and awareness about the potential mental health impacts of food insecurity. By equipping individuals with coping strategies and access to resources, practitioners may help mitigate the adverse effects of anxiety, sleep problems, and depression.

Strengths and limitations

This study is one of the few to examine the relationship between food insecurity and depression and the first to explore the extent to which anxiety and sleep serially explain this association among single parents in Ghana. Therefore, considering the little or no information on the subject of food insecurity among single parents in Ghana, the findings from this study may provide baseline data for future studies. Our key variables, based on a number of previous studies, were also assessed using validated scales with robust reliability and content validity. Moreover, several potential confounders were controlled to validate the results. Notwithstanding these strengths, the study had some limitations. The study used a cross-sectional design, which makes it difficult to draw causal conclusions. The research instrument (questionnaire) relied on retrospective self-reporting, which may lead to recall and reporting biases. Future studies should clinically assess depression, anxiety, and sleep problems to provide more validity to the results. However, self-reporting may be the most effective way to get respondents’ subjective judgments about their economic and psychosocial status.

Conclusions

This study examined the extent to which anxiety and sleep serially explain the food insecurity and depression link among single parents in Ghana. Food insecurity was positively associated with depression, suggesting that food-insecure single parents are likely to suffer depression. The results further indicate that anxiety and sleep problems significantly mediated the association between food insecurity and depression. Strategies that may address food insecurity and related psychological factors such as anxiety and sleep problems among single parents may, to some extent, contribute to reducing depression. Future studies may use a longitudinal design to draw causal conclusions. Moreover, we suggest that further studies could look into the key covariates’ mediating or confounding role in the association between food insecurity and depression. Again, to allow for more nuanced policy recommendations that account for the covariates, there could be the need for replication of the main mediation analysis on subgroups constructed by the values of the covariates, e.g., age group, sex, social networks, etc. This could show which groups of single parents may need more support.
