Low levels and determinants of appropriate complementary feeding practices among children aged 6–23 months from Tigray, Ethiopia: a community-based cross-sectional study
Authors: Afework Mulugeta, Haftom Temesgen Abebe, Getachew Redae, Mebrahten Gebremariam, Meseret Abay, Desta Gebreyohannes, Alessandra N Bazzano
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionComplementary feeding is considered appropriate when introduced timely at 6 months of age, and where it fulfils the minimum meal frequency, minimum dietary diversity and minimum acceptable diet. Sufficient evidence is available on the different individual indicators of appropriate complementary feeding.ObjectiveThis study was conducted to assess the prevalence and determinants of appropriate complementary feeding practices among children aged 6–23 months in Tigray, Northern Ethiopia.Design and settingA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted in 52 districts of Tigray.ParticipantsA total of 5321 children aged 6–23 months were included using stratified two-stage random sampling.ResultsApproximately 19% of children received appropriate complementary feeding. Maternal residence in urban areas (adjusted OR (AOR) 1.26; 95% CI 1.062 to 1.489), maternal education (AOR 1.34; 95% CI 1.111 to 1.611), antenatal care (ANC) visits (AOR 1.75; 95% CI 1.343 to 2.281), household food security (AOR 2.81; 95% CI 2.367 to 3.330) and provision of colostrum to newborns (AOR 1.76; 95% CI 1.139 to 2.711) were found predictors of appropriate complementary feeding. Moreover, children in the 12–17 and 18–23 months age groups were 1.3 (AOR 1.30; 95% CI 1.083 to 1.551) and 1.7 (AOR 1.73; 95% CI 1.436 to 2.072) times more likely to receive appropriate complementary feeding respectively, compared with children aged 6–11 months.ConclusionsAppropriate complementary feeding practices among children aged 6–23 months remain unacceptably low in Tigray. Recommendations to improve nutrition outcomes include counselling on age-appropriate complementary feeding, education for girls and women, targeting families through food security initiatives, provision of nutrition education on appropriate complementary feeding practices during ANC visits, supporting mothers to initiate breastfeeding within the first hour of delivery and crafting context-based messaging for rural families.Keywords: NUTRITION & DIETETICS, Community child health, Child protection

Introduction

Complementary feeding is the process of introducing other liquid and solid foods in addition to breast milk as the energy and nutrient demand of growing children is no longer sufficiently maintained by breast milk alone.1 Improving the quantity and quality of a child’s complementary food is among the most cost-effective strategies to improve overall health and ensure the nutritional well-being of young children.2 However, among children aged 6–23 months surveyed from 80 low-income and middle-income countries, only 21.3% had prevalence levels higher than 50% for minimum dietary diversity (MDD), 56.2% for minimum meal frequency (MMF) and 10.1% for minimum acceptable diet (MAD), respectively.3 The lowest prevalence for MMF, MDD and MAD was identified from Western and Central Africa; the highest prevalence for MDD and MAD was in Latin America and Caribbean, and for MMF in East Asia and the Pacific.3 By the same token, a pooled magnitude of MDD and MAD intake among children aged 6–23 months from sub-Saharan Africa was reported to be 23.5% and 9.9%, respectively.4 5 Furthermore, only 9.9% of children aged 6–23 months in Western Ethiopia,6 9.5% in southern Ethiopia,7 10.8% in Tigray, Ethiopia,8 30% from Shashemene, Ethiopia,9 13.8% in Northern Ghana10 and 10% in southern Nigeria11 received appropriate complementary feeding expressed as a composite four core indicators of complementary feeding.

Complementary feeding is considered appropriate when introduced timely at 6 months of age (INTRO) and if it has fulfilled the MMF, MDD and MAD WHO recommendations.12 13 These four validated composite WHO indicators offer metrics with which to adequately identify the determinants of appropriate complementary feeding practices (ACFP) more comprehensively than using the individual indicators. However, there is a dearth of evidence on ACFP as defined by the four WHO‐UNICEF complementary feeding criteria namely INTRO, MMF, MDD and MAD in resource-constrained settings such as Ethiopia. The identification of the determinants of the composite indicators is essential to develop strategies to improve ACFP in Tigray, Northern Ethiopia. Updated knowledge of ACFP will assist nutrition programmes to better monitor the changes in complementary feeding practices and to design interventions to increase ACFP, thereby contributing to reduction of child undernutrition in the study communities.

Thus, this study was conducted to determine the prevalence and identify the determinants associated with ACFP among children aged 6–23 months in Tigray, Northern Ethiopia. The ACFPs were assessed based on the four core composite indicators of ACFPs recommended by WHO and UNICEF.

Methods

Study setting

Tigray is 1 of the 11 regional states of Ethiopia located at latitudes ranging from 120 14’50.50” to 140 53’48.03” and longitudes from 360 26’48.74” to 390 59’0.09”. Tigray is home to 6.2 million people with an estimated area of 54 593 km2. Most (73%) of the population live in rural areas while 27% are urban dwellers. The regional state of Tigray shares common borders with Eritrea in the north, the state of Afar in the east, the State of Amhara in the south and the Republic of the Sudan in the west. The region is divided into 7 zones, 52 woredas (districts) and 814 tabias/kebelles (a tabia/kebele is the smallest administrative unit in the region). According to the Tigray Health Bureau’s report, the health sector in the region consists of two tertiary hospitals, 14 secondary hospitals, 24 primary hospitals, 224 health centres and 741 health posts.

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was conducted in all woredas/districts of Tigray region from June to July 2019.

Study population

The study participants for this study were children aged 6–23 months who lived in all the 52 woredas (districts) of Tigray.

Data source and sample size

The present study used data from a previous survey on under-5 children malnutrition.14 The survey was conducted in all urban and rural communities of Tigray with 11004 children under-5 years surveyed from June to July 2019 and the sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula. The sampling design was a stratified two-stage sampling technique to select 11004 households with children aged 6–59 months. The total sample size was allocated to each woreda using proportion to the size of the population. In the first stage, representative tabias/kebeles were selected from each of the 52 woredas using simple random sampling. In the second stage, households with under-5 children from the selected kebelles/tabias were selected at random. The present analysis was carried out for the youngest children aged 6–23 months, and the subgroup of children aged between 24 and 59 months was excluded from the analysis.

Independent variables

The explanatory variables were residence, child sex, child age, child illness, marital status, age of mother, maternal education, father education, household food security status, household size, antenatal care (ANC) visit during the last pregnancy, prelacteal feeds, colostrum and nutritional status of children. The Household Food Insecurity Access Scale was used to measure the food security and classified households into severe, moderate, mild food insecure and food secure households.16

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was conducted by using STATA software V.16. Descriptive analysis was conducted using frequencies, and percentages were calculated for categorical variables. Bivariate and multivariable analyses were conducted to investigate the association between selected independent variables and outcome variable. Binary logistic regression model was used to identify the predictors of outcome variable. A p<0.20 in the bivariate was used for inclusion in the multivariable binary logistic regression model. An OR with 95% CI was used to assess the strength of the association between the independent variables and outcome variable. Multicollinearity was checked using the variance inflation factor and the goodness of fit was assessed using Hosmer-Lemeshow test.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the public were not involved in the design of the study, the conduct of the study or the dissemination of the findings.

Results

Characteristics of children and mothers

A total of 5321 mother–child pairs were included in the study. Of the total children, 2729 (51.3%) were male and the majority (77.9%) were from rural areas of residence (table 1). The mean (±SD) age of children and mothers was 14.1±5 months and 28.3±6.5 years, respectively. About 18% of the children had a fever, 15.1% had signs of cough or pneumonia and 13.3% had diarrhoea in the last 2 weeks before the date of data collection (table 1). In this study, about 10% of children were wasted, 34.6% stunted and 17.4% underweight. The majority (86.2%) of mothers were married. More than one-third (35.5%) of mothers had no formal education, and about 89% of the mothers had ANC visits during their last pregnancy. About 7% of households were severely food insecure and 23.1% were moderately food insecure (table 1).

Complementary feeding practices

A total of 4264 (80.1%) mothers introduced complementary feeding at 6 months of age. The proportion of children who received the recommended MMF, MDD and MAD was 79.4%, 26.2% and 23.1%, respectively. When the data are further disaggregated by age, the older children (12–23 months) had relatively better MDD, MMF and MAD compared with younger age groups (6–11 months). The overall prevalence of ACFP (combining the four core composite validated indicators) was 18.9%. Colostrum was given as the first feed to 5076 (96.5%) of the babies (see table 2).

Factors associated with ACFP

In the bivariate analysis, residence, child sex, child age, marital status, maternal education, father education, regular ANC visits, colostrum, cough/pneumonia, fever and household food security were significantly associated with ACFP at p<0.20. In the multivariable analysis, the factors that influenced ACFP were residence, child age, marital status, maternal education, regular ANC visits, colostrum and household food security status. Accordingly, mothers who lived in urban areas were 1.3 times more likely (adjusted OR (AOR) 1.26; 95% CI 1.062 to 1.489) to provide the appropriate complementary feeding compared with mothers who lived in rural areas. Similarly, higher odds of receiving ACFP were observed among mothers who had formal education (AOR 1.34; 95% CI 1.111 to 1.611) and regular ANC visits during their last pregnancy (AOR 1.75; 95% CI 1.343 to 2.281). Children in the 12–17 and 18–23 months age groups were 1.3 (AOR 1.30; 95% CI 1.083 to 1.551) and 1.7 (AOR 1.73; 95% CI 1.436 to 2.072) times more likely to receive the appropriate complementary feeding, respectively, compared with children aged between 6 and 11 months. Mothers who were divorced, separated or widowed were less likely (AOR 0.42; 95% CI 0.297 to 0.590) to provide appropriate complementary feeding to their children compared with those who were married. This result also showed that children belonging to food secure households were about three times more likely to receive the recommended complementary feeding compared with food insecure households (AOR 2.81; 95% CI 2.367 to 3.330). The provision of colostrum to newborns (AOR 1.76; 95% CI 1.139 to 2.711) was positively associated with appropriate complementary feeding. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test confirmed that the model was a good fit for the data (p=0.3600). Further information is presented in table 3.

Discussion

In this study, the composite child feeding index that captures multiple dimensions of infant feeding practices was used. The use of the indicators in isolation does not address the multidimensional aspects of appropriate complementary feeding among children aged 6–23 months. Our study results revealed that 80.1% of mothers introduced complementary feeding at 6 months of age and the proportion of children who received the recommended MMF, MDD and MAD was 79.4%, 26.2% and 23.1%, respectively. Overall, only 18.9% of the children aged 6–23 months had appropriate complementary feeding. Child age, place of residence, maternal education, ANC follow-up, household food security and provision of colostrum were the determinants of appropriate complementary feeding among children aged 6–23 months in Tigray, Ethiopia. The prevalence of appropriate complementary feeding in our study was higher as compared with findings from other studies in Ethiopia which have found 9.9% in Horo district, Western Ethiopia,6 9.5% in southern Ethiopia,7 10.8% in Tigray, Ethiopia.8 Our results are also distinct from findings in other African country studies which found 13.8% in Northern Ghana and 10% in southern Nigeria.10 11 The overall low prevalence of appropriate complementary feeding in our study is a clear indication that more than 80% of children aged 6–23 months were not appropriately fed and, therefore, missed the benefits of appropriate complementary feeding. However, it was lower than the findings reported from another study in Shashemene town, southern Ethiopia.9 The variation in settings (rural vs urban) and differences in the definition of appropriate complementary feeding may be responsible for the resultant variation in the levels of appropriate complementary feeding across the studies. The lack of improvement over time inappropriate complementary feeding practices for children aged 6–23 months appears to be related to the high prevalence of stunting in Tigray, Ethiopia.17

Older children were more likely to receive appropriate complementary feeding than younger ones, thereby suggesting improvement over time. This result is in agreement with findings reported in Nigeria,11 Ethiopia6,9 and Ghana.10 Suboptimal complementary feeding at younger ages can be attributed to the late introduction of family foods to younger children compared with the integration of family foods into the meals of older children. In other words, the dietary diversity of the household is more easily reflected in the dietary intake of older children than younger children. Younger children are also more likely to have a reduced number of feeding occasions, potentially making it harder to meet the appropriate complementary feeding requirements. Mothers might also perceive that the digestive system of younger children is not ready and hence they avoid or withhold certain foods, such as fats or meat products, from the meals of the younger children.18 The low level of ACFP for younger children could also be influenced by the cultural practices of initiating complementary feeding with small and selected foods but gradual increase in the amount and diverse family foods.19

Low maternal education was associated with suboptimal complementary feeding practices in the study communities. Higher odds of practising appropriate complementary feeding were observed among mothers who had formal education compared with mothers without formal education. A similar positive impact of maternal education on feeding practices was also reported in previous studies indicating that ACFP increases as the educational status of mothers increases.6 8 Educated mothers are more likely to be engaged in paid work and may have a higher socioeconomic status which could impact infant and young child feeding practices.11 20 Moreover, educated mothers who may have an increased level of confidence and command over independent resources, could be more likely to have access to nutrition-related messages, which they may more easily comprehend and translate that information into practice.

In this study, households with improved food security had a higher chance of having children who received appropriate complementary feeding. Children from food-secure households were more likely to be appropriately fed compared with children from food-insecure households. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia9 and Nigeria11 indicating that mothers with the highest and middle wealth index were more likely to feed the recommended dietary diversity and meal frequency to their children. This situation likely stems from food secure households having more access to diverse foods compared with food insecure households, and thus easier access to appropriate complementary feeding. The food secure households may similarly be more able to purchase diverse foods from the nearby market.

Children who lived in rural areas were less likely to receive the appropriate complementary feeding as compared with others who lived in urban areas and analogously, mothers from urban areas were more likely to practice appropriate complementary feeding compared with mothers who lived in rural areas. This relationship has also been noted in a recent systematic review and pooled analysis from Ethiopia.21 The more optimal practices of complementary feeding among urban children might be due to the fact that mothers from urban areas have more access to the market, better access to health services and have been more exposed to nutrition messages for knowledge and awareness on nutritional requirements of their infants thus better caring practices for their children. Moreover, urban mothers have access to various media channels which promote the importance of ACFP as compared with rural mothers. Similar findings on urban residence and complementary feeding have been noted in Pakistan and South Africa.22 23

Children of mothers who had regular ANC visits during their last pregnancy were more likely to be appropriately fed.9 Utilisation of health services in the continuum of maternal care may be an important factor in the appropriate feeding of young children, for example, Mekbib8 has reported that postnatal care (PNC) follow-up was a significant predictor of ACFP in children from Tigray, Ethiopia. The findings indicate that mothers who had regular ANC and postnatal visits were more likely to practise appropriate complementary feeding to their children. Moreover, in the current study, the provision of colostrum to the newborn was found to be a strong determinant of appropriate complementary feeding for infants and young children. Since the promotion of colostrum is intricately related to ANC visits and skilled birth attendance, these factors may be related. Mothers who attended regular ANC visits are likely to be better informed and have greater access to counselling on infant and young child feeding practices during the ANC sessions and thus, more likely to gain knowledge, bring behavioural change and be able to practice appropriate complementary feeding. Nutrition education and counselling services often provided during ANC and PNC visits might be an important contribution to ACFP, given the role health professionals could play in improving infant and child feeding practices during the critical moments of contact with families. The present study findings highlight the need to intensify concerted efforts for multisectoral collaboration between the nutrition-sensitive and nutrition-specific sectors to address the poor ACFP in Ethiopia and other low-resource settings.

Strengths and limitations

This study was conducted at the community level with a relatively a large sample size using a design effect to increase the power of the study, which increases the probability of generalisability to the entire population from which the sample was drawn. This study used the metric of appropriate complementary feeding, a composite of four core indicators that are relatively simple to calculate and interpret and are applicable across sociocultural contexts. These measures are also applicable to both breastfed and non-breastfed children.

Seasonal variation in food availability, social desirability bias and recall bias are limitations of the study. A weakness of the appropriate complementary feeding indicator is that it does not provide quantitative information about children’s food and nutrient intake.

Conclusions

The prevalence of ACFP was unacceptably low among children aged 6–23 months in the study communities. The key determinants of ACFP were child age, household food security, maternal education, ANC visits, colostrum and residence. In order to promote and improve ACFP among children aged 6–23 months, emphasis should be given to the improvement of maternal education, interventions which target the reduction of household food insecurity and expanding access to maternal and child health services.
