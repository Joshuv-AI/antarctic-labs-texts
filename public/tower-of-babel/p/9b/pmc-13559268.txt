Associations between pneumonia with sepsis, sepsis-associated encephalopathy, and mortality in adult intensive care unit: a retrospective analysis using MIMIC-IV database
Authors: Liang Qian, Weirong Kong, Huijie Zhu, Xiaochan Luo, Ziwei Zhong, Xiaomin Huang, Jiong Chen, Xia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPneumonia is a common pulmonary infection that can lead to sepsis and sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE), both of which are associated with increased mortality. This study aimed to examine the relationship between pneumonia and sepsis, as well as SAE, and to determine the association between pneumonia and patient mortality.MethodsA retrospective study was conducted based on the data attained from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV database (version 3.1). Patients’ demographics, comorbidities, laboratory results, clinical characteristics, and 30-day mortality were extracted for patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU). Univariate and multivariate regression analyses were performed to assess the associations between pneumonia and sepsis, pneumonia and SAE, and pneumonia and 30-day mortality, both in the entire cohort and subgroups.ResultsA total of 46,061 patients were included in the analysis, of whom 7,289 (15.82%) had pneumonia and 38,772 (84.18%) did not. Among pneumonia patients, 5,700 (78.20%) developed sepsis, and 4,250 (58.31%) developed SAE, with a significantly higher mortality rate compared to non-pneumonia patients (25.79% vs. 11.65%, P<0.001). Regression analysis revealed that pneumonia was associated with sepsis [hazard ratio (HR), 1.22, 95% confidence interval (CI), 1.18–1.25], SAE [odds ratio (OR), 1.55, 95% CI, 1.44–1.67], and 30-day mortality (HR, 1.19, 95% CI, 1.12–1.27), all of which all associations were statistically significant (P<0.001). Subgroup analysis indicated that the association between pneumonia and sepsis, SAE, and 30-day mortality remained significant across most subgroups, although demographic and clinical factors influenced these relationships. Multicollinearity and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the findings.ConclusionsPneumonia significantly increased the risk of developing sepsis and SAE, as well as the risk of 30-day mortality in ICU patients. These risks may be modulated by various patient demographic and clinical factors.Keywords: Pneumonia, sepsis, sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE), mortality, intensive care unit (ICU)

Key findings

• Pneumonia significantly increased the risk of sepsis, sepsis-associated encephalopathy, and 30-day mortality in patients in intensive care unit.

What is known and what is new?

• Pneumonia is a pulmonary infection but can lead to systemic inflammatory responses. Sepsis and sepsis-associated encephalopathy are associated with increased mortality.

• Based on the analysis of 46,061 patients from Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database, pneumonia was significantly associated the risk of sepsis, sepsis-associated encephalopathy, and 30-day mortality. These associations could be modified by patient demographic and clinical characteristics.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Prompt diagnosis and management of pneumonia should be aggressively implemented to decrease morbidity and mortality, especially in those patients with high-risk characteristics.

Introduction

Pneumonia is an acute infection and inflammation of the lower respiratory tract, caused by a wide range of microorganisms, including bacteria, viruses, and fungi. It is frequently encountered in clinical practice, with an estimated incidence of 4,350 cases per 100,000 population worldwide in 2021, imposing a substantial burden on both society and patients’ families (1). Without appropriate management, pneumonia can progress from a localized lung infection to a systemic illness or even sepsis. Sepsis occurs when the human body’s response to infection triggers widespread dysregulated inflammation, leading to acute multiple organ dysfunction (2). It was reported that pneumonia accounted for nearly half of all septic hospitalized patients (3,4).

Sepsis-associated encephalopathy (SAE) is an acute diffuse brain dysfunction, which is recognized as a serious complication in septic patients (5). Its underlying pathophysiology is complex and multifactorial, involving systemic inflammation, blood-brain barrier disruption, neuroinflammation, microcirculatory disturbance, and neurotransmitter imbalance. In the clinic, affected patients may manifest a diverse spectrum of neurological symptoms, ranging from mild confusion, delirium, to deep coma, with prolonged hospital stay, a high mortality rate, and long-term disability in survivals. It was estimated that up to 70% of septic patients in the intensive care unit (ICU) could develop SAE (6).

Pneumonia is a leading cause of sepsis. SAE is a frequent and serious complication of sepsis, accompanying by morbidity and mortality. Pneumonia patients were a heterogenous population, with distinct demographics and comorbidities. Certain characteristics, such as age, chronic pulmonary illnesses, and diabetes, could contribute to poor prognosis in pneumonia patients (7,8). However, there is a knowledge gap on whether and how these demographic characteristics and patient clinical characteristics could impact the association between pneumonia with sepsis and SAE. A comprehensive understanding of the relationship between pneumonia, sepsis, SAE, and mortality may promote evidence-based, personalized management strategies for pneumonia patients who are at a high risk of adverse outcomes.

The Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV is a comprehensive, publicly accessible critical care database sourced from the electronic health record of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center in the USA (9). It contains de-identified, large-scale, rich health data from patients admitted to the ICU and has become a critical resource for research in intensive care. In the present study, patients’ data were extracted from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care-IV (MIMIC-IV) database to examine the associations between pneumonia and various patient outcomes, including the development of sepsis, SAE, and mortality. The objective was to provide evidence-based recommendations for the risk stratification of patients who are at a high risk of adverse outcomes, with the goal of guiding timely and appropriate management. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-0945/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants’ selection

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This retrospective study was conducted on data retrieved from the MIMIC-IV database (version 3.1). This database has received institutional review board approval from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (USA). All patients’ information has been de-identified in compliance with Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act standards. The authors have adhered to the requirements for obtaining access to the database. The informed consent was waived due to the retrospective study design.

Patients aged 18 years or older, admitted to the ICU between 2008 and 2022, were selected for the study. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) length of hospital stay <1 day, (II) incomplete medical records regarding the diagnosis of pneumonia, sepsis, or SAE, and (III) unknown outcomes. In cases where patients had multiple ICU admissions, only the first admission was included in the analysis.

Data extraction

Data were extracted using Postgres structured query language (version 15.12) and DataGrip (version 2025.1.3). Data related to demographics (age, sex, race, types of ICU), disease severity [Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)], and comorbidities (kidney disease, peripheral vascular disease, dementia, cirrhosis, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, chronic kidney disease, acute myocardial infarction, acute kidney injury, atrial fibrillation, intracranial infection, and altered mental status due to hepatic or renal disease, and epilepsy) were recorded. Laboratory test results, including white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet (PLT) count, hemoglobin, glucose, blood urea nitrogen, magnesium, hematocrit, red cell distribution width (RDW), anion gap, pH, lactate, partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), total bilirubin, neutrophils, lymphocytes, albumin, lactate dehydrogenase, and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), as well as vital signs [blood pressure, heart rate, respiratory rate, temperature, and oxygen saturation (SpO2)], weight, height, and treatments (antibiotics, vasopressor, mechanical ventilation, and opioids) were recorded. Additionally, data on 30-day mortality were also collected.

Diagnostic codes from the International Classification of Diseases (ICD), 9th or 10th revision (ICD-9 or ICD-10), were extracted. Pneumonia was identified if a patient had an ICD-9 code beginning with 481, 482, 483, 484, 485, 486, or 4870, or an ICD-10 code starting with J12, J13, J14, J15, J16, J17, or J18. The diagnosis of sepsis was based on the Sepsis-3 criteria, with documented or suspected infection and an acute change in the SOFA score ≥2 that was indicative of sepsis (ICD-9 diagnostic codes: 99, 591–99, 592; ICD-10 diagnostic codes: R652, R6520, R6521) (10,11). SAE was defined when septic patients had GCS ≤14 within the first 24 h of ICU admission or were categorized to be 2930 or 2931 in ICD-9 or F05 in ICD-10 (12).

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed using R 4.5.1 software. Missing data for variables were handled using multiple imputations (R language, mice package) when the proportion of missing values was less than 20%. Variables with missing values equal to or greater than 20% were excluded from the analysis. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The continuous data were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median with interquartile range (IQR), and compared by t-test (equal variance or t'-test when unequal variances) or Wilcoxon rank-sum test, depending on the normality test. The categorical data were presented as frequency with percentages, and were compared by the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Three regression models were developed to explore the associations between pneumonia and sepsis occurrence, pneumonia and SAE occurrence, and pneumonia with mortality. Each model was developed in three steps:

❖ Model 1: unadjusted association between pneumonia and outcomes.

❖ Model 1: unadjusted association between pneumonia and outcomes.

❖ Model 2: model 1 + adjustment for demographics.

❖ Model 2: model 1 + adjustment for demographics.

❖ Model 3: model 2 + adjustment for additional confounders.

❖ Model 3: model 2 + adjustment for additional confounders.

Confounders in these models were selected based on univariate regression analysis and bidirectional stepwise regression using the Akaike Information Criterion. Variables with a P<0.05 in both univariate and multivariate analyses were considered statistically significant. Hazard ratio (HR) or odds ratio (OR) with 95% confidence interval (CI) were reported. Multicollinearity was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF), and a VIF <10 indicated no significant multicollinearity among variables.

Furthermore, subgroup analysis was conducted in patients grouped by demographics, comorbidities, and treatments. Meanwhile, the P for interaction was reported to assess the interaction effect.

To evaluate the potential bias introduced by multiple imputation of missing data, sensitivity analysis was conducted on the entire patient cohort. Conventional statistical tests were employed to compare variables before and after imputation. P>0.05 indicated that no significant statistical differences were found between the distributions of the imputed and original datasets, demonstrating that the imputed data remained reliable for analysis.

Results

Study participants’ baseline characteristics

Data from a total of 65,366 ICU patients were reviewed, and 46,061 patients were included in the final analysis (Figure 1). Missing values were imputed using multiple imputations when the proportion of missing data was less than 20%, and variables with missing data equal to or greater than 20% were excluded (Table S1). Among patients included in the analysis, 7,289 (15.82%) and 38,772 (84.18%) patients presented with or without pneumonia, respectively. In patients with pneumonia, 5,700 (78.20%) and 4,250 (58.31%) patients had sepsis and SAE, respectively, with an overall mortality rate of 25.79%. Among all septic patients, 5,700 (24.68%) cases were reported to have pneumonia. SAE occurred in 31.76% of all patients admitted to ICU and 63.34% of all septic patients admitted to ICU (Table 1). Except for sex, history of diabetes, intracranial infection, diastolic blood pressure, and serum magnesium level, all other characteristics exhibited significant differences between patients with or without pneumonia (all P<0.05). In addition, the occurrence rates of sepsis and SAE, as well as mortality rate, were significantly higher in patients with pneumonia than those without pneumonia (all P<0.05).

Associations between pneumonia with sepsis, SAE, and mortality

Univariate and multivariate analyses were performed to reveal the associations between pneumonia with sepsis, SAE, and 30-day mortality (Table 2). Compared to ICU patients without pneumonia, patients with pneumonia had a higher HR of experiencing sepsis (HR: 1.22, 95% CI: 1.18–1.25) after adjusting for potential confounders. Compared to septic patients without pneumonia, septic patients with pneumonia had a higher OR for developing SAE (OR: 1.55, 95% CI: 1.44–1.67) and a higher 30-day mortality rate (HR: 1.19, 95% CI: 1.12–1.27) after adjusting for potential confounders (all P<0.001). The development processes of these models, including confounders, are detailed in the Tables S2-S4. Multicollinearity was assessed in these models, and all VIF values remained below 5, indicating no significant multicollinearity and stable regression models (Table S5).

Subgroup analysis

Given the heterogeneous nature of the pneumonia patient population, characterized by varying demographics and underlying conditions, exploratory subgroup analysis was attempted to investigate the associations between pneumonia, sepsis, SAE, and 30-day mortality. This analysis stratified patients based on their demographics, comorbidities, and treatments (Figures 2-4). Except for the subgroup of patients with liver cirrhosis, all other subgroups exhibited significant associations between pneumonia and sepsis. With the exception of patient subgroups with intracranial infection, epilepsy, or those not receiving mechanical ventilation, all other patient subgroups exhibited significant associations between pneumonia and the occurrence of SAE. In the analysis of 30-day mortality, except for the subgroups of patients aging below 65 years old, or with peripheral vascular disease, cirrhosis, acute myocardial infarction, or acute kidney injury, all other subgroups of patients showed significant associations between pneumonia with 30-day mortality rate. In addition, pneumonia and cirrhosis, diabetes, heart failure, or acute myocardial infarction had an interaction effect on the occurrence of sepsis (Figure 2). Pneumonia and age, sex, kidney injury, cirrhosis, heart failure, altered mental status due to liver or kidney disease, vasopressors, opioids, or eGFR had an interaction effect on the occurrence of SAE (Figure 3). Pneumonia and age, cirrhosis, acute myocardial infarction, or acute kidney injury had an interaction effect on 30-day mortality (Figure 4).

Sensitivity analysis

Multiple imputations were applied to address missing data in the variables. To assess whether these imputations introduced bias into the results, the variables were compared before and after imputation. No significant differences were identified, indicating the robustness of the findings (Table S6).

Discussion

Pneumonia is a common cause of hospitalization and mortality in adult patients (13). It is one of the most common influential etiologies of sepsis. As a severe infection of the lower respiratory tract, pneumonia can trigger a systemic inflammatory response that progresses to sepsis when the infection leads to widespread immune dysregulation, organ dysfunction, or hemodynamic instability (14). In the clinical settings, pneumonia-associated sepsis is frequently identified in ICU populations. Patients with pneumonia are at a significantly higher risk of developing sepsis compared to those with other infectious diseases (15,16). This strong association highlights the importance of early recognition and management of pneumonia to prevent progression to severe sepsis and other poor outcomes. Understanding the relationship between pneumonia and its associated outcomes, such as sepsis, SAE, and mortality, can provide early, evidence-based insights for targeted interventions to improve patient outcomes. In this study, the MIMIC-IV database was analyzed, and it was demonstrated that pneumonia could be a major contributor to the development of sepsis in ICU patients. In addition, in septic patients admitted to ICU, pneumonia significantly increased the risk of SAE and mortality rate. In addition, certain factors, such as demographics and comorbidities, were found that increased the incidence for poor outcomes, including sepsis, SAE, and mortality. Collectively, this evidence highlights the need for physicians to closely monitor patients diagnosed with pneumonia. In patients with pneumonia, especially in those with high-risk factors for poor prognosis (e.g., SAE and death), early appropriate antibiotics, vigilant observation, timely supportive care, and close monitoring of multi-organ functions should be swiftly applied to reduce morbidity and mortality rates.

MIMIC-IV is a large-scale, real-world clinical dataset, containing high-resolution information from ICU patients, and is free-accessible for reproducible research (17). Numerous research groups have used the MIMIC-IV dataset to conduct peer-reviewed, high-quality studies, demonstrating the credibility and scientific rigor of the dataset. In the present study, 15.82% of septic patients admitted to ICU were diagnosed with pneumonia, which fell within the previously reported range of approximately 10–40% in studies using MIMIC-IV (18,19). External studies conducted in hospitals outside the MIMIC-IV dataset have similarly reported pneumonia rates ranging from 10% to 20%, further supporting the consistency of the findings with broader clinical observations (15,20). In addition, 63.34% of septic patients admitted to the ICU in this cohort had SAE, representing a proportion consistent with the rate (67%) reported in other MIMIC-IV-based research and comparable to the 36–70% range described in studies across different clinical settings (21-24). In the present study, mortality was recorded in 25.79% of patients with pneumonia, which was also similar to previously published rates in both MIMIC-IV analyses and external clinical cohorts (7,25-28). Taken together, the consistency between the observed pneumonia prevalence, SAE rates, and mortality in this study and values reported in prior MIMIC-IV studies, as well as research from various medical institutions, strongly confirm that these findings reflect real-world ICU practice and are applicable across a broad range of clinical ICU settings.

The present study evaluated the associations between pneumonia with poor patient outcomes, including sepsis, SAE, and mortality. Sepsis is a systemic inflammatory response syndrome that mainly leads to multiple organ failure. SAE is a significant and common complication, characterized by brain dysfunction resulting from a systemic infection, without direct involvement of the central nervous system. Patients with SAE can experience diffuse cerebral dysfunction, which is a consequence of the body’s dysregulated response to the infection. While the association between pneumonia and sepsis has been extensively studied, research on the relationship between pneumonia and SAE remains relatively limited. In the present study, it was demonstrated that more than half of pneumonia patients (58.31%) developed SAE, and nearly two-thirds of SAE cases (63.34%) had pneumonia. These findings are consistent with previous reports. For instance, Juarez-Martinez et al. conducted a meta-analysis incorporating data from 126 studies and found that 22% of pneumonia patients developed delirium, as a key manifestation of SAE. Additionally, in a subset of 16 studies concentrated on ICU patients, a higher percentage (47%) of pneumonia patients developed delirium. Another study reported that 78.8% of patients with SAE had a respiratory tract infection. Variations in the reported percentages of SAE patients with pneumonia, or pneumonia patients with SAE, may be attributed to differences in SAE definitions and hospital settings. Nevertheless, the collective evidence strongly supports a close relationship between pneumonia and SAE.

The impact of pneumonia on sepsis, SAE, and 30-day mortality could be influenced by patients’ demographics, comorbidities, disease severity, and treatment methods. The multivariate regression analyses were conducted to examine the relationship between pneumonia and its outcomes. The results indicated that after adjusting for relevant confounders, patients with pneumonia had a 22% higher HR of developing sepsis over time compared to patients without pneumonia. Meanwhile, patients with pneumonia had a 19% higher HR of death overtime compared to patients without pneumonia following adjusting for relevant confounders. The multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that the OR of a patient with pneumonia developing SAE was 55% higher than that of a patient without pneumonia following adjusting for relevant confounders. These findings indicated that, compared to patients without pneumonia, those with pneumonia had higher risks of sepsis, SAE, and 30-mortality, aligning with previously reported findings (7,15,22).

The key management strategy for patients with pneumonia concentrates on prompt diagnosis, appropriate antibiotics, close clinical observations, supportive care, and complication prevention (29). Pneumonia patients constitute a heterogeneous population, making risk stratification crucial for determining the appropriate level of care, guiding treatment decisions, and preventing potential adverse outcomes for individual patients. To further investigate this finding, patients were categorized into distinct subgroups, and multivariate regression analyses were conducted to explore the relationship between pneumonia and its outcomes across these subgroups. These exploratory analyses revealed that most subgroups of patients maintained statistically significant associations between pneumonia with sepsis, SAE, and 30-day mortality. However, certain subgroups did not exhibit statistically significant associations between pneumonia and outcomes.

In patients with liver cirrhosis, no association was identified between pneumonia and sepsis. Previous studies have demonstrated that liver cirrhosis could cause immune dysfunction and altered host defenses, leading to an increased risk of bacterial infections, including pneumonia, and sepsis development (30). In patients with intracranial infection, epilepsy, or those not receiving mechanical ventilation, no significant associations were found between pneumonia and the occurrence of SAE. These patients might already have pre-existing altered mental status due to intracranial infection or epilepsy, which could complicate the assessment of SAE. Pneumonia without mechanical ventilation might be indicative of less severe pulmonary infection and disease severity, corresponding to a lower risk of poor prognosis, including SAE. In the subgroup analysis of 30-day mortality, no significant association was identified in subgroups of patients aging below 65 years old, or with peripheral vascular disease, cirrhosis, acute myocardial infarction, or acute kidney injury. Younger pneumonia patients were associated with a lower risk of mortality. Their mortality might be more likely due to other conditions. In patients with cardiovascular diseases and renal insufficiency, previous studies have reported a positive relationship between pneumonia and mortality (31). No strong relationship was identified between pneumonia and mortality in these patients, which could be due to disease severity, acute versus chronic illness, or treatment strategies. Further studies are required to elucidate the relationship between pneumonia with its outcomes in patients with different characteristics.

In the regression analyses, interaction effects were also assessed. It was found that pneumonia interacted with cirrhosis, diabetes, heart failure, and acute myocardial infarction in association with sepsis occurrence. Furthermore, pneumonia interacted with age, sex, kidney injury, cirrhosis, heart failure, altered mental status due to liver or kidney disease, vasopressor use, opioids, and eGFR in association with SAE occurrence. Additionally, pneumonia exhibited interaction effects with age, cirrhosis, acute myocardial infarction, and acute kidney injury concerning 30-day mortality. The positive interaction effects found between pneumonia and these characteristics demonstrate that these factors may amend the relationship between pneumonia and its outcomes. Therefore, physicians should consider these factors when managing ICU patients with pneumonia.

The present study had several notable strengths. Firstly, the analyses were based on the MIMIC-IV database, one of the largest publicly available critical care datasets, including detailed comprehensive clinical records on laboratory results, vital signs, diagnoses, and interventions. Secondly, the large sample size allowed for robust multivariable modeling to analyze the outcomes of pneumonia patients with different characteristics. However, several limitations of the study should also be acknowledged. Firstly, although MIMIC-IV provided extensive clinical detail, its retrospective, single-center design might introduce selection bias and limit generalizability of the study results to different patient populations or care practices. Secondly, multiple imputation was employed to handle missing data. Although sensitivity analysis demonstrated the robustness of the results, potential bias, and imprecision in the interpretation of these findings and missing of important variables such as neutrophil count and lactate level could cause inaccurate results. Thirdly, as is inherent in all observational studies, the possibility of unmeasured or residual confounding remains, necessitating caution when drawing causal inferences. Fourthly, the diagnoses of pneumonia, sepsis, and SAE were based on ICD codes, which might introduce misclassification of clinical information and outcome definitions. We studied the association between pneumonia with sepsis and SAE, but were also not able to determine the time sequence of these events and could not establish a causal relationship among these events in this retrospective study based on the MIMIC-IV database.

Conclusions

Although pneumonia was diagnosed in a relatively small proportion of ICU patients, a significant percentage of these patients developed sepsis and SAE. Pneumonia was found to be associated with sepsis, SAE, and 30-day mortality. In the management of pneumonia patients, clinicians should consider patients’ demographics and comorbidities, as these factors may significantly influence patient prognosis.
