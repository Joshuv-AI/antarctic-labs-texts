Stable hydrogen isotope in tropical bat hair and the Asian isoscape: A dataset to advance migration research
Authors: Ada Chornelia, Alice C Hughes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractStable hydrogen isotope analysis is an emerging technique in ecological studies, provides an approach to understand animal migration, and provides locality information for wildlife forensics. In Asia, the application of stable hydrogen isotopes has largely been limited to hydrology and climatology. Maps of stable isotopes in precipitation (isoscapes) have not previously been developed at high resolutions across Southeast Asia, hindering our ability to understand migratory patterns for species too small to radio-track or reliably recapture. To advance the use of stable hydrogen analysis in Asia, we collated data and constructed an isoscape for three regions (Eurasia, Asia and Southeast Asia) and provides a cross-reference stable hydrogen isotopes data from the hair of 25 tropical bat species. The dataset provides a foundational resource for Asian isoscape analysis, and important datapoints for calibration in assigning the geographic origins of migratory species in Asia.Subject terms: Animal migration, Element cycles, Tropical ecology, Ecological modelling, Stable isotope analysis

Background & Summary

Stable hydrogen isotope analyses are increasingly being used for animal migration research, as well in wildlife forensics1–5. Hydrogen is a common element, with three naturally occurring isotopes identifiable by the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, including protium (hydrogen-1), deuterium (hydrogen-2), and tritium (hydrogen-3). Deuterium (δ2H or δD) known as the heavy or stable hydrogen isotope, contains one proton, one neutron, and one electron6. Stable isotopes are isotopes that do not experience radioactive decay, they remain unchanged indefinitely under normal conditions, making them perfect for geospatial analysis7.

Stable hydrogen isotopes in the keratin of animals, such as hair, feathers and claws (thereafter, δ2Hh) correlates with stable hydrogen isotopes of local precipitation where the tissue was grown (thereafter, δ2Hp)8–11. Once these tissues fully develop, they become metabolically inactive, thus their isotopic signatures represent the dietary intake during growth12–14. Precipitation has distinct stable isotopes signatures in different locations, influenced by fractionation processes such as latitude, precipitation volume, temperature and elevation15,16. A map of variation in stable isotope ratios across the landscape is known as an isoscape7. Isoscapes are constructed using measurements of stable hydrogen isotope ratios (δ2Hp) combined with geographic and environmental data through the application of statistical or machine learning models17,18. Therefore, establishing the relationship between δ2Hh in animal tissue and δ2Hp in the isoscape can enhance our understanding of the movement of migratory species, especially for species where other means of assessing movement are challenging or unavailable19.

However, the successful use of isoscapes to trace the migratory species movements relies on the isoscape map resolution and the resolution of the data which underpins it2,3,20. While continental-scale isoscapes have been established for America, such as IsoMAP (https://isomap.org)21 and for Europe via the R package IsoriX18, Asia’s isoscapes have remained limited to regional or country levels; for instance east China22,23, a river isoscape24, or short-term monitoring in Brunei Darussalam25, Tianshan Mountain in Central Asia26, Thailand27, with little further work across the region. Additionally, the application of stable hydrogen isoscapes in Asia has primarily focused on hydrology and climatology15,16,28 and has not yet been applied to ecological studies. Thus, refining stable hydrogen isotope data for Asia is necessary to enable broader applications in ecology, forensics, and conservation. The data sources used to construct the isoscape are primarily derived from international observations of stable isotopes in precipitation, available through the Water Resources Program of the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO). This information is accessible from the Global Network of Isotopes in Precipitation (GNIP)29. However, challenges arise from varying observation lengths and uneven spatial distribution of stations due to inaccessible locations and high costs associated with measuring precipitation isotope ratios22,30. In recent years, interest in the spatio-temporal prediction of hydrogen and oxygen values in precipitation has increased, particularly in regions lacking GNIP data24. Efforts have been made to generate more accurate, high-resolution regional isoscapes, such as through Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN) and data fusion22,23 or isotope-equipped general circulation models (iGCMs)31. Building on this, we combined observational GNIP data and simulation data generated from published papers that aid its application in ecological studies in tracking animal migration and forensic applications in Asia.

Spatial information derived from isotopic tracking relies on transfer function or calibration (isotopic difference between precipitation and tissue) which requires baseline data across a wide geographical area17,18,21. Transfer function (or calibration) is defined as the relationship between the hydrogen isotopes within tissue (hair, nails or feathers) and the hydrogen isotopes in precipitation of the environment2. This is a critical step in the assignment of geographic origin of migratory species when using stable hydrogen isotopes18,20. Here we present a dataset of stable hydrogen isotope ratios from the hair of 739 individuals across 25 Asian bat species32. This dataset provides a vital calibration source for future studies aiming to enhance our understanding of bat movement using stable hydrogen isotope analysis. Tracking bat migration is challenging due to body-size limitations, challenging terrain, and low recapture rates33–35. Therefore, this dataset will be instrumental in future research focused on migratory bat species in Asia and accelerating development of this technique for future studies.

We present a dataset for construction of isoscapes for Eurasia, Asia and Southeast Asia, which can be reproduced for other movement ecology studies and wildlife forensic applications utilizing stable hydrogen isotopes. We provide a compiled dataset of stable hydrogen isotope values in precipitation, fitted geostatistical models, elevation raster file, isoscapes raster files and stable hydrogen isotope values from bat hair samples. We include the required resources, such as raw database sources and codes, to reproduce the results within this work. This dataset provides a valuable resource for (a) tracing long-distance migratory patterns of birds, bats or other terrestrial taxa, (b) identifying the origin of samples from illegal wildlife trafficking, and (c) enhancing calibration data for analysing migratory bat species in Asia.

Methods

Isoscapes

The dataset of stable hydrogen isotopes in precipitation (henceforth, δ2Hp) was primarily obtained from the Global Network for Isotopes in Precipitation (GNIP), which is an international isotope monitoring network for hydrogen and oxygen provided by the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) and the World Meteorological Organization (WMO). This information was accessed through the Water Isotope System for Data Analysis, Visualization, and Electronic Retrieval (WISER) portal. Additional data from published papers15,16,27,36–54 also included in the analysis. Data were curated, filtered, missing observations were discarded and prepared carefully using R package ‘dplyr’ to conform data entry requirements by R package ‘IsoriX’18. We provide aggregated data-files, and raw GNIP δ2Hp data is available directly from the original sources. The aggregated data were prepared using ‘prepsource’ function in IsoriX package. The elevation data were downloaded from the multisource Mapzen Terrain Tiles Dataset using ‘getelev’ function in IsoriX package, which has ground resolution of 1,223 meters at equator (0° latitude), and a pixel size of 30 arc seconds (1 km). All three isoscapes presented in this study provide high spatial resolutions, making them well-suited for predicting the geographic origins of organisms based on stable isotope analysis. Users who want to modify the resolution may consider the computational time and power. R code for adjusting these parameters is included within this publication.

Spatial landscapes of environmental isotopic variation maps were constructed utilizing the workflow outlined in the R package ‘IsoriX’. Detailed information regarding the workflow can be found in the IsoriX package documentation. Geostatistical models were fitted using ‘isofit’ function using linear mixed effect models, and the fitting procedures were conducted in R package spaMM55 which is integrated within IsoriX. The mixed model estimates the relationship between topographic features and its mean δ2Hp signature, while the spaMM package fits a linear mixed model with spatial autocorrelation using a Matern correlation models. To predict the average isotope precipitation values across months and years, elevation and latitude were used as fixed effects, while the location of weather stations was considered as random effect. The Matern correlation was applied to the sum of the longitude and latitude to account for spatial autocorrelation. The mean models and the residual dispersion models were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The first isoscape covers 10°S-80°N, and 20°E-140°E (Eurasian isoscape), we clustered (or aggregated) data with the total of 378 observations stations, all sourced from GNIP IAEA datasets. Given the extensive geographical area, constructing this isoscape requires significant computational resources. The second isoscape covers 10°S-60°N, and 70°E-140°E (henceforth, Asian isoscape), total of 172 GNIP IAEA observation stations after data aggregation. Then we constructed the third isoscape for the mainland of Southeast Asia and the southern part of China, encompassing the geospatial coordinates 5°N-32°N and 90°E-123°E (henceforth, Southeast Asian isoscape). Due to limited numbers of GNIP stations from this region, additional data mined from published papers were included, which totalled 108 observation stations after data aggregation. The ground resolution of elevation raster for this isoscape was 1,223 meters at the equator (0° latitude).

Stable hydrogen isotope ratio from bat hair

Stable Hydrogen Isotopes or Deuterium in bats hair (henceforth, δ2Hh) were collected from 739 bats, from seven families and 25 species. Bats were collected using harp traps and mist nets across 19 sampling sites in South China and Northern part of Vietnam, from 2018-2019. Hair samples were collected from the inter-scapular region (approximately one to two mg) using sharp dissection scissors, and were stored in numbered separated microtube for each individual. Species identification followed an integrated approach, combining morphology, acoustics and DNA barcoding56. Species, sex, age, reproduction status, date of collection, latitude, longitude and elevation were recorded for each sample. Each individual was photographed with a unique identification number, and echolocation was recorded prior to release. Only adults were included in this analysis to minimize potential biases in stable isotope ratios between sub-adults and adults57. Stable hydrogen isotope (δ2Hh) composition analyses were performed at the Laboratory of Stable Isotope Mass Spectrometry, Shenzhen High-tech Precision Isotope Testing, Inc China.

Prior to stable hydrogen isotope analysis, bat hair was cleaned from the surfaces oil using a 2:1 chloroform: methanol solution. Each dry bat hair sample (0.20−0.3 mg) was weighed into a silver foil capsule, lightly crimped, and loaded into the autosampler of a fully automated equilibration unit. The sample was dropped from the autosampler tray into an equilibration chamber heated to 110 °C, through which steam was passed in a flow of helium. The steam was produced from water of known isotopic composition, which was supplied to a heater block at 110 °C by a peristaltic pump operating at 2 μL min−1. The sample was allowed to equilibrate for at least 10 min, after which it was dropped into a low-blank autosampler purged with helium at 150 mL min−1 immediately prior to dropping into a Flash HT 2000 elemental analyzer (Thermo Fisher, USA), where it was pyrolyzed at 1,380 °C.

Pyrolysis was contained within a ceramic tube lined with a glassy-carbon insert part-filled with glassy carbon chips. The tube was flushed with helium (150 mL·min−1). The resultant products of organic material pyrolysis, H2 and CO, were resolved gas-chromatographically via a 5 Å molecular sieve maintained at 50 °C, prior to mass-spectrometric analysis using a Delta V Plus mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, USA) for hydrogen isotope ratio (D/H) and oxygen isotope ratio (18O/16O) in the Laboratory of Stable Isotope Mass Spectrometry, Shenzhen High-tech Precision Isotope Testing, Inc.

Data Records

There are two types of dataset included, the files to predict stable hydrogen isotope ratios in landscape (isoscape) and the stable hydrogen isotope value of bat hair (δ2Hh). All datasets have been uploaded to Zenodo and are publicly accessible under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International license (10.5281/zenodo.15636685)58 and (10.5281/zenodo.16595507)59. The data to construct the isoscapes is included in three separate folders, for Eurasian, Asian, and Southeast Asian isoscapes. Each of the isoscape folders contains four main datasets, including the aggregated δ2Hp from original sources (GNIP and published papers) (.csv), fitting geostatistical model data (.rda files), elevation raster (.tif) and isoscape files (.tif files). The.csv file contains an average of the δ2Hp values across month and years and the variance between monthly measurement spanning across the year corresponds to each weather observation. This data includes aggregated data for each weather station, mean (mean_source_value) and variance (var_source_value) of stable hydrogen isotope across month and year, total observations (n_source_value), and geographic location (latitude, longitude, elevation). To ensure the reproducibility of this study’s code and results, we have supplied high-resolution elevation raster files (.tif) corresponding to each isoscape. These raster files were generated by aggregating neighbouring cells from the original downloaded elevation data. This approach was taken to improve computational efficiency of predicting the isotope value in the landscape and to reduce the memory demands. Nevertheless, users have the option to download the elevation raster of their preferred resolution. The Isoscape files for Eurasian, Asian and Southeast Asian isoscape is provided in GTiff raster (.tif) formats and are compatible in other software for Geographic Information System (GIS). A detailed overview of the data files contained in each folder is also available in the “description” section of the repository.

Data Overview

The elevation raster for Eurasia is 5,355 × 8,299 cells, with resolution of each pixel measuring 0.016° × 0.016° (approximately 1,763 meters at the equator). The cell size determines the number locations for which we want to predict the isoscape, for instance, for Eurasia, the total number of cells is 5,355 by 8,299 (rows and columns), representing 44,441,145 locations (cells). The maximum elevation is 8150.375 meters. The elevation raster for Asia consists of 4,569 × 4,678 cells, with a resolution 0.016° × 0.016° for each cell, and maximum elevation of 8125.44 meters. The elevation map for Southeast Asia has dimensions of 2,732 × 3,305 cells, and a resolution for each cell of 0.01° × 0.01° (1,168 × 1,168 meters at equator), with maximum elevation of 7,121 meters. The Asian isoscape comprises 21,373,782 cells, while the Southeast Asian isoscape consists of 9,029,260 cells. Computational times for generating isoscapes varies from hours to weeks, heavily dependent on the number of data points, elevation raster resolution and computational power of the hardware. For instance, the Eurasian isoscape includes over 44 million cells, to represent variation across the continent. Processing such a large dataset typically requires several weeks on a mid-range workstation equipped with at least 12 cores, 32 to 64 GB of RAM and a dedicated GPU. However, high-performance computing (HPC) significantly can accelerate the process. In contrast, the Southeast Asian isoscape comprises over 9 million cells, enabling for faster computational time. Variations in cell size between the isoscapes result from the differences in the geographic coverage, while the raster resolution was selected to ensure reusability for other studies.

The second dataset includes δ2Hh ratio from a total of 739 individuals across seasons in 2018-2019. This included 25 tropical bat species (Table 1). Data files provide information on seasonal δ2Hh ratio for male and female bats in.csv file (total of 25 files). Each.csv files contains sample unique identifier number represents each individual bats, captured location (latitude and longitude) and stable hydrogen isotope values (δ2Hh) for each individual bat. Systematic variation of δ2Hh values of bat’s hair for this dataset are provided in the related paper32.

Technical Validation

We tested the reliability and robustness of the three isoscapes in the following steps, including model performance evaluation, δ2Hp variance assessment by plotted the predicted variance to estimate the uncertainty of prediction in isotope values at each location. These were generated using mixed-effects spatial models that account for both environmental predictors and spatial autocorrelation. Results were compared with existing regional isoscapes from previous studies. We also compare model performance across three isoscapes that use different geospatial coverage and data point quantities.

The Eurasian, Asian and Southeast Asian isoscapes were constructed based on hydrogen isotopes in precipitation data (see methods: Isoscape). We utilized all the three main datasets (aggregated δ2Hp data from observation station within study area, fitting geostatistical models and the elevation raster) to construct each isoscape (Eurasia, Asia and Southeast Asia) (Figs. 1–3) (modified from32). We included high-resolution elevation rasters in this database (see data overview for details). Users adjusting the raster resolution for computational purposes should note that higher resolutions produce more accurate isoscape maps, while lower or overly smoothed resolutions may lose important features. This decision may influence the geographic origin assessment of the migratory species.

The Asian isoscape (10°S-60°N, 70°E-140°E) covers Southeast, North and East Asia and surrounding islands, including Japan, the Philippines, and Indonesia. It was constructed based on the GNIP dataset and shows a latitudinal gradient in predicted mean δ2Hp precipitation values. Figures 1 and 2 both indicate regions lacking GNIP monitoring stations.

Elevation and latitude influenced the predicted δ2Hp values in precipitation

Geostatistical models were fitted using linear mixed-effects models using the spaMM package to investigate the influence of elevation and latitude in predicted stable hydrogen isotopes in precipitation while accounting for spatial and source-specific variability. The relationship between the predicted and observed mean δ2Hp values of the three isoscape is shown in Fig. 5. The results from the mean geostatistical models for the Eurasian isoscape showed that the intercept was estimated at −19.73 ± 44.2 (t = -0.45) (Table 2), representing the baseline mean predicted isotope hydrogen value when elevation and latitude are zero. Elevation and latitude had a significant negative effect on the mean predicted δ2Hp (elevation, β = −0.0085 ± 0.007, t = −11.01; latitude, β = −1.32 ± 0.046, t = −2.85), indicating a lower predicted δ2Hp value with increasing elevation and latitude. The spatial random effect (long + lat) exhibited a large variance (λ = 2657), suggesting strong spatial variability in the mean response, while source-level variance was small (λ = 1e-05). The small variance support the reliability of the model because the observed effects (elevation and latitude) were less likely to be confounded by source (variance from dataset)-related differences.

The residual dispersion model in the Eurasian isoscape, which was fitted with a gamma family with a log link to analyse the variability in residuals, indicating that both spatial effects (λ = 5.78) and source-specific effects (λ = 0.0009) significantly influenced the variance in residual variance. The intercept for the dispersion model was significant (β = 5.899 ± 2.23, t = 2.65), suggesting the baseline variability in residual dispersion. While source-specific effects matter for residual variance, they are far less impactful than spatial effects. Therefore, spatial context (elevation and latitude) is a critical driver of variation in the mean predicted δ2Hp, rather than differences due to the data’s source (different length of observation in each weather stations). The low variance suggests that the data collected from different sources were consistent and do not significantly contribute to the overall variability of the models.

All three isoscapes show latitudinal patterns (δ2Hp values tend to be depleted/more negative in higher latitudes), and elevation (δ2Hp values tend to be more negative as the elevation increased). Elevation is identified as the significant explanatory variable in the model, consistent with previous studies in China that also considered additional factors such as humidity24. Regions without observation stations are highlighted in the maps that informed the future studies to fill the data gaps (Figs. 1–3). Additionally, we also plot the uncertainty of the predicted isotope values generated from fitted geostatistical models for all the isoscapes (predicted variance maps, Fig. 4). These maps showed the level of uncertainty in the distribution of predicted δ2Hp across the landscape. Generally, the uncertainty of the prediction of mean δ2Hp tend to increase with distance from the weather stations. This helps identify data gaps and plan future studies.

Geostatistical model comparison for three isoscapes

For the comparison between the fixed effects in the mean models (Gaussian mixed model) of the three isoscape (.rda files) (Table 2), we found that elevation consistently showed a significant negative association with the mean source. This indicates that higher elevations are robustly associated with lower mean predicted isotopes in precipitation values, regardless of different numbers of data point. Latitude also had negative coefficient in all isoscape models, however the statistical significance and magnitude varied. The effect of latitude across the three isoscapes appears to diminish as geospatial coverage decreases (Figs. 1–3), latitudinal effect was more significant in the Eurasian isoscape at a continental scale, and the effect of latitude is not significant in the Southeast Asian isoscape with smaller geospatial coverage. This may indicate reduced statistical power or increased heterogeneity within the smaller geospatial coverages. The intercept varied between the three isoscapes (Table 2), and none were statistically significant, suggesting no strong baseline mean predicted isotope hydrogen value when elevation and latitude are zero.

Source-level random effects in the three models exhibit low variance, indicating that most between-source variability is accounted for by fixed and spatial effects. The spatial structure, as showed by random effects variance and Matern correlation parameters showed stronger spatial structure in the larger dataset (Eurasian isoscape), but less significant as geospatial coverage decreased (Southeast Asian isoscape). This suggests that sufficient geographical coverages and observation points are critical for detecting and modelling the spatial isotopes in precipitation patterns. Modelling residual variance with spatial and random effects showed consistent heterogeneity, however precise estimation maybe challenged by numbers of datapoints and spatial coverage. Future work should prioritize representative spatial sampling and consider autocorrelation in spatial modelling frameworks.

Model diagnostics, validation and consistency with regional δ2Hp isoscape pattern

Furthermore, to assess the fit and assumptions of above geostatistical models, we examined standard diagnostic plots for the mean model, to determine the relationship between the observed and predicted response to the fit of the mean isotopic values (Fig. 6: 1a, 2a, 3a) and to the fit of the residual dispersion variance in the isotope values (Fig. 6: 1b, 2b, 3b). Overall, all models (Eurasia, Asia and Southeast Asia) showed that the residuals distribution generally is centred around zero, although there are some outliers. All models suggest minor heteroscedasticity, Q-Q plots are mostly linear indicating good adherence to normality assumptions, and all histograms are symmetric and bell-shaped, consistent with normally distributed residuals. All three geostatistical models demonstrated well-behaved residuals, with Asia fitted showing the strongest conformity to model assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity. The Eurasian and Southeast Asian fitted models exhibited minor deviations, such as mild heteroscedasticity and outliers, but do not substantially undermine model validity. All diagnostic plots collectively support the suitability of the geostatistical fits for isoscape predictions.

The three isoscapes we provide here are consistent with previous work on regional stable hydrogen isotopes, for instance in southwest China16. In southwest China, temperature is the most sensitive meteorological factor to monsoon climate response, explaining about 37.5% of the variation of isotopes values on the intra-annual scale15,52. Similar patterns of correlation between temperature and precipitation volume has also been observed in isoscapes in east China23. Here we used latitude and elevation as indirect proxies to predict the effect of temperature on the isotope composition60. Factors such as moisture source play crucial roles in driving the variations in isotopes in precipitation in the subtropical region of China15,61–64. Similarly, previous studies in China also exhibited a continental effect, with values of isotope decreasing from coastal lowlands to high-elevations in mountainous area65.

Limitations

We were able to improve the resolution of predicted isoscape maps by using additional data from published papers. Despite our efforts to maximize dataset inclusivity by mining additional data from sources like GNIP and published papers, the observed data remains predominantly of short duration, resulting in uneven data distribution. While increasing the number of stations is a potential solution for the long term, it may be costly. One potential solution to address the limited observational data is to incorporate observation and simulation using isotope-equipped general circulation models (iGCMs)66, neural networks23, random forest24, etc which has been applied in some countries22. Isoscape variations in the region are not solely influenced by temperature; while the linear dependence of precipitation on temperature is well-known in middle to high latitudes, the relationship becomes more complex in many tropical and monsoon regions like Southeast Asia. Factors such as the precipitation volume, moisture origin, and the integrated histories of both condensation and mixing with surface flux during the transport of the air mass from the source to the precipitation site, as reviewed in15,27,52,62 influenced the isotope in precipitation and its annual variation. Future research should incorporate these variables, expand weather station coverage, and explore alternative data sources to enhance accuracy. Although our study offers valuable insights, addressing these limitations is essential for improving isoscape. By considering all of these factors and conducting in-depth analysis to construct isoscapes that reflect smaller details of the landscape, we can increase the prediction accuracy of species movement directions and determine the local origin for long-distance movements species. The application of the Asian isoscape in tracing in migration of bats can be viewed in32.

Usage Notes

This dataset offers key resources for building an isoscape based on stable hydrogen isotopes in precipitation across Asia and Southeast Asia. Users can construct their own isoscapes to explore species migration and should carefully consider the geographical scope of the isoscape before proceeding with the analysis (refers to the geostatistical mixed model results in the technical validation section). Users needing alternate resolutions should carefully consider the biology and behaviour of studied animals, for example, if using the Asian Isoscape for species with restricted movements, extrapolations may be less accurate. However, changes to resolution without additional data may miss-infer the origin of the individuals.

The resolution of the elevation raster also requires careful consideration, higher resolutions require higher computational power, but provide a higher accuracy when sufficient data is present. Utilizing the isoscape effectively also requires an understanding of species ecology to align resolutions, scale and to assess the accuracy of analysis. The second dataset of δ2Hh bat values can be used by bat researchers as calibration or transfer function for bat migratory studies in Asia.
