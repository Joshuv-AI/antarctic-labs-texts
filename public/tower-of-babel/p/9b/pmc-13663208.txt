Integrative analysis and in vitro assessment of the effects of α-mangostin on high-glucose-induced inflammatory and oxidative stress responses in RAW264.7 macrophages
Authors: Peng Cao, Yunwei Wang, Mingfan Xi, Kaituo Liu, Shuoyi Hui, Peng Zhao, Guozhong Lyu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPersistent macrophage-mediated inflammation under hyperglycemic conditions is an important feature of the inflammatory microenvironment associated with diabetic wounds. α-Mangostin (α-MG), a natural xanthone derived from Garcinia mangostana, has been reported to exert anti-inflammatory activity in various experimental models. However, its effects on high-glucose-induced macrophage inflammation and oxidative stress remain insufficiently characterized.ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the effects of α-MG on inflammatory and oxidative responses in high-glucose-stimulated macrophages and to explore its potential molecular mechanisms using an integrative strategy combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and in vitro assessment.MethodsPotential targets of α-MG were collected and intersected with diabetic wound-associated genes to identify candidate targets. Protein–protein interaction analysis and functional enrichment analyses were performed to identify hub targets and related biological pathways. Molecular docking was used to evaluate the binding affinity between α-MG and key targets, followed by a 110 ns MD simulation of the α-MG–TNF complex and a 50 ns supplementary MD simulation of the α-MG–PTGS2 complex. In vitro experiments were performed using high-glucose-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages, with mannitol included as an osmotic control. The effects of α-MG on inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress markers, PTGS2/COX-2 expression, and NF-κB activation were further evaluated.ResultsA total of 24 overlapping targets between α-MG and diabetic wound-associated genes were identified. Enrichment analyses suggested that these targets were mainly associated with inflammatory and immune-related biological processes, with TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways identified as computationally predicted and potentially relevant pathways. Molecular docking showed favorable binding interactions between α-MG and several hub targets, including PTGS2, AKT1, TNF, and PARP1. The 110 ns molecular dynamics simulation further suggested that the α-MG–TNF complex remained relatively stable during the simulation period. A supplementary 50 ns MD simulation also supported the conformational stability of the α-MG–PTGS2 complex. In high-glucose-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages, α-MG significantly reduced the mRNA expression and secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. α-MG also decreased PTGS2/COX-2 expression and inhibited NF-κB p65 phosphorylation. In addition, α-MG alleviated high-glucose-induced oxidative stress, as indicated by increased GSH content and SOD activity, together with reduced MDA levels. The inclusion of the mannitol control helped distinguish glucose-associated inflammatory and oxidative responses from osmotic stress-related effects.Conclusionα-MG attenuated high-glucose-induced inflammatory and oxidative responses in RAW264.7 macrophages. The integrative computational analysis suggested that computationally predicted TNF- and IL-17-related pathways may be involved, whereas the in vitro experiments provided supportive evidence only for changes in inflammatory cytokines, PTGS2/COX-2, oxidative stress markers, and NF-κB activation. These findings provide preliminary experimental and computational evidence for the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant potential of α-MG under diabetic-like macrophage inflammatory conditions. However, direct pathway-specific validation and functional diabetic wound-related models are required before drawing conclusions regarding TNF/IL-17 pathway regulation or therapeutic efficacy in diabetic wound healing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05463-7.

Introduction

Chronic wounds are among the most serious complications of diabetes and are associated with prolonged healing, increased susceptibility to infection, a higher risk of amputation, and substantial healthcare costs [1–5]. Diabetic wounds are difficult to manage because their local microenvironment is characterized by persistent inflammation, excessive oxidative stress, impaired angiogenesis, and dysregulated extracellular matrix remodeling [6–8]. These pathological alterations collectively contribute to delayed wound closure and impaired tissue regeneration. Among the cellular components involved in this process, macrophages play a central role in regulating inflammatory responses within the hyperglycemic microenvironment [9–11]. Under sustained high-glucose conditions, macrophages may exhibit enhanced pro-inflammatory activation, accompanied by increased production of cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which further aggravate the inflammatory milieu and may impair tissue repair [10, 12–14]. In addition, hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress can further amplify macrophage-mediated inflammation, suggesting that simultaneous modulation of inflammatory and oxidative responses may be important for attenuating diabetic-like inflammatory conditions.

Natural compounds have attracted increasing attention as potential modulators of inflammation and oxidative stress in chronic disease-related settings [15, 16]. α-Mangostin (α-MG), a major xanthone derived from the pericarp of Garcinia mangostana, exhibits a broad range of pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antimicrobial, and anticancer effects [17–20]. Previous studies have shown that α-MG can inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, suppress nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB)-related inflammatory signaling, and alleviate oxidative damage. However, its effects on high-glucose-induced macrophage inflammation and oxidative stress remain insufficiently characterized, particularly with respect to its potential molecular targets and pathway-related mechanisms under diabetic-like inflammatory conditions [21–23].

With the development of computational pharmacology, network pharmacology has become a useful strategy for elucidating the complex relationships among bioactive compounds, molecular targets, and disease-associated networks [24–26]. When combined with molecular docking and molecular dynamics (MD) simulation, this approach provides a systematic platform for predicting ligand-target interactions and evaluating the structural stability of compound-target complexes at the atomic level [27–29]. Such in silico methods are valuable for early-stage screening of candidate compounds and for generating mechanistic hypotheses. However, pathway predictions obtained from computational analyses generally require experimental validation. Therefore, cell-based in vitro models, particularly those based on high-glucose stimulation, provide a practical approach for assessing inflammatory and oxidative responses under diabetic-like conditions [30, 31].

In the present study, an integrative strategy was used to investigate the effects of α-MG on inflammatory and oxidative responses in high-glucose-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages [31, 32]. First, putative targets of α-MG were identified through network pharmacology and intersected with diabetic wound-associated genes. Protein-protein interaction and enrichment analyses were then performed to explore potential hub targets and related biological pathways, with particular attention to inflammation-related pathways predicted by the computational analyses, including TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways. Next, molecular docking was performed to evaluate the binding affinity between α-MG and selected hub targets, and a 110 ns MD simulation of the α-MG-TNF complex was conducted to assess its dynamic stability. Finally, the computational predictions were further examined in high-glucose-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages, with mannitol included as an osmotic control. Inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress markers, PTGS2/COX-2 expression, and NF-κB activation were evaluated to provide experimental evidence for the potential modulation of inflammatory and oxidative responses by α-MG.

To the best of our knowledge, relatively few studies have combined network pharmacology, molecular docking, MD simulation, osmotic control-based in vitro assessment, and inflammatory and oxidative stress-related assays to systematically investigate α-MG under high-glucose-induced macrophage inflammatory conditions [33, 34]. Therefore, the present study aimed to provide an integrative framework for evaluating the effects of α-MG and to offer preliminary mechanistic insights supported by computational prediction and in vitro evidence in diabetic-like inflammatory microenvironments. Materials and methods

Materials and reagents

The reagents and compounds used in this study were as follows: α-mangostin (purity ≥ 98%) was purchased from Solarbio Life Science Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). RAW264.7 macrophages were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from APExBIO (North Carolina, USA); mouse enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits for IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-10, and TGF-β1 were purchased from Lianke Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Hangzhou, China). The mouse PTGS2/COX-2 ELISA kit was purchased from Elabscience Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). TRIzol reagent was obtained from Invitrogen (California, USA), and the PrimeScript RT reagent kit and SYBR Premix Ex Taq II were purchased from TaKaRa (Osaka, Japan). High-glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), low-glucose DMEM, fetal bovine serum (FBS), and penicillin-streptomycin solution were obtained from Gibco (California, USA). The reduced glutathione (GSH) assay kit was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The lipid peroxidation malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit, superoxide dismutase (SOD) assay kit, and mannitol were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Rabbit primary antibodies against phospho-NF-κB p65, total NF-κB p65, and GAPDH, as well as horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody, were purchased from Proteintech Group, Inc. (Wuhan, China).

Network pharmacology analysis

Retrieval of the chemical structure of α-MG

The chemical structure and SMILES notation for α-MG were retrieved from the PubChem database (https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) by using its CAS number. The 2D structure of α-MG was downloaded in SDF format for subsequent target prediction.

Target prediction for α-MG

To predict the potential targets of α-MG, the term “α-mangostin” was searched in the Traditional Chinese Medicine Systems Pharmacology (TCMSP) database (https://www.91tcmsp.com/#/database), with the search limited to the “Chemical name” field. In addition, the SDF-format structure of α-MG was uploaded to the SwissTargetPrediction database (http://old.swisstargetprediction.ch/index.php), and the species was set to “Homo sapiens” to retrieve predicted targets with a probability greater than 0. The Comparative Toxicogenomics Database (CTD) (https://ctdbase.org/) was also queried using “α-mangostin” and filtered by “Chemicals” to obtain additional relevant targets. Targets retrieved from these databases were merged, and duplicate entries were removed. All target names were standardized using the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/) to generate a comprehensive and non-redundant target dataset for α-MG [35–39].

Identification of diabetic wound-associated inflammatory targets

To identify disease-related targets associated with diabetic wound-associated inflammatory conditions, the terms “diabetic wound” and “diabetic ulcer” were used to query the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/), DisGeNET database (https://www.disgenet.com/), and Online Mendelian Inheritance in Man (OMIM) database (https://www.omim.org/). The disease-related targets obtained from these sources were compiled and integrated, with duplicate entries removed, to generate a refined list of diabetic wound-associated inflammatory targets [40–43].

Construction and visualization of the PPI network

A Venn diagram was generated using the Venny 2.1 tool (https://bioinfogp.cnb.csic.es/tools/venny/) to visualize the overlapping targets between the drug-target predictions and the disease-related targets [28, 44, 45]. The overlapping targets were then uploaded to the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/), with the species set to “Homo sapiens” and the minimum required interaction score set to 0.40 (medium confidence). The resulting protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was exported in TSV format and visualized using Cytoscape software (version 3.10.0). Nodes with higher degree values were shown in darker shades. Topological analysis was performed using the CentiScaPe 2.2 plugin and Cytoscape’s “Network Analyzer” tool. Degree centrality (DC), betweenness centrality (BC), and closeness centrality (CC) were calculated to characterize network topology. In addition, the CytoHubba plugin was used to rank candidate hub genes, and the top 10 genes were identified according to degree ranking [46–48].

Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis

The overlapping genes were uploaded to the Metascape database (https://metascape.org/gp/index.html#/main/step1) for Gene Ontology (GO) functional annotation and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis [49–51]. The following analysis parameters were used: species = “Homo sapiens”, minimum overlap = 3, p-value cutoff = 0.01, and minimum enrichment factor = 1.5. The enriched biological processes and signaling pathways were visualized using bar charts and bubble plots.

Molecular Docking of α-MG with Core Inflammatory Targets

To assess binding interactions between α-MG and the top 10 hub targets (TNF, AKT1, TP53, PTGS2, CASP3, BCL2, CTNNB1, NFKB1, ESR1, and PARP1), molecular docking was performed using the CB-Dock2 online platform [52–54]. Crystal structures of the target proteins were retrieved from the RCSB Protein Data Bank. Water molecules and heteroatoms were removed during preprocessing. The 3D structure of α-MG was obtained from PubChem and uploaded in MOL2 format. CB-Dock2 was used for blind docking and automatic cavity identification. Vina scores and predicted binding poses were recorded. Ligand–receptor interactions were visualized using PyMOL software (version 3.0.3) and BIOVIA Discovery Studio Visualizer (version 4.5) [55].

Molecular dynamics simulations

To further evaluate the dynamic stability of the predicted ligand–target interactions, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed for selected α-MG–target complexes using YASARA Dynamics (version 10.3.16). The α-MG–TNF complex was subjected to a 110 ns molecular dynamics (MD) simulation. TNF was selected for the longer simulation because it was identified as a central inflammation-related hub target in the PPI network and was linked to the computationally enriched TNF signaling pathway, suggesting its potential relevance in the inflammatory response context. In addition, because PTGS2 showed the strongest docking affinity among the examined hub targets and is an important inflammation-related enzyme, the α-MG–PTGS2 complex was also subjected to a 50 ns MD simulation as a supplementary stability assessment. For each complex, the protein–ligand system was solvated in a TIP3P water box under periodic boundary conditions, neutralized with counter-ions, and supplemented with 0.9% NaCl. The AMBER14 force field was applied to the protein, and the GAFF2 force field was used for the ligand. The simulations were conducted under the NPT ensemble at 298 K and 1 atm pressure, with trajectory snapshots recorded every 100 ps [38, 56–58]. Post-simulation analyses included root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) to assess overall structural stability, root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) to evaluate local residue flexibility, and interaction energy profiles to describe ligand–protein interaction changes during the simulation. Considering the exploratory nature of the present computational analysis, these MD simulations were used to provide preliminary support for complex stability, while longer simulations, such as 200–300 ns simulations, may be required for more comprehensive stability assessment, as suggested in previous MD-based studies [59].

Cell culture and treatments

RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. Cells were divided into four groups: normal glucose group (NG), mannitol osmotic control group (Mannitol), high glucose group (HG), and high glucose plus α-MG group (HG + α-MG).

Based on the high-glucose model optimization results, 30 mM D-glucose stimulation for 48 h was used to establish the high-glucose-induced inflammatory model. The NG group was cultured with 5.5 mM D-glucose for 48 h. The Mannitol group was cultured with 5.5 mM D-glucose plus 24.5 mM mannitol for 48 h. The HG group was stimulated with 30 mM D-glucose for 48 h. For the HG + α-MG group, RAW264.7 macrophages were first stimulated with 30 mM D-glucose for 24 h and then treated with 10 µM α-MG for another 24 h in the continued presence of 30 mM D-glucose. During the second 24 h period, the NG, Mannitol, and HG groups were maintained under their corresponding culture conditions with vehicle treatment. The mannitol group was included to distinguish glucose-associated inflammatory and oxidative responses from osmotic stress-related effects. The final concentration of DMSO was kept below 0.1% in all groups.

Cell viability assay

To evaluate the cytotoxicity of α-MG, a CCK-8 assay was performed. RAW264.7 macrophages were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well and treated with α-MG at concentrations of 0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20, 40, and 80 µM for 24 h. After treatment, 10 µL of CCK-8 reagent was added to each well, followed by incubation for 2 h. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. Based on the cell viability results, 10 µM α-MG was selected for subsequent experiments.

qRT-PCR and ELISA analysis of inflammatory mediators

After the indicated treatment period, total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent, and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit. qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Bio-Rad, USA), with β-actin used as the internal reference gene. The expression levels of PTGS2, TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-10, and TGF-β1 were measured, and relative mRNA expression was calculated using the 2⁻ΔΔCt method. The primer sequences are listed in Table 1.

For cytokine analysis, culture supernatants were collected after treatment, centrifuged to remove cell debris, and analyzed using mouse ELISA kits specific for TNF-α, IL-6, IL-1β, IL-10, and TGF-β1 according to the manufacturers’ instructions. To further evaluate the PTGS2/COX-2-related inflammatory response, cells were harvested and lysed after treatment, and PTGS2/COX-2 levels in cell lysates were measured using a mouse PTGS2/COX-2 ELISA kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Measurement of oxidative stress markers

To evaluate oxidative stress status, GSH levels, MDA levels, and SOD activity were measured in RAW264.7 macrophages after treatment using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Cells were harvested and lysed as required for each assay. GSH was measured using a reduced glutathione assay kit. MDA levels were measured as an indicator of lipid peroxidation, and the absorbance was recorded at 532 nm. SOD activity was assessed using a colorimetric assay, and the absorbance was recorded at 450 nm. The results were normalized to total protein concentration where applicable.

Western blot analysis

Western blot analysis was performed to evaluate NF-κB p65 activation. After treatment, RAW264.7 macrophages were washed with cold PBS and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. Protein concentrations were determined using a BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein were separated by SDS-PAGE and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with rabbit primary antibodies against phospho-NF-κB p65 (1:1000), total NF-κB p65 (1:1000), and GAPDH (1:2000). After washing, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:5000). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents and quantified using ImageJ software. NF-κB p65 activation was expressed as the ratio of phospho-NF-κB p65 to total NF-κB p65, with GAPDH used as the loading control.

10 statistical analysis

Data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism 10.4.2 software (GraphPad Software, California, USA). Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) from at least three independent experiments. Exact p-values for all statistical comparisons are provided in Supplementary Table S1, and statistical significance was defined as ns, p ≥ 0.05; *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001; and ****, p < 0.0001.

Results

Identification of potential targets by network pharmacology

The predicted targets of α-mangostin (C24H26O6; Fig. 2a) were collected from the SwissTargetPrediction, TCMSP, and CTD databases, with the analysis restricted to human targets. A total of 177 targets were initially obtained, and 63 unique targets remained after duplicate removal. A drug–target network was subsequently constructed to visualize the relationship between α-MG and its predicted targets (Fig. 2c). To identify disease-related targets, the terms “diabetic wound” and “diabetic ulcer” were searched in the GeneCards, OMIM, and DisGeNET databases. After screening and duplicate removal, 704 unique diabetic wound-associated targets were identified.

Venn analysis revealed 24 overlapping targets between α-mangostin-related targets and diabetic wound-associated genes, which were considered candidate targets potentially relevant to diabetic wound-associated inflammatory conditions (Fig. 2b). A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of these overlapping targets was then constructed using STRING and visualized in Cytoscape. The network consisted of 24 nodes and 207 edges (Fig. 2d and e). Topological analysis using CytoHubba identified the top 10 hub genes based on degree centrality, namely TNF, AKT1, TP53, PTGS2, CASP3, BCL2, CTNNB1, NFKB1, ESR1, and PARP1 (Fig. 2f).

GO enrichment analysis indicated that these overlapping targets were mainly associated with inflammatory responses, apoptotic signaling, oxidative stress-related responses, and macrophage activation-related biological processes (Fig. 3b). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis further showed that the overlapping targets were significantly enriched in several inflammation- and immune-related pathways, including the TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways (Figs. 3a and S1). These computational results suggest that TNF- and IL-17-related signaling pathways were identified as bioinformatics-derived predictions and should not be interpreted as experimentally validated mechanisms. These computational findings generated hypothesis-level mechanistic clues for subsequent molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and in vitro assessment of selected inflammatory and oxidative-stress-related markers.

Molecular docking results of α-MG with core targets

Molecular docking was performed using the CB-Dock2 platform to evaluate the binding affinities of α-MG toward the 10 hub targets identified by network pharmacology, including TNF, AKT1, TP53, PTGS2, CASP3, BCL2, CTNNB1, NFKB1, ESR1, and PARP1. α-MG was used as the ligand, and the corresponding target proteins were used as receptors. The PDB IDs of the selected targets were as follows: TNF (7KP9), AKT1 (7NH5), TP53 (6VA5), PTGS2 (5F19), CASP3 (6BPO), BCL2 (9CPE), CTNNB1 (7AFW), NFKB1 (8TQD), ESR1 (1SJ0) and PARP1 (7KKP).

Binding affinity was evaluated according to the Vina score, with lower binding energy indicating stronger predicted interaction. In the present study, α-MG showed favorable binding affinities toward all selected hub targets. The strongest predicted interaction was observed with PTGS2 (-10.0 kcal/mol), followed by AKT1 (-9.7 kcal/mol), TNF (-9.6 kcal/mol), and PARP1 (-9.5 kcal/mol). These docking results represent computational predictions of ligand–target binding rather than experimentally validated interactions. The remaining targets also showed acceptable binding affinities, including ESR1 (-7.1 kcal/mol), NFKB1 (-7.0 kcal/mol), BCL2 (-6.7 kcal/mol), TP53 (-6.5 kcal/mol), CTNNB1 (-6.5 kcal/mol), and CASP3 (-6.0 kcal/mol) (Figs. 4a and S2).

TNF was selected for molecular dynamics simulation due to its central position in the PPI network and its association with the computationally predicted TNF signaling pathway. PTGS2 was additionally selected due to its strongest docking score and biological relevance as an inflammation-related enzyme. Docking poses were visualized using Discovery Studio to illustrate predicted interaction patterns.

Molecular dynamics simulation results

To further evaluate the dynamic stability of the predicted ligand–target interaction, MD simulations were performed for the α-MG–TNF and α-MG–PTGS2 complexes. Considering the central role of TNF in the PPI network and its relevance to the computationally enriched TNF signaling pathway, the α-MG–TNF complex was subjected to an extended 110 ns MD simulation. Because PTGS2 showed the strongest docking affinity among the examined targets, the α-MG–PTGS2 complex was additionally subjected to a 50 ns MD simulation as a supplementary stability assessment.

For the α-MG–TNF complex, root-mean-square deviation (RMSD) analysis was performed to evaluate the overall conformational stability during the 110 ns simulation. The RMSD trajectory showed that the complex gradually reached a relatively stable state after approximately 20 ns, with Cα RMSD values mainly maintained within approximately 0.17–0.38 nm during the subsequent simulation period (Fig. 4b). This result indicated that the α-MG–TNF complex maintained overall structural stability during the extended MD simulation at the computational level.

Root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) analysis was used to assess the flexibility of individual residues in TNF. Most residues showed relatively low fluctuation values, whereas higher RMSF values were mainly observed in localized flexible regions, including residues 5–8, 21–23, 87–88, and 106–107. These regions were likely associated with terminal or loop flexibility, while the remaining regions showed lower residue-level fluctuations, suggesting that the overall receptor structure remained stable in the ligand-bound state (Fig. 4b). The energy profile further suggested the overall stability of the α-MG–TNF complex, with the average energy maintained at approximately − 9.62 kcal/mol during the simulation period (Fig. 4b).

For the α-MG–PTGS2 complex, the 50 ns MD simulation was performed to provide supplementary support for the docking result. RMSD analysis showed that the α-MG–PTGS2 complex remained relatively stable after the initial equilibration period, with Cα RMSD values mainly maintained within approximately 0.13–0.25 nm after approximately 10 ns (Fig. 4d). This result indicated good conformational stability of the α-MG–PTGS2 complex during the supplementary simulation at the in silico level.

RMSF analysis showed that most PTGS2 residues exhibited limited flexibility, whereas relatively higher fluctuations were mainly observed in localized flexible regions, including residues 33, 51–53, and 136–143. The energy profile of the α-MG–PTGS2 complex remained relatively stable during the simulation period, with an average value of approximately 38.34 kcal/mol (Fig. 4d). Together with the low RMSD fluctuation range, these results provided supplementary computational support for the stability of the α-MG–PTGS2 complex.

Collectively, these MD simulation results provided additional support for the molecular docking findings. The extended 110 ns simulation supported the dynamic stability of the α-MG–TNF complex, while the supplementary 50 ns simulation provided preliminary evidence for the stability of the α-MG–PTGS2 complex. Together with the subsequent in vitro assessment of inflammatory mediators, PTGS2/COX-2 levels, oxidative stress markers, and NF-κB p65 activation, these findings suggest a potential association between α-MG and inflammation-related targets under high-glucose-induced macrophage inflammatory conditions, while not directly validating complete TNF- or IL-17-related signaling pathways.

Cytotoxicity of α-MG in RAW264.7 cells

The cytotoxicity of α-MG in RAW264.7 macrophages was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. As shown in Fig. 5a, although statistically significant changes in cell viability were detected at some concentrations, cell viability remained above 90% after treatment with α-MG at concentrations up to 10 µM for 24 h, suggesting no biologically substantial cytotoxicity within this concentration range. In contrast, higher concentrations of α-MG, particularly 20, 40, and 80 µM, caused a more pronounced reduction in cell viability. Based on these results, 10 µM α-MG was selected for subsequent in vitro experiments.

Establishment of a high-glucose-induced inflammatory model in RAW264.7 macrophages

To establish an in vitro macrophage inflammatory model under diabetic-like conditions, RAW264.7 macrophages were exposed to different glucose concentrations and treatment durations. CCK-8 assay showed that low-to-moderate glucose concentrations did not markedly impair RAW264.7 cell viability at 24 h, 48 h, or 72 h, whereas higher glucose concentrations, particularly 40–80 mM, significantly reduced cell viability. At 72 h, 30 mM glucose also showed a statistically significant effect on cell viability. Considering both inflammatory cytokine induction and acceptable cell viability, 30 mM glucose stimulation for 48 h was selected for subsequent experiments. (Fig. 5b).

The inflammatory response induced by different glucose concentrations was further evaluated by ELISA. Compared with the 10 mM glucose group, the levels of IL-6 and TNF-α were significantly increased after exposure to 20, 30, 40, 60, and 80 mM glucose, with the most pronounced increase observed in the 30 mM glucose group (Figs. 5c and d). These results indicated that high-glucose stimulation was associated with increased pro-inflammatory cytokine production in RAW264.7 macrophages. Therefore, 30 mM glucose was selected as the optimal concentration for subsequent high-glucose stimulation experiments.

To further determine the appropriate stimulation duration, RAW264.7 macrophages were exposed to 30 mM glucose for different time periods. ELISA results showed that, compared with the 12 h time point, IL-6 and TNF-α levels were significantly increased at 36, 48, and 60 h, with the highest levels observed at 48 h (Fig. 5e and f). Accordingly, treatment with 30 mM glucose for 48 h was selected to induce a high-glucose-associated inflammatory state in RAW264.7 macrophages.

α-MG attenuates high-glucose-induced inflammatory cytokine expression in RAW264.7 macrophages

To evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of α-MG under high-glucose conditions, RAW264.7 macrophages were divided into the NG, Mannitol, HG, and HG + α-MG groups. Based on the high-glucose model optimization results, cells in the HG and HG + α-MG groups were exposed to 30 mM D-glucose for a total of 48 h. In the HG + α-MG group, cells were first stimulated with 30 mM D-glucose for 24 h and then treated with 10 µM α-MG for another 24 h in the continued presence of 30 mM D-glucose. The NG, Mannitol, and HG groups were maintained under their corresponding culture conditions with vehicle treatment during the same period.

α-MG attenuates high-glucose-induced inflammatory gene expression in RAW264.7 macrophages

To evaluate the anti-inflammatory effects of α-MG under high-glucose conditions, RAW264.7 macrophages were divided into the NG, HG, and HG + α-MG groups. In addition, a mannitol osmotic control group was included to distinguish glucose-associated inflammatory responses from osmotic stress-related effects.

As shown in Figs. 6a–e, high-glucose stimulation significantly increased the mRNA expression levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β compared with the NG group. In contrast, the mRNA expression levels of the anti-inflammatory mediators TGF-β1 and IL-10 were significantly decreased in the HG group. Compared with the HG group, α-MG treatment significantly downregulated the mRNA expression levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, while increasing the expression levels of TGF-β1 and IL-10.

Because PTGS2 was identified as an important inflammation-related target in the network pharmacology and molecular docking analyses, PTGS2 mRNA expression was further evaluated. As shown in Fig. 6f, high-glucose stimulation markedly increased PTGS2 mRNA expression compared with the NG group, whereas the mannitol group did not show a significant increase. Compared with the HG group, α-MG treatment significantly reduced PTGS2 mRNA expression.

To further confirm that the inflammatory response was mainly associated with high-glucose stimulation rather than osmotic stress, the mRNA expression levels of IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 were compared among the NG, Mannitol, and HG groups. As shown in Figs. 6g–i, mannitol treatment did not significantly increase the expression of these pro-inflammatory genes compared with the NG group, whereas high-glucose stimulation significantly upregulated IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 expression. These findings indicate that high-glucose-induced inflammatory gene activation in RAW264.7 macrophages was not primarily caused by osmotic stress.

Collectively, these results suggest that α-MG attenuated high-glucose-induced inflammatory gene expression in RAW264.7 macrophages, as reflected by reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine expression, restored anti-inflammatory mediator expression, and decreased PTGS2 expression. These findings provide mRNA-level evidence supporting the anti-inflammatory effect of α-MG under diabetic-like inflammatory conditions.

Protein-level assessment of high-glucose-induced inflammatory activation and the anti-inflammatory effect of α-MG

To further assess inflammatory activation at the protein level, ELISA and Western blot analyses were performed. First, the mannitol osmotic control was used to determine whether high-glucose-induced inflammatory activation was mainly attributable to glucose stimulation rather than osmotic stress. Compared with the NG group, high-glucose stimulation significantly increased the secretion levels of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α. In contrast, the Mannitol group did not show comparable increases in these inflammatory cytokines (Figs. 7c–e). These results suggest that the enhanced secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines was mainly associated with high-glucose stimulation rather than osmotic stress.

The effects of α-MG on inflammatory cytokine secretion were then evaluated. Compared with the NG group, high-glucose stimulation significantly decreased the levels of the anti-inflammatory mediators TGF-β1 and IL-10, while increasing the secretion levels of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β. Compared with the HG group, α-MG treatment significantly increased TGF-β1 and IL-10 levels and reduced the secretion of IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-1β (Figs. 7f–j). These results were consistent with the qRT-PCR findings and further supported the anti-inflammatory effect of α-MG at the cytokine secretion level.

Because PTGS2 showed the strongest predicted binding affinity with α-MG in the molecular docking analysis, PTGS2/COX-2 protein levels were further measured by ELISA. Compared with the NG group, high-glucose stimulation markedly increased PTGS2/COX-2 levels, whereas the Mannitol group did not show a significant increase. Compared with the HG group, α-MG treatment significantly reduced PTGS2/COX-2 levels (Fig. 7k), suggesting that α-MG may suppress high-glucose-induced PTGS2/COX-2 upregulation.

To provide additional selected inflammatory marker-level evidence, NF-κB p65 activation was examined by Western blot analysis. Compared with the NG group, high-glucose stimulation increased the phosphorylation level of NF-κB p65, as reflected by an elevated ratio of phospho-NF-κB p65 to total NF-κB p65. In contrast, the Mannitol group did not show a comparable increase. Compared with the HG group, α-MG treatment reduced the high-glucose-induced increase in NF-κB p65 phosphorylation (Figs. 7a and b).

Collectively, these results indicate that high-glucose-induced inflammatory activation in RAW264.7 macrophages was not primarily caused by osmotic stress. α-MG attenuated high-glucose-induced inflammatory responses by reducing pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion, partially restoring anti-inflammatory mediator levels, decreasing PTGS2/COX-2 expression, and suppressing NF-κB p65 phosphorylation. These findings provide protein-level experimental evidence supporting the anti-inflammatory effect of α-MG under high-glucose conditions; however, they do not constitute direct validation of the complete TNF or IL-17 signaling pathways.

α-MG alleviates high-glucose-induced oxidative stress in RAW264.7 macrophages

Oxidative stress markers were measured to evaluate the antioxidant effect of α-MG under high-glucose conditions. Compared with the NG group, high-glucose stimulation significantly decreased GSH levels and SOD activity, while increasing MDA levels, indicating enhanced oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation in RAW264.7 macrophages. The Mannitol group did not induce comparable changes in these oxidative stress markers, suggesting that the oxidative stress response was mainly associated with high-glucose stimulation rather than osmotic stress.

Compared with the HG group, α-MG treatment significantly increased GSH levels and SOD activity and decreased MDA levels (Figs. 8a–c). These results suggest that α-MG alleviated high-glucose-induced oxidative stress in RAW264.7 macrophages. Together with the inflammatory cytokine, PTGS2/COX-2, and NF-κB p65 phosphorylation results, these findings provide additional experimental support for the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of α-MG under diabetic-like inflammatory conditions.

Discussion

This study used an integrative strategy combining network pharmacology, molecular docking, molecular dynamics simulation, and in vitro assessment to investigate the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects of α-MG under high-glucose-induced macrophage inflammatory conditions [33, 60, 61]. The computational analysis identified multiple inflammation-related candidate targets of α-MG, including TNF, AKT1, PTGS2, and PARP1. Molecular docking suggested favorable predicted interactions between α-MG and several hub targets, while molecular dynamics simulations provided computational support for the stability of selected α-MG–target complexes. In vitro experiments showed that α-MG attenuated high-glucose-induced inflammatory responses in RAW264.7 macrophages, as evidenced by reduced levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, decreased PTGS2/COX-2 expression, reduced NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, and alleviated oxidative stress. These findings provide experimental support for the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant potential of α-MG under diabetic-like inflammatory conditions, while the mechanistic interpretation remains preliminary and requires further validation in wound-healing-related models.

Persistent macrophage-mediated inflammation is an important feature of the diabetic wound-associated microenvironment. Under hyperglycemic conditions, macrophages can produce excessive pro-inflammatory cytokines, which may further amplify local inflammatory responses and impair tissue repair. In the present study, high-glucose stimulation increased the expression and secretion of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in RAW264.7 macrophages, indicating successful establishment of a high-glucose-induced inflammatory state. α-MG treatment significantly reduced these pro-inflammatory mediators and partially restored the anti-inflammatory mediators IL-10 and TGF-β1. These results are consistent with previous studies reporting the anti-inflammatory activity of α-MG in different experimental models [17, 62–64]. However, the present findings should be interpreted within the scope of a macrophage-based high-glucose model rather than as direct evidence of diabetic wound healing efficacy.

The inclusion of mannitol as an osmotic control further strengthened the interpretation of the high-glucose model. High glucose, but not mannitol, induced marked increases in inflammatory cytokine expression, PTGS2/COX-2 levels, MDA levels, and NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, together with reduced GSH levels and SOD activity. These observations suggest that the inflammatory and oxidative responses observed in RAW264.7 macrophages were mainly associated with glucose stimulation rather than osmotic stress alone. This additional control is important because high-glucose culture conditions may introduce osmotic effects that could otherwise confound the interpretation of macrophage activation.

Network pharmacology and enrichment analyses suggested that the overlapping targets of α-MG and diabetic wound-associated genes were mainly enriched in inflammation- and immune-related pathways, including TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways. These findings provided a computational basis for exploring the potential anti-inflammatory mechanisms of α-MG. However, enrichment analysis alone cannot establish direct pathway regulation. Therefore, the present study further evaluated inflammatory cytokines, PTGS2/COX-2, NF-κB p65 activation, and oxidative stress-related markers in vitro. The results support the possibility that α-MG affects inflammatory responses associated with these predicted pathways, but they do not fully demonstrate direct regulation of the complete TNF or IL-17 signaling cascades.

Molecular docking showed that α-MG had favorable predicted binding affinities toward several hub targets, with PTGS2 showing the strongest predicted binding affinity, followed by AKT1, TNF, and PARP1. In response to the importance of both target centrality and docking affinity, two representative targets were further evaluated by molecular dynamics simulation. TNF was subjected to an extended 110 ns simulation because it was identified as a central hub target and is closely associated with the computationally enriched TNF signaling pathway. PTGS2 was also evaluated by a supplementary 50 ns simulation because it exhibited the strongest docking affinity among the examined targets. The simulation results provided additional computational support for the predicted stability of the α-MG–TNF and α-MG–PTGS2 complexes. Nevertheless, docking and molecular dynamics simulations remain predictive tools, and the ligand-target interactions require further experimental confirmation using direct binding assays or target-specific functional experiments.

TNF-related inflammatory responses may be relevant to the biological effects observed in this study. TNF was identified as a core hub gene in the PPI network and showed favorable predicted docking affinity with α-MG. The extended molecular dynamics simulation further suggested computational stability of the α-MG–TNF complex. Consistently, α-MG reduced TNF-α expression at both the mRNA and protein levels in high-glucose-stimulated RAW264.7 macrophages. These findings suggest that TNF-related inflammatory responses may be involved in the anti-inflammatory effects of α-MG. However, because the present study did not perform TNF-specific inhibition, knockdown, or rescue experiments, the role of TNF should be considered as a supported mechanistic hypothesis rather than a definitively proven direct target.

PTGS2/COX-2 was another important target highlighted by the present study. PTGS2 showed the strongest predicted docking affinity with α-MG, and the supplementary molecular dynamics simulation provided preliminary computational support for the stability of the α-MG–PTGS2 interaction. In vitro assessment further showed that high-glucose stimulation increased PTGS2 mRNA expression and PTGS2/COX-2 protein levels, whereas α-MG treatment significantly reduced both markers. These results provide experimental support for the involvement of PTGS2/COX-2-related inflammatory responses in the effects of α-MG. Given the role of COX-2 in inflammatory mediator production, the reduction of PTGS2/COX-2 may partly contribute to the decreased inflammatory cytokine levels observed after α-MG treatment.

NF-κB is a key transcriptional regulator of inflammatory responses and is closely related to the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. In the present study, high-glucose stimulation increased NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, while α-MG reduced the ratio of phospho-NF-κB p65 to total NF-κB p65. This provides protein-level evidence that α-MG may suppress high-glucose-induced inflammatory activation at least partly in association with reduced NF-κB p65 activation. This result strengthens the biological relevance of the cytokine findings and provides selected marker-level evidence consistent with inflammatory modulation. However, the present data do not prove that α-MG directly regulates the entire TNF or IL-17 signaling pathways. Additional pathway-specific validation, such as analysis of upstream receptors, downstream MAPKs, AKT, or IL-17-related signaling components, would be needed to clarify the detailed signaling cascade.

Oxidative stress is closely associated with hyperglycemia-induced macrophage inflammation. Excessive oxidative stress can aggravate inflammatory activation and contribute to tissue damage in diabetic-like microenvironments. In this study, high-glucose stimulation decreased GSH levels and SOD activity while increasing MDA levels, indicating impaired antioxidant capacity and enhanced lipid peroxidation. α-MG treatment reversed these changes by increasing GSH and SOD and reducing MDA. These findings suggest that α-MG may exert antioxidant effects in high-glucose-stimulated macrophages. The combined reduction of inflammatory cytokines, PTGS2/COX-2 expression, NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, and oxidative stress markers suggests that α-MG may alleviate high-glucose-induced macrophage activation through coordinated anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects.

Another observation was that α-MG not only reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines but also increased the anti-inflammatory mediators IL-10 and TGF-β1. This suggests that α-MG may help rebalance the inflammatory state of macrophages under high-glucose conditions. However, IL-10 and TGF-β1 alone are insufficient to establish macrophage polarization changes. Since macrophage polarization plays an important role in chronic inflammation and tissue repair, future studies should include M1/M2 polarization markers, such as CD86, iNOS, Arg1, and CD206, to determine whether α-MG directly affects macrophage phenotypic transition under hyperglycemic conditions.

The present study has several limitations. First, although RAW264.7 macrophages provide a useful in vitro model for investigating high-glucose-induced inflammatory activation, this model cannot fully recapitulate the complex cellular and tissue microenvironment of diabetic wounds. No wound-healing assays, fibroblast proliferation or migration assays, keratinocyte migration assays, endothelial angiogenesis assays, or in vivo diabetic wound models were performed. Therefore, the current findings should be interpreted as evidence of anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects under diabetic-like macrophage inflammatory conditions, rather than direct evidence of diabetic wound healing efficacy. Second, although additional validation of PTGS2/COX-2 and NF-κB p65 phosphorylation was performed, the complete TNF and IL-17 signaling pathways were not fully examined. Third, although the 110 ns α-MG–TNF simulation and the supplementary 50 ns α-MG–PTGS2 simulation provided supportive computational evidence for the conformational stability of the predicted complexes, longer MD simulations, such as 200–300 ns simulations, may provide more robust stability assessments, as suggested by previous MD-based studies [59]. Therefore, the present MD results should be interpreted as preliminary and supportive rather than conclusive evidence of direct ligand–target interactions. Direct binding assays and longer MD simulations are still needed to confirm the predicted interactions between α-MG and key targets. Finally, macrophage polarization and other signaling molecules such as AKT, MAPKs, CASP3, and MMP9 were not systematically evaluated in the present study.

Future studies should validate these findings in wound-healing-related cell models and in vivo diabetic wound models to determine whether the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects observed in macrophages translate into improved wound repair outcomes. Additional mechanistic studies should examine TNF-, IL-17-, NF-κB-, MAPK-, AKT-, and PTGS2/COX-2-related signaling in greater detail. Pathway-specific inhibition, knockdown, rescue experiments, direct ligand–target binding assays, macrophage polarization analysis, and direct ROS detection would further clarify the molecular mechanisms underlying the effects of α-MG under diabetic-like inflammatory conditions.

In conclusion, this study suggests that α-MG attenuates high-glucose-induced inflammatory and oxidative responses in RAW264.7 macrophages. The integrative computational analysis predicted that TNF- and IL-17-related pathways may be associated with these effects, whereas the in vitro results provided supportive evidence only for changes in inflammatory cytokines, PTGS2/COX-2 expression, NF-κB p65 phosphorylation, and oxidative stress markers. These findings provide preliminary evidence for the anti-inflammatory and antioxidant potential of α-MG under diabetic-like inflammatory conditions and support further mechanistic investigation using pathway-specific assays, wound-healing-related cell models, and in vivo diabetic wound models.
