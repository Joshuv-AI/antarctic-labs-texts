Ultrasound-responsive biomimetic nanocarrier triggers spatiotemporal PROTAC release and ROS storm to disrupt TNBC immunosuppression via coordinated apoptosis/ferroptosis/senescence activation
Authors: Zhipeng Liao, Haiqin Liao, Yi Luo, Sihan Chen, Yuanyuan Shen, Ming Zhang, Yan Xu, Long Wang, Chengcheng Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTriple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) poses significant therapeutic challenges due to its aggressive metastasis and immunosuppressive microenvironment. Herein, we developed a tumor-targeting biomimetic nanosystem (MTB@LM) integrating BRD4-targeting PROTAC (dBET6), Mn-TCPP-based sonosensitizers, and a tumor cell membrane-fusogenic liposome hybrid membrane coating. MTB@LM efficiently accumulates in tumors via homologous targeting and bypasses lysosomal degradation through membrane fusion-mediated delivery. Under ultrasound activation, the nanosystem triggers a multimodal cascade: (1) Mn-enhanced sonodynamic therapy (SDT) amplifies ROS to overcome hypoxia/GSH resistance; (2) BRD4 degradation synergizes with SDT to induce apoptosis, while concurrent ferroptosis (via lipid peroxidation) and senescence (via p21/p16 activation) amplify immunogenic cell death (ICD); (3) Senescent tumor cells are eliminated by αPD-L1 to block immune evasion. In orthotopic 4T1 models, MTB@LM + US achieved > 85% tumor suppression and remodeled the immunosuppressive microenvironment by promoting dendritic cell maturation, CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, and M1 macrophage repolarization. Combining MTB@LM with αPD-L1 synergistically inhibited lung metastasis (> 90%) and established durable immune memory against recurrence. Introduction

Breast cancer, the most prevalent malignancy among women globally, imposes an escalating disease burden. Current projections from the World Health Organization indicate a 38% rise in new cases and 68% increase in mortality by 2050, underscoring urgent unmet clinical needs [1, 2]. Therapeutic outcomes vary significantly across subtypes, with triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC)—defined by the absence of estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2 expression—exhibiting heightened metastasis, recurrence, and therapeutic resistance due to its aggressive biology and lack of actionable targets [3–5]. While immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has emerged as a promising strategy for TNBC, leveraging its immunogenic features (elevated tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, mutational burden, and PD-L1 expression), monotherapy efficacy remains constrained by immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) components, necessitating combinatorial approaches [6–8].

Regulated cell death modalities (apoptosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis) and therapy-induced senescence can trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD) via damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) release, potentiating antitumor immunity and ICB sensitivity [9–12]. The ability of ICD to elicit immune responses is generally determined by two key attributes: antigenicity (characterized by T-cell recognizable antigens such as tumor-associated antigens) and adjuvant activity (mediated by ancillary signals arising from tissue damage, including DAMPs) [13, 14]. However, ferroptosis-driven ICD often lacks sufficient neoantigen generation due to rapid progression [15], whereas senescence—despite enhancing antigen presentation (MHC-I) and DAMP release (e.g., HMGB1)—risks tumor dormancy and relapse [16, 17]. Sequential strategies combining senescence induction with senolytic clearance (e.g., anti-PD-L1 targeting senescence-associated PD-L1 upregulation) may mitigate these risks [18].

BRD4 (bromodomain-containing protein 4) is a pivotal epigenetic regulator governing tumor progression through modulation of cell cycle, proliferation, apoptosis and senescence, with particularly pronounced overexpression in TNBC that establishes it as a promising therapeutic target [19, 20]. Emerging evidence reveals BRD4 inhibitors induce multimodal tumor suppression via three distinct mechanisms: (1) apoptosis through downregulating c-Myc and BCL-2 family proteins; (2) ferroptosis via iron accumulation, ROS elevation and suppression of GPX4/SLC7A11/SLC3A2 axis; (3) senescence mediated by p21/p16 upregulation [20–23]. Notably, these inhibitors synergize with immunotherapy by downregulating PD-L1/PD-1 axis and suppressing Treg infiltration [21, 24]. To overcome drug resistance challenges, proteolysis-targeting chimeras (PROTACs) have emerged as novel therapeutic agents that catalytically degrade BRD4 through ubiquitin-proteasome system, bypassing traditional inhibition limitations [25]. Recent advances demonstrate nanotechnology-enabled PROTAC delivery systems (e.g., dBET6-loaded nanoplatforms) not only suppress c-Myc/PD-L1 but also polarize tumor-associated macrophages to M1 phenotype, synergizing with photodynamic therapy to remodel tumor microenvironment and potentiate antitumor immunity [26]. However, PROTACs face pharmacological challenges (e.g., poor solubility, low bioavailability) that nanocarriers may resolve [27, 28].

Sonodynamic therapy (SDT), a non-invasive and precisely controllable tumor treatment strategy, eliminates malignant cells by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) through ultrasound-activated sonosensitizers, while sparing healthy tissues and inducing ICD and ferroptosis to transform immunosuppressive “cold” tumors into immunoreactive “hot” microenvironments [29, 30]. In addition to activating sonosensitizers, ultrasound itself has also been explored for site-targeted drug delivery and controlled cargo release [31]. Emerging evidence suggests ROS may also trigger tumor cell senescence via oxidative DNA damage and p53/p21-mediated G1 arrest, though this remains underexplored in SDT [32, 33]. However, hypoxia and elevated glutathione (GSH) in the TME often compromise SDT efficacy by limiting ROS generation [34]. Notably, manganese-porphyrin frameworks (Mn-TCPP) demonstrate redox-cycling capabilities critical for TME modulation: mixed-valence Mn species (Mn³⁺/Mn⁴⁺) simultaneously catalyze H₂O₂ decomposition to generate oxygen, deplete GSH reserves via redox reactions, and release porphyrin sonosensitizers. Subsequent Mn²⁺ ions participate in Fenton-like reactions to produce hydroxyl radicals while activating the STING pathway for immune potentiation [35–37]. This self-sustaining redox cycle enables continuous oxygen supply, ROS amplification, and GSH depletion, positioning Mn-TCPP-based MOFs as ideal platforms for enhanced SDT. Building on these advances, we propose a synergistic strategy employing Mn-TCPP-based MOF nanocarriers co-loaded with a BRD4-targeting PROTAC degrader to drive SDT-mediated apoptosis, ferroptosis, and senescence, thereby amplifying ICD-driven immune activation and remodeling the immunosuppressive TME.

Our group’s prior preclinical studies demonstrated the therapeutic benefits of combining SDT with immunotherapy, while accumulating critical experience in the design and application of cell membrane-biomimetic nanodelivery systems [38–41]. Although these biomimetic nanocarriers exhibit favorable delivery properties, lysosomal degradation remains a persistent challenge for nanomedicine. Recent advances suggest that membrane-fusogenic liposomes can bypass lysosomal entrapment through a biomimetic fusion process involving three sequential steps: tight membrane contact, lipid mixing, and cytosolic content transfer. For instance, Kong et al. (2024) developed a protein corona-resistant membrane-fusogenic liposome (MFlip), which achieves stable and efficient intracellular delivery via membrane fusion in vivo, significantly reducing lysosomal drug degradation [42].

Therefore, this study designed a Mn-TCPP-based MOF nanocarrier to encapsulate the BRD4-targeting PROTAC degrader dBET6 (MTB). The biomimetic nanosystem MTB@LM was constructed through hybridization between tumor cell membranes (4T1m) and membrane-fusogenic liposomes (MFlip), resulting in a fused membrane (LM) coating. This system leverages the homologous targeting capability of tumor cell membranes for precise delivery and utilizes membrane fusion-mediated endocytosis via MFlip to minimize lysosomal drug degradation. Subsequently, manganese ions in the MOF nanocarrier react with intracellular GSH, triggering dBET6 release. The multimodal antitumor effects are achieved through a sequential “Drug Delivery-Immune Activation-Immune Reinforcement” therapeutic cascade: (1) Mn-TCPP-enhanced SDT effects synergize with dBET6-mediated BRD4 degradation to eliminate the majority of tumor cells via apoptosis; (2) concurrently, SDT-induced ferroptosis and senescence amplify ICD-driven antitumor immunity; (3) anti-PD-L1 antibody (αPD-L1) administration selectively targets and clears residual senescent cells with immune evasion potential. This sophisticated combinatorial approach is expected to substantially enhance therapeutic outcomes for TNBC while mitigating metastasis and recurrence risks (Scheme 1).

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of MTB@LM

The preparation of MTB@LM nanoparticles is illustrated in Scheme 1. First, Mn-MOF nanocarriers were synthesized via a solvothermal reaction. Subsequently, dBET6 was loaded into the Mn-MOF under continuous stirring at room temperature to form MTB. Finally, MTB@LM was obtained by surface modification with a fused membrane (LM). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed spherical morphologies for both MTB and MTB@LM, with MTB@LM exhibiting a visible membrane coating on its surface (Fig. 1a). Dynamic light scattering (DLS) measurements showed hydrated particle sizes of Mn-MOF, MTB, and MTB@LM as 93.4 ± 1.6 nm, 96.2 ± 0.9 nm, and 112.6 ± 1.0 nm, respectively (Fig. 1b). Zeta potential values were 20.2 ± 0.8 mV, 27.1 ± 0.8 mV, and −14.7 ± 0.5 mV, respectively (Fig. 1c). High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) confirmed the uniform distribution of C, N, O, Zr, Mn, P, and S within MTB@LM (Fig. 1d), with P and S originating from the biomimetic membrane. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) further corroborated the elemental composition (Fig. 1e) and revealed multiple manganese oxidation states: Mn²⁺ (60.6%), Mn³⁺ (28.7%), and Mn⁴⁺ (10.8%) (Fig. 1f and S1a). Powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) confirmed identical crystalline structures for MTB and MTB@LM, with characteristic peaks at 2θ = 7.12° (Figure S1b). Ultraviolet-visible-near-infrared (UV-Vis-NIR) spectroscopy confirmed successful dBET6 loading, with MTB and MTB@LM exhibiting characteristic peaks at 324 nm (dBET6) and 416 nm (TCPP) (Fig. 1g). A linear correlation between absorbance at 324 nm and dBET6 concentration (Figures S2a and S2b) allowed quantification of encapsulation efficiency. The results show that the encapsulation efficiency of dBET6 decreases with increasing drug input (Figure S2c). We selected a 1:0.2 ratio for subsequent experiments, at which the encapsulation efficiency of dBET6 was 66.7 ± 1.2%.

To verify the 4T1m-MFlip fusion, DiO/DiI-labeled 4T1m vesicles were incubated with MFlip at varying molar ratios. Increasing the MFlip concentration led to enhanced DiO fluorescence intensity and reduced DiI fluorescence (Fig. 1h), indicating disruption of Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) due to increased intermolecular distances following fusion. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) confirmed co-localization of DiO (green) and DiI (red) signals in fused membranes, with a Pearson’s coefficient of 0.625 (Fig. 1i). Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and western blot analyses further demonstrated retention of 4T1 membrane proteins (including CD44, CD47, and E-cadherin) in MTB@LM (Fig. 1j and k), which are critical for tumor-targeting capabilities [43].

We investigated the catalase-like activity, SDT performance, Fenton-like reaction, and GSH degradation capability of MTB@LM. To validate the catalase-like activity of MTB@LM, different nanoparticles were added to H2O2 solution, and dissolved oxygen measurements were employed to evaluate O2 generation. The results demonstrated that Mn-MOF, MTB, and MTB@LM all sustainably produced oxygen (Fig. 1l). Subsequently, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy combined with specific spin traps was utilized to detect reactive ROS generation, including singlet oxygen (1O2) and hydroxyl radicals (·OH). DMPO (5,5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide) and TEMP (2,2,6,6-tetramethylpiperidine) were used as trapping agents for ·OH and 1O2, respectively. The results indicated that MTB@LM under ultrasound irradiation generated significant 1O2 and ·OH signals (Fig. 1m and n), and the addition of H2O2 further enhanced ROS production. This phenomenon may be attributed to the catalase-like activity of high-valence Mn species (Mn3+ and Mn4+), which decompose H2O2 to generate O2, thereby promoting 1O2 production, as well as the Mn2+-mediated Fenton-like reaction chain that amplifies ·OH generation. Furthermore, to assess GSH degradation capacity, nanoparticles were incubated with GSH solution, and the remaining GSH concentration in the supernatant was measured after centrifugation. The results revealed that Mn-MOF, MTB, and MTB@LM significantly degraded GSH (Fig. 1o), likely due to the direct oxidation by Mn3+/Mn4+. This GSH depletion capability is particularly critical for enhancing SDT efficacy.

The as-prepared MTB@LM exhibited excellent colloidal stability, with negligible size variation observed in PBS over 10 days (Figure S2d). In vitro release kinetics of dBET6 from MTB@LM demonstrated significantly accelerated release under acidic PBS conditions, which was further amplified in the presence of GSH, achieving a cumulative release rate of 72.1% at 24 h (Fig. 1p). Morphological changes of MTB@LM under different conditions were monitored via TEM. While MTB@LM retained its structural integrity in neutral PBS for 24 h, gradual framework collapse was observed after 2 h incubation in acidic PBS containing GSH (Figure S2e), and near-complete degradation occurred within 24 h (Fig. 1q). These findings confirm the acid/GSH-responsive drug release capability of MTB@LM. Hemolysis assays demonstrated that both MTB and MTB@LM at various concentrations induced hemolysis rates below 3% (Figure S3a, b). Cytotoxicity evaluation in HUVECs treated with MTB@LM (0–100 µg/mL) for 24 h revealed cell viability exceeding 80% across all tested concentrations (Figure S3c). Collectively, these results underscore the favorable biocompatibility of MTB@LM in vitro.

Enhanced cellular uptake efficiency of MTB@LM via membrane fusion

To investigate whether MTB@LM improves intracellular delivery efficiency, CLSM imaging and flow cytometry were employed to evaluate nanoparticle internalization in 4T1 cells. Results demonstrated significantly higher intracellular accumulation of MTB@LM and MTB@4T1m than MTB and MTB@MFlip (Fig. 2a and b), supporting the contribution of 4T1 membrane-derived components to homologous cellular uptake. Lysosomal colocalization was then examined to assess whether incorporation of MFlip could further alter the intracellular trafficking fate after uptake. For this purpose, MTB@4T1m and MTB@LM were directly compared, because both formulations contain 4T1 membrane components and thus provide a more comparable homologous-uptake background for evaluating the additional effect of the fusogenic membrane element. MTB@4T1m exhibited prominent colocalization with lysosomes at both 4 h and 12 h, with Pearson’s coefficients exceeding 0.5 (Fig. 2c). In contrast, MTB@LM showed markedly reduced colocalization, suggesting decreased lysosomal retention after incorporation of the fused membrane.

To further examine the internalization behavior of MTB@LM, inhibitor studies were conducted using chlorpromazine (CPZ) for clathrin-mediated endocytosis, amiloride (AM) for macropinocytosis blockade, and Z-Phe-Phe-OH (ZPO) as a membrane fusion inhibitor [44]. After 4 h co-incubation of 4T1 cells with MTB@LM in the presence of these inhibitors, flow cytometry analysis showed that only ZPO significantly reduced cellular uptake (Fig. 2d and e), supporting the involvement of membrane fusion-related processes in MTB@LM internalization.

Collectively, these results support that LM integrates 4T1m-associated homologous recognition with MFlip-related fusogenic properties, thereby improving intracellular delivery efficiency.

In vitro antitumor efficacy

The antitumor effects of MTB@LM under ultrasound irradiation were initially evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. Results revealed a significant negative correlation between 4T1 cell viability and MTB@LM concentration, with the ultrasound-treated group (MTB@LM + US) exhibiting the most pronounced inhibitory effect (Fig. 3a). At a concentration of 100 µg/mL, cell viability in the MTB@LM + US group dropped to approximately 47.3%.

To elucidate the regulatory mechanism of MTB@LM on the BRD4-mediated apoptotic pathway, western blot analysis was performed. Compared to controls, the MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups significantly downregulated BRD4 and its downstream target c-Myc expression (Fig. 3b and S4). Notably, all three experimental groups demonstrated dual regulation of caspase-3: reduced expression of its inactive precursor (caspase 3) and increased expression of its activated form (cleaved caspase 3). The MTB@LM + US group showed the most pronounced regulatory effect, suggesting that ultrasound irradiation synergistically enhances the suppression of the BRD4 apoptotic pathway.

The ROS generation capacity of MTB@LM-mediated SDT was assessed using the DCF fluorescent probe. While Mn-MOF@LM and MTB@LM alone produced minimal ROS, ultrasound irradiation significantly amplified ROS production, with mean fluorescence intensity increasing by 2.9- and 2.7-fold, respectively (Fig. 3c and d). Additionally, intracellular GSH depletion by MTB@LM was further confirmed (Figure S5), indicating that ROS amplification likely arises from the combined effects of GSH consumption and SDT. Mitochondrial damage induced by ROS was evaluated via the JC-1 mitochondrial membrane potential probe. The MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups exhibited intense green fluorescence, with the MTB@LM + US group showing the most significant JC-1 monomer percentage increase (55.4% versus controls; Fig. 3e and f), confirming severe mitochondrial dysfunction. Fluorescence microscopy revealed pronounced cell death in MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups compared to other treatments (Fig. 3g). Quantitative apoptotic analysis by flow cytometry demonstrated that the MTB@LM + US group induced the highest apoptosis rate (54.7%, Fig. 3h).

These findings collectively support that MTB@LM-mediated treatment exerts substantial cytotoxic effects associated with both BRD4 pathway suppression and ROS/SDT-related oxidative injury, with the strongest effect observed under the combined MTB@LM + US condition.

In vitro antitumor immunity

The elevated GSH levels in the TME suppress ferroptosis by maintaining GPX4 activity. Depletion of GSH disrupts redox homeostasis, thereby promoting ROS-triggered ferroptosis. Our prior experiments confirmed that MTB@LM effectively depletes GSH and triggers nanoparticle degradation. Lipid peroxidation (LPO), a hallmark downstream event of ferroptosis, was evaluated using the BODIPY fluorescent probe. Results demonstrated a marked increase in oxidized BODIPY (green fluorescence) in the MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups, with the most pronounced enhancement observed in the MTB@LM + US group (Fig. 4a and e), indicative of significant LPO accumulation. This finding was corroborated by elevated malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, where the MTB@LM + US group exhibited a 7.0-fold increase in MDA content compared to the control (Fig. 4f). Western blot analysis further revealed downregulation of GPX4 in the MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups, with the most substantial reduction (0.2-fold relative to control) observed in the MTB@LM + US group (Fig. 4b and S6). These data collectively support ferroptosis-associated changes in MTB@LM combined with SDT, which may contribute to ICD-related danger signaling and antitumor immunity.

Cellular senescence was assessed using SA-β-gal staining, where senescent cells exhibiting high β-galactosidase activity stained blue in the presence of X-Gal. Compared to controls, the MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups displayed significantly elevated SA-β-gal expression (Fig. 4c and g). The MTB@LM + US group exhibited the highest proportion of senescent cells (51.9%), alongside a notable reduction in total cell count. Western blot analysis of DNA damage (γ-H2AX) and senescence-associated proteins (p16, p21) confirmed upregulation of these markers in the aforementioned treatment groups, with the most robust increase observed in the MTB@LM + US cohort (Fig. 4d and S6). These findings suggest that BRD4 inhibition or SDT-mediated ROS generation independently induces tumor cell damage and senescence, with synergistic effects upon combination. To evaluate whether senescence enhances tumor immunogenicity, MHC-I expression (critical for ICD amplification) was analyzed by flow cytometry. Results revealed elevated H-2Kb/Db levels (indicative of MHC-I) in the MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups, with the MTB@LM + US group showing the highest mean fluorescence intensity (1.9-fold increase vs. control; Fig. 4l and q).

Having established MTB@LM-induced ferroptosis and senescence, we further quantified ICD-associated DAMPs (CRT, HMGB1, ATP) to assess immune cell recruitment and activation. CLSM revealed substantial CRT translocation to the cell membrane (red fluorescence) in the MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups (Fig. 4h and j), accompanied by reduced intracellular HMGB1 (Fig. 4i and k). Consistently, extracellular HMGB1 (Fig. 4m) and ATP levels (Fig. 4n) were significantly elevated in these groups, peaking in the MTB@LM + US treatment. These results support that MTB@LM combined with SDT enhances ICD-associated responses more effectively than monotherapy, accompanied by concurrent ferroptosis- and senescence-associated changes.

To elucidate the immunostimulatory effects of ICD enhancement, pretreated 4T1 cells were co-cultured with bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) in a Transwell system. Flow cytometry revealed that the MTB@LM + US group induced the highest proportion of mature DCs (CD80+CD86+: 86.6%; Fig. 4r and s), alongside elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α and IFN-γ (Fig. 4o and p). These results collectively support that MTB@LM combined with SDT potentiates antitumor immunity, accompanied by enhanced ICD-associated responses, DC maturation, and subsequent immune activation.

Mechanistically, the advantage of MTB@LM + US lies not merely in the simultaneous induction of apoptosis, ferroptosis, and senescence, but in their functional cooperation within one treatment framework. Apoptosis contributes to rapid tumor cell elimination, whereas ferroptosis further amplifies lipid peroxidation and immunogenic damage, thereby favoring stronger ICD-associated DAMP release. Meanwhile, senescence provides a persistent stress-associated tumor state that may enhance immune visibility, consistent with the increased MHC-I expression observed here. Recent nano-PROTAC studies further support this interpretation, showing that BRD4-targeting nano-PROTACs can amplify therapy-induced damage through targeted BRD4 degradation [45], while other nano-PROTAC platforms can further couple protein degradation with immune remodeling or immune activation [46, 47]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the therapeutic advantage of MTB@LM + US arises from the coordinated integration of cytotoxic elimination, lipid-peroxidation-driven immunogenic amplification, and senescence-associated immune modulation, rather than from any single pathway alone.

In vivo antitumor efficacy

Prior to evaluating antitumor efficacy, the biodistribution profiles of MTB@LM in 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were analyzed via an in vivo imaging system (IVIS). Fluorescence imaging at various time points post-injection and ex vivo organ imaging at 48 h (Fig. 5a) revealed that both MTB and MTB@LM accumulated in tumors in a time-dependent manner, peaking at 24 h followed by gradual reduction. Notably, MTB@LM exhibited significantly higher tumor fluorescence intensity than MTB at 8 h post-injection (Fig. 5b). Ex vivo quantification demonstrated predominant accumulation of MTB in the liver and spleen, whereas MTB@LM displayed enhanced tumor targeting with reduced hepatic and splenic fluorescence (Figure S7a). Dynamic serum fluorescence monitoring further confirmed progressive attenuation of MTB@LM over time (Figure S7b and c). These results indicate that MTB@LM improves tumor targeting while minimizing reticuloendothelial system clearance compared to MTB, providing a foundation for subsequent therapeutic evaluation.

To systematically assess the antitumor efficacy of MTB@LM combined with SDT, a 4T1 breast cancer-bearing mouse model was established with six treatment groups: PBS control, Mn-MOF@LM, MTB@LM, US control, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US (Fig. 5c). Longitudinal tumor volume monitoring (Fig. 5d and e) revealed that MTB@LM + US elicited the most potent tumor suppression, reducing final tumor volume by 85.8% compared to PBS, outperforming MTB@LM monotherapy (57.5% inhibition) and Mn-MOF@LM + US (73.8% inhibition). Photographic documentation of excised tumors and weight measurements (Fig. 5f and g) corroborated these findings. The efficacy differences between combination and single treatments aligned with in vitro results, confirming the synergistic effects of SDT and the MTB@LM nanoformulation in both in vivo and in vitro models. All groups exhibited minimal weight fluctuations during treatment (Fig. 5h), indicating negligible systemic toxicity. Histopathological analysis of major organs (Figure S8a) revealed preserved tissue architecture with no signs of inflammatory infiltration or necrosis. Serum biochemical profiles further validated the biosafety of this nanosystem: hepatic (ALT, AST), renal (Cr, BUN), and cardiac (CK) biomarkers all remained within physiological baseline ranges (Figure S8b-f). These results conclusively demonstrate the safety of MTB@LM at tested doses.

Posttreatment histopathological analysis of tumors (Fig. 5i-m and S9) revealed distinct biological alterations in the MTB@LM + US group. SA-β-gal and Ki67 staining demonstrated not only a 19.9% increase in senescent cells but also a 51.7% reduction in proliferating Ki67+ cells compared to controls. Additionally, a 3.9-fold upregulation of p16 protein was observed (Figure S10). These results confirmed robust apoptosis and senescence activation. Immunohistochemistry for GPX4 showed a 29.9% reduction in the MTB@LM + US group, validating ferroptosis pathway activation in vivo. Concurrently, analysis of DAMPs revealed a 44.4% decrease in HMGB1 expression and a 26.1% increase in CRT exposure in the MTB@LM + US group relative to controls, confirming the induction of ICD. These in vivo outcomes were consistent with the in vitro data, supporting that MTB@LM + US synergistically inhibited tumor growth in association with apoptosis-, senescence-, ferroptosis-, and ICD-related changes.

In vivo antitumor immune profiling

Tumor and lymph node tissues harvested on day 14 post-treatment were analyzed via flow cytometry to evaluate immune cell subpopulations. As shown in Fig. 6a and e, MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US treatment groups exhibited enhanced DC maturation, with the MTB@LM + US group demonstrating the most pronounced effect, elevating the proportion of mature DCs to 73.4%. This aligns with prior in vitro BMDC maturation results. Within the TME, MTB@LM, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US treatments significantly increased the infiltration of CD8+ cytotoxic T cells (Fig. 6b and f) while reducing the proportion of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs; Fig. 6c and g). The MTB@LM + US group achieved the most robust modulation of these parameters. Furthermore, tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) polarization was markedly altered, with the MTB@LM + US group elevating the M1/M2 TAM ratio from 1.0 (control) to 1.5 (Fig. 6d, h and S11), indicative of a reversal of immunosuppressive TME dynamics.

Analysis of antitumor immune response. Cytokine profiling revealed that the MTB@LM + US group exhibited a distinct antitumor immune signature in both tumor tissue (Fig. 6i-k) and peripheral blood (Figure S12), characterized by elevated TNF-α and IFN-γ levels alongside reduced TGF-β expression.

Taken together, these findings support that MTB@LM + SDT promotes antitumor immune remodeling in vivo, as reflected by enhanced DC maturation, increased CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, reduced Treg proportion, altered TAM polarization, and a more pro-inflammatory cytokine profile. These results further support the therapeutic potential of MTB@LM + SDT as a multimodal immunomodulatory platform.

In vivo antitumor metastasis inhibition and long-term immune memory

Building on the robust antitumor immune activation achieved by MTB@LM + US therapy, we further investigated its synergistic efficacy with αPD-L1 in suppressing TNBC pulmonary metastasis and establishing long-term immune memory. Mice were randomized into four treatment groups: PBS control, αPD-L1 monotherapy, MTB@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 (Fig. 7a). Longitudinal monitoring (Fig. 7b and S13) revealed that the combination therapy achieved the highest tumor suppression, reducing primary tumor volume by 87.3% compared to the PBS group, surpassing both MTB@LM + US (75.1% inhibition) and αPD-L1 alone. Crucially, the MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 group demonstrated superior inhibition of pulmonary metastasis, with a 93.0% reduction in metastatic nodules versus controls, significantly outperforming MTB@LM + US (71.8% inhibition) and αPD-L1 (21.1% inhibition) groups (Fig. 7c and d).

To elucidate the mechanism underlying αPD-L1 synergism, histopathological analysis of primary tumors was performed. SA-β-gal and Ki67 staining (Fig. 7e-h) revealed that the MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 group exhibited a substantial reduction in SA-β-gal-positive cells (4.2%) compared to MTB@LM + US group (19.8%), alongside the lowest proportion of proliferating Ki67+ cells (10.9%). These findings suggest that αPD-L1 further enhances immune-mediated tumor control, accompanied by reduced SA-β-gal positivity and suppressed residual tumor cell proliferation. This interpretation is also consistent with a recent study showing that therapy-induced senescent cancer cells can upregulate PD-L1 and that PD-1-targeted therapy may reduce senescence-associated tumor burden through enhanced cytotoxic T-cell activity [18]. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 treatment significantly enhanced CD8⁺ T cell infiltration in primary tumors compared to the control group (Figs. 7i and S14a). This increase was accompanied by a 2.1-fold upregulation of the effector molecule granzyme B in CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 7j), concurrent with a marked reduction in the proportion of immunosuppressive regulatory T cells (Tregs) within the TME (Figs. 7k and S14b). Furthermore, MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 treatment shifted the polarization of TAMs, elevating the M1/M2 TAM ratio from 0.9 in the control group to 1.5 (Figs. 7l and S14c-d). Additionally, systemic immunomodulation was evidenced by expanded splenic CD8⁺ T cell populations compared to other groups (Figures S14e-f). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that MTB@LM + US synergizes with αPD-L1 to suppress tumor metastasis by remodeling the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment.

To assess long-term immune memory, tumor rechallenge experiments were conducted. At day 24, mice from MTB@LM + US and MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 groups (exhibiting therapeutic efficacy) and PBS controls (post-tumor resection) were rechallenged with 4T1 cells subcutaneously. While the PBS group showed rapid tumor regrowth, both the MTB@LM + US and MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 groups exhibited delayed progression, with the MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 group displaying superior suppression (Fig. 7m). Furthermore, the MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 group demonstrated an 18.9% increase in effector memory T cells (TEM) compared to controls, significantly exceeding the MTB@LM + US group (6.5% increase; Fig. 7n and o). These results confirm that MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 induces potent immune memory to prevent tumor recurrence.

Conclusions

This study constructed a biomimetic nanosystem (MTB@LM) by encapsulating the BRD4-targeting PROTAC degrader dBET6 within a Mn-TCPP MOF nanocarrier coated with a tumor cell membrane-fusogenic liposome hybrid membrane, which significantly enhanced tumor-targeting specificity and cellular uptake efficiency via membrane fusion while evading lysosomal degradation. In vitro, MTB@LM under ultrasound activation synergized SDT-amplified ROS generation with BRD4 degradation to induce apoptosis, while concurrently triggering ferroptosis (via lipid peroxidation) and senescence (via p21/p16 activation), thereby enhancing ICD and BMDC maturation. In 4T1 orthotopic tumor models, MTB@LM combined with ultrasound achieved > 85% tumor growth inhibition through coordinated activation of senescence, ferroptosis, and ICD, alongside excellent biosafety. The treatment remodeled the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment by promoting DC maturation, boosting cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cell infiltration, repolarizing TAMs toward the M1 phenotype, and modulating cytokine profiles. Moreover, the triple combination of MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1 synergistically suppressed lung metastasis (> 90% reduction) and established durable immune memory, characterized by protection against tumor recurrence. This work presents a multimodal therapeutic paradigm integrating nanotechnology-driven PROTAC delivery, SDT-enhanced ICD, and immune microenvironment reprogramming, offering a promising strategy for combating aggressive triple-negative breast cancer and its metastasis.

Materials and methods

Preparation of 4T1 Cell Membranes (4T1m), Membrane-fused Liposomes (MFlip), and Fused membranes (LM)

4T1m were isolated through hypotonic treatment and ultrasonication. Briefly, harvested 4T1 cells were washed with PBS to remove debris and extracellular products. The cells were then resuspended in deionized water and subjected to ice-cold ultrasonication (100 W, 5 s on/5 s off cycles, total duration 3 min) until achieving a clarified suspension. The lysate was centrifuged at 3,000 rpm (4 °C, 20 min) to pellet organelle-containing precipitates. The supernatant was further centrifuged at 15,000 rpm (4 °C, 30 min) to collect 4T1m. The final pellet was resuspended in minimal deionized water, freeze-dried, weighed, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent use.

MFlip were prepared via the thin-film hydration method. In brief, DOPC, DOTAP, and DSPE-PEG (molar ratio 90:5:5) were dissolved in 3 mL chloroform. The mixture was transferred to a round-bottom flask, evaporated at 50 °C under rotary vacuum (100–300 rpm) for 2 h, and rehydrated with 3 mL deionized water. The lipid film was sonicated in an ice bath for 10–20 min until membrane detachment occurred, followed by ice-bath ultrasonication (100 W, 5 s on/5 s off cycles, 3 min duration) in a 50 mL centrifuge tube to generate MFlip.

For LM synthesis, MFlip and 4T1m were mixed at a 1:1 mass ratio in deionized water and ultrasonicated on ice for 5 min. The mixture was subsequently extruded 11 times through a 0.22 μm polycarbonate porous membrane using a mini extruder (Avestin, Germany) to facilitate membrane fusion.

Membrane fusion assessment

Membrane fusion between MFlip and 4T1m was investigated via fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET) and confocal imaging. Prior to LM preparation, 4T1m membranes were dual-labeled with lipophilic dyes DiO (excitation/emission = 484/501 nm, final concentration: 5 µM) and DiI (excitation/emission = 549/565 nm, final concentration: 10 µM) under light-protected conditions for 20 min. Unbound dyes were removed by washing, followed by mixing with unlabeled MFlip membranes at weight ratios of 1:0, 1:1, 1:2, and 1:3 (4T1m: MFlip) to prepare fused membranes. Fluorescence spectra (500–650 nm) were recorded using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (excitation: 488 nm). For confocal imaging, 4T1m and MFlip membranes were separately labeled with DiO and DiI, respectively, and fused to generate LM. Fluorescence colocalization was visualized using a laser scanning confocal microscope (CLSM, Leica TCS SP8).

Preparation of MTB and MTB@LM

MTB nanoparticles were synthesized by incubating dBET6 (dissolved in DMSO) with Mn-MOF suspensions at varying molar ratios (1:0.1, 1:0.2, 1:0.5) under overnight magnetic stirring at room temperature. Unbound dBET6 was removed via centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 20 min) and repeated washing with deionized water. For LM encapsulation, MTB nanoparticles were mixed with LM in deionized water and subjected to ice-bath sonication for 20 min. The MTB@LM composite was isolated by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 20 min) and washed to eliminate uncoated LM.

Characterization of MTB@LM

The morphology of nanoparticles was characterized by transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HITACHI HT7800). Elemental distribution was analyzed via high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM), while elemental composition and manganese valence states were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Crystalline structural properties were evaluated using powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). Hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential were measured with a dynamic light scattering (DLS) analyzer (Malvern Nano ZS, UK). Optical properties were assessed using a UV-Vis-NIR spectrophotometer (Cary 5000, USA), and a standard calibration curve of dBET6 was established to determine the encapsulation efficiency (EE) based on the following formula: EE (%) = (1 − weight of free drug/weight of added drug) × 100%. Free dBET6 concentration was quantified by measuring the supernatant of centrifuged MTB@LM nanoparticle suspensions.

To evaluate the oxygen-generation capacity, nanoparticles (MOF, Mn-MOF, MTB, and MTB@LM) at 100 µg/mL were incubated with 400 µM H₂O₂, and dissolved oxygen levels were measured in real-time using a portable dissolved oxygen meter (Leici JPB-607A). For sonodynamic performance assessment, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR, Bruker EMXplus-6/1, Germany) was employed to detect hydroxyl radicals (trapped by DMPO) and singlet oxygen (trapped by TEMP) under different treatments.

To assess stability, MTB@LM was suspended in PBS (pH 7.4, 37 °C), and hydrodynamic size was monitored over time. For intracellular dBET6 release kinetics, MTB nanoparticles were resuspended in 1 mL PBS under varying conditions (37 °C, gentle agitation). At predetermined intervals, 200 µL of supernatant was collected after centrifugation to measure dBET6 concentration via absorbance, followed by replenishment with an equal volume of fresh PBS. Morphological changes in MTB@LM were further observed by TEM.

Cellular uptake analysis of MTB@LM

To compare the intracellular accumulation of MTB in different nanoparticles, DiI-labeled MTB (DiI-MTB) was prepared and subsequently encapsulated with MFlip, 4T1m, or LM to generate DiI-MTB@MFlip, DiI-MTB@4T1m, and DiI-MTB@LM nanoparticles. 4T1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 5 × 10⁴ cells/well and cultured overnight in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. Cells were then treated with DiI-MTB, DiI-MTB@MFlip, DiI-MTB@4T1m, or DiI-MTB@LM (100 µg/mL) for 4 h, followed by PBS washing to remove unbound nanoparticles. The median fluorescence intensity (MFI) of intracellular DiI-MTB was quantified via flow cytometry. For CLSM, 4T1 cells grown on glass-bottom dishes were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 20 min, stained with DAPI for nuclear labeling, and imaged. Pearson’s coefficients were calculated in ImageJ using the cytoplasmic region of the fluorescence colocalization images as the region of interest (ROI).

To investigate lysosomal colocalization, 4T1 cells treated with DiI-MTB@4T1m or DiI-MTB@LM for 4 h or 12 h were incubated with Lyso-Tracker Green DND26 (100 nM, Thermo Fisher) for 60 min and Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime) for 20 min before CLSM imaging. For cellular uptake mechanism studies, 4T1 cells pretreated with inhibitors (CPZ, AM, or ZPO) for 1 h were incubated with DiI-MTB@LM for 4 h, and intracellular MFI was analyzed by flow cytometry.

Cell viability assessment

The cytotoxic effect of MTB@LM on tumor cells was evaluated using the CCK-8 assay. 4T1 cells seeded in 96-well plates (5 × 10³ cells/well) were treated with MTB@LM (0–100 µg/mL) for 4 h. Subsequently, the MTB@LM + US group underwent ultrasonic irradiation (1.0 MHz, 1.0 W/cm², 50% duty cycle) for 3 min. In vitro ultrasound irradiation was delivered through the bottom of the culture plate, with ultrasound coupling gel applied between the transducer and the plate to ensure efficient acoustic transmission. After replacing with fresh medium, cells were cultured for an additional 24 h, and cell viability was assessed. The control group received no ultrasonic irradiation.

ROS, JC-1, and live/dead staining

ROS generation, mitochondrial membrane potential, and live/dead status were assessed using fluorescence microscopy (Leica, Germany). 4T1 cells were seeded in 24-well plates and treated for 4 h with PBS, Mn-MOF@LM, MTB@LM, ultrasound (US), Mn-MOF@LM + US, or MTB@LM + US (100 µg/mL). US-irradiated groups (US, Mn-MOF@LM + US, MTB@LM + US) were exposed to ultrasound (1.0 MHz, 1.0 W/cm², 50% duty cycle) for 3 min and incubated for an additional 2 h. Cells were then loaded with 10 µM DCFH-DA (Beyotime, China) for 30 min, stained with DAPI, and imaged. ROS levels were quantified via green fluorescence intensity (DCFH-DA oxidation). Mitochondrial depolarization was evaluated using JC-1 (Elabscience, China). Cells were incubated with JC-1 (37 °C, 20 min) and imaged. Healthy mitochondria exhibit red fluorescence (JC-1 aggregates), whereas depolarized mitochondria show green fluorescence (JC-1 monomers). The red/green fluorescence ratio was calculated to quantify mitochondrial membrane potential loss. Live/dead staining was performed using a Calcein AM/PI Double Staining Kit (Elabscience, China). Cells were incubated with Calcein AM/PI working solution (37 °C, 30 min), with live cells fluorescing green (Calcein AM) and dead cells fluorescing red (PI).

Apoptosis analysis by flow cytometry

Cell apoptosis was evaluated using the Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (Elabscience, China). 4T1 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated according to experimental groups for 4 h. After ultrasound irradiation, cells were further incubated for 2 h. Harvested cells were stained with Annexin V-FITC and propidium iodide (PI) for 15–20 min at room temperature in the dark. Apoptotic cells (Annexin V-FITC+/PI− and Annexin V-FITC+/PI+) were quantified via flow cytometry.

SA-β-gal staining

Cellular senescence was analyzed using the Senescence β-Galactosidase Staining Kit (Beyotime, China). Treated 4T1 cells in 6-well plates were subjected to ultrasound irradiation and cultured for 24 h in fresh medium. After PBS washing, cells were fixed with SA-β-gal fixative (1 mL/well) for 15 min at room temperature. Following three PBS washes, cells were incubated with 1 mL staining working solution in a CO₂-free 37 °C incubator overnight. Senescent cells were identified by blue staining under bright-field microscopy.

In vitro maturation of BMDCs

BMDCs were isolated from female BALB/c mice. After euthanasia, mice were immersed in 75% ethanol for 5 min for surface sterilization. Femurs and tibias were dissected, and the bone marrow was flushed out using a syringe with RPMI-1640 medium. The marrow suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged to collect bone marrow cells. Red blood cells were lysed using RBC lysis buffer, followed by centrifugation to remove debris. The cells were then seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 6 days in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 20 ng/mL rmGM-CSF, and 20 ng/mL IL-4 to generate immature dendritic cells.

For co-culture experiments, 4T1 cells were seeded into the upper chamber of a Transwell system and treated for 4 h under the following conditions: PBS, Mn-MOF@LM, MTB@LM, US, Mn-MOF@LM + US, or MTB@LM + US. Ultrasound irradiation was applied to the US, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups. Subsequently, BMDCs were seeded into the lower chamber of the Transwell system and co-cultured with treated 4T1 cells for 24 h. After co-culture, BMDCs were harvested and stained with the Zombie Aqua Fixable Viability Kit, PE anti-mouse CD11c, APC anti-mouse CD80, PE/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD86, and FITC anti-mouse I-A/I-E (MHC-II) antibodies. Flow cytometry was performed to analyze the maturation markers. Supernatants from BMDC cultures were collected, and the levels of TNF-α and IFN-γ were quantified using ELISA kits (Elabscience, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Establishment of tumor-bearing mouse model and treatment regimens

To establish an orthotopic 4T1 breast cancer model, 4T1 cells (1 × 10⁶ cells/mouse) were subcutaneously injected into the right mammary fat pad of female BALB/c mice. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula: V = (π/6) × length × (width)². When tumors reached approximately 60 mm³ (7 days post-inoculation), mice were randomly divided into six treatment groups (n = 6): PBS, Mn-MOF@LM, MTB@LM, US, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US. Nanoparticles (200 µL, 10 mg/kg) were intravenously administered on days 0, 3, 6, and 9. Ultrasound irradiation (1.0 MHz, 1.5 W/cm², 50% duty cycle, 3 min) was applied on days 1, 4, 7, and 10 for the US, Mn-MOF@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US groups. For in vivo treatment, the hair over the tumor region was removed before irradiation, and ultrasound coupling gel was applied between the transducer and the skin overlying the tumor to ensure effective acoustic coupling. Ultrasound was directed to the orthotopic tumors located in the right mammary fat pad. Tumor size and body weight were monitored throughout the 18-day treatment period. On day 18, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, photographed, weighed, and processed for paraffin embedding (H&E staining and IHC) or frozen sectioning (SA-β-gal staining). Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) were harvested for H&E staining. Serum samples were collected for biochemical analysis.

Combination therapy with αPD-L1

To establish a metastatic 4T1 model, mice bearing orthotopic 4T1 tumors (1 × 10⁶ cells/mouse) received intravenous injection of 4T1 cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/mouse) 3 days prior to treatment initiation. Mice were randomized into four groups (n = 6): PBS, αPD-L1, MTB@LM + US, and MTB@LM + US + αPD-L1. Nanoparticles (200 µL, 10 mg/kg) were administered intravenously on days 0, 3, 6, and 9; ultrasound irradiation was applied on days 1, 4, 7, and 10; and αPD-L1 (200 µL, 10 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally administered on days 12, 15, and 18. Tumor growth was monitored until day 24, when mice were euthanized. Tumors and lungs were excised for imaging, and tumor sections were prepared for Ki67 and SA-β-gal staining. Tumor cell suspensions were analyzed via flow cytometry. Granzyme B levels in tumors were measured by ELISA.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test, whereas one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess statistical significance among three or more groups. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0 (GraphPad Software, USA).
