Positive experiences of medication self-management among older adults with chronic multimorbidities in Chinese city communities: a qualitative study
Authors: Xiao Yang, Xiao Fan Wang, Lin Lin Su, Xiao Chen Lv, Chun Hui Zhang, Xian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study utilises the Positive Emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment (PERMA) framework to explore positive experiences in medication self-management among older adults with chronic multimorbidities from a positive psychology perspective.DesignA descriptive phenomenological qualitative research method was adopted. Introduction

As population ageing continues to intensify, the health status of the elderly population is deteriorating, with a sharp rise in chronic disease prevalence.1 Multimorbidities represent a common manifestation of chronic diseases. Chronic multimorbidities refer to the coexistence of two or more chronic diseases in an individual,2 which is particularly prevalent among the elderly population.3 Epidemiological data indicate that 40% to 56% of older adults worldwide live with chronic multimorbidities, while in China, this figure is notably higher, ranging from 55% to 98%.4 Chronic multimorbidities have emerged as a prominent public health challenge among the world’s ageing populations.5

Patients with multimorbidities often require multiple medications. Polypharmacy refers to patients taking five or more medications.6 7 At the same time, multimorbidities complicate the management of chronic diseases. Patients with chronic diseases require long-term medication to treat or alleviate their conditions, and older adults have gradually become the largest consumer group of pharmaceuticals.8 Patients with chronic multimorbidities often face issues such as improper medication management, poor adherence to treatment regimens, and compromised safety. In severe cases, these problems can lead to prolonged hospital stays, reduced quality of life, and early mortality.9 The chain of problems caused by polypharmacy must be addressed urgently.

The patient’s experiences of medication self-management are described as ‘the total of all medication-related events encountered throughout a patient’s lifetime, encompassing perceptions and feelings regarding both the benefits derived from and burdens associated with medication treatment’.10 Experiences of medication self-management are usually categorised as positive or negative.11 Positive experiences typically involve patients adapting to medication routines, actively learning drug-related information, and effectively managing side effects. Conversely, negative experiences refer to negative emotions and non-adherence behaviours induced by adverse drug events, low medication satisfaction, heavy medication burden, and inadequate medication practices, which in turn affect patients’ physical and mental health.9 The positive experiences of medication self-management in older adults represent a critical dimension of therapeutic success. Specifically, such experiences can strengthen patients’ confidence in medication and improve adherence,1 10 12 which in turn supports better health outcomes.13 Therefore, focusing on the positive experiences of medication self-management may offer a new perspective for addressing the challenges associated with polypharmacy in this population.

Despite the global burden of chronic multimorbidity, research on patients’ medication self-management remains scarce, with a particular paucity of studies exploring positive experiences. The Positive Emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning and Accomplishment (PERMA) theory, proposed by Seligman in 2011 as a foundational model of well-being,14 offers a valuable lens for addressing this gap. Seligman posits that well-being comprises five core elements: Positive Emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning and Accomplishment.14 This multidimensional framework has been widely applied to understand how individuals derive fulfilment and purpose in various life contexts, including chronic disease management.15,17 Medication self-management encompasses patients’ emotional and cognitive responses, involvement in treatment and interactions with both healthcare providers and informal support networks,18 which closely align with the PERMA framework. For older adults with chronic multimorbidities as a special population, medication self-management is not only a technical task for disease control but also a complex process involving a variety of psychological and social factors, during which patients may experience numerous positive experiences.

Therefore, this study explores positive experiences in medication self-management among older adults from a positive psychology perspective based on PERMA theory, aiming to gain deeper insights into their intrinsic emotions and needs. It seeks to provide evidence for guiding patients towards developing positive medication-taking behaviours.

Methods

Aims

This study explored the positive experiences of elderly Chinese patients with chronic multimorbidities in medication self-management.

Design

This study was designed as a qualitative descriptive phenomenological study, aiming to gain an in-depth understanding of the positive experiences of older adults with chronic multimorbidities during medication self-management. Semi-structured interviews were conducted among older adults with chronic multimorbidities living in city communities.

Participants

From January 2025 to June 2025, semi-structured face-to-face interviews were conducted among 24 older adults with chronic comorbidities who were recruited from two community health service centres in Zhengzhou City, Henan Province, China. These two community health service centres, which are close medical alliance partners of several tertiary hospitals in Zhengzhou, have established a comprehensive follow-up system for older adults with chronic diseases covering tens of thousands of residents. Their full range of disease categories and relatively mature chronic disease management services were instrumental in supporting participant recruitment for this study.

Purposive sampling, combined with a maximum variation strategy, was adopted in this study to recruit participants, ensuring sample diversity in terms of age, sex, educational level, types of chronic diseases and number of medications used. The research team first used the chronic disease follow-up database of the community health service centres, with assistance from community staff to preliminarily screen potential participants, and eligible older adults were invited during outpatient follow-up. Meanwhile, participants were also recruited through referrals by community staff and community activity organisations. Inclusion criteria: (1) Age ≥65 years; (2) diagnosed with at least two chronic diseases by a secondary or higher-level hospital (chronic diseases were defined according to the International Classification of Diseases, 11th Revision (ICD-11),19 including hypertension, diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidaemia, coronary atherosclerotic heart disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, etc); (3) taking ≥5 types of medications, with a medication duration of ≥3 months6 7; (4) conscious and clear, with a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score ≥17,20 and able to understand study instructions and express responses clearly; (5) provided informed consent. Patients with severe hearing impairment, mental illness, intellectual disability or critical illness that prevents them from managing their medications independently were excluded.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Data collection

Sociodemographic data of older adults with chronic multimorbidity were collected using a brief questionnaire prior to each interview. This study designed an interview outline based on the PERMA theoretical framework and integrated the interview inquiry taxonomy proposed by Robinson.21 Robinson employs descriptive probes, idiographic memory probes, exploratory probes and clarifying probes as core interview tools to ensure the rigour and systematic nature of information collection and analysis. Among these, idiographic memory probes guide patients to recall specific periods for detailed information; descriptive probes focus on exploring practical experiences of daily medication self-management; exploratory probes aim to reveal patients’ perceptions of medication management; and clarifying probes are used to specify the precise meaning of key statements made by interviewees. Based on this structured design, this study aimed to conduct an in-depth analysis of the positive experiences of medication self-management among older adults with chronic multimorbidities. Prior to formal interviews, two patients with chronic multimorbidities participated in pre-interviews. Their responses were excluded from the final analysis and used solely to refine the interview guide. Based on pre-interview findings, the research team developed the formal interview outline through discussion. The interview outline is shown in table 1.

Interview outline

A semi-structured, face-to-face, individual interview approach was adopted in this study, and the principle of data saturation was followed. The interviewers were the first and second authors, both of whom were masters students in nursing who had received systematic training in courses related to qualitative research. To ensure a quiet environment, all interviews were conducted in a relatively quiet office at the community health service centres. Before the interview, the two interviewers introduced themselves; explained the purpose, content and methods of the study to the participants; and informed them that the interview recordings would only be used for research purposes and that their privacy would be strictly protected. Participants were then guided to sign the informed consent form. During the interview, the interviewers collected data on the basis of respecting objective facts by listening carefully and applying appropriate interview techniques, and avoided asking leading questions. Meanwhile, the interviewers paid close attention to the participants’ facial expressions and body language and recorded non-verbal information such as their tone and intonation. Each interview lasted approximately 30–40 min.

Ethical consideration

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhengzhou University (No. ZZUIRB2023-166). Before conducting in-depth interviews, all participants were informed of the study’s purpose and content. To protect the privacy and confidentiality of participants during the study and the publication of results, each participant was sequentially coded as N1 to N24. Written informed consent was obtained from patients for their participation. Participants were made aware that they could withdraw from the study at any time.

Data analysis

Data collection and analysis were conducted simultaneously. Within 24 hours after the interviews, the first author and the second author completed the transcription using NVivo V.11.0 software. To protect participant privacy, all personally identifying information was removed during this transcription phase, and each participant was assigned a unique code. Two researchers independently coded the interview data separately. Any discrepancies were discussed by the research team until a consensus was reached. The data were analysed using the Colaizzi seven-step method22: (1) Read the interview carefully and repeatedly; (2) Identify meaningful statements that are consistent with this study; (3) Summarise meaningful statements; (4) Construct meaning units, list common concepts and characteristics, and extract themes and theme groups; (5) Cluster themes for detailed description; (6) Build the basic structure; (7) The results were returned to all respondents for verification to ensure the authenticity of the content.

Rigour

The rigour of this study was ensured through the following approaches. First, the results were reported in accordance with the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR)23 (see online supplemental file 1). Second, a heterogeneous sample was purposively selected to ensure adequate representation of diverse perspectives and experiences. Additionally, two interviewers remained linguistically and non-judgmentally neutral during the interviews while observing and recording. Field notes were consistently documented to guarantee comprehensive and detailed data collection. Finally, the researchers conducted independent data analysis and the final codes and themes were developed through consultation with the research team.

Researcher characteristics and reflexivity

The research team consisted of six members: two second-year master’s students (XY and XFW), two third-year masters students (LLS and XCL), one professor (CHZ) and one associate professor (XZ). XY and XFW focus on chronic disease management and have systematically completed coursework in research methods, qualitative nursing research, advanced nursing and advanced health assessment during their studies. They have 2 years of clinical internship experience and have accumulated interview skills and expertise through their learning and practice. LLS and XCL have professional backgrounds in chronic disease management, as well as clinical work experience and qualitative research experience. Professor CHZ has over 20 years of research experience in the field of chronic diseases and has provided guidance on study design and methodology selection. Associate Professor XZ, whose specialty is also chronic disease management, offered support in data analysis and theoretical advancement. The team members possess diverse academic backgrounds and levels of expertise: the master’s students have recent clinical practice experience and direct patient contact, while the senior researchers have deep theoretical knowledge and extensive research experience. This diversity of backgrounds provided a solid foundation for open discussion and multi-perspective interpretation throughout the research process. An egalitarian atmosphere among the researchers facilitated mutual critique and the integration of complementary insights regarding the data.

Analysis of interview results

Through organising and analysing interview data in conjunction with PERMA theory, five themes and fifteen subthemes were identified. The five themes included: (1) positive emotional experiences in medication self-management; (2) proactive engagement in medication management; (3) perceived social support from multiple sources; (4) profound understanding of the intrinsic value of medication management; (5) personal growth and realisation of life value through medication self-management. Theme 1: Positive emotional experiences in medication self-management

Optimistic acceptance

Older adults with chronic multimorbidities often experience negative emotions such as frustration, anxiety, and even stigmatisation due to prolonged illness duration and multiple conditions. As they adapt to long-term medication regimens and adjust their mindset, patients gradually accept their reality while offering themselves comfort and encouragement.

N7 Honestly, taking meds for so long gets annoying, but I feel they keep my hypertension and diabetes well-controlled. I can't even imagine if I stopped taking them—would I still have this level of health? N10: At my age, everyone has their share of health issues. After all, this body’s been running for decades. I'm grateful I can stay positive, keep things stable with my meds, and spend time playing with my grandchildren. That’s more than enough for me.

Gratitude and contentment

Older adults with chronic multimorbidities express deep gratitude for support from family and society. This appreciation transforms their medication burden into a positive experience of being cared for, motivating them to proactively adhere to their treatment schedules.

N13 The national healthcare system is excellent now. Except for a few out-of-pocket medications, most drugs are covered, significantly easing our financial burden. We can still enjoy such social benefits despite our illnesses—all thanks to the country. N16 My daughter has money. When she’s free, she takes me travelling and shopping. I feel very happy in life and am more proactive about taking my medicine on time—just hoping to stay healthy.

The pleasant sensation of stable physiological indicators

Some interviewees indicated that consistent, standardised self-management of medication can stabilise physiological indicators like blood pressure and blood sugar, alleviating physical discomfort. This health improvement positively motivates them to persist with self-management while easing anxiety about their condition, fostering a relaxed and pleasant mental state.

N1: After taking my medication, my blood pressure is no longer high, and my headaches have stopped. This medicine controls my condition so well—I'm not nearly as worried as when I first got sick. N3: I take my medication very regularly. After my hospital follow-up, the doctor said my condition is well-controlled and allowed me to gradually reduce my dosage to 1/4. This is all thanks to my consistent medication adherence, and I will definitely keep it up.

Theme 2: proactive engagement in medication management

Take the initiative to learn about health knowledge

The health perceptions of older adults with chronic comorbidities are evolving, as they have acquired a certain level of knowledge about their conditions and treatments. They are capable of independently considering many issues related to their illnesses and therapies, and they aspire for continuous improvement in their health status.

N2 My son asked me to follow a health information platform. I regularly read the information there and have learnt about diseases, health, and the medications I'm taking.N11 You learn something new every day. In the past, when you got sick, they said you should rest quietly. Times have changed, and health concepts have undergone a complete transformation. Now, it’s not just about taking your medicine consistently; you also need to exercise more and practice health preservation.

Actively seeking medication strategies

Older adults with chronic multimorbidities use external tools to ensure medication accuracy and adherence, transforming internal memory demands into external reminder systems. This effectively safeguards the regularity of self-managed medication.

N8: For my regular medications, I clearly remember how many doses to take daily and the dosage per dose. I know their basic functions by heart—I've been a long-term patient for over a decade. For new medications, I pay extra attention. N9: Now that I'm older, my memory isn't what it used to be—I can't even remember if I've taken my meds. Since most of my medications are three times a day, I set three alarms. When they go off, I take my pills right away and don't worry about forgetting.

Dynamic adjustment of medication management methods

Some interviewees mentioned common challenges during medication adherence, such as forgetting doses, difficulty detecting adverse reactions, and unstable blood pressure or blood sugar levels. To address these difficulties, they dynamically adjust their self-management plans based on their individual circumstances.

N15: Since my spouse and I take many medications, our son bought us a smart pill organiser. I usually prep a week’s worth of doses in advance. This way, we take them on time and rarely miss a dose. These pill organisers are truly convenient. N17: Sometimes when I check my blood sugar and find it higher than usual, I adjust my diet or go to the hospital to have my doctor adjust my dosage to stabilise my blood sugar.

Theme 3: perceived social support from multiple sources

Support from family

Patients with chronic multimorbidities in older adults face significant psychological and physical stress burdens, and receiving family support can effectively enhance their positive treatment experiences.

N4: My son takes care of everything without me even having to ask. He accompanies me to the hospital without fail, and even when he can't make it, he writes down clear instructions on how to get prescriptions at the big hospital and the entire process. N18 My son cares deeply for me. Since my diagnosis, I no longer feel alone in facing these challenges. Having him by my side gives me strength and takes away my fear of suffering.

Support from friends

Assistance and companionship from peers with similar experiences effectively alleviates the loneliness older adults with chronic multimorbidities feel during disease management, relieving them from bearing the burden of fighting illness alone.

N12: During my hospitalisation, I met a fellow patient. We monitored each other’s medication adherence while in the hospital and stayed in touch afterward, continuing to check on each other’s medication compliance. N14: My neighbour and I have the same condition. We often discuss medication issues and even go downstairs together to dance at the square.

Support from healthcare providers

Patients gradually become familiar with healthcare providers through long-term treatment experiences, and on this basis, a reliable doctor-patient trust relationship is established.

N7: I frequently visit the hospital to pick up medication, and the nurses there all recognise me. They're always very kind. For any new medications, they provide detailed instructions on usage, follow-up appointment times, and precautions, and even make clear markings on the medication boxes. N20: The hospital doctors created a WeChat group for us and post daily medication reminders in it. I review the group messages every night before bed, and it’s really helpful.

Theme 4: profound understanding of the intrinsic value of medication management

Recognising the implicit value of medication

Patients with chronic multimorbidities in older adults widely recognise that long-term self-management medication reduces the likelihood of disease progression, while deeply appreciating the health benefits of consistent, regular medication adherence.

N6 My 2019 coronary heart disease hospitalisation cost over 40 000 yuan. Now, after 3 years of taking medication on schedule, I haven't been hospitalised. My annual medication costs are just over 1000 yuan. I finally understand that sticking to medication saves money and protects my health. N9 I used to think medication harmed my body, so I often skipped doses. Whenever my blood pressure and blood sugar normalised, I'd stop taking them, causing my condition to keep recurring and forcing me to constantly visit the hospital. After I started taking medication regularly, my condition stabilised.

Sustaining the drive for social engagement

Through effective self-management of medications, older adults with chronic multimorbidities minimise disease interference in daily life, enabling them to maintain normal social activities, provide intergenerational care, or participate in community events.

N19: My condition is well-controlled now. Not only can I shop for groceries and cook independently, but I've also joined the community’s senior volunteer service team. Every Tuesday and Thursday, I help neighbours measure blood pressure and promote health knowledge at the community entrance. N24 I can attend calligraphy classes at the community senior university in the mornings and sometimes help my son pick up his grandson in the afternoons. If I didn't manage my medication well and felt unwell, these activities would be out of the question. It’s precisely because I take my medication properly and my health is stable that I can participate in these activities just like before.

A renewed understanding of the meaning of life

Many interviewees admitted that despite their illnesses, they still crave a high-quality life. They integrate medication management into their daily routines, viewing illness not as an insurmountable obstacle. With consistent medication, their physical condition approaches that of healthy individuals, allowing them not only to enjoy a good quality of life but also to embrace life with a positive attitude.

N1: I've had hypertension for over a decade, but no one knows I have it. I've never been hospitalised—I just get my regular prescriptions. I'm no different from anyone else. N21 I'm now used to taking my medication in the morning. It doesn't affect my daily life, and after taking it, I feel no discomfort. I just go about doing what I want to do. N24 Living with a chronic illness long-term has taught me that properly managing my medication and taking care of my body is being responsible for myself. It also means less trouble for my family and gives my life more purpose.

Theme 5: personal growth and realisation of life value through medication self-management

The sense of mastery after overcoming difficulties

Through long-term self-management of medications, patients gradually overcome practical challenges such as maintaining medication adherence, adapting treatment regimens and managing adverse reactions. With accumulated experience, their medication management skills become increasingly proficient.

N11: I used to think taking three medications simultaneously was too complicated—I'd always miss doses or mix them up. Now I've figured out the ‘morning, noon, and night pill organiser’ method and haven't made a mistake since. I think I'm doing pretty well! N13: I'm a long-term patient with extensive medication management experience. I understand the mechanisms of my drugs, and adverse reactions and drug interactions are second nature to me. N22: In the early years of my illness, my adherence was terrible. After several hospitalizations, I started taking my meds properly. Now I'm consistent and don't need to go to the hospital anymore.

The value of alleviating the burden on families

Some interviewees hold the belief that they shouldn't burden their families. Even with their illnesses, they don't become a care burden to their families and gain a strong sense of self-worth in the process.

N5: I manage my own illness, take my medication on time, and stay quite healthy. I even help look after my grandchildren. N15: My son is busy with work. My wife and I don't want to burden our children. We remember the doctor’s instructions clearly, manage our own medications, and pay attention to our diet.

The sense of accomplishment from sharing one’s own experiences

Some older adults with chronic multimorbidities have accumulated extensive experience in medication self-management. They frequently assist others by sharing their successful practices as examples, helping those in need improve their medication self-management skills.

N3: I keep a notebook to record my blood pressure, blood sugar, and diet daily. I'm a role model for medication management in our community and often share my experiences with others. Every time I share, I feel a great sense of accomplishment. N6: I used to skip my meds and end up hospitalised often. Now I use my own story as a cautionary tale to urge others to take their medications properly. N23: I'm quite experienced with taking my meds. Several fellow patients often ask me questions, and I can answer them all. Explaining things to them makes me feel quite proud.

Discussion

This study employed the PERMA framework to explore patients’ positive experiences in medication self-management from a positive psychology perspective. Research findings indicate that older adults with chronic multimorbidities have demonstrated multidimensional positive experiences in medication self-management. These include developing positive emotional experiences in medication self-management; proactively practising medication self-management behaviours; perceiving support from multiple sources; establishing a profound understanding of the intrinsic value of medication management; and achieving personal growth and value realisation through medication self-management. This complements previous studies focusing on negative experiences, such as medication burden and adverse feelings,24 25 suggesting that positive experiences and negative experiences may coexist in the same patient’s medication journey, while the former has received insufficient attention in prior research. Focusing only on negative feelings and solving problems one by one may fail to foster continuously improving self-efficacy. This study shifts the research perspective and emphasises a positive evaluation of patients’ medication self-management experiences based on positive psychology.

This study found that patients developed positive emotional experiences during medication self-management, including optimistic acceptance, gratitude and satisfaction, as well as pleasure derived from stable physiological indicators. These positive emotional experiences encouraged patients to spontaneously form a habit of regular medication use. Granata et al26 found that positive emotion has direct physiological effects, and a higher level of positive affect is an independent predictor of non-pharmacological treatment adherence among older adults. This finding echoes the emphasise in positive psychology on the role of individual developmental resources in cognitive reconstruction.27 The shift in medical models has prompted scholars to focus not only on patients’ negative psychological changes but also to explore their positive emotional experiences from the perspective of positive psychology, fully embodying the philosophy of ‘people-oriented, harmony between humanity and nature’.28

Therefore, it is recommended that healthcare practitioners pay attention to the positive affective experiences of older adults with chronic diseases in clinical practice. By identifying and strengthening the positive emotions exhibited by patients during medication self-management, the interference caused by negative medication-related emotions can be reduced, and confidence in long-term medication can be established, which may promote the development of positive illness perceptions.15 In addition, future studies may further explore the influencing mechanisms of varying levels of positive affective experiences on medication self-management behaviours among older adults with chronic diseases, as well as how to effectively stimulate and maintain patients’ positive affective experiences through nursing interventions, so as to provide empirical evidence for the development of more targeted nursing strategies.

This study found a significant association between positive experiences and medication self-management behaviours among patients, which is consistent with the findings of KRSKA et al.29 Medication self-management experiences emphasise a patient-centred approach,30 and attention to patient experiences is key to reducing medication-related problems.31 Among these, positive experiences help enhance patients’ understanding of underlying diseases and the necessity of treatment, and strengthen their medication beliefs, thereby improving medication adherence, promoting the formation of self-management behaviours, ultimately facilitating disease recovery.32 In contrast, negative experiences may lead to doubts or resistance towards pharmacotherapy, weaken patients’ recognition of the significance of long-term medication, thus inducing medication non-adherence, and even giving rise to multiple problems in psychological, physical and social functioning.33 Medication non-adherence is relatively common among older adults with multimorbidity, which not only compromises individual treatment efficacy but may also accelerate disease progression.34

On this basis, the present study further found that most patients with positive experiences perceived multiple sources of support from family, healthcare providers and the community. This suggests that individuals with positive experiences tend to mobilise internal and external resources more efficiently, thereby demonstrating stronger medication management execution.8 35 In other words, positive experiences may indirectly improve patients’ medication behaviour through two pathways: ‘enhancing awareness of autonomous management’ and ‘activating the social support network’. Thus, cultivating positive experiences not only helps improve the standardisation of patients’ medication behaviour but may also further enhance their intrinsic motivation and autonomy in long-term treatment by strengthening their understanding of the disease and the significance of medication, which has important practical significance for older adults with chronic multimorbidities.

This study found that patients with positive experiences demonstrated a deep value identification with medication management and achieved growth and self-actualisation during the process of medication self-management. This may be because positive experiences have driven patients’ health-seeking demands to shift from a mere pursuit of physical health to higher-level goals of improving quality of life and realising self-worth. Strengthening patients’ positive experiences in medication self-management essentially guides individuals to complete cognitive reappraisal and the reconstruction of life meaning. As shown in the study by Tabernero et al,36 brief interventions combining mindfulness and positive reinforcement can effectively improve emotional well-being and self-efficacy among patients with chronic diseases. This process not only enhances patients’ psychological resilience but also significantly boosts their subjective well-being. It can both urge patients to conduct autonomous management in disease coping and drive them to continuously practice positive medication self-management behaviours and healthy lifestyles.37

Exploring the medication self-management experiences of older adults from the perspective of positive psychology is consistent with their disease cognition and adaptation process. It helps to deepen the patient-centred care philosophy and has a unique value, especially for the elderly with multimorbidity. Previous studies have shown that positive psychology interventions can improve self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation among patients with chronic diseases,38,40 thereby promoting the adoption and maintenance of health behaviours, including regular exercise, reasonable diet and medication adherence.41 The findings of the present study further confirm this mechanism. Therefore, it is recommended that medical staff guide patients to focus on the positive experiences and gains in medication management in clinical practice, and pay attention to exploring the positive value in long-term self-management tasks rather than only focusing on negative experiences, so as to help patients avoid falling into a negative cycle and improve overall health outcomes. Future research and practice should go beyond individual-level psychological interventions. From the perspective of positive psychology, patients should be encouraged to shift their mindset from compensating for deficits to developing strengths. Meanwhile, greater attention should be paid to how institutional factors shape or constrain the potential space for positive psychological experiences.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, this study mainly included older adults with intact cognitive function and independent living ability, which may not fully represent the complex elderly population with comorbidities and polypharmacy who have cognitive or functional limitations. In addition, although the study suggests that disease duration may influence medication experience, information on patients’ disease duration was not collected, which limits the analytical depth of this study. Second, the mechanism underlying the role of family support remains unclear: whether strong family support directly promotes patients’ autonomous medication ability or functions mainly by shaping positive attitudes and beliefs still needs to be explored. Third, this study did not assess potential psychological and behavioural moderating variables such as locus of control and health literacy, which may significantly influence patients’ medication cognition and engagement in self-management. Future studies should adopt a longitudinal design and include more clinically representative samples to investigate the dynamic interaction of cognitive status, family dynamics and individual psychological characteristics in the formation of medication experiences. Furthermore, this study only explored positive medication experiences from the patient’s perspective. Future research could include caregivers and healthcare providers to enrich the understanding of the dimensions of positive medication experiences from multiple perspectives, so as to provide a more comprehensive basis for developing individualised intervention programmes.

Conclusion

This study, based on in-depth interviews with 24 older adults with chronic comorbidities, reveals that positive medication self-management experiences can serve as a motivational resource supporting rational medication use in this population. Accordingly, the findings of this study provide the following implications for healthcare practitioners: they may consider helping patients develop accurate medication knowledge, facilitating their access to internal and external resources to strengthen autonomous motivation and jointly exploring ways to enhance positive medication experiences in clinical practice.
