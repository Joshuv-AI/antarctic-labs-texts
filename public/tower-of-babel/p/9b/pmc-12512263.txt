Socioeconomic inequality and modifiable cardiovascular risk factor clusters among consulters with and without osteoarthritis in England
Authors: Dahai Yu, Xiaoyang Huang, Mamas A Mamas, Ross Wilkie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe study aims to investigate the socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of clustering of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors (MCRFs) among English individuals with and without osteoarthritis (OA), and examines the temporal trends of these inequalities from 1992 to 2017.MethodsThe study included 109,142 incident cases of OA aged ≥ 35 years and 1:1 age-, sex-, practice- and index year- matched non-OA individuals from the Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) GOLD with linkage to the English Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) 2015. We estimated the slope index of inequality (SII) and relative index of inequality (RII) for the period prevalence of clustering of MCVRFs (≥ 1, ≥ 2, ≥3).ResultsA significant association was observed between socioeconomic deprivation and a higher period prevalence of clustering of MCRFs (≥ 1, ≥ 2, ≥3) in individuals with OA between 1992 and 2017. The SII and RII values indicated greater socioeconomic disparities among OA patients compared to non-OA counterparts. These disparities were consistent across genders but varied by age group.ConclusionsSocioeconomic deprivation is linked to a higher prevalence of clustering of multiple MCRFs in people with OA, with this association being more pronounced than in those without OA. Addressing socioeconomic inequality should be integral in cardiovascular disease prevention strategies for the OA population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24601-3.Keywords: Osteoarthritis, Socioeconomic deprivation, Modifiable risk factor, Health ineuqality, Cardiovascular diseases, Electronic health record, Primary care

Background

Osteoarthritis (OA) is associated with a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) [1]but the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Neighbourhood socioeconomic deprivation is associated with an increased prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) such as obesity and diabetes and its effects are reversible [2–6]. A case-control study in England and Wales has demonstrated that people with OA with lower socioeconomic status (SES) (unskilled or partly skilled occupation groups) were more likely to have six or more clinical comorbidities including obesity than those with higher SES (professional and managerial groups) (mean difference = 3.4%; 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.9–5.9%) while the socioeconomic variation in clinical comorbidities was not marked among age- and sex-matched non-OA controls [7].

Since the 1970 s local measures of deprivation in England have been calculated by the Department for Communities and Local Government and its predecessors [8]. With patients’ consent, primary care electronic health records (EHRs) are linked to Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) 2015, which provides a unique opportunity to investigate the socioeconomic inequalities in health status or clinical outcomes [9]. Based on the primary EHRs IMD linkage, previous studies investigated the prevalence of several modifiable CVRFs by each decile/quintile of IMD in the general population [6, 10]or incorporate IMD as a predictor in the prediction tool (e.g., QRISK3, ASSIGN) to predict the risk to develop CVD in the general population [11, 12]. The clustering approach, using counts of ≥ 1, ≥2, and ≥ 3 CVRFs, has been widely adopted in public health research to characterise cumulative cardiovascular risk burdens and socioeconomic inequalities [5, 6, 13–15]. Few works using Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD)-IMD linked database have addressed the socioeconomic inequalities at the population level using measurements such as slope index of inequality (SII) or relative index of inequality (RII) that are commonly used in the public health evaluation [13]. The SII quantifies absolute inequality, measuring the difference in health outcomes between the most and least deprived groups while accounting for population size and distribution. The RII assesses relative inequality, reflecting the ratio of outcomes between these groups, adjusted for their respective population proportions. Some studies have measured the absolute and relative difference in multiple chronic conditions between the least and the most deprived group but without accounting for the size of the groups and the intermediate deprived groups [14].

This study examines socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of modifiable CVRFs in consulters with and without OA between 1992 and 2017 using large populations based on EHRs from a representative primary care EHR database, CPRD GOLD, linked with the patient-level English IMD 2015. The findings of this study will provide useful health information for public health practice, for example, if socioeconomic inequalities widen in the population with OA, preventive strategies and integrated clinical care should be prioritised in the deprived population with OA.

Methods

Data setting

The study used an incident OA population aged 35 and over with linkage to English IMD 2015 data and an age-, sex-, practice-, and index year-matched non-OA population derived from CPRD. CPRD GOLD is a national primary care EHR database including anonymised data from patient records extracted from general practices using the Vision system [9]. CPRD GOLD is a continually updated primary care EHR database; for this study, we restricted our study period up to December 2017 to define the index year as approved by the CPRD ISAC protocol, rather than to represent the end of data collection. Patients in CPRD GOLD have been given a unique identifier to other parts of data that are mainly recorded using Read codes 2nd version, a clinical classification system [15]. This system includes over 250,000 codes for not only diseases but also medical history, symptoms, signs, examination findings, diagnostic procedures, therapies, lifestyle factors, and demographical information. Data on laboratory results are added to patients’ records through links to laboratories.

Ethics approval

This study involves human participants. The study was approved by the Independent Scientific Advisory Committee for CPRD research (protocol reference: 18_031R2). No further ethical permissions were required for the analyses of these anonymised patient-level data. Participants gave informed consent to participate in the study before taking part.

Population with OA

All individuals aged 35 and over who had an incident diagnosis of OA between January 1992 and December 2017 with up-to-standard data and registration in CPRD GOLD for at least three years prior to the date of the incident diagnosis of OA were eligible. OA was defined as a primary care consultation with a read code (e.g., N05.11) for diagnosed OA. OA individuals with a history of OA diagnosis prior to the incident diagnosis of OA were excluded. Individuals who transferred out or had a record of death prior to the incident diagnosis of OA were excluded. OA individuals without a linkage to English IMD 2015 were also excluded. The date of the incident OA consultation was the index date for inclusion in the study.

Population without OA

One non-OA individual who had the linkage to English IMD 2015 was matched for each OA individual based on age stratification (35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 85 and over), sex, and practice by risk set sampling (identifying non-OA individuals from a group of people who are at-risk at the same index date of the matched OA individual) [16]. The index date was defined as the date of OA diagnosis for cases. Non-OA individuals, who doe not have an OA diagnosis, were assigned the same index date as their matched OA individual to ensure they are population at-risk after index date.

Non-OA individuals were defined as individuals aged 35 and over with at least one primary care consultation or clinical event (i.e., symptom, diagnosis, therapy, test, referral, immunisation) between January 1992 and December 2017 (to exclude ‘ghost-patients’), with up-to-standard data and registration in CPRD GOLD for at least three years prior to the index consultation, and without a history of OA diagnosis within three years prior to or after the index consultation. Matching on GP practice redeuces variability caused by differences in coding variation over practices, ensuring comparability of recorded data between cases and controls. Non-OA individuals who transferred out or had a record of death prior to the index consultation were excluded.

Measurement of socioeconomic deprivation

IMD 2015 was linked to patients’ data in CPRD GOLD using the patient postcode of residence. Deciles were calculated by ranking the 32,844 small neighbourhoods in England from the most deprived to least deprived and dividing them into 10 equal groups [8]. The IMD score for a neighbourhood might change over the study period. However, census data have shown that using a fixed IMD decile allocation for small areas in England offered a stable ranking of areas [17]. IMD2015 was treated as the primary exposure variable in this study and calculated based on individual LSOA data. Matching participants on GP practice was implemented to reduce information bias caused by variations in GP coding behavior, rather than to control for socioeconomic factors. While GP practice matching may partially align IMD distributions due to geographic clustering of patients, IMD2015 values reflect individual-level deprivation and retain sufficient variability to explore its relationship with CVRF clustering. Matching on IMD2015 directly was avoided, as it would homogenise the exposure and limit the ability to assess its differential impact on OA and non-OA groups.

Outcomes

Five modifiable CVRFs, including smoking status, obesity, hypertension, T2DM and dyslipidaemia, were selected for this study based on their widespread recognisation as critical controbutors to CVD and their frequent clustering in public health evaluations [5, 6]. These CVRFs were identified using Read codes recorded within three years prior to the index consultation of each study participant and validated in the CPRD database, a standard methodology in similar studies [18].

Smoking status was recorded as “current” and “former/never” based on Read codes in the CPRD database, following methodology established in previous research [19]. Individuals with no record of smoking status within three years prior to the index consultation were categorised as “never smoked”, consistent with standard practices.

Obesity was identified using a body mass index (BMI) (Read code: 22 K.00) ≥ 30 kg/m2 [20].When more than one BMI assessment was recorded within three years prior to the index consultation, the nearest one to the date of the index consultation was adopted. Hypertension diagnosis was identified using Read codes. Individuals with no record of hypertension diagnosis were classified as non-hypertensive. T2DM diagnosis was identified using Read codes. Individuals with no record of T2DM diagnosis were classified as non-diabetes.

Dyslipidaemia was defined based on lip[id profile thresholds recommended by NICE guidelines [21]including total cholesterol (TC) level (≥ 5 mmol/L), high triglyceride level (≥ 1.7 mmol/L or low high-density lipoprotein cholesterol level (< 1.0 mmol/L for men and < 1.2 mmol/L for women). These thresholds are consistent with definitions used in national and international studies.Individuals with normal lipid profiles or those without recorded lipid profiles within three years prior to the index consultation were classified as non-dyslipidaemia. When more than one lipid profile was recorded within three years prior to the index consultation, the nearest one to the date of the index consultation was adopted. Individuals with a record of ≥ 1, ≥2, and ≥ 3 of the five above-listed modifiable CVRFs within three years prior to the index consultation were also identified.

The main predictor (socioeconomic deprivation) and outcome measures (CVRF prevalence and clustering) were validated using approaches commonly applied in CPRD studies. The Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) has been validated as a robust proxy for socioeconomic status at the neighborhood level, and CVRF definitions, including BMI, blood pressure thresholds, lipid profiles, and diagnostic codes for T2DM, adhere to WHO and NICE guidelines, ensuring comparability with other large-scale studies [9, 22].

Measurement of socioeconomic inequalities

We used the slope index of inequality (SII) to assess absolute differences across deprivation, and the relative index of inequality (RII) to compare rates between extremes, with both measures derived via weighted regression accounting for group size and full distribution (Supplemental Technical Note) [13].

Statistical analyses

Analyses were conducted in STATA/MP 14.2 (StataCorp. 2015. Stata Statistical Software: Release 14. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP), where Poisson regression models were fitted to estimate the period prevalence of having ≥ 1, ≥2, and ≥ 3 CVRFs with corresponding 95% confidence intervals for each deprivation decile in both OA and non-OA populations over 1992–2017.

The SII and RII for each modifiable CVRF were also stratified by age groups (35–44, 45–54, 55–64, 65–74, 75–84, 85+), sex (male and female), region, and each calendar year of the index consultation (1992–2017) in this study.

To address missing BMI data required for defining obesity, we applied multiple imputation via chained equations, incorporating covariates including age, sex, region, index year, IMD, smoking status, and diabetes. This process resulted in 13 imputed datasets, reflecting the proportion of incomplete cases in the study population [23]. All statistical analyses were done by use of Stata MP 17.0 taking a 2 tailed P-value < 0.05 as significant.

Results

Between 1992 and 2017, 215,190 of 409,791 consulters with incident physician-diagnosed OA and aged 35 and over from the CPRD GOLD were 1:1 matched with non-OA individuals. Among them, 109,142 (50.72%) sets of matched OA and non-OA individuals who give consent to their linkage to English IMD 2015 were included in this study (Supplemental Fig. 1). The mean age was 62 years, and the proportion of the female gender was 66% both for the whole study population with and without OA (Table 1). The distribution of socioeconomic deprivation levels (IMD deciles) was similar between populations with and without OA.

Socioeconomic inequalities in the number of CVRFs were evident across both genders and in individuals with and without OA, with a generally more pronounced gradient observed in women with OA (Supplementary Table 1). Similarly, disparities by age group were consistently present, particularly for having two or more CVRFs, which tended to widen in those with OA (Supplementary Table 2). The highest absolute and relative inequalities were generally seen in middle-aged groups, notably among individuals aged 35–64 years with OA.

Geographic patterns also revealed that socioeconomic inequalities in CVRF clustering varied across English regions, with stronger gradients observed in the North compared to the South (Supplementary Table 3). These regional differences were more pronounced among those with OA, underscoring potential area-level disparities that compound individual socioeconomic disadvantage.

Over the study period from 1992 to 2017, socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of multiple CVRFs increased in both populations with and without OA (Fig. 1; Supplementary Table 4). The widening of both SII and RII was more marked among those with OA, suggesting that socioeconomic disadvantage in cardiovascular risk burden has become increasingly concentrated over time within this group.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that the socioeconomic inequality in the prevalence of CVRFs is consistently more common in the population with OA compared with those without OA, especially in women, younger (35–64), and residents in Northern English regions. The socioeconomic inequality in the prevalence of CVRF increased between 1992 and 2017 in both populations with and without OA and consistently gaped between those with and without OA.

No previous studies to date have quantitatively measured socioeconomic inequality in the prevalence of modifiable CVRFs among consulters with and without OA between 1992 and 2017 using the English primary care EHRs. Instead of measuring the overall socio-economic inequalities, previous studies in the general population focused on the comparison of the prevalence of CVRF in the population from the most and the least deprived neighbourhood [24–28]. Although these comparisons consistently revealed a higher prevalence in the most deprived neighbourhood than the least deprived neighbourhood, the overall socio-economic inequalities might be estimated with bias by ignoring the weights of the estimations from the subpopulation from the neighbourhoods between the most and the least deprived neighbourhoods. The population health survey is the most popular way to investigate the socioeconomic gap in health indicators in the general population. For example, a longitudinal English GP survey reported both absolute and relative inequalities in the prevalence of obesity (SII = 13.4% in women and 7.7% in men; RII = 1.9 in women and 1.4 in men, controlling for age, sex, year of the survey, etc.) between the lowest and highest SES groups (defined by occupation class) between 1991 and 2007 24. Restricted by the aim, the cost, and potential low response rate, few longitudinal nationally representative population surveys have been routinely processed in the population with specific conditions (i.e., OA). The current study provided an alternative method to longitudinally surveillance of the socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of CVRFs (or health indicators) in the population with specific conditions based on the routinely recorded primary care electronic health records which have been proved to be representative, efficient (in terms of cost and response rate), powerful and highly valid [9].

Different from the overall evaluation, some previous studies reported the relative measurement (i.e., odds ratio) of the prevalence of single CVRF within the general population at a single time point [22, 29, 30]which yielding similar patterns as RII for the period prevalence of CVRFs identified in this study, as significant higher odds ratio of CVRFs in the most deprived population compared with the least deprived population. A meta-analysis of nine studies showed an association between lower SES and hypertension (pooled odds ratio = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.27–2.79), diabetes (1.90, 1.25–2.87), obesity (1.57, 0.95–2.59) and dyslipidaemia (3.68, 2.03–6.64) [22]. The higher relative measurements of odds ratio compared with the RII could be explained by (1) the lack of weighting of the middle population whose SES is between the most and the least deprivation; (2) the different time coverage between period prevalence in this study and the one-time point measured prevalence from the surveys; (3) different validity of CVRFs information derived from survey data and EHR data. Another distinctive difference is for dyslipidaemia, as either in the population with or without OA, no significant SII in the population with OA or even reverse SII in the population without OA for the prevalence of dyslipidaemia was revealed after controlling age, sex, geographical region and period effect, which could be explained by the higher comorbidities in the deprived population which potentially trigger the CVD management at primary care settings (for example, statin prescription for CVD management) [21].

Comparisons of socioeconomic inequality in the prevalence of CVRFs between the population with OA and a comparable control population without OA were seldomly processed in prior studies. One prior study presented that the gap in high comorbidity counts (≥ 6 clinical conditions) was greater in primary care consulters with OA (number of cases = 951 for low SES VS. 692 for high SES) compared with non-OA individuals (565 VS. 552) with the use of survey data in England and Wales in 1991/1992,7 and a more recent study reporting a greater gap in the onset of self-reported cognitive impairment in primary care consulters with OA (43.3% for those with inadequate income VS. 29.7% for those with adequate income) than those without OA (20.2% VS. 17.1%) [31]. Both studies might suggest that socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of common health indicators are more common in the population with OA than in those without OA.

The specific subgroup analysis by age in the current study revealed the socioeconomic inequality gap in the number of CVRFs in the English population aged 35–64 years, and especially more common among those with OA. This might suggest that the number of CVRFs is more common in younger deprived populations, especially the younger deprived population with newly diagnosed OA. Considering the potential healthy life expectancy and future population-level health burden, strict CVD management might be considered in the primary care settings targeting the young, deprived population with newly diagnosed OA.

As the first study to investigate the temporal trend of socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of single and number of CVRFs in the population with and without OA in England over 1992–2017, the current study revealed that the socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of single and number of CVRF consistently exist both in the population with and without OA and widened over the recent years in England over 1992–2017, with the inequality consistently more common in the population with OA than the population without OA. This is consistent with the recent findings of the increased prevalence of CVRFs in the deprived English population over the past decades [32]. As the CVRFS and comorbidities are more likely to be clustered in the population with OA, these joint findings would partly explain the more common socioeconomic inequalities in CVRFs observed in the population with OA over recent years.

This study is the first to quantify the socioeconomic inequality in CVRFs among consulters for OA and to provide comparisons of the inequality between consulters with and without OA over two decades based on the nationally representative primary care EHRs. The study suggested that routinely collected data in England from a large primary care database could be a good source to longitudinally surveillance socioeconomic inequality for routinely recorded health indicators either in the general population or the population with specific conditions with efficiency and validity. The measurements of absolute and relative socioeconomic inequalities were made by the toolbox recently developed by OHID, which makes the estimations comparable to national estimations in terms of methodology.

Several limitations should be addressed when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the SES measured as IMD decile provides overall socioeconomic information at the neighbourhood level which are not exact individual-level socioeconomic measurements like occupation, income, and education. Second, there would be some unmeasured confounders that might have an impact on socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of CVRFS. Third, coding quality might vary over primary care settings within socioeconomic neighbourhoods, which might be relevant to under-diagnoses or under-recording bias. Moreover, matching at the GP practice level, while intended to minimise differences in clinical recording, may have partially balanced deprivation between groups, potentially attenuating the observed socioeconomic inequalities. Finally, a common concern for using primary care records is misclassification however other studies suggest that this may be minimal with consistent prevalence estimates in modifiable CVRFs between CPRD GOLD and other data resources such as population surveys and secondary care databases [19, 33].

IMD2015 is a stable measure of socioeconomic deprivation, encompassing multiple domains such as income, education, and health. Although its stability strengthens its use in this study, there may still be potential insights from examining shifts in IMD over time. These shifts might influence the causal association between socioeconomic inequality (as measured by SII) and CVRFs. However, this study does not aim to explore such causal relationships, and we acknowledge this as a limitation. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to investigate these aspects further. Second, using IMD at the index date as a single measure of socioeconomic position does not capture any changes in deprivation over the life course, which may have already influenced CVRF clustering prior to cohort entry. Finally, as the IMD measure at index date likely captures cumulative socioeconomic disadvantage up to that point, the observed inequalities in CVRF clustering may primarily reflect the influence of long-standing deprivation, aligning with a life course perspective on cardiovascular risk accumulation.While our study does not emphasise direct interventions to reduce socioeconomic deprivation, targeted strategies such as smoking cessation programs have been shown to effectively address CVRFs in deprived populations. Highlighting these approaches offers practical solutions for reducing health inequalities without solely focusing on altering deprivation itself.

In this study, we analysed the clustering of cardiovascular risk factors (CVRFs) using thresholds of ≥ 1, ≥2, and ≥ 3 CVRFs, an approach commonly used in previous research. While analyzing exact combinations of CVRFs (e.g., smoking + obesity + hypertension) could offer additional insights, the sparsity of certain patterns and associated uncertainties precluded meaningful analysis. Moreover, given the descriptive and non-causal nature of this study, clustering rather than specific patterns was more appropriate for achieving the study’s objectives. Future studies with larger sample sizes could explore exact combinations of CVRFs to provide further insights into their role in health inequalities.

While this study utilised a retrospective cohort design, which allows for temporal alignment between the assessment of CVRFs and the diagnosis of OA, the potential for reverse causality remains. CVRFs such as obesity, hypertension, and diabetes are established risk factors for OA, but OA may also contribute to worsening CVRFs through reduced mobility, systemic inflammation, and lifestyle changes. This bidirectional relationship complicates causal interpretation. Although the retrospective design provides temporal context, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive rather than causal. Future prospective studies or interventional research are warranted to clarify the causal pathways between CVRFs, OA, and socioeconomic inequalities.

Conclusions

In conclusion, socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of modifiable CVRFs were very common in populations with and without OA between 1992 and 2017 in England and widened in the population with OA, especially for smoking, T2DM, obesity, and number of CVRFs. In the younger population with OA, the increased socioeconomic inequalities in the prevalence of modifiable CVRFs suggests that the early onset of number of CVRFs in the young, deprived population could be a concern for health policymakers in terms of future loss of healthy life expectancy, and health burden due to the disability. More prevention strategies and intensive care strategies should target this specific deprived population.

Declarations

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The Clinical Practice Research Datalink (CPRD) is an ongoing primary care database of anonymised medical records from general practitioners, with coverage of over 19 million patients from 937 practices in the UK. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Protocol approval was received from the CPRD Independent Scientific Advisory Committee (ISAC), the ISAC number was 18_031R2. Informed consent was not required for conducting this study, as the CPRD database provides anonymised data from medical records, so it is not possible to identify patients from GP practice in CPRD database.
