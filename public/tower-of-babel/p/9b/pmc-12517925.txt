Risk factors for secondary fractures in postmenopausal osteoporosis: A retrospective study
Authors: Haiwan Zhang, Yuchen Du, Lan Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Osteoporosis, the most common metabolic bone disease, leads to fragility fractures that cause acute pain, functional impairment, and disability, premature mortality. Although risk factors for initial fractures in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis are well-documented in previous studies, predictors of secondary fractures have not been thoroughly investigated. This study aimed to investigate risk factors for secondary fractures in postmenopausal osteoporosis patients. We conducted a retrospective study of patients with postmenopausal osteoporosis-related fractures admitted to Hunan Aerospace Hospital between January 2018 and December 2023. Participants were divided into the case group (patients with secondary fractures) and the control group (patients with a single primary fracture event). The incidence of fractures peaked during the winter season (29.8%), with the vertebral body being the most commonly affected site (80.4%). We collected comprehensive patient data, including demographic and clinical characteristics, laboratory measurements, comorbidities, lifestyle factors, and treatment data. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to identify independent risk factors among variables showing significant association (P < .05) in univariate analyses. Age, body mass index (BMI), bone mineral density (BMD) T-score, hemoglobin levels, diabetes, ophthalmic diseases, prior surgical history, and basic treatment were chosen for inclusion in the multivariate logistic regression model. And 4 independent factors were associated with secondary fractures: low BMI (OR = 0.726, 95% CI: 0.618–0.953; P = .018), reduced BMD T-score (OR = 0.441, 95% CI: 0.218–0.770; P = .005), diabetes (OR = 3.312, 95% CI: 1.097–9.992; P = .032), and ophthalmic diseases (OR = 7.365, 95% CI: 1.156–45.535; P = .033). Our study highlights the combined association of metabolic factors (low BMI, diabetes), skeletal fragility (reduced BMD), and visual impairment (ophthalmic diseases) with secondary fracture risk in postmenopausal women. The identification of these modifiable risk factors may be important in developing strategies to improve patients’ quality of life and reduce secondary fracture incidence and healthcare burdens in the future.

1. Introduction

Postmenopausal osteoporosis, a prevalent metabolic bone disorder, is characterized by decreased bone mineral density (BMD) and deterioration of trabecular architecture, which results in an elevated risk of fractures among older women. This condition results from a complex interaction of genetic, hormonal, and environmental factors, with estrogen deficiency playing a central role.[1] Epidemiological studies show that 1 in 3 women over 50 years of age will experience an osteoporotic fracture. Osteoporotic fractures negatively affect the quality of life of postmenopausal women and increase their mortality. Several studies have indicated that hip fracture survivors often experience a decline in functional independence, with 40% unable to walk without assistance, 80% restricted in other activities of daily living, and approximately 33% entirely dependent or in a nursing home. Additionally, hip fractures are associated with a first-year mortality rate of 20% to 24% and more than a 2-fold increase in mortality.[2,3]

In recent years, advancements have been made in osteoporosis management and the assessment of fracture risk factors. According to orthopedics clinical experience, refracture is usual. The study suggests that patients with osteoporotic fracture have an increased possibility of additional fracture, with an approximate 2-fold risk of recurrence rate. However, few studies have addressed risk factors for postmenopausal osteoporosis second fractures separately.

Previous studies indicated that risk factors for osteoporosis include low body weight, certain comorbidities, glucocorticoid use, and low levels of BMD.[4] So we hypothesized that factors, such as bone fragility(reduced BMD), metabolic status (low BMI, diabetes), sensory impairment (ophthalmic disease), and prior treatment, would be associated with the risk of secondary fractures in postmenopausal osteoporosis.

In this study, we aim to: identify the clinical characteristics and independent risk factors associated with secondary fractures in postmenopausal osteoporosis; provide evidence to guide clinical decision-making; and explore new research directions to reduce secondary fracture rates and improve prevention strategies in this population.

2. Methods and materials

2.1. Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective study with data extracted from electronic medical records. Participants were selected using the following criteria: postmenopausal women with osteoporotic fractures; admitted to the Orthopedic Department of Hunan Aerospace Hospital between January 2018 and December 2023; complete clinical records available for analysis. Patients with pathological fractures, high-energy trauma-related fractures, fractures associated with neoplastic lesions, or other metabolic bone disorders were excluded.

Participants were divided into 2 groups based on fracture recurrence during the 5-year study period: the control group, consisting of patients with a single fracture event and complete clinical data; and the secondary fracture group, comprising patients who experienced an initial fracture, subsequently complicated by a secondary fracture within the 5-year study period.

2.2. Data collection

Patient data were comprehensively collected, including age, hospital stay, body mass index (BMI), systolic and diastolic blood pressure, the season of fracture occurrence, and the fracture site. Laboratory measurements included hemoglobin, serum albumin, alanine aminotransferase, blood uric acid, blood potassium, blood glucose, serum creatinine levels.

BMD was assessed using dual X-ray absorptiometry scans, with T-scores representing the minimum value obtained from these assessments. Comorbidities, such as hypertension, diabetes, renal insufficiency, cardiovascular disorders, ostarthritis, ophthalmic diseases, and nervous system diseases were meticulously recorded. Lifestyle factors, including smoking history and alcoholism history, were also documented.

Treatment data included basic treatment (calcium and vitamin D supplementation), anti-osteoporosis treatment (bisphosphonates, RANKL inhibitors or calcitonin), and surgical treatment.

2.3. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago). Normally distributed continuous variables (confirmed by the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test) were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and subjected to independent Student’s t tests for comparison. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies (percentages) and analyzed through chi-square or Fisher exact tests. Variables with P < .05 in univariate analyses were included in a multivariate logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors for secondary fractures. Adjusted odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were computed. A P-value of <0.05 was regarded as indicative of statistical significance.

2.4. Ethics approval

All procedures adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hunan Aerospace Hospital, and all procedures adhered to pertinent guidelines and regulations. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

3. Results

3.1. Study population and baseline characteristics

A total of 107 patients were included in the final analysis. And participants were stratified into the secondary fracture group (n = 51) and the control group (n = 56). A flowchart illustrating patient selection and grouping is presented as Figure 1. The included patients aged 51 to 100 years (mean age: 78.81 ± 4.44 years). Fracture incidence exhibited seasonal variation, with 32 cases (29.9%) occurring in winter, followed by 27 cases (25.2%) in spring, 26 cases (24.3%) in summer, and 22 cases (20.6%) in autumn. Vertebral fractures were the most common fracture type (P < .001), and the BMD T-score was −3.29 ± 0.26. Surgical treatment was performed in 92 patients (86.0%), while 15 patients (14.0%) received nonsurgical management. Basic treatment was performed in 76 patients (71.0%). Anti-osteoporosis therapy was performed in 70 patients (65.4%) (Table 1).

No significant differences were observed between the case and control groups in hospital stay, blood pressure, serum albumin, alanine aminotransferase, blood uric acid, blood potassium, blood glucose, or serum creatinine levels (P > .05). However, significant differences were noted in age, BMI, BMD T-scores, and hemoglobin levels (P < .05) (Table 2).

3.3. Comorbidities and lifestyle factors

The prevalence of hypertension, renal insufficiency, cardiovascular diseases, ostarthritis, neurological disorders, smoking history, and alcoholism history did not differ significantly between groups (P > .05). In contrast, the case group exhibited higher rates of diabetes (33.33% vs 17.86%, P < .05), ophthalmic diseases (17.65% vs 3.57%, P < .05), and prior surgical history (49.01% vs 32.14%, P < .05) compared to the control group (Table 2).

3.4. Treatment patterns

Among the 51 case group patients, 42 (82.35%) received basic treatments (calcium and vitamin D supplementation), while 34 (66.66%) underwent anti-osteoporosis therapy. In the control group (n = 56), 33 patients (58.93%) received basic treatments, and 37 (66.07%) were administered anti-osteoporosis therapy. A significant difference was observed in basic treatment rates between groups (P < .05), but not in anti-osteoporosis therapy rates (P > .05). Surgical intervention was performed in 47 case group patients (92.16%) and 48 control group patients (85.71%), with no significant difference in surgical treatment distribution (P = .282) (Table 2).

3.5. Multivariate logistic regression analysis

Multivariate analysis identified ophthalmic diseases (OR = 7.365, 95% CI: 1.156–45.535; P = .033) and diabetes (OR = 3.312, 95% CI: 1.097–9.992; P = .032) as independent risk factors for secondary fractures. Conversely, higher BMD T-scores (OR = 0.441, 95% CI: 0.218–0.770; P = .005) and BMI (OR = 0.726, 95% CI: 0.618–0.953; P = .018) were associated with a decreased risk of secondary fractures (Table 3).

4. Discussion

Osteoporosis, a prevalent skeletal disorder, significantly increases fracture risk and affects over 200 million individuals worldwide, posing a growing public health challenge as global aging intensifies.[5] Fragility fractures, the main clinical consequence of osteoporosis, are linked to psychological distress, secondary fracture risk, functional independence loss, activities of daily living impairment, and elevated mortality.[6] While numerous studies have identified risk factors for initial osteoporotic fractures, research on predictors of secondary fractures in postmenopausal women remains limited. Our study addresses this gap by investigating risk factors for secondary fractures in this population, and further investigation into whether interventions targeting these associated factors can reduce secondary fracture incidence is warranted.

Our findings revealed the incidence of osteoporotic fractures in winter is the highest among all seasons. This aligns with previous studies, which suggested that insufficient sunlight exposure during winter, leading to a deficiency in vitamin D, increases the risk of fractures.[7] Additionally, the wet and slippery roads due to snow and rain, reduced visibility, and the increased clothing worn by the elderly in cold weather, which further diminishes their coordination and mobility, may also contribute significantly to the higher risk of falls and consequently, the increased prevalence of fractures.

Several studies have suggested age as a primary predictor of secondary fracture, though study outcomes present apparent discrepancies. Wen-Dong Ruan et al demonstrated a progressive elevation in secondary fracture risk with advancing age, particularly noting an 85% increased risk in patients aged > 85 years compared to those over 75 years.[3] Conversely, Melton LJ 3rd revealed a decline in secondary fracture incidence with increasing age following initial fracture events, potentially due to reduced life expectancy and higher postfracture mortality.[8] In our study, age had no significant association with the secondary fractures of osteoporosis, possibly due to the limited sample size. Future studies with larger cohorts are needed to further investigate and clarify this relationship.

Our study identified low BMI as a significant risk factor for secondary fractures, with each unit increase in BMI associated with a 0.766-fold reduction in fracture risk. However, the relationship between BMI and osteoporotic fractures is complex and nonlinear. Studies suggests that the impact of BMI on the risk of osteoporotic fractures may not be significant within a BMI range of 22 to 26 kg/m²,[9] while obesity appears protective against osteopenia and osteoporosis compared to normal or overweight status.[10] The potential mechanisms likely involve the combined regulation of nutrient uptake, energy expenditure, and bone conversion by central and peripheral nerve factors.[11] Notably, high BMI may inhibit osteoclast bone resorption, thereby preserving BMD.[12] These findings highlight the importance of maintaining appropriate body weight in postmenopausal women to mitigate fracture risk.

Our results corroborate previous studies demonstrating a strong association between diabetes and fracture risk. A cohort study of 5469 Chinese individuals with type 2 diabetes (T2D) revealed a significantly higher hip fracture incidence in diabetic participants compared to nondiabetic controls. Postmenopausal women with osteoporotic fractures and T2D demonstrated a 3.31-fold risk of subsequent fractures versus nondiabetic participants.[13] Additionally, Li et al indicated a significant association between heightened blood glucose levels and an increased risk of fractures; notably, when HbA1c surpassed 9%, the risk of hip fractures increased to over 50%.[14] Several potential mechanisms have been proposed to elucidate this phenomenon, including microvascular complications associated with diabetes, such as neuropathy and retinopathy, which may elevate fracture risk due to gait disturbances and an increased likelihood of falls.[15] Moreover, chronic hyperglycemia may promote nonenzymatic interactions between glucose and proteins, leading to the formation of advanced glycation end products which might lead to bone microstructural alterations.[16] Additionally, certain antidiabetic medications, including insulin, sulfonylureas, pioglitazone, and canagliflozin, may exacerbate fracture risk and should be used cautiously in high-risk patients.

In our study, ophthalmic diseases is a significant risk factor for secondary fractures. For patients with osteoporosis, the deterioration or loss of vision can significantly impair their ability to judge their surroundings, making them more prone to falls and increasing the risk of secondary fractures. Yu et al indicated that visual impairment serves as a significant risk factor for contralateral hip refractures among individuals over the age of 80 with intertrochanteric femoral fractures.[17] These results highlight the importance of integrating vision assessments into fall prevention strategies for osteoporotic patients.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective design and single-center approach may restrict the applicability of the findings to broader populations. Furthermore, the limited sample size and short follow-up duration might have hindered the identification of further risk factors. Therefore, it is essential to conduct future multicenter prospective studies with longer observation periods and more extensive participant groups to confirm our results. Crucially, due to the observational and retrospective nature of this study, the identified relationships represent associations and cannot establish causation. Confirming causal links between these factors and secondary fracture risk requires randomized controlled trial designs or analytical methods specifically addressing causality.

5. Conclusion

This study identifies low BMI, reduced BMD, diabetes, and ophthalmic diseases as independent risk factors for secondary fractures in postmenopausal osteoporotic patients. The findings suggest that focusing on these modifiable factors (nutritional status, glycemic control, vision care, fall prevention) may be a valuable component of comprehensive management strategies aimed at reducing secondary fracture rates in this population. Further research is needed to confirm these associations and evaluate the efficacy of targeted interventions.
