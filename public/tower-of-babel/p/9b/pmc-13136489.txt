LoRA-enhanced whisper for resource-efficient heliox speech recognition
Authors: Weichang Mao, Haojie Gu, Jia He, Yu Li, Shifeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn saturation diving, reliable speech communication under helium–oxygen (Heliox) conditions is critical for operational safety and efficiency. Heliox speech exhibits severe acoustic mismatch relative to standard air speech, and recognition performance further degrades in the presence of chamber/environmental noise and domain-specific terminology. To study this problem in a realistic setting, we collected Heliox speech recordings at two saturation conditions (12 m and 25 m equivalent depths) and constructed a corresponding dataset. We then adapt Whisper-large-v3 via Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) to enable parameter-efficient domain adaptation, and enhance decoding using practical inference-time components, including hotword biasing, language-model (LM) reranking, test-time augmentation (TTA) with speed perturbation, and rolling context prompts, together with chunked decoding for stable deployment. On our Heliox evaluation sets, the proposed system achieved a character error rate (CER) of 4.725% at a water depth of 12 m and a CER of 7.165% at a water depth of 25 m, under the reported decoding configuration, while maintaining practical inference cost on GPU/CPU server platforms. We note that inference-time strategies provide complementary robustness gains but do not fully eliminate the need for domain adaptation under severe Heliox-induced shifts.Keywords: Helium speech recognition, LoRA, Whisper, Hotword bias, LM rerankingSubject terms: Engineering, Ocean sciences

Introduction

In contemporary times, the technological development of human society has reached a new level. It is no longer limited to exploring land, but has expanded to outer space and the deep sea. Deep-sea exploration, as a key area for humans to expand their cognitive boundaries and develop strategic resources, has immeasurable scientific and economic value. The ocean covers more than 70% of the Earth’s surface and contains rich biodiversity, mineral resources and energy potential. The deep sea areas, in particular, are considered to be an important space for future energy supply and marine science research.

However, the extreme environment of high pressure, low temperature and darkness in the deep sea poses severe challenges to human activities. In complex operations such as deep-sea exploration, seabed resource exploitation, marine engineering construction, and underwater rescue, equipment such as unmanned remotely operated vehicles, manned submersibles, and atmospheric diving equipment cannot replace divers in carrying out sophisticated operations in confined underwater spaces, and divers must go directly into the water to perform tasks. To this end, deep-sea saturation diving technology came into being, enabling divers to live and work in deep-sea high-pressure environments for a long time, greatly expanding the depth and duration of human deep-sea diving operations.

During this process, the choice of breathing gas is crucial to ensure diver safety and work efficiency. Heliox is currently the only breathing gas of choice for deep-diving operations due to its inertness, low density, and anti-anesthesia properties. However, due to the low density of helium and the influence of the high-pressure environment, the speed of the diver’s voice will increase significantly, seriously changing the resonance characteristics of the human voice, forming the so-called “helium speech”, causing voice distortion.

Helium speech is characterized by a significant upward shift in resonance peak frequency, abnormal pitch, and nasal sound1. The clarity of vowels and consonants is severely reduced, and it sounds similar to “duck calls” or “Donald Duck”2,3. It seriously affects the speech communication between divers and between divers and surface support personnel, and poses serious challenges to the success of underwater operations and even the life safety of divers.

Traditional helium speech correction methods4–6 suffer from nonlinear distortion, limited robustness, and functional rigidity. Deep learning approaches, while promising, are hindered by data dependency, high computational demands, and interpretability issues.To address these challenges, we leverage the Whisper-large-v3 model7 with Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA)8 for efficient fine-tuning on helium-oxygen speech. We further enhance decoding accuracy through three inference-time strategies:

Hotword bias incorporates domain-specific priors to emphasize technical terms, reducing recognition errors on helium–oxygen terminology.

These strategies are integrated into a lightweight decoding framework, which additionally employs a rolling context prompts to maintain inter-chunk coherence by reusing the tail of previous outputs, thereby preventing semantic drift and boundary errors. The complete solution significantly boosts transcription accuracy and robustness in challenging deep-sea helium environments.

Related works

Our proposed helium speech recognition framework is built on two core technical pillars: an advanced Whisper-based ASR backbone that provides robust end-to-end speech–text mapping, and LoRA-based lightweight fine-tuning for efficient domain adaptation under few-shot constraints. The following subsections briefly introduce these foundational techniques.

Automatic speech recognition

In recent years, research in the field of automatic speech recognition (ASR) has focused on model performance optimization, scene adaptation, and efficiency improvement9–12. The Whisper model we use boasts high robustness for complex real-world environments and practicality for multi-task integration. As shown in Fig. 1, its recognition process is as follows: the input audio is converted into a log-Mel spectrogram through feature extraction; the encoder encodes the acoustic features through a self-attention mechanism; and the decoder generates text in an autoregressive manner, guided by the encoded output. This model is pre-trained on large-scale multilingual data and exhibits strong robustness. Based on Whisper, this paper adapts it to speech recognition tasks in specific scenarios through efficient parameter fine-tuning and decoding optimization.

Proposed framework

We develop an end-to-end helium–oxygen speech recognition system built upon the large-scale pre-trained Whisper model. The overall framework is illustrated in Fig. 3. During training, we adopt a leave-one-speaker-out cross-validation strategy to fine-tune Whisper’s LoRA parameters while preserving cross-speaker generalization. During inference, a lightweight decoding enhancement pipeline is integrated into the fine-tuned model, combining hotword bias for keyword precision, TTA for acoustic robustness, LM reranking for linguistic coherence, and rolling context prompts for cross-segment consistency. Specifically, each audio chunk is time-scaled via TTA, decoded with beam search under hotword bias, and all candidates across TTA beam are rescored by LM reranking to produce the final transcription.

Experiment

Dataset collection

Saturation diving process description

Data were collected in a ship-mounted mobile saturation diving system that accommodates four divers. During saturation diving operations, saturation divers enter the shipboard saturation diving chamber and close the hatch. The chamber is pressurized to the target operating depth. Pressurization stops once the target depth pressure is reached, allowing personnel to acclimatize. When underwater operations are required, personnel enter the transition chamber through the main cabin, closing the doors to both. The transition chamber is then connected to the diving bell, which is pressurized to the same pressure as the transition chamber. The hatch between the transition chamber and the diving bell is opened, and personnel enter the diving bell, closing both doors. The shipboard platform lowers the diving bell, detaching it from the saturation diving chamber, and lowers it to the designated depth in the target sea area. Once the pressure inside the diving bell reaches equilibrium with the underwater pressure, the diver opens the bell door and exits to begin underwater operations.

After completing underwater operations, the diver returns to the diving bell, closes the bell door, and the shipboard platform lifts the diving bell back to the platform. The diving bell is then docked with the transition chamber. After pressure equilibrium is reached, the doors are opened, and the diver enters the transition chamber, closing the hatch between the transition chamber and the diving bell. After the transition cabin and the main cabin are pressure balanced, the hatch between the transition cabin and the main cabin is opened, and personnel return to the main cabin to live and wait for the next operation or decompression and exit the cabin.

Gas mixture and pressure conditions

During saturation exposure, the oxygen partial pressure (PO) was controlled at approximately 40 kPa for safety. Two representative working conditions were recorded after the chamber pressure had stabilized:

(i)12 m equivalent depth at 0.12 MPa with 21% O /79% He, yielding PO kPa;

12 m equivalent depth at 0.12 MPa with 21% O /79% He, yielding PO kPa;

(ii)25 m equivalent depth at 0.25 MPa with 14.28% O /85.72% He, yielding PO kPa.

25 m equivalent depth at 0.25 MPa with 14.28% O /85.72% He, yielding PO kPa.

The O concentration was adjusted accordingly to maintain a stable PO during pressurization and decompression.

Recording chain and acoustic environment

Speech was captured using a TASCAM DR-05X recorder connected to the chamber wall intercom output. Audio was recorded at 96 kHz sampling rate and 24-bit quantization. No dedicated front-end gain or anti-clipping strategy was enabled. The dominant background noise originated from the chamber environmental control system (ventilation fans), together with typical enclosure reverberation.

Data organization and split strategy

To avoid speaker leakage and over-optimistic evaluation, the corpus was split by speaker and session into training/validation/test sets. All evaluations were conducted separately for each depth condition (12 m and 25 m). Key reproducible parameters (gas composition, chamber pressure, recording chain, and noise sources) are summarized in Table 1. Since data acquisition under real heliox saturation conditions is highly constrained, the raw recordings contain approximately 3 min of speech per speaker. To increase acoustic coverage and obtain sufficient training material, we applied manual data augmentation to expand the effective speech duration to about 30 min per speaker. The augmentation includes (i) speaking-rate perturbation, (ii) segment-level cropping and concatenation, and (iii) mixing with recorded seabed background noise at controlled SNRs. We emphasize that augmentation is applied only to the training portion, while validation/test sets remain strictly non-overlapping by speaker to avoid leakage.

Implementation details

All experiments were conducted on a computational node equipped with an NVIDIA A40 GPU (46GB memory) using CUDA 11.8 and cuDNN 9.1 for hardware acceleration. The software environment was built on Python 3.8.20 within a Conda environment, utilizing PyTorch 2.4.1 as the deep learning framework. Key libraries included Hugging Face Transformers 4.39.3, Datasets 3.1.0, Accelerate 0.31.0, and PEFT 0.11.1 for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. Audio processing was handled by Librosa 0.11.0 and SoundFile 0.12.1, with FFmpeg 4.4.2 providing backend support. The system ran on Linux kernel 5.15 with 16 logical CPU cores and 62.63GB of available RAM.

Both adaptation strategies substantially improve accuracy, reducing CER by nearly an order of magnitude. Notably, LoRA achieves the best CER at both depths (12 m: 0.07555, 25 m: 0.09038) while updating only 7.86M parameters (0.51%), demonstrating strong parameter efficiency. Compared with full fine-tuning, LoRA provides similar (or slightly better) recognition performance with drastically fewer trainable parameters, which is especially beneficial when computational resources or training data are limited, or when frequent re-adaptation to new acoustic conditions is required.

From a deployment perspective, Table 2 also reports inference cost. LoRA maintains comparable inference efficiency (RTF 0.24) and similar GPU peak memory (around 22.7,GB) to full fine-tuning, suggesting that parameter-efficient adaptation does not introduce additional inference overhead in our setting. Overall, these results support the main claim that LoRA offers an effective and resource-efficient solution for Heliox ASR, achieving competitive error rates (often best) with minimal trainable parameters and practical inference cost.

As shown in Table 3, at a distance of 12 m, increasing the beam size from 4, to 6, to 8 leaves the CER unchanged at 0.07555, while latency rises almost linearly from 23.38 s to 28.64 s and 33.05 s. This indicates that a beam size of 4 in the first-pass search suffices to capture most recoverable hypotheses; wider beams merely increase computation without improving recognition accuracy. Although beam 8 offers no accuracy benefit, it is retained in the 12 m ablation setup (Table 4) to enhance N-best diversity and better isolate the effects of TTA and LM reranking. The optimal hotword bias is 1.2. Increasing it to 1.6 or 2.0 degrades performance, raising the CER to 0.08536 and 0.08031 respectively, with a negligible change in runtime. Separately, TTA alone provides no accuracy improvement (CER remains at 0.07555) but increases the inference time by roughly 2.7, from 33.05 s to 89.02 s.

At 25 m, where acoustic distortion is more severe, a different pattern is observed. A beam size of 6 achieves the optimal balance between accuracy and latency, yielding a CER of 0.09038 in 25.59 s. This represents an 18% error reduction compared to beam 4 (0.11028 0.09038), while matching the accuracy of beam 8 at a lower latency (29.41 s). Thus, beam 6 lies on the accuracy-latency Pareto frontier by being sufficiently broad to capture meaningful hypotheses while remaining narrow enough to filter out redundant or noisy candidates. The optimal hotword bias is again 1.2, and increasing it to 2.0 causes a sharp performance degradation (CER = 0.14447). Consistent with the 12 m results, TTA alone offers no accuracy gain but increases runtime by approximately 2.5.

The ablation study at 12 m (Table 4) further confirms that LM reranking is the most efficient enhancement. It reduces CER by 19.46% (from 0.07555 to 0.06085) with only a 0.6% increase in latency (33.05 s 33.24 s). When combined with TTA, the additional hypothesis diversity enables the reranker to achieve the best performance at this depth, yielding a CER of 0.04725–a 37.47% reduction from the baseline—at approximately 2.6 the baseline runtime. In summary, TTA alone is ineffective, but when paired with reranking it produces significant gains when computational resources permit.

Under high distortion at 25 m (Table 5), LM reranking remains the principal source of improvement, reducing CER by 20.72% (from 0.09038 to 0.07165) with virtually no latency cost (25.59 s 25.43 s). In contrast, adding TTA to reranking yields no further accuracy gain (CER remains 0.07165) while increasing runtime by about 2.5. This suggests that, under noisy conditions, the reranker already exploits all useful diversity within the N-best list, leaving little for TTA to contribute.

Based on these findings, we recommend a depth- and latency-aware decoding strategy. The n-gram order should be fixed at 4, and the hotword bias set to 1.2 universally. LM reranking should always be enabled, as it delivers the highest accuracy improvement per unit of latency. For 12 m recordings, beam sizes between 4 and 6 are advised, with a preference for beam 4 in real-time applications. At 25 m, beam 6 is optimal. TTA should be disabled by default and activated only in moderately distorted, latency-tolerant scenarios, such as offline batch processing, where its synergy with reranking can be fully leveraged.

In conclusion, the experiments reveal a consistent and practical trend. LM reranking provides a universal and nearly cost-free accuracy boost across depths, whereas TTA is conditionally beneficial—useful only in moderate-distortion environments similar to 12 m and when adequate computational budget is available. Adopting this adaptive policy captures the majority of attainable accuracy gains while preserving overall system responsiveness.

Conclusion

We proposed a Heliox ASR framework that adapts Whisper via LoRA and improves decoding with lightweight inference-time components, including hotword bias, LM re-ranking, TTA, and rolling context prompts. Controlled evaluations at 12,m and 25,m show consistent CER reductions, where LM re-ranking yields the largest gain under our configuration, while TTA provides depth-dependent improvements at the cost of increased latency. Overall, the framework is parameter-efficient and practical for deployment on GPU/CPU server platforms, improving recognition of domain-specific terminology under real Heliox saturation-system recording conditions.

A key limitation is that inference-time techniques cannot fully compensate for severe Heliox-induced acoustic shift: although beam search tuning, hotword bias, and TTA can provide training-free robustness gains, the poor zero-shot baseline indicates that domain adaptation remains necessary. Future work will explore semi-supervised or unsupervised adaptation to further reduce labeling requirements, while retaining these inference-time strategies as lightweight deployment modules.
