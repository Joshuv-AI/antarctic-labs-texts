Phylogeny, taxonomy and intraspecific variation of the rocky-intertidal trematode Opecoelus adsphaericus (Opecoelidae: Opecoelinae), a parasite of Clinocottus analis (Perciformes: Cottidae) from Baja California
Authors: Andrés Martínez-Aquino, Arturo Rincon-Sandoval, Carlos A Flores-Lopez, Gorgonio Ruiz-Campos, M Leopoldina Aguirre-Macedo, F Sara Ceccarelli
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe trematode Opecoelus adsphaericus is a common parasite of Clinocottus analis in rocky intertidal biotopes of southern California (USA) and northwestern Baja California (Mexico). Since the original description of O. adsphaericus, morphological and molecular data have been lacking. This study aims to characterize the morphological and molecular variation of this trematode to support future inter- and intra-specific comparisons in the genus Opecoelus. Over a year (August 2021–September 2022), ten sampling events were carried out to obtain biological material for morphometric and molecular analyses of O. adsphaericus at El Mosquito (n = 9) and Playa Piedra Blanca (n = 1), Baja California. Morphometric data were obtained from 44 specimens, along with Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) images. Intra-specific morphological variation was quantified using Principal Component Analysis (PCA). Eight nuclear 28S gene sequences were obtained for phylogenetic analysis (Bayesian Inference and Maximum Likelihood), while 27 mitochondrial Cytochrome C Oxidase subunit I gene (COI) sequences were generated to characterize population genetic variation. The phylogenetic analyses placed O. adsphaericus within the Opecoelinae clade of Opecoelidae, as a sister group to Opegaster. This relationship suggests that Opegaster may be synonymous with Opecoelus, although broader taxon sampling is required to test reciprocal monophyly and clarify generic boundaries. The PCA revealed marked morphological polymorphism in O. adsphaericus. Our morphological analysis detected morphometric characters among O. caballeroi, O. cameroni, and O. pacificus that largely overlap with those reported in this study and with the original description of O. adsphaericus, suggesting that these taxa may represent junior synonyms of O. adsphaericus. Based on the analyzed barcode dataset, O. adsphaericus shows high intraspecific genetic variation at both study locations. Future research on populations along the Baja California Peninsula and California may reveal greater genetic and morphological diversity.Keywords: COI, Molecular phylogenetics, Morphological polymorphism, PCA, SEM, 28S rDNA (D1–D2)

Introduction

The family Opecoelidae Ozaki, 1925 is the most prominent digenean family, comprising over 100 genera and nearly 1000 valid species, distributed across 12 subfamilies (Bray et al., 2016; Martin, 2024; WoRMS, 2025a). The subfamily Opecoelinae Ozaki, 1925 comprises approximately 240 species, distributed across 21 genera, which are reported as parasites of freshwater and marine fishes (Shimazu, 1988; Cribb, 2005; WoRMS, 2025b). Species of the genera Opecoelus Ozaki, 1925 and Opegaster Ozaki, 1928 have a complex taxonomic history, analyzed in multiple studies, with the number of valid species under debate (Manter, 1940, 1954; Crowcroft, 1946; Banerjee, 1965; Mehra, 1966; Prudhoe and Bray, 1973; Cribb, 1985; Bray, 1987; Shimazu, 1988; Bray and Cribb, 1989; Aken’Ova, 2007; Bray and Justine, 2013). Although morphological similarities between Opecoelus and Opegaster have historically led some authors to propose synonymy (Crowcroft, 1946; Cribb, 1985; Shimazu, 1988; Aken’Ova, 2007; Bray and Justine, 2013), this overlap largely reflects incomplete descriptions, subtle diagnostic characters, and scarce molecular data. Nevertheless, several studies (e.g., Cribb, 2005; Bray and Justine, 2013) emphasize maintaining them as separate genera until a robust phylogenetic framework is available.

Six taxa of the genus Opecoelus have been recorded in Mexican waters, all originating from the Pacific of the Baja California Peninsula, as parasites of 11 teleost species (see Supplementary Table S1). These include Opecoelus adsphaericus Manter and Van Cleave, 1951, Opecoelus cameroni (Caballero y C. and Caballero R., 1969) Aken’Ova, 2007, Opecoelus pacificus Caballero y C. and Caballero R., 1976, Opecoelus inimici Yamaguti, 1934, Opecoelus mexicanus Manter, 1940, and Opecoelus sp. (Manter, 1940; Arai, 1963; Alvarado-Villamar and Ruiz-Campos, 1992; Pérez-Ponce de León et al., 1999; Aguilar-Aguilar and Martorelli, 2024; Garduño-Martínez et al., 2025).

Particularly, O. adsphaericus is the first Opecoelus species described from the southern California Bight ecoregion (Spalding et al., 2007; Briggs and Bowen, 2012) as an intestinal parasite of two marine teleost fish, the Woolly Sculpin Clinoccotus analis Girard, 1958 (Perciformes, Cottidae) and the Opaleye Girella nigricans Ayres, 1860 (Centrarchiformes, Khyposidae) from La Jolla, California, USA. In the original description of O. adsphaericus, Manter and Van Cleave (1951) mention that the 11 specimens from G. nicricans differ in their measurements from the individuals measured from C. analis, but still consider them as the same species. However, it is not clear whether the original measurements were based only on individuals from C. analis, or if the ranges included measurements from G. nigricans as well. Opecoelus adsphaericus is the most prevalent adult parasite reported in C. analis from the southern California Bight (Manter and Van Cleave, 1951; Arai, 1963; Wells, 1986). Recently, this high-prevalence pattern was also observed in the same host species in the rocky intertidal zone of Punta Morro, Baja California, Mexico (Aguilar-Aguilar and Martorelli, 2024; Garduño-Martínez et al., 2025). Opecoelus adsphaericus is an ideal biological model for testing intraspecific morphological and molecular variation as a parasite associated with C. analis from the rocky intertidal zone of Baja California, because its year-round high prevalences allows for continuous sampling.

The morphological polymorphism in Platyhelminthes parasites has been studied through integrative taxonomy approaches and can be characterized by combining morphological and molecular analyses; e.g., Principal Component Analysis (PCA), phylogenetic tools with molecular data (i.e., DNA sequences) and morphological information from Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) images (e.g., Víllora-Montero et al., 2020; Martínez-Aquino et al., 2020; Corner et al., 2022).

This study aims to investigate the intraspecific morphological variation of O. adsphaericus, a parasite of C. analis, in the rocky intertidal zone of Baja California, using a combined data source approach, as in integrative taxonomy. Therefore, the aims of this study were to 1) provide a detailed morphological description of O. adsphaericus specimens and offer new morphometric data, SEM images, and molecular data (i.e., DNA sequences) to facilitate future inter and intra-specific systematic comparisons in Opecoelus; 2) determine the sister-group relations of O. adsphaericus within a phylogenetic framework of Opecoelidae using genetic data (DNA sequences) and 3) explore the intraspecific genetic variation of O. adsphaericus from two localities of the rocky intertidal zone in Baja California.

Materials and methods

Collection of woolly sculpin and trematodes

Trematode specimens used for morphological and molecular analysis in this study were collected from the intestine, pyloric caeca and stomach of C. analis, from El Mosquito (rocky intertidal), Todos Santos Bay, Ensenada, Baja California (31° 51′ 36.4″ N; 116° 38′ 55.5″ W). Samples were obtained from August 2021 to September 2022. Also, trematode specimens were collected from the same infection sites of C. analis in Piedra Blanca Beach (rocky intertidal), Ejido Eréndira, Ensenada, Baja California (31° 17′ 37.5″ N; 116° 24′ 55.1″ W) in August 2021. Following the recommendations of Horn et al. (1998), intertidal fish were collected during low tide conditions, identified in advance using MAR v.1.0 software (González, 2011). Fish sampling was carried out using manual aspersion pumps containing a solution of 20 % Quinaldine as anesthesia (Gibson, 1967, 1999). After 10 min, dip nets were used to remove the narcotized fish from the rocky tide pools. Fish were kept alive in separate 20 L containers with clean marine water and artificial aeration for transportation to the Evolutionary Biology of Parasites Lab, Facultad de Ciencias, Universidad Autónoma de Baja California, Ensenada campus. Fish hosts were examined within 24 h after capture. Freshly euthanized, unfrozen fish were examined to obtain suitable material for morphological studies and trematode identification. Total length (Tl), Standard length (Sl), and Height (H), were recorded for each fish using a caliper (±0.1 mm).

Each fish was dissected, and all internal organs were separated and placed in Petri dishes with 0.75 % saline, then observed under a stereomicroscope. Trematodes were isolated using a fine brush (No. 00), preliminarily identified and counted. Live, unflattened specimens were fixed in near-boiling 4 % formalin for morphological treatment and in 100 % ethanol for molecular analyses. They were stored in vials and cryotubes, labelled accordingly. Additionally, prevalence (% of infected hosts in a sample), mean abundance (mean number of parasites of a single species in the sample), and mean intensity (mean number of parasites per infected host) were assessed following Bush et al. (1997) and Reiczigel et al. (2019).

Morphological data

Trematode specimens were stained with hydrochloric carmine and mounted on permanent slides using Canada Balsam (Vidal-Martínez et al., 2002). Opecoelus specimens were observed using a Zeiss Primo Star (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) optical microscope with phase contrast. A digital camera (VE-LX1000, Velab Co., Texas, USA) was attached to the same microscope, and photomicrographs were captured using ImageView software v. 4.10.17614.20200822 (x64). Opecoelus specimens’ drawings were produced with the aid of a drawing tube attached to an Olympus BX50 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) microscope, equipped with differential interference contrast, and digitized in a Huion Inspiroy Q11K V2 digital drawing tablet using Adobe Illustrator 2023 (Adobe Inc., Portland, OR, USA). Several live specimens were examined in 0.75 % saline under a Zeiss Primo Star phase-contrast light microscope. Microvideos were recorded with a VE-LX1000 digital camera attached to the microscope, using ImageView software to document body contractions, ventral sucker activity, and the retractile digitiform papillae.

For SEM, eight Opecoelus specimens were fixed in 3 % glutaraldehyde with 0.2 M cacodylate buffer for 24 h, dehydrated in different ethanol concentrations (50 %, 70 %, 80 %, 90 %, 96 %, and 100 %) and dried at a critical point using CO2. Samples were mounted on metal stubs and covered with a layer of palladium-gold (Au-Pb). Finally, they were observed and photomicrographed on a Philips XLE30 ESEM microscope (FEI Company, Hillsboro, Oregon, USA). Opecoelus specimens were identified following/contrasting the taxonomic criteria of Manter and Van Cleave (1951), Caballero and Caballero, 1969, Caballero and Caballero, 1976, Caballero R. (1977), Cribb (2005), Jones et al. (2005), Aken’Ova (2007) and Madhavi and Bray (2018). The O. adsphaericus holotype and paratype described by Manter and Van Cleave (1951) (No. 1337837 and No. 1338191, respectively, now in the Smithsonian Institution National Museum of Natural History [NMNH] [ex USNM Helm. Col. No. 37143 and 37510, respectively] from C. analis from La Jolla, San Diego, California, USA) were studied to compare with the newly collected specimens. Furthermore, Opecoelus congeneric voucher specimens reported from the southern California Bight also were revised to compare with our samples, i.e., paratypes of Opecoelus caballeroi Caballero, 1977 and O. pacificus (No. 902 and 1012, respectively, Colección Nacional de Helmintos [CNHE], Instituto de Biología, Universidad Nacional Autónoma de México [IB-UNAM] [ex No. 227–9 y 227–14, in the same collection] from C. analis from Isla Catalina, California, USA). The holotypes of O. cameroni, O. pacificus and O. caballeroi described by Caballero and Caballero, 1969, Caballero and Caballero, 1976 and Caballero R. (1977), respectively, were not found in the CNHE-IB-UNAM and any scientific collections in the USA, Europe or Australia, and are considered lost.

In this study, several trematode specimens chosen for morphological analysis were to designated as vouchers for deposited DNA sequences specimens according to Pleijel et al. (2008); i.e., paragenophores (different individuals obtained from the same host, infection site, location, and/or sampling month). The paragenophores were deposited in the Helminthological Collection of CINVESTAV–Unidad Mérida (CHCM), Department of Marine Resources, Centro de Investigación y de Estudios Avanzados del Instituto Politécnico Nacional, under voucher numbers 707–708.2.

Morphometric data

Two hundred and twelve trematodes were permanently mounted in Canada Balsam. After mounting, all specimens were examined using a Zeiss Primo Star light microscope at magnifications of 400–1000x. Based on three observed size categories with relative morphological similarity, we selected intact specimens (i.e., not broken, contracted, stretched, wrinkled, or folded) suitable for morphological studies. The size categories were defined as follows: Small (<1000 μm), Medium (1000–2000 μm), and Large (2000–3200 μm). A total of 40 adult and gravid specimens from El Mosquito, representing the three size categories, were selected. Additionally, four adult gravid trematodes from Piedra Blanca Beach (two small and two medium-sized) were selected for morphological examination (Supplementary Table S2).

Morphological measurements were taken from digitalized images using ImageView and expressed in micrometers, with the range followed by the mean in parentheses. Based on the taxonomical diagnosis of the species of the genus Opecoelus (Cribb, 1985, 2005; Manter and Van Cleave, 1951; Caballero y C. and Caballero R. 1969; 1976; Caballero R. 1977), 24 morphological variables and three ratios (i.e., Sucker Ratio–Length, Sucker Ratio–Width, and Forebody-to-Hindbody Length Ratio) were taken from each specimen. The presence/absence of the anus was considered as a qualitative morphological character.

Multivariate morphometric analyses

A PCA was performed on 44 Opecoelus specimens collected during nine sampling events from two localities: 40 from El Mosquito (20 hosts, from eight sampling events [2–9]) and four from Piedra Blanca Beach (three hosts, from one sampling event). Opecoelus specimens were classified into three size categories (Supplementary Table S2), using 24 morphological variables to evaluate patterns of morphological variation (Table 1). Highly correlated morphological variables (>0.65) were removed using the Pearson correlation test.

DNA extraction, PCR and sequencing

To estimate the genetic variability of trematodes analyzed in this study, DNA was extracted from different individual worms as follows. 1) Samples were obtained from worms parasitizing different host individuals within the same locality (to avoid sequencing multiple worms from a single host) and from hosts of varying sizes. 2) Worms were selected from the intestine, pyloric cecum, and stomach of the same host individual as possible. 3) To detected genetic variability associated with size variation, trematode specimens were selected based on the same morphological categories; i.e., Small (<1000 μm), Medium (1000–2000 μm), and Large (2000–3200 μm). These specimens were analyzed by sequencing the mitochondrial Cytochrome C Oxidase subunit I gene (mtDNA COI), a universal molecular marker widely used in DNA barcoding approaches. The COI region was selected due to its rapid evolutionary rate, which enhances molecular differentiation (Avise, 2000; Hebert et al., 2003), and for its demonstrated effectiveness in trematode studies to resolve intra- and interspecific variation, delimit species boundaries, link metacercariae with adult stages, and clarify phylogenetic relationships (Moszczynska et al., 2009; Van Steenkiste et al., 2015; Montes et al., 2020, 2024, 2025; Kudlai et al., 2021; Vermaak et al., 2021, 2023; Truter et al., 2025).

DNA was extracted from 30 adult trematodes (Table 2; for details see Supplementary Table S3) using the DNeasy blood and tissue extraction kit (Qiagen, Valencia, CA, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. For eight trematodes for which sequence data were generated, the partial 28S ribosomal DNA (=lsrDNA region D1–D2, henceforth referred to as 28S) was amplified by PCR (Saiki et al., 1988), using the LSU-5 forward (5′-TAG GTC GAC CCG CTG AAY TTA AGC A- 3′) (Littlewood, 1994) and 1500 R reverse primers (5′ -GCT ATC CTG AGG GAA ACT TCG- 3′) (Olson et al., 2003; Tkach et al., 2003). For 27 trematodes, the COI was amplified by PCR, using the primers Dice1F forward (5′ -ATT AAC CCT CAC TAA ATT WCN TTR GAT CAT AAG- 3′) and Dice11 R reverse (5′ -TAA TAC GAC TCA CTA TAG CWG WAC HAA ATT THC GAT C- 3′) (Van Steenkiste et al., 2015). The reactions were prepared using the Green GoTaq Master Mix (Promega Inc., Madison, WI, USA). The PCR was performed using the Veriti 96-Well Thermal Cycler (Applied Biosystems, Inc., Foster City, CA, USA) with the following thermal protocol. For 28S, an initial denaturing step of 4 min at 94 °C, followed by 35 cycles of 94 °C for 45 s, 56 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 2 min, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min (present work). For COI, an initial denaturing step of 5 min at 94 °C, followed by 35 cycles of 92 °C for 30 s, 47 °C for 45 s, and 72 °C for 1 min, and a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min (Martínez-Aquino et al., 2020). The PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis in 1 % agarose gel using TAE 1X buffer and visualized in an Azure Imaging System C200 transilluminator (Azure Biosystems, Dublin, CA, USA). The purification and Sanger sequencing of the PCR products were carried out by Macrogen Inc. (South Korea; https://dna.macrogen.com) using an automated ABI 3730XL capillary sequencer. For each reaction, 5 μL of PCR product per well were submitted, along with the corresponding forward and reverse primers. Both strands of all genes (28S and COI) were sequenced for each individual sample to minimize sequencing errors, following Macrogen's standard procedures.

Phylogenetic reconstruction using 28S

To infer the position and determine sister-group relations of Opecoelus within an Opecoelidae phylogenetic framework, the 28S sequence data generated herein were aligned together with reference sequences from GenBank. The sequences included representatives of 49 genera belonging to 10 subfamilies of Opecoelidae, following the dataset and classification of Bray et al. (2016), Fayton and Andres (2016), Martin et al. (2017, 2020a, b) and Sokolov et al. (2019a, 2022) (see Supplementary Table S4 for more details). Stephanostomum pristis Deslongchamps, 1824 and Zalophotrema hepaticum Stunkard and Alver, 1929 were used as outgroups, based on their previously established close phylogenetic relationship with Opecoeloidea (Bray et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2020b; Sokolov et al., 2020, 2022). All sequences were aligned in MAFFT Web Site v. 7.263 (Katoh and Standley, 2016). The Gblocks Web Server (Castresana, 2000; Talavera and Castresana, 2007) was used to remove ambiguously aligned regions of 28S. The software jModelTest v.2.1.10 (Darriba et al., 2012) was used to select the most appropriate DNA evolutionary model according to the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) (Schwarz, 1978). The best-fit nucleotide substitution model was GTR + I + G (Tavaré, 1986).

The 28S dataset was analyzed with Bayesian inference (BI) and Maximum Likelihood (ML), both implemented under the nucleotide substitution model identified with jModelTest. The BI analysis was performed through the CIPRES Science Gateway v. 3.3 (Miller et al., 2010). The BI was carried out with MrBayes v. 3.2.2 (Ronquist et al., 2012). The Bayesian phylogenetic tree was reconstructed using two parallel runs of Metropolis-Coupled Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMCMC) for 20 × 106 generations each, sampling topologies every 1000 generations, ensuring that the average standard deviation of split frequencies reached <0.01, as recommended by Ronquist et al. (2012). A consensus tree with branch lengths was obtained for the two runs after discarding the first 5000 sampled trees as burn-in. Support for nodes in BI was evaluated by posterior probabilities (PP), where PP > 0.95 was considered strongly supported. The ML analysis was conducted with IQ-TREE Web Site v. 1.6.12 (Nguyen et al., 2015). Support for nodes in ML was evaluated with 10,000 ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot2) approximations (Hoang et al., 2018), considering values ≥ 75 % as strong support for a clade. Finally, phylogenetic trees generated by both methods were visualized and edited using FigTree v. 1.4.4 (Rambaut, 2018) and Adobe Illustrator.

Genetic distance estimations for the 28S fragment between individuals of the subfamily Opecoelinae, including the sequences generated in this study plus those available on GenBank, were performed using p-distance with variance estimation, with the bootstrap method (500 replicates) and with a uniform nucleotide substitution (transitions + transversions) rate, in MEGA v. 12 (Kumar et al., 2024).

Genetic variation parameter estimation using COI

To detect genetic variation associated with worm size, infection sites (intestine and pyloric caeca), and/or fish host size, we analyzed 18 adult O. adsphaericus specimens collected over a year (six sampling events from El Mosquito site). A genealogical tree was constructed using the COI sequences. Furthermore, the genealogical analysis included nine O. adsphaericus specimens from Piedra Blanca Beach (single sampling event) (see Supplementary Table S3). Two sequences of Plagioporus sinitsini Muller, 1934 (KM538106, FJ477213) (Moszczynska et al., 2009; Van Steenkiste et al., 2015) and one of Plagioporus cf. skrjabini (MT756015) (Plagioporinae) (Rothmeier et al., 2021) were used as outgroups because of their relationships within the family Opecoelidae (Fayton and Andres, 2016; Martin et al., 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020b; Sokolov et al., 2019a).

The COI sequence dataset was checked, and its nucleotides were aligned and examined for the presence of pseudogenes in Geneious Prime (2025) v.0.3 (https://www.geneious.com) using the translated amino acid sequences based on the trematode mitochondrial genetic code in MEGA. The best partitioning scheme and substitution model for each DNA partition was chosen under the BIC (Schwarz, 1978) using the ‘greedy’ search strategy in Partition Finder v. 2.1.1 (Lanfear et al., 2017). The COI dataset was partitioned into first-, second and third-codon positions with the appropriate nucleotide substitution model implemented for each codon position (HKY + G for the first [Hasegawa et al., 1985]; F81 for the second [Felsenstein, 1981]; and HKY for the third codon position [Hasegawa et al., 1985]). The BI and ML analyses were performed in MrBayes through the CIPRES portal, and IQ-TREE online, respectively, with the same parameters used in the phylogenetic analyses of the 28S dataset. Bayesian and ML inferences were analyzed using a data set partitioned by codon position, and the same models of nucleotide substitution detected by Partition Finder were applied. The mean genetic divergence value for COI between groups with nodal support was obtained using MEGA v. 12, with variance estimation, with the bootstrap method (500 replicates) and a uniform nucleotide substitution (transition + transversions) rate.

Haplotypes for the COI fragment were obtained for 27 adults of the O. adsphaericus collected from two sampling sites. To assess the completeness of sampling, a haplotype accumulative curve was obtained (Brown et al., 2012; Coeur d’acier et al., 2014). The genetic variation of the O. adsphaericus samples studied here was calculated based on the number of haplotypes (h), haplotype diversity (H) and nucleotide diversity (π) (Nei, 1987), using DnaSP v. 6.12.01 (Rozas et al., 2017). The spatial population distribution for haplotypes was represented on a map using the program popART (Leigh and Bryant, 2015).

Results

Description

Body elongated, oval with rounded anterior and posterior ends (Fig. 1A–E), 1560 μm (758–3019) long, 261 μm (116–496) wide. Tegument slightly rugose with random papillae on forebody (Fig. 2A–C; Supplementary Figure S1 A–D). Forebody short, 240 μm (55–588) long; hindbody elongated, 1128 μm (459–2287) long. Hindbody approximately four times longer than forebody, with an average ratio 1:4.7 (0.78–41.58). Oral sucker subspherical, subterminal, smaller than ventral sucker, 105 μm (63–219) long, 105 μm (63–242) wide. Oral sucker with six pairs of bilateral papillae (Fig. 2D; Supplementary Fig. S1E–F). Ventral sucker larger, slightly spherical, protuberant, contractile, 191 μm (114–288) long, 193 μm (116–426) wide. Ventral sucker with three pairs of digitiform papillae aligned along the anterior and posterior margins of the aperture. Papillae highly retractile in living specimens (Supplementary Microvideo S1, S2), but barely discernible when retracted in several fixed specimens. The middle digitiform papilla slightly larger than lateral ones (Fig. 2A and B; Supplementary Figure S2 A, B, D–F). Ventral sucker nearly twice the size of oral sucker; average ratio between the two suckers 1:1.82 (0.50–4.57) x 1:1.84 (0.48–6.76). Prepharynx short, followed by an oval pharynx, 69 μm (38–100) long and 52 μm (28–86) wide. Smooth, dorsal, and lateral intestinal caeca united near the posterior end of the body through a common anus, 30 μm (13–55) wide. Genital atrium sinistral, slightly left of midline, between pharynx and caeca bifurcation. Vitellarium extends from the posterior edge of ventral sucker to the posterior end of the body, along the hindbody, spreading over and beneath the intestinal ceca, without occupying the spaces of the testes and ovaries. Vitelline follicles 22 μm (12–40 μm) long and 21 μm (14–38 μm) wide. In small and medium-sized specimens, vitelline follicles are smaller, whereas in large specimens they are disproportionately larger.

Male reproductive system

Testes two, in tandem, inter-caecal, located in the superior central-middle hindbody; smooth, irregular, slightly symmetrical, slightly oval and rounded; longer than wider in specimens with medium and large sizes, but occasionally wider than longer in several specimens with small and medium sizes, due to the contraction and relaxation of the specimen when being fixed alive (Fig. 1D and E). Anterior testis 141 μm (65–297) long and 146 μm (92–244) wide. Posterior testis 160 μm (78–312) long and 152 μm (84–241) wide. Seminal vesicle large, unarmed, extending from end of the forebody into the hindbody, reaching the anterior border of the ovary, 286 μm (82–680) long and 52 μm (27–119) wide (Fig. 1E). Cirrus sac small and difficult to observe, located in the inferior mid-hindbody, 144 μm (82–289) long and 32 μm (17–65) wide, containing cirrus, 57 μm (24–124) long.

Female reproductive system

Ovary trilobed, poorly distinguishable, pretesticular, 75 μm (33–160) long and 109 μm (35–180) wide. Seminal receptacle absent. Uterus poorly distinguishable, restricted to area between ovary and genital atrium. Ootype and Laurer's canal barely visible. Yellow operculate eggs, 52 μm (41–65) long and 26 μm (20–34) wide.

Remarks

Based on the morphological characterization of 44 O. adsphaericus specimens from two Baja California locations, representing three size categories (small, medium, and large), along with molecular phylogenetic homology of two genes (see below), we found high morphological polymorphism. We compared our morphometric characteristics with those reported for congeneric Opecoelus species from the southern California Bight, and we observed some inconsistencies in the taxonomic descriptions of O. caballeroi, O. cameroni, and O. pacificus, as well as a high morphological similarity with O. adsphaericus, which are summarized below.

Specimen origin effect and morphological measurement overlap. All morphological measurements of O. caballeroi, O. cameroni, and O. pacificus overlap with those reported in our study, as well as with the description of O. adsphaericus (Manter and Van Cleave, 1951). The taxonomic descriptions of O. caballeroi, O. cameroni, and O. pacificus were based on specimens previously collected specimens of limited sample sizes; i.e., O. caballeroi was described from two specimens collected by Howard A. Winter (HAW), Nov. 5, 1948 (see Caballero R., 1977), O. cameroni was described from one specimen collected by HAW, Aug. 12, 1948 (see Caballero and Caballero, 1969), and O. pacificus was described from ten specimens collected by HAW, Dec. 3, 1948 (see Caballero and Caballero, 1976).

Only the width of the anterior testis, posterior testis, and ovary of O. cameroni do not overlap with those of any congeneric Opecoelus species in this study; however, these measurements are derived from a single specimen (the holotype) (Caballero and Caballero, 1969). The holotype of O. cameroni is unknown (lost), making it impossible to verify and compare these measurements.

In this study, we observed that O. adsphaericus occasionally has testes wider than long in small and medium-sized individuals, similar to those described for O. caballeroi and O. cameroni. It should be noted that hot formalin fixation, as applied here, generally standardizes the variation arising from contraction and expansion in live specimens. It is likely that Caballero and Caballero (1976) and Caballero R. (1977), who worked with previously collected specimens of unknown fixation methods, interpreted testicular variation as a species-level characteristic. As the holotype of O. caballeroi is inaccessible, we reviewed the only paratype (No. 902 CNHE) and detected a high similarity with O. adsphaericus.

Seminal receptacle. Caballero and Caballero (1976) described the seminal receptacle in O. pacificus as comma-shaped (”,“); however, this observation should be taken with caution, as the taxonomical diagnosis of the subfamily Opecoelinae indicates that its taxa lack a seminal receptacle, except for the genus Genitocotyle Park, 1937 (see Cribb, 2005). The holotype of O. pacificus is also unavailable. Only one paratype (No. 1012 CNHE) from the type locality is known. Examination of this paratype reveals that the “seminal receptacle” described by Caballero y C. and Caballero R. (1976) corresponds to a part of the uterine duct. Opecoelus adsphaericus, O. caballeroi, and O. cameroni lack a seminal receptacle (Caballero and Caballero, 1969; Caballero and Caballero, 1976; Caballero R., 1977).

Number and arrangement of papillae on the ventral sucker. Opecoelus adsphaericus has three pairs of digitiform papillae on the ventral sucker, three on the upper margin and three on the lower margin. Manter and Van Cleave (1951, p. 318) state that the papillae are inconspicuous when retracted. Caballero and Caballero (1976, p. 192) and Caballero R. (1977, p. 136). mentioned that O. pacificus and O. caballeroi possess three sessile papillae on the anterior margin of the ventral sucker. Caballero and Caballero (1969, p. 962) describe O. cameroni as having “large sessile papillae on the anterior margin of the ventral sucker”, two of which are “slightly digitiform”; Fig. 3 in the O. cameroni description (p. 961) shows three papillae on the ventral sucker's underside and, possibly, “a fourth digitiform papilla” on its upper margin. Caballero and Caballero (1969) do not specify the total number of papillae of O. cameroni. In the absence of the holotype, their interpretation is speculative. Finally, analysis of the paratypes of O. caballeroi (CNHE 902) and O. pacificus (CNHE 1012) reveals that both specimens possess three pairs of digitiform papillae on the ventral sucker – three on the upper margin and three on the lower margin – consistent with the diagnosis of O. adsphaericus by Manter and Van Cleave (1951), as reiterated in this study.

Deficiencies in the original comparison and description of Opecoelus species. Caballero and Caballero (1969), in their description of O. cameroni, did not distinguish it from O. adsphaericus, as it was originally described as a species of the genus Opegaster. Aken’Ova (2007; p. 31) transferred “Opegaster cameroni” to the genus Opecoelus based on the presence of “vitelline follicles extending anterior to the anterior margin of the ventral sucker, a seminal vesicle extending into the hindbody, entire to irregular testes, and an entire ovary”. However, Aken’Ova (2007) did not provide a morphological comparison between O. cameroni and O. adsphaericus and did not mention examining type specimens of either species. In fact, most of the taxonomic observations in that study were based on previously published descriptions. A detailed review of Aken’Ova's (2007) taxonomic suggestions was later provided by Bray and Justine (2013). Furthermore, Caballero and Caballero (1976; p. 193) did not explicitly distinguish O. pacificus from O. adsphaericus. Indeed, they did not establish differences between O. pacificus and the other 32 species of Opecoelus recognized in their study at that time. They stated that “Opecoelus pacificus sp. n. differs fundamentally from the 32 known species of the genus by the length and shape of the seminal vesicle and the cirrus sac; by the ovoid shape of the male reproductive organs; by the dorsal subterminal position of the anus; and by the distribution of the vitelline follicles”. Caballero R. (1977) distinguished O. caballeroi from O. adsphaericus based on artificial characters, without performing metric or qualitative comparisons. For example, Caballero (1977; p. 138) stated that O. caballeroi differs from O. adsphaericus by “1) the elongated body shape, 2) the position of the genital pore, 3) the position of the testes, and 4) the distribution of the vitelline follicles”. However, as mentioned above, these characters fall within the range of variation described for O. adsphaericus. Therefore, the original descriptions of O. cameroni, O. pacificus, and O. caballeroi lack clear comparisons with O. adsphaericus, and the proposed differences are unconvincing upon critical evaluation. Morphological measurements of these species overlap extensively, and their taxonomic accounts, including host use, are inadequate. Together, these factors suggest that they may represent junior synonyms of O. adsphaericus. A revision using new specimens from their type localities and host species is therefore necessary to confirm their identity or validate their synonymy with O. adsphaericus.

Inconsistencies among individuals from different host species. As noted in the introduction, O. adsphaericus from C. analis and G. nigricans “showed some fairly consistent differences”, according to Manter and Van Cleave (1951). All the size measurements – except for the egg size – obtained in this study for specimens from C. analis fall within the range reported by Manter and Van Cleave (1951), which in turn was based on seven individuals selected to represent the size range. However, it remains unclear whether these seven individuals were exclusively from C. analis or whether they also included specimens from G. nigricans. If no measurements were in fact provided for O. adsphaericus from G. nigricans, added to the fact that there are no available paratypes from this host species, the taxonomic identity of the latter population becomes uncertain, underscoring the need for further study using specimens exclusively from G. nigricans. Preliminary data support the existence of size differences and possible molecular divergence between individuals from the two host species (Martínez-Aquino, unpublished data).

Principal Component Analysis

Pearson correlation analysis of 24 morphometric variables resulted in the selection of eight variables with low correlation coefficients (<0.65). The eight morphometric variables included in the PCA and their loadings on the first two principal components were as follows: Ventral sucker–Length (0.55; 0.34), Ventral sucker–Width (0.68; −0.68), Pharynx–Length (0.20; 0.03), Ovary–Length (0.23; 0.40), Ovary–Width (0.33; 0.48), Egg–Length (−0.01; −0.01), Egg–Width (−0.002; 0.0008), and Intestinal cecum (0.08; 0.04). According to the principal component loadings and PCA plots (Fig. 3), the length and width of the ventral sucker and the width of the ovary were the most influential variables in PC1, while the length and width of the ovary and the length of the ventral sucker were most important in PC2. The three groups analyzed from El Mosquito and Piedra Blanca Beach represent high morphological polymorphism in O. adsphaericus. Based on the correlations among the eight morphometric variables, the PCA reveals (Fig. 3) that 80.71 % of the total variation is explained by the first two principal components (PC1 and PC2), with PC1 accounting for 60.53 % and PC2 for 20.19 %.

Parasitic infection parameters

A total of 1036 and 38 adult specimens of O. adsphaericus were recovered in the intestine, pyloric caeca and stomach from 77 to 16 C. analis examined from El Mosquito and Piedra Blanca Beach, respectively. In the El Mosquito, the total number of O. adsphaericus per host varied from 31 to 227 and came from two (August and June) out of the nine months sampled for one year. Prevalence, mean abundance, and mean intensity are shown in Supplementary Table S2. Except for September, when the prevalence value of O. adsphaericus was 90 %, the prevalence in the remaining months was 100 %.

Discussion

High morphological variation in Opecoelus adsphaericus

Opecoelus adsphericus adults exhibited variation in body size; therefore, immediate fixation of live specimens was crucial to minimize contraction artifacts and obtain reliable morphometric data, avoiding distortions associated with delayed or post-mortem fixation, as recommended by previous studies (Martínez-Aquino et al., 2013; Hechinger, 2019; Chervy, 2024). Microscopic observations and microvideo recordings of several live specimens in 0.75 % saline revealed body relaxation and contraction, as well as movements of the ventral sucker, including the retractile digitiform papillae. This allowed us to discern that the ventral sucker and its three pairs of digitiform papillae exhibited pronounced contractile and retractile movements, which are important to note as they may be neglected in permanent preparations. Cribb (1985) also observed a ventral sucker with retractile papillae in O. variabilis; therefore, this feature may be important when characterizing Opecoelus species.

Principal Component Analysis

Principal component and phylogenetic analyses supported that O. adsphaericus exhibits body lengths ranging from 1000 to 3200 μm, consistent with the measurements reported by Manter and Van Cleave (1951). Such a range is comparable to the variation described by Pinto et al. (2017) in Platynosomum spp., trematodes parasitizing non-human primates in Brazil, where small parasite sizes were interpreted as recent infections linked to active transmission, while larger sizes were attributed to the age of both parasite and host. In this context, the occurrence of small O. adsphaericus in C. analis may similarly indicate recent infections acquired through ingestion of infected intermediate hosts. Now, sampling data show that variation in parasite size does not follow a seasonal pattern, since small, medium, and large O. adsphaericus individuals were present throughout all collection periods. This finding indicates that morphological differences appear to reflect parasite developmental stage rather than seasonal effects. The presence of enlarged vitelline follicles exclusively in large individuals supports the interpretation that these specimens represent older developmental stages. Thus, size variation in O. adsphaericus most likely reflects parasite growth developmental processes within the host rather than seasonal environmental changes. The life cycle of O. adsphaericus remains unknown; however, empirical evidence from a congeneric species suggests a complex life cycle involving a snail as first intermediate hosts, crustaceans as second intermediate hosts, and trophic transmission to teleostean fishes as definitive hosts (Cribb, 1985; Cribb et al., 2001). In Todos Santos Bay, a year-long survey of the parasite communities of C. analis (November 2018–September 2019) reported O. adsphaericus as the dominant species during all sampled months (Garduño-Martínez et al., 2025), consistent with the results of the present study. Amphipods, small crustaceans, and mollusks (e.g., chitons and gastropods, including limpets) were documented in the intestinal contents of C. analis in that study, which were also observed in our samples (Rincon-Sandoval and Martínez-Aquino, pers. obs.). The constant presence of these intermediate hosts in the rocky intertidal zone of Ensenada likely promotes recurrent infections of small individuals, while the presence of large O. adsphaericus may reflect more prolonged infections associated with the age of both the parasite and the host. Preliminary observations suggest a potential size-related pattern, as small to medium parasites were found in fish measuring 6.2–12.0 cm (total length), whereas larger parasites occurred in hosts measuring 11.2–12.9 cm. It is important to note that larger fish also harbored small and medium-sized trematodes, which may reflect a relatively constant parasite recruitment rate in the ecosystem, partly due to the sedentary habits of older adult fish (Davis, 2000; Davis and Levin, 2002). In a follow-up study, this pattern is being analyzed by quantifying parasite numbers in each size class (small, medium, large) per host size category, comparing them with seasonal infection parameters (abundance, prevalence, mean intensity), examining seasonal sea temperature patterns in the rocky intertidal zone, and applying allometric variation analyses to test for correlations between parasite size and factors influenced by host size and season.

Phylogenetical reconstruction

The phylogenetic relationships inferred by BI and ML from the 28S dataset for taxa of Opecoeloidea were consistent with those found in previous phylogenetic analyses carried out for similar taxa using the same gene (Fayton and Andres, 2016; Bray et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2017, Martin et al., 2018, Martin et al., 2020a, Martin et al., 2020b; Sokolov et al., 2019a, 2019b). In this work, we tested for the first time the phylogenetic relationship between Opecoelus and Opegaster within Opecoelinae. Because of their morphological similarity, some authors have proposed synonymizing the two genera (Crowcroft, 1946; Cribb, 1985; Shimazu, 1988; Aken’Ova, 2007; Bray and Justine, 2013). However, Cribb (2005) and Bray and Justine (2013), argued that both should be kept as separate genera until a phylogenetic framework is available to delimit their species. Our results support that Opegaster may be synonymous with Opecoelus, since O. adsphaericus clusters with Opegaster sp. (MK648306) as sister to Opecoelus sp. (ON661329). Nevertheless, a comprehensive taxonomic revision of all possible species is required to confirm the reciprocal monophyly of Opecoelus and Opegaster and to validate the status of these genera.

In this study, 20 sequences representing 13 Opecoelinae taxa were examined. Pairwise genetic distances based on the 28S dataset ranged from 0.64 % to 11.07 %, consistent with previously reported interspecific divergence levels (Bray et al., 2016; Martin et al., 2020a, 2020b; Sokolov et al., 2019a). Most taxa were associated with marine teleost hosts from six families; i.e., Gobiesocidae, Lutjanidae, Muraenolepididae, Priacanthidae, Sciaenidae, and Scorpaenidae (Tkach et al., 2000; Andres et al., 2014a, b; Shedko et al., 2015; Bray et al., 2016; Pérez-Ponce de León and Hernández-Mena, 2019; Sokolov et al., 2019b; Bennett et al., 2022; present study). The only exception was Dimerosaccus oncorhynchi (Eguchi, 1931; Shimadzu, 1980, which has been reported from two anadromous salmonids (Oncorhynchus masou Brevoort, 1856 and Salvelinus curilus Pallas, 1814) and one freshwater salmonid (Brachymystax tumensis Mori, 1930; Shedko et al., 2015; Dolganov, 2022). The marine teleost hosts of Opecoelinae inhabit shallow waters in rocky intertidal and infralittoral zones (e.g., coastal reefs, estuaries and rocky tide pools). Although D. oncorhynchi represents an exception, being associated with anadromous and freshwater salmonids, the remaining opecoeline taxa analyzed in this study form a monophyletic group primarily associated with littoral and neritic marine environments.

COI dataset

The COI sequences generated here represent the first genetic data for opecoelids from two localities and proved useful in addressing three key points. First, the genealogical trees inferred from the COI dataset were congruent in their topology. A well-supported clade grouped all 27 sequences of O. adsphaericus. Within this clade, two subgroups were detected: the first, included three sequences from Piedra Blanca Beach, while the second, comprised fifteen sequences from El Mosquito. However, among the 27 sequences of O. adsphaericus, no association was found with parasite or host size, sampling month, infection site, or haplotype(s). This suggests that parasite size, and thus its morphological polymorphism, is not linked to genetic variation in the COI dataset.

Secondly, the COI sequences appear to suggest that both populations are structured, since it was observed that the haplotypes identified in both localities were not shared (El Mosquito had haplotypes 3, 4, 5, 8, 9, 10 and 11, while Piedra Blanca Beach presented haplotypes 1, 2, 6 and 7). This geographic separation of haplotypes indicates limited gene flow between populations. Such differentiation may be reinforced by local environmental features, including temperature gradients, coastal upwelling, and nearshore circulation patterns known to restrict dispersal in the southern California Bight (Davis, 2000; Dawson et al., 2006, 2014; Ritter, 2009; Johnson et al., 2016). In rocky intertidal habitats, host biology imposes additional constraints on parasite connectivity (Granovitch and Mikhailova, 2004; Muñoz and Cortés, 2009; Leiva et al., 2020). Clinocottus analis is a benthic intertidal fish with strong seasonal recruitment and largely sedentary adults (Davis, 2000; Davis and Levin, 2002; Ramon and Knope, 2008). Consequently, gene flow between parasite populations depends almost entirely on the dispersal of host larvae or juveniles, while adult movement contributes minimally to long-distance connectivity. Spatial and seasonal habitat partitioning further reduces host contact, limiting opportunities for parasite transmission. Parasite life-history traits, such as short-lived dispersive larval stages and limited adult longevity, compound these constraints, further restricting gene flow between distant populations. This fragmentation pattern can also be reinforced by the distribution of intertidal invertebrates (e.g., barnacles, crustaceans, isopods, limpets, snails), which can serve as potential intermediate hosts for trematodes in the southern California Bight. Their distributions are structured by geographic distance, sea temperature, and local conditions, producing regional clusters with occasional exceptions (Blanchette et al., 2008; Dawson et al., 2011, 2014; Eberl et al., 2013). Altogether, the interplay of host life-history, intertidal habitat uses, environmental variability, and parasite traits provides an evolutionary–ecological mechanism for the observed genetic subdivision and suggests that broader-scale population structure may also be detectable, as observed for other marine organisms in the region (Eberl et al., 2013; Dawson et al., 2014; Garcia et al., 2020).

Thirdly, the COI dataset from O. adsphaericus individuals collected at two rocky intertidal sites in Ensenada revealed a high intraspecific genetic variation (0.0072 nucleotide diversity; 11 haplotypes). Similarly, Repkin et al. (2020) reported a high genetic diversity in the marine trematode Microphallus piriformes Galaktionov, 1983 (Microphallidae), identifying 29 haplotypes among 64 individuals from four North Atlantic sites using the same molecular marker. Nevertheless, a comprehensive understanding of parasitic population genetics requires broader geographic sampling, inclusion of diverse host taxa (e.g., teleostean fishes and invertebrates), and the application of multiple molecular markers. These approaches are essential for uncovering the evolutionary processes that shape parasite population structure across the southern California Bight and nearby islands, where environmental and ecological gradients may influence fluctuations in genetic variation (e.g., Dawson et al., 2006, 2014).

Conclusion

Opecoelus adsphaericus exhibits pronounced intraspecific morphological variation, which appears to reflect parasite developmental stage rather than seasonal effects, as small, medium, and large individuals occurred throughout all sampling periods. The PCA confirmed marked morphological polymorphism, and comparative analyses revealed overlapping morphometric traits among O. caballeroi, O. cameroni, and O. pacificus, suggesting that these taxa may represent junior synonyms of O. adsphaericus. Phylogenetic reconstruction based on 28S rRNA sequences placed O. adsphaericus within the Opecoelinae, as sister to Opegaster, a relationship that raises the possibility of generic synonymy pending expanded taxon sampling. COI data revealed high intraspecific genetic variation across both study sites, highlighting the need for further phylogeographic analyses along the Baja California Peninsula and southern California to assess genetic and morphological diversity.
