The potential role of serum and urine galectin-3 values in the diagnosis and staging of Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Miray Erdem, Derya Ozdogru, Elif Banu Soker, Burak Ademoglu, Nevzat Unal, Onur Serdar Gencler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParkinson’s disease (PD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterised by both motor and nonmotor symptoms. Galectin-3, a protein involved in the regulation of inflammatory responses, has emerged as a potential contributor to neurodegenerative processes. This study aimed to measure serum and urinary galectin-3 levels in patients with PD and to evaluate the associations of this biomarker with disease stages and clinical symptoms.MethodsThe present cross-sectional study included 30 PD patients and 29 healthy controls. Galectin-3 levels in serum and urine samples were measured via Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA). The clinical data recorded included demographic information, Hoehn and Yahr (H&Y) stage, Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) motor score, nonmotor symptom score (NMS), and total dopaminergic treatment dose parameters. Multivariate analyses were adjusted for age and sex due to demographic differences between groups.ResultsSerum galectin-3 optical density (OD) levels (0.2 [0.1–1.4]) and urinary galectin-3 OD levels (0.2 [0.1–0.6]) were significantly higher in PD patients than in controls (p < 0.050). Serum and urinary galectin-3 concentration (C) values were also elevated in the PD group. A moderate negative correlation was observed between serum galectin-3 levels and both the H&Y stage (r = − 0.418, p = 0.021) and the UPDRS motor score (r = − 0.541, p = 0.002). After adjusting for age and sex, serum galectin-3 C remained independently associated with motor severity. ROC analysis revealed that serum galectin-3 levels demonstrated promising diagnostic performance, with high specificity and sensitivity in distinguishing patients with PD from healthy controls (AUC = 0.716, p = 0.001). The serum/urine galectin-3 concentration ratio also showed significant discriminative value (AUC = 0.722, p = 0.035).ConclusionOur findings suggest that elevated levels of galectin-3 in serum and urine are associated with Parkinson’s disease and may reflect underlying neuroinflammatory mechanisms contributing to its pathophysiology. The diagnostic performance of galectin-3 markers remained robust even after adjusting for age and sex. However, the strict exclusion criteria and the age imbalance between groups may limit the generalizability of these results to broader clinical populations. Therefore, these findings should be considered exploratory and require confirmation through larger, longitudinal studies to determine their diagnostic and prognostic utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04390-3.

Background

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is characterized by the misfolding of alpha-synuclein, followed by the formation and accumulation of intraneuronal amyloids, low-grade neuroinflammatory changes, and selective neurodegeneration, affecting 3.7% of the population over the age of 65 [1, 2]. PD involves the gradual and chronic loss of dopaminergic neurons in the substantia nigra pars compacta, accompanied by the formation of α-synuclein protein aggregates, known as Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites [3].

Galectin-3, a galactose-binding protein with no known catalytic activity, is expressed mainly by activated microglia within the central nervous system (CNS). Microglia, which are macrophages that reside in the CNS, play a central role in regulating neuroinflammation and maintaining homeostasis. The classification of microglial activity is based on the distinction between proinflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) phenotypes. Each phenotype is characterised by specific markers and mediators released into the environment. The persistence of M1 microglia has been implicated in the development of several inflammatory diseases, including neurodegenerative diseases such as PD [4]. Galectin-3 is known to play a pathological role in neurodegenerative diseases [5]. It is predominantly located in the cytoplasm; however, it has been observed to translocate to the nucleus and is secreted to the cell surface and into biological fluids, such as serum and urine, where it can be quantitatively assessed via galectin-3 optical density (OD) and concentration (C) values. It plays a crucial role in various biological processes, including cell growth, apoptosis, pre-mRNA splicing, differentiation, transformation, angiogenesis, inflammation, fibrosis, and host defense. Galectin-3 has been validated as a highly effective diagnostic and prognostic biomarker for several diseases, particularly at an early stage [6].

Despite advances in symptomatic therapies, there is currently no treatment capable of halting or reversing the progression of PD. This underlines the urgent need for reliable, noninvasive biomarkers to facilitate early and accurate diagnosis, monitor disease progression, and potentially inform therapeutic decisions. Galectin-3 is a promising candidate in this regard due to its involvement in neuroinflammatory processes and its measurable presence in both serum and urine, as indicated by galectin-3 OD and C. However, its clinical utility in PD remains underexplored. In this study, we investigated the potential of serum and urinary galectin-3 levels as PD biomarkers. Specifically, we sought to determine whether galectin-3 could differentiate PD patients from healthy individuals and correlate with disease severity to contribute to diagnostic and prognostic evaluations. By evaluating galectin-3 in both blood and urine, we also aimed to assess the feasibility of using these easily accessible biological fluids in clinical practice. Our findings may offer new insights into the neuroinflammatory mechanisms underlying PD and support the development of more effective, biomarker-based disease management strategies.

Materials and methods

Study population and sampling criteria

In our cross-sectional study, we included patients who were followed up in the movement disorders outpatient clinic at our hospital between 4 October 2023 and 4 March 2024 and were diagnosed with idiopathic PD according to the Movement Disorder Society (MDS) Clinical Diagnostic Criteria [7], as well as healthy individuals aged 18–85 years without additional neurodegenerative or systemic diseases who applied to the neurology outpatient clinic with subjective complaints. All participants were included consecutively. The Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale (UPDRS) Motor Scale [8], modified Hoehn and Yahr staging (H&Y) [9], and the metric properties of a novel nonmotor symptoms scale (NMS) for PD were recorded [10]. The levodopa equivalent daily dose (LEDD) was calculated [11].

All PD diagnoses were made by board-certified neurologists specializing in movement disorders, and patients were followed for a minimum of two years to confirm diagnostic stability and exclude atypical Parkinsonian syndromes. Brain MRI was performed in all PD participants to rule out secondary or structural causes of Parkinsonism. Advanced biomarker or imaging tools, such as DAT-SPECT or alpha-synuclein seeding aggregation assays, were not routinely used due to institutional limitations.

Due to the nature of the clinical setting, the recruitment of healthy controls was limited to individuals without neurological symptoms, resulting in a lower median age in the control group. To address this imbalance, all statistical models were adjusted for both age and sex to minimize potential confounding.

We also evaluated the impact of age through multivariate models to distinguish the specific contribution of galectin-3 levels from age-related effects.

The exclusion criteria included H&Y stage 5 disease, receiving device-based therapy, and the presence of additional chronic systemic inflammatory or neurodegenerative diseases. Patients and controls were excluded if they had a malignancy, a Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) score less than 25, or a history of acute or chronic infectious disease in the last three months. Other exclusion criteria included malnutrition, obesity, smoking/alcohol use, and the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs or other drugs that may affect neuroinflammation (e.g., steroids or immunosuppressants).

An a priori power analysis was conducted via G*Power 3.1.9.7 to determine the minimum required sample size for detecting a significant difference in serum and urinary galectin-3 levels between patients with PD and healthy controls. On the basis of the effect size (d = 0.88) derived from Yazar et al.‘s (2019) [12] previous study, which investigated serum galectin-3 levels in idiopathic PD patients and assumed a one-tailed test with α = 0.05 and power (1 − β) = 0.95, the required sample size was calculated to be 58 participants (29 per group). These calculations informed the design of our study and the determination of the required sample size to ensure adequate statistical power to detect clinically meaningful effects.

Galectin-3 measurement via ELISA

Blood samples were collected from both patients and controls in serum separator tubes. After the samples were allowed to acclimate for two hours at room temperature, they were centrifuged at 1000 × g for 20 min. The samples were then aliquoted and stored at −80 °C until analysis. For the urine samples, the first urine of the day (midstream) was collected directly into a sterile container, centrifuged, and stored at −80 °C.

All biological sample collections were performed under standardized protocols in the morning hours to minimize the potential impact of circadian variation.

The concentration of galectin-3 in the samples was determined via the Human GAL3 (galectin-3) ELK2790 ELISA Kit (ELK Biotechnology, USA). The contents of the kit were stored at −20 °C and transferred to 4 °C one day before the start of the study. Additionally, the samples were stored at 4 °C for one day before testing. The assay was performed on the DS2 Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) Automated Processing System (DYNEX, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Galectin-3 levels were quantified in both serum and urine samples based on OD and calculated C values. The detection range was 0.16–10 ng/ml. The absorbance was then read at 450 ± 10 nm. The galectin-3 C was determined by comparing the OD values of the samples with the standard curve.

All samples were analysed in duplicate to ensure assay reproducibility, and intra-assay coefficients of variation were maintained below 10% as per the manufacturer’s guidelines.

To further explore the diagnostic value of galectin-3, two additional derived variables were calculated: the serum/urine galectin-3 OD ratio and the serum/urine galectin-3 C ratio. These ratios were obtained by dividing the serum OD or C value by the corresponding urinary OD or C value for each participant, respectively.

For example, the serum/urine galectin-3 C ratio was calculated as: Serum galectin-3 (ng/mL) ÷ Urine galectin-3 (ng/mL). These derived variables were used in correlation and ROC analyses to evaluate whether normalization to urinary values enhances diagnostic performance. The rationale for including ratio-based biomarkers was to reduce potential interindividual variability and improve clinical applicability in routine settings.

Statistical analysis

To synthesise the data obtained from the study, descriptive statistics were tabulated as the mean ± standard deviation or median, minimum, and maximum, depending on the distribution of the continuous variables. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Considering the normality of the numerical variables, the sample size, and the characteristics of the data, the most appropriate tests and visualizations were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test (n < 50). In addition, visual tools such as histograms and Q‒Q (quantile‒quantile) plots were used to assess the normality assumption of the data. The Pearson chi-square test was chosen to compare categorical variables between groups, as it is particularly effective for large sample sizes in 2 × 2 tables with expected cells of 5 or more. When comparing two independent groups, the Mann-Whitney U test was used as the preferred statistical analysis when the numerical variables in question were not normally distributed. Spearman’s Rho correlation coefficient was used to evaluate the relationships between numerical variables when any of the numerical variables did not have a normal distribution.

In addition to age, sex was included as a covariate in all multivariate regression models to control for potential confounding effects, as both variables are known to influence inflammatory and neurodegenerative biomarkers. These adjustments were particularly critical given the modest sample size and age imbalance between groups. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis was performed to assess the ability of serum and urine galectin-3 markers to discriminate between the PD and control groups. The analysis aims to quantify the ability of these biomarkers to differentiate between the two groups via the area under the percentage curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, and optimal cut-off values. The AUC, sensitivity, specificity, cut-off point, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated and presented for each biomarker. The DeLong test was used to determine whether the differences between the ROC curves were statistically significant. Both univariate and multivariate linear regression analyses were performed to determine the factors predicting LEDD, the UPDRS motor score, and NMS. In the univariate analyses, the effects of each independent variable, including sex, age, UPDRS motor score, NMS, serum/urine galectin-3 OD ratio, and serum/urine galectin-3 C ratio, on the above outcomes were examined separately. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values were calculated for each variable. Multivariate linear regression analyses were used to assess the combined effects of the selected variables and their impact on these outcomes. To address the critical age and sex imbalance between groups, all regression models were adjusted for both age and sex as covariates.

This adjustment aimed to reduce potential confounding effects, particularly in comparisons of galectin-3 levels and clinical outcomes between patients with PD and healthy controls. These covariates were also incorporated into ROC models to assess whether biomarker-based discrimination remained significant independent of age and sex.

The statistical analyses were performed via the Jamovi (Version 2.3.28, Jamovi project, 2023, https://www.jamovi.org) and JASP (Version 0.18.3, Jeffreys’ Amazing Statistics Program, 2024, https://jasp-stats.org) software packages. The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.050.

Univariate linear regression analyses confirmed that serum galectin-3 C was significantly associated with both the UPDRS motor score (β = − 0.443, p = 0.014) and LEDD (β = − 0.363, p = 0.048). Likewise, the serum/urine galectin-3 C ratio showed inverse associations with the UPDRS motor score (β = − 0.571, p = 0.013) and H&Y stage (β = − 0.555, p = 0.017).

To account for potential confounding factors such as age and sex, multivariate regression models were adjusted accordingly. After these adjustments, serum galectin-3 C remained an independent predictor of the UPDRS motor score (β = − 0.402, p = 0.009). However, urinary galectin-3 OD and C levels did not show significant associations with clinical severity measures after adjusting for age and sex (p > 0.05).

According to the ROC analysis results shown in Table 3, the AUC value for the serum/urine galectin-3 C ratio was 0.722 (95% CI: 0.508–0.881, p = 0.035), with 100% specificity and 61.1% sensitivity at a cut-off point > 1.166.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that serum and urinary galectin-3 levels are significantly higher in patients with PD than in healthy individuals. Importantly, statistical adjustments for both age and sex confirmed that serum galectin-3 levels remained independently associated with disease severity, strengthening the reliability of our findings. Notably, serum galectin-3 levels showed a moderate inverse correlation with both the H&Y stage and UPDRS motor scores. These findings suggest that galectin-3 may serve not only as a diagnostic biomarker but also as a marker of clinical severity in PD. The fact that galectin-3 can be reliably detected in urine enhances its practical utility as a non-invasive, accessible biomarker.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to investigate urinary galectin-3 levels in patients with PD, making our work a novel contribution to the search for scalable biomarkers in neurodegenerative diseases. Furthermore, ROC analyses, adjusted for age and sex, demonstrated that serum and urine galectin-3 markers retained significant discriminative ability, particularly the serum/urine C ratio.

A review of the literature revealed several studies on serum galectin-3 levels in patients with PD [12–14]. A study by Yazar et al. [12] suggested that the serum galectin-3 C may be a potential biomarker for PD. It has also been reported that galectin-3 OD and C values may be valuable diagnostic and monitoring tools, particularly in the early stages of the disease. The inverse correlation between serum galectin-3 C and UPDRS motor scores in our study suggests that galectin-3 may serve as a biomarker for assessing disease severity. These findings are consistent with the markedly elevated serum galectin-3 OD and C values observed in our PD group compared with those in the control group.

Galectin-3 is a stable biomarker that is not associated with age, body mass index, or sex and does not show circadian variation. However, given the substantial age difference between our PD and control groups, we conservatively adjusted all models for age and sex to ensure robust, unbiased estimates. Therefore, galectin-3 may be utilized in the diagnosis and prognosis of various diseases and may also serve as a therapeutic target [15].

The current literature suggests that inhibiting galectin-3 may reduce neuroinflammation in PD by modulating microglial activation [16–18]. A study by Hsiu-Chuan Wu et al. [14] found that galectin-3 levels were significantly higher in patients with PD and positively correlated with H&Y stage. This contrasts with our findings of an inverse correlation, suggesting that galectin-3 dynamics may vary across disease stages. This discrepancy might reflect stage-dependent shifts in galectin-3 levels over the disease course, possibly indicating a biphasic inflammatory response in PD progression. One possible interpretation is a dynamic temporal change in galectin-3 levels during disease progression—from an initial neuroinflammatory state (associated with increased galectin-3) to a later degenerative phase where galectin-3 declines. This speculative hypothesis requires confirmation via longitudinal studies.

Regarding diagnostic confirmation, while PD diagnoses were made by experienced neurologists using the MDS Clinical Criteria, we acknowledge the absence of adjunct diagnostic tools such as MRI, DAT-SPECT, or α-synuclein seeding assays. However, our strict exclusion criteria (e.g., for MMSE < 25, comorbidities, NSAID use) were applied to reduce misclassification and confounding. We recommend that future studies incorporate neuroimaging or biomarker-supported diagnostic confirmation to strengthen diagnostic specificity.

Our study has several limitations. First, although the sample size is relatively small, it was determined a priori through power analysis and is sufficient for the reported findings. However, confirmation in larger and more demographically diverse cohorts is warranted. Second, the strict exclusion criteria—such as the exclusion of patients with cognitive impairment, comorbid diseases, or medication use that might influence inflammation—may limit the generalizability of our findings. These were necessary to eliminate confounding but may not reflect the heterogeneity of real-world PD populations. Third, as this is a cross-sectional study, causal relationships cannot be inferred. Therefore, longitudinal studies are needed to evaluate whether galectin-3 levels predict disease progression, treatment response, or differentiation from PD-plus syndromes.

In summary, our results highlight the role of galectin-3 in the pathophysiology of PD and support its potential utility as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. Despite limitations, our data provide foundational evidence for further research, particularly into the potential of serum/urine galectin-3 ratios as clinically actionable, non-invasive indicators of PD severity and progression.

Conclusion

The results of our study support the hypothesis that galectin-3 is a promising non-invasive biomarker for Parkinson’s disease (PD). Both serum and urinary galectin-3 levels, as well as serum/urine ratios, were significantly elevated in patients with PD. Notably, serum levels were inversely associated with clinical severity, including motor impairment and dopaminergic treatment needs, and these associations remained significant after adjusting for age and sex, underscoring the marker’s diagnostic and prognostic potential.

Among all parameters, the serum/urine galectin-3 concentration ratio demonstrated the highest specificity (100%) for distinguishing PD from healthy controls, suggesting its potential clinical utility for disease differentiation.

Given its accessibility, biological stability, and correlation with disease-related metrics, galectin-3, especially when assessed simultaneously in serum and urine, may serve as a practical tool for early diagnosis, disease staging, and ongoing monitoring. However, due to the limited sample size and cross-sectional design, these findings should be regarded as exploratory in nature and hypothesis-generating. Further longitudinal and mechanistic studies in broader and more heterogeneous cohorts are warranted to confirm the clinical relevance of galectin-3 in PD.
