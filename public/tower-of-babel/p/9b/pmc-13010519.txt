Introduction

Patients frequently present to primary care or urgent care with infections related to cat bites or for antibiotic prophylaxis after a cat bite to prevent infection. Antibiotics are recommended for all cat bites since they tend to cause deep puncture wounds [1]. A beta-lactam/beta-lactamase inhibitor combination such as amoxicillin-clavulanate is the recommended first-line empiric antibiotic for treatment of infected cat bites in the United States since it targets traditional feline-associated pathogens including Pasteurella multocida and Capnocytophaga canimorsus. Though more commonly associated with dogs, C. canimorsus can also be found in the mouths of cats [1]. Here we present a case of human infection with Francisella tularensis from a cat bite. This occurred during a time of increased F. tularensis spread in the local cat population identified by the Minnesota Department of Public Health [2]. F. tularensis is a recognized possible pathogen in cat-associated infections, but it does not respond to amoxicillin-clavulanate. It is important to identify F. tularensis when present so that effective antibiotics, such as ciprofloxacin or doxycycline, can be used instead. Epidemiology of F. tularensis can vary, so it is also important for clinicians to be aware of public health trends in their areas of practice.

Tularemia is a zoonotic infection caused by a Gram-negative bacteria, Francisella tularensis. Though human infection is rare, sporadic cases have been reported in all U.S. states aside from Hawaii, with the highest incidence in the south-central U.S. [3]. Arthropods (primarily ticks) and infected wild rabbits and rodents are involved in the sylvatic cycle of tularemia. Transmission to humans can be via arthropod bite, inhalation of contaminated aerosols, contact with infected wildlife or pets, consumption of contaminated water or meat, or from laboratory exposure [3], [4]. Domestic animals such as dogs and cats can become infected and, in turn, can transmit tularemia to humans, particularly when domestic animals are allowed to roam outdoors where they can be exposed to arthropods or wildlife that harbor F. tularensis. Direct transmission to humans via cat scratch or cat bite has been well described in the literature [5], [6], and exposure to infected cats is reported to be the most common source of non-arthropod-based transmission of tularemia to humans in the United States [7]. Tularemia is found mostly throughout North America, Europe, and Asia but to date, most reported cases of infected cate bites due to tularemia have been reported in the United States and Canada [8], [9], [10]. Cases have also been reported in Norway and Sweden [11], [12]. We were not able to identify any case reports of tularemia causing infection from cat bites in other areas of the world, though it’s unclear whether this is because it doesn’t occur as often or because published reports aren’t available.

Tularemia can cause multiple different primary clinical syndromes (in descending order of incidence): ulceroglandular, oropharyngeal, glandular, oculoglandular, pneumonic, typhoidal, and meningitic disease [13]. The clinical manifestation of disease is in part driven by the mode of acquisition of F. tularensis infection. In ulceroglandular disease, an ulcer appears at the site of inoculation and is often accompanied by regional lymphadenopathy. Ulceroglandular disease is the most common presentation in the United States, as can occur in cases where cat bites or cat scratches are the mode of transmission [4], [5], [13].

Diagnosis is made if the patient has a clinically-compatible illness with confirmation via isolation of F. tularensis in culture or via four-fold change in acute and convalescent serum antibodies. In the United States, a probable diagnosis can be made based on clinically-compatible illness (based on clinical and epidemiologic findings) and a single elevated antibody titer or if F. tularensis is detected in a clinical specimen via positive PCR or via direct fluorescent antibody testing [3], [13].

Treatment is dependent on severity of infection; for severe cases, an aminoglycoside (such as gentamicin or streptomycin) is preferred therapy. For milder cases, a fluoroquinolone (such as ciprofloxacin) or a tetracycline (such as doxycycline) are generally effective alternatives. Beta-lactam antibiotics such as penicillins and cephalosporins are ineffective in treating tularemia. Patients should be monitored for any signs of symptom relapse due to the high rates of relapsed infection with F. tularensis that may require repeated/extended antibiotic courses or use of alternative antibiotic classes [13].

Case presentation

Presentation

A 64-year-old woman presented to the hospital 2 weeks after sustaining a cat bite to her left third finger. She presented with fevers, chills, weakness, nausea, and malaise in addition to worsening swelling, pain, erythema, and new-onset purulent drainage from her finger. She had previously received one dose of intramuscular ceftriaxone and a 10-day course of clindamycin from an urgent care clinic without improvement.

The cat that bit her was a free-roaming cat in a suburban neighborhood in Minnesota. The cat’s vaccination history was unknown, but it appeared healthy and behaved normally. The patient routinely provided food and shelter to the cat, but on that spring day she sustained a bite when attempting to pick the cat up to examine it.

The patient had a past medical history of paroxysmal atrial tachycardia. She was not on any chronic medications.

On physical examination, the patient was afebrile and had stable vital signs. She had tenderness, edema, and erythema of the dorsal side of the left third finger distal to the proximal inter-phalangeal (PIP) joint and there was a dried, non-bleeding wound overlying her distal inter-phalangeal (DIP) joint. There were also pustular lesions proximal and lateral to the nail bed with associated fluctuance but no active drainage (Fig. 1). Her sensation and capillary refill were intact.

Left third finger on initial presentation with edema, erythema, subcutaneous pustules, and a small wound.

Initial work-up included a normal white blood cell count, a C-reactive protein (CRP) of 130 mg/L (normal <5 mg/L), and 2 sets of blood cultures which had no growth. An x-ray revealed soft tissue swelling but no overt osteomyelitis, and an ultrasound showed a 3 cm fluid collection in the dorsal aspect of her distal phalanx. Orthopedic surgery was consulted who performed a bedside incision and drainage (I&D) with a dorsal 2 cm incision, and purulent fluid was expressed from the finger. The fluid was sent for aerobic/anaerobic bacterial cultures which only grew Staphylococcus epidermidis. She was treated initially with IV vancomycin and IV piperacillin-tazobactam, which was later changed to IV ampicillin-sulbactam. She was given tetanus vaccination and was offered but declined rabies vaccination or rabies immunoglobulin.

Diagnosis

The finger infection failed to clinically improve over the following 3 days with ongoing pain, swelling, and seropurulent drainage. Subcutaneous pustules were visible along the dorsal surface of her finger, and a new ulcerating wound formed on the ulnar aspect of her distal phalanx (Fig. 2). Her inflammatory markers also continued rising with a CRP of 151 mg/L on day 3, so on day 4 she underwent surgery for a more extensive surgical debridement. Intraoperatively, gross purulence and friable subcutaneous tissue containing multiple distinct pustules were debrided. Portions of her terminal extensor tendon were found to be frayed on examination. The ulnar and dorsal aspects of her finger were debrided from her PIP to her distal phalanx, requiring removal of her nail plate. Tissue from the procedure was sent for 3 sets of aerobic/anaerobic bacterial tissue cultures and fungal cultures. One aerobic bacterial tissue culture grew Francisella tularensis.

Left third finger after bedside I&D with ongoing swelling, subcutaneous pustules, and an ulcerating ulnar lesion.

Management

Once Francisella tularensis was identified in tissue culture, oral ciprofloxacin was initiated. Clinical improvement was noted over the following days with decreasing swelling, pain, and drainage (Fig. 3), and so she was ultimately discharged on day 5 post-operatively on oral ciprofloxacin (for F. tularensis) and oral amoxicillin-clavulanate (for additional empiric coverage of feline oral flora). Her CRP was 55 mg/L at the time of discharge. At a 10-day follow up visit, she had ongoing swelling and tenderness of the finger but no active drainage. At that time, amoxicillin-clavulanate was discontinued, but oral ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily was continued due to concern for ongoing infection with F. tularensis. At 3-weeks post-operatively, sustained signs of clinical improvement were noted so ciprofloxacin was discontinued; her CRP was 5 mg/L at that time.

Left third finger appearance 3 days after extensive OR debridement and initiation of ciprofloxacin.

Three days after discontinuing ciprofloxacin, she contacted the clinic due to recurrence of malaise, chills, and the formation of a new pustule on the dorsum of her distal left third finger. The pustule was lanced and purulent fluid was expressed and sent for aerobic/anaerobic bacterial culture which had no growth. Due to concern for relapsed F. tularensis infection at that time, treatment was restarted but ciprofloxacin was changed to doxycycline 100 mg twice daily due to concern for side effects from ciprofloxacin. Doxycycline was continued for a 6-week course with clinical improvement noted upon completion, including improved chills/malaise, resolved finger abscesses, healed wounds, and resolved erythema and tenderness. It is possible that the longer total duration of antibiotics contributed to success of the doxycycline. Her CRP was < 3 mg/L upon antibiotic completion and she had no clinical signs of recurrent/relapsed infection at 2-week and 4-week follow up visits after antibiotic completion (Fig. 4).

Left third finger well-healed 2 weeks after antibiotic completion without signs of relapsed infection.

Discussion and conclusions

This case illustrates several important clinical considerations. In the setting of an infected cat bite or scratch that is not responding to first-line therapy (usually amoxicillin-clavulanate or another beta lactam antibiotic), it is important to consider F. tularensis in the differential diagnosis in tularemia-endemic areas. Diagnosis by culturing deep tissue samples is ideal because F. tularensis may not routinely grow on superficial drainage fluid cultures as was seen in this case. If culture is unavailable, acute and convalescent serologies may also be used for diagnosis. Empiric treatment with a fluoroquinolone, tetracycline, or aminoglycoside class of antibiotic should be initiated in the meantime for a patient with consistent epidemiologic history (history of cat bite/scratch, not improving on empiric antibiotic therapy with beta-lactams) while awaiting serologic confirmation.

Additionally, patients with known or suspected F. tularensis infection should undergo close monitoring given the risk of relapse, especially if an aminoglycoside (which can cause irreversible ototoxicity or nephrotoxicity so is generally reserved for more severe cases) is not used as primary therapy [13].

Finally, keeping abreast of local epidemiologic trends, such as the increase in cases of tularemia in the local cat population for this case, can help clinicians to predict more unusual pathogens in the setting of infections that aren’t responding to first line treatments [2].
