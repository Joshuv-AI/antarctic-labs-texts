IgA Vasculitis Developing during Trastuzumab Emtansine Therapy for HER2-positive Breast Cancer
Authors: Nanami Watanabe, Shun Yoshida, Keiichi Osano, Kie Ohkoshi, Toshihisa Ishii, Makiko Konishi, Haruka Nakada, Naoki Ashizawa, Yuko Nakayama, Hiroshi Mitsui, Kazuya Takahashi, Ayumu Nakashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An elderly woman undergoing trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) treatment for HER2-positive breast cancer visited our department with purpura, hematuria, proteinuria, and a decreased renal function. A skin biopsy revealed leukocytoclastic vasculitis with immunoglobulin A (IgA) deposition, confirming the diagnosis of IgA vasculitis. Owing to active renal lesions, the patient was treated with prednisolone and cyclophosphamide, which resulted in clinical remission. A renal biopsy performed five months post-treatment revealed persistent mild proliferative glomerulonephritis with fibrotic crescent formation. This case was considered to have T-DM1-associated IgA vasculitis. Although rare, an awareness of the possibility of serious adverse effects associated with drug administration is important.

Introduction

Immunoglobulin A vasculitis (IgAV) is a systemic small-vessel vasculitis primarily characterized by the deposition of IgA1 immune complexes (1,2). In pediatric cases, the prognosis is generally favorable; however, in adults, the frequency of associated renal impairment is high and the disease often becomes severe (3-5). In addition to malignancies, infections, and vaccinations, there have been case reports of adult IgAV being associated with biological agents and molecularly targeted therapies (6-12). Although antibiotics and tumor necrosis factor-α inhibitors are frequently reported as causative drugs, reports of cases involving epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) inhibitors are limited (7-9,13). Animal and preclinical studies have suggested that EGFR family signaling may have dual effects on kidney disease. For example, the activation of secreted heparin-binding EGF-like growth factor (HB-EGF)-EGFR signaling may exacerbate crescentic glomerulonephritis, whereas EGFR inhibition may have a renoprotective effect (14,15).

Trastuzumab emtansine (T-DM1) is an antibody-drug conjugate that combines trastuzumab, an anti-human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) monoclonal antibody, with the cytotoxic agent DM1, which exerts microtubule-inhibiting effects (16). Adverse events such as thrombocytopenia and interstitial lung disease have been reported; however, reports of vasculitis are rare (8,9,16,17). We herein report the case of an elderly woman who developed IgAV during T-DM1 administration as a postoperative adjuvant therapy for breast cancer and achieved a favorable outcome after discontinuation of the drug and immunosuppressive therapy.

Case Report

This patient was a 77-year-old woman with a history of hypertension. One year earlier, she was diagnosed with cancer of the left breast and underwent neoadjuvant chemotherapy with trastuzumab and paclitaxel, followed by left mastectomy and sentinel lymph node dissection six months earlier. Five months earlier, she had begun receiving adjuvant chemotherapy with T-DM1 (3.6 mg/kg every three weeks) for HER2-positive breast cancer. Three months earlier (after the third course), she experienced recurrent purpura in her lower legs. One month before the presentation (before starting the sixth course), the patient visited a dermatologist. A skin biopsy revealed leukocytoclastic vasculitis (Fig. 1), and immunofluorescence showed IgA deposition, leading to the diagnosis of IgAV. Two weeks prior to presentation, she developed gross hematuria and was referred to the nephrology department.

Skin biopsy findings. Hematoxylin and Eosin staining (light microscopy) revealed neutrophils with nuclear debris and a few eosinophils surrounding the blood vessels in the subcutaneous tissue.

Previous findings of occult blood or protein have not been reported in the urine during health checkups. The patient was taking pregabalin, mecobalamin, olmesartan, and amlodipine regularly, but did not use any supplements or over-the-counter medications. She had not received any vaccinations, experienced any infectious episodes, or traveled abroad in the past six months.

On admission, the patient's vital signs were as follows: blood pressure, 145/90 mmHg; pulse, 80 bpm; body temperature, 37.1°C; and respiratory rate, within the normal range. Symptoms such as fever, headache, joint pain, cough, shortness of breath, abdominal pain, and bloody stools were not observed. Physical examination revealed no superficial lymphadenopathy, and the chest and abdomen were intact. Purpura with palpable infiltration were observed on the extensor surfaces of the distal limbs, and pitting edema was noted in the lower limbs.

The serum creatinine (Cr) level was 0.93 mg/dL (0.79 mg/dL, one month earlier), the estimated glomerular filtration rate was 45 mL/min/1.73 m2 (54 mL/min/1.73 m2, one month earlier), and the C-reactive protein level showed a mild increase over several months. Hypoalbuminemia was also observed, and urinalysis revealed a urine protein-to-creatinine ratio of 3.58 g/gCr and ＞100 red blood cells (RBCs) per high-power field, with the presence of dysmorphic RBCs. The platelet count fluctuated intermittently after T-DM1 administration (Fig. 2). Immunological testing showed elevated IgA levels, whereas other immunoglobulin and complement levels remained within the normal ranges. Antinuclear antibody, anti-Ro/SS-A antibody, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibody (ANCA), anti-glomerular basement membrane antibody, and cryoglobulin tests were negative.

Clinical course of the patient.

Whole-body computed tomography and positron emission tomography performed after the appearance of purpura did not reveal recurrence, metastasis, or any other apparent tumorous lesions. Additionally, the upper and lower endoscopic examinations performed after hospitalization did not reveal any hemorrhagic or tumorous lesions.

Clinical course

The overall clinical course of the patient is shown in Fig. 2. Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis was suspected based on the subacute onset and worsening of hematuria, proteinuria, and a decreased renal function. The presence of purpura led to a diagnosis of IgAV with renal lesions. Due to severe thrombocytopenia (minimum value: 31,000 /μL), a renal biopsy was postponed, and treatment was initiated with intravenous cyclophosphamide and high-dose corticosteroid therapy. No new organ lesions or complications developed and the purpura subsided. Furthermore, proteinuria decreased and the patient was discharged on day 39 of hospitalization. Treatment with T-DM1 was then discontinued. As renal dysfunction and microscopic hematuria persisted, a renal biopsy was performed five months after the start of treatment once the platelet count had returned to normal. As the renal pathology images showed chronic phase changes, no additional immunosuppressive agents were administered and corticosteroids were gradually tapered. Eight months after starting treatment, hematuria completely and persistently resolved. Corticosteroid therapy was completed within one year, and no relapse was observed for more than one year was observed. For breast cancer, regular blood tests and imaging studies were performed; however, no additional treatment was required and no recurrence was observed.

Renal biopsy

The renal pathology findings are shown in Fig. 3. Of the 22 glomeruli examined, four showed global sclerosis. Two fibrous crescents were observed. Light microscopy with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) staining demonstrated diffuse segmental increases in the mesangial matrix and cellular proliferation (Fig. 3a). Segmental endocapillary proliferation was observed in only two glomeruli by PAS staining (Fig. 3b), and immunofluorescence revealed the segmental deposition of IgA, predominantly in the mesangial areas (Fig. 3c). In contrast, deposits of IgG, IgM, C1q, and C3 are minimal. Electron microscopy revealed sparse paramesangial electron-dense deposits with only minor structural alterations (Fig. 3d). Moderate interstitial fibrosis, focal tubular atrophy, and mild tubulitis were observed in the tubulointerstitial compartments. The changes at the small artery level were minimal. On the basis of these findings, the patient was diagnosed with mesangial proliferative glomerulonephritis, thus reflecting post-treatment changes.

Discussion

This case involved IgAV, which developed during T-DM1 administration. Purpura in the lower limbs first appeared, followed by renal impairment, characterized by hematuria and proteinuria. Based on the temporal association between T-DM1 administration and symptom onset, the absence of recurrence after drug discontinuation, and the exclusion of infection, vaccination, other medications, or tumor recurrence, we concluded that this drug-induced IgAV was attributable to T-DM1.

Reports on trastuzumab-associated vasculitis are limited. Chang et al. reported a case of ANCA-positive interstitial pneumonia (17), whereas Huo et al. described a severe case of ANCA-associated vasculitis that developed during trastuzumab chemotherapy (18). Additionally, cases of ANCA-negative vasculitis with T-DM1 use have also been reported (8,9). Bhardwaj et al. reported ANCA-negative leukocytoclastic vasculitis associated with T-DM1 and Escobar-Dávila et al. described IgAV with intestinal involvement occurring during the fifth cycle of T-DM1. These previously reported cases are similar to the present case in terms of the timing of onset after T-DM1 administration and the presence of skin lesions. These similarities further suggest that the IgA vasculitis in this case was associated with T-DM1, rather than developing incidentally during breast cancer treatment. However, a distinctive feature of the present case was the severe renal involvement, which required immunosuppressive therapy and was characterized by massive proteinuria, hematuria, and progressive renal dysfunction. The trend observed in recent years likely reflects an increase in the use of trastuzumab-related agents owing to their expanding indications. Therefore, reports of vasculitis associated with HER2 inhibitors are expected to increase and heightened awareness in clinical practice is thus warranted.

In this case, the pathogenesis likely involved two main mechanisms. The first is a hypersensitivity reaction to drugs, which represents the general mechanism underlying drug-induced vasculitis. The second possible mechanism involves the EGFR pathway. EGFR-targeting drugs have been reported to induce or exacerbate IgA-mediated glomerular injury (7). Furthermore, basic studies have demonstrated that activation of the EGFR pathway occurs during immune-mediated glomerular injury and that the inhibition of this pathway can alleviate inflammation in glomerulonephritis (14,15). However, trastuzumab, a monoclonal antibody component of T-DM1, is specific to HER2 and it does not directly inhibit any other EGFR pathways. Therefore, it is difficult to conclude that direct HER2 inhibition contributes to vasculitis development, and a hypersensitivity reaction to the drug appears to be the primary pathological mechanism.

The coexistence of malignant tumors has been reported in adults with IgAV (2,6,19). In the present case, a comprehensive search for systemic recurrence was conducted; however, imaging and endoscopic examination ruled out this possibility. Remission was achieved after the discontinuation of the suspected drug and initiation of immunosuppressive therapy. As no relapse occurred thereafter, this finding strongly supports the likelihood of drug-induced IgAV rather than tumor-associated IgAV. Although IgAV with an early onset generally has a poor renal prognosis (3,4), early therapeutic intervention proved to be effective in this case, resulting in a favorable long-term outcome. Even in the renal biopsy performed five months after treatment initiation, only partial fibrous crescent formation and mild tubulointerstitial fibrosis were observed, further supporting this conclusion.

Based on this experience, we propose the following two clinical recommendations. First, during the administration of HER2 inhibitors, close attention should be paid to the appearance of purpura and abnormal urine findings, and drug-induced vasculitis should be considered in the differential diagnosis. Second, even in situations where a renal biopsy may be deferred because of bleeding risk or other factors, early therapeutic intervention, including diagnostic treatment, should not be delayed considering its potential impact on the renal prognosis.
