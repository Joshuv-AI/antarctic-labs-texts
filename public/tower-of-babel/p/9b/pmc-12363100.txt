Efficacy of two brands of Mebendazole (500 mg) in the treatment of Ascaris lumbricoides and hookworm infection among school-aged children in South Gondar zone, Northwest Ethiopia: a randomized open label trial
Authors: Birhanu Malede, Ayalew Jejaw Zeleke, Yenesew Mihret Wondmagegn, Abebaw Setegn, Hailegebriel Wondimu, Ayenew Berhan, Tadesse Misganaw, Gashaw Azanaw Amare, Tegegne Eshetu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundParasitic worms remain a serious public health problem in developing countries. Mebendazole is a widely used anthelmintic, but variations in drug efficacy among different brands of mebendazole may impact treatment outcomes. This trial investigated efficacy of two different brands of mebendazole (Vermox® and X-WORM®) used in the deworming program and available in local pharmaceutical shops.MethodsA randomized, open label trial was conducted among 212 children in Alember and Addis Zemen primary schools. Convenient sampling technique was used to select schools and study participants. McMaster egg counting technique was used to determine the intensity of infection. Eligible Ascaris lumbricoides or hookworm infected study participants were randomized into either a single dose of 500 mg Vermox® or X-WORM® mebendazole treatment arm. Follow-up data were collected between 14 and 21 days after treatment. Egg reduction rate was primary outcome and cure rate was used as secondary outcome, Paired sample t-test was used to determine egg reduction rate, and odds ratio was used to determine cure rate.ResultsA total of 212 school-aged children participated in the study, of which 106 school-aged children were infected by Ascaris lumbricoides and the remaining 106 were infected by hookworm parasites. From each Ascaris lumbricoides and hookworm infection group 53 school-aged children received Vermox®, and the remaining 53 school-aged children received X-WORM® 500 mg mebendazole. The egg reduction rate of Vermox® against Ascaris lumbricoides was 97.63% (95% CI: 86.1-108.7), while that of X-WORM® was 95.87% (95% CI: 85.1-106.9). For hookworm the egg reduction rate of Vermox® was 74.96% (95% CI 65.6–85.2), while that of X-WORM® was 71.5% (95% CI: 61.5–81.6). The cure rate of Vermox® and X-WORM® against hookworm was 29.4% (95% CI: 17.6–43.1) and 27.5% (95% CI: 15.7–39.2), respectively. Against Ascaris lumbricoides the cure rates were 92.3% (95% CI: 84.6–98.1) for Vermox® and 90.4% (95% CI: 82.7–98.1) for X-WORM®.Conclusion and recommendationThe efficacy of both brands of mebendazole against Ascaris lumbricoides and hookworm infections showed a satisfactory level based on the egg reduction rate. However, the cure rates against hookworm remains questionable, despite all study participants having light-intensity infections, which are generally expected to respond well to treatment. Further investigation is needed to evaluate the therapeutic outcomes of mebendazole in cases of moderate and heavy infection intensity to generate stronger evidence.Trial registryThis trial was retrospectively registered on https://pactr.samrc.ac.za/ and the unique identification number for registry was PACTR202503808932606 and the registration date was 27 March 2025.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11462-9.Keywords: Mebendazole, Soil-transmitted helminth, Efficacy, School-aged children

Background

Soil-transmitted helminthes (STHs) are intestinal nematodes that constitute a major public health concern globally, particularly in low and middle income countries. An estimated 1.5 billion people, approximately 24% of the world population are estimated to be infected with at least one STH [1]. According to World Health Organization (WHO) 2023 report on STH, greater than 260 million preschool-age children (PSAC), 654 million school-age children (SAC), 108 million adolescent girls, and 138.8 million pregnant and lactating women live in STH endemic area, and are in need of treatment and preventive interventions. The burden of the disease is highest among children in low-income countries where water, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) practices is inadequate [1, 2].

Soil-transmitted helminth infections are classified as neglected tropical diseases (NTDs) due to their disproportionate impact on poorest communities and their contribution to cycle of poverty through impairing physical growth and cognitive development in children [3–6]. Due to the significant role of contaminated soil in their transmission, they represent a common public health term known as STHs [7]. The major causative species include Ascaris lumbricoides, Trichuris trichiura, hookworms (Ancylostoma duodenale and Necator americanus), as well as Strongyloides stercoralis [6, 8]. These parasites do not multiply inside the host; rather they produce multiple eggs or larvae and shed them with the feces of infected persons, leads to contaminating soil and water particularly in areas where open deification is common and this is a crucial feature for the epidemiology of these parasites [2, 5].

Hookworm infection is primarily acquired through skin penetration by the infective filariform larvae, although Ancylostoma duodenale is rarely acquired through ingestion [9]. Hookworms are the most common cause of maternal and childhood illnesses in developing tropical and subtropical countries [10]. Their chronic intestinal blood loss, can leads to iron-deficiency anemia; and malnutrition in pregnancy that may have adverse effects on pregnancy outcomes (including low birth weight) [11]. Globally, STHs are responsible for over 5 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), with hookworm being the most significant contributor [12].

In 2018, a geospatial analysis study conducted in sub-Saharan Africa regarding the prevalence and intensity of STHs indicated that 13% of school aged children (SAC) were infected, and of these 2.9 million children whose age is from 5 to 14 still suffer from moderate to heavy intensity infection [13]. In Ethiopia out of roughly an estimated 110 million population, beyond 81 million are expected to live in STH endemic areas, which is comprised of 25.3 million SAC, and 44.6 million adults [14].

To control STH infections, the WHO recommends preventive chemotherapy (PC) using benzimidazole drugs such as albendazole (400 mg) or mebendazole (500 mg), in endemic settings administered without any prior diagnosis [15–19]. Mebenadazole acts by binding with β-tubulin in parasite intestinal cells, hence suppressing the development of microtubules as a result helminth becomes immobile, its ability to produce eggs is impeded, and it dies as a result of the disruption of glucose intake and the parasites’ digestive and reproductive processes. The drug is poorly absorbed systematically, which enhances its efficacy [20, 21].

However, the risk of potential resistance to benzimidazole drug particularly to mebendazole, may occur due to modification of β tubulin protein, which reduces mebendazole’s ability to bind to β-tubulin [22]. Although a similar class of drugs is principally designed with the same active components, due to brand variations the drug formulation affecting dissolution rates, absorption, and bioavailability results in varied clinical outcomes since these determine a drug’s ability to be properly absorbed into the host circulation and parasite’s cuticle and produce therapeutic efficacy [23, 24].

Most existing studies have focused either on a single brand of mebendazole or on comparisons with other benzimidazole like albendazole against STHs. There is limited information available for efficacy variation of different brands of mebendazole (500 mg) against STHs. Thus, undergoing an in-depth head-to-head comparative efficacy assessment of commonly used mebendazole drugs is found to be crucial. Herein, this study aims to evaluate and compare the therapeutic efficacy of two widely used 500 mg mebendazole brands Vermox® and X-WORM, ® in treating Ascaris lumbricoides and hookworm infections among school-aged children in Addis Zemen and Alember town primary School, northwest Ethiopia.

Materials and methods

Study area

The study was conducted at Addis Zemen and Alember primary schools. Addis Zemen and Alember are found in the South Gondar administration zone in the Amhara national regional state of northwest Ethiopia and are around 637 km far from Addis Ababa. Addis Zemen is the capital town of Libokemkem district [25]. The wide spread practice of open defecation, limited access to clean water, and inadequate sanitation and, moreover community reliance on agriculture and barefoot farming practice contribute to parasite transmission. According to the district health office in Alember town, there is only one government health center and in Addis Zemen town there is one primary district hospital and one health center that provide service for their respective and nearby communities. Existing unpublished data at Addis Zemen hospital and Alember health center laboratory logbook indicates high prevalence of STHs in the community, especially among school-aged children [26, 27].

Study design and period

A Randomized, open-labeled trial was selected to allow direct head-to-head comparisons of two brands of mebendazole under routine field conditions. The 2013 WHO guidelines ensure standardized methodology for evaluating drug efficacy, which enhances the reliability and comparability of the results [28]. The study was conducted from March to June 2021.

Population

All School-aged children (6–14 years old) who are attending primary schools in Addis Zemen Alember towns were considered the source population. The study population consisted of school-aged children from these schools who were confirmed to be infected either with Ascaris lumbricoides or hookworm.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Study participants whose age ranges from 6 to 14 years and individuals confirmed for the presence of Ascaris lumbricoides or hookworm mono-infection. However, this study exclude children with confirmed known major systemic or chronic illness based on their medical history and interview, children’s received anthelminthic treatment or mebendazole within the past four weeks and/or attending other clinical trials during study periods, also if the children’s are diarrheic or vomited within 4 h after the drug take, and unable to give sufficient sample was excluded from the study.

Variables of the study

Dependent variables

In this study, the dependent variables were egg reduction rate and cure rate, as these reflect the outcomes used to assess anthelminthic treatment efficacy.

Independent variables

The brand of mebendazole administered, participants’ age and sex, and baseline infection intensity were considered independent variables in the study.

Sample size and sampling technique

For this, study schools and study participants were selected purposefully based on the WHO efficacy assessment guideline the intended sample size was calculated based on the WHO 2013 anthelminthic efficacy assessment guideline [28]. According to the guideline, a minimum of 50 parasitologically confirmed positive individuals for each of the targeted parasites was needed to evaluate the efficacy of the investigated drug. As a result, a minimum of 200 infected children (50 children with Ascaris lumbricoides and 50 children with hookworm infection for each of the two treatment arms) were needed to evaluate the efficacy of the two drugs. To get this minimum required number of individuals who had microscopically confirmed positive for the targeted parasites, we used the recently estimated prevalence of STH in the Amhara region of 33.4% [29] and a compliance rate of 80%. Moreover, 10% of the calculated sample size was added by considering the possibility of loss to follow-up of the study.

Finally, 824 school-aged children were screened for Ascaris lumbricoides and hookworm infection to get the minimum desired sample size for each of the two treatment arms. Then, schools were selected purposefully based on the WHO efficacy assessment guideline and study participants were selected using a convenient sampling technique.

Data collection and laboratory methods

Data collection tools

Prior to the participant enrolment, each child at the school was requested to take a well-written information sheet and informed consent form written in Amharic language, to their parents or legal guardians to get legal permission for enrolling their child in the study, and reminded to take back the signed informed consent in the next day at school. The objective, study procedure, purpose, and the benefit and possible risks of participating in the study were indicated in the information sheet. In addition, verbal assent was also sought from each child after a signed informed consent was received from each child-parent/legal guardian. Using the WHO efficacy assessment data collection sheet, socio-demographic and health-related information of each consented child was recorded by data collectors (three laboratory professionals and one senior health officer).

Then, a leak-proof stool container labelled with the participant’s unique identification number was given and requested to provide approximately 10gm of their own fresh stool specimen. During sample collection, each child was instructed the collection procedure and reminded to avoid any possible contamination of the sample. Each collected sample was cross-checked with its label with the specified participant identification number to avoid if any possibilities of container exchange during their collection. Moreover, a child who gave an insufficient sample was asked again to provide if he/she can otherwise the sample rejected before transporting to the laboratory. Samples were transported to the laboratory room within an hour.

Laboratory procedures for baseline screening

In this study we used wet-mount for initial screening for intestinal parasites, and McMaster diagnostic techniques was used to determine the number of eggs per gram of stool according to standard operating procedure (SOP) which was stated in WHO bench aid [30]. McMaster diagnostic technique is a standard reference method for the evaluation of anthelmintic drug efficacy [28, 31]. To keep the consistency of workflow, the laboratory class was temporarily classified into three working areas; wet-mount preparation and investigation area, sample weighting and preparation for McMaster diagnosis using flotation solution, and McMaster slide sample loading and examination area. During McMaster diagnostic procedure, following the stool samples was homogeneously mixed, 2 g of stool was suspended in 30 ml saturated sodium chloride flotation solution and mixed thoroughly. Double layered gauze was used to remove the debris, and the suspension was mixed by converting from one stool container to another ten times. Then, a Pasteur pipette was exclusively used for each sample to fill both counting chambers of McMaster slide and rest on the labeled surface for 2 to 3 min to make the egg to become float to the top.

Brands of mebendazole and its origin

Two brands of mebendazole (500 mg) drugs: Vermox® (Janssen Pharmaceutical company; Belgium, lot number: 171Q109, expiry date: 09/2022) and X-WORM® (East Africa pharmaceutical company; Addis Ababa, lot number: jc102, expiry date: 02/2023) with a label claim of 500 mg/tablet were used in this trial. The Vermox® brand of mebendazole was obtained from the local deworming office while the X-WORM® mebendazole brand was purchased from the local pharmacy.

Randomization, drug administration, and outcome measure

Before randomly allocating different brands of mebendazole, those parasitological confirmed children was assessed their eligibility through undergo clinical and, physical examination by a senior health officer. Moreover, questionnaires were also administered to collect previous health-related issue and drug exposure status. Then, using a simple lottery technique, children who fulfilled the pre-specified eligibility criteria were randomized with the respectively assigned treatment arms in a 1:1 ratio. Eligible children who had microscopically confirmed for Ascaris lumbricoides infection were randomized either into the Vermox® or X-WORM® treatment arms. Similarly, individuals who had microscopically confirmed hookworm cases were also randomized into either Vermox® or X-WORM® treatment arms in a 1:1 ratio. Participant’s randomization and allocation into the assigned treatment arm were performed by the principal investigators of the study. Then, the senior health officer administered the allocated treatment to each eligible child after the intactness and the expiration date of the drug were checked. Each child was monitored for approximately 4 h for the possible occurrence of any acute side effects following the treatment in the school. Moreover, each child was reminded to notify if any medical discomfort or side effects occurred after leaving the school to the principal investigators or the nearby health extension worker. The Health officer who administers the drug and children were not blinded for the treatment type. However, to minimize observer bias, laboratory personnel who participated in the fecal egg count during follow-up stool examination were not aware of the types of allocated treatment brand for each child.

Outcome measures

Fecal egg reduction rate (ERR) and cure rate (CR) were used for the measurement of the therapeutic efficacy status of the mebendazole in this trial. In line with WHO recommendation, we select Egg reduction rate as primary outcome measure because it is continuous variables indicate the percentage reduction in egg excreted per gram of faces before and after treatments in all study participants. Cure rate it is a binary measure cured (egg excretion per gram of faces is zero); it does not assess the amount of egg reduced. CR was considered as a secondary outcome measure of this trial. Follow-up data collection

Treated children were revisited between 14 and 21 days of post-treatment and requested to provide a second stool sample using a stool container labeled with their identification number. Each individual was reminded to avoid any possible sample contamination as we instructed them during baseline collection. Children who did not attend the school during the follow-up sample collection time frame were considered as a loss to follow-up. McMaster diagnostic technique was implemented for the quantification of fecal egg count during the follow-up investigation.

Quality assurance

The specific gravity of saturated sodium chloride solution was checked to be 1.2. McMaster slide and flotation solution were checked by a known positive sample. To ensure the accuracy of the egg counts conducted by laboratory technicians, quality control were performed by re-reading 10% randomly selected slides of each laboratory technician by a senior lab technologist. If the expert identifies a difference in the egg count per gram of stool more than 10%, the laboratory technician re-read the slide until a consensus had reached and discussed the reasons for the discrepancy.

Data analysis and interpretation

All baseline and follow-up data were entered to Epi-data software to check and maintain the data completeness and clearance, and then exported to statistical package for social science (SPSS) version-21 software for a statistical analysis. Only participants who had a complete follow-up data were considered in the final efficacy estimation. Descriptive statics like frequency, mean, and standard deviations were used to express the baseline characteristics’ of the study participants. Egg reduction rate and CR were used to assess the therapeutic efficacy of each treatment arm. Arithmetic mean were used to express ERRs [32]. Using WHO guideline, the intensity of infections among infected individuals was grouped in to light, moderate, and heavy infections [28]. Bootstrap re-sampling method with 5000 iterations was used to determine 95% confidence intervals for ERR. An independent t-test was used to compare the baseline characteristics of the randomized participants between the two treatment arm and group means. Paired sample t-test model was used to estimate the ERR and 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) using mean Egg per gram of stool (EPG) at baseline and follow-up data for each treatment group, whereas odds ratio (OR) using logistic regression was used to compare the cure rate. A P-value less than or equal to 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Cure rates (CRs): the percentage of children’s who are egg-negative after administration of a single dose of Vermox®® or X-WORM® brand of mebendazole (500 mg) [33].

Egg-reduction rates (ERRs): the relative reduction in fecal egg counts after administration of Vermox® or X-WORM® mebendazole (500 mg) compared with baseline eggs count [34].

Light intensity of infection: If the number of excreted egg per gram of stool are between 1 and 1,999 for hookworm and Ascaris lumbricoides, if between 1 and 4,999.

Moderate intensity of infection: if the numbers of excreted egg per gram of stool are between 2000 and 3999 for hookworm and Ascaris lumbricoides, if between 5,000 and 49,999.

Heavy intensity: if the number of excreted egg per gram of stool are greater than or equal to 4000 for hookworm and Ascaris lumbricoides, if greater than or equal to 50,0000 [35].

Moreover, therapeutic outcomes of the investigated drugs in terms of ERR was interpreted as satisfactory, doubtful or reduced based on WHO guideline (Table 1).

Result

Participant flow and Baseline characteristics

A total of 824 school-aged children (438 from Alember and 386 from Addis Zemen primary schools) were enrolled during baseline screening. Of these 34.5% (285/824) were positive for either Ascaris lumbricoides or Hookworm mono infection; 150 Hookworm (90 from Alember and 60 from Addis Zemen) and 135 Ascaris lumbricoides (51 from Alember and 84 from Addis Zemen) infected individuals were found. Of these, 25.79% (212/824) SAC were parasitologically confirmed for either Ascaris lumbricoides or hookworm infections and meet the minimum parasitological eligibility criteria of the study (participants mono-infected either for hookworm or Ascaris lumbricoides were considered parasitologically eligible). Using the simple lottery randomization technique, 212 eligible participants were allocated to each treatment arm (106 participants in the Vermox® and 106 participants in the X-WORM® mebendazole brands). In the concern of parasite species distribution in each treatment arm, 53 participants infected with Ascaris lumbricoides were assigned to the each treatment (Vermox® and X-WORM®) arms. While, 53 eligible participants confirmed for hookworm infections were allocated in each of the treatment arm. Of all randomized SAC, 3 individuals from Vermox® (2 Ascaris lumbricoides and 1 hookworm infected) and 3 individuals from X-WORM treatment arms (1 Ascaris lumbricoides and 2 hookworm infected) were lost to follow-up due to their absenteeism in the school. Finally, 206 SAC (103 Ascaris lumbricoides (52 for X-WORM and 51 Vermox) and 103 hookworm cases (51 for X-WORM and 52 for Vermox)) were included in each of the treatment arm for the final analysis (in the available case analysis protocol) (Fig. 1).

Efficacy status of the two mebendazole (500 mg) brands against hookworm and Ascaris lumbricoides infections

In this study the follow-up fecal egg intensity of Ascaris lumbricoides in terms of arithmetic mean was 15.38 and 26.92 among participants who were treated with Vermox® and X-WORM® mebendazole brands, respectively. While, arithmetic mean of the follow-up fecal egg intensity measurement among individuals in Vermox® and X-WORM® treatment arms against hookworm were 157.84 EPG and183.33 EPG, respectively.

The ERR estimated in terms of arithmetic mean of participants infected with Ascaris lumbricoides in Vermox® treatment arm were 97.63% whereas in the X-WORM® treated arm were 95.87%. On the other hand, the ERRs estimated using arithmetic mean were 74.96% in Vermox® and 71.53% in X-WORM® treatment group among hookworm infected individuals (Table 3). In addition, the efficacies of Vermox® and X-worm® in the treatment of Ascaris lumbricoides were 92.3% and 90.4% in terms of CR, respectively. The CR of both brands was nearly similar with odds ratio of 1.27 (95% CI: 0.3-5.0, P-value: 0.73). The efficacy of Vermox® evaluated using CR against hookworm was 29.4%, and the CR of X-WORM® was 27.5% in the treatment of hookworm infection. However, 4 SAC in the Vermox® and 5 SAC in the X-WORM® treatment arms were remained positive at follow up in Ascaris lumbricoide infection. Among hookworm infected study participant 36 from Vermox® and 37 from X-WORM® treated SAC were remained positive at follow-up time. There was no a significant variation on cure rates of both Vermox® and X-WORM® mebendazole against hookworm with odds ratio of 1.10 (95% CI: 0.5–2.60, P-value: 0.826).

Discussion

The principal preventive and control efforts of STHs in endemic tropical countries strongly rely on the mass drug administration campaign of single dose of albendazole or mebendazole drug [16]. However, there is a need of continuous monitoring of the efficacy of the drugs used in the deworming programs and purchased in local pharmaceutical shops through well designed trial to provide up to dated evidence for the continued use of such drugs. Thus, in this trial, we investigated efficacy of two different brands of mebendazole (Vermox® and X-worm®) used in the deworming program and available in local pharmaceutical shops.

In this trial, we found that both brands of single dose of mebendazole were effective (satisfactory) in reducing the intensity of fecal egg counts (ERR in terms of AM; Vermox®: 97.63%, 95%CI: 86.1-108.7 and X-worm®: 95.87%, 95% CI: 85.1-106.9) among SAC who had microscopically confirmed Ascaris lumbricoides infection. These estimated efficacy status of both brands of mebendazole using ERR were supported with our secondary or qualitative measurement (CR) of mebendazole efficacy against Ascaris lumbricoides with an estimated cure rate of Vermox® (CR: 92.3%, 95% CI: 84.6%−98.1%) and X-worm® (CR: 90.4%, 95% CI: 82.7–98.1). When we interpreted these efficacy status according to WHO anthelmintic efficacy status classification criteria, the ERR of both brands of mebendazole for the treatment of Ascaris lumbricoides had a satisfactory therapeutic efficacy level with ERR ≥ 95% [28]. Moreover, the complete parasite egg clearance rate of both brands of mebendazole were not showed any significant variation for the treatment of Ascaris lumbricoide confirmed cases (CR; Vermox®: 92.3 versus X-worm®: 90.4, OR: 1.27, P-value: 0.728).

The therapeutic efficacy results of the current study in both brands of mebendazole were in line with a previous study conducted in Northwest Ethiopia (ERR: 99.1% & CR: 96.9%) and with many more clinical trials which were conducted in China (ERR: 100% & CR: 93%) [36], Lao PDR(ERR: 100% & CR: 90%) [37], Mafia Island (ERR: 100%) [38], Pemba Island Tanzania (ERR: 100% & CR: 95%) [39], Zanzibar (ERR:99.1) [39]. Moreover, our finding is supported with a multi-centered clinical trial conducted in six countries including Ethiopia ERR ranged from 92.3% to 99.8% [40].

On the other hand, so far studies have investigated the efficacy of mebendazole (500 mg) against hookworm infections despite their finding is inconsistent. Egg reduction rates (ERRs) reported in previous studies ranged from 49.6 to 83.6% [36–41], while cure rates (CRs) varied between 7.6% and 76.3% [36, 38, 39, 42–45]. In our finding, the efficacious level of both brands of mebendazole showed a satisfactory therapeutic outcome per WHO efficacy classification criteria [28] for treating hookworm infection with an estimated value of Vermox® (ERRAM: 74.96%, 95% CI: 65.6–85.2) and X-worm® (ERRAM: 71.53% (61.5–81.6). Despite, the therapeutic status of mebendazole for treating hookworm infection indicated as satisfactory level per the WHO guideline, the majority of study participants remained under light infection intensity as they were too in the baseline screening. Unlike the ERR efficacy status of mebendazole against hookworm infection, the CR of both brands of mebendazole for the treatment of hookworm proved its unsatisfactory fecal egg clearance rate among treated individuals with a curative status of Vermox® (CR: 29.41%, 95% CI: 17.6–43.1) and X-worm® (CE: 25.49%, 95% CI: 13.7–37.3).

Our finding regarding the efficacy of mebendazole brands against hookworm infection in terms of ERR and CR was supported by a study conducted in Burie and Debre eliyas town northwest Ethiopia CR-ERR% (30.8–68.9) [42], Pemba Isaland Tanzania ERR % (68) [39], Lao PDR CR-ERR% (17.6–76.3) [46], Mafia island Tanzania CR-ERR% (31.3–78.1) [44], Pemba Isaland Tanzania ERR % (70.8), and China CR-ERR% (31.3–76.3) [36] and in line with only with CRs of clinical trials conducted in Pemba Isaland Tanzania CR% (24.4) [43] Northwest Ethiopia % (23.1) [47].

The therapeutic outcomes observed in this study, particularly the egg reduction rate (ERR), were notably higher than those reported in previous studies conducted in other regions. For instance, an ERR of 49.6% was reported in a study from Ethiopia [47], while studies from Zanzibar and Tanzania reported ERRs of 52.1% and 59.5%, with corresponding cure rates (CRs) of 7.6% and 13%, respectively [43, 44] and the CR is higher than clinical trials conducted VCR.

This inconsistent result may be related to the variation of baseline infection intensity level, diagnostic techniques used, participant’s age, sample size, genetic diversity hookworm species, number of sampling, study area and study participants in association with previous anthelmintic exposure status. For instance, a previous study conducted in the northwest part of Ethiopia applied a Kato-Katz diagnostic technique for estimation of the therapeutic outcome of mebendazole in terms of ERR against hookworm unlike our study. Regarding the Kato-Katz diagnostic technique, a recently published study reported that failure to examine slides within 20–30 min after preparation during hookworm diagnosis can result in a 10% reduction in fecal egg counts [48]. This finding contrasts with the current WHO guideline, which allows examination of Kato-Katz slides up to 60 min post-preparation.

Moreover, a study conducted in Tanzania used two repeated stool sample for the quantification of fecal egg intensity and enrolled small number of study participants when we compared with the present study. Geometric mean estimation was also used for the measurement of fecal egg reduction in the study conducted at Tanzania and Zanzibar. However, recently published scientific comments and articles [34]. Besides, the study participants enrolled in a study conducted in Zanzibar the intensity of hookworm infection were under the category of heavy and moderate level unlikely in our trial where the infection intensity of enrolled participants was under light intensity level.

Possible explanations for the low response of mebendazole against hookworm include the inherent lower susceptibility of hookworms, particularly Necator americanus, to benzimidazoles compared to Ascaris lumbricoides [49]. Mebendazole also has poor absorption and low systemic bioavailability, which may limit its effectiveness against tissue-dwelling hookworms [50]. Furthermore, emerging resistance due to β-tubulin gene mutations similar to patterns observed in veterinary medicine may also contribute to reduced efficacy [51].

Although comparing commonly used different brands of mebendazole in head to head manner to provide valuable evidence on drug efficacy and support informed decision-making for deworming programs considered as strength of the study, the use of single stool sample for the quantification of fecal egg count was considered as a limitation of the study. Moreover, the therapeutic outcomes of this trial did not consider the moderate and heavy infection intensity of individuals infected with Ascaris lumbricoides and hookworm infection due to all screened participants at baseline screening were found only in light infection intensity level.

Conclusion and recommendation

Based on our findings, we conclude that the efficacy status of both brands of mebendazole against Ascaris lumbricoides and hookworm infections showed a satisfactory efficacious level in terms of ERR. However, despite, effective fecal ERRs of both brands of mebendazole in the treatment of hookworm infections were observed, the complete clearance of parasite egg expressed in terms of CR remained under question even all study participants were in light intensity of infection. Therefore, we strongly recommend that the relevant stakeholders revisit the current anthelmintic efficacy assessment guidelines, with particular consideration of the baseline intensity of infection in individuals, prior to appreciating the continued use to prevent development drug resistance. Based on our findings, we are highly concerned about the potential emergence of resistance to mebendazole in treating hookworm infections, as reflected by low cure rate observed, similar to resistance pattern reported in the veterinary use benzimidazole drugs. Moreover, we recommend further investigation regarding the therapeutic outcomes of mebendazole by considering heavy and moderate infection intensity level to generate strong evidence.
