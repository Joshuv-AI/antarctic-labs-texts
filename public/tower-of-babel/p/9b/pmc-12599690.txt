Causal associations between food intakes and autoimmune skin diseases: A 2-sample Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Ting Xiao, Haishan Lu, Shaoying Pan, Xudong Hong, Aifen Chen, Xudong Zhang, Yan Hu, Chunping Ni, Shengxian Shen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Autoimmune skin diseases, such as bullous pemphigoid, pemphigus, lupus erythematosus, vitiligo, psoriasis, and atopic dermatitis, are chronic conditions driven by dysregulated immune responses. While emerging evidence suggests a potential role of food intakes in modulating these diseases through immune and inflammatory pathways, the causal relationships remain unclear due to limitations of observational studies. This study employed a 2-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) approach to evaluate the causal effects of 25 food intakes on the risk of autoimmune skin diseases, using genetic variants as instrumental variables. Exposure data were derived from the UK Biobank, and outcome data were obtained from the FinnGen Biobank. The inverse variance weighted method, complemented by MR-Egger, weighted median, and MR-PRESSO analyses, identified significant causal associations. After Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (P < 4 × 10⁻⁴), no associations remained statistically significant. However, several nominally significant (P < .05) associations were identified. Genetically predicted oily fish intake was inversely associated with bullous pemphigoid, and melon intake with lupus erythematosus. Cereal, milk, poultry, and salted nuts intake showed inverse associations with pemphigus. Tea and salted nuts intake were associated with reduced atopic dermatitis risk, while cheese, dried fruit, and salted nuts intake were associated with lower psoriasis risk. Conversely, alcohol and poultry intake were associated with increased psoriasis risk. No causal associations were observed for vitiligo. Sensitivity analyses (including MR-Egger intercept, Cochran Q test, MR-PRESSO, and leave-one-out analysis) supported the robustness and directionality of these nominally significant effects, with no evidence of directional pleiotropy. Multiple nominally significant associations were observed, suggesting potential protective roles for foods such as oily fish, nuts, fruits, and tea in specific autoimmune skin conditions, and a potential risk associated with alcohol consumption for psoriasis. These suggestive findings highlight the need for further investigation in larger cohorts to clarify the role of diet in the prevention and management of autoimmune skin conditions.

1. Introduction

Autoimmune skin diseases represent a group of heterogeneous disorders driven by dysregulated immune responses targeting structural components of the skin. These diseases are characterized by chronic inflammation and tissue damage mediated by autoantibodies, autoreactive T cells, and pro-inflammatory cytokines.[1] Common autoimmune skin diseases include bullous pemphigoid (BP), pemphigus, lupus erythematosus (LE), vitiligo, psoriasis, and atopic dermatitis (AD).[2] Despite advances in understanding these diseases, their etiopathogenesis remains incompletely elucidated. Notably, the interplay between genetic and environmental factors, including diet, appears to play a critical role but requires further exploration.

The role of food intakes in modulating the immune system and influencing autoimmune diseases has gained increasing attention. Diet is known to impact gut microbiota composition and function, which in turn affects systemic inflammation and immune responses through the gut–skin axis.[3] For instance, high-sugar and high-fat diets are associated with increased pro-inflammatory cytokines, while anti-inflammatory dietary patterns, such as the Mediterranean diet, have shown promise in reducing inflammation and modulating immune responses.[4] Moreover, specific nutrients and food components have been demonstrated to exert direct immunomodulatory effects. Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFAs), found abundantly in fish oils, can suppress the production of pro-inflammatory eicosanoids and cytokines and promote the synthesis of anti-inflammatory mediators such as resolvins and protectins.[5] Similarly, nuts are rich in unsaturated fats, fiber, and polyphenols, which collectively contribute to anti-oxidative and anti-inflammatory responses, thereby potentially mitigating chronic inflammatory processes.[6]

Emerging evidence suggests potential associations between diet and autoimmune skin diseases.[7] For example, certain dietary interventions have been linked to improvements in psoriasis and AD symptoms.[4] However, most existing studies are observational and prone to confounding and reverse causation, making it difficult to draw definitive conclusions about causality. For other autoimmune skin diseases such as BP, pemphigus, LE, and vitiligo, the evidence regarding the influence of food intakes remains sparse and inconclusive. Although mechanistic insights from nutritional immunology support the plausibility of dietary influence, robust human evidence is lacking, particularly for less common autoimmune dermatoses. Consequently, there is an urgent need for robust studies to clarify the causal role of diet in autoimmune skin diseases, which could inform novel preventive and therapeutic strategies.

To address these challenges, this study employs the Mendelian randomization (MR) approach, which uses genetic variants as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causality between exposures (food intakes) and outcomes (autoimmune skin diseases).[8,9] MR provides a robust framework to overcome biases inherent in traditional observational studies and allows for stronger causal inference.[10] By focusing on BP, pemphigus, LE, vitiligo, psoriasis, and AD, this study aims to elucidate the causal relationships between food intakes and these diseases, providing critical insights into potential dietary interventions for their prevention and management.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This study represents a secondary analysis of genome-wide association study (GWAS) datasets. Employing a 2-sample MR approach, we aimed to explore the causal relationships between food intakes and autoimmune skin diseases. A schematic overview of the study design and data sources is presented in Figure 1. Only publicly available GWAS summary statistics were utilized in this analysis, thereby exempting the study from the need for additional ethical approval or informed consent.

A directed acyclic graph was used to represent the hypothetical effects of 25 dietary habits on 6 autoimmune skin diseases. Dotted lines in the graph indicate potential direct causal relationships or pleiotropic effects between the exposures and the outcomes.

2.2. Data sources

The exposure data used in this study were obtained from the United Kingdom Biobank (UKB), a large-scale biomedical database that collected genetic and health-related data from approximately 500,000 participants across various regions of the United Kingdom between 2006 and 2010.[11] The dietary exposure factors analyzed in this study were based on previous studies,[8,9] including categorized as follows: meat intake (beef intake, pork intake, poultry intake, lamb/mutton intake, non-oily fish intake, oily fish intake, processed meat intake, and shellfish intake), vegetable and fruit consumption (dried fruit intake, fresh fruit intake, cooked vegetable intake, salad/raw vegetable intake, and melon intake), drinks consumption (alcohol intake, coffee intake, drinking water intake, and tea intake), carbohydrate consumption (bread intake, cake intake, cereal intake, chocolate intake) dairy product consumption (cheese intake and milk intake), and other dietary types (nut intake and mushroom intake). To generate the outcome genetic instrumental variants, the GWAS summary statistics for autoimmune skin diseases were downloaded online from FinnGen Biobank Documentation of the R12 release, which includes 507 BP cases, 163 pemphigus cases, and 375,767 controls (https://storage.googleapis.com/finngen-public-data-r9/summary_stats/R9_manifest.tsv). Detailed information regarding the exposure and outcome datasets, including data sources, measurement units, and summary statistics, is provided in Table 1.

2.3. Selection of instruments

To ensure the integrity and accuracy of the research findings, we implemented the following rigorous procedures to select optimal IVs. First, single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) significant at the genome-wide level (P < 5 × 10⁻⁶) and independent (r² < 0.001 and distance > 10,000 kb) were selected as exposure IVs. Second, SNPs strongly correlated with the outcome (P < 5 × 10⁻⁸) were excluded from the analysis. Third, we harmonized the SNPs for exposure and outcome by removing or adjusting inconsistent alleles. Finally, the F statistic was employed to ensure a strong association between IVs and exposure, and SNPs with an F statistic (calculated as F = β2exposure/SE2exposure) < 10[12] were excluded from the study.

2.4. Statistical analyses

The random-effects inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was employed as the primary approach to estimate the association between each dietary factor and autoimmune skin disease risk. The IVW model combines the Wald ratios of individual SNPs to produce an overall causal estimate, making it the most statistically powerful method for detecting causal relationships in 2-sample MR analyses.[13] Additionally, complementary methods, including MR-Egger, weighted median, and MR-PRESSO, were utilized to enhance the reliability of the results. The weighted median method allows for up to 50% of invalid IVs, while the MR-Egger method accommodates scenarios where all IVs may be invalid.[14,15] Given that we tested 25 food exposures across 5 primary autoimmune skin disease outcomes, we set a stringent significance threshold of P < .05/(25 × 5) = 4 × 10−4 (P < .0004). The MR-PRESSO test was applied to identify potential outliers and perform corrections by excluding these outliers.[16] Heterogeneity within the IVW model was assessed using Cochran Q test, with a P-value < .05 indicating significant heterogeneity. Horizontal pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept test, where a P-value < .05 suggested the presence of horizontal pleiotropy. To examine the influence of individual SNPs on the causal estimates, leave-one-out analyses were performed. All data analyses were conducted using the TwoSampleMR packages in R (version 4.2.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). In this study, a causal effect was considered valid only when all MR methods demonstrated consistent results in the same direction.

3. Results

3.1. Genetic instruments for 25 food intakes

Table 1 provides detailed information about each GWAS study included in the analysis. A total of 25 distinct food intakes were examined as exposures. The number of SNPs used for each dietary habit ranged from 10 to 232. Table S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q579 presents comprehensive details on the IVs utilized for these 25 food intakes. The impact of various dietary exposures on the outcome was assessed, and, in all cases, the F statistics of the selected SNPs exceeded the empirical threshold of 10, ranging from 20.93 to 493.65. These findings indicate that the results are robust and unlikely to be influenced by weak IVs.

3.2. Causal effects of food intakes on autoimmune skin diseases

The summary of causal effects of food intakes on different types of autoimmune skin diseases are shown in Figure 2. Based on the inverse (IVW) method, several associations demonstrated nominal significance (PIVW < .05). However, variance weighted after applying a strict Bonferroni correction for multiple testing (significance threshold set at P < 4 × 10⁻⁴ to account for 25 exposures and 5 outcomes), none of the associations retained formal statistical significance.

Summary results of casual association between food intakes and autoimmune skin diseases according to the inverse variance weighted method.

Among the most notable nominally significant associations (all PIVW < .05), genetically predicted oily fish intake was inversely associated with BP (odds ratio [OR], 0.26; 95% CI, 0.09–0.72; PIVW = .009). Alcohol intake showed a positive association with psoriasis risk (OR, 1.33; 95% CI, 1.09–1.61; PIVW = .004), while dried fruit intake was inversely associated (OR, 0.65; 95% CI, 0.48–0.88; PIVW = .005). Other nominally significant protective associations included milk intake with pemphigus (OR, 0.002; 95% CI, 0.00–0.15; PIVW = .003), tea intake with AD (OR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.61–0.95; PIVW = .016), and salted nuts intake with AD (OR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.35–0.92; PIVW = .022), pemphigus (OR, 0.007; 95% CI, 0.00–0.57; PIVW = .026), and psoriasis (OR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.26–0.84; PIVW = .011). The complete list of all nominally significant findings is presented in Figure 3.

Forest plot showing results from Mendelian randomization study by the inverse variance weighted method.

These results suggest that while none of the associations surpassed the stringent corrected threshold, several exposures showed suggestive evidence of a potential relationship with autoimmune skin disease risk. The consistency of effect directions across multiple food intake–disease pairs, particularly for salted nuts, warrants further investigation in larger-scale studies. No causal relationships were identified between the examined food intakes and vitiligo.

3.3. Sensitivity analyses

The MR-Egger intercept analysis showed no evidence of directional pleiotropy between food intakes and autoimmune skin diseases (P > .05) (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q579). Cochrane Q test in IVW suggested that there was no heterogeneity except causal relationship between tea intake with AD, alcohol intake, dried fruit and poultry intake with psoriasis, and oily fish intake with BP (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q579). Furthermore, the MR-PRESSO analysis (Tables S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q579) detected some outliers between tea intake with AD, alcohol intake, dried fruit and poultry intake with psoriasis, and oily fish intake with BP (P < .05). The precise P-values and intercept estimates are provided in the corresponding Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q579. The direction and amplitude of the WM and MR-Egger methods remained consistent with the IVW method, which supported the robustness of the causal relationships (Figure S1, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q579). The leave-one-out analysis plot demonstrated that the association between cereal intake, milk intake, poultry intake and salted nuts intake with pemphigus, salted nuts intake with AD, and melon intake with LE remained consistent regardless of individual SNPs (Figure S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q579), while the symmetry of funnel plots was maintained (Figure S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/Q579).

4. Discussion

This study utilized a 2-sample MR approach to evaluate the causal effects of food intakes on the risk of autoimmune skin diseases, including BP, pemphigus, LE, psoriasis, and AD. Our findings demonstrate significant causal associations between specific food intakes and the risk of autoimmune skin diseases, providing critical insights into the role of diet in modulating disease susceptibility and progression. These results underscore the potential of dietary interventions as preventive or therapeutic strategies for managing autoimmune skin conditions.

While our results demonstrate statistical significance, the interpretation of effect sizes is crucial for assessing their potential clinical relevance. The observed ORs range from very substantial (e.g., OR = 0.002 for milk intake on pemphigus, 0.09 for melon intake on LE) to more modest but still important effects (e.g., OR = 0.76 for tea intake on AD, 1.15 for alcohol intake on psoriasis). These estimates represent the change in disease risk per genetic standard deviation in intake. From a public health perspective, even modest effect sizes can translate to substantial population-level impact for common exposures and diseases. For instance, the protective effect of oily fish intake on BP (OR = 0.26) suggests a 74% reduction in relative risk, which could be clinically meaningful given the severity of this condition. Similarly, the more modest association between alcohol consumption and psoriasis (OR = 1.15) gains importance considering the high prevalence of alcohol use. However, we acknowledge that MR estimates reflect lifelong genetic predisposition rather than short-term dietary modifications, and the clinical implementation of these findings would require further investigation through randomized trials to establish optimal dietary recommendations and quantify absolute risk reductions.

Several protective effects of food intakes were observed across different autoimmune skin diseases. For BP, oily fish intake was identified as a protective factor, primarily attributed to the anti-inflammatory properties of omega-3 fatty acids (EPA and DHA).[17] Mechanistically, these long-chain fatty acids are incorporated into cell membranes and serve as precursors for specialized pro-resolving mediators, including resolvins and protectins, which actively terminate inflammatory processes, reduce neutrophil infiltration, and decrease production of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) – key mechanisms in preventing autoimmune blister formation.[5,18] Similarly, melon intake demonstrated a protective effect against LE, likely due to its antioxidant properties, which mitigate oxidative stress, a key contributor to lupus pathogenesis.[19] The high content of vitamin C, carotenoids, and superoxide dismutase in melons may scavenge reactive oxygen species, reduce oxidative damage to DNA and proteins, and decrease neutrophil extracellular trap formation, thereby attenuating the type I interferon signature characteristic of systemic LE.[20]

In pemphigus, cereal intake, milk intake, poultry intake, and salted nuts intake showed protective associations. Nutritional components such as dietary proteins, vitamins, and minerals may contribute to maintaining immune homeostasis and mitigating tissue-specific autoimmune attacks.[17] Whole cereals are rich in fiber and SCFAs like butyrate, which enhance regulatory T-cell function and intestinal barrier integrity, potentially reducing systemic autoantibody production.[21] Similarly, vitamin D from fortified milk modulates adaptive immunity by inhibiting Th17 differentiation and promoting regulatory T-cells, which may suppress autoreactive B cells involved in pemphigus pathogenesis.[22] For AD, both tea intake and salted nuts intake were negatively associated with disease risk, possibly due to their immune-regulatory properties. Tea polyphenols, such as epigallocatechin gallate, have been shown to modulate T-cell responses and reduce pro-inflammatory cytokine production,[23] specifically by inhibiting NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways, suppressing Th2/Th17 polarization, and enhancing skin barrier function through upregulation of filaggrin expression.[24] Nuts provide zinc and selenium, which are crucial for maintaining skin integrity and modulating Toll-like receptor signaling, thereby potentially limiting aberrant innate immune activation in AD.[25]

For psoriasis, a complex interplay between food intakes was observed. While alcohol intake and poultry intake increased the risk of psoriasis, cheese, dried fruit, and salted nuts showed protective effects. These findings align with evidence linking alcohol consumption to increased pro-inflammatory cytokine levels and impaired immune regulation.[26,27] Ethanol metabolism acetaldehyde can promote keratinocyte hyperproliferation and enhance IL-23/Th17 axis activation, central drivers of psoriatic plaque formation.[28] Conversely, protective food intakes like dried fruit and nuts contain bioactive compounds with anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties.[29–31] Dried fruits are concentrated sources of polyphenols and fiber, which may dampen psoriatic inflammation by inhibiting phosphodiesterase-4 and promoting anti-inflammatory microbiota such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus species.[32]

Interestingly, no causal relationships were identified between food intakes and vitiligo. It is important to note that the limited number of vitiligo cases (n = 260) in the FinnGen cohort reduces the statistical power of our analysis to detect causal effects, particularly for modest effect sizes. Therefore, our findings should be interpreted as an absence of evidence for large-effect associations, rather than as definitive evidence of no association. Beyond power limitations, the null findings may also reflect the unique pathogenesis of vitiligo, driven predominantly by melanocyte-specific autoimmune mechanisms rather than systemic inflammatory pathways influenced by diet.[33] The absence of association may stem from the T-cell-mediated cytotoxicity directed against melanocytes, which appears less sensitive to nutritional modulation compared to the cytokine-driven inflammation seen in psoriasis or AD. Additionally, oxidative stress in vitiligo is primarily driven by intrinsic defects in melanocyte antioxidant defenses rather than dietary antioxidant availability.[34] Future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to conclusively evaluate the potential causal effects of diet on vitiligo risk.

This study offers several key contributions to the understanding of diet–disease relationships in autoimmune skin diseases. First, the use of a 2-sample MR approach mitigates biases inherent in observational studies, such as confounding and reverse causation, thereby strengthening causal inference.[35] Second, the analysis leveraged large-scale GWAS datasets from the UKB and FinnGen, providing robust statistical power and generalizability. Third, the comprehensive examination of 25 food intakes allowed for nuanced insights into how specific dietary patterns influence different autoimmune skin diseases.

Despite its strengths, this study has limitations. First, the MR approach relies on 3 key assumptions: the relevance assumption (genetic instruments strongly associate with exposure), the independence assumption (instruments are not associated with confounders), and the exclusion restriction assumption (instruments affect outcome only through the exposure). While we employed multiple sensitivity analyses (including MR-Egger, weighted median, and MR-PRESSO) to evaluate horizontal pleiotropy, and no significant pleiotropy was detected in most analyses, residual bias due to undetected pleiotropy cannot be fully excluded. For instance, genetic variants influencing food intakes may also affect disease risk through other biological pathways (e.g., metabolic or inflammatory pathways), potentially violating the exclusion restriction assumption. Although we selected instruments from GWASs of specific food intakes to minimize pleiotropy, the complexity of genetic contributions to diet and disease means that complete avoidance of pleiotropy is challenging. Furthermore, while MR reduces confounding compared to observational designs, residual confounding due to unknown or unmeasured factors cannot be entirely ruled out. Although we used genetic variants as instruments, these variants may be associated with other lifestyle or environmental factors that influence disease risk, introducing potential bias. Additionally, dietary intake may influence autoimmune skin diseases through pathways not fully captured by our analysis, such as effects on gut microbiota composition, epigenetic modifications, or interactions with specific medications. These unaccounted pathways represent important avenues for future research. Third, the study primarily included European populations, limiting its applicability to other ethnic groups. Specifically, both the exposure data (from UKB) and outcome data (from FinnGen) were derived from European-ancestry populations. While this reduces bias from population stratification, it limits the generalizability of our findings to non-European populations who may have different genetic architectures, dietary patterns, and cultural food preferences. For instance, certain food items considered in our study (e.g., specific types of fish, nuts, or dairy) may be consumed in different quantities or preparations across ethnicities, and genetic variants associated with these dietary habits may vary in frequency and effect size across ancestral groups. Furthermore, differences in genetic background could influence the metabolism of dietary components or the immune response to these components, potentially modifying the causal effects observed in European populations. Therefore, our results should be interpreted with caution when considering their applicability to non-European populations, and future replication in diverse ethnic groups is necessary.

The findings of this study have important implications for clinical practice and public health. Promoting anti-inflammatory dietary patterns, such as increased consumption of oily fish, nuts, and fruits, could serve as cost-effective and noninvasive strategies to reduce the risk of autoimmune skin diseases. Conversely, public health efforts to reduce alcohol consumption may help mitigate the risk of psoriasis. Personalized dietary recommendations based on genetic susceptibility may further optimize prevention and management strategies.[36,37]

Future studies should focus on validating these findings in longitudinal cohorts and randomized controlled trials. Additionally, integrating dietary data with microbiome and metabolomic analyses could shed light on the gut–skin axis and its role in autoimmune skin diseases.[38–40] For example, investigating how dietary components are metabolized by gut microbiota into immunomodulatory metabolites (e.g., tryptophan derivatives, bile acids, and SCFAs) and how these metabolites influence skin-resident immune cells would provide deeper mechanistic insights. Expanding genetic analyses to diverse populations will also enhance the generalizability and applicability of these findings globally. We strongly emphasize the need for future MR studies to include multi-ancestry datasets to examine whether the causal relationships between dietary factors and autoimmune skin diseases are consistent across different ethnic backgrounds. Such efforts would not only improve the generalizability of findings but also help identify population-specific causal effects that could inform targeted dietary recommendations for different ethnic groups.

5. Conclusions

This MR study did not identify any statistically significant causal associations between genetically predicted food intakes and autoimmune skin diseases after strict correction for multiple comparisons. However, multiple nominally significant associations were observed, suggesting potential protective roles for foods such as oily fish, nuts, fruits, and tea in specific autoimmune skin conditions, and a potential risk associated with alcohol consumption for psoriasis. These suggestive findings, supported by consistent sensitivity analyses, highlight the need for further investigation in larger, more powerful genetic studies and randomized controlled trials to confirm these relationships and explore their clinical relevance for dietary interventions in autoimmune skin diseases.
