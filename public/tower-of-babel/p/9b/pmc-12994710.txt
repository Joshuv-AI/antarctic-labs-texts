Genome Evolution and the Enigmatic Axial Skeleton of the Hero Shrew (Soricidae: Scutisorex Somereni)
Authors: Austin S Chipps, Carl R Hutter, Terrence C Demos, Jacob A Esselstyn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLinking genetic variation with traits of interest has been a longstanding challenge for evolutionary biologists. Here, we explore elements of genome evolution in the hero shrew (Soricidae: Scutisorex somereni): a small, elusive mammal with the most distinctive vertebral column in the entire tree of life. We first assembled and annotated a draft genome from short-read, long-read, and chromosome-conformation-capture sequencing for S. somereni. We combined this genome with publicly available genomes from 30 other mammal species spanning millions of years of evolutionary history to search for genes potentially related to the evolution of the S. somereni axial skeleton. We first performed an orthology clustering analysis to identify gene duplications and single copy orthologs. With orthogroups, we tested for gene family expansion and contraction. The single copy orthologs that we identified were used to test for positive selection or changes in selective constraint in S. somereni compared to the other species considered. We found significant gene family size change in immune, sensory, and metabolic gene families, and identified multiple HOX genes under positive selection that might help explain the evolution of the hero shrew's extreme phenotype. Overall, our study is a critical first step that has highlighted candidate genes and gene families for further genomic exploration, underscoring the continuing challenge of understanding the genomic underpinnings of unusual phenotypes.

Connecting genetic variation with traits is critical for understanding the evolution of biodiversity. This study explores genome evolution in the hero shrew, an animal with the strangest axial skeleton among all vertebrates that has been modified to have interlocking vertebrae that resist compressive forces. The findings highlight the occurrence of multiple evolutionary mechanisms when compared to other mammals, and provide target genes for future exploration.

Introduction

Mammals epitomize diversity (Jones et al. 2011)—they can be found everywhere on Earth ranging in size from 3 g shrews and bats to 100,000 kg whales. Placental mammals represent an especially rich radiation of more than 6,000 species that evolved over a period of 100 million years (Burgin et al. 2018; Foley et al. 2023). Across this radiation, numerous complex, ecologically important traits have arisen. Well-known examples include echolocation, foregut fermentation, and various locomotory specializations (Polly 2007; Jones et al. 2009; Grossnickle et al. 2019; Chai et al. 2020; Moeller and Sanders 2020). Other traits shared by all mammals like the axial skeleton, for example, have remained relatively unchanged for the past 100 million years or so. Regionalization of the mammalian axial skeleton is highly conserved across the placental radiation, where cervical vertebrae count is 7 in nearly all species, thoracolumbar vertebrae count is typically 19 to 20, and sacral vertebrae count is typically 3 to 4 (Narita and Kuratani 2005; Buchholtz et al. 2012). While vertebral count changes are rare, axial skeleton evolution is more often realized through changes to the size and shape of vertebrae (Asher et al. 2011; Buchholtz 2012). Nowhere is this more extreme than in the small mammals known as hero shrews (Soricidae: Scutisorex; Allen et al. 1917; Stanley et al. 2013).

Scutisorex somereni is a 50 to 70 g animal found in the Congo Basin of Africa, where it inhabits the undergrowth of lowland and montane forests, feeding on invertebrates (Churchfield et al. 2007). It was first described by Thomas (1910), who at the time was able to examine only a study skin and skull. A few years later, Allen et al. (1917) examined additional specimens and noted the extremely modified axial skeleton. Scutisorex somereni possesses what is perhaps the most underappreciated, yet enigmatic skeletal trait among mammals, and to a certain extent, vertebrates. Externally, S. somereni resembles other shrews (Fig. 1). The axial skeleton, however, has evolved reinforced thoracic and lumbar vertebrae, massive ribs, a rugose cranium, and robust trabecular bone (Smith and Angielczyk 2020, 2022; Fig. 2). The vertebrae possess numerous accessory tubercles which interlock adjacent vertebrae and, combined with the ribs, make the axial skeleton incredibly robust to compressive forces without sacrificing mobility (Cullinane et al. 1998; Cullinane and Aleper 1998). This novel axial skeleton evolved in less than ca. 7 million years (Hutterer et al. 2018) and is so different from those of closely related shrews that it seemingly must be adaptive. Nevertheless, researchers have yet to convincingly identify an ecological explanation of the hero shrew's anomalous skeleton (Smith and Angielczyk 2020). Given the rapid evolution of this trait, there is a need to identify the genetic elements responsible for such phenotypic novelty.

Whole genomes can be sequenced and assembled quicker than ever, even in non-model taxa (Rhie et al. 2021; Paez et al. 2022). Genome studies have provided medical and basic biological insights, while novel analysis methods have facilitated large comparative genomics studies across taxonomic groups to understand the relationships among genome organization and variation, evolution, and natural history (Hiller et al. 2012; Claussnitzer et al. 2020; Hilgers and Hiller 2025). Increased access to curated sets of genetic sequences (genomic resources) has furthered our understanding of enduring problems in biology, such as linking genetic variation with phenotypes of interest. Genomic resources, however, are still unevenly spread across the tree of life. Factors contributing to this pattern include economic value, organismal charisma, genome complexity, and the challenge of obtaining high-quality DNA samples from wild animals or museum specimens (Blom 2021; Raxworthy and Smith 2021).

A concerted effort has been made to sequence reference genomes across Mammalia, with 908 genomes currently available from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI; www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov: accessed January 2026; Zoonomia Consortium 2020; Christmas et al. 2023). The growth of genomic resources for mammals has facilitated both large-scale and clade-specific comparative genomics studies aiming to relate genetic variation with traits of interest (Tollis et al. 2019; Roycroft et al. 2021; Yuan et al. 2021; Chavez et al. 2022; Christmas et al. 2023; Cossette et al. 2024; Kopania et al. 2024; Morales et al. 2024; Steinbinder et al. 2024; Telizhenko et al. 2024; Pospelova et al. 2025; Zhang et al. 2025). Genetic variation is the raw material for evolution by natural selection, and comparing genomes among species highlights shared or distinct variation underlying target traits. In general, most observed genetic variation within and between species is neutral (Kimura 1968; King and Jukes 1969), but rare, advantageous mutations can facilitate the evolution of novel phenotypes. Comparing rates of molecular evolution is a critical step in determining the strength and direction of natural selection (Kimura 1977; Miyata and Yasunaga 1980). In addition to mutations, gene gains and losses are other evolutionary processes that alter the genomic landscape of natural selection (Demuth and Hahn 2009). Duplicated genes can yield novel phenotypes through dosage effects or neofunctionalization of the duplicate gene copy (Lynch and Conery 2000; Magadum et al. 2013). Likewise, gene loss can generate phenotypic novelty through nonfunctionalization of genes related to traits that have become maladaptive (Olson 1999; Albalat and Cañestro 2016). Whereas nucleotide sequences are used to calculate rates of molecular evolution, gene orthology can be used to understand gene family size changes. Orthogroups are sets of genes descended from a single gene in the last common ancestor of the species being considered and therefore contain both orthologues and paralogues. In this way, constructing orthogroups can facilitate the detection of gene duplication and loss (Emms and Kelly 2015, 2019). Interrogating gene families and nucleotide substitutions together can help to uncover the evolutionary processes facilitating adaptation and the accumulation of biodiversity.

The rapid modification of the enigmatic S. somereni axial skeleton presents an opportunity to explore the genomic elements underlying extreme phenotypic evolution. Given its complexity, we hypothesized that multiple genes and evolutionary mechanisms underly this phenotype. To identify candidate genes, gene families, and processes, we assembled and annotated the first draft genome for S. somereni. We then compared the S. somereni draft assembly to 30 total reference genomes, representing all superorders of Mammalia (Xenarthra, Afrotheria, Euarchontoglires, and Laurasiatheria), and including 3 other shrews, with an orthology clustering analysis. We next estimated rates of gene duplication and gene family expansion and contraction for S. somereni. By using nucleotide sequences from single copy orthologs shared among eutherian species, we quantified the strength and direction of selection in S. somereni.

Results

Genome Assembly and Annotation

Our sequencing runs generated 6.7e8 Illumina paired-end reads (∼200 Gbp), 4.4e8 paired-end HiC reads (∼130 Gbp), and 5.0e6 PacBio reads (∼91 Gbp). Our final draft genome assembly of S. somereni (LSUMZ 38908; GenBank accession: GCA_054737625.1; NCBI BioProject Accession: PRJNA1337422) comprised 2.6 Gbp and 3,000 scaffolds at 30X coverage (fig. S1 and Table S2). Fifty percent of the assembly was in scaffolds 109.8 Mbp or larger (the largest being 290 Mbp) and 9 scaffolds made up 50% of the assembly. The average scaffold length was 868 Kbp, and repetitive regions made up 47.8% of the genome assembly. The mitochondrial genome was 17,300 bp, and we removed this as well as scaffolds smaller than 1,000 bp prior to downstream analyses (GenBank accession: PX870159). The Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) assessment indicated that the S. somereni genome was highly complete and comparable to the other shrew RefSeq genome assemblies from NCBI—95.8% of orthologs were present in single copies based on the set of 12,234 Laurasiatheria orthologs (fig. S2). Our annotation of the S. somereni genome identified 38,011 (predicted) protein-coding genes.

Orthology and Gene Families

OrthoFinder analyzed 818,775 protein coding sequences from 31 eutherian mammal species and placed 96.1% of them into 24,474 orthogroups. There were 2,389 species-specific orthogroups comprising 10,798 genes. The median size of an orthogroup was 33 genes (mean = 32.1 genes). Fifty percent of assigned genes were in orthogroups with 37 or more genes. Additionally, 50% of assigned genes were contained in 7,126 orthogroups. Lastly, there were 10,499 orthogroups with all species present; 540 of these were single-copy orthologs. Almost all species had at least 95% of genes assigned to orthogroups. Of note, 20,397 (53.7%) genes from S. somereni were assigned to orthogroups, and S. somereni was found in 15,594 (63.7%) orthogroups. There were 1,863 (4.9%) genes from S. somereni in 591 species-specific orthogroups. In addition to orthology clustering, OrthoFinder used all ortholog gene trees and the species tree topology to count the number of well-supported gene duplications where at least 50% of the descendant species retained both copies (Fig. 3).

OrthoFinder species tree with the node labels being the number of well-supported gene duplications. Duplications are considered well-supported if both copies of the duplicated gene are present in at least 50% of descendent species. Branch lengths are in substitutions per site.

CAFE5 tested for gene family expansion and contraction in a total of 18,881 gene families (orthogroups inferred by OrthoFinder). The two λ rates of gene family size change per million years were 0.00558 for Homo sapiens and 0.00255 for the rest of the eutherian phylogeny. Of all gene families considered, 1,123 evolved more rapidly than would be expected with rate λ (P-value < 0.01). We compiled the number of significant expansions and contractions for each species (Fig. 4). The degree to which gene family size significantly changed per species varied. For instance, Condylura cristata had 13 expanding and 9 contracting gene families, whereas H. sapiens had 501 expanding and 103 contracting gene families. Scutisorex somereni had 53 expanding and 132 contracting gene families. By extracting the H. sapiens protein IDs from each significantly expanding or contracting gene family in S. somereni, we tested for enrichment of biological process gene ontology (GO) terms. We found significant enrichment of 42 GO terms for expanding gene families and 26 for contracting gene families (FDR P-value < 0.01). The top 20 GO terms for expanding gene families were related to sensory, immune, and metabolic pathways (Fig. 5a). Similarly, the top 20 GO terms for contracting gene families were related to sensory and immune pathways (Fig. 5b).

Selection

We next tested for positive selection and change in selective constraint across 540 single copy orthologs produced by the OrthoFinder analysis. Sequences from some amino acid alignments could not be matched with their corresponding nucleotide coding sequence in at least one species for 79 single copy orthologs, and we proceeded to selection analyses with the remaining 461 single copy ortholog alignments. aBSREL tested for positive selection in S. somereni compared to the rest of the eutherian mammal phylogeny in a total of 453 single copy ortholog alignments (after HyPhy-specific filtering). Of these, we found evidence of positive selection in 115 genes (FDR P-value < 0.05). RELAX tested for changes in selective constraint in the same 453 single copy orthologs. We found support for a change in selective constraint in 56 genes (FDR P-value < 0.05). Selection strength was intensified for 17 genes and relaxed in the others. We examined GO terms for biological process related to bone growth or skeletal development for all significant genes identified in the HyPhy analyses. We found 14 such genes with evidence of episodic positive selection in S. somereni (Table 1). We found six such genes with evidence of relaxation in selective constraint in S. somereni. Three of these genes were identified by both the aBSREL and RELAX analyses. Of the genes under positive selection in S. somereni, we found that MATN3, MSX-1, HOXA10, HOXA11, CRKL, SFRP5, RDH10, OVOL2, and ALX4 had GO terms associated with skeletal system and cranial development, as well as body pattern formation and anatomical structure morphogenesis. Other genes under positive selection, like TNF11, MMP13, SPD2A, BNC2, and TTC9A had GO terms associated with ossification, osteoclast development, bone growth, and in utero embryonic development. Genes CO3A1, MPRI, and GAS1 showed evidence of change in selective constraint and had GO terms associated with bone morphogenesis and post embryonic growth and development. Of the genes considered, TNF11, SFRP5, and RDH10 were identified by both aSBREL and RELAX analyses.

Discussion

A striking diversity of mammalian phenotypes has accumulated over 100 million years of evolutionary history, and the skeletal anatomy of S. somereni represents perhaps the greatest, fastest evolving, and most mysterious of anatomical deviations. Our goal was to investigate the genomic features and evolutionary processes that might have contributed to the enigmatic axial skeleton of S. somereni. Our approach was integrative—by combining genome assembly and annotation with orthology inference, analyses of gene family evolution, and selection analyses, we identified candidate genes and orthogroups potentially underlying this trait. Here, we highlight the evolutionary processes and candidate genes that could provide clues as to how extreme phenotypic change can arise over short timeframes.

Genome Assembly and Annotation

The annotated hero shrew draft genome is similar in size to published shrew (Soricidae) reference genomes. While N50 for the genome was marginally less than that of Suncus etruscus and higher than that of Sorex fumeus, there were many more small scaffolds. This, combined with the absence of RNA-seq data, likely lead to an inflated number of predicted genes and is a known problem of draft genome annotation (Denton et al. 2014). We performed a BUSCO analysis and found that this assembly retained 95% of expected single copy orthologs from the set of 12,234 Laurasiatheria reference genes, meaning the S. somereni draft genome had comparable completeness to other shrew reference genomes.

Orthology Inference and Gene Families

To better understand gene evolution in Eutheria, and specifically S. somereni, we used OrthoFinder to cluster related genes and CAFE5 to better understand patterns of gene family size change over time. Over 95% of genes considered in the OrthoFinder analysis were assigned to 24,474 orthogroups, and over 10,000 of these orthogroups had representative sequences from all species. While the study-wide percentage of genes in species-specific orthogroups was low (1.3%), it was slightly higher for S. somereni (4.9%) when compared to other species in this study (ranging from 0.1% to 4.2%). Similarly, other species had nearly 95% of their genes assigned to orthogroups, and S. somereni had only around 50% of genes assigned. This can be attributed to the quality of genome annotation in S. somereni, which had more protein coding genes than other species, probably due to over-prediction. However, sequences from S. somereni could still be found in 15,594 orthogroups. OrthoFinder was able to infer gene trees for orthogroups, allowing us to count species-specific gene duplications across the eutherian phylogeny. Gene duplication rate varied widely, with H. sapiens and murine rodents having over 10,000 duplications and S. somereni having 3,221, a total similar to what we inferred in other shrews and matching results from a recent study of orthology in Holarctic shrews (Cossette et al. 2024). Gene duplications have been considered an important evolutionary force for almost 100 years (Bridges 1936; Ohno 1970). While many gene duplicates become pseudogenes, others can provide raw material for natural selection through dosage effects or by relaxation on selective constraint and accumulating mutations (Lynch and Conery 2000; Zhang 2003; Innan and Kondrashov 2010). Unfortunately, a paucity of functional gene annotation data makes it challenging to determine which specific genes are duplicated in S. somereni. Nonetheless, gene duplication could be an important evolutionary process underlying axial skeleton evolution, and improved genome annotation will be useful moving forward.

We next used CAFE5 to examine gene family size change using the output from OrthoFinder. We found that the rate of gene gain and loss per million years was 0.00558 for H. sapiens and 0.00255 for the rest of the eutherian phylogeny. These estimates agree with previous estimates in mammals (Demuth et al. 2006; Mendes et al. 2021). Of the 18,881 gene families (orthogroups) tested, 1,123 showed evidence of significant size change. Homo sapiens showed the greatest degree of gene family size change with 501 expanding and 103 contracting gene families. In contrast, there were 53 expanding and 132 contracting gene families in S. somereni. Gene families comprise homologous genes, many of which retain similar functions (Demuth et al. 2006). Of the gene families evolving rapidly in S. somereni, there was significant enrichment of GO pathways related to sensory, metabolic, and immune processes. Enrichment of GO terms related to sensory perception in S. somereni suggests an important role of enhanced acoustic, tactile, and olfactory senses in allowing individuals to interact with environmental stimuli, given their reduced eyes and poor vision (Churchfield 1990). Similar enrichment of sensory processes was found in Holarctic shrews (Cossette et al. 2024). We found metabolic processes were also enriched in S. somereni, which could be due in part to the high metabolic rate of shrews (Taylor 1998; Ochocińska and Taylor 2005). Enrichment of immune pathways in S. somereni gene family evolution is not unusual, but rather a shared pattern of gene family evolution seen in many studies, as pathogens exert a strong selective pressure on vertebrates (Enard et al. 2016; Shultz and Sackton 2019; Nandakumar et al. 2025). Enrichment for metabolic and immune processes has been found in gene family analyses of mammals broadly (Demuth et al. 2006), rendering it difficult to determine whether this pattern reflects S. somereni-specific trends in gene family evolution or a more general pattern among shrews, among mammals, or among vertebrates.

Positive Selection and Changes to Selective Constraint

Of the 115 total genes under positive selection in S. somereni, there were 14 genes related to skeletal system development, anatomical structure morphogenesis, embryonic development, bone development, and bone morphogenesis. Of the 53 total genes with changes to selective constraint in S. somereni, there were 6 with relaxed selection that were related to bone growth and development, and post-embryonic growth and development. Three of these genes were also identified as being under positive selection. Importantly, we found four homeobox genes, specifically from the HOX gene family, that were under positive selection in S. somereni. These genes encode transcription factors that regulate embryonic development along the major bilaterian body axis and play a critical role in vertebral structure and development (McGinnis and Krumlauf 1992; Krumlauf 1994; Burke et al. 1995; Carroll 1995; Wellik 2007; Iimura et al. 2009; Holland 2013; Mallo 2018). Both HOXA10 and HOXA11 were under positive selection in S. somereni. In model organisms, these genes play a role in sacral and caudal vertebral development, and loss-of-function mutations have drastically altered the axial skeleton formula in Mus musculus (Wellik and Capecchi 2003).

Additionally, we found genes from two other homeobox gene families known to affect craniofacial development, MSX1 and ALX4 (Blin-Wakkach et al. 2001; Alappat et al. 2003; Lan et al. 2024), under positive selection in S. somereni. Results from our selection analyses suggest that the evolution of homeobox genes could play a role in the development of the S. somereni axial skeleton. However, this is a complex trait likely controlled by multiple gene families and differential patterns of gene regulation. We were able to examine only 453 single copy orthologs for evidence of selection in S. somereni. While this allowed direct comparison to 30 other eutherian mammal species, there are likely many more genes associated with the evolution of this phenotype.

Caveats and Future Directions

This study is an essential first step in understanding the evolution of the S. somereni axial skeleton. While our results provide valuable insight, we considered only a fraction of protein coding genes that are likely associated with this complex trait. The single-copy orthologs we analyzed here span over 100 million years of mammalian evolutionary history, and the 540 that we recovered is a downstream effect of the broad evolutionary timescales we considered, rather than an inherent limitation of the data. Additionally, our study design did not account for the roles that gene regulation, development, or selection in non-coding regions might play in generating this phenotype. Further work examining RNA-seq data would improve the genome annotation and enable investigation of the role of gene regulation. The absence of S. thori (the only other species in the genus) genomic data limits our understanding the evolutionary history of the genes identified herein, and whether they facilitated phenotypic evolution in the ancestor of hero shrews. To that end, narrowing the phylogenetic scope to consider only shrews (including all recognized hero shrews) would allow investigation of finer-scale patterns of genome evolution.

Conclusion

Several studies have investigated morphological and biomechanical properties of the S. somereni axial skeleton (Ahmed and Klima 1978; Cullinane and Aleper 1998; Cullinane et al. 1998; Cullinane and Bertram 2000; Churchfield et al. 2007; Smith and Angielczyk 2020). While there has been a niche fascination with this trait for over 100 years, the adaptive significance is still unknown. In a set of almost 500 single copy orthologs, we found evidence of genes under positive selection and relaxed selective constraint that are potentially associated with the S. somereni axial skeleton. Despite identifying a small subset of genes from S. somereni, this is an important first step in learning about the genomic processes involved in dramatic and rapid phenotypic changes.

Materials and Methods

Sampling and Sequencing

The individual S. somereni used in the study was an adult female collected from montane rainforest in the Democratic Republic of the Congo in 2015. Liver tissue was preserved in dimethyl sulfoxide, and the specimen was deposited into the mammal collection at the LSU Museum of Natural Science (LSUMZ 38908; Louisiana, USA). Genomic DNA was extracted from the preserved liver tissue sample using a SPRI bead-based protocol with Cytiva Sera-Mag Speedbeads (Marlborough, MA). Library preparation was performed with ½ volume reactions of the KAPA HyperPrep Kit (Roche; Madison, WI) with 10 to 13 PCR cycles. Genomic DNA fragments were dual-indexed with i5 and i7 primers prior to sequencing (Glenn et al. 2019; https://baddna.uga.edu/) on the NovaSeq 6000 (Novogene; Sacramento, CA) platform with 150 base pair (bp) paired-end reads. High molecular weight DNA was also extracted from the liver tissue sample using the Nanobind CBB Kit for PacBio long read sequencing (Pacific Biosciences; Menlo Park, CA). The PacBio SMRTbell library preparation kit was used to prepare the extract for PacBio continuous long read (CLR) sequencing on the Sequel II platform at Maryland Genomics (Baltimore, MD). Lastly, subsampled tissue was used to prepare a HiC library with the Proximo (Seattle, WA) Hi-C Kit prior to sequencing on the NovoSeq 6000 platform with 150 base pair (bp) paired-end reads. Our paired-end sequencing targeted 300 million read pairs per sequencing technology and we used an entire lane for PacBio sequencing.

Assembly and Annotation

We first trimmed short reads of adapters and low-quality reads with fastp v1.0.0 (Chen et al. 2018), and then trimmed long reads with Cutadapt v4.5 (Martin 2011). Trimmed long reads were assembled using flye v2.9.3 (Kolmogorov et al. 2019), which included an extra round of assembly polishing. We corrected the resulting assembly with short reads using Hapo-G v1.3.7 (Aury and Istace 2021). We removed mitochondrial contigs from the assembly by using MitoFinder v.1.4.1 (Allio et al. 2020) with the Suncus etruscus mitochondrial genome serving as a reference (NCBI GenBank accession: CM044019.1). We next removed adapter and other contaminants from the assembly with the NCBI Foreign Contamination Screen v0.5.0 (FCS; Astashyn et al. 2024). Then, purge_dups 1.2.6 (Guan et al. 2020) removed haploid and contig overlaps from the assembly based on sequence similarity and read depth. To improve the contiguity of the assembly, we used the Hi-C data with YaHS v1.2a.1 (Zhou et al. 2023) to scaffold the contigs based on chromatin contact maps. We then built a custom library of repetitive elements using RepeatModeler v2.0.5 and RepeatMasker v4.1.6 (Flynn et al. 2020; Smit et al. 2024) from the Dfam TE Tools container (Dfam Consortium 2023: https://github.com/Dfam-consortium/TETools). We soft-masked repeats using bedtools v2.31.1 (Quinlan and Hall 2010) and removed scaffolds smaller than 1000 bp using a custom shell script. We estimated genome size by building a k-mer histogram from the short-read data and a k-mer length of 21 in Meryl v1.4.1 (Rhie et al. 2020). The histogram was used as input to GenomeScope v2.0 (Ranallo-Benavidez et al. 2020) to visualize the results. Lastly, we assessed the final assembly quality and completeness with gfastats v1.3.6 (Formenti et al. 2022) and compleasm v0.2.5 (Huang et al. 2023 ).

We used the BRAKER3 v3.0.8 container (Gabriel et al. 2024) and the OrthoDB v11 Vertebrata protein dataset (Kuznetsov et al. 2023) to annotate ab initio the S. somereni genome assembly. We converted the GTF file produced by BRAKER3 to GFF format using AGAT v1.4.0 (Dainat 2024: https://github.com/NBISweden/AGAT) and sanitized the GFF with gfftk v24.2.4 (Chen et al. 2019). Lastly, we added functional annotation information to the predicted genes using both interproscan v5.68 to 100.0 (Jones et al. 2014; Blum et al. 2021) and eggNOG-mapper v2.1.12 (Cantalapiedra et al. 2021). Functional information from both sources was combined with the BRAKER3 GFF file using funannotate v1.8.15 (Palmer and Stajich 2020).

Orthology Analysis

We downloaded protein coding sequences from the NCBI RefSeq genomes of 30 species (Table S1). These species were chosen because they represent broad taxonomic sampling of eutherian mammals. First, we cleaned the input protein sequences of all but the longest isoform using primary_transcript.py (https://github.com/davidemms/OrthoFinder). We next used OrthoFinder v3.1.0 (Emms and Kelly 2015; Emms and Kelly 2019; Emms et al. 2025) to assemble orthogroups of protein coding genes. Orthogroups comprise genes descended from a single gene in the common ancestor to the species of interest, analogous to gene families (Altenhoff et al. 2011), and species-specific orthogroups are made up of genes from a single species only. OrthoFinder inferred orthogroups by clustering together protein sequences based on sequence similarity. Then, gene trees were inferred from orthogroups and subsequently summarized to build a rooted species tree. OrthoFinder lastly characterized gene duplication events using gene trees together with the species tree. OrthoFinder provided a list of single copy orthologs, or genes with a one-to-one relationship in all species considered. We tested for selection using these genes (described below).

OrthoFinder outputs counts of genes per orthogroup per species. We used these data as input for an analysis of gene family expansion and contraction in CAFE5 v1.1 (De Bie et al. 2006; Mendes et al. 2021). Prior to gene family analysis, we time-calibrated the species tree from OrthoFinder using secondary calibrations for crown ages compiled in TimeTree v5 (Kumar et al. 2022) of Eutheria (94.7 to 101.9 mya), Laurasiatheria (83.3 to 88.0 mya), Euarchontoglires (81.3 to 91.0 mya), Xenarthra (63.0 to 72.5 mya), and Eulipotyphla (46.5 to 76 mya) in treePL (Smith et al. 2012). CAFE5 uses a random birth-death process to model changes in gene family size across a phylogeny over time. CAFE5 estimated λ, which is the probability of both gene gain and loss per gene per unit of time in the phylogeny (Hahn et al. 2005; De Bie et al. 2006). Using λ, the time-calibrated phylogeny, and species’ gene counts per gene family, CAFE5 calculated the expected distribution of gene family sizes at ancestral nodes under the birth-death model. Gene family size changes from parent to daughter node that were greater than would be expected with rate λ were considered as significant expansions or contractions (P-value < 0.01). We first ran CAFE5 with default parameters and three evolutionary rate categories for the gene families: slow, medium, and fast. This initial run indicated Homo sapiens experienced a rapid rate of gene family size change, so we ran CAFE5 a final time with three rate categories and two different values of λ, one for H. sapiens and one for all other species.

Selection Testing

OrthoFinder generated single copy ortholog amino acid alignments. To test for selection i.e. calculate dN/dS ratios, we converted the amino acid alignments to nucleotide alignments with PAL2NAL v14.1 (Suyama et al. 2006). PAL2NAL takes an amino acid alignment and the corresponding nucleotide coding sequences (which we took from the NCBI RefSeq genomes) and converts the input amino acid alignment to a codon-aware nucleotide alignment. We next used MACSE v2.07 (Ranwez et al. 2021) to account for potential frameshifts in these alignments. In some cases, MACSE introduced large blocks of gaps into the alignments. To trim these alignments of excessive gaps, we used Gblocks v0.91b (Castresana 2000).

We used HyPhy v2.5.75 to test for episodic positive selection across single copy ortholog alignments (Pond et al. 2005; Pond et al. 2020). Specifically, we used aBSREL (Smith et al. 2015), which models branch and site heterogeneity in dN/dS ratios to assess whether positive selection occurs at any point on specified branches. aBSREL allowed branches to have different dN/dS rate classes and inferred the optimal number of rate classes per branch. For branches of interest, aBSREL performed a likelihood-ratio test comparing the fit of a model with dN/dS ≥ 1 to one where dN/dS ≤ 1. We ran aBSREL by specifying an a priori hypothesis that S. somereni experienced positive selection. This test compared dN/dS rates along the S. somereni branch (foreground) to the rest of the eutherian phylogeny (background). The result of the aBSREL test was a list of single copy orthologs inferred to have experienced positive selection in S. somereni.

We next tested whether selective constraint had been relaxed or intensified in single copy orthologs of S. somereni relative to the eutherian phylogeny. We did this using RELAX in HyPhy (Wertheim et al. 2015). RELAX does not explicitly model positive selection, but rather it uncovers trends in selective constraint on genes. We ran RELAX by again specifying S. somereni as the foreground and the rest of the eutherian phylogeny as background. RELAX first fit three dN/dS rate categories (purifying, neutral, and diversifying) to the whole phylogeny. Then a selection intensity parameter, k, was introduced that scaled the values of the three dN/dS rate categories to the power of k for the S. somereni branch. If k < 1, all dN/dS categories shift closer to 1, representing relaxed selection. If k > 1, all dN/dS categories shift away from 1, representing intensified selection. The null hypothesis of RELAX was that k = 1. A likelihood-ratio test determined whether k ≠ 1 on the S. somereni branch. The result of the RELAX test was a list of single copy orthologs that experienced a significant change in selective constraint in S. somereni.

Following both aBSREL and RELAX analyses, we compiled lists of genes (relative to the Homo sapiens RefSeq genome annotation) under positive selection or that experienced changes in selection intensity in foreground branches. We accounted for false discovery rate (FDR) by applying a Benjamini-Hochburg FDR correction to each test (Benjamini and Hochbery 1995). We used the Gene Ontology Knowledgebase with PAN-GO (Aleksander et al. 2023; Feuermann et al. 2025) to test for significant enrichment of GO terms using the CAFE5 results. To profile the biological process, GO terms from the aBSREL and RELAX analyses, we used UniProt (UniProt Consortium 2023).
