Evaluating voluntary care seeking effects on COVID-19 outcomes and health system costs
Authors: Sileshi Sintayehu Sharbayta, Jaehun Jung, Youngji Jo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In an endemic COVID-19 setting with reduced enforcement measures, intervention success increasingly depends on voluntary compliance behaviors shaped by risk perceptions and population immunity. We used a previously developed behavior adaptation model, calibrated to Omicron wave data (February 1–May 31, 2022) in South Korea, to compare health outcomes (cumulative incidence, treated cases) and costs (vaccination, testing, treatment) under two scenarios: only mandatory compliance versus a combination of mandatory and voluntary compliance. The model included constant (mandatory) rates, independent of risk perceptions, and dynamic (voluntary) rates driven by changing perceptions of prevalence and severity. Sensitivity analyses identified key drivers of cumulative incidence and total costs. Introducing voluntary compliance reduced incidence by 14% while increasing total costs by 35% compared with a mandatory-only approach (37 million COVID-19 infected cases and $1.02 billion total cost over the four months in a 52 million Korean population). Information dissemination was one of the key drivers: under high coverage, emphasizing responsiveness to prevalence reduced incidence by 46% (vs. 11% under low coverage) but increased costs by 57% (vs. 29% under low coverage) due to greater care-seeking. Under high partial immunity, enhanced testing responsiveness reduced cases by 18% yet raised costs up to 40%, whereas improved vaccination responsiveness lowered incidence by 5% with only up to 10% cost increase. From the cost-effectiveness perspective, however, enhanced vaccination responsiveness outperformed testing alone (ICER $51, 95% Confidence Interval, CI 35–81, vs. $64, 95% CI 42–96, per case averted). Maximizing both testing and vaccination responsiveness provided the largest reduction in cases (−23%) at a 46% increase in total costs, leading to an ICER of $57, 95% CI 42–80, per case averted compared to the mandatory-only scenario. Voluntary testing plays a larger role in reducing disease burden than voluntary vaccination but incurs significantly higher costs. Strategies to improve cost-effectiveness could include lowering testing costs while encouraging voluntary vaccination. Given uncertainties around risk perceptions and voluntary behaviors, more detailed behavioral data and adaptive modeling can guide sustainable and cost-effective public health strategies.

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has disrupted billions of lives worldwide with its unprecedented scale and duration. Policymakers implemented measures to curb the spread of SARS-CoV-2, supported by models comparing interventions such as non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs), testing, and vaccination1–3. These models, which rely on uncertain assumptions, forecast cases, deaths, and medical needs; predict case peaks; and estimate potential surges. However, many models lack direct integration of adaptive behavioral components, and fail to capture how shifting risk perceptions, protective behaviors, and compliance influence transmission dynamics and related costs. COVID-19 transmission patterns both shape and are shaped by behavioral responses4,5. Changes in risk perception throughout the pandemic affected adherence to NPIs and vaccination willingness, with evolving views on vaccine risks and benefits fueling hesitancy and complicating efforts to achieve herd immunity6. Decision-makers must interpret predictions while accounting for these behavioral dynamics, which are critical to refining forecasts and developing effective policies.

Incorporating cost-effectiveness analysis (CEA) into behavioral adaptation models for COVID-19 ensures that public health interventions are both effective and resource efficient. The dynamic interaction between human behavior and disease transmission requires policies to consider adaptive responses to obtain accurate long-term impact predictions. Evidence from previous outbreaks, such as H1N1, demonstrate that changing risk perceptions drive vaccine demand fluctuations, complicating coverage goals7. Without quantifying the economic implications of these behavioral patterns, policymakers risk misjudging both the costs and benefits of interventions. A behavioral adaptation model integrated with CEA provides crucial information on complex trade-offs between costs, behavioral influences, and health outcomes, enabling more nuanced and practically applicable decisions in real-world settings.

In our previous model8, we found that voluntary care-seeking for vaccination and testing−functioning as a response to individual immunity status, disease prevalence, risk perception, information coverage, and reactivity to information—results in substantial differences in peak and total incidence compared with scenarios with constant rates (representing only mandated care-seeking). Feedback loop mechanisms, where high disease burden triggers increased vaccine uptake and testing, which subsequently reduce disease burden over time, create a fundamental trade-off between disease burden and care-seeking responses. This dynamic generates uncertainty about how much health improvement can be achieved relative to the increase in costs in the system. Therefore, based on our behavioral adaptation model, we evaluated the impact of mandatory and voluntary compliance with vaccination and testing policies on health outcomes (measured by cumulative incidence, tested and treated cases) and health system costs (including vaccine administration, testing, and treatment expenses). Our analysis is particularly relevant for the endemic era of COVID-19, where the effectiveness of the intervention increasingly depends on voluntary behaviors driven by individual risk perceptions and levels of population immunity, especially when perfect enforcement of mandates is impractical or unsustainable over long periods.

Methods

We employed a behavioral COVID-19 transmission model calibrated to Omicron data in South Korea, covering the period from February 1 to May 31, 2022, as reported in a recent study8. The model examines how behavior changes among partially immune and nonimmune individuals influence disease spread, specifically by affecting care-seeking behaviors such as vaccination and testing rates. Below, we provide a brief description of the model and calibration method.

The transmission model

A compartmental model was used to represent the transmission dynamics of the disease. In the model, the total population is divided into two major groups depending on the immune status of the individuals: partially immune—individuals who are vaccinated or have recovered from infection and are assumed to be partially immune against infection and nonimmune, who are never vaccinated or exposed to the disease. People in each group are further grouped into compartments based on the natural history of diseases—susceptible (S1, S2), exposed (E1, E2), asymptomatic infection (A1, A2), symptomatic infection-undetected (I1, I2), symptomatic infection—detected (It1, It2), hospitalized (H), and recovered (R)-and vaccination status-vaccinated with a primary series vaccination (V1) and booster vaccination (V2). The model is based on several key assumptions: First, the initial distribution of the susceptible population (S1: naive 15%, S2: immune 85%) reflects observed data, where 85% of Koreans had received at least a primary COVID-19 vaccine dose by the starting time for model simulation (February 01, 2022)9. The model accounts for waning immunity by considering factors such as time since vaccination, vaccine type (primary vs. booster), and prior infection. Second, individuals will seek testing only if they exhibit symptoms or if they are asymptomatic but believe they have been in contact with an infected person. Third, 80% of individuals who test positive and all hospital admissions comply with self-quarantine, reducing transmission. Importantly, the vaccination and testing rates are assumed to depend on two rates: the mandatory rate and the voluntary rate. The mandatory rate reflects baseline uptake among individuals or groups—such as older adults, teachers, and healthcare workers—for whom vaccination or testing is required or strongly recommended, thereby capturing inherent acceptance and compliance. This rate is assumed to remain unaffected by changes in the disease level within the population and is therefore represented as a constant. The voluntary rate is a rate for a portion of the population that voluntarily chooses to be vaccinated or tested depending on the information about the level of disease prevalence and severity. The voluntary rates are influenced by several factors, including information coverage, individuals’ responsiveness to information, the delay in information reaching the population, and information prioritization (among prevalence and severity) that differs between nonimmune and partially immune populations. The rate of change in the number of individuals within each compartment was determined by solving a system of ordinary differential equations describing the transitions of the state variables over time. Data

Parameter values in the model are determined through two approaches: (1) utilizing demographic and epidemiological data from Our World in Data9 and prior studies and (2) fitting the model to Korean COVID-19 data on vaccination, incidence, and mortality during the Omicron wave, covering the period from February 1, 2022, to May 31, 2022 (see Supplementary Fig. S2 online). A detailed description of the parameters, their values, and references is provided in Supplementary Table S1 online.

Cost

We accounted for direct costs associated with vaccination, testing and treatment. The vaccination (testing) cost is obtained by multiplying the average per person cost of vaccination (test) and the total number of vaccinated (tested) individuals. The vaccination and testing costs encompass the average per-person cost of vaccines (or test kits), procedural expenses, and logistics. South Korea’s COVID-19 control strategy combined rapid antigen tests (RATs) and RT-PCR assays, with RATs widely used for home based screening due to their low cost and fast turnaround, and RT-PCR for confirmatory testing12–14. However, it was difficult to ascertain the exact distribution between PCR and RAT use, and given the possibility of duplicate testing across modalities, we assumed RAT to be the primary diagnostic practice during the peak of the Omicron wave. Treatment costs cover both inpatient and outpatient care. We assume that 20% of detected cases are managed as outpatients. The inpatient costs are calculated as the product of the average daily hospital cost per patient, the mean length of stay ( 10 days), and the cumulative number of hospitalised cases. The outpatient costs are the average cost per visit multiplied by the total number of outpatient visits. Testing costs in this study were estimated using South Korean currency and converted to U.S. dollars at the 2024 exchange rate, while other costs were drawn from published literature based on South Korean data. All costs are therefore reported in U.S. dollars. The costs for vaccination, testing, and treatment are detailed in Table 1. Additionally, the table includes descriptions and values for certain epidemiological and behavior-related parameters, which are pertinent to the sensitivity analysis.

Scenarios

To evaluate the impact of voluntary compliance, we analyzed two scenarios: (1) care-seeking driven solely by mandatory policies, where individuals do not perceive COVID-19 as a threat and therefore vaccinate or test only if required (i.e., individuals’ behavior no longer responds to disease burden); and (2) care-seeking influenced by both mandatory policies and voluntary decisions, where individuals adjust their vaccination or testing behavior in response to population-level prevalence. Comparing these scenarios highlights the incremental effect of voluntary care seeking compliance on incidence, healthcare demand, and health system costs, compared to the mandatory components alone. The economic evaluation adopts a healthcare system perspective, including direct medical costs of testing, vaccination, and treatment. Outcomes include averted cumulative incidence, averted total cost, and ICER to measure the effect of voluntary care-seeking strategies relative to a mandatory-only strategy. We assessed the distribution of the population in the care cascade between the two scenarios–mandatory policies and voluntary decisions—and its consequences to costs related to vaccination, testing, and treatment (Fig. 1).

Sensitivity analysis

We conducted several sensitivity analyses related to voluntary care-seeking behavior. First, we examined how changes in voluntary compliance—through varying information coverage, reactivity, and prioritization of prevalence versus severity—affected cumulative incidence and total costs. In this framework, information coverage reflects government-driven efforts, while reactivity and prioritization represent patient-driven responses (Fig. 2). Second, we explored the possibility that immune individuals may respond differently to vaccination and testing. Our hypothesis was that immunized individuals might be less inclined to seek additional vaccination but more likely to seek testing if they suspect infection. We considered three scenarios for clarity: (i) reactivity only to voluntary testing (vaccination = 0, testing = 10); (ii) reactivity only to voluntary vaccination (vaccination = 10, testing = 0); and (iii) reactivity to both voluntary testing and vaccination (vaccination = 10, testing = 10) (Fig. 3 and Table 2).

Finally, we also performed one-way sensitivity analyses with a comprehensive list of parameters and a probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA) to capture the combined impact of parameter uncertainty on model outcomes. Results

Impact of incorporating voluntary vaccination and testing into the model on health outcomes and costs

Simulation results comparing COVID-19 response strategies in South Korea reveals significant differences between mandatory-only and combined (mandatory and voluntary) care-seeking approaches. Figure 1 contrasts two care-seeking policies applied to an initial population that is 85% partially immune and 15% naive (52 million total). Under the mandatory-only care-seeking scenario (Fig. 1, panel a), 72% of the total population (37 million) becomes infected and 14% (7 million) is vaccinated over four months; only 38% of infections are detected and 8% treated, leaving the majority (62%) of cases undetected. With the same susceptible population distribution, the combined mandatory and voluntary care-seeking scenario (Fig. 1, panel b) results in a 62% (32 million ) infection and a 19% (10 million) vaccination uptake—a 14% reduction in infections and a 43% increase in vaccinations compared to the mandatory-only scenario. The combined approach increased case detection and treatment to 53% and 12.5%, respectively—improving detection by 21% and treatment by 33% over the mandatory-only strategy—and reduced deaths by 20% (data not shown here). Under the combined scenario, increased utilization of vaccination, testing, and treatment increases total programm costs by 35% compared with the mandatory-only approach, reaching $1.05 billion (or $2.1 million per 100,000 people), comprising 250 million for vaccination, 343 million for testing and 456 million for treatment (Fig. 1, panel c). Probabilistic sensitivity analysis

The result of the sensitivity analysis—distribution of the incremental costs and cases averted- is shown in Fig. 4 panel a. Under responsiveness to voluntary testing only, the model estimated a mean ICER of $64 per case averted (95% CI $42–96). Voluntary vaccination alone yielded a lower mean ICER of $51 (95% CI $35–81), while the combined testing–vaccination scenario produced a mean ICER of $57 (95% CI $42–80). Cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs, Fig. 4, panel b) further illustrate these findings, suggesting investing in health promotion to encourage voluntary care seeking for vaccination and testing can be highly cost-effective, with all strategies falling below the $100 willingness-to-pay threshold per case averted. Furthermore, our probability sensitivity analysis showed that responsiveness to voluntary testing is associated with greater uncertainty in cost-effectiveness outcomes. Discussion

Our study examined the incremental value of voluntary care-seeking compared with mandatory-only policies in epidemic control, suggesting that voluntary compliance significantly impacts both health outcomes and economic costs. Incorporating voluntary behaviors for vaccination and testing lowered the cumulative number of cases by 14%—averting 5 million infections—while raising healthcare costs by 35% ($374 million), compared with mandatory measures alone, which resulted in 37 million (71,153 per 100,000 people) infections among a 52 million population and a US $1.02 billion (US$2.1 million per 100,000 people) cost over the four-month period. Information dissemination was critical as it encouraged both voluntary vaccination and testing. Under low information coverage (10%), shifting risk perception from prevalence- to severity-focused (or vice versa) and from high- to low-risk responses (or vice versa) varied incidence by 11% and costs by 29%. By contrast, under higher coverage (90%), similar changes produced incidence variations of 46% and corresponding cost shifts of 57%. The results imply that investments in public health communication to promote voluntary vaccination and testing potentially yielding substantial returns in disease reduction but may also increase the budgetary pressure. Differences in risk perception and behavioural responsiveness reveal that individuals’ risk perception and behavioral response to health information is as critical as the information coverage itself. Sensitivity analyses identified voluntary testing as the key lever: expanding testing consistently cut cases by 18 % but raised costs by 40-46 %, regardless of vaccination uptake, whereas boosting vaccination alone trimmed cases by 5 % at a 10 % cost increase. This highlights the need for targeted health communication strategies that align with how different populations (i.e., strengthening vaccination cues for susceptibles and testing cues for those at risk of infection) perceive and respond to disease risks.

From a cost-effectiveness perspective, our findings indicate that although voluntary testing has a larger impact on absolute disease outcomes and costs, maximizing voluntary vaccination responsiveness remains the most cost-effective strategy (ICER $51 per case averted, 95% CI 29–87, under 80% quarantine compliance as a baseline)—despite a high existing population immunity and its higher unit cost ($36 for vaccination vs. $25 for testing). Cost effectiveness of testing however can be further improved under high prevalence conditions if quarantine compliance rises: once compliance exceeds 90%, maximizing vaccination and testing responsiveness yields the lowest ICER (US $44), and at full compliance (100 %) testing alone becomes the most cost-effective option (ICER = US $32), see Table S2 online. Moreover, a 30% reduction in testing costs—achievable through innovative diagnostics—would cut total health-system expenditure by roughly 35 % and further improve cost-effectiveness. If our model was applied in an earlier epidemic phase before the omicron wave, both intensified testing (for rapid case identification) and accelerated vaccination (to reduce incidence and severity) would act synergistically, delivering larger epidemiological gains and even more favourable ICERs than our current estimates under the high-immunity context.

Most existing cost-effectiveness analyses assume fixed coverage levels for care-seeking, e.g., vaccination, overlooking behavioral feedback driven by disease prevalence and risk perception17,20,22. For instance, age-based booster strategies were evaluated17 and COVID-19 costs were estimated under varying vaccination and contact rates shaped by social distancing20-yet both analyses assumed constant care-seeking behavior and did not explicitly model the care-seeking (vaccination or testing) dynamics. In contrast, our model incorporates voluntary compliance influenced by prevalence, information coverage, perceived risk, and response to risk based on immune status, offering a more detailed assessment of behavior-dependent outcomes and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, by accounting for the interdependence of vaccination and testing, our model captures how individuals’ dynamic responses between these strategies affect transmission and overall healthcare costs. This emphasis on voluntary care-seeking behavior and responsiveness is especially relevant in an endemic context-such as the evolving COVID-19 immunity conditions-where fluctuating risk perceptions and changing public sentiment can shape long-term health outcomes and healthcare resource utilization. This also suggests that policymakers should consider not only the absolute coverage of interventions but also how and when individuals decide to participate in vaccination or testing based on their evolving perceptions of disease threats.

Our study has several limitations. First, behavioral parameters such as risk perception and responsiveness to public health messaging could vary across demographic and socioeconomic contexts23,24. For instance, risk perception and care seeking could be higher among younger, wealthier, and urban populations compared to older, poorer, and rural population25. Yet, our model assumed that care-seeking decisions—such as vaccination uptake and testing—are primarily driven by differential risk perception according to individuals’ immunity status for model simplicity and tractability— i.e., naive individuals were assumed to perceive greater fear, while immune individuals were assumed to perceive 50% less fear based on reported prevalence. To partially capture unobserved heterogeneity beyond immune status, we introduced a variation (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pm 10\%$$\end{document}) in our probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA), allowing the model outputs to reflect plausible ranges of behavioral responses across diverse subgroups. This approach balances computational feasibility with recognition that real-world behavioral dynamics are more heterogeneous than our baseline assumptions. Second, we reported direct health outcomes as cumulative incidence rather than QALYs or DALYs, given the lack of detailed data on symptom severity and mortality both across and within immunity groups (e.g., by prior vaccine doses or age). Since risk perception and care seeking are also likely influenced by symptom profile and age—and these subgroups may further affect progression and hospitalization beyond infection—future studies should collect empirical data on subgroup-specific risk perception and care-seeking behaviors, along with more granular clinical endpoints beyond infection. Third, the model was calibrated to a four-month Omicron wave in a highly immunized population, limiting insights into long-term dynamics such as pandemic fatigue, evolving immunity, and shifting compliance, which means the results should be interpreted as illustrative rather than predictive over extended periods. Fourth, our costing approach adopted a health system perspective, excluding broader societal benefits such as productivity gains, and costs related to health advocacy/communication for vaccination and testing due to data limitations. These assumptions likely led to an underestimation of total costs and cost-effectiveness ratios. Nonetheless, because our research question focused on the incremental value of adding a voluntary behavior component to mandatory policies and on identifying key drivers of voluntary behaviors, these limitations are unlikely to affect our main conclusions.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that incorporating voluntary care-seeking behaviors significantly influences both health outcomes and economic costs in epidemic management. Incorporating voluntary behaviors for vaccination and testing reduced cumulative cases by 14% (averting 5 million infections) while increasing healthcare costs by 35% ($374 million) relative to mandatory measures alone, highlighting a critical trade-off between improved health outcomes and economic burden. Information dissemination played a crucial role: under high coverage maximizing responsiveness and prioritizing prevalence reduced incidence by 46% (vs. 11% under low coverage) while increasing costs by 57% (vs. 29% under low coverage) due to increased care seeking. Under high partial immunity, enhanced testing responsiveness yielded an 18% case reduction with up to 40% higher costs, while enhanced vaccination and testing responsiveness together can improve cost effectiveness (-23% cases, +46 % cost; ICER = US$57) Given the uncertainties surrounding care-seeking behaviors for vaccination and testing among susceptible and infected individuals, incorporating detailed behavioral data and leveraging behavioral adaptation models can help develop more sustainable and cost-effective public health strategies.
