Simulated microgravity induces time-dependent enhancement of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilm formation
Authors: Wentian Zhang, Yuanhang Xi, Hanxiao Zhang, Xinxin Li, Wenlan Wang, Zhining Zhao, Manzhi Gao, Sumin Li, Peijun Han, Yong Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilm growth exhibits time-dependent enhancement under simulated microgravity (SMG).•Reveals the core mechanism of biofilm enhancement mediated by the quorum sensing (QS) system under SMG conditions.•Novel targets for infection prevention and control in aerospace environments are proposed.

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a ubiquitous opportunistic pathogen, causes severe respiratory and urinary tract infections in immunocompromised individuals (Jault et al., 2019). Its biofilm formation constitutes a primary mechanism for chronic infections and antibiotic resistance (Flores et al., 2022; Kim et al., 2013). Biofilms construct protective barriers via self-secreted extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), enhancing bacterial tolerance to extreme environments and accelerating spacecraft material corrosion. Critically, biofilm colonization has been repeatedly detected in the International Space Station's (ISS) Environmental Control and Life Support System (ECLSS), causing filter clogging and efficiency reduction (Flores et al., 2022). During space exploration, microgravity (SMG) further compromises astronaut immune function, substantially increasing infection risks. Thus, elucidating Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilm dynamics under SMG holds significant practical importance.

Biofilm formation in Pseudomonas aeruginosa, a critical virulence determinant, is governed by hierarchically organized quorum sensing (QS) networks that coordinate density-dependent biofilm development and virulence expression. This regulatory framework initiates with the Las system where lasI synthesizes N-3-oxo-dodecanoyl-l-homoserine lactone (3-oxo-C12-HSL) to activate lasR. This primary signal induces early virulence factors such as elastase (lasB) and exotoxins while activating downstream systems including the Rhl pathway and Pseudomonas Quinolone Signal (PQS) network (Colvin et al., 2012; Crabbé et al., 2008, 2011; Fleming et al., 2022; Grossich et al., 2023).The Rhl system employs N-butanoyl-l-homoserine lactone (C4-HSL) to drive rhamnolipid synthesis and key exopolysaccharide production (Pel, Psl), thereby facilitating biofilm maturation. Concurrently, PQS enhances extracellular DNA release and reinforces matrix structural integrity. Complementing these QS systems, the alternative sigma factor AlgU modulates extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) production through alginate biosynthesis genes such as algD, while the RNA chaperone Hfq stabilizes small RNAs to coordinately regulate stress-response pathways and QS components (Crabbé et al., 2010, 2011).Collectively, these synchronized mechanisms enable density-dependent matrix component production that creates protective niches. Within these environments, bacteria upregulate virulence factors and activate resistance mechanisms including efflux pumps and metabolic adaptations (Simanek and Paczkowski, 2022; Thakur et al., 2023; Thi et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2022). This integrated regulatory program ultimately enhances immune evasion, promotes antimicrobial tolerance, and sustains recalcitrant chronic infections.

Despite this understanding under terrestrial conditions, the pathogenic mechanisms and biofilm-mediated pathogenicity of Pseudomonas aeruginosa under SMG remain poorly characterized. This study specifically investigated SMG-induced upregulation of biofilm expression. Employing integrated transcriptomic and metabolomic analyses on 15-day (SMG15) and 30-day (SMG30) SMG-adapted strains following phenotypic characterization, we confirmed time-dependent biofilm enhancement, with significant upregulation observed at SMG30. Transcriptomics revealed upregulation of biofilm-associated genes (pel, pqs, rhl) and QS regulators at SMG30, while metabolomics identified enrichment in oxidative phosphorylation and lysine biosynthesis pathways. Integrated multi-omics analysis demonstrated that biofilm upregulation under prolonged SMG is driven by LasI-centered QS acting synergistically with metabolic reprogramming. These findings elucidate the time-dependent molecular mechanisms governing biofilm adaptation in microgravity, providing a foundation for developing targeted infection control strategies.

Results

The biofilm formation of Pseudomonas aeruginosa under SMG conditions is time-dependent

The pattern of biofilm formation in Pseudomonas aeruginosa under long-term SMG remains poorly characterized. To investigate this, we established an SMG culture system using a simulated microgravity device (Fig. 1A), with normal gravity (NG) conditions serving as the control. Pseudomonas aeruginosa strains were cultured under SMG for varying durations. Growth curve analysis revealed that at 8 h post-logarithmic phase, the SMG30 strain (exposed to SMG for 30 days) exhibited a significantly higher OD600 value compared to the NG control (P < 0.05), suggesting upregulated growth rate under prolonged SMG (Fig. 1B, C). Subsequently, crystal violet quantification demonstrated a statistically significant upregulation in biofilm formation by the SMG30 strain (OD570, P < 0.01) relative to the control, with a sustained increasing trend observed at 45 and 60 days (Fig. 1E–G). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) further corroborated these findings: the SMG15 strain (15-day exposure) displayed sparse cell distribution lacking structured biofilm, whereas SMG30 exposure induced a significant increase in cell density and the formation of dense biofilms encased in extracellular matrix (Fig. 1H). Upregulation of biofilm regulatory gene expression in Pseudomonas aeruginosa under simulated microgravity

Based on previous studies, Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 exhibited enhanced virulence factor synthesis, biofilm formation, and environmental sensing under long-term SMG. To verify SMG’s regulatory impact on biofilm formation, RT-qPCR analysis of biofilm-related genes was performed. These genes were functionally categorized into adhesion initiation (lecA, lecB), matrix remodeling (lasA, lasB, pslA), QS systems (lasI/lasR, rhlI/rhlR) along with their effectors (rhlA, rhlB for rhamnolipid synthesis), structural and metabolic regulators (wspR, phzM, pqsR), and stress adaptation (rpoS). Compared to NG15d controls, SMG15d significantly upregulated lecA (adhesion), lasB (matrix protease), and lasI (QS) (Fig. 6A). SMG30d further elevated expression of 12 genes, including adhesion (lecA, lecB), matrix proteases (lasA, lasB), and QS regulators (lasR) (Fig. 6B). Temporal analysis revealed prolonged SMG exposure enhanced expression of lasA, lasI, and rhlB (rhamnolipid synthesis) (Fig. 6C). Crucially, as lasI (mediating AHL signal synthesis for QS) was consistently upregulated, its deletion mutant was constructed (Fig. 6D). Discussion

Pseudomonas aeruginosa poses significant infection risks during extended space missions owing to its robust biofilm-forming capacity and multidrug resistance. Short-term microgravity exposure (e.g., 3-day spaceflight) enhances Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilm viability, biomass, and thickness by 3-fold, 1.3-fold, and 1.8-fold, respectively (Kim et al., 2013; McLean et al., 2001). However, prolonged low-shear modeled microgravity (LSMMG) exposure (e.g., 6-day) suppresses biofilm formation in strain PA14 (Chen et al., 2024), with converging evidence demonstrating diminished biofilm capacity after 14-day simulated microgravity (SMG) exposure (Zhang et al., 2020). Consequently, the temporal dynamics of biofilm development under extended SMG conditions remain incompletely characterized.

We systematically evaluated Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 biofilm formation across progressive SMG exposures (15, 30, 45, and 60 days). Growth curve analysis revealed that following SMG30, Pseudomonas aeruginosa exhibited significantly higher growth rates compared to NG30 controls (Fig. 1B and C). The initial SMG15 produced less biofilm biomass than the NG15 control. However, SMG30 significantly increased biofilm biomass versus NG controls (P < 0.05), with further enhancement observed at 45 and 60 days (Fig. 1D–G). Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) demonstrated progressive densification of biofilm architecture throughout extended SMG exposure (Fig. 1H). We therefore speculate that biofilm formation by Pseudomonas aeruginosa is time-dependent under SMG conditions and that 30 days is a critical turning point for structural maturation.

The mechanistic basis of Pseudomonas aeruginosa's transcriptomic adaptation to SMG remains incompletely understood. Current data demonstrate that 3-day SMG exposure upregulates virulence factors (lecA, lecB, rhlA) and denitrification genes (nirS, norB, norC, nosZ) in strain PAO1, while concurrently downregulating ribosomal genes (McLean et al., 2001). Following 6-day low-shear modeled microgravity (LSMMG), three key adaptive responses emerge comprising activation of the AlgU-regulon (algU, mucA, mucB, mucC, algR, rpoH) enhancing alginate biosynthesis and stress tolerance; Hfq-mediated induction (>50 targets) modulating quorum sensing; and metabolic reprogramming via upregulated denitrification (nirS, norB, norC, nosR, nosZ) and arginine fermentation (arcA, arcB, arcC) genes, collectively facilitating hypoxia adaptation (Crabbé et al., 2008, 2010). Notably, prolonged 14-day SMG exposure significantly downregulates flagellar assembly (flhB, fliQ, fliR) and type IV pilus biosynthesis genes (pilD, pilE, pilV, pilW) (Zhang et al., 2020).

Therefore, in our study, transcriptomic analysis comparing SMG30 and control NG30 under SMG conditions revealed changes in biofilm formation. Whole-genome transcriptome analysis of Pseudomonas aeruginosa exposed to 30-day continuous SMG revealed significant upregulation of himD arcA, aceE, aceF, and rhlR compared to NG30(Fig. 2B). HimD encodes the β-subunit of the integration host factor (IHF) complex, a global transcriptional regulator that modulates bacterial pathogenicity through virulence gene expression, biofilm formation, and motility (Eichner et al., 2014). ArcA, a key enzyme in the arginine deiminase pathway, mediates arginine catabolism under hypoxic conditions, providing energy (ATP) and nitrogen for bacterial persistence in chronic infection foci (e.g., the lungs). AceE and aceF, core components of the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, catalyze pyruvate conversion to acetyl-CoA, a central hub in carbon metabolism and energy production (Wang et al., 2024). RhlR regulates virulence factor secretion and extracellular matrix synthesis via quorum sensing, with its activation closely linked to chronic infection persistence (Ali et al., 2021). The coordinated upregulation of these genes aligns with the enhanced biofilm formation phenotype under SMG.Downregulated genes, including glmS (LPS synthesis regulation), hfq (RNA chaperone), desB (membrane fatty acid desaturase)(Crabbé et al., 2011; Ha et al., 2020; Ramos-Aires et al., 2004), and dctA (C4-dicarboxylate transporter)(Valentini and Lapouge, 2013), suggest that bacteria adapt to microgravity by inhibiting cell wall synthesis, adjusting membrane fluidity, and reprogramming carbon metabolism to reallocate resources. KEGG enrichment analysis revealed four synergistically regulated functional modules(Fig. 2D): Altered AKT1/AKT2 expression in host-pathogen interactions, indicating bacterial manipulation of host glucose metabolism for colonization advantage;Co-upregulation of exoS/exoY in the type III secretion system (T3SS) (Ullah et al., 2023), directly enhancing cytotoxicity and immune evasion;Enhanced xenobiotic metabolism (e.g., xylXYZ/BenABC) and invasiveness(Mahto and Das, 2020), reflecting optimized environmental adaptation via aromatic hydrocarbon detoxification; Enrichment of adhesion factors (e.g., pilA/fliC) in pathogenic Escherichia coli infection pathways (Gonzaga et al., 2022), suggesting shared colonization strategies among pathogens under microgravity.

Metabolomic analysis showed that SMG30d strains shared 1145 core metabolites with NG15d strains but possessed 15 unique metabolites (e.g., betaine, leukotrienes), indicating metabolic network reprogramming (Fig. 3B). Differentially expressed metabolites were significantly enriched in pathways such as lysine biosynthesis, β-alanine metabolism, and pantothenate-CoA biosynthesis. Upregulated amino acids (e.g., 2-aminoheptanedioic acid, alanine) provided precursors for biofilm matrix synthesis, while betaine and citrate accumulation likely enhanced bacterial survival under SMG via osmotic regulation (Taylor et al., 2019; Wargo, 2013). Boxplot analysis showed significantly higher concentrations of osmoprotectants (e.g., betaine) and coenzyme precursors (e.g., pantothenic acid) in SMG30d vs. NG groups (P < 0.01), synergistically supporting energy demands and structural assembly during biofilm formation (Underhill and Cabeen, 2022). KEGG enrichment further indicated that SMG-induced activation of amino acid metabolism and oxidative phosphorylation pathways provided metabolic support for bacterial adaptation to hypoxic microenvironments within biofilms(Fig. 3D).

Integrative and chord diagram analyses revealed dense interaction networks between metabolites (e.g., acetylcholine, indoline) and transcription factor PA3720 under SMG, suggesting metabolic feedback regulation of gene expression(Fig. 4A). Combined KEGG enrichment highlighted activation of benzoate degradation, β-lactam resistance, and ABC transporter pathways, reflecting bacterial strategies to enhance biofilm survival by integrating metabolic detoxification and drug efflux(Fig. 4B) (Kukor and Olsen, 1990). For example, upregulation of xylXYZ in xylene degradation pathways enhanced aromatic hydrocarbon detoxification, while increased ABC transporter gene expression promoted antibiotic efflux from biofilm matrices (Hulen et al., 2022; Mahto and Das, 2020), collectively shaping physiological adaptations under SMG.

Transcriptomic comparison between SMG15d and SMG30d revealed time-dependent regulation of biofilm formation(Fig. 5B): SMG30d strains showed significant upregulation of PA5227, hupB/himD, PelA, and rsaL vs. SMG15d. HupB/himD activated T3SS effector genes via DNA topology remodeling (Delic-Attree et al., 1995; Hasnat et al., 2025), synergizing with wbpZ to reinforce virulence factor synthesis for biofilm structural support (Wang et al., 2015). PelA (polysaccharide synthase) and rsaL (quorum sensing regulator) promoted extracellular matrix assembly and AHL signal molecule synthesis via the lasI/rhlI system, driving biofilm maturation from adhesion to maturity (Dong et al., 2019; Van Loon et al., 2025). Suppression of downregulated genes (e.g., liuR for fatty acid metabolism regulation (Díaz-Pérez et al., 2018); mifS for zinc sensing (Tatke et al., 2015) likely prioritized biofilm formation through resource reallocation, reflecting metabolic trade-offs in environmental adaptation. KEGG enrichment showed that SMG30d strains formed multi-dimensional adaptive networks by activating axon regeneration pathways (pilus/flagellum assembly), phospholipase D signaling (membrane phospholipid metabolism), and aminoglycoside biosynthesis (resistance enzyme expression)(Fig. 5D) (Yamamoto et al., 2012). Choline metabolism activation provided precursors for biofilm matrix synthesis (Zhou et al., 2024), while upregulated resistance enzymes maintained bacterial viability within biofilms by reducing antibiotic sensitivity.

As reported, lecA/lecB (Faltinek et al., 2025)mediate bacterial initial adhesion to hosts or surfaces by encoding lectins; lasA/lasB participate in extracellular protease secretion to reshape the biofilm matrix. lasI/lasR and rhlI/rhlR (Gambello and Iglewski, 1991)constitute quorum sensing systems, with the former regulating early biofilm development and the latter modulating membrane structural fluidity via rhamnolipids (synthesized by rhlA/rhlB) (Soberón-Chávez et al., 2005). PslA mediates extracellular polysaccharide PSL synthesis to construct the biofilm skeleton (Pajavand et al., 2023); wspR regulates biofilm morphogenesis through a two-component system (Chen et al., 2014); phzM/pqsR involve in phenazine metabolite synthesis to enhance membrane stability (Parsons et al., 2007); and rpoS, as a stress response factor, mediates environmental adaptation and drug resistance integration during biofilm maturation (Robinson et al., 2020). These genes synergistically drive the dynamic formation and functional maintenance of biofilms through adhesion initiation, matrix construction, quorum sensing signaling networks, and stress regulation.

RT-qPCR validation confirmed time-dependent upregulation of biofilm-related genes under SMG(Fig. 6A–C). LasI, by synthesizing AHL signaling molecules, integrates biofilm formation, virulence secretion, and metabolic regulation as a core mediator of bacterial adaptation to host environments and chronic infection establishment (Dong et al., 2019; Kanak et al., 2023; Van Loon et al., 2025). Construction of a lasI deletion mutant showed significantly reduced biofilm mass under SMG compared to wild-type strains (Fig. 6E and F)(P < 0.001), confirming SMG regulation of biofilm formation via the lasI-mediated AHL signaling pathway. This finding aligns with temporal expression of quorum sensing genes (rsaL, rhlR) in transcriptomic data, forming a core regulatory axis: "SMG stimulation-lasI expression-AHL signaling-biofilm assembly". Notably, synergistic upregulation of lasI and rsaL in SMG30d strains may amplify biofilm formation signals through enhanced cascade reactions of the las/rhl dual quorum sensing system (Deshamukhya et al., 2023; He et al., 2024; Mirpour and Zahmatkesh, 2024).

Our findings suggest that SMG triggers upregulation of Pseudomonas aeruginosa biofilm formation within 30 days by coordinating quorum sensing, virulence expression, and metabolic remodeling. This regulatory paradigm not only directly activates biofilm structural genes, but also provides multifaceted support for biofilm stability and pathogenicity through metabolic reprogramming, environmental perception, and resistance mechanisms, which may contribute to the pathogenesis of biofilm diseases, it provides new insights into the adaptive evolution of microorganisms under microgravity. Future studies combining space flight experiments with investigations of combined effects such as microgravity, radiation, nutrient limitation will drive the development of biofilm-based defense strategies against space microbiology.

Method details

Bacterial strains

The Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 strain (substrain 10,905) was obtained from the National Center for Medical Culture Collections (CMCC; Beijing, China; https://www.cmccb.org.cn). Prior to experimentation, bacterial stocks were cryopreserved at −80 °C in lysogeny broth (LB; Difco, Sparks, MD) supplemented with 40 % (v/v) glycerol. Complementary strain information is available through the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection (https://china-cicc.org).

SMG and NG conditions were established using the Gravite™ system (Space Bio-Laboratories, Hiroshima, Japan) and a horizontal vibrating incubator (Model YJWY-96C). The Gravite™ apparatus - a multidirectional g-force simulator employing dual-axis rotation at constant angular velocity to nullify cumulative gravity vectors - was sterilized and assembled under laminar flow. Culture vessels were filled bubble-free with zero headspace and maintained in continuous suspension. NG controls were generated by vertical axis rotation. All incubations occurred at 37 °C.

Bacterial growth monitoring

Overnight cultures were diluted 1:100 in LB medium (pH 7.0) and subsequently incubated under SMG and NG conditions as described previously. Both SMG and NG cultures were maintained at 37 °C for 30 days with continuous rotation at 2 rpm. The culture medium was refreshed every 24 h. Gas exchange was facilitated by a gas-permeable membrane incorporated into the flask cap. Bacterial growth was monitored by measuring the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) using a spectrophotometer (BioRad, Hercules, CA, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

RNA extraction and RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted from cells cultured in LB medium for 30 days under SMG or NG conditions, using TRIzol® Reagent (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. RNA integrity and purity were evaluated using an Agilent 5300 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) and quantified using a NanoDrop ND-2000 spectrophotometer (NanoDrop Technologies, USA). High-quality RNA samples meeting the following criteria were used for library construction: OD260/280 = 1.8–2.2, OD260/230 ≥ 2.0, RQN ≥ 6.5, 28S:18S ratio ≥ 1.0, and total RNA >1 μg (standard library) or RIN > 6.5 and total RNA >10 ng (low-input library).

RNA-seq data analysis

RNA-seq libraries were prepared using either standard (1 μg total RNA) or low-input (10 ng total RNA) protocols. For standard libraries, polyA-selected mRNA was fragmented, reverse-transcribed into cDNA, and processed for Illumina or DNBSEQ-T7 sequencing. Low-input libraries were prepared using the SMART-Seq V4 Ultra Low Input RNA Kit, with cDNA amplification and Tn5 transposase-mediated fragmentation. Sequencing was performed on the NovaSeq X Plus or DNBSEQ-T7 platforms (PE150). Raw reads were quality-filtered and trimmed using fastp, then aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2. Transcript assembly and quantification were performed with StringTie and RSEM, respectively. Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 or DEGseq, with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05 (DESeq2) or FDR < 0.001 (DEGseq) as significance thresholds. Functional enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed using GO and KEGG databases (Bonferroni-corrected P < 0.05). Alternative splicing events were identified using rMATS, focusing on exon inclusion, exclusion, and intron retention. Statistical analyses and visualization were conducted using Goatools and Python scipy(Li and Dewey, 2011; Love et al., 2014; Pertea et al., 2015; Shen et al., 2014; Wang et al., 2010).

Metabolite extraction and transcriptomics data analysis

50 mg sample was added to a 2 mL centrifuge tube and a 6 mm diameter grinding bead was added. 400 μL of extraction solution (methanol: water = 4:1 (v:v)) containing 0.02 mg/mL of internal standard (L-2-chlorophenylalanine) was used for metabolite extraction. Samples were ground by the Wonbio-96c (Shanghai wanbo biotechnology co., LTD) frozen tissue grinder for 6 min (−10 °C, 50 Hz), followed by low-temperature ultrasonic extraction for 30 min (5 °C, 40 kHz). The samples were left at −20 °C for 30 min, centrifuged for 15 min (4 °C, 13,000 g), and the supernatant was transferred to the injection vial for LC-MS/MS analysis.

Raw UHPLC-MS data were processed using Progenesis QI (Waters) for baseline correction, peak alignment, and feature extraction. Metabolites were annotated against HMDB, Metlin, and MJDB databases. Data preprocessing retained features present in ≥80 % of samples, with missing values imputed and sum normalization applied. Quality control (QC) samples with RSD >30 % were excluded before log10 transformation.Multivariate analysis (PCA and OPLS-DA) was performed using the ropls R package (v1.6.2), with significant metabolites (VIP >1, p < 0.05) identified via t-tests. Pathway enrichment analysis (KEGG) and functional classification were conducted using scipy.stats in Python.

Growth curve analysis

Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 was revived from glycerol stock on PIA plates (37 °C, 12 h static). A colony was inoculated into Pseudomonas broth and activated overnight (37 °C, 200 rpm). Overnight cultures (NG/SMG) were subcultured (10 % v/v) into filled flasks (n = 12/group) to minimize air-liquid interfaces. Cultures were rotated at 2 rpm (37 °C) for 24 h. Bacterial growth was monitored via OD600 measurements every 4 h.

Biofilm formation ability assessment

Activated Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 cultures were adjusted to an optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) of 1.0 in fresh medium. Biofilms were cultured in 96-well plates equipped with peg lids (Dojindo, Kunshan, China). Bacterial suspensions (200 μL/well) were incubated statically at 37 °C for 24 h. The peg lids were then gently rinsed thrice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to remove planktonic cells. Adherent biofilms were fixed with 0.1 % (w/v) crystal violet for 15 min at room temperature, followed by three PBS washes. Bound dye was solubilized in 200 μL of 95 % ethanol for 10 min, and biofilm biomass was quantified by measuring OD₅₇₀ using a microplate reader (Model: SpectraMax iD5, Molecular Devices).

Real-time qPCR

qPCR analysis evaluated gene expression in Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 cultured under NG/SMG for 15/30 days. Bacteria grown in LB broth (37 °C, 220 rpm) were harvested at logarithmic (OD₆₀₀ ≥ 0.4) or early stationary phase (OD₆₀₀ ≥ 1.0). Cell pellets from 1.5 mL aliquots were collected by centrifugation (10,000 × g, 5 min, 4 °C). Total RNA was extracted using Fast Bacterial RNA Kit (Sangon Biotech) with DNase I treatment. cDNA was synthesized from 500 ng RNA using MightyScript™ Master Mix (BBI). qPCR reactions (20 μL) contained 10 μL 2× SGExcel FastSYBR Mixture, 0.4 μL each primer (10 μM), 0.8 μL cDNA, and 8.4 μL nuclease-free water. Amplification used LightCycler® 480 II (Roche) under standard cycling conditions with melting curve analysis. Three biological/technical replicates were performed.

CRISPR-B™ for microbial knockout

Competent E. coli DH5α/DH10B were prepared from frozen stocks on LB agar (37 °C overnight). Single colonies were grown in LB broth to mid-log phase (OD₆₀₀=0.6), chilled on ice, pelleted (4000 × g, 10 min, 4 °C), and resuspended in ice-cold 10 % glycerol. After three washes, cells were resuspended in 250 μL glycerol for electroporation. The CRISPR-B plasmid (50 ng) was introduced into 50 μL competent cells by ice incubation (10 min) and electroporation (2.5 kV, 2-mm cuvettes). Cells were resuscitated in SOC medium (37 °C, 1 h), concentrated, and plated on selective agar. Transformants were screened by colony PCR and verified by Sanger sequencing. Validated knockouts were preserved in 25 % glycerol at −80 °C.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Bacterial growth data from Pseudomonas aeruginosa PAO1 cultures under SMG and NG conditions were evaluated by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Significant differences between experimental groups were determined by Tukey's multiple comparisons test (p < 0.05).
