The relationship between academic stress and depressive symptoms in middle school students: a network analysis model
Authors: Ke Zheng, Zijun Chen, Liang Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveBased on network analysis methodology, a network model was constructed to elucidate the relationship between academic stress and depressive symptoms among middle school students. By identifying core nodes (high-strength symptoms) and bridge nodes (cross-group associated symptoms) within the two symptom clusters, the study reveals the interactive pathways between these constructs. This approach provides a foundation for precise mental health interventions tailored to middle school students.MethodsA total of 1,576 middle school students in central Anhui Province were selected by the cluster sampling method, and questionnaire surveys were conducted using the Academic Stress Scale for Middle School Students and the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D). Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 26.0 and R software (Version 4.4.1). The partial correlation network was estimated using the EBICglasso algorithm (γ = 0.5) in R software (Version 4.4.1). Node strength centrality and bridge strength centrality were calculated, and the stability of the network was assessed using the correlation stability (CS) coefficient.ResultsThe network structure of academic stress and depressive symptoms among middle school students comprises 308 valid edges, forming two tightly connected clusters. In the Academic Stress Scale, SS16 (strength = 1.169) and SS12 (strength = 1.136) were identified as core nodes; SS20 (bridge strength = 0.248) and SS14 (bridge strength = 0.185) exhibited higher bridge strengths, with SS20 (bridge strength = 0.248) serving as the critical bridge node.In the Depression Scale, CES-D6 (strength = 1.211) and CES-D3(strength = 1.189) exhibited higher strengths, while CES-D16(bridge strength = 0.189) and CES-D20(bridge strength = 0.157) showed higher bridge strengths. The cross-symptom clusters edge between SS20 (“When I am upset, I feel I have no close friends to talk to.“) and CES-D14(“I felt lonely.“) had the highest edge weight (0.137). The network stability test revealed that both strength centrality (CS = 0.75) and bridge strength centrality (CS = 0.67) exceeded the recommended threshold (> 0.5).ConclusionAcademic stress and depressive symptoms form a dynamic interconnected network through social isolation (SS20) and low life satisfaction (CES-D16), suggesting the need to design targeted intervention strategies focusing on bridge nodes to mitigate their negative impact on middle school students.Keywords: Middle school student, Academic stress, Depressive symptoms, Network analysis

Introduction

Academic stress refers to the psychological pressure and tension caused by academic tasks, mainly including the psychological pressure caused by the results of studies such as exams, the process of competition with peers, and the expectations from parents and teachers [36]. Under the pressure of the high-intensity competitive mechanism of the college entrance examination, the academic stress borne by the middle school student groups has risen significantly. Li Weibo and fellow researchers, through interviews and surveys, discovered that a significant 89.36% of students experience academic stress [6, 21]. In the context of China’s educational environment, where teachers, parents, and students themselves hold high expectations and demands for academic performance, the learning pressure experienced by students in such a highly competitive setting is particularly pronounced [9]. A cross-national comparative analysis reveals that middle school students in China experience the highest levels of academic workload and learning pressure among their international peers [47]. The impact of academic stress on students’ physical and mental health is of paramount importance. The Educational Stressor Hypothesis posits that the expansion of education and the shift towards a knowledge-based economy have rendered adolescents’ life prospects increasingly dependent on academic performance, thereby generating heightened levels of academic stress, which in turn precipitates mental health issues [33, 34]. Long-term exposure to high-stress environments can lead students to experience extreme emotional fatigue, resulting in negative self-evaluations and potentially triggering maladaptive behaviors [16]. In addition, the detrimental effects of academic stress on students’ physical and mental health are also manifested in the decline of sleep quality and the onset of symptoms such as mental fatigue [15, 22], which may further increase the risk of students developing depressive symptoms [17].

Depression is a state of persistent low mood, accompanied by comprehensive emotional experiences such as sadness, helplessness, desolation, despair and somatic discomfort [45]. The middle school stage is an important period of psychological development, but also a variety of psychological problems frequently appear in the stage, this special period is prone to depression [41]. Multiple studies have indicated that depression has become one of the most prevalent and challenging psychological issues among middle school students [39]. A Meta-analysis indicated that the overall prevalence of depressive symptoms among Chinese middle school students was 24.3% [29]. The Diathesis-Stress Model posits that when an individual possesses a pre-existing vulnerability or diathesis, which is then activated by highly stressful life events, the interaction between these factors exceeds a certain threshold, ultimately leading to the onset of illness [19]. The emergence of most depressive symptoms usually follows the occurrence of stressful life events, so stress factors are considered to be high-risk predictors of depression [8]. Academic stress, as a specific category within the broader spectrum of stressors, can similarly precipitate the emergence of depressive symptoms. Middle school students, enduring the persistent burden of academic stress alongside significant physical and psychological changes during their growth, exhibit increased vulnerability in their mental health status [13]. Zhang Yong and other researchers found that as academic pressure intensifies, the severity of anxiety, depression, and suicidal ideation also escalates [44]. Concurrently, other studies have identified a significant positive correlation between academic stress and depressive symptoms [2].

Academic pressure, as a prevalent negative life event among adolescents, constitutes a significant external stressor that can trigger depression [4, 11].Theoretically, the transactional model of stress [20] posits that external environmental factors (e.g., academic pressure) interact with internal individual factors during the cognitive appraisal process. When pressure intensity exceeds coping capacity, it generates subjective stress perception and adverse physiological/psychological responses; prolonged accumulation of such responses ultimately leads to depression. Further elaborating this dynamic, the diathesis-stress model [24] emphasizes that depression results from the interaction between stressors (like academic pressure) and individual vulnerability, thereby transforming latent depressive tendencies into manifest disorder.

Empirically, studies consistently demonstrate that academic pressure is a key factor precipitating negative emotions such as anxiety and depression. Excessive academic pressure not only contributes to psychological problems including cognitive distortions and depression [48]but also exhibits a significant positive correlation with depression levels, acting as a direct predictor—higher academic pressure corresponds to elevated depressive symptoms in adolescents [32]. For many adolescents, particularly secondary school students, academic pressure often represents the primary stressor. Research confirms that heightened academic pressure is significantly positively associated with depressive mood and suicidal ideation [2] and may induce a cascade of adverse outcomes: it functions as a critical negative life event capable of directly triggering depression; in severe cases, it may even precipitate risky behaviors such as fighting or self-harm [25].

In recent years, the mental health problems of middle school students in China have become increasingly serious, and the co-morbidity of academic stress and depressive symptoms is particularly prominent. According to the Diathesis-Stress Model, individual vulnerability is prone to trigger psychological disorders in high-pressure environments. However, traditional linear models are inadequate for uncovering the complex interaction mechanisms among multiple symptoms. Network analysis theory, by visualizing symptom networks, enables the identification of core and bridge nodes, offering a novel perspective for dynamic intervention strategies. This study hypothesizes that academic stress and depressive symptoms form a dynamic associative network through specific core and bridge nodes, with “social isolation” (SS20) and “I did not enjoy life”(CES-D16) serving as critical bridge factors. Supportive evidence indicates that social isolation in adolescents significantly exacerbates the risk of depression [40], while the bidirectional relationship between academic stress and low life satisfaction has been cross-culturally validated. This study integrates network theory and the diathesis-stress model, transcending traditional causal assumptions to identify central symptoms and bridge symptoms within symptom clusters [37]. Constructing a network of academic stress and depressive symptoms among middle school students aims to provide a deeper understanding of the intricate associations between these factors. The research seeks to elucidate the complex pathways through which stress and depression interact, thereby offering a foundation for effective prevention and intervention strategies.

Method

Procedure and participants

From July to December 2024, a cluster sampling of 1,605 middle school students (55.3% male, 44.7% female; age 16.8 ± 0.85 years) was conducted in the central Anhui Province, China. Data were collected using paper-based questionnaires, and all participants voluntarily took part in the survey. After excluding invalid responses, a total of 1,576 valid samples were obtained, resulting in a sample inclusion rate of 98.2%.

Measurement

General information survey questionnaire

Including general demographic characteristics, behavioral and lifestyle factors, interpersonal relationships, and health status.

Academic stress scale for middle school students

This study employed the Academic Stress Scale for Middle School Students Questionnaire developed by Xu Jiajun [36]. The scale consists of 21 items, which are divided into four dimensions: Parents Pressure, Self Pressure, Teacher Pressure, and Sociality Pressure. Responses are scored on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “Never” to “Always,” with higher scores indicating greater levels of academic stress. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for this scale in the current study was 0.878.

Center for epidemiologic studies depression Scale(CES-D)

This study employed the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) revised by Zhang Jie [43]. The scale consists of 20 items, divided into four dimensions: Somatic and Retarded Activity, Interpersonal Problems, Depressed Affect, and Positive Affect. The items are scored on a 4-point Likert scale, ranging from “rarely or none of the time” to “most or all of the time.“The Cronbach’s α coefficient for this scale in the current study was 0.890.

In this study, to enhance the clarity of the network graph presentation, we recoded the reverse-scored items into forward-scored items and inverted the meaning of the items (e.g., “CES-D12: I felt happy” was transformed into “CES-D12: I did not feel happy”). The criterion validity test demonstrated a significant correlation with the total score of the PHQ-9 (r = 0.72, p < 0.001). The reliability did not show a significant change after the modification of the reverse-scored items (Δα = 0.01).

Meanwhile, based on previous research [7], we categorized the symptoms of the CES-D scale into three dimensions: Depressed Affect(CES-D3 = depressed, CES-D4 = inferior to others, CES-D6 = low spirits, CES-D8 = hopelessness about the future, CES-D9 = feelings of failure in life, CES-D10 = fearful, CES-D12 = unhappiness, CES-D14 = loneliness, CES-D16 = low life satisfaction, life is uninteresting, CES-D17 = crying, CES-D18 = sadness); Somatic and Retarded Activity(CES-D1 = bothered, CES-D2 = poor appetite, CES-D5 = poor concentration, CES-D7 = effortful in doing things, CES-D11 = poor sleep, CES-D13 = reduced speech, CES-D20 = lack of motivation); and Interpersonal Problems(CES-D15 = people are unfriendly, CES-D19 = people dislike me).

Quality control

All selected students were thoroughly informed about the details and methodologies of the survey. Professional guidance and training were provided by experts. Upon completion of the survey, data were screened to eliminate invalid responses, ensuring the integrity and accuracy of the questionnaires.

Statistical analyses

In this study, SPSS 26.0 and R software (Version 4.4.1) were utilized for descriptive statistical analysis and network model construction.

We utilized the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion Graphical Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (EBICglasso) algorithm to estimate the partial correlation network. The tuning parameter γ was set to 0.5, following established methodological recommendations [10]. This specific value optimally balances model complexity with goodness-of-fit by controlling the sparsity level of the network. The EBICglasso algorithm employs LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) regularization to shrink trivial edges to zero, thereby preventing overfitting and producing a sparse, interpretable network structure.

The qgraph package in R, along with the EBICglasso algorithm (γ = 0.5), was employed to estimate the partial correlation network. LASSO regularization was applied to control overfitting, resulting in the generation of a visualized network model. This model primarily consists of circular nodes and edges that represent the strength of associations between nodes. The centrality metrics of network nodes were calculated using the R-package qgraph and R-package networktools, with results displayed as Z-scores. Particular emphasis was placed on Strength Centrality (the total strength of node connections) and Bridge Strength Centrality (the strength of connections across symptom clusters). These metrics include Strength, which is the sum of the absolute values of all edges connected to a node [10]. A higher value indicates greater relative importance of the node within the network. Bridge Strength is defined as the sum of the absolute values of all edges connecting the node to nodes in other clusters. A higher value signifies a greater risk transmission from the node to other clusters and a more pronounced bridging effect [18]. Using the R package bootnet, a 1000-time Bootstrap method was employed to calculate the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edge weights and the correlation stability coefficients (CS-coefficients, with a cut-off value of 0.25). All statistical analyses were conducted at a significance level of α = 0.05.

Centrality metrics of nodes in the network structure of academic stress and depression

At the core nodes, within the domain of academic stress, SS16 (“I am often dissatisfied with my exam results.“, strength = 1.169) and SS12 (“If my exam results are not excellent, I feel frustrated.“, strength = 1.136) emerged as central nodes. In the realm of depressive symptoms, CES-D6 (“ I felt depressed.“, strength = 1.211) and CES-D3 (“I felt depressed even with help from my family and friends.“, strength = 1.189) were identified as core nodes (Table 3).

In the bridge strength centrality metrics, SS20, which states “When I am upset, I feel I have no close friends to talk to.” (bridge strength = 0.248), and SS14, which states “Even during holidays, I prefer to stay alone in my room and rarely communicate with my parents.” (bridge strength = 0.185), exhibit the strongest connections within the academic stress cluster. Meanwhile, CES-D16, which states “I do not enjoy my life.” (bridge strength = 0.189), and CES-D20, which states “I lack the motivation to do things.” (bridge strength = 0.157), show the strongest connections within the depression cluster. Notably, SS20 (“When I am upset, I feel I have no close friends to talk to.” bridge strength = 0.248) and CES-D16 (“I did not enjoy life.” bridge strength = 0.189) serve as bridges linking the academic stress and depression subclusters.

At the critical edge, the cross-group edge weight between SS20 (“social isolation “) and CES-D14 (“loneliness”) is the largest (0.137), forming a “social isolation-loneliness” feedback loop (Fig. 2).

The centrality stability test is illustrated in Fig. 3. The x-axis of Fig. 3 represents the percentage of the original sample used, while the y-axis indicates the correlation level of node centrality between the original sample and the reduced sample. Stability test of centrality metrics in the network structure of academic stress and depression

The Bootstrap difference test (as visualized in Figs. 4 and 5) revealed statistically significant differences between strength centrality and bridge strength centrality metrics across nodes. Specifically, the minimal overlap in confidence intervals (represented by the non-overlapping black boxes in the figures) indicates that these two types of centrality measures capture distinct aspects of node influence within the network. This differentiation confirms that nodes play unique roles—some acting as core hubs within their own symptom communities (strength centrality), while others serve as critical connectors between communities (bridge strength centrality).

Discussion

This study represents the first application of network analysis to investigate the stress-depression relationship among middle school students. Through visualized network analysis, we explored the associations between various dimensions of academic stress and depressive symptoms in adolescents, revealing interconnections among different symptoms and identifying core symptoms within the network. Research findings indicate that social isolation (SS20) serves as a pivotal bridge node connecting two categories of symptoms, contrasting with the study by Xiu et al. on junior high school students, which identified examination anxiety as the central factor [35]. This highlights the dual challenges faced by high school students, stemming from both academic competition and social needs. The network model further reveals that academic stress exacerbates depressive symptoms through the “self-evaluation—social withdrawal” pathway, supporting the cognitive theory of stress [19].

Identification and analysis of core symptoms

The findings of this study indicate that CES-D6, “I felt depressed,” exhibited the highest centrality in terms of strength, serving as a core nodal symptom within the network. “I felt depressed” represents the most direct self-disclosure of depressive mood and is an early manifestation of depressive symptoms among middle school students. Another core node identified was CES-D3, “I felt depressed even with help from my family and friends.“The findings of this study are consistent with the clinical manifestations of depression. The《Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders》(5th Edition) identifies depressed mood and markedly diminished interest or pleasure as the two core symptoms of a depressive diagnosis [3]. Feelings of dejection and discouragement are both manifestations of a low mood. During high school, students tend to be more emotionally sensitive compared to other stages of their education. Persistent low mood during this period can have a negative impact on their daily lives and academic performance. When such low mood continues uncontrollably, it may serve as a precursor to depression [31]. The emotional states represented by CES-D3 and CES-D6 also align closely with Beck’s cognitive triad theory, which involves negative cognitive assessments of the self (‘I am incapable’), the future (‘hopeless’), and experiences (‘feeling distressed’). This cognitive bias is not only a symptom of depression but also a crucial factor in its maintenance.In the Academic Stress Scale, item SS16, “I am often dissatisfied with my exam results,” and item SS12, " If my exam results are not excellent, I feel frustrated,” are identified as core metrics. This indicates that high school students generally hold high expectations for their academic performance, worry that their grades may not meet their personal expectations, and fear falling behind in class rankings [23].From the perspective of achievement motivation theory, SS12 and SS16 reflect a motivation orientation of “avoiding failure” rather than “pursuing success.” This orientation tends to cause students to experience high anxiety and low self-efficacy when facing academic challenges, thereby forming a cognitive burden and exacerbating their sense of pressure. The modern educational environment has simultaneously fostered a strong atmosphere of peer comparison, further stimulating students’ competitive instincts. The interplay between internal expectations and external environmental factors has led to an increasingly heavy psychological burden, resulting in escalating self-imposed pressure. The cognitive theory model of stress posits that the generation of stress is contingent upon an individual’s appraisal of both the situation they are in and the resources at their disposal. Should the individual assess that they are unable to resolve the situation or that their available resources are insufficient to aid in its resolution, they may experience stress, potentially leading to a loss of control and the onset of tension or anxiety. To alleviate self-imposed pressure, it is imperative to relinquish the excessive pursuit of success and the fear of failure. This approach can guide adolescents to adopt a constructive perspective on the learning process, viewing each academic assessment as an opportunity for growth rather than a definitive measure of their capabilities.

Identification and analysis of bridge symptom

In network analysis, bridge symptoms play a crucial role in connecting two subnetworks. The bridge strength of SS20, which is “When I am upset, I feel I have no close friends to talk to,” serves as a significant bridge within the entire network. The Significant Others Theory emphasizes that peers, as central figures in an individual’s life, profoundly shape the individual’s values, behavioral patterns, and ways of thinking through daily interactions, thereby exerting a long-term influence on the individual’s growth trajectory and developmental direction [1, 14]. During the high school period, the enhancement of adolescents’ independent consciousness and the development of self-identity lead to a gradual increase in their interactions with peers. At this stage, the establishment of peer relationships becomes the primary channel for individuals to obtain social support. The statement “When I am upset, I feel I have no close friends to talk to.” can be described as social isolation, which hinders adolescents from building a positive social network and subsequently has a negative impact on their mental health [30]. Social isolation can lead to an increase in individual stress levels [12], while also affecting physiological indicators such as elevated cortisol levels, thereby contributing to the onset of depressive symptoms [27]. From the perspective of psychological needs, Maslow’s hierarchy of needs theory identifies social needs as one of the fundamental human requirements. When individuals encounter distress, confiding in a close friend serves not only as a means of information transmission but also fulfills an intrinsic need for understanding and acceptance. In the absence of such a confidant, the negative emotions harbored within an individual may struggle to find relief. It can also be understood from the perspective of attachment theory: the lack of a secure attachment figure (such as a close friend) prevents individuals from effectively utilizing external resources for emotion regulation in stressful situations, making them more susceptible to being trapped in internal negative emotions and forming a cross-network propagation pathway from stress to depression.Educational institutions can address this by designing a “Peer Confidence Workshop” which offers simulated social scenarios and training courses aimed at enhancing adolescents’ communication and emotional sharing skills. This initiative can gradually foster a supportive social network, thereby alleviating feelings of loneliness and bolstering mental health levels.

Secondly, the CES-D16 item, “I did not enjoy life,” also plays a significant bridging role within the network.This item directly reflects anhedonia—the diminished capacity to experience pleasure—which constitutes a core pathological feature of depression [5]. When persistent anhedonia prevents individuals from deriving satisfaction from daily activities, their overall life satisfaction markedly declines, leading to an accumulation of negative emotions. This emotional state triggers a bidirectional vicious cycle: on the one hand, anhedonia directly impairs students’ learning motivation and cognitive functioning [26], resulting in diminished academic efficacy; on the other hand, academic setbacks further reinforce perceived stress [28], compelling individuals to overcommit to academic pursuits at the expense of social and leisure time necessary for emotional regulation. As demonstrated in the empirical study by Yovita et al., when adolescents experience heightened stress levels, this stress significantly exacerbates their unhappiness, irritability, and depressive symptoms, ultimately weakening their life satisfaction and forming a negative feedback loop [38]. To address this issue, schools could implement a “Life Satisfaction Enhancement Program,” which would focus on teaching emotion regulation strategies and fostering a positive mindset, thereby assisting adolescents in overcoming the challenges posed by negative emotions. Simultaneously, teachers are also advised to reduce public comparisons of grades to avoid exacerbating student stress. Parents should integrate the “growth mindset” into daily life, focusing more on the process of their children’s efforts rather than solely on outcomes, and encouraging them to learn and grow from setbacks. Through home-school collaboration, a supportive environment can be fostered, thereby enhancing the life satisfaction of adolescents.

The SS14 item, “Even during holidays, I prefer to stay alone in my room and rarely communicate with my parents,” and the CES-D20 item, “I lack the motivation to do things,” also serve as crucial bridging symptoms within this network structure. SS14 not only reflects a lack of social support but may also be related to the dimensions of “emotional expression” and “communication quality” in family functioning theory. Poor family communication patterns can weaken the family’s role as a stress buffer system, leading adolescents to internalize stress as depressive symptoms.A lack of motivation can lead individuals to exhibit a diminished interest in various activities, while also impacting their interpersonal interactions, thereby resulting in social withdrawal. Furthermore, it reflects the common excessive activation of the “Behavioral Inhibition System” (BIS) in depression, manifesting as difficulty initiating goal-directed behavior. This state can severely impair students’ academic engagement and problem-solving abilities, creating a bidirectional reinforcement cycle between academic pressure and depressive symptoms.Consequently, schools and families should provide positive feedback and companionship, fostering an external environment rich in positive reinforcement to alleviate psychological burnout. Additionally, parents, as primary guides for their children, can keenly identify their children’s areas of interest, creating a relaxed social atmosphere to help them overcome the psychological barriers of motivational deficiency and social pressure.

The overall characteristics of the network analysis model

The network analysis revealed the formation of two closely interconnected subnetworks between adolescent academic stress and depressive mood, indicating a complex interactive relationship between the two. Among the edges bridging the two subnetworks, the connection between SS20 “When I am upset, I feel I have no close friends to talk to.” and CES-D14 " I felt lonely.” exhibited the highest edge weight and was positively correlated. Loneliness stems from the discrepancy between an individual’s perceived actual social interactions and interpersonal relationships and their desired level, representing a prevalent negative emotional experience. According to researchers such as Sullivan, loneliness arises when individuals fail to fulfill their need to establish interpersonal relationships, and he posits that the primary cause of loneliness is the absence of secure attachments and intimate relationships. Numerous researchers have also indicated that individuals experience loneliness when they are dissatisfied with their social relationships or unable to fulfill their desire for intimate connections [46, 49]. When individuals feel lonely, they tend to maintain negative evaluations of their surroundings and excessively focus on environmental cues related to rejection. This emotional distress makes them more likely to adopt maladaptive emotion regulation strategies when facing stress, ultimately impacting their mental health status [42]. Therefore, it is essential to encourage adolescents to broaden their social interactions, guide them to engage in in-depth communication with their peers, and promote their active participation in group activities. This approach can help alleviate academic pressure while preventing the onset of depressive emotions. Additionally, designing group counseling sessions that include enhancing interpersonal communication skills and fostering the establishment of intimate relationships can empower adolescents to transform superficial social interactions into deep, meaningful connections. This can break the vicious cycle of “having no one to talk to about troubles,” thereby preventing them from sinking into loneliness.

Recommendations for intervention strategies

This research provides robust empirical evidence for addressing adolescents’ academic stress and effectively alleviating their depressive symptoms by targeting key bridge nodes identified in the network analysis. To effectively mitigate social isolation and enhance life satisfaction, we recommend implementing Interpersonal Psychotherapy-Adolescent Skills Training (IPT-AST) in school settings, which has been empirically shown to improve interpersonal relationships and reduce feelings of loneliness by enhancing communication skills and social support networks. Complementing this, Positive Psychology Interventions (PPIs) should be integrated to boost life satisfaction through activities such as gratitude journaling, strengths identification, and positive social engagement, which foster a sense of meaning and pleasure in daily activities.

At the school level: Systematically implement Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) programs, which utilize breathing exercises and relaxation techniques to regulate excessive academic pressure. Establish peer support workshops, employing role-playing and empathy training to mitigate social isolation. Supplement these initiatives with group counseling sessions specifically focused on enhancing life satisfaction. At the family level: Conduct parent training programs centered on fostering a growth mindset. Encourage parents to establish regular, high-quality communication channels with their adolescents. Through dialogue on an equal footing and emotional exchange, parents can gain timely insight into their adolescents’ psychological state and needs, thereby providing appropriate emotional support and behavioral guidance. At the community level: Actively involve adolescents in community volunteer service activities. Participation in such programs can enhance their communication skills, cultivate a sense of responsibility, strengthen social connections, and boost self-efficacy.

The limitations of the study and future research directions

This study has several limitations: The cross-sectional design employed in this research makes it difficult to reveal the dynamic relationships between symptoms. Future studies could adopt a longitudinal research design and explore these relationships through longitudinal network analysis. The study was conducted only in the central Anhui region, with Han Chinese accounting for 97.8% of the participants. Future research should include more diverse cultural groups. This study relied solely on questionnaire surveys. Future research could incorporate intervention studies to further clarify the associations between symptoms. Although sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of using strength centrality as the core metric in this study’s network characterized by weak negative correlations, future research involving networks with stronger negative correlations should employ the expected influence metric.

In summary, this study employed network analysis to delve into the core node symptoms of academic stress and depressive symptoms among middle school students in the central Anhui region. It conducted a comparative analysis of the relationships between these symptoms and various items, revealing the existing correlations among them. The strength centrality results indicated that CES-D6 had the highest centrality, followed by CES-D3, CES-D18, and SS16. The bridge strength centrality results showed that SS20 played the most significant bridging role in the entire network, followed by CES-D16, SS14, and CES-D20. Among the edges spanning the two subnetworks, the edge between SS20 and CES-D14 had the highest weight. This study provides a theoretical foundation for more precise and effective prevention and intervention efforts targeting academic stress and depressive symptoms in adolescents.
