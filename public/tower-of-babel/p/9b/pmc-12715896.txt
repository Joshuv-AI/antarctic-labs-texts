Global, regional, and national burden of brain and central nervous system cancer in children and adolescents, 1990–2021: A systematic analysis of the Global Burden of Disease study 2021
Authors: Yafang Deng, Qiang Gao, Wei Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImportanceBrain and central nervous system (CNS) cancers are the most common pediatric solid tumors, significantly affecting the patients’ life expectancy and quality.ObjectiveTo examine the disease burden of brain and CNS cancer in children and adolescents from 1990 to 2021 across various age groups, sexes, and socio‐demographic index levels, at the global, regional, and national levels.MethodsData, including the incidence, mortality, and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) of brain and CNS cancer in children and adolescents, were obtained from the 2021 Global Burden of Diseases, Injuries, and Risk Factors Study. Temporal trends were evaluated using the Joinpoint regression model. The relationship between disease burden and socioeconomic development, as well as health inequality, was examined.ResultsIn 2021, the global age‐standardized incidence rate of brain and CNS cancers among patients aged 0–20 years was 1.55 per 100 000 population. From 1990 to 2021, the global age‐standardized mortality rate (ASMR) declined from 1.14 to 0.76 per 100 000, and the age‐standardized DALY rate (ASDR) decreased from 94.29 to 62.79 per 100 000. Over the past three decades, most regions and countries and territories have shown declining trends in ASMR and ASDR. Global inequality of disease burden persists, with regions possessing higher socioeconomic statuses exhibiting lower ASMR, ASDR, and mortality‐to‐incidence ratio.InterpretationThe decline in ASMR and ASDR of pediatric brain and CNS cancer indicates improvements in disease management and patient outcomes. Enhancing diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, improving life quality, and promoting health equity are crucial directions for future research.

INTRODUCTION

Brain and central nervous system (CNS) cancers are among the most common solid tumors in children and adolescents, posing a significant threat to their survival and quality of life. 1 , 2 These tumors cause severe neurological impairments and systemic complications, leading to substantial morbidity and mortality. Unlike in adults, pediatric CNS cancers exhibit distinct molecular profiles, 3 clinical phenotypes, and epidemiological characteristics, requiring focused research attention. Despite advancements in diagnostic methods and treatment strategies, 4 , 5 along with a growing number of epidemiological studies on brain and CNS cancers in various countries, 6 , 7 comprehensive research specifically targeting pediatric and adolescent populations remains limited.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study is a comprehensive global health database that quantifies disease burden at global, regional, and national levels. The GBD 2021 study provides data on 371 diseases, enabling the analysis of temporal trends, regional variations, and demographic distributions. 8 While previous research has assessed the disease burden of brain and CNS cancers in the general populations, 9 , 10 , 11 studies specifically examining pediatric and adolescent populations using the latest GBD data are lacking.

This study utilized the GBD 2021 data to analyze the incidence, mortality, and disability‐adjusted life years (DALYs) of brain and CNS cancers in individuals aged 0–20 years. The analysis examined the disease burden across different time periods, age groups, sexes, regions, and countries. Additionally, it explores the correlation between disease burden and socioeconomic factors, offering insights to enhance strategies for improving health equity and survival outcomes in pediatric and adolescent patients.

METHODS

Ethical approval

The GBD study follows strict ethical guidelines under the oversight of the University of Washington Research Ethics Committee. The Institutional Review Board granted an exemption for this study due to using publicly available data.

Overview and data source

The GBD 2021 study provides a comprehensive analysis of 371 diseases and 88 risk factors across 204 countries and territories, offering multiple detailed epidemiological metrics. 8 , 12 For this study, data on the global, regional, and national burden of brain and CNS cancers in children and adolescents from 1990 to 2021 were retrieved from the Global Health Data Exchange (GBD Results Tool) (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd‐results/).

Disease and term definition

In this study, brain and CNS cancer refers to malignant neoplasms classified under ICD‐10 codes C70‐C72 and C75.1‐C75.3. Key disease burden metrics include: number and rates of incidence, mortality, and DALYs.

The socio‐demographic index (SDI) measures the developmental status of countries or regions. The SDI incorporates factors including income inequality, women's status, and educational attainment, ranging from 0.005 to 1, with higher values indicating greater socioeconomic development. 8 , 13 Countries and territories are categorized into five SDI regions: low, low‐middle, middle, high‐middle, and high.

Statistical analysis

Data on brain and CNS cancers from 21 regions and 204 countries and territories were obtained from GBD 2021. Patients were categorized into four age groups: <5 years, 5–9 years, 10–14 years, and 15–19 years. Incidence, mortality, and DALY numbers and age‐standardized rates (ASRs) with 95% uncertainty intervals (95% UI) were collected. ASRs were calculated using the following formula: ASR = ∑(ai×wi)/∑wi×100 000, in which i denotes the i th age group, a i indicates the age‐specific rates in this group, and w i represents the population weight for the corresponding age group in the reference standard population. The mortality‐to‐incidence ratio (M/I ratio) was calculated by dividing the age‐standardized mortality rate (ASMR) by the age‐standardized incidence rate (ASIR).

Joinpoint regression was used to analyze the trends of the disease burden from 1990 to 2021. The model applies the least squares method to minimize subjectivity in estimation. The average annual percent change (AAPC) was computed as: AAPC = {exp(∑bi×wi/∑wi)−1}×100, where i denotes the i th time segment, b i is the slope coefficient for the segment, and w i is the segment's duration in years. A positive AAPC with a 95% confidence interval (CI) indicates an increasing trend, while a negative AAPC with a 95% CI indicates a decreasing trend.

To assess disparities in global health, the correlation between the disease burden and socioeconomic development levels was examined. The slope index of inequality (SII) was employed to measure the linear disparity between socioeconomic status and disease burden. The SII in this study was calculated using the following formula: SII = Cov(x,h)/Var(x), where x represents the relative position ranked by the SDI, and h represents the DALY rate. The SII reflects the absolute inequality in disease burden, and the greater the absolute value of the SII, the more pronounced the inequality in disease burden. An SII greater than zero indicates that the disease burden is more concentrated among higher socioeconomic groups, whereas an SII less than zero suggests that the disease burden is more concentrated among lower socioeconomic groups. Relative inequalities were assessed using the concentration index by applying the Lorenz curve to cumulative DALYs and cumulative population. The concentration index spans from −1 to 1. Similar to the SII, a positive concentration index indicates that the disease burden is more concentrated in countries and territories with higher SDI values. 14 , 15

Data analysis and presentation were performed using the Joinpoint Regression Program (version 5.1.0.0) and R (version 4.3.2).

RESULTS

Global trends

In 2021, the global prevalence of brain and CNS cancer among children and adolescents was estimated at 199.21 × 103 cases (95% UI: 166.47 × 103 to 241.71 × 103), reflecting a 34.6% increase from 1990. Correspondingly, the age‐standardized prevalence rate rose from 6.55/100 000 (95% UI: 5.19/100 000 to 7.94/100 000) in 1990 to 7.64/100 000 (95% UI: 6.22/100 000 to 9.37/100 000) in 2021. Despite a modest rise in absolute incident cases, from 38.82 × 103 (95% UI: 30.30 × 103 to 48.09 × 103) in 1990 to 40.53 × 103 (95% UI: 33.35 × 103 to 49.61 × 103) in 2021, the ASIR declined from 1.72/100 000 (95% UI: 1.31/100 000 to 2.14/100 000) to 1.55/100 000 (95% UI: 1.25/100 000 to 1.90/100 000), with an AAPC of −0.38% (95% CI: −0.48% to −0.28%) (Table 1).

Both mortality count and rate associated with brain and CNS cancer among children and adolescents exhibited a substantial decline over the past three decades. The number of deaths dropped from 25.70 × 103 (95% UI: 19.40 × 103 to 32.23 × 103) in 1990 to 19.92 × 103 (95% UI: 16.06 × 103 to 24.53 × 103) in 2021. Compared to incidence, the decline in mortality was more pronounced. The ASMR decreased from 1.14/100 000 (95% UI: 0.84/100 000 to 1.46/100 000) to 0.76/100 000 (95% UI: 0.60/100 000 to 0.94/100 000), with an AAPC of −1.31% (95% CI: −1.47% to −1.14%). A similar downward trend was observed in age‐standardized DALY rates (ASDRs), which dropped from 94.29/100 000 (95% UI: 69.46/100 000 to 121.47/100 000) in 1990 to 62.79/100 000 (95% UI: 49.35/100 000 to 77.82/100 000) in 2021, corresponding to an AAPC of −1.33% (95% CI: −1.48% to −1.17%) (Table 1).

Age and sex patterns

From 1990 to 2021, the incidence of brain and CNS cancer in children and adolescents varied by age. The highest incidence rate was observed in children < 5 years (1.77/100 000, 95% UI: 1.36/100 000 to 2.25/100 000) in 2021. The lowest incidence rate occurred in adolescents aged 15–19 years (1.19/100 000, 95% UI: 0.98/100 000 to 1.40/100 000). Over time, incidence rates declined in children under 10 years but increased in older age groups (Table 2 and Figure S1A).

By 2021, deaths had declined across most age groups except for a slight increase in those aged 10–14 years. In 2021, the highest number of deaths occurred in children aged 5–9 years, and the highest death rate occurred in children under 5 years of age (0.86/100 000, 95% UI: 0.64/100 000 to 1.13/100 000). The lowest death rate was reported in adolescents aged 15–19 years (0.57/100 000, 95% UI: 0.47/100 000 to 0.68/100 000). All age groups showed decreasing mortality trends, with the most pronounced decline in children aged < 5 years (AAPC −2.12%, 95% CI: −2.26% to −1.98%). The trend in DALYs was largely consistent with that of mortality. Children younger than 5 years had the highest DALY rate in both 1990 and 2021, decreasing from 143.38/100 000 (95% UI: 101.31/100 000 to 193.18/100 000) to 76.36/100 000 (95% UI: 57.26/100 000 to 99.81/100 000) (AAPC −2.11%, 95% CI: −2.25% to −1.97%). Adolescents aged 15–19 years had the lowest in both years. All age groups showed negative AAPCs in DALY rate, with the most pronounced decline found in children aged under 5 years (Table 2 and Figure S1B, C).

The overall sex difference in the disease burden of brain and CNS cancer among children and adolescents was more pronounced in 1990 than in 2021. Males presented higher ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR, despite a greater decline over time. In 2021, ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR for females were 1.54/100 000 (95% UI: 1.28/100 000 to 1.80/100 000), 0.73/100 000 (95% UI: 0.60/100 000 to 0.85/100 000), and 60.38/100 000 (95% UI: 49.61/100 000 to 70.53/100 000), respectively, while for males, the corresponding rates were 1.57/100 000 (95% UI: 1.08/100 000 to 2.12/100 000), 0.79/100 000 (95% UI: 0.54/100 000 to 1.08/100 000), and 65.06/100 000 (95% UI: 44.32/100 000 to 89.13/100 000) (Table 1). Sex‐specific patterns varied by age. Among patients aged over 5 years, males exhibited higher ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR, while rates in children younger than 5 years were nearly identical between sexes. Overall, sex differences in brain and CNS cancer burden were not significant (Figure S2).

Regional trends

In 2021, the middle SDI region had the highest number of brain and CNS cancer cases among children and adolescents, whereas the highest ASIR was observed in the high SDI region (2.66/100 000, 95% UI: 2.42/100 000 to 2.90/100 000). The low SDI region had both the lowest number of incidence cases and the lowest ASIR. The ASIRs generally increased with the SDI levels. From 1990 to 2021, the low SDI and low‐middle SDI regions exhibited increasing trends in ASIRs. No significant changes were observed in the other three regions (Table 1 and Figure 1A).

Among the 21 geographic regions, East Asia, High‐income North America, and Western Europe exhibited the highest ASIRs for brain and CNS cancers among pediatric populations during the study period. East Asia also recorded the highest number of cases in 2021. The High‐income Asia Pacific region demonstrated the most substantial increase in ASIR (AAPC 1.16%, 95% CI: 0.35% to 1.97%), while Central Europe experienced the largest decrease (AAPC −0.89%, 95% CI: −1.29% to −0.48%). Overall, regions with higher SDI levels were associated with higher incidence rates (Figure 2A).

National trends

In 2021, pediatric brain and CNS cancer incidence varied widely. China (10.47 × 103 cases, 95% UI: 7.99 × 103 to 14.07 × 103), India (3.87 × 103 cases, 95% UI: 2.95 × 103 to 5.29 × 103), and the United States of America (2.36 × 103 cases, 95% UI: 2.20 × 103 to 2.55 × 103) reported the highest number of incident cases. The highest ASIRs were observed in Monaco (7.61/100 000, 95% UI: 3.92/100 000 to 13.21/100 000), San Marino (6.31/100 000, 95% UI: 3.38/100 000 to 11.05/100 000), and Norway (6.10/100 000, 95% UI: 4.78/100 000 to 7.43/100 000), while the lowest ASIRs were recorded in Gambia (0.13/100 000, 95% UI: 0.06/100 000 to 0.22/100 000), Mali (0.17/100 000, 95% UI: 0.06/100 000 to 0.31/100 000), and Sao Tome and Principe (0.19/100 000, 95% UI: 0.08/100 000 to 0.38/100 000). Tokelau, Niue, and Ecuador showed the most significant increases in ASIRs, while Greenland, Serbia, and Panama experienced the most notable declines (Figure 3A and Figures S3A and S4A).

In 2021, the highest ASMRs were recorded in Tajikistan (2.42/100 000, 95% UI: 1.43/100 000 to 3.91/100 000), Tokelau (2.40/100 000, 95% UI: 1.17/100 000 to 3.90/100 000), and Azerbaijan (2.18/100 000, 95% UI: 1.27/100 000 to 3.53/100 000). The highest numbers of death cases were reported in China (3.62 × 103 cases, 95% UI: 2.72 × 103 to 4.85 × 103), India (2.69 × 103 cases, 95% UI: 2.08 × 103 to 3.66 × 103), and Pakistan (1.06 × 103 cases, 95% UI: 0.78 × 103 to 1.47 × 103). Similar to the incidence, Tokelau, Niue, and Ecuador exhibited the most significant increases in ASMRs. Serbia, Greenland, and Hungary showed the most substantial declines from 1990 to 2021. Consistent with the regional patterns, the M/I ratio demonstrated a significant negative correlation with the SDI across 204 countries and territories (Figure 3B and Figures S3B and S4B, C). Aligning with the patterns observed in mortality rates, the highest ASDRs in 2021 were observed in Tokelau (202.28/100 000, 95% UI: 98.05/100 000 to 330.21/100 000), Tajikistan (200.50/100 000, 95% UI: 117.92/100 000 to 323.89/100 000), and Azerbaijan (179.47/100 000, 95% UI: 103.88/100 000 to 291.22/100 000), and the highest absolute numbers of DALY cases were reported in China (297.41 × 103, 95% UI: 222.28 × 103 to 399.26 × 103), India (218.11 × 103, 95% UI: 167.57 × 103 to 298.11 × 103), and Pakistan (87.67 × 103, 95% UI: 63.71 × 103 to 121.20 × 103). Tokelau, Niue, and Ecuador exhibited the most pronounced increases in DALY rates, whereas Serbia, Greenland, and Hungary showed the greatest decreases over the past three decades (Figure 3C, Figures S3C and S4D).

Health inequalities

In 1990, the SII of ASDR caused by brain and CNS cancer in children and adolescents was 49.86/100 000 (95% CI: 34.11/100 000 to 65.62/100 000), indicating a positive relationship between disease burden and socioeconomic development. By 2021, the SII had reduced to 23.01/100 000 (95% CI: 10.63/100 000 to 35.39/100 000), indicating that the health equality in the distribution of disease burden has improved in the past three decades. In terms of relative equality, the concentration indices of DALY cases were positive in both 1990 and 2021 (0.13 in 1990 and 0.15 in 2021), also suggesting that disease burden was relatively more concentrated on individuals of higher socioeconomic status (Figure 4).

DISCUSSION

Brain and CNS cancers are one of the most common solid tumors in children and adolescents, posing a significant threat to life expectancy and quality. Compared to adult cancers, pediatric brain and CNS tumors exhibit distinct characteristics. 16 Advances in diagnosis, treatment, and social support have improved patient outcomes. This study examined epidemiological trends over three decades to highlight progress and guide future research.

The incidence of brain and CNS cancers generally decreases with age among individuals under 20 years of age, which contrasts with the increasing trend observed in adults over 20 years old. 10 This discrepancy may arise from the varying prevalence of brain and CNS cancer in each age group. The most common types of pediatric brain and CNS cancers are gliomas, medulloblastomas, and ependymomas. For gliomas, the median age of pediatric patients varies according to tumor grade, anatomical location, and molecular characteristics. 1 In children under 1 year of age, the most common malignant CNS tumors are embryonal tumors. Some embryonal tumors, such as those attributed to germline gene mutations, are poorly differentiated and highly malignant, leading to a poor prognosis for affected children. 17 Pediatric patients in different age groups may also exhibit distinct behaviors in terms of diagnosis, treatment, and prognosis. In this study, the mortality rates across different age groups followed a trend similar to that of incidence rates. Another study revealed that the rate of receiving therapy (including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy) and the 5‐year overall survival of pediatric patients with CNS cancers were significantly lower in infants under 3 months of age than in older patients. 18 Given the unique molecular variations and the neurotoxic side effects of conventional therapies, the development of targeted and molecular therapies is urgently needed for these young children. Additionally, considering their originally expected long life expectancy, the disease burden imposed on these young children in the context of GBD is substantial, further underscoring the necessity for focused attention on these patients.

Based on the existing evidence, sex differences in the incidence and mortality of brain and CNS cancers appear to be minimal, particularly among pediatric patients. 10 , 19 However, in specific tumor types, previous cohort studies have reported some sex differences, which may be driven by a variety of distinct mechanisms. 20 In terms of genetic and molecular characteristics, female glioblastoma (GBM) patients exhibit greater enrichment of MGMT promoter methylation, which correlates with increased overall survival, prolonged post‐recurrence survival, and enhanced response to chemotherapy. 21 Regarding metabolism, GBM in male individuals demonstrates significantly higher glutamine uptake, indicating promoted tumor proliferation and progression capabilities. 22 In the context of antitumor immunity, distinct subsets of myeloid‐derived suppressor cells are differentially distributed in males and females, suggesting that targeting these distinct subsets may offer differential therapeutic benefits for patients of different sexes. 23 These research conclusions collectively indicate that there are substantial opportunities to develop personalized treatments for patients of different sexes in the future. 24 Sex may also play a role in outcomes beyond survival, yet the specific mechanisms remain to be further investigated. For example, male survivors exhibited lower IQ 25 but better verbal processing, 26 while females experienced greater cognitive impairments. 27 , 28 These results underscore the necessity to extend our focus beyond incidence and survival to encompass a broader spectrum of outcome indicators for pediatric patients.

Over the past three decades, the global ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR rates for brain and CNS cancers in children and adolescents have declined. This reduction is largely attributed to advancements in prevention, diagnosis, and treatment, along with an increased awareness of the disease. The slight decrease in incidence aligns with findings suggesting that preventive measures, such as maternal folic acid supplementation, have reduced malignancy risk. 29 The more pronounced decline in mortality and DALY rates could be attributed to advances in diagnostic technologies such as optimization of magnetic resonance imaging, novel imaging agents, and the application of artificial intelligence, enabling earlier diagnosis and more accurate molecular subtype predictions. 30 The continuous updates to CNS tumor classification emphasize molecular diagnostics, 5 and multiomic neuropathology significantly improves the diagnostic accuracy and personalized treatment strategies. 31 , 32 Advances in treatment options, including surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapies, immunotherapy, and tumor‐treating fields, have improved survival and quality of life for pediatric patients. 33 , 34 , 35 Moreover, basic research and clinical trials work together to expand treatment options, 36 , 37 highlighting the need for continued investment in both areas to improve patient outcomes.

The burden of brain and CNS cancers in children and adolescents varies by region and socioeconomic status. The ASIR generally positively correlates with SDI, reflecting better access to healthcare and diagnostic methods in regions with higher SDI. Diverse environmental exposure may represent another factor influencing incidence rates. 38 ASMR and ASDR, especially the M/I ratio, are negatively correlated with SDI, which is consistent with the conclusions of other studies. 39 , 40 Higher income regions show a more pronounced decline over the past three decades, where patients have greater access to advanced techniques and specialized care teams, improving their survival and quality of life. 41 , 42 , 43 The disease also imposes socioeconomic burdens on individuals. 44 , 45 Moreover, disparities exist among countries and regions with similar SDI, indicating that other elements, such as race, genetics, and environmental factors, also influence disease outcomes. 46 , 47 Encouragingly, more countries have established cohorts for pediatric brain cancers, 48 , 49 and unique experiences from each country will facilitate precision treatment for different populations. In future practice, several key policies could enhance the diagnostic and therapeutic efficiency and promote health equity. First, establishing telemedicine networks could expand patient access to medical resources, facilitate timely referrals, and provide greater access to multi‐disciplinary treatment (MDT). By integrating the expertise of oncologists, neurologists, surgeons, radiologists, and supportive care specialists, MDT can improve systematic evaluation and enable precision treatment tailored to each patient. Second, improving the diagnostic and therapeutic quality is essential, particularly in socioeconomically underdeveloped regions. This can be achieved by adopting molecular pathological diagnosis for more precise guidance and further developing artificial intelligence‐assisted imaging and pathological recognition to enhance diagnostic efficiency and telemedicine accessibility. Finally, adequate national and governmental support is crucial. Implementing standardized guidelines, establishing regional and national disease surveillance systems, constructing rational hierarchical medical networks, and increasing medical insurance coverage for pediatric malignant CNS cancer are vital measures to reduce the disease burden and promote health equity.

This study has some limitations. First, the GBD study aggregates all brain and CNS cancers into a single category, overlooking significant differences in epidemiology, clinical features, and treatment strategies for each cancer subtype. Future research should focus on individual subtypes to enable targeted intervention. Second, the data in the GBD database are predominantly derived from secondary sources, which may fail to capture the full spectrum of the disease burden, particularly in regions with limited healthcare. Subsequent statistical modeling and estimation introduce uncertainty. To improve the accuracy, more primary data collection is required, especially in underrepresented regions. Lastly, this study focused on factors including sex, age, geographical distribution, and socioeconomic status, without exploring other critical risk factors, such as environmental exposure, genetic predisposition, and drug‐related factors, which are also crucial for understanding cancer progression. However, the GBD database does not include detailed information regarding these variables. Future studies should integrate data from diverse sources to enhance prevention and early intervention strategies.

This study provides a comprehensive overview of the global, regional, and national disease burden of brain and CNS cancers in children and adolescents over the past three decades. Significant progress has been made in reducing global mortality and DALY rates, driven by advancements in diagnostics, treatment, and healthcare systems. However, disparities persist across regions, highlighting the impact of socioeconomic inequality. Future efforts should focus on ensuring equitable access to healthcare, promoting early detection, and addressing broader aspects of patient well‐being, including survival, cognitive health, psychosocial support, and quality of life.
