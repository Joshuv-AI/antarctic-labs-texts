Efficacy and safety of sacubitril/valsartan in end-stage renal disease patients with heart failure: a review
Authors: Peiyun Li, Yupei Li, Ling Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeEnd-stage renal disease (ESRD) is associated with a heavy global public health burden and an increased risk of cardiovascular diseases. Sacubitril/valsartan has been shown to improve cardiovascular outcomes in patients with heart failure and delay the progression of chronic kidney disease. We aim to perform a comprehensive analysis of current clinical evidence on the use of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD patients.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science using keywords such as ‘sacubitril/valsartan’, ‘end-stage renal disease’, ‘hemodialysis’ and ‘heart failure’. The search deadline is December 20, 2024. In this review, we discuss the pharmacological mechanisms, pharmacokinetics, and latest clinical evidence of the administration of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD patients with heart failure.ResultsSacubitril/valsartan contains sacubitril and valsartan that can regulate the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system and natriuretic peptides simultaneously. For ESRD patients with heart failure, particularly those with reduced ejection fraction, sacubitril/valsartan demonstrated potential benefits including improved ventricular remodeling, enhanced left ventricular ejection fraction, and reduced mortality and heart failure re-hospitalization rates, while maintaining a favorable safety profile with no significant increased risk of hyperkalemia or hypotension. This review further discusses current challenges and future directions, including health economic evaluations and adverse event management strategies, to optimize clinical decision-making for the use of sacubitril/valsartan in this high-risk population.ConclusionSacubitril/valsartan may benefit ESRD patients with heart failure by improving ventricular remodeling and cardiac function while reducing mortality and hospitalization risks, without major safety concerns. However, larger studies are needed for confirmation.Keywords: Sacubitril/valsartan, end-stage renal disease, hemodialysis, heart failure

Introduction

End-stage renal disease (ESRD) is a highly prevalent condition that is associated with a heavy global public health burden. A recent cross-sectional study in mainland China revealed a weighted estimate of 1.48 million adults with ESRD in China [1]. Over 2.5 million individuals require renal replacement therapy worldwide, with projections indicating this number will nearly double to 5.4 million by 2030 [2]. Cardiovascular disease (CVD), including heart failure (HF), angina pectoris, myocardial infarction, and stroke, is the leading cause of death in ESRD patients. The cardiovascular mortality rate of dialysis patients is 10–20 times higher than that of age-matched individuals in the general population [3,4]. A meta-analysis involving 1,371,990 patients with non-dialysis-dependent chronic kidney disease (CKD) further showed that the risk of all-cause and cardiovascular mortality increased exponentially with the decline in renal function after adjusting for other established risk factors [5]. Moreover, HF and ESRD frequently coexist [6]. Approximately 36% of patients with ESRD who require dialysis have HF [7]. The prognosis of patients with both conditions is substantially worse than that of patients with either condition alone.

HF can be classified into HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), HF with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF), and HF with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF). Activation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) and the sympathetic nervous system, release of antidiuretic hormones, renal anemia, secondary hyperparathyroidism, underlying inflammation, and oxidative stress are pathological mechanisms that lead to the interplay between the kidneys and the heart [8]. From the PARADIGM-HF trial [9], robust evidence from large-scale clinical trials revealed that the use of sacubitril/valsartan, an angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitor, was associated with a significant reduction in both cardiovascular mortality and all-cause mortality in HF patients. Notably, in the PARADIGM-HF subgroup analysis [10], sacubitril/valsartan demonstrated significantly slower estimated glomerular filtration rate decline compared to enalapril (−1.61 vs. −2.04 mL/min/1.73 m2/year; p < 0.001), despite showing a modest increase in urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio. According to the guidelines of the American College of Cardiology and the Heart Failure Society of America, sacubitril/valsartan is a Class I recommended medication for patients with HFrEF [11]. However, HF patients with ESRD have been excluded from these landmark trials of sacubitril/valsartan, making it challenging for physicians to administer sacubitril/valsartan to HF patients who have ESRD or are on dialysis due to safety and tolerability concerns.

In 2022, a Chinese expert consensus recommended the use of sacubitril/valsartan in patients with CKD receiving non-dialysis and dialysis to manage HF [12]. In contrast, the latest Indian expert consensus highlighted that sacubitril/valsartan was not recommended for patients with severe renal dysfunction. However, for ESRD patients on dialysis, sacubitril/valsartan administration might have cardioprotective effects, including a reduction in all-cause mortality and HF hospitalization rate, and an increase in cardiac function [13]. During the past 5 years, a growing number of clinical studies have evaluated the efficacy and safety of sacubitril/valsartan in patients with ESRD, especially those receiving dialysis. As the incidence of sacubitril/valsartan on hyperkalemia in ESRD patients might be greater than that in individuals with early-stage CKD, we conducted an updated narrative review to assess the existing evidence for sacubitril/valsartan in this vulnerable population by discussing the pharmacological mechanisms, pharmacokinetics, latest clinical evidence, and current challenges regarding sacubitril/valsartan administration in ESRD patients.

Pharmacological mechanisms of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD patients with HF

Neprilysin, also known as enkephalinase (NEP), is a membrane-bound metalloproteinase primarily expressed in the brush border membrane of proximal renal tubular cells and is responsible for the degradation of natriuretic peptides (NPs) [14]. NPs are a group of polypeptides with a wide range of physiological effects, including potent diuretic and natriuretic effects, vasodilatory effects, inhibition of the RAAS and sympathetic nervous system, anti-proliferation, and anti-fibrotic actions. There are three types of NPs present in humans: atrial natriuretic peptide, brain natriuretic peptide (BNP), and C-type natriuretic peptide [15]. C-type natriuretic peptides are mainly expressed in endothelial cells, whereas atrial natriuretic peptide and BNP are synthesized in cardiac myocytes. Sacubitril/valsartan is a co-crystalline compound composed of sacubitril, an NEP inhibitor, and valsartan, an angiotensin receptor blocker [16]. On the one hand, sacubitril can effectively inhibit NEP, thereby inhibiting the degradation of NPs and enhancing the beneficial effects of NPs. On the other hand, valsartan can block the angiotensin II type 1 receptor, thus inhibiting RAAS (see Figure 1). Notably, sacubitril and valsartan work synergistically to play a role in vasodilation, prevention and reversal of cardiovascular remodeling, and promotion of natriuretic and diuretic effects. Moreover, sacubitril/valsartan has been shown to alleviate cardiac hypertrophy and aortic fibrosis in mouse models by reducing oxidative stress and inflammation [17]. Sacubitril/valsartan also inhibits inflammation and renal fibrosis, and attenuates angiotensin II-mediated renal cell collagen synthesis, thereby improving the progression of CKD in patients [18,19]. Furthermore, sacubitril/valsartan is related to the protective effect of NPs on podocytes, as evidenced by reduced glomerulosclerosis with the use of sacubitril/valsartan [20]. Another study indicated that sacubitril effectively inhibited angiogenesis through the VEGFR2/ERK1/2 and Notch1 pathways, which could be one of the mechanisms by which sacubitril/valsartan improved peritoneal transport function in peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients [21].

Pharmacological mechanisms of sacubitril/valsartan.

Unlike sacubitril/valsartan’s dual inhibition, conventional angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitor (ACEI) or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) only suppresses RAAS without affecting neprilysin. This key mechanistic difference results in significantly weaker natriuretic and diuretic effects, as ACEI/ARB cannot potentiate endogenous NPs or prevent their degradation. Consequently, these agents demonstrate reduced capacity for volume control, frequently require higher diuretic doses, and show diminished long-term efficacy due to aldosterone escape - limitations that are clinically relevant in managing fluid-overloaded HF patients.

Pharmacokinetics of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD patients with HF

After oral administration, sacubitril/valsartan is rapidly absorbed and dissociates into sacubitril, which quickly converts to sacubitrilat (the active metabolite of the NEP inhibitor LBQ657) and valsartan [22]. Valsartan is primarily excreted through the biliary tract [23], and renal impairment does not affect its pharmacokinetics. In contrast, sacubitrilat is mainly excreted through the kidneys [24], with less than 2% of the total administered dose excreted in urine and feces [25], indicating that its exposure increases with a decline in renal function. A pharmacokinetic study demonstrated that renal dysfunction increased exposure to sacubitrilat, but did not affect sacubitril or valsartan exposure. LBQ657 exposure increased by 2.29-fold, 2.90-fold, and 3.27-fold in patients with moderate, severe, and non-dialysis ESRD, respectively. Besides, the half-life of LBQ657 was prolonged from 12 h to 21.1, 23.7, and 38.5 h in patients with mild, moderate, and severe renal impairment, respectively [26]. Feng et al. reported that hemodialysis did not eliminate LBQ657 or valsartan in hemodialysis patients with HF. However, administration of sacubitril/valsartan at a dose of 100 mg twice daily was safe and effective in patients undergoing hemodialysis [27]. Another study suggested that PD and residual renal function contributed only to a minor degree to the elimination of LBQ657. Additionally, a dose of 100 mg sacubitril/valsartan twice daily is safe and effective in patients with PD and complications of hypertension or HF [28]. For patients receiving maintenance dialysis, the Chinese expert consensus recommends initiating sacubitril/valsartan therapy at a low dose of 25–50 mg once daily [12]. Clinicians should closely monitor blood pressure throughout treatment and gradually titrate the dosage up to 100–150 mg once or twice daily based on individual patient tolerance.

Efficacy and safety of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD patients with HF

Patients with CKD may benefit from the use of sacubitril/valsartan. Several observational studies and meta-analyses have collectively shown that sacubitril/valsartan reduces the risk of cardiovascular death or HF hospitalization (HFH) and prevents the worsening of renal function in CKD patients with HF without increasing the risk of adverse effects such as hyperkalemia and hypotension [29–33]. A retrospective cohort study of 1,276 HFrEF patients with CKD demonstrated significantly higher all-cause mortality in the ACEI/ARB group compared to the sacubitril/valsartan group (14.89% vs. 10.50%; HR 1.46; 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.06–2.00; p = 0.02) [32]. No between-group differences were observed in composite renal outcomes (urgent dialysis initiation or renal death), HFH rates, or cardiovascular mortality. Notably, the dialysis-dependent ESRD subpopulation was balanced between groups (sacubitril/valsartan: 58.6% [374/638] vs. ACEI/ARB: 59.4% [379/638]). Another randomized controlled trial and meta-analysis further demonstrated that sacubitril/valsartan had the additional effect of lowering blood pressure and cardiac biomarkers in CKD patients [34,35].

However, the efficacy and safety of sacubitril/valsartan administration in patients with ESRD and HF remains controversial. For example, Lin et al. and Liu et al. demonstrated that the use of sacubitril/valsartan significantly reduced all-cause mortality and HF readmission in dialysis patients with HF, with no significant side effects [36,37]. The change in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) in the sacubitril/valsartan group (14.51 ± vs. 6.91 ± 18.4, p = 0.0408) was significantly greater than that in the candesartan or valsartan group after a 3-year follow up [37]. Another meta-analysis including 2494 patients with HF and ESRD also suggested that sacubitril/valsartan improved LVEF (mean difference [MD]: 4.03%; 95% CI 2.90–5.16), especially patients in HFrEF [38]. A retrospective Korean study involving 23 HFrEF patients on dialysis demonstrated that sacubitril/valsartan treatment (123 ± 62 mg per day) significantly reduced high-sensitive troponin T levels from 236.2 ± 355.3 to 97.0 ± 14.0 pg/mL (p = 0.002) and improved LVEF from 29.7 ± 4.4% to 40.8 ± 10.4% (p = 0.002). Symptomatic hypotension (systolic blood pressure <100 mmHg) occurred in four patients, one of whom experienced dizziness [39]. However, none required discontinuation of sacubitril/valsartan therapy. In contrast, another subgroup analysis from multiple centers showed that in dialysis patients with HFrEF, sacubitril/valsartan treatment increased the short-term frequency of HFH rates [40]. In the next section, we summarize the latest clinical evidence regarding the use of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD patients undergoing hemodialysis or PD, respectively.

ESRD patients on hemodialysis

Sacubitril/valsartan can significantly improve cardiac function and structure in hemodialysis patients with HF, especially in those with HFrEF or HFmrEF. A case report described a hemodialysis woman with HFrEF who was treated with a combination of sacubitril/valsartan and dapagliflozin [41]. After a 10-month follow-up, her cardiac structure and function returned to normal, and there was a consistent improvement in cardiac biomarkers and exercise performance. Wang et al. included 110 hemodialysis patients with HFrEF [42], and reported that after the use of sacubitril/valsartan for 12 months, there was a significant improvement in the LVEF (35.1 vs. 49.8%, p < 0.001), the severity of New York Heart Association (NYHA) symptoms, mean N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and cardiac structure parameters, such as the left ventricular end-diastolic diameter, left ventricular mass index, left ventricular end-systolic diameter, and left atrial diameter (167.8 vs. 154.9 g/m, p < 0.001; 52.2 vs. 51.5 mm, p < 0.05; 35.9 vs. 36.9 mm, p < 0.001; 42.2 vs. 40.3 mm, p < 0.001). Similarly, another retrospective study involving 11 hemodialysis patients with HFrEF or HFmrEF demonstrated that sacubitril/valsartan significantly improved LVEF in these patients (p < 0.05) [27]. Wen et al. retrospectively included 54 patients and demonstrated that sacubitril/valsartan administration (50–200mg, twice a day) significantly improved cardiac structure and function, reduced HHF, and effectively lowered blood pressure [43]. A multicenter study enrolling 89 HFrEF patients undergoing hemodialysis (42 cases in the sacubitril/valsartan group and 47 cases in the ACEI/ARB group) also demonstrated that there were significant differences in LVEF(29.0 ± 7.7% vs. 37.6 ± 9.5%, p < 0.01) between the two groups after 1 year of treatment [44]. An Israeli study involving 12 hemodialysis patients with HFrEF treated with sacubitril/valsartan (100–400 mg per day) demonstrated gradual improvement in LVEF at 6-month follow-up, though no significant differences were observed in NT-proBNP levels [45]. Recently, a meta-analysis involving 799 hemodialysis patients showed that compared with conventional treatment, sacubitril/valsartan significantly improved the LVEF in patients with HFrEF and HFmrEF (MD 6.58%; 95% CI: 1.86–11.29) [46]. In terms of safety, there was no difference in the incidence of hyperkalemia or hypotension, and no cases of angioedema were reported.

Sacubitril/valsartan may also decrease mortality and hospitalization rates in patients on hemodialysis. Recently, a large study enrolling 2868 hemodialysis patients with HFrEF indicated that, compared with ACEI/ARB, sacubitril/valsartan treatment was associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality (hazard ratio [HR], 0.82 [95% CI, 0.73–0.92]) and all-cause hospitalization (HR, 0.86 [95% CI, 0.79–0.93]), but not with cardiovascular mortality (HR, 1.01 [95% CI, 0.86–1.19]) or HHF (HR, 0.91 [95% CI, 0.82–1.02]). In addition, the risk of hyperkalemia was reduced with the use of sacubitril/valsartan (HR, 0.71 [95% CI, 0.62–0.81]) [47]. In contrast, another study suggested that the primary composite rate of HHF or mortality was not significantly different between the sacubitril/valsartan group (20.6 events vs. 26.1 per 100 patient-years, HR 0.98, 95% CI: 0.28–3.43, p = 0.97) and the ACEI/ARB group [44].

However, the cardiorenal benefits of sacubitril/valsartan in patients with HFpEF on dialysis are controversial. Guo et al. studied 247 patients with HFpEF and found that sacubitril/valsartan administration (50–200 mg, twice a day) was associated with a significant reduction in blood pressure, heart rate, NT-proBNP, and cardiac troponin I. Improvements in cardiac structure and NYHA functional classification were also observed after 3–12 months of follow-up [48]. Similarly, Huang et al. indicated that sacubitril/valsartan (25–100 mg, twice a day) improved left ventricular hypertrophy, diastolic function, and the ADQI class in HFpEF patients undergoing hemodialysis even though first and recurrent HFH or cardiovascular death were not significantly different [49]. In contrast, Mima et al. showed that sacubitril/valsartan could only lower blood pressure and heart rate insignificantly but had no beneficial effect on BNP and LEVF levels [50]. A meta-analysis further suggested that there was no statistically significant effect on LVEF (MD 2.6%; 95% CI: 1.15–6.35) in HFpEF patients [46]. Notably, no drug-related adverse reactions were observed in any of the studies.

ESRD patients on PD

Sacubitril/valsartan may significantly improve cardiac structure and function in patients with PD and HFpEF. A retrospective study included 64 patients with HFpEF undergoing PD, 31 of whom used sacubitril/valsartan and the rest used valsartan. The reduction range of NT-proBNP, left ventricular end-systolic dimension, left ventricular fractional shortening, and increase in LVEF were all superior to those of the control group (p < 0.05) after sacubitril/valsartan treatment [51]. Similarly, Sheng et al. included 160 patients with HFpEF undergoing PD and randomly assigned them to the control group or the sacubitril/valsartan group [52]. After six months of treatment, patients in the sacubitril/valsartan group showed superior cardiac function and HF scores compared to those in the control group. Fu et al. included 21 PD patients with sacubitril/valsartan with HFpEF and showed that NT-proBNP levels [9769.0 (3093.5–21941.0) vs. 3034.0 (1493.2–6503.0), p = 0.002] and heart rates of patients significantly decreased after sacubitril/valsartan administration. The signs and symptoms of HF were markedly alleviated [53].

In the context of peritoneal ultrafiltration, Zhang et al. indicated that after treatment with sacubitril/valsartan, 30 patients (63.8%) experienced a significant increase in peritoneal ultrafiltration [up to 150.4 (110.7, 232.1) mL per day], whereas the remaining 17 cases (36.2%) experienced a slight decrease. Within 7 days of sacubitril/valsartan treatment, the overall PUF increased by 66.4 (21.4, 123.2) mL/24 h, which was significantly greater than that before treatment (p = 0.004). There was also a significant improvement in the total output, blood pressure, and body weight [54]. Existing barriers and future perspective

Despite recent advances in the use of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD patients have greatly shaped our current clinical practice, there are several existing barriers that need to be addressed in the near future to offer valuable insights for clinicians.

First, the diagnosis of HF and CKD requires integration of characteristic clinical manifestations with documented structural or functional cardiac and renal abnormalities. While BNP and NT-proBNP serve as recommended initial diagnostic biomarkers for suspected HF, NT-proBNP exhibits particularly limited diagnostic reliability in ESRD patients due to substantial interindividual variability stemming from multiple confounding factors - including hydration status, residual renal function, and dialysis modalities - that influence its clearance and preclude establishment of validated diagnostic thresholds. Consequently, these diagnostic challenges contribute to the suboptimal implementation of sacubitril/valsartan therapy in ESRD patients with concomitant HF [55].

Second, sodium restriction and fluid volume control constitute the cornerstone of cardiovascular protection in CKD and dialysis patients [56], with pharmacological interventions providing additional therapeutic benefits. Current research has largely neglected key parameters like sodium intake and volume control, with most trials lacking detailed ultrafiltration data. These variations (e.g. sodium restriction and fluid volume control) in fluid management may significantly confound therapeutic outcome comparisons. Also, the variability in sacubitril-valsartan dosing regimens among various studies could introduce substantial heterogeneity, which might affect the comparability of therapeutic outcomes across trials.

Third, although sacubitril/valsartan is generally safe, potential adverse events may occur during treatment. Specifically, if hypotension occurs during sacubitril/valsartan treatment, initial measures should include adjusting diuretics and concomitant antihypertensive medications before addressing other potential causes; if hypotension persists, dose reduction or temporary discontinuation of sacubitril/valsartan may be considered [12]. For hyperkalemia, modifiable risk factors should be mitigated (e.g. discontinuing potassium supplements), with potassium-lowering agents administered as needed, and sacubitril/valsartan dose adjustment or cessation considered if warranted. Close serum potassium monitoring is essential, and sacubitril/valsartan may be reintroduced once levels normalize (<5.0 mmol/L). A 3-year follow-up study demonstrated that ESRD patients with HF could tolerate sacubitril/valsartan well [37]. However, larger studies with longer follow-up durations are required to further validate the long-term safety profile of sacubitril/valsartan in this population.

Fourth, the quality of clinical studies in this field is still unsatisfactory. The majority of existing studies are single-center retrospective investigations with relatively small sample sizes, which inevitably introduces selection bias and limits the generalizability of findings. To establish robust clinical guidelines, large-scale, multicenter randomized controlled trials with standardized protocols are urgently needed to comprehensively evaluate long-term efficacy and safety profiles of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD patients with HF. Meanwhile, current clinical evidence is inadequate to establish modality-specific dosing of sacubitril/valsartan in dialysis patients, necessitating dedicated pharmacokinetic studies.

Fifth, whether the administration of sacubitril/valsartan in dialysis patients with HF will result in a healthcare economic benefit by reducing HFH rates remain unknown. An American study of 13,755 matched pairs of Medicare patients with HF found that the mean annual total healthcare costs per patient were comparable between sacubitril-valsartan group and ACEI/ARB group (mean difference: $701; 95% CI: −$132 to $1593) [57]. However, cost structure analysis revealed distinct patterns: sacubitril-valsartan initiators had significantly higher prescription drug costs (mean difference: $1,911; 95% CI: $1704–$2113) but experienced lower inpatient costs (mean difference: -$790; 95% CI: −$1468 to −$72) and slightly reduced outpatient costs (mean difference: −$330; 95% CI: −$664 to −$11). Based on another large-scale pharmacoeconomic modeling study of 13,264 U.S. patients [58], sacubitril-valsartan demonstrated superior cost-effectiveness compared to renin-angiotensin system inhibitors, with favorable incremental cost-effectiveness ratios particularly in HFrEF and HFmEF patients. The economic evaluation further revealed that sacubitril-valsartan therapy maintained at least intermediate cost-effectiveness at current undiscounted wholesale acquisition costs even for patients with HFpEF. However, a specific economic evaluation on the use of sacubitril-valsartan in patients with coexisting dialysis and HF is still required.

Conclusion

The high incidence and mortality of ESRD and HF remain a heavy healthcare burden. Sacubitril/valsartan can block the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system with valsartan and inhibit neprilysin to enhance the activity of natriuretic peptides, leading to vasodilation, natriuresis, and diuresis. Current clinical evidence suggests that the use of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD is safe without increasing the risk of hyperkalemia. In previous studies, the use of sacubitril/valsartan in ESRD has been shown to be safe. In terms of efficacy, Sacubitril/valsartan may have good effects on improving clinical outcomes in ESRD patients with HF, such as improving ventricular remodeling, increasing left ventricular ejection fraction, and reducing mortality and hospitalization rates.
