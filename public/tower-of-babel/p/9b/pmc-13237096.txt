Metagenome-assembled genomes, and gene and protein catalogues from the global wild boar faecal microbiome
Authors: Yu Chyuan Heng, Sumit Singh Dagar, Katerina Fliegerova, Giuseppe Moniello, Wakako Ikeda-Ohtsubo, Kei Okuda, Sandra Kittelmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Prophylactic antibiotic use in pig farming has contributed to the rise of antimicrobial resistance, spurring interest in probiotics to enhance pig gut health and immunity. Wild relatives of domestic pigs may harbour beneficial microbes, yet their gut microbiomes remain underexplored. In this study, we reconstructed 3,288 metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from 89 wild boar faecal samples collected across four countries, all meeting at least MIMAG medium-quality standard (≥50% completeness, <10% contamination). These MAGs represented 968 distinct species, including 956 bacterial species from 113 families and 419 genera, and 12 archaeal species from 2 families and 7 genera, with half classified as novel. In addition, we also constructed catalogues of genes and proteins from the wild boar faecal metagenomes. Notably, most species (58%), genes and proteins (85%) identified in the wild boar faecal microbiomes were absent from equivalent catalogues of domestic pigs. Our catalogues highlight wild boars as a reservoir of previously untapped microbial resources for microbiome research and the exploration of biotechnological applications including probiotics.

Background & Summary

Pork is a major source of animal protein worldwide. To sustain high productivity, domestic pigs are often raised under stressful and intensive farming conditions, which commonly necessitates the routine use of antibiotics as a prophylactic measure to prevent disease outbreaks and to improve productivity1,2. However, the overuse of antibiotics has been increasingly regulated or banned in many countries due to their roles in driving the emergence and spread of antimicrobial resistance, posing serious risks to both animal and human health3. As a result, probiotics have been proposed as an alternative to reduce antibiotic reliance by enhancing gut health and immunity in pigs4,5. Probiotics are live microorganisms that confer health benefits to the host and are generally believed to be more effective when derived from the gut of the same or a closely related host species4,6,7. In this context, several studies have isolated microbes and reconstructed metagenome-assembled genomes (MAGs) from various regions of the gastrointestinal tract and faeces of pigs7–10, including an expanded catalogue of microbial genes and MAGs by Chen et al.11. However, most of these earlier efforts have focused on domestic pigs, with limited representation of their wild counterparts. Notably, wild animals may harbour microbial species that have co-evolved with the host over millions of years and that may have gone missing from domesticated populations, with potential implications for host health and resilience7,12. Therefore, the wild relative of domestic pigs, the wild boar (Sus scrofa), represents a largely untapped and rich resource for probiotic discovery. Yet, a comprehensive, systematic assessment of the wild boar gut microbiome, including the recovery of MAGs, is still lacking.

In this study, we performed metagenomic sequencing on 89 faecal samples collected from wild boars belonging to the four recognized Sus scrofa subspecies groups, spanning seven geographic regions across four countries in Asia and Europe13 (Table 1 and Fig. 1). A custom bioinformatics workflow was developed to reconstruct MAGs, incorporating multiple quality control steps to optimize genome bin quality (Fig. 1a). This included integrating and refining bins produced by three independent binners into a non-redundant, optimized bin set, followed by decontamination using two complementary tools. Although these steps reduced the total number and cumulative size of genome bins (Fig. 1c,e) and, in some cases, slightly compromised completeness, they significantly lowered contamination levels (Fig. 1g). For instance, 68 genome bins initially classified as MIMAG unclassified (UC) were recovered to either high-quality (HQ) or medium-quality (MQ). Notably, while the total number of downgraded bins (n = 817) exceeded those that were upgraded (n = 100), the overall number of HQ and MQ genome bins (hereafter referred to as MAGs) increased from 3,273 to 3,288 after decontamination (Fig. 1h). Following dereplication of these MAGs at a threshold of 95% average nucleotide identity (ANI), 968 clusters were obtained, each representing a distinct species. The 968 SGBs comprised 385 HQ and 583 MQ MAGs (Fig. 2a), with HQ MAGs generally exhibiting larger genome sizes (Fig. 2b). In addition to meeting a minimal MQ standard, 80% of these SGBs also fulfilled at least one other recommended genome quality criterion (Fig. 2c). Taxonomic classification using GTDB-Tk assigned 956 SGBs to bacterial species spanning 22 phyla, 27 classes, 59 orders, 113 families, and 419 genera, while the remaining 12 SGBs were archaeal species distributed across 2 phyla, 2 classes, 2 orders, 2 families, and 7 genera (Fig. 2d). The most abundant bacterial family was Lachnospiraceae (number of SGBs, n = 117), followed by Acutalibacteraceae (n = 89), Ruminococcaceae (n = 78), Bacteroidaceae (n = 64), and Oscillospiraceae (n = 50; Fig. 2e). Archaeal species were predominantly from Methanobacteriaceae (n = 8) and Methanomethylophilaceae (n = 4). Comparison with 2,777 SGBs from Chen et al.11 revealed that 404 SGBs represented species shared with pigs (≥ 95% ANI), while the remaining 564 were unique to wild boar (< 95% ANI; Fig. 2f). Despite the comprehensive recovery of MAGs, only a limited fraction (on average ~11.7%) of the total sequence information present in the metagenomes was captured within MAGs (Fig. 1c,d), underscoring the importance of directly cataloguing the full metagenomic content. To this end, a custom bioinformatics workflow was developed to construct gene and protein catalogues from the wild boar faecal microbiome (Fig. 3a), including a quality control step that filtered potentially contaminated proteins originating from humans and pigs (Fig. 3b). The resulting “clean” proteins from all 89 metagenomes were consolidated to generate the wild boar protein catalogue (WBPC), which contained at least 8–10 times as many proteins as those recoverable from all genome bins, MAGs, and SGBs, combined (Fig. 3d). Proteins within the WBPC were subsequently clustered and dereplicated at 100%, 90%, and 50% sequence similarity thresholds to produce three nested catalogues: WBPC100, WBPC90, and WBPC50 (Fig. 3e). Notably, 85.8% proteins within WBPC50 shared less than 50% sequence similarity with proteins from the nested pig integrated gene catalogue, PIGC50, by Chen et al.11 (Fig. 3f). These represented wild boar-specific proteins hereafter referred to as uWBPC50. Among these, 98.4% were ≤ 500 amino acids in length (Fig. 3g), while 92.6% were annotated as hypothetical proteins with unknown functions (Fig. 3h). Our catalogues of MAGs, genes and proteins from the wild boar faecal microbiome represent a significant resource not only for studying microbial diversity, community structure, and function in wild boars, but also for comparative analyses with other species of Sus, including domestic pigs. Importantly, our study reveals that a substantial proportion of these MAGs, genes, and proteins were previously uncharacterized, representing a vast, untapped microbial resource with diverse potential applications. While the availability of MAGs enables in silico exploration of the functional potential of the resident strains, particularly with regard to probiotic applications, it also facilitates the development of targeted strategies for their isolation and subsequent phenotypic characterization14–16. Additionally, the unique genes and proteins identified represent valuable biological parts with potentially novel functions, which can be cloned into heterologous hosts for various biotechnological applications17,18.

Methods

Sample collection and metagenomic sequencing

89 fresh faecal samples were opportunistically and non-invasively collected from wild boars across seven regions in four countries13 (Table 1) using sterile spoons and tubes and subsequently stored at −80 °C until further processing. Total nucleic acids were extracted from the inner part of 250 mg subsamples using the QIAamp PowerFecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen) following manufacturer’s instructions, after which they were digested with RNAse to remove RNA. The wild boar origin of each sample was confirmed by PCR amplification of the host-specific mitochondrial COX1 gene using the COI-F1 and COI-R1 primer pairs19, followed by Sanger sequencing (Axil Scientific, Singapore). PCR-free metagenomic libraries were then constructed from the extracted genomic DNA (using 250 bp inserts) and subjected to paired-end sequencing on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 system with PE150 chemistry (NovogeneAIT Genomics Singapore). Approximately 5.55–18.09 Gb of raw sequence data were generated per sample.

Metagenomic assembly and binning

The raw metagenomic reads were quality-trimmed and filtered using Trimmomatic v0.39 (parameters: ILLUMINACLIP:TruSeq3-PE-2.fa:2:30:10 LEADING:3 TRAILING:3 SLIDINGWINDOW:4:15 MINLEN:36)20. The resulting clean reads (3.95–16.27 Gb; Fig. 1b) were assembled individually by sample into metagenomes using metaSPAdes assembler v3.15.3 with default parameters21. Contigs longer than 1,500 bp in each metagenome were binned based on depth-of-coverage, nucleotide composition, marker genes, and/or source strain abundance using CONCOCT v1.122, MetaBAT2 v1.723, and MaxBin2 v2.2.424 with default parameters. The three candidate bin sets generated were further refined into a non-redundant, optimized bin set using DAS Tool v1.1.2 (parameters: --score_threshold 0.5 --duplicate_penalty 0.6 --megabin_penalty 0.5)25. The refined bins generated were decontaminated based on DNA G+C content, coverage, and tetranucleotide signatures using RefineM v0.1.2 (modules: scaffold_stats, outliers, and then filter_bins)26 and based on marker genes using MAGpurify v2.1.2 (modules: phylo-markers, clade-markers, known-contam and then clean-bin)27. CheckM v1.2.2 (mode: lineage_wf)28 was then applied to filter genome bins based on MIMAG quality standard29. Low-quality bins (LQ; < 50% completeness, < 10% contamination) and unclassified bins (UC; ≥ 10% contamination) were discarded. The remaining bins, which were of MIMAG high-quality (HQ; > 90% completeness, < 5% contamination) and medium-quality (MQ; ≥ 50% completeness, < 10% contamination), were regarded as MAGs, and were dereplicated at a threshold of 95% ANI using dRep v3.4.3 (module: dereplicate)30. Representative SGBs were selected among MAGs that shared ≥ 95% ANI based on overall genome quality. Priority was given to MAGs with the highest MIMAG quality standard, and in the case of multiple MAGs shared the same highest MIMAG quality standard, priority was given to MAGs fulfilling as many of the following recommended criteria as possible: < 100 contigs, N50 > 25 kbp, the largest contig > 100 kbp, read coverage ≥ 10 ×, presence of 16S rRNA, and presence of > 80% tRNA types (for the first 20 amino acids)31. QUAST v5.0.232 was used to assess basic genome statistics including sizes, number of contigs, N50, and the largest contig. MAGs were annotated with Prokka v1.14.6 using the “metagenome” mode33 to identify 16S rRNA and tRNA genes. Bowtie2 v2.3.2 (parameters: --phred33 --end-to-end --trim5 0 --trim3 0 --np 1 --minins 0 --maxins 500 -a)34 was used to align clean reads to individual metagenomes. The BAM files generated were used in conjunction with CoverM v0.6.135 to infer percentage of mapped reads (parameters: genome -m relative abundance) and read coverage (parameters: genome -m mean). Data analyses using metaSPAdes, CONCOCT, MetaBAT2, MaxBin2, and DAS Tool were conducted through DOE Systems Biology Knowledgebase (KBase) platform36.

Taxonomic classification

GTDB-Tk v2.4.0 (database release 220)37 was used for taxonomic classification of SGBs. SGBs with a reference genome available in GTDB were regarded as known species, while those without a reference genome were considered as novel species.

Construction of wild boar gene and protein catalogues

All 89 metagenomes were annotated by Prokka v1.14.6 using the “metagenome” mode33. The resulting protein collections were decontaminated individually by sample to remove proteins sharing more than 50% sequence similarity with proteins from the human (GenBank: GCF_000001405) and the pig (GenBank: GCF_000003025) reference genomes, using CD-HIT-2D v4.8.1 (parameters: -c 0.5 -n 3 -d 100 -s 0.8 -A 0.8)38. Each clean protein collection was then clustered at 100%, 90%, and 50% protein sequence similarity thresholds (Fig. 3c) using CD-HIT v4.8.138. WBPC (57,102,524 proteins) was generated by consolidating proteins from all 89 clean protein collections. WBPC100 (39,241,656 proteins), WBPC90 (23,542,230 proteins), and WBPC50 (12,120,321 proteins) were generated by dereplicating WBPC at 100%, 90%, and 50% protein sequence similarity thresholds using CD-HIT v4.8.138. The equivalent gene catalogues, WBGC, WBGC100, WBGC90, and WBGC50 were generated by extracting the corresponding genes from the metagenomes.

Comparison to pig gut microbiome

The expanded catalogues of genes (PIGC) and MAGs from the pig gut microbiome were downloaded from the China National GeneBank DataBase (CNGBdb: CNP0000824). The 6,339 MAGs from the pig gut microbiome were first dereplicated at 95% ANI threshold using the “dereplicate” module of dRep v3.4.3, yielding 2,777 SGBs. This count was slightly higher than the 2,673 SGBs reported by Chen et al., who used the “cluster” module of dRep v2.2.311. Comparisons of SGBs and proteins from wild boar and pig microbiomes were performed using dRep v3.4.3 (at 95% ANI threshold)30 and CD-HIT-2D v4.8.1 (at 50% protein sequence similarity threshold)38, respectively.

Data Records

The raw metagenomic sequence data from the 89 wild boar faecal samples have been deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive under accession SRP53178039. The 968 SGBs are available in NCBI GenBank under accession PRJNA115895940. The SGBs have also been deposited in Zenodo, together with all MAGs, the wild boar protein catalogues (WBPC, WBPC100, WBPC90, WBPC50, and uWBPC50), and the corresponding gene catalogues (WBGC, WBGC100, WBGC90, WBGC50, and uWBGC50), under 10.5281/zenodo.1640192041. Additional information on faecal samples, metagenomes, MAGs and SGBs, including their BioSample, SRA, WGS, and GenBank accession numbers, sequencing and assembly statistics, and GTDB-Tk taxonomy classifications, is available under the same Zenodo DOI in the file “Metadata_wbfm”41.

Technical Validation

The wild boar origin of the collected faecal sample was confirmed by detecting the wild boar mitochondrial COX1 gene in the extracted genomic DNA. To minimize biases and enhance the reliability of the binning results, three binning algorithms (CONCOCT, MetaBAT2, and MaxBin2) were applied, in combination with the DAS Tool binning optimizer (Fig. 1a). Coupled with two decontamination steps using RefineM and MAGpurify, and the removal of poor-quality genome bins with CheckM, this pipeline ensures the optimal recovery of near-complete, clean MAGs from the wild boar faecal samples. Additionally, to maximise the recovery of proteins truly representing the wild boar faecal microbiome, protein collections reconstructed from individual samples were decontaminated by removing sequences sharing more than 50% similarity with proteins from the human and the pig reference genomes (Fig. 3b).
