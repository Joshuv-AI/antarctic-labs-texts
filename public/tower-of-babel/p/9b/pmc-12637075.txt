Overcoming chemoresistance via NO-mediated HSP inhibition using hybrid membrane-coated multifunctional nanoplatforms
Authors: Maierhaba Aili, Fangrui Lin, Yu Chen, Nuernisha Alifu, Rong Ma, Chenyang Chu, Jiabao Xiong, Yaqi Cui, Zhong Du, Cailing Ma, Biao Dong, Junle Qu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chemotherapy resistance and the precise delivery as well as controlled release of drugs at tumor sites remain major challenges in cancer treatment. Heat shock proteins (HSPs) have been identified as key contributors to chemotherapy resistance, yet effective strategies to overcome this issue are still lacking. In this study, we developed a hybrid biomimetic nanoparticle system (CuS-PTX-ICG-L-arg@HR) that integrates photothermal therapy (PTT), chemotherapy, and gas therapy to enhance the efficiency of cervical cancer treatment. By fusing the red blood cell membranes with HeLa cell membranes, the nanoparticles combined immune evasion capabilities with tumortargeting properties, and further improved circulation time and tumor accumulation. To address chemotherapy resistance, we leveraged a novel light-activated nitric oxide (NO) generation strategy, where L-arginine (L-arg) was introduced as a NO precursor to inhibit HSPs expression under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first attempt to overcome chemotherapy resistance through HSPs inhibition via NO generation, significantly enhancing the efficacy of paclitaxel (PTX) in drug-resistant tumors. Furthermore, the plasmonic properties of hollow copper sulfide (CuS) nanoparticles enable light-triggered drug release and localized therapeutic effects, ensuring precise tumor targeting and reducing off-target toxicity. This multifunctional nanoplatform offers a promising strategy for improving drug delivery, reversing chemotherapy resistance and advancing precision cancer therapy.

Introduction

Cervical cancer (CC) as the fourth most prevalent cause of cancer-related morbidity in women worldwide [1,2]. For inoperable patients, chemotherapy remains the standard treatment [3], yet its efficacy is limited due to pervasive chemotherapy resistance [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Heat shock proteins (HSPs) [9] have been implicated in chemotherapy resistance across multiple cancer types, including lung, ovarian, liver, and breast cancers [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Although HSPs play crucial roles in maintaining normal cellular homeostasis, their aberrant overexpression in tumor cells contributes to cancer progression by stabilizing misfolded proteins and suppressing apoptosis [14,15]. Notably, HSP70 and HSP90 are upregulated in response to stressors such as hyperthermia during photothermal therapy (PTT), and their inhibition has been shown to enhance PTT efficacy [[16], [17], [18]]. In the context of chemotherapy, while direct mechanistic evidence remains limited, emerging studies suggest that targeting HSPs may improve treatment outcomes [19,20]. For instance, HSP90 inhibitors have been reported to induce apoptosis in melanoma cells, and Dilay Karademir et al. [21]. demonstrated that the heat shock inhibitor J2 effectively downregulates HSP27, sensitizing ovarian cancer cells to apoptosis [[21], [22], [23]]. These findings highlight the therapeutic potential of HSP inhibition in overcoming chemoresistance. However, despite these promising observations, no clinically viable strategy has yet been established to specifically target HSPs for reversing chemotherapy resistance. A key challenge lies in achieving precise spatiotemporal control and tumor-selective inhibition of HSPs, which is critical for minimizing off-target effects and maximizing therapeutic efficacy [23,24].

Gas therapy [25], particularly utilizing nitric oxide (NO) [26], has emerged as a promising strategy to combat HSP-mediated therapy resistance [[27], [28], [29]]. As a versatile signaling molecule, NO modulates multiple intracellular pathways [30,31], effectively suppressing HSP expression [32]. This mechanism not only enhances tumor cell sensitivity to PTT [33] by promoting apoptosis and regulating oxidative stress but may also improve chemotherapeutic efficacy when NO is co-administered [34,35]. However, the clinical translation of NO-based therapies faces significant challenges, primarily due to the poor stability and limited controllability of gaseous molecules in vivo. To address these limitations, integrating NO with advanced nano-delivery systems [36] has become a critical research focus, enabling precise spatiotemporal release and targeted delivery to tumor sites.

In this study, we engineered a novel type of hybrid biomimetic nanoplatform by coating hollow CuS nanoparticles (NPs) with hybrid cell membranes to achieve enhanced tumor targeting and prolonged blood circulation (see Scheme 1). This innovative system simultaneously delivers indocyanine green (ICG) as a photosensitizer, L-arginine (L-arg) as a NO precursor, and paclitaxel (PTX) as a chemotherapeutic agent, creating a synergistic combination of PTT and chemotherapy sensitization. ICG is an NIR fluorescent dye approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), which shows a high strong NIR absorbance and simultaneously achieves PTT/Photodynamic therapy (PDT) and imaging capability under 808 nm laser irradiation. Nevertheless, the intravenous administration of free ICG was subjected to several shortcomings such as poor bioenvironmental stability, aggregate propensity, and rapid elimination from the body, etc [[37], [38], [39], [40]]. To enhance the stability and circulation time of ICG, it is often encapsulated in nanocarriers such as liposomes [41], polymers [40,41], or inorganic nanoparticles [42]. CuS NPs [[43], [44], [45]] stand out as an excellent carrier candidate for photothermal therapy due to their straightforward synthesis, high near-infrared absorption, and remarkable photostability [46]. Under near-infrared (NIR) irradiation, CuS NPs generate localized hyperthermia that triggers PTX release while simultaneously activating ICG to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS), which then convert L-arg into NO, effectively suppressing HSP expression. The downregulation of HSP overcomes thermoresistance and simultaneously enhances the sensitivity of cancer cells to both thermal and chemotherapeutic effects. Materials and methods

Materials

Poly(vinylpyrrolidone) (PVP-K30), CuCl2, Na2S, and anhydrous hydrazine were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (Shanghai, China). PBS (99 % purity) was acquired from Shanghai Hyclone (China). Solarbio Life Science (China) provided membrane protein extraction kits, BCA protein assay kits, Hoechst 33342, DiD/DiI/DiO, Calcein-AM, and PI. TUNEL kits were sourced from Servicebio (China). Antibodies included: HSP70 Rabbit mAb (ABclonal, A23457; 1:10,000).

Instruments

CuS-PIA@HR NPs were characterized as follows: size distribution and zeta-potential: Marvin ZS90 analyzerNanostructure. Morphology: Bright-field TEM (FEI TF20; JEOL JEM-1230). Chemical composition: FTIR spectroscopy (Nicolet iS20). Optical properties: UV–Vis absorption (Shimadzu UV-2700), Fluorescence spectra (Shimadzu RF-5301 PC; emission band: 220–900 nm). Biofunctionality: NIR fluorescence imaging (Zeiss LSM 900/Airyscan 2), Photodynamic & photothermal quantification (BD LSR II flow cytometry), In vivo biodistribution (BLT imaging system). The content of copper element in the sample was determined using an inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer. The instrument model is Agilent 7800 from Agilent Technologies in the United States.

Animal model

Female Balb/c nude mice were used to establish a CC model by subcutaneous injection of HeLa cells (1 × 106 cells per mouse). All animal procedures strictly adhered to current ethical guidelines and the National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines, and were approved by the Animal Experiment Ethics Committee (IACUC-20220308-55) and the Animal Care and Use Committee (20191113-08) of Xinjiang Medical University.

Preparation of CuS NPs

CuS NPs were synthesized according to a previously reported method [47]. Briefly, an aqueous solution of CuCl2 and polyvinylpyrrolidone K30 (PVP-K30, 240 mg) were added to deionized water (DIW), under magnetic stirring at room temperature. Subsequently, a NaOH solution was introduced, followed by the addition of hydrazine hydrate (N2H4·H2O, 6.4 μL) after 2 min. After a further 5 min, Na2S solution (200 μL, 320 mg mL−1) was added. The reaction mixture was then maintained at a constant temperature of 75 °C for 2 h to facilitate CuS formation. The resulting CuS were collected by centrifugation (10,000 rpm, 10 min), washed with DIW, and resuspended for subsequent use [35].

Preparation of CuS-PTX-ICG NPs and CuS-PTX-ICG-L-arg NPs

Based on previous studies, slight modifications were made to the procedure [48]. The PTX solution (1 mg mL−1, in Dimethyl Sulfoxide (DMSO)), ICG solution (1 mg mL−1, in PBS), L-arg solution (2 mg mL−1, in DMSO) were prepared. The CuS NPs (1 mg, 1 mg mL−1) were dispersed in the PTX solution (500 μg, 1 mg mL−1). PTX was absorbed into the cavities of the CuS NPs after stirring at room temperature for 12 h. The PTX-loaded CuS NPs were dispersed in 500 μL of ICG solution (1 mg mL−1). ICG was absorbed into the cavities of the CuS NPs after stirring at room temperature for 12 h. The CuS-PTX-ICG (abbreviation of CuS-PI without L-arg) NPs were dispersed in the L-arg solution (2 mg mL−1). The CuS-PI NPs were dispersed in an L-arg solution (2 mg mL−1). After stirring at room temperature for 12 h, L-arg was adsorbed onto the NPs, resulting in the formation of CuS-PTX-ICG-L-arg (abbreviated as CuS-PIA) NPs.

Preparation of HeLa cell membrane (Hm) and red blood cell membrane (Rm)

Blood samples from Kunming mice (8 weeks old) were centrifuged (3500 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C) to remove plasma. The resulting red blood cell (RBC) pellet was washed thrice with normal saline (NS). After adding 0.25 × PBS to induce hemolysis (4 °C, 1 h), hemoglobin was removed by centrifugation (13,000 rpm, 5 min). The Rm pellet was then extensively washed with DIW until colorless supernatants were obtained (Fig. S1). The purified Rm was stored at −80 °C for subsequent use [49].

HeLa cells were collected by centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min), resuspended in hypotonic lysing buffer, and homogenized on ice using a Dounce homogenizer (20 strokes). Following centrifugation of the lysate (3000 rpm, 10 min), the supernatant was pooled and centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 2 h. This final pellet, representing purified Hm, was stored at −80 °C for further use (Fig. S1).

Preparation and characterization of HeLa-blood cells hybrid membrane (HR)

The protein concentrations of Rm and Hm were quantified using a BCA assay. Total membrane mass was determined to be twice the mass of membrane-associated protein. Rm was combined with Hm at defined protein ratios (Rm:Hm = 5:1, 3:1, 1:1, and 0:1), followed by ultrasonication (37 °C, 10 min) to induce membrane fusion. Fusion efficiency was monitored via Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) using DiI (donor) and DiD (acceptor) dye-paired labels. Briefly, Hm was co-labeled with DiI/DiD, then mixed with Rm at specified ratios. Fluorescence emission spectra (550–750 nm) were recorded at 525 nm excitation. Progressive membrane fusion was quantified by donor (DiI) dequenching, measured as increased fluorescence intensity at 565 nm.

Preparation of CuS-PIA@HR NPs

The NPs were prepared as previously described [50]. The Rm and Hm were dissolved in DIW. The CuS NPs dispersion was sterilized under UV light for 30 min. Subsequently, the two types of cell membranes were added to the drug-loaded CuS NPs dispersion, followed by water bath sonication to promote fusion. After sterilization, the resulting solution was sealed and stored.

In vitro release of PTX

In vitro PTX release profiles were assessed under four conditions: (1) pH 7.4 (no NIR), (2) pH 5.6 (no NIR), (3) pH 7.4 + NIR (808 nm, 1 W cm−2), (4) pH 5.6 + NIR (808 nm, 1 W cm−2). Release media comprised 10 mM PBS (pH 7.4) or 10 mM acetate buffer (pH 5.6), both supplemented with 1 M sodium salicylate to maintain sink conditions. Samples (1 mL) were loaded into dialysis tubing (MWCO 3.5 kDa) and immersed in 19 mL of preheated medium at 37 °C under constant agitation (100 rpm). At predetermined intervals, 200 μL aliquots were withdrawn from the external medium, replaced with equal volumes of fresh buffer, and analyzed for PTX content via HPLC.

Photothermal performance of CuS-PIA@HR NPs

To systematically evaluate the photothermal performance of the CuS-PIA@HR NPs, we first investigated the temperature variation under different nanoparticle concentrations (0, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2, and 0.3 mg mL−1) and laser power densities (0.6, 0.7, 0.8, and 0.9 W cm−2) using an 808 nm NIR laser. The temperature changes were recorded in real-time with an infrared thermal camera. A comparative assessment was then conducted by irradiating aqueous dispersions of water, free ICG, CuS NPs, and the CuS-PIA@HR nanocomposite (at an equivalent concentration) under the same conditions (808 nm, 0.9 W cm−2) to directly compare their photothermal heating capabilities.

Furthermore, the photothermal stability of free ICG, and the CuS-PIA@HR NPs was rigorously examined over five heating-cooling cycles. Each sample underwent five consecutive cycles, each consisting of 9 min of laser irradiation (808 nm, 0.9 W cm−2) followed by natural cooling to ambient temperature. This test was designed to evaluate the durability and resistance to photodegradation of each component and the composite. Photodynamic characterization of CuS-PIA@HR NPs

ROS generation under 808 nm laser irradiation was quantified using a 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) assay. DPBF solution (1 mg mL−1 in DMSO) was prepared under dark conditions. CuS-PIA@HR NPs were dispersed uniformly in DIW and mixed with the DPBF solution at a 1:1 vol ratio. During irradiation (808 nm, 1 W cm−2), the mixture was monitored in real-time: Absorbance at 420 nm was recorded every 2 min for 10 min using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer to track DPBF consumption.

Quantification of NO production

NO release was quantified using a Griess reaction assay kit. A sodium nitrite (NaNO2) standard curve (0–100 μM) was first established (Fig. S2). CuS-PIA@HR NPs dispersed in DIW at concentrations of 0.5, 1, and 2 mg mL−1 were irradiated (808 nm; 0.5, 1, or 2 W cm−2) for 10 min. Post-irradiation, samples were centrifuged to remove nanoparticles, and 100 μL of supernatant was immediately incubated with Griess reagent to form a chromogenic azo dye. Absorbance at 540 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Cell culture

HeLa cells (human cervical carcinoma; ATCC) and RAW 264.7 cells (murine macrophage leukemia; Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China) were acquired. H8 cells (immortalized human cervical epithelium) were generated in-house. All cell lines (HeLa, RAW 264.7, H8) were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1 % penicillin-streptomycin (Pen-Strep), and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5 % CO2 atmosphere.

Cellular uptake

HeLa, H8, and RAW 264.7 cells were seeded into 35-mm glass-bottom dishes (5 × 104 cells/dish). After 24 h incubation, cells were treated with fresh medium containing ICG or CuS-PIA@HR NPs (10 μg mL−1 ICG-equivalent concentration) for 4 h at 37 °C/5 % CO2. Subsequently, cells were washed thrice with PBS, stained with Hoechst 33342 (10 min), washed again three times with PBS, and imaged by CLSM.

Intracellular ROS detection

The intracellular ROS generation capacity of CuS-PIA@HR NPs was evaluated using DCFH-DA fluorescent probes via confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM). For experimental groups: (1) NPs-treated cells were incubated with CuS-PIA@HR NPs for 24 h, washed thrice with PBS, and stained with 5 μM DCFH-DA (30 min); (2) Laser-irradiated cells underwent identical CuS-PIA@HR NPs treatment followed by 808 nm laser exposure (1 W cm−2, 5 min) post-PBS washing. All samples were imaged at 488 nm excitation with emission collection at 505–525 nm.

Intracellular NO imaging

The NO release capacity of CuS-PIA@HR NPs was assessed using DAF-FM DA fluorescent probe via CLSM. HeLa cells (5 × 104 cells/dish) in 35 mm glass-bottom dishes were cultured for 24 h, then incubated with CuS-PIA@HR NPs (200 μg mL−1, 4 h). After PBS washing, cells were stained with 5 μM DAF-FM DA (30 min). For laser-treated groups, cells were irradiated with 808 nm laser (1 W cm−2, 5 min) post-staining. All samples underwent PBS wash before immediate CLSM imaging (Ex/Em = 495/515 nm).

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of CuS-PIA@HR NPs

As illustrated in Fig. 1a, CuS-PIA@HR NPs were fabricated through a three-step synthesis process. First, CuS NPs were prepared as the core structure (Fig. 1b and S3). Subsequently, PTX, ICG, and L-arg were co-loaded into the CuS matrix, yielding the intermediate product CuS-PTX-ICG-L-arg NPs (abbreviated as CuS-PIA NPs, Fig. 1c). TEM image of CuS-PIA NPs (Fig. 1c), after drug loading still exhibit a circular, hollow morphology with uniform size and shape. The size of CuS-PIA NPs around 50 nm (Fig. S4). Finally, the CuS-PIA NPs were functionalized with a hybrid membrane coating to obtain the complete CuS-PIA@HR NPs. After membrane coating, the diameter of CuS-PIA@HR NPs was approximately 70 nm (Fig. S5). TEM images revealed a distinct membrane layer on the surface of the CuS (Fig. 1d and S6), with a uniform size increase of ∼20 nm, consistent with other reported studies [51,53]. The elemental distribution of the CuS-PIA@HR NPs was investigated through energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (TEM-EDS), as shown in the elemental maps in Fig. 1e and S7. The CuS-PIA@HR NPs was successfully synthesized, and its colloidal properties were characterized by dynamic light scattering (DLS). As summarized in Table 1(in the supporting information), the fabricated nanoparticles exhibited a hydrodynamic diameter of 82.76 ± 8.18 nm, indicating a relatively small size with good batch-to-batch reproducibility. Notably, the polydispersity index (PDI) was measured to be 0.171 ± 0.008(Table 1). These desirable physicochemical characteristics provide a solid foundation for their subsequent application as a promising drug delivery platform. To evaluate the stability of nanoparticles under physiologically relevant conditions, we dispersed them in PBS and incubated at 37 °C, measuring their particle size and PDI at time points of 0h, 12h, 24h, and 48h (Supporting information Table 2). We used absorption spectroscopy to measure the EE and DLC of CuS-PIA@HR NPs. The results showed that the EE of PTX was 86.3 %, the EE of ICG was 65 %, the EE of L-arg was 61.4 %. At the same time, the DLC of PTX was 5.2 %, the DLC of ICG was 1.3 %, and the DLC of L-arg was 4.3 % (Supporting information Table 3). Before preparing CuS-PIA@HR NPs, we fused HM with RM. To verify the fusion, the HM was labeled with the FRET pair dyes DiD and DiI. Subsequently, RM was progressively added to the DiD/DiI dual-labeled HeLa cell membranes. As shown in Fig. 1i, fluorescence recovery was observed at 565 nm with the increasing amount of RM, while the fluorescence at 670 nm gradually decreased. Next, we performed protein gel electrophoresis and Coomassie brilliant blue staining to confirm the successful transfer of Hm and Rm proteins onto the CuS-PIA@HR NPs, which is considered crucial for achieving the biomimetic functions of the membrane shell. The results showed that the protein bands of the CuS-PIA@HR NPs lysate were enriched with protein bands from both Hm lysate and Rm lysate, indicating that the original membrane proteins were well preserved during the mechanical extrusion process (Fig. 1g).

Zeta potential analysis shows that the surface charges of free ICG, free CuS and CuS-PIA@HR NPs were −35.9 mV, −16.5 mV and −36.6 mV respectively (Fig. 1h), which contributes to the great dispersion stability of CuS-PIA@HR NPs. As depicted in Fig. 1f, characteristic absorption peaks occur at: 240 nm (free PTX), 800 nm (free CuS), 780 nm (free ICG), CuS-PIA@HR NPs exhibit a redshifted peak at 780 nm, attributed to ICG-phospholipid/protein interactions altering molecular energy levels [51]. As shown in Fig. 1j, the fluorescence peak of free ICG is located at 800 nm, while the fluorescence peak of CuS-PIA@HR NPs is at 810 nm. The 10-nm redshift (800 → 810 nm) of ICG fluorescence in CuS-PIA@HR NPs primarily results from: confinement by membrane proteins, hybrid membrane (Hm/Rm) proteins restrict ICG molecular vibration, reducing emission energy. weak surface coupling between ICG and CuS NPs alters electron distribution. To better understand the chemical modification and functional groups of CuS-IPA@HR NPs, we used infrared Fourier spectrum (Fig. 1k). In the 2700-3800 cm−1 range, the Near-infrared Fourier Transform Spectrometer (FT-IR) shows strong absorption peaks. These peaks suggest that the sample contains amino groups (N-H) or hydroxyl groups (O-H). The peaks in the 2700-3800 cm−1 range likely come from amino groups in arginine and phospholipids or proteins in the cell membrane. In the 1800-2400 cm−1 range, a clear peak appears in the FT-IR. This peak suggests the presence of C=O carbonyl stretching vibrations. It may come from ester groups in PTX, carbonyl groups in ICG, or ester groups in phospholipids. At 1638 cm−1, another peak is observed. This peak is usually linked to C=C double bond vibrations. It might be related to the aromatic ring structures in ICG. In the 400-900 cm−1 range, typical peaks are seen. These peaks match the vibrations of metal-sulfur bonds in CuS, confirming the presence of CuS in the composite. The results show that the composite surface is rich in amino groups, carbonyl groups, and unsaturated double bonds. It also contains CuS and cell membrane components. These functional groups may play key roles in the material's biocompatibility, drug delivery performance, and optical properties.

As depicted in the X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Fig. S8), the appearance of Cu, O, C, N, and S characteristic peaks in the NPs demonstrates the successful incorporation of CuS, ICG, PTX, and L-arg into the system. Specifically, the Cu 2p peak around 930 eV confirms the presence of Cu in the NPs, verifying the CuS core. The O 1s peak around 530 eV, along with the C 1s peak at 285 eV, suggests the presence of oxygen-containing groups and carbon-based structures, likely originating from the RM and HM hybrid coating. The N 1s peak at 400 eV indicates the incorporation of L-arg, further confirming its presence in the NPs system. Lastly, the S 2p peak at 160 eV verifies the presence of sulfur, consistent with the CuS core material. These XPS results validate the successful modification of the NPs with ICG, PTX, L-arg, and the hybrid membrane, supporting their design for enhanced drug delivery and targeted therapy. To comprehensively evaluate the stability of the synthesized NPs, multiple parameters were systematically investigated. Initially, the particle size distribution and Zeta potential of CuS-IPA@HR NPs was monitored over a 7-day period post-synthesis (Figs. S9–10). The results indicated that the NPs maintained excellent stability, with the average particle size consistently remaining around 70 nm, and average zeta potential −36.6 mV.

To elucidate the mechanism of drug loading, we conducted nitrogen adsorption desorption isotherm tests on the synthesized blank CuS-PIA@HR NPs to analyze their pore structure. The textural properties of the fabricated CuS and the composite CuS-PIA@HR NPs, which are critical for their application as drug carriers, were investigated by N2 adsorption-desorption analysis. The BET specific surface area was determined to be 39.46 m2/g for pristine CuS and 32.57 m2 g−1 for the CuS-PIA@10.13039/501100014832HR NPs (Supporting information Table 4). The observed decrease in surface area for the composite strongly indicates the successful incorporation of the functional components onto the CuS surface or within its pores. Pore size distribution analysis revealed that both materials possess an average pore diameter of approximately 21 nm (based on the BJH desorption model), which classifies them as well-defined mesostructures. Notably, this pore width is substantially larger than the kinetic diameter of common chemotherapeutic agents such as PTX. This size compatibility is pivotal, as it ensures the effortless diffusion and high-capacity loading of drug molecules into the porous framework, rather than mere superficial adsorption.

In vitro photothermal and PTX release analysis

To validate the efficacy of our designed nanoplatform, we first investigated the photothermal properties of CuS-PIA@HR NPs were assessed under 808 nm laser (0.9 W cm−2, 10 min) at varying concentrations (0.1, 0.15, 0.2, 0.3 mg mL−1), with water as the control (Fig. S11). Results demonstrated a direct correlation between temperature elevation and both CuS-PIA@HR NPs concentration and irradiation time. The experiment systematically investigated the temperature elevation performance of CuS-PIA@HR NPs at a fixed concentration (0.3 mg mL−1), under varying laser power densities (0.6, 0.7, 0.8, and 0.9 W cm−2). As shown in Fig. 1m, after 10 min of 808 nm laser irradiation, the solution temperature increase demonstrated significant power dependence.

Then we compared the temperature curves of water, free ICG, CuS NPs, and the CuS-PIA@HR composite at equivalent concentrations under identical irradiation conditions (808 nm, 0.9 W cm−2). The temperature changes were recorded in real-time with an infrared thermal camera (shown in Fig. S12). As shown in Fig. 1l, free ICG showed a moderate temperature increase (ΔT ≈ 13.3 °C) but suffered from significant photobleaching. In contrast, CuS NPs demonstrated stable and efficient heating (ΔT ≈ 21.7 °C). Remarkably, the CuS-PIA@HR NPs achieved a dramatically enhanced temperature elevation (ΔT ≈ 28.6 °C), which is substantially higher than the arithmetic sum of the individual components.

The superior stability of the CuS-PIA@HR NPs was further corroborated by the cycling experiments (shown in Fig. S13). While free ICG exhibited a rapid decline in photothermal performance after the first cycle due to degradation, the CuS-PIA@HR composite maintained excellent photothermal stability over five cycles. The temperature change for the composite remained almost constant, highlighting the role of the CuS nanocarrier in protecting the encapsulated ICG from photodegradation, thereby ensuring reproducible performance.

To quantitatively evaluate the η, we calculated the η value based on the Roper method. The η of the CuS-PIA@HR NPs was calculated to be 42.5 % (Fig. 1n, Figure S14-15), which is higher than that of pure CuS NPs (η = 23.5 %) and significantly surpasses that of free ICG (η = 12.6 %). This quantitative data provides compelling evidence that the integration of ICG with CuS NPs not only combines their functionalities but also creates a synergistic effect that enhances the overall energy conversion efficiency (Supporting information Table 5).

In summary, this section demonstrates that the CuS-PIA@HR nanocomposite successfully integrates the advantages of its components: It exhibits stronger photothermal heating than either component alone, proving a synergistic effect. It possesses excellent photostability, addressing the inherent weakness of ICG. It achieves a high photothermal conversion efficiency, making it a highly efficient photothermal agent. These findings solidly support our design strategy that combining CuS with ICG leads to a composite material with superior properties, fulfilling the goal of a “1 + 1>2” theranostic effect. This enhanced performance is crucial for effective and reliable photothermal therapy in vivo.

The in vitro drug release behavior of the CuS-PIA@HR nanocomposite was systematically investigated under different pH conditions mimicking the physiological (pH = 7.4) and tumor microenvironment (pH = 5.6), with or without NIR laser irradiation. The release profiles of PTX are presented in Fig. S16. In the absence of NIR irradiation, PTX release was slow and sustained over the tested period. At pH = 7.4, the cumulative release reached approximately 56.6 ± 0.9 % after 72 h, a slightly accelerated release was observed at pH = 5.6, with a cumulative release of 69.7 ± 8.9 % at 72 h, suggesting that the acidic condition mildly promotes drug diffusion, likely due to subtle swelling of the hydrogel matrix. At pH = 7.4, each laser application induced an immediate and significant jump in PTX release. The cumulative release under pH = 7.4 + L conditions reached 74.5 % within 24 h, which was approximately two-fold higher than that of the pH = 7.4 group without irradiation. Most notably, the most efficient release was achieved under the combined conditions of an acidic pH and NIR irradiation (pH = 5.6 + L). The release profile showed the most pronounced burst pulses after each irradiation, culminating in a plateau of 88.3 % cumulative release. This result demonstrates a clear synergistic effect between the acidic tumor microenvironment and the external photothermal stimulus.

In vitro cellular uptake, cytotoxicity and PTT/PDT assay

CCK-8 assays demonstrated concentration-dependent cytotoxicity of CuS-PIA@HR NPs in tumor (HeLa) and normal (H8) cells (Figs. S17–20). At the established safe concentration (200 μg mL−1), HeLa viability remained at 86.6 % while H8 cells retained 97.1 % survival. Minimal cytotoxicity (<100 μg mL−1) contrasted sharply with 4 % HeLa survival at 300 μg mL−1, confirming: (1) High biocompatibility derived from ICG and hybrid membrane components; (2) Laser-dependent therapeutic selectivity, where 808 nm irradiation selectively killed cancer cells (p < 0.001) without harming normal cells regardless of laser exposure.

In this study, to validate the targeting ability of the drug after the hybrid membrane design, we used CLSM to investigate the uptake of CuS-PIA@HR NPs by HeLa cells. HeLa cells were incubated with PBS, ICG and CuS-PIA@HR for 3 h. The cell nuclei were stained with Hoechst 33342 (blue), as shown in Fig. 3a. The fluorescence intensity originated from CuS-PIA@HR NPs in HeLa cells and was stark superior to that from ICG and PBS as the control Consistent with its inherent physicochemical properties, free ICG exhibited significant cellular uptake in both cancer cells (Fig. 3c, p < 0.05 vs. control), attributable to passive diffusion and receptor-mediated endocytosis via serum protein carriers. In vivo biosafety

CuS-PIA@HR NPs exhibit excellent biocompatibility, in vivo safety, and targeting capabilities. As shown in Fig. 4k and Fig. S22, no significant drug toxicity or inflammatory signs were observed in the tissue sections of the heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys of the mice within 24 h, 7 days or 14 days. These results indicate that CuS-PIA@HR NPs no appearently side effects to experimental mice, confirming their biocompatibility and safety. As depicted in Fig. 4e the hemolysis assay demonstrated a concentration-dependent increase in hemolysis for CuS-PIA@HR NPs, though the hemolytic percentage remained consistently below the 3 % threshold, indicating minimal toxicity. At higher concentrations (up to 300 μg mL−1), the extent of hemolysis increased, as visually confirmed by the intensifying red coloration in the samples (inset of Fig. 4e). Despite this increase, hemolysis was still within the safe and acceptable limits, suggesting that CuS-PIA@HR NPs exhibit excellent biocompatibility. These findings underscore the potential of CuS-PIA@HR NPs as non-toxic candidates for biomedical applications, including drug delivery systems, with a high safety profile.

Blood biochemical analysis revealed no significant differences in liver function markers (ALT, AST) and kidney function markers (BUN, UREA) between the CuS-PIA@HR NPs group and the control groups (Fig. 4f–j and Table 6). Additionally, levels of HGB remained within the normal range, suggesting minimal systemic inflammation or immune response. Other blood routine indicators are also within the normal range (Fig. S23). Therefore, in vivo biosafety studies confirmed the great biocompatibility and safety profile of CuS-PIA@HR NPs. Future work will focus on long-term toxicity evaluation and dose optimization to further support its clinical translation.

Inhibition of HSP70 expression by CuS-PIA@HR NPs

Chemotherapy remains a cornerstone in tumor treatment, yet chemoresistance continues to pose a significant therapeutic challenge. A key factor limiting treatment efficacy is the heat shock response (HSR), which protects cells from protein denaturation and thermal damage during PTT, ultimately reducing its therapeutic impact. Notably, HSPs-critical molecular chaperones-are upregulated under various external stresses, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and drug exposure, thereby promoting tumor cell survival and diminishing treatment outcomes. To overcome this limitation, we explored NO as a potential HSR inhibitor. Our findings reveal that NO effectively downregulates HSP70, a central mediator of the HSR. By suppressing HSP70, tumor cells exhibit enhanced sensitivity to both heat and chemotherapy, allowing for stronger antitumor effects even at mild hyperthermic temperatures. This dual-action strategy not only improves therapeutic efficacy but also minimizes thermal damage to surrounding healthy tissues, offering a promising approach to overcoming treatment resistance in cancer therapy.

To confirm whether CuS-PIA@HR NPs can effectively suppress HSP70 expression and function under laser irradiation, the following experiment was designed. The study comprised several treatment groups. One group was a positive control treated with the known HSP70 inhibitor VER-155008. Another group received CuS-PIA@HR NPs at different concentrations combined with laser irradiation. The results clearly showed that the nanoparticles with laser irradiation inhibit HSP70 function effectively. This inhibition occurred in a dose-dependent manner. The effect was comparable to or even better than the standard inhibitor. This led to strong synergistic cancer cell killing (Figs. S24–25).

In vivo antitumor efficacy

To test how well CuS-PIA@HR NPs work against tumors in live animals, we did a detailed study on mice with tumors. We focused on three main factors: changes in body weight, tumor weight, and tumor size. These are key signs to tell if the treatment works and if it causes any harm. The mice were divided into seven groups: 1) Control (PBS), 2) Laser only, 3) PTX, 4) CuS-PI@HR NPs, 5) CuS-PI@HR NPs + Laser, 6) CuS-PIA@HR NPs, and 7) CuS-PIA@HR NPs + Laser. CuS-PIA@HR NPs were administered intravenously every 7 days for a total of 14 days. Tumor size was measured every 2 days using calipers, and the body weight of mice's was monitored to check for any treatment-related side effects. At the end of the study, tumors were excised and weighed, and various tests, including levels of HSPs in tumor tissues using fluorescence-stained sectioning technique analysis.

Tumor volume was calculated using formula (7). The results showed that CuS-PIA@HR NPs significantly slowed tumor growth compared to the control groups (Fig. 5g). By the end of the treatment, the average tumor size in the CuS-PIA@HR NPs + Laser group was much smaller. The treatment reduced tumor growth by 73.3 %, indicating its high effectiveness (Fig. 5i), confirming that CuS-PIA@HR NPs can effectively reduce tumor size. Significantly, compared with the all groups,the CuS-PIA@HR NPs + L group showed the smallest tumor weight, which was consistent with the results of tumor volume (Fig. S26).

Body weight was monitored throughout the study to assess potential side effects. No significant changes were observed in the body weight of the CuS-PIA@HR NPs group compared to the control groups (Fig. 5h). All mice remained healthy and active, suggesting that the treatment did not cause substantial harm. We used HE and TUNEL staining to observe changes in major organs (including the heart, liver, lungs, and kidneys) and tumor tissues. The results showed that after 14 days of treatment, the nano-drug delivery system (CuS-PIA@HR NPs) significantly caused tumor cell death and necrosis (Fig. 5f). Compared to the control group, the number of tumor cells in the CuS-PIA@HR NPs + L group was greatly reduced, and the area of cell death was also significantly expanded. These results indicate that CuS-PIA@HR NPs + L can effectively inhibit tumor growth and shrink tumors through precise delivery and controlled release of the drug. The combined effect of the nanocarrier and the drug suggests that CuS-PIA@HR NPs + L has great potential in cancer treatment. The treatment group and the control group showed no significant differences in the histological features and functional indicators of major organs, which strongly supports the safety profile of our drug (Fig. S27).

Conclusion

Chemotherapy resistance remains a critical challenge in cancer treatment, necessitating innovative strategies to enhance drug efficacy. This study successfully developed CuS-PIA@HR NPs, a multifunctional drug delivery platform that integrates PTT, PDT, and chemotherapy to improve therapeutic outcomes. The hybrid membrane coating, which combines RBC and HeLa cell membranes, effectively enhances immune evasion and tumor-targeting capabilities. This dual-membrane strategy prolongs systemic circulation, reduces immune clearance, and significantly increases drug accumulation at tumor sites, ensuring sustained therapeutic effects. More importantly, this work introduces a novel approach to overcoming chemotherapy resistance through NO-mediated HSP inhibition. Under NIR laser irradiation, CuS-mediated photothermal effects and ICG-generated ROS trigger the conversion of L-arginine into NO, leading to the downregulation of HSP70. This process significantly enhances tumor cell sensitivity to chemotherapy, resulting in a 1.5-fold increase in apoptosis compared to treatments without HSP inhibition. In vivo toxicity studies further confirm the excellent biocompatibility and biosafety of CuS-PIA@HR NPs, with no significant adverse effects on major organs. Overall, the combination of hybrid membrane coating and NO-mediated HSP inhibition presents a promising strategy for targeted cancer therapy. This study provides a strong foundation for the clinical translation of multifunctional NPs and underscores the potential of hybrid membrane-based drug delivery systems in overcoming chemotherapy resistance.
