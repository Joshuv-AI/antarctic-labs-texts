Long‐Term Prognosis and Surgical Outcomes of Culture‐Negative Infective Endocarditis
Authors: Lanlin Zhang, Sheng Yang, Yuanhang Zhai, Yuheng Dong, Yanxue Zhao, Xinpei Liu, Ruiming Yu, Fupin Guo, Xiaojun Ma, Xiaoxiao Guo, Guotao Ma, Xingrong Liu, Shangdong Xu, Qi Miao, Jun Zheng, Chaoji Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCulture‐negative infective endocarditis (CNIE) presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges due to the absence of identifiable pathogens, leading to empirical antibiotic treatment and potentially worse clinical outcomes. This study aims to compare the clinical characteristics, surgical outcomes, and long‐term prognoses of patients with CNIE and culture‐positive infective endocarditis.MethodsA total of 698 patients who underwent surgery for infective endocarditis between 2013 and 2024 were included, with 154 (22.1%) classified as CNIE. Baseline, intraoperative, and postoperative factors were analyzed in several ways including multivariate Cox regression and structural equation modeling.ResultsPatients with CNIE had worse preoperative cardiac and renal function and a higher incidence of postoperative renal replacement therapy. While overall long‐term survival did not differ significantly between CNIE and culture‐positive infective endocarditis, female patients with CNIE showed a significantly worse prognosis (hazard ratio, 2.13 [95% CI, 1.02–4.43]; P=0.04). Mediation analysis indicated that reduced renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate) partially mediated the relationship between culture status and death, highlighting a potential mechanism.ConclusionsAlthough the survival rates of patients with CNIE and culture‐positive infective endocarditis are similar, the prognosis of female patients with CNIE is significantly worse.Keywords: culture‐negative, infective endocarditis, prognosis, risk analysis, surgerySubject Categories: Infectious Endocarditis, Cardiovascular Surgery, Mortality/Survival

The treatment of infective endocarditis (IE) remains a significant clinical challenge. With societal advancements and medical progress, the incidence of IE has been on the rise, yet its mortality rate remains persistently high. 1 , 2 The type of causative pathogen plays a crucial role in determining treatment strategies and patient prognosis, making it a key focus for clinicians.

However, identifying the causative pathogen can be difficult due to factors such as prior antibiotic use, challenges in pathogen culture requiring specialized conditions, and advanced patient age. These factors often lead to culture‐negative infective endocarditis (CNIE), complicating clinical decision making. 3 , 4 , 5 With the widespread use of antibiotics, the proportion of CNIE remains high, ranging from 5.2% to 31% in recent studies. 6 , 7 This can result in treatment delays and the need for empirical antibiotic therapy, which will compromise the treatment efficacy.

Recently, only a few studies have analyzed the differences in surgical death in IE with or without identification of the pathogen, and they have obtained inconsistent results. 8 , 9 Therefore, we conducted a study to explore whether the prognosis differs in these patients compared with those with CNIE.

Methods

Data Availability

Data and materials are available from the corresponding author upon request.

Study Design

This study is a single‐center retrospective cohort study that includes only patients with IE who underwent surgical treatment at Peking Union Medical College Hospital between January 2013 and December 2024. The study was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (I‐24PJ0716). Given the retrospective nature of the study and the anonymization of patient identity information, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. The specific research process can be found in Figure S1.

Definitions and Data Collection

If IE was diagnosed on the basis of the modified Duke criteria, then the multidisciplinary team would determine the antibiotic therapy and the need for surgical intervention. Patients with CNIE were defined as cases in which at least 2 blood cultures (different sampling sites, with sampling intervals >12 hours) failed to identify a pathogen. Patients with pathogen identification through other methods (eg, tissue culture, polymerase chain reaction, or metagenomic next‐generation sequencing) were also classified as culture‐positive infective endocarditis (CPIE), which aimed to reflect contemporary diagnostic practices. The surgery, which used standardized myocardial protection and perfusion protocols, was performed by a team of senior cardiac surgeons, minimizing interoperator variability.

Patient data were collected from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. Detailed definitions, units, and data types for each variable are provided in Table S1. Follow‐up data were collected by 4 cardiologists, with verbal consent obtained from patients or relatives before each call. Follow‐up was achieved in 99.4% (n=692) of cases, with details of the 6 lost cases provided in Table S2.

Statistical Analysis

All data analyses were performed using R Studio software version 4.2.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables were tested for normality by Shapiro–Wilk, and normally distributed data were expressed as mean±SD and compared using an independent sample t test; nonnormally distributed data were expressed as median (interquartile range) and analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were described as number of cases (percentage), and comparisons were performed using Pearson χ2 test or Fisher’s exact probability method according to the sample size.

Variable selection was performed using bidirectional stepwise regression to ensure clinical relevance alongside statistical significance; the stepwise results were reviewed in consultation with domain experts. Finally, age, sex, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), diabetes, pathogenesis results, reoperation, heart function, perivalvular situation, neurological complication, prosthetic valve, and surgery time were selected as predictors to create a multivariate logistic regression model. For survival analysis, the Cox proportional hazards regression model was constructed to calculate the hazard ratio (HR) and its 95% CI. The cumulative survival rate was evaluated by plotting Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and the log‐rank test was used for gender‐stratified comparisons. All statistical tests were 2‐sided, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Mediation analysis was conducted using structural equation modeling. Based on their association with CPIE (P< 0.1), 3 variables—eGFR, heart failure, and perivalvular abscess—were selected as mediators. Age and sex were included as covariates in all models. The temporal sequence of variables was clearly defined. Bootstrapping with 5000 resamples was used to calculate bias‐corrected CIs.

To evaluate the robustness of the study results, we conducted a sensitivity analysis as follows:

1. Secondary end points (including sepsis, multiple organ failure, intra‐aortic balloon pump/extracorporeal membrane oxygenation use, and secondary thoracotomy). A multivariate logistic regression model was used to adjust confounding factors and analyze the association between patients with CNIE and patients with CPIE with secondary end points.

2. Patients were divided into 2 groups according to the time of surgery: before 2021 and after 2021 for stratified analysis to evaluate the impact of diagnosis and treatment strategies in different periods.

3. According to the pathogen culture methods of patients with CPIE, they were divided into 2 groups—blood culture and other culture methods—and the effects of different culture methods on the diagnosis and prognosis of infective endocarditis were further analyzed.

Results

A total of 698 patients with IE who underwent surgical treatment were included in this study, with 544 cases (77.9%) in the CPIE group and 154 cases (22.1%) in the CNIE group.

Baseline Characteristics

The baseline characteristics of both groups are summarized in Table 1 (overall and stratified). The median age of the overall population was 47 (interquartile range, 34–57) years, with 476 (68%) being male, and there were no significant differences in baseline characteristics. Regarding comorbidities, there were no statistically significant differences in the prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease between the 2 groups. However, the proportion of patients with CNIE with reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (<50%) was significantly higher than that in CPIE group (10% versus 3%, P<0.001), and the incidence of clinical heart failure was also higher (37% versus 29%, P=0.047). In addition, CNIE had a higher incidence of moderate to severe renal impairment (eGFR <60 mL/min per 1.73 m2, 21% versus 14%; P=0.02). The Charlson comorbidity score and European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation score calculated before surgery did not find any statistical difference.

In echocardiographic results, there were no significant differences in the incidence of paravalvular complications and the size of vegetations. IE most commonly affected the left heart (83%), while the tricuspid valve is more likely to be involved in CNIE (14% versus 8%, P=0.066) but did not reach statistical significance. The most common preoperative embolic event was ischemic stroke (23%), with no difference between the 2 groups (CNIE, 20%; CPIE, 24%; P=0.3). In terms of surgical intervention, the 2 groups were comparable in the timing of surgery (emergency surgery ratio, 6% versus 5%; P=0.6).

Postoperative Complications

The postoperative complications analysis shown in Table S3, which reveal that the need for continuous renal replacement therapy after surgery was significantly higher in the CNIE group (18% versus 9%, P<0.01). Although the incidence of postoperative low cardiac output syndrome was higher in the CNIE group compared with the control group, the difference did not reach statistical significance (12% versus 8%, P=0.12). Similarly, there were no statistically significant differences between the 2 groups in the use of circulatory assist devices, like intra‐aortic balloon pump, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, and permanent pacemaker implantation (all P>0.05).

Outcomes and Follow‐Up

After a median follow‐up duration of 5.1 years (interquartile range, 2.3–7.3), survival analysis demonstrated similar outcomes between patients with CNIE and patients with CPIE on 1‐month (3% versus 3%; HR, 0.93 [95% CI, 0.36–3.25]; P=0.89) and 1‐year mortality rates (9% versus 7%; HR, 1.15 [95% CI, 0.47–1.63]; P=0.67; Table S4). Figure 1 shows that there was no significant difference in the cumulative mortality rate between patients with CNIE and patients with CPIE (14% versus 12%; HR, 1.08 [95% CI, 0.56–1.51]; P=0.75). Multivariate analysis identified age (HR, 0.98 [95% CI, 1.00–1.04]; P=0.02), perivalvular abscess (HR, 0.34 [95% CI, 1.70–5.05]; P<0.001), neurological complications (HR, 0.53 [95% CI, 1.16–3.04]; P=0.01) and emergency surgery (HR, 0.39 [95% CI, 1.28–5.21]; P=0.01) as significant factors associated with the long‐term mortality rate (Table 2). Moreover, patients undergoing emergency surgery presented with more critical preoperative conditions (Table S5); stratified analysis revealed no significant differences in outcomes between patients with CPIE and patients with CNIE across different surgical timings (emergency/urgent/elective) (Figure S2). However, gender‐stratified multivariate analysis showed that the clinical prognosis of female patients with CNIE was significantly worse than that of patients with CPIE (HR, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.23–0.98]; P=0.04; Figure 2). Further analysis of preoperative baseline characteristics in female patients revealed that patients with CNIE exhibited lower eGFR levels (P=0.02) and longer disease duration (P=0.03) compared with patients with CPIE (Table 3).

Through mediation analysis, it was found that CPIE itself did not have a significant effect on patient survival (P=0.988). However, the total indirect effect was significant, with the indirect path through eGFR influencing the risk of death (odds ratio, 0.991 [95% CI, 0.978–0.999]; P=0.09; Table S6).

Sensitivity analysis further showed that there was no statistical difference in the risk of secondary outcomes between CNIE and CPIE (odds ratio, 1.29 [95% CI, 0.69–2.34]; P=0.42), and the lower eGFR, function of heart and paravalvular abscess were independent risk factors for secondary outcomes (Table S7). In addition, stratified analysis by surgical year (before or after 2021) also found no significant effect of CNIE on prognosis (before 2021: HR, 1.36 [95% CI, 0.42–1.28]; P=0.27; after 2021: HR, 0.52 [95% CI, 0.58–6.43]; P=0.28; Figure S3). By excluding the population in which the pathogens were identified by other culture methods and recomparing them, it was found that there was no significant difference in the prognosis between patients with CNIE and patients with CPIE (HR, 1.14 [95% CI, 0.53–1.45]; P=0.62; Figure S4).

Discussion

This study examined the long‐term outcomes of 698 patients with IE, comparing the clinical characteristics of CNIE and CPIE. Patients with CNIE had lower ejection fraction and preoperative eGFR, contributing to a higher incidence of postoperative renal failure. At the same time, mediation analysis indicated that reduced eGFR may serve as a potential mechanistic pathway through which CNIE contributes to adverse clinical outcomes. Mortality analysis showed that older age, emergency surgery, perivalvular abscess, and central nervous system involvement were independent risk factors for long‐term death among patients with IE. Although the overall analysis showed no significant difference in survival between patients with CNIE and patients with CPIE, sex‐stratified analysis found that the prognosis of female patients with CNIE was significantly worse than that of the CPIE group (HR, 0.47 [95% CI, 0.23–0.98]; P=0.04), while there was no difference in male patients.

The inability to identify the causative pathogen in patients with IE presents a significant clinical challenge. CNIE often require empirical antibiotic therapy due to the lack of precise targeted treatment, which may result in insufficient coverage or improper dosing, thereby increasing in‐hospital mortality risk. 10 The most common reason for CNIE is prior antibiotic use, 11 while true CNIE is typically associated with fastidious organisms that are difficult to detect using conventional methods, such as the HACEK group (Haemophilus species, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Cardiobacterium hominis, Eikenella corrodens, and Kingella kingae), Coxiella burnetii, Bartonella species, and so on. 12 , 13 , 14 Although some studies suggest that CNIE may have a milder clinical presentation than CPIE, 15 our findings indicate that among patients requiring surgical intervention, CNIE exhibits worse preoperative conditions. These findings align with the study by Zamorano et al, which demonstrated that CNIE is more likely to present with advanced heart failure and significant valvular destruction. 16

Patients with CNIE tends to have lower preoperative eGFR, which may be attributed to multiple factors. First, these patients often receive prolonged empirical antibiotic therapy to manage unexplained fever, and some antibiotics have nephrotoxic effects that can further exacerbate kidney damage. 17 Additionally, in certain cases, underlying autoimmune diseases may contribute to the formation of vegetations and persistent fever, with immune complex deposition and vasculitic glomerulonephritis further impairing renal function. Hemodynamic disturbances caused by heart failure may also reduce renal perfusion, worsening kidney dysfunction. Patients with CNIE often have renal impairment, which not only significantly increases the demand for continuous renal replacement therapy but also may worsen their prognosis and thus reduce their quality of life. 18

Multivariate analysis showed that age, paravalvular complications, emergency surgery, and neurological complications were independent risk factors for death in patients undergoing surgery. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that independent risk factors affecting the prognosis of patients with IE include renal insufficiency, impaired cardiac function, large vegetations, neurological complications, and paravalvular complications. 19 , 20 Menchi‐Elanzi et al also showed that the in‐hospital mortality rate of patients aged >80 years was significantly increased. 21

Regarding the factors affecting the postoperative prognosis of patients with CNIE, there are still differences in the conclusions of existing studies. Murashita et al proposed that CNIE, postoperative heart failure, and prosthetic valve infection are independent risk factors for long‐term prognosis, 8 while the study by Di Mauro et al showed that postoperative death was not significantly correlated with blood culture results. 9 This difference may be related to factors such as study population characteristics, treatment strategies (such as empirical use of antibiotics) or follow‐up duration. It is worth noting that although mainstream IE prognostic scoring systems do not include CNIE as a core variable, 22 , 23 , 24 recent evidence shows that integrating blood culture status can improve the predictive power of the model (area under the curve increased from 0.684 to 0.71, P=0.439), suggesting its potential prognostic value. 25 This study demonstrated no statistically significant difference in long‐term prognosis or the incidence of major complications between patients with CNIE and patients with CPIE who underwent surgical treatment. These findings are consistent with those reported in the ESC‐EORP EURO‐ENDO (European Society of Cardiology EURObservational Research Program European Endocarditis) registry, 7 further confirms the effectiveness of surgical intervention in IE with unidentified pathogen type. Although the long‐term survival rates between patients with CNIE and patients with CPIE did not differ significantly, mediation analysis revealed a potential underlying mechanism. CNIE was associated with an increased risk of death by accelerating the deterioration of renal function, as evidenced by a progressive decline in eGFR, which suggests that regular monitoring of dynamic eGFR changes is essential, early identification of renal function decline should be prioritized, and proactive renal protection strategies should be implemented.

In clinical studies of IE, the impact of sex differences on prognosis has always been controversial. 26 , 27 , 28 Our study found through stratified analysis that the risk of death in female patients with CNIE was significantly increased. This sex difference may be related to the fact that women often experience delayed diagnosis and treatment, which was confirmed in our comparison between women with CNIE and women with CPIE in the symptoms‐to‐surgery intervals, possibly due to lower initial clinical suspicion, often delayed by common conditions such as autoimmune diseases. 29 , 30 Moreover, such delays in therapeutic intervention also may contribute to a higher incidence of renal impairment, ultimately adversely affecting long‐term prognosis in female patients. Anatomically, smaller cardiac structures in women can increase the complexity of surgical operation. 31 In addition, in young nulliparous women, decision making around valve type is further complicated by concerns regarding future pregnancy and anticoagulation management, which could inadvertently impact long‐term outcomes. Furthermore, the higher prevalence of autoimmune diseases in women leads to increased use of immunosuppressive therapy among female patients, 26 , 32 which not only elevates infection risk but also impairs pathogen detection, potentially contributing to the development and progression of CNIE, leading to a poor prognosis. Based on the above research results, it is recommended to use molecular diagnostic techniques (such as broad‐spectrum polymerase chain reaction and metagenomic next‐generation sequencing) for patients with CNIE (especially women) who require surgical intervention to accurately identify pathogens and optimize perioperative antimicrobial treatment regimens, thereby improving long‐term prognosis.

Limitation

A key limitation of this study is that it included only patients who underwent surgery for IE. Therefore, our findings may not be fully generalizable to the entire CNIE population, especially those managed medically without surgical intervention.
