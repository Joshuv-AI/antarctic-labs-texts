Laparoscopic partial splenectomy with modified Warshaw method for severe traumatic splenic rupture: a case report
Authors: Weiguang Zhou, Liang Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe spleen is one of the most frequently damaged organs in abdominal trauma, which may lead to rapid hemodynamic instability and require emergency surgical intervention. With the development of interventional techniques, more and more splenic injuries can achieve good results through non-surgical treatment measures such as interventional therapy. However, for some patients with severely damaged spleens, surgery remains the preferred treatment.Case presentationThis case report details the surgical treatment process of a 26-year-old male of Han Chinese ethnicity who suffered a grade IV splenic laceration following a motor vehicle accident. Due to the large area of splenic injury starting from the lower pole of the spleen and involving the splenic hilum, the patient underwent laparoscopic partial splenectomy, with the lower pole of the splenic tissue resected. Based on the ligation of the lower pole branches of the splenic artery and vein, to control the bleeding from the wound surface, the upper pole branch of the splenic artery was also ligated. Through the blood supply of the short gastric artery and the reflux of the splenic vein, the patient avoided total splenectomy. The patient recovered smoothly and was discharged after 10 days of hospitalization.ConclusionThe partial splenectomy method of artery resection and vein preservation introduced by us provides a feasible alternative for patients with severe splenic injuries involving the splenic hilum who need surgical treatment.Keywords: Modified Warshaw method, Partial splenectomy, Splenic rupture

Introduction

With the progress of interventional embolization techniques, more and more patients with severe splenic injuries are treated with interventional embolization, mainly aiming to preserve splenic function [1]. However, for patients with severe injuries involving the splenic hilum, there is a great controversy over whether to use interventional treatment. Moreover, the experience of different hospitals in interventional treatment varies. More hospitals still choose surgical treatment, mainly open or laparoscopic total splenectomy. For a patient with severe splenic injury involving the splenic hilum, we successfully performed partial splenectomy with preservation of short gastric blood supply using the modified Warshaw splenic preservation technique (ligating the splenic artery while preserving the splenic vein). By reviewing recent domestic and foreign relevant literature, only one case report in 2024 on laparoscopic distal pancreatectomy with spleen preservation mentioned the method of artery resection and vein preservation [2]. It is hoped that through the detailed introduction of this case, new ideas and references can be provided for high-risk splenic preservation surgeries.

Case presentation

A 26-year-old male patient of Han Chinese ethnicity was admitted to the hospital due to "chest and abdominal pain caused by a car accident for 1 hour". Physical examination on admission: body temperature 36.8 °C, pulse 90 beats per minute, respiration 20 times per minute, blood pressure 118/88 mmHg. The patient was conscious, with abrasions on the skin of the left chest and abdomen. The thoracic compression test was positive. The abdomen was flat and soft, with tenderness in the left upper abdomen and no rebound tenderness. There were multiple abrasions on the left forearm and left lower leg, and the limb movement was normal. Bedside ultrasound suggested free fluid in the abdominal cavity and possible splenic injury; abdominal enhanced CT suggested a large amount of blood in the abdominal cavity, a laceration of about 7.0 cm at the lower pole of the spleen, involving the splenic hilum and part of the upper pole (AAST grade IV injury) (Fig. 1); chest enhanced CT suggested multiple rib fractures on the left side and contusion of the lower lobe of the left lung. Limb X-ray showed no abnormality. Blood routine: white blood cell count 18.4 × 10⁹/L, hemoglobin 163 g/L, platelet count 76 × 10⁹/L. Although the patient's vital signs were stable, the splenic injury was severely graded, and emergency surgery was chosen. Then, laparoscopic exploration was performed. During the operation, the main trunk of the splenic artery was quickly dissected and blocked above the tail of the pancreas. Exploration showed rupture and bleeding at the lower pole of the spleen and the splenic hilum (Fig. 2). The branches of the splenic artery and vein were dissected. After ligating the branches of the splenic artery and vein at the lower pole of the spleen, there was still obvious bleeding from the injured area of the splenic hilum. The upper pole branch of the splenic artery was ligated, and the upper pole branch of the splenic vein was preserved. An ischemic line was visible at the upper pole of the spleen, showing blood supply from the short gastric artery. About 2.0 cm away from the ischemic line, partial resection of the lower pole of the spleen was performed. The wound surface was coagulated with bipolar electrocoagulation. The course of the splenic artery and vein at the splenic hilum was explored again, and the upper pole branch of the splenic vein was intact (Fig. 3). The patient was discharged without complications after 10 days of hospitalization. The total operation time was about 110 min. The amount of intra-abdominal bleeding during the operation was about 2800 mL, and about 1750 mL of autologous blood was transfused, without allogeneic blood transfusion. On the first day after the operation, 15 mL of light bloody fluid was drained from the splenic fossa. The drainage volume was small thereafter, and the amylase in the drainage fluid was normal. The drainage tube was removed on the 4th day. On the 6th day after the operation, abdominal enhanced CT was re-examined, showing good blood supply to the residual spleen and no obvious abdominal effusion (Fig. 4). The patient was discharged on the 10th day after the operation, and the peak platelet count was 434 × 10⁹/L.

Discussion

The latest AAST guidelines recommend non-surgical treatment as the first choice for low-grade injuries (grade I–II), and even for high-grade injuries (grade III–V), interventional embolization is selected. Some literature reports that its success rate has exceeded 80% [3]. A retrospective study of the German Trauma Registry Database found that although SAE is recommended in the guidelines, it is rarely used in clinical practice, especially for high-grade splenic injuries, and surgical treatment still dominates [4]. There are also reports that for some patients with high-grade splenic injuries, if the injured part can be partially resected and the remaining splenic tissue can maintain normal function, partial splenectomy is a feasible treatment method [5].

Among the many surgical methods for splenic preservation, the Warshaw method and the Kimura method are two relatively common ones. They were initially mainly applied in pancreatic body-tail surgeries to deal with the problem of whether to preserve the spleen or how to preserve it. When comparing the two methods, it was found that the Kimura technique is superior to the Warshaw technique in reducing the risk of splenic infarction and gastric varices [6, 7]. Early literature reports showed that the incidence of splenic infarction (including partial infarction) in the Warshaw method was 24%–55%, but due to the improvement of surgical techniques, this incidence has recently decreased to less than 2% [8–10]. In this case, the lower pole of the spleen was completely damaged and extended to the upper pole. After ligating the upper pole branch of the splenic artery, the bleeding from the wound surface of the residual spleen was effectively controlled. The main trunk of the splenic vein and its upper pole branch were intact and preserved. This method also draws on the concepts of splenic artery embolization for splenic injury and the Warshaw method for splenic preservation in pancreatic body–tail surgery. The long-term incidence of gastric varices after Warshaw splenic preservation surgery is relatively high, reported in the literature to be 12%–27% [11, 12], and the preservation of the splenic vein can prevent the occurrence of long-term regional portal hypertension. In this case, the postoperative enhanced CT review showed no obvious ischemic changes in the splenic tissue near the splenic hilum, indicating that the blood supply from the short gastric artery can meet the basic needs of the residual spleen.

In addition, when choosing this surgical method, the following key points need to be noted: First, for cases with splenic hilum injury or those in which it is difficult to judge whether there is splenic hilum injury before surgery, as many omental vessels as possible should be preserved before exposing and blocking the main trunk of the splenic artery. The omental vessels are important blood-supplying branches of the spleen. Preserving these vessels can, to a certain extent, increase the feasibility of partial splenectomy. Second, the protection of the splenic vein during the operation is the key to the success of the operation. The splenic vein is thin and often runs parallel to the splenic artery. When dissecting the splenic artery, due to bleeding causing unclear surgical fields, it is easy to damage the splenic vein, affecting the splenic reflux, resulting in increased bleeding from the wound surface, and even possible conversion to laparotomy. Finally, in terms of the selection of the pre-cutting line, during standard partial splenectomy, the splenic parenchyma is generally transected 0.5–1.0 cm away from the non-blood-supplying side of the ischemic line [13]. In the modified Warshaw partial splenectomy in this case, to have more residual spleen survive after the operation, we chose to resect 2.0 cm away from the ischemic line. This method preserves the reflux vein, which can effectively reduce the risk of splenic sinus congestion, thereby reducing endothelial cell apoptosis and providing an opportunity for more splenic tissue to survive. However, whether this choice can achieve the expected effect still needs to be verified by more cases.

Conclusion

The partial splenectomy method of artery resection and vein preservation introduced by us provides a feasible alternative for patients with severe splenic injuries involving the splenic hilum who need surgical treatment. By quickly blocking the splenic artery to control bleeding and combining with fine dissection of the splenic hilum, the function of the spleen can be preserved to a certain extent. Even in the case of the increasing maturity of interventional embolization techniques, this method can also be used as an option for re-surgery after the failure of embolization treatment.
