Effects of two different types of resistance exercise on sleep quality and nocturnal physiological responses in young males
Authors: Mayo Suzuki, Tomotaka Natsui, Hikaru Matsudo, Hiroki Furukawa, Kazushige Goto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
[Purpose]We compared the effects of different types of resistance exercise (maximum muscle strength training (MST) and muscle hypertrophy training (HYP)) on sleep quality and nocturnal physiological responses.[Methods]Eleven males (22.5 ± 1.7 years) completed 3 trials on different days, consisting of 6 exercises of 4 sets at 85–90% of one-repetition maximum (1RM) (3-min inter-set rest periods) for MST, the same exercises of 3 sets at 70–80% of 1RM (1-min inter-set rest periods) for HYP, and control condition (CON, 60 min of rest). Sleep quality was evaluated via electroencephalography. Heart rate (HR), HR variability (HRV), and skin temperature were measured during sleep.[Results]MST significantly reduced time of rapid eye movement (REM) compared to CON (P < 0.05). However, relative time of REM sleep for entire sleep, total sleep time, sleep efficiency, sleep onset latency, wake after sleep onset, and absolute and relative time of non-REM sleep did not differ significantly among conditions. Proximal skin temperature during sleep first 90 min was significantly higher in both MST and HYP than in CON (P < 0.05), with no significant differences between MST and HYP. However, the gradient between distal and proximal temperature, HR, and HRV during sleep did not differ among conditions.[Conclusion]These findings imply that sleep architecture is not markedly altered following a single session of resistance exercise, regardless of regimen type.

INTRODUCTION

Balancing training stress and recovery is essential for athletes to optimize physiological and psychological performance [1,2]. During a single bout of resistance training, factors such as the number of exercises, training load, repetitions, and interset rest periods influence exercise-induced fatigue and subsequent recovery [3]. High-intensity training can disrupt internal homeostasis due to excessive exercise stress [4]. Among various recovery strategies, adequate sleep quality is crucial to prevent overreaching or overtraining during intensified training periods [5,6]. However, athletes often experience insufficient sleep quality and reduced sleep duration due to sports-specific demands, such as early morning or nighttime practices and consecutive days of high-intenisty training or competition [6]. Inadequate sleep delays glycogen resynthesis and neuromuscular recovery [7], while shortened sleep duration (i.e., sleep deprivation) increases injury risk and impairs both cognitive and motor function [1,7,8]. Therefore, exploring strategies to improve sleep quality is valuable to promote recovery following intensive training sessions.

The impact of exercise on sleep quality appears to depend on the regimen employed. Several studies have demonstrated that endurance exercise at low to moderate intensity improves sleep quality [9,10]. By contrast, excessive training stress from high-intenisty endurance exercise or ultra-endurance running (> 42 km or > 6 h) impairs sleep quality [11]. Most previous studies that have examined exercise and sleep have focused on endurance exercise, whereas the effects of acute resistance exercise on sleep parameters remain less well defined. A few studies have reported improvements in sleep quality following resistance exercise in young males [2,12], while others have found no positive effects [13]. Thus, findings remain inconsistent.

A systematic review demonstrated that resistance training at moderate intensity (50–70% of the one-repetition maximum [1RM]) improved subjective sleep quality [14]. However, Shioda et al. [15] reported that acute resistance exercise at 75% of 1RM did not affect sleep quality or nocturnal parasympathetic activity. These results imply that the impact of resistance exercise on sleep parameters depends on exercise intensity. Importantly, physiological stress during resistance exercise is also influenced by the number of repetitions and interset rest periods, in addition to intensity. Resistance exercise regimens are generally classified into muscle hypertrophy (HYP; moderate intensity [70–80% of 1RM] with short interset rests [0–2 min]) and maximum strength training (MST; high intensity [85–100% of 1RM] with longer interset rests [3–5 min]) [16]. To date, however, no studies have directly compared sleep quality and nocturnal autonomic activity between these two regimens.

Therefore, in the present study, we compared the effects of two different types of resistance exercise on sleep quality and nocturnal physiological responses. Specially, we focused on changes in sleep quality and sleep-related variables (e.g., nocturnal autonomic nerve activity, body temperature). We hypothesized that MST and HYP would reduce sleep quality compared to non-exercise condition. Also, we hypothesized that MST would further reduce sleep quality compared to HYP.

METHODS

Participants

Eleven healthy men with at least 1 year of resistance training experience were recruited. They reported no chronic sleep disturbance, based on the result from original questionnaire. The mean (± SD) age, height, and body weight were 22.5 ± 1.7 years, 175.4 ± 4.9 cm, and 68.0 ± 5.3 kg, respectively. Participants were instructed to maintain a regular sleep–wake schedule (12:00 am–07:00 am) beginning 1 week before the experiment and to refrain from alcohol and caffeine consumption. All participants were informed of the study procedures and objectives and provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Ritsumeikan University (BKC-LSMH-2023-104) in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental Overview

Each participant visited the laboratory four times during the experimental period. On the first visit, body composition and 1RM for each exercise (bench press, shoulder press, seated row, leg press, leg extension, leg curl) were assessed to determine workloads for the main trials. One week later, participants completed three randomized trials on separate visits. The HYP condition consisted of three sets of 6 exercises to failure at 70–80% of 1RM with 1-min rests between exercise and with 3-min sets. The MST condition consisted of four sets of 6 exercises to failure at 85–90% of 1RM with 3-min rests between sets and exercises. The control condition [CON] consisted of 60 min of seated rest. The MST and HYP trials were separated by 7 days, and the order of trials was randomized. Each resistance exercise session (main session) lasted approximately 60 min, and it was performed between 12:30 pm and 3:30 pm. The time of the exercise was standardized across all experimental conditions for each participant. After exercise, participants remained in the laboratory, consuming only water until receiving a standardized meal (888 kcal) at 7:00 pm. The PFC (protein-fat-carbohydrate) percentage of the meal was normalized for each participant (PFC, P:14%, F:25%, C:61%). At 8:30 pm, they showered for 10 min, then moved to the university dormitory. Sleep preparation began at 11:00 pm, and the sleep period was set from 12:00 am–07:00 am (7 h). Sleep duration was set in accordance with previous studies [6,15]. Also, sleep duration was determined based on reports indicating that the average sleep duration is approximately 7 h in Japanese men [17,18]. Sleep quality was assessed via electroencephalography (EEG), and autonomic nervous activity (HRV) and skin temperature were measured during sleep.

Resistance Exercise Session

The exercise in MST and HYP consisted of 6 exercises for upper body muscles (bench press, shoulder press, seated row) and lower body muscles (leg press, leg extension, leg curl) using weight stack machines or free weight for bench press.

Each exercise session commenced with 10-15 min warmup, which consisted of stretching, submaximal cycling exercise, and resistance exercise performed at 50% of the load prescribed for the subsequent 6 exercises. MST was conducted at 85–90% of 1RM across 4 sets, inter-set rest period of 3 min [16]. In HYP, the exercise was conducted at 70–80% of 1RM across 3sets, inter-set rest period of 1min [16]. The duration of exercise in MST and HYP was 60 min (including rest periods between sets and exercises). Participants were required to continue exercises in each set until failure with proper exercise form. Following the completion of all exercises, participants performed 10 min of stretching as cooldown. In CON, participants remained rest for 60 min. The room temperature was maintained at 20℃ in all conditions.

Measurements

1RM

Participants were familiarized with the exercises by performing 5-10 repetitions with a light load. After warm-up, the load was increased by 5 kg for lower body exercises (leg press, leg extension, leg curl) and by 2.5 kg for upper body exercises (bench press, shoulder press, seated row) [20]. The load was progressively increased until the participants were unable to complete a successful lift.

Sleep architecture

Sleep architecture was assessed using a portable two-channel EEG and electrooculography device (Brainwave Sensor, ZAX; Proassist, Ltd., Japan). Electrodes were placed above and lateral to the outer canthus of the left eye and on the right mastoid. Two additional electrodes were placed below and slightly lateral to the outer canthus of the right eye and above the chin. Sleep stages were analyzed every 30 s. Several previous studies have reported that sleep variables obtained from this device correlated with those measured via polysomnography [19]. Sleep stages were classified into five categories: awake, nonREM (NREM; stages N1–N3), and rapid eye movement (REM). In addition, total sleep time, sleep efficiency, wakefulness after sleep onset (WASO), and sleep onset latency (SOL) were calculated. Prior to measurement, participants were not allowed to use electronic devices for at least 60 min before bedtime (11:00 pm). Sleep measurements were conducted from 12:00 am to 7:00 am.

Heart rate and heart rate variability

Heart rate (HR) and HR variability (HRV) during sleep were determined using a wearable sensor (my beat, UNION TOOL Co., Japan) attached to the surface of the skin of the left side of the chest. Data were analyzed using HRV software (RRI ANALYZER 2, UNION TOOL Co., Japan) to calculate time-domain parameters (SDNN; standard deviation of NN intervals, RMSSD; root mean square of successive differences, pNN50; the proportion of NN50 divided by the number of NN (R-R) intervals) [15].

Skin temperature

Skin temperatures before and during sleep were measured using sensors (N543R, NIKKISOTHERM Co., Ltd.) attached to the surface of the skin of the right side of the chest (proximal site) and the right foot (distal site). The distal–proximal skin temperature gradient (DPG) was also assessed [21]. Data collection began at 11:30 pm and continued until 7:00 am the following morning.

Statistical Analysis

All data are presented as means ± SDs. Sleep variables, HR, and HRV variables were compared using oneway analysis of variance (ANOVA). When a significant main effect was evident, the post hoc Tukey test was performed to identify differences. Changes in skin temperature were compared using two-way repeated-measures ANOVA (condition, time) followed by post hoc testing. The amount of weight lifted was compared using a paired ttest. Effect sizes (ESs) were calculated to indicate the magnitude of differences (partial η2 values for ANOVA). For all tests, a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Workload During Resistance Exercise session

The total workload (weight lifted × number of repetitions) was 12191 ± 2359 kg in MST and 15465 ± 6418 kg in HYP, with no significant difference between the two conditions (P = 0.07). The number of repetitions in total was significantly lower in MST (4.4 ± 0.7 reps) than in HYP (8.7 ± 2.0 reps, P < 0.01).

Sleep Architecture

Table 1 shows the results of sleeprelated parameters. Sleep efficiency, total sleep time, SOL, and REM latency, did not differ significantly among the three conditions. In addition, absolute (min) and relative (%) time spent in WASO and nonREM sleep (N1, N2, N3) did not differ significantly among the three conditions. While absolute time of REM sleep was significantly shorter in MST than in CON, relative time did not differ significantly among conditions.

HRV

Table 2 summarizes the HR and HRV variables during the entire night (7 h). HR and time-domain HRV variables (i.e., SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50) during the night did not differ among conditions.

Table 3 shows the HR and HRV variables during the first 90 min after bedtime. HR, and time-domain HRV variables did not differ significantly among conditions.

Skin Temperature

Figure 1 shows the time-course changes in skin temperature during the night. Proximal skin temperatures tended to be higher in HYP and MST than in CON, but no significant interaction or main effect of condition was observed. Distal skin temperature did not differ among conditions. For DPG, a significant main effect of time was observed (P < 0.01), but these were not significantly different among conditions, with no significant interaction or main effect of condition.

Table 4 shows average skin temperatures during the first 90 min after bedtime. Proximal skin temperatures were significantly higher in both HYP and MST than in CON (P < 0.05), with no significant difference between HYP and MST. Distal skin temperature and DPG did not differ significantly among conditions.

DISCUSSION

A key finding of the present study was that sleep architecture was not markedly altered following MST and HYP. Although absolute time of REM sleep was significantly shorter in MST than in CON, most sleep variables, including sleep latency, time spent in deep sleep (N3), and WASO, did not differ significantly among conditions. These findings imply that daytime resistance exercise does not markedly disturb sleep architecture, regardless of the resistance exercise regimen (MST or HYP).

Exercise has previously been shown to reduce REM sleep duration [22-24]. Frish et al. [25] reported that high-intensity interval exercise reduced relative time of REM sleep. In line with these findings, we also observed reduction of absolute REM sleep in the MST condition. However, no significant difference among conditions was found for relative time of REM sleep. Also, like several acute phase previous studies [15,24], the time spent in N3 did not differ significantly among conditions. Shioda et al. [15] reported that acute resistance exercise (5 sets of 10 repetitions at 75% of 1RM) did not affect N3 duration. By contrast, moderate-to-high intensity endurance exercise has been shown to increase N3. For example, Driver and Taylor [22]. reported that endurance exercise at 50–70% of VO2max for an average of 2.5 h or at 75% of VO2max for 1.2 h increased N3. These findings imply that exercise modality (endurance exercise vs. resistance exercise) influences sleep quality following a single exercise session.

Reduction of absolute REM sleep may be associated with increased exercise-induced stress [26]. However, caution is necessary since no significant difference among conditions was found for relative time of REM sleep. HRV reflects autonomic nervous activity, and nocturnal HRV can be used to indicate the magnitude of exercise-induced stress [27]. In our study, MST and HYP did not affect nocturnal HRV, with no significant differences compared to CON. Similarly, Shioda et al. [15] reported that acute resistance exercise (5 sets of 10 repetitions at 75% of 1RM) did not affect nocturnal HRV. According to a review article, resistance exercise (85% of 1RM) appears to have no effect on nocturnal HRV in healthy young adults, who do not have sleep disorders [28], implying that nocturnal HRV is not altered markedly following resistance exercise.

Although we were not able to determine core body temperature during sleep, we did evaluate DPG. During daytime, DPG generally has negative values but gradually increases toward zero before bedtime [30]. In the present study, no significant differences among conditions were found for the time-course changes in DPG. However, proximal skin temperatures immediately before bedtime and during the first 90 min after bedtime were significantly elevated in both HYP and MST compared to the CON condition, implying that daytime resistance exercise increased proximal skin temperature at night. The elevated proximal skin temperature may have reflected elevated core body temperature [29], but this did not impair sleep quality.

The present study had several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small. Consequently, the lack of statistically significant differences in some outcomes may reflect limited statistical power to detect subtle physiological responses among conditions. Second, because diet and sleep environment were strictly controlled, the results may not completely reflect free-living conditions. Therefore, caution is required when applying the findings to athletes in real-world settings. Third, it is difficult to determine which specific exercise components (e.g., exercise intensity, number of repetitions, and rest periods between sets) contributed to the observed findings. Fourth, the present study utilized sleep EEG to assess sleep architecture as the primary outcome and emphasized objective measurements. However, future studies should incorporate subjective assessments of sleep quality, recovery status, fatigue, and next-day sleepiness alongside objective physiological measures. Fifth, because all participants were healthy and free of sleep disorders, our findings cannot be generalized to populations with impaired sleep quality or sleep disorders. Sixth, all participants were male. Because sex hormones influence thermoregulation [30], caution is necessary when applying these findings to other populations. Seventh, sleep time was set from 12:00 am-7:00 am for all participants. Total sleep time data may have varied across MST, HYP, and CON if participants were allowed to sleep until natural awakening. In addition, HRV analysis was limited to time-domain parameters. Future studies incorporating frequency-domain and sleep stage-specific analyses may provide further insight into nocturnal autonomic regulation following resistance exercise. Furthermore, the lack of an adaptation night or baseline sleep assessment might affect sleep architecture. Finally, because we focused on the impact of a single session of resistance exercise, further investigation of both short and long-term resistance exercise programs is warranted.

In spite of limitations of the study, the present findings provide practical implications for athletes, coaches and practitioners. Resistance training is widely implemented in athletic populations, often in combination with other training modalities. Based on the present findings, overall sleep architecture and nocturnal autonomic nervous activity are largely preserved regardless of resistance exercise regimens. This indicates that MST and HYP can be incorporated into daily training schedules without substantial concern for impairing sleep quality. Future studies should determine the cumulative effects of repeated resistance exercise sessions to further elucidate the interaction between resistance exercise and sleep regulation.

In conclusion, both MST and HYP increased proximal skin temperature during the early phase (90 min) of sleep in young males. However, the different resistance exercise regimens (i.e., MST, HYP) did not affect most other sleep variables or sleeprelated physiological measures (total sleep time, sleep efficiency, SOL, REM latency, WASO, relative time of REM, N1, N2, N3). These findings suggest that sleep architecture is not altered markedly following resistance exercise, regardless of the exercise regimen.
