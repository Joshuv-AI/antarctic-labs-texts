Prospective external validation of ADNEX and RMI for estimating risk of malignancy in adnexal masses in general gynecological settings: Danish multicenter study
Authors: I M Dueholm Hjorth, B H Bech, A Ledger, C Møller, T Van den Bosch, O Mogensen, Collaborators, P Bor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe primary aims of this study were to validate externally the diagnostic performance of the Assessment of Different NEoplasias in the adneXa (ADNEX) model for distinguishing between malignant and benign adnexal masses, when used by non‐expert ultrasound examiners in general gynecological settings with a low prevalence of ovarian cancer, and to compare the performance of ADNEX with that of the Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI) and serum CA 125 alone. The secondary aim was to assess model performance in detecting early‐stage ovarian malignancy.MethodsThis was a prospective multicenter external validation study conducted in all gynecological departments (five hospitals) in the Central Denmark Region between May 2021 and December 2022, including all consecutive patients diagnosed with a non‐physiological adnexal mass on transvaginal and/or transabdominal ultrasound. Standardized ultrasound terminology developed by the International Ovarian Tumor Analysis (IOTA) group was implemented uniformly across study sites, all of which received training in IOTA terminology before and during the study period. IOTA certification was offered to clinicians performing the ultrasound examinations. Sonographic features according to IOTA terminology were recorded systematically using a structured electronic form integrated into the medical record, which automatically calculated the RMI score and ADNEX risk estimates. The reference standard was histopathological diagnosis within 12 months after the inclusion scan. For patients managed expectantly, the reference standard was determined by follow‐up during a 1‐year observation period. This included serial ultrasound examinations and review of medical records and national histopathology records. Ovarian cancer, borderline tumors and metastases to the ovary were considered malignant. Areas under the receiver‐operating‐characteristic curve (AUCs) for ADNEX, RMI and CA 125 were calculated and compared using meta‐analysis of center‐specific results and DeLong's test. Diagnostic metrics were estimated at different cut‐offs for any‐stage and early‐stage malignancy. Clinical utility for guiding referral was assessed by decision‐curve analysis, with net benefit calculated over a range of risk thresholds (1–50%).ResultsA total of 1815 patients diagnosed with an adnexal mass on ultrasound examination were included, of whom 1656 were examined by non‐experts in general gynecological settings (59.2% postmenopausal, 8.3% prevalence of malignancy). The AUC of the ADNEX model (0.93 (95% CI, 0.90–0.95) with CA 125 and 0.92 (95% CI, 0.89–0.94) without CA 125) was significantly greater than that of the RMI (0.88 (95% CI, 0.85–0.91)) and CA 125 alone (0.82 (95% CI, 0.77–0.87)). Including CA 125 did not improve the discriminative ability of ADNEX for identifying malignancy. For diagnosing early‐stage malignancy, ADNEX without CA 125 had an AUC of 0.90 (95% CI, 0.85–0.94), which was significantly greater than that of the RMI (0.82 (95% CI, 0.76–0.88)) and CA 125 alone (0.73 (95% CI, 0.65–0.80)). At a cut‐off of 5%, the ADNEX model without CA 125 detected 84.4% (95% CI, 69.0–93.0%) of early‐stage ovarian malignancies, with a specificity of 81.6% (95% CI, 76.9–85.6%). Clinical‐decision curves showed that ADNEX had clinical utility superior to that of the RMI at a cut‐off score of 200 over the entire range of risk thresholds.ConclusionsThe ADNEX model demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance for identifying adnexal malignancy when used prospectively in routine clinical practice in a general gynecological setting by non‐expert ultrasound examiners trained in IOTA terminology. ADNEX significantly outperformed the RMI and serum CA 125 alone, including in the detection of early‐stage ovarian malignancy. Including CA 125 in the ADNEX model did not provide additional diagnostic benefit for estimating the likelihood of malignancy. © 2026 The Author(s). INTRODUCTION

Optimal management of adnexal masses relies on the initial ultrasound assessment, which is typically performed by general gynecologists (non‐expert examiners) in outpatient clinics or private practices, rather than by imaging subspecialists 1 , 2 . Lesions of the ovaries, Fallopian tubes and surrounding connective tissues are common in women of all ages 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 . Critically, patients with malignancy must be referred to an onco‐gynecological center for specialized treatment to improve outcomes 11 , 12 . Benign masses can be managed expectantly 2 , 10 , 13 , 14 or removed using minimally invasive surgery to preserve fertility and reduce the risk of morbidity 15 , 16 , 17 . An initial ultrasound examination of adequate diagnostic quality enables appropriate triage and referral of high‐risk patients for a second‐line (expert) ultrasound examination or magnetic resonance imaging 18 .

Ultrasound‐based diagnostic prediction models have been developed to support clinical decision‐making via risk stratification. The Risk of Malignancy Index (RMI) serves as the standard for guiding patient management in several countries 19 , 20 . The RMI combines simple ultrasound features with menopausal status and serum CA 125 level into a score used for consideration of onco‐gynecological referral 21 . However, meta‐analyses have demonstrated limited performance of the RMI, particularly in premenopausal women and those with early‐stage malignancy 22 , 23 . The International Ovarian Tumor Analysis (IOTA) group established standardized nomenclature for describing adnexal lesions 24 and subsequently developed diagnostic prediction models, including the high‐performing Assessment of Different NEoplasias in the adneXa (ADNEX) model 2 , 25 , 26 . The ADNEX model estimates the overall risk of malignancy and the risk of four malignant subtypes using six ultrasound features, patient age and type of center, with the optional inclusion of CA 125 25 .

The case mix of tumors and of the training and experience of ultrasound examiners in recognizing and reporting sonographic features influence the diagnostic performance of the prediction models 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 . The ADNEX model was developed using data from ultrasound experts examining high‐risk patients at onco‐gynecological centers 25 . The diagnostic performance of ADNEX and RMI in routine gynecological practice, in which non‐expert examiners care for patients with predominantly benign masses, has not been investigated thoroughly 23 , 31 . A critical diagnostic challenge in this setting is distinguishing early‐stage adnexal malignancy from benign lesions; advanced cancers are easier to diagnose clinically.

This study aimed primarily to validate externally the ADNEX model, used in general gynecological settings, for distinguishing between malignant and benign adnexal masses, and to compare the performance of ADNEX with that of the RMI and serum CA 125 alone. Secondarily, we aimed to assess the diagnostic performance of the models in detecting early‐stage ovarian malignancy.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This was a prospective multicenter external validation study, in which all medical doctors working in the participating centers consecutively included all women diagnosed with a non‐physiological adnexal mass on ultrasound between May 2021 and December 2022. Inclusion generally required performance of both a transabdominal and transvaginal ultrasound examination, description of the ultrasound appearance of the mass using standardized IOTA terminology, and entering of all the variables needed to calculate the RMI score 21 and the ADNEX risk estimate 25 (among other ultrasound variables) into a form for systematic ultrasound description (Appendix S1). This form was integrated into the electronic patient record and allowed for automatic calculation of the RMI score and ADNEX risk estimates. The results of the RMI and ADNEX model were available immediately to the ultrasound examiner and were used for clinical management.

The study setting was the gynecological departments of all five hospitals in the Central Denmark Region, covering a population of approximately 1.4 million inhabitants. A general gynecological setting was defined as a clinical environment in which ultrasound examinations are performed by gynecologists and supervised residents in gynecology without subspecialty training in advanced gynecological imaging and working outside of tertiary oncology or dedicated imaging centers, i.e. Level‐I–II ultrasound examiners according to the European Federation of Societies for Ultrasound in Medicine and Biology (EFSUMB) 32 . These settings typically comprise outpatient clinics receiving referrals from general practitioners in primary care or private gynecologists, and are characterized by a low prevalence of ovarian cancer. The study sites included four regional hospitals (general gynecological settings) and one university hospital (Aarhus University Hospital), which consists of a benign and acute gynecological unit (general gynecological setting) and a separate onco‐gynecological unit (tertiary referral center). Patients referred directly to the onco‐gynecological unit were considered a high‐risk cohort; they were evaluated exclusively by an ultrasound expert and were not included in our primary analysis.

The reporting of this study adheres to the Transparent Reporting of a multivariable prediction model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) guidelines for validation studies and Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy (STARD) guidelines for diagnostic accuracy studies 33 , 34 . This investigation was approved by the Ethical Committee of Central Denmark Region (reference: 1‐10‐72‐1‐21) as a quality improvement study, which, according to Danish legislation and General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), does not require patient consent. All participating hospitals approved the investigation and data usage. The study was registered in the Internal Registry of the Central Denmark Region and with ClinicalTrials.gov (reference: NCT05842629), and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki 35 . The study objective and design were discussed with a representative from the Danish Patient Foundation for Gynecological Cancer prior to initiation.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were the presence of a non‐physiological adnexal mass (i.e. a lesion inconsistent with normal physiology 24 ) identified on transvaginal and usually transabdominal ultrasonography and diagnosed in a general gynecological unit. Only the first ultrasound examination for each patient was included. One adnexal mass was included per patient; if more than one mass was present, only the most complex mass on ultrasound was considered. Patients were included regardless of management strategy.

Exclusion criteria were masses deemed to be physiological or non‐adnexal at the time of the ultrasound examination, and patients with an existing diagnosis of malignancy. Further exclusion criteria were an uncertain reference standard owing to death within 12 months after the inclusion scan if an adnexal malignancy had not been diagnosed before death and no autopsy was available, or if the patient had a mass suspicious for malignancy but could not or would not undergo treatment. The study did not include patients seen in obstetric or fetal medicine units unless they had been referred to a gynecological unit or if a gynecologist was consulted.

Implementation

The RMI was the standard for guiding the management of adnexal masses in all centers, and clinicians were accustomed to using this model. Gynecologists in the region had been offered the official IOTA course and IOTA certification between 2017 and 2020 36 . All gynecologists and residents in gynecology were introduced to the IOTA criteria, the ADNEX model and the electronic form for systematic ultrasound description (Appendix S1) during regular teaching sessions in each department and by provision of written and electronic materials. Additionally, monthly online 1‐h ovarian tumor case conferences were held with gynecologists and residents in gynecology from all the participating hospitals to support ultrasound learning. Systematic ultrasound description and use of dedicated software for automatic calculation of the RMI score and ADNEX risk estimates formed part of standard care for patients with an adnexal mass.

Ultrasound examination

Transvaginal and transabdominal ultrasound examinations were performed according to regional guidelines. The medical doctor who examined the patient immediately described ultrasound features in the electronic form for systematic ultrasound description. All input boxes in the form had to be completed in order to save the form. The time at which the electronic form was saved was recorded automatically. The ultrasound examiner could use pattern recognition 37 to suggest a specific diagnosis in the form. Additional fields were available for free‐text descriptions of ultrasound findings. Ultrasound examinations were performed using standard intermediate‐to‐high‐end machines available routinely in the participating departments.

Clinical management

The same doctors who performed the ultrasound examinations used the RMI scores and ADNEX risk estimates, alongside relevant clinical information, to decide the management and treatment of all patients, according to regional guidelines. Asymptomatic, benign‐looking masses were managed expectantly. Expectantly managed patients were offered a follow‐up examination, including an ultrasound scan, every 3–4 months. Patients with a symptomatic, benign‐looking mass were offered surgery. Patients with a mass suspected of being malignant, namely those with a RMI > 200, were referred to the onco‐gynecological unit. Additionally, ultrasound examiners could confer with onco‐gynecologists on an individual basis and refer patients with a mass deemed suspicious for malignancy, based on ADNEX risk estimates, clinical information or pattern recognition 37 . Postmenopausal status was established by patient reporting of at least 12 months of amenorrhea unexplained by disease or medication, or age ≥ 50 years in women who had previously undergone hysterectomy.

Data collection and management

Data collection and management were handled in accordance with GDPR using Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) 38 , 39 . Details of data management are presented in Appendix S2. Consistent with regional guidelines, the level of serum CA 125 (kU/L) was usually measured at the time of the diagnosis using an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay. If CA 125 level was not available at the time of the ultrasound examination, it could be added to the form for systematic ultrasound description afterwards. However, CA 125 measurement was not mandatory for inclusion in this study.

Index tests

The formulae for RMI version 1 (Appendix S3) 21 and ADNEX (Appendix S4) 25 were applied prospectively. For the RMI, a cut‐off score of 200 is used in Denmark for referral to an onco‐gynecological center 19 , 40 . Universal thresholds for patient management based on ADNEX risk estimates have not been established, but a cut‐off of 10% for referral to an onco‐gynecological center has been recommended in a joint consensus statement 2 .

By design, the reference standard was not available until after patient inclusion, registration of ultrasound features and application of models. Thus, at the time of the index test, examiners were blinded to the reference standard.

Reference standard

The histopathological diagnosis (based on guidelines of the World Health Organization 41 ) from surgery or a representative biopsy performed within 12 months after inclusion served as the reference standard. A central histopathology review was conducted by specialized onco‐gynecological pathologists for all patients with a malignant, borderline or inconclusive tumor result. Borderline ovarian tumors were grouped as malignant for the primary analysis in line with the approach adopted by the IOTA group in the development of the ADNEX model 25 . Stage of malignancy was defined according to the International Federation of Gynaecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) classification system 42 . If the patient did not undergo upfront surgery, the onco‐gynecological team determined the FIGO stage based on imaging. The reference standard was deemed malignant if the patient was diagnosed with malignancy in the pelvis within 12 months of inclusion, provided the anatomical location and laterality were consistent with ultrasound findings. In the absence of a histopathological tissue specimen, cytological diagnosis of malignancy most likely originating from the adnexa was sufficient to confirm a malignant reference standard.

Masses managed expectantly were followed for 12 months. The reference standard of masses managed expectantly was deemed benign in the absence of signs of pelvic malignancy within a 12‐month observation period with sequential ultrasound examinations. Follow‐up included review of clinical findings and examinations recorded in the electronic patient medical file and the national histopathology bank. The national histopathology bank lists full histopathology reports available immediately upon completion of histopathological examinations performed in Denmark, including biopsies and surgeries conducted in any private or public clinic. The reference standard was assessed blinded to index‐test results.

Statistical analysis

The sample size was determined to achieve 80% power (Type‐II error = 0.2) at a 5% two‐sided significance level (Type‐I error = 0.05). To detect a difference in sensitivity between RMI (assumed sensitivity of 75%) and ADNEX (assumed sensitivity of 90%) at a fixed specificity of 80% using McNemar's test for paired proportions, assuming that 30% of malignant cases would yield discordant results between the two tests (i.e. the proportion of discordant positive results among malignancies, p10+p01, ≈ 0.3), at least 103 malignant cases were required. A predefined analysis plan was followed. Missing CA 125 values were handled by multiple imputation (Appendix S5).

Primary analysis

To assess the ability of ADNEX (with and without CA 125), RMI and CA 125 alone to discriminate between benign and malignant adnexal masses, estimations of receiver‐operating‐characteristics (ROC) curves were generated. The estimations were based on each of the imputations, combined using Rubin's rules for each center, and then combined using meta‐analysis. Areas under the ROC curves (AUCs) with 95% CI were estimated in a similar manner. From the meta‐analysis, we also reported the 95% prediction intervals (95% PIs). 95% PIs are an estimate of heterogeneity between centers, and provide an indication of the expected performance in a new center 43 . Using the DeLong paired test, we calculated the difference in AUCs using meta‐analysis for the five centers 44 . We calculated center‐specific sensitivity, specificity and positive (PPV) and negative (NPV) predictive values for prespecified ADNEX risk‐of‐malignancy thresholds (1%, 3%, 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, 30%) used to guide clinical management. These were compared with corresponding performance metrics at standard RMI score cut‐offs (25, 50, 100, 150, 200, 250) and CA 125 at a cut‐off of 35 kU/L (the Danish upper reference limit). We combined the center‐specific sensitivities, specificities, PPVs and NPVs using meta‐analysis. Clinical utility for deciding which patients to refer for specialized onco‐gynecological care was estimated by calculating the net benefit for risk thresholds of 1–50%. The net benefit of the model at each risk threshold was plotted on a decision curve alongside lines for the treat‐all and treat‐none strategies (representing refer all or refer none to an onco‐gynecological center, respectively). A model can be considered clinically useful if it is superior to treat‐all and treat‐none strategies at the relevant risk threshold 45 . The method with the greatest clinical utility was considered the model with the highest net benefit over the entire range of risk thresholds 46 .

We calculated the differences in sensitivity at fixed specificity, and differences in specificity at fixed sensitivity, between RMI and ADNEX (with and without CA 125). We tested for statistically significant differences using McNemar's test for paired proportions.

To assess the calibration of ADNEX with and without CA 125, we constructed flexible multicenter calibration curves using a locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) function 47 . To evaluate the multinomial performance of ADNEX, pairwise AUCs for differentiating between pairs of tumor subtypes were calculated on a pooled dataset (i.e. without meta‐analysis) using the conditional‐risk method 48 .

Secondary analysis

A secondary analysis evaluated the diagnostic performance of the models for discriminating between early‐stage malignancy (FIGO Stage‐I–II borderline ovarian tumors or ovarian cancer) and benign masses. We used FIGO Stage‐I–II malignancy as the positive reference standard, and benign outcome as the negative reference standard.

Sensitivity analysis

All patients referred to the onco‐gynecological unit with suspected ovarian cancer during the study period underwent an expert ultrasound examination utilizing the form for systematic ultrasound description. For patients first examined in a general gynecological setting and later referred to the onco‐gynecological unit, only the initial non‐expert examination was used in our analysis. The sensitivity analysis additionally included the expert examination conducted in patients who were referred directly to the onco‐gynecological unit (e.g. from other hospital departments), who lacked a non‐expert ultrasound assessment. Therefore, the sensitivity analysis included the full cohort and treated the expert assessments as an additional center, resulting in six centers in the analysis.

Statistical analysis was performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp. LLC, College Station, TX, USA) and R version 4.4.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

The study objective and design were discussed with a representative from the Danish Patient Foundation for Gynecological Cancer prior to initiation.

RESULTS

Patient cohort

A total of 1815 consecutive patients were entered initially in the study (Figure 1). Thirteen patients were excluded owing to an unavailable reference standard, because of death, comorbidity or patient choice. Therefore, the complete cohort comprised 1802 patients. Patients who were not examined in a general gynecological setting but were referred directly to the onco‐gynecological unit (n = 146) were excluded from the primary and secondary analyses. Accordingly, the primary cohort with a non‐expert ultrasound evaluation comprised 1656 patients from five hospitals. The median age was 57.0 (interquartile range, 42.3–71.1) years, and 59.2% were postmenopausal (Table 1). Each hospital included between 187 and 515 patients during the study period. The patients reported the following dominant symptoms and complaints at the time of their first ultrasound scan: no symptoms, 461/1656 (27.8%); abdominal pain, 462/1656 (27.9%); abnormal uterine bleeding, 197/1656 (11.9%); urinary symptoms, 185/1656 (11.2%); abdominal distension or palpable abdominal mass, 147/1656 (8.9%); weight loss, 78/1656 (4.7%); gastrointestinal complaints (e.g. constipation, dyspepsia), 30/1656 (1.8%); and other symptom (e.g. dyspnea, tiredness), 96/1656 (5.8%).

Ultrasound examiners

In 315/1656 (19.0%) patients, the primary ultrasound examiner was IOTA certified; in 348/1656 (21.0%) patients, the primary examiner was a specialist in gynecology without IOTA certification; and in the remaining 993/1656 (60.0%) patients, the primary examiner was a resident in gynecology. A colleague provided supervision in 811/1656 (49.0%) patients: in 324/811 (40.0%) of these cases, the supervisor was IOTA certified; in 414/811 (51.0%), the supervisor was a gynecological consultant without IOTA certification; and in 73/811 (9.0%), the supervisor was a senior resident in gynecology.

Follow‐up information was available for all expectantly managed patients, and histopathology was available for all surgically managed patients. The prevalence of malignancy was 137/1656 (8.3%), and most malignancies occurred in postmenopausal patients (109/980 (11.1%) vs 28/676 (4.1%)).

Primary analysis: detection of malignancy

Discriminative performance

ADNEX with CA 125 had the best discriminative ability for the diagnosis of any ovarian malignancy (AUC, 0.93 (95% CI, 0.90–0.95)), followed by ADNEX without CA 125 (AUC, 0.92 (95% CI, 0.89–0.94)) (Figure 2a). The RMI had an AUC of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.85–0.91) and CA 125 alone had an AUC of 0.82 (95% CI, 0.77–0.87). Compared with the RMI, the AUC was significantly greater for ADNEX with CA 125 (ΔAUC, 0.05 (95% CI, 0.02–0.06); P < 0.01) and ADNEX without CA 125 (ΔAUC, 0.04 (95% CI, 0.01–0.06); P = 0.02). The AUC did not differ significantly between ADNEX with CA 125 and ADNEX without CA 125 (ΔAUC, 0.01 (95% CI, 0.00–0.01); P = 0.07). The 95% PI was narrower for both ADNEX with CA 125 (95% PI, 0.87–0.97) and ADNEX without CA 125 (95% PI, 0.86–0.96) compared with the RMI (95% PI, 0.81–0.95).

Classification performance

Setting the cut‐off at 10% for ADNEX with or without CA 125 yielded sensitivities of 81.6% (95% CI, 69.0–89.8%) and 80.1% (95% CI, 64.9–89.8%), respectively, with corresponding specificities of 88.3% (95% CI, 83.4–91.8%) and 87.9% (95% CI, 83.5–91.2%) (Table 3). At a cut‐off of 5%, ADNEX without CA 125 had a sensitivity of 89.6% (95% CI, 81.6–94.3%) and a specificity of 81.9% (95% CI, 77.2–85.8%) for the detection of malignancy; the corresponding values at a 1% cut‐off were 98.4% (95% CI, 93.1–99.6%) and 24.0% (95% CI, 19.3–29.5%). To reach a sensitivity of 90% using the RMI, a cut‐off score of < 25 would have to be used, with a corresponding specificity of < 70%. A NPV of 98.8–99.0% could be reached using a cut‐off of 5% for ADNEX with or without CA 125, while a cut‐off score of < 25 was needed to achieve a comparable NPV of 98% for the RMI.

Performance of ADNEX for discrimination between tumor types

Pairwise AUCs for the differentiation between tumor subgroups (benign, borderline, Stage‐I invasive malignancy, Stage‐II–IV invasive malignancy and metastasis) by the ADNEX model, with and without CA 125, are presented in Table S3. The diagnostic performance of the ADNEX model was lowest for discriminating between Stage‐I ovarian cancer and borderline ovarian tumors (AUC, 0.64 (95% CI, 0.49–0.77) with CA 125 and AUC, 0.66 (95% CI, 0.51–0.78) without CA 125). Including CA 125 improved discrimination between Stage‐II–IV and Stage‐I cancer (AUC, 0.85 (95% CI, 0.74–0.92) with CA 125 and AUC, 0.75 (95% CI, 0.63–0.84) without CA 125), and between Stage‐II–IV cancer and metastases (AUC, 0.76 (95% CI, 0.60–0.87) with CA 125 and AUC, 0.66 (95% CI, 0.50–0.79) without CA 125). For the remaining pairs of tumor subtypes, discriminative ability was comparable for ADNEX with and without CA 125.

Comparison with model development cohorts

A key difference between this study and the model development studies 21 , 25 is our introduction of the models into routine management. The examiners in the ADNEX development study were ultrasound experts, whereas those in our primary cohort were not. The prevalence of malignancy was 8.3% in our primary cohort, compared with 32.6% in the ADNEX development study and 29.4% in the RMI development study 21 , 25 (Table S4). Our primary cohort included a larger proportion of postmenopausal patients (59.2%) compared with the ADNEX development study (41.0%). The present study included patients with an expectantly managed mass, whereas the development studies did not. Further comparison is presented in Appendix S6.

Secondary analysis: detection of early‐stage malignancy

On ROC‐curve analysis, ADNEX without CA 125 (AUC, 0.90 (95% CI, 0.85–0.94)) and ADNEX with CA 125 (AUC, 0.89 (95% CI, 0.84–0.94)) performed better than the RMI (AUC, 0.82 (95% CI, 0.76–0.88)) and CA 125 alone (AUC, 0.73 (95% CI, 0.65–0.80)) for discriminating between early‐stage malignancy (FIGO Stage‐I–II ovarian cancer and borderline tumors) and benign masses (Figure 2b). The difference in AUC between ADNEX with CA 125 and the RMI was 0.077 (95% CI, 0.038–0.117) (P < 0.001), and that between ADNEX without CA 125 and the RMI was 0.079 (95% CI, 0.036–0.122) (P < 0.001). The performance was similar for ADNEX with and without CA 125 (ΔAUC, −0.005 (95% CI, −0.010 to 0.001) (P = 0.098).

Using a cut‐off of 10%, the ADNEX model without CA 125 had a sensitivity of 71.5% (95% CI, 49.6–86.4%) and a specificity of 87.8% (95% CI, 83.3–91.2%) for the detection of early‐stage malignancy (Table 5). Meanwhile, a 5% cut‐off for ADNEX without CA 125 detected 84.4% (95% CI, 69.0–93.0%) of patients with early‐stage malignancy, with a corresponding specificity of 81.6% (95% CI, 76.9–85.6%).

Sensitivity analysis

The findings of the primary analysis were confirmed in the sensitivity analysis including the full cohort (n = 1802), in which the prevalence of malignancy was 13.8% (Figure S1, Tables S5–S8). ADNEX significantly outperformed the RMI and CA 125 alone in detecting ovarian malignancy. The calibration curves and clinical utility assessment also corroborated the primary analysis (Figures S2 and S3). The diagnostic models showed higher sensitivities and lower specificities at each risk cut‐off in this complete cohort compared with the primary cohort.

DISCUSSION

The ADNEX model outperformed RMI and serum CA 125 alone across all performance metrics when applied by non‐expert ultrasound examiners in general gynecological settings with a low prevalence of malignancy. ADNEX also showed superior performance in distinguishing early‐stage malignancies from benign tumors.

Strengths of this study include the large, well‐defined, prospective cohort representative of general gynecological settings, characterized by a low prevalence of malignancy (8.3%) and a high proportion of early‐stage disease (58.2%). Training in IOTA terminology was provided systematically to all centers. A standardized electronic form using IOTA terminology enabled consistent documentation of ultrasound findings. Non‐expert examiners applied the models and used risk estimates and scores in clinical management. Use of the models in routine clinical practice with consecutive patient inclusion minimized selection and disease‐spectrum bias. Completeness of inclusion was verified using regional registry data. A national pathology bank and shared electronic patient medical files enabled complete follow‐up for both expectantly and surgically managed patients. Sensitivity analyses support the robustness of our results.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, local conditions may limit external validity. The population was predominantly Caucasian and was examined using intermediate‐to‐high‐quality ultrasound equipment. Our findings may not be generalizable to settings with lower‐quality machines or predominantly non‐Caucasian populations, which may exhibit a different distribution of benign histopathological diagnoses (e.g. fibroids). In line with national standards, CA 125 values were available during some examinations and, very rarely, patients had been referred based on a computed tomography (CT) scan ordered by their general practitioner. This reflects clinical reality; however, knowledge of CA 125 level or CT findings may have influenced ultrasound interpretation in unpredictable ways. Minor data‐entry errors by the non‐expert examiners were corrected prior to analysis in a process blinded to outcome; any impact on performance estimates was probably small and not necessarily directional. Regardless of management, patients were followed up for 12 months (with a maximum of 12 months between inclusion and surgery), providing systematic outcome assessment for all patients. This avoided bias from excluding expectantly managed patients and reflects clinical practice. Yet, this approach may have introduced differential verification bias 49 . During the 12‐month follow‐up, de‐novo malignancies or malignant transformations could have occurred, or baseline malignancies might have remained undetected. Although these events are probably rare 13 , 14 , 50 , and unlikely to affect RMI or ADNEX selectively, performance estimates may have been affected. Nevertheless, identification of an elevated risk of malignancy in masses that transform within 1 year may still be clinically relevant. Finally, the low prevalence of malignancy widened 95% CIs for sensitivity measures.

A Cochrane analysis of ultrasound models for the diagnosis of ovarian cancer underlined the absence of well‐performed external validation studies in non‐specialist and secondary referral settings 23 . In a systematic review of studies comparing RMI with ADNEX, most studies showed a high risk of bias 51 . The single study (conducted by the IOTA group) deemed to have a low risk of bias reported AUC estimates of 0.94 (95% CI, 0.92–0.96) for ADNEX with CA 125 and 0.89 (95% CI, 0.85–0.92) for RMI, consistent with our findings 26 . Two recent prospective studies evaluated patients managed surgically or expectantly 52 , 53 . One such study, a UK study of 1242 postmenopausal patients with a 17% prevalence of malignancy, showed no significant differences in AUC between ADNEX with CA 125, RMI and CA 125 alone 52 . Using a 10% cut‐off for ADNEX and 250 for RMI, the authors favored ADNEX for higher sensitivity. The UK study had lower volumes (on average, 18 patients) per hospital per year compared with the present study, and examiners were sonographers rather than medical doctors 52 . Our study differed in design, inclusion criteria and cohort, and showed a clearer benefit of ADNEX over the RMI. We did not perform subgroup analysis by menopausal status, but postmenopausal patients comprised 59.2% of our primary cohort. Menopausal status and patient age are included as variables in RMI and ADNEX. The second study was a Spanish single‐center study of 2039 patients with a 2% prevalence of malignancy, who were predominantly undergoing gynecological‐cancer screening by experienced ultrasound examiners as part of the IOTA‐5 framework 53 . The AUC for ADNEX without CA 125 was 0.95 (95% CI, 0.89–0.98). The Spanish study reported a sensitivity for ADNEX without CA 125 of 82% at a 10% cut‐off, consistent with our findings, whereas the corresponding specificity of 96% exceeded that in our cohort, probably reflecting differences in populations and examiner expertise.

Our findings support the use of ADNEX as a first‐line diagnostic tool in general gynecological settings for individualized risk assessment and management. Additionally, the results suggest that inclusion of CA 125 does not improve the distinction between benign and malignant masses in routine assessment conducted in non‐onco‐gynecological settings. Decision curves showed that ADNEX was clinically useful for selecting patients for referral to an onco‐gynecology center across the entire range of risk thresholds and had superior clinical utility compared with RMI at a cut‐off score of 200. Specific ADNEX cut‐off values may guide subsequent diagnostic work‐up and treatment of patients with an adnexal mass 2 . Our results indicate that a simple threshold of 10% for referral to an onco‐gynecological center would miss a substantial proportion (27.5–28.5%) of early‐stage malignancies; however, most (> 60%) patients would have a benign tumor at this threshold. Reliable diagnosis of early‐stage ovarian cancer and borderline tumors at the initial ultrasound examination is critical for timely and appropriate treatment. Ovarian malignancy outside the pelvis (Stage III–IV) is often diagnosed more easily without prediction models or high‐quality ultrasonography. Our results show that an ADNEX cut‐off of 5% detects 84% of early‐stage malignancies with a NPV of 99% and a specificity of 81–82%. In general gynecological settings, an intermediate‐risk interval for the ADNEX model (e.g. 5–20%) may identify patients who would benefit from second‐line diagnostic assessment, such as expert ultrasonography or magnetic resonance imaging, before referral for onco‐gynecological treatment. A 20% ADNEX cut‐off yielded a specificity > 90% (PPV, 47–50%) for diagnosing any malignancy and could be used to guide direct referral for onco‐gynecological treatment, albeit with lower sensitivity (71–75% for any malignancy and 63–65% for early‐stage malignancy). According to our data, surgical management at the 1% risk threshold, as recommended in a joint consensus statement 2 , would result in the surgical removal of 98% of malignant tumors but also 75–76% of all benign tumors. Importantly, local conditions should be considered when selecting cut‐offs for different management.

In conclusion, this study evaluated systematic ultrasound assessment and the ADNEX model in the routine assessment of adnexal masses in general gynecological settings by non‐expert examiners. The ADNEX model provided improved risk assessment and clinical utility for referral decisions compared with the RMI.
