Accurate and 30-plus days reliable cuffless blood pressure measurements with 9-minutes personal photoplethysmograph data and mixed deduction learning
Authors: Bitewulign Kassa Mekonnen, Wei‑Ru Lu, Tung‑Han Hsieh, Justin Chu, Fu-Liang Yang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCuffless blood pressure (BP) measurements have long been anticipated, and the PPG (Photoplethysmography)-only method is the most promising one since already embedded in many wearable devices. To further meet the clinical accuracy requirements, PPG-only BP predictions with personalized modeling for overcoming personal deviations have been widely studied, but all required tens to hundreds of minutes of personal PPG measurements for training. Moreover, their accurate test periods without calibration practice were not reported. In this work, we collected records of PPG data from our recruited subjects in real-life scenarios instead of relying on the openly available MIMIC dataset obtained from intensive care unit (ICU) patients. Since our objective is commercial application and a substantial reduction in training data, we tailored our model training to closely mimic real-world usage. To achieve this, we developed a training approach that only requires 9-minutes of personal PPG signal recordings and mixed with other PPG data from our recruited 364 subjects. The modeling is conducted with two-channel paired inputs to the convolutional neural network (CNN)-based model, which we called Mixed Deduction Learning (MDL). The test results of 88 samples from 15 subjects, under testing period up to 30-plus days without extra calibration, revealed that MDL meets most of the standards of AAMI, BHS, and IEEE 1708–2014 (for static test only) for BP measurement devices, which indicates MDL’s long-term stability and consistency. Furthermore, we found that the model with two-channel inputs presents a trend of improving performance as the pool of mixed training data increased, while the conventional one-channel input revealed degraded performance. The outperformance of MDL is attributed to many significant features remained in the first CNN layer even when mixing personal 9-minutes data with the other 364 subjects. Consequently, PPG-only with MDL introduces a new avenue for overcoming challenges in training due to personal physiological variations. Introduction

The current clinically accurate blood pressure measurements (BPM) are either the invasive arterial catheter measurements or the inflatable cuff-based non-invasive blood pressure (NIBP) meters. The former involves a catheter insertion into an artery to obtain continuous and precise BPM, which is considered the gold standard1, and is often used in exceptional clinical circumstances. Nevertheless, it is harshly invasive with risks of bleeding, necrosis, and infection, and is not applicable outside of clinical settings2. Due to its harmless and non-invasive characteristics, the latter method (NIBP), which is usually measured by a sphygmomanometer, has become the most preferred method of BPM in the last few decades. However, these devices only provide discrete BPM. Additionally, the recorded BP varies depending on patients’ preparation and positioning, and relies upon an inflatable cuff which may lead to discomfort and inconvenience during measurement2–5. In order to address these shortcomings, cuffless and better with wearable BPM devices are in high demand. Various attempts and studies in recent years have shown that nonobstructive sensors can measure BP without cuffs. A salient example is photoplethysmography (PPG), which is commonly found in most wearables. These devices detect blood volume changes in the microvasculature based on measuring near-infrared (NIR) reflections or transmissions by optical sensors6,7. It is clinically used in pulse oximetry for acquiring blood oxygen saturation8. Nowadays, it has been demonstrated to have remarkable potential for non-invasive blood glucose9–12 and BP2,6,7,13,14 measurements.

There are two approaches to acquire the estimated BP from PPG: (i) hybrid (multiple) sensors, and (ii) single sensor. In the first approach, combinations of two or more measurement data from multiple sensors were implemented to correlate to BP. For example, by combining PPG and Electrocardiography (ECG), one can calculate pulse wave transition time (PWTT), pulse wave velocity (PWV)2, and pulse arrival time (PAT) of arteries, which are good indices to correlate to BP2,6–8. On the other hand, for the single PPG sensor approach, a new innovative development recently emerged via the Pulse Wave Analysis (PWA) with deep learning. Through PWA, BP is predicted based on the morphology features extracted from the PPG waveform, which is one of the most appealing research topics in this area2.

Traditionally, there are two categories of deep learning models for BP prediction from PPG-only data; namely, (i) a global model trained on an aggregated dataset of the recruited subjects to make the BP prediction suitable for everyone. This approach requires a great deal of PPG data with the corresponding BP measurements collected from many subjects, which is then randomly split into training and testing sets for model development However, the large physiological and pathological variations among the individuals causes inconsistency in the features extracted from the PPG waveforms with respect to BP15. In addition, although some information learned through the global data can be generalized to the entire population, information specific to an individual may be missed, which may significantly reduce the prediction accuracy. Furthermore, the calibration of a global model could be a nontrivial issue. A comprehensive review of performance evaluation of cuffless BP measurement devices, including challenges and proposals, can be found in the reference55. Thus, (ii) a personalized model trained on limited datasets collected from the target subject makes the prediction applicable to the individual.

Recently, a few studies of personalized models with PPG-only data were developed. Figure 1 and Supplementary Data Table 1 compare these studies with our work. Except our work, in which the data for model building and testing was collected from our recruited subjects, all the other works adopted data from the publicly available MIMIC-III (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III) database16,17, which provide continuous records of PPG and BP data collected from patients in intensive care units (ICU). In Slapničar et al.18, 16 min of personal data (about 20% of 1.3 h of recordings per individual) from the MIMIC-III database were used to train the personalized model with a parallel architecture consisting of three residual neural networks (ResNet) for 510 patients. Chu et al.19 presented a personalized model of MLM (Masked Language Modeling)-Transformer developed with PPG data of 1,732 subjects. They performed unsupervised model pre-training using data of 1,385 subjects, and then finetuned the model using data of the remaining subjects, with a mean training data period (in the finetuning stage) of approximately 28 min (in average of 86 windows, each window has data of 20 s). They claimed that their results achieved the requirements of the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation (AAMI)20 and grades “A” of the British Hypertension Society (BHS)21 standards. Schrumpf et al.22 used approximately 133 min of personalized training data (20% of the 2000 samples from each targeted subject, each sample has data of length 20 s) to fine-tune the pre-trained neural network using the LSTM network. Leitner et al.23 developed a personalized model based on random forest (RF), with wavelet decomposition for relevant feature extraction from 480 min of PPG data (80% of 10 h data) of each patient for a total of 40 ICU patients. They claimed that their result met the AAMI standard. Later on, Leitner et al.13 presented another work of personalized BP-CRNN-Transfer algorithm with 300 min of re-training data (3600 windows, each window has data of 5 s) of 100 patients, based on a pre-trained model built with data of 50 subjects. They reported that their results met both AAMI and BHS grade “A” standards. Liu et al.54 applied both fully personalized model and a universal with personalize fine-tuning approach using MIMIC-III data set. On their best performing fully personalized model, they used approximately 4.2 h of data in 10 s segments to train the personalized bidirectional gated recurrent unit (BiGRU) model. They tested their performance on 15 subjects (7 with cardiovascular diseases and 8 without) with average data length of 14.16 h.

Most of the above-mentioned works relied on a constant stream of continuous PPG and arterial BP recordings collected from the specific clinical settings of ICU patients. Their physiological and pathological conditions are quite different to that of normal subjects. These differences could reveal in the deformed PPG signals, making the models learned the PPG waveform features not commonly seen in the normal population. Therefore, their approaches may not seamlessly translate to real-world applications before widely testing. Furthermore, it is much easier to collect a long period of data, consisting of a large number of samples, from an individual lying on the sickbed, particularly with the BP measurement done by the arterial catheter. As a result, they straightforwardly employed the data of several tens to hundreds of minutes collected from the target individual for model training. Nevertheless, in real-life scenarios outside the hospital environment, it is quite non-trivial to accumulate a large number of PPG and BP records from an individual, especially with sphygmomanometer for BP measurement. Finally, personalized models cannot take advantage of the shared features in the global dataset24, and the traditional one-channel input convolutional neural network (CNN) based models do not take into account the changes between the preceding (past history) and the present data, as well as the related causal effect, which may result in performance degradation over time. Thus, to increase the acceptance of PPG-only technology, a more thorough investigation is still required for ensuring such devices in practical usages25.

In this study, we propose an innovative two-channel inputs CNN-based model, which we called Mixed Deduction Learning (MDL), that takes information from the pairing of the preceding and the present PPG signals as input, aiming at substantially improving BP prediction accuracy, with very limited personal data that mixed with the global data collected from our recruited subjects for model training. For the personal data part, a total of 9-minutes, obtained from consecutive nine rounds of measurements of the target subject were acquired. For the global data part, our database of 364 recruited subjects, each had two to fifteen rounds of measurements was utilized. The separation of each round of measurement (consisting of one minute of PPG signal and one record of BP) ranges from a few days to several weeks (see Supplemental Data Figs. 1 and 2). To the best of our knowledge, our work is unique in using intermittent PPG data with a total of the shortest period to tune the model for each individual, that achieves comparable performance to the most of the existing related works. Model architecture

In this work, four CNN-based model architectures namely Mixed Induction Learning (MIL), Personalized Induction Learning (PIL), Personalized Deduction Learning (PDL), and Mixed Deduction Learning (MDL) were designed and compared in the process of accumulated learning. Figure 2 presented their schematic diagrams, and their evolution is also illustrated in Supplementary Data Fig. 3. PIL and MIL model represents the baseline performance of using a conventional deep learning model in comparison to the deduction learning model of PDL and MDL. All models were built for 15 target subjects, each has at least 10 rounds of measurement data (see Supplementary Data Table 2). Aiming at reducing the training data acquired from each individual significantly in practical usage, we set a total of 9-minutes (obtained from nine consecutive rounds of measurements, each round contains one minutes of PPG signal) of personal data for all models. However, there are differences in the approach of each model training. PIL, PDL are fully personalized model only trained using each person’s 9 min of calibration data. Whereas MIL, MDL are trained on a general data base with the 9 min of personal calibration data.

Figure 2a and e present the proposed models with one-channel input, PIL and MIL. In these models, the first CNN layer (see Fig. 2b) is fed with a single window of PPG data and outputs the learned feature map, where a window of PPG data consists a period of 1.6 s of PPG signal, and 6 morphological features extracted from the PPG waveform9. The feature maps were extracted from the weight values of convolutions of the input data in the first layer of CNN (see Figs. 2 and 4), which consists 404 neurons with 256 filters. After convolution, the superposition of the weights of the 256 filters were extracted and presented as the feature maps. Our study began with the MIL model. It was trained with nine consecutive preceding rounds of data (round to for ) of each target subject, and mixed with the training data of 364 recruited subjects, each has two to fifteen measurement rounds. Then the model was tested with the following measurement rounds (to) of PPG data for each target subject. This model was a trial to tackle the challenge of personal model trained with limited data availability. However, its performance was found to be extremely poor. We suspect that the inter-subject variations in physiology may contribute to the poor outcomes. In order to test our suspicion, we built a purely personalized model, PIL, using only the 9 rounds of training data obtained from the target subject. The performance of PIL seems slightly improved over MIL (see Results), but it is still far from the clinical requirements. As a rule of thumb, MIL and PIL models should be able to predict well if there is an ample well-annotated data for model training.

To overcome the drawbacks of MIL and PIL, we designed PDL and an innovative MDL approach that uses two-channel inputs strategy to further enhance model training efficacy and prediction accuracy, for PPG-only cuffless BPM. The architecture of PDL and MDL models are shown in Fig. 2c and f. In contrast to the one-channel input strategy, the first CNN layer (see Fig. 2d) is fed with the pairing of two windows of PPG data from two consecutive rounds, respectively. Each channel of input is convoluted independently, and then combined together to form the feature map. This design was motivated by the assumption that there may exist a correlation between the change of BP ( and the difference of the two-channel PPG data (the preceded data and the present data ). This idea was imposed in the model architecture as the rule of taking account the causal relationship in the training data, in order to guide the model training9,26,27. Results

In this section, the prediction performance of four deep learning models: two purely personalized models (PIL and PDL) and two models trained with mixed subjects (MIL and MDL) were compared. All models were developed for each of the 15 target subjects, who had at least 10 rounds of data (see Supplementary Data Table 2), as nine rounds of data for model training is the requisite in this study. This study was carried out on an ASUS ESC8000 GPU server, which equipped with dual Intel Xeon Silver 4114 CPUs, 256 GB host memory, and 6 Nvidia RTX 2080Ti GPU cards. The system was operated by Ubuntu 18.04.6 LTS, with the model development environment of CUDA-10.1, Python-3.6.9, Keras-2.2.2, TensorFow-1.11.0, and Jupyter Notebook 5.7.0. For the training parameters, the batch size was set to 3000 for optimal GPU memory utilization, with the learning rate 0.001, the Adam optimization algorithm, and ReLU activation function, for all the models. All the reference BP were measured by a cuff-based Omron model HEM-7320 automatic sphygmomanometer.

Performance comparison of our models is presented in the correlation plots shown in Fig. 3 (a), (b), and Supplementary Data Fig. 5, for the ground truth SBP / DBP measured by a cuff-based sphygmomanometer versus the corresponding model predictions. Following the BHS protocol, a BPM device receive grade “A” in performance validation if it reaches 60%, 85%, and 95% of BP measurements with deviations within ± 5 mmHg, ± 10 mmHg, and ± 15 mmHg, respectively (see Table 1 for details). These deviations of BP predictions are illustrated in the solid lines surrounding the central diagonal (dot) line in each figure, and the percentage of data points of predicting deviation within the region ± 15 mmHg is also presented. As shown in the figures, the percentages of data points within the region ± 15 mmHg given by MDL are 92.0% for SBP and 95.5% for DBP, respectively, both are significantly increased versus PDL, PIL, and MIL. We notice that subjects with SBP higher than 150 mmHg exhibit significant errors. This was due to insufficient number of data samples. From our recruited subjects, we only collected about 13% of total data samples with SBP 150 mmHg.

The commonly used global-view scatter plot of multi-subjects’ reference versus prediction is insufficient to objectively evaluate the performance of a personalized device that requires calibration data, as highlighted by Mukkamala et al.55. It is important to show the model’s response to the actual BP change for each subject to avoid the illusion of having a seemingly good global coefficient of determination when the model is not responding to individual’s BP change relative to the preceding cuff BP measurement (reference) obtained for calibration (hereafter referred as calibration BP). To solve this, Mukkamala proposed a figure using each subject’s calibration BP to normalize the prediction and reference value, as shown in Fig. 3 (c), (d), which depicts the relationship between the true BP change from calibration and the predicted BP change from calibration. From Fig. 3(c), (d) we can see the MDL’s superior performance, having significantly better linear correlation along the diagonal line compared to MIL, PIL, and PDL. The MDL outperforms the other three from both global and individual perspectives of modeling performance. While most of the data falls within the ± 20 mmHg range, it also shows it’s ability of deliver accurate predictions up to ± 50 mmHg changes.

To examine whether the performance of each model could fulfill the standard of AAMI, which stated that a valid BPM device should attend the measured accuracy of ME (mean error) 5 mmHg, SD of ME 8 mmHg, and the number of testing subjects should be at least 85 (see Table 1 for details), the plots of the average of the BP reference and model prediction versus their difference for both SBP and DBP, together with their SD of ME, are presented in Supplementary Data Fig. 5. As shown in the figures, the SD of ME predicted by MDL for SBP / DBP is 8.74 / 5.90 mmHg, respectively, which is smaller than that predicted by all the other models. It is even appealing to note that, following the AAMI criteria, the percentage of predicted data points falling within the region of ME 8 mmHg are 64.8% for SBP, and 84.1% for DBP, respectively, which are also considerably larger than those predicted by the other models. Therefore, it is evident that MDL outperforms the other models also in the AAMI standards.

The quantities of ME, MAE, SD, and the percentage of samples with predicted difference within a threshold (CD (%)) were carried out using Eq. 2 to 5 (see Methods section), respectively. Moreover, the MDL results also met the AAMI requirement for the DBP prediction, and nearly met the AAMI requirement for the SBP prediction. Overall, we found that all the models performed better in predicting DBP than SBP. Additionally, we also present the predicted accuracy scores in terms of the commonly used performance metrics in Table 2, such as (percentage accuracy), RMSEP (root-mean-square-error prediction), and (Pearson correlation coefficient) (see Eq. 6 to 8). Our result shows that MDL performed much better in (0.910 for DBP, and 0.914 for SBP) and RMSEP (6.4 for DBP, and 8.9 for SBP) over the other models. As a result, it can be concluded that MDL excelled over MIL, PIL, and PDL models. This demonstrates an encouraging breakthrough that MDL could be an ideal candidate for cuffless NIBP estimation, providing continuous and reliable BP monitoring for individuals.

The performance trends of training the models with one-channel and two-channel inputs to test with respect to the size of mixed training samples were investigated. In this test, we used the PIL and PDL training data, and mixed with data from 0, 199 of randomly selected, and all the 364 of recruited subjects, respectively, to train our models, where the first one is equivalent to PIL and PDL; while the two remaining ones are corresponded to MIL and MDL. Figure 5 illustrated a comparison of performance trends of our models in accordance with the BHS standard of BP variation thresholds. It appears that adding data of more subjects to the training set for conventional model with one-channel input did not lead to any improvement in performance when transitioning from PIL to the more inclusive MIL. This may be due to that the inclusion of a larger set of subjects’ data introduced ampler variations, which overwhelmed the weak footmark of the correlation between PPG and BP. In contrast, the training performance was significantly enhanced in models with two-channel inputs as data of more subjects were mixed to the MDL training set. This is the performance we could achieve so far. It is expected that if we could recruit more subjects to enlarge our training data set, it is possible that the required number of personal data samples of the target subject could be further reduced in training MDL. To gain a better understanding of what had been learned in the models with one-channel and two-channel inputs, we examined the feature maps produced by the first CNN layer with 256 filters of the models, as shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Data Figs. 4–9. The detailed discussion is presented in the Discussion section.

To objectively compare the significant differences between MDL versus the other models with a statistically significant testing strategy, the p-value results in percentage accuracy (, computed using Eq. 6, see Table 2) were presented. In order to choose a suitable statistically significant test, the distribution of the data population of pA was first checked. Supplementary Data Fig. 10a and b presented histogram plots of probability density versus pA for the SBP and DBP, respectively, for MDL compared to the other models. Since the probability distribution of all the models were positively skewed from normal distribution, a nonparametric paired Friedman test29,30 on pA for MDL, PDL, PIL, and MIL together was computed. The computed Friedman test p-values were 0.00029 and 0.00336 for SBP and DBP, respectively, which were all < 0.05. This result revealed that at least one method significantly outperformed the others. Next, the post hoc tests on pA for MDL versus the other models were performed using nonparametric Wilcoxon paired and Mann-Whitney U two-tailed tests. As seen in Supplementary Data Table 4, both tests yielded p-values 0.05 for MDL versus PDL, PIL, and MIL for SBP, demonstrating its superiority over other methods. For DBP, both tests gave p-values 0.05 for the MDL versus MIL and PIL, but, yielded 0.08725 and 0.08420 (both were near 0.05) for MDL versus PDL, respectively (see Supplementary Data Table 4). From these findings, we can conclude that MDL was significantly outperformed PDL, PIL, and MIL in terms of the cuffless BP prediction using PPG signals.

Discussion

As shown in our results, the performance comparison of PIL versus MIL, and PDL versus MDL revealed quite different scenario, due to the different design in the data input layer of the models.

By using the raw PPG signal as the major part of the input data, it offered the advantage of automatically extracting relevant features and preventing unintentional omissions of pertinent information via handcrafted operations13,31–33. Presumably, people could expect that adding the data of more volunteers should have a direct effect on training improvement34–36. Nevertheless, since not all the features drawn out of the raw PPG signal were relevant to the BP prediction, the disadvantage of misleading the model training due to accumulating the irrelevant features37 was also apparent. These opposite effects were directly observed in our results of PDL versus MDL, and PIL versus MIL, respectively. For the conventional deep learning methods, MIL did not show any performance improvement over PIL. In contrast, the predicting accuracy was remarkably improved for MDL over PDL, as data of more subjects was included in training (see Fig. 5). Examining the extracted feature maps from the first CNN layer, it is apparent that there are considerable differences between the models of one-channel and two-channel inputs. For example, as shown in Fig. 5c1 and 5d1, the peaks and valleys in the feature maps are quite different for PDL versus PIL, and there are some additional structures only appeared in that of PDL. The same phenomenon is also observed in Fig. 5c2 and 5c3, and 5d2 and 5d3 when comparing the feature maps of MDL and MIL. More examples of the first CNN feature maps are presented in Supplementary Data Figs. 4–9.

Since the input data of the two-channels were sampled from the preceded and the presented rounds of measurements, the processes of independent convolution in each channel and followed by the feature combination for the two successive rounds led the models to learn the evolution by comparing their differences, and the evolution could be easier to correlated to the changes in BP. In this case, accumulating the training data of more subjects could have a considerable effect on performance enhancement, provided that the relevant features could be statistically stressed. This fact is illustrated in Supplementary Data Figs. 7 and 9, in which the comparison of feature maps of MIL and MDL trained for SBP and DBP predictions, respectively, with training data of employing 5, 15, 100, 200, and 364 subjects are presented. Comparing MIL and MDL, it seems that the feature map of MIL tends to be more complicated as more and more subjects are recruited in training, indicating that the variations of extracted features are continuously increasing. Since all the additional peaks and sub-structures appeared in the feature maps are of the same order in magnitude and not overlapped, it seems that the resulting feature map was flattened by the fluctuations, suggesting that the variations of extracted features are diverged. Hence, more training data is not helpful in the model building. On the other hand, for MDL, the significant changes in the feature map happened in increasing the number of subjects from 5 to 100. After that, including more subjects does not change the feature map too much, and the major features, i.e., the apparent peaks and valleys, seem to remain the same. This phenomenon is especially observed in training MDL for SBP prediction. This may indicate that as more subjects recruited, the major features found by MDL were statistically relevant (labelled in red circles in Supplementary Fig. 7), and these features could be more easily correlated to the BP estimation. Thus, adding more training data does benefit the model training. This makes an essential difference between MIL and MDL.

Furthermore, it is interesting to note that training models for SBP prediction are more difficult than that for DBP prediction. As shown in the feature maps of models with one-channel and two-channel inputs, there are more apparent differences of SBP training (see Supplementary Data Fig. 7) than that of DBP training (see Supplementary Data Fig. 9), which suggests that the performance difference of DBP training of the two approaches is less significant than that of SBP training. Recall that in Table 1, comparing the performance score of mmHg of BHS protocol, MDL has 26.1% and 10.3% improvement over MIL, and PDL has 7.9% and 2.3% improvement over PIL, for SBP and DBP predictions, respectively. Moreover, comparing the prediction accuracy within each model, DBP is also better than SBP. As a result, in our study, it is a little tougher to train a model to predict SBP well, and a substantial enhancement in predicting SBP relies on the model of two-channel inputs. We suspect that SBP is significantly affected by various factors, including blood viscosity38, vascular resistance39, and vascular stiffness40,41. These factors may considerably affect the PPG readings in the systolic phase42, which may be more difficult to control.

The influence of external factors, like temperature and other hemodynamic conditions, could potentially affect the prediction results. These influences on our model predictions were not explicitly tested in this work. But since the duration of our data collection was spanned almost two years (see Supplementary Data Figs. 1 and 2), the influence of large temperature variation from winter to summer was implicitly embedded in our collected data, Therefore, we expect that these external factors would not affect our model predictions significantly.

Although our MDL model possesses the potential of further enhancement for PPG-only BP estimation, some limitations need to be addressed. According to previous studies, adding the subject-specific demographic features, such as age, height, weight, waist circumference, and body mass index, etc., has an impact on the predictions of both SBP and DBP11,43. But the impact of including these subject-specific demographic features in combination with the morphological features and raw PPG data as our model input was not validated. In addition, the impact of medication on the SBP and DBP prediction has not been considered. Cardiovascular-related medications can affect the condition of blood vessels12, leading to deviations in PPG readings for a given subject, which can cause substantial inaccuracy in BP predictions43–46. Furthermore, to evaluate the usefulness of our proposed models, we relied on a limited number of 15 subjects with 88 testing PPG records, due to the challenges of collecting enough rounds of measurement from more subjects. Hence, to ensure the generalizability of our model to a broader range of subjects, further testing is required. Moreover, instead of developing independent MDL models for SBP and DBP predictions, a single model training for both predictions can be explored. These issues remain to be further investigated, in order to translate our models more seamlessly to real products.

Of course, we would like to use even less than 9 min of personal training data as the ideal scenarios would be using as few data as possible. However, further reducing the amount of personal training data for modeling would hinders the prediction performance significantly as shown in Supplementary Data Fig. 11. For further reducing 1 min of data (one less round) will reduce the percentage of prediction within 5 mmHg deviation by 10%. On the other hand, increasing the personal data in training could potentially improve the model performance further. However, we do not have sufficient amount of data to add more than 9 min of personal data into training while maintaining over 88 records for meaningful testing and performance evaluation. Thus, the current optimal result is using 9 min of personal data for model training.

Conclusion

In the past, the PPG-only personalized model for cuffless BP prediction has been constrained by the scarcity of training data, presenting a substantial difficulty in attaining clinically acceptable requirements. In an effort to considerably reduce the required amount of personalized training data, this study introduces an innovative CNN-based MDL model with two-channel inputs for cuffless BP prediction using only PPG data. It was compared with two conventional models with one-channel input (MIL, PIL), and a two-channel inputs model (PDL), along with the findings of the other related works13,18,19,22,23. However, the proposed three models suffer from inter-subject physiology variation (MIL), and limited training data availability (both PIL and PDL), limiting their ability to achieve a state-of-the-art BP prediction accuracy.

In order to address these problems, MDL involves the design of paired consecutive rounds of PPG signal recordings as the input, training with only 9-rounds of measurements (equivalent to a total period of 9-minutes) of personal PPG data, and mixed with the global data from a pool of the other 364 subjects. This design leveraged a wealth of information from PPG waveforms, enhanced the ability to extract relevant features correlated to BP, and compensated the information from the data pool of the other subjects. More importantly, the embedded rule of the two-channel inputs guides the model to learn the changes of PPG data between two consecutive rounds of measurements, leading to more opportunities to extract the statistically relevant features, and making it meaningful to collect more data from other subjects for training. Our tests from the 15 target subjects, containing 88 samples showed that its BHS accuracy with ME 15 mmHg surpassed that of the PDL, PIL, and MIL by 6.8%, 14.7%, and 26.1% for SBP, and 4.6%, 6.9%, and 10.3% for DBP, respectively. Its AAMI accuracy exceeded that of PDL, PIL, and MIL by 39.9%, 41.9%, and 70.3% for SBP, and 36.8%, 94.7%, and 87.6% for DBP, respectively. For the static test of IEEE standard, its accuracy outperformed PDL, PIL, and MIL by 39.0%, 39.8%, and 81.7% for SBP, and 25.6%, 65.1%, and 67.1% for DBP, respectively. Overall, MDL has passed grade A of the international standards (including BHS and the static test of IEEE) in predicting DBP, and grade B of BHS standard in predicting SBP, and also fulfills / nearly fulfills the standard of AAMI in predicting DBP / SBP. Furthermore, the tests of the post hoc Wilcoxon paired and Mann-Whitney U two-tailed on pA showed statistically significant with p-values < 0.05 for MDL versus PDL, PIL, and MIL for SBP, and MDL versus PIL and MIL for DBP, respectively; while MDL versus PDL for DBP presented nearly statistically significant (see Supplementary Data Table 4). In summary, the performance of MDL is comparable to that of the other related works13,18,19,22,23, but with considerably fewer training samples collected from the target subject.

In addition, we demonstrated that, in contrast to the conventional models with one-channel input (MIL), the model with two-channel inputs (MDL) exhibits the performance improvement with incorporating more subjects in model training. Finally, we showed that MDL is also capable of accurate prediction for over 30 days, which indicates its usefulness for long-term BP monitoring without the need for frequent calibrations. With the development of MDL, we have made the BP prediction based on PPG exclusively feasible. The innovative design of two-channel input opened an opportunity to analogically find indirect correlations between the measurement data and the predicting target for a complicated system.

Methods

Datasets

To develop and compare the proposed algorithms, PPG waveforms along with the corresponding BP measurement were collected from human volunteers from various administrative regions of northern Taiwan between 2017 and 2020. A total of 365 subjects (234 females and 131 males) with an age range of 39–80 years, a height range of 139 –182 cm, a weight range of 31.5 –116.8 kg, a waist circumference range of 60 –122 cm, and BMI range of 14.0–44.5 were enrolled and participated. For the characteristics of our recruited subjects, please see Supplementary Data Table 3 and Supplementary Data Fig. 6. Prior to the BP measurement, the subjects were requested to be seated in a relaxed position on a chair for at least five minutes and asked for the personal information questionnaire. The detailed measurement protocol and the experimental setup are found in47. In one round measurement for a subject, a 1-minute transmitted PPG signal was collected twice with a meanSD of 5 8 min gap from the index finger of the volunteers’ left hand with the TI AFE4490 Integrated Analog Front End module. An Omron model HEM-7320 sphygmomanometer with an arm cuff was utilized to measure the reference BP (SBP and DBP). From each of the subjects, two to fifteen recurring rounds of PPG and reference BP data were recorded (See Supplementary Data Table 2). The period between each successive round of measurement spanned from several days to months for each subject. The testing samples were collected until the requirement specified by the Association for the Advancement of Medical Instrumentation (AAMI) was fulfilled, which is at least 85 for the BP testing devices.

Data preprocessing and pulse extraction

A raw PPG signal is a superposition of a low-frequency non-pulsatile direct current (DC) and a high-frequency pulsatile alternating current (AC) components7,48,49. The detailed data preprocessing techniques were explained in our previous work9. As part of the preprocessing procedure, the raw PPG record was first preprocessed in order to remove the DC part by applying a Butterworth filter to clean the signal at a cut-off of 0.75 Hz frequency. The pulse-based AC part of the PPG waveform contains information regarding BP and the average of blood volume change2. In order to select appropriate morphological features of the waveform, the filtered PPG waveform (AC) is segmented into fixed-sized windows, each window containing 1.6 s of signal (400 data points), assuming one window covers one to two pulses of the PPG signal. One full pulse in the context of a PPG waveform, spanning from a valley to the next valley, corresponds to one complete cycle of the systolic to the diastolic processes of the heartbeat. For each subject with a one-minute PPG measurement, an average of 60 windows were generated. In each window, six morphological features of heart rate variability (HRV), and areas under the curve of the PPG waveform of 25%, 50%, 75%, and 100% of maximum peak amplitude were extracted. These morphological features were concatenated to the segmented 400 data points of PPG signal in the corresponding window to create an input vector of 406 elements, and fed into our proposed deep learning models.

The architecture of our models

In this work, we proposed four deep learning models for personalized BP estimation. The detailed layouts of the model architectures are depicted in Fig. 4a and b. The baseline conventional one-channel input models, MIL and PIL, were constructed with five blocks of convolutional layer with the number of filters 256, 256, 512, 1024, and 2048, each with a kernel size of 3 and a stride of 1, followed by the batch-normalization and the ReLU activation function. After each CNN block, the obtained feature maps were down-sampled via max-pooling with a kernel size of 2. The last CNN Block was flattened after max-pooling, and two fully connected layers of the number of neurons 4096 and 2048 were utilized. During training, our MIL and PIL were given a vector of shape [406, 1] as the one-channel input (see also Fig. 2a and e) and passed through the network architecture (see Fig. 4a). For PDL and MDL, a pair of vectors with dimensions of [406, 2] as the two-channel inputs (see also Fig. 2c and f) was passed into the models for independent convolution and followed by the feature combination. After the CNN blocks, a concatenation and batch normalization layers were added between the flattening layer and the fully-connected layers in order to incorporate the input of. All the other layers and parameters were the same as those of MIL and PIL. The total parameters were 100,791,041(for MIL and PIL) and 100,800,005 (for PDL and MDL).

Standard of model evaluation

The overall performance of our proposed methods are evaluated according to the standards of BHS21, AAMI20 and IEEE STD 1708–2014 28 protocols (see Table 1). The BHS and IEEE STD 1708–2014 protocols assess the model performance by the scoring their grades to “A”, “B”, “C”, or “D”. For BHS, the grades are based on the percentage of model predictions that agree with the reference (ground truth) BP given by the Sphygmomanometer, up to designated deviations. To meet BHS protocol, a model for BP estimation being recognized as clinically accurate must reach at least grade B, with the requirement that the percentage of estimations (Eq. 5) reaches 50% with deviation within ±5 mmHg, 75% within ±10 mmHg, and 90% within ±15 mmHg, respectively50,51. The IEEE standard requires the mean absolute error (MAE) (Eq. 3) of model estimation falling within certain ranges. The AAMI protocol specifies that the overall model efficacy must reach a mean difference (Eq. 2) of at most 5 mmHg and a standard deviation (Eq. 4) of at most 8 mmHg against the ground truth measurement20.
