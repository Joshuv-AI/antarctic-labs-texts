Annulated cationic tetramethyl indo(di)carbocyanine dyes exhibit potent dark cytotoxicity in cancer cells
Authors: Zhumin Zhang, Hailey S. Sanders, Aktar Ali, Bradley D. Smith
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report the synthesis, characterization, and biological evaluation of a new series of chain-substituted pentamethine cyanine dyes, including annulated cationic derivatives. Palladium-catalyzed Sonogashira coupling of γ-bromo-substituted pentamethine cyanine (Cy5) dyes with electron-neutral or weakly donating arylalkynes yielded the expected linear products, whereas strongly electron-donating arylalkynes produced annulated dyes with blue-shifted absorption and pH-responsive “turn-on” fluorescence with pKa values between 4.9 and 6.3. Anionic sulfonated dyes were biocompatible, while cationic N-methyl derivatives selectively localized to mitochondria and exhibited potent dark cytotoxicity in multiple cancer cell lines, with submicromolar IC50 values and some cases selectivity over a noncancerous cell-line. Fluorescence microscopy confirmed mitochondrial targeting, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) imaging demonstrated dose-dependent induction of oxidative stress. Seahorse Mito-Stress and Glyco-Stress assays revealed mild mitochondrial uncoupling by the cationic annulated dyes, leading to increased basal and maximal respiration and compensatory glycolytic activity. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of these new annulated cationic cyanine dyes as mitochondria-targeting cytotoxic agents with pH-responsive fluorescence properties.

Introduction

Cyanine dyes are broadly useful in various technology sectors including biotechnology, photography, medical imaging, laser technology, and materials science [1–5]. Typically, they exhibit good chemical and photochemical stabilities, good solubilities, and predictable spectral properties since the absorbance and emission maxima wavelengths correlate with the number of methine carbons in the polymethine chain [6,7]. For example, trimethine indo(di)cyanines (Cy3) absorb and emit light in the yellow-green region around 550 nm [8,9], while pentamethine indo(di)cyanines (Cy5) correspond to the deep-red region around 650 nm and heptamethine indo(di)cyanines (Cy7) extend from deep-red into the near-infrared (750–950 nm) making them attractive dyes for biomedical imaging and therapeutic applications [1,10–14].

Cyanine dyes are typically synthesized by modular methods that condense two heterocycles to the ends of a central polymethine chain containing an odd number of methine carbon atoms [15–17]. The past decade has seen emerging interest in late-stage modification of cyanine dyes to produce functional bioconjugates for imaging and phototherapeutic applications [18]. The most common synthetic approach is to attach functionality to one or both terminal heterocycles. However, this modification site is distant from the polymethine backbone and typically does not have a large effect on the dye’s photophysical properties [7]. Synthetic methods that directly modify the polymethine chain are more likely to produce a major change. In this regard, the availability of precursor cyanine dyes with a halogen atom on the polymethine chain has motivated effort to pursue cross coupling reactions with the halogen acting as a leaving group. The best-known examples of this halogen substitution method are treatment of a meta-Cl substituted Cy7 dye with an oxygen, nitrogen or sulfur nucleophile to produce an SRN1 reaction [19–22]. Another halogen substitution reaction is a metal catalyzed cross-coupling that forms a C–C bond [23]. In this regard, a limited amount of synthetic work has examined γ-Br substituted Cy5 dyes as precursors for palladium catalyzed coupling reactions [24–26]. For example, we recently reported a series of successful Suzuki reactions that reacted a γ-Br substituted Cy5 dye with coupling partners containing reactive boronic acids and produced modified Cy5 dyes for use in fluorescence sensing applications (Fig. 1a) [26].

Here, we describe a series of palladium-catalyzed Sonogashira coupling reactions that reacted γ-Br substituted Cy5 dyes with arylalkyne partners bearing a range of different substituents. With electron-withdrawing or weakly electron-donating substituents the expected Sonogashira C–C coupled products were obtained. But when the substituent was a strong electron donating dialkylamine group, we observed an unexpected reaction outcome, namely an annulated Cy5 product (Fig. 1b). Photophysical investigation of these new annulated Cy5 dyes revealed “turn-on” deep-red fluorescence in acidic conditions. Cell culture studies in the absence of light showed that the annulated Cy5 dyes with a net anionic charge were not cytotoxic but the dyes with net cationic charge exhibited potent dark cytotoxicity in multiple cancer cell lines and in some cases there was selectivity over a noncancerous cell-line. The results of cell microscopy and cell metabolism experiments revealed a high level of dye localization in the mitochondria and inhibition of mitochondrial respiration with a shift of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production towards glycolysis. Together, these findings establish annulated cationic cyanine dyes as a new class of mitochondria-targeting cytotoxic agents with tunable photophysical and biological properties.

Results and discussion

Synthesis

The study started by preparing the known γ-Br substituted pentamethine indo(di)carbocyanine dye 1-BS with two N-butylsulfonate substituents [27]. A series of homologous palladium-catalyzed Sonogashira coupling reactions reacted 1-BS with the eight different para-substituted phenylacetylene compounds in Table 1 and the structures of the isolated products were confirmed by standard spectrometric compound characterization methods. When the para-substituent on the aryl component of the phenylacetylene compounds was not a strong electron donor, the reactions gave the expected Sonogashira coupling products 4a-BS, 5a-BS, 6a-BS, 7a-BS, 8a-BS, and 9a-BS as the only isolated materials. It is worth noting that Sonogashira couplings at the central γ-position of pentamethine cyanine dyes were first reported by Johannes and coworkers in 1995, although only two examples were described and the photophysical properties were not thoroughly investigated [28]. An interesting structural feature of these six homologous dyes is the π-connectivity of the appended phenylacetylene unit with the pentamethine chain, which permits intramolecular through bond energy transfer (TBET) after excitation of the phenylacetylene component. That is, these six modified dyes acted as Cy5-cassettes and fluorescence emission at around 670 nm, achievable by either direct excitation of the pentamethine fluorochrome at 640 nm or by excitation of the appended phenylacetylene unit at 390–400 nm (Figs. S1–S8 and Table S1). Consequently, these dyes exhibit large pseudo-Stokes shifts, a very useful property for different types of fluorescence sensing and imaging applications [26]. This current study did not pursue these fluorescence applications and focused instead on the discovery of a new coupling reaction that produced annulated dye structures.

Unexpectedly, we found that when the substituent on the aryl component of the phenylacetylene compound was a strongly electron-donating dialkylamine group, the palladium-catalyzed coupling reaction produced the annulated products 2b-BS and 3b-BS as the only isolated products. The chemical structures were elucidated by one and two-dimensional NMR methods, and mass spectrometry. Annulation reactions that involve cyanine polymethine chains are rare, however, a palladium-catalyzed Sonogashira coupling reaction followed by intramolecular C–C bond formation via C–H activation is a well-documented multistep process [23,29]. Spectral properties of annulated dyes

Absorption and emission spectra for annulated dyes 2b-BS and 3b-BS in methanol are shown in Fig. S9. The absorption maxima (~580 nm) correspond to a visible purple color. Fluorescence intensities for these methanol solutions are remarkably weak, which we attribute to a quenching effect caused by internal photoinduced electron transfer (PET) from the alkylamino aryl unit to the acceptor pentamethine backbone (Fig. 2a) [30,31]. Experimental support for this hypothesis was gained by conducting titration experiments that incrementally decreased the solution pH and produced slightly blue-shifted absorption bands (Fig. 2b and d) with more than 10-fold increased fluorescence at 606 nm (Fig. 2c and e). The “turn-on” fluorescence effect is attributed to protonation of the N,N-dimethylamino groups, which disrupts PET-mediated quenching. Computational confirmation of this acceptor-PET picture was obtained by using density functional theory (DFT) to calculate frontier molecular orbitals for one of the annulated analogs and its protonated form. The difference in transition orbitals indicates charge transfer from the non-protonated 4-(N,N-dimethylamino)phenyl group to the π-conjugated polymethine chain and terminal indolinium acceptor groups (Fig. S11), and supports the energetic feasibility of the dialkylamino electron-donating group inducing fluorescence quenching by an acceptor-PET process.

The set of structural homologues was expanded by making the γ-bromo substituted Cy5 dye 1-Me with two N-methyl substituents and converting it to the annulated dyes 2b-Me and 3b-Me (Scheme 1). The pKa for each annulated dye was measured by conducting fluorescence titration experiments and as shown by the plots in Fig. 3, the difference in molecular charge has a small but predictable effect on the pKa. That is, the measured pKa values for the acidic forms of cationic 2b-Me and 3b-Me (N-methyl substituents) were slightly lower than the values for anionic homologues 2b-BS and 3b-BS (N-butylsulfonate substituents). Moreover, homologues with an N,N-isopropylmethylamino group (3b-BS, 3b-Me) displayed higher pKa values than those with N,N-dimethylamino groups (2b-BS, 2b-Me), consistent with higher basicity (Fig. 3). Absorption spectra for the cationic 2b-Me and 3b-Me in buffer or solutions containing culture media show that the dyes do not self-aggregate (Fig. S10) and are unlikely to do so in biological imaging conditions.

Cell toxicity

Preliminary dark cytotoxicity screening revealed that 2b-BS and 3b-BS induced virtually no toxicity in the dark, whereas 2b-Me and 3b-Me caused substantial cell death (data not shown). Subsequent quantitative measurements of 2b-Me and 3b-Me cytotoxicity used a commercially available luminescence based antiproliferative assay (CellTiter-Glo®) to test four different cancer cell lines (A549 lung adenocarcinoma, HeLa cervical adenocarcinoma, SKBR3 breast adenocarcinoma, SKOV3 ovarian adenocarcinoma) and a noncancerous cell line (MRC5 lung fibroblasts). In each case, the cells were plated in a 384 well dish and allowed to attach to the plate surface overnight; then the wells were treated with increasing concentration of 2b-Me or 3b-Me (0–100 μM). After a 72-h incubation period, cell viability was measured relative to untreated cells and cell viability plots were generated (Fig. 4). In all cases, there was a dose dependent response with noticeable loss in cell viability when the dye concentrations were above 1 μM. Non-linear curve fitting of the cell viability plots provided the IC50 values listed within Fig. 4. Virtually all IC50 values were submicromolar and notably the cytotoxicity of 3b-Me was not cancer cell specific, whereas 2b-Me was 3–8 times more toxic to cancer cells than the noncancerous cell-line as judged by the ratio of IC50 values.

Cell microscopy

Cationic cyanine dyes are known to preferentially accumulate in cell mitochondria of living cells, largely due to the highly negative mitochondrial membrane potential [32–35]. Moreover, some of these cationic dyes can induce mitochondrial membrane depolarization, triggering a cytotoxic effect [36–38]. We hypothesized that the cationic annulated dyes 2b-Me and 3b-Me (bearing N-methyl substituents) would localize in cell mitochondria, whereas their anionic analogs 2b-BS and 3b-BS (bearing N-butylsulfonate substituents) would not. This hypothesis was confirmed by conducting multicolor cell microscopy of cultured A549 cells that were incubated with 2b-BS, 3b-BS, 2b-Me, or 3b-Me for 1 h followed by a 10-min incubation with the commercial fluorescent probe MitoTracker Green (MTG). Multicolor micrographs were captured and the images revealed high spatial overlap of the green MTG signal with the red 2b-Me or 3b-Me signals consistent with extensive mitochondrial accumulation (Fig. 5). In contrast, there was very low overlap of the green MTG signals with the red 2b-BS and 3b-BS signals. The extent of colocalization was quantified using the Colocalization Finder plugin within ImageJ software to generate Pearsons Correlation Coefficient values (R) and associated scatter plots. For the annulated dyes 2b-Me and 3b-Me, the R values were 0.94 and 0.93, respectively, consistent with high mitochondrial localization. In contrast, R values of 0.37 and 0.38 were obtained for 2b-BS and 3b-BS, respectively, indicating low mitochondrial localization. The high mitochondrial localization of annulated dyes 2b-Me and 3b-Me is consistent with the behavior of many other fluorescent dyes with pKa values between 4.9 and 6.3 that become cationic or dicationic upon protonation. These dyes have been shown to report mitochondrial acidification due to membrane damage associated with processes such as mitophagy [39]. Under normal physiological conditions, mitochondria remain weakly alkaline environment (pH = ~8), but during mitophagy, the damaged mitochondria within lysosomes become weakly acidic (pH = ~4.5). Thus, it is possible that dyes 2b-Me and 3b-Me are causing and reporting mitochondrial acidification due to dye-induced mitochondrial damage.

The photostability of these mitochondrial-targeting probes 2b-Me and 3b-Me was assessed by measuring their photobleaching rates, along with the commercial mitochondrial dye MitoTracker Deep Red in pH 7.4 PBS buffer. As shown in Figs. S12–14 and Table S2, both compounds exhibited ~1.8-fold slower photobleaching rate than the MitoTracker Deep Red, indicating enhanced photostability for potentially improved microscopy. It is noted that the caveat with using 2b-Me and 3b-Me for mitochondrial imaging is their inherent cytotoxicity which would likely limit their use in living cells to submicromolar concentrations.

To evaluate if cell treatment with the annulated dyes induces intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), we performed a second set of live cell fluorescence imaging experiments using the cell permeable ROS reporter, DCFH-DA, which indicates the appearance of intracellular ROS by producing enhanced green fluorescence [40]. Separate microscopy plates of cultured A549 cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (10 μM) for 1 h in the dark prior to a 1 h incubation with different amounts of 2b-Me or 3b-Me (0, 1.0, or 2.5 μM). As shown in Fig. 6, increasing doses of either annulated dye produced a concomitant increase in cell green fluorescence intensity indicating increased ROS production. Comparison of the green channel signal of the ROS reporter with the red channel signal of the added annulated dye revealed little colocalization confirming that the red fluorescence signal from an added annulated dye does not bleed into the green fluorescence detection channel.

Modification of cellular respiration

Given the mitochondrial localization, ROS production, and cytotoxicity of the dyes, we investigated the effects of 2b-Me and 3b-Me on cellular metabolism using Seahorse Mito-Stress and Glyco-Stress assays. A549 cells were preincubated with 0–4 μM dye for 3 h before measuring oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) following sequential injection of the stress compounds oligomycin, FCCP, and rotenone/antimycin A.

For the Seahorse Mito-Stress Test, A549 cells were sequentially treated with oligomycin (1 μM) to inhibit mitochondrial Complex V, FCCP (0.5 μM) and uncouple oxygen consumption from ATP production, and rotenone/antimycin A (2 μM) to block mitochondrial respiration, with OCR measured after each injection (Fig. 7) [41]. Prior to oligomycin addition, cells treated with 2b-Me or 3b-Me at low dye concentrations (<2 μM) exhibited higher basal OCR compared to untreated controls, consistent with partial mitochondrial uncoupling due to enhanced proton leak across the inner mitochondrial membrane. Following oligomycin injection, ATP-linked respiration was elevated, likely reflecting compensatory mitochondrial activity in response to low dye concentrations. Subsequent FCCP injection revealed increased maximal OCR, indicative of elevated respiratory capacity and demand. Overall, treatment with 2b-Me and 3b-Me increased maximal respiration relative to untreated controls. Together, these results support the conclusion that 2b-Me and 3b-Me act as mild mitochondrial uncouplers at low concentrations (<2 μM), leading to increased basal and maximal OCR through compensatory respiratory activity.

As disruption of mitochondrial respiration can activate compensatory glycolysis to maintain ATP levels, we performed a Seahorse Glyco-Stress Test to assess how the dyes influence glycolytic function. A549 cells were incubated with each dye (0–4 μM) in glucose-free media for 3 h, then ECAR was measured following sequential injections of glucose, oligomycin, and 2-deoxyglucose (2-DG). For cells treated with 2b-Me or 3b-Me, increasing dye concentrations led to higher glucose-stimulated ECAR, with a more pronounced effect observed in 3b-Me treated cells (Fig. 8). This metabolic shift is consistent with the mild uncoupling phenotype observed in the Mito-Stress Test, where insufficient mitochondrial ATP production shifts energy generation toward glycolysis. Following oligomycin injection, glycolytic capacity increased for both dyes at concentrations <2 μM, demonstrating the cells’ ability to upregulate glycolysis in response to mitochondrial dysfunction. At the highest concentration of 2b-Me (4 μM), glycolytic capacity and glycolytic reserve were notably reduced, consistent with cytotoxic effects that compromise overall cellular respiration. Together with the Mito-Stress Test, these results indicate that incubation with the cationic dyes triggers cellular metabolic rewiring, characterized by enhanced glycolytic function in response to mitochondrial uncoupling.

A final set of experiments evaluated the effect of the known ROS scavenger N-acetyl-l-cysteine (NAC) on the intracellular ROS levels and cytotoxicity produced by the dyes 2b-Me or 3b-Me. As shown by the cell imaging data in Fig. S15–S16, treatment of A549 cells with NAC (1 mM) slightly reduced the fluorescence produced by added ROS reporter probe, DCFH-DA, but the effect was not statistically significant. Similarly, cell viability assays showed that cell treatment with NAC (5 mM) slightly reduced the IC50 values for 2b-Me or 3b-Me, but the effect was also not statistically significant (Fig. S17). Further studies are needed to ascertain if other types of ROS scavengers have a stronger effect on cell ROS production.

Conclusions

We synthesized and characterized a series of chain-substituted pentamethine cyanine dyes, including novel annulated cationic derivatives. Expected C–C coupled Sonogashira products were obtained when the reaction used electron-neutral or weakly donating arylalkynes, whereas strongly electron-donating substituents on the arylalkyne produced annulated dyes exhibiting blue-shifted absorption and pH-dependent “turn-on” fluorescence with pKa values between 4.9 and 6.3, providing potential applications as pH-responsive molecular probes. The anionic sulfonated dyes were non-cytotoxic, whereas the cationic N-methyl derivatives preferentially accumulated in mitochondria and displayed potent dark cytotoxicity in cancer cells. Mechanistic studies revealed that these cationic dyes act as mild mitochondrial uncouplers, inducing a shift of cellular ATP production toward glycolysis and increasing intracellular ROS. Cell treatment with the ROS scavenger, N-acetyl-l-cysteine, did not significantly lower the amount of measured intracellular ROS or ameliorate the dye-induced cytotoxicity. Overall, the new annulated cationic cyanine dyes combine unique photophysical properties with mitochondria-targeted cytotoxicity, offering promising opportunities for the development of theragnostic agents and anticancer applications [35].

Experimental

General information

Organic reactions were performed under an atmosphere of argon using anhydrous solvents unless otherwise noted. Reagents and solvents were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich, VWR, Oakwood, Alfa Aesar, Ambeed, and Thermo Fisher and used without further purification. Column Chromatography was performed using Biotage Sfär C18 Duo columns (part # FSUD-0401) and Biotage SNAP Ultra columns. Reverse phase thin layer chromatography (TLC) experiments were performed on C18 TLC plates with F254s indicator (MilliporeSigma, part # 1.15683). 1H NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker 400 NMR spectrometer. Multiplicities were given as singlet (s), broad signal (br), doublet (d), doublet of doublets (dd), triplet (t), quartet (q) or multiplet (m). 13C NMR spectra and 2D NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker 500 NMR spectrometer. Chemical shifts are presented in ppm and referenced by residual solvent peaks. High-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS) was performed using a time-of-flight (TOF) analyzer with electrospray ionization (ESI).

This known compound was synthesized following a literature procedure [27]. A mixture of 1,2,3,3-tetramethyl-3H-indolium iodide (0.95 g, 3.3 mmol, 2.5 equiv.), N-((1Z,3E)-2-bromo-3-(phenylimino)prop-1-en-1-yl)aniline hydrobromide (0.50 g, 1.3 mmol, 1.0 equiv.), sodium acetate (0.53 g, 3.9 mmol, 3.0 equiv.), and acetic anhydride (5 mL, cat.) in EtOH (10 mL) was stirred at 70 °C for 16 h under Ar in the dark. Photophysical characterization

Spectra:

Absorption spectra were recorded on an Evolution 201 UV/vis spectrometer with Thermo Insight software. Fluorescence spectra were collected on a Horiba Fluoromax-4 fluorometer with FluoroEssence software. Analyte solutions were prepared in HPLC grade water (Sigma-Aldrich), HPLC grade methanol (Sigma-Aldrich), or pH 7.4 1x phosphate buffered saline (Thermo Fisher). All absorption and fluorescence spectra were collected using quartz cuvettes (1 mL, 1 cm path length; for emission and excitation spectra, slit width = 2 nm, unless stated otherwise).

Photobleaching:

Quantitative photobleaching rates were measured for mitochondrial-targeting probes 2b-Me and 3b-Me, along with the commercial mitochondrial dye MitoTracker Deep Red. The solution of each dye (10 μM) in pH 7.4 PBS buffer was placed in a 1 × 1 × 4 cm3 cuvette that was exposed to air at room temperature and irradiated at 3 cm by a 150 W Xenon lamp with a 475 nm long-pass filter. An absorbance spectrum was recorded every 5 min.

Cell microscopy

Cell Culture Conditions:

All cell lines used in this research were obtained from ATCC (Manassas, VA, USA) and maintained in cultured in DMEM, RPMI-40 and F–12K media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin–streptomycin at 37 °C under 5% CO2.

Cell Colocalization:

A549 cells were seeded onto a 35 mm glass bottom dish (MatTek) at a density of approximately 1 × 105 cells and were grown 48 h at 5% CO2 atmosphere and 37 °C. F–12K media was then removed and fresh F–12K media containing each molecular dye (5 μM) was added onto the cells and the cells incubated 1 h. The media containing the molecular dyes was removed and the cells were washed twice with 1X PBS. Fresh F–12K media containing Mito Tracker Green (MTG; 200 nM) was added onto the cells for 10 min. The MTG containing media was removed and the cells were washed once with 1X PBS. Optimized Minimum Essential Medium (Opti-MEM) media was added onto the cells before imaging using a Zeiss Axiovert 100 TV epifluorescence microscope (63x) equipped with an X-Cite 120Q metal-halide light source with metal-halide lamp. The images were acquired using FitC (ex: 485/20 nm, em: 524/24 nm) and TxRed (ex: 562/40 nm, em: 624/40 nm) filter sets and Hamamatsu sCMOS (orca-flash 4.0LTplus) camera. Micrographs of 6 separate viewing fields were obtained and analyzed using ImageJ software. For each micro-graph, a background subtraction with a rolling ball radius of 50 pixels was applied. Pearson’s Correlation Coefficient were calculated using the colocalization finder plugin on ImageJ.

DCFH-DA ROS Imaging:

A549 cells were seeded onto an 8-well Ibiti-treat glass bottom dish (Ibidi) at a density of approximately 5 × 103 cells and were grown 48 h at 5% CO2 atmosphere and 37 °C. F–12K media was then removed and fresh F–12K media containing DCFH-DA (10 μM) (Sigma Aldrich) was added onto the cells and the cells were incubated for 1 h. The media containing DCFH-DA was removed and the cells were washed once with 1X PBS. Fresh F–12K media containing molecular dyes (0, 1, 2.5, or 5 μM) was added onto the cells for 1 h. The media containing molecular dye was removed and 1X PBS was used to wash the cells once. Optimized Minimum Essential Medium (Opti-MEM) media was added onto the cells before imaging using a Zeiss Axiovert 100 TV epifluorescence microscope (63x) equipped with an X-Cite 120Q metal-halide light source with metal-halide lamp. The images were acquired using FitC (ex: 485/20 nm, em: 524/24 nm) and TxRed (ex: 562/40 nm, em: 624/40 nm) filter sets and Hamamatsu sCMOS (orca-flash 4.0LTplus) camera. Micrographs of 3 separate viewing fields were obtained and analyzed using ImageJ software.

CellTiter-Glo (CTG) assay for antiproliferative activity

Antiproliferative Assay:

All cells for the antiproliferative assays were maintained in DMEM Media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and the culture was kept at 37 °C, 5% CO2 over air. The antiproliferative effects of the newly synthesized compound were evaluated using the CellTiter-Glo (CTG) Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Madison, WI) in 384-well plates. Immortalized human astrocyte (IHA) cells were seeded at a density of 1000 cells per well in 25 μL of culture medium using a multichannel pipette. After 24 h of incubation to allow cell attachment, cells were treated with varying concentrations of the test compound diluted in complete growth medium. Control wells received equivalent amounts of the vehicle. Following 72 h of dye treatment at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2, 40 μL of CTG reagent was added directly to each well. The plate was gently shaken for 2 min to ensure reagent mixing and cell lysis. After an additional 40-min incubation at room temperature in the dark, luminescence was measured using a cytation 5 microplate reader. Luminescence values were normalized to vehicle-treated controls, and the percentage of cell viability was calculated. IC50 values were determined by fitting dose-response curves using nonlinear regression analysis with GraphPad Prism (version 10.2.2).

Cell metabolism measurements

Mito-Stress Test:

Mitochondrial function was measured by monitoring the oxygen consumption rate (OCR) and extracellular acidification rate (ECAR) of A549 cells with a Seahorse XF96 Analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience). A549 cells were seeded in a Seahorse XF96 well microplate at a density of approximately 1.5 × 104 cells per well and were allowed to adhere for 1 h at room temperature and then allowed to grow overnight at 5% CO2 atmosphere and 37 °C. Media was removed from the cells and cells washed once with XF media (1 mM pyruvate, 2 mM glutamine, and 10 mM glucose in Seahorse XF DMEM). Then molecular dye (0 – 4 μM) in XF media was added onto the cells and cells incubated 3 h in a non-CO2 incubator at 37 °C and humidified. Stock solutions of Oligomycin (final concentration: 1 μM), FCCP (final concentration: 0.5 μM), and Rotenone (final concentration: 2 μM) were prepared in XF media and added to the sensor cartridge. Changes to OCR and ECAR were monitored using a modified cycling program on Agilent Seahorse Wave Desktop software. p values were calculated using an unpaired t-test for each tested concentration of molecular dye versus untreated control. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001.
