Associations between depression and risk of cardiometabolic multimorbidity among middle-aged and older Chinese adults
Authors: Yao Xiao, Youliang Zhou, Linli Xu, Liping Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNumerous studies have performed the association between depression and increased risk of cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs). However, there is limited understanding regarding the impact of the depression on cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), particularly the various combinations of CMM. The purpose of this study was to explore the association between depression and CMM. We followed 9746 adults in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study from 2012 to 2020. CMM was defined as the coexistence of ≥ 2 CMDs, including diabetes, heart disease, and stroke. Depression was assessed by the scale of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale-10 (CESD-10). We used Cox proportional hazards regression models and restricted cubic spline regression models to examine associations between depression and CMM. During the 8 years of follow-up, 779 incident CMM cases (8.0%) were identified. Depression was independently associated with CMM after adjusting for age, sex, residence, marital status, educational level, smoking status, and drinking status (adjusted hazard ratio, 1.64; 95% CI, 1.15–2.13). Prevalent CMM combinations were associated with a higher risk of depression when compared with no CMM, except for CMM combinations of “Diabetes + Stroke”. Depression was significantly associated with excessive risk of CMM among middle-aged and older Chinese adults. These results suggest intervention targeting at individuals with depression might have substantial benefit in minimizing the risk of CMM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-11546-1.Keywords: Depression, Cardiometabolic multimorbidity, CHARLSSubject terms: Risk factors, Cardiovascular diseases, Human behaviour, Outcomes research

Introduction

Multimorbidity, commonly defined as the coexistence of two or more chronic diseases in an individual, is emerging as an international healthcare challenge and a global health research priority1,2. The prevalence of multimorbidity increases with the number of single chronic diseases. However, some chronic diseases often do not cluster randomly3. Chronic diseases that co-occur may share common underlying risk factors. Cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM) refers to the co-presence of at least two cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs), diabetes, heart disease and stroke, is one of the most prominent and representative multimorbidity profiles. Compared to single CMDs occurring on their own, the combination of multiple CMDs has been found to be associated with a multiplicative increase in mortality risk and a substantial reduction in life expectancy4. Evidence shows that CMM is common among older adults, with an estimated prevalence of 30% among individuals in China5,6, which contributes to the higher socioeconomic burden on family and society7,8. Therefore, it is necessary to detect risk factors and develop preventive strategies related to CMM from the perspective of individual and public health.

Depression is a common chronic disease that seriously affects health. It is expected that depression will become the top cause of the global burden of disease in 20309. The Global Burden of Disease Study 2019 reported that more than 46.9 million disability-adjusted life-years (DALYs) were attributed to depression in 201910,11. Since the COVID-19 pandemic, there were about 246 million cases of major depression, up by 28%, compared to the previous year12. In China, it is estimated that the prevalence of depression was 17% among people aged 55 years and its prevalence will increase with age13–16. Epidemiological studies17–19 have found that depression is associated with poor quality of life, chronic diseases, suicide and mortality, and thus may lead to a heavy burden on families, communities and health services.

Numerous studies have performed the association between depression and increased risk of cause-specific mortality, such as stroke, diabetes and heart disease17,18. However, the available studies on CMDs and depression are limited to the examination of single disease in isolation, but with minimal emphasis on the association between CMM and depression. However, CMM represents a distinct clinical phenotype with potentially unique mechanisms beyond the sum of single disease. Furthermore, the existing relevant studies are mostly cross-sectional and have been conducted in Western countries20,21. To date, no study has investigated the role of depression in specific pattern of CMM among middle-aged and older adults in China. Examining the association between depression and CMM could provide novel implication of mental health prevention for middle-aged and older adults with chronic health conditions2. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between depression and CMM, including prevalent pattern of CMM, using a nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older Chinese adults.

Methods

Study design and population

The study data come from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) with a multistage stratified probability-proportionate-to-size sampling design established in 2011, which is an ongoing nationally representative longitudinal survey in China. All participants underwent an assessment using a standardized questionnaire to collect data on sociodemographic characteristics(i.e., age, sex, education level, and marital status), lifestyle factors(i.e., smoking, drinking and health-related information. The baseline wave of CHARLS is being fielded in 2011–2012 and includes approximately 17708 participants in 10257 households and 450 villages/resident committees. All participants were followed up every two years by repeating similar baseline examinations after baseline survey, and five waves of data are available to date: waves in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018 and 2020. Details of the study design, methods, and participant characteristics have been previously reported22. All methods were performed in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations.

In the present study, we enrolled data from the CHARLS 2011 to 2020. The exclusion criteria for the present study were as follows : (1) participants with missing data of CESD-10 scores at baseline. (2) participants with missing data of cardiometabolic multimorbidity information at baseline. (3) participants with prevalent CMM at baseline. (4) participants who were lost to the follow-up survey during the wave 2013 to 2020. A total of 9746 participants were final included in our analysis. Assessment of depression

Depression was assessed using the scale of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale-10 (CESD-10), which is reliable and widely used among Chinese adults23. The CESD-10 consists of 10 items, ranging from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating greater depressive symptoms. A score ≥ 10 was used as the cut-off to define depression according to previous population-based studies23,24.

Assessment of CMM

Cardiometabolic multimorbidity was defined as the coexistence of ≥ 2 cardiometabolic diseases including diabetes, heart disease, and stroke, which were based on self-reported diagnosis, biomarkers, and treatment from the CHARLS survey. Heart disease and stroke were ascertained by self-reported diagnosis through the following questions, for heart disease, “Have you been diagnosed with heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems”, for stroke, “Have you been diagnosed with stroke by a doctor? ” Diabetes was defined if the participant met any of the following criteria: (1) glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c, measured by Boronate affinity HPLC method) ≥ 6.5%, (2) fasting plasma glucose (measured by Enzymatic colormetric test) ≥ 126 mg/dl, (3) random plasma glucose (measured by Enzymatic colormetric test) ≥ 200 mg/dl, (4) self-reported diabetes diagnosed by a physician, and (5) self-reported current use of antidiabetic medication according to the American Diabetes Association criteria. Cardiometabolic diseases were long-term chronic diseases that were considered to persist during the follow-up period.

Participants with CMM were classified into 4 subgroups according to the presence of specific cardiometabolic disease: heart disease + stroke, diabetes + stroke, heart disease + diabetes, and heart disease + stroke + diabetes.

Covariates

Sociodemographic and health-related factors were collected through the structured questionnaire at baseline, including age, sex, residence(rural/urban), marital status(married/others), educational level(primary school or below/ middle school or High school/ college or above), smoking status(never/former/current) and drinking status(never/former/current). Blood pressure was measured by a trained nurse three times and we used the mean value of three readings in the analysis. Some of CHARLS participants performed metabolic examinations at wave1 and wave3, including total cholesterol, blood glucose, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and C-Reactive Protein.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were summarized according to CMM, with mean (standard deviation) for continuous variables and frequency (percentage) for categorical variables. Differences between the groups were tested by Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and chi-squared tests for categorical variables. Missing data were handled by multiple imputation of chained equations method using R version 4.1.2 with the mice package25.

Survival time was defined as the period from the date of baseline interview to the date of CMM, loss to follow-up, death, or the end of follow-up, whichever came first. The association between depression and CMM was estimated using Cox proportional hazards regression model, which calculated hazard ratios (HRs) and corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). We tested the proportional hazards assumption using Schoenfeld residuals test and no violation was found. Two models were estimated: in model 1, age and sex were adjusted; in model 2, age, sex, sociodemographic (residence, marital status and educational level), and lifestyle (smoking status and drinking status) factors were adjusted.

The depression score was examined as a continuous and categorical variable. For the continuous analysis, we calculated the risk of CMM per one-point increase in the depression scores. For the categorical analysis, we compared the risk of incident CMM among participants with depressive symptoms, using those without depressive symptoms group as the reference category. In addition, the depression score was also split into quintiles to further examine the association between the severity of depressive symptoms and incident CMM, using quintile 1 as the reference group17. We additionally utilized 3-knotted restricted cubic spline regression to depict detailed descriptions of the dose-response curves between the depression score and incident CMM. Furthermore, we estimated the associations between the specific CMM combinations and depressive symptoms, with no CMM as the reference category.

We performed a series of sensitivity analyses to test the robustness of the results: (1) further adjusting for blood pressure, metabolic biomarkers in model 2 in the sample of participants who underwent metabolic examinations; (2) repeating all analyses using the data set without multiple imputations; and (3) estimating models stratified by age and gender. All analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc., Cary, NC) and R version 4.1.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing), with statistical significance set at a two-tailed P < 0.05.

Results

A total of 9746 CMM-free participants were included in the analyses and followed until 2020. The mean (SD) age at baseline was 57.5(9.2) years; 4460 (45.8%) of the participants were male and 5286 (54.2%) were female. At baseline, 3564 participants (36.6%) had depressive symptoms (CES-D total score > 10). Compared with participants without CMM, those with CMM were more likely to be older, female, living in rural, less education, never smoking, never drinking, and having depressive symptoms (P value for all < 0.05). The association between depression and CMM remained unchanged in the sensitivity analysis of excluding individuals who didn’t have metabolic biomarkers measurements, and similar associations were observed after adjusting for total cholesterol, blood glucose, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and C-Reactive Protein (Supplementary Table S1). Similar results were found in data set without multiple imputations (Supplementary Table S2). Subgroup analysis showed similar results by age and gender. Likewise, we did not find any significant modification effect of gender or age (Supplementary Table S3-S4). In addition, the non-linear association between depression scores and the risk of specific CMM combinations events were slightly attenuated, but did not change materially (Supplementary Figure S1).

Discussion

In order to elucidate the relationship between depression and CMM, a longitudinal analysis was performed on 9746 individuals from the CHARLS. The prevalence of depression was 37% among middle-aged and older Chinese adults at baseline. Our findings suggested that depression was associated with a significantly elevated risk of CMM, and the associations were independent of sociodemographic factors, and lifestyle factors, and the effects remained consistent in patients with specific pattern of CMM.

Numerous studies have demonstrated that depression is a risk factor for incident cardiovascular diseases. In a pooled analysis of 563,255 participants in 22 cohorts, depressive symptoms were associated with stroke, CHD and their composite end point CVD26. A large cohort study consisting of approximately 0.5 million Chinese adults indicated that compared with participants free of depression, individuals with depressive episode had a 19% increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes27. Moreover, a meta-analysis of 293 studies from 35 countries showed that depression was also associated with a 72% increased risk of cardiovascular mortality28.

Previous studies focused mainly on the adverse effects of multimorbidity on depression29–31, but the effects of depression on multimorbidity, particularly CMM, was under-explored. A large multinational cross-sectional study demonstrated that depressive episode was significantly associated with 3.44 times higher odds for multimorbidity compared with those with no depression, the odds of depression and multimorbidity were particularly higher in China (OR: 8.84) compared with other countries32. However, this study had a cross-sectional design with uncertain temporal relationship. Another Chinese study using longitudinal data found a bidirectional relationship between multimorbidity and depression: baseline depression was associated with the risk of incidence multimorbidity, and baseline multimorbidity also contributed to the increased risk of incident depression33, but there is no further division of multimorbidity. A large-scale cohort study from the UK Biobank demonstrated that depression was significantly associated with the risk of developing CMM, which is consistent with our results20. Our study extends previous work on this topic by investigating a cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults using longitudinal analysis, and we found that there are linear associations of depressive symptoms with CMM incidence, suggesting no clear evidence for a threshold level below which depression are not associated with CMM risk. This observation supports that prevention of CMM through addressing depressive symptoms could be amenable to everyone and highlights the necessity of screening for depression symptoms in the primary prevention of CMM. Considering that the population attributable risk of CMM due to lower mood could still be substantial because depressive symptoms are common, further studies are needed to determine whether there is a cause-and-effect relationship between depression and CMM.

As we know, this is the first cohort study to investigate the impact of depression on specific pattern of CMM. Previous studies focused mainly on CMM, therefore, direct comparison of our results with existing evidence could not be done. We found that only the combination of “Diabetes + Stroke” has no statistical significance and any combination including “heart disease” has statistical significance, suggests that heart disease presented more contribution toward associations between depression and CMM. A comprehensive meta-analysis found that the risk of depression on heart disease mortality was higher than diabetes and stroke28, which may confirm our results.

The pathogenesis of CMM is intricate and influenced by genetic, psychological, environmental, and biological factors34–36. There are several underlying mechanisms of associations between CMM and depression, including altered brain and neuronal function affecting neuroendocrine pathways, autonomic nerve dysfunction, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, platelet activation and thrombosis37–40. Another explanation lies in common health behaviors. Depression are associated with obesity, smoking, sedentary lifestyle, and insufficient physical activity, all of which are risk factors of CMM41,42.

Several strengths should be noted in the current study. The data for this study were based on nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, ten-year follow-up and prospective study design, which strengthened the generalizability of the findings. Furthermore, this study is the first to access depression associated with specific pattern of CMM after adjustment for related variables. However, several limitations need to be addressed. First, the assessments of heart disease and stroke were mainly self-reported conditions, it is possible that the existing cases that were not yet diagnosed were missed, which might lead to underestimation of risk estimates. Second, the sample size for some pattern of CMM might be too small to obtain robust result. Third, given the survey question setting in CHARLS, it is not possible to differentiate diabetes from high blood sugar. Consequently, individuals with high blood sugar may have been inadvertently included in the CMM analysis, potentially leading to an overestimation of its incidence. Fourth, this study couldn’t exclude all confounders between depression and CMM, such as obesity, physical activity status, etc. Fifth, despite multiple statistical adjustments, the potential for selection bias due to loss to follow-up need to be cautious. The lost-to-follow-up group may exhibit higher health-risk behaviors, which could lead to underestimation of the true association between depression and CMM. Future studies incorporating dynamic follow-up designs may help address selection bias. Sixth, while we employed cox proportional hazards models, we did not account for death as a competing risk. However, the low mortality rate in our cohort ( 3.84% over follow-up) suggest minimal bias. Caution is still needed when generalizing these findings to populations with higher mortality risk. Finally, the possibility of reverse causation cannot be ruled out.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the findings derived from a nationally representative and prospective cohort study indicate a robust association between depression and CMM. In particular, the adverse effects of depression were still significant for specific pattern of CMM. Our findings highlight the importance of the efforts in the implementation of established public health and healthcare interventions to sustain and enhance cardiovascular health.
