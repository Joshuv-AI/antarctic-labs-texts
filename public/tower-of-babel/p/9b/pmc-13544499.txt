Assessing Surgical Extent Before Biologic Therapy for CRSwNP
Authors: Alison J Yu, Kristina A LaPointe, Kaitlyn Ann Roberts, Michelle Yu, James N Palmer, Alan D Workman, Jose L Mattos, Rodney J Schlosser, Zachary M Soler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points Nearly two‐thirds of patients had no surgery or incomplete sinus surgery before biologics.Among all sinuses, the frontal sinus was the least frequently addressed before biologics. Keywords: biologics, complete surgery, Completion of Surgery Index, CoSI, nasal polyp, surgical extent

Introduction

Biologic therapies are generally recommended for refractory chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps (CRSwNP) after sinus surgery [1]. The Completion of Surgery Index (CoSI) was recently developed to define the adequacy of previous sinus surgery based on radiographic evidence of residual bony partitions [2, 3]. Prior work demonstrated that a previous incomplete surgery, defined by CoSI <70, experienced a substantial improvement in sinonasal symptoms after revision surgery [2]. To our knowledge, no prior study has objectively assessed the extent of prior surgery before biologics. In this study, we analyzed the CoSI scores in CRSwNP patients electing for biologics to understand practice patterns.

Methods

This was a prospective, multicenter cohort study that included adults (≥18 years old) that met the diagnostic criteria for CRSwNP according to ICAR‐2021 [4]. All patients had failed traditional medical therapies and were required to have bilateral NPs (Grade ≥ 1 each side) and olfaction score ≥1 (0–3 scale, 0: normosmia, 3: severe smell loss). Patients were recruited into the study after they elected to receive biologics after shared decision‐making with their treating otolaryngologists. The study took place at the Medical University of South Carolina and the University of Virginia but included patients referred to the study by otolaryngologists from across South Carolina and Virginia. Patients were excluded if there was previous biologic use, prior sinonasal surgery within 3 months, or systemic steroid use within 1 month before start of the study. Comorbidities, such as uncontrolled asthma, eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis, cystic fibrosis, and primary ciliary dyskinesia, were excluded. All participants were evaluated with noncontrast computed tomography (CT) sinus scan at baseline. Local Institutional Review Board approval was obtained at each institution.

For each patient, the total CoSI score was calculated from the CT scan along with individual tabulation of the five subcategories (maxillary, ethmoid, sphenoid, and frontal sinuses, and middle turbinate/Draf 3). Untouched anatomy represented 0 points and a maximal surgery was 100 points [2]. Scores were independently calculated by two blinded fellowship‐trained rhinologists and compared for inter‐rater reliability.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the CoSI distribution. Baseline characteristics were compared using chi‐square or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables and ANOVA or Kruskal–Wallis test for continuous variables. p Value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 30 (IBM, New York).

Results

The study cohort included 58 CRSwNP patients treated with biologics. The mean CoSI score was 60.1 ± 19.3, ranging from 0 to 90 (Figure 1). Sixteen (27.6%) patients did not have sinus surgery before biologics, whereas 42 (72.4%) patients did. Using the previous threshold of 70, 21 (50.0%) had CoSI scores 1–69 (incomplete surgery) and 21 (50.0%) had CoSI scores ≥70 (complete surgery) (Figure 1). Seventeen (40.5%) had one prior surgery, and 25 (59.5%) had ≥2 prior surgeries. The mean CoSI score was significantly greater for ≥2 prior surgeries than one prior surgery (65.0 ± 17.5 vs. 52.9 ± 20.7, p = 0.019).

CoSI distribution of the entire study cohort. Abbreviation: CoSI: Completion of Surgery Index.

By individual CoSI subcategory, the maxillary sinus had the highest percentage of CoSI scores 15–20 (n = 38/42, 90.5%), followed by ethmoid sinus (n = 35/42, 83.3%) and sphenoid sinus (n = 26/42, 61.9%) (p < 0.001) (Figure 2). Conversely, the frontal sinus (n = 13/42, 31.0%) and middle turbinate/Draf 3 (n = 22/42, 52.4%) were the least likely to have had any intervention (CoSI = 0) (p < 0.001).

Discussions

Most guidelines generally reserve the use of biologics for patients with recalcitrant nasal polyps after prior sinus surgery [5, 6]. Despite these recommendations, in those referred for biologics in this study, 28% of the patients were surgery naive. Of the remaining patients who did have a surgery history, half had an incomplete surgery with the data suggesting considerable variability in the quality of prior surgical interventions. Taken together, approximately two‐thirds may have been a candidate for a primary or a revision complete sinus surgery prior to biologics. It is not clear to what degree the decision to treat with biologics was driven by various factors including patient preferences, medical comorbidities, or contraindication to surgery. Although studies have published treatment algorithms that incorporated prior medical and surgical treatments [7], the practical use of biologics may be heavily influenced by the treating physician's perspective. With emerging evidence suggesting the importance of surgical completeness on patient outcomes for CRSwNP, careful attention should be paid to ensure adequate intervention at the time of surgery and when considering future treatments.

Our findings suggest that not all sinuses were uniformly addressed with the frontal sinuses least frequently treated. This likely reflects the well‐recognized challenges associated with frontal sinus procedures, particularly in cases of severe inflammation and polyp disease [8]. Whether this pattern reflects a tendency to prioritize the more accessible sinuses for surgical intervention while deferring the more difficult sinuses to possible future adjunctive therapies is not clear, highlighting the need for future studies.

Main limitation is that many factors underlying the shared decision‐making were not characterized in the study. A recent review on shared decision‐making in the management of CRSwNP emphasized a personalized approach that incorporates discussion of patient's symptoms, goals, and treatment compliance along with considerations of treatment efficacy and cost [9]. Finally, these findings should be interpreted with caution as practice patterns may vary widely. Nevertheless, they offer preliminary insight into the extent of prior surgery in patients electing biologic therapy and suggest that there could be a trend toward biologics initiation before any quality surgical intervention by otolaryngologists. Further studies powered by a larger sample size representative of national‐wide practice patterns are needed as well as to examine the motivations that drive both patient and provider decision‐making.
