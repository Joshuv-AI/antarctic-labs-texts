The differences in levels of poly- and perfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) in the hair of wild terrestrial mammals with various feeding behaviour
Authors: Sławomir Gonkowski, Alice Menozzi, Antonio Petrini, Antonio Cocco, Maria Chiara Cantelmi, Szczepan Mikołajczyk, Krystyna Makowska, Carmine Merola
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Poly- and perfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) are persistent environmental pollutants found across various ecosystems. While human and domestic animal exposure to PFASs is well documented, data on wild terrestrial mammals remain scarce, particularly using hair sample analysis. This study aimed to assess the levels of selected 12 PFASs in the hair of different wild mammal species with varying feeding behaviours, using liquid chromatography—tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS). The mean concentrations (± SD) were observed for PFNA (0.309 ± 0.33 ng/g), PFOA (0.272 ± 0.52 ng/g),L-PFOS (0.154 ± 0.3 ng/g), PFBS (0.013 ± 0.04 ng/g), PFHxA (0.067 ± 0.06 ng/g), PFHpA (0.064 ± 0.1 ng/g), PFUnDA (0.088 ± 0.13 ng/g) and PFDoDA (0.083 ± 0.15 ng/g) while mean concentrations of PFHxS, B-PFOS, PFPeS and PFHpS were lower than limits of quantification (LOQ). The results also indicated that PFAS concentrations were generally higher in predatory species compared to herbivores (p < 0,05), likely due to biomagnification processes in food chains. This study supports the applicability of hair analysis as a non-invasive method for monitoring PFAS exposure in wild terrestrial mammals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-39984-5.

Introduction

Endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) are a broad group of substances capable of interfering with the hormonal system, exerting diverse and harmful effects on both human and animal health1. Among these, poly- and perfluoroalkyl substances (PFASs) are of particular concern. PFASs are synthetic organic compounds characterized by the presence of at least one fully fluorinated methyl or methylene carbon atom in their structure2. The total number of PFASs is estimated to exceed 90002. Their unique chemical structure confers remarkable stability, resistance to high temperatures, and mechanical strength3,4, which has led to their widespread use across multiple industries since the 1950s4,5. PFASs are found in numerous everyday products, including food packaging, automotive and electronic components, clothing, cosmetics, household goods, paints, medical devices, and even fertilizers6–8 . Due to their extensive use, PFASs can enter the environment at various stages of their lifecycle, during production, use, and disposal9. Consequently, PFASs have been detected in surface and inland waters, soil, air, and plants10–12. These compounds can enter living organisms through ingestion, inhalation, or dermal absorption, and may disrupt the functioning of numerous organ systems13. Previous studies have reported adverse effects of PFASs on the reproductive, nervous, cardiovascular, and immune systems13,14 , as well as their genotoxic, cytotoxic, and carcinogenic potential15,16. Given their persistence and toxicity, monitoring PFAS exposure in humans and animals is a key concern in modern toxicology.

To date, most studies have focused on human exposure, with PFASs detected in various biological matrices such as blood, urine, breast milk, amniotic fluid, semen plasma, and placenta17–19. These studies have also shown that exposure levels vary widely between regions, influenced by factors such as industrial activity, urbanization, and lifestyle20,21. A relatively large body of research has also addressed PFAS contamination in aquatic organisms, highlighting the global scale of the problem22.

However, data on PFAS exposure in wild terrestrial mammals remain limited. A few studies have reported the presence of PFASs in internal organs23, blood24, hair25, and feces26 of wild animals. Among available matrices, hair appears particularly valuable for PFAS biomonitoring in wildlife27: hair accumulates chemicals over time and reflects long-term exposure, unlike blood or urine, where PFAS concentrations can fluctuate significantly within hours or days27,28. Hair also remains intact after death, allowing for post-mortem sampling, which is especially relevant for wildlife research in protected areas, where non-invasive or opportunistic sampling is preferred. In addition, hair samples are easy to store, transport, and analyse.

Despite this potential, very few studies have investigated PFAS accumulation in the hair of wild terrestrial mammals. To date, only two studies have explored this matrix in roe dear and red fox25,29. Furthermore, there is a complete lack of research examining the relationship between feeding behaviour and PFAS levels in hair. Understanding how trophic level influences PFAS accumulation is crucial, given the known processes of bioaccumulation and biomagnification.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to determine the concentrations of selected PFASs commonly found in the environment, including perfluorobutanesulfonic acid (PFBS), perfluorohexanoic acid (PFHxA), perfluoroheptanoic acid (PFHpA), perfluorohexanesulfonic acid (PFHxS), perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA), perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA), linear and branched perfluorooctane sulfonic acid (L-PFOS and B-PFOS), perfluoropentanesulfonic acid (PFPeS), perfluoroheptanesulfonic acid (PFHpS), perfluoroundecanoic acid (PFUnDA), and perfluorododecanoic acid (PFDoDA), in the hair of wild terrestrial mammal species with different feeding behaviours, including herbivores, omnivores, and carnivores. This is the first comparison of PFASs concentrations in hair among wild mammals with different feeding behaviours therefore the obtained results contribute valuable insights into the environmental exposure of wild terrestrial mammals to PFASs, and answers the question whether hair can be a good matrix for biomonitoring the exposure of wild animals to endocrine-active compounds widespread in the environment.

Materials and methods

Sampling

Samples were collected from January to June 2023 in a region called “Abruzzo”, located in the centre of Italy. Figure 1 shows the distribution of animals included into the study. The study area spans an altitudinal gradient ranging from sea level (0 m a.s.l.) up to approximately 2,900 m a.s.l. This extensive and heterogeneous territory includes a diverse array of land use types, encompassing agricultural areas, forested regions, as well as urban and industrial zones. The specimens were collected from carcasses submitted to the “Istituto Zooprofilattico Sperimentale dell’Abruzzo e del Molise” for anatomopathological examination, always obtaining the authorization for necropsy of wild animals from the appropriate local regulatory authorities. In all cases, cause of death was attributed to trauma resulting from road traffic accidents. Carcasses included in the study exhibited no visible signs of decomposition. Hair samples were collected by cutting a 10 cm2 area close to the skin, located posterior to the costal arch on the left side of the body, using sterilized metal scissors (Fig. 2). The study encompassed 58 adult individuals, belonging to three groups in term of feeding behaviour: 1) herbivores (n = 23): roe deer (Capreolus capreolus, n = 22), red deer (Cervus elaphus, n = 1); 2) predators (n = 25): red fox (Vulpes vulpes, n = 9), European badger (Meles meles, n = 10), wolf (Canis lupus, n = 6); 3) omnivores (n = 10): wild boar (Sus scrofa, n = 10). Sample collection procedure.

Exogenous substances absorbed on the hair surface were removed through four washing steps. First samples were washed in ultrapure water, then with SDS (0.1%, w/v) and again twice with ultrapure water. During each washing step samples were sonicated for 5 min. Next hair was cut into pieces of about 2–3 mm, air dried and stored in aluminium foil at room temperature until analysis.

Methods

Sample preparation was performed according to the protocol described in detail by Makowska et al.30. Quantification of analytes was conducted using liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS), employing a Sciex Triple Quad™ 7500 system (Sciex, Framingham, MA, USA) operated in negative electrospray ionization (ESI-) mode. MS/MS transitions are presented in the Table 2. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Gemini® C18 column (3 μm, 50 × 2.0 mm) (Phenomenex, Torrance, CA, USA). The mobile phase consisted of methanol (A) and a 20 mM aqueous solution of ammonium acetate (B). Methanol was obtained from Promochem (Germany), ammonium acetate from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), and ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ/cm) from Supelco (USA). The separation was achieved using a gradient elution at a constant flow rate of 0.6 mL/min. The chromatographic gradient was as follows: 25%A,75%B for 2 min, 30%A,70%B for 5 min, 98%A,2%B for 4 min, 25%A,75%B for 5 min.

Standards

All native and isotopically labelled standards were obtained from Wellington Laboratories Inc. (Ontario, Canada). The compounds that were investigated and the corresponding internal standards are shown in the Table 1. The concentrations of the standards added to the sample were 1 ng for both the quantitative determination standards and the recovery standard.

Quality assurance and quality control (QA/QC)

All solvents used in the analyses were tested for purity prior to use. Spiked samples and blanks were analysed with each batch of twenty samples to provide internal quality control measures. The concentrations measured in spiked samples were within ± 20% of the nominal values, confirming the trueness of the method, while all PFAS concentrations in blank samples were below the established LOQ.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 9.2.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize the data. To compare PFAS concentrations among animals with different feeding behaviours (herbivores, omnivores, and predators), the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was employed. This test was selected due to the non-normal distribution of the data, as confirmed by the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. For statistical purposes, values below the limit of quantification (LOQ) were imputed as LOQ/2.

Each hair sample contained at least one PFAS compound with a concentration exceeding the limit of quantification (LOQ), although concentrations above 1 ng/g were only occasionally observed (Table 2). When considering all 58 hair samples collectively (regardless of feeding behaviour), the highest mean concentrations were observed for perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA) and perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA). PFNA exhibited a mean ± SD of 0.309 ± 0.33 ng/g (median: 0.37 ng/g) and was detected above LOQ in 93.1% of samples. PFOA was present in 96.6% of samples (mean ± SD: 0.272 ± 0.51 ng/g; median: 0.15 ng/g). Linear perfluorooctane sulfonate (L-PFOS) followed with a mean concentration of 0.154 ± 0.3 ng/g (median: 0.025 ng/g), being above LOQ in 60.3% of the samples. Long-chain PFASs (PFUnDA and PFDoDA) showed slightly lower means: PFUnDA averaged 0.088 ± 0.13 ng/g (median: 0.048 ng/g) detected above LOQ in 79.3% of samples, while PFDoDA had a mean of 0.083 ± 0.15 ng/g (median: 0.026 ng/g), above LOQ in 48.3%. Shorter-chain compounds PFHxA and PFHpA demonstrated lower yet notable presence. PFHxA (mean 0.067 ± 0.06 ng/g; median 0.052 ng/g) was above LOQ in 74.1% of samples, and PFHpA (mean 0.064 ± 0.11 ng/g; median < LOQ) was above LOQ in 44.8%. PFBS was detected in 15.5% of samples (mean 0.013 ± 0.04 ng/g; median < LOQ). Branched PFOS (B-PFOS) was rarely above LOQ (only 3.4% of samples); its mean and median remained below LOQ. PFHxS, PFPeS, and PFHpS were never detected above LOQ in any sample.

Differences depending on feeding behavior

Compounds detected above the LOQ in more than 40% of samples were compared across feeding behaviour categories: herbivores, predators and generalist omnivores (Table. 2). Overall, predators exhibited the highest total PFAS burden: the sum of all PFAS mean concentrations was 1.460 ± 1.481 ng/g (median: 1.038 ng/g), compared to 0.999 ± 0.69 ng/g (median 0.562 ng/g) in generalist omnivores and 0.707 ± 0.444 ng/g (median 0.562 ng/g) in herbivores. Discussion

The results obtained confirm that wild terrestrial mammals are exposed to PFASs, consistent with previous observations23,25,29,31. Among all analysed hair samples, at least one PFAS was detected at concentrations exceeding the limit of quantification (LOQ). The highest concentrations were observed for PFNA and PFOA, detected in 93.1% and 96% of samples, respectively; both are long-chain perfluoroalkyl carboxylic acids (PFCAs). Other compounds detected with significant frequencies (> 40%) included L-PFOS, PFUnDA, PFDoDA, PFHxA, and PFHpA.

These findings align with previous studies assessing the suitability of hair as a matrix for evaluating environmental exposure to PFASs, particularly for PFCAs and PFSAs25,30, as already demonstrated for human hair27,28,32,33. However, most research on PFAS concentrations in hair has focused on humans and domestic animals. Hair samples are gaining increasing relevance in modern toxicology27,28 because they provide a valuable matrix for assessing long-term exposure to environmental pollutants. Unlike blood, urine, or even liver, where PFAS concentrations may fluctuate rapidly (within hours or days), hair concentrations reflect accumulation over the entire hair growth period, which can last weeks to months28. In wild mammals, hair analysis offers additional advantages: sampling is non-invasive and can be easily performed during capture operations, such as collaring for telemetry. Moreover, hair does not undergo autolysis post-mortem, enabling analysis of found carcasses29. However, this matrix has limitations requiring further investigation to establish whether it can be considered a reliable proxy for internal tissue concentrations and/or toxic effects in exposed individuals40. Indeed, the relationship between PFAS presence and concentrations in hair and internal organs or body liquids is not always clear25. Some studies have highlighted significant discrepancies between PFAS levels in hair compared to blood, urine, or internal organs25,36.

Differences between PFAS levels in the hair and other matrices may concern frequency of occurrence of individual substances and their concentrations. It should be pointed out that data comparing frequency of occurrence and levels of PFAS in different matrices are ambiguous and sometimes even contradictory, and the reason for this state of affairs remains unknown. For example Li et al.36 have shown in the human urine the presence of PFDA in 100% of samples and PFOS in 32% of samples, while they did not detect these substances in any hair samples. At the same time, the frequencies of other PFASs in urine and hair were similar, for example PFHxA (58% in the urine and 59% in the hair) or PFNA (68% in the urine and 70% in the hair)36. Other results have been obtained in studies conducted by Wang et al.41 who observed the presence of PFOS a larger percentage of hair samples (92.31%) than urine ones (76.92%), and the frequency of PFOS occurrence in hair was more similar to those in serum (100%) than to those in urine41. Clear differences in PFAS concentration levels between internal organs and hair have also been reported in previous studies25,42. However, the extremely significant correlations between levels of PFAS in the hair and serum and internal organs, such as liver, kidney heart and lungs have been shown42. Additionally, it is known that the levels of PFAS in the hair reflect orally exposure to these substances42. These observations strongly suggest that the hair could be employed as a matrix to studies on the exposure to PFAS.

On the other hand the hair is a specific matrix. It should be remembered thatorganisms exposed to PFAS shortly before sampling may exhibit elevated concentrations in blood and liver but not in hair due to delayed accumulation. Additionally, PFAS can enter hair both internally (via capillaries of the hair follicle) and externally through direct exposure; measured concentrations are expected to result almost exclusively from dietary intake and inhalation25. Therefore, meticulous washing protocols are essential to remove possible surface contamination. It has been shown that washing protocols can significantly affect the levels of PFASs observed in hair42. Therefore, the hair is thoroughly rinsed several times before analysis (as in this research) although probably washing protocols cannot completely eliminate the risk of environmental absorption28. For this reason the possibility of the direct penetration of pollutants into the hair from the internal environment should be taken into account during the interpretation of results28. On the other hand, consistent with results from Picone et al.29, our samples predominantly contained long-chain PFCAs and PFSAs, supporting the hypothesis that internal deposition is the primary mechanism responsible for PFAS concentrations detected in hair. However, a lot of aspects connected with PFAS levels in the hair are not fully understood and still need explanation. These include, among others, transfer of PFAS through blood/hair barrier, the precise mechanisms governing PFAS accumulation in hair and intraspecies differences in PFAS metabolism. Nevertheless, the hair appears to be one of the better matrices for determining long-term environmental exposure to PFAS.

The widespread presence of these substances in the environment results from their extensive industrial use, leading to pervasive environmental contamination3,4. PFASs contaminate even remote regions distant from urban or industrial centres, as evidenced by their detection in Antarctica43. This dissemination occurs because PFAS precursors are photo-oxidized in the atmosphere, producing PFAS that are transported long distances via atmospheric currents44. Moreover, PFASs are known to re-enter the atmosphere as aerosols from surface waters45. These processes contribute to global environmental pollution and consequent wildlife exposure. The sampling area is influenced by multiple PFAS sources, including atmospheric transport, commercial and domestic use, and riverine transport, with the precise origin indeterminate. Wildlife exposure primarily occurs through contaminated water, air, and food25,29, contrasting with humans and domestic animals, which encounter numerous anthropogenic PFAS sources such as clothing, cosmetics, utensils, and occupational exposure20,46. Generally, PFAS levels in humans and domestic animals are higher than those observed in wildlife (Table 4), though exceptions exist. For instance, PFBS was undetected in some human hair studies35,37 but was found in wildlife hair in both previous and current studies25. This suggests that local factors—such as agriculture, illegal dumping, tourism traffic, or wildfires—may influence environmental PFAS levels even far from industrial hubs47–49.

This study represents the first comparison of PFAS concentrations in hair among wild mammals with different feeding behaviours. Results indicate generally higher levels in predators compared to herbivores. Probably such higher PFAS levels in predators may result from biomagnification of these substances. This assumption is supported by the fact that previous studies have described the presence of PFAS in the muscles and livers of herbivores, which may be a food for predators25. The phenomenon of biomagnification implies that predators at the top of the food chain tend to accumulate higher concentrations of toxic substances than their prey50. In the case of PFAS biomagnification has been previously observed in aquatic organisms and birds50,51. Although air and water are common exposure routes, a carnivorous diet appears to convey greater PFAS loads than an herbivorous diet, suggesting the presence of PFAS at lower trophic levels too. The predominance of long-chain PFAS, particularly PFOS and long-chain PFCAs, in the compound profile seems to support dietary trophic transfer as the primary exposure pathway, consistent with Picone et al.29. Although some studies have detected PFAS in plants, these typically involve agricultural crops subjected to irrigation or fertilization52. Conversely, there is a lack of data on PFAS levels in understory plants, which constitute the main food source for wild herbivores. Our results suggest that PFAS concentrations in these plants are relatively low. Observed differences may also reflect divergent lifestyles among the examined species. For example, wild boar and red foxes frequently approach human settlements and may consume food waste, increasing their PFAS exposure23,29,53,54.

However, elevated PFAS levels in badgers and wolves (typically elusive species distant from human settlements), seem to confirm that biomagnification plays a significant role in PFAS bioaccumulation. However, although the present results may suggest that higher levels of PFAS in predators’ hair are the result of biomagnification and that hair is a useful matrix to assess this phenomenon, further comprehensive studies are required to fully elucidate these issues. Such studies should include determining the levels of PFAS in the environment where animals live (especially in plants that are food for herbivores) and in the various internal organs of herbivores that are food for predators.

Another critical question is whether the PFAS levels detected in hair negatively impact animal health. Currently, this cannot be conclusively answered due to the unclear correlation between hair and internal organ PFAS levels25. Furthermore, PFAS metabolism varies among animal species55, and the species included in this study have not been analysed in this context yet. Nevertheless, it is well established that PFAS, even at low concentrations, can induce toxic effects on multiple organs56,57, including endocrine disruption, reproductive toxicity, and immunosuppression58. Therefore, the levels observed here cannot exclude potential adverse health effects. This risk is heightened considering wildlife exposure to multiple environmental pollutants that may interact synergistically59.

Comparing PFAS concentrations across studies is challenging due to differences in biological matrices used for biomonitoring, PFAS panels analysed, analytical methodologies, and ecological, physiological, and behavioural traits of species studied. Considering the only other two studies conducted in Italy on the hair of wild animals (by Draghi et al.25 and Picone et al.29, both carried out in Northern Italy), we attempt to compare the mean concentrations of overlapping PFAS compounds and interpret the results. For example, Draghi’s study on roe deer reported PFNA values consistent with Picone’s findings for red fox hair (0.080 ± 0.01 ng/g) but lower than those found in the present study (0.309 ± 0.33 ng/g), with PFNA detected in 93.1% of samples. Conversely, PFNA concentrations in liver from Draghi’s study (0.414 ± 0.322 ng/g) were higher, indicating significant bioaccumulation in this organ. PFOS levels were highest in Picone’s study (1.40 ± 0.48 ng/g, detected in all samples), intermediate in Draghi’s hair study (0.912 ± 2.258 ng/g), and lowest in the present study (0.154 ± 0.3 ng/g, 60.3% prevalence). PFOS is particularly abundant in predatory species such as foxes but less so in herbivores. PFOA concentrations in Picone and the present study were similar (0.31 ± 0.19 ng/g and 0.272 ± 0.51 ng/g, respectively, > 90% prevalence), whereas lower concentrations were reported by Draghi (0.144 ± 0.414 ng/g). Overall, PFOA was present in high percentages across studies, though at lower concentrations compared to PFOS or PFNA.

PFDoDA levels were higher in Picone’s study (0.31 ± 0.05 ng/g) than in the present one (0.083 ± 0.15 ng/g, 48.3% prevalence), possibly due to the latter encompassing both herbivorous and carnivorous species; this long-chain compound tends to bioaccumulate primarily at higher trophic levels. PFDoDA was undetected in Draghi’s hair samples. PFUnDA appeared in the present study at 0.088 ± 0.13 ng/g (79.3% prevalence), predominantly in predators, while higher levels were observed in Picone’s study (0.17 ± 0.02 ng/g); Draghi’s study reported no detection.

PFHxA and PFHpA concentrations were comparable between the present study (0.067 ± 0.06 ng/g and 0.064 ± 0.11 ng/g, respectively) and previous ones, although PFHpA was not quantifiable in Picone and Draghi’s studies. PFHxA values in Picone and Draghi were 0.14 ± 0.02 ng/g and 0.071 ± 0.134 ng/g, respectively, roughly consistent with current findings. PFBS concentrations were low across studies: 0.004 ± 0.013 ng/g in Draghi, not quantifiable in Picone, and 0.013 ± 0.04 ng/g in the present study, reflecting lower persistence and bioaccumulation potential compared to PFOS or PFNA but still present in notable proportions. PFPeS and PFHpS were below LOQ in both the present and Picone’s studies and were not analysed in Draghi’s work.

In summary, Picone’s study revealed the highest mean concentrations, especially for PFOS and PFDoDA, demonstrating a strong dominance of long-chain PFAS. Draghi’s study indicated lower concentrations in hair relative to internal organs (liver and muscle) analysed only in this study, showing consistent trends with Picone and the current work. Notable differences among herbivores, predators, and generalist omnivores emerged in the present study, with generally lower but highly prevalent PFAS levels.

Conclusion

This study has described the presence of various PFAS in the hair of wild terrestrial mammals. Although, PFAS levels noted in the present study were clearly lower than those noted in the hair of humans and companion animals in previous observations, the obtained results have shown that wild terrestrial mammals may also be exposed to these compounds. Despite generally low concentrations, the toxic potential of PFASs at environmentally relevant levels, combined with possible mixture effects, raises concerns for wildlife health and conservation, Additionally, the present observations seem to confirm, the utility of hair as a biomonitoring matrix. Hair emerges as a promising non-invasive matrix, particularly useful for wildlife monitoring, due to its ability to reflect long-term exposure and its stability post-mortem. Moreover, this study has shown differences in PFASs levels in the hair collected from animals with various feeding behavior: herbivores, predators and omnivores. Generally higher levels of PFASs have been found in predators, although differences between animals clearly depended on the type of compound studied. These differences may result from various reasons, such as the presence of PFAS in the environment and/or species-specific behaviors (such as opportunistic feeding near human settlements). However, higher PFAS levels in predators observed in this study probably may also result from the phenomenon of biomagnification in organisms at the top of the food chain.

However, it should be underlined that further comprehensive studies are needed to explain some aspects connected with the subject of this study. The final confirmation of the usefulness of hair samples for monitoring the exposure of various wild animal species to PFAS requires thorough studies on metabolism, and distribution of these compounds in animal body, as well as on the transfer of PFAS through blood/hair barrier, In turn, confirming whether higher levels of PFAS in predators noted in this study result from the biomagnification of these compounds requires comprehensive toxicological and environmental studies, including, among others, analysis of PFAS levels in plants and the muscles of herbivorous animals. Nevertheless, the present study enriches our knowledge about exposure of wild animals to PFAS and highlights the need to monitor PFAS in the environment. However, further coordinated efforts toward methodology of PFAS analysis in various matrices are essential for advancing PFAS biomonitoring and understanding their ecological and toxicological implications.
