Dual phagocytosis-checkpoint blockade revitalizes immune surveillance in mouse models of glioblastoma

ABSTRACT
Macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of tumor cells elicits potent antitumor immunity. Nonetheless, sole-blockade of the anti-phagocytosis molecule CD47 has yielded insufficient therapeutic outcomes. Here, we report that glioblastoma (GBM) cells expressed abundant levels of phagocytosis checkpoint CD24. We further show that dual blockade of CD24 and CD47 synergistically enhances the pro-phagocytic activity of macrophages, thereby improving tumor antigen cross-presentation and activating the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway. This innate immune activation facilitates T cell infiltration into tumors and sensitizes tumors to anti-PD1 therapy, improving survival outcomes in murine GBM models, including immunosuppressive tumors reflecting human GBM-like features. Thus, our results indicate that dual-phagocytosis checkpoint blockade offers a promising therapeutic avenue to potentiate cancer immunotherapy.

Introduction

The innate immune system acts as the frontline defense against infections and cellular malignancies1. Antigen-presenting cells (APC), such as macrophages, dendritic cells (DC), and monocytes, specialize in phagocytosis to facilitate malignant cell clearance and present tumor-derived antigens2,3. This process bridges innate and adaptive immunity, priming T cells and activating subsequent adaptive immune responses4,5. However, tumor cells evade phagocytosis by upregulating phagocytosis checkpoints3,6. Among these, CD47 has been intensively studied to overcome innate immune evasion7–13. Although blockade of CD47 with monoclonal antibodies has demonstrated clinical potential in various preclinical human cancer models, the therapeutic efficacy of anti-CD47 monotherapy varies across different tumor models14,15. CD24, known to interact with Siglec-10 to suppress inflammatory responses, has emerged as a novel phagocytosis checkpoint16. Both genetic ablation and antibody-mediated blockade of CD24 have been shown to promote phagocytosis and elicit macrophage-dependent immune responses16.

Here, we show that CD24 is predominantly expressed in glioma cells, whereas Siglec-10 is specifically expressed in myeloid cells, suggesting that CD24 is a promising therapeutic target for glioblastoma (GBM). Combined blockade of CD24 and CD47 synergistically promotes GBM cell phagocytosis and antigen presentation, accompanied by activation of the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase–stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway in APCs and downstream adaptive immune activation. This strategy effectively remodels the cold tumor microenvironment and restores responsiveness to conventional PD1 blockade in an immunosuppressive tumor model. Together, our findings highlight the potential of targeting multiple phagocytosis checkpoints as a strategy to overcome immune resistance and improve cancer immunotherapy.

Results

CD24–Siglec-10 axis is selectively expressed on glioma and myeloid cells

To identify key regulators of phagocytosis resistance in GBM, we analyzed previously published single-cell RNA sequencing data obtained from 18 patients with glioma (Fig. 1a)17. We observed selective and prominent expression of CD24 within glioma cells, with its receptor Siglec-10 was predominantly expressed on myeloid cell populations (Fig. 1b). By contrast, CD47 and its cognate receptor SIRPα demonstrated relatively widespread expression across various cell types, lacking specificity to glioma and myeloid compartments (Fig. 1b). Similarly, other well-known “don’t eat me” signals, including PD1–PDL1 and B2M–LILRB1 axes, as well as the “eat me” signal SLAMF7, exhibited non-selective enrichment patterns across diverse cellular subsets (Fig. 1c)18–20. In addition, protein-level expression of CD24 and CD47 was confirmed with immunohistochemistry in human GBM patient tissues (Fig. 1d). Next, we evaluated the expression of CD47 and CD24 on various mouse and human GBM cell lines (Fig. 1e, f and Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). CD47 was highly expressed across all tested cell lines. CD24 expression was detected on most of the tested GBM cell lines except for the astrocyte-like CT-2A and U138 cells21,22. In contrast, higher CD24 expression was observed in proneural-like SB28 cells and epithelial-like U87 cells23,24.

Dual blockade of CD24 and CD47 synergistically improves glioma cell phagocytosis by macrophages

To assess the therapeutic potential of CD24 and CD47 blockade, we evaluated phagocytic activity using bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDM) and mouse glioma cells treated with varying antibody concentrations (Fig. 2a, b). CD24 blockade induced a dose-dependent increase of phagocytosis in CD24-expressing GL261 and SB28 cells, whereas no effect was seen in CD24-negative CT-2A cells (Fig. 2a). CD47 blockade also enhanced phagocytosis in a dose-dependent fashion across all glioma cell lines tested, although slightly higher antibody concentrations were required compared with CD24 blockade (Fig. 2b). Both anti-CD47 clones MIAP301 and MIAP410 showed comparable phagocytic activity when combined with anti-CD24 (Supplementary Fig. 3), and MIAP301 was used for subsequent experiments. To evaluate synergy between CD24 and CD47 blockade, various antibody concentration ratios were tested (Supplementary Fig. 4). The highest numerical value of phagocytosis was observed at a CD24:CD47 ratio of 5:3 in GL261 cells and 1:3 in SB28 cells; however, both ratios demonstrated no significant advantage over the simpler 1:1 ratio, which was selected for subsequent studies. By using this 1:1 ratio, we observed that dual blockade significantly enhanced macrophage-mediated phagocytosis as compared with mono-treatments in all cell lines tested except for the CD24-negative cell line CT-2A (Fig. 2c, d and Supplementary Fig. 5). In addition, treatment with soluble CD24-Fc neutralized the effect of anti-CD24, demonstrating that the observed increase was dependent on CD24 engagement (Supplementary Fig. 6). Conversely, CD24 transfection in CT-2A cells conferred sensitivity to the dual blockade, further validating CD24-dependent responsiveness (Supplementary Fig. 7). To distinguish between phagocytosis from trogocytosis, GBM cells were labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE) and PKH26 to stain the intracellular and membrane compartments, respectively. Antibody treatments increased the proportion of double-positive macrophages fully engulfing tumor cells, indicating that antibody treatment increased bona fide phagocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 8). In human GBM cell lines, synergy was less pronounced than in mouse models, but the combination treatment still improved phagocytosis in CD24-positive T98G cells (Supplementary Fig. 9). Macrophages process and present tumor-derived antigens after phagocytosis to facilitate crucial crosstalk between innate and adaptive immunity, consequently leading to T cell priming. To explore whether enhanced phagocytosis by dual blockade could improve downstream immune responses, we co-cultured macrophages with cytoplasmic ovalbumin (cOVA)-expressing GBM cells, followed by exposure to ovalbumin (OVA)-specific CD4+ (OT-II) and CD8+ (OT-I) T cells. Dual blockade markedly enhanced OVA peptide presentation on macrophages co-cultured with CD24-positive cell lines, whereas mono-blockade conditions produced only modest increases (Fig. 2e, f and Supplementary Fig. 11). Furthermore, dual blockade consistently resulted in significant and robust enhancement of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell proliferation compared with IgG and mono-treated groups (Fig. 2g, h and Supplementary Figs. 12 and 13).

Phagocytosis-mediated lysis of tumor cells releases double-stranded DNA into the macrophage cytosol, initiating cGAS-dependent synthesis of cyclic GMP-AMP. This, in turn, triggers STING activation and downstream type I interferon (IFN) signaling, resulting in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines that promotes T cell priming25–31. In line with this mechanism, dual blockade induced significant STING and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) phosphorylation in macrophages co-cultured with GL261 cells, as assessed by immunoblotting (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 14), whereas this effect was absent in CT-2A co-cultures (Supplementary Figs. 15 and 16). Confocal microscopy further demonstrated nuclear translocation of phosphorylated IRF3 (pIRF3) in macrophages co-cultured with GL261 cells, but not CT-2A cells (Fig. 2j). Activation of the cGAS-STING pathway was observed between 6 and 24 h and declined by 48 h (Supplementary Figs. 17 and 18). NF-κB, another key effector of the cGAS–STING pathway, was likewise activated upon dual blockade, as evidenced by increased phosphorylation of IKKα/β, IκBα, and p65 only in GL261 co-cultures (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Figs. 14–16)32. Consistent with these findings, combination treatment led to substantially higher IFNβ and TNF mRNA expression relative to anti-CD47 monotherapy in GL261 co-cultures. By contrast, in CT-2A co-cultures, the combination treatment did not further enhance these cytokine transcripts beyond levels observed with anti-CD47 alone (Supplementary Fig. 19).

Dual blockade-mediated phagocytosis fosters an antitumor immune environment

To evaluate the in vivo therapeutic efficacy of dual blockade–mediated phagocytosis observed in vitro, we tested orthotopic mouse GBM models, including the GL261 and CT-2A cell lines. Intratumoral administration of optimized dosages (Supplementary Fig. 20) led to significantly improved survival outcomes in mice treated with anti-CD24 antibodies alone or in combination with anti-CD47 blockade, with the combination therapy demonstrating notably greater effectiveness relative to anti-CD24 monotherapy (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 21). To elucidate cGAS-STING pathway activation after dual blockade-enhanced phagocytosis, we visualized the nuclear translocation of pIRF3. Consistent with the survival outcomes, anti-CD24 monotherapy moderately increased nuclear pIRF3, but the combination therapy resulted in a greater increase (Fig. 3c). Given that the cGAS-STING pathway is a critical signaling cascade known to initiate macrophage activation, we next assessed the expression of ionized calcium-binding adaptor molecule 1 (Iba1), a representative macrophage activation marker. In line with our prior findings, abundant numbers of Iba1-positive macrophages were identified within tumors treated with dual blockade (Fig. 3d, e). Moreover, dual blockade synergistically skewed the intratumoral macrophage population toward an M1-like phenotype, as evidenced by a reduced CD206+/CD86+ ratio (Fig. 3h). In addition to macrophage activation, a marked increase in CD4+ and CD8+ T cell infiltration was noted exclusively in the combination treatment group, underscoring that dual blockade-mediated phagocytosis fosters an antitumor immune environment (Fig. 3d, f, g). However, the CD24low CT-2A model did not exhibit a notable increase in immune cell infiltration, except for a moderate increase in Iba1-expressing macrophages in the anti-CD47 antibody-treated groups (Fig. 3e–g and Supplementary Fig. 22). Additionally, within T helper cell population, pro-tumoral Th2 cells were remarkably reduced following antibody treatments, while anti-tumoral Th1 cells remained unchanged, with no synergistic effects observed in the dual blockade compared to mono-treatments (Supplementary Fig. 23).

This immune modulation extended beyond the tumor site to the systemic circulation, as evidenced by increased antigen-specific CD8+ T cells in spleen in both OVA and endogenous GL261 antigen models (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Fig. 24), as well as elevated serum levels of IFNα, IFNγ, and tumor necrosis factor (TNF) (Fig. 3i and Supplementary Figs. 25 and 26). Together, these findings illustrate the capability of dual blockade therapy to stimulate both innate and adaptive antitumor immune responses in the GL261 murine GBM model.

Antitumor efficacy of dual blockade depends on cGAS-STING activation in host macrophages and the presence of CD8+ T cells

Given the established role of dual blockade in activating intratumoral macrophages via the cGAS-STING pathway, we sought to delineate the necessity of this signaling cascade in host macrophages for effective antitumor responses. To this end, we initially isolated BMDMs from STING knockout (KO) mice and co-cultured them with GL261 cells, observing markedly diminished phosphorylation of key signaling molecules within the cGAS-STING and NF-κB pathways after dual blockade treatment (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 27). Furthermore, nuclear translocation of pIRF3 and pNF-κB was substantially impaired in STING KO BMDMs, indicating disrupted pathway activation (Fig. 4b). In line with these findings, the survival advantage conferred by dual blockade observed in wild-type mice was significantly reduced in STING KO mice, underscoring the crucial involvement of macrophage cGAS-STING signaling in mediating antitumor efficacy (Fig. 4c). Flow cytometric analysis further confirmed significantly reduced levels of pIRF3 within the intratumoral CD11b+ macrophage populations of STING KO mice (Fig. 4d). Mounting an effective antitumor immune response necessitates the activation of adaptive as well as innate immunity. As type I IFN bridges these immune responses, dual blockade showed markedly diminished survival benefits in Ifnar1 KO mice (Fig. 4g). To investigate the contribution of CD8+ T cells in the antitumor effects mediated by dual blockade, we depleted CD8+ T cells through intraperitoneal administrations of an anti-CD8 antibody (Fig. 4h). This depletion notably attenuated the therapeutic efficacy of dual blockade, leading to decreased survival outcomes (Fig. 4i). Analysis of intratumoral immune cell populations demonstrated a significant reduction in IFNγ+CD8+ T cells upon CD8+ T cell depletion, whereas regulatory T cell (CD4+FoxP3+CD25+) populations remained unaltered (Fig. 4j).

Dual blockade sensitizes tumors to PD1 blockade

Although activation of innate immune responses can enhance T cell infiltration, it may simultaneously induce adaptive immune resistance through increased expression of PD1 and PDL1 within the tumor microenvironment33–36. To examine whether such adaptive immune resistance was triggered by dual blockade, we assessed PD1 and PDL1 expression levels in GL261 tumors after phagocytosis checkpoint blockade. Notably, increased expression of PD1 and PDL1 was solely associated with dual blockade treatment (Fig. 5a, b). These effects were dependent on type I interferon signaling, as IFNAR1 blockade significantly abrogated the upregulation of PD1 and PDL1 following dual blockade (Supplementary Fig. 28). We subsequently explored the therapeutic potential of combining anti-PD1 antibody with dual blockade to overcome this resistance. Remarkably, the triple combination therapy significantly enhanced survival outcomes in GL261-bearing mice relative to either dual blockade or anti-PD1 monotherapy alone (Fig. 5c). Although the other treatment groups showed no significant changes, the triple combination uniquely reduced the population of myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSC; CD11b+Gr1+) (Fig. 5d). In adddition, this combination therapy enhanced the infiltration of IFNγ+CD8+ T cells, whereas regulatory T cell populations remained unchanged (Fig. 5e). To ascertain whether triple combination therapy could establish long-term tumor-specific immune memory, surviving mice previously treated with the triple combination were rechallenged with GL261 tumor cells. As expected, all naïve (unimmunized) mice succumbed by day 25, whereas rechallenged mice exhibited prolonged survival up to 60 days (Fig. 5g). Visualizing with in vivo imaging system confirmed rapid tumor progression in naïve mice, but no detectable signals were present in rechallenged mice, indicating effective tumor-specific immune memory capable of preventing tumor engraftment (Fig. 5h, i).

To further validate the broader applicability of this combination therapy, we investigated its efficacy in immunosuppressive GBM models, including SB28 and QPP8. The SB28 model is characterized by low intrinsic MHC-I expression and minimal immunogenicity, closely mimicking human GBM phenotypes23,37. The QPP8 model harbors recurrent human GBM mutations, including Qki, PTEN, and p53, providing translational similarity to clinical immunotherapy scenarios38–40. Dual phagocytosis checkpoint blockade significantly prolonged survival in both models (Fig. 5j and Supplementary Fig. 29). Moreover, triple combination therapy further improved survival duration in the SB28 model compared with IgG-treated controls, extending median survival time from 23 to 35 days (Fig. 5j). Magnetic resonance imaging further substantiated these outcomes, demonstrating significant suppression of tumor growth after triple combination therapy (Fig. 5k, l and Supplementary Figs. 30 and 31).

Discussion

Although macrophage-driven tumor phagocytosis is effective for eliciting antitumor immunity, achieving robust therapeutic modulation through strategies harnessing phagocytosis has remained challenging3,6,41. Monoclonal anti-CD47 antibodies have shown variable efficacy across different cancer models, with inconsistent and limited clinical benefits observed in certain tumor types12,14,42. This discrepancy may stem from inherent tumor heterogeneity, differential patterns of myeloid cell infiltration, and multifaceted immunoregulatory signals within the tumor microenvironment. Recent studies have indicated that tumors frequently exploit additional phagocytosis checkpoints beyond CD47, including CD24 and β2-microglobulin (B2M)3,6. CD24, in particular, has emerged as a potent inhibitory signal through its interaction with Siglec-10 on myeloid cells, thereby dampening macrophage inflammatory responses and impairing tumor cell clearance16. B2M, as an integral component of MHC class I molecules, can modulate leukocyte immunoglobulin-like receptors (LILR) to inhibit macrophage-mediated recognition19.

In this study, we identified selective enrichment of CD24 expression on glioma cells, accompanied by predominant expression of its cognate receptor Siglec-10 on myeloid lineages, highlighting the significance of the CD24–Siglec-10 axis in GBM immune evasion. Accordingly, we combined CD24 blockade with CD47 inhibition, resulting in robust enhancement of tumor cell phagocytosis and antigen cross-presentation accompanied by activation of the cGAS–STING pathway. These augmented innate immune responses culminated in enhanced T cell priming and tumor infiltration, thereby fostering a potent antitumor immune microenvironment. Our dual-blockade approach consistently outperformed either monotherapy alone and notably sensitized GBM models to subsequent PD1 blockade. These results underscore the dynamic interplay between innate and adaptive immunity, providing a strong translational rationale for combining phagocytosis checkpoint blockade with T cell checkpoint inhibition. This dual-layered immunomodulation may broaden the therapeutic window, potentially benefiting patients with immunosuppressive malignancies refractory to conventional therapies. While this study establishes robust preclinical efficacy in murine models, future investigation using humanized antibodies and humanized mouse model will be required to extend these findings toward clinical translation.

Currently, two phase I trials are ongoing in patients with solid tumors and B cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma (NCT06028373, NCT05985083). In the clinical study of ATN-031 developed by Antennova, no dose-limiting toxicities have been reported in the initial five patients, and one heavily pretreated case showed tumor shrinkage on computed tomography imaging after completion of initial dosing cohort. These preliminary results point to the clinical promise of CD24 blockade and further suggest that dual blockade with anti-CD47 may also have therapeutic applicability in the clinical setting. Given the poor outcomes of PD1 monotherapy in many clinical trials, a triple combination incorporating dual phagocytosis checkpoint blockade and PD1 inhibition may provide a strategy to overcome resistance. Since CD24 expression is largely absent from most normal tissues, significant off-target toxicity is not expected. However, it is expressed on certain immune subsets, including B cells and conventional type 1 DCs (cDC1). Given the essential role of cDC1 in antitumor immunity, engineering antibodies for tumor-targeted delivery of CD24 blockade will be beneficial to improve clinical translation.

Methods

Mice

Age-matched male and female C57BL/6 (bred at MD Anderson Cancer Center), C57BL/6-Ifnar1tm1.2Ees/J (The Jackson Laboratory, USA, #028288), and C57BL/6J-Sting1gt/J mice (The Jackson Laboratory, USA, #017537) were used for tumor engraftment and growth studies. For T cell isolation, C57BL/6-Tg(TcraTcrb)1100Mjb/J (The Jackson Laboratory, USA, #003831) and C57BL/6-Tg(TcraTcrb)425Cbn/J mice (The Jackson Laboratory, USA, #004194) were used. All animals were maintained in a specific pathogen-free animal facility under controlled conditions (22 °C, 50% relative humidity, 12 h light/dark cycle) with access to standard diet and water. Experimental and control mice were co-housed under identical conditions. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center. In the orthotopic glioblastoma model, direct measurement of tumor size is not practical and endpoints were therefore determined based on the clinical condition of mice. Humane endpoints for euthanasia included signs of distress such as labored breathing, abnormal movement, hypothermia, hunched posture, or body weight loss of greater than 20% of the baseline. Mice were monitored three times per week for clinical signs starting two weeks after the tumor inoculation. Mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation followed by cervical dislocation. This study was conducted in accordance with all relevant ethical regulations. All experimental procedures were approved by and carried out in compliance with the institutional policies of The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center (no. 00002163).

Cell lines

GL261 (ACC 802) and SB28 (ACC 880) cell lines were purchased from the German Collection of Microorganisms and Cell Cultures GmbH (DSMZ). CT-2A (SCC194) and U87 (89081402-1VL) cell lines were purchased from Millipore Sigma Aldrich. THP-1 (TIB-202), U138 (HTB-16), and T98G (CRL-1690) cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). QPP8 cells were kindly provided by Dr. Michael A. Curran.

Cell cultures

CT-2A, GL261, and SB28 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. U87, U138, T98G, and THP-1 cells were cultured in Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 medium containing 10% heat-inactivated FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. QPP8 cells were cultured in NeuroCult Basal Medium (Mouse & Rat) (Stemcell Technologies, #05700) supplemented with 10% NeuroCult Proliferation Supplement (Stemcell Technologies, #05701) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. All cell lines were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2 except for the SB28 cells, which required 10% CO2.

Monocytes were isolated from the femurs and tibias of C57BL/6 or C57BL/6J-Sting1gt/J mice according to previously established protocols43. The surrounding skeletal muscles were removed, and bone marrow was flushed out with PBS and filtered through cell strainer to obtain monocytes. For BMDM differentiation, monocytes were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 10 ng/mL macrophage-colony-stimulating factor. For differentiation into dendritic cells (BMDC), monocytes were cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS, 1% sodium pyruvate, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 20 ng/mL granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor, and 10 ng/mL IL-4. THP-1 macrophage differentiation was achieved by treating THP-1 cells with 200 nM phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate for 3 days, followed by an additional 2 days of incubation with fresh medium to generate human macrophage-like cells.

cOVA-expressing CT-2A, GL261, and SB28 tumor cells were generated by transfection with pLenti-cOVA plasmid according to previously published protocols44. Lentivirus was generated in 293 T cells by using Lipofectamine 3000 and ViraPower packaging mix (ThermoFisher). Tumor cells were plated at 106 cells in 60 mm culture dishes and 10 µg/mL polybrene (EMD Millipore) and lentivirus were adeed dropwise. These cells were selected and maintained in DMEM complete medium supplemented with 0.5 mg/mL G418 sulfate.

CD24-expressing CT-2A cells were generated by transfection with Lipofectamine 3000 following the manufacturer’s protocol. Phagocytosis assays were conducted 2 days after transfection.

Human single-cell RNA sequencing data

Expression of “Eat me” and “Don’t eat me” signals in GBM patient cells was analyzed by using publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing data (GEO accession: GSE182109), comprising 201,986 cells from 18 glioma patients (1 astrocytoma, 1 oligodendroglioma, 11 newly diagnosed GBM, and 5 recurrent GBM)17. Data visualizations are available via the Single Cell Portal: https://singlecell.broadinstitute.org/single_cell/study/SCP1985/single-cell-analysis-of-human-glioma-and-immune-cells-identifies-s100a4-as-an-immunotherapy-target-gse182109.

Phagocytosis assay

Tumor cells (1.0 × 105) labeled with CFSE were treated with antibodies (IgG control, anti-CD24, anti-CD47, or a combination) for 15 min, followed by co-culture with 1.0 × 105 macrophages or BMDCs labeled with eFluor670. For CD24-Fc competition assay, GBM cells were treated with CD24-Fc for 15 min before antibody treatment. Co-cultures were maintained for 2 h at 37 °C in ultra-low attachment 24-well plates, harvested, stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), and analyzed with an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (ThermoFisher). The phagocytic index was defined as the percentage of CFSE+ tumor cells within eFluor670+ phagocytes. For concentration-response experiments, relative phagocytic index was determined by normalizing the phagocytosis ratio of each treatment condition to that of the IgG control within the same cell line.

Distinguishing phagocytosis and trogocytosis

GBM cells (GL261 or SB28) were dual-labeled with CFSE and PKH26 according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Labeled tumor cells (1.0 × 105) were treated with antibodies and co-cultured with eFlour670-labeled macrophages. For confocal microscopy, cells were incubated in µ-Slide 8 Well chambers (Ibidi, #80826) for 2 h and visualized using an LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss). For flow cytometry, co-cultures were maintained for 2 h in ultra-low attachment 24-well plates, stained with DAPI, and analyzed with an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (ThermoFisher). Phagocytosis and trogocytosis were defined as PKH26+CFSE+ and PKH26+CFSE− populations, respectively, within eFlour670+ macrophage populations. The detailed gating strategy is provided in Supplementary Fig. 32.

Antigen presentation

GBM-cOVA cells were treated as described in the phagocytosis assay and then co-cultured with BMDMs in 24-well cell culture plates for 24 h. After incubation, cells were detached with trypsin, stained with the corresponding antibodies, and resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 2% FBS and 300 nM DAPI. Antigen presentation was quantified with an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (ThermoFisher), measuring the mean fluorescence intensity of the SIINFEKL–H2Kb complex on CD11b+F4/80+ BMDMs.

Visualizing phagocytosis and antigen presentation by confocal laser scanning microscopy

For visualizing phagocytosis, tumor cells were treated as described in the phagocytosis assay and co-cultured with eFluor670-labeled BMDMs in µ-Slide 8 Well chambers (Ibidi, #80826) for 4 h. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution. To visualize antigen presentation, cells were co-cultured for 24 h, followed by washing and fixation. Fixed cells were then stained with SIINFEKL-H2kb and F4/80 antibodies overnight at 4 °C and images obtained with an LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss).

T cell proliferation

Spleens harvested from OT-I (CD8) or OT-II (CD4) transgenic mice were washed with cold PBS, mechanically dissociated, and filtered through a 70-μm cell strainer. Naïve T cells were isolated using a Naïve CD4+ or CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-104-453 for CD4+ and #130-093-244 for CD8+). GBM-cOVA cells and BMDMs were treated and co-cultured as described for antigen presentation, then incubated with CFSE-labeled naïve T cells for an additional 72 h. Cells were harvested, stained with the corresponding antibodies, and resuspended in PBS containing 2% FBS and 300 nM DAPI. T cell proliferation was evaluated using an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (ThermoFisher), calculating the percentage of CFSEmed cells within CD3+CD4+ or CD3+CD8+ T cell populations.

Western blotting

Tumor cells (1.5 × 107 cells) treated with different antibody combinations (20 μg/mL) were co-cultured with BMDMs (1.5 × 107 cells) in 150-mm dishes for 24 h, except for kinetic studies in which co-cultures were assessed at 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. After co-culture, macrophages were isolated using CD45 MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-052-301) and lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitor (1%) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (1%). Protein concentration was normalized to 2 mg/mL using a BCA kit. Protein samples were separated by gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride membranes. Membranes were blocked in TBS containing 2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 h at room temperature, incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, rinsed four times, and subsequently incubated with secondary antibodies. The blot signals were detected using Amersham ECL Prime Western Blotting Detection Reagent (GE Healthcare, #RPN2232) and visualized with a ChemiDoc MP imaging system (BioRad). Antibodies include: GAPDH (Cell Signaling, #2118, 1:2000), STING (Cell Signaling, #50494, 1:1000), IRF-3 (Cell Signaling, #4302, 1:1000), pSTINGSer366 (ThermoFisher, PA5-105674, 1:1000), pIRF-3Ser396 (ThermoFisher, MA5-14947, 1:1000), NF-κB p65 (Cell Signaling, #8242, 1:1000), IκBα (Cell Signaling, #4814, 1:1000), IKKα (Cell Signaling, #2682, 1:1000), IKKβ (Cell Signaling, #8943, 1:1000), pNF-κB p65Ser536 (Cell Signaling, #3033, 1:1000), pIκBαSer32 (Cell Signaling, #2859, 1:1000), pIKKα/βSer176/180 (Cell Signaling, #2697, 1:1000). For densitometric analysis, band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software. Phosphorylated protein levels were normalized to their corresponding total protein levels, and the resulting values were further normalized to the IgG-treated control group. A full list of the antibodies used for Western Blot analysis is provided in Supplementary Table 1.

Immunofluorescence

Tumor cells (1.0 × 105) treated with different antibody combinations (10 μg/mL) were co-cultured with BMDMs (1.0 × 105) in µ-Slide 8-well chambers (Ibidi, #80826) for 24 h. After incubation, cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, blocked and permeabilized using PBS containing 2% BSA, anti-CD16/32 antibody (10 μg/mL), and 0.3% Triton X-100. Cells were subsequently stained with primary antibodies against CD11b and p-IRF3Ser396 overnight, followed by secondary antibodies. Immunofluorescence images were acquired using an LSM880 confocal microscope (Zeiss).

Quantitative PCR with reverse transcription

Tumor cells and BMDMs were co-cultured, and BMDMs were isolated as described for western blot. RNA samples were extracted using a PureLink RNA Mini kit (ThermoFisher, #12183025) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Isolated RNA was reverse transcribed into complementary DNA using High-Capacity cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit (ThermoFisher, #4368814). Quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR) was conducted using TaqMan Fast Advanced PCR Master Mix (ThermoFisher, #4444557) with corresponding primers (7500 FAST real-time PCR system, Applied Biosystems). The ΔCt values were calculated by Cttarget–CtGAPDH, and −ΔΔCt values were obtained by ΔCtcont–ΔCttreated. Relative mRNA levels were determined as 2−ΔΔCt.

Tumor implantation

For flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry analyses, only female mice were used. For survival experiments, both male and female were used. All experiments were performed using age-matched mice (6–12 weeks old).

CT2A-Luc-mCherry, GL261-Luc-mCherry, SB28, or QPP8 tumors were inoculated into the caudate nucleus via a guide screw system. Mice were anesthetized, and analgesia was provided with buprenorphine in polymer (0.1 mg/kg) immediately before surgery and 48 h post-surgery. After disinfecting the surgical site, a 3-mm midline scalp incision was made to expose the skull. A 2-mm burr hole was drilled at coordinates 2 mm lateral and 2 mm anterior to bregma, and a guide screw was inserted. Seven days later, tumor cells (1 × 105 cells for CT-2A-Luc-mCherry and GL261-Luc-mCherry, 1 × 104 cells for SB28, and 2 × 105 cells for QPP8) in 5 μL of PBS were infused slowly at 0.2 μL/min through the guide screw. The mice were allowed to recover from anesthesia on a heating pad. Engrafted tumors were visualized using an IVIS Spectrum Imaging System (IVIS Lumina XR, PerkinElmer), and mice were randomized into four treatment groups.

Tumor inhibition

For the GL261 and CT-2A models, MIAP-301 (anti-CD47, 40 μg) and M1/69 (anti-CD24, 40 μg) antibodies were administered intracranially on days 7, 14, and 21 post-implantation, and anti-PD-1 antibody (100 μg) was injected intraperitoneally on days 8, 15, and 22. In the SB28 model, anti-CD47 and anti-CD24 antibodies (80 μg each) were administered intraperitoneally on days 5, 7, and 9, while anti-PD-1 antibody (200 μg) was given intraperitoneally on days 10, 12, and 14. For the QPP8 model, anti-CD47 and anti-CD24 antibody cocktails (80 μg each) were administered intraperitoneally on days 5, 7, and 9. Tumors were harvested one day following the final treatment for flow cytometry and immunohistochemical analyses. Survival outcomes were assessed by Kaplan–Meier survival analysis, and statistical significance was evaluated using a log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test.

CD8+ T cell depletion

C57/BL6 mice received anti-CD8α antibody (300 μg) intraperitoneally every 4 days, starting 2 days before treatment was begun. To confirm CD8+ T cell depletion, splenocytes were isolated and analyzed by flow cytometry.

IFNAR1 blockade

C57/BL6 mice were administered anti-IFNAR1 antibody intraperitoneally, with an initial dose of 500 μg per mouse, followed by maintenance doses of 250 μg per mouse every 3 days, beginning 2 days prior to treatment initiation.

Immunohistochemical analyses

For human samples, primary GBM tumor specimens were obtained from patients with pathologically confirmed diagnoses under the University of Arizona. All samples were de-identified, and written informed consent was obtained from each patient after approval by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Arizona (IRB number: 1507974522). At the time of tissue collection, all patients were treatment naïve. For mouse samples, brain tissues were collected 1 day after the final treatment, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, and then placed in 30% sucrose solution until completely submerged. Fixed tissues were embedded in Tissue-Tek optimal cutting temperature compound, frozen on dry ice, and stored at –80 °C. Frozen blocks were sectioned into 10-μm slices and mounted on adhesive glass slides. Slides were blocked with 10% goat serum solution for 1 h, with 0.3% Triton X-100 added for intracellular staining. Blocked slides were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C, washed three times, and subsequently incubated with fluorescent secondary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After three additional washes, slides were stained with 300 nM DAPI solution, mounted with coverslips, and visualized with an LSM880 microscope (Carl Zeiss). Image processing was performed with Zeiss Zen software.

Flow cytometry in vivo

Tumors and spleens were harvested 1 day after the final treatment. Tumors were digested at 37 °C for 30 min in a solution containing 300 U/mL collagenase D and 15 U/mL DNase I. The dissociated tumor cells were passed through a 70-μm cell strainer, and leukocytes were enriched by using CD45 or CD11b microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, #130-052-301 for CD45 and #130-097-142 for CD11b) as required by the assay. Splenocytes were obtained by grinding the spleens and then lysing red blood cells. The isolated cells were blocked with 2% FBS solution containing anti-CD16/CD32 antibody for 10 min to minimize nonspecific Fc receptor binding. Subsequently, the cells were stained with fluorescence-labeled antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C and analyzed on an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (ThermoFisher) or Cytek Aurora (Cytek Bioscience). Fluorescence-labeled antibodies included: CD3 (clone 145-2C11, BioLegend, 100330, 1:100), CD4 (clone RM4-5, BioLegend, 100510 or 100552, 1:100), CD8 (clone 53-6.7, BioLegend, 100722 or 100744, 1:100), IFN-γ (clone XMG1.2, BioLegend, 505808, 1:50), CD25 (clone PC61, BioLegend, 102012, 1:50), FoxP3 (clone MF-14, BioLegend, 126404, 1:200), F4/80 (clone BM8, BioLegend, 123116 or 123137, 1:100), CD11b (clone M1/70, BioLegend, 101226, 101259, or 101208, 1:100), pIRF-3Ser396 (clone PSH06-75, ThermoFisher, MA5-57852, 1:1000), CD11c (clone N418, BioLegend, 117318, 1:100), Gr-1 (clone RB6-8C5, BioLegend, 108406 or 108457, 1:50), CD45 (clone 30-F11, Biolegend, 103114, 1:100), Thy1.2 (clone 30-H12, Biolegend, 105327, 1:100), T-bet (clone 4B10, Biolegend, 644814, 1:50), GATA3 (clone 16E10A23, Biolegend, 653814, 1:50), CD86 (clone GL-1, Biolegend, 105008, 1:100), CD206 (clone C068C2, Biolegend, 141703, 1:100), H-2Db Tetramer-AALLNKLYA (MHC Tetramer Production Facility in Balyor College of Medicine), and H-2Kb OVA Tetramer-SIINFEKL (MBL Life Science, MBL-TS-5001-4C, 1:10). Detailed gating strategies are provided in the Supplementary Figs. 33–38. A full list of the antibodies used for Flow cytometry analysis is provided in Supplementary Table 2.

Magnetic resonance imaging

Tumor growth was monitored by using a 7 Tesla (T) small animal MRI scanner (Bruker Biospin, Billerica, MA) at the MD Anderson Small Animal Imaging Facility. Mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane during imaging. To detect and measure tumors, T2-weighted axial and coronal MR images were captured. Axial and coronal images were acquired by using a rapid acquisition with relaxation enhancement (RARE) sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) of 3000 ms and echo time (TE) of 57 ms. For coronal images, slices were 0.75 mm thick with a field of view (FOV) of 30 × 40 mm and a matrix resolution of 256 × 192 pixels, yielding an in-plane resolution of approximately 0.156 µm. Axial images were similarly acquired with 0.75 mm thickness, a FOV of 30 × 22.5 mm, and the same matrix resolution, resulting in an in-plane resolution of approximately 0.117 µm. Tumor regions were manually delineated on each MRI slice in a blinded fashion with ImageJ, and tumor volumes were calculated by summing the delineated areas in mm2 × 1 mm slice intervals.

Statistics and reproducibility

Statistical analyses were done with GraphPad Prism 10, and data are presented as mean ± SD. Statistical significance was determined with unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests or one-way analysis of variance.
