Choline and DNA methylation: impact across metabolic, oncogenic and neurodevelopmental disorders
Authors: Sanya Sareen, Marica Bakovic, Clara E. Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAberrant DNA methylation patterns are increasingly recognized as contributors to a wide range of conditions, including metabolic, oncogenic, and neurodevelopmental disorders. Nutritional factors, such as choline, can shape methylation potential via methyl group donation. The purpose of this narrative review is to synthesize current evidence on the DNA methylation landscapes underlying health and disease paradigms, with a focus on the role of choline as a compelling target for modulating epigenetic states. A comprehensive literature review search was conducted in PubMed to identify relevant studies, with additional articles retrieved from review papers.Main bodyIn models of metabolic-associated steatotic liver disease, choline deficiency yields perturbed methylation of genes involved in lipid metabolism and mitochondrial function, whereas supplementation restores methylation balance and improves metabolic outcomes. In hepatocellular carcinomas, choline modulates methylation of tumor suppressor genes and oncogenes, interacting with one-carbon metabolism enzymes and the Wnt/β-catenin pathway. During development, gestational choline supplementation induces long-lasting epigenetic changes across multiple systems, affecting genes involved in stress regulation, neurodevelopment, and metabolism. Postnatal choline supplementation can also induce epigenetic remodeling, mitigating established aberrant methylation patterns in adulthood. Across models, choline availability modulates the methylation status of key regulatory genes in a highly context-dependent manner, as it is capable of either increasing or decreasing DNA methylation depending on molecular targets, developmental stage, tissue type, and disease state in a sex-specific manner.ConclusionAlterations in DNA methylation span a wide spectrum of health conditions and diseases, with choline being a potential nutritional modulator through its role as a methyl donor. However, these modulatory effects are not unidirectional as they show subtle variations with context-specificity, highlighting the importance of precision in clinical methyl donor interventions. Background

The term epigenetics, first coined in 1942 by British embryologist, Conrad Waddington, blends epigenesis—the process by which organisms develop from a single unit—with genetics to describe the influence of genetic and environmental factors on cellular differentiation during embryogenesis [1]. Initially, epigenetics was applied to broadly categorize all developmental events leading from a fertilized zygote to the mature organism [2]. Any phenomenon that could not be explained by genetics was called upon as an epigenetic event, without considering underlying molecular mechanisms. Over the past few decades, the meaning of the term has evolved to parallel an advance in understanding of mechanisms regulating gene expression. This has led to a working definition of epigenetics as the study of mitotically/meiotically heritable changes in gene expression that occur without altering the underlying DNA sequence [3–6]. Present definitions reflect our understanding that while the genetic code is essentially identical across all somatic cells of an organism, patterns of gene expression vary greatly between different cell types, and these patterns can be clonally inherited. Modern epigenetics research focuses on identifying and characterizing distinct epigenetic mechanisms, including DNA modifications such as methylation [7], post-translational modifications of histone proteins [8], and transcriptional and post-transcriptional control through short non-coding microRNAs [9]. The discovery of key molecular players in epigenetic machinery has shifted the focus of epigenetics research from its original developmental and conceptual frameworks to measuring single epigenetic marks and their functional impacts.

Methylation of DNA is one of the most extensively studied and established epigenetic systems, making it a central focus of this review. DNA methylation plays a critical role in many biological processes, including tissue-specific regulation of gene expression [7], genomic imprinting [10], and mediating X chromosome inactivation [3], all of which highlight its biological importance in genome stability and normal development. Several nutrients can shape DNA methylation through their active roles in one-carbon metabolism [11, 12], a network of pathways that supplies methyl groups for epigenetic modifications. Among these nutrients, choline is of particular interest. Through its metabolite betaine, choline serves as a key methyl donor and is considered an essential nutrient for optimal development and function [13].

The purpose of this narrative review is to comprehensively evaluate the current state of knowledge on the regulation of DNA methylation with a focus on choline. We begin by outlining key features of methylation, including the enzymes responsible for its regulation and its malleability to environmental influences, followed by an introduction to choline and its role in the DNA methylation landscape. We then examine the impact of both choline deficiency and supplementation on DNA methylation patterns in relation to various health outcomes and pathologies. Given that the liver and brain are metabolically active tissues in which choline metabolism is prominent [14], we bring special attention to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), obesity, diabetes, metabolic dysfunction, neurodevelopment, prenatal alcohol exposure, regulation of stress responses, and Wilson’s disease—all of which are conditions associated with disturbed DNA methylation patterns, and where choline supplementation may offer therapeutic promise for restoration. Finally, we discuss the broader implications of modulating choline pathways to influence phenotype through DNA methylation, and identify key directions for future research aimed at developing novel, targeted epigenetic therapies.

Methods

Peer-reviewed, primary scientific articles were gathered from the PubMed database for the purpose of this narrative review. A series of independent Boolean searches were performed, including the following key terms: “choline”, “methylation”, “hypomethylation”, “hypermethylation”, “health”, “disease”, “liver”, “brain”, “prenatal”, “obesity”, “metabolic disorder”, “cancer”, “stress”, “alcohol exposure”, and “genetic disease”. Titles, abstracts, and when necessary, full texts of retrieved articles were screened according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria consisted of primary research studies (preclinical or clinical) that investigated choline through direct supplementation or analysis of choline metabolites in the context of health or disease. Exclusion criteria encompassed studies that did not evaluate the effects of choline, lacked measurements of DNA methylation or related methylation markers, employed supplementation with multiple methyl donors such that the independent effects of choline could not be determined, or focused on non-human mammalian species without relevance to human health or disease. Key review articles were also manually examined to identify any additional studies not captured by the search. The search was unrestricted by publication date. The final set of articles was selected based on their direct relevance to the topic of this review, covering the intersections between choline, DNA methylation and health or disease outcomes.

DNA methylation and enzymatic regulators

In eukaryotes, DNA methylation commonly occurs at the fifth carbon of cytosine, forming 5-methylcytosine (5mC), predominantly at CpG dinucleotides [15, 16]. Although once viewed as a static epigenetic mark, methylation is now understood as a dynamic and context-dependent regulator of gene activity. Patterns of 5mC vary across tissues and developmental stages, and can shift in response to environmental influences, including nutrient availability [17–19].

DNA methylation machinery can be categorized into ‘writers’ and ‘erasers’, which add and remove methyl marks, respectively (Fig. 1A). In mammals, DNA methylation is catalyzed by a family of three conserved DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs)—DNMT1, and two isoforms of DNMT3 consisting of DNMT3a and DNMT3b [20, 21]. DNMT3a and DNMT3b catalyze de novo methylation [22], whereas DNMT1 preserves existing patterns during DNA replication by copying methylation patterns onto daughter strands [23]. Although DNMT3a and DNMT3b are often grouped as de novo methyltransferases, they perform biologically distinct functions. DNMT3a primarily establishes methylation at major satellite repeats and developmental regulatory regions [24], while DNMT3b preferentially targets minor satellites and pericentromeric satellite II repeats, which are sites where DNMT3a cannot compensate for loss of DNMT3b [25, 26]. The activities of these methyltransferases are also regulated differentially; DNMT3a is modulated by interactions with DNMT3L and chromatin factors [27], while DNMT3b is activated through distinct conformational changes and histone-mark-dependent chromatin binding [28]. Together, these differences highlight that DNMT3a and DNMT3b contribute non-redundant roles to establishing methylation landscapes during development.

Given the dynamic nature of DNA methylation at both global and local contexts, removal of 5mC marks is a key regulatory mechanism (Fig. 1B). Methylation can be lost passively through inhibited or dysfunctional DNMT1 activity, whereby newly incorporated cytosine remains unmethylated, progressively reducing methylation levels over successive cell cycles [29, 30]. Active demethylation can also occur in non-dividing cells [31, 32] through a series of chemical modifications that convert 5mC into intermediates recognized and processed by base excision repair (BER) pathways [33]. For instance, deamination by activation-induced cytidine deaminase/apolipoprotein B mRNA editing catalytic polypeptide-like enzymes convert 5mC into thymine, creating a G:T mismatch that triggers the BER-mediated correction pathway [34, 35].

The functional consequence of DNA methylation depends on the genomic context in which it occurs [36]. Methylation at regulatory regions, such as gene promoters and enhancers, is typically associated with transcriptional silencing [3, 7]. In contrast, methylation within gene bodies is not generally repressive and may even promote transcription [37, 38]. Effector or ‘reader’ proteins recognize methylated DNA and initiate downstream cellular processes. Although DNA methylation can directly suppress gene expression by interfering with the binding of transcriptional activators, it also mediates repression through methylation-sensitive-DNA-binding proteins. 5mC is recognized by three main families of reader proteins including methyl-CpG-binding proteins, ubiquitin-like with PHD and RING finger domains proteins, and zinc-finger proteins [39–41]. These readers can recruit co-repressors and chromatin-remodeling complexes, thereby reinforcing transcriptional repression at methylated loci [42]. Notably, the establishment of 5mC requires methyl donors, making methylation patterns sensitive to fluctuations in donor availability [43]. Nutrients that influence this availability, including choline, therefore have the potential to reshape epigenetic landscapes in a cell- and region-specific manner [17–19]. Such epigenetic reprogramming provides the foundation for investigating choline as a modulator of DNA methylation.

Importance of choline

Choline was officially recognized by the Institute of Medicine as an essential micronutrient in 1998, and Adequate Intake (AI) levels were established at 550 mg/day for men and 425 mg/day for women [44] based on estimated dietary intakes and studies linking lower choline intake to liver damage [45]. Life stages, such as pregnancy and lactation, and certain genetic polymorphisms can increase the demand for choline, and thus some individuals may need a higher intake [46]. Through its oxidized product betaine, choline serves as a methyl donor in the methionine cycle, where betaine-homocysteine methyltransferase (BHMT), highly expressed in hepatic and renal tissues [47], converts homocysteine to methionine [48]. Methionine is used to generate S-adenosylmethionine (SAM), the universal methyl donor, which is used to methylate a substrate such as DNA, forming S-adenosylhomocysteine (SAH) and then homocysteine [49]. Accordingly, the SAM:SAH ratio is widely regarded as a marker of DNA methylation [50]. To sustain this cycle, homocysteine must be remethylated to regenerate methionine and thus replenish SAM [49]. Homocysteine remethylation occurs via two principal pathways: a folate-independent route catalyzed by BHMT [51], and a folate-dependent route catalyzed by methionine synthase which relies on 5-methyltetrahydrofolate and methylcobalamin to transfer a methyl group to homocysteine [52]. BHMT activity thus represents an alternative, choline-responsive pathway for methionine regeneration, particularly when folate availability is limited [53], and highlights the metabolic interrelationship between choline and folate [54]. Beyond its involvement in DNA methylation, choline is needed for the synthesis of phospholipids in cell membranes, cholinergic neurotransmission, transmembrane signaling, and lipid transport and metabolism [45]. Choline supports lipid metabolism through phosphatidylcholine synthesis via the cytidine diphosphate-choline pathway and through phosphatidylethanolamine methylation, thereby integrating methylation with membrane biogenesis (Fig. 2).

The physiological importance of choline is evident in its role in supporting fetal brain development [55], protecting against neural tube defects [56], and providing long-term neurocognitive benefits such as enhanced offspring cognition, processing speed, and visuospatial memory [57, 58]. Although inconsistent findings have been reported regarding associations between choline and body composition, blood lipids, and cardiovascular health [59], emerging studies suggest potential metabolic benefits of prenatal choline supplementation in offspring subject to obesogenic postnatal environments, including lower adiposity, body weight and food intake, and improved insulin sensitivity [60, 61].

Given that DNA methylation may be a modifier of functional outcomes through alterations in gene expression, and that choline is an important component within one-carbon metabolism, the broader significance of choline as a modulator of health and disease across the life course may rest within its impact on DNA methylation.

DNA methylation across health and disease with a focus on choline

Considering that choline plays an important role in maintaining proper brain and liver function [14], the following sections explore its relevance in hepatic and neural health and disease, including MASLD, diabetes, obesity, metabolic dysfunction, neurodevelopmental processes and functions, prenatal alcohol exposure, stress responses, and Wilson’s disease - contexts in which DNA methylation patterns may serve as underlying modulators (Fig. 3). Across these conditions, both choline deficiency and supplementation can modify DNA methylation patterns globally and in a gene-specific manner, influencing development, health, and risk of disease. Hepatic steatosis and MASLD—preclinical

MASLD is one of the most common forms of chronic liver disease [62]. Previously known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, MASLD is characterized by an accumulation of lipids in the liver in the absence of excessive alcohol consumption [62]. Histologically, MASLD presents as a spectrum ranging from mild hepatic steatosis to non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), which can progress to cirrhosis and its associated complications, including liver failure and hepatocellular carcinoma [63].

Across both dietary and genetic preclinical models of MASLD, an altered methylation capacity has consistently been associated with transcriptional dysregulation of genes involved in lipid oxidation [64, 65], antioxidant defense [64, 66], and lipoprotein export [67]. These defects are often accompanied by global or gene-specific methylation changes, contributing to hepatic lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and inflammation [64–67]. Although many animal models have demonstrated that a choline-deficient diet can induce hepatic lipid accumulation and promote the development of NASH [68–70], the underlying mechanisms by which choline deficiency contributes to disease progression remain poorly understood. Recent studies show that suppression of peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor alpha (PPARα) transcription may be one such mechanism. The effects of PPARα repression observed in knockout (KO) mice are captured in preclinical models of choline-deficient high-fat diet (HFD) and excess energy substrates [64, 65]. In these models, hypermethylation of the PPARα promoter (+27.5% methylation vs. untreated controls) [64] and downstream gene targets including retinol saturase (Retsat;+13% methylation vs. untreated controls) and enoyl-CoA delta isomerase 1 (Eci1;+36% methylation vs. untreated controls) [65], coincided with metabolic disturbances and impaired lipid metabolism and redox regulation - characteristic histopathological features of MASLD [65]. Notably, these changes were accompanied by upregulation of DNMT1, a key DNA methyltransferase with PPAR response elements in its promoter, suggesting reciprocal feedback between PPARα signaling and DNA methylation machinery [65]. Recent work in a mouse model with heterozygous KO of Pcyt2 - the gene encoding CTP: phosphoethanolamine cytidylyltransferase - revealed extensive, liver-wide epigenetic reprogramming, including over 9500 differentially methylated regions (DMRs), predominantly in gene bodies, intergenic regions and gene promoters [71]​. Integrated methylome-transcriptome analyses further identified differentially methylated and expressed genes involved in mitochondrial function, lipid metabolism, inflammation and glucose homeostasis, mapping to insulin, phosphoinositide 3-kinase / protein kinase B, and forkhead box O signaling pathways​[71]​. Importantly, the hepatic methylome and transcriptome signatures in this model overlapped with those in a methionine-choline-deficient NASH model, suggesting a shared, epigenetically regulated gene network linking choline metabolism to MASLD pathogenesis beyond PPARα [71]​.

Currently, MASLD treatments are based on lifestyle changes, such as dietary modifications and physical activity, as there is no consensus on effective pharmacological therapies [72]. Preclinical evidence suggests that choline supplementation shows promising potential in ameliorating MASLD-associated loss of PPARα. In one model, choline restored transcription of Pparα and its downstream gene network, therefore enabling genes critical for lipid oxidation [65]. In a model of hepatocyte cultures under metabolic stress, choline treatment restored expression and activity of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A—a direct PPARα target and crucial enzyme involved in fatty acid oxidation—while concurrently reducing fatty acid synthase expression and activity, consistent with lower triglyceride accumulation [64]. These effects appeared to be mediated, in part, by dynamic regulation of DNA methylation, supporting the broader role of choline as an epigenetic modulator of lipid metabolism [64]. When hepatocytes were treated with choline outside of a state of induced metabolic stress, increased methylation of the Pparα promoter was seen (+30% methylation vs. untreated control) [64]. In contrast, choline treatment during excess energy substrate exposure reduced promoter methylation (-50% methylation vs. untreated control), indicating that methylation changes are both dynamic and context-dependent [64]. This is particularly relevant given choline’s dual role as a methyl donor and a precursor for phospholipid synthesis; under certain physiological conditions, its metabolic partitioning toward phosphatidylcholine synthesis may deplete SAM and contribute to reduced methylation capacity, despite choline availability [73]. Although phosphatidylcholine synthesis is an important fate of choline, the present review centers on choline’s methylation-related functions, and thus broader metabolic pathways implicating phosphatidylcholine will not be considered in detail. Further, fibrates and omega-3 fatty acids are established activators of PPARα, widely used to lower triglycerides and reduce cardiovascular disease risk [74]. Although pharmacological modulation of DNMT1 by these agents has not been demonstrated, PPARα activation may influence inflammatory signaling and transcriptional networks that could indirectly alter DNMT1 activity [75]. Choline-derived betaine supports DNMT1-dependent DNA methylation, and thus may intersect with the role of fibrates and omega-3 fatty acids, although direct links have yet to be defined.

Given the hallmark feature of oxidative stress in MASLD pathogenesis, the role of choline in enhancing oxidative defense is also promising [64, 66]. In particular, choline has been shown to increase glutathione (GSH) peroxidase activity and expression in a human hepatic cell culture model [64]. This upregulation of antioxidant enzymes aligns with the findings of another study where choline supplementation in a MASLD-rat model increased and restored hepatic DNA methylation and SAM:SAH levels, and improved redox status [66]. Although gene-specific methylation was not examined, improvements in antioxidant balance (e.g., GSH:glutathione disulfide) correlated with restored DNA methylation levels (+0.75% DNA methylation vs. untreated control), suggesting reactivation of epigenetically silenced metabolic and antioxidant genes [66]. Choline-treated rats also exhibited improvements in disease-relevant clinical parameters, including lowered fasting glucose levels, serum triglyceride levels, homeostasis model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) values, and hepatic lipid content, and corrected hepatic histopathology [66].

Beyond hepatic lipid metabolism, choline plays a crucial role in maintaining intestinal lipid handling, in part, through site-specific DNA methylation. In a yellow catfish HFD model, choline supplementation ameliorated HFD-induced intestinal triglyceride accumulation, impaired chylomicron (CM) assembly, and structural damage by suppressing fatty acid re-esterification, notably via downregulating expression of the diacylglycerol O-acyltransferase (Dgat1) gene, and restoring expression and activity of CM assembly genes apolipoprotein B (Apob), microsomal triglyceride transfer protein (Mtp), and secretion associated ras-related GTPase 1B (Sar1b) [67]. Mechanistically, choline suppressed protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK)-mediated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress—a key disruptor of CM assembly—by increasing methylation of the Perk promoter (+0.5 change in relative methylation level), with the − 211 methylation site showing the greatest sensitivity to dietary choline [67]. This epigenetic regulation required DNMT activity and was functionally necessary as inhibition of DNA methylation machinery or mutation of the − 211 site abolished the protective effects afforded by choline [67].

Taken together, findings from preclinical studies position choline as a potential candidate for MASLD prevention and treatment through targeted epigenetic reprogramming. Given the role of choline in modulating methyl donor availability and hepatic DNA methylation, its influence may extend beyond steatosis and inflammation to impact hepatocarcinogenesis.

Hepatocarcinogenesis—preclinical

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), the predominant type of primary liver cancer, is one of the leading causes of cancer-related mortality around the world [76]. Due to its often-silent progression and late diagnosis, considerable attention has turned to identifying early-detection biomarkers, including DNA methylation patterns [77]. The progression from MASLD to HCC is a recognized clinical trajectory in which aberrant DNA methylation patterns may act as active drivers of hepatic tumor development [78–81]. However, changes in DNA methylation do not occur uniformly throughout hepatocarcinogenesis, and their timing and functional consequences are nuanced with context-specificity modulated by the availability of choline.

Emerging preclinical evidence suggests that methylation changes in HCCs evolve alongside pathological changes in the cellular landscape of liver, making them highly context-specific and stage-dependent [78–81]. In rats chronically deprived of dietary choline, global hepatic DNA hypomethylation (-5% methylation vs. supplemented group) correlated with the timing of tumor development and frequent HCC formation, appearing around 14 months [78]. Although this methylation change was observed, its functional relevance remains unclear as a direct link between methylation and disease risk was not established. Loss of methylation was slow, and seen only after signs of liver injury subsided, suggesting a role in late-stage tumor progression rather than initiation [78]. Hypomethylation was notably not uniform either, as many regions of the liver maintained normal methylation patterns despite choline deficiency [78]. This concept of stage-specific epigenetic reprogramming is further supported by a study of liver carcinogenesis in flatfish, which found that both hepatocellular adenomas (HCA) - benign liver tumors - and adjacent, non-tumor-bearing regions of liver tissue in the same fish exhibited elevated levels of SAH and DNA hypomethylation (-3% methylation) [82]. Unlike the epigenetic shifts seen in late-stage tumor progression, these changes occurred in HCAs and histologically normal-appearing tissue, suggesting that, at least in this model, epigenetic dysregulation begins early and may prime the liver for carcinogenesis [82].

Furthermore, epigenetic changes driven by choline deficiency may not only serve as early markers but also shape hepatic cancer trajectories. In the flatfish model of hepatic cancer, tumorigenesis involved epigenetic reprogramming as HCA samples displayed distinct methylation and gene expression profiles from distal tissue of the same fish at many genes involved in regulating the cell cycle, apoptosis, and intercellular signaling [82]. As well, Dnmt1 was hypermethylated while de novo methyltransferases were hypomethylated in HCA tissue (absolute changes in methylation not reported), indicating reprogrammed methylation machinery [79]. Notably, choline levels and Bhmt expression were lower in HCA-bearing livers, implicating disrupted one-carbon metabolism in hepatocarcinogenesis [79].

In another study using HFD mouse models of NASH-driven HCC, DMRs emerged during the transition from NASH to dysplasia, rather than during tumor formation, suggesting an earlier dysregulation of epigenetic remodeling in NASH -driven HCC [80]. Metabolic status was another important consideration as obese mice exhibited a higher number of DMRs between HCC and NASH stages than lean mice, indicating greater epigenetic dysregulation in obesity-driven HCC [80]. The nature of the pathways affected also differed between these groups; in lean, choline-deficient mice, methylation changes were enriched in genes involved with cancer progression and prognosis, and lipid metabolism [80]; in contrast, obese, choline-supplemented mice showed altered methylation patterns in genes associated with HCC, including components of Wnt/β-catenin signaling—a pathway implicated in several mechanisms of hepatocarcinogenesis [80].

The Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway has also emerged as a key player in hepatocarcinogenesis in other preclinical studies, which may indicate its high sensitivity to methylation changes [79, 81]. In non-obese, flatfish HCA samples, methylation and/or corresponding expression patterns of various genes - including those involved in the Wnt signaling pathway - differed compared to normal tissue (≥ 1.5-fold change), suggesting that methylation-mediated dysregulation of this pathway can occur in the absence of obesity [79]. In a mouse model with KO of the adenomatous polyposis coli tumor suppressor gene (ApcKO), where β-catenin is aberrantly activated, HCC development and hepatocyte proliferation ensued; such pathogenesis occurred in addition to disrupted choline metabolism, widespread DNA hypermethylation (+0.75% of total methylated DNA vs. control), and greater expression of DNMT1/3a [81]. ApcKO HCC cells incorporated radiolabeled methyl groups from choline into DNA, indicating that choline contributed directly to the tumor methylome [81]. While choline deficiency has been implicated in promoting liver cancer [78–80], dietary choline restriction in these mice reversed hypermethylation (-1.5% of total methylated DNA) and promoted regression of β-catenin-driven HCCs [81], suggesting that choline may play a dual role in hepatic cancer via epigenetic modulation.

Ultimately, preclinical studies to date emphasize the potential for DNA methylation changes to serve as a dynamic and modifiable layer of regulation in hepatocarcinogenesis. Moreover, choline administration may influence the balance between choline’s role in phosphatidylcholine synthesis - required for rapid membrane biogenesis - and its role in one-carbon metabolism as a methyl donor for DNA methylation in cancer cells [83]. Tumors with elevated choline kinase activity may preferentially channel choline into membrane synthesis [84], whereas alterations in methylation potential via betaine may influence DNA methylation of oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes [85]. Altogether, further evaluations of the role of choline in carcinogenesis are warranted, as this line of inquiry may hold the key to earlier detection, more precise classification, and metabolically informed treatment of cancer.

Diabetes, obesity and metabolic dysfunction—preclinical and clinical

Aberrant DNA methylation can disrupt key metabolic pathways, promoting insulin resistance, altered glucose handling, and lipid dysregulation, and has been raised as a key feature in the pathogenesis of diabetes, obesity and metabolic dysfunction ​[86]​. A consistent theme across both in vitro and in vivo models is that choline metabolism and methylation potential are dynamically regulated by the interplay between hormonal signals and nutrient status ​[87–89]. In a rodent model of type 1 diabetes, insulin deficiency upregulated hepatic Bhmt expression and activity, and reduced the SAM:SAH ratio, suggesting an impaired methylation capacity [87]​. Insulin treatment largely reversed these alterations, demonstrating its regulatory role in methyl donor flux [87]​. In vitro, choline supplementation enhanced Bhmt1 expression in HepG2 hepatocytes under low-glucose conditions, enhancing methylation capacity [88]​. However, when both choline and glucose were in excess, this effect was blunted, suggesting a nutrient-sensitive feedback mechanism to prevent excessive methyl group donation [88]​. In parallel, choline increased Dnmt1 and Dnmt3a expression and global CpG methylation levels, regardless of glucose concentration; although under nutrient-rich conditions, upregulation of methylation machinery did not translate into further increases in CpG methylation [88], which may indicate fine-tuning of epigenetic mechanisms in response to nutrient and hormonal cues.

Nutrient intake and metabolic status are modulators of choline utilization, suggesting context-specificityin the role of choline in regulating metabolic health. In healthy humans, a glucose load lowered plasma SAM levels and increased urinary excretion of choline and betaine, indicating a potential downregulation of BHMT-mediated methylation [89]​. Similarly, fat ingestion depleted circulating choline and betaine levels, possibly because of diversion of these nutrients into phosphatidylcholine synthesis and lipid transport [89]​. These postprandial shifts highlight a macronutrient-sensitive methylation network that dynamically prioritizes choline’s role as a methyl donor or structural component, depending on metabolic demand. Beyond influencing methylation marks, choline can also mitigate hyperglycemic stress-induced dysregulation of metabolic genes involved in glucose production and fatty acid oxidation. In a preclinical model of hyperglycemia, choline supplementation was associated with reduced expression of phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase and increased expression of acyl-CoA oxidase 1 ​[88]​, without impacting genes that mediate triglyceride synthesis and lipogenesis; these findings suggest that choline can enhance catabolic pathways to counteract hyperglycemia and lipid accumulation.

Given the role of choline in mediating nutrient-sensitive epigenetic responses in adulthood, its influence during early development—a period marked by heightened epigenomic plasticity—is of particular interest. Responsiveness to external signals in the early life period can be reflected by changes in DNA methylation [90]. Choline has been proposed to contribute to the establishment of these developmental epigenetic marks, potentially shaping long-term metabolic outcomes and disease risk [61, 91–93]​. Across preclinical and clinical models, prenatal choline intake has been shown to alter offspring metabolism, accompanied by changes in DNA methylation in the placenta and in offspring liver and brain. However, reported effects vary with choline dose, offspring sex, and timing of exposure; composition of the postnatal diet can further modify the metabolic and epigenetic impacts of prenatal choline intake.

Gene-level analyses reveal that choline-mediated changes in methylation are central to developmental metabolic programming. In a human birth cohort study, analysis of placental tissue revealed that higher maternal choline levels were associated with reduced methylation of several key metabolic genes, such as Pparγ1, Pparα, and retinoid X receptor alpha (Rxrα) (absolute changes in methylation not reported) [92]. In particular, reduced placental methylation of the Rxrα gene correlated with increased Rxra expression and lower offspring birth weight and BMI, implicating Rxra as a potential epigenetic mediator of choline’s growth-regulating effects [92]​. In fetal livers of mice exposed to both a HFD and choline supplementation prenatally, CpG hypermethylation (+0.025 methylation rate vs. controls of normal fat diet with/without supplementation) of the sterol regulatory element binding transcription factor (Srebf1) gene promoter was associated with reduced gene expression and lower hepatic triglyceride levels. However, this increase in methylation did not persist when offspring continued on a HFD; instead, a distinct CpG site became hypermethylated (+0.07 methylation rate vs. normal fat diet, supplemented control) without changes in gene expression ​[91]​. Additional loci showed similar patterns; in fetal mouse hippocampi, prenatal choline supplementation increased methylation at the DMR0 region of the insulin-like growth factor (Igf2) (+0.05 methylation rate vs. normal fat diet, unsupplemented control), without changes in gene expression [91]. Yet these prenatal effects were again overridden in an obesogenic postnatal environment, where all groups fed a post-weaning HFD showed elevated methylation of the Igf2 DMR0 region, regardless of prenatal choline exposure. As well, no methylation differences were observed at the leptin promoter, despite reductions in adipocyte size, suggesting that choline may regulate adipose tissue through mechanisms beyond promoter methylation [91]. Further complexity arises when deciphering markers of DNA methylation as prenatal choline supplementation can increase global DNA methylation and upregulate Dnmt1 expression in the fetal mouse liver and brain, yet is accompanied by reduced SAM:SAH ratios [91]​. Similarly, SAM:SAH ratios have been found to not differ between offspring of dams fed diets of excess or deficient choline, despite differences in metabolic outcomes [93]​, cautioning the use of SAM:SAH as a linear predictor of methylation capacity.

Choline’s epigenetic effects on metabolic regulation are reported to be both time- and sex-dependent. In a rodent study, prenatal choline supplementation led to lower body weight, reduced food intake, and enhanced insulin sensitivity in male and female offspring. However, insulin sensitivity decreased over time, likely due to the adverse, HFD post-weaning environment ​[61]​. Similarly, prenatal choline supplementation resulted in significantly higher levels of global DNA methylation in the rodent fetal liver (+1.4-fold difference) and brain (+0.7-fold difference) compared to unsupplemented controls, but methylation levels of supplemented groups were significantly lower postnatally in the liver (-4.5-fold difference) and visceral adipose tissue (-0.35-fold difference) after 6 weeks of high-fat feeding, suggesting that early methylation marks may be reversible and sensitive to postnatal exposures [91]​. Notably, female rodent offspring supplemented prenatally displayed more favorable metabolic outcomes, including higher insulin sensitivity both at weaning and after 12 weeks post-weaning [61]​, whereas improvements with prenatal choline supplementation in males emerged at the later time point [61]​. These differences were accompanied by sex-specific epigenetic modifications: females exhibited lower promoter methylation (-3% average methylation at weaning, -15% at post-weaning vs. untreated control) and higher expression of insulin receptor substrate 2 (Irs2), while males showed higher methylation (+14% average methylation at post-weaning vs. untreated control) and reduced expression of Irs1 [61]​. Collectively, these preclinical findings highlight the dynamic and environmentally modifiable nature of choline-mediated epigenetic programming, with sex-specific outcomes in metabolic health.

The long-term effects of prenatal choline intake may also depend on the nature of the postnatal dietary environment. In the absence of obesogenic postnatal conditions, both high and insufficient maternal choline intake during pregnancy were associated with disrupted energy balance in offspring ​[93]​. Offspring of high choline-fed dams that were weaned to a regular-fat diet exhibited increased food intake and adult weight gain, while offspring of low-choline dams showed reduced locomotor activity and energy expenditure despite similar caloric intake and birth weight [93]​. In the same study, high prenatal choline intake was associated with increased expression of orexigenic neuropeptide Y (Npy) in the offspring hypothalamus, consistent with hyperphagia, while choline deficiency reduced leptin receptor (Ob-R) expression, potentially impairing leptin signaling and reducing energy expenditure ​[93]​. These region-specific changes persisted into adulthood [93], suggesting a form of fetal “metabolic memory”​; however, gene-specific DNA methylation patterns remain to be elucidated to confirm the epigenetic basis of these long-term changes.

Taken together, these findings highlight the multifaceted role of choline in shaping metabolic health through DNA methylation.

Neurodevelopmental process and function—preclinical

Beyond metabolic outcomes, accumulating evidence suggests that DNA methylation plays a crucial regulatory role in hippocampal neurogenesis, angiogenesis, and neuronal maturation, and choline has emerged as a modulator of these processes [94–96]. Across various developmental stages and hippocampal subregions, choline availability influences DNA methylation in context-dependent and gene-specific manners that can shape the development and function of the hippocampus [94–96].

Maternal choline deficiency in rodent models has been implicated in targeted DNA hypomethylation in the fetal hippocampus, though this change can diverge by subregion [94, 95]. Niculescu et al. (2006) found lower global DNA methylation levels (-50 methylation units vs. control, qualitative scoring) and hypomethylation of the Cdkn3 promoter in the ventricular and subventricular zones of fetal mouse hippocampus (-15 in qualitative methylation scores of CpG islands), which correlated with greater expression and nuclear localization of cell cycle inhibitors (p15INK4b and KAP), and with suppression of neural progenitor cell (NPC) proliferation [94]. Similarly, Mehedint et al. (2010) observed hypomethylation of CpG islands in proximity to promoters of angiogenic genes vascular endothelial growth factor C (Vegfc) and angiopoietin 2 (Angpt2) (absolute changes in methylation not reported), and higher expression of these genes in NPCs of fetal mouse hippocampus under choline-deficient conditions [95]. This maladaptive response promoted endothelial cell differentiation and reduced proliferation and vessel density in the hippocampus [95]. Interestingly, the impact of maternal choline deficiency was not uniform across hippocampal subregions as, unlike the vulnerable ventricular and subventricular zones, the dentate gyrus did not differ in DNA methylation [94]. Instead, this region exhibited higher expression of calretinin, a marker of neuronal differentiation, through non-methylation-dependent mechanisms [94], highlighting a nuanced and region-specific role for choline in neural development.

Following fetal development, a second period of heightened hippocampal plasticity is adolescence, during which choline continues to support neurodevelopment and may contribute to epigenetic restoration. Nicotine exposure during adolescence in mice results in hypermethylation of promoter regions in over 400 genes in the adult dorsal hippocampus (absolute changes in methylation not reported), corresponding with decreases in gene expression and impairments in hippocampus-dependent learning tasks [96]. These effects were not observed when nicotine exposure occurred only in adulthood, highlighting adolescence as a critical developmental window [96]. Notably, adolescent choline supplementation reversed nicotine-induced changes in methylation and restored performance in hippocampus-dependent learning tasks in the rodent model [96]. The direction of methylation change was also gene-specific as choline both increased and decreased methylation across different gene promoters, suggesting that its effects are context-dependent and corrective (absolute changes in methylation not reported) [96]. The genes most responsive to nicotine and choline in preclinical models were noted to be involved in chromatin remodeling and neurodevelopmental regulation, including SWI/SNF related matrix associated, actin dependent regulator of chromatin, subfamily A, member 2 (Smarca2) and BAH domain and coiled-coil containing 1 (Bahcc1) [96]; in both genes, nicotine upregulated promoter methylation (Smarca2 +0.1 relative methylation score, Bahcc1 +0.3 relative methylation score with nicotine vs. saline exposure) and decreased expression, while choline reversed these effects (Smarca2 -0.2 relative methylation score, Bahcc1 -0.3 relative methylation score) [96].

Overall, these preclinical findings position choline as both a developmental safeguard and repair agent, suggesting its potential to support neurodevelopmental resilience.

Effects of prenatal alcohol exposure—preclinical and clinical

Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder resulting from prenatal alcohol exposure (PAE) describes a wide spectrum of neurodevelopmental and physical impairments including deficits in learning, memory, and executive function [97]. Across preclinical and clinical models, PAE has been shown to induce both global and locus-specific disruptions in DNA methylation in the brain and placenta [98–100], while gestational choline supplementation has emerged as a candidate factor that may mitigate these effects [98–100].

PAE has been shown to induce global DNA hypo- and hyper-methylation, with the direction of change varying across study models. In a rodent model, ethanol exposure during the neonatal period—developmentally equivalent to the third trimester of pregnancy in humans—led to increased global DNA methylation (absolute methylation changes not reported) in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, regions central to cognition and memory [98]. In human placental tissue, global methylation diverged by sex, whereby male placentas from PAE pregnancies exhibited decreased global DNA methylation, and female placentas showed a trend toward increased methylation (absolute methylation changes not reported) [99]. Additionally, PAE has been shown to upregulate the expression of DNMT1 and DNMT3a in adult male rat offspring, suggesting that alcohol-related epigenetic disruptions may extend to the enzymes responsible for establishing and maintaining DNA methylation patterns [100].

Choline appears to act as a regulatory agent that can normalize aberrant methylation and enzymatic profiles in the context of PAE. In rodent models exposed to ethanol during the neonatal period, choline supplementation partially reversed ethanol-induced hypermethylation patterns in the prefrontal cortex (+0.180 in global DNA methylation index values) and hippocampus (+0.110 in global DNA methylation index values), while increasing methylation in these regions of control rats (-0.200 & -0.100 in global DNA methylation index values) [98]; these bidirectional effects suggest that choline has a state-dependent role through which it can rescue methylation profiles altered by ethanol exposure while enhancing baseline methylation under non-teratogenic conditions [98]. In line with this interpretation, another study found that maternal plasma choline concentrations correlated with dietary intake only among women reporting alcohol exposure prior to conception, however not among women without exposure or those exposed during both preconception and pregnancy [99]. Although these findings are likely influenced by suboptimal choline intake amongst participants [99], they imply differences in endogenous choline metabolism between groups and suggest that women with PAE may have an increased reliance on choline intake to support adequate methylation during pregnancy.

In addition to normalizing global methylation patterns, choline supplementation in the context of PAE appears to modulate the expression of key methylation enzymes. When supplemented alongside PAE, choline normalized DNMT1 expression at the mRNA and protein levels in the hypothalamus of adult, male rodent offspring, suggesting potential corrective effects on ethanol-induced methylation errors [100]. On the contrary, DNMT3a remained elevated in both ethanol-exposed and choline-supplemented rodent offspring groups, indicating that some enzymes may be less responsive to choline or upregulated as part of a broader adaptive mechanism [100]. These brain-based findings contrast with patterns observed in the human placenta where DNMT expression was decreased in male placentas - particularly when alcohol exposure occurred prior to conception and during pregnancy - consistent with observed decreases in global DNA methylation [99]. In females, DNMT expression did not show significant changes, however a compensatory increase in SAM and global methylation levels was seen when alcohol exposure was limited to the preconception period [99]. These sexually dimorphic and timing-dependent responses suggest that placental sensitivity to alcohol varies by fetal sex and exposure window, highlighting the need to clarify how early epigenetic changes translate to long-term neurodevelopmental trajectories.

Beyond global changes, PAE also induces gene-specific hypermethylation, and choline may restore the expression of functionally critical genes through its influence on DNA methylation. In a rodent model, analysis of β-endorphin (β-EP)-producing neurons revealed that alcohol exposure led to hypermethylation of the proopiomelanocortin (Pomc) gene promoter (-7000 of methylation/unmethylation ratio vs. control), resulting in lower mRNA expression and reduced β-EP neuron counts in offspring [100]. Choline supplementation during alcohol exposure reversed these effects by normalizing both Pomc promoter methylation (-5000 of methylation/unmethylation ratio vs. alcohol exposure) and expression, and by restoring neuron counts to control levels in rodent offspring [100]. These changes were accompanied by a reduced corticosterone hyperresponsiveness to an immune challenge, suggesting that the corrective effects from choline may extend to neuroendocrine systems disrupted by PAE [100].

Taken together, current preclinical and clinical findings support a role for choline in mitigating alcohol-induced epigenetic disruption at both global and gene-specific levels, with functional consequences for neurodevelopmental outcomes.

Developmental regulation of stress responses—clinical

Neuroendocrine responses to stress are orchestrated by the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical (HPA) axis, which plays a crucial role in maintaining mental and physical health. Dysregulation of the HPA axis is often marked by persistently high concentrations of corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) and cortisol, contributing to the pathophysiology of stress-related disorders, including anxiety, mood disorders, depression, and metabolic dysfunction [101–104]. During fetal development, the HPA axis can be shaped by epigenetic mechanisms, particularly through DNA methylation of key regulatory genes [105]. Choline has emerged as an important modulator of these epigenetic processes and in early-life programming of stress responses [106, 107].

Current clinical evidence supports a potential role for choline in protecting the developing fetus from environmental stressors and epigenetic dysregulation. In pregnant women, higher choline intake has been positively associated with increased DNA methylation at the Crh gene promoter in both the placenta (+4% average methylation) [106] and cord blood (absolute changes in methylation not reported) [107], resulting in reduced mRNA levels of Crh and lower fetal cortisol levels [106, 107]. Cord blood leukocytes displayed reduced methylation in response to higher choline intake (-2.7% average methylation), possibly reflecting a compensatory or feedback mechanism within the fetal immune system [106]. These effects appear to be independent of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) [107], as maternal choline intake was linked to higher Crh methylation and lower cord blood cortisol levels regardless of GDM status (absolute changes in methylation not reported). This raises the possibility that choline may buffer the developing fetus against a broad range of stressors [107]. Similar patterns were observed in the Nr3c1 gene, which encodes the glucocorticoid receptor: higher methylation in placental tissue (+0.7% average methylation) but lower methylation in cord blood leukocytes (-0.55% average methylation), with no corresponding changes in gene expression, highlighting the tissue-specific complexity of interpreting DNA methylation changes [106]. Together, these findings suggest that choline can regulate the HPA stress axis through gene- and tissue-specific epigenetic mechanisms.

Wilson’s disease—preclinical

Wilson’s disease (WD) is an autosomal recessive disorder arising from mutations in the ATPase copper transporting beta (Atp7b) gene [108]. Loss of Atp7b results in excessive copper accumulation, primarily in the liver and brain, which can manifest as a broad spectrum of hepatic presentations, ranging from fatty liver to cirrhosis and hepatic failure, as well as neurologic and psychiatric abnormalities [109, 110]. As genetic mutations alone have provided limited insights into different phenotypes, alternative mechanisms, including epigenetic disturbances and aberrant DNA methylation patterns, have been proposed to explain variations in WD presentation and progression [111–113].

Global DNA hypomethylation and widespread transcriptional abnormalities have been found in Atp7b-deficient, Jackson toxic milk (tx-j) mouse models of WD [111–113]. In one study, over 600 DMRs were identified between wild-type and tx-j livers [111], with elevated hepatic SAH levels, and downregulation of genes involved in antioxidant defense, such as thioredoxin 2 (Txn2), DNA methylation and repair, mitochondrial respiration, lipid metabolism, and cell cycle control [111–113]. Although Dnmt1 was downregulated at baseline, global hypomethylation did not ensue, suggesting that other determinants of methyl donor availability may be involved [112].

Currently, copper chelation with penicillamine (PCA) remains a standard treatment [114], but its use in severe or advanced cases of WD is cautioned due to potential neurotoxicity [115]. Nutritional interventions, such as choline supplementation, have garnered interest as therapeutic modifiers of epigenetic abnormalities associated with WD [111–113]. In tx-j models, gestational choline supplementation corrected global hypomethylation (-0.1 5-mC/DNA signal intensity vs. tx-j control) and many DMRs in fetal livers [111, 112], including Txn2 (absolute changes in methylation not reported). Expression levels of several genes affected by choline also positively correlated with body weight in tx-j pups [112], linking epigenetic changes to phenotypic outcomes. Moreover, changes in one-carbon metabolism were seen: SAH levels, already elevated in tx-j mice, were further increased by maternal choline intake in both control and tx-j offspring, potentially reflecting enhanced methyl group turnover [112]. While elevated SAH can impair methylation, choline supplementation continued postnatally may mitigate this effect by upregulating expression of S-adenosylhomocysteine hydrolase (Sahh) [113].

Sex-specific responses to choline and PCA treatments have been consistently observed in tx-j mouse models of WD, with females exhibiting more pronounced molecular and phenotypic improvements [111, 113]. While PCA has shown to lower hepatic copper in males and improve steatosis in females, changes in methylation markers such as SAM:SAH ratios and DNA methylation enzymes have not been observed [113]. In contrast, choline supplementation resulted in higher Sahh gene expression and altered global methylation in a sex-dependent manner (-0.11 5mC/DNA signal intensity males; -0.21 5mC/DNA signal intensity females) [113]. These molecular effects were accompanied by reduced steatosis, inflammation, and liver injury markers in females treated with either choline or PCA, while males showed more modest transcriptomic changes and improved oxidative stress profiles [111]. Choline was also seen to reduce nuclear-to-cytoplasmic diameter ratios in both sexes without altering liver histology, suggesting improved hepatocyte morphology [113].

Ultimately, these preclinical findings suggest that choline-targeted regulation of epigenetic and metabolic pathways may complement traditional copper-focused treatments and offer potential new avenues of intervention for WD.

Limitations and future directions

While evidence supports the role of choline in modulating DNA methylation and improving metabolic, hepatic, and neurodevelopmental outcomes, several limitations remain. Most findings to date are based on preclinical, rodent models, which can differ from humans in metabolic and developmental trajectories. Translational studies in humans are essential to confirm relevance, long-term safety, efficacy, and optimal dosage and timing windows for supplementation. As well, the potential for choline as an adjunct therapy in various disease contexts requires further evaluation.

Methodological limitations may also hinder interpretation. Many studies rely on bisulfite-based assays that cannot distinguish between 5mC and 5-hydroxymethylcytosine—epigenetic marks associated with gene repression and activation, respectively. This concern complicates the assessment of regulatory effects of choline and highlights the need for more refined molecular techniques to accurately map site-specific epigenetic effects. Moreover, while typically viewed as a methyl donor, choline can also reduce aberrant hypermethylation in certain contexts. Emerging evidence also suggests that choline influences not only overall methylation levels but also the genomic location of epigenetic changes, including non-canonical regions beyond CpG island promoters. Dissecting the cell-type-, gene- and locus-specific epigenomic landscapes affected by choline, and the temporal boundaries of choline responsiveness will be essential in mapping epigenetic impact, understanding long-term outcomes, and informing future interventions.

In the context of prenatal choline supplementation, the durability of epigenetic effects, particularly in adverse postnatal environments, remains unclear. In addition, although choline can modulate hippocampal and stress-related gene methylation, the behavioral relevance of these changes is not well understood. Sex-specific responses across developmental stages further complicate interpretation of the effects of choline supplementation. In models of PAE, a lack of gene-specific analyses and well-defined therapeutic windows make it difficult to assess choline’s full clinical potential. Looking forward, a deeper understanding of tissue-, sex- and gene-specific responses will be critical to developing targeted interventions. Beyond the role in one-carbon metabolism, it is important to recognize that choline can be metabolized by gut microbiota to trimethylamine, which is subsequently oxidized in the liver to trimethylamine-N-oxide (TMAO), a recently emerged marker of cardiovascular disease [116, 117]. Of note, microbial choline metabolism has also been reported to induce transgenerational epigenetic effects and anxious behaviors in both adult mice and their offspring [118]. In humans, plasma TMAO levels can vary considerably across individuals [119], likely reflecting interindividual differences in gut microbiota composition and genetics [119, 120], and the complex roles of choline metabolism [121].

An additional key limitation is that many studies reporting links between DNA methylation and gene expression describe correlations rather than demonstrating functional causality. It remains unclear whether methylation changes actively regulate transcription or simply accompany other molecular processes. This mechanistic ambiguity likely contributes to conflicting findings in choline literature where supplementation has been associated with both increases and decreases in methylation depending on the genomic context and developmental timing. Further work incorporating causal approaches will be essential to elucidate the biological relevance of methylation changes.

In light of the current paucity of clinical evidence on choline and DNA methylation, future research should combine methylomic and transcriptomic analyses with behavioral and physiological outcomes, and works towards strengthening translational impact through long-term human studies. Overall, a deeper understanding of nutrient-epigenome interactions across tissues, developmental stages, and disease conditions will be critical for the development of choline-based strategies to improve health.

Conclusions

DNA methylation is a dynamic and reversible epigenetic mechanism with a central role in regulating gene expression across health and disease. Dysregulated methylation patterns are implicated across models of metabolic, hepatic, and neurodevelopmental disease, where choline supplementation has shown potential to correct aberrant methylation and transcriptional profiles while ameliorating pathological phenotypes. However, the effects on DNA methylation afforded by choline appear to be highly context-dependent, capable of inducing a greater or reduced methylation status, depending on the gene targets, tissue types, developmental timing, and state of disease. This complexity is especially evident in the distinction between gestational and adult choline supplementation; while prenatal supplementation may program resilience in stress and metabolic pathways, later-life supplementation can also reverse or mitigate established pathological methylation profiles. Sex-specific responses further highlight the nuanced nature of choline’s epigenetic activity. Although modulation of DNA methylation may represent a promising strategy for disease prevention and intervention, across various developmental and disease contexts, there exists a growing need for precision in therapeutic approaches that account for timing, dosage, sex, and disease phenotype. Taken altogether, comprehensive analyses examining DNA methylation patterns that integrate with other omics platforms may identify actionable epigenetic targets and help guide effective methyl donor therapies for clinical applications.
