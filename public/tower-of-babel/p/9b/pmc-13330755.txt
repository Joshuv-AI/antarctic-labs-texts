Introduction

Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS), a type of immune-mediated acute self-limiting peripheral neuropathy, exhibits a wide spectrum of clinical manifestations. Its documented incidence is between 0.30 and 6.08 cases per 100,000 individuals, with no specific predilection for age or sex. GBS is classified into two major categories: classic and variant.1 Pharyngeal-cervical brachial (PCB) is a rare variant of GBS spectrum disorder. It is clinically characterised by dysphagia, weakness of the neck and upper extremities, and reduction or loss of upper limb deep tendon reflexes.1,2 It may also present with symptoms overlapping other GBS variants. Half of patients with PCB carry IgG anti-GT1a antibodies.2 Many neurologists are unfamiliar with PCB, which is often misdiagnosed as brainstem stroke, myasthenia gravis or spinal cord inflammation or infection. This report discusses a case of atypical PCB, which starts with shoulder and neck pain, and leads patients to first seek care initially in orthopedics, spinal surgery, or otolaryngology. Owing to its atypical initial symptoms and rapid progression, this condition is easily misdiagnosed. Thus, analyses of the clinical data, diagnosis, and treatment of this case were performed to improve the clinical understanding of this disease.

Materials and Methods

A case report of atypical Guillain-Barré syndrome in Academy of Traditional Chinese Medicine Affiliated Hospital, China.

Case Presentation

The patient was a 58-year-old woman with no preexisting chronic conditions, substance use, family history of disease. She reported a 10-hour history of neck pain, which gradually progressed to pain in the shoulders and limbs, prompting her to visit the emergency department early in the morning on foot. During the clinical interview, she mentioned experiencing a common cold 2 days before admission and no fever.

Initially, her vital signs included a temperature of 36.3°C, blood pressure of 135/80 mmHg, and a heart rate of 78 beats per minute (bpm). On physical examination, limb motor function was normal, and there were no signs of ataxia, dysarthria, or altered consciousness. Therefore, the attending doctor requested a cervical CT scan due to suspicion of cervical spondylosis.

As her condition progressed, the patient developed dysphagia accompanied by weakness in the left upper limb. In the afternoon, the left upper limb motor strength was weaker, and the right upper limb was weak. The patient experienced weakness in both lower limbs, bladder dysfunction, hoarseness, and nighttime dyspnoea within 10 hours of admission. Given the breathing difficulties, she underwent orotracheal intubation and was transferred to the intensive care unit(ICU).

Physical examination in the ICU: the blood pressure was110/68 mm Hg and heart rate was 109 times/min, orotracheal intubation and ventilator assisted breathing, cardiopulmonary and abdominal examinations revealed no obvious abnormalities. Neurological examination: clear, bilateral pupils were equally large, d = 3.0 mm, photosensitivity, adequate eye movements, and no nystagmus. Gross bilateral hearing was normal, with bilateral frontal lines were symmetrical. The motor exam showed Medical Research Council (MRC) scale power of 1/5 in both upper limbs and 3/5 in both lower limbs, with absent deep tendon reflexes and no muscle atrophy. Pathological signs were not elicited, and meningeal irritation signs were negative. Neurological investigations included brain and cervical MRI, lumbar puncture for cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis (protein levels, white blood cell counts, culture), ganglioside antibody testing, and electromyography (EMG) to assess nerve conduction. The patient was treated with intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and supportive care, including tracheostomy management, as needed.

Brain and cervical MRI yielded unremarkable results (Figure 1). CSF analysis showed increased protein levels, slightly increased CSF white blood cell counts, and normal culture results. Discussion

The symptoms of GBS vary in terms of presentation and severity. Besides weakness and sensory disturbances, patients may experience cranial nerve involvement, respiratory insufficiency, autonomic dysfunction, and pain. PCB is a rare variant of GBS spectrum disorder and is clinically characterised by dysphagia, weakness of the neck and upper extremities, decreased or absent tendon reflexes in the upper extremities, and mild-to-minimal lower-limb involvement. However, different variants of GBS can overlap, and some patients progress to lower-limb involvement.1 In this case, the initial symptoms were neck and shoulder pain, and the patient had dysphagia accompanied by weakness in both upper limbs. The condition progressed to peak severity within 24 h, and the symptoms successively included lower-limb and bladder dysfunction. In summary, the patient was diagnosed with GBS with features of the PCB-variant, possibly overlapping with those of the paraparetic variant. A similar case was previously reported.3 PCB-variant GBS is often preceded by prodromal infection. Ganglioside GT1a is mainly distributed in the oropharyngeal and cervical brachial musculatures, and the anti-GT1a antibody is most strongly associated with the PCB-variant of GBS.3 However, autoimmune peripheral neuropathy antibodies in both serum and CSF were negative in this case.

Pain is a notable symptom, which may be severe and often occurs in patients with GBS at any time during the disease course, even before the onset of weakness.4 Pain has rarely been reported as an initial symptom of GBS. Neck pain may be related to the PCB-variant5 or inflammation or infection in the spinal cord,6 which complicates early clinical diagnosis. In this case, the initial symptoms were persistent neck pain, which gradually developed into pain in the shoulders and limbs, then the patient was transferred to the orthopaedic ward. It makes complications of the diagnosis. Pain, especially the neck pain, maybe the initial symptoms of the GBS. Therefore, for patients with acute neck pain followed by weakness, clinicians should consider a differential diagnosis of cervical spondylosis, brainstem or spinal cord inflammation or infection, and GBS.

Diagnosis of GBS relies on a combination of clinical features, in addition to medical history, neurological examination, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, and electrodiagnostic results. In patients with suspected GBS, a diagnosis of GBS is more likely to occur if there is a history of recent (within 6 weeks prior) influenza-like illness (specificity up to 100%).7 In cases of diagnostic uncertainty, the European Academy of Neurology (EAN) Task Force recommends assessing antecedent events such as diarrhoea, respiratory infection, or fever as their presence can support a GBS diagnosis.8

CSF examination is often considered helpful in supporting GBS diagnosis, particularly when the diagnosis is uncertain or an alternative diagnosis needs to be excluded. Our patient underwent CSF examination on day 3 of the disease course to exclude inflammation or infection of the brainstem or spinal cord as the cause of her symptoms. The findings of mildly increased CSF protein and a slightly increased white blood cell count also supported the GBS diagnosis.8 A mildly elevated CSF white cell count (5–50/μL) was observed in approximately 19% of patients with GBS.9 Electrodiagnosis is an important test that is simple to perform and may aid in the early diagnosis. Prospective and retrospective studies that compared patients with either clinically suspected GBS (n = 84) or AIDP cases (n = 66) with varying control groups, indicated that multiple nerve conduction parameters become abnormal early in the disease course.8 Nerve conduction studies suggest that the PCB-variant is a subtype of GBS characterised by axonal rather than demyelinating neuropathy.2 Electrodiagnostic features of this patient were mainly axonal conduction failure, supporting this diagnosis.

The current American Academy of Neurology (AAN)-approved treatments for GBS include Plasma Exchange (PE) and intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) monotherapy. The patient received IVIG treatment, and the condition improved. After one year of follow-up, the patient’s condition had fully resolved, and she resumed work. Studies suggest that most individuals with GBS experience partial to full recovery, with 60–80% regaining independent ambulation within 6 months.10 Clinical observations have found that patients with PCB variants can recover rapidly and with significantly improved quality of life, even if the patient’s prior condition was severe.3,5,11

Conclusion

This case of the PCB - variant of GBS not only highlights its clinical complexity and the challenges posed by an atypical presentation but also carries clear clinical and educational implications. Specifically, it can progress rapidly and overlap with other GBS variants. Electrodiagnosis played an important role in this case due to its ease of use and may facilitate early diagnosis. Neck pain may be an initial symptom of GBS, particularly in the PCB-variant. Timely initiation of immunomodulatory therapies, such as IVIG, plays a crucial role in improving outcomes. Most PCB variants can achieve good progression and healing, even in severe cases.
