Multiomics analysis of the molecular and single-cell responses of rice after deep-space flight on Chang’e-5
Authors: Kai Sun, Jiameng Zhang, Haonan Li, Wenjing Song, Qunjie Zhang, Liqiu Ma, Jiafeng Wang, Wuming Xiao, Guili Yang, Ming Huang, Cuihong Huang, Danhua Zhou, Renjia Shen, Chun Chen, Meng Zhang, Chenyang Zhao, Zeyan Huang, Ping Wang, Jian Zhang, Jian Zeng, Yongzhu Liu, Hui Wang, Zhiqiang Chen, Tao Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Deep-space conditions exert severe stress on plant genome stability, gene expression, epigenetic modification, and cell differentiation. In this study, multiomics analysis is used to observe changes in rice at the molecular and cellular levels after deep-space flight, including an increase in the frequency and types of mutations. While overall DNA methylation levels do not significantly change, CHG methylation levels present an increase that is correlated with DNA methylation responses. RNA presents significantly elevated m6A modification levels, which positively regulate gene expression. The proportion of mesophyll cells decreases, and 188 genes are identified as affecting the differentiation of mesophyll cells. Integrated multiomics analysis supports a hypothesis that the NAC family transcription factor suppressor of variation transmission 1 (SVT1) negatively regulates MAPK pathway genes, potentially influencing differentiation of cells harbouring mutations. Overall, this study comprehensively describes the molecular map of rice after deep-space flight and proposes a putative mechanism through which SVT1 may adapt to deep-space flight by inhibiting the differentiation of cells harbouring mutations.

Introduction

Over the past several decades, scientists have investigated the mechanisms by which plants adapt to space flight by transporting plants on various platforms, such as recoverable satellites, spacecraft, and the International Space Station (ISS), and assessing changes in factors ranging from agronomic traits to the levels of different molecules1–5.

Space flight environments involve extreme conditions such as ionising radiation, microgravity, and variations in the magnetic field6. Research on the response of plants to space flight cannot only reveal the impact of extreme environmental factors on organisms in space but also lay a foundation for crop mutation breeding in space and provide interdisciplinary data for earth agriculture and medicine. Plants exposed to space flight undergo a variety of biological changes, such as genomic mutations induced by DNA damage2,5,7,8; transcriptional reprogramming of stress-responsive genes and other genes4,9–11; alterations in epigenetic modifications such as DNA and RNA methylation12–15, oxidative stress and defence responses16–19; metabolic adjustments in pathways such as phenylpropanoid biosynthesis20–23; and effects on cellular structure and function that lead to changes in cell proliferation and differentiation, along with abnormal growth and development17,24–27. Cascading systemic responses triggered by spaceflight not only directly influence plant growth and stress responses during missions but also may generate heritable molecular imprints that can affect plant responses postflight. These effects provide a unique resource for crop mutation breeding28–30. Furthermore, deep-space environments, such as those encountered in lunar or Martian missions, differ from those of low Earth orbit, with prolonged exposure to combined radiation and continuous microgravity coupled with the absence of Earth’s protective magnetic field, which may have more severe effects on organisms31.

Rice, as one of the world’s major food crops, was one of the earliest species used for space mutagenesis research and has been included in many experimental missions and different lines of research32–34. However, existing studies on space mutagenesis in rice have focused primarily on mutation frequencies, allelic mutations in key agronomic traits, and the identification of mutant offspring. There is still a lack of systematic and cross-omic in-depth analyses on how complex environmental factors unique to spaceflight trigger molecular cascade reactions in rice, including genomic instability, dynamic changes in epigenetic regulatory networks, and transcription and metabolic pathway remodelling.

The widespread application of omics technology in recent years has provided new opportunities for exploring how organisms adapt to spaceflight. While existing omics studies have achieved some progress, multiomics research on the molecular response mechanisms of plants to space flight is scarce, with only a few recent studies, mostly based on independent omics analyses and rarely involving the integration of two or more omics approaches35–37. Additionally, space flight may trigger specific molecular responses in different cell types. Single-cell sequencing can overcome the averaging effects of traditional sequencing technologies, which obscure cellular heterogeneity, enabling the identification and tracking of gene expression dynamic regulatory networks and key molecular pathways in responsive cells. Therefore, combining multiomics and single-cell sequencing could contribute to a more comprehensive and in-depth understanding of the mechanisms of rice adaptations to space flight. The construction of a high-quality reference genome also serves as a critical technical prerequisite for ensuring accurate integration and in-depth interpretation of multiomics and single-cell data. A high-quality reference genome enhances the continuity and accuracy of genome reconstruction, reduces annotation errors, captures specific mutations, and supports complex queries in multiomics integration38–41. This information provides a cornerstone for subsequent omics analyses, thereby improving the reproducibility and depth of research findings.

To clarify the molecular response and adaptation mechanism of the rice plant and cells to deep-space flight, rice seeds were transported by the Chang’e-5 lunar probe. After the seeds returned to the ground, 30 treated (TK) and 10 control (CK) plants were randomly selected, and molecular and single-cell maps of the rice after deep-space flight were constructed using multiomics technology combined with mutation screening, epigenetic modification detection, differential expression and cell differentiation analysis. Furthermore, these multiomics analyses allowed for the identification of the transcription factor SVT1. Combined with target gene identification and mutant phenotype analysis, it was found that SVT1 might inhibit the differentiation of cells harbouring mutations by negatively regulating the expression of genes involved in the plant MAPK signalling pathway (Fig. 1). Results

HJXSM de novo sequencing and reference genome assembly

To precisely identify mutation sites and analyse the differences in gene expression and epigenetic modifications between flown (treated-TK) and unflown (control-CK) rice samples, we performed de novo sequencing and genome assembly of the carried rice variety (HJXSM) using PacBio third-generation sequencing, Illumina next-generation sequencing, and Hi-C technology. PacBio sequencing yielded 18.5 Gb (47×) of data, Illumina sequencing provided 19.4 Gb (49×) of data, and Hi-C technology generated 61.4 Gb (155×) of data. The assembled genome size was 396.4 Mb, with a contig N50 value of 17.4 Mb (Supplementary Fig. 2a). Aligning the second-generation sequencing data back to the assembled genome resulted in a mapping rate of 98.52%, with 89.29% of the bases covered at a sequencing depth greater than 30× (Supplementary Fig. 2c). The completeness of the BUSCO genome was 96.92% (Supplementary Fig. 2b). A total of 99.73% of the Hi-C data were accurately anchored to the 12 chromosomes, and the completeness of the BUSCO genome after chromosome anchoring was 96.88% (Supplementary Fig. 2d). Gene annotation predicted a total of 36,836 protein-coding genes, with a BUSCO completeness of 95.51% (Supplementary Fig. 2e). Compared with the IRGSP1.0 genome, HJXSM presented high similarity in structural features such as gene length, coding sequence (CDS) length, exon length, exon count, and mRNA length (Supplementary Fig. 2f). Mutation characteristics and preferences induced by deep-space flight

To characterise the mutations induced by deep-space flight, whole-genome sequencing (WGS) was performed on 30 samples after deep-space flight (TK1-TK30) and 10 samples of the ground control (CK1-CK10). WGS yielded 770.6 Gb (48.6×) of clean data in total, which was then filtered, and the mutation sites that appeared in only a single sample were retained. A total of 276,898 true mutation sites were identified, which were uniformly distributed across the chromosomes without any obvious hotspots (Fig. 2b). The mutation frequency in TK samples ranged from 1.97 × 10 − 7 to 6.69 × 10 − 4, with a significantly greater number of mutation sites than in CK samples (Fig. 2c). The average mutation frequency of the TK samples was 306.64 times that of the CK samples. The number of mutation sites varied significantly among the different samples, with TK15 having the highest number of mutation sites (265,320) and TK28 having the fewest (78) (Fig. 2d). The observed differences in mutation frequency among samples might have been attributed to variations in the dose and site of space radiation exposure, as well as stochastic differences in individual biological responses. Both the CK and TK mutation sites were composed primarily of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs); however, insertion and deletions (InDels) accounted for a greater proportion of the TK mutations, reaching 42.37%. The percentage of heterozygous mutations in the TK samples was greater than that in the CK samples, ranging from 90.15% to 100%, with heterozygous frequencies mostly between 0.1 and 0.2 (Supplementary Fig. 3a). The distribution of mutation sites across the genome with respect to gene bodies was similar for the CK and TK samples, in the following order: intergenic>CDS>upstream and downstream (2 kb)>UTR (Fig. 2e). Among the exon mutations, the TK samples exhibited a greater proportion of nonframeshift and nonsense mutations (Fig. 2e). Additionally, the proportions of A → G and T → C transitions were significantly greater in the TK samples, whereas the G → A transition proportion was significantly lower (Fig. 2e). The above data suggests that the deep-space environment induces, in rice, a higher mutation rate and, in particular, is more likely to induce InDel and heterozygous mutations compared with other types of mutations.

In the TK samples, a total of 9154 genes carried at least one mutation site in the CDS, upstream and downstream sequence, or UTR. Enrichment analysis of the mutated genes revealed that these genes were related to biological processes such as DNA integration, recombination, packaging, and conformational changes (Supplementary Fig. 3c) and function in multiple DNA damage repair pathways (Supplementary Fig. 3d).

To further explore the relationship between mutation susceptibility and gene structure, the genes were classified into three categories according to their mutation rates (number of mutations/gene length): high-frequency mutated genes (HMGs), low-frequency mutated genes (LMGs), and non-mutated genes (NMGs) (Supplementary Data 1). Statistical analysis of 13 gene structural features revealed that the number of mutations was significantly positively correlated with 8 features, including gene length, and significantly negatively correlated with the GC content and exon GC content (Supplementary Fig. 3b). Compared with LMGs and NMGs, HMGs presented longer gene length, lower GC content, more and longer exons, lower exon GC content, more and longer introns, and significantly increased 5’UTR length (Supplementary Fig. 3e). These results indicated that DNA damage repair genes are more likely to mutate, resulting in the accumulation of mutations, and that the gene structure is more likely to vary.

DNA methylation patterns after deep-space flight

To investigate the impact of deep-space flight on DNA methylation patterns, whole-genome bisulfite sequencing (WGBS) was performed on 10 CK and 30 TK samples. Although there was no significant difference in genome-wide methylation levels between the CK and TK groups, the average methylation rate in the TK samples was slightly lower than that in the CK samples (Fig. 3a). Among the three methylation types in the CK and TK samples, CG methylation was the most common, followed by CHG and CHH (Fig. 3b). The distribution of methylation levels across gene regions showed distinct trends; while all three types of methylation were higher in the upstream and downstream regions and lower at transcription start sites (TSSs) and transcription termination sites (TTSs), CG methylation was also relatively high in the gene body and exhibited a peak-like pattern. CHG methylation in the gene body was only slightly greater than that at the TSS and TTS, whereas CHH methylation in the gene body was nearly identical to that at the TSS and TTS (Fig. 3c). The distribution of methylation in transposable element (TE) regions was opposite to that in gene regions, with all three types showing lower methylation levels in upstream and downstream regions and higher levels at the TSS and TTS. A total of 148,914 differentially methylated cytosines (DMCs) were identified between the CK and TK groups. However, compared with individual DMCs, differentially methylated regions (DMRs) often have more significant biological implications. In this study, 2381 DMRs were detected, with CG accounting for 72.69%, CHG accounting for 19.54%, and CHH accounting for 7.77%. CG and CHH DMRs were distributed relatively uniformly across the genome, whereas CHG DMRs were primarily concentrated in the gene body. Additionally, CG DMRs were associated mainly with increased methylation rates, whereas CHG and CHH DMRs were associated primarily with decreased methylation rates (Fig. 3e). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the differentially methylated genes (DMGs) associated with the three types of DMRs revealed that CG DMGs were related to biological processes such as DNA recombination and nucleotide metabolism, CHH DMGs were associated with oxidative reduction and amino acid metabolism, and CHG DMGs were linked to nucleic acid metabolism and chromosome assembly (Fig. 3f). Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that CG DMGs were involved in pathways related to the synthesis of various organic compounds, CHH DMGs were involved in the synthesis and metabolism of glutathione and other organic compounds, and CHG DMGs were involved in DNA damage repair pathways and plant MAPK signaling pathways (Fig. 3g). Notably, 83.3% of the CHG DMRs associated with these pathways presented increased methylation rates. In summary, the overall level of DNA methylation did not significantly change after deep-space flight, but the DNA methylation response after deep-space flight led to an increase in the CHG methylation level.

Effects of deep-space flight on gene expression and m6A modification

After assessing the mutation and methylation changes of the DNA, we subsequently assessed the changes in RNA methylation and gene expression. To determine the effect of deep-space flight on gene expression, RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) was performed on 10 CK and 30 TK samples. RNA-seq revealed 2648 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) (Supplementary Data 2), with 1390 upregulated and 1258 downregulated in the TK samples (Fig. 4a). m6A RNA methylation plays a significant role in gene expression. The methylated RNA immunoprecipitation sequencing (MeRIP-seq) results for the 10 CK and 30 TK samples showed that deep-space flight significantly affected m6A modification (Fig. 4b). The number of peaks in the TK samples was significantly greater than that in the CK samples (Fig. 4c), with a particular increase in peaks at the start codon and CDS (Fig. 4d). There were 429 differential peaks between CK and TK (Supplementary Data 3), with 134 RNAs showing increased methylation rates and 295 showing decreased methylation rates (Fig. 4e). These 429 differential peaks were located at 428 genes, and GO enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were involved in protein phosphorylation and hydrolytic processing (Supplementary Fig. 5a). KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that these genes are associated with the synthesis and metabolism of various organic compounds, including phenylpropanoids (Supplementary Fig. 5b). These enrichment results were similar to those for the DEGs identified by RNA-seq.

The correlation of the MeRIP-seq and RNA-seq data indicated that 61 out of the 428 genes with differential peaks also exhibited significant differences in expression levels (Fig. 4f), and m6A-modified mRNAs presented overall higher expression levels than did those without m6A modification (Fig. 4g, h). As m6A modification positively regulates mRNA expression, we focused on genes whose expression changes were consistent with the m6A peak abundance trends. Specifically, m6A modification led to a significant increase in the expression of 17 genes and a significant decrease in that of 14 genes, as shown in Fig. 4i (Supplementary Data 4). These results suggested that deep-space flight significantly regulates the level of m6A in RNA through m6A methylation and affects the expression of genes related to protein phosphorylation and the phenylpropanoid metabolic pathway.

Single-cell expression atlas after deep-space flight

To analyse the single-cell response mechanism and gene expression pattern after deep-space flight, a single-cell expression atlas was constructed using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) on 3 scCK samples and 3 scTK samples. The cells were classified into 13 clusters according to their distinct gene expression patterns (Fig. 5a). These clusters were further organised into 5 cell types, namely, primordium, mesophyll, parenchymal, bulliform and epidermal (Fig. 5b), according to their expression of cell marker genes (Supplementary Fig. 6a). The proportion of mesophyll cells in the scTK data was 54.28%, which was significantly lower than that in the scCK data (72.4%), whereas the proportions of the other 4 cell types increased slightly (Fig. 5c, d). Partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) analysis of these 5 cell types revealed that the primordium presented the earliest developmental stage, whereas the epidermis and mesophyll presented the highest differentiation levels (Fig. 5e). The results of the pseudotime analysis were consistent with those of the PAGA, indicating that differentiation started from the primordium, passed through 3 differentiation nodes, and ultimately produced 7 differentiation states (states 1–7) (Fig. 5f, Supplementary Fig. 6b). On this branch of state 4, the mesophyll ratio of the scTK samples was significantly lower than that of the scCK samples (Supplementary Fig. 6c–i). This would suggest that the primordium first differentiated into parenchymal and bulliform, after which fewer parenchymal cells differentiated into mesophyll, and more differentiated into epidermal cells, resulting in the reduction of mesophyll in the scTK samples, which may also be a regulatory and resistance mechanism of rice to deep-space flight stress. Among the 5 cell types, 7715 intergroup DEGs (4424 after removing duplicates) were identified (Fig. 5g, Supplementary Data 5). Pseudotime analysis revealed 2065 genes influencing cell differentiation fate around differentiation node 3 (Supplementary Data 6), with 1438 of these genes showing significant differences in expression between the scTK and scCK samples (Fig. 5h). In addition to the scRNA-seq data and in combination with the 2648 DEGs identified by RNA-seq, 188 out of the 1438 between-group DEGs that influence cell differentiation fate were differentially expressed (Fig. 5h). This “space-responsive gene set” (SRGS) of 188 genes was closely associated with single-cell response mechanisms following deep-space flight. Motif analysis of the SRGS suggested that the 4 most significant motifs all contained ACACG and were similar to those in the NAC family (Fig. 5i). Concurrently, KEGG enrichment analysis of the SRGS suggests significant enrichment in pathways such as MAPK signalling pathway-plant and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis (Fig. 5j). Taken together, the results suggest that the mesophyll proportion decreased significantly after deep-space flight. This process appeared to be influenced by the differential expression of genes in the SRGS, whereas the expression of NAC transcription factors, MAPK signalling pathway, and phenylpropanoid biosynthesis was associated with the single-cell adaptation mechanism of rice to the deep-space environment.

Joint multiomics identification of the transcription factor SVT1

Compared to single-omics approaches, multi-omics integration enables a more comprehensive elucidation of multi-layered and synergistic stress response networks under deep-space flight conditions. Integrative analysis across omics layers revealed that HMGs exhibited higher expression levels and were located in regions more prone to differential methylation, particularly of the CHG type. Furthermore, such epigenetic alterations, along with specific gene expression changes and an m6A-regulated gene network associated with cell differentiation, collectively responded to environmental stress (Supplementary Fig. 10).

To identify key regulatory factors influencing the response mechanisms to deep-space flight, integrated analyses of expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) and methylation quantitative trait loci (meQTL) were performed by combining WGS, WGBS, and RNA-seq. Among the 40,327 significant association signals detected by eQTL analysis, 99.57% were trans-associations. Similarly, among the 19,339 significant association signals detected by meQTL analysis, 99.32% were trans-associations, suggesting that trans-associations, specifically interactions with transcription factors, play an important regulatory role in adaptation to deep-space flight. When the association signals from eQTLs and meQTLs were plotted on the chromosome in 1 Mb intervals, a hotspot region of colocalisation was observed at the 24–30 Mb region on chromosome 4 (Fig. 6a). The gene with the greatest number of association signals in this region was SK1G00053966 (Os04g0515900), which is 2,387 bp in length and contains 3 exons and 2 introns, with the first and third exons encoding highly conserved NAC domains (Fig. 6b). These positioning results were consistent with the motif analysis results of the SRGS. To simulate the effects of radiation on seeds during deep-space flight, seeds of the wild type (WT), SVT1 knockout lines (svt1-1 and svt1-2) (Supplementary Fig. 8a), and overexpression line (SVT1-OE) (Fig. 7b) were irradiated with 300 Gy of γ-rays. scRNA-seq and WGS were subsequently performed on the WT and svt1-1 embryos. In addition, WGS and RNA-seq were performed on the WT, svt1-2, and SVT1-OE embryos. ScRNA-seq clustered the embryo cells into 24 clusters (Fig. 7c), which were grouped into 10 cell types (coleoptile, coleorhiza, endosperm, plumule, epidermis, mesophyll, radicle, lateral scale, and scutellum) according to marker gene expression (Supplementary Fig. 8b). Additionally, some cells with similar expression patterns were difficult to distinguish and formed 5 cell regions: scutellum-lateral scale (SL), endosperm-plumule (EP), scutellum-radicle (SR), coleoptile-lateral scale (CL), and scutellum-lateral scale-coleorhiza (SLC) (Fig. 7d). The proportions of SL, EP, plumule, and coleorhizal cells were significantly greater in svt1-1 than in WT, whereas the proportions of mesophyll and SR were lower in svt1-1 (Fig. 7e, f).

SVT1 was specifically expressed in the SL cell region (Fig. 7g), with particularly high expression in cluster 6 (SL-6) and cluster 16 (SL-16) (Supplementary Fig. 8c). SL-6 and SL-16 exhibited a direct differentiation relationship, and pseudotime analysis revealed that 637 genes influenced this cell differentiation fate (Fig. 7h, Supplementary Data 8). CytoTRACE analysis revealed that the differentiation potential of cluster 6 and cluster 16 was significantly lower than that of the other clusters (Fig. 7i, Supplementary Fig. 8e), further supporting the role of SVT1 in suppressing cell differentiation.

The WGS results revealed that, compared with the WT, svt1-1 presented a significantly greater number of mutations, with an average increase of 291 mutation sites per sample (Fig. 7j). Consistent with these findings, compared with that in the WT, the number of sites associated with mutation significantly increased in svt1-2, whereas it markedly decreased in SVT1-OE (Fig. 7k). The WGS results of the two batches showed a consistent trend, which confirmed that the function of SVT1 affects mutation. RNA-seq analysis revealed that the expression of genes associated with DNA damage repair was not significantly correlated with SVT1 expression. The expression trends of genes related to cell differentiation (PSK) and the MAPK pathway-plant (PR1) were inversely correlated with SVT1 expression (Supplementary Fig. 8d), suggesting that SVT1 may influence mutation by negatively regulating cell differentiation and the MAPK pathway-plant. Furthermore, the mutation sites identified in WT and svt1-1 were mapped onto the single-cell atlas. In WT, these mutation sites were predominantly concentrated in the SL region, whereas in svt1-1, they were uniformly distributed without obvious clustering (Fig. 7l, m). This distribution pattern resembled the single-cell mutation profiles obtained from the scCK and scTK analyses, in which the mutation sites were largely confined to cells specifically expressing SVT1. Together, these results support the hypothesis that SVT1, a nucleus-localised transcription factor, may influence the number of mutations by being specifically expressed in cells with high mutation rates and suppressing their differentiation.

To provide evidence for transcriptional regulation by SVT1, RNA-seq was performed on svt1-2 and SVT1-OE as well as WT plants, leading to the identification of 4322 DEGs (Fig. 8d, Supplementary Data 12). The intersection of these 4322 DEGs with the 1112 candidate target genes and 2150 genes known to affect cell differentiation yielded 40 common genes (Fig. 8e). Among these genes were three genes from the MAPK signalling pathway in plants, SK1G00063486 (Os07g0126301), SK1G00070333 (Os12g0586100), and SK1G00060048 (Os05g0474800), suggesting that they may be regulated by SVT1 and influence cell differentiation.

SK1G00063486, a gene from MAPK signalling pathway-plants and a potential target of SVT1, showed expression patterns correlated with cell fate transitions from parenchymal-5 to mesophyll-2 and from SL-6 to SL-16. Furthermore, the expression patterns of SK1G00063486 and SVT1 were consistent, with high expression in cells with high mutation rates, such as SL cells (Supplementary Fig. 9b). The expression level and proportion of SK1G00063486 were greater in svt1-1 than in WT (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Additionally, the RNA-seq results indicated that SK1G00063486 expression was significantly greater in svt1-2 and significantly lower in SVT1-OE than in WT (Supplementary Fig. 9d). These findings suggest that SVT1 may negatively regulate the expression of SK1G00063486.

To further investigate the regulatory mechanism of SVT1 on SK1G00063486, transcriptional activity assays were first conducted in rice protoplasts, revealing that SVT1 negatively regulates the expression of SK1G00063486 (Fig. 8f, and Supplementary Fig. 9e). Similarly, transcriptional activity assays in tobacco yielded consistent results, where the LUC signal driven by the pSK1G00063486 promoter was significantly reduced upon the addition of SVT1 (Fig. 8g, and Supplementary Fig. 9f). On the basis of the binding motif of SVT1 in the promoter region of SK1G00063486, three primers were subsequently designed for electrophoretic mobility shift assays (EMSAs). SVT1 reduced the electrophoretic mobility of probes containing the SK1G00063486 binding motif. In competition assays, the addition of unlabelled probes alleviated the probe retardation, whereas the addition of mutant unlabelled probes had no significant effect (Fig. 8h, Supplementary Fig. 9g), confirming a genuine interaction between SVT1 and SK1G00063486. These results confirm that SVT1 binds to the promoter of SK1G00063486 and represses its transcriptional activity.

Discussion

The deep-space environment imposes severe and multifaceted stress on plants, yet the underlying molecular and cellular adaptation mechanisms remain poorly understood. In this study, we performed an integrated multiomics and single-cell analysis of rice following deep-space flight on the Chang’e-5 mission. We systematically mapped genome-wide variations, epigenetic modifications, transcriptomic changes, and cell differentiation trajectories. Our integrative analysis identified the NAC transcription factor SVT1 as a key regulator that might inhibit the differentiation of mutation-carrying cells by negatively modulating the MAPK signaling pathway. Together, this study depicts a comprehensive molecular atlas of rice adaptation to deep-space flight and proposes a “differentiation suppression” mechanism for maintaining genome stability.

In this study, the daily absorbed dose rate DADR was measured using a Thermoluminescent dosimeter (TLD) associated with the rice seed samples aboard the Chang’e-5 spacecraft. The observed DADR during the Chang’e-5 mission was as high as 2.6 mGy/d, which was greater than that measured by the NASA HERA detector in the cislunar orbit (0.53 mGy/d) and in low Earth orbit42,43. The several-fold difference in radiation doses measured by the HERA detector and the Chang’e-5 mission in cislunar space was primarily attributed to differences in solar activity cycles. The Chang’e-5 mission (2020) occurred at the end of solar cycle 24, which was a solar minimum period, during which the shielding effect of the magnetic field against galactic cosmic rays (GCR) was minimized, resulting in a substantial increase in the GCR dose rate in deep space. In contrast, the HERA detector (2022) was in the rising phase of solar cycle 25, where stronger solar activity effectively suppressed the GCR flux, leading to a significantly lower dose rate compared with the former44,45. Indeed, onboard the ISS a DADR of 0.36 mGy/d is normally recorded, whereas the SJ-10 satellite measured only 0.006 mGy/d42,43. This suggests that the deep-space environment may impose more complex and severe conditions for plant cultivation and life in general.

Space flight can lead to mutations in the genomes of organisms, in which DNA repair genes are particularly fragile8. The frequency of mutations caused by space flight is typically between 10–8 and 10–446–49. However, we found significant differences among the singular plants, with mutation rate ranging from 1.97 × 10 − 7 to 6.69 × 10 − 4. In particular, in the TK15 plant, whose number of mutations was high, we found that exposure to the space environment had caused abnormal expression and function of DNA damage repair-related genes, likely leading to the inability to recognise and repair mutations, resulting in the accumulation of mutations (Supplementary Fig. 11, Supplementary Fig. 12, and Supplementary Data 14). The research report of SJ-10 stated that mouse embryonic stem cells at 1 day and 5 days after spaceflight downregulated DNA repair gene expression and exacerbated the accumulation of genomic damage, and this result was consistent with the data from the present study50. Additionally, some mutation characteristics in this study are consistent with previous findings, including the predominance of SNPs, a high proportion of heterozygous mutations, and low heterozygosity due to somatic mutation mosaicism48,51,52. Furthermore, while experiments on mice on the ISS indicated that space mutagenesis primarily involves CG → TA base transitions53, we found a significant increase in A → G and T → C transition types, indicating a difference in base transition trends between deep-space flight and low-Earth orbit. This also suggests a possible divergence between animals and plants in their susceptibility to space radiations. The assumption that these mutations are random is increasingly being questioned54–56. Studies have shown that the mutagenic effects of spaceflight are often concentrated in hotspot regions of the genome52,57,58. Although no significant hotspot enrichment was detected under the methodological conditions of this study, our characterisation of the structural features of highly mutated genes suggested that genes related to DNA conformation and damage repair are more prone to mutations, exacerbating the tendency towards genomic instability and indicating a certain preference for mutations caused by deep-space flight. Therefore, this study has advanced our understanding by demonstrating that deep-space flight mutagenesis preferentially targets key genes that maintain the stability of the genome itself, thus forming a vicious cycle of damage accumulation.

Multiple studies have shown that while the space environment may influence DNA methylation patterns, most of the significantly altered CpG sites return to baseline levels upon returning to Earth. For example, in the SJ-10 task, Arabidopsis seedlings cultured in microgravity for 60 h presented relatively low genome-wide CG, CHG, and CHH methylation levels, with methylated genes involved in hormone signalling, protein phosphorylation, and cell wall modification pathways29,59. Here, rice seeds flown in lunar orbit for 23 d presented reductions in average CG, CHG, and CHH methylation levels upon return to Earth and planting, although this change was not statistically significant. The differentially methylated genes were associated primarily with metabolic processes, stress responses, and transport processes13. Interestingly, the DNA methylation levels in human astronauts appear to be similar to those in plants. For example, genome-wide methylation characteristics of six astronauts during long-term isolation in the Mars-500 project indicated minimal changes in genome-wide DNA methylation, and similar conclusions were reported in the NASA twin study60,61. The results of this study are consistent with these previous findings, with no significant differences in whole-genome methylation levels between the TK and CK samples. The three types of methylation differed only slightly in their genomic distribution. The functions of the differentially methylated genes, in addition to stress responses and metabolic processes, as previously mentioned, included phenylpropane biosynthesis, plant MAPK signalling, and DNA damage repair. Furthermore, the functional relevance of CHG-type methylation is closely related to the findings of this study, potentially representing an important form through which deep-space flight affects rice DNA methylation. By identifying CHG methylation as a key mediator of space-induced epigenetic changes in rice. This study advances our understanding of how organisms adapt to deep-space stressors and highlights the need for targeted investigations into context-specific methylation mechanisms to mitigate long-term biological risks in space exploration.

Space flight also leads to changes in RNA methylation, but few reports on this topic exist. Currently, only studies on m6A methylation have been conducted in astronauts. The first m6A methylation atlas of human astronauts revealed that they experienced a significant increase in m6A methylation levels during their mission, impacting RNAs involved in pathways such as red blood cell regulation, stress induction, and immune changes62. Our study examines m6A methylation in rice after deep-space flight and similarly revealed that deep-space flight caused a significant increase in m6A methylation levels. However, unlike that in humans, the m6A modification in rice after deep-space flight in this study is related mainly to protein phosphorylation and phenylpropane synthesis, and nine genes affect cell differentiation, among which the DNA restriction modification system gene SK1G00051650 is also regulated by SVT1. Humans have a more complex immune system than plants do, and plants lack an active immune system, relying primarily on enhanced phenylpropanoid metabolism to strengthen their cellular defences and antioxidant capabilities63,64. Additionally, astronaut missions typically have longer durations, allowing cumulative systemic immune adaptation12, whereas the deep-space flight period for rice in this study was only 23 d, potentially triggering only acute stress responses and concentrating m6A modifications in rapid-response pathways. Despite these differences, both studies demonstrated the significant regulatory role of m6A methylation in response to environmental stress.

Space environments reshape gene expression through multidimensional mechanisms, with effects characterised by species and tissue specificity, time dependence, and environmental interactivity. For example, space flight primarily suppresses the expression of genes related to mouse embryonic stem cells, inflammatory responses, and oxidative stress while significantly upregulating genes associated with the cell cycle and apoptosis. Similarly, DNA repair genes, such as BRCA1, are also upregulated in human astronauts65–68. Changes in gene expression in plants under space flight conditions are a key focus of current space biology research, with reports on pathways involving cell wall remodelling, oxidative stress responses, metabolic adaptation, photosynthesis regulation, and DNA repair4,20,69–73. In this study, the transcriptomic DEGs were also involved in metabolic network reconstruction, including the sustained activation of genes related to lipid, flavonoid, and various sugar metabolism pathways. Additionally, space flight significantly affects the expression of genes associated with protein phosphorylation, phenylpropanoid synthesis, plant hormone signalling, and the plant MAPK signalling pathway. Future research should prioritise elucidating the functional consequences and adaptive significance of these specific pathway alterations, particularly the sustained metabolic shifts and MAPK signalling dynamics in plants.

The application of single-cell sequencing analysis to samples exposed to space environmental conditions provides a perspective on the effects of space environments on cellular molecular mechanisms. scRNA-seq can reveal cellular heterogeneity in populations under space flight conditions, such as the significant differences in gene expression observed in breast cancer cell populations74. Using scRNA-seq, researchers previously identified a “space flight signature” gene set that included genes related to oxidative phosphorylation, the UV response, immune function and the TCF21 pathway. This gene set was verified in independent datasets (such as those from the NASA twin study), indicating its conservation across species and experimental conditions65. In this study, scRNA-seq was conducted on rice subjected to deep-space flight, revealing cellular heterogeneity, specifically a significant reduction in the proportion of mesophyll cells in postflight samples. By combining pseudotime analysis and differential expression analysis, 188 genes responsible for this phenomenon were identified, including genes related to oxidative stress, phenylpropanoid synthesis, sugar metabolism, and plant MAPK signalling. This result highlights the impact of deep-space flight environments on gene expression and cellular adaptability. This study not only deepens our understanding of how the space environment reshapes plant cell composition and functional pathways but also, more importantly, elucidates the conservative stress response mechanisms and specific cell type sensitivities, identifying key molecular targets for predicting and intervening in the effects of the space environment on more complex biological systems, including future astronauts.

The genomic instability induced by space flight is not only a critical pathological mechanism linking exposure to the space environment to various health risks for astronauts but also a core adaptive mechanism that allows plants to cope with extreme conditions in extraterrestrial environments. In-depth research on this phenomenon is vital for ensuring astronaut safety, developing space crops, and elucidating space biology. Genomic instability caused by space flight typically manifests as DNA damage and mutations20,59. Organisms respond primarily by enhancing their DNA damage repair capabilities75–77. In addition, human studies have reported cases in which P53, BRUCE, PUMA and other proteins influence genome stability by altering cell differentiation78–81. This study proposed a mechanism through which rice maintains genomic stability and adapts to deep-space flight; this mechanism relies on the NAC family transcription factor SVT1 to inhibit the differentiation of cells harbouring mutations. The NAC family of transcription factors is among the largest plant-specific transcription factor families and plays a central role in the response to abiotic stress82. For example, SOG1 in plants assumes a role analogous to that of P53 in animals, responding to DNA damage, activating downstream genes, and coordinating processes such as cell cycle arrest, DNA repair, and programmed cell death83,84. Although SVT1 belongs to the same NAC family as SOG1, its mechanism of action differs. SVT1 does not directly participate in the DNA damage response (DDR) but may instead negatively regulate the MAPK signalling pathway, inhibiting the differentiation of cells harbouring mutations. This proposed mechanism could reduce mutation transmission and maintain overall genome stability. This “differentiation inhibition” mechanism complements other strategies for coping with genomic instability induced by the space environment, such as direct repair, metabolic support, and epigenetic regulation, resulting in the formation of a multilevel regulatory network. The discovery of SVT1 not only reveals the unique strategies by which plants can adapt to space but also provides a perspective for understanding the trade-off mechanism of “repair differentiation survival” in stress biology.

This study has several limitations. Removing shared mutation sites across multiple samples can effectively distinguish true mutations from background noise, significantly enhancing the specificity of mutation detection85,86. However, this approach carries the inherent risk of filtering out genuine recurrent mutations if they exist. Consistent with this concern, no significant mutation hotspots were identified in our analysis, but we cannot rule out the possibility that such hotspots, if they occurred recurrently across multiple samples, might have been excluded as “background noise.” Therefore, future validation using single-cell or independent approaches may help clarify whether deep-space flight induces truly non-random mutation patterns. In addition, we provide an initial exploration of SVT1 function. While the plant MAPK signalling pathway has been demonstrated to play a significant regulatory role in cell differentiation87,88, the molecular details of how SVT1 regulates the MAPK pathway to influence cell differentiation (such as protein phosphorylation and complex formation) remain unclear. Additionally, mutations exhibit substantial heterogeneity among cells, and WGS of tissue samples cannot provide comprehensive information about the full spectrum of mutation in each individual cell, necessitating single-cell level analysis of the effects of SVT1 on cells carrying mutations. In addition to the limitations discussed above, we acknowledge that direct experimental evidence supporting the core regulatory role of SVT1 in suppressing cell differentiation remains incomplete. Specifically, while scRNA-seq revealed correlations between SVT1 expression and altered mesophyll proportions, we have not yet provided direct functional validation to demonstrate that SVT1 actively inhibits differentiation of mutation-bearing cells. Furthermore, although we identified SK1G00063486 as a putative downstream target of SVT1, we have not yet provided direct genetic evidence that SK1G00063486 regulates rice cell differentiation or genomic variant accumulation. Genetic epistasis experiments have not been performed, and whether SK1G00063486 is the genuine downstream mediator of SVT1’s effects on differentiation and mutation transmission remains to be rigorously tested. Future studies will focus on these genetic validations, along with single-cell-level tracking of variant-carrying cells and quantitative assays of differentiation efficiency and mutation propagation.

In summary, this study used rice seeds transported by the Chang’e-5 spacecraft and combined multiple omics techniques to elucidate the mechanisms through which the deep-space environment affects plant mutation, gene expression, epigenetic modifications, and cell differentiation. By integrating these data, we propose an adaptive mechanism centered on the transcription factor SVT1, which may suppress the differentiation of cells harbouring genomic variations. This hypothesis provides a framework for future mechanistic studies on how plants maintain genomic stability under extreme environmental stress.

Methods

Plant materials

The main material was the pure indica rice line HJXSM, which was developed by the National Engineering Research Center of Plant Space Breeding of South China Agricultural University (SCAU) through hybridization of the high-quality disease-resistant indica rice variety Huahang 31 and the restorer line Hanghui 1508 and pedigree selection. This line integrates multiple disease resistance and fragrance genes and exhibits stable agronomic traits. SVT1 knockout (svt1-1 and svt1-2) and overexpression (SVT1-OE) transgenic lines in the ZH11 background were obtained using CRISPR/Cas9 technology and Agrobacterium-mediated genetic transformation methods89. All the plants were grown under natural conditions in the experimental field of SCAU (Guangzhou, Guangdong, China)90.

Transport of seeds by Chang’e-5

On November 24, 2020, the Chang’e-5 lunar probe carried 32.8 g of dry HJXSM rice seeds from a single plant. The seeds returned to Earth on December 17, 2020, after a total flight duration of approximately 23 d. During the flight, a thermoluminescent dosimeter (TLD) was used as the radiation detection material and placed in the same storage bag as the seeds to measure the radiation absorbed dose received by the rice samples in the experiment. The measured cumulative radiation dose to the seeds during the flight was 59.85 mGy. The average ambient temperature of the seeds during flight was 5-8 °C, and the relative humidity was less than 50%. The ground control seeds were stored at Dalian Maritime University in Dalian, Liaoning Province, China. The storage temperature and humidity for the ground control seeds were maintained consistent with those for the flight seeds.

Materials sampling

The seeds were sown at the teaching and research base of SCAU on February 26, 2021, with seeds from the same plant that had not been flown on Chang’e-5 serving as controls. On April 29, 2021, 30 plants (numbered TK1-TK30) grown from seeds exposed to deep-space were randomly selected (Supplementary Fig. 1), along with 10 plants (numbered CK1-CK10) from unflown seeds as controls. One tiller was taken from each sample, and the young stem was then removed from the tiller for subsequent analysis.

The seeds of the transgenic lines and WT plants were irradiated with 300 Gy γ-rays at a dose rate of 1 Gy/min for 5 h. Then, the seed coat was peeled off, and the plants were germinated in the dark at 28 °C. After 3 d, the seed embryo tissue was removed for subsequent experiments.

DNA and RNA extraction and quality inspection

For each sample (young stem and seed embryo), 500 mg of DNA was extracted using the cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method, and total RNA was extracted using an Omega Plant RNA Kit (Omega Bio-Tek, R6827). The quality of the DNA and RNA was evaluated using a Qubit instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and a NanoDrop instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and the integrity of the RNA was determined using an Agilent2100 instrument (Agilent Technologies, Germany). The samples that passed quality control were stored at −80 °C.

Reference genome assembly

For the qualified young-stem DNA, PacBio Sequel (Pacific Biosciences, USA) and Illumina (Illumina, USA) technologies were used, and Fastp (v0.20.0) was used to control the quality of the Illumina sequencing data. The PacBio reads were spliced and assembled using MECAT (v1.0), and the Illumina reads were aligned to the assembled genome using Pilon (v1.23). The Illumina reads were realigned to the completed and error-corrected reference genome, and metrics such as the mapping rate, genome coverage, and depth distribution were calculated to evaluate the completeness of the genome assembly. On the basis of the three-dimensional spatial structure characteristics of chromatin and the interactions between sequences, scaffolds were constructed. The scaffolds were anchored to chromosomes through clustering models to determine the correct order and direction for mounting scaffolds. Hi-C data assembly was performed using LACHSIS (v2014-09-12.12), ALLHIC (v0.9.8), and 3D-DNA (v180114).

Parametric full-length transcriptome analysis and reference genome annotation

PacBio data were aligned to the reference genome using SMRTLink (v8.0.0) to obtain known and novel transcripts, which were combined with Illumina data for gene functional annotation, gene structure analysis, and gene expression analysis. The coding genes were predicted using Augustus (v3.2.1), GeneMark (v4.72), and Maker (v2.29) to align known homologous coding protein sequences from other species with the new genome sequences. The results of sequence alignment were integrated using HISAT2 (v2.1.0), and sequence assembly was performed using StringTie (v1.3.1) to obtain a transcriptome-based gene set. Maker was used to integrate the data from the transcriptomic analysis, homology analysis, and de novo analysis in proportion to obtain the final gene set, followed by BUSCO (v4) evaluation of the predicted coding genes. The predicted gene protein sequences were aligned with the database for functional annotation, and a threshold of <1e-5 was used for filtering.

WGS and true mutation site screening

Young-stem and seed-embryo DNA samples that passed quality control were sequenced on an Illumina platform. The filtered reads were aligned to the reference genome using BWA (v0.7.15), and mutation detection was performed using GATK. Raw mutation data were obtained after functional annotation using ANNOVAR (v2). Mutation sites that were present in two or more samples were classified as either background mutations or false positives. Only the mutation sites existing in a single sample were retained as the true mutation sites.

RNA-seq

After high-quality total RNA was obtained from young stems and seed embryos, a library was constructed and sequenced. HISAT2 was used for reference genome-based alignment analysis, including type statistics, gene coverage, sequencing randomness, and sequencing saturation analysis. StringTie was used to reconstruct the transcripts, and RSEM (http://deweylab.Giyhub.Io/RESM/) was used to calculate the expression levels of all genes in each sample, displayed as fragments per kilobase of exon model per million mapped fragments (FPKM). Using FPKM as the screening criterion, genes with |log2FC | ≥1 and FDR < 0.05 were identified as DEGs. The target genes were mapped to terms in the GO database (http://www.geneontology.org/), and significantly enriched GO terms were identified. The target gene set was combined with the KEGG database (https://www.kegg.jp/) for enrichment analysis to screen for significantly enriched pathways.

WGBS

The DNA library of sodium bisulfite sequencing was prepared from young-stem DNA. Genomic DNA was fragmented into 100–300 bp fragments by sonication (Covaris, USA) and purified with a MiniElute PCR Purification Kit (QIAGEN, USA). The fragmented DNA was end repaired, and a single “A” nucleotide was added to the 3’ end of the blunt fragment. Afterwards, the genomic fragments were ligated to methylated sequencing adapters. Fragments with adapters were bisulfite converted using a Methylation-Gold kit (ZYMO, USA), and unmethylated cytosine was converted to uracil during sodium bisulfite treatment. Finally, the transformed DNA fragments were amplified by PCR and subjected to Illumina sequencing. The sequencing data were converted into raw sequence data through base calling. Filtered data were aligned to the genome using BSMAP (v2.90), generating genome-wide base alignment information and genome-wide C base methylation information. MethylKit (v1.4.1) was used to identify DMCs. The average DNA methylation rate within each 200 bp window of the genome was calculated, and the methylation levels of each window across samples were compared to identify DMRs.

MeRIP-seq

A library was constructed using young-stem RNA samples. A reagent kit was used to break the RNA into fragments approximately 100 nt in length. The RNA was divided into two parts, one of which was used as an input control, and a transcriptome sequencing library was directly constructed to eliminate the background during the process of capturing methylated fragments. After the m6A-modified RNA was captured, elution was performed using magnetic beads to reduce background noise from nonspecific binding. Strand-specific libraries for the two RNA samples were constructed separately. After the construction of the library was completed, quality testing was performed on the library. The quality of the libraries was subjected to Illumina sequencing. Using exomePeak2 (v1.1.0), genome-wide peak scanning was performed with a p value < 1e-5 as the threshold, and peaks from replicate samples were filtered and merged. The genomic locations and peak sequences were analysed to identify peak-associated genes. MEMESuite (http://meme-suite.org/) was used for TF motif analysis to identify significant motif sequences within the peaks. The relative methylation level of each peak was calculated, and exomePeak2 was subsequently used for differential methylation analysis of RNA across all the peaks in the comparison groups. Peaks with P < 0.05 and |log2FC|>1 were selected as differential peaks.

Preparation of protoplast suspensions

Young stems from the 10 CK and 30 TK samples were cut into thin slices with a blade, and the seed embryos of WT and svt1-1 were cut into thin slices and placed in 50 ml centrifuge tubes. The tissue slices were subsequently washed twice with 0.8 M mannitol, followed by enzymatic hydrolysis. Afterwards, 15 ml of enzymatic solution was added to each centrifuge tube, including cellulose RS (1.5%), pectinase Y23 (0.03%), mannitol (0.5 M), KCl (0.5 mM), MgCl2 (0.5 mM), MES (0.5 mM, pH 5.7), CaCl2 (10 mM), and BSA (0.1%). The centrifuge tube was subsequently placed in a shaker and incubated in the dark. The samples were shaken at 30 °C and 75 rpm for 5 h. During the incubation period, the protoplast status (including morphology, quantity, size, number of fragments, and cell viability) was observed under a microscope. After enzymatic hydrolysis, the centrifuge tube was briefly shaken to completely release the protoplasts. The enzymatic hydrolysate was filtered through a 40 μm cell sieve into a new 15 ml centrifuge tube, 2 ml of 0.8 M mannitol was added, the sample was shaken well, and the sample was centrifuged at 150×g for 5 min; this process was repeated 3 times, after which the sample was used. After the supernatant was discarded, 10 ml of 0.8 M mannitol was added, and the cells were resuspended. Five microlitres of protoplast suspension was mixed with 5 µl of 0.4% trypan blue staining solution, and the cell concentration and activity were detected using a cell counting plate. 0.8 M mannitol was used to uniformly adjust the concentration of the protoplast suspension for 40 samples to 1,000 cells/µl. A 1 ml suspension was taken from each of the 30 TK samples and mixed equally. This process was repeated three times, with the resulting mixtures being renumbered scTK1–scTK3. The CK samples were mixed in the same manner and renumbered scCK1–scCK3. Similarly, the WT samples were mixed and renumbered scWT1-scWT3, while the svt1-1 samples were mixed and renumbered scsvt1-1-scsvt1-3.

ScRNA-seq

Gel beads containing barcode information were combined with a mixture of cells and enzymes and then wrapped with oil surfactant droplets in a microfluidic “double-cross” system to form gel beads-in-emulsions (GEMs). GEMs flow into the reservoir and are collected. The gel beads dissolve and release the barcode sequences, reverse transcribe the cDNA fragments, and label the samples. The gel beads were broken, the oil drops were broken, and the cDNA was used as the template for PCR amplification. All GEM products were mixed, and a standard sequencing library was constructed. High-throughput sequencing of the constructed library was performed using the Illumina NovaSeq X Plus dual-end sequencing mode. The reads were aligned to the reference genome using CellRanger (v2.0.0) and annotated to specific genes. After UMIs were corrected and counted, an unfiltered feature-barcode matrix was obtained. The cells and noncells in the data were identified and distinguished. Gene quantification was performed using UMI counts to obtain cell-gene expression profiles. The expression matrix was imported into Seurat (v4.2.0), and multicell filtering was conducted using DoubletFinder (v3). Data integration and batch effect correction were performed using Harmony (https://github.com/immunogenomics/harmony). The cell subcluster classification results were subjected to uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) for nonlinear clustering to visualise the different single-cell subclusters.

The PAGA first inputs the expression matrix into Scanpy (v1.6.0) and then performs dimensionality reduction transformation and maps it to a low-dimensional scatter plot. The cells are then divided into several subgroups and represented in the form of nodes on the basis of topological structure, with the strength of connectivity represented by the thickness of the lines. For pseudotime analysis, the gene expression matrix was input into Monocle2 (v2.26.0), and cells were arranged into a differentiation trajectory containing branches and nodes. The cell cluster with the most primitive differentiation state was defined as the cell cluster with the lowest pseudotime value in the trajectory, and pseudotime values for all cells were calculated. Negative binomial generalised linear models were fitted for the two branches, and differential genes dependent on different branches were identified and tested by comparing the two models. The screening criterion was an FDR<1e-7. CytoTRACE2 (v1.1.0) was used to assess the differentiation potential of the cells. First, the number of genes whose expression was detectable per cell was quantified from the gene expression matrix. The genes whose expression patterns subsequently correlated with the number of genes whose expression was detected were identified as the gene count signature (GCS). Finally, the GCS was used as an outcome measure to predict the differentiation state of the cells.

Single-cell mutation site mapping

Vartrix (https://github.com/10×Genomics/vartrix) with default parameters was used with VCF mutation files, BAM files, mutation data, and single-cell matrix files as input and mutation site information, the row number of barcodes in barcodes.tsv, and the corresponding barcode (cell) mutation detection results as output. A UMAP plot was generated to visualise the aforementioned results.

EQTL and meQTL analyses

Genomic mutation sites were jointly analysed with transcriptome gene expression levels for eQTL analysis, and genomic mutation sites were jointly analysed with DMCs for meQTL analysis. The qqnorm() function in R software (https://www.R-project.org/) was used for normalisation, and the normalised values were used as the final values. The peer (https://github.com/PMBio/peer/wiki/Tutorial) tool was used to check for hidden effects and compute the matrix. The GLM from MatrixEQTL (v2.3) was utilised for the eQTL and meQTL analyses, with a significance threshold of 0.01; pairs with p values below this threshold were considered significant. In the genome-wide analysis of the annotated genes, the majority of the eQTL and meQTL distances were within 10,000 kb, and this distance was used to distinguish between cis- and trans-association signals.

Subcellular localisation

The CDS of SVT1 was cloned and inserted into pAN580 to generate the p35S:SVT1-GFP expression vector. Protoplasts were isolated from etiolated rice seedlings as described previously. After centrifugation, the supernatant was discarded, and the collected protoplasts were incubated at 28 °C in the dark for 24 h. Images were acquired by laser scanning confocal microscopy (Carl Zeiss, LSM800).

DAP-seq

After quality inspection, the young-stem DNA of 10 CK samples was mixed in equal quantities and divided into two replicates. Then, the samples were crushed into short fragments using ultrasonic technology. After end repair and 3’ end addition, the fragments were connected to Illumina sequencing adapters, and 100–300 bp DNA fragments were selected for PCR amplification. Finally, a qualified library for sequencing was obtained. The CDS of SVT1 was subsequently cloned and inserted into a plasmid containing the Halo tag, followed by in vitro expression. The purified SVT1 protein was incubated with the HJXSM DNA library, after which the DNA fragments bound to SVT1 were extracted. Sequencing was performed using the Illumina platform. Reads were statistically analysed using deepTools (v3.2.0), with a window size of 50 bp, and the average read depth within each window was calculated. MACS2 (v2.1.2) was used for peak calling across the genome with a q value threshold <0.05, and the genomic positions and sequences of the peaks were analysed to identify peak-associated genes. The peaks between two replicates were merged on the basis of the criterion of overlap>50%. All the peaks within the group were then merged, and unified peak data were output. The ChIPseeker (v1.16.1) R package was used to annotate the peak-associated genes.

ChIP-seq

Young stems (10 g) of HJXSM were fixed by formaldehyde cross-linking, and three replicates were used for the experiments. The cross-linked samples were subjected to cell lysis, followed by ultrasonication to shear the genomic DNA into fragments of 100–500 bp. A 20 μl aliquot was taken as the input sample. Specific antibodies (A rabbit polyclonal antibody was used at a dilution of 1:200) were prepared in advance, and the beads were added to the cell lysate and incubated. After the samples were washed, the cross-links were reversed. The precipitated products were recovered and used to construct a library with the NEBNext® UltraTM DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, E7645L), followed by sequencing on the Illumina platform. The raw data were quality controlled using Fastp to obtain clean data. The clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie2 (v2.4.2). Peak calling was performed with MACS2, and the peaks between three replicates were merged on the basis of the criterion of overlap>50%. The resulting peaks were annotated using ChIPseeker. Motif analysis of the peaks was then conducted with HOMER (v4.11.1).

Dual-LUC reporter assay and transcription activity assay

The ORF sequence of SVT1 was cloned and inserted into the pGreenII 62-SK vector. Each promoter of SK1G00063486 was individually cloned and inserted into the pGreenII 0800-LUC vector. The constructs were transformed into rice protoplasts91, and the LUC and REN activities were subsequently assessed using a Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay Kit (Yeasen, China). The relative activity was calculated as the LUC:REN ratio.

The recombinant plasmids were subsequently introduced into Agrobacterium tumefaciens strain GV3101. After culture, the bacterial suspensions were infiltrated into tobacco leaves. LUC live imaging was conducted to capture luminescence signals92.

EMSA

SVT1 was cloned and inserted into the pET-28a-SUMO prokaryotic expression vector. Positive plasmids were transformed into E. coli DE3 competent cells. Positive strains were identified by PCR. The bacterial lysates were sonicated, and the supernatant was collected as the protein sample. After SDS‒PAGE, the protein was purified using magnetic beads to obtain the purified SVT1 protein. Three DNA binding probes targeting the promoter regions of the target genes were synthesised (Supplementary Data 13), along with mutant probes. After biotin labelling and annealing to form double-stranded DNA, unlabelled binding probes were used as competitive probes. The SUMO protein was incubated with binding probes as a negative control. The SVT1 protein was separately incubated with biotin-labelled binding probes and mutant probes, and differences in migration rates were evaluated for the competition experiments.

Statistical analysis

In the statistical analysis, the data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation with error bars attached. We used a two-tailed t test to determine significant differences between two groups. To analyse datasets containing three or more experimental groups, we performed one-way ANOVA using IBM SPSS software (v21.0) and combined it with Duncan’s multiple range test. A p value less than 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).
