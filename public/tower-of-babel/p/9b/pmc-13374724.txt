Impact of a COPD Bundled Payments for Care Innovation Program on Readmissions and Inpatient Care Quality
Authors: Nathan C Nowalk, Juan C Rojas, William F Parker, Ashley Smith-Nunez, Sarah E Gray, Ria Sood, Devon O Lewis, Stephanie Chia, Rajlakshmi Krishnamurthy, Valerie G Press
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOne-fifth of US patients hospitalized for acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPDs) are readmitted within 30 days, contributing to high morbidity and costs. Medicare included COPD in their Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP) in 2014 and offered voluntary value-based COPD care programs (Bundled Payments for Care Innovation [BPCI]).Research QuestionWas our COPD HRRP effective in preventing hospital readmissions and improving inpatient care quality at our medical center?Study Design and MethodsWe performed a single-center, retrospective cohort study using Medicare data of BPCI-eligible hospitalizations for AECOPDs occurring pre-BPCI (October 2013-September 2014) and during BPCI (October 2015-September 2018) implementation. Our interprofessional COPD HRRP includes inpatient consultation and postdischarge interventions. We compared outcomes between BPCI program recipients and nonrecipients including inpatient COPD care quality metrics derived from international COPD guidelines and program-specific interventions using multivariable regressions. We evaluated 30- and 90-day all-cause readmission rates with multivariable logistic regression and 2-stage least squares instrumental variable analysis.ResultsOf 287 AECOPD hospitalizations (pre-BPCI: n = 57; BPCI: n = 230), 132 received the COPD HRRP. Patients were more likely to receive inhaler education (pre-BPCI: 12.0% vs BPCI: 71.8%; P < .01), tobacco cessation therapy (pre-BPCI: 24.3% vs BPCI: 71.8%; P < .01), and predischarge oxygen walk test (pre-BPCI: 49% vs BPCI: 81.0%; P < .01). In instrumental variable analysis, controlling for unobserved confounders correlated with receiving the COPD HRRP, our intervention was associated with a significantly lower adjusted 30-day all-cause readmission rate (pre-BPCI: 28.6% vs BPCI: 6.6%; P < .05; absolute decrease, −22.0%; 95% CI, −43.6 to −0.5) but not in 90-day readmissions.InterpretationThe implementation of an evidence-based BPCI COPD program was associated with significant decrease in 30-day all-cause readmissions. Future studies are needed to assess individual interventions that may impact readmission reduction.Key Words: bundled payments, COPD, quality of health care, readmissions, value-based care

Take-Home Points

Study Question: Was our COPD Hospital Readmission Reduction Program effective in preventing hospital readmissions and improving inpatient care quality at our medical center?

Results: The COPD Hospital Readmission Reduction Program was associated with improved inpatient care processes and a significant reduction in 30-day all-cause readmissions, but not 90-day readmissions.

Interpretation: A bundled, evidence-based COPD intervention can reduce short-term readmissions, warranting further study of which components drive this effect.

COPD carries significant public health burden. Acute exacerbations of COPD (AECOPDs) account for nearly 750,000 hospitalizations annually.1 One-fifth of AECOPD hospitalizations are readmitted within 30 days, making COPD the third leading cause of readmissions.2 The transitional period posthospital discharge for AECOPD carries substantial risk with an estimated 8% 30-day and 20% 1-year mortality among Medicare beneficiaries.3,4

To decrease COPD preventable readmissions, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) designated COPD as a condition under the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP) in October 2014.5,6 CMS also initiated voluntary value-based care programs, the Bundled Payments for Care Innovation (BPCI), including COPD (2014-2020).7 Studies examining AECOPD readmission reduction have shown inconsistent results.8, 9, 10, 11, 12 A prior COPD BPCI evaluation did not find reductions in readmissions in their program’s first year.12 Trials motivated by CMS’s HRRP seeking to identify specific interventions to reduce readmissions show similar inconclusive findings.13, 14, 15, 16 Regarding COPD care quality, we previously demonstrated a nationwide slowdown in improvements for inpatient AECOPD-specific treatment post-HRRP implementation.17

Less known is whether care transition interventions, beyond inpatient AECOPD-specific treatment, improved post-HRRP and/or BPCI implementation. At our academic hospital, we implemented our COPD HRRP using evidence-based care transition interventions to decrease 30-day readmission rates and improve COPD care quality in October 2014. We then adapted our HRRP program to 90-day episodes for a COPD BPCI program (October 2015-December 2020).18 We found a nonsignificant cost-saving effect after 3-year implementation of our program.18 In this present study, we evaluated whether our COPD HRRP was associated with changes in hospital readmissions and inpatient care quality.

Study Design and Methods

Data Source

We conducted a single-site retrospective cohort study of AECOPD hospitalizations among CMS beneficiaries ≥ 65 years of age. As a participant of model 2 of BPCI, our institution was provided CMS administrative data for BPCI-eligible hospitalizations (October 2013-September 2018) by COPD diagnosis-related group (DRG) (Fig 1). All other patient-level data were extracted from the electronic medical record by physician auditors (N. C. N., J. C. R., and A. S.-N.). Work was deemed quality improvement and exempt from Institutional Review Board.

Study Cohort

There is no validated approach for using administrative data to identify AECOPD hospitalizations,6,19, 20, 21 and evaluation solely by DRG may lead to misdiagnosis and inflation of 30-day readmission rates.21,22 To address this, hospitalizations identified by DRGs were screened by physician auditors (N. C. N., J. C. R., and A. S.-N.) for diagnostic accuracy of hospitalization for AECOPD (Fig 1). AECOPD determination was based on interpretation of clinical documentation (admission history, and physical and discharge summary). Hospitalizations meeting inclusion criteria were grouped by admission date to preimplementation period (October 2013-September 2014) or intervention period (October 2015-September 2018) (Fig 1); October 2014 to September 2015 was excluded due to program implementation.

Outcomes and Covariate Definitions

Primary outcomes were postdischarge 30- and 90-day all-cause readmission, derived from CMS administrative data. Secondary outcomes included 30- and 90-day COPD-related readmission, care transition interventions, and inpatient care quality (recommend care, nonrecommended care, and ideal care) based on prior similar evaluations17,23 (e-Appendix 1).

The treatment variable was defined as the inpatient consult with the advanced practice nurse (APN), reflecting the first engagement between patients and our COPD HRRP. The program was designed to address all hospitalizations for AECOPDs, which included BPCI-eligible patients. For patient identification, see e-Appendix 2. During the hospitalization, the clinician recommends guideline-directed medical management of AECOPD, tobacco cessation therapy, predischarge oxygen walk test, and our pharmacy-led medication reconciliation and inhaler education. After hospitalization, the patient receives a nurse-initiated 48-hour phone call; 1-to-2-week appointment with COPD APN and pharmacy team; and referrals to pulmonology, palliative care, and pulmonary rehabilitation as appropriate (for full description of our program, see e-Appendix 3).

We collected patient-level data on demographics, preadmission diagnosis of COPD and previously recognized COPD-relevant comorbidities,24,25 absolute and predicted FEV1 from preadmission spirometry, cigarette use (former, active, or never), preadmission therapeutics (domiciliary oxygen use or long-acting inhaler use), inpatient need for noninvasive ventilation, and admission on weekend.

Instrumental Variable Analysis

The utilization of our program was limited by screening sensitivity, staffing, and the discretionary preferences of consulting clinicians (e-Appendix 4). To address our noncomplete program reach, we performed an instrumental variable analysis (IVA) for the primary outcomes. This analysis addresses unmeasured confounders from our study’s nonrandom selection (e-Fig 1). Ideally, the instrumental variable should estimate random assignment similar to a randomized clinical trial.26,27 Time period, whether preimplementation or intervention, was the selected instrument.

Statistical Analysis

Student t test for continuous variables and χ2 tests were used to compare baseline characteristics. Variables of interest were then grouped into adjusted logistic regression models to assess the primary outcomes of 30- and 90-day all-cause readmission rates and the secondary outcomes of 30- and 90-day COPD-related readmission, inpatient care quality, and program interventions. Covariates were selected for clinical relevance and significant imbalance between groups. Our final analyses adjusted for age, gender, history of asthma, and cigarette use (former, active, or never). The IVA was completed by 2-stage least squares regression with adjustment of the same covariates for all hospitalizations. The adjusted result reflects the estimated difference in 30- and 90-day readmission risk when hospitalization receives an inpatient consult.

Statistical significance was defined as P < .05, and all analyses were performed using Stata 16.0 (StataCorp).

Instrumental Variable Assumptions

To perform an IVA, a set of specific conditions must be met (e-Appendix 5). To fulfill the first-stage assumption, the instrumental variable (time period) must be associated with the treatment variable (inpatient consult). By the exclusion restriction, the instrumental variable (time period) must not be associated with the outcome (30- and 90-day all-cause readmissions) except through the treatment variable (inpatient consult) denoted as X in e-Figure 1. Finally, the independence condition requires no mutual confounders between the instrumental variable (time period) and the outcome (30- or 90-day all-cause readmissions). Although this condition cannot be definitively proven, it can be assessed by stratifying observed confounders on the instrumental variable (time period) (e-Table 1).28,29

Alternative Comparisons

To assess the strength and consistency of our findings, we performed a series of sensitivity analyses using unadjusted and adjusted logistic regression models. We completed an intention-to-treat analysis by comparing the 30- and 90-day all-cause readmission rates of hospitalizations in the preimplementation period against all hospitalizations in the intervention period (Fig 1). Likewise, as a per protocol analysis, these same outcomes were compared between hospitalizations of the preimplementation period and hospitalizations of BPCI program recipients during the intervention period. Both analyses are at risk for unmeasured confounders associated with time periods. For this reason, we performed a contemporaneous comparison within the intervention period for 30- and 90-day all-cause readmission rates between BPCI program recipients and nonrecipients (Fig 1). Although valuable as a robustness check, the contemporaneous comparison carries potential selection bias (e-Appendix 4). A summary of all comparisons is provided in e-Table 2.

Results

There were 452 hospitalizations among eligible patients during the preimplementation and intervention periods (Fig 1). After physician review, 165 hospitalizations (36.5% of total; preimplementation: 37 of 94 [39.3%]; intervention: 128 of 358 [35.8%]) were excluded for absence of AECOPD. The final cohort consisted of 287 hospitalizations for AECOPD (preimplementation: n = 57; intervention: n = 230). Inpatient consult (treatment variable) was completed in zero hospitalizations in the preintervention period and 132 hospitalizations in the intervention period (57.4%). For all admissions, the mean age ± SD was 70.7 ± 10.0 years, and most self-identified as Black (n = 259; 90.2%) and as a woman (n = 184; 64.1%). Women were significantly less likely to receive consult (69.7% no consult vs 57.6% consult; P < .05). Most hospitalizations included unique patients (n = 229; 79.7%) with no significant difference between comparison groups. Remaining demographics, cigarette use, comorbidities, preadmission therapeutics, and markers of COPD severity were similar between hospitalizations that did and did not receive consult (Table 1).

No significant differences for individual treatments were observed, nor when bundled into care quality indices (recommended, nonrecommended, or ideal care) (Table 2). Transition of Care Interventions

Inpatient Recommendations

Patients actively using cigarettes were more likely to receive tobacco cessation therapies if seen by the program (P < .05) (Table 2). Tobacco cessation prescription was associated with 90% lower odds of 30-day all-cause readmission on adjusted analysis (OR, 0.1; 95% CI, 0.02-0.7; P = .02) (Table 3). Inpatient inhaler teaching occurred more frequently for patients receiving inpatient consult (P < .05) (Table 2). Receiving this education was associated with 60% lower odds of 30-day all-cause readmission on adjusted analysis (OR, 0.4; 95% CI, 0.2-0.9; P = .03) (Table 3). Predischarge oxygen assessment was more common among patients receiving inpatient consult (P < .05) (Table 2) but did not show a significant association with all-cause readmissions (Table 3).

Postdischarge Follow-Up

After discharge, 31.8% of patients (n = 42) receiving inpatient consult also received a phone call within 48 hours from our COPD nurse (Table 2). These telephone calls were not significantly associated with all-cause readmissions (Table 3). Two-week outpatient follow-up appointments with a COPD APN were scheduled for 56.8% (n = 75) of the inpatient consult group with an attendance rate of 71% (n = 53) (Table 2). On adjusted analysis, scheduling an appointment with a COPD APN was associated with 70% lower odds of all-cause readmissions at 30 days (OR, 0.3; 95% CI, 0.1-0.7; P = .01) and 60% lower odds at 90 days (OR, 0.4; 95% CI, 0.2-0.8; P = .01). Attendance of this appointment was associated with 60% lower odds of 90-day all-cause readmissions (OR, 0.4; 95% CI, 0.2-0.8; P = .01), but showed no association with 30-day all-cause readmissions (Table 3). Patients receiving inpatient consult were more likely to receive a scheduled pulmonary clinic appointment within 4 weeks of discharge (P < .05) (Table 2), but this intervention did not show a significant association with all-cause readmissions (Table 3).

Hospital Readmissions

BPCI program recipients had significant reductions in the primary outcomes of 30-day all-cause readmissions rates (25.2% no consult vs 10.6% consult; absolute difference, −15.1%; 95% CI, −24.5% to −5.7%) and 90-day all-cause readmission rates (46.5% no consult vs 31.8% consult; absolute difference, −14.5%; 95% CI, −25.3% to −3.7%) (Table 4). Reductions in 30- and 90-day all-cause readmission rates remained significant in adjusted analyses (Table 4).

The IVA demonstrated BPCI program recipients had a significant reduction in 30-day all-cause readmission rates compared with hospitalizations not receiving consult (28.6% no consult vs 6.6% consult; absolute difference, −22.0%; 95% CI, −43.6% to −0.5%). However, no significant difference in 90-day all-cause readmission rate was demonstrated (Table 4).

Regarding COPD-related readmissions, there was no significant difference in the reduction of 30- or 90-day COPD-related readmissions between BPCI program recipients and nonrecipients, across all analyses—unadjusted, adjusted, and IVA (Table 4).

Instrumental Variable Assumptions

The inpatient consult was not available during the preimplementation period; thus, the first-stage assumption was upheld through perfect agreement between the instrumental variable (time period) and treatment variable (inpatient consult). In adjusted regression, the instrumental variable (time period) was associated with 30-day all-cause readmission rate (P = .03), but not 90-day all-cause readmission rate (e-Tables 3, 4). To evaluate the instrument’s independence, observed confounders were stratified by the instrumental variable (time period) and found to be similar between groups (e-Table 1), suggesting that unobserved confounders were comparable as well.27, 28, 29

Alternative Comparisons

Statistically significant reductions in 30-day all-cause readmission rates were also recognized in unadjusted and adjusted comparisons of time periods. These findings were present whether comparing the preimplementation period with all hospitalizations in the intervention period (intention-to-treat analysis) (e-Table 5) or the subset of BPCI program recipients within the intervention period (per protocol analysis) (e-Table 6). For the contemporaneous comparison limited to the intervention period, BPCI program recipients showed significant reductions in 30- and 90-day all-cause readmissions (e-Table 7).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to report on the inpatient care quality and services for a full 3-year period of a CMS BPCI program. Our COPD HRRP implementation led to a significant reduction in 30-day all-cause readmissions, likely driven by improvement in care transition interventions, including inhaler education, tobacco cessation therapy, and 2-week follow-up appointment scheduled with a COPD APN. We found no improvement in inpatient care quality. In multivariable logistic regression, the program significantly reduced 90-day all-cause readmission rates, although not with IVA. Finally, we found no significant difference between program recipients and nonrecipients for 30- or 90-day COPD-related readmissions.

Our finding of reductions in 30-day all-cause readmissions was robust across multiple analyses, including unadjusted, adjusted, and IVA. This reduction in 30-day readmissions is noteworthy because the 22% estimated absolute reduction in 30-day readmissions is markedly greater than the approximately 2% post-HRRP national declines previously reported.9,17,30 It is unlikely that a secular trend beyond our program explains this significant reduction, which effectively upholds the exclusion restriction, a necessary condition for IVA. Prior analyses of BPCI programs have not demonstrated such reductions.12,18 Differences in 30-day all-cause readmission rates compared with the study by Bhatt et al12 may partly reflect our longer reporting time (3 vs 1 year) and larger potential effect size (non-BPCI = 25.2% vs BCPI = 17.4%). COPD-related readmissions accounted for a small portion of 30- and 90-day all-cause readmissions, consistent with prior findings from a large Medicare claims analysis.24 Given the low frequency of COPD-related readmissions, larger sample sizes are likely necessary to detect meaningful differences, highlighting the need for larger-scale studies.

Inpatient care quality, assessed against guideline-recommended care, did not change with our program and was not associated with 30- and 90-day all-cause readmission rates. In our present analysis, we found high quality of care even among patients not receiving our program. Further improvement in inpatient care quality may be difficult with our program due to this floor effect; it is likely our study was underpowered to detect a difference in such conditions. Using the large Premier Healthcare Database, we previously reported the quality of inpatient care for AECOPDs showed no improvement after this condition was added to the HRRP.17 However, due to the limitations of our data source (administrative claims), we were unable to examine care coordination and other established strategies (eg, smoking cessation, inhaler education) in that study.

Therefore, in this study, we evaluated COPD care quality by assessing our individual program interventions using patient-level data unavailable in prior HRRP analyses.9,11,13 Using the teach-to-goal method, inhaler education was featured prominently in our program and has shown reduction in 30-day acute care visits in isolation and when bundled.16,31 Patients seen by our inpatient program were more likely to receive inpatient inhaler education with a reduction in odds of 30-day all-cause readmissions but not at 90 days postdischarge, which may reflect the waning nature of educational interventions without repeated dosing.16,32 Patients actively using cigarettes were significantly more likely to receive tobacco cessation therapy if seen by our inpatient program with reduced odds of 30-day all-cause readmissions when tobacco cessation therapy was prescribed. Prior tobacco cessation interventions aimed at hospitalized patients have also shown significant reductions in readmissions.33,34 Although predischarge ambulatory oxygen assessment was significantly different between BPCI recipients, this intervention was not associated with readmission reduction. This finding is consistent with the mixed literature where prior works have shown significant improvement in inpatient interventions that do not translate to readmission reduction.35

Regarding postdischarge care transition interventions, our program included a 48-hour telephone call with our team’s nurse and a 2-week appointment with our COPD APNs. Notably, less than one-third of BPCI patients received postdischarge telephone calls. Telephone calls showed no association with readmissions, consistent with prior programs that attempted this resource-intensive intervention.12,36 Scheduling a short-term follow-up appointment with our COPD APN significantly reduced the odds of 30- and 90-day all-cause readmissions. Attendance of this 2-week follow-up appointment was associated with a significant reduction in 90-day readmissions, but not 30-day readmissions. Our findings are significant and contrast with prior work showing limited benefit of early follow-up.36 No association was found between readmissions and postdischarge follow-up appointments with primary care or a pulmonary specialist. Referrals for palliative care were suboptimal, even though the mean FEV1 % predicted was in the severe range and greater than one-third of the cohort required domiciliary oxygen. Similarly, pulmonary rehabilitation was underused but well above a recent analysis showing single-digit rates of pulmonary rehabilitation initiation after hospitalization for AECOPD.37

Despite reporting a single-center experience, the cohort has several strengths. A significant portion of unique patients (more than three-quarters) were included in the sample. By linking our hospitalizations to CMS administrative data, our analysis accurately counts 30- and 90-day readmissions. Additionally, the sample of hospitalizations for AECOPD was enriched with patients self-identifying as Black. Because clinical trials in COPD are often underrepresented across diverse patient populations, these data help fill an important evidence gap.38 Higher readmission rates after an AECOPD have been reported for patients identifying as Black, which initially raised concern the HRRP could widen health disparities.6,38, 39, 40 After the HRRP, there is evidence that racial disparities persist,41,42 but narrowed for some targeted conditions42,43 and widened for nontargeted conditions.44 The impact of the HRRP on health disparities in COPD is currently unknown.

Several important limitations require noting. As a BPCI program under Medicare, our analysis was limited to CMS beneficiaries,45 and readmissions were recorded by HRRP metrics that excluded observation stays.46 The sample size was modest and likely explained by 2 factors: our exclusion criteria and incomplete penetration (see e-Appendix 4 for more information). After reviewing clinical documentation, physician auditors (N. C. N., J. C. R., and A. S.-N.) excluded hospitalizations failing to meet our defined definition of AECOPD. For this reason, unless COPD hospitalizations are similarly confirmed for diagnostic accuracy, our impact on COPD readmissions may not translate externally. Comparisons of our results with other studies relying solely on indiscriminate DRGs should also note this exclusion criteria. Importantly, exclusion by physician auditors (N. C. N., J. C. R., and A. S.-N.) impacted preimplementation and intervention periods in similar fashion (both with over one-third of hospitalizations excluded). Our data set lacks information on nonhospitalized AECOPD events; therefore, we cannot claim a reduction in overall AECOPD incidence—only in hospital readmissions. Additionally, data regarding interactions with pulmonologists before index hospitalization for AECOPD were unavailable but have previously shown association with readmission reduction.47 As a single-center study, our findings are not necessarily generalizable. For individual intervention analysis, associations were derived from adjusted multivariable logistic regression and may be biased by unmeasured confounders addressed by IVA. Finally, although our IVA was designed to address unmeasured confounding (eg, telephone access, variation in patient consent, cognition), this quasi-experimental method does not replace randomization. However, because we aimed to reduce readmissions for all patients hospitalized for AECOPDs, randomization was impractical and unethical. Furthermore, our sensitivity analyses comparing hospitalizations between time periods (intention-to-treat and per protocol analyses) and within the intervention period (consult vs no consult) demonstrated the robustness of our findings because no change was found in the significance or direction of coefficients for 30-day all-cause readmissions.

Interpretation

This study demonstrates a significant reduction in 30-day readmissions and increased utilization of care transition interventions after implementation of our readmissions reduction program for AECOPDs. The findings of our work support continued investigation to identify which interventions are most likely to improve care quality and reduce readmissions for patients hospitalized with AECOPD.
