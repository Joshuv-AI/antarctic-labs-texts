Preparation of cold‐stored platelets on‐demand: A novel approach to inventory management
Authors: Lacey Johnson, Lauren Waters, Christopher Roan, Pearl Lei, Janhavi Mahajan, Mailys Portier, Ben Winskel‐Wood, Denese C. Marks
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCold‐stored platelets (CSP) are now being used to treat acute bleeding. However, as CSP are less suitable for prophylaxis, both room temperature (RT) platelets and CSP will be required, which complicates inventory management. The production of CSP “on‐demand” from RT platelets may be a desirable option. This study investigated whether CSP could be prepared rapidly by cooling RT platelets in a fridge or freezer.Study design and methodsDouble apheresis platelet components (40% plasma/60% PAS‐E) were sampled on Day 1 (RT baseline), then split into matched pairs (n = 10). One component was allocated to cold (2–6°C) storage and sampled at 2 h and 14 days. The other component was placed in a freezer (−30°C) and sampled every 5 min for 20 min (on‐demand), then transferred to the fridge and sampled at 14 days.ResultsStorage in the fridge for 2 h reduced the temperature to 4.1 ± 0.7°C and induced loss of swirl, shape change, enhanced aggregation, and reduced clotting time. Changes to surface glycoproteins (CD61, CD42b, GPVI) and activation markers (phosphatidylserine, P‐selectin, release of alpha‐granule proteins, and extracellular vesicles) were not observed after 2 h but became evident after 14 days. Storage in the freezer for at least 15 min induced the expected “cold storage lesion” changes.DiscussionThe morphological and functional characteristics of CSP can be induced within 15 min by exposure to a standard blood‐bank freezer, or 2 h in the fridge. This approach to prepare CSP “on‐demand” may represent a viable option for balancing patient‐tailored transfusion and inventory management.

INTRODUCTION

While platelet components have been almost exclusively stored at room temperature (RT; 20–24°C) with agitation for the last 50 years, there has been a recent resurgence in the use of refrigerated storage. Refrigeration, or cold storage (2–6°C), is attractive as it minimizes bacterial growth and preserves the metabolic and hemostatic capability of platelets. 1 , 2 These advantages support an extension of the shelf life beyond the RT standard of 7 days, which may facilitate improved platelet availability in settings with limited options for treating patients with critical bleeding, such as rural or military environments. 3 , 4 However, the circulation time of cold‐stored platelets (CSP) is significantly reduced compared to platelets stored at RT. 5 Therefore, CSP are not the preferred option to support patients requiring prophylactic transfusions. As such, the implementation of CSP will likely require the management of both RT and cold inventories. 6 This approach may pose some logistic challenges, including unavailability and wastage. 6 , 7 In instances where CSP are unavailable, it may be desirable to manufacture them “on‐demand,” from RT platelets, to support critically bleeding patients.

Traditional in vitro indicators demonstrate that the morphology and metabolic profile of CSP are altered. Specifically, CSP express higher levels of activation markers, retain their intracellular contents, but release more extracellular vesicles (EVs). 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 These cold‐induced changes translate to improved aggregation responses, faster thrombin generation, and maintenance of viscoelastic properties during storage for up to 21 days. 9 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 Many of these changes occur progressively over storage; however, changes in signal transduction and certain downstream effects occur within 1–2 h of refrigeration. 18 , 19 , 20

In this study, we investigated the phenotypic and functional changes platelets undergo as they reach the cold transition point (2–6°C temperature range). In parallel, we aimed to determine whether platelets acquire the same features if they are rapidly transitioned to cold temperatures by placement in a standard blood bank freezer (−30°C).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Collection of platelet concentrates

Ethics approval was obtained for this study from the Australian Red Cross Lifeblood Human Research Ethics Committee. All donations were from eligible, voluntary donors. Double‐dose apheresis platelets (n = 10) were collected using a Trima Accel apheresis system (TerumoBCT, Lakewood, CO) in 60% platelet additive solution (PAS)‐E/40% plasma. Leukoreduction was performed within the leukocyte reduction chamber of the system.

The components were stored at RT with agitation overnight. Both bags from each collection were pooled, sampled on Day 1 (RT baseline), and then split into matched units. A temperature probe (Temprecord G4‐LCD Scientific Probe Logger, Temprecord International Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand) was inserted into the port of each unit and the temperature was recorded every minute for 4 h. One component was randomly allocated to refrigerated storage (2–6°C; cold) without agitation, on flat plastic trays. This component was sampled after 2 h, as by this time the temperature was within the range of 2–6°C and then returned to the fridge until Day 14. The other component was placed in a flat plastic tray in a freezer set to −30°C and sampled at 5‐min intervals for 20 min (on‐demand). The on‐demand component was then transferred to the fridge for 14 days. A schematic of the study design is depicted in Figure S1, Supporting Information. At each time point, sampling was performed by pouring 10–15 mL of platelets from the port opened for the temperature probe within a biological safety cabinet.

In vitro quality analysis

Platelet samples were assessed for platelet swirl and visible aggregates (macroaggregates) by holding the platelets up to a light source. The platelet count (PLT‐I), mean platelet volume (MPV), and platelet distribution width (PDW) were measured using a hematology analyzer (XN‐1000; Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan). The pH was measured using a pH meter at 22°C (Mettler‐Toledo GmbH, Greifensee, Switzerland). Supernatants were prepared by centrifugation at 1600g for 20 min followed by 12,000g for 5 min at RT and stored at −80°C until analysis. Soluble P‐selectin (sCD62P) was measured from the supernatant using a commercially available enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay kit, according to the manufacturer's instructions (R&D Systems Inc., Minneapolis, MN). The time to clot formation was measured using the Procoag‐PPL kit (Diagnostica Stago Ltd., Asnières, France), as described previously. 21

Platelet glycoproteins and activation markers were measured by flow cytometry (FACSCanto II, Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ), with a total of 10,000 events collected. The platelet population was established based on forward scatter (FSC) and side scatter (SSC) properties. Platelets (3 × 106 in Tyrode's buffer) were labeled for 20 min in the dark with the following antibodies: CD61‐FITC (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark), CD42b‐PE, CD62P‐PE, PAC‐1‐FITC (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA), GPVI‐efluor660 (eBioscience Inc., San Diego, CA), fibrinogen‐FITC (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Phosphatidylserine externalization was measured by labeling 1 × 106 platelets with annexin V‐fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) in calcium‐containing annexin V‐binding buffer (BioLegend, San Diego, CA) in the dark for 15 min. The percentage of positive events or median fluorescence intensity (MFI) was reported, as indicated. The absolute number of platelet EVs was enumerated from the platelet concentrate, without centrifugation, by flow cytometry. The platelet sample (5 μL) was stained with CD61‐APC (Dako) and annexin‐V‐FITC in TruCount tubes (BD Biosciences), which contain a known number of fluorescent beads. Events were collected to achieve a total of 7500 beads, and EVs were defined as CD61+/annexin‐V+ events, which were ≤1.0 μm, based on FSC and SSC set using submicron particle microspheres (Life Technologies Corp. Eugene, OR). Mitochondrial membrane potential was assessed using tetramethylrhodamine ethyl ester (TMRE; Abcam) staining by flow cytometry, as previously described. 22 To measure basal intracellular calcium, platelets were diluted to 10 × 109 cells/L in Tyrode's buffer and loaded with 5 μM Fluo‐3 AM (Biotium Inc., Fremont, CA) in the dark at 37°C for 15 min. Fluorescence was recorded for 30 s by flow cytometry, and the MFI was reported. Platelet morphology was assessed using imaging flow cytometry (Imagestreamx Mk II; Cytek, Fremont, CA) as previously described, 8 with the following modifications. Briefly, platelets were stained with CD61‐APC and diluted in Tyrode's buffer. Platelets were identified based on brightfield (area, aspect ratio), darkfield (scatter), and positive staining for CD61, with 7500 events collected at 60× magnification using a low flow rate. The mask function was used to refine brightfield (Object, Ch01, Tight) and darkfield (Intensity, Ch06, 60‐4095) images prior to analysis. Circular platelets were identified based on CD61 intensity and brightfield aspect ratio intensity, using a similar approach to Özpolat et al. 23 The percentage of circular events was used for statistical analysis. The gating strategy is shown in Figure S2.

Platelet aggregation was assessed using light transmission aggregometry (Helena Laboratories, Beaumont, TX) in response to 20 μM adenosine diphosphate (ADP; Sigma, MO) or 10 μg/mL collagen (Helena Laboratories) for 5 min, as previously described. 24 Clot formation was measured using a viscoelastic testing platform (ClotPro, enicor GmbH, Munich, Germany), through the intrinsic coagulation pathway (IN‐test). Platelets were diluted in freshly thawed fresh frozen plasma (FFP) to 200 × 109/L, and 340 μL was pipetted through the dried reagents contained within the IN‐test pipette tip and loaded into standard ClotPro cups (enicor GmbH). Clot formation proceeded at 37°C for 60 min. The following ClotPro variables were recorded: clotting time (CT; seconds), defined as the time taken to initiate clot formation, with a minimum clot firmness of 2 mm; maximum clot firmness (MCF; mm), defined as the maximal amplitude of the clot, indicating strength and stability.

The platelet dilutions for functional assays were prepared in allogeneic FFP. Multiple bags of ABO‐matched FFP were thawed, pooled, aliquoted, and refrozen at −40°C. At each day of testing, an aliquot of FFP was thawed, spun at 3000g for 15 min, and used for platelet dilutions. The same batch of FFP was used for testing all replicates at all time points.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.4.1 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA) and results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and post hoc Dunnett's multiple comparisons test was used to determine differences between baseline, cold, and on‐demand platelets during the initial exposure to fridge and freezer temperatures, respectively. Similarly, one‐way ANOVA and post hoc Dunnett's multiple comparisons test were used to determine differences between RT, 2 h CSP, and CSPs at Day 14. A p‐value of less than .05 was considered to be significant.

RESULTS

The starting volume was equivalent in the cold (238 ± 7 mL) and on‐demand (239 ± 3 mL, p = .909) platelet components. The starting platelet concentration was 1184 ± 145 × 109/L. The temperature was monitored in real‐time (Figure 1A) demonstrating that 2 h in the refrigerator was sufficient to reduce the temperature to within the 2–6°C range required for CSPs, with a mean temperature of 4.1 ± 0.7°C. Storage in the freezer rapidly reduced the temperature of the platelets, where 10 min was sufficient for the components to reach the 2–6°C range. After 15 min, the platelet temperature was between 0 and 2°C, and by 20 min, the temperature of all platelet components was below zero degrees. Of note, although the platelet components stored for 20 min contained visible ice crystals, the component remained liquid.

Complete loss of swirl occurred within 2 h of refrigeration (Figure 1B). Exposure to refrigerated temperature for 2 h also led to a significant increase in the proportion of circular platelets, as well as a higher MPV and PDW (Table 1). The pH, intracellular calcium levels (Fluo‐3AM), platelet phenotype and activation markers GPIIIa (CD61), activated GPIIb/IIIa (PAC‐1), GPIbα (CD42b), GPVI, surface bound fibrinogen, TMRE, P‐selectin (CD62P), phosphatidylserine (annexin‐V), and release of EVs and granule contents (supernatant CD62P) were not significantly altered by 2 h of refrigerated storage (Table 1). Interestingly, the functional parameters of agonist‐induced aggregation (ADP and collagen) were higher and clotting times (IN‐test and PPL) were faster after just 2 h of refrigeration (Table 1). This data shows that morphological and functional parameters associated with CSPs, but not phenotypic changes, are altered within 2 h of refrigerated storage.

When platelets were stored in the freezer, morphological changes were evident within 15–20 min. Swirl progressively decreased during storage in the freezer, with all components exhibiting a score of between 0 and 0.5 after 20 min (Figure 1B). The MPV, PDW, and the proportion of platelets with circular morphology gradually increased as a function of the time in the freezer to levels equivalent to cold platelets by 20 min (Figure 1C–E).

Aligned with the above data showing that short‐term refrigeration (2 h) does not alter the abundance of GPIIIa (CD61), activated GPIIb/IIIa (PAC‐1), GPIbα (CD42b), GPVI, surface bound fibrinogen, or CD62P, these markers were not affected by short‐term exposure to freezer temperature (Figure 2A–F). In contrast, a small, but statistically significant, increase in annexin‐V‐positive platelets and EVs was observed after 15–20 min in the freezer (Figure 2G,H).

Functionally, the aggregation responses of on‐demand platelets were similar to cold platelets within 5 min for collagen and 15 min for ADP (Figure 3A,B). The contribution of platelets to global clot formation was measured using viscoelastic testing. The IN‐test clotting time was similar between platelets stored in the fridge and freezer (Figure 3C), while the MCF (strength of the clot) was reduced in the cold platelets, compared to the on‐demand platelets (Figure 3D). No clot lysis was observed in either group (data not shown). The phospholipid clotting time was progressively shortened during frozen storage (Figure 3E), which is likely linked to the changes in phosphatidylserine externalization on the platelets and EVs. Taken together, these data demonstrate that storage of platelets in a standard blood bank freezer (set to −30°C) induces changes in platelet morphology, phenotype, and function to an equivalent extent as platelets stored in the fridge for 2 h.

To maximize the utility of the on‐demand platelets in instances where the product may have been requested, but then no longer required, the on‐demand components were transferred to the fridge after 20 min in the freezer to determine whether they remained equivalent to standard CSPs. The platelet concentration was similar after 14 days in the fridge (1198 ± 145 × 109/L) and on‐demand (1180 ± 185 × 109/L; p = .9703) platelets. However, changes in almost all other parameters occurred between 2 h and 14 days of refrigeration (Table 1). The exceptions were MPV, PDW, CD61 abundance, and ADP‐induced aggregation, which remained stable following the initial cold‐induced change. The formation of aggregates was not observed in any components during 14 days of storage. Given that the majority of the quality parameters in the on‐demand platelets at 20 min were not different to the CSP, it was unsurprising that the cold and on‐demand platelet parameters remained similar after 14 days of refrigerated storage. Of the parameters that differed, the on‐demand platelets appear less activated, as indicated by a lower abundance of activated GPIIb/IIIa and externalized P‐selectin. Although these differences were statistically different, they were small and unlikely to be of clinical relevance. These data demonstrate that extended storage of rapidly prepared CSP is appropriate, if required.

DISCUSSION

Internationally, CSP are being manufactured by several institutes, with a maximal shelf life of 14 days, 6 , 25 , 26 , 27 although a further extension of the shelf life to 21 days is being explored. 28 Several recent clinical trials have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of CSP for the treatment of bleeding patients. 29 , 30 , 31 CHIPS, a large, international, multi‐center, clinical trial that has recently completed recruitment, was designed to address efficacy in bleeding cardiac surgery patients. 28 , 32 If CSP are shown to be more effective for the treatment of bleeding, it may be desirable to rapidly make them “on‐demand” from RT platelets if a cold inventory is unavailable. This study demonstrated that the in vitro morphological and functional changes characteristic of CSP could be induced quickly by refrigeration for 2 h, or by exposure to freezer temperatures (−30°C) for 15–20 min. These platelets could then be stored in the fridge until expiry (14 days), with in vitro characteristics and function comparable to standard CSP.

The concept of creating cold platelets “on‐demand” has previously been proposed. 18 , 19 , 33 These studies were performed by refrigerating platelets for 1–2 h and focused on the effect of short‐term refrigeration on signal transduction and selected downstream functional outcomes (aggregation and adhesion). Further, the studies assessed platelets stored in plasma, 18 , 19 , 33 which is known to differentially affect platelet parameters during storage, compared to storage in PAS. 13 , 15 , 34 , 35 , 36 As such, the study presented here confirms the on‐demand approach is suitable for blood bank platelets stored in a combination of plasma and additive solution (PAS‐E). Further, this study extends this concept by demonstrating that exposure to freezing temperatures can achieve similar outcomes in minutes, rather than hours, which is critical for use in emergency bleeding.

A significant amount of foundational research has been conducted to explore the mechanisms mediating the reduced circulation time of CSP, 5 , 37 which highlighted the sensitivities of platelets to temperatures below 22°C. 20 , 38 , 39 , 40 However, since that time, platelet collection and storage methods have advanced, such that confirmation of these findings is warranted. Unfortunately, the majority of the studies performed on contemporary CSP have assessed the quality changes after 5 or 7 days of storage; hence, there is limited data on the immediate effects of refrigeration. It has long been known that platelets stored at temperatures below 15°C exhibit increased intracellular calcium, which is accompanied by changes in morphology, cytoskeletal rearrangements, and the phase transitioning of phospholipids. 11 , 20 , 38 , 41 By 5 min of storage in the freezer, the component temperature was approximately 12°C, and alterations in both the MPV and PDW were observed, suggestive of a change in size and/or shape. However, the loss of swirl, which is routinely used to assess platelet morphology 42 was not consistently reduced until the temperature was closer to 6°C. An increase in intracellular calcium was not observed during short‐term exposure to fridge or freezer temperatures; however, this may be an artifact due to the complexities of measuring calcium flux—specifically, the transient nature of calcium fluxes that occur with short‐term chilling and rewarming. 38 An increase in annexin‐V binding was observed in both platelets and EVs following 15–20 min of exposure to freezer temperature, but not after 2 h in the fridge, suggesting that the rapid temperature change may have a greater effect on phospholipid phase transitioning in the platelet membrane. However, these differences were resolved during extended storage in the fridge. While the abundance of platelet glycoproteins was unaffected by the membrane changes occurring during rapid short‐term temperature exposure, changes in functionality occurred. This may be particularly beneficial in the context of preparing more “reactive” platelets for transfusion to critically bleeding patients on demand and is in line with previous literature on short‐term refrigeration. 18 , 19

While we have shown that the cold changes induced rapidly by exposure to freezer temperatures are maintained during extended storage in the cold for at least 14 days, it is also valuable to understand how stable the changes are upon rewarming. This is important as previous data suggest that temperature cycling (between 4 and 37°C for 1 h in a 12‐h cycle) can ameliorate some of the cold‐induced changes. 43 , 44 In this study, efforts were made to minimize the time between sampling and testing, so that the cold‐induced changes could be assessed. However, in the clinical scenario there is a period of time between removal of the CSP from the fridge and transfusion, where the platelets will equilibrate towards RT. Further to this, it is likely that CSP will be rapidly warmed to 37°C as they are being infused, via the use of blood warmers. 45 As such, further consideration should be given to understanding the effects of temperature fluctuations on platelet quality parameters, and whether these effects are influenced by factors such as the storage solution, duration of time at 2–6°C, pathogen inactivation, and other collection and/or processing factors.

Given that cold platelets are currently being reserved for the treatment of bleeding, implementation of CSP has occurred alongside the RT inventory, rather than as a replacement; presenting some challenges. 6 , 7 , 25 One approach to assist inventory management is the use of delayed‐cold platelets which maximize the RT inventory while minimizing wastage. 46 , 47 , 48 This concept involves storing platelets at RT for a period of time close to expiry (e.g., 4–7 days) and then transferring them to the cold if they are not required to support the RT inventory requirements. 49 In vitro and clinical efficacy parameters appear to be similar between delayed‐cold platelets and those stored in the fridge as soon as practical following collection (within 24 h). 26 , 46 , 47 , 48 , 49 Given that the response of platelets to cold exposure occurs similarly, regardless of prior storage time at RT, we anticipate that this may also be the case when platelets are rapidly cooled in the freezer. However, it would be valuable to confirm that the changes are replicable in platelets that are closer to expiry (e.g., Days 5 and 6). The “on‐demand” approach for producing CSP may be most logistically practicable in a hospital blood bank, rather than the blood manufacturer, depending on the local jurisdiction and regulatory framework. Specifically, this approach could be utilized in instances where there is a critically bleeding patient and the current stock of CSP has been depleted, but RT platelets are available. This premise was the rationale for choosing a standard blood bank freezer, such as those already used to store FFP, which is typically stored below −25°C. As described recently by Cohn et al., each institute has its own unique settings, clinical scenarios and regulatory constraints that may differentially challenge their ability to maintain adequate platelet stocks. Thus, evidence to support multiple options to manufacture, store, and use CSP may be valuable.

As a limitation, it should be highlighted that as collection platforms and storage solutions are known to differentially affect the in vitro and in vivo quality parameters of CSP, 13 , 34 , 36 , 50 , 51 these findings may not be applicable to all scenarios. Further, this investigation was performed in vitro, which may not fully reflect the functional properties of platelets in vivo. Importantly, the timeframes indicated in this study are dependent on component volume, so the time required to decrease the temperature to within the 2–6°C window will need to be established within specific institutes.

We have shown that refrigeration for 2 h or short‐term exposure of platelets to standard blood bank freezer temperatures represents options to rapidly induce the functional changes typified by CSP. As such, these methods may represent a reasonable approach for the preparation of platelets with enhanced responsiveness to treat bleeding patients more effectively.
