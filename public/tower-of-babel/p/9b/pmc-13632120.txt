Improving interventions for oral frailty in older adults: a qualitative study of barriers and pathways from the perspective of oral healthcare professionals
Authors: Fan Xie, Jing Li, Siqi Chen, Han Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo analyze the current understanding of oral frailty and identify barriers to optimization of interventions for oral frailty in older adults among oral healthcare professionals in China, to inform the development of systematic intervention programs.MethodsA Husserlian descriptive phenomenological methodology was adopted to explore the lived experiences and perceptions of oral healthcare professionals regarding oral frailty interventions. A total of 15 oral healthcare professionals from Shenzhen Luohu Hospital Group in Shenzhen, China, were selected by purposive and snowball sampling. Participants underwent semi-structured interviews, and themes were analyzed using Colaizzi’s method.ResultsFour themes emerged: fragmented understanding of oral frailty, differing views on health education settings, inconsistencies in assessment and management practices, and systemic barriers affecting intervention implementation.ConclusionsOral healthcare professionals in China exhibited a “high willingness–low initiative” conflict regarding intervention for oral frailty in older adults. These findings suggest that strengthening education and training, developing standardized assessment and intervention tools, promoting multidisciplinary collaboration, and improving policy support may help bridge the gap between understanding and practice and facilitate the implementation of oral frailty interventions for older adults.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-08781-x.Keywords: Oral frailty, Healthcare professionals’ understanding, Barriers to intervention, Qualitative study

Background

With the acceleration of population aging in China, health problems in older adults have become a central public concern and have sparked widespread academic interest. Among various health issues, oral health serves as a key factor affecting the quality of life (QOL) of older adults and plays an important role in the shift from disease treatment to systemic function maintenance [1]. Currently, oral health management in older adults is not limited to conventional treatments for tooth or gum diseases but places greater emphasis on maintaining and enhancing overall function [2]. Therefore, oral frailty has emerged as an interdisciplinary concept and serves as a key framework for this shift. Oral frailty refers to the decline in oral function that occurs with increasing age. It involves several changes, including loss of teeth, reduced chewing ability, and weakened articulatory motor skills [3, 4]. These changes are often accompanied by an overall decline in physical and psychological functions [5]. Oral frailty should be distinguished from oral hypofunction. Oral hypofunction refers to measurable impairment in specific oral functions (e.g., tongue pressure, chewing, and swallowing), whereas oral frailty is a broader geriatric concept describing the decline of multiple oral functions associated with aging and systemic vulnerability. It is also distinct from general physical frailty, although the two conditions often coexist and may influence each other.

Oral frailty has been closely associated with malnutrition [6], sarcopenia [7], cognitive impairment [8], reduced QOL, and increased risk of mortality [9]. Although global studies have made progress, an in-depth understanding of oral frailty remains essential for formulating effective preventive and corrective measures to improve the QOL of older adults. In China, intervention measures for oral frailty have become increasingly diverse and include improvement of dietary habits, oral hygiene education, and regular professional nursing care. These measures have been reported to improve symptoms and enhance QOL in older adults [10].

Previous studies have mostly focused on disease definition, development of assessment tools, and treatment strategies [5, 11]. However, studies exploring the barriers to the development of intervention programs for oral frailty in older adults from the perspective of Chinese oral healthcare professionals are lacking. Because barriers among frontline oral healthcare professionals directly influence successful implementation in clinical practice, this study focused on real-life work experiences of oral healthcare professionals in China to provide recommendations for overcoming these barriers. We hope that our results can address the limitations of existing research in this field and offer empirical evidence for the improvement of oral health services for older adults.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study adopted a Husserlian descriptive phenomenological approach to explore oral healthcare professionals’ lived experiences and perceptions of oral frailty and to identify barriers to implementing interventions for older adults. The study was reported in accordance with the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist. The study was conducted within the Shenzhen Luohu Hospital Group (Shenzhen, China) between October and December 2024. Researcher reflexivity was addressed by acknowledging the interviewers’ clinical backgrounds and documenting their assumptions throughout data collection and analysis (see Data collection and Quality assurance sections).

Participants and sampling

Participants were oral healthcare professionals selected using purposive and snowball sampling methods. Initial participants were identified through purposive sampling based on publicly available institutional information within the Shenzhen Luohu Hospital Group. To maximize variation at study entry, three oral healthcare professionals from different practice settings were selected as the initial participants according to their clinical involvement in oral healthcare for older adults. These participants met the inclusion criteria and represented different specialties and clinical settings. Subsequently, participants were asked to recommend additional eligible colleagues who met the inclusion criteria, forming a snowball sampling chain to expand the sample. The initial recruitment stage did not rely on participant recommendation. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) oral healthcare professionals involved in oral healthcare for older adults; (2) nurses with at least a vocational qualification, and doctors having at least a bachelor’s degree; and (3) informed consent and voluntary participation. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) oral healthcare professionals who were not on duty owing to reasons such as training or leave and (2) individuals who withdrew from the study during the interview period. The sample size was determined based on the principle of data saturation. Saturation was considered reached when no new themes, categories, or meaningful codes emerged from consecutive interviews during preliminary analysis. Preliminary analysis was conducted after every two to three interviews, and the emergence of new codes was tracked throughout the analytic process. In this study, thematic saturation was observed after the 13th interview, and two additional interviews were conducted to confirm the stability of the thematic framework, resulting in a final sample of 15 participants.

Ultimately, 15 oral healthcare professionals were included in the study, comprising 11 women and 4 men, with a mean age of (32.8 ± 6.0) years and an average work experience of (6.9 ± 3.2) years. Seven participants worked in outpatient clinics, five in community health services, one in an inpatient department, one in a nursing home, and one in a day care center. In terms of specialty distribution, there were three participants each in general dentistry and endodontics; two each in pediatric dentistry, periodontology and oral mucosal disease, oral surgery, and implantology; and one each in orthodontics and oral medicine. Doctors and nurses were represented by the serial numbers D1–D7 and N1–N8, respectively. As outpatient clinics and community health services were the main practice fields, the participants’ specialties were diverse yet clinically relevant, providing a comprehensive perspective for the interviews. All participants provided written informed consent and voluntarily participated in the study (2025-LHQRMYY-KYLL-070).

Data collection

Data were collected through face-to-face semi-structured interviews conducted by Fan Xie and Han Zhao, both of whom had clinical backgrounds in oral healthcare and received training in qualitative research methods prior to the study. Fan Xie, the primary interviewer, is a male researcher with a master’s degree in stomatology and more than 15 years of clinical experience in geriatric oral healthcare. The interviewers had no prior personal or clinical relationship with the participants before recruitment. Participants were informed about the purpose of the study and the researchers’ professional roles before the interviews were conducted. Participants were contacted in advance to schedule the interview, and interviews were held at appropriate, quiet venues. During and immediately after each interview, field notes were recorded to document contextual information, interview atmosphere, non-verbal cues, key moments during the interview, and the researchers’ immediate reflections alongside the audio recordings. Interview techniques such as active listening and responding were used to create a relaxed atmosphere. Each interview lasted 40–60 min and concluded once data saturation was reached.

Based on a review of the literature related to oral frailty published in China and other countries, and considering the purpose and significance of this study, we formulated a draft interview structure. Four oral healthcare professionals (two doctors and two nurses) participated in pilot interviews, and the structure was revised accordingly. In addition, the primary interviewer conducted two rounds of pre-interview practice before formal data collection to become familiar with the interview process and improve neutrality. The interview outline was based on the latest systematic review of oral frailty prevalence and risk factors [12], a case study on qualitative interviews with oral healthcare professionals [13], and a recent scoping review of barriers and strategies [14].

The interview included the following questions: (1) Are you aware of the concept of oral frailty? What are the clinical manifestations of oral frailty? (2) In your opinion, how is oral frailty related to other diseases? (3) Are you willing to promote oral frailty-related knowledge? Why? (4) In your opinion, where should health education regarding oral frailty be implemented? Why? (5) Have you ever adopted intervention measures for patients with oral frailty-like presentation? What methods do you usually adopt for assessment and data collection? (6) In your opinion, what is the current level of intervention for oral frailty among healthcare professionals? What is the cause of such a situation? (7) In your opinion, what are the factors influencing your implementation of oral frailty interventions? What are the areas that can be improved or optimized? When participants made absolute statements or expressed seemingly definitive views, the interviewer used probing questions (e.g., ‘Could you tell me more about that?’ ‘What led you to that conclusion?’) to encourage elaboration.

Data analysis

All interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim within 24 h using the Tencent Meeting speech-to-text function. The transcripts were manually checked against the original recordings to ensure accuracy. Two researchers independently reviewed and coded the transcripts. After independent coding, the researchers compared their coding results and discussed any discrepancies until consensus was reached. When disagreements arose, the coders returned to the original transcripts and reviewed the relevant passages in context before discussing their interpretations. When disagreements could not be resolved through discussion, the research team jointly reviewed the relevant transcripts to reach a final agreement. Data were analyzed using Colaizzi’s seven-step method [15]. First, the researchers repeatedly read the transcripts to gain a comprehensive understanding of participants’ experiences. Significant statements related to perceptions, experiences, and practices regarding oral frailty interventions were extracted from the transcripts. These included statements directly relevant to the research question, statements expressing key experiences or views, and statements reflecting repeated or emphasized issues across interviews. The extracted statements were then condensed into formulated meanings representing key ideas expressed by participants. The formulated meanings were subsequently grouped into thematic clusters, which were compared across interviews to identify common patterns. An exhaustive description of the phenomenon was then developed, and the fundamental structure of participants’ experiences was summarized. Finally, the transcripts were returned to participants for verification to ensure the accuracy of the recorded information. After preliminary theme development, a summary of the main themes and representative quotations was also shared with participants for feedback on whether the findings adequately reflected their experiences. The final thematic structure was validated by cross-checking with the original transcripts to ensure internal consistency and accurate reflection of participants’ perspectives.

Quality assurance

To ensure methodological rigor, the study followed the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist and the four criteria of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability. The study included participants with varied lengths of work experience, job positions, and educational levels, ensuring comprehensive perspectives [16]. Researchers underwent systematic training in qualitative research theories, which included aspects such as record writing, data coding, and theme extraction. Researchers maintained a neutral stance and refrained from making judgments on participants’ responses. To address researcher reflexivity, the interviewers documented their assumptions and reflections during data collection and analysis and regularly discussed these reflections within the research team to minimize potential bias. During interviews, the primary interviewer adopted a neutral and exploratory stance and avoided leading or assumption-based questioning. Field notes were reviewed together with the transcripts during analysis to support contextual interpretation and reflexive examination of the data. Transcription and data analysis were primarily performed by two researchers, and the results were discussed several times within the research team. To minimize the influence of researcher beliefs and experiences on the study results, researchers constantly reflected on the data during data analysis. Researchers accurately described participants’ feelings and experiences to allow readers to assess and interpret the findings. Quotations from participants were appropriately cited during the reporting of study results, and the organized transcripts were provided to the participants for verification to ensure accuracy of the recorded information. Participants were invited to confirm whether the transcripts accurately reflected their views. In addition, after preliminary theme development, participants were invited to provide feedback on whether the thematic summary adequately reflected their experiences. Participant feedback was considered during final theme refinement, while responsibility for the final interpretation remained with the research team.

Results

A total of 15 oral healthcare professionals were interviewed in this study, and no second interviews were conducted. Table 1 shows the characteristics of the participants. Analysis of the interviews identified four themes related to participants’ understanding, attitudes, and clinical practices regarding oral frailty intervention (Fig. 1). Overall, the interview data showed that participants generally recognized the importance of oral frailty intervention, but also described difficulties in carrying it out in routine practice. Themes 1 and 2 mainly present participants’ views and attitudes toward oral frailty, whereas Themes 3 and 4 describe the assessment, management, and barriers discussed in relation to practice.

Comparison between the ideal and real-world KAP chains for oral healthcare professionals’ intervention in oral frailty in older adults

Theme 1: oral healthcare professionals exhibited inadequate understanding of the concept of oral frailty with characteristics of fragmentation

All participants stated that they had not received systematic education on the concept of oral frailty. When asked to interpret the term based on its literal meaning, participants often hesitated before responding or equated oral frailty with “tooth loss” or “poor oral hygiene”. Among the participants, most had encountered the term for the first time during their interview, with D7 stating “I have never heard of this concept”. Regarding understanding of the concept, most nurses focused on local symptoms, such as “tooth loss or periodontal disease”, whereas doctors provided responses related to functional dimensions, such as “decline in soft-tissue function” mentioned by D3. However, in response to the interview question ‘In your opinion, how is oral frailty related to other diseases?’, none of the participants described an association between oral frailty and systemic diseases. Few participants regarded oral frailty as an inevitable consequence of aging, with N2 stating “it happens when you grow old”, whereas others mistakenly perceived it as a routine dental disease, with D5 expressing the belief that “it simply refers to severe periodontitis”. N3 stated “My understanding is that it is related to a decline in oral function, such as chewing and swallowing, with age, but I do not know how it is related to malnutrition.”

Theme 2: oral healthcare professionals showed a high level of agreement with the oral frailty concept but had differing opinions regarding health education settings

After researchers provided a brief explanation of oral frailty, all participants expressed strong agreement with the concept and willingness to provide health education. However, their opinions on the proposed pathways for implementation in practice differed.

All participants agreed that promotion of the concept of oral frailty is extremely important and is a key step in preventing disability during old age. For instance, D1 stated “This is a key step in preventing disability during old age.” Few participants mentioned that this concept aligns with national policies, with N2 stating “consistent with the requirements of the Healthy China Initiative”.

However, participants’ opinions regarding the proposed settings for health education on oral frailty differed. A majority of participants felt that health education should be conducted during diagnosis and treatment and that patient waiting periods could be utilized for playing related videos. For instance, D4 suggested “utilize patient waiting periods to play videos”. Several participants advocated for centralized health education at labor unions or senior activity centers. For instance, N1 suggested “centralized health education at nursing homes.” A few participants suggested the incorporation of the oral frailty concept in the health-education curriculum for primary and secondary schools to “foster an awareness of lifelong care from a young age,” as suggested by D2.

Theme 3: differences in oral frailty assessment and management practices

Regarding the assessment and management of oral frailty, participants generally agreed on the importance of early identification and intervention. At the same time, they described a range of practical difficulties in implementation, which was reflected in differing views on assessment methods and intervention approaches.

Deficiencies in assessment criteria

Although all participants believed that early assessment is extremely important, their opinions regarding assessment methods differed. About half of the participants felt that objective physical signs should be the core of assessment. For instance, N4 suggested that “judgement should be made based on the oral hygiene status, tooth loss, and alveolar bone resorption”. Some participants suggested that comprehensive functional assessment must be emphasized. For instance, D5 stated “language expression, intelligence level, muscle response, and systemic medical history must be taken into consideration”. However, standardized scales were lacking in clinical practice. Doctors often relied on personal clinical experience when making diagnoses, leading to notable differences in assessment outcomes.

Time and funding constraints

Participants highlighted difficulties in implementing clinical intervention measures. Few participants highlighted a lack of suitable assessment tools and equipment. For instance, D1 mentioned “Tongue pressure measurement devices must be imported. Because the unit cost is approximately 20,000 yuan, most hospitals cannot afford it. Even training tools such as tongue depressors are often listed as unnecessary expenses.” Most participants mentioned a lack of time, with N7 frankly stating “There are too many patients, and doctors and nurses have limited time and energy. Therefore, basic treatment will take precedence over assessment and health education.”

Deficiencies in the multidisciplinary collaboration process

Many participants mentioned that “multidisciplinary collaboration is needed” and felt that oral frailty management requires collaboration among multiple disciplines such as oral surgery, rehabilitation, and geriatrics. Participants reported that collaboration processes were unclear and raised questions such as ‘who should do it’ and ‘how it should be done’.

Fragmentation of intervention measures

Regarding specific intervention measures, participants mainly described symptom-focused interventions. Most participants admitted that a greater emphasis is placed on symptomatic treatment in clinical practice. For instance, in patients with periodontitis, the treatment is targeted towards periodontal issues, and the overall maintenance of oral function is neglected. Although a small number of participants mentioned swallowing training or tongue muscle training, such practices were described as uncommon because of instrument and process constraints.

Theme 4: multiple factors influence differences in the understanding and intervention of oral frailty

The interviews revealed that although participants agreed on the importance of oral frailty, they had a limited understanding of its concept and underlying mechanisms. Upon further questioning, the following three categories of barriers were identified:

Deficiencies in educational training

All participants stated that “oral frailty” was not mentioned in the curricula for bachelor’s and master’s programs. D5 stated “This concept cannot be found in textbooks. Therefore, students lack a basic understanding.” Twelve of the 15 participants mentioned that continuing education and training at the hospitals or departments where they worked had “seldom or never” involved content related to oral frailty. N3 further elaborated that “The focus of training remains on modules such as endodontics and orthodontics. Oral frailty is not included.”

Barriers to information acquisition

A majority of participants believed that differences in educational level directly affect the efficiency of acquiring new information. D3 stated that individuals with a bachelor’s degree or higher found it easier to access relevant literature published in English. Participants suggested that individuals with lower educational backgrounds may experience greater difficulty accessing and interpreting English-language academic literature. Eight of the 15 participants reported that there were “almost no” expert reports on oral frailty during the academic conferences that they had attended during the last 3 years. D4 stated “This topic is seldom discussed during academic conferences. You cannot listen to it even if you wanted to.”

Lack of institutional support

Participants reported an overall lack of institutional support. N8 stated that “Without requirements for appraisal indicators, implementation in clinical practice can only be driven by personal interest.” Other participants mentioned that constraints were posed by health insurance. For instance, D2 stated that “Functional training is not covered under health insurance. Therefore, patients reject it when they learn that it will be an out-of-pocket expense, and doctors lack the motivation to promote it.”

Discussion

The knowledge, attitudes, and practices framework can be used to formulate intervention programs for oral frailty

Although the Knowledge-Attitudes-Practices framework was not predefined in the study design, it is used here as a conceptual lens to interpret the themes identified through phenomenological analysis. This study revealed a system-related gap in the understanding of oral frailty in older adults among oral healthcare professionals. This is consistent with the rate of awareness of oral frailty among Japanese community healthcare professionals reported by Kugimiya et al. [17]. Among the participants, most participants had encountered the term for the first time during their interviews and equated oral frailty with local concepts such as ‘tooth loss’ or ‘poor oral hygiene’, neglecting its association with systemic nutrition, disability risk, and geriatric syndromes (e.g., sarcopenia and cognitive impairment). This interpretation also suggests partial conceptual overlap with oral hypofunction, as participants tended to focus on isolated oral functional impairments rather than recognizing oral frailty as a broader geriatric syndrome and a potentially reversible intermediate state requiring earlier and more comprehensive intervention. This fragmented understanding stems from deficiencies in the medical education system. The exclusion of oral frailty from curricula and continuing education has led clinicians to rely on personal experience rather than systematic knowledge [18, 19]. Although healthcare professionals expressed a strong willingness for intervention (attitude), the gap in knowledge hindered the transformation of attitude into action. The lack of standardized assessment tools and operational procedures reduced the sensitivity of early detection and increased the rate of missed diagnoses [11]. This demonstrates the disconnect in knowledge, attitudes, and practices and suggests the need for restructuring of the educational ecosystem as follows: (1) Inclusion of oral frailty in the core curricula at the foundational level; (2) Development of simple “screening–intervention” pathways at the practice level; (3) Inclusion of oral frailty as a hospital performance appraisal indicator at the system level. Such measures will contribute to the formation of a complete knowledge, attitudes, and practices loop.

A cross-disciplinary “prevention–intervention” collaborative system should serve as the core pathway for the development of oral frailty intervention programs

The cross-disciplinary attributes of oral frailty management necessitate breaking of inter-specialty barriers. In this study, healthcare professionals had a high level of agreement regarding the importance of prevention and control. They suggested that health education should span the full course of life, including school education for children, workplace interventions for working adults, and clinical education for older adults. However, a “high willingness–low action” disconnect was observed in the implementation pathways. Participants expressed support for different settings for oral frailty education, including clinic waiting areas (60%), community settings (26%), and schools (13%). The root cause for this phenomenon lies in deficiencies in multidisciplinary collaboration. To improve coordination between prevention, intervention, and management of oral frailty, participants in this study highlighted the potential importance of stronger multidisciplinary collaboration. Within the context of the healthcare system represented in this study, several practical directions may be considered. For example, hospitals could explore collaborative clinics involving oral healthcare, nutrition, and geriatrics to address the interaction between swallowing function and nutritional risk. At the community level, oral function assessment may be incorporated into existing public health services for adults aged ≥ 65 years. At the policy level, expanding insurance coverage to include functional interventions, such as oral frailty screening or tongue pressure training, could also be considered. These approaches are consistent with the goals of the “Healthy China 2030” initiative and may support the development of more coordinated prevention and management pathways for oral frailty.

Resource assurance and institutional design are key breakthrough points for the formulation and implementation of oral frailty intervention programs

Owing to the lack of standardized tools, most participants stated that they could focus on the treatment of single diseases alone and were unable to conduct comprehensive functional assessments. The lack of key equipment, such as tongue pressure measurement devices, led healthcare professionals to rely on personal experience for judgment, resulting in greater subjectivity [20, 21]. In addition, unclear collaboration processes caused ambiguity in responsibility for implementing interventions such as swallowing training. These shortcomings resulted in fragmented management and highlighted the need for resource assurance and institutional design. From a policy perspective, these strategies should be aligned with the objectives of the Healthy China 2030 initiative, which emphasizes healthy aging and the prevention of functional decline among older adults. In the short term, practical measures within the operational scope of oral healthcare professionals include strengthening continuing education on oral frailty, integrating simple screening tools into routine dental examinations, and promoting health education during routine clinical encounters. At the system level, broader reforms such as incorporating oral frailty assessment into national oral health programs, establishing multidisciplinary collaboration mechanisms, and expanding insurance coverage for functional oral rehabilitation will be required to support sustainable implementation.

Selection of assessment methods such as chewing efficiency as alternatives to diagnostic tests is recommended to reduce costs, and oral frailty functional training programs should be included under health insurance coverage [22, 23]. Additionally, healthcare institutions should provide more opportunities for continuing education and learning, consolidate knowledge on geriatric syndromes through cross-disciplinary training programs, and allocate dedicated funds for equipment upgrades, which will contribute to the translation of academic progress into clinical capabilities. Furthermore, oral frailty screening should be included as a routine assessment parameter for older adults, and multidisciplinary consultation and referral pathways should be established. Bridging the gaps between understanding, willingness, and implementation will enable achievement of early prevention and treatment of oral frailty, thereby reducing the risk of disability in old age.

Strengths and limitations

This study has two main strengths. First, it provides one of the first qualitative analyses in China focusing on oral frailty from the perspective of oral healthcare professionals, capturing authentic experiences and practical barriers in clinical settings. Second, the use of Colaizzi’s phenomenological method and a diverse sample from both hospital and community institutions ensured systematic analysis and enhanced the credibility of findings.

However, some limitations should be acknowledged. The study was conducted within a single healthcare group in Shenzhen, which may limit the transferability of findings to other regions, institutional settings, or healthcare systems. Although participants were drawn from multiple service settings within the group, including outpatient clinics, community health services, an inpatient department, a nursing home, and a day care center, the shared organizational context may still have shaped participants’ perspectives in ways that differ from those in independent clinics or other healthcare systems. Although participants represented multiple specialties and practice settings within the hospital group, their perspectives may not fully reflect those of oral healthcare professionals working in different geographic regions or organizational contexts. The use of snowball sampling may have introduced selection bias, as participants were partly recruited through professional networks within the same healthcare group, which may have resulted in participants with similar professional backgrounds or perspectives. Furthermore, although efforts were made to maintain neutrality during interviews and analysis, the researchers’ clinical backgrounds in oral healthcare may have influenced the interpretation of participants’ responses. In addition, the qualitative design does not allow quantitative measurement of the prevalence or strength of specific barriers. Finally, participants’ self-reported experiences may have been subject to recall bias or social desirability bias. In addition, because the concept of oral frailty was briefly explained during the interviews, participants’ responses may have been influenced by this explanation, introducing a potential priming effect. Future studies should expand the sample to include administrators, caregivers, and community workers, and integrate mixed-method or quantitative approaches to further validate the results, and establish standardized screening, intervention, and management protocols.

Conclusions

This study identified a “high agreement–low action” gap in oral frailty management among Chinese oral healthcare professionals. Although participants recognized the importance of oral frailty prevention and expressed strong willingness to engage in health education, implementation remained limited by insufficient knowledge, inadequate resources, and weak interdisciplinary collaboration. Strengthening education and training, developing standardized tools, and integrating oral frailty services into health insurance and institutional systems are essential to bridge the gap between understanding and practice, particularly in healthcare settings with organizational structures similar to those examined in this study.
