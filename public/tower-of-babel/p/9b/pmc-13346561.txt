Characterization of Biochar from Prunus armeniaca shells collected in cold desert Kargil, Ladakh, as a soil enhancer
Authors: Sajad Hussain, Pratima Gajbhiye, Sonawane Chandrakant, Amol Vedpathak, Choon Kit chan, Saurav Dixit, Md Irfanul Haque Siddiqui
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCold desert regions such as Kargil in Ladakh face severe agricultural challenges due to poor soil fertility, limited nutrient availability, water scarcity, and short growing seasons, leading to a heavy reliance on chemical fertilizers. This study explores the potential of biochar produced from locally available apricot seed shells (Prunus armeniaca), an abundant agro-waste in the region, as a sustainable soil amendment. The biochar was applied to native cold desert soil to evaluate its influence on soil fertility and plant growth using spinach (Spinacia oleracea), a commonly cultivated vegetable in household gardens. Compared with untreated soil, biochar-amended soil showed improved plant growth, reflected in increased leaf size and leaf number. The results demonstrate that apricot seed shell biochar can enhance soil quality and support healthier vegetable growth under cold desert conditions. By converting locally generated apricot agro-waste into a value-added soil amendment, this study proposes a region-specific and sustainable strategy to improve soil productivity while reducing dependence on external chemical fertilizers. The findings highlight the potential of apricot seed shell biochar as an environmentally sustainable option for soil management and agricultural resilience in fragile cold desert ecosystems such as Kargil, Ladakh.Keywords: Biochar, Apricot seed shell, Cold desert soil, Sustainable agriculture, Soil fertility, Spinacia oleracea, Ladakh agro-ecosystemSubject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Excessive use of synthetic fertilizers exerts significant pressure on agro-ecosystems, necessitating sustainable alternatives such as biochar1,2. Biochar is a carbon-rich material produced through the pyrolysis of organic wastes under oxygen-limited conditions, offering a sustainable solution for various environmental and agricultural challenges. It is primarily derived from biomass such as forestry waste, animal manure, and crop residues, and is characterized by its stability and recalcitrant nature. The production process involves heating biomass to temperatures between 300 °C and 1000 °C, with slow pyrolysis being the most common method due to its higher yield and carbon stability. The properties of biochar, including pH, surface area, and nutrient content, are influenced by the feedstock and production conditions, making it a versatile material for multiple applications3,4. It is characterized by high mineral carbon content, abundant surface functional groups, and essential nutrients such as potassium (K⁺), which enhance soil organic matter, cation exchange capacity (CEC), and overall crop nutrition5,6. Biochar exhibits a non-aggregated carbon microsphere structure with a well-developed porous network, resulting in high surface area and enhanced adsorption capacity7. Adsorption occurs through surface diffusion and physicochemical interactions, including hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, facilitated by oxygen-containing functional groups such as hydroxyl (–OH), carboxyl (–COOH), and carbonyl (C = O) groups7. With increasing pyrolysis temperature, biochar becomes more aromatic and carbonaceous, as indicated by shifts in FTIR spectral bands in the 1750–1500 cm⁻¹ region8. The properties and agronomic performance of biochar are strongly influenced by feedstock type, residence time, and pyrolysis temperature9,10. Biochars derived from wood biomass generally exhibit higher carbon content and surface area than those derived from animal wastes10. Lower pyrolysis temperatures (≤ 400 °C) favor higher nutrient retention, particularly nitrogen, whereas higher temperatures (≥ 400–700 °C) increase surface area, porosity, pH, aromaticity, and structural stability while reducing H/C and O/C ratios5,11. Biochar produced at around 500 °C has been shown to significantly enhance soil organic carbon compared to untreated soils12. As a soil amendment, biochar improves soil physical, chemical, and biological properties. Its porous structure enhances water-holding capacity, aggregation, aeration, and root penetration, while reducing bulk density13,14. Biochar creates microhabitats for beneficial microorganisms, stimulating microbial activity and nutrient cycling15. The ash fraction of biochar supplies essential macro- and micronutrients, including N, P, K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn, and Zn, which are vital for plant growth14,15. High CEC and porosity enable biochar to retain nutrients, reduce nitrate and ammonium leaching, and promote gradual nutrient release16–18. Beyond soil fertility improvement, biochar acts as an effective adsorbent for organic and inorganic contaminants, immobilizing heavy metals and reducing their bioavailability in polluted soils18–20. Its stability and aromatic carbon structure enhance long-term carbon sequestration, supporting climate mitigation and sustainable land management21,22. In cold-arid agro-ecosystems such as Kargil, Ladakh, biochar derived from locally available apricotPrunus armeniacaseed shells has shown strong potential to improve soil quality and vegetable productivity under harsh climatic conditions23. Application of apricot seed shell biochar at optimal rates (≈ 5%) significantly improves spinach growth parameters, including leaf length, number, plant height, and biomass, primarily through improvements in CEC, nutrient availability, and soil moisture retention1,24. Although global research on biochar has advanced considerably, systematic region-specific studies utilizing locally available agro-wastes under uniform agro-climatic conditions remain limited, particularly in fragile cold-desert ecosystems. Investigating apricot seed shell-derived biochar addresses this critical knowledge gap by offering a sustainable alternative to chemical fertilizers while supporting the development of location-adapted soil amendment strategies and closed-loop agricultural systems in cold-arid regions.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET)-Surface Area Analysis

Nitrogen Adsorption–Desorption Analysis (BET)

The textural properties of the prepared biochar samples, including the specific surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution, were evaluated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) nitrogen adsorption–desorption technique. The measurements were performed using a surface area and porosity analyzer (Micromeritics ASAP 2020, Micromeritics Instrument Corporation, Norcross, GA, USA). Prior to the analysis, the biochar samples were degassed under vacuum at 120 °C for 12 h to remove moisture and any physically adsorbed gases that might interfere with the adsorption measurements. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were recorded at 77 K using liquid nitrogen as the cryogenic medium. The specific surface area was calculated using the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) equation within the relative pressure range of 0.05–0.30 (P/P₀), where the BET model provides reliable linear fitting for multilayer adsorption. The total pore volume of the samples was estimated from the amount of nitrogen adsorbed at a relative pressure of P/P₀ ≈ 0.99, assuming complete pore filling at near-saturation conditions. Furthermore, the pore size distribution was determined using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) method based on the desorption branch of the nitrogen isotherm. This analysis provided important information regarding the surface characteristics and porosity of the biochar, which are critical factors influencing its adsorption performance and potential applications in environmental remediation and catalysis.

Physicochemical Characterization of Biochar

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis

The surface area, pore volume, and pore size distribution of the biochar were evaluated using BET with a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 surface area and porosity analyzer. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K after degassing the samples under vacuum at 200 °C for 12 h. The specific surface area was calculated using the BET method, while pore size distribution and total pore volume were determined using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) model.

Field Emission Scanning Electron Microscope (FESEM, Carl Zeiss SIGMA)

The surface morphology and microstructural features of the biochar were investigated using a FESEM. Samples were mounted on aluminum stubs using carbon tape and sputter-coated with a thin gold layer to enhance conductivity. Imaging was carried out at accelerating voltages ranging from 5 to 15 kV at various magnifications to assess pore structure, surface texture, and particle morphology.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

The mineralogical composition and crystalline structure of the biochar samples were analyzed using XRD with a Rigaku Ultima IV X-ray diffractometer employing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å). Diffraction patterns were recorded over a 2θ range of 10–80° with a step size of 0.02° and a scanning speed of 2° min⁻¹. Phase identification and analysis were performed using Rigaku PDXL software, providing insights into the crystalline and amorphous phases present in the biochar .

Fourier Transform Infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy

The surface functional groups and chemical bonding characteristics of the biochar were examined by FTIR using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two FTIR spectrometer. Samples were prepared using the KBr pellet method and analyzed in transmission mode. Spectra were collected over the range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ at a resolution of 4 cm⁻¹ with 32 scans, enabling identification of oxygen-containing functional groups relevant to adsorption and nutrient retention .

Energy Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDS)

The elemental composition of the biochar was determined using EDS equipped with an Oxford Instruments X-Max detector integrated with the FESEM system. Analyses were conducted at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV across multiple regions (50 × 50 μm²), with 5–10 spectra collected per sample and averaged to obtain representative elemental distributions(Table 1).

Adsorption–Desorption BET Analysis of APSSBC at 300 °C

Figure 4(a) presents the nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm of apricot seed shell biochar produced at 300 °C. The adsorption increases gradually with increasing relative pressure (P/P₀), indicating limited pore development. According to the IUPAC classification, the observed adsorption behaviour resembles a Type II isotherm, typically associated with non-porous or macroporous materials where multilayer adsorption occurs on the external surface. The biochar exhibits a small adsorption uptake at low relative pressures (P/P₀ < 0.1), indicating a lack of well-developed micropores (< 2 nm)35. As relative pressure increases, multilayer adsorption becomes significant, reflecting the biochar’s macroporous nature36. Biochar produced at lower temperatures, such as 300 °C, typically has lower surface area and pore volume due to incomplete carbonization and retention of volatile compounds, which inhibit pore formation37. Higher pyrolysis temperatures (e.g., 600 °C) enhance pore development and surface area, suggesting that the 300 °C biochar is less effective for applications requiring high porosity38. Conversely, while lower pyrolysis temperatures yield biochars with limited surface area, they may retain more functional groups, which can enhance sorption of polar molecules, indicating a trade-off between surface area and chemical functionality. Consequently, the surface area and porosity of the 300 °C biochar remain comparatively low.

Adsorption–Desorption BET Analysis of APSSBC at 400 °C

Figure 4(b) shows the nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherm of apricot seed shell biochar produced at 400 °C, which exhibits higher nitrogen uptake across the entire relative pressure range compared with the 300 °C sample. The nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm of apricot seed shell biochar produced at 400 °C demonstrates enhanced nitrogen uptake compared to the 300 °C sample, indicating improved pore development and surface area due to increased carbonization. This isotherm is classified as Type IV, typical of mesoporous materials, with moderate adsorption at low pressures suggesting micropore formation and increased adsorption at higher pressures indicating capillary condensation in mesopores. The presence of a hysteresis loop confirms mesopore development, which is further supported by findings that higher pyrolysis temperatures promote structural rearrangement and volatile matter removal, enhancing pore characteristics35. Higher carbonization temperatures lead to increased BET surface area and total pore volume. Studies show that biochar produced at 400 °C exhibits a significant number of mesopores, optimizing its adsorption properties39,40.The hysteresis loop in the isotherm indicates the presence of mesopores, which are crucial for effective adsorption. The transition from micropores to mesopores is essential for the biochar’s functionality in applications like soil amendment35. While the benefits of higher pyrolysis temperatures are evident, some studies suggest that excessively high temperatures may lead to a reduction in micropore volume, potentially affecting the overall adsorption capacity of biochar35.

Adsorption–Desorption BET Analysis of SBC-300 °C

Figure 4(c) shows the nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm of soil-mixed biochar produced at 300 °C (SBC-300) indicates limited pore development and low surface area, characteristic of biochar produced at lower pyrolysis temperatures. This behavior aligns with the Type II isotherm, suggesting a predominance of macroporous structures and external surface adsorption rather than internal pore filling. The gradual increase in the adsorption branch and the small hysteresis loop further confirm the presence of limited mesopores, which are often obstructed by residual volatile compounds from incomplete carbonization36,41. SBC-300 exhibits low specific surface area, typically ranging from 1.06 to 70.22 m²/g for biochars, influenced by feedstock and pyrolysis conditions41. The total pore volume and porosity are also limited, with significant storage pores accounting for about 85% of total volume41. Nitrogen adsorption is generally lower than water vapor adsorption, indicating that nitrogen surface areas are insufficient to accommodate surface-charged groups responsible for cation exchange36.The adsorption of nitrogen is influenced by the pyrolysis temperature, with higher temperatures yielding biochars with better adsorption properties42. While SBC-300 shows limited nitrogen adsorption capacity, it is essential to consider that biochars produced at higher temperatures may exhibit enhanced adsorption characteristics, potentially improving their effectiveness as soil amendments and in nitrogen cycling processes42,43.

Adsorption–Desorption BET Analysis of APSSBC at 500 °C

Figure 4(e) illustrates the nitrogen adsorption-desorption isotherm of apricot seed shell biochar synthesized at 500 °C reveals significant insights into its porous structure. The Type IV profile with a hysteresis loop indicates the presence of mesopores, while the sharp increase in adsorption at low relative pressures suggests effective micropore filling. This behavior is indicative of multilayer adsorption and capillary condensation within mesopores, which is crucial for enhancing the material’s surface area and adsorption capacity. The biochar exhibits a well-developed pore structure, with mesopores (2–50 nm) facilitating capillary condensation35. The specific surface area can reach up to 1313 m²/g, significantly influenced by the carbonization temperature35. The observed hysteresis loop is associated with capillary condensation in slit-shaped or ink-bottle pore geometries, common in carbonaceous materials35,41. This hysteresis indicates that the biochar can effectively adsorb and release gases, enhancing its utility in environmental applications36. While the findings highlight the advantages of higher pyrolysis temperatures in developing a robust pore structure, it is essential to consider that lower temperatures may yield different adsorption characteristics, potentially beneficial for specific applications38.

Surface Morphology of Apricot Seed Shell Biochar (SEM Analysis)

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) was used to investigate the surface morphology and microstructural characteristics of apricot seed shell biochar. The SEM micrographs at different magnifications (Fig. 3a–c) provide insights into the morphological transformations occurring during the pyrolysis process.

The SEM image in Fig. 5(a) (1,100×) shows the general surface morphology of the biochar. The structure appears irregular and heterogeneous, with several macropores and cavity-like openings distributed across the surface52. These pores are likely generated due to the thermal decomposition of lignocellulosic constituents such as hemicellulose and cellulose, which release volatile gases during pyrolysis53. The escape of these gases creates voids and channels within the carbon matrix, leading to the formation of a porous structure. Additionally, the presence of surface cracks and uneven textures suggests structural shrinkage and rearrangement of the biomass framework during carbonization52.

The higher magnification SEM micrograph in Fig. 5(b) (5,000×) reveals more detailed microstructural features of the biochar surface. At this scale, micro-sized pore openings and circular cavities become clearly visible within the carbon matrix. These pores appear to be surrounded by relatively smooth carbonized walls, indicating the collapse and restructuring of the original plant cellular framework during pyrolysis54. Small particulate fragments attached to the surface may represent mineral ash residues or partially carbonized organic fragments remaining after thermal degradation. The presence of such micro-pores is important because they contribute to the development of internal pore networks that enhance surface accessibility and adsorption capacity54.

The SEM image in Fig. 5(c) (2,500×) further illustrates the heterogeneous pore distribution and surface irregularities across the biochar structure. Compared with the lower magnification image, this micrograph highlights larger interconnected pores and fissure-like openings, which may act as pathways for the diffusion of water, nutrients, or other molecules. The irregular morphology and the presence of multiple pore sizes indicate that the pyrolysis process has significantly modified the original biomass structure, transforming it into a porous carbon framework with improved structural complexity55. Pyrolysis leads to the formation of micro-pores and cavities within the carbon matrix, which are essential for creating internal pore networks that improve accessibility and adsorption54.

These structural characteristics may enhance the potential application of apricot seed shell biochar as a soil amendment and adsorbent material.

X-ray Diffraction

Figure 6(a) presents the peaks in XRD analysis at different temperature shows an amorphous nature of materials. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of biochar produced from apricot seed shells at various pyrolytic temperatures (400 °C& 500 °C) reveal significant insights into its structural characteristics and potential applications. Peaks at both temperatures show the amorphous nature of material, as corresponded by smooth peaks. Hence, the biochar produced at mentioned temperature is good for adsorption. The X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis of biochar produced from apricot seed shells at pyrolytic temperatures of 400 °C and 500 °C indicates an amorphous structure, characterized by broad peaks. This amorphous nature is beneficial for adsorption applications, as it typically correlates with a high surface area and porosity, which are critical for effective adsorption. The XRD patterns reveal broad peaks at 2θ values of approximately 25° and 43°, indicating low crystalline and an amorphous structure56. Amorphous biochar often exhibits a higher specific surface area, enhancing its adsorption capabilities. The amorphous structure allows for more active sites for adsorption, facilitating the capture of contaminants from solutions, as seen in various studies on biochar57. The minor peaks are observed, they are cautiously attributed to possible siliceous or carbonate residues, without over interpretation. No crystalline mineral phases such as quartz or metal oxides were observed at mentioned temperatures indicating that inorganic constituents are either present in low concentrations or are poorly crystalline. Further identification of inorganic phases would require complementary techniques such as ash analysis or higher-resolution XRD, which were beyond the scope of the present study.

Figure 6(b) illustrates the X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern of apricot Prunus armeniaca seed shell biochar (ASSSBC) produced at 600 °C exhibits a significant increase in specific surface area, reaching values which facilitates better adsorption characteristics58. Higher temperatures promote the development of a porous structure, enhancing the biochar’s ability to retain nutrients and water25. The XRD patterns indicate a sharper peak in the 2θ range of 22–25°, signifying enhanced structural ordering of carbon domains at 600 °C, which corresponds to the development of turbostratic graphitic (002) planes. At 600 °C, significant changes in structural parameters, such as a decrease in d002 and Lc values, indicate enhanced microcrystallite growth, which is essential for developing graphitic planes59. As temperature rises, the crystallite size increases while interplanar spacing decreases, leading to a more ordered structure60. Although complete graphitization is not achieved, the degree of graphitization is notably higher than that at lower temperatures10. The reduction in amorphous carbon and the decomposition of organic volatiles at 600 °C further enhance the biochar’s stability and reactivity20. The physicochemical properties of biochar, influenced by pyrolysis temperature, determine its suitability for various applications, including soil fertility improvement and carbon sequestration10.

Figure 6(c) shows the X-ray diffraction (XRD) corresponds to biochar derived from apricot Prunus armeniaca shells pyrolyzed at 700 °C. The pattern displays dominant crystalline features, characterized by a sharp and prominent peak in the range of 2θ ≈ 26–28°, corresponding to the (101) plane of quartz (SiO₂), a prevalent mineral in the soil composition61. An additional distinct peak observed around 2θ ≈ 55° is also characteristic of quartz and may include minor contributions from other soil minerals such as feldspar or mica56. Higher pyrolysis temperatures, such as 700 °C, enhance the crystallinity of biochar, leading to increased stability and adsorption capabilities57.

FTIR Analysis

Figure 7(a) presents the FTIR spectra of apricot shell–derived biochar (APSSBC) produced at different pyrolysis temperatures, namely 300 °C (blue line), 400 °C (green line), 500 °C (violet line), and 700 °C (yellow line). The spectra exhibit a clear temperature-dependent evolution of surface functional groups. At lower pyrolysis temperatures (300–400 °C), distinct absorption bands corresponding to hydroxyl (–OH), carboxyl (–COOH), and ether groups are observed, particularly in the 1000–1300 cm⁻¹ region, indicating the presence of abundant oxygen-containing functional groups and incomplete carbonization62,63. As the pyrolysis temperature increases to 500 °C, the intensity of these oxygenated functional groups decreases significantly due to dehydration and thermal decomposition, accompanied by the progressive development of aromatic carbon structures64. At the highest temperature (700 °C), most oxygen-containing functional groups are substantially diminished, reflecting advanced carbonization and the dominance of stable aromatic frameworks, which enhance the chemical stability of biochar65. Overall, these temperature-induced transformations demonstrate that pyrolysis conditions strongly govern the surface chemistry and functional behavior of APSSBC, thereby influencing its interactions with soil constituents and environmental pollutants63,65.

Soils blended with APSSBC

Figure 7(b) is the FTIR of biochar blended with soil sample reveal the intensity of functional groups such as –COOH and –OH decreases as the pyrolysis temperature increases beyond 600 °C. The effect of pyrolysis temperature on the functional groups in biochar and its subsequent impact on soil properties is a critical area of study. Biochar produced at 400 °C retains more –COOH and –OH groups, enhancing nutrient retention and soil fertility66. As the pyrolysis temperature increases beyond 600 °C, these functional groups decrease, leading to a decline in biochar quality for soil applications. This is because higher temperatures result in the breakdown of hemicelluloses, cellulose, and lignin, reducing the biochar’s nutrient content and altering its physical properties. At temperatures above 600 °C, these functional groups decrease, reducing effectiveness of biochar in soil applications19,66. Low-temperature biochar (≤ 400 °C) have higher water-soluble phosphorus and nitrogen, making them suitable for nutrient-deficient soils56. High temperature biochar have increased stable carbon content but lower nutrient availability, which may not be ideal for immediate soil fertility improvement19. The FTIR spectra of soil and biochar-amended soil samples exhibit absorption bands associated with mineral and organic components, including Si–O stretching vibrations around 1000–1100 cm⁻¹ and O–H vibrations related to clay minerals and soil organic matter. Changes observed in the spectra of biochar-amended soil are discussed separately from biochar FTIR and are attributed to interactions between soil minerals, native organic matter, and added biochar. The FTIR analysis indicates the presence of oxygen-containing functional groups such as hydroxyl and carboxyl-related moieties on the biochar surface. These functional groups are known to influence surface reactivity and interactions with soil components. However, the present study did not directly measure greenhouse gas emissions, including N₂O; therefore, any potential influence of biochar functional groups on N₂O emissions cannot be experimentally confirmed within this work.

In Ladakh, the use of chemical fertilizers is often limited by transportation costs and irregular supply due to the region’s remoteness. In contrast, apricot seed shells are abundantly available as agro-waste from local orchards and processing units. Converting this biomass into biochar provides a low-cost and locally accessible soil amendment that can be produced at small or community scales. While chemical fertilizers supply readily available nutrients, apricot shell biochar contributes to longer-term soil improvement by enhancing nutrient retention and soil structure, making it a practical complementary strategy for sustainable agriculture in cold desert regions. Biochar is generally recognized for its high stability in soils due to its aromatic carbon structure. In cold desert environments such as Ladakh, low temperatures and limited microbial activity may further slow biochar degradation, potentially enhancing its long-term persistence in soil. Although leaching or long-term degradation was not directly assessed in this study due to the short experimental duration, previous studies have reported that biochar can remain stable in soils for extended periods while gradually improving soil properties. The findings of this study may be applicable to other cold desert regions that share similar environmental constraints, such as low soil organic matter, coarse soil texture, and limited nutrient availability. In such ecosystems, the use of locally available biomass to produce biochar could similarly improve soil fertility and support sustainable crop production. However, the extent of the benefits may vary depending on local soil characteristics, climate conditions, and available feedstock.

Implication

Table 7; Fig. 10 (a and b) is the experimental data for spinach, Spinacia oleracea, reveals that plants showed a significant improvement in growth parameters when treated with APSSBC compared with the untreated ones. Triplicate measurements indicate that APSSBC application markedly improved spinach growth compared to the control. Plants treated with APSSBC exhibited consistently higher leaf length (18.6–19.4 cm) and leaf number (48–52) than untreated plants, which showed lower values (8.7–9.3 cm and 22–24 leaves). The low variability among replicates demonstrates good experimental consistency and confirms the positive effect of APSSBC on vegetative growth. Biochar amendments have been shown to increase spinach fresh leaf yield by 63.7% compared to control treatments without biochar27. Studies also indicate that specific ratios of biochar and compost lead to increased plant height and leaf area, with optimal combinations yielding the best results.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that apricot seed shell biochar (APSSBC), produced from locally available feedstock in Kargil–Ladakh, is an effective soil amendment for cold-arid and resource-limited agro-ecosystems. Biochar synthesized at a high pyrolysis temperature (700 °C) exhibited high surface area and porosity, abundant hydrophilic functional groups, and a predominantly amorphous carbon structure, resulting in enhanced water and nutrient retention and improved soil reactivity. These physicochemical advantages translated into significant agronomic improvements in Spinacia oleracea at a 4% (w/w) application rate under marginal soil conditions. APSSBC-treated plants showed substantially greater vegetative growth compared to untreated controls. Leaf length increased from 8.7 to 9.3 cm in control plants to 18.6–19.4 cm in treated plants, representing an approximate 105–120% increase in leaf length. Similarly, leaf number increased from 22 to 24 leaves in control plants to 48–52 leaves in treated plants, corresponding to an approximate 110–130% enhancement in leaf production. These results indicate that APSSBC application more than doubled key vegetative growth parameters of Spinacia oleracea under marginal soil conditions. All values were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3). Statistical analysis using an independent two-sample Student’s t-test confirmed that these increases were highly significant (p < 0.01), demonstrating a pronounced positive effect of APSSBC on spinach growth performance. The ability of APSSBC to function as a slow-release reservoir for water and nutrients highlights its potential to reduce fertilizer dependence, minimize nutrient losses, mineral nutrient content, surface functional groups, high porosity, and structural stability are common to biochar derived from other ligno-cellulosic agricultural residues and support sustainable agriculture in environmentally fragile regions such as Ladakh. Biochar application can contribute to climate mitigation through long-term carbon sequestration. Biochar typically contains about 60–80% stable carbon, much of which can persist in soils for decades to centuries. Converting apricot seed shell biomass into biochar therefore stabilizes carbon that might otherwise be released through decomposition, providing an additional environmental benefit alongside soil improvement. The high aromaticity and thermal stability of the biochar indicate strong persistence in soil, supporting long-term improvements in soil quality and carbon sequestration. However, biochar performance may vary with crop type, soil properties, and application rate; thus, long-term, site-specific field evaluations are recommended.
