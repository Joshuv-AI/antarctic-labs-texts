Thermal and moisture characteristics of air from a galvanized steel tubular solar air heater for potential drying applications
Authors: Hesham A. Ahmed, Ahmed E. Al-Ghaithy, Sameer A. Al-Mashriqi, Moath B. Othman, Adel Mohammed Ahmed, Saleem M. Al-Koul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Solar air heating is a promising approach for improving agricultural drying efficiency; however, few studies have comprehensively characterized the psychrometric behavior of drying air in tubular solar air heaters. This study presents an experimental investigation of a galvanized steel tubular solar air heater designed to enhance the thermal and moisture-related properties of drying air under real outdoor conditions. The novelty of this work lies in the integrated evaluation of the thermal and full psychrometric performance, including temperature, relative humidity, enthalpy, and moisture-carrying capacity, together with a systematic assessment of the effects of heater length and airflow velocity. The system was tested using three heater lengths (3, 6, and 9 tubes corresponding to 6–18 m) and airflow velocities of 1–3 m/s. The results showed that the heater length was the dominant factor affecting the performance, whereas the airflow velocity had a secondary but statistically significant influence. The maximum outlet air temperature reached 45.8 °C, and the highest percentage temperature increase was 41.3%. The relative humidity reduction reached 57.1%. The moisture-carrying capacity of the air increased significantly with heater length, reaching a maximum value of 84.4 g/kg at 12 m and 1 m/s airflow velocity, closely followed by a heater length of 18 m under the same airflow conditions (83.8 g/kg). Overall, the proposed system improved the thermodynamic state of the drying air by simultaneously increasing the temperature and reducing the relative humidity, demonstrating its suitability for solar-assisted agricultural drying in arid and semi-arid regions. The integration of solar reflectors beneath each tube is suggested as a potential improvement for further enhancing the thermal efficiency. These findings provide quantitative design guidelines for optimizing tubular solar air heaters for sustainable drying.

Introduction

Drying is a critical post-harvest operation in agriculture, as it plays a key role in reducing the moisture content of harvested crops, thereby minimizing microbial growth and post-harvest losses1. Effective drying extends the shelf life of crops and preserves their nutritional, physical, and sensory qualities2. In many developing countries, post-harvest losses can reach 30–40%3, highlighting the importance of efficient drying in enhancing food security and increasing the economic value of agricultural products4.

Conventional drying methods, especially open-air sun drying, are widely used because of their simplicity and low cost5. However, they have notable limitations, as drying rates are strongly dependent on ambient temperature, relative humidity, and natural airflow6. These methods also involve long drying times, expose products to environmental contaminants, and are highly influenced by weather conditions7. Consequently, uneven moisture removal frequently occurs, resulting in over-drying in some areas and under-drying in others8. These challenges underscore the need for alternative drying technologies that are energy-efficient, environmentally sustainable, cost-effective, and easy to implement, particularly in rural and developing regions9.

In this context, solar thermal technologies have emerged as promising solutions, providing a sustainable and efficient alternative to conventional drying methods9,10. Solar-assisted drying systems offer a renewable, environmentally friendly, and cost-effective energy source11,12. These systems enhance energy efficiency and reduce operational costs while minimizing post-harvest losses and preserving the quality of agricultural products13.

Solar drying systems are generally classified into direct and indirect configurations. Direct solar systems are simple and cost-effective; however, they often result in uneven air heating. In contrast, indirect systems improve the drying performance by separating the solar heater from the drying chamber14. Various heater designs have been investigated for indirect heating. Flat-plate heaters typically provide moderate temperatures suitable for a wide range of crops15. However, tubular or evacuated tube heaters exhibit higher thermal efficiency and a greater ability to produce air with higher thermal characteristics, making them particularly well-suited for use in enclosed drying chambers16.

In addition to the structural characteristics of solar heaters, the thermophysical properties of drying air play a crucial role in optimizing the drying performance17. Specifically, the drying air temperature, relative humidity, and airflow velocity are the primary operational parameters governing heat and mass transfer during drying processes18. These parameters directly affect the drying kinetics, energy efficiency, and quality of the final product19.

Elevated drying air temperatures enhance the vapor pressure gradient between the product surface and the surrounding air, thereby accelerating moisture diffusion20. Conversely, the relative humidity of the drying air determines the magnitude of the moisture transfer driving force; a lower relative humidity, particularly when coupled with elevated temperatures, promotes rapid and more uniform moisture removal18. Furthermore, airflow velocity significantly influences boundary layer development and internal moisture migration, ultimately determining overall drying uniformity and system efficiency21.

Despite their simple design and low cost, galvanized steel tubular solar air heaters effectively convert solar radiation into hot, dry air, making them particularly suitable for crop drying applications. These heaters are durable, corrosion-resistant, and well-suited for long-term operation in harsh agricultural environments. Their compact and modular configuration also allows for integration with auxiliary fans to enhance airflow into the drying chamber, thereby promoting uniform drying and reducing the overall drying time.

Nevertheless, systematic investigations of the thermal and moisture characteristics of drying air generated by galvanized steel tubular solar air heaters remain limited to date. Existing literature has predominantly emphasized thermal performance metrics, such as air temperature enhancement and overall system efficiency, while comparatively less attention has been devoted to the comprehensive psychrometric characterization of the drying air. In particular, key thermodynamic variables, including relative humidity, humidity ratio, enthalpy, and moisture-carrying capacity, are frequently underreported.

To bridge this research gap, this study investigates a galvanized steel tubular solar air heater designed to condition the thermodynamic state of drying air by increasing its temperature while simultaneously reducing its relative humidity. In addition to thermal enhancement, the system was evaluated in terms of heater length and airflow velocity, particularly under the operational constraints typical of rural and resource-limited settings in developing regions, thereby ensuring the practical relevance of this investigation.

Accordingly, this study presents a detailed experimental characterization of the psychrometric behavior of air produced by a galvanized steel tubular solar air heater operating under real outdoor environmental conditions. The novelty of this study lies in the integrated assessment of both thermal and psychrometric performance, together with a systematic examination of the influence of key operating parameters, namely heater length and airflow velocity, on the thermodynamic and moisture-related properties of the drying air. Furthermore, this study provides practical design and operational insights aimed at optimizing the performance of tubular solar air heaters, thereby supporting the development of sustainable agricultural drying systems in arid and semi-arid regions of low-income countries.

Materials and methods

Tubular solar air heater design

A tubular solar air heater made of galvanized steel was designed to provide hot and dry air for drying crops in an enclosed chamber (Fig. 1). The heater comprised nine tubular elements, each 2 m in length, 0.16 m in diameter, and 1.5 mm thick (Fig. 2A). The tubes were arranged in a parallel side-by-side layout with a center-to-center spacing of 0.21 m to increase the air residence time within the heater (Fig. 2B). To enhance solar radiation absorption, reduce reflection losses, and elevate the temperature of the air passing through, the tubes were coated with matte black paint. The tubular elements were connected in a serpentine configuration using flexible aluminum connectors to ensure uniform airflow through the heater.

The heater was mounted on an inclined base at an angle of 14° relative to the horizontal plane and was optimized to capture the maximum solar radiation during peak sun hours (Fig. 2B). The airflow within the galvanized steel tubular solar air heater was regulated by two fans: an inlet fan that drew in ambient cold, humid air and an outlet fan that expelled hot, dry air (Fig. 3A). Each fan (Teucer, Shenzhen Chuangliangiang E-Commerce Co., Ltd, China) had dimensions of 120 × 120 × 25 mm and provided an airflow rate of 144 m³/h (DC 12 V and rated current 0.35 A), according to the manufacturer’s specifications. Both fans were powered by a 60 W photovoltaic panel, which enabled off-grid operation and continuous airflow supply.

Although the system is powered by photovoltaic energy, the fans operate under fixed electrical specifications (DC 12 V, 0.35 A) and are equipped with electric current regulators that allow for the adjustment of the rotational speed. This configuration reduces the sensitivity of airflow to short-term variations in solar radiation intensity, particularly during low-irradiance periods, such as early morning, and maintains the operation within a controlled and stable range.

Consequently, any fluctuations in airflow are limited and are expected to have a moderate effect on the convective heat and mass transfer processes without altering the overall thermal and moisture characteristics of the drying air. Figure 3B shows the galvanized steel tubular solar air heater designed specifically for this study and integrated with the drying chamber.

Drying chamber design and configuration

The drying chamber was constructed using hollow square steel rods as the framework, providing structural stability while minimizing material weight. The chamber was enclosed with polycarbonate sheets selected for their high transparency, thermal resistance, and ability to minimize heat loss (Fig. 3D). The chamber featured a square base of 100 × 100 cm, with a height of 100 cm at the outlet side (fan side) and 75 cm at the door side, resulting in a top surface inclined at approximately 14° (Fig. 3C).

Air was supplied from the tubular air heater through a perforated horizontal pipe with a diameter of 3.8 cm, which branched into four outlets of the same diameter to ensure the uniform distribution of heated air across the drying volume (Fig. 3D). Moist air was removed via a top-mounted exhaust vent equipped with a 120 × 120 × 25 mm fan (DC 12 V and rated current 0.35 A) fitted with electric current regulators to allow precise control of the airflow (Fig. 3C). The bottom-to-top airflow configuration promotes convective heat and mass transfer, leading to uniform drying and minimization of localized moisture accumulation in the grain layer.

Experimental site and setup

The experiment was conducted from June 1 to 30, 2025, on the rooftop of the Department of Agricultural Engineering and Modern Technologies, Faculty of Agriculture, Food and Environment, at Sana’a University in Sana’a, Yemen (15°21′ N, 44°10′ E; 2,300 m above sea level). The rooftop site was deliberately selected to provide unobstructed solar exposure under actual environmental conditions. All measurements were conducted under clear sky conditions to ensure data consistency and reduce atmospheric effects. The heater was oriented toward the south and inclined at an angle approximately equal to the local latitude to achieve maximum solar radiation incidence. Continuous airflow was ensured by installing fans at both the inlet and outlet of the heater, with electrical power supplied by a solar panel system.

Experimental treatments

Three heater lengths were tested: 6 m (3 tubes), 12 m (6 tubes), and 18 m (9 tubes). For each heater length, air was drawn through the heater using exhaust fans and delivered to the drying chamber at three preset air velocities (1, 2, and 3 m/s). The experimental matrix consisted of nine treatment combinations. Each treatment was conducted over a full daytime operating period (06:00–18:00) and replicated thrice on non-consecutive days to ensure data reliability and to account for daily environmental variations.

The selected experimental ranges for the heater length and airflow velocity were established based on system design constraints and practical operating conditions. Heater lengths of 6, 12, and 18 m corresponded to the modular configuration of nine galvanized steel tubes, each 2 m long. This arrangement enabled realistic variations in the heat transfer surface area and airflow residence time while preserving the structural integrity of the system and avoiding design modifications. Furthermore, an airflow velocity range of 1–3 m/s was determined by the operational capacity of the inlet and outlet fans, which aligns with the commonly reported values in solar air heating and agricultural drying systems19.

Experimental measurements

The air temperature and relative humidity at the inlet and outlet of the galvanized steel tubular solar air heater were recorded using RC-51 H combined sensor data loggers (Technology Inc., USA) with a storage capacity of 32,000 readings. The sensors provided accuracies of ± 0.5 °C for temperature and ± 3% for relative humidity, and data were recorded at 5-min intervals throughout the experimental run.

Global solar radiation was measured using a Veinasa XS-TBQ02A pyranometer (Sichuan Veinasa Technology Co., Ltd, China), featuring a range of 0–1999 W/m2 and an accuracy of ± 10 W/m². The radiation data were logged every 5 min using a GT68R06V0G data logger (Anhui Juiie Automation Technology Co., Ltd, China). The system was calibrated prior to installation, and all measurements were exported in CSV format for subsequent analysis.

The airflow velocity at both the inlet and outlet of the heater was measured using a hot-wire anemometer (Benetech GM8903; Shenzhen Jumaoyuan Science & Technology Co., Ltd., China), with a measurement range of 0–30 m/s and an accuracy of ± 0.1 m/s. Measurements were performed at the beginning of each experimental run to ensure consistent operating conditions. During the experiments, the airflow was maintained at nominal average velocities of 1, 2, and 3 m/s, with a tolerance of ± 0.2 m/s. The anemometer probe was installed near the inlet and outlet fans of the heater. The measured values corresponded to the local air velocities at the measurement points rather than the cross-sectional average velocities within the 0.16 m diameter tube.

Uncertainty analysis

To ensure the reliability of the experimental data, an uncertainty analysis was conducted using the error propagation method described by Kline and McClintock31. Air temperature and relative humidity were measured using RC-51 H data loggers with accuracies of ± 0.5 °C and ± 3%, respectively. These were combined with statistical uncertainties obtained from repeated measurements. Statistical uncertainty was calculated as the standard error of the mean.

Statistical analysis

Experimental data collected between 08:00 AM and 04:00 PM were analyzed using SPSS version 27.0 (IBM SPSS Statistics, Chicago, IL, USA). Measurements were recorded at 5-minute intervals, yielding 97 time-series observations per experimental run. For the correlation analysis, the complete dataset was utilized, comprising 873 observations (97 observations × 9 treatments). Pearson’s correlation coefficient was employed to evaluate the relationships between the independent variables (heater length (L) and airflow velocity (V)) and the dependent variables, including outlet air temperature, absolute humidity, percentage increase in air temperature, percentage reduction in relative humidity, thermal content, moisture content, and moisture-carrying capacity. Effect sizes for the independent variables and their two-way interactions were determined according to Cohen32 and classified as trivial (< 10%), small (10–30%), moderate (30–50%), or large (> 50%). For comparative analysis, each heater length condition included three airflow velocity levels, resulting in 291 observations per heater length (97 × 3 airflow velocities). Differences among treatment means were evaluated using the least significant difference (LSD) test at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05, with n = 97 per treatment. In addition, multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to develop predictive models for the thermal and moisture characteristics of air. The relationships between the heater length (L), airflow velocity (V), and response variables were quantified, with the interaction term (L × V) included to account for the coupled effects between the design and operating conditions. The model performance was evaluated based on the regression coefficients and coefficients of determination (R²) at a significance level of p ≤ 0.05.

Percentage increase in temperature and decrease in relative humidity

The diurnal performance of the tubular air heater revealed a strong coupled response between the percentage increase in drying air temperature (∆T, %) and the percentage decrease in dry-air relative humidity (∆ϕ, %). Both parameters were significantly influenced by heater length and airflow velocity (Table 2). Heater length exhibited a significant positive correlation with ∆T, % (r = 0.51), whereas airflow velocity showed a negative correlation (r = − 0.23). The associated effect size for the heater length (47%) was substantially greater than that for airflow velocity (10%). A two-way ANOVA further indicated a statistically significant interaction between the two factors, although its magnitude was classified as trivial (3%) (Table 2).

In contrast, ∆ϕ, % was positively correlated with both heater length (r = 0.56) and airflow velocity (r = 0.44), indicating enhanced moisture removal under conditions of increased thermal input and intensified convective air exchange (Table 2). The effect size associated with the heater length was substantial (50%), whereas the airflow velocity was moderate (34%). Although the interaction between the two variables was statistically significant, its magnitude remained relatively small (16%), suggesting that the system response was primarily governed by the individual contributions of each factor.

Both ∆T, % and ∆ϕ, % followed a consistent diurnal pattern across all experimental configurations. The values increased rapidly after sunrise, reaching peak levels between 8:00 and 9:00 AM, and then gradually declined during the afternoon and evening (Figs. 8A–C and 9A–C). Lower airflow velocity (1 m/s) consistently produced the highest ∆T, % values, whereas higher velocities enhanced moisture removal, resulting in increased ∆ϕ, % but reduced temperature gain. Additionally, longer heater lengths systematically enhanced both the temperature increase and humidity reduction, reflecting improved heat transfer capacity and extended air–surface interaction time (Figs. 8D and 9D).

Thermal and moisture content

For thermal content (h), heater length showed a significant positive correlation (r = 0.45; Table 2) and a large effect size (59%). Airflow velocity was not significantly correlated with h; however, it had a statistically significant effect with a trivial magnitude (1%), indicating limited practical relevance. A two-way ANOVA revealed a statistically significant interaction between the heater length and airflow velocity, although the effect size was relatively small (19%). Averaged across the airflow velocities, increasing the heater length resulted in higher h values (Fig. 10D). The temporal variation of h showed a clear diurnal pattern, with peak values occurring around midday for all configurations (Fig. 10A–C). The maximum mean thermal content was 59.2 kJ/kg at a heater length of 12 m (6 tubes). Increasing the heater length from 6 to 18 m increased h by 10.7% (Table 7). No significant differences were observed among the airflow velocities (1–3 m/s), with an average thermal content of 55.3 kJ/kg (Table 7). Among all experimental combinations, the highest h (59.7 kJ/kg) was recorded at 12 m (6 tubes) with an airflow velocity of 2 m/s, closely followed by 59.0 kJ/kg at 18 m (9 tubes) with an airflow velocity of 1 m/s (Table 6).

Moisture carrying capacity

To elucidate the effects of heater length and airflow velocity on the moisture-carrying capacity of the drying air (∆ω, g/kg), the diurnal variation in ∆ω was systematically examined (Fig. 12). As summarized in Table 2, the heater length demonstrated a significant positive correlation with ∆ω (r = 0.55), whereas the airflow velocity exhibited a significant negative correlation (r = − 0.30). The effect size associated with the heater length (57%) was markedly greater than that of the airflow velocity (19%), highlighting its predominant influence on ∆ω. Furthermore, two-way ANOVA revealed a statistically significant interaction between the two factors; however, the corresponding effect size was trivial, indicating that the interaction had a negligible practical impact on the observed variations in ∆ω.

Building on these statistical relationships, the temporal evolution of ∆ω (Fig. 12A–C) revealed distinct diurnal patterns across all heater configurations. Specifically, ∆ω increased steadily during the morning hours, reached its maximum between 9:00 AM and 2:00 PM, and subsequently declined gradually throughout the late afternoon and evening. With respect to airflow velocity, an air speed of 1 m/s consistently yielded the highest ∆ω values, whereas 3 m/s yielded the lowest ∆ ω values. In parallel, the average temporal profiles (Fig. 12D) indicate that longer heater lengths (12 and 18 m) are consistently associated with an enhanced moisture-carrying capacity, irrespective of the airflow velocity.

Further confirmation of these trends was provided by the statistical comparisons presented in Table 7, which demonstrate that both the heater length and airflow velocity exerted significant effects on ∆ω. The maximum mean ∆ω values were 80.0 g/kg for a heater length of 12 m (6 tubes) and 75.5 g/kg for an airflow velocity of 1 m/s. Overall, extending the heater length from 6 to 18 m resulted in a 39.0% increase in ∆ω, whereas increasing the airflow velocity from 1 to 3 m/s led to a 17.8% reduction in ∆ ω.

A comparative evaluation of the experimental treatments (Table 6) further substantiated these findings. The highest ∆ω value (84.4 g/kg) was achieved with a heater length of 12 m (6 tubes) combined with an airflow velocity of 1 m/s, followed closely by 83.8 g/kg for a heater length of 18 m (9 tubes) at the same airflow velocity.

Overall, these findings demonstrate that the heater length exerted a more pronounced influence on the moisture-carrying capacity of the drying air than the airflow velocity, with optimal performance achieved at a heater length of 12 m and an airflow velocity of 1 m/s.

The moisture-carrying capacity was modeled as a function of the governing parameters to evaluate its sensitivity under different operating conditions (Table 5). The regression results showed a moderate level of agreement between the predicted and observed values, with a correlation coefficient of 65% and an R² of 0.40. This indicates that the moisture transport behavior is reasonably well captured by the developed linear model, although some deviations suggest the presence of additional influencing factors not included in the present formulation.

Discussion

Drying kinetics are fundamentally governed by the thermophysical properties of air and its capacity to remove moisture through coupled heat and mass transfer processes36. In hot-air drying systems, the air temperature, relative humidity, and airflow velocity collectively regulate the vapor pressure gradients, moisture diffusion, and boundary layer development19. Air temperature directly influences the vapor pressure difference between the material and its surroundings, whereas relative humidity controls the driving force for moisture migration from the material surface37. Airflow velocity affects the thickness of the boundary layer and the rate of convective transport. Together, these parameters determine the overall drying efficiency38. Accordingly, evaluating the combined effects of heater length and airflow velocity on both the thermal and psychrometric properties is essential for optimizing solar drying system performance.

Thermal characteristics

The results demonstrated that the heater length was the dominant parameter governing the thermal behavior of drying air. As indicated in Table 2, the heater length exhibited significant positive correlations with the outlet air temperature (To), percentage temperature increase (ΔT, %), and thermal content (h), with medium to large effect sizes (47–59%). These relatively large effect sizes confirm that the heater geometry exerts a primary and consistent influence on thermal performance.

This trend is further supported by the quantitative results in Tables 3 and 7. The percentage temperature increase (ΔT, %) increased markedly from 18.7% at 6 m to 38.7% at 12 m, followed by a slight decline to 35.2% at 18 m (Table 3). A similar pattern was observed for the thermal content, which increased from 50.7 to 59.2 kJ/kg and subsequently decreased to 56.1 kJ/kg (Table 7). These results indicate that increasing the heater length enhances the thermal gain up to an optimal range, beyond which an additional length yields diminishing thermal returns.

From a heat transfer perspective, this behavior can be attributed to the combined effects of an increased heat transfer surface area and prolonged airflow residence time within the heater channel39,40. A longer heater allows the air to absorb more solar energy, thereby increasing its temperature and thermal energy content41. However, at extended lengths, additional heat losses along the structure partially offset this gain, limiting further temperature rise42. In addition, the diurnal variations presented in Fig. 6 further support this interpretation, where the temporal evolution of the outlet air temperature reflects the dynamic balance between the solar energy input and heat loss. The progressive increase in temperature during peak solar radiation hours indicates enhanced convective heat transfer from the heated tube walls to the flowing air, whereas the subsequent decline in the evening suggests a reduction in the driving temperature gradient14. This behavior highlights the coupled role of external solar forcing and internal convective mechanisms in governing the overall heat transfer process.

This interpretation is consistent with previous studies that have identified heater length as a key parameter controlling thermal performance, while also highlighting the onset of performance saturation at larger scales43,44.

Further insights were obtained from the interaction analysis (Tables 4 and 6). Under identical airflow conditions, the thermal performances of the 12 m and 18 m configurations became comparable, indicating that the apparent superiority of the 12 m heater observed in the main effect analysis was primarily a result of averaging across the airflow velocities. This suggests that the influence of the heater length, although dominant, is not strictly linear and is moderated by the interaction effects. From a fluid–thermal interaction perspective, this behavior can be further explained by the development of thermal boundary layers along the length of the tube. As the airflow progresses, the boundary layer thickens, which may locally reduce the convective heat transfer coefficient, despite an increase in the surface area. Consequently, the net heat transfer is the result of the competing effects of surface area enhancement and boundary layer resistance45.

In addition, the serpentine tube configuration and the presence of an outlet suction fan may have contributed to the nonuniform airflow distribution along the heater length. Higher velocities in downstream sections can reduce the residence time and weaken heat transfer in the final tubes, thereby limiting the outlet air temperature despite the increased length41.

In contrast to the heater length, the airflow velocity exhibited a comparatively smaller and predominantly negative influence on the temperature increase. As shown in Table 2, the airflow velocity had a small effect size (≈ 10–12%), confirming its limited contribution relative to the heater length. This is quantitatively reflected in Table 3, where ΔT decreases from 34.6% at 1 m/s to 27.2% at 3 m/s. However, the thermal content remained nearly constant at approximately 55 kJ/kg across all velocities (Table 7), indicating that airflow velocity primarily affects the rate of heat transfer rather than the total thermal energy gained46.

Within the investigated velocity range (1–3 m/s), the negligible variation in enthalpy suggests that the heat gain per unit mass of air remains relatively stable, likely due to the sufficient residence time for thermal equilibration within the heater, particularly at longer lengths21,38. Moreover, the influence of airflow velocity is partially overshadowed by external factors, such as solar radiation intensity and ambient conditions, which play a dominant role in determining the thermal performance47. Overall, these findings confirm that the heater length is the primary structural determinant, and the airflow velocity is the secondary operational parameter. It is also important to note that the relatively large standard deviation values observed in Tables 3, 7, 4 and 6 indicate a certain degree of variability in the thermal measurements. The reported values represent daily averages, and this variability can be attributed to fluctuations in solar radiation intensity, transient ambient conditions, and possible non-uniform airflow distribution within the tubes. Despite this dispersion, the consistency of the observed trends and statistical significance of the results confirm the reliability of the overall thermal behavior.

Moisture characteristics

The thermal behavior of a system is directly reflected in its psychrometric performance. As the air temperature increases, the relative humidity decreases, whereas the moisture-carrying capacity increases, thereby enhancing the drying potential26,48.

Consistent with this relationship, heater length exhibited strong positive correlations with both the percentage decrease in relative humidity (Δϕ, %) and moisture-carrying capacity (Δω), with medium-to-large effect sizes (35–57%) (Table 2). These effect sizes confirm that the heater length plays a major role in enhancing the moisture characteristics of drying air.

This is quantitatively supported by the results in Tables 3 and 7. The percentage decrease in relative humidity increased from 40.7% at 6 m to 51.6% and 53.3% at 12 and 18 m, respectively (Table 3). Similarly, the moisture-carrying capacity increased from 54.1 to 80.0 g/kg at 12 m, followed by a slight reduction to 75.2 g/kg at 18 m (Table 7). Notably, the 12 m configuration yielded the highest moisture-carrying capacity, indicating that an excessively long heater does not necessarily improve the effectiveness of the drying air.

From a thermodynamic standpoint, this behavior is explained by the nonlinear increase in saturation vapor pressure with temperature, which reduces the relative humidity while substantially increasing the air’s capacity to retain moisture49. Importantly, the reduction in relative humidity reflects an enhancement in the moisture-holding capacity rather than actual moisture removal within the heater50. Consequently, the air exiting the heater is thermodynamically “drier,” enabling more effective moisture extraction during the drying process51. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies that reported similar trends in solar drying systems52. The diurnal patterns shown in Figs. 9 and 12 provide additional insight into the moisture-related behavior, where the reduction in relative humidity and the increase in moisture-carrying capacity closely follow the thermal trends. This confirms that moisture transfer is strongly coupled with heat transfer, as higher air temperatures enhance vapor pressure gradients and promote moisture migration53.

Airflow velocity has a complex influence on the moisture characteristics. As shown in Table 2, airflow velocity had a moderate effect size on relative humidity reduction (≈ 34%) but only small effects on moisture content and moisture-carrying capacity of the air. This is reflected in Tables 3 and 6, where Δϕ increases from 43.5% to 53.5% with increasing velocity, whereas the moisture-carrying capacity decreases from 84.4 g/kg at 1 m/s to 73.1 g/kg at 3 m/s for the 12 m heater.

This apparent contrast reflects the dual role of the airflow velocity in the drying system. Higher velocities enhance convective mass transfer and accelerate moisture removal54,55, while simultaneously reducing residence time, limiting heat absorption, and moisture capacity56. Consequently, the airflow velocity primarily regulates the rate of moisture transfer rather than the total moisture-carrying potential of the air57. The relatively small effect sizes associated with moisture parameters further confirm that airflow velocity plays a secondary role compared to heater length. Furthermore, the noticeable standard deviation values associated with the moisture parameters (Tables 3, 7, 4 and 6) reflect the inherent variability of the psychrometric processes under fluctuating environmental conditions. Variations in the inlet humidity, temperature, and solar radiation contributed to this dispersion. However, these variations did not obscure the overall increasing or decreasing trends, indicating that the system response remained consistent and reproducible.

The interaction between the heater length and airflow velocity was statistically significant (Table 2), although the corresponding effect sizes were generally small. This indicates that although coupled thermo-fluid mechanisms exist, their practical impact remains limited relative to the dominant influence of the heater geometry20,21. Therefore, optimal drying performance requires a balanced combination of sufficient residence time and effective convective transport58.

Comparison with previous studies and practical implications

The comparative analysis presented in Table 8 provides a deeper insight into the thermal and moisture performance of the proposed tubular solar air heater relative to previously reported systems. The obtained temperature rise (6.6–16.1 °C) and moisture enhancement (54.1–84.4 g/kg) fall within, and in some cases approach or exceed, the performance ranges reported for conventional and enhanced solar air heaters. Previous studies have typically documented temperature increases in the range of 10–30 °C under comparable operating conditions, which confirms that the proposed system demonstrates competitive thermal behavior, although its absolute temperature gain remains lower than that of high-performance configurations, such as finned or evacuated tube collectors16.

Despite this, the relatively high moisture removal efficiency observed in this study indicates that the drying effectiveness cannot be solely attributed to thermal gain59. However, it is strongly governed by coupled heat and mass transfer mechanisms. In this context, tubular geometry plays a critical role in increasing the effective heat transfer surface area, enhancing air–surface interaction, and promoting a more uniform thermal field along the flow direction. Moreover, the extended airflow path within the tubular configuration increases the residence time, thereby improving heat exchange and significantly enhancing the moisture-carrying capacity of the air.

Therefore, the enhanced performance can be attributed to the structural advantages of the proposed design, which remains simple and cost-effective while improving the overall thermodynamic efficiency35. Importantly, the integrated interpretation of the experimental data and statistical analysis suggests that a heater length of approximately 12 m represents an optimal design point, achieving a balance between heat gain and thermal losses. Beyond this threshold, diminishing returns were observed in both thermal and moisture performance, primarily due to cumulative heat losses and reduced temperature gradients along the flow direction. This behavior is consistent with the performance characteristics of extended surface heat exchangers operating under non-uniform thermal boundary conditions60.

Furthermore, the system demonstrated stable performance across a wide range of solar radiation levels (254–863 W/m²), indicating strong adaptability to fluctuating climatic conditions. This operational robustness is particularly important for real-world solar drying applications in arid and semi-arid regions, where solar intensity varies significantly33.

From a practical design perspective, the findings indicate that optimization should prioritize the geometric configuration over simply increasing the airflow velocity. While higher airflow rates may enhance drying kinetics, they may simultaneously reduce thermal efficiency and moisture-carrying capacity because of decreased residence time within the heater21. Therefore, the most effective operational strategy involves maintaining a moderate airflow velocity in conjunction with optimizing the heater length.

Overall, the strong agreement between the experimental observations, statistical interpretation, and established thermodynamic principles confirmed the reliability of the proposed system. The results further demonstrate that the tubular solar air heater offers a well-balanced performance, where moderate thermal gains are effectively compensated by superior moisture removal capability, making it a promising and efficient solution for sustainable agricultural drying applications in arid and semi-arid environments.

Research limitations and future research directions

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the experiments were conducted under a single climatic condition—summer clear-sky conditions in Sana’a city—which limits the generalization of the findings. Therefore, future studies should evaluate the system performance under diverse climatic and seasonal conditions. Second, the analysis focused on psychrometric air properties without direct validation through agricultural-product drying experiments. Integrating such drying trials would enable the assessment of drying kinetics, moisture content reduction, product quality, and energy efficiency under actual operating conditions. Third, the investigated airflow velocity range was limited to 1–3 m/s, thereby restricting comprehensive operational optimization; future research should explore extended velocity ranges to address this limitation. Fourth, only three discrete heater lengths were examined, which may not fully capture the optimal geometric configuration of the system for the heater length. Accordingly, subsequent studies should consider finer geometric resolutions or optimization-based design approaches. Fifth, transient effects and computational fluid dynamics (CFD)-based modeling were not included; incorporating dynamic simulations would allow a more detailed understanding of time-dependent thermofluid behavior. Finally, although dimensionless parameters such as Reynolds and Nusselt numbers are commonly used to characterize flow and heat transfer behavior, the present study focuses on experimentally observed performance indicators directly relevant to solar drying applications, namely, air temperature enhancement, relative humidity reduction, and moisture-carrying capacity. These parameters provide a practical and application-oriented assessment of the system performance under actual operating conditions. Therefore, the inclusion of detailed dimensionless and CFD-based analyses is suggested for future studies to further enhance the theoretical interpretation and generalization of the results. Techno-economic and life-cycle assessments were also beyond the scope of the present study, and future investigations should include comprehensive sustainability and economic evaluations.

Despite these limitations, the findings provide compelling evidence of the practical applicability of this system. Although direct drying experiments using agricultural products were not performed, the significant enhancement in the drying air temperature, reduction in the relative humidity, and increase in the moisture-carrying capacity collectively indicate a strong potential for efficient moisture removal in the future. These thermophysical properties are widely recognized as the primary drivers of drying kinetics and overall drying efficiency61. Consequently, the developed galvanized steel tubular solar air heater demonstrates considerable promise for future integration into solar-assisted drying systems.

Economic and feasibility analysis

The economic feasibility of the proposed galvanized steel tubular solar air heater is a decisive factor in establishing its suitability for agricultural drying applications in rural and resource-constrained environments. Unlike conventional drying technologies that rely on grid electricity or fossil fuels, the proposed system operates entirely on solar energy, thereby eliminating direct fuel consumption and ensuring a substantial and continuous reduction in operating costs.

From a construction perspective, the system is fabricated primarily from galvanized steel, a material that is both widely available and cost-effective in local markets compared to advanced solar thermal materials. In addition, the tubular configuration is deliberately simple, avoiding complex geometries, specialized components, and precision manufacturing requirements. The simplicity of this design directly translates to reduced fabrication complexity and significantly lower initial capital investment, which is a critical factor for adoption in small-scale agricultural settings.

In terms of operation and maintenance, the system exhibits a highly favorable profile owing to the absence of moving mechanical parts, except for a low-power auxiliary fan used for the air circulation. As a result, the system requires only minimal maintenance, primarily limited to periodic cleaning of the air passages and basic structural inspections. This operational simplicity markedly reduces the lifecycle costs and enhances the system reliability compared to conventional mechanical dryers, which depend on the frequent servicing of heating elements and control systems.

Compared to conventional drying systems, such as electric resistance or diesel-fired dryers, the proposed solar air heater offers a clear and inherent economic advantage through the complete elimination of fuel-related expenditures. Although a detailed quantitative cost–benefit assessment was not within the scope of this study, the removal of recurring energy costs represents a structurally significant reduction in long-term operational expenditure, particularly in regions characterized by high energy prices or an unreliable electricity supply.

From a feasibility standpoint, the system is inherently well-suited for deployment in arid and semi-arid regions with high solar irradiance, where post-harvest drying demand is substantial, and the energy infrastructure is often limited or unstable. Reliance on passive solar energy not only ensures environmental sustainability but also strengthens energy autonomy at the rural community level.

Overall, the convergence of low-cost materials, structurally simple design, minimal maintenance requirements, and complete elimination of fuel dependency provides strong and compelling evidence that the proposed tubular solar air heater is both technically robust and economically viable for sustainable agricultural drying applications in low-income and energy-constrained regions.

Conclusion

This study experimentally investigated the thermal and psychrometric performance of a galvanized steel tubular solar air heater with different heater lengths and airflow velocities. The findings indicate that the heater length is the dominant geometric parameter governing the thermodynamic conditioning of the drying air, whereas the airflow velocity plays a secondary role by primarily influencing the residence time and convective heat transfer intensity. The results further demonstrate that the system performance is governed by a coupled thermo–psychrometric mechanism, in which improvements in drying potential arise from the simultaneous increase in air temperature, reduction in relative humidity, and enhancement of the moisture-carrying capacity of the air. This coupled behavior confirms that drying enhancement is not governed by a single parameter but rather by the interaction between sensible heating and moisture transport processes. Under the tested conditions, the system achieved a maximum outlet air temperature of 45.8 °C and a peak moisture-carrying capacity of 84.4 g/kg, confirming its suitability for solar-assisted agricultural drying applications. A notable outcome of this study was the identification of a performance threshold between 12 and 18 m heater lengths. Beyond this range, additional length resulted in marginal thermal gains, indicating diminishing returns and highlighting the necessity of optimizing the balance between the heat transfer enhancement and airflow residence time. It is important to note that this investigation is limited to the evaluation of outlet air thermodynamic and psychrometric characteristics and does not include experimental validation through crop drying trials under real operating conditions. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as an assessment of the air conditioning performance rather than a direct confirmation of agricultural drying efficiency. Overall, this study provides practical design guidance for optimizing tubular solar air heaters, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions, where efficient low-cost drying technologies are essential. Future improvements may include the integration of solar reflectors beneath each tube to enhance the incident radiation absorption and further improve thermal efficiency and system performance.
