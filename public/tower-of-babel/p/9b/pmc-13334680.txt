Diagnostic Accuracy of Tests for Hymenoptera Venom Hypersensitivity: A Systematic Review and Bayesian Meta‐Analysis Protocol
Authors: Bharat Kumar, Madalyn Walsh, Amir Abidov, Melissa Swee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsHymenoptera venom hypersensitivity is a potentially life‐threatening condition that may affect up to 7.5% of adults worldwide and can result in systemic allergic reactions ranging from generalized cutaneous symptoms to severe anaphylaxis. Accurate diagnosis following a systemic sting reaction is essential to guide initiation of venom immunotherapy, a highly effective but resource‐intensive intervention. However, the diagnostic performance of commonly used tests, including venom skin testing, serum venom‐specific IgE, and component‐resolved diagnostics, varies across studies, contributing to uncertainty in clinical decision‐making. Prior evidence syntheses have largely relied on frequentist meta‐analytic approaches, which may be limited in handling sparse data and heterogeneity. This protocol describes a systematic review and Bayesian diagnostic test accuracy meta‐analysis to estimate the performance of tests for identifying IgE‐mediated Hymenoptera venom allergy in patients evaluated after systemic sting reactions.MethodsElectronic searches will be conducted in MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. Eligible studies will include diagnostic accuracy studies enrolling patients of any age evaluated after a systemic Hymenoptera sting reaction. Index tests will include venom skin testing, serum venom‐specific IgE (whole‐extract and component‐resolved diagnostics), and basophil activation testing. Study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment using QUADAS‐2 will be performed independently by two reviewers. Where sufficient comparable data are available, diagnostic accuracy will be synthesized using a Bayesian bivariate random‐effects meta‐analysis to jointly estimate sensitivity and specificity.ResultsThe review will summarize study characteristics and construct study‐level 2×2 diagnostic tables for each index test family. When feasible, pooled estimates of sensitivity and specificity with 95% credible intervals will be generated.ConclusionsThis review aims to provide clinically interpretable estimates of diagnostic test performance to support evidence‐based decision‐making and improve consistency in the evaluation of suspected Hymenoptera venom hypersensitivity.

Introduction

Hymenoptera venom hypersensitivity represents a clinically significant and potentially life‐threatening condition that affects as many as 7.5% of adults worldwide [1]. Indeed, systemic reactions to Hymenoptera stings constitute a medical emergency and range from generalized cutaneous symptoms to severe anaphylaxis with cardiovascular or respiratory compromise [2]. Although fatal sting reactions are relatively uncommon, their unpredictability and severity generate substantial anxiety for patients and families and drive healthcare utilization across emergency, outpatient, and specialty settings [3]. As a result, patients who experience systemic sting reactions are frequently referred for diagnostic evaluation to determine whether the reaction was IgE mediated and whether preventive strategies, including venom immunotherapy, are indicated.

Despite the clinical importance of accurate diagnosis, the evaluation of suspected Hymenoptera venom hypersensitivity remains complex [4]. Diagnostic testing commonly relies on venom skin testing, measurement of serum venom‐specific IgE using whole‐extract assays or component‐resolved diagnostics, and, in selected cases, basophil activation testing. However, the performance of these tests varies widely across studies, and no single test can be considered definitive in isolation [5]. This diagnostic uncertainty contributes to a critical knowledge gap: clinicians must interpret test results within a complex clinical context in which both diagnostic tests and reference standards are imperfect. While prior studies and guidelines provide valuable summaries of test characteristics, variability in study design, patient populations, and reference standards complicates the interpretation and application of these estimates in practice. Accordingly, there remains a need for approaches that synthesize available evidence while explicitly accounting for heterogeneity and uncertainty.

The consequences of this knowledge gap extend beyond individual patient care. Diagnostic uncertainty may lead to delayed or missed diagnoses, unwarranted repeat testing, inappropriate reassurance, or over‐diagnosis [6]. These outcomes have important repercussions for healthcare systems, including increased costs associated with repeated evaluations, inefficient use of specialty services, and delayed initiation of effective preventive therapies [7]. Diagnostic variability may also exacerbate disparities in care, as access to specialized testing and interpretation often depends on geography, healthcare resources, and clinician expertise.

Accurate diagnosis is especially important because venom immunotherapy is a highly effective intervention that can dramatically reduce the risk of recurrent systemic reactions and is considered potentially life‐saving for appropriately selected patients [8]. Venom immunotherapy is resource‐intensive, requires long‐term commitment from patients and healthcare systems, and carries its own risks [9]. Consequently, inappropriate initiation or withholding of venom immunotherapy has substantial clinical, ethical, and economic implications. Robust diagnostic evidence is therefore essential to ensure that this intervention is offered to patients most likely to benefit while avoiding unnecessary treatment in those who are unlikely to have clinically relevant venom hypersensitivity.

Although prior systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have examined aspects of diagnostic testing in Hymenoptera venom allergy, these syntheses have largely relied on frequentist approaches [10, 11]. Traditional frequentist meta‐analytic methods face important limitations in this setting, including difficulty handling sparse event data, instability of estimates when study sizes are small, and limited ability to model heterogeneity and correlation between sensitivity and specificity [12, 13]. These challenges are particularly pronounced in diagnostic accuracy studies of venom hypersensitivity, where sample sizes are often modest, reference standards are imperfect, and clinical heterogeneity is expected.

Bayesian diagnostic test accuracy meta‐analysis offers a principled and flexible alternative that is well‐suited to addressing these limitations. Bayesian bivariate random‐effects models allow joint estimation of sensitivity and specificity while explicitly accounting for between‐study heterogeneity and correlation [14]. These models accommodate sparse data without reliance on arbitrary continuity corrections and permit incorporation of weakly informative priors that stabilize estimation while allowing the data to drive inference [15]. Importantly, Bayesian methods enable clinically interpretable probability statements that may be more intuitive for decision‐making than conventional confidence intervals [16].

Given the clinical, economic, and societal importance of accurately diagnosing Hymenoptera venom hypersensitivity and the limitations of existing evidence syntheses, a comprehensive Bayesian diagnostic test accuracy meta‐analysis is warranted. This protocol describes the planned methods for a systematic review and Bayesian meta‐analysis evaluating the diagnostic performance of commonly used tests for identifying IgE‐mediated Hymenoptera venom allergy in patients evaluated after systemic sting reactions.

Methods

Design and Registration

This protocol has been registered on the Open Science Framework prior to study initiation to ensure transparency, prevent selective reporting, and facilitate reproducibility (https://osf.io/2jyf9/). This protocol follows the 2015 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analysis for Protocols (PRISMA‐P) guidelines (Supporting Information S1) [17].

Additionally, the systematic review will be conducted and reported in accordance with the guidelines of the Cochrane Collaboration Handbook of Systematic Reviews and the PRISMA extension for Diagnostic Test Accuracy studies (PRISMA‐DTA) (Figure 1) [18, 19].

The PRISMA 2020 flow diagram will be utilized to document the identification, screening, and inclusion of studies within the systematic review.

Objectives and PIRD

The primary objective of this review is to estimate the diagnostic accuracy, expressed as sensitivity and specificity, of venom skin testing for identifying IgE‐mediated Hymenoptera venom allergy in patients evaluated after a systemic sting reaction. Secondary objectives include (1) estimating the diagnostic accuracy of serum venom‐specific IgE testing (including whole‐extract assays and component‐resolved diagnostics), (2) exploring the performance of basophil activation testing when sufficient data are available, and (3) examining sources of heterogeneity in diagnostic accuracy estimates.

The guiding PIRD (Population–Index test–Reference standard–Diagnosis) question is: In all patients evaluated after a systemic reaction to a Hymenoptera sting, what is the diagnostic accuracy of venom skin testing, venom‐specific IgE testing (including component‐resolved diagnostics), or basophil activation testing for identifying IgE‐mediated Hymenoptera venom allergy, using a clinically defined reference standard?

Search Strategy

Electronic searches will be conducted in MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials. Search terms will combine controlled vocabulary and free‐text keywords related to Hymenoptera stings, venom allergy, diagnostic testing, and systemic allergic reactions. Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews will be screened to identify additional eligible studies. Full search strategies and search dates are reported in Supporting Information [Link], [Link].

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible studies will include randomized controlled trials, prospective cohort studies, retrospective cohort studies, and cross‐sectional diagnostic accuracy studies. Case reports and case series enrolling fewer than five participants will be excluded, as will narrative reviews, editorials, commentaries, and animal or in vitro studies. This threshold was selected to balance inclusion of the limited available diagnostic accuracy literature in this field with the need to exclude very small reports unlikely to contribute stable estimates; given the relatively small size of many venom allergy studies, a higher threshold (e.g., 30 participants) was considered but not adopted to avoid excluding a substantial proportion of the evidence base.

Studies evaluating patients of any age evaluated after a reported systemic reaction to a Hymenoptera sting will be included. For studies with patients diagnosed with mastocytosis or another mast cell disorder, results will be included and examined as a prespecified subgroup when data permit.

Index tests of interest include venom skin testing (skin prick testing and intradermal testing), serum venom‐specific IgE testing using whole extracts, component‐resolved diagnostic testing, and basophil activation testing. Studies reporting multiple index tests will contribute data separately for each test when extractable. The primary reference standard will be a clinically defined diagnosis of IgE‐mediated Hymenoptera venom allergy as determined by the study authors. Of note, this reference standard is typically based on clinical history rather than prospective sting challenge testing, which is rarely performed in routine practice [20]. As such, the reference standard in most studies represents an imperfect but pragmatic clinical standard rather than a definitive biological gold standard [21]. This review will estimate the agreement between diagnostic test results and clinically assigned diagnoses as reported in the literature, rather than the true predictive value of these tests for future sting reactions.

Study Selection

Two reviewers (MW and BK) will independently screen titles and abstracts for potential eligibility. Full‐text articles of potentially relevant studies will be retrieved and assessed for inclusion based on predefined eligibility criteria. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion and consensus or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer (AA). Reasons for exclusion at the full‐text stage will be documented.

Data Extraction

Data extraction will be performed using a standardized and piloted extraction form implemented in Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation; Redmond, Washington). Study‐level data will include publication details, country, clinical setting, study design, population characteristics, age group, venom type, inclusion of mastocytosis or elevated baseline tryptase, and timing of diagnostic testing relative to the sting when reported.

Index test–level data will be extracted separately for each diagnostic modality and will include test methodology, venom tested, positivity thresholds as defined by study authors, and diagnostic accuracy data in the form of true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives on a per‐patient basis. Where reported, specific thresholds for serum venom‐specific IgE (e.g., 0.10 kU/L vs 0.35 kU/L) will be extracted to allow exploration of threshold‐related variability in diagnostic performance. Whole‐extract serum IgE testing and component‐resolved diagnostics will be extracted and analyzed separately. Basophil activation testing will be extracted as an exploratory modality.

Assessment of Methodological Quality and Risk of Bias

Risk of bias and applicability concerns will be assessed independently by two reviewers (BK and MW) using the QUADAS‐2 (Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies‐2) tool, which evaluates patient selection, index test conduct and interpretation, reference standard, and flow and timing. Disagreements will be resolved by consensus with a third reviewer (AA).

Data Synthesis

Extracted study characteristics will be summarized descriptively, including study design, population characteristics, clinical setting, venom type, index test methodology, and reference standard definitions. Where possible, 2×2 diagnostic tables (true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives) will be constructed for each index test family based on reported study data.

Quantitative synthesis using meta‐analysis will be conducted if sufficient data are available and studies are considered sufficiently comparable in terms of population, index test, and reference standard. In cases where quantitative synthesis is not appropriate due to limited data availability or substantial clinical or methodological heterogeneity, findings will be summarized narratively.

Where meta‐analysis is feasible, pooled estimates of diagnostic accuracy will be generated for each index test family. Post‐test probabilities may be calculated across a range of clinically plausible pre‐test probabilities to support interpretation of diagnostic performance in clinical decision‐making contexts. Potential meta‐bias, including small‐study effects and publication bias, will be explored through comparison of study‐level estimates and model‐based assessments of asymmetry when sufficient studies are available.

Data Analysis

When quantitative synthesis is deemed appropriate, diagnostic accuracy data will be analyzed using a Bayesian bivariate random‐effects meta‐analysis. This modeling framework allows joint estimation of sensitivity and specificity for each diagnostic test family while accounting for between‐study variability arising from differences in patient populations, clinical settings, and testing methodologies. Joint modeling is essential in diagnostic test accuracy studies because sensitivity and specificity are interdependent measures; improving sensitivity may reduce specificity, and vice versa.

For each included study, the reported numbers of true positives, false positives, false negatives, and true negatives will be used as the basis for analysis. Study‐level 2×2 diagnostic tables will be used to ensure that all relevant information contributes to pooled estimates.

Weakly informative prior assumptions will be specified to stabilize estimation while allowing the data from included studies to drive inference. Between‐study variability will be explicitly modeled to reflect the expectation that true test performance may differ across clinical settings rather than assuming homogeneity across studies.

Posterior estimates of sensitivity and specificity will be generated using simulation‐based Bayesian methods. Model convergence and performance will be assessed using standard diagnostic procedures, including evaluation of trace plots, effective sample sizes, and R‐hat statistics. Posterior predictive comparisons will be performed to evaluate model fit.

Because the reference standard in most included studies is expected to be based on clinically defined diagnoses rather than prospective sting challenge testing, the primary analysis will estimate agreement between diagnostic test results and clinically assigned diagnoses. Although a Bayesian bivariate model does not correct for bias introduced by an imperfect reference standard, it enables explicit modeling of uncertainty and between‐study heterogeneity and supports probabilistically coherent inference under such conditions. Prespecified subgroup analyses will be conducted when sufficient data are available to examine differences in diagnostic performance by venom type, age group (pediatric vs. adult), diagnostic modality, reference standard used, and inclusion of patients with mastocytosis. Additional subgroup analyses will explore differences by sex (when reported) and by diagnostic thresholds, particularly for serum venom‐specific IgE. Where feasible, analyses will also consider variation in reference standard definitions, including studies incorporating sting challenge testing versus those relying on clinical history alone.

To further assess the impact of imperfect reference standards, exploratory sensitivity analyses will be conducted, where data permit, to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates under alternative assumptions regarding potential misclassification in clinically defined diagnoses. These analyses will use plausible prior distributions to represent uncertainty in the sensitivity and specificity of the reference standard, allowing assessment of how such uncertainty may influence estimates of index test performance.

Sensitivity analyses will be performed to evaluate the robustness of pooled estimates to alternative modeling assumptions and exclusion of studies at high risk of bias. All analyses will be conducted using Python with established probabilistic programming tools.

Discussion

This systematic review and Bayesian meta‐analysis have the potential to provide the most comprehensive and methodologically rigorous synthesis to date of diagnostic test performance in Hymenoptera venom hypersensitivity. This analysis is expected to yield more stable and clinically interpretable estimates than prior frequentist syntheses because it jointly models sensitivity and specificity while explicitly accounting for between‐study heterogeneity, correlation, and uncertainty. In doing so, this study is positioned to provide a structured synthesis of how commonly employed diagnostic tests align with clinically defined venom allergy across diverse study settings.

Importantly, this is not designed to establish the absolute predictive value of diagnostic tests for future sting reactions, which would require prospective validation using sting challenge data that are rarely available. Instead, the analysis characterizes the extent to which commonly employed diagnostic tests align with clinically defined diagnoses across diverse study settings, reflecting real‐world diagnostic frameworks in which both clinical history and testing contribute to decision‐making.

By systematically aggregating evidence across diverse clinical settings, populations, and testing strategies, this review will provide a clearer empirical foundation for interpreting diagnostic test results in real‐world practice. Rather than relying on single studies with limited sample sizes or highly variable methodologies, clinicians and guideline developers will have access to pooled, probabilistically coherent estimates of test performance. This is particularly important in venom allergy, where individual studies are often small, observational, and dependent on imperfect reference standards. The Bayesian framework used here is specifically designed to handle these limitations, allowing uncertainty to be quantified rather than obscured.

Moreover, more precise estimates of sensitivity and specificity for venom skin testing, serum venom‐specific IgE (including component‐resolved diagnostics), and basophil activation testing can reduce diagnostic ambiguity at the point of care. By providing pooled estimates of diagnostic test performance within the context of existing clinical diagnostic frameworks, this review may help inform interpretation of test results alongside clinical history, recognizing that sensitization and clinically meaningful allergy are not synonymous. The findings are intended to support clinical reasoning rather than to provide definitive prediction of future sting outcomes.

At a systems level, the findings of this review may also inform more efficient use of healthcare resources. If certain diagnostic approaches are shown to have consistently higher accuracy, these could be prioritized in clinical pathways, potentially reducing unnecessary repeat testing, shortening diagnostic timelines, and decreasing reliance on less reliable or more costly investigations. Conversely, identifying tests with limited diagnostic value could help prevent low‐yield evaluations that contribute to healthcare waste without improving patient outcomes.

This work may also have implications for equity in care. Variability in access to specialized testing and expert interpretation currently contributes to geographic and socioeconomic disparities in venom allergy evaluation. Clarification of which tests perform best across a broad range of settings may support more standardized diagnostic strategies that are less dependent on local expertise or resource availability. Such standardization has the potential to narrow gaps in care for patients in rural, underserved, or resource‐constrained environments who currently face longer diagnostic delays or inconsistent management recommendations.

Nevertheless, this review will remain constrained by the quality and consistency of the underlying literature. A key limitation of this review is the reliance on clinically defined reference standards, which in most studies are based on retrospective assessment of prior sting reactions rather than prospective sting challenge testing. This limitation is inherent to the existing literature and reflects both ethical and practical barriers to conducting challenge‐based studies in venom allergy. Consequently, pooled estimates derived from this analysis will reflect agreement with clinical diagnoses rather than true prospective predictive value. This distinction is critical for interpretation and underscores the importance of integrating diagnostic test results with careful clinical assessment in practice.

Additionally, variability in reference standards, testing thresholds, and patient selection across studies may limit the precision of pooled estimates. These limitations will be transparently addressed through prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses, but they underscore the need for more standardized diagnostic research in Hymenoptera venom allergy.

Conclusion

This protocol describes the planned systematic review and Bayesian diagnostic test accuracy meta‐analysis of tests used to identify IgE‐mediated Hymenoptera venom allergy in patients evaluated after systemic sting reactions. The findings are intended to strengthen the evidence base for diagnostic evaluation and support more consistent, equitable, and effective care for patients at risk of recurrent venom‐induced anaphylaxis, while acknowledging the inherent uncertainty that characterizes clinical decision‐making in venom hypersensitivity.
