Proteolysis at the extracellular matrix interface: Molecular architects and regulators in health and disease
Authors: David Jurnečka, Matija Hrovatin, Bor Bokalj, Boris Turk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a dynamic and multifaceted scaffold that orchestrates tissue architecture, signalling and cell behaviour in multicellular organisms. Its function arises from the interplay between composition, three‐dimensional organisation and mechanics, together with the tightly regulated activity of proteolytic enzymes and cell surface receptors. Cluster of differentiation (CD) molecules function as regulators at the cell–matrix interface by directing adhesion, migration, immune surveillance and the localisation of proteases within pericellular niches. Cathepsins, along with matrix metalloproteinases, ADAM and ADAMTS sheddases, orchestrate ECM turnover, basement membrane and interstitial remodelling, and the generation of bioactive fragments that influence cell fate and signalling. Disruption of these networks shifts tissues towards fibrosis, defective development, chronic inflammation, vascular pathologies and cancer. This review integrates foundational principles with recent discoveries to map the molecular architects and regulators of the matrix in health and disease, emphasising how composition and mechanics interact with protease‐driven turnover and receptor‐mediated sensing. Finally, we outline emerging therapeutic strategies that restore matrix balance through targeted modulation of protease activity, engineering of ECM‐mimetic scaffolds with programmable mechanics and degradability, and tuning of CD‐mediated signalling to promote regeneration and resolution.

Introduction

The extracellular matrix (ECM) is a dynamic, three‐dimensional network of macromolecules that occupies the spaces between cells and tissues. It is not a passive scaffold but an active, communicative milieu that regulates cell behaviour, tissue architecture and organ function. The ECM is synthesised by resident fibroblasts, epithelial cells and smooth muscle cells and is continuously remodelled through a balance of synthesis, assembly, modification and degradation. These processes enable tissues to grow, repair and adapt to changing mechanical and biochemical demands over time [1, 2, 3, 4].

The ECM network is organised into two functional compartments: the interstitial matrix, which supports cells and facilitates diffusion across tissues, and the basement membrane, a specialised sheet‐like structure beneath epithelia that envelops specific vessels and organs. Although both compartments employ similar molecular components, their overall organisation differs to fulfil distinct tissue‐specific functions [5]. The interstitial matrix comprises fibrillar collagens and elastin fibres associated with proteoglycans, forming an open network that permits molecular diffusion. In contrast, the basement membrane is enriched in type IV collagen and laminin networks, together with nidogen and heparan sulphate proteoglycans such as perlecan, establishing a selective barrier and signalling platform [6, 7, 8].

The mechanical properties of stiffness, viscoelasticity and poroelasticity primarily stem from lysyl oxidase‐mediated crosslinking (LOX/LOXL) and other enzymatic processes regulated via cellular mechanoreceptors mechanical [9]. The signalling environment of the ECM is sensed through cell surface receptors, most notably integrins [10], discoidin domain receptors [11, 12], syndecans [13] and cluster of differentiation (CD) receptors [14, 15], enabling cells to sense biochemical and mechanical cues that direct intracellular responses controlling adhesion, migration, proliferation, survival, and differentiation [10, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

Additionally, the ECM serves as a reservoir for growth factors (e.g. TGF‐β, FGF and VEGF), which bind to proteoglycan‐sulphated chains or latent complexes [21, 22]. Their bioavailability is regulated by mechanical strain and proteolysis, processes that are fundamental to ECM remodelling and degradation [20, 23, 24, 25]. Matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) and related Disintegrin and Metalloprotease (ADAM) and ADAMs with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS) degrade collagens, fibronectin, elastin, proteoglycans and cell surface adhesins [26, 27, 28], while cysteine cathepsins and serine proteases (plasmin, uPA, neutrophil elastase) complement this activity within extracellular and pericellular compartments [29, 30, 31]. These enzymes facilitate morphogenesis and wound repair by opening migratory tracks, releasing or activating matrix‐bound factors, shedding receptors and ligands, and generating bioactive fragments that modulate angiogenesis and immune responses [32].

Spatial and temporal control of protease activity is achieved through zymogen activation mediated by hemopexin or thrombospondin motifs, and regulation by endogenous inhibitors, including TIMP family members for MMPs and ADAMs [28, 33, 34], cystatins [35], thyropins and serpins for cathepsins [25, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40]. When the balance between deposition, crosslinking and degradation is disrupted, tissues develop pathological states such as cancer invasion and metastasis, aneurysm formation, arthritis or chronic inflammation [27, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45, 46].

While proteolysis at the level of the ECM has been extensively highlighted, the specific interplay between proteases and CDs within this context remains comparatively underexplored. This review synthesises fundamental concepts and contemporary findings to offer a cohesive, health‐relevant perspective on the ECM, with particular emphasis on proteases, especially on the cysteine cathepsins as one of the pivotal mediators of pericellular and matrix remodelling. In addition to outlining ECM composition and biophysical properties, we examine how cathepsins contribute to ECM turnover, processing of matrix components and generation of matrikines. A special focus is placed on CD‐driven cell–matrix interactions modulating cathepsin localisation, activation and substrate specificity. By integrating insights on ECM composition, mechanical cues and protease networks, the review highlights how cathepsin and metalloprotease activity intersects with CD‐mediated signalling to influence tissue homeostasis, inflammation and disease progression, and it discusses therapeutic implications targeting cathepsin activity, CD regulation and their crosstalk.

Composition of the ECM

The ECM is a highly organised composite whose protein constituents confer structural integrity, biomechanical resilience and signal‐regulatory capacity to tissues. ECM composition is highly tissue‐specific: Bone ECM is mineralised and rich in type I collagen for rigidity, cartilage contains aggrecan and type II collagen for compression resistance, brain ECM is dominated by hyaluronan and proteoglycans like brevican for neural plasticity, while basement membranes are defined by laminin and type IV collagen networks for filtration and polarity. Interstitial matrices are rich in fibrillar collagens and fibronectin for tensile strength, and elastic matrices are dominated by elastin and fibrillin for reversible deformation. Collectively, these proteins and their regulators comprise the ‘matrisome’, which includes core structural proteins (collagens, elastin–microfibril assemblies, proteoglycan core proteins and adhesive glycoproteins) and associated factors such as modifiers, remodelling enzymes and ECM‐bound growth factors [5, 47, 48] (Fig. 1).

Structural composition of the ECM. Schematic overview of the ECM and its major parts. Key fibrous components include collagens, elastin, laminin and fibronectin. Proteoglycans, along with other polysaccharides, produce a hydrated, gel‐like matrix and help organise the structure by linking collagen fibrils to form larger fibres. Integrin heterodimers, shown as primary transmembrane receptors, form focal adhesions by binding to specific ECM ligands (e.g. fibronectin's RGD motif, laminins). This critical link facilitates outside‐in signalling for cell adhesion, migration and survival, and inside‐out signalling for matrix remodelling and mechanotransduction.

Collagens constitute the principal load‐bearing framework in most interstitial matrices. Fibrillar collagens assemble into hierarchically organised fibrils whose tensile strength and viscoelastic properties are determined by enzymatic crosslinks formed by the lysyl oxidase family [49]. Increasing crosslink density stiffens the matrix and enhances integrin–FAK signalling and downstream mechanoeffectors such as YAP and TAZ, thereby biasing cells towards more contractile phenotypes and glycolytic metabolism [50, 51, 52]. Among these, collagens I and II are particularly abundant in most tissues, whereas basement membranes are defined by laminin isoforms and type IV collagen rather than fibrillar collagens. Collagen biosynthesis is tightly regulated through post‐translational modifications, including prolyl and lysyl hydroxylation and specific glycosylation of hydroxylysines, which control triple‐helix stability, fibril assembly and ligand engagement [53]. With ageing, nonenzymatic glycation introduces advanced glycation end product (AGE) crosslinks (e.g. pentosidine) that further stiffen fibrils, reduce plasticity and alter binding interfaces for integrins and other ligands [54, 55, 56, 57].

Fibronectin functions as a provisional scaffold that nucleates matrix organisation and serves as an instructive ligand for cell receptors. Cells assemble soluble fibronectin into fibrils via α5β1 and αv‐class integrins, creating an RGD‐rich (Arg‐Gly‐Asp) network that directs adhesion, migration and differentiation while templating collagen fibrillogenesis [58]. Splice variants add further regulatory nuance. The extra domain A isoform, which is upregulated during remodelling, modulates both integrin and innate immune pathways, illustrating how ECM composition provides context‐specific signals beyond structural support [59, 60, 61].

Laminins organise basement membranes, establishing a specialised, sheet‐like scaffold that instructs epithelial and endothelial cells and maintains stem cell niches. Laminins 511 and 521, acting through α6β1 integrins and in concert with collagen IV, nidogen and perlecan, promote stemness and quiescence while maintaining a selective permeability barrier [62, 63]. The specific laminin isoforms, such as laminin‐111 during development and laminin‐511/521 in adult tissues, and the accompanying collagen IV variants define niche identity and influence cell fate decisions [64, 65, 66].

Proteoglycans and their glycosaminoglycan (GAG) chains govern hydration, tissue spacing and growth factor bioavailability. A key family of cell surface heparan sulphate proteoglycans are the syndecans, which act as coreceptors that synergize with integrins to transduce mechanical and chemical signals from the ECM, influencing cell adhesion, migration and proliferation [13]. Syndecan‐1 regulates epithelial barrier function and wound healing, syndecan‐2 promotes cytoskeletal organisation and neural development, syndecan‐3 is involved in neuronal growth and muscle differentiation, and syndecan‐4 enhances focal adhesion formation and mechanotransduction [67]. Other heparan sulphate proteoglycans such as perlecan and agrin bind and present FGF, VEGF and PDGF, thereby shaping gradients and receptor activation kinetics [68]. Small leucine‐rich proteoglycans, including decorin and biglycan, modulate collagen fibrillogenesis and signal through receptors such as EGFR and toll‐like receptors (TLR). Decorin also sequesters TGF‐β, fine‐tuning cytokine activity at the matrix interface [69]. Age‐ and context‐dependent changes in GAG sulphation patterns and core‐protein expression proportionally reconfigure these signalling landscapes [70].

The elastic fibre system provides resilience and recoil. Tropoelastin, the soluble precursor to elastin, is a highly hydrophobic and self‐assembling molecule which domain structure, including alternating hydrophobic and crosslinking domains, facilitates coacervation and alignment prior to crosslinking. Its self‐assembly is entropy‐driven and critical for the formation of functional elastic networks. It is crosslinked by lysyl oxidase to form desmosine‐containing networks that impart extensibility, while fibrillin‐rich microfibrils organise elastin deposition and contribute to surface mechanobiology [71]. Associated proteins such as fibulins stabilise the assembly and fine‐tune its mechanical behaviour. With ageing, fragmentation of elastin and microfibrils diminishes compliance and shifts load bearing towards collagen, amplifying stiffness‐dependent signalling [72, 73].

Latent TGF‐β–binding proteins (LTBP1–4) position growth factors within the ECM and couple their activation to matrix mechanics and proteolysis. LTBPs tether the latent TGF‐β complex to fibronectin and fibrillin microfibrils, storing cytokine reservoirs in defined microenvironments [74]. Activation may be triggered by proteases or by integrins such as αvβ6 and αvβ8, which apply force to the latent complex, releasing active TGF‐β and linking tissue tension to fibroblast, epithelial and immune cell phenotypes [7, 75, 76].

Proteolytic remodelling of the ECM generates matrikines, bioactive fragments that signal independently of the parent scaffold. Endostatin (from collagen XVIII) and tumstatin (from collagen IV) are canonical examples that modulate angiogenesis, metabolism and survival through interactions with endothelial integrins and other receptors, demonstrating that matrix turnover produces instructive cues rather than merely degrading structure [77, 78].

Post‐translational modifications, crosslinking chemistry and controlled proteolysis together define the mechanical context and the distribution of ligands and growth factors [79, 80]. Cells interpret these cues through integrins, syndecans and growth factor receptors, adjusting cytoskeletal tension and transcriptional programmes via signalling pathways such as FAK/Src and YAP/TAZ [81, 82]. Over the life course, enzymatic crosslinking and age‐related glycation progressively stiffen matrices, remodel ligand affinity and alter growth factor release, aligning ECM mechanics with shifts in cellular metabolism and state [46, 50, 54, 56].

Mechanical cues associated with the ECM

Cells actively interpret their mechanical environment through an integrated suite of biophysical cues from the ECM. This process is initiated upon sensing substrate stiffness, which calibrates cellular tension and guides differentiation. However, this foundational signal is refined by additional structural properties: topography directs cell polarity and migration, while porosity constrains or permits cellular movement and infiltration. Beyond structure, cellular responses are modulated by dynamic factors such as extracellular pH, which regulates enzyme activity and ligand binding, and viscoelasticity, which determines how the matrix yields to cellular forces. Cells also generate traction forces to probe their surroundings and respond to fluid flow, which imposes shear stress and chemotactic signals. Together, these mechanical inputs form a cohesive language that cells integrate to orchestrate their behaviour, fate and tissue organisation [2, 9, 83].

Tissues range from very soft (brain) to very stiff (bone), and cells adjust accordingly by reorganising focal adhesions, tuning actomyosin tension and even altering fate through metabolic reprogramming. In mesenchymal stem cells, stiffness steers neurogenic, myogenic or osteogenic differentiation. Integrin clustering and downstream nuclear localisation of FAK–Src, RhoA–ROCK and YAP/TAZ scale with rigidity [50, 52, 84, 85, 86]. Yet cells sense more than stiffness alone. Ligand tethering to the network, stress relaxation and plasticity also strongly modulate cellular responses. Weakly tethered ligands can blunt rigidity signalling, whereas viscoelastic and stress‐relaxing matrices allow cells to spread and differentiate even when the elastic modulus is low [87, 88, 89].

Topography represents a fundamental element that combines nanoscale ligand spacing with fibre diameter, curvature, micron‐scale alignment and roughness features that act above bulk mechanical properties. Cells align and migrate along oriented collagen fibres or engineered ridges through contact guidance, which promotes adhesion maturation, cytoskeletal organisation and directional migration in contexts such as axon growth, smooth muscle positioning and tumour invasion through stromal paths [90, 91]. The geometry of adhesions and local membrane curvature further regulate signalling by modulating the molecular clutch that couples integrins to actin. The three‐dimensional structure also defines porosity, that is, the size and connectivity of pores that govern how cells and solutes move through the network. When pore sizes approach or fall below nuclear dimensions, cells face several options: switch to protease‐dependent remodelling, deform the nucleus at energetic cost or adopt bleb‐based, high‐pressure migration. Additionally, the organisation of pores, together with the ability of cells to interconnect them, influences molecular transport and chemokine gradient formation, while matrix degradability determines whether cells invade by proteolytic path‐clearing or by squeezing through existing channels. The biochemical composition overlays and interacts with all physical constraints [92, 93].

Another factor influencing these cues is extracellular pH, which varies across tissues and pathological states. For instance, tumours, inflamed tissues and bone resorption lacunae tend to be more acidic [94]. Acidification alters protonation of adhesive motifs, charge density and the swelling of sulphated glycosaminoglycans, as well as integrin–ligand affinities and protease activity [95, 96]. Cysteine cathepsins are optimally active and stable in acidic environments, whereas many MMPs function best near‐neutral pH [97, 98]. Intriguingly, certain MMP activities, such as matrilysin‐mediated (MMP‐7) proteolysis and MMP‐9‐driven gelatin degradation, are actually enhanced under moderately acidic conditions. This complementary pH dependence allows the acidic microenvironment to simultaneously regulate the activation and substrate preferences of both cathepsins and MMPs, thereby promoting their synergistic action in ECM degradation and the modulation of receptor signalling [99, 100]. In pathological settings marked by sustained acidification, often driven by high metabolic rates and hypoxia, these coordinated shifts in cathepsin and MMP activity contribute to accelerated matrix remodelling and altered protease‐receptor crosstalk [101, 102].

Beyond elasticity, the microenvironment exhibits viscosity and viscoelasticity, which dissipate forces and determine how rapidly the material yields and deforms. When hydrogels relax stress rapidly, cells can strengthen adhesions, spread more extensively and more readily adopt osteogenic fates. Cells actively probe these time‐dependent properties through rhythmic contractions and adjust traction magnitude according to the material's viscous component [88, 89, 103].

At the cell–matrix interface, traction forces generated by non‐muscle myosin II engage integrin catch bonds, unfold talin to expose vinculin‐binding sites and reinforce adhesions. This feedback enables cells to migrate towards stiffer regions (durotaxis) and organises protrusion formation [104, 105, 106, 107]. These forces integrate with tissue‐scale mechanics, as collagen networks transmit directional stresses that guide collective cell behaviour, while balanced tension and compression maintain structural integrity. In cartilage, compression elicits responses through pressure‐sensitive ion channels, and cyclic mechanical loading regulates bone remodelling and cartilage development. Overall, mechanical cues ranging from molecular bonds to tissue stresses orchestrate cellular behaviour and tissue organisation [9, 108, 109].

Fluid flow adds another layer of regulation. Endothelial cells interpret the magnitude and spatiotemporal pattern of shear stress: steady laminar flow induces an atheroprotective phenotype through a mechanosensory complex spanning integrins, PECAM‐1, and VE‐cadherin that activates KLF2 and eNOS, whereas disturbed flow promotes inflammation and permeability [110, 111]. In interstitial spaces, slow fluid flow shapes chemokine gradients, promotes ‘autologous chemotaxis’ towards draining lymphatics, and influences morphogenesis and metastasis [112, 113]. Primary cilia and the endothelial glycocalyx contribute to shear sensing and convert drag into biochemical signals [114, 115, 116, 117, 118].

All these cues converge through mechanotransduction pathways that span molecular, cellular and nuclear scales. Collectively, stiffness, topography, porosity, pH, viscosity, tractions, load and flow compose a multiplexed code that cells interpret and modify through dynamic reciprocity, ensuring that structure and signalling remain interwoven across development, homeostasis and disease.

Cathepsins

Among the cathepsins, the best known are the cysteine cathepsins, including cathepsins B, L, S, K, V, F, C, X, H, O and W in humans. In mice, the situation differs slightly: mouse cathepsin L resembles human cathepsin V, whereas there is no direct cathepsin V orthologue. In addition, mice possess several placental cathepsins that are absent in humans [119]. Aspartyl cathepsins D and E, and the serine cathepsin G are also known, but they will not be discussed further here.

Cysteine cathepsins are now recognised as versatile mediators of ECM remodelling and degradation across development, homeostasis and disease. Although classically confined to acidic lysosomes, multiple trafficking routes deliver cathepsins to the pericellular space. These routes include regulated exocytosis of secretory lysosomes, targeting to invadopodia, binding to cell surface heparan sulphate proteoglycans and packaging into extracellular vesicles, which together enable focused proteolysis at the cell–matrix interface [25, 31, 120].

They are synthesised as zymogens with N‐terminal propeptides that maintain latency until proteolytic or physicochemical cues trigger activation. Following endo‐lysosomal trafficking, propeptides are removed by autocatalysis or by resident proteases under acidic conditions. Once activated, they are small enzymes of approximately 30 kDa, consisting of two domains with the active‐site Cys located in the N‐domain and the active‐site His in the C‐domain. In the active form, Cys and His form a thiolate‐imidazolium ion pair with pKa values of approximately 4 and 8.5, respectively, a broad range ensuring enzymatic activity under diverse conditions, including extracellular environments. A major limitation, however, is their stability at neutral pH, as most cathepsins, except cathepsin S, are rapidly inactivated and unfolded at the near‐neutral pH characteristic of the extracellular milieu. Nevertheless, local microdomain acidification, binding to glycosaminoglycans or other negatively charged surfaces, and substrate‐assisted catalysis expand their pH tolerance and enable activation and activity at or near‐neutral pH in specific niches, such as inflammatory, tumour or osteoclastic microenvironments [29, 38, 121]. Their substrate repertoire spans fibrillar and network‐forming collagens, laminins, fibronectin, elastin, nidogen, perlecan, decorin, biglycan and aggrecan, allowing both bulk turnover and precise topological remodelling of matrix architectures [31, 122].

Regulation of cathepsin activity is multilayered. Transcriptional activation provides the first checkpoint, followed by zymogen latency. In addition, several cathepsins, including cathepsins B, L, S and K, are sensitive to oxidation, linking redox status to protease control. Endogenous inhibitors of the cystatin superfamily, including stefins, cystatins and kininogens, as well as serpins and inhibitors from the tyropin superfamily, impose spatial and temporal restraint and protect nontarget proteins from off‐site cleavage [39, 123, 124]. Microenvironmental factors further modulate enzymatic efficiency. Acidic pH favours optimal activity of collagen‐degrading cathepsin K and other cathepsins, as it also aids unfolding of protein substrates. Redox conditions, chloride and calcium concentrations and allosteric binding to glycosaminoglycans such as heparan sulphate additionally modulate specificity and stability within the ECM [38, 121].

The most prominent modulators of the ECM are cathepsins K, S and V, with contributions from cathepsins B and L. Cathepsin K, the best‐known ECM modulator, is highly expressed in osteoclasts and is the most potent mammalian collagenase and elastase under acidic conditions. Unlike other cathepsins, it can accept a Pro residue in the P2 position, facilitating collagen cleavage. Complex formation with chondroitin sulphate is crucial for cleavage of the collagen triple helix and resilient elastin, thereby driving bone resorption and contributing to cartilage erosion in arthritis [25, 125, 126]. Cathepsin S remains catalytically active at neutral pH and is secreted by macrophages, dendritic cells and vascular cells, where it degrades elastin and collagens within interstitial and arterial matrices and disrupts basement membranes to facilitate leukocyte trafficking [127, 128, 129]. Another important cathepsin, cathepsin V, a close ortholog of mouse cathepsin L, efficiently cleaves elastin [130].

Cathepsins B and L are activated at the invasive front of cancer cells through complexes containing annexin II, S100A10 and the uPA receptor. This localisation enhances pericellular proteolysis, promotes escape from basement membranes and initiates proteolytic cascades by processing pro‐MMPs and components of the plasminogen system, thereby amplifying matrix turnover and invasion [131].

Beyond bulk degradation, cathepsins modulate matrix‐derived signalling. They liberate growth factors such as VEGF, FGF and TGF‐β from heparan sulphate proteoglycans and generate matricryptins, bioactive fragments of collagen and elastin that can be chemotactic, pro‐angiogenic or pro‐inflammatory [25, 132]. Elastin‐derived peptides exemplify this dual role by signalling through the elastin receptor complex and integrins to recruit monocytes and stimulate smooth muscle cell migration, coupling matrix turnover to cellular responses during tissue stress or injury [133, 134].

Crosstalk with metalloproteinases and ADAMTS family members broadens their impact on the matrisome. Cathepsins can activate pro‐MMPs, while MMPs can degrade cystatins, creating feed‐forward loops that intensify matrix breakdown. In cartilage, cathepsins cooperate with ADAMTS aggrecanases to cleave aggrecan and type II collagen during arthritic matrix erosion, linking inflammatory signalling to structural failure of the tissue [135, 136, 137]. The deployment of cathepsins is influenced by mechanical and environmental cues. Hypoxia, acidosis and inflammatory cytokines acting through HIF and NF‐κB increase cathepsin expression, while changes in matrix stiffness and integrin signalling enhance secretion and pericellular retention [138, 139, 140]. Macrophage polarisation shifts the cathepsin repertoire, with inflammatory states favouring cathepsins S and K. Glycation and crosslinking of the ECM alter its susceptibility to cleavage, and cathepsin K is uniquely capable of degrading even highly crosslinked collagen, with implications for ageing tissues and diabetes [141, 142, 143].

Physiologically, cathepsins enable controlled passage via basement membranes during branching morphogenesis, angiogenic sprouting and wound remodelling. Pathologically, they drive osteolysis and osteoarthritis, vascular remodelling in atherosclerosis and aneurysm, tumour invasion and premetastatic niche conditioning, including vesicle‐associated cathepsins [42, 144, 145, 146]. Furthermore, in the nervous system, cathepsin S cleaves the chemokine CX3CL1 to promote microglia‐dependent neuropathic pain signalling [120, 127, 147]. Taken together, cathepsins act as context‐dependent editors of the ECM that integrate trafficking, activation chemistry, environmental signals and inhibitor networks to coordinate both structural turnover and matrix‐encoded signalling.

In addition to cysteine cathepsins, aspartyl cathepsin D is also associated with ECM remodelling, although mostly in diseases. The enzyme is frequently secreted as a zymogen and becomes active in acidic microenvironments typical of tumours or osteoclast lacunae, where it cleaves laminins and proteoglycans and processes latent factors that shape the tumour microenvironment and osteolytic niches [148, 149].

Cluster of differentiation molecules and ECM

Cluster of differentiation molecules, commonly known as CDs, were originally defined as cell surface markers distinguishing immune cell subsets. It is now evident that many CDs are functional receptors or coreceptors that transduce biochemical and mechanical cues from the ECM into cellular behaviours. CDs encompass multiple protein families and structural classes, including integrins (e.g. integrin heterodimers such as CD11/CD18), tetraspanins (e.g. CD9, CD81, CD151), the immunoglobulin superfamily (e.g. CD2, CD4, CD8, ICAMs, and members of the CD80/86 family), selectins (e.g. CD62 family members L‐, E‐ and P‐selectin), complement and Fc receptors (e.g. CD35/CR1, CD21/CR2, Fcγ receptors such as CD16), cytokine and chemokine receptors (numerous CD markers denoting receptors for interleukins, chemokines and growth factors), and other diverse groups including scavenger receptors, lectins and signalling coreceptors. Beyond serving as receptors, CDs participate in adhesion, costimulation, phagocytosis and signal modulation, enabling cells to integrate biochemical and mechanical inputs from their microenvironment to regulate activation, migration, differentiation and tissue organisation [150, 151, 152, 153, 154].

Integrins provide a prototypical and direct link between CDs and the ECM. CD29, the β1 integrin subunit, pairs with CD49 family α subunits to generate receptors for fibronectin, collagens and laminins. β2 integrins, composed of CD18 and CD11 family α subunits, mediate leukocyte adhesion to ICAMs during the multistep process of transendothelial migration [155]. Engagement of these integrins by matrix ligands nucleates focal adhesions and activates FAK/Src together with RhoA/ROCK signalling [156]. These pathways enable cells to sense substrate stiffness, organise actomyosin contractility and transmit traction forces, which in turn regulate morphogenesis, immune surveillance and wound repair [16, 157].

CD44 exemplifies how a CD receptor modulates both matrix structure and signalling. As a principal receptor for hyaluronan and other glycosaminoglycans, CD44 anchors a pericellular hyaluronan coat that acts as a selective diffusion barrier and scaffold for chemokines and growth factors [158]. CD44 clusters with integrins and receptor tyrosine kinases, modulating adhesion and migration, and directing leukocyte homing and tumour invasion by controlling matrix organisation and ligand presentation [17, 159]. Syndecan‐1, also known as CD138, is a heparan sulphate proteoglycan that binds fibronectin, collagens and growth factors including FGFs and VEGF through its glycosaminoglycan chains. It functions as a coreceptor that concentrates morphogens at the cell surface, regulates their distribution in the pericellular space and coordinates cytoskeletal coupling to the matrix through its cytoplasmic tail [18, 160].

Tetraspanins such as CD9, CD81 and CD151 organise tetraspanin‐enriched microdomains that laterally assemble integrins and other CDs on the membrane. This mesoscale organisation modulates ligand affinity, adhesion strength and migration on ECM substrates. CD151 is particularly important for laminin‐binding integrins including α3β1, α6β1 and α6β4. By stabilising these complexes, CD151 supports hemidesmosome assembly and basement membrane integrity in epithelial and endothelial tissues [161, 162]. Several CDs also directly interact with matricellular proteins. For example, CD47 is a high‐affinity receptor for thrombospondin‐1. The CD47–thrombospondin‐1 axis modulates integrin activation, suppresses nitric oxide signalling and alters mechanoadaptive responses, collectively restraining angiogenesis in remodelling matrices [15]. CD36 binds collagens and thrombospondins and serves as a scavenger receptor for lipids. Its engagement can inhibit endothelial migration, regulate angiogenesis and promote matrix remodelling processes relevant to atherosclerosis and fibrosis [163, 164]. Endothelial CDs such as CD146, also known as melanoma cell adhesion molecule (MCAM), and CD93 reinforce vessel–matrix interactions. CD93 binds the ECM protein multimerin‐2 to regulate sprouting angiogenesis and lumen stability, illustrating how specific CD–matrix pairs pattern vascular architecture [165].

Regulation of ECM turnover depends partly on CD‐linked proteolysis and pericellular enzymatic activity. CD147, also called EMMPRIN or basigin, stimulates stromal and tumour cells to produce matrix metalloproteinases, thereby facilitating local matrix degradation and invasion. CD147 also associates with monocarboxylate transporters, which acidify the pericellular space and enhance protease activity [166, 167]. Ectoproteases such as CD10, CD13 and CD26 remodel the matrix indirectly by processing bioactive peptides and chemokines that regulate fibroblast activation and leukocyte trafficking. ADAM family members, including CD156, shed extracellular domains of matrix receptors and growth factor precursors. These shedding events reprogramme how cells engage their surroundings and how growth cues are presented and activated within the matrix [168]. Other CDs fine‐tune integrin mechanosignalling, linking nutrient status to matrix assembly. For instance, CD98 heavy chain, also known as SLC3A2, couples amino acid transport to β1 integrin signalling and is required for efficient force transmission and fibronectin fibrillogenesis [169, 170].

These molecular interactions create reciprocal feedback between cell identity and tissue mechanics. Stiff, collagen‐rich matrices amplify β1 integrin and CD44 signalling, driving myofibroblast differentiation and desmoplastic remodelling [16, 157]. Hyaluronan‐rich and viscoelastic matrices promote CD44‐dependent motility and immune cell trafficking [17]. Within tumours, CD147‐induced matrix metalloproteinase production, CD47‐thrombospondin‐1 signalling and CD44‐hyaluronan interactions cooperate to establish an immunosuppressive and pro‐invasive ECM [171, 172, 173]. Therapeutic targeting of these axes can normalise matrix architecture, improve perfusion and enhance responses to therapy. CDs are therefore not merely cell‐type markers but active effectors at the matrix interface, translating matrix composition and mechanics into cellular decisions and reciprocally remodelling the microenvironment in development, homeostasis and disease.

MMPs and ADAMs in ECM

MMPs are zinc‐dependent endopeptidases that execute pivotal functions in ECM remodelling and regulate signalling by cleaving matrix components and cell surface molecules. Twenty‐eight vertebrate MMPs are recognised, at least 23 of which are encoded in the human genome [27, 174, 175, 176]. Members of the MMP family share homology with MMP‐1 (collagenase‐1) and typically exhibit a multidomain architecture comprising an N‐terminal propeptide, a catalytic metalloproteinase domain, a flexible linker (hinge) and a C‐terminal hemopexin‐like domain. MMPs are functionally categorised into collagenases (MMP‐1, MMP‐8, MMP‐13), gelatinases (MMP‐2, MMP‐9), stromelysins (MMP‐3, MMP‐10, MMP‐11), matrilysins (MMP‐7, MMP‐26) and other subgroups including enamelysin (MMP‐20) and epilysin (MMP‐28). In addition to these canonical groups, other MMPs participate in specialised proteolytic events such as membrane‐associated proteolysis, processing of matricryptins and modulation of signalling cascades through ectodomain shedding of various receptors and ligands [174]. Most MMPs are secreted, whereas the membrane‐type MMPs (MT‐MMPs) are tethered to the plasma membrane through transmembrane segments (MMP‐14, MMP‐15, MMP‐16, MMP‐24) or glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI) anchors (MMP‐17, MMP‐25), thereby facilitating pericellular proteolysis [177, 178, 179, 180].

The ADAM and ADAMTS protease families complement and extend proteolytic activities of MMPs. ADAMs, including ADAM10 and ADAM17, act as principal sheddases for Notch and epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) ligands, converting membrane‐anchored precursors into soluble factors that drive morphogenetic gradients and downstream signalling, a process that often intersects with MMP‐regulated pathways in both normal physiology and disease. These membrane‐anchored enzymes share structural and mechanistic similarities with MMPs, including zinc‐dependent catalytic domains, yet are distinguished by their disintegrin and cysteine‐rich domains that mediate cell adhesion and interactions with integrins and ECM [176, 177, 178]. ADAMTS proteases, such as the aggrecanases ADAMTS‐4 and ADAMTS‐5, the procollagen N‐propeptidases ADAMTS‐2 and ADAMTS‐3, and the von Willebrand factor‐cleaving ADAMTS13, are soluble metalloproteases that operate in specific ECM contexts, frequently targeting proteoglycans and basement membrane constituents. These enzymes exhibit substrate overlap with certain MMPs, contributing to coordinated turnover of ECM macromolecules and modulation of pericellular environments. The convergent and complementary activities of MMPs, ADAMs and ADAMTS shape pericellular proteolysis, matrix architecture, and signalling cues that together govern cellular decisions in development, homeostasis and pathology [41].

All metalloproteases are regulated at multiple levels, including transcription (responsive to cytokines, growth factors and mechanotransduction), zymogen activation (via furin‐like proprotein convertases, autocatalysis, or other proteases), localisation (binding to ECM components and clustering at invadosomes) and suppression by endogenous tissue inhibitors (TIMPs 1–4) [178, 181, 182, 183, 184]. Notably, TIMPs can inhibit both MMPs and ADAM/ADAMTS family members, though with varying specificity, illustrating a shared regulatory framework among these enzyme families.

Although MMPs, ADAM and ADAMTS are central to ECM remodelling and mediate key cellular interactions, a comprehensive discussion of their mechanisms is beyond the scope of this review, as they are discussed in detail elsewhere [1, 6, 20, 70, 185, 186, 187, 188]. However, their specific roles in the context of health and disease will be highlighted where relevant.

Healthy vs pathological ECM environment

In healthy tissues, the ECM maintains organ‐specific architecture and balances synthesis, crosslinking and proteolysis to preserve function. Cutaneous wound healing exemplifies these physiological ECM dynamics (Fig. 2). Immediately after injury, a fibrin‐ and fibronectin‐rich provisional matrix, assembled from plasma proteins and activated platelets, provides a permissive substrate for neutrophil and monocyte infiltration and for keratinocyte migration [41]. Fibroblasts then infiltrate and deposit collagen III, fibronectin and hyaluronan, while lysyl oxidase family enzymes initiate crosslinking that stabilises nascent fibrils and restores mechanical continuity [46]. Integrin‐mediated traction, working with TGF‐β signalling, drives differentiation of a subset of fibroblasts into contractile myofibroblasts that align fibres and close the wound [9]. Proteases including MMPs and ADAMTS remodel the provisional matrix, liberating cryptic matrikines and growth factors such as VEGF and TGF‐β from latent ECM reservoirs, which in turn coordinate angiogenesis and epithelial closure [41, 189]. As re‐epithelialisation completes, collagen III is replaced by collagen I, crosslinking is modulated to recover tensile strength, inflammation resolves and mechanotransduction normalises so that myofibroblasts deactivate or undergo apoptosis. Balanced MMP‐to‐TIMP activity and restoration of appropriate stiffness prevent excessive scarring and permit a return to homeostasis [9, 190]. This choreography demonstrates how composition, topology and mechanics are integrated to support repair and subsequently re‐establish equilibrium.

Fibroblasts synthesise and secrete ECM proteins (e.g. collagens, elastin, laminin, fibronectin and proteoglycans) and organise them into a structured, hydrated mesh that remains closely associated with the surface of producing cells. The ECM supports both cell–cell and cell–ECM contacts, enabling integrin‐ and cadherin‐mediated signalling that regulates adhesion, migration and tissue homeostasis. Epithelial cells interface with the ECM and neighbouring immune cells via coordinated cell–ECM and cell–cell interactions, maintaining barrier function and enabling surveillance and rapid responses to perturbations. The healthy ECM thus acts as a dynamic scaffold that preserves tissue architecture while permitting controlled remodelling and communication among resident cells and infiltrating immune cells. (B) Pathological ECM remodelling, characterised by excessive matrix deposition and reorganised structure, is accompanied by altered remodelling dynamics. This panel highlights abnormal collagen and proteoglycan accumulation, aberrant angiogenesis and enhanced matrix degradation, all contributing to tissue stiffening, altered mechanical properties and disrupted cell signalling. The diseased ECM often exhibits imbalanced turnover with either excessive deposition or degradation, accompanied by vascular remodelling that supports pathological processes such as inflammation and fibrosis. Overall, this panel contrasts the healthy, regulated ECM–cell interactions with the dysregulated ECM milieu that underpins disease progression.

Another great illustration of ECM‐related transformation is angiogenesis and vasculogenesis. During vascularisation, signalling cues such as Hedgehog ligands, establish arterial–venous specification, while VEGF‐A motifs and their coreceptors direct sprouting and tip/stalk selection [191, 192]. FGF supports endothelial proliferation and matrix deposition, and TGF‐β family members promote mural cell recruitment and basement membrane assembly [193, 194]. The ECM is capable to not only stores and presents these signals in spatial gradients but also transduces them through receptor engagement, enabling precise endothelial responses (e.g. integrin–ECM signalling) [61]. Endothelial basement membranes rich in laminins (e.g. laminin‐411/511), collagen IV networks and proteoglycans provide a scaffold whose integrity regulates sprouting: laminin–integrin interactions (α6β1/β4) maintain quiescence until localised basement membrane loosening initiates invasion [195, 196]. Proteases then act as pivotal mediators. MMP‐2, MMP‐9 and MT1‐MMP (MMP‐14) perforate basement membranes and interstitial collagens to permit tip‐cell penetration while preserving the underlying framework [197, 198]. By processing multimerin‐2 (MMRN2), MMPs modulate VEGF‐A bioavailability, restraining and subsequently unleashing angiogenic signalling as MMP‐9 disrupts the MMRN2–VEGF‐A bond to promote vessel formation [199]. In this orchestration, endothelial CDs serve as crucial conduits linking ECM signals to angiogenic programmes. CD146 strengthens endothelial cell contacts and amplifies VEGFR2 signalling, while CD93 collaborates with multimerin‐2 and dystroglycan to shape pericellular ECM architecture, regulate ECM fragment uptake, and reinforce barrier function [200, 201]. Proteolytic matrilines generate anti‐angiogenic cues‐endostatin and arresten/tumstatin, whose integrin‐ and thrombospondin‐1–dependent interactions with CDs (CD36, CD47) attenuate nitric oxide (NO) and VEGF signalling, illustrating a counterbalance between protease‐driven remodelling and ECM/CD‐mediated restraint [15, 202]. Sprout guidance is further refined by interstitial flow that aligns ECM fibrils, with lumenisation governed by membrane tension downstream of integrin–Rho signalling [203, 204]. Termination of angiogenesis involves basement membrane reassembly, TIMP upregulation and shear‐responsive mechanotransduction that restores YAP/TAZ quiescence [205, 206].

Disease then eventually arises when these same ECM programmes are chronically engaged or dysregulated, causing the matrix to switch from a homeostatic scaffold to a driver of dysfunction (Fig. 3). Across fibrotic disorders of the lung, liver, kidney and heart, excessive collagen I and III deposition together with lysyl oxidase‐mediated crosslinking increase stiffness and activate YAP/TAZ and MRTF pathways that stabilise myofibroblasts and amplify matrix production [24, 207]. Proteolysis and αv‐containing integrins then release latent TGF‐β from its ECM stores to maintain profibrotic signalling [208, 209]. The purinergic and thrombospondin axes add further layers of regulation. CD73 generates adenosine that reduces nitric oxide bioavailability and biases fibroblast activation, while thrombospondin‐1 signals through CD47 and CD36 to modulate adhesion, angiogenesis and protease deployment, thereby linking vascular tone to ECM remodelling [15, 210, 211]. Hyaluronan biology exemplifies context dependence: CD44‐hyaluronan signalling can either suppress or promote fibrosis depending on hyaluronan size and receptor isoform, illustrating how cells decode matrix cues in situ [212, 213]. Ageing and metabolic status similarly modulate these axes. Nonenzymatic glycation and age‐associated crosslinking elevate stiffness and decrease collagen susceptibility to proteolysis, predisposing tissues to fibrotic set‐points and impairing repair in diabetes and with advancing age [190].

Schematic representation of organ‐level localisation and predominant niches of selected cathepsins, CD molecules, metalloproteases and ADAMs across major human tissues. Their coordinated expression defines the functional identity of each organ and is dynamically regulated in processes such as extracellular matrix remodelling, inflammation, wound healing, and cancer progression.

In cancer, the ECM is dynamically remodelled to drive malignant progression, with proteases and their interplay with CD molecules acting as key orchestrators. This involves cancer cells, tumour‐associated macrophages and cancer‐associated fibroblasts (CAFs) that upregulate and secrete proteases including cathepsins B, L, S and K, as well as MT1‐MMP (MMP‐14) [214, 215, 216]. These enzymes reshape the peritumoural environment by degrading structural components such as collagens I and IV, fibronectin and laminin, thereby increasing matrix porosity to facilitate invasion [29, 120]. Beyond degradation, proteolytic processing releases sequestered growth factors (e.g. VEGF) and cryptic peptide motifs (matrikines) from the ECM, which engage integrins (e.g. via RGD‐like motifs) and receptor tyrosine kinases to enhance promigratory and angiogenic signalling through FAK–Src and Rho GTPase pathways [41, 217, 218]. The mechanical consequences of this activity, manifest as increased stiffness, interstitial fluid pressure and hypoxia further upregulate protease expression in a feed‐forward loop [219, 220], whereas LOX‐mediated crosslinking enhances YAP/TAZ activity to sustain a fibrotic, tumour‐supportive niche.

This proteolytic cascade is closely coupled to CD molecule signalling, extending its effects to immune modulation and cellular adhesion. For instance, cathepsin S cleaves CD74 on antigen‐presenting cells, skewing CD4+ T‐cell priming towards an immunosuppressive microenvironment [221, 222], while cleavage of CD47 impairs phagocytic clearance to promote immune evasion [223]. Protease‐dependent processing of adhesion receptors is also critical. CD44 variants enhance hyaluronan binding to promote motility, stemness and therapy resistance, with cleavage fragments driving a pro‐migratory phenotype via Rac1/Cdc42 activation [224]. Similarly, CD146 coordinates angiogenic remodelling, and receptors such as CD36 and CD47 link ECM degradation to thrombospondin‐1‐mediated suppression of immunity and angiogenesis. The net effect is a coordinated axis in which proteases sculpt ECM architecture, regulate bioactive cue availability and modulate CD‐mediated adhesion and immune signalling to collectively promote dissemination, immune evasion and therapy resistance [41].

In contrast to the cancer ECM, the skeletal ECM forms a heavier repository of mineralised and organic components whose turnover governs bone pathologies such as osteoporosis and arthritis. Central to this process is cathepsin K, a protease expressed by osteoclasts that is indispensable for bone resorption, as it efficiently degrades collagen I and other organic matrix components [29, 225]. Beyond its canonical role, recent research indicates that cathepsin K also cleaves nonmatrix substrates within the bone microenvironment. For instance, it processes the ectodomain of CD38, a multifunctional receptor and enzyme on osteoblasts and immune cells. This cleavage can attenuate CD38's cyclic ADP‐ribose hydrolase activity, potentially disrupting intracellular calcium signalling pathways vital for osteoblast differentiation and function, thereby uncoupling the bone remodelling process [226].

This activity is not purely destructive; proteolytic processing also releases bioactive matrikines and growth factors sequestered within the bone matrix, influencing neighbouring osteoblast behaviour. Interplay with CD molecules further modulates these processes. For example, CD44, a receptor for hyaluronan and osteopontin, mediates osteoclast adhesion and motility on the bone surface, and its proteolytic shedding by MMP‐14 (MT1‐MMP) and ADAM10 modulates these interactions, generating soluble CD44 (sCD44) fragments that act as decoy receptors to competitively inhibit pro‐osteogenic hyaluronan signalling [227, 228]. Furthermore, cleavage of signalling molecules by other proteases, such as MMPs, can activate or inactivate receptors that control bone formation and resorption cycles [229].

Emerging evidence also highlights a key role for calpain‐1 in osteocytes, the master regulators of bone mechanosensation. Calpain‐1 activation in response to mechanical unloading cleaves the CD molecule CD13 (Aminopeptidase N) on the osteocyte surface. This proteolytic event disrupts CD13's role in stabilising focal adhesion complexes, leading to cytoskeletal disassembly and promoting pro‐osteoclastic signalling (e.g. increased RANKL secretion) that underlies disuse osteoporosis [230, 231]. In disorders such as osteoporosis and arthritis, an imbalance in this system emerges: Overactive cathepsin K drives excessive bone loss, while altered CD‐mediated adhesion and signalling impair osteoblast function and aberrantly enhance osteoclastogenesis. The resulting disruption of the delicate coupling between bone resorption and formation underscores a paradigm in which protease–CD–ECM crosstalk governs both physiological maintenance and pathological degradation of skeletal integrity [41, 232].

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis highlights how maladaptive repair becomes self‐perpetuating. Repetitive epithelial microinjury drives persistent TGF‐β activation, fibroblast recruitment and myofibroblast differentiation, resulting in aberrantly crosslinked collagen I and III, fibronectin, and periostin deposition [220, 233]. LOX and LOXL enzymes, together with advanced glycation end products, stiffen the interstitium, which enhances traction through integrins such as αvβ6 and αvβ1, further releasing latent TGF‐β and reinforcing YAP or TAZ nuclear localisation and myofibroblast activation [41, 46, 234]. Disorganised fibrillar architecture alters pore size and disrupts alveolar mechanics and gas exchange, while excessive proteolysis generates matrikines that polarise macrophages and perpetuate fibrosis [6, 233]. Single‐cell analyses reveal shifts towards ECM‐producing and damage‐responsive fibroblast states enriched for matrisome and mechanotransduction programmes, coupling cellular heterogeneity to matrix remodelling and locking the lung into a fibrotic attractor state [235].

Similar mechanisms govern cancer‐associated desmoplasia. In many solid tumours, CAFs construct dense, anisotropic collagen tracks aligned with invasive fronts. The resultant desmoplastic matrix stiffens tissue, reinforces integrin‐to‐FAK signalling, stabilises YAP or TAZ, and promotes malignant phenotypes including proliferation, invasion and therapy resistance [220, 236]. The tumour ECM also shapes immune responses. High‐density collagen and hyaluronan elevate interstitial pressure and compress vessels, limiting perfusion and T‐cell infiltration, while ECM fragments and matricellular proteins steer myeloid cells towards immunosuppression and further matrix remodelling [41, 236].

Cardiovascular disease provides another vantage point on ECM regulation of tissue fate. In atherosclerosis, fibrous caps depend on collagens I and III for tensile strength and elastin for recoil, while proteoglycans such as versican, biglycan and decorin, together with hyaluronan, retain apoB lipoproteins and regulate leukocyte and smooth muscle cell behaviour [237]. Matrix turnover is controlled by MMPs (matrix metalloproteinases), including interstitial collagenases, gelatinases and MT1‐MMP, and by cysteine cathepsins B, S, K and L, which degrade collagen and elastin and generate matrikines that thin the fibrous cap. Serine proteases and the plasmin system amplify MMP activation and create cross‐activation loops, whereas ADAMTS‐5 degrades versican and heparinase and remodels heparan sulphate gradients [238]. Endogenous inhibitors such as TIMPs, cystatin C and serpins restrain this cascade, and lower cystatin C levels correlate with higher cardiovascular risk [239]. Wall stiffening through LOX activity and advanced glycation enhances YAP‐ or TAZ‐dependent mechanotransduction in smooth muscle cells, promoting dedifferentiation and inflammation [44]. Thrombospondin‐1 engages CD47 and CD36 to regulate adhesion, angiogenesis and protease deployment, while integrins including α5β1 and αvβ3, DDR1, CD44, syndecans and CD147 translate matrix composition and mechanics into FAK, Src and Rho signalling and MMP induction [15, 16, 240, 241]. Whether plaques mature into stable collagen‐rich caps or into protease‐rich lesions prone to rupture depends on the balance among these pathways [242].

Loss of an appropriate pericellular matrix also destabilises cartilage and intervertebral disc homeostasis [243]. In osteoarthritis and intervertebral disc degeneration, failure of the aggrecan and collagen II networks reduces compressive stiffness and disrupts chondrocyte mechanosensing. ADAMTS‐5‐mediated aggrecanolysis can initiate pathology in murine models, and subsequent MMP‐driven collagen degradation locks in irreversible structural failure [135, 244]. Imbalances in endogenous inhibitors such as TIMPs accelerate cartilage destruction and degrade the pericellular hyaluronan–proteoglycan coat, altering CD44 signalling and chondrocyte survival [245].

Vascular softening disorders exemplify the opposite extreme of ECM failure. In arterial aneurysms and elastopathies, cysteine cathepsins S and K, together with MMPs, degrade elastin and interstitial collagens, while cystatin C deficiency accelerates medial degeneration and aneurysm formation [246, 247]. Altered proteoglycan content and heparan sulphate sulphation modify poroelastic properties and growth factor gradients, compromising adaptive remodelling [248]. Heritable matrix disorders, including vascular Ehlers–Danlos syndrome caused by COL3A1 mutations, Alport syndrome due to COL4A3–COL4A5 defects, and Marfan syndrome arising from FBN1 mutations, disrupt fibrillogenesis or basement membrane assembly. These defects weaken tissues and alter TGF‐β bioavailability and signalling, amplifying maladaptive remodelling [249, 250, 251].

Basement membrane diseases in the kidney and lung further demonstrate how excessive gelatinase and cathepsin activity drive barrier failure and impair repair [252, 253]. Proteinuria in glomerular disease and emphysema in the lung progress through unchecked proteolysis, while endothelial receptors that maintain ECM homeostasis, such as CD146 and CD93, are essential for vascular integrity and reparative angiogenesis [200, 201, 254, 255]. Dysfunction of these receptors contributes to vascular leakage and adverse remodelling.

Across health and disease, ECM homeostasis depends on three interlocking axes that operate through shared molecular machinery. Composition dictates the ligand repertoire and growth factor sequestration [61]. Mechanics regulate cell fate through mechanosensitive pathways, including integrin‐to‐FAK‐to‐Rho signalling and nuclear effectors, such as YAP, TAZ and MRTF [50, 256]. Turnover, orchestrated by MMPs, ADAMTS, the plasmin and serine protease systems, and cysteine cathepsins, counterbalanced by TIMPs, serpins and cystatin C, sculpts the matrix and releases signalling intermediates [41, 189]. Physiological healing is characterised by timely transitions along these axes followed by resolution and restoration to baseline [257]. Pathology arises when crosslinking, stiffness, protease activity or receptor signalling become trapped in positive feedback loops that couple matrix composition to mechanics and cell state, converting the ECM from a regulator of homeostasis into an active determinant of disease trajectory [220].

Therapeutics in context of ECM

Dysregulation of the ECM is a unifying driver of tissue degeneration, maladaptive repair and chronic inflammation across disorders such as fibrosis, osteoarthritis, osteoporosis and atherosclerosis. Consequently, therapeutic strategies have increasingly focused on enzymes and receptors that regulate ECM turnover and cell–matrix signalling.

Cysteine cathepsins have been extensively explored as drug targets, with over 20 clinical trials initiated. However, among the 11 human cathepsins, only four cathepsins, K, S, B and C, have reached clinical trials. Not all of these trials targeted ECM‐related diseases; several, particularly those involving cathepsin S inhibitors, addressed inflammation and neuropathic pain [42]. Most trials involved cathepsin K, which cleaves type I collagen in bone and is overactive in high‐turnover states, making it a leading target for osteoporosis treatment [43]. Developing inhibitors against cathepsin K has, however, proved highly challenging. Compounds that showed strong potency against the human enzyme exhibited markedly lower affinities for rodent orthologues due to subtle but critical active‐site differences. This has essentially precluded the use of rodent models of osteoporosis besides proof‐of‐concept studies, in addition to several in vitro models, prompting GlaxoSmithKline to develop a cynomolgus monkey model for more efficient inhibitor testing, given the high similarity between human and nonhuman primate cathepsin K [258].

Earlier cathepsin K inhibitors, such as balicatib, failed in clinical trials largely due to unexpected off‐target effects associated with lysosomotropism. Although the potent cathepsin K inhibitor odanacatib initially demonstrated efficacy in Phase I and II trials by increasing bone mineral density and reducing fracture rates, development was halted after Phase III trials due to excessive cerebrovascular events. Newer agents, including MIV‐711 and ONO‐5334, have shown antifracture activity and structural modification in bone and cartilage with a more favourable off‐target profile in early phase studies. These results support the concept that partial and tissue‐restricted inhibition of cathepsin K can rebalance bone remodelling without the cardiovascular toxicity that compromised first‐generation drugs [259, 260, 261]. Nonetheless, the main problem with all active‐site‐directed small cathepsin K inhibitors is on‐target toxicity, resulting in undesirable side effects. Hence, despite early success, it appears unlikely that new generations of active‐site‐directed small‐molecule cathepsin K inhibitors will succeed.

One promising solution lies in exosite inhibitors such as tanshinones, which prevent degradation of collagen and other large fibrillar substrates without affecting the processing of active‐site‐directed substrates such as TGF‐β, as demonstrated in vitro and in vivo. Tanshinones have shown strong efficacy in a murine osteoporosis model without detectable side effects and were markedly superior to existing active‐site‐directed inhibitors [262, 263, 264, 265]. Another potential strategy involves allosteric inhibitors, though these have not advanced beyond early development [143, 266, 267, 268].

In the vasculature, cysteine cathepsins such as cathepsins S, K and L degrade elastin and collagen within atherosclerotic plaques and aneurysmal aortas. Small‐molecule inhibitors of cathepsin S have reduced plaque progression and improved fibrous cap stability in preclinical models, consistent with human genetic and proteomic data linking heightened cathepsin activity to vulnerable plaque phenotypes and increased cardiovascular events. However, definitive outcome trials of cathepsin blockade in atherosclerosis remain incomplete, and optimal patient selection will likely depend on imaging or circulating biomarkers of protease activity such as elastin degradation fragments and cathepsin‐specific neoepitopes [246, 269, 270]. Furthermore, targeting protease networks (for example, cathepsin K inhibitors) or modulating CD47‐CD36‐CD146 axes holds promise for rebalancing remodelling and improving repair efficiency, illustrating how ECM proteolysis and CD‐mediated communication orchestrate skeletal health and disease progression [46, 190, 220].

The most advanced cathepsin programme in clinical trials involves cathepsin C, with the lead inhibitor currently in Phase III trials for the treatment of bronchiectasis. However, this inflammatory indication does not target ECM remodelling but instead prevents the activation of lung serine proteases such as neutrophil elastase [271, 272].

Beyond direct enzyme inhibition, cathepsins have also been explored for diagnostic imaging, antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) activation, and targeted drug delivery, primarily in cancer [43]. Development of imaging agents has progressed into image‐guided surgery, with one molecule entering clinical trials [273]. ADC activation exploits cathepsins for drug release, as they are among the few proteases capable of cleaving the Val‐Cyt bond and are abundant at tumour sites. Targeting cathepsins for drug delivery remains in early development, making it premature to assess clinical potential [274, 275, 276].

MMPs represent prototypical ECM regulators whose broad inhibition using hydroxamate small molecules such as batimastat and marimastat led to dose‐limiting musculoskeletal toxicity and poor efficacy in oncology [277]. Despite these limitations in clinical settings, these compounds remain useful reagents for cellular and in vitro studies, where they can achieve near‐complete inhibition of MMP activity in highly invasive cancer cell lines (e.g. MDA‐MB‐231 & 435, HL‐60, Panc1) [278, 279, 280]. These experiences redirected the field towards more selective approaches that spare homeostatic MMPs and exploit context‐dependent delivery. Nonetheless, clinical experience has validated that reducing excessive MMP activity can be beneficial in certain matrix‐driven diseases. Subantimicrobial‐dose doxycycline, which reduces MMP activity, is approved for periodontal disease and rosacea [281]. Similarly, local or targeted MMP inhibition is under investigation for aneurysm stabilisation, arthritis and corneal disease using antibodies, engineered TIMPs and prodrugs activated by pathological protease environments to minimise systemic effects [33, 282, 283].

Selectivity remains central to next‐generation inhibitors such as monoclonal antibodies against MMP‐9 and MMP‐14 [284], catalytic‐pocket chemotypes optimised for MMP‐13 in cartilage protection and MMP‐12 in emphysema, and allosteric modulators that target exosites or hemopexin domains rather than the conserved zinc site [285, 286, 287]. This strategy has demonstrated disease‐relevant target engagement in preclinical cartilage and vascular models but will require biomarker‐guided clinical trials to confirm efficacy and avoid the historical toxicities associated with broad zinc chelation [288, 289].

A closely related therapeutic axis involves the ADAM and ADAMTS families, which combine sheddase activity with ECM proteoglycan cleavage, thereby modulating cytokine, growth factor and adhesion receptor bioavailability alongside structural matrix turnover. Clinical inhibition of ADAM17, the tumour necrosis factor‐converting enzyme, using small molecules such as apratastat and DPC‐333, reduced TNF shedding but was limited by off‐target effects and on‐target gastrointestinal and infectious risks [290, 291]. This has redirected development towards tumour‐ or tissue‐selective delivery, substrate bias and biologics that indirectly temper ADAM17 function through iRhom and phosphatidylserine binding, or that restrict activity to pathological microdomains such as inflamed synovium or tumour stroma [292, 293, 294].

In osteoarthritis, ADAMTS‐4 and ADAMTS‐5 (aggrecanases) are key mediators of cartilage proteoglycan loss. Although an oral small‐molecule ADAMTS‐5 inhibitor failed to meet primary endpoints in a large Phase 2 knee osteoarthritis trial, pharmacodynamic evidence of reduced aggrecan neoepitopes and emerging intra‐articular biologics, such as nanobodies that neutralise ADAMTS‐5 and antibodies that block exosite binding, suggest that route, exposure, and target engagement will be decisive for future structure‐modifying efficacy. Patient enrichment using synovial fluid biomarkers of aggrecan turnover may also be required to reveal therapeutic benefit [295, 296, 297].

Beyond proteases, several cell surface CD proteins coordinate matrix sensing and remodelling and represent actionable drug targets. CD44 binds hyaluronan and functions as a hub for integrin, growth factor and MMP signalling in fibroblasts, chondrocytes and immune cells. Blocking CD44‐hyaluronan interactions using antibodies, peptides or hyaluronidase variants reduces fibroblast activation and collagen deposition in lung, liver and kidney fibrosis models [171, 224, 298, 299]. Engineered hyaluronan fragments that bias CD44 signalling towards resolution are being investigated for joint disease and tissue repair, with early human studies indicating effects on synovial inflammation and pain, although structural outcomes remain to be demonstrated [300, 301, 302].

CD147, also known as basigin or EMMPRIN, potently induces MMP expression in neighbouring stromal and immune cells and is upregulated in fibrotic and tumour microenvironments. Antagonising CD147 with antibodies or small molecules has reduced MMP‐mediated ECM degradation and angiogenesis in preclinical liver and myocardial remodelling [303, 304, 305]. A humanised anti‐CD147 antibody entered clinical testing and showed mechanistic evidence of reduced MMP activity in accessible tissues, supporting further development for fibrotic diseases where stromal MMP induction sustains a feedforward loop of injury and repair [167, 173, 303, 306].

In atherosclerosis, CD36 acts as a scavenger receptor for oxidised lipids and links lipid uptake with inflammasome activation and matrix remodelling [307]. Therapeutic strategies, including peptide agonists that bias CD36 signalling, antibodies that block oxLDL binding, and small molecules that disrupt CD36–TLR heteromerisation, have reduced foam cell formation, necrotic core expansion and protease‐rich cap thinning in animal models [164, 307]. Translational evidence is supported by Mendelian randomisation studies linking reduced CD36 activity with lower coronary risk, though systemic metabolic effects remain a concern for chronic therapy [308, 309, 310].

These ECM‐targeted strategies intersect with established disease‐specific therapeutics in osteoporosis and atherosclerosis, where matrix quality, not only mass, determines outcomes. In osteoporosis, antiresorptives such as bisphosphonates and denosumab rapidly reduce bone turnover and fracture risk, but prolonged oversuppression can impair microdamage repair [311]. Conversely, anabolic agents such as teriparatide and abaloparatide stimulate osteoblasts and remodel matrix architecture [312]. Sclerostin blockade with romosozumab uniquely couples rapid increases in trabecular and cortical bone mass with improved material properties by modulating collagen crosslinking and mineralisation while secondarily reducing osteoclastic protease load [313]. This translates into significant reductions in vertebral and clinical fractures in Phase 3 trials and measurable improvements in bone tissue mechanics, although cardiovascular safety signals necessitate individualised risk assessment in patients with recent myocardial infarction or stroke [259, 314, 315, 316].

By contrast, atherosclerosis requires stabilisation of a protease‐rich, inflamed plaque without impairing physiological remodelling. Targeting the protease nodes most strongly associated with cap thinning and matrix degradation, namely MMP‐9, MMP‐12, cathepsin S and ADAMTS‐7, appears promising in combination with lipid‐lowering and anti‐inflammatory therapy. Genetic studies have repeatedly implicated ADAMTS‐7 in human coronary artery disease and restenosis, and mechanistic work shows that ADAMTS‐7 promotes smooth muscle cell migration through cleavage of thrombospondin family substrates and cartilage oligomeric matrix protein within the arterial wall [317, 318]. Inhibition of ADAMTS‐7 reduces neointimal hyperplasia and atherogenesis in mice, motivating the development of selective small molecules and antibodies and companion diagnostics that quantify ADAMTS‐7 activity or substrate fragments to guide therapy in high‐risk patients undergoing stenting or with thin‐cap fibroatheroma on imaging [319, 320, 321, 322, 323].

Across these targets, two translational lessons consistently emerge. First, the matrix environment is highly contextual, and therapeutic success depends on spatiotemporal precision. This can be achieved through local delivery, prodrugs that unmask in protease‐rich tissues, biologics recognising ECM neoepitopes or nanoparticles homing to leaky neovessels and fibrotic stroma [324, 325, 326]. Second, pharmacodynamic assessment of enzyme activity and matrix turnover is critical, as protein abundance alone often fails to reflect catalytic flux. Recent assays detecting neoepitope fragments of collagens, elastin, aggrecan and versican, along with activity‐based probes for MMPs and cathepsins, now enable mechanism‐based dosing and patient selection. Such tools address limitations of earlier trials that lacked documentation of target engagement [327, 328, 329, 330, 331].

Collectively, these advances suggest that therapeutics targeting CDs that regulate matrix signalling, MMPs, ADAMs and ADAMTS enzymes that degrade structural and regulatory ECM, and cathepsins that drive bone and vascular proteolysis can be integrated with existing standards of care for osteoporosis and atherosclerosis. This approach aims not only to alter mass or lumen size but also to restore the quality, organisation and resilience of the matrix, provided that selectivity, delivery and biomarker strategies are used to balance efficacy against the physiological roles of these enzymes in normal tissue homeostasis.

In addition to direct therapeutic targeting of proteases, focus in the field of cysteine cathepsins shifted to diagnostic imaging and targeted drug delivery in cancer, where cathepsins have been considered very viable targets [31, 43, 332]. Moreover, endogenous cathepsins have been also widely used as prodrug activating enzymes, which is heavily explored especially in the activation of antibody–drug conjugates, which has been extensively discussed elsewhere. Especially linker optimisation was given special attention to get away from the classical Val‐Cit linker that was for a long time believed to be cleaved solely by cathepsin B [333, 334].

Conclusion and future perspectives

In conclusion, the ECM is a dynamic and tightly regulated system that integrates compositional, mechanical and enzymatic cues to coordinate tissue development, maintenance and repair [6]. CD molecules act as fundamental gatekeepers at the cell–matrix interface, serving a dual role: they translate physical and biochemical signals into intracellular programmes, and they critically direct where and how proteases like cathepsins are activated, tethered or silenced within specific pericellular niches [335, 336]. Under homeostatic conditions, cathepsins operate within intricate feedback networks that couple synthesis with degradation, ensuring that matrix turnover meticulously preserves tissue architecture and function [337]. When these proteolytic circuits become dysregulated, often through a breakdown in CD‐mediated signalling and localisation, tissues drift towards pathological states such as fibrosis or calcification [338, 339]. This reflects a profound loss of spatiotemporal control over cathepsin activity, rather than a simple excess or deficit of any single enzyme [340].

Recent breakthroughs in spatial omics, high‐resolution imaging and mechanobiology are now revealing the three‐dimensional architecture and dynamic chemistry of the ECM with unprecedented detail, allowing us to pinpoint precisely where and when specific cathepsins (e.g. B, K, L, S) are active and which CD‐defined cell populations, such as CD44‐high tumour cells, CD206/CD163 macrophages or αVβ3+ osteoclasts, are orchestrating that activity [341, 342, 343]. The central challenge is to translate this knowledge into solutions that restore balance to the matrix, rather than bluntly suppress its remodelling [344]. Emerging strategies are therefore highly targeted and include the use of isoform‐selective inhibitors and allosteric modulators for precise protease control, the development of cathepsin‐activatable biologics and antibody–drug conjugates and the design of activity‐based delivery systems that engage only in diseased microenvironments defined by low pH, hypoxia and high cathepsin activity [37, 276]. In parallel, ECM‐mimetic scaffolds are now engineered with controlled stiffness, ligand density and cathepsin‐cleavable motifs to actively instruct regeneration, while CD‐guided targeting layers cell specificity onto these approaches. For example, to deliver cathepsin S inhibitors specifically to CD206+ macrophages to temper fibrotic turnover or to direct cathepsin K modulation to αVβ3+ osteoclasts to confine effects to bone [345, 346, 347].

Manipulation of CD‐mediated signalling provides complementary leverage by retuning how cells sense and remodel their surroundings. For instance, biasing hyaluronan‐CD44 or integrin crosstalk towards resolution over scarring can reduce the assembly of protease‐rich invadopodia and realign traction forces with constructive remodelling, while tetraspanins like CD63 can be leveraged to modulate the lysosomal and exosomal release of cathepsins [341, 348, 349]. Future studies must define the spatiotemporal rules of ECM remodelling at single‐cell and subcellular scales by comapping active cathepsins with multiplex CD panels, resolve pericellular versus extracellular proteolysis, and tie these rules to measurable biomarkers such as circulating cathepsin‐generated neoepitopes, soluble CD fragments (e.g. sCD44), and exosome signatures enriched for CD63 and proteases, alongside dual‐modality imaging that pairs CD‐targeted immunoPET with cathepsin activity probes [45, 189, 350, 351, 352]. Integrating these datasets with computational reaction–diffusion and mechanobiology models, and embedding logic‐gated, cathepsin‐responsive circuits into synthetic biology therapies and smart biomaterials, will be crucial for programming reparative microenvironments with true context specificity [353, 354].

Despite major progress in understanding ECM biology, the development of therapeutics has not advanced rapidly, particularly in the field of cysteine cathepsins, following the failure of odanacatib in osteoporosis treatment after successfully concluding Phase III trials. The major progress is more likely to come from cancer diagnosis and therapy. Activity‐based probes have found their niche in image‐guided surgery [350, 352] and multimodality imaging (see above) with first molecules already in clinical trials [42, 332, 355]. However, even more is expected in the further development of highly selective linkers that would allow for precise antibody–drug activation, tailoring it towards specific cancer subtypes. First such example is the development of cathepsin B and cathepsin L‐selective linkers by incorporating unnatural amino acids in trastuzumab‐based ADCs that displayed higher selectivity in HER2‐positive breast cancer cell models as the classical Val‐Cit linker, and a higher plasma stability. Such selective linkers would enable the development of patient‐tailored ADCs with greater protease selectivity and minimised off‐target activation, based on patient stratification [333].
