Comparative assessment of oral hygiene awareness and periodontal knowledge among caregivers of children with and without intellectual disabilities: a cross-sectional comparative study
Authors: Gülseda Çetin Demirol, Ahmet Doğukan Gündoğdu, Yasemin Turan Akansel, Emre Yaprak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesIndividuals with intellectual disabilities (ID) experience disproportionately higher rates of periodontal disease. While biological vulnerability contributes, the oral health knowledge and awareness of primary caregivers is increasingly recognized as a critical modifiable determinant. This study aimed to explore potential differences in the periodontal knowledge, oral hygiene awareness, personal hygiene practices, and child-directed supervision behaviors of caregivers of children with ID versus caregivers of neurotypical children.MethodsA cross-sectional comparative study was conducted among 402 parents in Kocaeli, Türkiye (201 in each group). Caregivers of children with ID (aged 6–12) were recruited from special education centers, while controls were recruited from a university dental clinic. A structured, interviewer-administered questionnaire assessed four domains: sociodemographic characteristics, periodontal knowledge (17 items), personal oral hygiene behaviors (10 items), and child-directed oral care supervision practices (5 items). Between-group comparisons were performed using Pearson’s Chi-square test (with Cramér’s V as effect size measure), Student’s t-test, and Mann–Whitney U test (α = 0.05).ResultsThe ID group demonstrated significantly lower educational attainment and household income (p < 0.001). A pronounced disparity in periodontal knowledge was observed across the majority of knowledge items (14 of 17 items at p < 0.001; Cramér’s V range: 0.06–0.36). Only 39.3% of the ID group correctly defined periodontitis versus 75.1% of controls. Notably, 56.2% of the ID group did not correctly identify gingival bleeding as a pathological sign. Regarding personal habits, twice-daily brushing was reported by 50.7% of the ID group compared with 75.1% of controls (p = 0.001). In child-directed care, only 20.4% of ID-group caregivers actively brushed their child’s teeth, with “verbal reminding” being the predominant strategy (31.3%) despite its limited efficacy in this population. After adjustment for maternal education, household income, and marital status, group membership remained an independent predictor of knowledge for 14 of 17 items (adjusted OR range: 1.54–8.62).ConclusionsA critical deficit in oral health knowledge and awareness exists among caregivers of children with ID, compounded by socioeconomic disadvantage. The failure to recognize gingival bleeding as pathological and the reliance on passive supervision strategies represent modifiable barriers. These findings suggest that public health interventions could benefit from caregiver-centered education that may emphasize assisted brushing techniques, early recognition of periodontal signs, and the importance of primary dentition. However, these results should be interpreted with caution given the cross-sectional design and differences in recruitment settings between the groups.Clinical significanceThe widespread misconception that gingival bleeding is a benign consequence of “hard brushing” leads caregivers to reduce oral hygiene efforts, paradoxically accelerating disease progression. Clinicians should incorporate structured caregiver education into routine appointments, with particular emphasis on the pathological significance of bleeding on probing.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08675-y.Keywords: Intellectual disability; Periodontal diseases; Oral health knowledge and awareness; Caregivers; Health knowledge, attitudes, practice; Cross-sectional studies

Introduction

Periodontal diseases are chronic, multifactorial inflammatory conditions initiated by the accumulation of dental plaque biofilm and propagated by the dysregulated host immune response [1, 2]. While largely preventable through effective plaque control and regular professional care, periodontitis remains a major global public health burden, affecting approximately 19% of the world’s adult population in its severe form [3]. Beyond the oral cavity, periodontal inflammation has been linked to adverse systemic outcomes, including cardiovascular disease, diabetes mellitus, and adverse pregnancy outcomes [4]. In pediatric populations, while destructive periodontitis is less prevalent, plaque-induced gingivitis is nearly ubiquitous and constitutes the primary precursor for future periodontal attachment loss [5, 6].

Individuals with intellectual disabilities (ID) bear a disproportionate burden of oral disease. Systematic reviews have consistently documented higher plaque accumulation, increased gingival inflammation, elevated caries experience, and poorer overall periodontal status in children and adolescents with ID compared to their neurotypical peers [7–9]. Historically, these disparities were attributed predominantly to intrinsic biological factors, including drug-induced gingival overgrowth (e.g., from antiepileptic medications), craniofacial anomalies, malocclusion, and immunological deficits associated with conditions such as Down syndrome [10, 11]. However, contemporary frameworks increasingly emphasize the primacy of behavioral, environmental, and socioeconomic determinants [12, 13].

Children with ID frequently lack the manual dexterity, cognitive processing, and executive functioning required for effective self-performed plaque control [14]. Consequently, the practical responsibility for their oral health resides almost entirely with their parents or primary caregivers. The caregiver’s role is multifaceted: it encompasses not only securing access to professional dental services but also the daily execution or active supervision of oral hygiene routines, dietary management, and recognition of early pathological signs [15]. Therefore, the caregiver’s own oral health knowledge emerges as a critical and modifiable predictor of the child’s oral health outcomes [16]. Because the success of daily oral care and disease prevention depends directly on the caregiver’s understanding of these practices, their level of oral health knowledge is fundamental to maintaining the child’s well-being. Recognizing this vital connection, the present study specifically aims to assess the oral health knowledge and awareness levels of these primary caregivers.

Despite the well-established clinical disparities, research has predominantly focused on characterizing the oral health status of children with ID (e.g., DMFT scores, Community Periodontal Index values) rather than investigating the upstream cognitive and behavioral determinants residing within their caregivers [17, 18]. There is a notable paucity of studies directly comparing the periodontal knowledge, awareness of warning signs, and supervision practices of caregivers of children with ID against a reference group of parents of neurotypical children. Such comparative data are essential for designing targeted, evidence-based interventions.

The present study was therefore designed to address this gap by exploring whether caregivers of children with ID exhibit lower levels of periodontal knowledge, less favorable personal oral hygiene habits, and less effective child-directed supervision practices compared to caregivers of neurotypical children. Specifically, the study aimed to: (i) compare the sociodemographic profiles and their potential influence on oral health awareness; (ii) quantify the disparity in periodontal knowledge across multiple domains; (iii) evaluate differences in personal oral hygiene behaviors; and (iv) characterize and compare caregiver supervision strategies for children’s oral care in the province of Kocaeli, Türkiye.

Materials and methods

Study design and ethical approval

This cross-sectional comparative study was conducted in Kocaeli, Türkiye, between September 2023 and March 2024, in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013 revision) and the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies [19]. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Kocaeli University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: GOKAEK 2023/14.18). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment.

Participants and sampling

The study population consisted of primary caregivers (parents or legal guardians) of children aged 6–12 years residing in the Kocaeli province. Two groups were established:

Study group (ID)

A total of 201 primary caregivers of children with a documented diagnosis of intellectual disability (mild to severe, per ICD-10 criteria) were recruited from Special Education and Rehabilitation Centers affiliated with the Ministry of National Education. Eligibility required that the respondent be the primary caregiver directly involved in the child’s daily care routines.

Control group (Neurotypical)

A total of 201 parents of systemically healthy, neurotypical children presenting for routine dental examination at the Kocaeli University Faculty of Dentistry were recruited as controls. Children in this group had no diagnosed neurodevelopmental, psychiatric, or systemic conditions known to affect periodontal health. It should be noted that recruitment from a dental clinic setting may introduce selection bias toward a population with higher-than-average oral health awareness, which could overestimate the magnitude of intergroup differences.

Exclusion criteria

Respondents who were not the legal guardian or primary caregiver; caregivers with self-reported cognitive or communication impairments precluding informed consent or reliable survey completion; and parents of children in the control group with any systemic condition known to independently affect periodontal status (e.g., leukemia, neutropenia, uncontrolled diabetes) were excluded. Caregivers of children with such systemic conditions were excluded from the control group not because clinical periodontal examination was performed, but to prevent confounding: these caregivers may have received disease-specific oral health education that could artificially elevate their periodontal knowledge, thereby reducing the validity of between-group comparisons.

Sample size estimation

A priori power analysis was performed using the G*Power software (version 3.1.9.7). Based on pilot data and the findings of Liu et al. [16], a medium effect size (w = 0.3) was assumed for the primary outcome (proportion of correct responses to periodontal knowledge questions). With a two-sided α of 0.05 and statistical power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated as 176 per group. To account for potential incomplete responses and a non-response rate of approximately 10–15%, the target enrollment was set at 201 participants per group, yielding a total sample of 402.

Data collection instrument

A structured questionnaire was developed specifically for this study, informed by instruments used in previous comparable studies among caregivers of individuals with disabilities [18, 20, 21]. The complete English-language version of the questionnaire is provided in Supplementary File 1. Content validity was assessed through expert review by two periodontologists and one public health specialist. A pilot study involving 30 participants (15 per group, not included in the final analysis) was conducted to evaluate clarity, comprehension, and feasibility; minor linguistic modifications were made accordingly. Internal consistency for the periodontal knowledge domain (Domain 2) was assessed using Kuder–Richardson Formula 20 (KR-20) for dichotomous items, yielding a coefficient of 0.80, indicating good reliability. To minimize response bias attributable to literacy disparities, all questionnaires were administered via standardized face-to-face interviews by a single calibrated researcher (GÇD).

The questionnaire comprised four domains:

Domain 1 – Sociodemographic Characteristics (11 items): Parental age, marital status, educational attainment (categorized as primary school or below, high school, and university or above), household income, child’s age and sex, and consanguinity.

Statistical analysis

All data were entered into a digital database and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequency and percentage for categorical variables, and mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) for continuous variables, depending on data distribution. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and visual inspection of histograms.

Between-group comparisons of categorical variables were performed using Pearson’s Chi-square (χ²) test. For continuous variables, the independent-samples Student’s t-test was applied when parametric assumptions were met; otherwise, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used for comparisons involving more than two independent groups. Cramér’s V was calculated for all chi-square comparisons as a measure of effect size (interpreted as small ≥ 0.10, medium ≥ 0.30, large ≥ 0.50). Crude odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals were computed for key binary outcome variables. To account for the significant baseline differences in sociodemographic characteristics between groups (Table 1), multivariable binary logistic regression analyses were performed for each knowledge item, with group membership as the primary independent variable and maternal education (university or above vs. below), household income (≥ 20,000 TRY/month vs. below), and marital status (married vs. divorced/widowed) as covariates. Adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals are reported in Supplementary Table S1. For multi-category variables with significant overall chi-square results, post hoc analyses were performed using adjusted standardized residuals to identify which specific categories contributed most to the observed differences. Cells with adjusted standardized residual values exceeding |1.96| were considered to contribute significantly (p < 0.05), while values exceeding |2.58| were considered highly significant (p < 0.01) (Supplementary Table S2). Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF); all values were below 1.35, indicating no problematic collinearity. All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Of 404 questionnaires distributed, 402 met inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis (response rate: 99.5%). The Study Group (ID) and Control Group each comprised 201 participants. Sociodemographic characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

The distribution of child age groups did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.084). However, the Study Group had a significantly higher proportion of male children (60.2% vs. 48.3%; χ² = 5.302, p = 0.021, Cramér’s V = 0.115), a finding consistent with the known higher prevalence of intellectual disability among males.

Significant intergroup differences were observed in several sociodemographic variables. The educational attainment of both mothers and fathers was significantly lower in the Study Group compared with the Control Group (p < 0.001 for both). Among mothers in the Study Group, 9.5% were non-literate and 28.4% had only primary school education, compared with 1.0% and 15.9%, respectively, in the Control Group. The proportion of university-educated mothers was 8.5% in the Study Group versus 37.8% in the Control Group.

Post hoc analyses using adjusted standardized residuals revealed that the intergroup difference in maternal education was driven primarily by the non-literate category (z = + 3.81, p < 0.01), the primary school category (z = + 3.00, p < 0.01), and the university category (z = − 6.98, p < 0.01). For family income, the < 10,000 TRY (z = + 2.69) and 10,000–20,000 TRY (z = + 3.14) categories were significantly over-represented in the Study Group (Supplementary Table S2).

Household income was significantly lower in the Study Group (p < 0.001). The proportion of families reporting income below 10,000 TRY per month (below the 2023 minimum wage) was 68.9% among Study Group families versus 31.1% among Controls. The divorce/widowhood rate was markedly higher in the Study Group (75.4% of all divorced/widowed participants were from the ID group; p < 0.001). The consanguinity rate was also significantly higher in the Study Group (77.4% of consanguineous marriages involved the ID group; p < 0.001).

Periodontal knowledge and awareness

Statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups for the majority of periodontal knowledge items (Table 2; Fig. 1). Of the 17 items assessed, 14 reached statistical significance at p < 0.001, while three items showed smaller, non-significant differences (gingival bleeding interpretation, p = 0.072; correct brushing time, p = 0.229; best interdental tool, p = 0.081). Effect sizes (Cramér’s V) ranged from 0.06 to 0.36, with the largest effects observed for disease definition (V = 0.36), periodontal disease in primary teeth (V = 0.30), and disease continuity to permanent teeth (V = 0.30). Key findings included:

Definition of periodontal disease: Only 39.3% (n = 79) of the Study Group correctly identified periodontitis as an infection of the surrounding tissues, compared with 75.1% (n = 151) of the Control Group. Notably, 52.2% (n = 105) of the Study Group selected the “Don’t know” option for this item.

Significance of gingival bleeding: When asked what gingival bleeding signifies, 43.8% (n = 88) of the Study Group correctly identified it as a sign of inflammation versus 52.7% (n = 106) of controls; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.072, Cramér's V = 0.09). Among the remaining respondents in the Study Group, 32.4% (n = 65) attributed bleeding to non-inflammatory causes (e.g., “hard brushing” or “vitamin deficiency”), and 23.9% (n = 48) selected “Don’t know.”

Multivariable analysis

To determine whether the observed knowledge differences persisted after accounting for sociodemographic confounders, multivariable logistic regression was performed for each knowledge item (Table 3; covariate details in Supplementary Table S1). After adjustment for maternal education, household income, and marital status, group membership remained a statistically significant independent predictor for 14 of 17 knowledge items (all adjusted p < 0.05). The three items that were non-significant in unadjusted analysis remained non-significant after adjustment. The adjusted OR ranged from 1.54 (oral care start time) to 8.62 (interdental cleaning importance). Marital status emerged as the strongest independent covariate, reaching significance in 11 of 17 models, while maternal education and income were significant in a smaller number of models. Crude (unadjusted) ORs were also significant for 14 of 17 items, ranging from 1.27 to 7.75. The three items that were non-significant in crude analysis (gingival bleeding, brushing time, best interdental tool) remained non-significant after adjustment. For the significant items, adjusted ORs ranged from 1.54 to 8.62.

Brushing frequency

Twice-daily brushing was reported by 75.1% of the Control Group versus 50.7% of the Study Group (p = 0.001). In the Study Group, 7.0% reported brushing only when they “remember,” and 1.0% reported not brushing at all.

Post hoc analysis revealed that the difference in brushing frequency was driven primarily by the under-representation of twice-daily brushing in the Study Group (z = − 5.06, p < 0.01) and the over-representation of those who reported never or rarely brushing (z = + 4.82, p < 0.01).

Interdental cleaning

Dental floss use was low in both groups; however, the proportion reporting “never” using floss was 64.7% in the Study Group versus 57.2% in controls (p < 0.001). Interdental brush use was similarly infrequent, with 80.1% and 84.1% of the Study and Control Groups, respectively, reporting no use.

Self-reported gingival bleeding

Frequent gingival bleeding was reported by 27.4% of the Study Group compared with 6.5% of the Control Group (p < 0.001). The proportion reporting “no bleeding” was 29.9% in the Study Group versus 44.3% in controls.

Dental visit patterns

While the time since the last dental visit did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.13), the reason for visits diverged markedly. In the Study Group, 20.4% had never visited a dentist, and 53.7% only visited for pain. In contrast, 7.5% of the Control Group reported attending regular annual check-ups compared with only 1.0% of the Study Group (p < 0.001).

Post hoc analysis confirmed that the difference was driven by the ‘never visited’ category (z = + 4.65 in Study Group, p < 0.01) and the ‘annual check-up’ category (z = − 3.22, p < 0.01).

The approach to the child’s oral care differed significantly between groups (Fig. 2). No involvement

Strikingly, 18.4% of the Study Group reported no involvement whatsoever in their child’s oral hygiene routine, compared with 5.4% of controls.

Attitudes toward primary teeth

Only 51.7% of the Study Group considered primary tooth decay to be significant, compared with 71.6% of the Control Group (p < 0.001). Among the Study Group, 34.3% explicitly selected “Don’t know,” suggesting pervasive uncertainty regarding the clinical relevance of primary dentition.

Post hoc analysis confirmed that the difference was attributable to the under-representation of the ‘important’ response (z = − 4.10) and the over-representation of the ‘don’t know’ response (z = + 4.13) in the Study Group.

Discussion

This study provides robust comparative evidence of a significant oral health knowledge deficit among caregivers of children with intellectual disabilities in Kocaeli, Türkiye. Our findings suggest a consistent pattern of lower periodontal knowledge, less favorable personal oral hygiene practices, and less effective child-directed supervision behaviors in the Study Group compared with controls. These results are consistent with our initial research question and align with the broader body of evidence indicating that children with ID face a “double burden”: biological susceptibility to oral disease compounded by the knowledge and behavioral deficiencies of their caregivers [7, 13, 21].

It should be acknowledged that the terminology used in Q1, which described periodontitis as ‘an infection of the tissues surrounding the teeth,’ does not fully align with the current consensus classification. The 2017/2018 World Workshop defines periodontitis as a chronic, multifactorial disease initiated by dysbiotic dental biofilm rather than a classical infection [1]. This framing may have introduced a degree of measurement error. Future studies should adopt terminology consistent with the current classification framework.

One notable observation in this study is the frequency with which caregivers in the Study Group did not correctly identify gingival bleeding as a sign of inflammation, although this specific difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.072). According to the 2017 World Workshop on Periodontal and Peri-Implant Diseases and Conditions, bleeding on probing is the primary objective clinical indicator of gingival inflammation and a prerequisite for the diagnosis of gingivitis [22]. In our study, 56.2% of the Study Group attributed bleeding to non-pathological causes or expressed uncertainty. This finding corroborates observations by Wyne [20], who reported similar misconceptions among parents of children with cerebral palsy in Saudi Arabia, and Liu et al. [16], who found that caregivers of children with disabilities in Taiwan frequently attributed oral symptoms to the disability itself rather than to preventable disease.

The potential clinical implications of this knowledge gap merit consideration. A caregiver who interprets gingival bleeding as a consequence of “hard brushing” is likely to reduce the frequency or force of brushing which is precisely the opposite of the required therapeutic response. This may contribute to a self-reinforcing cycle: reduced brushing leads to increased plaque accumulation, which exacerbates gingival inflammation and bleeding, which further discourages the caregiver from brushing [23]. Breaking this potential cycle may benefit from targeted educational messaging: “Bleeding gums are sick gums; brushing more gently but thoroughly is the treatment, not cessation.”

Our data suggest a socioeconomic gradient that may partly underpin the observed disparities in oral health knowledge and awareness. The Study Group had significantly lower levels of parental education, lower household income, and a higher rate of single-parent households. These findings are consistent with the broader literature on health inequities, which identifies socioeconomic status (SES) as one of the strongest predictors of health literacy and health outcomes [24, 25]. Hobdell et al. [24] demonstrated a strong inverse relationship between SES indicators and the prevalence and severity of oral diseases across populations.

An unexpected finding in the sociodemographic analysis was the over-representation of postgraduate-educated mothers in the Study Group (14.4% vs. 5.0%; z = + 3.07). This apparently paradoxical result may reflect a subgroup of caregivers who pursued further education in disability-related fields (e.g., special education, rehabilitation sciences) following their child’s diagnosis. Nevertheless, the overall educational profile of the Study Group remained significantly lower than that of the Control Group.

The economic burden of caregiving for a child with ID, including lost employment opportunities, costs of specialized education, and medical expenditures, may further deplete family resources available for preventive health behaviors [26]. Standard oral health promotion strategies, such as written brochures or clinic-based counseling, may fail to reach these families if they do not regularly access dental services. Alternative dissemination channels, through special education centers, social workers, and community health workers, warrant investigation.

The significantly higher proportion of male children in the Study Group (60.2% vs. 48.3%; p = 0.021) is consistent with the well-documented higher prevalence of intellectual disability among males. While child sex was not included as a covariate in the logistic regression models (as the primary outcomes were caregiver knowledge rather than child clinical status), a sensitivity analysis including child sex as an additional covariate confirmed that the results were robust: the significance status of all items remained unchanged (data not shown).

A key insight from this study is the stark mismatch between the supervision strategy employed and the functional needs of the child. In the Study Group, the most prevalent approach to the child’s oral care was “verbal reminding” (31.3%), followed by “supervising/watching” (29.9%). While verbal reminding may be an appropriate strategy for a cognitively intact child transitioning toward independent oral care, it is functionally inadequate for a child with ID who may have deficits in comprehension, memory, and fine motor coordination [27]. The finding that only 20.4% of caregivers in the Study Group actively brushed their child’s teeth is noteworthy, given that the literature consistently recommends assisted or “hand-over-hand” brushing for individuals with ID well into adolescence and adulthood [28, 29]. The reasons for this low rate likely include caregiver fatigue, the child’s resistance to oral care reported as a major barrier in qualitative studies [30], and a lack of training in adapted brushing techniques [31]. Future intervention programs may benefit from addressing both the knowledge gap and the practical skills required for effective assisted brushing.

Additionally, the designation of the ‘sweeping’ technique as the sole correct brushing method is debatable. A recent systematic review found no definitive evidence to support the superiority of any single brushing technique [35]. Future studies should assess fundamental brushing principles rather than prescribing a specific technique. Furthermore, Q16 designated the interdental brush as the single ‘correct’ interdental cleaning aid. However, current evidence indicates that the optimal tool depends on the individual’s interdental anatomy: interdental brushes are recommended when interproximal attachment loss has created sufficient space, whereas dental floss remains appropriate for intact interdental papillae [36]. Notably, this item did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.081).

The Social Cognitive Theory posits that observational learning (“modeling”) is a fundamental mechanism of behavior acquisition [32]. Our finding that caregivers in the Study Group had significantly poorer personal oral hygiene behaviors; lower brushing frequency, less interdental cleaning, and higher rates of self-reported bleeding is therefore of direct clinical relevance. Parents with poor oral health attitudes and behaviors are less likely to establish or enforce effective hygiene routines for their children [33]. Moreover, the high rate of self-reported gingival bleeding among caregivers in the Study Group (27.4%) suggests that these individuals are themselves living with untreated periodontal disease, further underscoring the need for family-centered interventions that address the oral health of the entire household rather than the child in isolation.

Nearly half (48.3%) of the Study Group considered caries in primary teeth to be clinically insignificant. This misconception has direct implications for oral health behavior, as caregivers who dismiss primary teeth as “temporary” are less likely to invest effort in preventive care. However, evidence clearly demonstrates that caries in the primary dentition is a strong predictor of caries in the permanent dentition [34] and that early gingivitis, if left unchecked, can establish a pathogenic microbial environment that persists into the permanent dentition [6]. Correcting this misconception should be a priority in caregiver education programs.

The present findings are broadly consistent with international data, although the magnitude of the knowledge gap varies by context. Al-Abdaly et al. [18] reported that only 33% of caregivers of disabled individuals in Saudi Arabia were aware that periodontal disease could lead to tooth loss, compared with 54.7% in our Study Group—suggesting marginally higher baseline awareness in the Turkish population. Liu et al. [16] in Taiwan reported that 67.99% of caregivers used their toothbrush until it was “unusable,” whereas in our study, 66.2% of the Study Group correctly identified a three-month replacement interval. These cross-cultural differences highlight the importance of context-specific intervention design rather than one-size-fits-all approaches.

This study has several strengths. It is one of the first to employ a matched comparative design comparing caregivers of children with ID against a reference population of parents of neurotypical children within the same geographic area, thereby controlling for regional healthcare access. The relatively large sample size (n = 402), the use of a face-to-face interviewer-administered questionnaire to minimize literacy-related bias, and the comprehensive scope of the questionnaire (spanning knowledge, personal behavior, and child-directed care) add to the robustness of the findings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged and carefully considered when interpreting these findings.

First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences; therefore, a prospective cohort design examining the longitudinal impact of caregiver literacy on children’s clinical periodontal outcomes would be a logical next step. Self-reported data may be subject to social desirability bias, although the anonymized, interviewer-administered format was designed to mitigate this. Third, and importantly, the control group was recruited from a university dental clinic, which likely introduces systematic selection bias toward parents with higher-than-average oral health awareness and dental care utilization. This may overestimate the true magnitude of the knowledge gap relative to the general population of parents of neurotypical children, and readers should interpret the reported differences with this caveat in mind. Fourth, the significant baseline differences in sociodemographic characteristics between groups—particularly maternal education, household income, and marital status—represent potential confounding variables. Although multivariable logistic regression was performed to adjust for these covariates (Supplementary Table S1), and group membership remained independently significant for the majority of outcomes, residual confounding by unmeasured variables (e.g., access to dental services, health insurance status, parental oral health status) cannot be excluded. Fifth, although internal consistency was assessed (KR-20 = 0.80), the questionnaire instrument was not subjected to more comprehensive psychometric evaluation including test-retest reliability or confirmatory factor analysis. Sixth, although the questionnaire was informed by instruments from comparable studies, it was not adapted from a formally validated instrument for the Turkish population, and this represents a limitation. Seventh, the terminology used in Q1 (describing periodontitis as an ‘infection’) does not align with the current consensus classification (2017/2018 World Workshop), which may have introduced measurement error. Eighth, the designation of a single ‘correct’ brushing technique and interdental tool was overly simplistic given current evidence [35, 36]. Ninth, given that 17 separate logistic regression models were performed, the possibility of inflated Type I error due to multiple comparisons should be acknowledged. No formal correction (e.g., Bonferroni) was applied, as the analyses were confirmatory; nevertheless, borderline significant results should be interpreted with caution. Finally, the study was conducted in a single province in Türkiye; generalizability to other regions or countries with different healthcare systems and cultural norms should be interpreted cautiously.

Conclusions

The present study suggests a notable pattern of lower levels of periodontal knowledge, personal oral hygiene practices, and child-directed supervision behaviors among caregivers of children with intellectual disabilities compared with caregivers of neurotypical children. The failure to recognize gingival bleeding as a pathological sign, the reliance on passive supervision strategies that are functionally inadequate for children with cognitive and motor impairments, and the dismissal of primary dentition as clinically insignificant represent critical, modifiable barriers to oral health.

These findings suggest that targeted, caregiver-centered oral health education programs may be beneficial, particularly those embedded within special education centers and community health services. Such programs could emphasize practical assisted brushing skills, the pathological significance of gingival bleeding, and the long-term consequences of neglecting primary teeth. Addressing the oral health knowledge and awareness of caregivers may represent an important complement to clinical care in efforts to improve the oral health of children with intellectual disabilities. However, longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to confirm these cross-sectional observations and to evaluate the effectiveness of targeted educational programs.
