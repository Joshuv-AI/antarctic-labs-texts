CRAFT: a 3D-printed modular design library for task-specific continuum robots
Authors: Puspita Triana Dewi, Chloe Pogue, Chengnan Shentu, Andrea Gotelli, Jessica Burgner-Kahrs
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Continuum robots offer safe and adaptive interaction through body compliance, yet this same property often limits their load-bearing capability. Addressing this design tradeoff typically requires application-specific designs with long development cycles. We introduce CRAFT, a 3D-printed modular design library for tendon-driven continuum robots that enables rapid mechanical reconfiguration to achieve task-specific stiffness and morphology. The library comprises six interchangeable modules with distinct stiffness profiles, directional compliance, and degrees of freedom, which can be stacked and reconfigured within minutes using inexpensive components. Each module’s behavior under bending, axial, and torsional loading is experimentally characterized. We demonstrate the generality of the approach through three representative robots: a reconfigured long teleoperated probe for aircraft-wing inspection (achieving a 41% reduction in sag), a pipe-crawling robot for confined environments (capable of navigating a 90∘ bend and a 30∘ incline), and a soft robotic hand for fragile object manipulation (achieving an 85% success rate in an egg-cracking task). Together, these results show how task-specific mechanical properties can be realized through modular composition rather than bespoke redesign, positioning CRAFT as a reusable modular design library for rapidly adaptable continuum robots.

Introduction

Continuum robots achieve motion through continuous deformation of their bodies, providing intrinsic compliance that allows safe and adaptive interaction with complex or cluttered environments. Their ability to conform to tortuous geometries has made them transformative in medicine, where small-diameter, tendon-driven or concentric-tube continuum robots navigate within the body for minimally invasive medical interventions1,2. These systems have matured to the point of commercial adoption, with clinically approved devices now used in endoscopic and endovascular procedures.

In contrast, the uptake of continuum robots in industrial domains, such as inspection, maintenance, and repair in confined environments, has remained more limited. Industrial continuum robots may appear in several morphologies, including long-reach manipulators for inspection, articulated structures for locomotion in pipes or confined spaces, and compliant fingers or grippers for safe interaction with fragile objects. Across these applications, compliance enables safe interaction with complex environments, but it also introduces challenges related to load-bearing capability, structural stability, and controllability under external loads. In longer robots in particular, insufficient stiffness can lead to gravity-induced sagging and reduced positioning accuracy, motivating design strategies that balance compliance with load-bearing performance.

Prior work has addressed the challenge of tailoring mechanical behavior in continuum robots through both variable–stiffness actuation and structural design strategies. Variable–stiffness approaches include magnetic locking3, thermally activated sleeves4,5, shape-memory alloy (SMA) springs6, dielectric elastomers7, and jamming-based sheaths8,9. While these methods can achieve substantial stiffness modulation, they introduce practical constraints, including high voltages, elevated operating temperatures, bulky pneumatic infrastructure, or increased system complexity. Moreover, their effectiveness is fundamentally constrained by the length and integration of the stiffening mechanism itself.

Structural design innovations offer an alternative by embedding stiffness directly into the robot morphology. Examples include adapted tendon routing10,11, multi-backbone designs12, rhombus-patterned architectures13, morphing lattice structures14, origami-inspired structures15,16, cruciform configurations17, and tensegrity-based continuum robots that program stiffness distribution across serial modules18,19. Although these approaches can improve load-bearing capability or deformation controllability, they typically rely on application-specific architectures and do not directly support reconfiguration of mechanical behavior through interchangeable modules.

A common limitation across both actuation-based and structural approaches is that they remain inherently application-specific. Adapting a continuum robot to new task requirements often necessitates substantial redesign of the backbone geometry, actuation layout, or fabrication process. As a result, each new application incurs significant development time and cost, hindering rapid iteration and broader deployment.

A modular design paradigm offers a path beyond this trade-off20. A systematic library of reconfigurable modules with distinct mechanical behavior could enable rapid prototyping and task-specific adaptation without redesigning the robot from first principles. Several recent studies have explored modular or scalable continuum robot designs21–24. However, these efforts primarily emphasize structural reusability, fabrication scalability, or application-specific embodiments, rather than enabling systematic, task-driven tuning of stiffness and morphology through mechanical reconfiguration.

In this work, we introduce CRAFT (Continuum Robots with Assorted Flexure-based modules for Task-specific requirements), a 3D-printed modular design library for tendon-driven continuum robots (Fig. 1). The CRAFT design library comprises six interchangeable modules spanning representative mechanical behaviors. This library builds on our prior conference papers25,26, which introduced three modules with low, medium, and high stiffness, and a parametrization approach for stiffness tuning. For CRAFT, we extend these works by introducing three new module designs enabling directional stiffness, uni-directional bending, and axial extensibility, and providing systematic mechanical characterization across bending, axial, and torsional loading. The CRAFT library supports straightforward extension with additional modules as application requirements evolve. Modules can be stacked and reconfigured within minutes using inexpensive components, enabling rapid, task-specific tuning of stiffness and morphology while maintaining a lightweight and robust structure.Fig. 1The CRAFT library.CRAFT, a 3D-printed, flexure-based, extensible modular design library that enables rapid construction of tendon-driven continuum robots with tunable stiffness and different morphologies.

The current module library spans a set of discrete representative design points rather than a continuous parameterized design space. In practice, users select modules whose experimentally characterized properties best match task requirements. Where intermediate stiffness values are required, geometric parameters of the compliant elements can be adjusted using the parametrization approach introduced in our prior work26. In this paper, we focus on establishing the modular architecture and a library of modules, while continuous inverse design of arbitrary stiffness values remains future work.

As continuum robots appear in diverse morphologies with different mechanical requirements, a modular design approach offers a promising pathway to reuse mechanical building blocks across inspection, locomotion, and manipulation systems. We demonstrate the versatility of the CRAFT library through three representative robots: a long teleoperated probe for aircraft-wing inspection, a pipe-crawling robot for confined-space locomotion, and a soft robotic hand for fragile object manipulation. Together, these examples show how task-specific stiffness, morphology, and function can be achieved through modular assembly rather than bespoke redesign, positioning CRAFT as a general design library for rapidly adaptable continuum robots.

Results

The low-stiffness module exhibits low bending and axial rigidity (EI = 0.0024N m2, EA = 43.75N) and torsional rigidity comparable to the extensible module. Its high compliance makes it suitable for applications requiring large bending deformations, such as navigating tight turns or highly constrained environments. In contrast, the medium-stiffness module approximately doubles bending, axial, and torsional rigidity relative to the low-stiffness design, consistent with its increased number of flexure elements. This balance of flexibility and load resistance makes it well suited for segments that must both bend and transmit moderate forces. The high-stiffness module provides the greatest resistance to deformation, with the highest measured bending and torsional rigidity (EI = 0.028N m2, GJ = 0.38Nm rad−1) and axial rigidity comparable to that of the most axially stiff module. This module is therefore well suited for base segments that must counteract large bending moments and gravitational loads. Two modules introduce directional mechanical behavior. The directional-stiffness module exhibits anisotropic bending response, with different flexural rigidities depending on the direction of loading. Its stiffness values lie between those of the low- and medium-stiffness modules, but vary across axes, enabling selective resistance to deformation based on loading direction. The uni-directional stiffness module shows pronounced anisotropy, with more than an order-of-magnitude difference in bending stiffness between orthogonal axes and the highest axial rigidity of all tested modules (EA = 1150.19 N). This combination enables bending in a single plane while strongly resisting axial and out-of-plane deformation. Finally, the extensible module provides a distinct mechanical function, combining the lowest bending and axial rigidity (EI = 0.00078N m2, EA = 6.8 N) with large axial extension. This behavior enables contraction–extension motions suitable for locomotion mechanisms, such as inchworm-style crawling.

Together, these results demonstrate that the CRAFT library comprises modules with differentiated, experimentally characterized behavior across multiple loading modes. This diversity provides the mechanical basis for reconfiguring stiffness, compliance, and directional response at the system level through module selection and ordering, without redesigning individual components.

Mechanical reconfiguration enables task-specific stiffness profiles

The modular design of CRAFT allows stiffness and morphology to be tuned through module selection and ordering, without redesigning or refabricating the robot structure. This section demonstrates how mechanical reconfiguration alone can be used to adapt a continuum robot to task-specific requirements, using an aircraft wing inspection scenario as a representative example.

Reconfiguration strategy

CRAFT modules can be combined into homogeneous or heterogeneous segments by varying the type, number, and order of modules along the robot body. Homogeneous segments comprise repeated instances of the same module type and exhibit uniform mechanical behavior, whereas heterogeneous segments combine different modules to locally tailor stiffness and compliance. Individual modules can be removed, replaced, or reordered without disassembling the entire robot, enabling rapid reconfiguration using the same physical components and actuation hardware. The module assembly process and on-the-fly reconfiguration capability are described in Section “Fabrication, scaling, and assembly” and demonstrated in Supplementary Video 1.

Wing inspection robot reconfiguration

We evaluate the mechanical reconfiguration capability of CRAFT using a long tendon-driven continuum robot previously developed for aircraft wing inspection26. The robot is 816 mm long with three segments (base composed of 12 high-stiffness modules, middle composed of 2 medium-stiffness modules, and tip segment of 3 low-stiffness modules). Each module has a height of 48 mm at a diameter of 32 mm. In its original configuration26, the robot exhibited gravity-induced sagging, particularly in the middle and distal segments, which reduced load-bearing capability and degraded teleoperation stability.

To mitigate sagging while preserving bending dexterity, the mechanical configuration of the bendable segments was modified using CRAFT modules. Specifically, low-stiffness modules in the tip segment were replaced with directional-stiffness modules, and medium-stiffness modules in the middle segment were replaced with uni-directional stiffness modules. These substitutions were selected to increase stiffness along the gravity-loaded direction while maintaining compliance in the inspection plane. The dimensions of the directional-stiffness modules were adapted using the parametrization model introduced in prior work26, with the goal of retaining sufficient bending range to reach inspection targets. No changes were made to the base segment, actuation unit, tendon routing, or control interface.

Figure 5 (left) shows an overlay of the original and reconfigured robot shapes under self-weight. The reconfigured robot exhibits a reduction in combined sag of the middle and distal segments from approximately 55 to 33 mm, corresponding to a reduction of about 41%. All measurements were performed with zero tendon tension to isolate the structural response of the robot. A single module library supports distinct robot morphologies

The diversity of mechanical behaviors available within the CRAFT module library enables the construction of continuum robots with markedly different morphologies and functions using the same underlying components. To demonstrate this generality, we assembled three robots that target distinct classes of tasks: long-reach inspection, confined-space locomotion, and delicate yet forceful manipulation.

The first robot is a long tendon-driven continuum robot designed for aircraft wing inspection (Fig. 5). Its morphology prioritizes reach and directional stiffness to mitigate gravitational sag while maintaining sufficient bending capability for teleoperated inspection. This configuration combines high-stiffness modules in the base with directionally biased modules in the middle and distal segments.

The second robot targets in-pipe inspection and locomotion (Fig. 6). In this case, extensible modules are used to realize contraction–extension cycles that enable inchworm-style crawling. Task-level performance emerges from morphology selection

The previous sections establish that CRAFT modules provide differentiated, experimentally characterized mechanical behavior and can be reconfigured into distinct robot morphologies. We now show that these mechanical choices translate directly into task-level performance improvements.

Wing inspection reach

For aircraft wing inspection, task performance is defined by the robot’s ability to reach and inspect internal structural features, including ribs and spars, while maintaining stable and intuitive teleoperation. In prior work, a long tendon-driven continuum robot achieved inspection of internal wing structures up to the third panel26. While this demonstrated the feasibility of the task, further extension of the robot increased sensitivity to gravity-induced deformation, placing greater demands on teleoperation.

Building on the reduced sag demonstrated in Section “Wing inspection robot reconfiguration”, the reconfigured robot was able to extend inspection beyond the third panel and reliably reach the fourth panel while maintaining sufficient bending capability (Fig. 5, right). In particular, the robot could bend to inspect the rear side of ribs after passing through access openings with improved operational stability and reduced need for compensatory input from the operator. This improvement was achieved without modifying the actuation hardware or increasing control complexity.

The inspection experiment, including teleoperated navigation within the wing, visual inspection of internal structures, and comparison between the original and reconfigured robot, is shown in Supplementary Video 2. During the retraction of the robot, occasional contact with the wing structure was observed, which led to small disturbances in the camera view. Since the morphology was selected to reduce sag while preserving inspection compliance, rather than to resist contact during retraction, more targeted redistribution of directional or anisotropic modules is a promising route to improving retraction robustness. These results demonstrate that mechanical reconfiguration using CRAFT modules extends the effective reach of long continuum robots for inspection tasks, complementing and advancing the task-level capabilities demonstrated in prior work26.

Locomotion through bends and inclines

In the pipe inspection task, performance is defined by the robot’s ability to traverse confined geometries rather than positioning. The crawling robot assembled from extensible and stiffer CRAFT modules successfully navigated a pipe containing straight sections, a 90∘ bend, and a 30∘ incline (Fig. 6). Locomotion was achieved through contraction–extension cycles enabled by the extensible modules, while selectively stiffer modules provided propulsion and force transmission.

The robot traversed the entire pipe length using passive steering, without active shape control. During a representative trial, the average crawling speed was approximately 1.2 mm s−1 in straight sections, 1.0 mm s−1 in the 90∘ bend, and 0.53 mm s−1 in the inclined section. These results demonstrate proof-of-concept locomotion across varying pipe geometries using the module library. A systematic characterization of locomotion performance is left to future work.

The crawling motion sequence and traversal of straight, curved, and inclined pipe sections are shown in Supplementary Video 3.

Reliable manipulation of fragile objects

For the egg-cracking task, performance requires both gentle grasping and the ability to transmit high impact forces. A soft robotic hand assembled from uni-directional stiffness modules was used to perform repeated egg-cracking and separation cycles (Fig. 7). The module selection provided bending motion predominantly in a single plane, combined with high axial stiffness for impact transmission.

Across 13 consecutive trials, the system successfully cracked 12 eggs and achieved complete cracking and separation in 11 cases, corresponding to an overall task success rate of 84.6%. Analysis of the unsuccessful trial indicates that failure was primarily due to egg misalignment. The egg was not centered and oriented as intended relative to the fingers, leading to off-axis force transmission rather than structural limitations of the modules.

The egg-cracking and separation process is shown in Supplementary Video 4.

Summary

Across these three tasks, performance is achieved by selecting and arranging modules to realize task-appropriate stiffness, compliance, and directional behavior. While the actuation systems differ across the three robots, in each case the module-level mechanical design provides the primary means of tailoring load-bearing response, locomotion behavior, and manipulation capability.

Discussion

This work introduced CRAFT as a modular design library for tendon-driven continuum robots, enabling task-specific mechanical adaptation through reconfiguration and geometric scaling of a small set of reusable modules. Rather than optimizing a single robot morphology for a specific application, CRAFT shifts the design process toward composing mechanical behavior from characterized building blocks. The results demonstrate that this approach supports empirical tuning of stiffness, compliance, and directional response through characterized module selection. These properties translate into task-level performance across different robotic applications.

A central implication of this work is that mechanical reconfiguration can serve as an effective design lever for continuum robots operating under varied loading and interaction conditions. In the wing inspection example, directional and unidirectional stiffness modules mitigated gravity-induced sag without compromising dexterity. In the pipe crawling example, extensible modules enabled locomotion through confined geometries using passive steering, reducing control complexity. In the manipulation example, unidirectional stiffness modules allowed a single structure to accommodate both gentle grasping and high-impact loading. Across these cases, performance emerged from morphology selection and module arrangement, highlighting the value of treating mechanical behavior as a composable design element.

CRAFT complements, rather than replaces, existing approaches to stiffness modulation in continuum robotics. Variable-stiffness actuation mechanisms and application-specific structural designs remain powerful tools, particularly where continuous or dynamic stiffness tuning is required. By contrast, CRAFT targets scenarios where rapid prototyping, reuse, and task-driven mechanical adaptation are priorities. The library does not require specialized actuation hardware or materials, and its reliance on additive manufacturing supports low-cost fabrication, repair, and iteration. This positions CRAFT as a practical alternative for early-stage design exploration and deployment in environments where specialized solutions are costly or impractical.

The modular structure of the CRAFT library also opens new research directions in understanding how combinations and ordering of modules influence task-level performance. For example, future studies could investigate how different module configurations affect locomotion efficiency and speed in crawling robots, load-bearing capability and sag mitigation in long inspection manipulators, or force transmission and grasp stability in manipulation tasks.

The current work also has limitations. The module library is intentionally small and spans discrete representative design points rather than a continuous inverse-design space which may be relevant to continuum robotics. Synthesis of arbitrary intermediate stiffness values from geometric parameters remains future work. Fatigue testing was limited to a representative module and loading condition, and longer-term durability under diverse operational profiles remains to be explored. The fatigue behavior may differ across module types, particularly those incorporating alternative compliant elements such as leaf springs. In addition, while geometric scaling preserves relative mechanical trends, absolute stiffness values depend on material choice and print quality. The mechanical characterization is likewise based on single-sample measurements: the reported rigidities should be read as comparative descriptors that distinguish modules and inform selection rather than as precise, validated design constants, and absolute values should be re-established when required for a specific material, scale, and fabrication process.

CRAFT does not yet include a validated model that predicts the shape or sag of arbitrary assembled robots directly from module properties alone, nor an inverse-design strategy for synthesizing arbitrary intermediate stiffness values. Future work will focus on expanding the module library, exploring alternative materials, integrating sensing within modules, and formalizing design guidelines that map task requirements to module selection and arrangement. Lastly, the robotic systems presented in this paper are proof-of-concept embodiments illustrating the range of morphologies achievable with the module library rather than optimized solutions for each individual application.

Overall, CRAFT provides a structured pathway toward modular, reusable, and mechanically programmable continuum robots. By decoupling mechanical design from application-specific embodiment, the library lowers the barrier to adapting continuum robots to new tasks and environments, and offers a foundation for further integration with modeling, control, and learning-based design tools.

Methods

This section describes the design principles, fabrication procedures, and experimental protocols used to develop and evaluate the CRAFT modular design library. We first outline the mechanical design rationale underlying the module library, followed by a description of the module components, fabrication, scaling, and assembly. We then detail the experimental methods used for mechanical characterization, library extension, fatigue testing, and the robotic example application setups.

Design rationale and mechanical primitives

The design of the CRAFT modular design library is guided by the need to enable rapid, task-specific adaptation of continuum robots without requiring application-specific redesign. Rather than optimizing a single robot morphology, the goal is to provide a small set of reusable mechanical building blocks whose behavior can be combined and reconfigured to span a broad range of continuum-robot tasks.

During the design phase, we identified a set of fundamental mechanical primitives that commonly arise in continuum robotic applications. Based on prior work in continuum and soft robotics, four primitives were selected as particularly relevant: high compliance for large bending deformations, increased stiffness for load-bearing and sag mitigation, directional stiffness to bias deformation along preferred axes, and axial extensibility to enable contraction–extension motions. These primitives inform the subsequent design of the module library.

All modules are designed to support large curvature without self-contact, exhibit distinct, experimentally characterized behavior under bending, axial, and torsional loading, and remain lightweight to minimize gravitational sag in long structures. In addition, the modules are required to be compatible with additive manufacturing using rigid materials, enabling low-cost fabrication, reproducibility, and geometric scaling across different robot sizes.

The resulting library adopts a consistent design language based on compliant flexure mechanisms combined with disk–ring assemblies. Variations in component composition and arrangement are used to realize different mechanical primitives while maintaining compatibility across modules. This approach allows individual modules to be combined, reordered, or replaced to tailor the mechanical response of a continuum robot at the system level, without altering the underlying design principles.

Module components and unit configurations

The CRAFT modules are constructed from a small set of compliant components that can be combined in different arrangements to realize distinct mechanical behaviors. All modules share a common outer geometry and interface, enabling interchangeability and compatibility during assembly.

Design exploration and selection of the Octo-Flex backbone

To realize the mechanical primitives identified in Section “Design rationale and mechanical primitives,” several candidate compliant patterns were considered as potential backbones for the CRAFT modules27–29. These patterns include auxetic, compliance, chiral, shear, shear-and-rigid array, and Flex-16 geometries (Fig. 8, top). Among the considered patterns, the Flex-16 compliant mechanism29 provided the most suitable balance of these requirements. Its distributed flexure architecture enables large angular deformation while maintaining geometric symmetry and predictable motion. To integrate this mechanism into a modular continuum-robot architecture, the Flex-16 geometry was adapted through a sequence of design modifications (Fig. 8, bottom). These modifications include the introduction of a central opening to support tendon routing, removal of unnecessary flexure elements, and extension of rigid ends to interface with disk components. The resulting geometry, referred to as the Octo-Flex, preserves the large-displacement rotational behavior of Flex-16 while enabling stacking, tendon actuation, and modular assembly.

Module components

The primary compliant element used in most modules is the Octo-Flex as described above. The Octo-Flex consists of eight flexures arranged in series, allowing large angular deformation while distributing strain across multiple elements to reduce local stress concentrations. A central opening is incorporated to permit tendon routing or integration of tools and sensors.

Rings are placed along the length of each module to connect multiple Octo-Flex elements and promote coordinated deformation during bending. In addition, the rings define a central channel that facilitates tendon routing through consecutive modules, reducing bending-coupling effects in multi-segment tendon-driven continuum robots.

Disks are positioned at the proximal and distal ends of each module to provide structural alignment and mechanical interfaces for stacking. The disks include attachment features for tendons as well as holes for rivets, which are used to connect adjacent modules. This design enables individual modules to be removed or replaced without rerouting tendons.

Several auxiliary compliant components are used to modify the mechanical response of specific module types. Reinforcement fillers are placed along the outer circumference of a module to increase its effective moment of inertia. Their internal geometry is designed to exhibit near-zero Poisson’s ratio, limiting lateral contraction under axial loading. Leaf springs are used to constrain bending motion to a preferred plane while remaining compliant in the orthogonal direction. Parallel springs consist of multiple slender members arranged in parallel to enable large axial extension while maintaining a compact footprint. All components and their key design parameters are shown in Fig. 2, top. The geometric parameters of the compliant elements (e.g., flexure thickness t and width w) were selected to realize representative stiffness regimes within the module library while remaining compatible with fabrication constraints. Where applicable, prior parametrization models26 informed the selection of these dimensions, while other values were chosen to span distinct mechanical behaviors across the module set.

Module configurations

By combining the above components in different arrangements, six module types were realized (Fig. 2, bottom). Three modules are derived from a common base design that employs Octo-Flex elements: a low-stiffness module, a medium-stiffness module, and a high-stiffness module. These variants differ in the number of Octo-Flex elements and the inclusion of reinforcement fillers, resulting in progressively increased stiffness while preserving bending capability.

Two modules introduce directional mechanical behavior. The directional-stiffness module incorporates asymmetric placement of Octo-Flex elements to create anisotropic bending response, whereas the uni-directional stiffness module integrates leaf springs to restrict bending to a single plane while providing high axial stiffness.

The extensible module differs from the other variants in that it does not employ Octo-Flex elements. Instead, it uses parallel spring components to achieve large axial deformation, enabling contraction–extension motions suitable for locomotion mechanisms.

All module types are designed to be geometrically scalable and mechanically compatible, allowing them to be combined within a single continuum robot despite differences in internal structure. While these six modules provide a representative set of mechanical behaviors, the library is designed to be extensible. The principles used to design additional modules are described in the following section.

Expansion of the module library

The CRAFT library is designed to be extensible so that additional modules can be introduced as new application requirements arise. The design of new modules should follow the same principles used to construct the current library.

Mechanical primitives: Each module is intended to realize a mechanical primitive commonly encountered in continuum robots. In this work, the module set was designed around primitives such as high compliance for large bending deformation, increased stiffness for load-bearing and sag mitigation, directional stiffness to bias deformation along preferred axes, and axial extensibility for contraction–extension motions. New modules can be introduced by designing compliant mechanisms that realize additional primitives or extend the achievable range of these behaviors.

Geometric parametrization: Module behavior is governed primarily by the geometry of the compliant elements (e.g., flexure thickness, width, and length). These parameters can therefore be adjusted to achieve different stiffness regimes. Where available, parametrization models such as the one introduced in ref. 26 can guide the selection of geometric dimensions to meet specific mechanical requirements.

Interface compatibility: All modules share a common external geometry and interface, including disk–ring assemblies and a central tendon-routing channel. These constraints ensure that newly designed modules remain mechanically compatible with existing ones and can be stacked interchangeably within the same robot structure.

Characterization: Any new module introduced into the library should undergo the same mechanical characterization protocol, described in Section “Mechanical characterization below, to quantify its bending, axial, and torsional behavior.” This ensures that the mechanical properties of new modules are experimentally validated and directly comparable with those of existing modules. As with the present library, this characterization is intended to establish a module’s relative placement within the design space for comparative selection rather than to provide validated absolute properties; the single-sample, vision-based procedure used here identifies the stiffness regime a module occupies but does not by itself quantify variability or repeatability. As the characterization methodology matures, modules added in future work would benefit from repeated, multi-sample measurements.

The specific dimensions, materials, and fabrication parameters used for the initial set of six modules in the CRAFT library are described in the following sections.

Fabrication, scaling, and assembly

The CRAFT modules are designed to support low-cost, reproducible fabrication while enabling geometric scaling and rapid mechanical reconfiguration. All module components are compatible with additive manufacturing using rigid polymer materials, allowing the same design concepts to be applied across a range of robot sizes and applications.

Fabrication

Module components were fabricated using fused deposition modelling (FDM) or stereolithography (SLA), depending on the target module scale (Fig. 9). For the majority of modules used in this paper, FDM printing was performed on an Ultimaker S5 printer. A 0.25 mm print core and a layer height of 0.1 mm were selected to resolve fine geometric features, with 100% infill used to ensure consistent mechanical behavior. Disks and rings were printed using polylactic acid (PLA), as these components primarily provide structural alignment and do not experience significant flexural strain. Octo-Flex elements, reinforcement fillers, leaf springs, and parallel springs were printed using polycarbonate (PC) to improve impact resistance and durability under repeated bending. For small-scale modules, SLA printing was used to eliminate manual assembly and enable finer feature resolution. In this case, modules were printed as monolithic structures using a Formlabs printer with a layer height of 0.025 mm. SLA modules were oriented horizontally to simplify support removal. Resin selection was constrained to non-brittle materials to avoid fracture of compliant flexure elements during post-processing.

Geometric scaling

All module designs are parametrized and geometrically scalable while preserving their underlying mechanical principles. In this work, module diameters were adapted to match task requirements. The wing inspection robot employed modules with a diameter of 32 mm to support long reach and load-bearing behavior, the pipe-crawling robot used modules with the same diameter, and the soft robotic hand used scaled-down modules with a diameter of 22 mm to achieve a compact, hand-sized morphology. Scaling was applied uniformly to component geometry to preserve the underlying mechanical design principles and relative stiffness trends across module types. However, absolute stiffness values are not preserved under scaling, as they depend on both geometric scaling laws and material properties. In particular, differences in fabrication processes and materials (e.g., FDM versus SLA) can lead to variations in mechanical response.

Assembly and reconfiguration

Modules are assembled by stacking individual units along the robot body and connecting them using split-shank rivets inserted through holes in the end disks. This connection method provides sufficient structural integrity while enabling individual modules to be removed, replaced, or reordered without disassembling the entire robot.

The assembly process of a single module begins with constructing the base low-stiffness module by bonding Octo-Flex elements between two disks and inserting rings along the module length. Additional components, such as reinforcement fillers or springs, are then added to form other module variants. The extensible module follows the same assembly procedure but replaces Octo-Flex elements with parallel springs. The assembly of a single module is demonstrated in the first part of Supplementary Video 1.

Tendons are secured at designated attachment points on the segment they actuate. Within an actuated segment, tendon routing at the module interface is guided by arc-shaped slots located at the edge of each disk (Figs. 2 and 10). When adjacent modules are assembled, the interfacing disks form closed tendon channels that confine the tendon path. These channels are secured by inserting rivets through the disk interfaces, which mechanically lock the modules together and prevent tendon escape during operation (Fig. 10).Fig. 10Tendon routing and securing mechanism.Tendons are guided by arc-shaped slots at the edge of each disk. For multi-segment robots, tendons that actuate distal segments pass through the central channel of previous segments until reaching their target segment, where they are routed through the disk slots as described above. During module replacement, it is not necessary to redeploy the entire tendon length. Mechanical characterization

Mechanical characterization was performed to quantify the bending, axial, and torsional stiffness of individual CRAFT modules (Table 1, Figs. 3 and 4). In this work, the terms flexural rigidity (EI), axial rigidity (EA), and torsional rigidity (GJ) refer to equivalent module properties derived from the measured load-displacement responses using simplified beam and torsion relations. The term stiffness is used more generally to describe the resistance of a module or robot segment to deformation. All tests were conducted under quasi-static conditions using controlled loads and fixed boundary conditions. For each test, a new prototype was used to avoid the influence of prior deformation or fatigue.

Measurement procedure

Module deformation was measured using a camera-based approach. Prior to testing, the camera view was calibrated using a measurement grid to minimize distortion near the image center, where the module was positioned. For each loading condition, images were captured in two states: the undeformed reference configuration and the deformed configuration under load. Images were imported into Fusion 360 (Autodesk, Inc.) and scaled by matching the module length in the image to its measured physical length. Tip displacements and angular rotations were then obtained by manually tracing the marked reference points.

Bending stiffness

For bending tests, modules were mounted horizontally with one end rigidly fixed and the other end free. Calibrated masses ranging from 10 to 200 g were incrementally applied at the free end, producing lateral tip deflection. For modules with symmetric geometry (low-stiffness, medium-stiffness, high-stiffness, and extensible), bending was tested along a single axis. Modules with asymmetric geometry were tested along multiple axes to capture directional behavior. The experimental setup is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1a.

Repeatability considerations

Each characterization test was conducted once per module type. The reported flexural, axial, and torsional rigidities should therefore be interpreted as representative single-sample measurements obtained under controlled boundary conditions, rather than as a statistical characterization of variability. Each value nonetheless reflects the load-displacement response measured across a range of incrementally applied calibrated loads rather than a single applied load (Figs. 3 and 4). These responses are reasonably smooth across the applied ranges, providing internal consistency for each measurement and supporting the linear-elastic relations used to extract each rigidity. This consistency across the load range does not, however, capture sample-to-sample variability arising from fabrication tolerances, material inconsistencies, assembly conditions, or the image-based tracking procedure. Statistical variability and error bars are not reported in this study. Future work will include repeated measurements across multiple fabricated modules and improved measurement methods to more rigorously quantify variability and repeatability.

Accordingly, the rigidities reported in Table 1 are therefore intended as comparative descriptors that place each module within the stiffness range of the library rather than as absolute constants for precise design calculation. The modules occupy distinct stiffness regimes spanning more than an order of magnitude in bending and axial rigidity, a separation large enough that module selection is unaffected by the variability expected from fabrication and measurement. Where absolute values are needed for predictive modeling, they should be re-established for the specific material, scale, and fabrication process in use.

Fatigue testing

Fatigue testing was conducted to obtain a baseline understanding of the durability of the compliant flexure architecture used in the CRAFT modules. The low-stiffness module was selected for this test because it represents the baseline Octo-Flex geometry shared across several module types. While this experiment provides insight into the fatigue behavior of the flexure elements under repeated bending, it should not be interpreted as a comprehensive durability assessment of the entire module library.

The tested module was fabricated from PC, chosen for its higher impact resistance compared to PLA while maintaining similar stiffness characteristics. The module was mounted vertically with one end rigidly fixed and the other end free. A tendon routed through the tendon hole of the module was connected to an Instron ElectroPuls E1000 all-electric dynamic testing system operating in displacement control mode.

Cyclic bending was applied using a sinusoidal displacement profile with a frequency of 0.2 Hz and an amplitude of 5 mm, corresponding to tendon displacements between 0 and 10 mm. The test was conducted for 10,000 bending cycles. Throughout the experiment, the applied tendon force and tendon displacement were recorded continuously. The experimental setup is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1d.

Representative force and displacement histories are shown in Fig. 11. During testing, the maximum recorded force reached 3.14 N, corresponding to a bending angle of approximately 64.4∘. The force-cycle history revealed an initially stable response, followed by gradual reductions in peak force consistent with the accumulation of material damage. A noticeable reduction in maximum force beginning after several hundred cycles is visible in Fig. 11 and likely reflects early-stage flexure degradation and local stiffness loss prior to visible fracture. Post-test inspection identified multiple fracture locations along the flexure regions of the module, indicating progressive crack initiation and propagation during cyclic loading. Robotic application experimental setups

Three robotic systems were constructed using CRAFT modules to evaluate the library across distinct application contexts: long-reach inspection, confined-space locomotion, and manipulation of fragile objects. This section describes the experimental setups used for each application.

Aircraft wing inspection setup

The wing inspection experiments were conducted using a physical mock-up of an aircraft wing modeled after the wing of a Cessna 172 with a NACA 2412 airfoil profile. The mock-up included major internal structural elements such as ribs, front and rear spars, stringers, leading edge, and trailing edge. The wing was fabricated at a 1:3 scale, resulting in a span of 1571 mm. This reduced scale increases task difficulty by limiting available clearance for robot bending.

The continuum robot was actuated using a previously developed tendon actuation unit comprising eight Dynamixel XC430-T240BB-T motors mounted on a custom 3D-printed frame. The robot was teleoperated using a PlayStation DualSense controller, with a Python-based interface mapping controller inputs to motor commands for translation and bending. The robot entered the wing through a circular access port with a diameter of 40 mm located at the wing root. A camera mounted at the robot tip provided visual feedback to the operator. The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. 2.

Pipe crawling setup

The pipe crawling experiments were conducted using a tendon-driven crawling robot assembled from extensible and stiffness-modulated CRAFT modules. Actuation was provided by three Dynamixel XL430-W250-T motors mounted on a custom 3D-printed frame. One tendon actuated the front segment, two tendons actuated the middle segment, and two tendons actuated the rear segment. Tendons were fabricated from spring steel wire with a diameter of 0.38 mm.

Tendon forces were transmitted to the robot through Bowden tubes with an outer diameter of 3.2 mm, an inner diameter of 2.5 mm, and a length of 914.4 mm. The Bowden tubes were selected to provide flexibility while resisting compression, ensuring that tendon pulling resulted in forward motion rather than tube deformation.

The robot was tested inside a pipe with an inner diameter of 50mm and a total length of 826 mm. The pipe consisted of two straight sections connected by a 90∘ bend, followed by a 30∘ inclined section. The straight sections featured ribbed inner surfaces, while the bend and incline had smooth inner surfaces. The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 6 and Supplementary Fig. 3.

Egg-cracking setup

The egg-cracking experiments were conducted using a soft robotic hand assembled from unidirectional stiffness CRAFT modules and mounted on a robotic arm (Panda, Franka Robotics). The hand comprised five fingers: four bending fingers arranged symmetrically around a central axis and one centrally mounted translating finger. Each bending finger was actuated by a single Dynamixel XL430-W250-T motor via a tendon, while the central finger was actuated through a rigid linkage (Fig. 7, top left).

The fingers were fitted with rubber-covered tips to increase friction during grasping. The central finger included a foam layer beneath a rubber surface to absorb excess impact energy during cracking. The finger diameter was 22 mm, and each finger had a length of 83 mm. The fingers were mounted at an angle of 22.5∘ to provide sufficient clearance for cracking and separation.

The robotic arm was controlled using a combination of workflow-based programs implemented in Franka DESK and a Python-based teleoperation interface. Large motions between task locations were executed at 15–50% of the robot’s maximum speed and 10–50% of its maximum acceleration, while fine positioning motions were executed at 5% of both speed and acceleration. Cracking motions were performed using force thresholds of 60 N along the translational axes and 30 N m about the rotational axes. The experimental setup is shown in Fig. 7 and Supplementary Fig. 4.

Across all experiments, no modifications were made to the module designs during testing, and the same fabrication and assembly procedures described above were used.
