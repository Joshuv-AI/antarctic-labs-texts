Secondary prevention of recurrent ischemic stroke: from guidelines to precision medicine
Authors: Shiyang Liu, Siat Yee Fong, Hui Liu, Peiyang Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recurrent ischemic stroke remains a major global health challenge, accounting for substantial disability and mortality despite advances in acute management. Secondary prevention has undergone a fundamental paradigm shift—from standardized, population-based regimens toward individualized, mechanism-targeted precision medicine. This review integrates evidence from recent AHA/ASA and ESO guidelines[2,3] and landmark clinical trials including NAVIGATE ESUS[5], RE-SPECT ESUS[6], CHANCE-2[2,13], INSPIRES[18], and COMPASS[24], highlighting how etiological diagnosis and multimodal risk stratification have reshaped secondary prevention strategies. In Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source (ESUS), empirical anticoagulation has given way to systematic etiological investigation guided by advanced imaging and biomarkers. For non-cardioembolic stroke, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) exemplifies precision in patient selection, timing, and pharmacogenomic-guided drug choice. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have become the standard for atrial fibrillation–related stroke and are being explored for broader vascular protection, while lipid management now pursues earlier, lower, and more intensive LDL-C targets through combination therapy. Finally, emerging fields—including metabolomics, genomics, and artificial intelligence—are driving the next generation of risk prediction and individualized therapeutic optimization; yet a substantial translational gap persists between these research advances and their routine clinical operationalization, a theme this review addresses explicitly throughout. Together, these developments define the future landscape of precision secondary prevention, bridging evidence-based guidelines with personalized vascular medicine.

Introduction

The severity of the problem

Ischemic stroke is characterized by high rates of recurrence, disability, and mortality. Recurrent events exert a profound socioeconomic burden on patients, families, and healthcare systems worldwide [1]. Approximately 9%–15% of survivors experience a recurrence within one year post-stroke [2], a stark reality that underscores the irreplaceable importance of secondary prevention as a cornerstone for improving long-term patient outcomes.

Paradigm evolution in prevention

In recent years, with the continuous accumulation of evidence-based medical data and a deepening understanding of stroke pathophysiology, secondary prevention strategies for ischemic stroke are undergoing a profound paradigm evolution. Its core feature is the transition from previous non-individualized, standardized treatments based on broad etiological classifications (e.g., the TOAST criteria) to a new era of individualized precision medicine. This paradigm underscores individualized treatment planning—dynamically refined through etiological precision, multimodal biomarkers, and personalized risk-benefit calculus.

Nevertheless, a substantial translational gap persists between the rapid evolution of precision diagnostics and therapeutics and their operationalization in everyday clinical practice. The promise of genomic-guided antiplatelet selection, advanced vessel wall imaging, continuous cardiac monitoring, and AI-driven risk prediction is well-established in research settings; yet the infrastructure required to deploy these tools equitably and routinely remains immature in most health systems. Barriers include resource heterogeneity (high-resolution MRI availability, point-of-care genotyping platforms, implantable cardiac monitor access), workflow integration challenges, cost-effectiveness uncertainties, and the relative scarcity of implementation science data. This review therefore deliberately delineates, throughout each section, which strategies reflect current guideline-endorsed clinical practice and which represent promising but not yet fully operationalized advances—with the objective of equipping clinicians to critically appraise both the evidence and the practical feasibility of precision secondary prevention.

Purpose and structure of this review

This article aims to systematically review recent major advancements in the diagnosis, risk stratification, and core therapeutic strategies (especially antithrombotic and lipid-lowering therapies) for secondary prevention of ischemic stroke. The article will first review new insights into stroke pathophysiology, then describe the diagnostic and risk stratification tools for precision assessment, deeply analyze the latest evidence-based strategies for antithrombotic and lipid-lowering treatments, and finally outline the future landscape of the field.

This narrative review synthesizes evidence from major clinical trials published between 2018 and 2024, authoritative guidelines (AHA/ASA 2021–2024 [3], ESO 2023 [4]), and emerging research in precision medicine [2]. We conducted systematic searches in PubMed, Embase, and Cochrane Library using terms related to secondary stroke prevention, antiplatelet therapy, anticoagulation, and precision medicine.

Pathophysiological advances and epidemiological trends

Dynamic epidemiology of recurrence risk

The risk of stroke recurrence follows a non-linear distribution over time, peaking in the early period post-stroke (especially the first 90 days). Recurrence rates and patterns also differ significantly among etiological subtypes. For instance, large artery atherosclerosis (LAA) stroke often presents the highest early recurrence risk, whereas cardioembolic stroke is a dominant contributor to long-term recurrence.

Deepening understanding of key pathophysiological mechanisms

Atherosclerosis

Assessment now prioritizes“plaque vulnerability” over traditional “luminal stenosis”. Technologies like high-resolution vessel wall magnetic resonance imaging (HR-VW-MRI) have revealed that high-risk plaque features—such as intraplaque hemorrhage (IPH), thin fibrous caps, and ulceration—are key drivers of stroke recurrence, even in vessels without significant stenosis [5].

Cardioembolism

In addition to atrial fibrillation (AF), the concept of “atrial cardiopathy” has been proposed to describe a thrombogenic tendency arising from structural or functional abnormalities of the left atrium (e.g., left atrial enlargement, fibrosis) in the absence of documented AF [6, 7]. Meanwhile, the efficient detection of occult AF remains a major challenge and a key area of research in clinical practice.

Embolic Stroke of Undetermined Source (ESUS)

The understanding of ESUS has undergone a fundamental transformation [8, 9]. It is no longer considered a distinct etiological entity but rather a “working diagnosis.” It conceals highly heterogeneous embolic sources [10], primarily including.

· (i) occult AF.

· (ii) patent foramen ovale (PFO).

· (iii) non-stenotic vulnerable plaques.

· (iv) complex aortic arch plaques.

· (v) atrial cardiopathy.

Multimodal risk assessment: integration of imaging and biomarkers

Traditional clinical scores (e.g., ABCD²) have limited precision for individualized prediction, prompting the medical community to explore a multimodal assessment system that integrates imaging and biomarkers to achieve more accurate risk stratification.

The key role of novel imaging markers

High-Resolution Vessel Wall MRI (HR-VW-MRI)

This technique can non-invasively visualize vessel wall structure and plaque components, offering unique value in identifying vulnerable plaques, differentiating stroke etiologies (e.g., atherosclerosis vs. vasculitis), and guiding risk stratification for LAA stroke [5].

Cerebral Small Vessel Disease (CSVD) Imaging Markers

Imaging findings of CSVD, such as cerebral microbleeds (CMBs), enlarged perivascular spaces (EPVS), and white matter hyperintensities (WMH), are not only independently associated with stroke recurrence risk but can also predict the risk of hemorrhagic transformation associated with antithrombotic therapy [5, 11], providing crucial safety references for treatment decisions.

Frontier Imaging Technologies

Emerging AI-powered analytical tools are set to revolutionize plaque assessment. Deep learning algorithms applied to HR-VW-MRI can now automatically quantify high-risk plaque features (e.g., intraplaque hemorrhage, lipid-rich necrotic core) with superior accuracy and reproducibility, providing prognostic information beyond visual assessment [12]. This AI-based plaque phenotyping is a pivotal step toward standardized, large-scale risk stratification.

Clinical translation of novel blood biomarkers

Complementing Imaging with Molecular Precision

Blood biomarkers offer a molecular lens that augments neuroimaging, capturing dynamic pathophysiological drivers of recurrence. While MRI and CTA characterize the structural and vascular substrate of ischemic injury, circulating biomarkers can capture neuroaxonal damage, cardiac dysfunction, inflammation, and endothelial impairment in real time, enabling continuous disease monitoring and individualized therapeutic adjustment.

Clinical Value and Mechanistic Insight

Traditional clinical risk scores such as Essen and ABCD² show limited discriminative capacity (C-statistic ≈ 0.60–0.65). In contrast, quantitative biomarkers can directly measure active biological processes, reveal hidden mechanisms, and provide objective tools for early risk stratification and treatment guidance. For instance, elevated NT-proBNP reflects underlying atrial cardiopathy even in the absence of atrial fibrillation, suggesting cardioembolic potential; persistently high hs-CRP indicates chronic vascular inflammation and residual risk despite optimized secondary prevention.

Major Biomarker Categories

(1) Neuroaxonal Injury

Serum neurofilament light chain (sNfL) is a sensitive marker of axonal injury released from damaged neurons. In ischemic stroke, sNfL levels correlate strongly with NIHSS scores and infarct volume, and persistently elevated concentrations predict worse 90-day outcomes and higher recurrence risk (HR ≈ 1.6) [13, 14]. Glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), indicating astrocytic activation and blood–brain barrier disruption, provides complementary information, particularly in distinguishing large infarctions or hemorrhagic transformation [15].

(2) Cardiac Dysfunction

N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) reflects myocardial strain and atrial remodeling. Elevated NT-proBNP not only aids in detecting covert cardioembolic sources but also serves as an independent predictor of recurrent ischemic events and all-cause mortality [6, 16]. High-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn), frequently increased after stroke, indicates concurrent myocardial injury and is associated with early neurological deterioration and long-term vascular death, highlighting the brain–heart axis [16].

(3) Inflammation and Immune Activation

Inflammation is a major driver of plaque instability and secondary thrombosis. High-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), a hepatic acute-phase reactant, consistently predicts poor functional outcomes and stroke recurrence, particularly in atherosclerotic subtypes. Emerging cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) further delineate pro-inflammatory cascades linked to endothelial dysfunction and post-stroke immune imbalance [17, 18].

(4) Endothelial Activation and Coagulation

Markers of endothelial perturbation, such as von Willebrand factor (vWF) and intercellular adhesion molecule-1 (ICAM-1), indicate microvascular injury and platelet activation. Elevated D-dimer levels, reflecting ongoing fibrinolysis, are associated with recurrent embolic stroke of undetermined source (ESUS) and can guide anticoagulation decisions. The integration of these markers into multimodal predictive models enhances risk stratification beyond clinical variables alone.

(5) Lipid and Metabolic Dysfunction

Recent lipidomic and metabolomic studies have identified plasma ceramides, lysophosphatidylcholines, and branched-chain amino acids as predictors of recurrent ischemic events, linking metabolic dysregulation to endothelial stress and thrombogenesis [11, 19]. These novel metabolites complement traditional lipids, offering insights into residual vascular risk despite statin therapy.

Integrative perspective

Collectively, these biomarkers delineate interconnected biological pathways—neuronal injury, cardiac dysfunction, inflammation, endothelial activation, and metabolic stress—that drive stroke recurrence and unfavorable outcomes. The transition from single-marker assessment to integrated biomarker panels may enable more precise and personalized risk prediction.

Development of integrated prediction models

Future risk stratification will integrate clinical, imaging, and biomarker data into dynamic, individualized prediction models. Achieving this multidimensional integration relies on artificial intelligence (AI) as the critical unifying framework. Traditional statistical models are fundamentally limited when handling high-dimensional, non-linear data. In contrast, advanced machine learning algorithms and deep learning architectures (such as graph neural networks or transformer-based models) are uniquely capable of fusing heterogeneous data streams—from multi-omics profiles to vessel wall imaging. This AI-driven integration paves the way for the conceptualization of a “Digital Stroke Twin”—a dynamic, patient-specific computational model that continuously updates recurrence probabilities and simulates targeted therapeutic responses in real-time. This paradigm will guide the implementation of intensive interventions and achieve truly personalized prevention. However, translating this aspiration into routine clinical reality demands a candid appraisal of the infrastructure, logistical, and economic requirements involved.

Implementation requirements and scale considerations

The scale of what comprehensive biomarker-guided precision prevention would require across all ischemic stroke patients is considerable. Consider three illustrative domains: (1) Point-of-care pharmacogenomic testing. CYP2C19 genotyping to guide antiplatelet selection (Sect. 4.2) requires either laboratory-based platforms or, ideally, bedside rapid genotyping devices with turnaround times under 2 h. While commercial point-of-care CYP2C19 assays exist, they remain unavailable in many stroke units globally, and their cost-effectiveness depends heavily on local drug cost differentials between clopidogrel and ticagrelor. Scaling this to the global stroke burden would necessitate substantial investment in testing infrastructure, clinician training in pharmacogenomics interpretation, and reimbursement pathways. (2) Vessel wall MRI for all stroke patients. HR-VW-MRI offers unparalleled plaque characterization but requires a 3-Tesla MRI scanner, specialized acquisition protocols, expert radiological reporting, and approximately 20–45 additional minutes of scan time per patient. Deploying this as a universal standard of care for all ESUS or atherosclerotic stroke patients is currently not feasible at most centers worldwide, even in high-income settings. A more realistic near-term approach is risk-stratified deployment: reserving HR-VW-MRI for patients where etiological uncertainty persists after standard workup, or where the imaging result would directly change management (e.g., confirming vulnerable plaque in a borderline DAPT candidate). (3) Novel circulating biomarker panels. Biomarkers such as sNfL, GDF-15, and plasma ceramides require validated immunoassay platforms and laboratory accreditation. Unlike NT-proBNP or hs-CRP—which are already embedded in routine care—these emerging markers lack standardized clinical decision thresholds, insurance coverage in most jurisdictions, and clear clinical decision pathways that specify when measurement changes treatment. Until outcome-driven clinical trials demonstrate that biomarker-guided treatment intensification improves patient outcomes beyond current standards, widespread adoption cannot be justified on health-economic grounds.

Bridging the translational gap: a tiered framework

A pragmatic, tiered implementation framework can help bridge the gap between research potential and clinical reality. At the first tier, biomarkers and technologies with robust evidence and existing clinical infrastructure (NT-proBNP, hs-CRP, standard echocardiography, conventional MRI) should be integrated into routine risk stratification protocols at all certified stroke centers. At the second tier, pharmacogenomics (CYP2C19) and HR-VW-MRI should be deployed selectively, prioritized at comprehensive stroke centers with appropriate resources and directed at patient subgroups most likely to benefit (e.g., East Asian patients with minor stroke for genotyping; unexplained ESUS with negative standard workup for vessel wall MRI). At the third tier, novel investigational biomarkers (sNfL, ceramides, GDF-15) should be restricted to clinical trials and registries until implementation trials demonstrate actionable benefit. Critically, the research community must prioritize not just discovery and validation of new biomarkers, but prospective implementation science studies that quantify the health system impact, workflow disruption, costs, and patient outcomes associated with deploying precision tools at scale.

Precision management of ESUS

From empirical treatment to etiological precision

ESUS management epitomizes the shift toward precision medicine in stroke prevention. Historically, the diagnosis of ESUS prompted empirical therapeutic trials, with clinicians seeking the “optimal drug” for this heterogeneous population. Two pivotal trials—NAVIGATE ESUS [8] and RE-SPECT ESUS [9]—definitively demonstrated that routine anticoagulation in unselected ESUS patients offers no benefit over aspirin while increasing bleeding risk. These negative results catalyzed a fundamental shift in clinical approach [10].

Current guideline-endorsed practice: rejection of empirical anticoagulation

Based on Level 1 evidence from these large randomized trials, current AHA/ASA [3] and ESO guidelines [4] explicitly recommend against empirical anticoagulation for ESUS. Single antiplatelet therapy remains the first-line treatment. However, the critical insight from trial subgroup analyses is that ESUS represents not a single disease entity but a syndrome encompassing multiple distinct embolic mechanisms—including occult atrial fibrillation, patent foramen ovale, non-stenotic vulnerable plaques, complex aortic arch atheroma, and atrial cardiopathy without documented AF.

Current guideline-endorsed practice: systematic etiological investigation

Recognition of this heterogeneity has transformed clinical practice. Rather than accepting diagnostic uncertainty, the modern standard of care mandates systematic investigation to unmask the specific embolic source, enabling mechanism-targeted therapy. This approach is supported by data showing that extended cardiac monitoring detects occult AF in 15–30% of ESUS patients, while advanced cardiac imaging identifies structural sources in an additional 25–40%.

To translate this principle into clinical practice, we present a comprehensive diagnostic and treatment algorithm (Fig. 1) that integrates current evidence across three complementary investigational domains: cardiac rhythm monitoring, structural cardiac assessment, and advanced vascular imaging.

Operationalizing the algorithm: key investigational components

The diagnostic pathway shown in Fig. 1 systematically addresses each potential embolic mechanism. Below we detail the evidence base and clinical application of each component.

Extended cardiac rhythm monitoring

Objective

Detection of paroxysmal atrial fibrillation.

Evidence

The CRYSTAL-AF trial [20] demonstrated that insertable cardiac monitors detected AF in 30% of ESUS patients by 3 years.

Clinical action

Any detected AF, regardless of duration, warrants anticoagulation. Critically, the negative result of the ARCADIA trial (2024) [6]—which showed apixaban was not superior to aspirin in ESUS patients with biomarker evidence of atrial cardiopathy—underscores that the detection of AF remains the sole evidence-based indication for anticoagulation in this population. This finding reinforces that extended monitoring is the definitive strategy to identify the patients who will truly benefit from DOAC therapy.

Structural cardiac assessment

Objective

Identification of anatomical embolic sources.

Evidence

The RoPE study identified clinical and imaging features predicting PFO-attributable stroke. Subsequent trials (RESPECT [21], CLOSE, REDUCE [22]) demonstrated superiority of PFO closure plus antiplatelet therapy over antiplatelet therapy alone in carefully selected patients.

Dual Antiplatelet Therapy (DAPT)

Complementary precision: from ESUS to atherosclerotic stroke

While Sect. 4.1 addressed the diagnostic precision required for ESUS management, dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) represents a parallel success story in precision medicine for non-cardioembolic stroke—but with a fundamentally different challenge. Unlike ESUS where empirical drug therapy failed, DAPT has proven effective. The precision imperative here lies not in whether to treat, but in optimizing three critical parameters: patient selection, treatment timing and duration, and drug choice.

Current guideline-endorsed practice: temporal precision and efficacy

Establishing efficacy with temporal precision

CHANCE (2013) [23] established that clopidogrel-aspirin DAPT initiated within 24 h and maintained for 21 days reduced recurrent stroke by 32% versus aspirin alone in minor stroke patients (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.57–0.81, P < 0.001). The subsequent POINT trial (2018) [24] confirmed these findings in Western populations [25] while revealing a critical temporal pattern: benefit was concentrated in the first 21 days, whereas bleeding risk accumulated over time, establishing that duration precision is as crucial as patient selection.

Expanding access: the INSPIRES contribution

The recent INSPIRES trial (2023) [26] challenged the restrictive 24-hour treatment window. Among 5,174 patients with minor stroke or high-risk TIA and evidence of atherosclerosis, extending the initiation window to 72 h maintained efficacy (recurrent stroke at 90 days: 6.1% vs. 7.6% with aspirin alone) without increasing major bleeding. This expansion significantly broadened the eligible population while maintaining the evidence-based 21-day duration.

Pharmacogenomic precision: the CHANCE-2 breakthrough

The CHANCE-2 trial (2021) [27] ushered in the era of genotype-guided antiplatelet therapy. Among 6,412 patients carrying CYP2C19 loss-of-function alleles (which impair clopidogrel activation), ticagrelor-based DAPT reduced stroke recurrence by 26% compared with clopidogrel-based DAPT (HR 0.74, 95% CI 0.62–0.89), without significant bleeding increase. This landmark study provided Level 1 evidence for personalized drug selection.

The complexity of clinical decision-making

The accumulated evidence [23–27] establishes DAPT as highly effective, but optimal application requires navigating multiple decision points:

(1) Who qualifies? Not all stroke patients—only those with minor severity, non-cardioembolic etiology, and acceptable bleeding risk.

(2) When to initiate? Within 24–72 h depending on atherosclerosis confirmation.

(3) Which combination? Standard clopidogrel-based or ticagrelor-based regimen [28, 29].

(4) How long? Precisely 21 days—shorter loses benefit, longer increases harm.

(5) What about genetics? Should CYP2C19 testing guide drug selection?

Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

Current Guideline-Endorsed Practice: Cornerstone Role in NVAF and Early Initiation

Compared to warfarin, DOACs have comparable or superior efficacy in preventing stroke recurrence and significantly reduce the risk of major bleeding events, such as intracranial hemorrhage. They have become the first-line choice for secondary prevention in NVAF-related stroke [31, 32]. The landmark ELAN trial (NEJM 2023) [33]provides Level A evidence for early DOAC initiation. In patients with minor-to-moderate acute ischemic stroke (NIHSS ≤ 15), initiating a DOAC within 48 h was non-inferior to delayed initiation (day 7–8) [33, 34] in preventing recurrent ischemic events and significantly reduced the composite risk of recurrent stroke, systemic embolism, or vascular death (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.49–1.02), without increasing major bleeding. This supports a paradigm of early protection in selected patients without large infarcts or hemorrhagic transformation.

Emerging Precision Therapeutics: Exploration in Atherosclerotic Scenarios

For patients with stroke and concomitant stable coronary artery disease or peripheral artery disease, the strategy of very-low-dose rivaroxaban (2.5 mg BID) plus aspirin, derived from the COMPASS trial [35] and supported by subsequent stroke-specific analyses, demonstrates a significant reduction in the composite endpoint of stroke, myocardial infarction, and cardiovascular death (NNT = 66), albeit with a small increase in major bleeding. This regimen is increasingly recognized as a viable option for high-risk atherosclerotic patients with low bleeding risk.

Emerging precision therapeutics: novel non-statin agents and non-LDL-C targets

Expanding the Therapeutic Arsenal

Novel Non-Statin Agents.

For statin-intolerant patients or those requiring additional LDL-C reduction after maximally tolerated statin and ezetimibe, new therapeutic classes offer powerful options. Bempedoic acid, an ATP-citrate lyase (ACL) inhibitor, provides an additional 15–25% LDL-C reduction and, as evidenced by the CLEAR Outcomes trial [39], demonstrates cardiovascular outcome benefits. Inclisiran [40], a first-in-class small-interfering RNA (siRNA) agent that inhibits hepatic PCSK9 synthesis, enables a convenient twice-yearly dosing schedule following initial loads. This novel mechanism addresses long-term adherence challenges and ensures sustained LDL-C lowering, positioning it as a transformative option for long-term risk management.

The Value of Non-LDL-C Targets

Lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] has been identified as an important, independent, and genetically determined cardiovascular risk factor. For patients who experience recurrent events despite achieving their LDL-C target, measuring Lp(a) levels has significant clinical implications. Targeted drugs for Lp(a) (e.g., Pelacarsen) [41] are under development and may become a new therapeutic option in the future.

Blood pressure and lifestyle interventions

Blood Pressure Targets

For most stroke/TIA patients, it is recommended to maintain long-term blood pressure control at < 130/80 mmHg [3, 42]. At the same time, an individualized approach is crucial; in elderly patients or those with severe intracranial or extracranial vascular stenosis, one must be cautious about the risk of hypoperfusion induced by excessive blood pressure reduction.

Comprehensive Lifestyle Interventions

Smoking cessation, limiting alcohol intake, adopting a healthy dietary pattern (e.g., the Mediterranean diet), and engaging in at least 150 min of moderate-intensity physical activity per week are the foundation of all pharmacological treatments [2]. Their importance must be repeatedly emphasized.

Tailored approaches for complex clinical scenarios

Stroke in the Young

This group has a unique etiological spectrum, often involving PFO, arterial dissection, hereditary thrombophilias, and vasculitis, which requires a broader and more targeted etiological workup.

Stroke with Concomitant Malignancy

These patients are in a state of both high thrombotic and high bleeding risk. The choice of antithrombotic strategy is extremely challenging and requires careful, individualized decision-making in collaboration with a multidisciplinary team (MDT).

Symptomatic Intracranial Arterial Stenosis (sICAS)

The SAMMPRIS trial [43] established the central role of aggressive medical management in managing sICAS (stenosis 70%–99%). The study showed that, on top of aggressive medical management (including 90 days of DAPT), endovascular intervention was not superior to medical therapy alone in preventing recurrence. Therefore, DAPT for up to 90 days is a key prevention strategy for these patients.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Conclusion

The secondary prevention of recurrent ischemic stroke is transitioning from standardized, population-based regimens to mechanism-targeted precision strategies. Future success will depend on integrating etiological diagnostics, multimodal biomarkers, and real-time data analytics into daily clinical workflows. By bridging evidence-based medicine with emerging technologies, clinicians can achieve not only lower recurrence rates but also a new standard of individualized vascular protection.

Future directions

The next decade of stroke prevention will be shaped by three convergent frontiers:

i.Next-Generation Therapeutics: Novel agents targeting specific pathophysiological pathways—such as Lp(a) [41] and key inflammatory cytokines [18] (e.g., via interleukin-6 monoclonal antibodies)—hold imminent promise to address the residual vascular risk that persists despite current best therapy. Several agents are actively under investigation in phase II/III trials. Pelacarsen, an antisense oligonucleotide targeting hepatic Lp(a) synthesis, has demonstrated 65–80% Lp(a) reductions in phase II studies and is now being evaluated in a dedicated cardiovascular outcomes trial (Lp(a)HORIZON). Zerlasiran and olpasiran (small interfering RNA agents) represent a second wave of Lp(a)-lowering platforms with even more durable dosing intervals. On the anti-inflammatory front, colchicine, following the LoDoCo2 and COLCOT trials in coronary artery disease, is under evaluation in cerebrovascular populations; the CONVINCE trial is specifically examining its utility after recent ischemic stroke in patients with elevated inflammatory markers. Additionally, factor XIa inhibitors (e.g., abelacimab, asundexian) represent a new antithrombotic class with a potentially superior safety profile for secondary stroke prevention, targeting thrombus propagation while preserving hemostatic plugs—an approach that could redefine the anticoagulation landscape beyond current DOACs. The critical translational challenge for all these agents is patient selection: identifying, through biomarkers or genetic profiling, which individuals carry sufficient residual risk from the targeted pathway to derive net benefit over background standard-of-care therapy.

ii.Deep Phenotyping and Biomarker Integration: The transition from a one-size-fits-all model to a truly personalized strategy will be accelerated by the standardized integration of multimodal data [11, 15]. This includes the routine use of pharmacogenomics (e.g., CYP2C19 testing to guide antiplatelet choice) [27] and the combination of circulating biomarkers with advanced imaging to create dynamic, individual risk trajectories. Realizing this vision requires moving beyond isolated biomarker measurement toward integrated phenotyping platforms that simultaneously characterize the etiological substrate, the residual biological risk, and the individual patient’s pharmacological response profile.Emerging approaches include multi-omic profiling (combining genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, and metabolomics) applied to post-stroke blood samples to classify patients into biologically distinct phenotypic clusters with differential recurrence risk and treatment responsiveness. However, the clinical operationalization of these omics-based platforms—particularly metabolomics—faces profound translational barriers. Bridging this gap requires resolving the algorithmic challenges of dimensionality reduction for high-dimensional data in acute clinical settings, engineering reliable point-of-care testing (POCT) devices for rapid, targeted metabolite quantification, and rigorously proving the cost-effectiveness of routine omic profiling against standard clinical metrics. Federated learning approaches—where AI models are trained across multiple institutions without centralizing patient data—offer a pathway to generate large, diverse training datasets for phenotyping models while preserving patient privacy and complying with data governance regulations. Biomarker standardization is also an imperative: multicenter studies have demonstrated substantial inter-laboratory variability in sNfL and NT-proBNP measurements, underscoring the need for reference standards and proficiency testing before clinical deployment. Ultimately, deep phenotyping will not replace clinical judgment but will augment it, enabling a more precise and dynamic characterization of each patient’s risk trajectory at the time of stroke and at subsequent follow-up visits, thereby facilitating truly individualized escalation or de-escalation of preventive therapy.

iii.AI-Driven Clinical Intelligence: Artificial intelligence is poised to revolutionize every facet of prevention [12]—from the automated interpretation of plaque vulnerability on imaging, to the development of self-learning predictive algorithms [44], and the provision of real-time, evidence-based decision support at the point of care. AI applications are already discussed in the body of this review in two contexts: in Sect. 3.1, where deep learning algorithms applied to HR-VW-MRI are described for automated plaque characterization and risk stratification; and in Sect. 3.3, where AI-driven integration of multiomics and imaging data is framed as the enabling framework for the “Digital Stroke Twin” concept. Here, we expand on these foundations to address both how AI is currently being studied in stroke prevention research and how these tools are envisioned for clinical implementation.

How AI is Being Studied. Current AI research in stroke secondary prevention spans multiple methodological approaches. In imaging, deep learning convolutional neural networks trained on large HR-VW-MRI datasets can automatically segment and quantify high-risk plaque features (intraplaque hemorrhage, lipid-rich necrotic core, thin fibrous cap) with reproducibility and accuracy that approaches or exceeds expert radiologists, and such algorithms have been externally validated in independent cohorts. In risk prediction, machine learning models (gradient boosting, random forests, deep neural networks) integrating clinical, laboratory, imaging, and wearable sensor data have been developed and validated in retrospective stroke registries, demonstrating modest but consistent improvement in discriminative performance (AUC gains of 0.05–0.10) over traditional scores such as ABCD² and Essen. In ECG and continuous monitoring data, AI-powered atrial fibrillation detection algorithms applied to single-lead wearable ECG or photoplethysmography signals are under prospective evaluation in post-stroke populations, with several ongoing trials (e.g., STROKESTOP, AF-SCREEN) incorporating AI rhythm analysis as part of extended monitoring protocols. In clinical decision support, natural language processing applied to electronic health record data is being used to identify missed prevention opportunities (e.g., undetected AF, undertreated hyperlipidemia) in real-world stroke populations, with pilot implementations at academic stroke centers demonstrating measurable improvement in prevention metric adherence.

How AI Will Be Implemented. Translating AI tools from research to clinical workflows requires several enabling conditions. First, prospective, randomized implementation trials must demonstrate that AI-assisted clinical decisions lead to improved patient outcomes, not merely improved algorithm performance metrics—a distinction that is often overlooked. Second, regulatory pathways (FDA Software as a Medical Device, CE marking) require prospective clinical validation, transparent performance reporting across diverse populations, and post-market surveillance plans. Third, EHR integration demands that AI algorithms be embedded as decision-support modules within existing clinical information systems, providing clinician-facing outputs (flagged risk, recommended action) within standard workflows rather than as standalone tools requiring separate log-in or interface. Fourth, algorithmic bias and equity considerations must be proactively addressed: many current AI models for stroke risk are trained on predominantly European or East Asian datasets, and performance may be attenuated in underrepresented populations including Black, Hispanic, and South/Southeast Asian patients who carry a disproportionate global stroke burden. Finally, clinician education and trust-building are essential prerequisites; AI recommendations that cannot be explained in clinically meaningful terms are unlikely to be adopted regardless of their statistical performance. Explainable AI frameworks (SHAP values, attention maps) and transparent uncertainty quantification will be critical to building the clinician confidence needed for real-world implementation.

Pivotal Unresolved Questions that will guide the research agenda include

i.Defining the optimal antithrombotic strategy for the critical ESUS subgroup with biomarker or imaging evidence of atrial cardiopathy in the absence of documented atrial fibrillation.ii.Establishing patient-specific protocols for the modality, duration, and timing of prolonged cardiac monitoring to maximize the cost-effective detection of occult arrhythmias.iii.Determining the long-term benefit-risk equilibrium of ultra-low LDL-C levels (< 1.0 mmol/L), particularly regarding the potential for hemorrhagic conversion and other non-cardiovascular risks.
