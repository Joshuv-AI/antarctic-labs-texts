Heterogeneous responses to memantine in Alzheimer's disease: A precision medicine approach using iPSC models
Authors: Cristina Zivko, Ram Sagar, Waqar Ahmed, Ariadni Xydia, Federica Farinelli, Isaac Ostlund, Debamitra Das, Brady J. Maher, Constantine G. Lyketsos, Vasiliki Mahairaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONAlzheimer's disease (AD) is a complex neurodegenerative disorder characterized by progressive cognitive and functional decline. Memantine, a commonly prescribed N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate receptor antagonist, appears to slightly delay symptom progression in moderate or advanced stages of AD. Clinical response is highly variable across patients with some benefiting but others not. Induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)–derived neurons can provide a donor‐dependent model to predict the therapeutic efficacy of memantine.METHODSWe generated iPSC‐derived cortical neurons from 19 individuals (12 AD and 7 cognitively unimpaired [CU]). Assays for calcium influx and oxidative stress were developed and optimized for neurons. Memantine was tested under different treatment conditions. Neurons were exposed to glutamate/glycine to induce calcium influx and menadione to generate oxidative stress. Memantine treatment was applied either acutely (1 hour) or as a 24‐hour pre‐treatment to evaluate its neuroprotective effects.RESULTSPeripheral blood mononuclear cell–derived iPSCs were successfully differentiated into functional neurons, exhibiting comparable electrophysiological properties between AD and CU lines. Calcium influx assays revealed a heterogeneous response among AD and CU neurons, with AD neurons generally displaying higher baseline fluorescence. Memantine treatment for 24 hours significantly reduced calcium influx, with a −9.85% average reduction and a range of −0.39% to −39%. Similarly, reactive oxygen species assays showed menadione‐induced oxidative stress was attenuated by 24‐hour memantine pre‐treatment for a mean −26.05% reduction and a range of −4.23% to −72.21%. The observed variability indicates differential susceptibility to excitotoxicity reduction and or oxidative stress mitigation across lines.DISCUSSIONThis exploratory study establishes a robust in vitro platform to test memantine efficacy using iPSC‐derived neurons to model calcium dysregulation and oxidative stress in AD. The observed variability in response highlights the importance of personalized approaches in AD treatment, emphasizing the potential for iPSC‐based platforms in precision medicine.

BACKGROUND

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative condition and the leading cause of dementia globally, with an expected rise of 13.8 million cases by 2060 resulting in a considerable socioeconomic burden. 1 , 2 The AD pathogenesis involves a complex interaction of amyloid beta (Aβ) aggregation, tau hyperphosphorylation, oxidative stress, disrupted calcium homeostasis, and synaptic dysfunction, leading to extensive neuronal damage and cognitive impairment. 3 , 4 , 5 While symptomatic therapies are standard treatment, the recent US Food and Drug Administration approval of modestly effective anti‐amyloid antibodies for early AD highlights significant unmet needs across all disease stages. 6 Among the current therapeutic options for AD, memantine, a neuroprotective, non‐competitive N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor antagonist, has been widely used to mitigate symptoms by modulating glutamatergic neurotransmission, reducing excessive calcium influx, and preventing excitotoxicity induced neuronal death. 7 , 8 , 9 However, patient responses to memantine vary significantly, reflecting the heterogeneity of the disease and underscoring the need for more personalized treatment strategies. 10 , 11

In recent years, the advancement of human induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology has revolutionized the study of neurodegenerative diseases by providing a platform to model individual variability pathophysiology in vitro. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Human iPSC‐derived excitatory glutamatergic neurons offer a valuable experimental platform to investigate the cellular and molecular mechanisms that drive AD and to assess drug responses in a donor‐relevant context. Given the critical involvement of oxidative stress and calcium imbalance in AD, these models serve as a valuable system for studying how therapeutics like memantine modulate these pathological processes. Oxidative stress, arising from the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and aberrant calcium ion (Ca2 +) influx are key contributors to neuronal damage and dysfunction in AD, making them important targets for therapeutic intervention. 17

To address the variability in memantine response among AD patients, we developed an in vitro platform to measure oxidative stress and Ca2 + influx in iPSC‐derived cortical neurons. By integrating patient‐derived cells with in vitro cellular and molecular assays, we offer a new perspective on the complex pharmacodynamics of memantine in AD, highlighting the importance of individualized therapeutic approaches.

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: We reviewed existing literature in Alzheimer's disease (AD) and use of memantine. Clinical studies have consistently show that memantine provides only modest benefit in AD, with considerable variability in patient response. The existing animal and cell models often fail to capture the heterogeneity of human disease or predict individual therapeutic outcomes. There is an urgent need for human‐centric models that can bridge this translational gap by incorporating patient‐specific biology into drug testing. Though induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC)–derived neuronal models have been used to study AD mechanisms, no prior study has systematically examined their potential to predict memantine response across individuals. Interpretation: In this study, we established the largest iPSC‐derived neuronal platform to date for evaluating memantine response, generating 19 cortical lines from 12 patients with AD and 7 healthy controls. We tested memantine's effects on calcium influx and oxidative stress, the two hallmarks of AD pathology. Our findings show that iPSC‐derived cortical neurons from patients with AD display highly variable responses to memantine in calcium influx and oxidative stress assays. These results demonstrate that iPSC‐based neuronal models can capture inter‐individual differences in drug responses relevant to AD. Future directions: Larger studies integrating diverse patient cohorts and advanced models such as co‐culture systems with astrocytes or 3D organoids could provide more comprehensive insight into AD pathology and drug response. Such efforts may guide individualized treatment strategies, optimize use of memantine and other medications, and expand precision medicine approaches for AD therapeutics.

METHODS

Study participants

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were successfully isolated from the blood of 7 CU individuals and 12 patients diagnosed with AD after obtaining informed consent under the approval and oversight of the Johns Hopkins Institutional Review Board (Table 1). All samples were obtained from the Johns Hopkins Alzheimer's Disease Research Center (ADRC), the Johns Hopkins Memory and Alzheimer's Treatment Center (MATC), as well as through the Escitalopram for Agitation in Alzheimer's Disease (S‐CitAD) clinical trial (NCT03108846). 18 , 19 PBMCs were reprogrammed to generate iPSCs, which were subsequently differentiated into cortical neurons (detailed protocols in Material S1 in supporting information).

Ca2 + influx assay development

To investigate Ca2 + influx in iPSC‐derived cortical neurons in response to glutamate and glycine stimulation, we used a fluorescence‐based calcium assay with a calcium indicator, cell permeant dye Fluo‐4 AM (Invitrogen). One representative CU control and one representative AD iPSC‐derived neuronal cell line were used. Cells were incubated with 4 µM Fluo‐4 AM, a calcium‐sensitive fluorescent dye, in Hank's Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) supplemented with 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.4). The cells were incubated at 37°C for 30 minutes. After dye loading, cells were gently washed three times with HBSS/HEPES buffer to remove excess dye, and the cells were allowed to equilibrate at room temperature for 10 minutes. Cells were stimulated with increasing concentrations of glutamate and glycine (2/2–100/100 µM) to stimulate NMDA receptor activation. The stimulation was performed in HBSS/HEPES buffer, and fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader. Fluorescence was recorded at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 520 nm every 5 minutes for 30 minutes, to find the optimal measurement conditions. Data were plotted and analyzed to compare the calcium influx under different concentrations of glutamate and glycine stimulation, as well as in the presence and absence of memantine.

ROS assay development

To assess oxidative stress in iPSC‐derived cortical neurons, a fluorescence‐based ROS assay was developed using the CellRox Green kit (Invitrogen). Cells were treated with the test compound or drug, followed by incubation with 5 µM CellROX Reagent for 30 minutes at 37°C. After incubation, the medium was removed, and cells were washed three times with phosphate‐buffered saline. Cells were treated with increasing concentrations of menadione (1 –50 µM) to induce oxidative stress. Each stimulant was added to the cells in HBSS/HEPES buffer, and the plates were immediately transferred to a fluorescence microplate reader. Fluorescence was measured at an excitation wavelength of 485 nm and an emission wavelength of 535 nm every 5 minutes for up to 60 minutes, then again 20 hours later, to monitor the generation of ROS over time. ROS generation was evaluated either by normalizing to respective values at t = 0 or to untreated controls. Dose–response curves were generated to assess the impact of different concentrations of menadione on ROS production. The effects of memantine on ROS levels under these conditions were also evaluated to determine its potential antioxidative properties.

RESULTS

Characterization of iPSC‐derived NgN2 neurons

The PBMCs were successfully reprogrammed into human iPSCs and differentiated into 19 distinct lines (AD = 12 and CU = 7) of excitatory glutamatergic neurons. The neuronal morphology was assessed by brightfield microscopy (Figure 1A), and neuronal identity was determined via immunocytochemistry using neuron‐specific markers, such as MAP2 (Figure 1B), on day 21. To access the early signs of AD pathology, we measured the Aβ42/40 ratio using enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay. The Aβ42/Aβ40 ratio showed a trend toward lower values in AD‐derived neurons compared to CU; however, this difference was not statistically significant (t[17] = 1.711, P = 0.7394; Figure 1C).

Electrophysiological measures indicated no significant differences in capacitance or membrane resistance between neurons derived from patients with AD and CU individuals (Figure 1D–E). Specifically, the capacitance for AD neurons was 2.54 ± 0.49 pF, while CU neurons had a capacitance of 2.35 ± 0.29 pF. Similarly, the membrane resistance for AD neurons was 8121.2 ± 1421.8 MΩ compared to 7131.7 ± 1328.5 MΩ for CU. No significant differences were observed in voltage‐gated sodium channel (VGSC) currents between AD and CU neurons (Figure 1F–G). No differences were observed in VGSC peak current density (AD, peak = −194.8 ± 50.6 pA/pF, N = 15; CU, peak = −222.4 ± 73.7 pA/pF, N = 16) or the voltage dependence of steady‐state activation between AD and CU neurons (AD, V1/2 = −15.8 ± 1.0 mV n = 15; CU, V1/2 = −14.16 ± 1.4 mV n = 16; Figure 1I; AD, z = 4.73 ± 0.72; CU z = 4.16 ± 0.33; Figure 1H). Furthermore, both AD and CU neurons generated expected VGSC currents in response to voltage steps (−80 to +30 mV; Figure 1I). These results show that iPSC‐derived neurons are electrophysiologically active and comparable, regardless of diagnosis. Electrophysiological measures were performed at baseline, without memantine application, and no differences were observed between AD and CU samples under these conditions.

Calcium influx modulation by memantine in iPSC‐derived cortical neurons

Our fluorescence‐based calcium influx assay demonstrated a dose‐dependent increase in intracellular calcium levels after stimulation with glutamate and glycine (GG), consistent with NMDA receptor activation. 20 Because NMDA receptor dysregulation is linked to excitotoxicity and neuronal damage in AD, we expected differences in response. Interestingly, iPSC‐derived neurons from different individuals displayed varying calcium influx patterns, suggesting inter‐individual variability in NMDA receptor activity. This variability could be due to genetic or epigenetic influences on receptor function. Notably, AD lines showed higher baseline fluorescence levels (Figure S1C, D in supporting information) than CU (Figure S1A, B). All the cell line results were normalized to untreated controls for comparison (Figure S1E, F).

The AD and CU lines were treated across a range of concentrations of memantine (2–100 µM) and at two time points (24 and 72 hours) to test drug cytotoxicity, with no significant cytotoxicity being observed in iPSC‐derived cortical neurons (Figure S2A, B in supporting information). Cytotoxicity experiments (Figure S2) were conducted using one representative CU and one representative AD iPSC‐derived neuronal line. Treatment with memantine significantly reduced calcium influx, particularly at 50 µM and 100 µM concentrations, supporting its role in blocking overactive NMDA receptors without affecting physiological synaptic transmission. 21 GG treatment significantly increased calcium influx levels compared to untreated cells. Notably, 24‐hour memantine pre‐treatment significantly reduced GG‐induced Ca2+ levels (Figure S1 and Table S1 in supporting information). This reduction occurred without evidence of cytotoxicity, suggesting a potential to suppress pathological overactivation without clearly disrupting baseline neuronal viability or function.

The response varied across cell lines (Figure 2A), with 24‐hour pre‐treatment reducing fluorescence by an average of −9.85%, reaching −39% in AD_8 (Figure 2B,C). This effect was twice as strong as acute (1‐hour) treatment (−4.80%), with AD_10 showing the highest reduction at −25.9%. Some lines (AD_1, 4, and healthy_2, 7) showed little benefit from memantine, while others (AD_3, 9, and healthy_6) responded better to extended pre‐treatment. While trends suggested higher effects at 1 hour and a larger reduction over time in CU compared to AD patients, these differences did not reach statistical significance, potentially due to the small sample size (Figure 2D) (Table S2 in supporting information). However, 24‐hour pre‐treatment in our in vitro platform better reflects the clinical situation of sustained pharmaceutical intervention with memantine, as opposed to acute treatment during a single insult.

Memantine reduces oxidative stress in iPSC‐derived cortical neurons

We established a reliable in vitro model to assess oxidative stress, a hallmark of AD pathology contributing to neuronal dysfunction and death. 17 Using a fluorescence‐based ROS assay, we evaluated the effects of memantine on oxidative stress induced by menadione. Given menadione's redox‐cycling mechanism, which rapidly generates superoxide anions, it was selected for ROS generation assays. As expected, menadione triggered a dose‐dependent increase in ROS levels, confirming successful oxidative stress induction in iPSC‐derived cortical neurons for both CU and AD lines (Figure S3A, C in supporting information). This was further supported by linear regression analysis of ROS accumulation over time (Figure S4 and Table S3 in supporting information), which revealed a steeper increase in ROS in AD cells compared to CU cells across all menadione doses (0–30 minutes), with consistently higher slopes and elevated baseline ROS levels in AD lines, indicating increased sensitivity to menadione‐induced oxidative stress.

Memantine (50 µM) significantly reduced ROS levels under menadione‐induced stress in both CU and AD lines (Figure S3B, D). This effect was supported by a significant overall treatment effect (one‐way repeated‐measures analysis of variance, F[4,72] = 14.28, P < 0.001), with post hoc analysis confirming attenuation of menadione‐induced ROS by memantine (P < 0.05). Normalized data (Figure S3E, F) suggest that memantine's neuroprotective effects extend beyond calcium modulation (Figure S1) to include oxidative stress reduction, likely via NMDA receptor and associated downstream signaling pathways. Comparing 24‐hour pre‐treatment to acute (1‐hour) co‐treatment, we observed that the measurable benefit of memantine was again highly variable across all cell lines (Figure 3A,B). For ROS, CU showed numerically higher 1‑hour responses and a greater decline over time than AD lines, but these differences were not statistically significant, likely reflecting limited power and inter‑line variability (Table S4 in supporting information). Further, pre‐treatment provided greater protection, reducing ROS by −26.05% (24‐hour treatment) versus −16.66% (1‐hour treatment; Figure 3C). All lines responded to memantine to some extent, with AD_1 showing the most pronounced reductions (−53.77% for 1 hour and −72.21% for 24 hours; Figure 3C). These findings suggest that, despite notable variability in response (Figure 3D), pre‐treatment better prepares neurons for oxidative and excitotoxic challenges, potentially by modulating NMDA receptor‐associated pathways.

In these circular plots, each axis represents a single cell line arranged sequentially around the circle, and the distance from the center reflects the normalized magnitude of the response. DISCUSSION

One of the foremost goals in AD research is to develop therapeutic approaches that can mitigate or prevent progressive neurodegeneration underlying cognitive decline. Despite significant progress, the complex cellular links among excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, and AD pathology remain poorly understood, underscoring the need for innovative human experimental models. These models should represent both the cellular complexity of the disease and the variability in patient responses to treatment.

Previous studies have reported that memantine, an uncompetitive NMDA receptor inhibitor, modestly preserves memory in moderate to severe AD 22 , 23 by blocking excessive Ca2 + influx and ROS formation. 24 However, its mechanism of action remains unclear because NMDA receptor activity is also essential for memory and synaptic plasticity. 25 Furthermore, a number of studies have shown that memantine effectively prevents Aβ‐induced calcium overload and reduces oxidative stress in rodent models. 26 , 27 , 28 However, existing models do not fully capture the genetic and molecular diversity of human disease.

In this discovery‐based study, we developed an in vitro platform using iPSC‐derived excitatory glutamatergic neurons from 19 individuals to investigate key mechanisms of AD and assess the pharmacological response to memantine, with a particular focus on oxidative stress and calcium influx. This approach offers new insight into the cellular and molecular mechanisms of AD and underscores the therapeutic potential of memantine in modulating these pathological processes. Human iPSC‐derived neurons were used to establish a calcium influx assay in response to glutamate and glycine stimulation. In parallel, we designed a complementary assay to monitor oxidative stress after exposure to menadione stimulation. The modulatory effects of memantine were subsequently evaluated across both assays in all the neuronal lines.

Our results indicate that memantine significantly attenuated both calcium influx and ROS generation in iPSC‐derived neuronal cells. Notably, we observed that memantine significantly improved the protective effect at 24 hours pre‐treatment compared to acute (1‐hour) co‐treatment during oxidative stress and calcium influx assays. The greater efficacy of pre‐treatment compared to acute co‐treatment could be attributed to the time‐dependent regulation of neuroprotective mechanisms by memantine, such as the upregulation of antioxidant defense systems 29 or the enhancement of calcium buffering capacity. 30 , 31 Additionally, pre‐treatment may promote more effective stabilization of the NMDA receptor conformation, thereby preventing excessive calcium influx and subsequent oxidative stress. 32 , 33 These findings suggest that sustained rather than acute memantine exposure should provide greater neuroprotection in AD patients. Moreover, memantine can modulate two critical aspects of AD pathology, that is calcium dysregulation and oxidative stress, highlighting its therapeutic potential beyond NMDA receptor antagonism. 34 , 35 , 36

Importantly, we observed substantial variability in the protective response to glutamate, glycine, and memantine across the 19 patient‐derived neuronal lines. Such heterogeneity likely reflects differences in genetic and molecular backgrounds, including known AD risk factors such as the apolipoprotein E genotype, which modulate neuronal susceptibility to excitotoxic and oxidative stress. 34 , 37 , 38 , 39 These findings underscore the value of iPSC‐derived neuronal models, not only for therapeutic screening but also for advancing our understanding of disease mechanisms in a patient‐specific context. The integration of these models with high‐throughput screening has the potential to identify novel therapies as well as to optimize existing treatments, such as memantine, for personalized patient care. 40 , 41 Additionally, our findings suggest the potential to use this platform to study the combined effects of memantine with other AD drugs, such as acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (AChEIs), and to explore potential synergistic benefits. 42

To further examine the relationship between amyloid pathology and drug response, we analyzed Aβ42/40 ratios on a per–cell line basis and assessed their association with memantine‐mediated Ca2 + and ROS responses (Figure S5 and Table S6 in supporting information). A modest positive trend was observed, with higher Aβ42/40 ratios tending to associate with greater cellular responses, although these relationships did not reach statistical significance. No correlation was detected between Ca2 + and ROS responses. Together, these findings suggest that amyloid burden alone is not a strong predictor of acute memantine responsiveness, likely to reflect substantial inter‐line heterogeneity and limited cohort size.

These findings are in line with other studies testing effects of memantine on human iPSC models. Recent work by Caneus et al. has shown that memantine prevents Aβ42‐induced deficits in iPSC‐derived cortical neurons. In their models, memantine preserved neuronal firing properties and enhanced long‐term potentiation activity. 43 Similarly, Gallo et al. reported that memantine rescued mitochondrial function, reduced oxidative stress, and restored synaptic integrity in iPSC‐derived neurons exposed to Aβ42. 44 In another study, the use of NitroSynapsin, a memantine derivative, normalized network hyperexcitability in iPSC‐derived neurons and organoids from AD patients, underscoring the translational value of NMDA receptor modulation in patient‐derived systems. 45 Together, these studies strengthen the utility of iPSC‐based platforms for evaluating memantine's mechanisms and optimizing its therapeutic application. Building upon the previous studies, we extend these findings by systematically evaluating these effects across a large panel of patient‐derived iPSC neuronal lines (7 CU and 12 AD). This multi‐line approach provides a more physiologically relevant assessment of drug responsiveness and highlights the importance of patient‐specific heterogeneity in determining therapeutic efficacy.

Study limitations

While our study provides valuable insights, several limitations should be considered. Notably, the relatively small cohort size limits the ability to draw statistically robust correlations between in vitro cellular responses and patient‐level clinical features. Power analyses based on the observed effect sizes for ROS and Ca2 + dynamics indicate that several hundred to > 1000 samples per group would be required to achieve adequate statistical power for such correlation or stratification analyses, underscoring that the present findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis generating.

While human iPSC‐derived excitatory glutamatergic neurons are a relevant model for studying neuronal dysfunction in AD, they do not fully recapitulate the complex microenvironment multicellular interactions of the human brain. Co‐culture systems with astrocytes, microglia, or 3D organoids could offer deeper insights into AD pathology and drug responses. Age is a significant risk factor for AD, 34 and while our neurons were differentiated from age‐matched iPSCs of AD patients and CU individuals, they were only matured for 21 days before experiments. Additionally, our study did not examine the long‐term effects of memantine on neuronal health beyond the 24‐hour pre‐treatment period. Future studies could use longitudinal approaches to examine the sustained impact of memantine on neuronal health, network activity, and synaptic integrity. 46 Finally, including individuals with diverse genetic backgrounds could help identify individual variability responses and potential biomarkers for treatment efficacy.

Nevertheless, our exploratory study establishes a human iPSC‐based neuronal platform for examining AD mechanisms and pharmacological responses to memantine, and potentially for other medications. We demonstrate that memantine effectively reduces calcium influx and oxidative stress, with stronger protection after 24‐hour pre‐treatment, suggesting that sustained exposure may improve therapeutic outcomes. The variability observed among patient‐derived lines emphasizes the need for personalized treatment strategies and highlights the importance of iPSC models in advancing precision medicine in AD patients.
