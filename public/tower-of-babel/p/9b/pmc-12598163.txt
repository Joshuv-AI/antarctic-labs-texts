Arthroscopic Figure-of-8 Loop Suture Technique for Anatomical Repair of the Lateral Fibulotalocalcaneal Ligament Complex
Authors: Keli Hu, Yunlu Liu, Xiaofei Jian, Yanbin Pi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The surgical management of chronic lateral ankle instability continues to be a prominent topic in the field of sports medicine. Patients with chronic lateral ankle instability often experience damage to the lateral fibulotalocalcaneal ligament complex. In this paper, we present a straightforward, concise, and reproducible arthroscopic technique using the arthroscopic figure-of-8 loop suture technique for anatomical repair of the lateral fibulotalocalcaneal ligament complex. This technique ensures adequate reconstruction of ankle joint stability while obviating the necessity for additional inferior extensor retinaculum enhancement.

The surgical management of chronic lateral ankle instability (CLAI) continues to be a prominent topic in the field of sports medicine.1,2 Commonly used surgical techniques for CLAI repair include the classical Broström procedure, its Gould modification, and the Karlsson modification.3 Nevertheless, none of these approaches directly address the repair of the lateral fibulotalocalcaneal ligament (LFTCL) complex.

The LFTCL complex consists of the inferior fascicles of the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL), the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL), and the arcuate fibers that interconnect these ligaments.4, 5, 6 This complex remains isometric throughout the full range of motion and plays a crucial role in stabilizing both the ankle and subtalar joints.7,8

Arthroscopic ATFL repair has recently gained popularity.9 However, the LFTCL complex, as an extra-articular structure, has received relatively little attention. Consequently, some patients may experience residual instability and develop ankle osteoarthritis during long-term follow-up.10, 11, 12 Therefore, exploring and repairing the LFTCL complex is essential for achieving favorable clinical outcomes in patients with CLAI. This Technical Note describes an arthroscopic figure-of-8 loop suture technique for repairing the LFTCL complex, accompanied by a video illustration (Video 1).

Surgical Technique

Installation and Surgical Preparation

The patient is positioned supine under spinal anesthesia, with the foot placed at the edge of the operating table. A triangular cushion is positioned under the ipsilateral buttock. The root of the thigh is fitted with a pneumatic tourniquet, which is then inflated to 300 mm Hg. Equipment

A 4-mm 30° arthroscope (Arthrex, Naples, FL) and a 4-mm shaver (Arthrex) are used. Two 2.3-mm suture anchors (Smith & Nephew, Andover, MA) are employed for ligament fixation. Additional instruments include a suture passer (Arthrex), blue polydioxanone (PDS) sutures (Ethicon, a Johnson & Johnson Company, New Brunswick, NJ), and a 16-gauge needle.

Arthroscopic Portals

The contours of the medial and lateral malleoli, the anterior joint line, and the peroneal tendons are palpated and delineated (Fig 1). The anteromedial portal is located medially, adjacent to the anterior tibial tendon at the level of the joint line. The anterolateral (AL) portal is positioned approximately 0.5 cm distal to the tibiotalar joint line, more lateral and inferior than the standard location. The accessory anterolateral (acAL) portal is situated 1.0 cm anterior to the tip of the fibula beneath the arcuate fibers.Fig 1Anatomical landmarks for arthroscopy. Arthroscopic Exploration of the LFTCL Complex

A comprehensive arthroscopic examination of the anterior ankle joint is first performed. The bundle running inferiorly and posteriorly below the lowest point of the fibula (LPF) is identified under arthroscopic view as the anterior talofibular ligament inferior fascicle (ATFLif) and the arcuate fiber. Subsequently, a shaver is used to carefully separate the deep fascia from the joint capsule toward the fibular tip via the AL portal, creating an artificial cavity. The arthroscope is introduced into this cavity through the AL portal. The peroneal tendon sheath, located beneath the fibular tip, is carefully excised using a shaver inserted through the acAL portal. The peroneal tendons are manipulated with a probe to visualize the CFL detached from the fibula and the injured arcuate fiber (Fig 2).Fig 2Peroneal tendons are manipulated with a probe (red star) to visualize the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL) detached from the fibula and the injured arcuate fiber.

LFTCL Complex Repair

The suture passer is navigated between the LFTCL complex and the lateral surface of the talus via the AL portal, extending posterior to the CFL and exiting through the skin at a location defined by anatomical landmarks: a triangular area formed by the fibular tip, the CFL, and the peroneal tendon. A PDS suture is inserted into this channel (Fig 3). Subsequently, a second PDS suture is introduced into the deep layer of the ATFLif and the arcuate fibers, passing beneath both structures by the suture passer from the AL portal to the acAL portal (Fig 4). Through the AL portal, a 2.3-mm suture anchor is drilled into the fibular obscure tubercle (FOT) at a safe angle of 45° relative to the longitudinal axis of the fibula.13 One tail of the anchor suture is designated as the primary suture and the other as the auxiliary suture. The middle segment of the auxiliary suture is routed from the deep layer of the arcuate fibers to the acAL portal using the second PDS suture, forming a loop. The tail end of the auxiliary suture is guided from the deep layer of the CFL to the skin surface via the first PDS suture (Fig 6A).Fig 6(A) Positioning of the relationship diagram concerning the auxiliary suture and the lateral fibulotalocalcaneal ligament complex. A probe is then used to draw the tail suture through the acAL portal (Fig 6B), passing it through the loop that has been rotated a half turn. The loop is rotated an additional half-turn to form a second loop, through which the tail suture continues. The tail suture is then tightened, and both loops are cinched to complete the lasso configuration of the CFL (Fig 7 A and B). The primary suture is passed through the space between the ATFL superior fascicle (ATFLsf) and ATFLif using a 16-gauge needle loaded with a PDS suture, directing it toward the acAL portal (Fig 8). ATFLsf Repair

The second anchor is positioned 5 mm superior to the first. A 16-gauge needle loaded with a PDS suture is percutaneously inserted into the joint cavity from beneath the ATFLsf. The PDS suture is retrieved through the AL portal using mosquito forceps, and the needle is withdrawn, leaving the PDS suture in place within the channel. The middle segment of the auxiliary suture from the second anchor is drawn out through the skin to create a loop using the PDS suture. Subsequently, a probe guides the loop through the AL portal, positioning it between the deep fascia and the joint capsule. The ATFLsf is then lassoed using the suture from the second anchor, employing the same lasso technique used for the CFL.

The loop around the CFL is tightened by pulling the auxiliary suture from the first anchor. The primary suture is then securely pulled and knotted with the auxiliary suture to stabilize the LFTCL complex at its fibular attachment (Fig 9A). The same technique is subsequently applied to tighten the ATFLsf and fix it at the fibular attachment (Fig 9B). Throughout the knotting process, the ankle is maintained in a consistent position, with 5° of dorsiflexion and 5 to 10° of eversion.Fig 9(A) Tying a knot to stabilize the LFTCL complex. Upon completion of the procedure, arthroscopic confirmation is obtained regarding the effectiveness of the sutures, while ankle stability is assessed using both the anterior drawer test and the talus tilt test.

Postoperative Management

A plaster splint is used for fixation during the initial two weeks postoperatively. Subsequently, an ankle brace is applied to facilitate dorsiflexion and plantarflexion functional exercises. Four weeks postoperatively, full weight-bearing exercises are initiated with the brace in place. The brace is discontinued by 6 weeks.

Discussion

The primary advantage of this technique lies in the convenience for anatomical repair of the LCFTL complex. Studies have demonstrated that, under arthroscopic visualization from the anteromedial portal, the LPF corresponds to the FOT.14 The FOT serves as an intraoperative anatomical landmark for distinguishing and repairing the ATFLsf and ATFLif. During arthroscopy, the LPF is used as an osseous reference point for identifying the ATFLif and its common attachment site with the CFL.14,15 where suture anchors are subsequently implanted. After the auxiliary suture from the anchor loops around the CFL, it is tied with the tightened main suture, which emerges from the space between the ATFLsf and ATFLif, thereby creating an figure-of-8 loop fixation for the LFTCL complex (Fig 9A).

Second, the arthroscopic assessment of the integrity of the LCFTL complex is also crucial. A cadaveric study demonstrated that suturing the ATFL can transmit tension to the CFL by the arcuate fibers.16 In some patients with CLAI, however, significant damage to the LFTCL complex may prevent the arcuate fibers from transmitting tension from the ATFLif to the CFL.8 Furthermore, diagnosing CFL injuries during preoperative evaluation remains challenging, as the sensitivity of magnetic resonance imaging for detecting CFL injuries is lower than that for ATFL injuries.17

Although additional inferior extensor retinaculum enhancement can significantly improve ankle joint stability,3,18 we do not recommend it. The figure-of-8 loop fixation technique for the LFTCL complex provides sufficient reconstruction of ankle joint stability.

This technique is particularly well-suited for treating CLAI and also can be applied to acute ankle sprains and concurrent tears of the ATFL and CFL. However, there is a risk of injury to the sensory branches of the superficial peroneal nerve at the AL portal. Moreover, the success of this surgical technique depends on the integrity of the remaining ligamentous tissue.

The arthroscopic figure-of-8 loop suture technique for LFTCL complex repair appears to be a reliable approach. Nonetheless, additional clinical research is required to assess recurrence rates and possible complications associated with this technique.
