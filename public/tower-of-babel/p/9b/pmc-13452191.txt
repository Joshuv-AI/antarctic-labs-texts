Stage-Specific Morphofunctional Evaluation of Idiopathic Macular Holes Using En Face OCT Imaging
Authors: Junji Kanno, Takuhei Shoji, Hirokazu Ishii, Hisashi Ibuki, Yume Noguchi, Yuji Yoshikawa, Kei Shinoda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo evaluate stage-specific morphofunctional relationships in idiopathic macular holes (IMHs) and determine whether stage 2‑specific features could be explained by hole size alone.DesignRetrospective observational study.ParticipantsA total of 105 eyes with IMHs and available 3-month postoperative follow-up data.MethodsEyes were stratified by Gass stage into stage 2 and stage 3 to 4 groups and by International Vitreomacular Traction Study size criteria into small and medium-to-large groups. Associations between preoperative optical coherence tomography (OCT) parameters and best-corrected visual acuity were assessed using interaction, stratified, and multivariable regression analyses.Main Outcome MeasuresAssociations between preoperative morphological parameters and best-corrected visual acuity before surgery and at 1 and 3 months postoperatively.ResultsPreoperative interactions with stage were significant for minimum linear diameter, minimum area/foveal avascular zone ratio, and minimum area. Stage 3 to 4 showed consistent morphofunctional associations, whereas stage 2 showed less consistent associations not fully explained by hole size. In multivariable analyses, predictors were more stable in stage 3 to 4 than in stage 2. Size-based classification showed less consistent patterns than stage-based stratification.ConclusionsStage 2 IMHs showed less consistent morphofunctional relationships than stage 3 to 4, and these differences were not fully explained by measured OCT-based size parameters alone. These findings support stage-aware interpretation of OCT-derived biomarkers and may inform future prognostic studies in IMHs.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Idiopathic macular hole, En face image, Stage-specific, Morphofunctional assessment, Automated image analysis

Idiopathic macular hole (IMH) is a vitreoretinal interface disease characterized by a full-thickness defect of the neurosensory retina at the fovea.1 Optical coherence tomography (OCT) has identified various prognostic factors for functional outcomes after surgery.2,3 Stage 2 is defined by persistent vitreofoveal adhesion, whereas stages 3 and 4 involve release of this adhesion and are associated with distinct macular morphologies.4,5 Nevertheless, many studies have evaluated morphofunctional relationships without distinguishing stage 2, leaving detailed stage-specific assessments limited. Staging systems should reflect the extent of tissue loss and outer retinal involvement to be prognostically meaningful.6 In this context, OCT-derived size metrics, particularly the minimum linear diameter (MLD), have been widely used to classify macular holes and to evaluate their associations with visual and anatomical outcomes. However, recent large-scale clinical studies have suggested that, particularly in larger macular holes, size-based classification alone may be insufficient to capture heterogeneity relevant to surgical decision-making and outcomes.7 Furthermore, we previously developed the Kanno–Saitama macro (KSM), an ImageJ-based tool for automated extraction of regions of interest from en face images, and used it to characterize morphofunctional features of IMHs, including those related to the foveal avascular zone (FAZ).8 In our previous study, we evaluated the overall associations between en face OCT/OCT angiography (OCTA)‑derived parameters and visual acuity in a heterogeneous IMH cohort. However, whether these morphofunctional relationships differ according to disease stage was not examined.

The present study aimed to characterize stage-specific morphofunctional relationships in IMHs by comparing stage-based and size-based classifications, with a particular focus on determining whether the distinct features observed in stage 2 can be explained by hole size alone.

Methods

Study Design

This retrospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Saitama Medical University Hospital (Institutional Review Board No. 2025-134) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients.

Study Participants and Examinations

Among 172 patients with IMHs (Gass stages 2, 3, or 4)9 who underwent swept-source OCTA (PLEX Elite 9000, Carl Zeiss Meditec) at our institution between February 2018 and October 2024, eligible eyes were identified after applying the predefined exclusion criteria. Among these, eyes with available 3-month postoperative follow-up data were included in the primary analysis. This cohort represents a subset of that used in our previous study.8 All 105 eyes included in the primary analysis were drawn from that previously reported cohort, and no new eyes were added. The present analysis focused on cases with available 3-month postoperative follow-up data. Preoperative data included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), slit-lamp biomicroscopy, noncontact tonometry (TONOREF II, Nidek), axial length measurement (Optical Biometer OA-2000, Tomei Corporation), and retinal thickness evaluation using spectral-domain OCT (SD-OCT) (Spectralis HRA 2, Heidelberg Engineering). Macular hole staging was primarily based on clinical records generated by retinal specialists during routine clinical practice. Vitreomacular adhesion status was assessed in all cases using high-density raster scans obtained from OCTA.10 In addition, when available, OCT and OCTA images were reviewed to assess the vitreomacular interface, including the presence of posterior vitreous detachment. Staging was not performed in a masked fashion, and intergrader agreement was not formally assessed. Therefore, some degree of classification bias cannot be excluded. All surgeries were performed by experienced vitreoretinal surgeons. Phacoemulsification with intraocular lens implantation was performed prior to vitrectomy in all phakic eyes. Standard 3-port, sutureless pars plana vitrectomy with internal limiting membrane (ILM) peeling was conducted in all cases, and successful macular hole closure was achieved in all eyes. Air or sulfur hexafluoride gas (SF6) was used for tamponade, and patients were instructed to maintain a face-down position during the day for 3 to 7 days. Postoperative BCVA was evaluated at 1 and 3 months. The primary outcome measure was BCVA at 3 months postoperatively. Exclusion criteria included axial length ≥26 mm, diabetic retinopathy or maculopathy, glaucoma, traumatic macular hole, retinal detachment, a history of vitrectomy for endophthalmitis, or poor-quality en face OCTA images.

B-Scan–Based Measurements

Conventional OCT-based parameters were measured on horizontal B-scan images obtained using SD-OCT, as previously described.11 Minimum linear diameter, base diameter (BD), and hole height were measured using the built-in caliper tool by a single examiner masked to postoperative visual outcomes. Minimum linear diameter was defined as the narrowest horizontal width of the macular hole. Base diameter was defined as the width of the hole at the level of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Hole height was defined as the maximum vertical distance from the RPE to the vitreoretinal interface.

OCT Angiography Imaging and En Face Image Analysis

The PLEX Elite 9000 uses a swept-source laser with a central wavelength of 1050 nm and a bandwidth of 100 nm, providing an axial resolution of approximately 5 μm and a transverse resolution of 14 μm.12 In this study, 3 × 3 mm macula-centered scans were obtained. Following our previously described method,8 the minimum area (MA) and base area (BA) were extracted from structural OCT en face images. Slab segmentation parameters were defined as follows: for MA, “Offsets/Top: ILM –70, Bottom: RPEFit –70,” and for BA, “Offsets/Top: RPEFit –65, Bottom: RPEFit –35.” An en face OCTA image of the FAZ, representing retinal blood flow, was obtained from the superficial retinal layer slab (ILM to inner plexiform layer). Only images with a signal intensity score of ≥8/10 were included. As in our previous report,8 the ImageJ macro KSM2 was used for MA and BA extraction, whereas KSM was used for FAZ measurement. The KSM2 macro performs automated preprocessing including denoising, local thresholding, and region-of-interest extraction to delineate the macular hole area. The macro code is provided in the Supplemental Methods (available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Evaluation of Stage-Based and Size-Based Characteristics

To evaluate stage-specific characteristics, all eyes were stratified using 2 classification approaches: a stage-based classification and a size-based classification. For the stage-based classification, eyes were grouped into stage 2 and stage 3 to 4 based on the presence or absence of vitreofoveal adhesion. For the size-based classification, eyes were categorized according to the International Vitreomacular Traction Study (IVTS) criteria into small (MLD <250 μm) and medium-to-large (MLD ≥250 μm) groups.10 Age, sex, axial length, lens status (presence of pseudophakia), and BCVA at each visit (preoperatively and at 1 and 3 months postoperatively) were evaluated for each group. Morphological parameters included MLD, BD, hole height, MA, FAZ, the minimum area/foveal avascular zone ratio (MFR),8 BA, central subfield thickness (CST) measured by SD-OCT, fluid cuff (BD – MLD), fluid cuff area (BA – MA), cystoid area, and membrane-like findings. “Membrane-like findings” refer to epiretinal or preretinal structures on OCT, such as epiretinal membrane (ERM), epiretinal proliferation, and ILM-like reflections. Identifiable ERMs were also assessed separately. Central subfield thickness was automatically calculated as the mean retinal thickness within the central 1-mm Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy Study subfield using the built-in segmentation algorithm of the Heidelberg Spectralis OCT system. In eyes with full-thickness macular holes, CST values were generated despite the presence of a central retinal defect, suggesting that the defect region was handled by interpolation or a similar internal processing step; no manual correction was applied. Therefore, CST values represent software-derived estimates rather than direct measurements of residual retinal tissue. The fluid cuff was defined as the difference between BD and MLD (BD – MLD). The fluid cuff area, cystoid area, and membrane-like findings were evaluated from OCT en face images. The fluid cuff area was defined as the region between BA and MA. The cystoid area was measured using deep retinal en face images (from the inner plexiform layer to the outer plexiform layer) within a 0.5-mm-radius circle centered on the MA. Membrane-like findings were identified using a slab optimized for preretinal abnormalities (Offsets/Top: ILM –6, Bottom: ILM +3),13 together with raster B-scan images from SD-OCT. All en face parameters were corrected for magnification based on axial length,14 and the size-related parameters were recorded (Fig 1). The sizes and ratios of these morphological parameters were compared between the groups, and the prevalence of membrane-like findings and identifiable ERM was also analyzed.

Association Analyses between Visual Acuity and Morphological Parameters

All morphological parameters used in the regression analyses and in the comparison of correlation coefficients were derived from preoperative OCT/OCTA images, thereby representing baseline anatomical predictors. Postoperative BCVA was evaluated in relation to these features. Linear regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations between BCVA and selected morphological parameters (MLD, BD, hole height, MFR, MA, BA, and CST), which were hypothesized to be associated with visual outcomes.8 First, to assess stage-specific differences in these associations, interaction terms between each morphological parameter and stage (stage 2 vs. stage 3–4) were incorporated into the regression models at each time point. Subsequently, stratified univariable analyses were conducted according to both stage-based and size-based classifications. Standardized regression coefficients (β) were calculated for each group. Differences between stage 2 and stage 3 to 4 were evaluated using Fisher r-to-z transformation, whereas no formal statistical comparison of correlation coefficients was performed between size-based groups. Finally, multivariable regression analyses were performed separately within each group to evaluate independent associations with BCVA. Models were constructed using conventional B-scan OCT parameters and en face OCT-derived parameters, and their contributions were compared across groups. Independent predictors were defined a priori as variables that remained statistically significant after adjustment for other covariates in the multivariable models.

Statistical Analysis

Data normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. As data were non-normally distributed, nonparametric tests were applied. Continuous variables are presented as medians (interquartile range) and categorical variables as counts (percentages). Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and Fisher exact test for categorical variables. Best-corrected visual acuity was converted to logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution. Multiple comparisons in the stratified univariable analyses were adjusted using the false discovery rate (FDR) with the Benjamini–Hochberg method, whereas no correction was applied to the interaction and multivariable analyses because these were based on predefined hypotheses. A P value < 0.05 and, where applicable, a q value < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using R (version 3.6.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

A total of 172 eyes from 172 patients with IMH were initially identified. Eighteen eyes were excluded due to poor image quality, 9 due to segmentation errors in the BA, and 20 due to an axial length >26 mm, leaving 125 eligible eyes (stage 2, n = 49; stage 3–4, n = 76). Among these, 105 eyes (stage 2, n = 45; stage 3–4, n = 60) had postoperative follow-up data available at 3 months and were included in the primary analysis. All subsequent analyses were performed using these 105 eyes. The median age was 69 years (interquartile range, 66–74), and the cohort comprised 49 men (47%) and 56 women (53%). Postoperative outcomes at 1 month were available in 125 eyes, whereas 3-month outcomes were available in 105 eyes. There were no significant differences in follow-up availability between the stage 2 and stage 3 to 4 groups (P = 0.593, Fisher exact test).

Evaluation of Stage-Based and Size-Based Characteristics

Table 1 summarizes baseline patient and morphological characteristics stratified by Gass stage (stage 2 vs. stage 3–4).9 Overall, there were no significant differences between the groups in demographic, visual, or most morphological parameters. The parameters that differed significantly were BD and fluid cuff, both of which were larger in stage 3 to 4 eyes (P = 0.032 and P = 0.017, respectively). Supplemental Table 1 (available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org) presents the baseline characteristics stratified by IVTS size classification (small vs. medium-to-large).10 In contrast to stage-based stratification, size-based classification revealed significant differences in several parameters. Visual acuity was significantly worse in the medium-to-large group at both preoperative and 1-month postoperative time points (all P < 0.01), whereas no significant difference was observed at 3 months (P = 0.110). Morphological parameters showed marked differences between groups, with significantly larger values in the medium-to-large group for MLD, BD, MA, FAZ, MFR, and BA (all P < 0.001). In addition, fluid cuff area was also significantly greater in this group (P = 0.002).

Association Analyses between Visual Acuity and Morphological Parameters

Table 2 summarizes the interaction analyses between stage and morphological parameters for visual acuity at each time point. At the preoperative time point, significant interactions with stage were observed for MLD, MFR, and MA (all P < 0.05), indicating that the associations between these parameters and visual acuity differed according to disease stage. In contrast, no significant interaction effects were observed at either 1 month or 3 months postoperatively for any of the morphological parameters, suggesting that stage did not modify these relationships after surgery.

Stratified Univariable Analyses According to Stage-Based and Size-Based Classification

Supplemental Table 2 (available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org) summarizes the results of stratified univariable regression analyses according to stage-based classification, evaluating the associations between morphological parameters and BCVA at each time point, with adjustment for multiple comparisons using the Benjamini–Hochberg FDR. Differences between stage 2 and stage 3 to 4 were assessed using Fisher r-to-z transformation. In the overall cohort, MLD, BD, MFR, MA, and BA were significantly associated with BCVA at all time points. When stratified by stage, stage 3 to 4 showed consistent and strong associations between BCVA and several morphological parameters, including MLD, BD, MFR, MA, and BA, across all time points, whereas stage 2 showed a less consistent pattern overall, particularly at the preoperative time point. Comparisons between stages revealed significant differences in the strength of associations for MLD, MFR, and MA at the preoperative time point (all P < 0.01). At postoperative time points, only limited differences were observed, including MA at 1 month (P = 0.048) and MFR at 3 months (P = 0.032). These stage-dependent differences in morphofunctional relationships are visually illustrated in Figure 2. Additional visual comparisons across parameters not selected for the main figure are presented in Supplemental Figure 1 (available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Supplemental Table 3 (available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org) summarizes the results of stratified univariable regression analyses according to size-based (IVTS) classification. When stratified by size, the medium-to-large group demonstrated consistent and strong associations between BCVA and MLD, BD, MFR, MA, and BA across all time points. In contrast, the small group showed generally weaker and less consistent associations, with no parameters reaching statistical significance after FDR correction.

Multivariable Regression Analyses of Visual Acuity Predictors

Table 3 summarizes the results of multivariable regression analyses stratified by stage-based classification. Model 1 included conventional B-scan OCT parameters, whereas Models 2 and 3 included en face OCT-derived parameters using alternative representations of minimum hole size (MFR or MA) to avoid multicollinearity. In the overall cohort, MLD, MFR, and MA were independently associated with BCVA at the preoperative time point. Postoperatively, MLD and BD in Model 1, and en face parameters (MFR in Model 2 and MA in Model 3), remained significant predictors across time points. In stage 2, no independent predictors were identified preoperatively. At 1 month postoperatively, BD was the only significant predictor, whereas at 3 months, BD and BA emerged as independent predictors in the respective models. In contrast, in stage 3 to 4, consistent independent associations were observed for MLD, MFR, and MA across all time points, indicating a more stable and robust morphofunctional relationship compared with stage 2.

A Case Illustrating the Morphological and Functional Characteristics of Stage 2

Figure 3 illustrates a stage 2 IMH case with initially preserved BCVA (20/16) despite a well-defined macular hole. One week later, BCVA deteriorated to 20/50. Corresponding B-scan images showed photoreceptor disruption at the hole margin at follow-up (red arrow), while the MLD remained essentially unchanged.

A representative case illustrating the morphofunctional characteristics of stage 2. (A) A B-scan image at the initial visit shows a hyper-reflective band at the hole margin, corresponding to preserved photoreceptor integrity (red arrow). The best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) at this time was 20/16. (B) One week later, a follow-up B-scan obtained at the same retinal location demonstrates disruption of the photoreceptor layer at the previously identified site (red arrow). Although partial residual reflectivity remained, there was no apparent change in the measured minimum linear diameter (MLD) (double-headed arrow). The BCVA decreased to 20/50. This case illustrates that visual acuity can deteriorate despite minimal changes in MLD, possibly in association with localized photoreceptor disruption.

Discussion

In this study, we compared stage-based and size-based classifications to characterize stage-specific morphofunctional relationships and to determine whether the distinctive features observed in stage 2 could be explained solely by macular hole size. At the preoperative time point, significant interactions with stage were observed for MLD, MFR, and MA, indicating stage-dependent differences in their associations with visual acuity. Furthermore, multivariable regression analyses of visual acuity predictors demonstrated more consistent and robust morphofunctional relationships in stage 3 to 4, whereas stage 2 showed less consistent associations.

Comparison of baseline characteristics between stage-based (Table 1) and size-based (Supplemental Table 1) classifications revealed distinct patterns. In stage-based stratification, only BD and fluid cuff differed significantly between stage 2 and stage 3 to 4, and no significant differences were observed in BCVA at any time point. In contrast, size-based stratification showed significant differences in multiple morphological parameters, including MLD, MA, BD, FAZ, MFR, BA, and fluid cuff area. Best-corrected visual acuity also differed significantly at the preoperative and 1-month postoperative time points, although this difference was no longer observed at 3 months. These findings likely reflect the strong association of MLD and MA/MFR with visual function.8 Because size-based classification is defined by MLD, it inherently separates eyes according to parameters closely related to BCVA. The attenuation of visual differences at 3 months may reflect postoperative structural recovery. Conversely, the absence of BCVA differences in stage-based stratification is consistent with the lack of significant differences in MLD and MA/MFR between stage 2 and stage 3 to 4.

Notably, discrepancies were observed between diameter-based and area-based parameters. Base diameter and fluid cuff (BD – MLD) differed significantly between stages, whereas BA and fluid cuff area (BA – MA) did not. These differences may be explained by their distinct measurement characteristics: BD is a one-dimensional parameter obtained along a single B-scan, whereas BA is a 2-dimensional parameter that integrates information across all orientations, resulting in spatial averaging. Previous 3-dimensional analyses have shown that the centers of the minimum and base areas are frequently misaligned, suggesting that macular holes are often asymmetric rather than concentric.15 Accordingly, the B-scan used to measure BD does not necessarily correspond to the maximal BD, which may contribute to discrepancies between diameter-based and area-based metrics. Interestingly, in size-based classification, fluid cuff area differed significantly between groups, whereas fluid cuff did not. This pattern may reflect redistribution of stage-related structural characteristics across size-based groups, thereby attenuating differences related to disease stage that are not fully explained by hole size alone. Taken together, these findings suggest that diameter-based and area-based parameters capture distinct aspects of macular hole morphology, and their interpretation should consider their inherent measurement differences. The discrepancy observed between stage-based and size-based classifications in fluid cuff and fluid cuff area suggests that fluid cuff may reflect structural features not fully explained by hole size alone and may be partially associated with stage-dependent morphological differences. These findings highlight the complementary roles of conventional B-scan–based and en face–based morphological parameters in characterizing stage-dependent structural features of IMHs.

In stage-based analyses, significant interactions with stage were observed at the preoperative time point for MLD, MFR, and MA, indicating that their associations with visual acuity differed according to disease stage (Table 2). Consistent with this finding, stage-stratified analyses demonstrated distinct morphofunctional patterns between stage 2 and stage 3 to 4. In particular, stage 3 to 4 showed more consistent and robust associations, whereas stage 2 exhibited weaker and less consistent relationships (Supplemental Table 2). In contrast, size-based classification revealed clearer between-group differences in univariable morphofunctional relationships (Supplemental Table 3). Comparisons using Fisher r-to-z transformation between stage 2 and stage 3 to 4 further supported the presence of stage-dependent differences at the preoperative time point for MLD, MFR, and MA, consistent with the interaction analyses (Table 2 and Supplemental Table 2). Although no significant interactions were observed postoperatively, r-to-z comparisons detected modest but significant differences for MA at 1 month and MFR at 3 months (P = 0.048 and P = 0.032, respectively), suggesting a residual effect of preoperative stage-dependent relationships. Multivariable regression analyses of visual acuity predictors also revealed distinct stage-dependent patterns (Table 3). In stage 2, no independent predictors were identified at the preoperative time point; BD emerged as an independent predictor at 1 month, while BD and BA were identified at 3 months. In contrast, stage 3 to 4 consistently demonstrated MLD, MFR, and MA as independent predictors across all time points, indicating a stable and robust morphofunctional relationship. Similarly, size-based classification demonstrated differences in morphofunctional associations between groups (Supplemental Table 4). No independent predictors were identified in the small group at any time point, whereas in the medium-to-large group, independent predictors varied across time points, including MLD preoperatively, multiple parameters at 1 month, and BD, MFR, and BA at 3 months. However, compared with stage-based analyses, these size-based patterns appeared less consistent across time points.

When comparing stage-based and size-based stratified analyses (Supplemental Tables 2 and 3), similar morphofunctional patterns were observed particularly between stage 3 to 4 and medium-to-large groups. In contrast, modest discrepancies were noted between stage 2 and the small group, which may be attributable to the inclusion of medium-to-large holes within stage 2. These findings suggest that the apparent instability of morphofunctional relationships in stage 2 may be partly explained by hole size, but is not fully accounted for by measured OCT-based size parameters alone. However, multivariable regression analyses of visual acuity predictors (Table 3 and Supplemental Table 4) revealed distinct patterns that were not fully explained by size-based classification. In stage-based analyses, BD and BA were identified as independent predictors in stage 2, whereas MLD, MFR, and MA consistently emerged as predictors in stage 3 to 4. In contrast, no independent predictors were identified in the small group, while multiple predictors were identified in the medium-to-large group, albeit with less consistency across time points. These findings indicate that factors beyond hole size contribute to the stage-dependent differences in morphofunctional relationships.

Previous studies have reported that the enlargement rate of MLD is faster in stage 2 than in later stages,11,16 suggesting that stage 2 may represent a more dynamic phase of disease progression. In line with this concept, Terasaki et al reported that macular hole diameter measured at a later preoperative time point was significantly associated with postoperative visual acuity,16 suggesting that the timing of measurement may influence its relationship with visual outcomes. In our cohort, we also observed a case in which relatively good visual acuity was initially maintained despite the presence of a macular hole, followed by rapid visual deterioration during follow-up (Fig 3). At the time of visual decline, OCT revealed apparent disruption of the photoreceptor layer, which had previously appeared relatively preserved. This illustrative case is hypothesis-generating and is not intended to establish the mechanism underlying the stage-dependent findings observed in the cohort.

Several studies have reported that photoreceptor disruption is associated with visual dysfunction in IMHs.17,18 Such disruption may manifest as irregular or “bumpy” configurations of the photoreceptor layer at the hole margin, as described in recent OCT-based analyses.19,20 These structural irregularities may not be fully reflected in simple geometric measurements such as MLD. Because MLD is defined as the minimum distance between the edges of the hole,2 it is inherently dependent on the integrity of the photoreceptor layer at the measurement points. Therefore, localized photoreceptor disruption may represent one possible explanation for discrepancies between MLD and visual function, highlighting a potential limitation of size-based metrics. Previous studies have reported inconsistent associations between preoperative BCVA and MLD, with some demonstrating significant correlations,21 whereas others have identified stronger associations with alternative parameters such as hole height.22 In the present stage-stratified univariable analyses (Supplemental Table 2), MLD was not significantly associated with preoperative BCVA in stage 2, whereas hole height showed a significant association. In contrast, in stages 3 to 4, MLD was significantly associated with BCVA, whereas hole height was not. When all stages were analyzed together, both parameters showed significant associations. These findings suggest that the variability reported in previous studies may, at least in part, be attributable to differences in stage composition across study cohorts. Our results further support the notion that the relationship between structural parameters and visual function differs according to disease stage. Multivariable regression analyses of visual acuity predictors (Table 3 and Supplemental Table 4) also revealed distinct patterns between stage 2 and stage 3 to 4. In contrast, in the size-based classification, the identified predictors showed less consistency across time points, particularly in the medium-to-large group. Taken together, these findings suggest that stage-based stratification may provide a more informative framework for evaluating morphofunctional relationships. Furthermore, separating stage 2 from stage 3 to 4 may support more nuanced interpretation of postoperative outcomes.

Our previous study demonstrated that en face OCT/OCTA-derived parameters, particularly MFR, are associated with postoperative visual acuity.8 However, that analysis did not consider stage-specific differences. The present study extends these findings by incorporating stage-stratified and longitudinal analyses. In the present study, we evaluated morphofunctional relationships in IMHs using both B-scan–based and en face–derived parameters. Although the overall performance of these parameters was largely comparable, 2 findings are noteworthy. First, in the stage-stratified analyses (Supplemental Table 2), significant between-stage differences in correlation coefficients were observed for MLD, MFR, and MA at the preoperative time point using Fisher r-to-z transformation. Second, in multivariable analyses of stage 2 (Table 3), BD was identified as a predictor at 1 month postoperatively, whereas BA emerged only at 3 months postoperatively. These findings suggest that B-scan–based parameters, such as MLD and BD, primarily reflect localized structural features at the foveal center, whereas en face–derived parameters, such as MA and BA, capture the overall configuration of the lesion. Although MLD showed a significant between-stage difference preoperatively, this difference was not sustained postoperatively and showed only a trend at 1 month (P = 0.091), suggesting that localized measurements may be less sensitive to persistent differences in global lesion characteristics. The temporal shift from BD to BA as a predictor in stage 2 may reflect differences in the recovery processes between focal and more extensive structural components. Taken together, these results indicate that the use of complementary morphological parameters with distinct structural characteristics enables a more comprehensive assessment of morphofunctional relationships in IMHs. This complementary information may be particularly useful in cases where conventional B-scan parameters fail to fully explain visual outcomes, thereby providing additional insight into morphofunctional relationships. For clinical reference, equivalent diameters calculated from MA and BA are presented in Supplemental Table 5 (available at https://www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

In this study, CST was used as a surrogate parameter corresponding to hole height. However, the measurement of CST depends on device-specific segmentation algorithms, particularly in how retinal defects are handled. In some OCT mapping protocols, central retinal thickness may not be reliably evaluated in eyes with full-thickness macular holes because the software may not appropriately recognize the gap formed by the hole itself.23 In the device used in this study, defect regions appeared to be interpolated, although the exact algorithm is not fully disclosed. Although CST and hole height showed similar overall trends, caution is warranted in interpreting CST due to these methodological limitations, and further validation is needed.

Finally, the most robust evidence for stage-dependent effect modification was observed at the preoperative time point, whereas postoperative differences were more limited and should be interpreted cautiously. Taken together, these findings may support more nuanced, stage-aware interpretation of OCT findings and may be useful for hypothesis generation in future prognostic modeling.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective, single-center study, which may introduce selection bias and limit generalizability. In addition, only surgically treated eyes with successful closure were included, which may limit the applicability of our findings, particularly to conservatively managed cases such as some stage 2 macular holes. Second, symptom duration, a known factor associated with anatomical and functional outcomes,18,24 was not assessed because of incomplete clinical records, which may have influenced the observed variability, particularly in stage 2. Third, quantitative assessment of outer retinal integrity, including the ellipsoid zone and external limiting membrane, was not performed. Because these structures are associated with visual outcomes,18, 19, 20 the stage-dependent differences observed in this study may, in part, be influenced by unmeasured photoreceptor status, and the proposed mechanistic interpretation cannot be directly validated. Fourth, the retrospective nature of the study precluded standardized, masked staging and formal assessment of intergrader agreement, and therefore some degree of classification bias cannot be excluded. In addition, although B-scan–based measurements were obtained by a single examiner masked to postoperative visual outcomes, intragrader or intergrader reproducibility was not formally assessed. Fifth, the follow-up period was limited to 3 months, and therefore only short-term outcomes were evaluated. Finally, surgical parameters such as ILM peeling extent, flap techniques, dye usage, and gas tamponade were not standardized, which may have introduced additional variability. Future prospective studies with larger sample sizes, longer follow-up, standardized imaging and surgical protocols, and quantitative assessment of outer retinal integrity are warranted to further validate these findings.

Taken together, our findings suggest that stage 2 IMHs do not necessarily share the same morphofunctional characteristics as later-stage IMHs. The morphofunctional relationships observed in stage 2 were less consistent and were not fully explained by measured OCT-based size parameters alone. These findings support stage-aware interpretation of OCT-derived biomarkers and may inform future prognostic studies in IMH.
