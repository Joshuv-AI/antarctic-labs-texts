Multiscale Modeling of Embryonic Morphogenesis from Fertilization to Organogenesis
Authors: Debanik Deb, Orishma Parida, Laasya Naduthota, Bandan Chakrabortty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Embryonic development transforms a single cell into a structured multicellular organism through coordinated molecular, cellular, and tissue-level processes. In this review, we discuss multiscale modeling studies spanning early embryogenesis from fertilization to organogenesis across diverse systems, emphasizing how distinct computational frameworks have been used to investigate different stages of embryonic morphogenesis. Drawing on complementary animal models, we highlight how multiscale approaches provide mechanistic insights into embryonic patterning and form generation.

Introduction

Through embryonic development, a fertilized egg gives rise to a highly organized, multicellular organism. Development begins with fertilization, which establishes the molecular, biochemical, and mechanical conditions that constrain and direct subsequent patterning. Across animal systems, fertilization strategies and early embryonic dynamics vary substantially. Consequently, the use of complementary model organisms is central to understanding how common developmental mechanisms are implemented in distinct biological contexts. Upon sperm entry, molecular and cellular asymmetries are initiated, triggering localized cytoskeletal rearrangements, cortical granule exocytosis, and the redistribution of maternal determinants that establish the first embryonic polarity1–3. These early symmetry-breaking events guide cleavage dynamics, axis specification, and the timing of major developmental transitions, such as the midblastula transition4–6. As development proceeds, initial molecular asymmetries are translated into large-scale deformations during gastrulation, a phase characterized by coordinated cell movements, tissue rearrangements, and biochemical remodeling. Morphogenetic processes such as invagination, epiboly, and convergent extension illustrate how local cellular behaviors integrate to drive tissue-scale shape changes that establish the primary body plan7,8. Following gastrulation, morphogenesis further refines embryonic architecture through axis elongation, segmentation, and organogenic programs. Key examples include neural tube closure, heart looping, and branching morphogenesis, in which patterned tissues are shaped into three-dimensional structures9,10. Across these stages, embryonic form emerges from interactions among genes, biochemical signaling pathways, cellular mechanics, and tissue-scale forces operating across multiple spatial and temporal scales11. Here, a key question is how local molecular interactions are stabilized, amplified, and translated into large-scale tissue architectures during development.

Mathematical and computational modeling provides a powerful tool for dissecting these processes by enabling a mechanistic interpretation of experimental observations and complementing experimental embryology12,13. This is particularly important because embryonic morphogenesis is inherently multiscale; fertilization-induced signaling sets the initial conditions for symmetry breaking at the cellular scale, collective cell movements during gastrulation occur at the tissue scale, and organogenesis integrates cellular behaviors to generate three-dimensional organ architectures. Modeling frameworks that explicitly bridge molecular, cellular, and tissue-scale processes, and that are grounded in experimentally tractable organisms, therefore play a critical role in advancing a mechanistic understanding of early development14,15. Accordingly, a broad spectrum of modeling strategies has been developed that are not necessarily interchangeable but are suited to specific developmental questions, appropriate spatiotemporal scales, and suitable experimental systems. No single modeling framework can capture the full complexity of embryonic morphogenesis. Instead, different modeling strategies emphasize distinct biological scales to capture complementary aspects of embryonic morphogenesis rather than representing interchangeable descriptions of the same processes. For example, reaction-diffusion systems and gene regulatory network (GRN) models have been widely used to study biochemical patterning and fate specification, often in experimentally tractable systems such as sea urchin, Xenopus, zebrafish, and Drosophila. Cell-based (CB) mechanical frameworks, including vertex models (VM) and Cellular Potts models (CPM), as well as agent-based (AB) models, have been particularly effective in analysing epithelial and mesenchymal dynamics in Drosophila, zebrafish, chick, and mouse embryos. Continuum biomechanical models, including finite element models (FEM) and hydrodynamic formulations, have further enabled quantitative links between tissue geometry, force generation, and large-scale morphogenetic movements, especially in mechanically accessible systems such as chick, mouse, and C. elegans embryos. In this review, we discuss modeling studies spanning fertilization to early organogenesis, emphasizing how distinct computational frameworks have been used to investigate various stages of embryonic morphogenesis across animal systems. We begin with models of fertilization and early embryonic patterning, focusing on gamete interactions, intracellular signaling, and mechanical symmetry breaking. We then examine modeling approaches to gastrulation and large-scale morphogenesis, followed by post-gastrulation processes such as axis refinement, segmentation, and neural tube formation. Finally, we discuss models of early organogenesis, highlighting frameworks that capture the shaping of organs, including the brain, heart, limbs, kidney, and lung. We emphasize that, here, we define multiscale modeling as computational frameworks that represent biological processes across multiple spatial and/or temporal scales, ranging from molecular interactions to tissue-level dynamics. This is different from frameworks that explicitly couple processes across scales. This review provides a representative, rather than exhaustive, overview of modeling approaches and emphasizes how different computational frameworks relate to specific developmental stages and biological scales. The studies discussed are selected based on their relevance to key developmental stages, their ability to illustrate distinct modeling frameworks across biological scales, and the availability of experimentally supported insights.

Fertilization and Initiation of Embryonic Patterning

We begin by reviewing modeling frameworks that explain how fertilization initiates the molecular, biochemical, and mechanical processes underlying early embryonic patterning. Rather than considering these processes as isolated events, we organize this section around key conceptual stages, including gamete encounter, intracellular signaling, symmetry breaking, and the emergence of spatial patterning. Because fertilization strategies and early embryonic dynamics vary substantially across animal phyla, mechanistic insight into this stage necessarily draws on diverse model organisms. Fertilization marks the initiation of the developmental continuum, during which interactions between gametes establish key molecular and mechanical conditions for later developmental processes. In many species with externally released gametes, these interactions require precise recognition and fusion, orchestrated by the directed movement of sperm toward the oocyte through gradients of chemoattractants released by the egg. Modeling approaches at this stage primarily focus on how physical transport and chemotactic response collectively enhance gamete encounter efficiency. Echinoderms, particularly sea urchins, provide a tractable system in which optical accessibility and abundant gametes enable quantitative analysis of sperm-egg encounter dynamics. Using this system, an efficient computational algorithm reconstructing sperm chemotaxis provided mechanistic insight into enhanced gamete encounters16 (Fig. 2a). While such models capture the physical dynamics of fertilization, complementary approaches extend this framework toward molecular regulation. For example, to link fertilization dynamics to the gene interactions that progressively shape embryonic patterning, a GRN model has been developed17. By integrating transcriptional kinetics with evolving embryonic geometry, this model quantitatively reproduces gene expression patterns across distinct spatial domains. Upon gamete fusion, the fertilized egg becomes the zygote, in which a series of Ca²⁺-mediated signaling pathways are triggered to mediate egg activation and enable zygotic transcription. At this stage, modeling frameworks shift from extracellular dynamics to intracellular signaling processes that define the temporal onset of development. Owing to their large size, optical accessibility, and experimental tractability, Xenopus embryos provide a complementary vertebrate system for quantitative analysis of fertilization-induced intracellular Ca²⁺ transients. Using this system, a mathematical framework identified the key determinants governing Ca²⁺ oscillations18. While the molecular and regulatory events triggered by fertilization establish the initial biochemical landscape of the embryo, they also create conditions permissive for physical asymmetries to arise. A central question addressed by modeling studies is how initially symmetric systems transition toward spatially organized states. In amphibian embryos such as Xenopus, the first physical symmetry-breaking cue is cortical rotation, an angular shift of the egg cortex relative to the inner cytoplasm. A mass-spring mechanical model revealed that stochastic anisotropy in microtubule organization can generate a net torque initiating cortical rotation19 (Fig. 2b). Furthermore, a mean-field Ising model showed that electrostatic interactions among microtubules can trigger spontaneous alignment, driving this process20. Despite differences in modeling approach, these studies converge on the idea that local interactions and stochastic anisotropies can give rise to coherent, large-scale mechanical behavior. Implicitly, this model also explains initiation of dorsal-ventral (DV) axis formation, as the rotation biases the embryo by repositioning dorsal determinants (Dsh). In this regard, C. elegans embryos enable quantitative analysis of axis formation owing to their fixed eggshell geometry and optical accessibility. Using this system, a FEM-based biophysical model integrated with live imaging, examined how the embryonic anteroposterior (A-P) axis aligns with the geometric long axis of the eggshell21. This study revealed that while posterior-directed cortical flow can reposition the A-P axis, the pseudo-cleavage furrow acts as a mechanical driver of alignment. In the context of earlier symmetry-breaking models, this suggests that axis orientation can arise from the interaction between internal flows and geometric constraints. More broadly, these models highlight the interplay between geometry, mechanical forces, and intracellular dynamics in determining axis orientation. Another form of symmetry breaking observed in this system is the establishment of embryonic polarity. Using this system, a partial differential equation (PDE) based modeling framework simulated the interactions between Par proteins and actomyosin contractility, elucidating both the nature of the polarity cue and the mechanism of polarity establishment22 (Fig. 2c). In contrast to purely mechanical models, these approaches explicitly relate biochemical signaling to mechanical feedback. This model demonstrated that inhibition of contractile elements by polarity cues is required for stable spatial asymmetry, whereas complementary biochemical feedback alone is sufficient for symmetry breaking. These results indicate that symmetry breaking can emerge through distinct yet related mechanisms, in which mechanical processes and signaling dynamics each provide routes to stable spatial asymmetry.

Embryonic polarity provides a spatial framework that influences cleavage, through which geometry is iteratively reproduced over time, translating chemical patterns into structural form. Modeling approaches to cleavage, therefore, focus on how biochemical cues and mechanical constraints jointly regulate division geometry. Sea urchins are well-suited for quantitative analysis of cleavage geometry, owing to their large, optically accessible blastomeres and stereotyped division pattern. Using this system, a reaction-diffusion model for morphogen distribution suggested that cleavage patterns can emerge from the chemotactic migration of centrosomes along morphogen gradients23 (Fig. 2d). In vivo, however, cleavage geometry is also influenced by spindle orientation and cytoskeletal organization, and extensions of this work reproduced species-specific cleavage patterns24. This highlights that cleavage geometry is not determined by a single factor but emerges from the combined influence of intracellular organization and external constraints. Collectively, these studies illustrate that cleavage patterns emerge from the integration of signaling gradients, cytoskeletal dynamics, and geometric constraints. In many externally developing embryos with large eggs, as cleavage proceeds, the progressive reduction in blastomere size increases the nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratio, which slows the cell-cycle oscillator and produces the longer divisions characteristic of the midblastula transition (MBT). At this stage, models emphasize the relationship between cell geometry and cell-cycle regulation. The sea urchin and Xenopus embryos provide complementary systems for analysing MBT regulation, owing to defined cleavage geometry and large, biochemically tractable cytoplasm, respectively. In sea urchins, a mathematical model coupling cell-cycle oscillators to cell geometry successfully reproduces observed division timing and spatial heterogeneity during early embryonic development25. In Xenopus, a biochemical reaction network model showed that cytoplasmic density globally scales biochemical parameters and modulates oscillator robustness and period through a hysteretic mechanism26. These findings highlight a broader principle that developmental timing emerges from the interaction between biochemical kinetics and evolving cellular geometry.

Through these modeling approaches, the emergence of early embryonic patterning can be explained by the interaction of biochemical, mechanical, and geometric processes acting across different biological scales. Despite differences in systems and methods, shared principles include symmetry breaking, feedback regulation, and the conversion of local interactions into global organization. These mechanisms jointly shape spatial patterning and developmental timing. However, most current models remain simplified and capture only parts of this continuum. This highlights the need for more integrated multiscale frameworks that unify molecular, cellular, and mechanical dynamics.

Gastrulation and Large-Scale Embryonic Morphogenesis

While early embryogenesis establishes molecular asymmetries, a key challenge is understanding how these asymmetries are converted into coordinated cell behaviors. Here, we review models that explain how early asymmetries are translated into large-scale cell behaviors and tissue deformations during gastrulation and early morphogenesis.These processes can be broadly understood as distinct but interconnected morphogenetic behaviors, including collective migration, tissue spreading, epithelial folding, and cell internalization. Because different animal models provide distinct experimental and theoretical access to these morphogenetic processes, mechanistic insights into gastrulation necessarily arise from complementary model systems.

In most vertebrates, the formation of the primitive streak represents a pivotal event that translates early molecular asymmetries into directed cellular dynamics. Here, modeling frameworks examine how coordinated cell migration emerges from local interactions and external cues. The chick embryo is well-suited for studying this process because its flat, optically accessible epiblast permits quantitative analysis of large-scale cell flows. Using this system, a PDE-based chemotactic model demonstrated how chemotactic cues coordinate collective migration, producing the characteristic anterior progression and posterior regression of the streak27. Complementary simulations using a CPM further showed that chemotactic migration of mesodermal populations is sufficient to initiate streak formation28 (Fig. 2e). Despite methodological differences, these models indicate that spatial gradients organize large-scale tissue flows by biasing local migration dynamics. The formation of the primitive streak constitutes a central structural and signaling feature of gastrulation, which comprises multiple, partially overlapping morphogenetic processes, including epiboly, invagination, involution, ingression, delamination, and convergent extension. In many embryos with large yolky eggs, a prominent morphogenetic movement is epiboly, during which embryonic tissues spread to envelop the yolk. Modeling efforts in this context examine how tissue spreading arises from mechanical properties and geometric constraints. The Zebrafish embryo offers a powerful system for analysing epiboly owing to its optical transparency and well-defined geometry. Using this system, a viscoelastic modeling framework demonstrated how embryo geometry influences stress distribution and collective migration during epiboly29. Furthermore, a combined live-imaging and hydrodynamic-regression modeling approach identified polarized cortical tension as the principal driver of tissue movement30. More recently, a center-based model of the enveloping layer revealed that mechanical feedback between neighbouring cells is essential for maintaining tissue cohesion during rapid surface expansion, a salient feature of epiboly31 (Fig. 3a). Another major morphogenetic process is invagination, which involves epithelial bending to internalize tissue layers. Models of invagination examine how localized force generation produces coordinated tissue-scale deformation. The simple epithelial architecture of the Drosophila embryo enables quantitative analysis of invagination mechanics. Using this system, FEM-based simulations showed that coordinated apical constriction and apico-basal elongation are mechanically sufficient to drive robust folding32. Further, simulations using an integrated VM reveal the interplay between mechanical and genetic regulation during mesoderm invagination33 (Fig. 3e). These approaches show that epithelial folding can emerge from tightly coordinated cellular forces modulated by regulatory inputs. Closely related internalization processes, namely involution, ingression, and delamination, operate across different epithelial contexts. Although these processes differ in their cellular organization, they rely on shared control of adhesion, contractility, and cell motility. During involution, cohesive sheets of cells roll inward and migrate along internal surfaces. For investigating involution, the Zebrafish embryo is an ideal model as its optical transparency enables direct visualization of coordinated cell movements. Using this system, a continuum modeling framework integrating GRN with cell adhesion revealed how Nodal-dependent regulation of N-cadherin sustains cohesive migration beyond the morphogen gradient, thereby generating a self-propagating wave of involution34 (Fig. 3b). Ingression involves the detachment and inward movement of individual or loosely connected cells. While hydrozoans are a simple system for studying unipolar ingression mechanics, Zebrafish enable the live imaging of collective ingression, offering advantages not easily achieved in other vertebrates. CB modeling of hydrozoan embryos demonstrated that global apical constriction, localized adhesion loss, and mediolateral intercalation collectively drive unipolar ingression35 (Fig. 3c). A complementary AB model in Zebrafish showed how Nodal signaling gradients mechanically partition mesendodermal cells into leader and follower populations, enforcing selective adhesion codes that ensure ordered ingression36. Across these systems, ingression can be understood as a transition between collective and individual cell behavior governed by changes in adhesion and signaling cues. Delamination represents a variant of ingression adapted to pseudostratified epithelia, during which existing epithelial cells divide with newly formed daughter cells that exit the epithelial layer through basal extrusion to internalise into the embryo. The mouse embryos provide a physiologically relevant system for studying this process, as their epiblast architecture and cell-cycle dynamics enable direct analysis of mitosis-driven epithelial exit. Using this system, an AB model demonstrated that accelerated cell cycles and actomyosin relaxation promote non-apical mitosis, leading to basal extrusion and delamination under conditions of impaired epithelial integrity37 (Fig. 3d). Despite differences in cellular organization, involution, ingression, and delamination can be understood as variations of tissue internalization governed by changes in adhesion, contractility, and cell motility.

In addition to internalization processes, a distinct class of morphogenetic behaviors involves large-scale tissue remodeling that extends the body axis and establishes fundamental body organization. Convergent extension (CE) is central to this class of large-scale tissue remodelling processes, in which cells intercalate to narrow (converge) along one axis and extend along the perpendicular axis. Modeling approaches to CE study how directional intercalation arises from anisotropic mechanical and signaling cues. The Drosophila embryo serves as a benchmark model for studying CE, as its germband epithelium clearly reveals how planar-polarized junctional mechanics drive tissue elongation. Using this system, a VM framework demonstrated that adhesion-dependent mechano-reciprocal integration between cell crawling and junction contraction enhances the efficiency of CE38 (Fig. 3f). Further, it has been shown that effective execution of CE requires alternating oscillations of cortical actomyosin across the membranes of neighbouring cells39. These findings indicate that tissue-scale elongation emerges from coordinated, polarized cellular behaviors shaped by underlying mechanical and regulatory cues. Across modeling frameworks, gastrulation is viewed as a multiscale reorganization where patterned cell behaviors drive coordinated tissue shape changes. Common themes include force generation, cell rearrangements, and the role of tissue geometry in guiding morphogenetic flows. However, simplified regulatory and mechanical couplings limit current models, highlighting the need for more integrative, predictive frameworks linking cellular dynamics to tissue-scale morphogenesis (Table 1).

Post-Gastrulation Morphogenesis and Patterned Tissue Formation

In the post-gastrulation context, we consider modeling frameworks that uncover how late axis refinement and early organogenic programs shape subsequent body-plan organization. The processes considered here span axis refinement, segmentation, tissue deformation, and migratory cell behavior, each contributing to the emergence of structured anatomy. We particularly focus on vertebrates, which exhibit distinctive morphogenetic processes such as somite formation, head fold morphogenesis, neural tube closure, and neural crest emergence that are absent or different in non-vertebrates. Because different animal models provide complementary access to symmetry-breaking, tissue dynamics, and cell-scale behaviors, mechanistic insights into these processes necessarily arise from multiple model systems.

As gastrulation movements give way to, and overlap with, late axis refinement, new symmetry-breaking events shape the global body plan. Modeling efforts at this stage aim to explain how localized processes scale up to organized anatomical features. Conceptually, these processes can be understood as the interaction between directional cues, tissue-scale mechanics, and regulated cell behaviors that collectively stabilize large-scale anatomical organization. During this stage, left-right (LR) asymmetry emerges as a mechanically guided process. Theoretical descriptions of LR asymmetry examine how directional fluid motion establishes consistent orientation across the embryo. Complementary studies in mouse and chick embryos have revealed distinct phases of this refinement. In the mouse embryo, continuum analytical Stokes-flow and PDE-based modeling demonstrated how microscale hydrodynamic forces translate into macroscopic LR asymmetry40. These results show how small-scale physical effects can bias global patterning outcomes. This principle, where localized physical biases are amplified into global patterning, also underlies subsequent processes such as posterior axis elongation. Concurrently with LR asymmetry, posterior axis elongation proceeds through caudal extension, whereby the embryo lengthens from its posterior end. Descriptions of this process focus on how sustained addition of cells and directed movement maintain elongation. The chick embryo serves as an ideal system for studying this process. Using this system, a combined PDE-based model and a CPM, demonstrated that chemotactic migration of progenitor cells drives elongation while preserving a dynamic stem zone41. This illustrates how spatial guidance and cell supply together support continuous extension.

Posterior axis elongation establishes the context in which the presomitic mesoderm transitions into a segmented morphology through somitogenesis, a process coupling dynamic cell-cell signaling with mechanical processes. The central question addressed by these models is how periodic patterns arise along the body axis. Conceptually, this requires coupling oscillatory signaling dynamics with spatial gradients and mechanical stabilization mechanisms. Dissecting this coupling benefits from complementary vertebrate models, with Zebrafish enabling live imaging of cell signaling dynamics and the chick embryo providing mechanical accessibility. In Zebrafish, somitogenesis is captured by a mathematical model integrating Notch-Delta signaling with global morphogen gradients along the anteroposterior axis42. While these models capture the spatiotemporal coordination of segmentation, resolving how signaling is converted into physical somite boundaries requires a mechanically tractable system such as the chick embryo. Using this system, CPM-based simulations combined with electron microscopy showed that somite boundaries emerge from tissue stresses generated by actomyosin contractility and adhesion, interacting with the segmentation clock43. An integrated clock-and-wavefront model incorporating cell sorting further explained the robustness of somite patterning44 (Fig. 4a). These results converge on the idea that robust segmentation emerges from the integration of temporal oscillations, spatial gradients, and mechanically stabilized boundary formation. In amniote vertebrate embryos, head fold (HF) morphogenesis constitutes a distinct large-scale tissue deformation process occurring alongside somitogenesis. This process exemplifies how distributed forces reshape embryonic geometry. The chick embryo is particularly well-suited for analysing HF mechanics. Using this system, FEM-based simulations that integrate imaging data with biomechanical modeling have revealed the forces driving HF formation45. Further, iterative refinement of these models has enabled the interpretation of complex, nonlinear tissue deformations46 (Fig. 4b). Related mechanical principles also underlie neural tube (NT) formation, during which the anterior neural plate gives rise to the brain, while the posterior region forms the spinal cord. NT formation spans cell-scale mechanical patterning and tissue-scale closure dynamics, and is widely studied using Xenopus and mouse embryos as complementary vertebrate models. Models in this context address how patterned cellular activity produces large-scale bending and closure. This requires linking cell-scale force generation with tissue-scale mechanical integration. In Xenopus, VM simulations demonstrated that alternating patterns of apically constricted and expanded cells can spontaneously initiate neural plate bending47 (Fig. 4c). While Xenopus model captures neural plate bending, NT closure and associated defects are best examined in the mouse embryo. Using this system, VM simulations revealed that supracellular actomyosin purse-string contractility and directed cell movements toward the closing gap are essential for successful closure48. More recently, AB models integrating signaling pathways with biomechanics have enabled the prediction of NT closure defects49. These approaches collectively show that neural tube morphogenesis depends on coordinated cellular force generation integrated across the tissue scale, while also accounting for failure conditions.

Upon NT formation, cells at the dorsal neural folds delaminate to form the neural crest (NC), transitioning from an epithelial to a migratory phenotype50. NC formation spans collective migration, chemotactic guidance, and tissue invasion across multiple scales. Conceptually, these behaviors arise from the integration of collective cell interactions, chemotactic guidance, and dynamic coupling with the extracellular environment. Theoretical descriptions of NC behavior, therefore, focus on how these interacting mechanisms guide directed cell dispersal. Dissecting these mechanisms requires complementary models, with Xenopus and Zebrafish enabling analysis of cell-cell coordination, and the chick embryo providing access to subcellular mechanics and tissue-level interactions. AB models integrating Xenopus and Zebrafish data reveal how interactions between leading and trailing cells drive organized NC migration51. Hybrid AB-PDE-based models in chick embryos demonstrated how stream cohesion and directional persistence are maintained during NC migration52. Extending this work, the incorporation of reciprocal interactions between NC cells and their extracellular matrix (ECM) revealed that ECM is required for stream-like migration patterns53. Chemotactic guidance was resolved by combining AB models, with experimental data showing that leading cells generate and respond to self-induced gradients, transmitting cues to trailing cells54 (Fig. 4d). Invasion of NC cells into target tissues was captured by a reaction-diffusion-proliferation model validated using quail grafting experiments, demonstrating that proliferation at the invasive front drives NC invasion55. This highlights that invasion is not solely migration-driven but also depends on proliferation and local tissue remodeling. These studies indicate that NC behavior emerges as a coordinated migratory process shaped by interactions between cell-cell communication, environmental cues, and spatial constraints.

Collectively, modelling approaches discussed in this section suggest that post-gastrulation morphogenesis is driven by coordinated cell behaviors, mechanical forces, and spatial cues that progressively refine the body plan. These processes connect local cell dynamics to emergent anatomical structures. However, current models often rely on simplifying assumptions that limit their ability to fully capture cross-scale integration. This highlights the need for more unified frameworks linking signaling, mechanics, and geometry across development.

Organogenesis and Multiscale Morphogenetic Patterning

Finally, we turn our focus from post-gastrulation morphogenesis to modeling approaches that dissect early organogenic processes. The phenomena considered here include large-scale tissue bending, directional growth, and the generation of branched architectures. We focus on vertebrate embryos, which uniquely exhibit three-dimensional, organ-scale morphogenetic processes, including neural tube formation, cardiac looping, limb outgrowth, and branching morphogenesis of the kidney and lung, that cannot be fully captured in invertebrate systems. Because no single model system captures all aspects of organogenesis, mechanistic insight necessarily emerges from integrating complementary animal models, each revealing distinct facets of multiscale morphogenetic patterning. Organogenic processes can be broadly categorized into bending and looping morphogenesis, anisotropic growth-driven outgrowth, and branching morphogenesis. These categories reflect distinct physical mechanisms through which organ shape and structure emerge.

During early organogenesis, overlapping temporally with NT closure and NC delamination, the paired cardiac primordia, positioned anterior and lateral to the neural plate, migrate ventrally and fuse to form the primitive heart tube. Concurrently, the anterior neural plate folds and expands to generate the brain tube, which subsequently remodels into three primary brain vesicles, including the prosencephalon that gives rise to forebrain structures. Modeling efforts in this context address how spatial variations in cellular activity generate distinct tissue compartments. The chick embryo, with its conserved NT morphogenesis, planar geometry, and experimental accessibility, provides a powerful system for analysing the mechanical processes underlying vesicle remodelling. Using this system, FEM-based simulations showed that regional differences in apical actomyosin contractility shape early brain vesicles and compartments56 (Fig. 5a). Modeling approaches further capture the coordination of molecular signaling and tissue mechanics during forebrain development57. Additionally, mathematical modeling identified compression exerted by the vitelline membrane as a key driving force underlying torsional deformations of the brain tube58. At the same organ scale, the primitive heart undergoes rightward looping, representing one of the earliest morphological manifestations of organ-level left-right asymmetry. This process provides a clear example of how intrinsic stresses can drive large-scale geometric transformations. The chick and Zebrafish embryos serve as complementary vertebrate models for studying the initiation and progression of this process. In chick embryos, FEM-based simulations demonstrate that ventral bending and rightward torsion of the initially straight heart tube arise from mechanical instability driven by stresses generated by geometric frustration59. Additional simulations incorporating embryonic heart geometry reproduced key features of looping morphogenesis60 (Fig. 5b). Elucidating how looping is coupled to coordinated organ-scale growth requires systems with high spatiotemporal imaging accessibility, such as Zebrafish. In this context, combining high-resolution imaging with VM simulations revealed that Notch-mediated actomyosin dampening induces a tipping-point transition, enabling rapid and stable growth of the Zebrafish heart61. These studies indicate that intrinsic mechanical stresses and growth dynamics act in concert to generate robust organ-level asymmetry. Whereas cardiac looping demonstrates how mechanical instabilities generate large-scale asymmetry, limb formation instead emphasizes sustained, anisotropic growth as the primary driver of directional organ outgrowth, rather than global bending or torsion. Models of limb development therefore focus on how directional cellular behavior drives elongation and shape formation. The mouse limb bud and Zebrafish pectoral fin serve as complementary vertebrate models for studying cellular behaviors and tissue-scale growth during limb morphogenesis. In the mouse, integrating imaging data with FEM-based simulations demonstrates that the limb shape is governed primarily by cellular polarity, anisotropic growth, and mechanical coordination rather than proliferation alone62. In Zebrafish, AB modeling shows that directional cell behaviors, rather than spatial differences in proliferation, drive fin outgrowth63 (Fig. 5c). These results highlight the importance of oriented growth and cell behavior in shaping developing appendages.

Beyond limbs, many organs undergo branching morphogenesis, generating structures such as kidneys and lungs through shared chemo-mechanical principles. This class of processes illustrates how repeated local growth events generate complex organ architectures. Branching systems offer a powerful framework for linking local growth, signaling gradients, and mechanical instability to global organ architecture. Here, we focus on mouse models, which offer unparalleled genetic, imaging, and quantitative access to kidney and lung branching morphogenesis. During kidney development, nephron formation depends on the precise elaboration of the ureteric tree, a branched epithelial network derived from the ureteric bud. The developing mouse kidney provides an ideal system for dissecting the cellular behaviors and signaling interactions underlying this process. Using this system, CPM-based modeling reproduces the directed migration of nephron progenitor cells toward ureteric bud branch corners, a prerequisite for the pretubular aggregate formation64 (Fig. 5d). For ureteric bud branching itself, AB models reveal that spatially regulated epithelial growth controls early branching events65. Coordination between nephron induction and collecting duct branching has been investigated using a combined reaction-diffusion and FEM-based simulation, demonstrating that branching geometry promotes localized morphogen enrichment sufficient to trigger pretubular aggregate formation66. Collectively, these results indicate that branching architecture emerges from the interaction between spatially organized growth and localized signaling, rather than from a single dominant mechanism. Lung development similarly relies on branching morphogenesis to generate the airway tree. The mouse lung provides a well-characterized system in which airway branching has been quantitatively analysed. Using this system, biophysical modeling shows that growth-induced mechanical instabilities within the proliferating epithelium can trigger localized buckling, initiating new branches67 (Fig. 5e). Complementary PDE-based models further demonstrate how variations in ECM degradation and fibroblast growth factor signaling regulate lung bud branching dynamics68. These results indicate that branching patterns arise from the coupling between growth dynamics, material properties, and signaling inputs.

Overall, the modeling studies discussed in this section show that organogenesis emerges from the coordinated interplay of growth, mechanical forces, and spatial signaling across scales. These processes translate cellular behavior into complex organ architecture. However, many current models still represent only selected components of this complexity, limiting their ability to capture full system-level integration. Therefore, integrated multiscale frameworks are needed to connect molecular, cellular, and organ-level dynamics.

Discussion

The modeling studies reviewed in this article provide mechanistic insights into biological processes that transform a single cell into a complex, multicellular organism. A central insight spanning fertilization, gastrulation, post-gastrulation morphogenesis, and organogenesis is that no single level of description is sufficient to explain robust developmental patterning. By utilizing modeling frameworks spanning across diverse spatiotemporal scales and biological systems, the study characterizes the interactions among genes, biochemical signaling pathways, cellular behaviors, and tissue-scale mechanics that enable early embryonic form and function.

We discussed how across diverse animal systems, modeling approaches have been used to dissect distinct morphogenetic processes, with different model organisms providing optimal experimental and computational access to specific developmental events. Reaction-diffusion and GRN models, often developed in experimentally tractable systems such as sea urchin, Xenopus, zebrafish, and Drosophila, elucidate how molecular gradients and transcriptional feedback generate spatial patterning. CB frameworks, including VM, CPM, and AB models, have been particularly effective in epithelial and mesenchymal contexts studied in Drosophila, zebrafish, chick, and mouse embryos, where local cell-cell interactions and mechanical constraints give rise to coordinated tissue-level behaviors. Continuum biomechanical models, including FEM and hydrodynamic formulations, are frequently applied in chick, mouse, and C. elegans embryos, further linking tissue geometry, force generation, and large-scale morphogenetic movements. While the modeling frameworks discussed in this review are successful at decoding specific developmental events in specific model organisms, their applicability and cross-species translatability are constrained by their dependence on organism-specific experimental data. Because each of these modeling frameworks operates at a distinct biological scale, they often identify different underlying mechanisms for the same morphology. Therefore, this review highlights the need for a common modeling framework that identifies a universal developmental mechanism spanning multiple biological scales and resolutions. Also, many of the models incorporate increasing biological complexity and experimental detail; here, advances in quantitative imaging, targeted perturbation methods, improved strategies for parameter estimation, and inference in mechanically active tissues will be beneficial for a better understanding of the biological processes.

Moving forward, modeling frameworks that incorporate Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Machine learning (ML) offer promising opportunities to shift from a traditional, mechanistic approach to a rigorous, data-driven, predictive one. Recent breakthroughs have enabled reverse characterization of parameters, i.e., quantifying physical parameters directly from high-dimensional imaging data, eliminating the need for manual parameter tuning69. Furthermore, Reinforcement learning (RL) based frameworks have been able to identify the underlying logic for cellular behavior70. AI-based approaches have mapped parts of reciprocal interactions between physical information and genetic pathways71. Incorporating these approaches will strengthen progressive understanding of how embryonic form emerges from fertilization through early organogenesis, thus providing a principled route for characterizing and phenotyping developmental disorders, and for identifying mechanistically grounded intervention strategies in disease biology.
