Ductus Arteriosus Aneurysms in Adults
Authors: MHD Osama Srour, Andrew Bowman, Viky Loescher, Katia Bravo-Jaimes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A ductus arteriosus aneurysm (DAA) in adults is an extremely rare aortic pathology that presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges because of its variable clinical presentation and lack of standardized management guidelines. We present 2 cases that illustrate the wide spectrum of DAA clinical presentation and management. The first involves a 54-year-old woman with a small, stable DAA managed conservatively. The second describes a 74-year-old man with a large, symptomatic aneurysm presenting with hoarseness, successfully treated with a hybrid surgical and endovascular approach. The literature on adult DAAs remains limited, with no standardized treatment guidelines. The current management approaches are primarily based on case reports and small case series, requiring individualized decision-making based on aneurysm characteristics, patient symptoms, and surgical risk. Additionally, we performed a review of 121 DAA cases reported in adults and analyzed their clinical presentation, diagnostic approaches, treatment modalities, and outcomes.

The ductus arteriosus (DA) is pivotal to the fetal circulation, connecting the main pulmonary artery to the aorta, which diverts blood flow from the high-resistance pulmonary circulation to the systemic circulation. Following birth, pulsed Doppler echocardiography studies have shown that the DA undergoes functional closure in 90% of normal term infants by 48 hours of age, with flow no longer detectable in 100% of infants by 96 hours.1 Arrested closure during obliteration of the pulmonary arterial end can lead to the formation of a ductus arteriosus aneurysm (DAA), a rare condition characterized by localized dilation of the ductal tissue. In adults, a DAA is multifactorial and can be attributed to several mechanisms, such as persistence of an infantile aneurysm, late spontaneous DA closure with or without superimposed bacterial endarteritis, and external chest trauma in patients with a persistent or partially persistent DA.3 A DAA can also occur as a late complication of surgical closure of patent ductus arteriosus (PDA).4

Although neonatal DAA incidence is estimated at 0.8% to 8.8% in echocardiographic studies, its true prevalence is likely underestimated because of the asymptomatic cases and spontaneous regression.5 In adults, a DAA is an extremely rare and incompletely understood entity. Its presentation ranges from asymptomatic, incidental findings on imaging to catastrophic rupture found at autopsy. This wide spectrum causes significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

We present 2 cases of adult DAAs that highlight the spectrum of clinical presentation and management strategies. Additionally, a literature review was performed to promote a better understanding of its diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes.

Case Presentation

Case 1

Pertinent history

A 54-year-old woman was referred to our institution for the evaluation of an aortic arch aneurysm. The lesion was first identified incidentally 7 years earlier on a computed tomography angiography (CTA) of the head and neck performed for facial paresthesia. The initial scan revealed a 2.6 × 2.8 × 1.5-cm saccular aneurysm in the inferior wall of the aortic isthmus with peripheral calcification. Her medical history was significant for hypermobile Ehlers-Danlos syndrome. Family history was notable for a sister with repair of an enlarging ascending aortic aneurysm at age 56.

Investigation

A recent CTA of the chest confirmed a saccular aneurysm at the aortic isthmus, which was unchanged in size (Figures 1A and 1B). The aneurysm displayed a smooth contour and a broad-based neck from the inferior aspect of the aortic isthmus with well-defined peripheral calcifications, consistent with a DAA. Over the 7-year surveillance period, the patient remained asymptomatic and the DAA size was unchanged. There was no evidence of rupture, dissection, or significant intraluminal thrombus. Management and follow-up

Considering the long-term stability, relatively small size, lack of symptoms, and the peripheral calcification, a conservative management strategy with continued surveillance was adopted. The decision was made in consultation with a multidisciplinary team, including vascular surgery and cardiology, considering the risks of surgical or endovascular intervention. Aspirin 81 mg daily was initiated as the peripheral calcification raised the possibility of chronic thrombus.

The patient remained asymptomatic at 12-month follow-up and continues to undergo regular follow-up without complications.

Case 2

Pertinent history

A 74-year-old man presented with a 1-month history of hoarseness. His past medical history was notable for surgical PDA ligation in childhood, atrial fibrillation, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia. He was a former smoker with a 20 pack-year history.

Investigation

A CTA of the chest revealed a 7.2-cm saccular aneurysm at the aortic isthmus, at the site of the previous PDA ligation (Figures 2A and 2B). The aneurysm was causing a significant mass effect on the adjacent left recurrent laryngeal nerve, providing an anatomical explanation for his symptoms. The ascending aorta was also aneurysmal in size, measuring up to 4.8 cm. Management and follow-up

Because of the symptomatic nature of the large aneurysm and the high risk of rupture, a 2-stage surgical intervention was planned. The first stage was a left carotid-subclavian bypass to ensure adequate left arm perfusion after the subsequent endovascular procedure. The second stage, performed a week later, involved thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) with a left brachial artery cutdown and coil embolization of the proximal left subclavian artery to completely exclude the aneurysm from the circulation.

The patient's postoperative course was uneventful, and his hoarseness resolved completely within 2 weeks. He remains asymptomatic on his 6-month follow-up, with the latest CTA demonstrating an appropriate postoperative appearance of the aorta (Figure 2C) without evidence of endoleak.

Discussion

These 2 cases illustrate the wide clinical spectrum of adult DAAs, ranging from a stable, asymptomatic DAA amenable to conservative management to a large, symptomatic aneurysm requiring urgent intervention. To better understand this condition, a narrative review of the literature was performed by searching both PubMed and Scopus from inception through March 2026 using the MeSH terms “ductus arteriosus,” “aneurysm,” and “adult” with no restrictions to publication date. Reference lists of all identified articles were manually reviewed to capture additional cases. A total of 121 reported adult DAA cases were identified (Supplemental Table 1).

Diagnosing a DAA is challenging as it often remains asymptomatic and may only be discovered incidentally, as in our first case and in 28.9% of the reviewed cases. When symptoms arise, they are typically nonspecific, resulting from the mass effect of an enlarging aneurysm on adjacent structures. Hoarseness, as in our second case, is the most common presenting symptom (37.7%), resulting from compression of the left recurrent laryngeal nerve (Ortner syndrome). Other less common symptoms include dyspnea (17.5%) and cough (8.8%).

Although historical reports from the 1990s suggested that nearly half of adult DAA cases presented with life-threatening complications such as rupture, thromboembolism, or endocarditis,6 such severe presentations were far less common in our literature review, likely because of earlier detection with modern imaging.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis of a DAA relies on imaging. On plain radiography, a DAA may be observed as a change in the contour of the aortic arch. Although more common conditions such as adenopathy, neoplasm, or aortic aneurysms are often considered first, certain radiographic features can suggest a DAA. Danza et al7 have identified 2 key features: 1) calcification at the periphery of a DAA and/or mottled calcification at the aortic side and 2) a small pedicle linking the lesion to the pulmonary artery, which may be obscured in large aneurysms. Although conventional chest radiography is usually the initial imaging, CTA is the cornerstone of diagnosis, providing detailed anatomical information about the aneurysm's size, morphology, and relationship to surrounding structures. CTA can also detect calcifications that plain radiographs might miss and identify the DAA diameter and the presence of mural thrombus.

The characteristics of aneurysms in our literature review are detailed in Table 1. Aneurysm size was reported for 90 patients, with a median maximum diameter of 5.0 cm (IQR: 3.9-6.5 cm). Of the 50 DAA cases with a specified morphological type, most were saccular (88.0%). Calcification was present in 23 of 81 cases (28.3%) and thrombus in 30 of 81 cases (37.0%), where reported.

Management

Adult DAA management may be a complex clinical challenge, lacking standardized guidelines. Therapeutic strategy must be tailored to the individual patient, considering the aneurysm's size, growth rate, and symptoms and the patient's surgical risk.

For adults with asymptomatic, relatively small, and stable DAAs, a conservative approach with serial imaging is often appropriate. Features suggesting chronicity, such as the peripheral calcification seen in our first case, may further support watchful waiting. However, this approach has risks as historical data show a significant risk of life-threatening complications in unoperated aneurysms.6 Furthermore, DAA size was not specified in many case series, and publication bias toward adverse outcomes may limit generalizability. Conversely, intervention is strongly indicated for symptomatic adults, those with a large aneurysm, or those showing enlargement on serial imaging.8

Historically, open surgical repair was the treatment of choice for DAAs, used in 77 patients (64.7%) in our review. Although effective, this intervention has considerable morbidity and mortality, particularly in older patients or those with significant comorbidities.

Recently, endovascular technology has revolutionized DAA treatment. TEVAR has emerged as a less invasive and increasingly favored alternative for suitable candidates.9 This approach was used in 14 patients (11.6%) in our review. TEVAR offers advantages over open surgery, including reduced operative time, avoidance of thoracotomy, elimination of cardiopulmonary bypass, and shorter hospital stays.

For patients with challenging anatomy, hybrid procedures offer an innovative solution, combining open surgical and endovascular techniques to address complex anatomical challenges. The hybrid approach typically involves surgical revascularization of arch vessels (such as carotid-carotid bypass) followed by TEVAR to exclude the DAA. This strategy was used in our second case and in 5 patients (4.1%) in the literature cohort. The hybrid approach allows for adequate proximal landing zones while maintaining cerebral and upper extremity perfusion, making it particularly valuable for aneurysms in difficult locations that would otherwise require extensive open arch reconstruction.

Outcomes

The overall outcomes of adult DAAs are variable, depending on the aneurysm's size, stability, or growth rate, symptoms, and treatment modality.

The natural history of an untreated large DAA is reported to be poor, with historical data suggesting a high mortality rate from aneurysm rupture. Our literature review supports this as we found a 43.4% mortality rate (10 of 23 patients) in the conservatively managed group with a median DAA size of 5.0 cm (range: 3.0-11.8 cm).Half (5 of 10) of these deaths were due to DAA rupture, highlighting the profound risk of a noninterventional approach. Additionally, all DAA-related deaths occurred in aneurysms with a maximum dimension of ≥4.0 cm. The median size in this group was 9.0 cm (range: 4.0-11.8 cm, n = 7), significantly larger than the 5.0 cm median (range: 0.5-12.0 cm, n = 71) in patients with uneventful outcomes. Conversely, no adverse DAA-related outcomes were reported in patients with aneurysms smaller than 4.0 cm. These findings, however, have significant limitations, including era bias, different measurement techniques, poor surgical candidacy, and small sample sizes for newer techniques.

Outcomes varied by intervention type (Tables 2 and 3). The overall mortality rate for the 121-patient cohort was 16.5% (20 deaths). The open resection group had an 11.6% mortality rate (9 deaths). Most of these deaths (8 of 9) were aneurysm-related, with 6 occurring intraoperatively. In contrast, no deaths occurred among the 14 patients who underwent endovascular repair, all with an uneventful outcome. The hybrid repair group had 1 death (20.0%), from an unrelated cause. Conclusions

A DAA in adults is an extremely rare condition with a highly variable clinical presentation, but it can be life-threatening. Our 2 cases illustrate this spectrum, ranging from a relatively small, stable, asymptomatic DAA managed conservatively to a large, symptomatic aneurysm requiring staged hybrid repair. Our review of 121 reported cases reveals that conservative management of large DAAs carries a 43.3% mortality rate, whereas endovascular and hybrid approaches achieved zero aneurysm-related deaths, though case numbers were limited. Our findings support individualized management based on aneurysm size, symptoms, anatomy, and operative risk. Although endovascular and hybrid strategies appear to offer lower perioperative morbidity and mortality in suitable candidates, larger studies with longer follow-up are needed to define optimal management and outcomes.
