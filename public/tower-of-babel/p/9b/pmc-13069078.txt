A rapid review of genetic association studies of parent-of-origin effects and fetal growth
Authors: Esther Joo Won Kang, Andrew Thomas Dewan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGenetic factors contribute, in part, to fetal growth restriction (FGR), a developmental condition of disrupted fetal development associated with serious health consequences. Among these factors, variants that display parent-of-origin effects (POE) may play a significant role in the variation of human fetal growth.Main BodyWe conducted a synthesis of the available evidence on genetic associations between POE variants and fetal growth traits, aiming to summarize identified variants and compare study designs. Literature searches were performed on the Ovid research platform using the bibliographic databases MEDLINE and Embase, identifying 17 genetic association studies that reported 38 study-wide significant POE variants associated with one or more fetal growth trait(s). Candidate gene approaches were dominant among the studies identified (n = 13), but the application of genome-wide association studies (GWAS) continues to expand as computational phasing and POE determination methods have advanced. While genetic association studies are useful for identifying POE variants, low replication of findings among independent studies remains a major hindrance to validation. Birth weight was the most common measure of fetal growth among our identified studies (n = 15). Further research is needed on alternative measures of fetal growth. Additionally, most of the studies were conducted in sample populations of White European ancestry (n = 9), indicating a gap in research among more diverse populations.ConclusionGenetic association studies have provided evidence that POE variants contribute to fetal growth variation, encouraging further investigations into the genetic regulation and biological mechanisms underlying fetal growth.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40348-026-00224-5.Keywords: Fetal growth, parent-of-origin effects, genetic association studies, genes, genetic variants

Background

Human fetal growth occurs through a complex interplay between the fetal genome and its environment [1–3]. Dysregulation of gene expression and epigenetic processes during this in utero period of development can lead to disruptions in development and the onset of the condition known as fetal growth restriction (FGR), also referred to as intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) [4–6]. FGR is defined as the pathological condition when the fetus fails to attain its full intrinsic growth potential, and it is one of the leading causes of perinatal morbidity and mortality worldwide [7–11]. One of the most common consequences of FGR is low birth weight (BW). Additionally, suboptimal fetal growth is associated with a wide range of negative health consequences not only in utero, but also throughout postnatal development [8, 12, 13].

There has been much attention on the association between fetal growth traits and the development of disease in adulthood. The occurrence of FGR has been previously theorized as a compensatory physiological response to fetal malnutrition that can lead to permanent structural and physiological changes that persist after birth, which may enhance disease risk not only perinatally, but even into adulthood [12, 14–16]. This theory is known as fetal programming and is a key part of the Fetal Origins of Adult Disease Hypothesis (also referred to as the Barker Hypothesis). This hypothesis proposes that the period of fetal development can have significant impacts on the developmental outcomes of individuals throughout their lifetime, with disruptions contributing to increased risk of disease in adulthood. It was born out of the seminal work of epidemiologist David Barker who observed an association between BW and the development of heart disease in adulthood [17]. Subsequent studies have provided evidence that fetal malnutrition and intrauterine stress can lead to physiological adaptations that result in altered fetal growth trajectories and developmental outcomes. For example, there is evidence of vascular changes in placental-fetal blood flow, abnormal development of organs, and alterations in cell signaling in response to limited nutrient availability [7, 18–24]. Genetic components, in addition to environmental factors, contribute to fetal growth regulation and variation. Investigation of genes and variants that contribute to the regulation and variation of fetal growth, and their association with later-life health outcomes, may explain, at least in part, the observations corroborating the Fetal Origins of Adult Disease Hypothesis.

One class of genetic effects that has been previously shown to contribute to variation in fetal growth are parent-of-origin effects (POE) [25, 26]. Parent-of-origin effects (POE) represent a variant-level epigenetic phenomenon in which the phenotypic effect of a genetic variant in the offspring differs depending on whether the allele was inherited maternally or paternally, even when the inherited alleles are identical [27]. Variants with POE have been predominantly linked to mechanisms related to genomic imprinting [28–30]. Genomic imprinting is a type of epigenetic regulatory process where epigenetic marks, such as DNA methylation or histone modifications, are added to inherited alleles in a parent-specific manner. This leads to monoallelic or biased allelic expression rather than typical biallelic expression, thereby contributing to phenotypic variation (Fig. 1). In classically imprinted regions, parent-of-origin specific epigenetic marks are established in the germline, regulate allele-specific transcription, and are stably maintained and inherited across subsequent generations. However, genetic variants with POE have also been observed in non-classically imprinted regions where biased allelic expression may arise through alternative mechanisms, including de novo mutations or epigenetic errors, environmental factors, or interactions within epistatic networks [31–34]. Maternal effects, which are the effects of the mother’s genotype on the offspring gene expression, may also mimic the effects of genomic imprinting and add to the overall complexity in characterizing POE within offspring [35, 36]. While multiple mechanisms may account for observed POE, parent-of-origin–specific allelic expression contributes to phenotypic variation of complex traits.

POE are evolutionarily conserved in the regulation of human development [37–39]. Imprinted genes identified in humans, which are predicted to represent an estimated 1% of the human genome, have been shown to regulate a diverse range of cellular functions [40]. A subset of these genes has been shown to be notably associated with prenatal and postnatal development, behavior, and metabolism [30]. Disruption of the parent-of-origin–specific expression at imprinted loci linked to fetal growth has been shown to be causally associated with FGR and other developmental disorders, referred to collectively as imprinting disorders (Table 1). These monogenic disorders can arise due to genetic mutations or epigenetic alterations of the imprinted regions, or due to uniparental disomy (UPD), where both alleles are inherited from the same parent-of-origin [41]. About ~200 imprinted genes have been identified in humans, and additional imprinted genes and other POE alleles remain to be discovered [42].

Although the role of POE in fetal growth is increasingly recognized, the full extent of its contribution to variability and heritability in fetal growth remains unclear. Substantial advances in the past decades have improved complex trait analysis through a genetic association framework [43]. However, a standardized and widely accepted approach for identifying POE genetic variants associated with complex traits, such as fetal growth, that extend outside of monogenic traits, has yet to be fully established. This may in part be due to the heterogeneity in available haplotyping methods to assign parent-of-origin and statistical methods to characterize POE across different populations. Given the evidence that POE variants may contribute to the regulation of fetal development, in this rapid review, we aim to identify and summarize POE association studies that have been conducted on fetal growth outcomes. In addition, we seek to compare the different approaches that have been applied to identify POE fetal growth variants, and highlight current limitations, as well as future directions for research.

Methods

This rapid review was planned, conducted, and reported following the rapid review methods guidance that is continually reviewed, published, and updated by the Cochrane Rapid Review Methods Group and the PRISMA-Protocols (PRISMA-P) [44–46]. The full protocol for this review following PRISMA-P guidelines can be found in Supplementary Table 3. Although PRISMA-P guidelines are designed for prospective protocol registration, this protocol was recorded retrospectively to comprehensively document and standardize the methods used in the completed rapid review.

Literature search strategy

We conducted a rapid literature search in order to summarize the literature on POE genetic association studies of fetal growth systematically and efficiently. The search strategy was developed with the assistance of expert information specialists with vast experience on performing literature reviews, including systematic and rapid reviews. The search was conducted in the Ovid research platform on February 5th, 2024 using the bibliographic databases MEDLINE and Embase. A repeat search was conducted using the exact same search terms on November 25th, 2025 to capture any additional records that may have been added since the original search date.

The search terms used were the following: ((POE or POO or imprint* or “parent-of-origin” or parental gen* or maternal gen* or paternal gen* or fetal gen* or foetal gen*) and (GWAS or “genome-wide association stud*” or “genet* associat*”)).mp. and ((“fetal growth” or “foetal growth” or birth or intrauterine or fetal or foetal or placental or infant).mp. or exp fetal development/).

Title and abstract screening were conducted by both reviewers (EJWK, AD) on all articles. Any conflicts were resolved through discussion and consensus between the two reviewers. Duplicate records not flagged by Covidence’s automated duplication-flagging software were additionally flagged and removed by the reviewers. In the subsequent full-text review step, the same two reviewers also independently reviewed all the articles that passed the title and abstract screening using the listed criteria. Again, any disputes over study inclusion or exclusion were discussed before reaching a consensus decision. In addition to the articles obtained from the search, four additional relevant articles were included in the final group of articles for inclusion in our review. These were discovered through team suggestions from manual backward citation chaining and expert recommendation. These additional studies were included as they met all the criteria for eligibility, but due to the imperfect nature of database searches, they were not identified in our initial search results.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Data extraction was performed on all included articles by one reviewer (EJWK), and the accuracy and completeness were verified by a second reviewer (AD). The data extraction step involved extracting data from the following fields: author, year published, title, type of POE association study, the number of variants tested for POE, fetal growth outcome, the number of POE variants that were study-wide significant, the study-wide significance threshold, type of study design (family- vs. population-based), type of sampling if family design, haplotyped sample size, study cohort, ancestry, genotyping platform. haplotype inference method, POE determination method, and article(s) referenced for either of the method categories. Due to the heterogeneity in study design, outcomes, sampling, and methods, we chose not to pursue a meta-analysis as the results could not be combined, nor could a formal publication bias analysis be conducted.

We chose to use the Quality of Genetic Association Studies (Q-Genie) tool to assess the quality of studies included in the final review [48, 49]. This tool was developed to improve confidence in the results of published genetic association studies by utilizing an empirically validated rationale to generate a quality score for published studies. The rationale encompasses careful assessment of both traditional epidemiologic biases as well as considerations of biases specific to the design of genetic association studies. The biases considered, but not limited to, included exposure and outcome misclassification bias, selection bias, confounding due to population effects such as ancestry or relatedness, sampling bias, reporting bias, and statistical validity. This step was important to establish the validity of study findings in each article included in our review. Each reviewer independently used the tool to generate scores for each study to assess whether the study was of poor, moderate, or good quality. Then we discussed any differences in scores to assign a final rating of study quality.

Results

Literature search

Our combined searches yielded 1,715 records from the MEDLINE and Embase databases (Fig. 2). After the removal of 421 duplicate records, 1,294 studies remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 54 studies were retained for full-text review. Out of these remaining studies, 43 studies were excluded for the following reasons: (1) lack of full-text availability (n = 12); (2) study design did not match the inclusion criteria (n = 24); (3) did not have the outcome(s) of interest (n = 7). Thirteen studies were retained after full-text review. Four additional studies were added to the pool of eligible studies through team suggestions. This resulted in a final total of 17 studies eligible for inclusion in this rapid review [50–66].

Data extraction

The extracted study characteristics are reported in Supplementary Table 1. The study years ranged from 2004 to 2025. Of the total number of studies (n = 17), 71% of the studies (n = 12) were solely candidate gene association studies. Out of the remaining studies (29%), two studies were genome-wide association studies (GWAS), two studies were two-stage GWAS, and one study conducted a separate candidate gene and genome-wide approach. We define two-stage GWAS as distinct from a traditional GWAS or candidate gene study in the sense that in the first stage, a traditional GWAS or GWAS meta-analysis is conducted, and in the second stage after collecting GWAS results, candidate genes are selected within the same study for subsequent POE analysis. Regarding sample selection, 82% of studies were family-based studies (n = 14), and the remaining 18% were population-based (n = 3). Family-based studies included parent-offspring duo, trio, quad, and twin sampling designs. A large proportion of studies were conducted in populations of white European ancestry, or included this ancestry group in their analyses (n = 13). Other ancestry groups included African-American (n = 3), Israeli (n = 1), and Japanese (n = 1). Regarding fetal growth outcomes, almost all studies, except one, used birth weight as an outcome measure or a measure derived from birth weight (n = 16). Other fetal growth outcomes investigated were birth length, birth body mass index (BMI), head circumference (at birth), and small for gestational age (SGA) or small for birth date (SFD). Methods used to phase haplotypes and determine POE varied across studies. Seven studies used observed pedigrees to determine haplotypes, three studies used a statistical phasing procedure to estimate haplotypes, six studies used both methods combined, and one study used estimated haplotype frequencies instead of phasing the haplotypes. Almost all studies explored POE associations with both parents except one study that sought to identify paternal POE, and one other study that was ambiguous in assigning parent-of-origin. Method of POE inference also varied by study and is further described in the discussion section. A total of 38 study-wide significant POE variants were reported and are summarized in Table 2.

Discussion

Candidate gene vs. genome-wide approaches for identifying POE

Among the included studies, most utilized a candidate gene association approach (12 out of 17 studies). These studies selected genes or gene regions with known roles in fetal growth or prior evidence of POE in mice and/or human models. Imprinted genes have been shown to be highly conserved across mammalian species in normal fetal development [37–39]. The rationale in applying a candidate gene association framework is to focus on genes with known biological plausibility to identify specific variants with a higher prior probability of having POE [67]. Aligning with this rationale, some of the most frequently selected candidate genes belonged to the insulin-like growth factor (IGF) family (IGF2, IGF1R), and genes linked to the IGF family (INS, H19) [68–72]. These growth-related genes are highly evolutionarily conserved and play a crucial role in fetal development. Disruptions in imprinted expression of these genes have been well documented to contribute to abnormal fetal development, suggesting that POE variants may epigenetically regulate their expression [73–78]. For example, the IGF2/H19 locus is a well-characterized imprinted gene cluster conserved in mammals [79]. As depicted in Fig. 3, through parent-specific epigenetic regulation, IGF2 is expressed from the paternal allele and promotes cell growth, whereas H19 is expressed from the maternal allele and acts to suppress cell growth. Complex epigenetic regulatory networks, such as those observed at this locus, may leverage POE through allele-specific expression to regulate fetal growth.

Parent-of-origin–specific epigenetic regulation of the imprinted IGF2/H19 gene cluster. The IGF2/H19 functional gene cluster regulates fetal growth through parent-of-origin–specific allelic expression. On the paternally inherited allele, methylation of ICR1 (Imprinting Control Region 1) prevents binding of the CTCF (CCCTC-binding factor), preventing the transcription of H19 and permitting enhancer-enabled transcription of IGF2. On the maternally inherited allele, ICR1 is unmethylated, allowing CTCF binding, which blocks enhancer access to IGF2 and instead promotes H19 transcription. Through the functional balance of parent-of-origin–specific expression of this cluster, fetal growth is regulated. Loss of imprinting within this cluster, such as through uniparental disomy (UPD), results in growth dysregulation

A major limitation of the candidate gene approach is that it is highly dependent on previous functional knowledge of the genes to inform variant selection, which, when this knowledge is insufficient, can introduce selection bias [80]. As sequencing and statistical methods have advanced, genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have risen as an alternative, hypothesis-free approach [81]. Rather than testing genetic markers limited to a specific gene or region, the GWAS design involves testing genetic markers across entire genomes in sample populations to find variants statistically associated with the phenotype of interest. This evolution in study design has allowed for rapid discovery of novel genes and genetic variants, including those with low effect sizes, with increasing efficiency. Results from GWAS can also be used to inform further investigation of specific variants and genes such as in candidate gene studies.

While there are several high-profile GWAS that have sought to identify variants associated with fetal growth, the incorporation of methodology to identify POE variants using a GWAS framework is a more recent approach. In this review, only three studies incorporated testing for POE directly within the genome-wide association scans [59, 64, 66]. Two other studies utilized a two-stage GWAS design in which candidate variants associated with fetal growth outcomes were first identified genome-wide and then subsequently tested for POE [60, 62]. GWAS offer the potential to discover new POE variants associated with fetal growth that have otherwise been overlooked. However, this study design requires substantially larger sample sizes than candidate gene approaches to overcome multiple-testing penalties and achieve sufficient power to detect true associations [81, 82]. These challenges are further compounded by the need for adequate numbers of phased haplotypes with assigned parental origin, which is dependent on underlying sample structure[83]. Improvements in haplotype phasing and POE determination may allow for wider application of GWAS in identifying variants that exhibit POE.

Haplotype phasing methods

Assigning parent-of-origin to alleles is essential to identify evidence of POE, and it first requires accurate reconstruction of haplotypes, referred to as phasing [83]. Haplotypes are sets of alleles inherited together that are located on the same chromosome. In this review, we discovered that the method of phasing haplotypes varied by study. Haplotype phasing has traditionally relied on direct inference of haplotypes through pedigree information in duos or trios [84, 85]. While haplotype phasing reconstructs the two chromosomal haplotypes carried by an individual, it does not by itself assign parental origin in the absence of pedigree information; additional inference methods are required to distinguish maternally and paternally inherited haplotypes in population-based datasets. Genotype data from family samples such as duos (single parent and offspring) and trios (both parents and offspring) is particularly useful in haplotype phasing, as the offspring/parent haplotype(s) can be phased directly from the pedigrees and allow the assignment of parent-of-origin. All of the family-based studies in this review incorporated direct inference of haplotypes based on observed genotypes in pedigrees. Sampling related individuals can be considered more informative, both for phasing, assignment of parent-of-origin, and association testing, which can lead to reduced error rates and improve power at smaller sample sizes compared to population-based studies [84, 86]. However, recruiting sufficient sample sizes of related individuals and obtaining pedigree information can be challenging [87].

The development of computational phasing methods has improved the ability to infer haplotypes where direct inference is not feasible, thereby enabling applications in population-based studies. For alleles where the haplotype cannot be directly determined, family or population haplotype frequency data can be used to statistically infer the phase for the offspring [84]. Both population-based studies and seven of the family-based studies included in this review utilized computational phasing to estimate missing haplotypes and/or calculate haplotype frequencies (Supplementary Table 1). Though computational phasing can be a powerful tool, there are important considerations, such as phasing accuracy and computational efficiency with different parameters and trade-offs. Modern computational phasing tools have made haplotype phasing in population-based biobanks highly accurate and increasingly computationally efficient [88–91]. However, a significant challenge remains: determining the parental origin of these haplotypes when pedigree information or parental genomes are missing or limited.

Whether individuals are related can both influence phasing accuracy and improve assignment of parent-of-origin. One method to phase haplotypes through incorporation of pedigree information is long-range phasing [92, 93]. Through this approach, related, and even distantly related, individuals can be phased by leveraging haplotypes that are shared identical-by-descent (IBD), which reduces the number of haplotype configurations to be considered when assigning parent-of-origin. This long-range phasing method was used to generate haplotypes in two studies included in this review [59, 62] and the methodology was further extended in two additional studies to infer parent-of-origin without reliance on prior genealogical information [64, 66]. By reconstructing pedigrees through relatedness estimates and identifying shared IBD segments, haplotype estimation can be improved in study populations regardless of whether relatives are explicitly recruited. These computational phasing methods that enable accurate parent-of-origin assignments in larger, more distantly related datasets, such as biobanks, may be a powerful strategy to improve downstream analyses aimed at identifying POE on an adequately powered scale.

POE inference methods

Besides the phasing methods, methods of POE inference also varied by study. The heterogeneity in methodology is likely due to the different study design considerations and the development of statistical methods to better capture POE. The simplest methods used comparison of groups with computed test statistics using either the distribution of alleles or haplotype transmitted by the mother vs. the father. In Kukuvitis et al. 2004, a t-test and permutation analysis were used to compare outcomes between offspring grouped by which parental allele they inherited [50]. In Li et al. 2018, a Z-test was used to compare the effect sizes on offspring birth weight and BMI Z-scores between maternal and paternal SNPs to identify POE SNPs associated with birth weight [61].

Moving beyond between-group comparisons, statistical modeling represented the next major category of methods commonly applied among studies in this review. In Horikoshi et al. 2016 regression modeling was performed to identify allele-specific associations between study variants and offspring birth weight according to which parent transmitted the allele [60]. Across studies, modeling strategies varied: some modeled haplotype frequencies rather than allele frequencies, others employed mixed models or multiple modeling approaches, including the addition of an explicit parent-of-origin effect (POE) parameter (Supplementary Table 1). In Benonisdottir et al. 2016, four different genetic models were tested: additive, recessive, and parent-of-origin (paternal and maternal), and the model chosen was based on the most significant association with birth length [59]. This multi-model approach was utilized in order to better dissect the category of effect of the observed association with fetal growth outcomes by testing the paternal and maternal transmitted alleles separately. A similar approach was used in Hofmeister et al. 2022, and Hofmeister et al. 2025, where multiple association models—additive, differential, paternal, maternal— were applied genome-wide to identify POE variants associated with birth weight [64, 66]. In contrast to the recessive model, the differential model was incorporated to compare paternally and maternally inherited alleles at heterozygous genotypes. Unlike two-stage GWAS designs, which initially identify variants under an additive GWAS model, the application of multiple association models allows for the identification of variants where maternal and paternal alleles exhibit opposite effects. Hofmeister et al. 2025 further proposed a standardized approach for POE detection within an association framework by proposing a POE differential test P value (PD), along with a strict significance threshold, to formally assess differences between maternal and paternal effects and promote greater inter-study comparability [66]. To date, this approach has not yet been widely applied in POE association studies.

The complexity in capturing POE in fetal growth is that there may be multiple genetic effects to account for when modeling associations. Not only may there be differences in effect sizes and strength of association between the transmitted paternal and maternal alleles in the offspring, but the non-transmitted allele may also pass an indirect effect onto the offspring outcome [35, 94]. In Juliusdottir et al. 2021, they modeled the transmitted effects (direct) and non-transmitted effects (indirect) of parental alleles in offspring out of the set of fetal growth variants they had identified and found evidence of both POE and maternal genetic effects on the offspring outcome [62]. In contrast to other studies in this review, this study used probabilistic model-based clustering of different observed effect sizes from the transmitted and non-transmitted parental alleles to categorize variants into patterns of POE. To better identify and characterize POE on fetal growth outcomes, care must be taken to distinguish between different patterns of genetic variation.

Conclusion

In this rapid review, we conducted a literature search in two databases for all studies that investigated an association between genes or variants that exhibit POE and fetal growth traits. This search yielded 17 studies and 38 study-wide significant reported POE variants. Within a single study, or between studies, reported SNPs may be potentially correlated, indicating that the variants may be tagging the same underlying genetic signal, which can be calculated by estimating linkage disequilibrium (LD) between variants. This was the case for the reported variants for each gene in three candidate gene studies, Adkins et al. 2008 (D’ > 0.8), Nagaya et al. 2009 (D’ ≥ 0.92), and Chen and Zhang 2022 (D’ > 0.8) [52, 53, 63]. This is consistent with the candidate gene study design in the sense that these linked SNPs were specifically selected to tag identical haplotype blocks or interacting regions within the candidate genes. For cross-study comparison, due to lack of access to genotype data for the studies included in this review, we used the LDpair function in the LDlinkR software package to do a preliminary check for potential correlation between reported variants across studies with similar ancestry populations [95]. This tool calculates pairwise metrics of LD using population genotype data from the 1000 Genomes Project, which may not match the allelic frequencies of the original study cohorts. We found that none of the reported POE variants that showed a significant association with fetal growth outcome(s) were replicated in independent studies, including those that were located in the same gene.

While both study designs have contributed to the discovery of POE variants that are associated with fetal growth traits, replication across studies remained limited in this review. This may reflect discrepancies between studies or aspects of study design. For example, there may have been differences in the availability of identical SNP markers, as sequencing platforms and genotyping arrays varied. For genetic association studies, the genetic variants selected for association testing are unlikely to be causal themselves [96, 97]. Instead, genotyped markers are typically chosen for genome coverage rather than to reflect biological function. When an association between a SNP and an outcome is observed, it usually reflects linkage disequilibrium between the tested marker and an underlying causal variant. However, when genotype imputation is performed, association testing can extend beyond directly genotyped markers to variants that may better capture the causal signal [98]. In Table 2, three studies included imputed variants rather than relying exclusively on directly genotyped markers [59, 61, 62]. Consequently, cross-study comparability may have been reduced, as observed associations may involve different genotyped or imputed variants that variably tag the same causal locus. Additionally, differences in statistical power across studies may have also contributed to the lack of replication. While we reported study-wide significant variants, it is possible that associations for the same variants or genes were observed in multiple studies but did not meet the significance threshold. These findings highlight a need for replication in independent populations with sufficient power to validate these discoveries.

With regards to fetal growth traits, birth weight (BW) was the most popular outcome measure of fetal growth in the studies included in this review (n = 15). This is likely due to the complexity in accurately measuring fetal growth [99, 100]. Large population datasets with fetal growth measurements, besides birth weight, are sparse. In addition, recall bias may be introduced when self-reported BW measurements are used versus records of clinical measurements [101]. Capturing more proximal measures of fetal growth may be more resource-intensive and impractical to obtain larger numbers of study subjects [102]. While BW is an important indicator of fetal growth, it does not accurately describe all aspects of fetal growth progression. More research of POE is needed in fetal growth traits alternative to BW, such as fetal growth velocity or biparietal diameter. Appropriate covariates should also be considered when modeling these outcomes including, sex, gestational age, and maternal factors [103–105].

The results of this review allowed us to compare different characteristics of study design and methodology in applying an association framework to investigate parent-of-origin effects and fetal growth. Candidate gene studies, which primarily focused on genes with known roles in fetal development and prior evidence of POE, were the most common study design. However, the application of GWAS in investigating POE and fetal growth has been expanding in recent years. Genetic association studies can corroborate existing evidence of associations between POE and fetal growth traits as well as provide opportunities to identify novel POE variants. Computational phasing methods are increasingly being developed to overcome limitations based on relatedness to expand phasing potential and assignment of parental origin to alleles in samples with limited pedigree information. This reduces reliance on family-based sampling, which may improve sample size and study power. Across the studies included in this review, there is considerable methodological variation in how POE are identified, and distinguishing between parentally inherited allelic effects and non-transmitted parental allelic effects to classify patterns of POE remains an ongoing challenge in the field.

Despite advancements in computational phasing and POE determination, further research is needed to extend the application of these methodologies in mixed or non-White European populations. Researchers may miss POE variants associated with fetal growth that are present in other genetic ancestries [106]. In this review, only two studies considered multiple genetic ancestries when identifying POE variants associated with fetal growth (Supplementary Table 1). Of the remaining 15 studies that investigated single-ancestry populations, the majority were of White European ancestry (n = 9). To date, no population-based, genome-wide investigation of POE in non-European populations has been published. The lack of research in diverse populations may reflect sampling bias towards White European populations. Evidence of fetal growth restriction is well documented in non-European populations, and the strength or direction of association between POE variants and fetal growth may vary by genetic ancestry [107, 108]. Future studies exploring evidence of POE in diverse populations are needed to improve generalizability, interpretation, and application of findings.

Across 17 studies, 38 genetic variants with parent-of-origin–specific associations with fetal growth traits were reported, corresponding to 32 independent loci after accounting for linkage disequilibrium. Replication of variant-level associations in independent cohorts was limited, although novel gene associations were identified, emphasizing the need for further investigation. Overall, while replicating findings across studies remains difficult, through adoption of a standardized framework in POE association models, there is substantial potential to both replicate known findings and identify novel POE variants that contribute to fetal growth variation. Expanding the known set of genes with POE variants may improve our understanding of the genetic regulation of fetal growth, particularly in corroborating evidence of the Barker Hypothesis, and inform future diagnostic and therapeutic pathways.
