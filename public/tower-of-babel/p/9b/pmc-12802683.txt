ICE-Guided False Lumen Occlusion Following Combined TAVR and TEVAR
Authors: Yusuke Kobari, Yinghao Lim, Arif A Khokhar, Yannick Willemen, Timothy Andrew Resch, Ole De Backer, Gintautas Bieliauskas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConcomitant severe aortic stenosis and descending aortic pathology pose unique challenges for transcatheter treatment strategies.Case SummaryA 75-year-old man with end-stage renal disease and chronic descending aortic dissection presented with symptomatic severe aortic stenosis. Preprocedural imaging revealed a heavily calcified aortic valve and a large thoracic aortic aneurysm with dissection. He underwent successful transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement using a 65-cm-long DrySeal sheath, immediately followed by thoracic endovascular aortic repair. Two weeks later, residual false lumen perfusion was treated with a custom-made false lumen occluder under intracardiac echocardiography guidance. The patient recovered uneventfully.DiscussionThis case illustrates the feasibility and potential advantages of simultaneous transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement and thoracic endovascular aortic repair in the setting of complex aortic pathology. It also underscores the emerging and pivotal role of intracardiac echocardiography in guiding endovascular interventions. Such combined strategies may help minimize access-related complications and contribute to favorable clinical outcomes.Key words: aortic dissection, aortic stenosis, thoracic endovascular aneurysm repair, transcatheter aortic valve replacement

History of Presentation

A 75-year-old man was referred to our institution with exertional dyspnea classified as NYHA functional class III. No signs of peripheral edema or overt heart failure were noted.

Take-Home Messages

•Descending aortic aneurysm and/or dissection should not preclude transfemoral TAVR, when appropriate access strategies are planned, and a simultaneous TAVR and TEVAR approach may be preferable to a staged approach to minimize access-related complications and reduce aneurysm rupture risk.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of arterial hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and glomerular nephropathy requiring hemodialysis (estimated glomerular filtration rate: 8 mL/min/1.73 m2). He also had a known chronic descending thoracic aortic dissection under regular surveillance.

Differential Diagnosis

In a hemodialysis-dependent patient with progressive exertional dyspnea, differential diagnoses included heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, valvular heart disease (particularly aortic stenosis), and anemia of chronic disease.

Investigations

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed concentric left ventricular hypertrophy with preserved ejection fraction (60%), and a mixed valvular lesion consisting of severe aortic stenosis and moderate aortic regurgitation (Videos 1 and 2). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) demonstrated a heavily calcified tricuspid aortic valve with an annular area of 490 mm2, large dimensions of the sinus of Valsalva, and a horizontal aorta (64°). A descending thoracic aortic aneurysm (maximum diameter: 63 mm) with a chronic dissection and a primary entry tear in the thoracic segment was also noted (Figure 1). Hence, the patient fulfilled clinical indications for aortic valve replacement and aortic repair. After a heart team discussion, it was decided to offer the patient transfemoral transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) treatment with concomitant thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) under general anesthesia.

Case Presentation With Procedural Planning and Execution

Preprocedural CT showed a calcified, tricuspid aortic valve and an extensive thoracic aortic aneurysm with a dissection starting just distal to the left subclavian artery and extending to the terminal abdominal aorta. A procedural strategy for concomitant TAVR and TEVAR treatment was made; this needed to be supplemented with a transcatheter occlusion of a persistent false lumen a few weeks later. AS = aortic stenosis; CT = computed tomography; TAVR = transcatheter aortic valve replacement; TEVAR = thoracic endovascular aortic repair.

Management

TAVR procedure

Due to severe tortuosity of the left femoral artery, the right femoral artery was chosen for primary access. The puncture site in the right common femoral artery was preclosed using a ProStyle device (Abbott), which was followed by insertion of an 8-F sheath. A hydrophilic guidewire and 5-F JR4.0 catheter were used to navigate through the true lumen, which was also confirmed by contrast injection. Next, a 65-cm-long 20-F DrySeal Flex Introducer sheath (W.L. Gore Inc) was inserted over a Lunderquist Extra Stiff double-curved guidewire—thereby bypassing the descending aortic aneurysm and dissection—and an Evolut FX+ 29 mm (Medtronic) transcatheter heart valve was delivered and successfully implanted through this sheath (Figure 2).

TEVAR procedure

Using the same femoral access after TAVR, 2 covered TEVAR stent grafts (Zenith Alpha Thoracic 36 × 209 mm and Zenith Alpha Thoracic 38 × 217 mm; Cook Medical) were deployed between the left subclavian artery and the celiac trunk (Figure 2). Final angiography confirmed successful occlusion of the dissection entry site at the proximal segment of the descending aorta. Hemostasis at the main access was achieved with a combined ProStyle and 8-F AngioSeal (Terumo) technique. The total procedure time for the combined TAVR and TEVAR procedures was 66 minutes (fluoroscopic time: 32 minutes), with only 68 mL of contrast dye used. The postprocedural transthoracic echocardiogram demonstrated a well-functioning transcatheter heart valve with mild paravalvular leakage (Videos 3 and 4). The patient was discharged the following day in good clinical condition.

False lumen occlusion under intracardiac echocardiography guidance

Two weeks later, the patient presented with new-onset chest pain. electrocardiogram was completely normal, and transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated a well-functioning Evolut transcatheter heart valve with normal left ventricular ejection fraction. A repeat CT scan revealed remaining perfusion of the false lumen in the thoracic part of the descending aorta and slight enlargement of aortic diameters in the same segment. A custom-made false lumen occluder (Candy plug 46 mm; Cook Medical) was chosen to exclude the false lumen and prevent further growth of the descending aortic aneurysm.

The right iliofemoral axis was again chosen for the primary access. Access to the false lumen was needed to deliver the false lumen occluder. However, it was difficult to identify dissection reentry sites within the abdominal aorta. Hence, a ViewFlex Xtra intracardiac echocardiography (ICE) catheter (St Jude Medical) was placed in the inferior vena cava for additional imaging (Figure 3). ICE provided clear visualization of the abdominal aorta and helped to identify a dissection reentry site, allowing accurate wire crossing into the false lumen, which was confirmed by contrast injection. The false lumen occlude device—also called candy plug—was deployed adjacent to the distal part of the previously placed TEVAR stent graft; this was to prevent backflow and promote thrombosis in the false lumen. The total procedure time was 62 minutes (fluoroscopic time: 19 minutes).

Outcome and follow-up

The patient was discharged the following day without complications. Three months after the procedure, the patient does not have any relevant symptoms. The follow-up CT is planned 6 months after the procedure.

Discussion

Coexistence of severe aortic stenosis and pathology in the descending aorta—such as aortic aneurysm and/or dissection—is not uncommon. Although no clear recommendations are provided in current guidelines regarding the mode and timing of treatment of such concomitant diseases, this case report nicely demonstrates that both pathologies can be elegantly treated concomitantly using transcatheter techniques.

Transfemoral TAVR in cases with aortic disease may (seem to) be more challenging due to a higher risk of vascular complications, and some operators may be willing to choose an alternative access for TAVR. However, as illustrated in this case report, the presence of an abdominal or thoracic aneurysm or dissection should not be prohibitive for a transfemoral TAVR approach, with appropriate planning and access management. The use of long introducer sheaths, such as the 65-cm-long DrySeal sheath, is instrumental in this.

Whether a concomitant or staged approach for TAVR and TEVAR/endovascular aneurysm repair should be chosen can be debated.1 A concomitant TAVR and TEVAR approach is often feasible and may even be more preferable than a staged strategy. Performing both procedures concomitantly can minimize access-related vascular complications and may reduce the risk of aneurysm rupture caused by post-TAVR fluctuations in blood pressure.1 This case supports the feasibility and safety of a concomitant approach in well-selected patients. However, the choice between a staged vs concomitant strategy should be guided by individual patient characteristics, and further research is needed to refine the decision-making algorithm for this complex clinical scenario. Although combined TAVR and TEVAR procedures are typically associated with increased contrast use and concerns regarding contrast-induced nephropathy, this was not a limitation in our case because the patient had preexisting end-stage renal disease requiring chronic hemodialysis.

False lumen occluders are sometimes used in patients with chronic type B aortic dissection after TEVAR to stop continued blood flow in the false lumen. This residual flow often comes from distal entry tears in the abdominal aorta or iliac arteries. The technique was originally known as the candy plug technique, which involved modifying a thoracic stent graft into a candy-shaped plug by adding a diameter-restricting suture in the middle of the stent graft. Today, custom-made false lumen occluders are available, making the procedure simpler and more standardized. In our case, visualization of the dissection membrane within the abdominal aorta was achieved by placing the ICE catheter within the inferior vena cava. ICE provided real-time intravascular imaging, allowing wire navigation into the false lumen and subsequent successful delivery of the false lumen occluder. This case highlights the growing utility of ICE in guiding endovascular interventions such as TAVR and TEVAR.2 ICE offers high-resolution imaging and color Doppler flow assessment, which can be particularly valuable in complex anatomies, as demonstrated here. Scherer et al3 also reported a case of ascending aorta pseudoaneurysm repair using intra-aortic 3-dimensional ICE guidance, further supporting the versatility of ICE. The role of ICE in complex structural and endovascular procedures is expected to expand.4

Conclusions

This case highlights the feasibility of simultaneous transfemoral TAVR and TEVAR, emphasizing the importance of having endovascular skills in structural heart teams. Furthermore, ICE offers a novel approach for guiding complex aortic procedures, such as endovascular aortic repair.
