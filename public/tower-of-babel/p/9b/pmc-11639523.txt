Exploring the learning environment through an organizational theory lens: A case study from nursing education
Authors: Rhoda Meyer, Elize Archer, Susan C. van Schalkwyk
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The calls for curricula review to adequately prepare nursing graduates have increased over the last decade. In response, many nursing education institutions across the world have considered ways to review and renew their curricula. Part of these renewal processes seeks to prepare nursing graduates to function within highly dynamic and challenging environments. The aim of this study was to explore the private healthcare context as a learning environment using an organizational theory lens, with a view to informing and enhancing curriculum development and implementation in nursing education.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A qualitative case study methodology within an interpretivist paradigm was used, drawing on organizational theory. The population included educators who teach undergraduate nursing students at a private higher education institution in South Africa and managers at the clinical sites where these students undertake their clinical learning. Data collection, which was undertaken over the period 2018–2020, included observations, focus group discussions, and interviews. Thematic analysis was undertaken, and a three-tiered analytical approach was followed to analyze the data.RESULTS:Four themes were identified: the learning environment as a site of tension, a site of cultural layering, a site of role complexity, and a site of hierarchy.CONCLUSION:Nursing education is dynamic, providing a range of learning experiences. An understanding of this environment from an organizational theory perspective provides some insights into potential challenges and opportunities for learning. Curriculum developers should consider ways of optimizing the opportunities so that students can be provided with learning experiences that enhance the development of the necessary competence.

Background

Calls for curriculum renewal to adequately prepare nursing graduates for the world of work have garnered significant attention over the last few years.[1] These calls stem from growing healthcare demands, the impact of increased student numbers, and the need to improve healthcare across the globe.[12345] In response, nursing education institutions are looking at ways to renew their curricula.[5] These renewal processes have, among others, highlighted the need to understand “learning environments” (the spaces where learning occurs) in the interest of improving the quality of learning.[4567] Liang and colleagues[8] have also reported how renewal processes often involve preparing nursing graduates to function within highly dynamic environments, thus pointing to implications for the learning environment.

Typically, the learning environment for students in nursing education may be distinguished from other higher education fields by the different types of environments encountered during learning, including the classroom, clinical environment, and simulation laboratories.[9] Exploring these contexts is important for various reasons, including academic success and student satisfaction.[1011] Similarly, factors within the clinical environment have the potential to influence learning experiences as students engage in what is known as workplace learning.[121314] The extent to which a workplace is designed for learning is determined by the degree to which learning is supported in that environment.[15]

Learning experiences are shaped by a range of factors within the learning environment including cultural, social, educational, organizational, and physical.[1617] The interaction between these domains creates environments that are unique and therefore pose variable challenges when it comes to implementing the curriculum.[18] Often, these challenges may be augmented by factors external to the institution, such as the need to increase the number of nursing graduates.[19] In some countries, this has meant that nursing education has expanded from being offered by traditional, often public-funded, university settings, to private higher education institutions (PHEI). In South Africa, for example, nursing education has seen significant shifts in terms of how and where nurses are educated, including a rapid growth in PHEIs.[202122] This growth was largely in response to a shortage in the nursing workforce,[23] resulting in PHEIs educating nurses specifically to meet their own workforce needs. These PHEIs, which are typically linked to private healthcare organizations, exist within the boundaries of various organizational structures, which might be quite different to those encountered in the traditional academic hospital context.

The link between organizational imperatives and the learning environment has previously been highlighted in the health professions education literature.[2425] However, few studies have focused specifically on nursing education in the context of PHEIs. If we accept that the environment is fundamental to student learning, it can be argued that understanding the PHEI context as it moves into offering more nursing degree programs warrants exploration to determine the enablers and constraints to learning in this context. Although PHEIs have been involved in the training of nursing students in South Africa, little is known about these institutions as environments for learning. This has created a knowledge gap, which, if addressed, could potentially facilitate effective curriculum development and implementation in this context. We, therefore, sought to explore the PHEI as a learning environment from a range of different perspectives. The research question was: How does the private healthcare context manifest as a learning environment?

Bolman and Deal’s organizational model was selected to inform the analysis of the data. This model has been described as useful in addressing the multifaceted nature of organizations in a contemporary context,[26] thus offering the opportunity to view the organization from multiple perspectives.[27] It has also previously been applied in the context of health professions education.[2628] The model offers an approach to gauge organizational processes from four frames namely, structural, human resource, political, and symbolic.[26]

The structural frame emphasizes the alignment of structural systems to organizational goals for the purpose of enhancing coordination.[29] Communication, and specifically the extent to which it contributes toward the overall efficiency within the organization, is also underpinned by the structural frame.[30] The human resource frame relates to the needs, skills, and relationships of employees within the organization, focusing on interpersonal dynamics between different role-players. The goal is to nurture a symbiotic relationship between employees and the organization centered around the alignment of employees’ needs with organizational needs.[26] The political frame offers a lens to analyze the extent to which power, conflict, and competition are distributed for the benefit of the employee and the organization.[31] The symbolic frame encompasses the culture within the organization. This frame is described in terms of individuals coming together, bringing their own culture into the organizational space, as well as conforming to the culture within the organization, possibly giving rise to conflict when these are not aligned.[29]

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A qualitative, interpretative case study design,[3233] drawing on organizational theory, was used to explore the PHEI as an environment for learning. Qualitative research allows for the co-construction of meaning between the researcher and participant, followed by the interpretation of these findings by the researcher.[34] The interpretivist paradigm was deemed appropriate as it supports the notion that the researcher is dependent on participants’ views with the intention of acquiring a deeper understanding of the complexities of their experiences.[35]

In keeping with the characteristics of case study research,[33] data were collected in several different ways including through observations, focus group discussions (FGDs), and interviews. These data generation activities were intentionally selected to create a high level of connection between the researchers and the participants and promote deep immersion in this field, thus aligning with the principles characteristic of the interpretivist paradigm.

The PHEI in this study belongs to a South African private hospital group. It is registered with the relevant authorities as a higher education institution that can offer undergraduate nursing programs. In South Africa, nursing education is offered by both government and privately funded education providers. Similarly, privately funded education providers are associated with privately funded healthcare organizations. The respondents in this study include various stakeholders in the setting; educators who teach nursing students in the classroom, simulation laboratories, and at various accredited hospitals; clinical educators who teach both staff and students in the clinical environment; ward managers who are responsible for allocating students to patients; and the training manager who coordinates nursing training across the PHEI. There are 16 hospitals across the Western Cape and Gauteng regions in this group where students are placed for clinical learning.

Study participants and sampling

Following ethics approval by the Health Research Ethics Committee at Stellenbosch University (S18/04/086), as well as from the PHEI, participants were invited to take part in the study. To obtain data that would be specifically relevant to understanding how learning manifested in this environment, purposive sampling was used.[36] The target group consisted of 20 educators across 2 campuses, 18 clinical educators, 18 ward managers, and 1 training manager. All educators and the training manager were invited to participate. The clinical educators and ward managers were purposively selected from six hospitals that have the largest student intakes. Eventually, 12 educators, six clinical educators, five ward managers, and the training manager agreed to participate.

Data collection tools and technique

For the observations, three visits to the classrooms where educators facilitated learning, and six visits to the hospitals where clinical educators facilitated clinical learning, were conducted. An observation schedule [Addendum A] was used to guide the process, and field notes were generated. The observations were followed by FGDs with educators (12), and interviews with the training manager (1), clinical educators (6), and ward managers (5). Each FGD and interview was guided by an interview schedule and audio-taped for transcription [Addendum B].

For this study, thematic analysis with an open coding strategy was used to analyze the data obtained from the observations, FGDs, and interviews.[37] This analysis was organized according to the three-tiered analytical ladder.[38] The research question, as well as Bolman and Deal’s organizational model, served to focus the three levels of analysis. Analysis commenced immediately after data collection. Thereafter the codes were reviewed to develop categories and themes while considering the different components of Bolman and Deal’s model and how it relates to the findings [Addendum C].

Ethical consideration

The study was approved by the Stellenbosch University ethics committee, with ethics clearance number (S18/04/086). Several ethical considerations were applicable to this study, including the right to self-determination, informed consent, and justice.[39] Participants were asked to complete a consent form after having received all necessary information relating to the study. They were also informed that they have a right to withdraw at any time should they wish to do so. No names of participants were reflected in the interview and FGDs, and participants’ responses were identified as respondents 1, 2, and 3.

Results

Four themes were derived from all the data, highlighting aspects of similarity and difference across the different sites within the PHEI. These themes, which are framed in relation to Bolman and Deal’s organizational model, point to the learning environment as a site of tension, a site of cultural layering, a site of role complexity, and a site of hierarchy.

Theme 1: The Learning Environment as a Site of Tension.

There are multiple factors within the learning environment that seem to be in tension with one another. These include the disconnect between policy intent and implementation, the tension between students as workforce and students as learners, the level of engagement between the academic and clinical entities, and the allocation of resources.

Despite the generally positive attitude toward the policies that were in place at the PHEI, many respondents reported a disconnect between what was declared in the policies and what was implemented at some of the hospitals:

They [policy] say that the student has six hours guided practice every month. But in practical, it doesn’t happen like that.” (CE Hospital C)2.

Theme 3: The Learning Environment as a Site of Role Complexity.

Respondents highlighted the complex array of roles and responsibilities that those responsible for learning must manage. These roles, which were further extended as a result of having students in the institution, included teaching, clinical work, and administration. This seemed to have an overwhelming effect on clinical educators who spoke of not being available to fill the educator role for the students on the one hand and being distracted from their clinical role when having to engage with students on the other:

“My role is split into so many parts that the student is one part. So, although we are nurse educators, we are not used in a nurse educator’s position.” (CE Hospital F)

Theme 4: The Learning Environment as a Site of Hierarchy.

Finally, the learning environment within the PHEI was characterized by a hierarchical structure consisting of a range of role-players, including the training manager, the ward managers, and the healthcare team. However, as indicated above, each ward has its own micro-culture, requiring students to navigate each hierarchy and individual culture—a process often associated with negative learning experiences.

Clinical educators and ward managers described negative attitudes of nursing staff toward students, such as responding aggressively to students, which can be distinguished from attitudes toward teaching and learning but highlights the hierarchical nature of this environment:

“There are some people who treat students a bit badly because they are jealous. Discussion

The PHEI in the context of this study belongs to the private healthcare organization. Thus, there are two main foci within this organization, namely caring for patients and educating students. Since the primary function of the private healthcare organization is to provide healthcare and satisfy the investors at the same time, other activities like education may not be given equal priority. The subsequent tensions identified in this study highlight the multi-layered influence this context has on teaching and learning, ranging from the lack of coordination across major departments to a sense that students are not supported consistently within the different wards. In their model Bolman and Deal acknowledge the value of power, conflict, and competition for stimulating constructive debate and compromise,[29] creating the potential to lead to change and progression within the organization. Similarly, these tensions in the learning environment, if acknowledged and engaged with appreciatively, can offer opportunities for improving flexibility and resilience in the organization. In terms of providing an environment for learning, these tensions can set the stage for improving collaboration among various entities in the organization, by collectively seeking ways to balance competing interests between healthcare delivery, and education as suggested by Turner and colleagues.[40]

The variation across the organization reveals a need for structures such as policies to be in place and applied to ensure that there is unified practice in terms of supporting teaching and learning. This unified practice is important to enhance consistency across the organization. However, it may not be possible to create consistency in all instances, given the multifaceted nature of these organizations, which are often in a state of flux. Bolman and Deal rather suggest that for organizations like these, a greater understanding of the differences across the organization and the reason for these differences is needed.[29] As opposed to trying to eliminate differences, an alternative would be to harness these variations to aim for a sense of synergy across the organization. Furthermore, variations across the organization require a more individual focus, which means that in a policy-led environment such as this organization, there needs to be greater flexibility within the policy framework. For educators, these contextual variations should be seen as an opportunity for students to have a more diverse learning experience.[41] What is essential is that educators work with students through this variation, so that curricula create opportunities for students to be “forced” to deal with complexity, thus developing critical skills such as problem solving.

Findings reveal that culture is multi-layered, with students also having a role in shaping this culture and, therefore, in co-constructing the learning environment. It appeared that students’ contribution to the ward and their willingness to learn, determined others’ attitudes toward them and their willingness to teach, thus highlighting the reciprocal relationship. The influence of culture within the learning environment can therefore not be separated into distinct parts but can be considered as spanning across different layers within the organization. This too calls for flexibility from the organization, with a focus on understanding these cultural differences and then balancing the various cultural conflicts that could arise.[42]

According to Bolman and Deal,[29] conflict is common between individuals with opposing or differing values and beliefs, and this was evident in this study. The main priorities of the educator and clinical educator are for the student to acquire the necessary competence to complete his/her program. The ward manager’s focus, however, is to ensure that patient care is provided while costs are contained. Staff in the ward prioritize patient care and, therefore, seem to have little time for students who need to learn. Since different priorities could ultimately affect the way students are supported,[43] it calls for a more shared understanding through open conversations between management and the different role-players involved in teaching. Furthermore, students need to be informed about the expected role conflicts and the skills to navigate these complexities.

This study also emphasized the influence that the hierarchical nature of the healthcare institution can have on student learning. Students seeking to integrate into the healthcare team must negotiate the different roles and power differences while building relationships.[44] Acknowledging that these hierarchies have an influence on learning experiences can inform educators on how to prepare students appropriately while encouraging the healthcare team to adopt a culture of collaboration and inclusiveness rather than disparity and division.[41]

Limitations and recommendation

This study focused on one PHEI in a South African context, and it is conceivable that the structural, political, human resources, and symbolic domains might manifest differently in other private healthcare constitutions. Nevertheless, we have endeavored to provide a detailed account of our methodology and an in-depth description of our findings such that others can make an informed decision as to the value of this work for their own contexts. If this study could be extended to other private and public higher education organizations, it may generate comparative data that will be useful for providing a greater understanding of how to improve learning environments in these changing contexts.

This study highlights the various tensions within the PHEI and private healthcare organizations. Managers in both the academic and clinical domains should work toward aligning organizational goals with strategies that ensure these goals are carried out, while acknowledging the contextual variation that exists across the sites. These strategies should include ongoing assessments of the learning environment to ensure that enabling environments that support the needs of students and the role-players involved in teaching are provided. These learning environments may be enhanced by improving coordination and implementing teaching and learning policies, while attempting flexibility within the policy framework. Management also needs to find ways to establish a cooperative effort in developing policies. This means, firstly, obtaining input from the various role-players involved in teaching and, secondly, establishing polices that are contextually relevant.

Conclusion

The successful implementation of curricula depends on understanding the environment within which it will be implemented. As nursing education undergoes various changes, this study provides insights into what a private healthcare environment has to offer nursing education from an organizational lens. What has been highlighted is that a culture of learning must be fostered by management through supporting educators, clinical educators, staff, and students in co-constructing the learning environment. This support may be facilitated by investing time and effort into acknowledging and preparing these individuals for their functions. These individuals should also be included in the decision-making related to the assessment and construction of learning environments in the organization. Furthermore, this study reveals that PHEI and private healthcare organizations are confronted with students who have diverse learning needs. However, this is not unique to the private healthcare context but also exists in other higher education contexts. To be responsive to the learning needs of students, educators must be encouraged to continuously re-evaluate and reflect on their practices to ensure that they are geared toward creating learning environments where students’ learning needs are identified and addressed.
