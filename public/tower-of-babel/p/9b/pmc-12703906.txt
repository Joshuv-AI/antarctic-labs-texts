Whole-genome analysis of multidrug-resistant Kluyvera intermedia strain L-40-1 isolated from a pig with respiratory disease
Authors: Hannuo Li, Nuo Xu, Zhijie Hong, Hui Su, Wanli Sha, Baishuang Yin, Wenlong Dong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractImportanceKluyvera intermedia (K. intermedia) is an emerging pathogen associated with respiratory disease in pigs. Its multidrug resistance poses a significant threat to livestock health.ObjectiveThis study characterized the resistance phenotype and genotype of K. intermedia L-40-1, isolated from a pig in Jilin Province, China, and investigated the genomic determinants of its antibiotic resistance.Methods16S rRNA sequencing was performed to identify the strain. The resistance phenotype was assessed by determining minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs), and resistance determinants were characterized by whole-genome sequencing. Comparative genomic analysis against the national center for biotechnology information (NCBI) database of K. intermedia strains was conducted to determine phylogenetic relationships and resistance gene profiles.ResultsL-40-1 exhibited high resistance to β-lactams, cephalosporins, macrolides, aminoglycosides, and sulfonamides. Comparative analysis identified 19 high-frequency resistance genes (e.g., arnT, rsmA, emrR, and acrA). A 159,618 bp plasmid (PYL-8) harbored multiple resistance genes (e.g., sul1, dfrA27, qnrB6, and tet(D)) and transposons (e.g., IS6, IS1, and Tn3), contributing to its multidrug resistance. Chromosomal and plasmid-borne resistance mechanisms were identified.Conclusions and RelevanceK. intermedia L-40-1 uses a combination of chromosomal and plasmid-mediated resistance genes, facilitated by transposons, to confer multidrug resistance. This highlights the need for surveillance of K. intermedia in livestock to mitigate the spread of antibiotic resistance.Keywords: Antibiotic resistance, whole genome sequencing, enterobacteriaceae, swine, mobile genetic elements

INTRODUCTION

Kluyvera is a genus from the Enterobacteriaceae family, first described in 1981 [1]. It is an opportunistic pathogen that is predominantly isolated from environmental sources, such as sewage in the soil [2]. This pathogen can cause urinary tract infections, septic shock, sepsis, and intra-abdominal abscesses in humans. Despite the clinical significance of Kluyvera, it remains an understudied pathogen [3]. This study conducted a comprehensive analysis of the K. intermedia species isolated from a pig with respiratory disease by comparative genomics.

The rising prevalence of antibiotic resistance in Kluyvera represents a significant concern. The types of resistance genes have various characteristics in different strains of Kluyvera: mcr-1, qnrA3, blaCTX-M-1, blaCTX-M-2, and blaKPC-2 in K. ascorbate [4]; gyrB, blaVIM-1, blaNDM-1, and KLUC-1 in K. cryocrescens [5]; fosA4, blaCTX-M, blaCTX-M-9, blaCTX-25, and blaKPC-2 in K. georgiana [6]; blaGES-5, qnrB, gyrA, sul2, blaOXA-48, and blaNDM-4 in K. intermedia [7]. Specifically, mcr-9 and mcr-1 have been identified in K. intermedia [8,9]. This study conducted a detailed analysis of its resistance mechanism, focusing on comparisons with similar strains.

METHODS

Isolation and identification of bacteria

The K. intermedia strain L-40-1 was isolated from nasal swabs collected from pig farms in Jilin Province, China. Briefly, the collected specimens were initially streaked onto brain heart infusion (BHI) agar plates (Sangon Biotech, China) and purified through successive streaking to obtain isolated colonies. Single colonies were then inoculated into 5 mL of BHI broth and cultured at 37°C for 12 h to facilitate bacterial enrichment. The isolates were preliminarily identified based on their colony morphology and confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing, with subsequent sequence analysis performed using the BLAST algorithm.

Assessment of antimicrobial susceptibility

The minimum inhibitory concentration of K. intermedia L-40-1 was determined using the agar diffusion method, as outlined in the 2023 33rd edition of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines. Twenty-one antibiotics, including azithromycin, penicillin G, amoxicillin, ampicillin, tilmicosin, ceftazidime, ceftiofur, aztreonam, enrofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, rifampicin, tigecycline, erythromycin, tetracycline, florfenicol, sulfamethoxazole, trimethoprim, chloramphenicol, lincomycin, polymyxin B, and amikacin, were selected based on the findings from various scientific studies. The concentration of the bacterial suspension was adjusted to 1 × 107 CFU/mL, and the antibiotic concentrations were prepared within a range of 0.03–512 μg/mL. After inoculation, the plates were incubated at 37°C for 16 h, and the results were recorded. Escherichia coli ATCC 25922 was included as a quality control strain to ensure the reliability of the assay.

Whole genome sequencing analysis and prediction of the resistance genes

The high-quality DNA of K. intermedia was extracted, and whole-genome sequencing was conducted by Qingdao Biomec Biotechnology Co., Ltd. as requested. For the genome assembly, the filtered reads were assembled using Canu v1.5 software. The resulting assembly was circularized by Circulator v1.5.5. The coding genes carried by K. intermedia L-40-1 were predicted using Prodigal v2.6.3. Antibiotic resistance genes on the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD; https://card.mcmaster.ca, version RGI 5.1.1/CARD 3.1.0), with the screening criteria set to perfect/strict hits or loose hits exhibiting ≥ 95% identity.

Genome data submission

The complete genome sequence was deposited in GenBank under accession numbers CP162029 (chromosome) and CP162030 (plasmid), with annotation performed using the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline.

RESULTS

Result of strain identification

A BLAST analysis was conducted on the nucleic acid sequences. The 16S rRNA sequencing confirmed the homology of K. intermedia L-40-1 with K. intermedia, achieving 99.99% accuracy.

Determination of antimicrobial susceptibility

L-40-1 showed high resistance to several antibiotics, including β lactams (aztreonam ≥ 512 μg/mL), penicillins (amoxicillin ≥ 512 μg/mL, penicillin G ≥ 512 μg/mL, and ampicillin ≥ 512 μg/mL), sulfonamides (sulfamethoxazole ≥ 512 μg/mL and sulfisoxazole ≥ 512 μg/mL), rifamycin (rifampicin ≥ 512 μg/mL), tetracyclines (tetracycline ≥ 512 μg/mL and tigecycline ≥ 128 μg/mL), synthetic (trimethoprim ≥ 512 μg/mL), aminoglycosides (amikacin ≥ 512 μg/mL), cephalosporins (cefazolin ≥ 512 μg/mL, ceftazidime ≥ 128 μg/mL, ceftriaxone ≥ 64 μg/mL, and ceftiofur ≥ 256 μg/mL), macrolides (erythromycin ≥ 512 μg/mL, tilmicosin ≥ 512 μg/mL, lincomycin ≥ 64 μg/mL, and azithromycin ≥ 64 μg/mL), carbapenem resistance (meropenem ≥ 16 μg/mL), and quinolone (enrofloxacin ≥ 256 μg/mL, fleroxacin ≥ 2 μg/mL).

Comparative analysis of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in chromosome

A phylogenetic tree was constructed based on the SNPs, which showed that MGYGHGUT02521 exhibited significant parenting with FOSA7093. L-40-1 showed higher levels of similarity with MAG 4159, DW18, and HAMBI1299. L-40-1 contains a chromosome of 5,020,770 bp and has been assigned the NCBI accession number CP162029. Among the 10 K. intermedia, there are 15 resistance genes with high frequency (ArnT, CRP, rsmA, emrR, marA, KpnF, KpnE, qacJ, acrA, vanG, ACT-8, UhpT, EF-Tu, PBP3, and AcrAB-TolC). The resistance genes AAC(6′)-Ib10, AAC(3)-IIg, and mcr-9.1 are rare antibiotic resistance genes. In addition, the resistance genes qnrB6, arr-3, tet(D), aadA16, AAC(6')-Ib10, and dfrA27 are unique to L-40-1, indicating its distinct evolution and antibiotic resistance potential. Furthermore, the efflux pump transcriptional regulatory protein genes (marA, marR, and marC), efflux pump connexin genes (acrA, acrC, acrD, acrT, acrE, and acrF), degradable genes (glgX), and glycogen metabolism genes (glgA, glgC, and glgB) are also present in different regions of the L-40-1 chromosome and play different roles (Fig. 1).

Analysis of the collinearity of the plasmid

The genome also contains a plasmid PYL-8 with 159,618 bp and has been assigned the NCBI accession number CP162030. The PYL-8 contains 19 antibiotic resistance genes that mediates the resistance to different drugs, including quinolones (qnrB6), sulfonamides (sul1 and sul2), trimethoprim (dfrA51 and dfrA27), aminoglycosides (aph(3′′)-Ib and ant(3′′)-Ia), glycopeptide (bleO), chloramphenicol (floR), rifamycin (dnaE), tetracyclines (tet(D), tetR(D), nrdB, and nrdA), and other functional genes (dcm, intI1, ydfZ, repA, and phoH). These elements are typically situated at the beginning and end of the PYL-8. The aforementioned resistance genes act in concert to mediate resistance in K. intermedia. Moreover, the plasmid also contains four major types of transposons, including replicating transposon (IS91), recombinant transposon (IS110), composite transposon (IS1 and IS6), and TnA transposon (Tn2 and Tn3) (Fig. 2A). Furthermore, the following were selected for comparative genomics analysis: the human-origin Enterobacter hormaechei with accession number CP044336.1 from Taiwan, China; the feline-origin Enterobacter roggenkampii with accession number CP095172.1 from Jilin, China; and PYL-8. The antibiotic resistance genes (repA, dnaE, phoH, ydfZ, tet(D), tetR(D), bleO, floR, aph(3′′) - Ib, and sul2) and transposons (IS110, IS6, IS1, and Tn3) are present in all three different plasmids. The collinearity relationship of the genomes of the three strains shows a high degree of similarity (Fig. 2B).

DISCUSSION

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a significant threat that humanity must confront in the 21st century. This study focused on L-40-1 and compared the resistance and resistance mechanisms of 10 different K. intermedia samples. The resistance genes, including sulfonamide resistance genes (sul1), β-lactam resistance genes (ACT-8), quinolone resistance genes (gyrB), and cephalosporin resistance genes (PBP3), as well as the efflux pump genes (CRP, kpnH, kpnF, kpnE, qacJ, marA, adeF, and emrR) and transporter genes (msbA, acrA, and uhpT), are identified more frequently in these 10 different K. intermedia. Specifically, the six drug resistance genes qnrB6, dfrA27, arr-3, tet(D), aadA16, and AAC(6')-Ib10 were unique to L-40-1. Moreover, aadA16 and AAC(6')-Ib10 have been observed in Klebsiella pneumoniae and Acinetobacter hydrophila, and have been proven to mediate the resistance to aminoglycosides, which can enhance the level of drug resistance [10].

PYL-8 exhibited a high degree of similarity with a human-derived E. hormaechei (AKB48) (accession number: CP044336.1) from Taiwan, China, and a cat-derived E. roggenkampii (pK666_111k) (accession number: CP095172.1) from Jilin, China. Comparative analysis revealed the presence of analogous drug-resistant genes and transposons in three organisms. The phoH gene has been identified in several bacterial species, including Enterococcus faecalis, K. pneumoniae, Staphylococcus aureus, and E. coli [11]. Tet(D) is a tetracycline efflux pump found exclusively in Gram-negative bacteria. The misuse of tetracyclines in the poultry industry has led to an increase in the prevalence of tetR(D) and tet(D), which are acquired tetracycline-resistance genes [12].

The dnaE gene is classified as a structural gene for a subunit of the DNA polymerase III holoenzyme, and its presence has been confirmed in E. coli [13]. Moreover, the IS6 family has been shown to facilitate the development of resistance to multiple antibiotics and has been identified in the plasmid of Mycobacterium flexneri, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Salmonella typhimurium, and Klebsiella oxytocitocium [14]. The IS110 is renowned for its DEDD transposases, and it is the sole family distinguished not by transposase differentiation but by the nature of its IS terminus [15]. The IS110 family comprises a variety of adjacent functional genes, among which the transposon IS110 has been identified in E. coli. In addition, IS110 and IS6, located close to the ydfZ resistance gene (AKB48) in CP044336.1, can disseminate antibiotic resistance and facilitate bacterial adaptation to the environment. Enterobacteriaceae can facilitate the transfer of AMR genes, which are often found in mobile genetic elements. These processes include natural ability, transformation, and plasmid transcoupling, and can occur in animals and the environment.
