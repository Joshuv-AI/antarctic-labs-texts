Background

Eikenella corrodens is the only microaerobic Gram-negative bacillus species in the genus Eikenella [1]. Moreover, it constitutes a part of the microbiota found on human oral microbiota, mainly residing in the oral cavity; infections caused by this organism are particularly rare [2]. E. corrodens, a conditional pathogen, is associated with co-infections with other bacteria because of its weak virulence. It triggers invasive bacterial infections, including pyogenic liver abscesses, infective endocarditis, lung infections, and intracranial infections under certain circumstances, such as trauma, immunosuppression, and other conditions [3–7]. However, the growth of E. corrodens requires specific environmental conditions, which render its identification using conventional microbiological cultures inefficient, resulting in disease aggravation and delayed diagnosis [8]. Streptococcus anginosus is a Gram-positive, non-motile bacterium that primarily colonizes the oral cavity, gastrointestinal tract, nasopharynx, and vaginal tract. Additionally, it can induce invasive pyogenic infections [9]. E. corrodens and S. anginosus are uncommon fastidious bacteria that are a part of the normal microbiota found in human mucosal tissues [10]. Previous studies have reported that coinfections of Streptococcus and E. corrodens caused abscesses in different body parts, including the liver, brain, and thigh [11]. Infections with both E. corrodens and S. anginosus group have been reported in injection drug users who hold the needles between their teeth while preparing the solution for injection [12]. In the current study, we report a case of a left-hand soft tissue infection caused by E. corrodens and S. anginosus, following the use of a toothpick.

Case presentation

A 66-year-old Chinese man was admitted to the Department of Hand and Foot Surgery at Shaoxing Hospital of Traditional Chinese Medicine affiliated with Zhejiang Chinese Medical University on June 16, 2024, complaining of redness, swelling, and pain in the left hand accompanied with fluid discharge from the wound for 5 days. After the patient had eaten dinner and used a toothpick to pick his teeth, he forgot the toothpick containing the bacteria in his right hand while patting a mosquito, that caused an infection when the toothpick poked into the ring finger of his left hand.

The patient reported a history of penicillin allergy. On admission, the patient had a body temperature of 36.8 ℃, a blood pressure of 129/83 mmHg, a pulse of 94 beats per minute, and a respiratory rate of 19 breaths per minute. The left ring finger was significantly swollen and showed an elevated skin temperature, a positive needle puncture response, and limited movement. The left hand showed a significantly elevated skin temperature, swelling, limited finger movement, and a positive needle puncture response (Fig. 1A). Initial laboratory blood sample results showed leukocyte count (9.7*109/L) and neutrophil count (6.7*109/L). Moreover, the results showed an increase in the monocyte count (0.91*109/L), mean volume of red blood cells (101.5 fl.), hypersensitive C-reactive protein (CRP) levels (40.19 mg/L) and serum amyloid A (SAA) levels (74.74 mg/L), and a decrease in the ratio of lymphocytes (16.8%), indicating an infection. Additionally, the red blood cell count (4.59*1012/L), hemoglobin levels (155.0 g/L), and platelet count (236*109/L) were normal (Table 1).

Upon admission, the patient underwent an examination using a 1.5 T magnetic resonance imaging scanner, revealing bone edema near the knuckles of the left ring finger, swelling and fluid accumulation around the flexor tendon, and multiple swellings in the soft tissue of the left hand. The patient was subsequently diagnosed with a left-hand soft tissue infection (Fig. 1B). On June 16, we empirically administered intravenous clindamycin (0.6 g twice a day) as a systemic anti-infection therapy for 4 days; however, the symptoms did not improve. The toothpick implicated in the left ring finger puncture injury had been previously used, leading us to hypothesize that S. anginosus - a bacterial species commonly colonizing oral niches including gingival crevices, dental biofilm, and root canal systems - was the probable causative pathogen. Because clindamycin was ineffective, we changed the antibiotic to levofloxacin. Unfortunately, this patient’s symptoms did not improve. On June 27, we performed an emergency surgery on the patient’s infected left ring finger under anesthesia because the infection decreased hand function and caused severe tissue destruction. The surrounding inflammatory edema and infected tissue were completely removed. The purpose of the surgery was to incise, drain, and debride the septic infection in the ring finger. During the surgery, the encapsulated pus cavity was opened.

Bacterial cultures were ordered three times for the drained pus, and the results were positive. The infecting bacteria included E. corrodens and S. anginosus. E. corrodens was identified using the matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (Fig. 2A). E. corrodens specimens were first introduced to an atmosphere containing 5% CO2, and then incubated on 5% sheep blood agar for 48 h at 35℃. After the incubation, needle-sized colonies were observed on the blood agar (Fig. 2B). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing using the E-test VR gradient diffusion assay showed that the E. corrodens isolate was susceptible to penicillin, amoxicillin clavulanate potassium, ceftriaxone, moxifloxacin, and imipenem but resistant to clindamycin. The results of the microbial biochemical reaction indicated the presence of S. anginosus (Fig. 2C). S. anginosus specimens were first introduced to an atmosphere containing 5% CO2, and then incubated on 5% blood agar for 48 h at 35℃. Within 48 h, colonies were observed on the blood agar (Fig. 2D). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing showed that the S. anginosus isolate was resistant to clindamycin and erythromycin, but susceptible to ceftriaxone, linezolid, penicillin, tetracycline, vancomycin, and levofloxacin, as determined using the Kirby–Bauer method. Gram staining of the collected pus revealed the presence of Gram-negative bacilli and Gram-positive cocci. Microscopy revealed that the colonies of E. corrodens contained long and slender Gram-negative bacilli without spores or capsules, and the colonies of S. anginosus were arranged in pairs or short chains of Gram-positive cocci with small colonies (Fig. 3). Therefore, On July 3, we changed the antibiotic from levofloxacin to ceftriaxone (2 g once a day) based on the sensitivity testing results and the patient’s penicillin allergy history. Moreover, the pathological examination of the wound granulation tissue of the left ring finger revealed chronic suppurative inflammation (Fig. 4).

On July 8, infection control in the left ring finger was unsatisfactory after the incision and drainage of the pus. Due to surgical indications, the patient was scheduled to undergo an antibiotic-loaded bone cement packing procedure for the debridement of the left ring finger. Hand and foot surgeons used 4000 mg of vancomycin mixed with 40 g of bone cement for the intraoperative packing. The postoperative hand X-ray images are shown in Fig. 5. Treatment with ceftriaxone was continued, and debridement was performed daily. Microbiological examination of the patient’s wound showed no bacterial or fungal growth on July 12. The patient had a good prognosis and was discharged on July 17.

Discussion and conclusions

Hand soft tissue infections cause significant disability and loss of productivity; many infections are caused by bacterial microorganisms [13]. Coinfections of S. anginosus and E. corrodens are rare. In addition, cases of soft tissue infections of the ring finger caused by S. anginosus or E. corrodens from a tooth pick injury has not been reported. Effective and accurate detection of pathogens depends on pus specimen cultures and microscopic examinations [14].

In the present case, the pathological analysis revealed that the granulation tissue of the patient’s wound was purulent using microscopic examinations. Thorough drainage and targeted antibiotics are key to achieving a cure. Mortality from bacterial sepsis has significantly decreased due to the crucial role of appropriate and timely antibiotic and supportive therapy. In our case, we observed Gram-negative bacilli and Gram-positive cocci under the microscope. E. corrodens belongs to the Neisseriaceae family and can be detected using microscopic examinations [15]. Members of the family Neisseriaceae have high nutritional requirements and grow slowly. E. corrodens is usually sensitive to amoxicillin/clavulanic acid, penicillin, imipenem, third- or fourth-generation cephalosporins, and fluoroquinolones, but it is typically resistant to metronidazole and clindamycin [16]. Ceftriaxone was administered based on the current drug sensitivity test results because the patient had a history of penicillin allergy. In addition, the rational use of antibiotics is essential for treating coinfections, and surgical treatment, including drainage, irrigation, and debridement of the infected suppurative site, can not only improve the curative effect but can also shorten the course of the disease, and it play a crucial role in improving patients’ conditions and prognosis. After a month of treatment, the patient was discharged from the hospital with stabilized symptoms. E. corrodens can only be cultured on chocolate or blood agar for relatively long periods [17]. Hence, the organism may be overlooked in routine cultures, particularly when mixed with other microorganisms in large colonies, such as S. anginosus. Based on these results, we appeal to all clinical colleagues, to pay more attention to the examination of smears. The pus revealed mixed growth which gave a clue to the etiology.

In summary, we report a rare case of co-infection of S. anginosus and E. corrodens in a patient with a left-hand soft tissue infection. Early microbial identification, proper drainage, and anti-infective therapy with appropriate antibiotics based on susceptibilities, is essential for a successful outcome. Ceftriaxone was the choice for this patient, but for those not allergic to penicillin, the treatment of choice would have been penicillin.
