Macrophage-derived CTSZ promotes prostate cancer progression via αVβ3 integrin-mediated activation of the AKT/FOXO1/JUNB signaling axis
Authors: Youxin Liu, Saipeng Chen, Jie Xu, Hang Yuan, Hanzi Yang, Bingqian Deng, Jie Feng, Heting Liu, Langlang Xie, Gang Huang, Wenhao Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe tumor microenvironment (TME), especially tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), plays a critical role in prostate cancer (PCa) progression. Cathepsin Z (CTSZ) is a lysosomal protease implicated in various malignancies, but its specific function and mechanism within the PCa-associated TAMs remain largely unexplored.MethodsWe analyzed single-cell RNA sequencing data (GSE141445, GSE137829) to identify CTSZ expression patterns. Its role in macrophage polarization was validated by immunofluorescence co-localization in human PCa tissues and in vitro experiments (qPCR, Western blot, flow cytometry) using THP-1-derived macrophages overexpressing CTSZ. The effects of CTSZ-polarized macrophages on PCa cell (PC3) malignant behaviors (proliferation, migration, and invasion) were evaluated in vitro using EdU, CCK-8, colony formation, scratch, and Transwell assays, and their functions were assessed in vivo through orthotopic and lung metastasis models. RNA sequencing of treated tumor cells was conducted to identify downstream pathways. Mechanistic insights were obtained using CTSZ-neutralizing antibodies, recombinant CTSZ protein, and the αVβ3 integrin inhibitor Cilengitide (Cyclo), followed by western blot, co-immunoprecipitation, and dual-luciferase reporter assays.ResultsCTSZ was predominantly highly expressed in macrophages of PCa tissues. Overexpression of CTSZ in macrophages promoted M2 polarization, as evidenced by increased CD163 and decreased CD86/iNOS expression. Conditioned medium from OE-CTSZ macrophages significantly enhanced PCa cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, and promoted tumor growth and metastasis in mouse models. RNA sequencing revealed significant enrichment in the TNF signaling pathway and marked downregulation of JUNB. Mechanistically, macrophage-derived CTSZ bound to the αVβ3 integrin receptor on tumor cells, activating the AKT/FOXO1 signaling axis, leading to phosphorylation of FOXO1 and subsequent transcriptional repression of JUNB. These pro-tumorigenic effects were effectively reversed by either a CTSZ-neutralizing antibody or the αVβ3 inhibitor.ConclusionOur study identifies a novel pathway in which macrophage-derived CTSZ promotes PCa progression by driving M2 macrophage polarization and directly activating the αVβ3/AKT/FOXO1 axis in tumor cells, ultimately leading to JUNB downregulation. Targeting the CTSZ/αVβ3 axis may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for PCa treatment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12935-026-04392-2.

Introduction

Prostate cancer (PCa) remains a leading cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality among men worldwide [1], [2]. While androgen deprivation therapy (ADT), which targets the androgen receptor pathway, is a primary treatment option, its efficacy is often transient and inevitably leads to the development of castration-resistant prostate cancer (CRPC) [3]. Immunotherapies have shown limited success in PCa, largely attributable to its immunologically “cold” tumor microenvironment (TME), characterized by T-cell exhaustion and a predominance of immunosuppressive cells [4], [5].

Among these immunosuppressive components, tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), particularly those polarized toward an M2 phenotype, are recognized as pivotal regulators of tumor progression, fostering immunosuppression, angiogenesis, and metastasis [6]–[10].

Cathepsin Z (CTSZ), a cysteine carboxypeptidase containing an Arg-Gly-Asp (RGD) motif, has emerged as a protein of interest in cancer biology [11]. The RGD motif enables CTSZ to interact with integrin receptors, such as αVβ3, suggesting roles beyond mere proteolysis, potentially in cell signaling [12], [13]. CTSZ is highly expressed in macrophages [14] and is upregulated in several malignancies, including gastric and PCa, where its expression correlates with increased invasiveness and poor prognosis [15], [16]. A recent study identified a CTSZ-TRA2A-IL-32 axis in PCa that promotes macrophage chemotaxis and M2 polarization [17]. However, the specific role and mechanism of macrophage-derived CTSZ within the PCa TME are not fully elucidated.

Given that M2-TAMs are established promoters of tumor growth [9] and that integrin αVβ3 is highly expressed in PCa and implicated in metastasis [18], we hypothesized that macrophage-secreted CTSZ might directly influence PCa progression via αVβ3-mediated signaling. In this study, we identified CTSZ as a key mediator derived from M2-polarized TAMs. We demonstrate that CTSZ not only reinforces M2 macrophage polarization but also binds to αVβ3 integrin on PCa cells in a paracrine manner. This interaction activates the AKT/FOXO1 signaling axis, leading to the transcriptional downregulation of the tumor suppressor JUNB [19], [20], thereby enhancing tumor malignancy. Our findings reveal a novel crosstalk pathway between TAMs and PCa cells and suggest that targeting the CTSZ/αVβ3 interaction could be a promising therapeutic strategy.

Materials and methods

Bioinformatics analysis

Publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) datasets (GSE141445, GSE137829) from human PCa tissues were selected for their high quality and comprehensive cellular profiling, and an integrated analysis was performed using the Seurat package (v4.2.1) in R to investigate the cell type-specific expression of CTSZ. Standard preprocessing included quality control, normalization using the LogNormalize method (which normalizes counts per cell, scales by a factor of 10,000, and log-transforms the result), and scaling. Batch effects between the two datasets were corrected using canonical correlation analysis (CCA) and mutual nearest neighbors (MNN) integration. Dimensionality reduction via principal component analysis (PCA) on highly variable genes was followed by graph-based clustering. Major cell types were annotated using canonical markers: macrophages (CD68, CD163, AIF1), T cells (CD3D, CD3E), epithelial/tumor cells (KRT8, KRT18), and fibroblasts (COL1A1, DCN). To determine CTSZ high expression, its normalized expression was visualized on UMAP plots, and the average expression was calculated per cell cluster. The statistical significance of expression differences between the macrophage cluster and other major clusters was assessed using an unpaired Student’s t-test, with a p-value < 0.01 considered significant. For the SU2C cohort, differential gene expression analysis and pathway enrichment were performed using limma and clusterProfiler. Metastatic PCa transcriptomic data (GSE3933, GSE68882) were analyzed to compare JUNB expression between primary and metastatic tumors, with statistical significance assessed by Student’s t-test. All analyses were conducted in R (v4.2.1), and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Survival analyses for CTSZ and JUNB, as well as immune infiltration analyses, were performed using public online databases. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis for CTSZ was conducted using the GEPIA web portal (http://gepia.cancer-pku.cn/). The survival and immune infiltration correlation analyses for JUNB were performed using the “camcAPP” tool (Explore Prostate Cancer Datasets, https://bioinformatics.cruk.cam.ac.uk/apps/camcAPP/). The correlation analyses between gene expression (CTSZ, JUNB) and immune cell infiltration levels (estimated by CIBERSORT) and tumor purity were performed using the TIMER2.0 web server (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer/).

Cell lines and culture

The cell lines employed in this study included human (PC3, LNCaP), human macrophage (THP-1) cell lines. These cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The cells were cultivated using the following media: RPMI 1640 medium (Servicebio, Wuhan, China), 10% fetal bovine serum (Servicebio), and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Servicebio) for PC3 cells and THP-1 cells; and DMEM medium (Servicebio), 10% fetal bovine serum (Servicebio), and 1% antibiotic-antimycotic solution (Servicebio) for LNCaP cells. The cultures were maintained at 37 °C, 5% CO₂, and 95% humidity in a culture incubator. THP-1 cells were treated with 100 ng/mL PMA to induce their adhesion. Subsequently, the adherent macrophages were polarized into an M2 phenotype with 20 ng/mL IL-4 and 20 ng/mL IL-13, while polarization into M1 macrophages was achieved with 100 ng/mL LPS and 20 ng/mL IFN-γ. PC3 and LNCaP cells were treated with recombinant human CTSZ protein (HY-P7758A, MCE, Shanghai, China). The protein was reconstituted according to the manufacturer’s instructions and diluted in complete culture medium to the desired concentrations. After overnight adhesion, the cell culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing the specified concentrations of CTSZ or vehicle control. Following treatment for the indicated time periods, cells were used for subsequent functional and molecular analyses.

Constructing stable cell lines

The lentiviral vector overexpressing CTSZ was synthesised by Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China). THP-1 cells were then subjected to transfection with pLenti-CMV-Puro-GFP-CTSZ (a vector modified by inserting CTSZ), with an empty plasmid serving as a negative control. PC3 cells were then subjected to transfection with pLenti-CMV-Puro-Luciferase.Following a 48-hour transfection period, puromycin (2 µg/mL) was introduced to screen for a monoclonal cell line. Collect the cell supernatant from the stable cells, centrifuge it and use it as the conditioned medium (CM). For the next experiment, add or omit cyclo-(RGDfK) (HY-P0023, MCE) or the CtsZ-neutralising antibody (PA5-47048, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, USA) to the conditioned medium (CM) of THP-1-OE-CTSZ cells.

RNA extraction and RT-PCR

The extraction of RNA was conducted in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol (317908, Ambion, Austin, USA), employing the Trizol reagent. The purified RNA was then employed in conjunction with the reverse transcription kit manufactured by Yeasen Biotech (Shanghai, China), with the objective of synthesising cDNA by means of reverse transcription PCR (RT-PCR). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using Hieff® qPCR SYBR Green (Yeasen Biotech). The reverse transcription primers were synthesised by Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), with the respective sequences listed in Online Supplemental Table 1. Relative gene expression levels were normalized to the internal reference gene GAPDH (Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase).

Flow cytometry

Macrophage cell lines were surface-stained with PE anti-CD163 (333605, Biolegend, San Diego, USA), PE/Cy7 anti-CD86 (381007, Biolegend), FITC anti-CD68 (333805, Biolegend), and APC anti-human CD68 (333809, Biolegend). The analysis was performed using a BD FACSVerse™ flow cytometer. The establishment of positive cell gates for each dye was achieved through the use of isotype controls and monochromatic cells.

Western blotting

Total protein extraction was achieved by utilising a lysis buffer (Servicebio), after which the resulting clear upper layer was collected. Protein quantification was performed using a BCA protein quantification kit (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, SDS-PAGE (polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis) and Western blotting analysis were performed according to the established protocol. The membrane was transferred to a PVDF membrane (Bio-Rad), then blocked for 5 min in rapid protein-free blocking solution (Servicebio), followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: CTSZ (1:500 dilution, 16578-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), GAPDH (1:5,000 dilution, 200306-7E4, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE, Chengdu, China), α-Tubulin (1:1,000 dilution, R23452, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), iNOS (1:1,000 dilution, 340668, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), CD163 (1:500 dilution, M50704, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), JUNB (1:1,000 dilution, A23225, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), AKT (1:1,000 dilution, R381555, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), P-AKT (1:1,000 dilution, R23412, ZEN-BIOSCIENCE), FOXO1 (1:1,000 dilution, CY7234, Abways, Suzhou, China), P-FOXO1 (1:500 dilution, CY6217, Abways), ITGAV (1:1000 dilution, A2091, ABclonal).

ELISA

For quantification of secreted protein levels, conditioned medium was collected from THP-1-derived macrophages 24 h after passage. The medium was centrifuged to remove cell debris, and the supernatant was assayed using a specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (ELK Biotechnology, Wuhan, China), strictly following the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration of the target protein in each sample was determined by measuring the absorbance and interpolating from a standard curve.

RNA sequencing

PC3 cells were treated for 24 h using conditioned medium from THP-1-NC or THP-1-OE-CTSZ macrophages. The RNA was then extracted from the PC3 cells and dispatched to NovelBio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. for transcriptomic sequencing. Subsequent to the return of the raw data, the data analysis platform (CytoNavigator) should be utilised for the purpose of performing differential gene analysis on the sequencing data. Furthermore, the platform should be employed to generate a volcano plot of differentially expressed genes, conduct KEGG analysis, and pathway gene heatmap analysis.

Wound-healing assay

Tumour cells were seeded in 6-well plates. When the cell density reached approximately 80%, a straight line was drawn on the plate using a sterile pipette tip. The cells were then treated with culture medium with or without recombinant CTSZ (HY-P7758A, MCE) or the CM collected in the previous step. At the designated starting time point, cell scratches were documented through photographic evidence captured using an inverted microscope. Twenty-four hours later, further photographs were taken, and the distances traversed by the cells during migration were measured using the ImageJ software. The percentage of scratch closure was then calculated and analysed.

EdU staining assay

Cell proliferation was detected using the E-Click EdU Cell Proliferation Imaging Assay Kit (Elabscience, Item No. E-CK-A377, Wuhan, China). The tumour cells were inoculated into 24-well plates and treated under the above conditions. Then, when the cell density reached approximately 70%, 10 µM of the EdU reagent was added. After two hours, the cells were fixed, permeabilized, stained, photographed, and counted using a fluorescence microscope, in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

CCK-8 assay

PC3 cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 3,000 cells per well in 100 µL of complete medium and cultured for 24 h. After 24 h, cells were treated according to the experimental design, with each group containing three replicate wells. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ atmosphere for the indicated time periods. Cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, BGBIOTECH, Chongqing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 10 µL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Colony formation assay

PC3 cells were seeded into 6-well plates at a density of 1,000 cells per well and cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator. Cells were treated according to the experimental protocol during the culture period. The culture medium was replaced with fresh complete medium or conditioned medium every 3–4 days to maintain optimal nutrient supply. After 1–2 weeks of culture, when visible colonies had formed, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, stained with a 0.1% crystal violet solution for 30 min, and gently washed with PBS. The plates were air-dried, and colonies were photographed and counted for quantitative analysis. Colonies containing more than 50 cells were considered positive clones.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

To investigate the protein-protein interaction between CTSZ and αVβ3 integrin, co-immunoprecipitation was performed. Briefly, PC3 cells were treated with recombinant CTSZ protein (20 ng/mL) for 60 min. Cells were then lysed in NP-40 lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors. For each immunoprecipitation, 500 µg of total protein lysate was incubated overnight at 4 °C with 2 µg of anti-αVβ3 antibody (or normal mouse IgG as a negative control) with gentle rotation. Protein A/G magnetic beads were added and incubated for an additional 2 h at 4 °C. The beads were then washed five times with ice-cold lysis buffer. The immunoprecipitated complexes were eluted by boiling in 1× SDS loading buffer, separated by SDS-PAGE, and subsequently analyzed by Western blotting using antibodies against αVβ3 and CTSZ. Input lysates (5% of total) were run in parallel to confirm protein expression.

Mice tumorigenicity assay

Male BALB/c nude mice (4 weeks old) were purchased from Hunan SJA Laboratory Animal Co., Ltd. (Changsha, China) and housed under standard conditions at the Experimental Animal Center of the First Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University. After one week of acclimatization, mice were randomly allocated into experimental groups using a random number table. The sample size for each group (n = 6) was determined based on a power analysis (α = 0.05, β = 0.8) to detect a predicted effect size of a 40% difference in tumor volume with 80% power, using preliminary data and established standards in similar xenograft studies. Investigators were blinded to the group allocation during the injection procedure, treatment administration, and all subsequent outcome assessments (tumor measurement, nodule counting, and histological analysis).

All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Army Medical University (Approval No. AMUWEC20232943) and conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines. THP-1-derived macrophages (1 × 10⁶ cells) transfected with NC or OE-CTSZ lentivirus were mixed with PC3 PCa cells (1 × 10⁶ cells) in a 1:1 ratio; the mixed cell suspension was supplemented with an equal volume of pre-cooled Matrigel, and 20 µL of the mixture was either injected via the tail vein (for the lung metastasis model) or surgically implanted into the dorsolateral prostate lobe of mice under a stereomicroscope (for the primary tumor model). Three days after injection, macrophage enrichment was monitored using an IVIS Spectrum CT in vivo imaging system. Mice were treated daily via tail vein injection with either cyclo (-RGDfK) (10 mg/kg) or an equal volume of PBS as control, starting from the day of cell injection until the end of the experiment. At the experimental endpoint, mice were euthanized, and orthotopic prostate tumors were excised, measured for size and weight, and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent H&E staining and histological analysis. Lung tissues were harvested and fixed in Bouin’s solution for 10 min, followed by PBS washing. Metastatic nodules larger than 1 mm were counted macroscopically, and lung tissues were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for H&E staining and immunohistochemical analysis.

Transient plasmid transfection assay

For functional rescue experiments, PC3 cells were transiently transfected to overexpress JUNB. Briefly, cells were seeded in appropriate culture plates and transfected at 60–70% confluence using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent according to the manufacturer‘s protocol, with either the pcDNA3.1-JUNB overexpression plasmid or the empty pcDNA3.1 vector as a control. Transfected cells were cultured for 24 to 48 h to allow for sufficient protein expression before being subjected to subsequent functional assays (EdU, wound healing, and Transwell invasion assays) using conditioned medium from macrophages. Overexpression of JUNB was confirmed by Western blot analysis prior to the functional experiments.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

PC3 cells were seeded into 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 10⁴ cells per well and cultured for 24 h. Cells were co-transfected with FOXO1 expression plasmid or empty vector control, along with wild-type or mutant JUNB promoter-luciferase reporter constructs using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After 48 h of transfection, cells were lysed, and luciferase activity was measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega). Firefly luciferase activity was normalized to Renilla luciferase activity to account for transfection efficiency. Each experiment was performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times independently. Data are presented as the mean ± SD of relative luciferase activity compared to the control group.

Immunofluorescence staining

PC3 cells were seeded on glass coverslips in 24-well plates and cultured to 70–80% confluence. Cells were treated with conditioned medium (CM) from NC or OE-CTSZ macrophages for 24 h. After treatment, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were then incubated with a primary antibody against CD163 (1:200) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1:1000) for 5 min. Coverslips were mounted with antifade mounting medium and visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss LSM 880). Images were captured and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry and immunofluorescence

Prostate cancer tissue microarrays from human patients were obtained from Changsha Yaxiang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Mouse orthotopic and lung tumor tissues were sectioned into 5 μm-thick slices. Both tissue microarrays and mouse tumor tissue sections were deparaffinized at 70 °C for 2 h. Antigen retrieval was performed using EDTA buffer at high temperature for 3 min, followed by PBS washing and blocking with serum for 1 h. Primary antibodies were applied to the samples and incubated overnight at 4 °C in the dark. After PBS washing, secondary antibodies were added and incubated at room temperature for 30 min. For immunofluorescence staining, the procedure was similar to immunohistochemistry, with the addition of DAPI for nuclear counterstaining. Finally, sections were mounted with antifade mounting medium containing an anti-fluorescence quenching agent. All sections were scanned using a whole-slide scanner. All tumor samples were processed in the same manner.

Molecular docking analysis

To predict the potential interaction between Cathepsin Z (CTSZ) and the αVβ3 integrin, molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina. The 3D structure of the αV subunit (ITGAV) was obtained from the Protein Data Bank, while a homology model for CTSZ was generated via SWISS-MODEL. After preparing both structures, docking simulations were conducted with the grid box centered on the known RGD-binding pocket of ITGAV. The resulting complexes were analyzed, and the conformation with the most favorable binding free energy was selected for further visualization and interaction analysis using PyMOL.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise specified, all data are derived from at least three independent experiments. The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. For comparisons between two groups meeting normality assumptions, unpaired Student’s t-tests were applied. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post-hoc test (for normally distributed data) or Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc test (for non-normally distributed data) was used, as appropriate. For time-series data, two-way ANOVA with appropriate post-hoc tests was employed. Corrections for multiple comparisons were applied where indicated. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software, and quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation.

Results

CTSZ is highly expressed in PCa-associated macrophages and drives M2 polarization

To investigate the relationship between macrophage-derived CTSZ and PCa, we first analyzed integrated single-cell RNA sequencing data from human PCa cohorts. This analysis demonstrated that CTSZ mRNA is specifically and predominantly expressed by macrophages within the prostate tumor microenvironment (Fig. 1A). This finding was validated by immunofluorescence staining of clinical PCa specimens, which showed distinct co-localization of CTSZ with the macrophage marker CD11b (Fig. 1B). Elevated CTSZ expression was significantly associated with poor clinical prognosis (shorter disease-free survival) and increased M2-like macrophage infiltration in PCa patients (Figure S1A–C). To explore the functional role of CTSZ in macrophages, we established a CTSZ-overexpressing (OE-CTSZ) THP-1-derived macrophage model. Successful overexpression was confirmed by qPCR and Western blot (Fig. 1C, D). Notably, OE-CTSZ macrophages exhibited a significant upregulation of the M2 marker CD163 and downregulation of the M1 marker iNOS/CD86 at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1C–F). Immunofluorescence staining further confirmed increased CD163 expression in OE-CTSZ macrophages (Fig. 1E), and flow cytometry validated an expanded CD163⁺ M2-like population upon CTSZ overexpression (Fig. 1F). These results indicate that CTSZ is not only a marker of PCa-associated macrophages but also an active driver of their M2 polarization.

Macrophage-derived CTSZ promotes PCa malignancy in vitro and in vivo

Given the pro-tumorigenic nature of M2 macrophages, we next explored the functional impact of CTSZ-polarized macrophages on PCa cells. Analysis of the SU2C dataset revealed that genes differentially expressed in correlation with CTSZ were significantly enriched in cytokine-related signaling pathways (Fig. 2A), suggesting a role for CTSZ in intercellular signaling. We therefore collected conditioned medium (CM) from control (NC) and OE-CTSZ macrophages and used it to treat PC3 cells. Transwell and wound healing assays showed that CM from OE-CTSZ macrophages significantly enhanced the migration and invasion of PC3 cells (Fig. 2B, C). Similarly, EdU incorporation, CCK-8, and colony formation assays demonstrated increased proliferative ability of PC3 cells in the OE-CTSZ CM group (Fig. 2D–F).

To validate these findings in vivo, we established an orthotopic xenograft model by co-injecting PC3 cells with either NC or OE-CTSZ macrophages into the prostates of nude mice. The OE-CTSZ group displayed markedly higher tumor bioluminescence signals and larger tumor volumes (Fig. 2G). Immunohistochemical analysis of tumor tissues revealed enhanced expression of Ki67 and EMT markers (SNAIL, CDH2) in the OE-CTSZ group (Fig. 2H). These data collectively confirm that macrophage-derived CTSZ-mediated intercellular signaling significantly promotes PCa progression.

The pro-tumorigenic effects of CTSZ depend on its secretion

We next sought to determine the mechanism by which macrophage-derived CTSZ influences cancer cells via intercellular signaling. Given that cytokines primarily function through secretion, we first used ELISA to measure secreted protein levels. CM from OE-CTSZ macrophages contained substantially higher levels of CTSZ protein, whereas levels of other pro-tumorigenic cytokines such as TGF-β, EGF, and IL-10 showed no significant difference (Fig. 3A). qPCR analysis of the cell lines yielded consistent results (Fig. 3B). To directly test the functionality of the secreted CTSZ, we treated PC3 and LNCaP cells with recombinant CTSZ protein, which recapitulated the pro-tumorigenic effects, leading to increased migration, invasion, and proliferation (Figure S2A–F). These findings suggested that secreted CTSZ protein plays a direct role. To test this, we performed rescue experiments using a CTSZ-neutralizing antibody. The antibody effectively abolished the enhanced migration, invasion, and proliferation of PC3 cells induced by OE-CTSZ CM (Fig. 3C-F). Consistent with the in vitro results, in vivo administration of the CTSZ-neutralizing antibody in the orthotopic model significantly attenuated tumor growth and reduced Ki67 and EMT marker expression in the OE-CTSZ group (Fig. 3G, H). These rescue experiments demonstrate that the pro-tumorigenic effects are specifically mediated by secreted CTSZ.

CTSZ binds to αVβ3 integrin on tumor cells, activating the AKT/FOXO1 pathway to downregulate JUNB

To elucidate the downstream signaling pathway in PCa cells, we performed RNA sequencing on PC3 cells treated with OE-CTSZ CM. KEGG pathway analysis highlighted significant enrichment in the TNF signaling pathway, with JUNB, a known tumor suppressor in PCa [19], [20], being the most significantly downregulated gene (Fig. 4A-C). Analysis of public datasets (GSE3933, GSE68882) confirmed that JUNB expression was lower in metastatic PCa compared to primary tumors (Fig. 4D). Functional rescue experiments further confirmed the tumor-suppressive role of JUNB (Figure S3A-D). Clinically, reduced JUNB expression was significantly associated with poor prognosis (shorter disease-free survival) and a higher degree of M2-like macrophage infiltration in PCa patients (Figure S3E–G). qPCR and western blot validated that OE-CTSZ CM downregulated JUNB expression in PCa cells, an effect reversed by the CTSZ-neutralizing antibody (Fig. 4E). Given the RGD motif in CTSZ, we investigated its interaction with integrins. αVβ3 is a well-characterized integrin receptor known to be upregulated in PCa and previously reported to bind CTSZ [18]. Structural modeling using AutoDock predicted a direct interaction between CTSZ and the αVβ3 subunit ITGAV (Fig. 4F). Consistent with its role, αVβ3 protein expression was higher in PCa cell lines (PC3, LNCaP) compared to the normal prostate epithelial cell line RWPE-1 (Fig. 4G). This interaction was further substantiated by co-immunoprecipitation assays in vitro (Fig. 4H) and by co-localization in human PCa tissues (Fig. 4I). Treatment of PCa cells with recombinant CTSZ protein led to increased phosphorylation of AKT and its downstream target FOXO1, concomitant with JUNB downregulation (Fig. 4J). Importantly, the activation of AKT/FOXO1 and suppression of JUNB by OE-CTSZ CM were effectively blocked by Cyclo(-RGDfK), a specific αVβ3 inhibitor [21] (Fig. 4K). These results establish a clear signaling cascade: macrophage-derived CTSZ binds to αVβ3 on PCa cells, activating AKT, which then phosphorylates and inactivates FOXO1, leading to reduced JUNB transcription.

FOXO1 transcriptionally represses JUNB, and targeting αVβ3 inhibits CTSZ-mediated metastasis

To further validate the regulatory mechanism of FOXO1 on JUNB, we used the JASPAR database to predict two FOXO1 binding sites within the 2,000 bp upstream of the JUNB transcription start site (Fig. 5A). Dual-luciferase reporter assays using wild-type and mutant promoters demonstrated that mutation of the − 669 to -680 bp site most effectively reduced FOXO1-mediated luciferase activity, identifying this as the key functional binding site (Fig. 5B, C). Functionally, the αVβ3 inhibitor Cyclo(-RGDfK) significantly mitigated the pro-migratory, pro-invasive, and pro-proliferative effects of OE-CTSZ CM on PC3 cells in vitro (Fig. 5D–G). In a lung metastasis model established by tail vein injection of a mixture of tumor cells and macrophages, the OE-CTSZ group developed a significantly higher number of pulmonary nodules, which was markedly suppressed by Cyclo(-RGDfK) treatment (Fig. 5H, I). Immunohistochemistry of lung tissues mirrored these findings, showing that Cyclo(-RGDfK) reversed the elevated expression of EMT markers induced by OE-CTSZ macrophages (Fig. 5J). Collectively, these data indicate that macrophage-derived CTSZ binds to αVβ3 on PCa cells, activating AKT, which phosphorylates and inactivates FOXO1, leading to reduced JUNB transcription and thereby promoting PCa progression (Fig. 5K).

Discussion

In this study, we describe a novel mechanism by which macrophage-derived CTSZ promotes pro-tumorigenic crosstalk within the PCa TME. We identified CTSZ as a key molecule linking M2 macrophage polarization to direct tumor cell promotion via the αVβ3/AKT/FOXO1/JUNB signaling axis. Our findings both align with and extend previous knowledge of CTSZ in cancer. Although CTSZ is highly expressed in macrophages [14] and promotes invasiveness in gastric cancer [15], its role in PCa has only recently gained attention [16].

Our previous study first described a pathway in which cancer cell-derived CTSZ degrades TRA2A to enhance IL-32 secretion, thereby recruiting and polarizing macrophages [17]. In this study, we provide a complementary yet distinct perspective by focusing on the function of macrophage-derived CTSZ. We demonstrate that CTSZ acts as an autocrine factor to reinforce M2 polarization, creating a positive feedback loop that amplifies the immunosuppressive TME. More importantly, we identify a critical paracrine role for secreted CTSZ. The RGD motif in CTSZ [12] led us to investigate its interaction with integrins. We confirmed that secreted CTSZ directly binds to αVβ3 integrin on PCa cells, a receptor known to be upregulated in PCa and associated with poor prognosis [18], [22]. This interaction is critical for activating the downstream oncogenic signaling. The identification of the AKT/FOXO1/JUNB axis as the key downstream pathway provides a mechanistic explanation for the observed malignant phenotypes. AKT is a central node in cancer signaling, and its activation by integrins is well established [23], [24]. We show that CTSZ/αVβ3 engagement leads to AKT-mediated phosphorylation of FOXO1, a transcription factor traditionally considered as a tumor suppressor [25]. Phosphorylation inactivates FOXO1, preventing it from transactivating its target genes. Our study identifies JUNB, a well-established tumor suppressor in PCa that inhibits invasion and metastasis [19], as a novel transcriptional target repressed by FOXO1. The dual-luciferase assays pinpointed the specific FOXO1 binding site responsible for JUNB repression. This connection between integrin signaling and JUNB downregulation via FOXO1 represents a significant advance in understanding PCa progression. The translational potential of our findings is underscored by the successful application of the αVβ3 inhibitor Cyclo(-RGDfK) in reversing the pro-tumorigenic effects of CTSZ-overexpressing macrophages both in vitro and in vivo.

We acknowledge a limitation of our current experimental approach. Our study primarily utilized a gain-of-function model (overexpression of CTSZ in macrophages) to establish the causal role of macrophage-derived CTSZ. Although this model, combined with neutralizing antibody experiments and recombinant protein treatment, provides strong supportive evidence, direct loss-of-function evidence (e.g., knockdown, or knockout of CTSZ in macrophages) is warranted in future studies to conclusively establish causality and to dissect potential cell-type-specific functions of CTSZ within the TME. Specifically, the mechanistic details by which macrophage-derived CTSZ autocrine reinforces M2 polarization require further investigation. Nevertheless, our findings collectively reveal a multifaceted role for macrophage-derived CTSZ in promoting PCa progression.

Given that integrin-targeted therapies are actively being investigation [21], our study identifies the CTSZ/αVβ3 interaction as a potential therapeutic target for PCa, especially in tumors with high TAMs infiltration and CTSZ expression. In conclusion, our study unveils a multifaceted role for macrophage-derived CTSZ in promoting PCa progression. It drives M2 polarization and, through paracrine signaling, activates the αVβ3/AKT/FOXO1 axis in cancer cells to suppress JUNB, thereby enhancing tumor growth and metastasis. Targeting this axis may offer a novel therapeutic strategy to disrupt the deleterious crosstalk between immune and cancer cells in the prostate TME.

Conclusion

In summary, our study demonstrates that macrophage-derived CTSZ plays a pivotal role in promoting PCa progression through a dual mechanism. First, CTSZ functions as an autocrine factor that drives M2 macrophage polarization, thereby amplifying the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment. Second, and more importantly, secreted CTSZ acts in a paracrine manner by binding to the αVβ3 integrin receptor on PCa cells. This interaction activates the AKT/FOXO1 signaling axis, leading to phosphorylation and inactivation of FOXO1, which subsequently transcriptionally represses the tumor suppressor JUNB. The downregulation of JUNB enhances tumor cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and metastasis both in vitro and in vivo. Crucially, the pro-tumorigenic effects of CTSZ can be effectively reversed by either a CTSZ-neutralizing antibody or the αVβ3 inhibitor Cyclo(-RGDfK), highlighting the therapeutic potential of targeting the CTSZ/αVβ3 interaction for PCa treatment. Our findings unveil a novel crosstalk pathway between TAMs and cancer cells and provide mechanistic insights into how the tumor microenvironment drives PCa progression.
