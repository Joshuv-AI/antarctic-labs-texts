Clinical determinants of severe vaso-occlusive crisis in pediatric sickle cell anemia: a retrospective cohort study

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVaso-occlusive crisis (VOC) is the most common cause of hospitalization and morbidity among children with sickle cell anemia (SCA). Identifying clinical predictors of severe VOC may help improve early risk stratification and management. This study aimed to identify clinical determinants associated with severe VOC among pediatric patients with SCA.MethodsA retrospective observational study was conducted using hospital-based data from pediatric patients with confirmed SCA admitted with VOC in southern Saudi Arabia between August 2025 and December 2025. Severe VOC was defined using a composite outcome including severe pain, prolonged hospitalization, or intensive care unit (ICU) admission. Demographic characteristics, treatment exposures, and admission clinical parameters were analyzed. Independent predictors of severe VOC were identified using multivariable logistic regression analysis.ResultsA total of 776 pediatric patients with SCA were included (mean age 9.5±3.6 years; 54.5% male). Severe VOC occurred in 385 patients (49.6%). In multivariable logistic regression analysis (n=596), higher admission temperature [adjusted odds ratio (aOR) 1.69, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.34–2.15, P<0.001] and increased annual blood transfusion frequency (aOR 1.28, 95% CI: 1.16–1.40, P<0.001) were independently associated with severe VOC. Hydroxyurea therapy demonstrated a protective effect, reducing the risk of severe crisis by approximately 42% (aOR 0.58, 95% CI: 0.43–0.78, P<0.001). The predictive model showed modest but statistically significant discrimination (AUC 0.626, 95% CI: 0.587–0.665, P<0.001).ConclusionsSevere VOC in children with SCA appears to be primarily associated with acute inflammatory triggers, particularly elevated admission temperature, and markers of underlying disease severity such as frequent transfusion requirements. Hydroxyurea therapy demonstrated a significant protective effect, supporting its role in reducing the risk of severe VOC complications.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Nearly half (49.6%) of hospitalized children with sickle cell anemia experienced severe vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC).

• Higher admission temperature and increased annual packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusion frequency were independent predictors of severe VOC.

• Hydroxyurea therapy was independently associated with a 42% lower risk of severe VOC.

• Readily available clinical variables at admission showed modest but significant ability to identify children at increased risk of severe VOC.

What is known and what is new?

• VOC is the leading cause of hospitalization and morbidity in children with sickle cell anemia, and its severity varies widely among patients. Hydroxyurea reduces VOC frequency, but reliable clinical predictors of severe VOC at hospital presentation remain limited.

• This study demonstrates that admission temperature and annual PRBC transfusion frequency are independent predictors of severe VOC in pediatric sickle cell anemia. This study confirms that hydroxyurea independently protects against severe VOC after adjustment for clinical and demographic factors. The findings provide evidence from a large pediatric cohort in southern Saudi Arabia, supporting the use of simple admission variables for early risk stratification.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Children presenting with fever or a history of frequent transfusions should be recognized early as being at increased risk for severe VOC and monitored more closely.

• Optimizing hydroxyurea initiation, adherence, and dose escalation should remain a priority to reduce severe VOC-related outcomes.

Introduction

Sickle cell anemia (SCA) is one of the most common inherited hemoglobin disorders worldwide. It is associated with high rates of morbidity and premature death, particularly in resource-limited settings and in areas where the disease is highly prevalent (1,2). The most common acute complication of SCA is vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC), a painful event caused by obstruction of the microvasculature. VOC occurs when hemoglobin S sticks together, leading to the formation of sickle cells, causing the blood vessel lining to activate, and blocking small blood vessels (3,4). VOC is the main reason for emergency room visits or hospitalization in both children and adults with SCA and account for a major share of healthcare utilization and economic burden (5). Besides causing immediate pain, repeated VOC can cause lasting damage to organs, lower quality of life, and raise the chances of serious issues like acute chest syndrome (ACS), splenic bleeding, and cardiac injury (6,7).

There are a lot of different ways that VOC can show up, from mild cases that can be treated at home with pain relievers to severe cases that need intensive care, blood transfusions, and long hospital stays (8,9). This variability reflects a complicated relationship among clinical, hematologic, hemorheological, genetic, and environmental determinants. Higher blood viscosity, changes in red blood cell shape, and increased red blood cell aggregation are examples of hemorheological abnormalities that have been linked to more frequent episodes of VOC and ACS in children (3). Hematologic and heart-related factors like higher hemoglobin levels, having the α-thalassemia trait, iron overload, and elevated tricuspid regurgitant jet velocity have been linked to more frequent severe pain episodes in children (10). Fetal hemoglobin levels and their distribution within red blood cells can affect the frequency of VOC, with each percentage-point increase in fetal hemoglobin linked to a significant decrease in the rate of VOC (11). In addition, age at the first VOC, baseline hematologic indices, and genetic modifiers have been implicated in overall disease severity and early severe events (12).

Despite this growing evidence, data on the clinical and hematological determinants of the severity of VOC in children with SCA in the Arabian Peninsula remain scarce, despite the prevalence of the Arab-Indian haplotype and its association with a high burden of these crises in regional populations (5). Southern Saudi Arabia has a high burden of SCA and a distinct genetic, health-system, and environmental factors that could affect how often and how severe vascular occlusive crises occur.

This study aimed to assess VOC severity in children with SCA and to identify associated clinical and hematologic predictors in this setting. The study focused on determinants of VOC severity rather than predictors of VOC occurrence, as knowledge of these factors may help identify risks, lead to early targeted treatments, and shape management plans specific to different areas to lessen VOC-related health issues in children with SCA. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tp.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tp-2026-0427/rc).

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was designed as a retrospective observational cohort study conducted among pediatric patients diagnosed with SCA who were admitted with VOC. Clinical and laboratory data were obtained from routinely collected hospital records at five pediatric hospitals in southern Saudi Arabia between August 2025 and December 2025.

Study population

All children aged <18 years with a confirmed diagnosis of SCA based on hemoglobin electrophoresis who were admitted to the hospital due to VOC with availability of admission laboratory investigations were included in this study. The study excluded admissions for causes other than VOC (such as infection without pain crisis, elective transfusion, or surgeries), cases with missing key clinical outcome or laboratory data, and patients with chronic inflammatory or malignant conditions affecting hematologic parameters.

The sample size was estimated based on the planned multivariable logistic regression analysis. Following the recommended rule of at least 10 outcome events per independent variable included in the model, a minimum sample of approximately 500 patients was considered adequate. As severe VOC was expected to occur in nearly half of the study population, the final sample of 776 pediatric patients was considered sufficient to provide reliable statistical estimates and improve the precision of the study findings.

Data collection

The unit of analysis was the individual VOC admission episode rather than the patient. Patients with recurrent VOC admissions could therefore contribute more than one hospitalization to the dataset. VOC severity was determined independently for each admission according to the predefined severity criteria.

Data were extracted using a standardized data collection sheet and included demographic variables (age, sex, residence, nationality), clinical characteristics [SCA genotype, blood group, hydroxyurea therapy status, history of packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusions, splenectomy status, frequency of VOC episodes during follow-up, vaccination, prophylactic penicillin, family history of SCA], clinical outcomes [pain severity score at admission, length of hospital stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) admission], laboratory measurements [admission laboratory parameters including hemoglobin level, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), reticulocyte count, total white blood cell count (TWBC), neutrophil count, serum ferritin, C-reactive protein (CRP)]. All laboratory values represented measurements obtained at the time of hospital admission prior to therapeutic intervention.

Definition of outcome variable

In this study, severe VOC was defined using outcomes that included severe pain on presentation (pain score ≥7/10), prolonged hospital stays (>3 days), or admission to the ICU. This definition was selected to include both the severity of the clinical condition and the healthcare utilization linked to VOC episodes. At present, there is no universally accepted definition of VOC severity in SCA, and the criteria differ between observational studies and clinical trials. Some studies define severe VOC based on hospital stay or the need for opioids, while others rely on complications, healthcare resource utilization, or prolonged hospital stay (13-15).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 28.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) according to distribution, while categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages. Patients were categorized into severe and non-severe VOC groups. Then independent t-test was used for continuous variables, and chi-squared test was used for categorical variables. Spearman correlation analysis assessed relationships between laboratory biomarkers and clinical severity indicators including pain score, ICU admission, and hospital LOS. Variables with clinical relevance or P<0.10 in univariate analysis were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent determinants of severe VOC. Results were reported as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with 95% confidence intervals. A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the institutional review board of Jazan Health Cluster (No. 2580). All other hospitals were informed of and agreed to the study. Patient confidentiality was maintained through anonymized data extraction, and no identifiable information was included in the analysis. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Results

A total of 776 pediatric patients with SCA were included. Of these, 54.5% were male, with a mean age of 9.5±3.6 years. Most were Saudi (82.35%), and 68.85% resided in Jazan. Genotypes were homozygous sickle cell disease (HbSS) 55.7%, hemoglobin sickle cell disease (HbSC) 10.0%, and HbSβ⁰ 10.5%. Blood groups were O+ 38.5%, A+ 17.0%, and B+ 9.5%. Regarding care, 51.7% were vaccinated, 82.5% were on hydroxyurea, 54% received penicillin prophylaxis, and 21% underwent regular PRBC transfusions as shown in Table 1.

Based on predefined criteria, patients were categorized according to VOC severity into severe and non-severe VOC groups using outcomes incorporating severe pain, prolonged hospitalization, or ICU admission. Among the study population, 385 patients (49.6%) met the criteria for severe VOC, whereas 391 patients (50.4%) were classified as having non-severe VOC according to the outcome definition.

Univariate analysis demonstrated significant differences between patients with severe and non-severe VOC episodes. Patients experiencing severe VOC showed higher hydroxyurea use, higher admission temperatures, and greater annual blood transfusion frequency compared with non-severe cases, as shown in Figures 1-3. The box plot analysis as shown in Figure 4 demonstrates differences in laboratory biomarkers between the two VOC groups. Hemoglobin levels were similarly distributed in both groups, with overlapping interquartile ranges. In contrast, patients with severe VOC exhibited a higher TWBC, indicating increased inflammatory activity during the acute crisis. Markers of hemolysis and inflammatory burden, especially ferritin and LDH, also demonstrated greater variability and higher peak values in the severe VOC group compared to the non-severe VOC group. However, none of the laboratory measurements was statistically significant after adjustment in multivariable analysis.

Following the exclusion of cases with incomplete data required for multivariable modeling, 596 patients were included in the final regression analysis, as shown in Table 2. A multivariable logistic regression model was constructed to identify independent determinants of severe VOC while adjusting for demographic characteristics, disease subtype, treatment exposure, and hematologic parameters. The final model included admission temperature, frequency of blood transfusion per year, hydroxyurea use, TWBC, SCA type, age, and sex, as shown in Table 2. The regression model was statistically significant (omnibus test P<0.001), indicating improved prediction of severe VOC compared with the null model. Model calibration was adequate, as demonstrated by a non-significant Hosmer–Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test (P=0.16).

As shown in Table 2, higher admission temperature emerged as a strong independent predictor of severe VOC. Each one-unit increase in temperature was associated with a 69.5% increase in the odds of severe crisis [aOR 1.695, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.338–2.147, P<0.001]. Similarly, frequency of blood transfusion per year independently predicted VOC severity, as increasing transfusion frequency was associated with a 27.6% increase in the likelihood of severe VOC (aOR 1.276, 95% CI: 1.160–1.403, P<0.001), suggesting that patients requiring repeated transfusion represent a clinically more severe disease phenotype. In contrast, hydroxyurea therapy demonstrated a significant protective effect. Patients receiving hydroxyurea had substantially reduced odds of severe VOC compared with non-users (aOR 0.577, 95% CI: 0.425–0.783, P<0.001), corresponding to an approximate 42% reduction in severe crisis risk.

Figure 5 demonstrates the discriminative ability of admission temperature, annual blood transfusion frequency, and hydroxyurea therapy for predicting severe VOC in pediatric SCA. The diagonal reference line indicates no predictive discrimination. The analysis demonstrated modest but statistically significant discriminative performance of the multivariable model in identifying severe VOC, with an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.626 (95% CI: 0.587–0.665, P<0.001). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve remained consistently above the line of no discrimination, indicating that the combination of admission temperature, transfusion burden, and hydroxyurea therapy provides clinically relevant information for differentiating severe from non-severe VOC episodes. Although the model does not achieve high discriminatory accuracy, this finding is expected given the multifactorial nature of VOC severity, which is influenced by complex biological, environmental, and behavioral determinants not fully captured in routine clinical datasets. The observed performance therefore supports the utility of readily available clinical variables for early risk stratification rather than precise outcome prediction.

Discussion

In this study, which included a cohort of 776 pediatric patients with SCA from southern Saudi Arabia, approximately half of the vascular pain episodes met the criteria for severe cases based on defined severity outcomes, such as severe pain, prolonged hospital stays, or admission to the ICU. This nearly equal distribution of severe and non-severe VOC highlights the significant clinical burden of pain crises in this patient population.

Although sex, age, and SCA genotype were evaluated in the multivariable analysis, none were independently associated with severe VOC. This is consistent with results from previous studies, which found that demographic factors are not always clearly associated with the frequency and severity of vascular occlusion episodes (16-18). Our findings indicate that, among a relatively uniform cohort of children with SCA undergoing standardized care, acute clinical and treatment-related variables may be more effective in forecasting the severity of vascular occlusion episodes than baseline demographic characteristics.

The patient’s temperature at the time of admission to the hospital was the best independent predictor of severe VOC. For every degree rise in temperature, the chance of a severe crisis rose by 69.5%. From a biological perspective, this association is logical, as elevated temperature in SCA often indicates a concomitant infection, systemic inflammation, or both, which can trigger endothelial cell activation, leukocyte adhesion, and microvascular occlusion (19,20). This finding supports current clinical practice, which considers fever in SCA a warning sign and recommends increased monitoring, even in cases of mild fever upon hospital admission, in anticipation of complex or prolonged crises (21). Another possible explanation for the association between higher admission temperature and severe VOC is the presence of ACS. ACS often occurs during VOC episodes and is commonly associated with fever, longer hospital stays, and the need for intensive care. Since VOC episodes complicated by ACS were not excluded from our analysis, the observed relationship between temperature and VOC severity may partly reflect the contribution of ACS to more severe clinical outcomes.

Similarly, the annual transfusion rate independently predicted the severity of VOC, with each additional transfusion per year increasing the likelihood of a severe VOC by 27.6%. Transfusion burden is likely an indirect predictor of overall disease severity, rather than a direct causative factor. Children requiring frequent transfusions often exhibit more severe symptoms, with recurrent pain episodes, severe anemia, or a history of complications such as ACS or stroke (22-24). Our findings are consistent with previous studies that identified prior disease burden as a key factor in determining the risk of future VOC and support the use of transfusion rate as a practical marker for identifying high-risk patients in routine clinical practice (25,26).

The use of hydroxyurea showed a strong protective effect, with treated patients having about a 42% lower chance of having severe VOC than those who didn’t use it. This finding aligns with substantial evidence from randomized trials and observational studies demonstrating that hydroxyurea diminishes pain crises, hospitalization rates, and the overall burden of SCA-related acute complications in children (27-29). In our cohort, hydroxyurea was widely prescribed (82.5%), reflecting improved adherence to the disease-modifying therapy recommended in the regional guidelines. The sustained large protective effect after adjusting for age, sex, SCA type, and hematological parameters reinforces the clinical value of hydroxyurea in routine care and supports ongoing efforts to improve its dosage, adherence, and early initiation. Univariate analysis indicated that patients with severe VOC were more likely to receive hydroxyurea, which likely reflects the indication effect, as hydroxyurea is prescribed for children with more severe disease. However, multivariate analysis demonstrated that hydroxyurea remained independently effective in providing protection even after accounting for baseline disease severity and other covariates.

In univariate analysis, patients with severe VOC had numerically higher TWBC levels; however, this difference did not reach statistical significance. Ferritin and LDH levels were significantly higher in the severe VOC group. These results align with the known role of leukocytosis and hemolytic endothelial dysfunction in causing vascular occlusion (19,30). However, none of these laboratory parameters remained statistically significant in the multivariate model. These findings might be influenced by factors like infection, hydration status, sampling timing, and prior interventions, which can impact laboratory values during the acute phase, potentially introducing confounding and decreasing predictive accuracy. Additionally, clinical variables such as temperature and transfusion history may mediate or partially overlap with the effects of TWBC and hemolytic markers, causing linear correlations within the model.

This study has several strengths: it adds to the regional literature on SCA by identifying factors influencing the severity of VOC in children in southern Saudi Arabia, where SCA is widespread and where healthcare infrastructure and practices may differ from those in other regions. This study benefits from a relatively large cohort of children and reflects real-world clinical practice rather than controlled clinical trial conditions. The integration of clinical symptom variables with treatment exposure allowed for the identification of independent factors determining the severity of VOC in the pediatric population.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the retrospective design of the study limits the possibility of inferring causal relationships. Second, the incomplete availability of some laboratory biomarkers reduced the inclusion of certain biologically relevant variables in the multivariate analysis. Third, the data were drawn from a single-region population, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings. Therefore, future multi-regional studies that include a longitudinal assessment of the biomarkers are essential.

Clinically, these findings support the use of readily available variables, such as patient temperature upon admission, number of recent transfusions, and exposure to hydroxyurea, to assess primary risk rather than accurately predict individual outcomes. For example, children with a high annual transfusion rate and fever, especially if they have not received hydroxyurea, may be prioritized for close monitoring, more effective pain and infection management, and early consideration of specialist care.

Conclusions

In this multicenter retrospective cohort of pediatric patients with SCA, nearly half of hospitalized VOC episodes were classified as severe. Higher admission temperature and more frequent annual PRBC transfusions were independently associated with an increased risk of severe VOC, whereas hydroxyurea therapy was associated with a significantly lower risk. These findings suggest that simple clinical variables available at hospital admission may help identify children at increased risk for severe VOC and support timely clinical decision-making. They also reinforce the importance of optimizing hydroxyurea therapy as part of routine disease management. Further prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate these predictors and develop more accurate risk prediction models for severe VOC in pediatric SCA.
