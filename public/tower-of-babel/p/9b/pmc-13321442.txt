ZmWAKL21 is a maize wall-associated kinase-like protein that interacts with AvrRppC and contributes to southern corn rust resistance
Authors: Juan Sun, Qidong Xu, Meng Lv, Xinwei Li, Jiale Yang, Thanhliem Nguyen, Zhiqiang Tian, Zhimin Li, Xinyang Liao, Mingjian Hu, Haonan Li, Jinge Bo, Fucheng Jia, Chuang Geng, Xiaoqi Kang, Zihong Chen, Junjie Hao, Jihua Tang, Tianhui Zheng, Junqiang Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Southern corn rust (SCR), caused by the fungal pathogen Puccinia polysora (P. polysora), is a major threat to maize production worldwide. To date, two major nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat (NLR) resistance genes, RppC and RppK, have been identified that confer protection against P. polysora. However, their durability is frequently undermined by the rapid evolution of the pathogen. While key components of NLR-mediated resistance have been characterized, the molecular interface between maize and P. polysora—particularly the roles of fungal effectors such as AvrRppC—remains largely unexplored. In particular, the early immune signaling events at the plasma membrane are still poorly defined in this pathosystem. In this study, an MBP-tagged AvrRppC pull-down MS (Mass spectrometry) screening system within the maize–P. polysora pathosystem was first established. Using this approach, we identified ZmWAKL21, which encodes a wall-associated receptor kinase-like protein. Molecular, biochemical, and cellular assays show that AvrRppC can bind ZmWAKL21 on the plasma membrane. During P. polysora infection, ZmWAKL21 positively increases the expression of ZmrbohD and ZmPR1, resulting in elevated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Kinase assays further revealed that ZmWAKL21 possesses intrinsic serine/threonine kinase activity, which is directly suppressed by AvrRppC, highlighting an arm race between host and pathogen. Despite this antagonistic interaction, transgenic maize plants overexpressing ZmWAKL21 exhibited significantly enhanced resistance to P. polysora compared with wild-type controls, whereas CRISPR-Cas9-generated knockout mutants were more susceptible. Together, our findings uncover an immune interface in the maize–P. polysora pathosystem, in which the fungal effector AvrRppC suppresses the kinase activity of ZmWAKL21, yet overexpression of ZmWAKL21 still enhances ROS-associated defense responses. This study provides new mechanistic insights into fungal effector–kinase interactions and the regulation of immune signaling in monocot plants.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11658-026-00868-1.

Introduction

Molecular plant–pathogen interactions underlie many crop diseases, causing huge economic losses and threatening food security globally [1]. The molecular dialog between plants and pathogens is highly complex and dynamic. A major challenge for pathogens is to break through the physical barrier of the host cell wall while avoiding detection by the plant’s immune receptors to elicit an appropriate defense response [2, 3]. In plants, secreted pathogen effectors and conserved microbial patterns represent the primary molecular cues recognized by the host immune system, leading to effector-triggered immunity (ETI) and pattern recognition receptor (PRR)-triggered immunity (PTI). Cytoplasmic resistance proteins, chiefly NLRs, are responsible for initiating ETI by either directly recognizing pathogen-specific secreted effector proteins or sensing the alterations in host proteins that are targeted by these effectors. Pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), which identify pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) such as bacterial flagellin, lipopolysaccharides, peptidoglycans, and fungal chitin initiate the PTI [4]. This “binary model” of plant immunity has been widely acknowledged since 2006 [5]. However, how this host–pathogen interaction paradigm applies to southern corn rust (SCR) disease remains largely unclear.

In recent years, southern corn rust, caused by the fungus P. polysora, has become increasingly frequent and widespread, emerging as one of the major threats to maize production globally [6, 7]. Cloning of resistance genes from maize and identification of pivotal pathogenic factors within P. polysora are vital for the effective control of southern corn rust. To date, two SCR resistance genes (RppC and RppK) and the cognate Avr genes (AvrRppC and AvrRppK) have been cloned [8, 9]. During infection, AvrRppC has been identified as a pivotal effector in P. polysora pathogenicity, capable of circumventing RppC-mediated resistance through mutations [8]. This evolutionary flexibility underscores the urgent need to investigate alternative resistance strategies, such as multilayered resistance systems beyond classical R–Avr interactions or structural defenses less susceptible to pathogen adaptation.

Wall-associated receptor kinases (WAKs) and WAK-like proteins (WAKLs) play pivotal roles in plant immunity by mediating diverse molecular mechanisms. These receptors typically perceive extracellular pathogens through their extracellular domains (ECDs), by either binding to pectin or recognizing pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs), and subsequently transduce immune signals via intracellular domains (ICDs), often culminating in reactive oxygen species (ROS) bursts through respiratory burst oxidase homolog (RBOH) activation [10–12]. Positioned at the cell wall–plasma membrane interface, WAKs/WAKLs function as sentinels that detect pathogen-induced cell wall perturbations and initiate immune responses. These responses include (1) activating signaling cascades that lead to ROS production, and (2) reinforcing structural defenses through callose deposition and cell wall remodeling. Recent advances in the cloning of several new gene members of WAKs and WAKLs from various crops have revealed their direct involvement in managing broad-spectrum resistance against a range of plant pathogens. In maize, the ZmWAKL gene, which encodes a cell-wall-associated receptor kinase-like protein, is the causative gene at the major quantitative disease resistance locus against gray leaf spot (GLS), a significant foliar maize disease caused by the fungal pathogens Cercospora zeae-maydis and Cercospora zeina [13]. Upon pathogen infection, the ZmWAKLY/ZmWIK receptor complex, encoded by the resistance allele, triggers an immune signaling cascade from ZmWAKLY, ZmWIK, ZmBLK1 to ZmRBOH4, leading to a reactive oxygen species burst and increased resistance to GLS [12]. Another wall-associated kinase-like gene, ZmWAK, has been identified as the causal gene underlying the qHSR1 locus, conferring approximately 25% reduction in disease incidence caused by Sporisorium reilianum. ZmWAK is prominently expressed in the mesocotyls of maize seedlings, where it suppresses certain secreted differentially expressed genes necessary for Sporisorium reilianum invasion. This inhibits the growth of endophytic Sporisorium reilianum, leading to fewer hyphae reaching the florescence meristems, thereby mitigating disease incidence [10, 14]. Recently, ZmWAK was found to mediate resistance to Sporisorium reilianum through a phosphorylation cascade involving ZmSnRK1α2 and WRKY53, which downregulates nutrient-related genes and limits pathogen access to host resources [15]. ZmWAK-RLK1 is a gene that provides quantitative resistance to northern corn leaf blight (NCLB), a disease caused by the hemibiotrophic fungal pathogen Exserohilum turcicum. By regulating the host’s metabolic pathway, ZmWAK-RLK1 reduces the biosynthesis of benzoxazinoids, indirectly inhibiting the germination and penetration of Exserohilum turcicum spores. This action prevents the pathogen from penetrating host tissues, delays the formation of lesions and sporulation, and consequently endows maize with partial resistance to NCLB [16]. Beyond maize, additional WAK and WAKL genes in other crops also play significant roles in conferring resistance to pathogens. For instance, in wheat, silencing TaWAK-6D weakens resistance to Fusarium pseudograminearum and Rhizoctonia cerealis and lowers defense gene expression [17]. TaWAK6 boosts resistance to leaf rust (Puccinia triticina) [18]. In rice, OsWAK14, OsWAK91, and OsWAK92 enhance resistance to rice blast fungus, while OsWAK112d reduces it [19]. In cotton, GhWAK7A plays a key role against fungal wilt by interacting with chitin receptors [20]. In soybean, GmWAK1 positively affects resistance to Phytophthora sojae [21]. However, the function of WAKs and WAKLs in host–pathogen interactions remains to be thoroughly analyzed, and their role in southern corn rust resistance has not yet been reported. Notably, direct interactions between WAK/WAKL proteins and fungal effectors have been rarely described, and mechanistic insights into such interactions remain limited.

Here, we identified a wall-associated kinase-like protein, ZmWAKL21, as a novel contributor to maize basal immunity against P. polysora, the causal pathogen of southern corn rust. The fungal effector AvrRppC suppresses the kinase activity of ZmWAKL21; however, increased ZmWAKL21 expression still enhances ROS-associated defense responses. This work expands our understanding of WAK/WAKL-mediated immune pathways in monocot crops and provides new insights into membrane-associated immune regulation in the maize–P. polysora pathosystem.

Materials and methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Maize inbred lines B104, B73, CML496, and C01 were used in this study. To generate ZmWAKL21 overexpression lines, the 2034-bp coding sequence from cDNA of maize inbred B73 was inserted into the vector pBECXUN containing the ubiquitin promoter to construct the recombinant plasmid pUbi:Myc-ZmWAKL21-3xFlag and transformed into maize inbred line B104. To generate ZmWAKL21 knockout lines, a target sequence of ZmWAKL21 was selected for Cas9 cleavage. The sgRNA fragment was cloned into vector pBUE411 and transformed into the maize inbred line C01. Positive transgenic lines were screened in Xingyang (Henan Province, China), and the phenotypes were evaluated in Yazhou (Hainan province, China). The maize used in laboratory experiments was cultured in an incubator at 26 °C/24 °C (light/dark) with 60% humidity and a cycle of 16 h/8 h (light/dark).

Protein expression and purification

To generate maltose-binding protein (MBP) and His tag fusion proteins, the coding sequences of AvrRppC (AvrRppCref unless otherwise noted) and AvrRppCF were amplified and cloned into the vector pMal-c2x, and the coding sequences of ZmWAKL21 (ZmWAKL21B73 unless otherwise noted), ZmWAKL21B104 and ZmWAKL21MY73 were amplified from inbred line B104 and the parental line of hybrid MY73, respectively, and subsequently cloned into the pSmartI vector. The resulting constructs were expressed in cells of Escherichia coli strain BL21 (DE3) (TSINGKE, Beijing, China) under induction with 0.4 mM isopropyl-β-d-thiogalactoside (IPTG) while shaking at 28 °C for 8 h. Proteins were collected and resuspended in column buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 7.6], 200 mM NaCl, 1 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT)). The resuspended liquid was sonicated to break the cells until the liquid was clear (cycles of 3 s on, 3 s off, 40% power rate) and then centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 30 min to collect the protein supernatant. MBP, MBP-AvrRppC, or MBP-AvrRppCF protein was purified with MBP beads (SAE0218, MilliporeSigma, MA, USA). His, His-ZmWAKL21, His-ZmWAKL21B104, or His-ZmWAKL21MY73 protein was purified with Ni Beads (Ni Smart Beads 6FF, SA036100, Smart-Lifesciences, Changzhou, China). Primers used in this section are listed in Additional file 5: Table S1.

Field disease scoring

Wild-type and transgenic plants were grown in the same field plots and naturally infected. Southern corn rust (SCR) severity was evaluated 3–4 weeks after pollination on the basis of the infected area of the ear leaf, the leaf above the ear, and the leaf below the ear, using a 1–9 rating scale, where 1 = no visible symptoms or only scattered uredinia, 2 = < 10% leaf area infected, 3 = 11–20% leaf area infected, 4 = 21–30% leaf area infected, 5 = 31–50% leaf area infected, 6 = 51–60% leaf area infected, 7 = 61–75% leaf area infected, 8 = 76–90% leaf area infected, and 9 = > 90% leaf area infected with most leaves severely damaged or plants dead.

Artificial inoculation of P. polysora in the incubator

P. polysora spores were collected from naturally infected maize plants in the field. Genotyping confirmed the presence of multiple isolates, including PP.CN1.0 (harboring AvrRppC and AvrRppCE) and PP.CN2.0 (harboring AvrRppCC and AvrRppCF). Spores were diluted with 0.01% Tween-20 solution to a concentration of 1 × 106 spores/mL, then 4–5 leaf–stage maize were sprayed until the leaf surface was wet. The inoculated maize plants were cultured in the dark for 12 h, at 26 °C and with a humidity of 70%–90% in an incubator, and then cultured according to the ordinary photoperiod.

Pulldown-MS analysis

The leaves of B73 inbred seedlings were ground into powder in liquid nitrogen. Protein extraction buffer (20 mM Tris–HCl [pH 8.0], 1 mM EDTA [pH 8.0], 50 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100) was added to the powder (2 mL/1 g) and incubated at 4 °C for 1 h. After incubation, the supernatant was obtained by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min, and then filtered by a 0.22-μm filter membrane. The filtered protein supernatant was mixed with MBP or MBP-AvrRppC protein and then purified with MBP beads (SAE0218, MilliporeSigma, Massachusetts, USA). Purified proteins were separated on 8% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS)-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gels, the gel strips were cut and detected on the Thermo Q Exactive™ HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher, MA, USA) for protein identification. Functional annotation of the identification results was performed using COG (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/research/cog/), Gene Ontology (GO, https://www.geneontology.org/), and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/kegg1.html) databases.

Following the initial mass spectrometry analysis of proteins pulled down with MBP-AvrRppC, 454 candidate interactors were identified onthe basis of peptide enrichment compared with the MBP-only control. To refine the candidate list, we performed a multistep prioritization process. First, we filtered for proteins predicted to localize to either the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) or plasma membrane using the WoLF PSORT (version 0.2) and TargetP (version 2.0) subcellular localization prediction tools, given the ER localization of AvrRppC and the relevance of membrane-associated proteins in immune signaling. This reduced the list to 30 candidates. Second, among these, ZmWAKL21 was the top-ranking candidate, exhibiting a relative protein intensity of 11,207,481, which is approximately 1.5-fold higher than the second-ranking candidate (7,508,675). This combination of subcellular localization relevance and enrichment ranking constituted the multistep strategy used to identify ZmWAKL21 as the most promising interactor for further validation.

Pull-down assay

To detect the interaction between AvrRppC/AvrRppCF and ZmWAKL21 variants (ZmWAKL21B104, ZmWAKL21MY73, ZmWAKL21ECD, ZmWAKL21ICD), approximately equal amounts of MBP, MBP-AvrRppC, or MBP-AvrRppCF proteins were mixed with His-tagged ZmWAKL21, His-ZmWAKL21B104, His-ZmWAKL21MY73, His-ZmWAKL21ECD, or His-ZmWAKL21ICD separately, and the mixed supernatants were incubated with MBP beads (SAE0218, MilliporeSigma, MA, USA) at 4 °C for 3 h. The beads were washed three times with column buffer and boiled with 1× SDS protein loading buffer at 100 °C for 10 min. The proteins were separated on SDS-PAGE gels and detected by western blot. Anti-MBP antibodies (AM1225, Beyotime, Shanghai, China, 1:5000) were used to detect MBP-tagged protein, and anti-His antibodies (AH367-1, Beyotime, Shanghai, China, 1:5000) were used to detect His-tagged protein. AlphaFold3-based complex prediction

The interaction between AvrRppC and ZmWAKL21 was predicted using AlphaFold3 (Multimer mode). Separate models were generated for AvrRppC with the extracellular (ECD) and intracellular (ICD) domains of ZmWAKL21, defined on the basis of domain annotation and TMHMM prediction. For each pair, the top-ranked models were selected according to ipTM + pTM scores. Model confidence was evaluated using pLDDT values and predicted aligned error (PAE) plots, while binding affinities were estimated with PRODIGY. Interfacial residues and contact types (hydrophobic interactions, hydrogen bonds, and salt bridges) were analyzed with PLIP version 2.3 and visualized in PyMOL version 2.6.

In vitro phosphorylation assay

Phosphorylation-dependent mobility shifts were subsequently analyzed by western blotting. Results

Employing pull-down MS screening to identify maize proteins interacting with AvrRppC

While AvrRppC is recognized as an important effector in P. polysora infection, the molecular basis of its function in maize remains largely unknown. In particular, the identity of host proteins that associate with AvrRppC has not been clarified. To address this, we employed pull-down MS to identify host proteins that interact with AvrRppC. Subsequently, both MBP-AvrRppC and MBP alone were heterologously expressed in E. coli and immobilized on MBP-affinity beads (Fig. 1A, B). These beads were then incubated with total protein extracts derived from leaves of the maize inbred line B73. Proteins specifically interacting with AvrRppC were isolated via pull-down assay and subsequently identified by mass spectrometry (Fig. 1C). The initial MS analysis identified 454 candidate interactors. To narrow down the candidates, we retained 30 proteins predicted to localize to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) or plasma membrane, considering AvrRppC’s ER localization and the functional relevance of membrane-localized proteins in immune signaling [8]. Among these, ZmWAKL21 was the top-ranking candidate, exhibiting a relative protein intensity of 11,207,481, which is approximately 1.5-fold higher than the second-ranking candidate (7,508,675). The full dataset of MS results is provided in Additional file 1: Data 1. This multistep selection process highlighted ZmWAKL21 as the most promising candidate for further functional investigation. The interaction between ZmWAKL21 and AvrRppC was further validated by pull-down assay, confirming the physical association of these two proteins (Fig. 1D). Although ZmWAKL21 was the only WAK/WAKL family protein among these 454 candidate interactors, we next sought to assess the specificity of its interaction with AvrRppC. To this end, we selected ZmWAKL1, the closest evolutionary homolog of ZmWAKL21 in maize, as a control. Phylogenetic analysis and structural features of ZmWAKL21

There are a total of 52 ZmWAKs/ZmWAKLs in maize (B73 genome V5) [26, 27]. Chromosome mapping revealed that ZmWAKL21 is located on chromosome 3, with other genes being unevenly distributed across the ten chromosomes of maize (Additional file 5: Fig. S2A and Additional file 2: Data 2). This indicates significant variation in the genomic abundance of WAKs/WAKLs in maize. A comparative synteny analysis was conducted to understand the evolutionary mechanisms underlying ZmWAKL21 and its homologs across Zea mays (maize), Oryza sativa (rice), ‌Sorghum bicolor (sorghum), and Glycine max (soybean) (Additional file 5: Fig. S2B). The analysis revealed that ZmWAKL21 exhibits syntenic relationships with rice, soybean, and sorghum, specifically identifying one syntenic orthologous gene pair with rice, three with soybean, and one with sorghum (Additional file 5: Fig. S2B).

To elucidate the evolutionary relationship between ZmWAKL21 and its homologs across Arabidopsis, soybean, maize, rice, and sorghum, we performed phylogenetic analyses. Six primary clusters were identified, with ZmWAKL21 demonstrating a close relationship to ZmWAKL1, GmWAKL14, and OsWAK25. These genes were grouped together in group II, sharing the same neighboring clades (Additional file 5: Fig. S3A). Then, we identified the conserved motifs of ZmWAKL21 and its homologs, uncovering a total of 20 conserved motifs (Additional file 5: Fig. S3B). Most WAK proteins clustered in the same group have similar conserved motifs (Additional file 5: Fig. S3B). WAK/WAKL proteins, characterized by highly conserved galacturonan-binding (GUB) domains and intracellular serine/threonine kinase (STK) domains, mediate cell wall–plasma membrane connections and function as signal transducers or modulators in various cellular processes [28–30]. Sequence analysis revealed that ZmWAKL21 includes the key functional domains characteristic of WAK/WAKL proteins, including GUB, STK, and WAK association domain (Additional file 5: Fig. S3C, Fig. S4, and Table S10). This demonstrates that ZmWAKL21 could potentially function as a wall-associated receptor kinase. Promoter element analysis unveiled that ZmWAKL21 and its homologs harbor methyl jasmonate (MeJA)- and salicylic acid (SA)-related elements (Additional file 5: Fig. S3D and Additional file 3: Data 3), potentially enhancing resistance to both biotic and abiotic stresses. To validate this, ZmWAKL21 expression was assessed post-treatment with MeJA and SA. The findings demonstrated that ZmWAKL21 was upregulated following MeJA and SA treatment (Additional file 5: Fig. S5). The combined evidence from comparative synteny analysis, phylogenetic analysis, conserved motif distribution, and conserved domain analysis further supports the conservation and potentially wall-associated kinase function of ZmWAKL21.

The expression level of ZmWAKL21 is triggered by P. polysora infection.

From the V9 stage of maize development, rising field temperatures and humidity significantly promote the spread and infection of P. polysora, triggering the onset of southern corn rust. Hence, we analyzed the expression level of ZmWAKL21 in maize leaf tissues from the V1 stage through to 30 days after pollination. The results indicate that the expression level of ZmWAKL21 progressively escalates, reaching its peak during the V9 to R2 stages, and remains at a relatively high level thereafter (Fig. 2A). To investigate the expression patterns of ZmWAKL21 during P. polysora infection, we analyzed its expression levels at various time points post-infection in the susceptible inbred line B73 (lacking the RppC gene) and resistance inbred line CML496 (carrying the RppC gene). The results demonstrate that P. polysora infection induces of ZmWAKL21 expression. In both the B73 and CML496 inbred lines, ZmWAKL21 expression was markedly upregulated following P. polysora infection compared to mock-treated controls, peaking at 12 h post-infection (hpi) before gradually declining (Fig. 2B, C). However, a notable difference emerges in the CML496 line, where the expression level of ZmWAKL21 maintains a relatively elevated state even 36 h after the onset of infection, a pattern not observed in B73 (Fig. 2B, C). ZmWAKL21 encodes a plasma membrane-localized protein

ZmWAKL21 is characterized by a conserved N-terminal signal peptide, an extracellular WAK galacturonan binding domain, a WAK-associated domain, a transmembrane domain, and a C-terminal cytosolic Ser/Thr protein kinase domain (Fig. 2D and Additional file 5: Fig. S4), and is strongly postulated to be a membrane protein. To ascertain the subcellular localization of ZmWAKL21, we engineered a construct by fusing the full-length coding sequence with a green fluorescent protein (GFP) at the C-terminus and subsequently infiltrated this into the leaves of N. benthamiana. These ZmWAKL21-GFP fusion proteins were found to be co-localized with the membrane marker (PIP2A-mCherry) when they were transiently expressed in tobacco leaves (Fig. 2E). Thus, ZmWAKL21 was confirmed to be a plasma membrane-localized protein.

Discussion

ZmWAKL21 contributes to maize resistance against Puccinia polysora and is targeted by the fungal effector AvrRppC

In this study, we present multiple lines of evidence that elucidate the molecular function of ZmWAKL21, a wall-associated kinase-like protein in maize, in its interaction with the Puccinia polysora effector AvrRppC and its role in activating ROS-associated immune signaling. Importantly, the immune regulatory role of ZmWAKL21 appears independent of developmental costs, pointing to a dedicated role in stress-responsive signaling. Firstly, we conducted a pull-down MS screening of maize proteins for interaction with AvrRppC, and confirmed ZmWAKL21 as the most promising candidate (Fig. 1 and Additional file 1: Data 1). Secondly, we observed an increase in the expression level of ZmWAKL21 after P. polysora infection (Fig. 2A–C). These findings suggest that maize may enhance its resistance to P. polysora through the abundant expression of ZmWAKL21. Thirdly, we further confirmed that ZmWAKL21 is a conserved membrane protein (Fig. 2D, E, Additional file 5: Fig. S4 and Additional file 2: Data S2, Additional file 3: Data 3) and demonstrated that AvrRppC binds ZmWAKL21 on the plasma membrane (Fig. 3). On the plasma membrane, WAKs and WAKLs typically perceive extracellular signals through their extracellular domains (ECDs), such as pectin binding or recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns, and transduce these signals via intracellular domains (ICDs) to activate immune responses [10–12]. Like other WAK/WAKL proteins, ZmWAKL21 contains both ECD and ICD domains, and pull-down assays demonstrated that each domain is capable of interacting with AvrRppC (Fig. 3D, E). To gain structural insights into the AvrRppC–ZmWAKL21 interaction, we modeled the binding between AvrRppC and both the ECD and ICD of ZmWAKL21 using AlphaFold3. The comparable binding energies between AvrRppC–ZmWAKL21ECD and AvrRppC–ZmWAKL21ICD complexes, together with the presence of multiple contact interfaces, point to a well-defined dual-interface interaction rather than a nonspecific association (Additional file 5: Fig. S6). Integrating these structural insights with our functional data, we propose an interface model in which the ECD engages AvrRppC at the cell surface, while the ICD is targeted by AvrRppC to directly suppress kinase activity.

Our biochemical assays confirmed that ZmWAKL21 possesses intrinsic kinase activity, as it was able to phosphorylate MBPα in vitro (Fig. 4). However, co-incubation with the fungal effector AvrRppC significantly suppressed this phosphorylation activity, indicating that AvrRppC directly targets and inhibits ZmWAKL21’s kinase function. Intriguingly, the virulent allele AvrRppCF also interacted with ZmWAKL21 and similarly suppressed its kinase activity (Fig. 5A, B). Combined results from CRISPR-Cas9-mediated knockout and overexpression lines of ZmWAKL21 provide compelling genetic evidence that ZmWAKL21 acts as a positive regulator of maize immunity against P. polysora (Figs. 6–9). Intriguingly, despite being targeted by AvrRppC, overexpression of ZmWAKL21 in maize still conferred enhanced resistance to P. polysora (Figs. 7–9). This suggests that increasing the abundance of ZmWAKL21 may substantially overcome the suppressive effect of AvrRppC, tipping the balance in favor of the host. In line with these observations, ZmWAKL21 overexpression also led to elevated expression of defense-related genes ZmrbohD and ZmPR1, increased H2O2 accumulation, and localized cell death upon P. polysora infection, indicating that ROS signaling is a major downstream response (Fig. 8). Although the precise downstream substrates remain to be identified, previous studies on WAK/WAKL proteins (e.g., ZmWAK and ZmWAKL) have shown they can activate RBOH-dependent ROS production through phosphorylation cascades [10, 12, 15]. We therefore propose that ZmWAKL21 may function upstream of ROS-producing components such as RBOH proteins, possibly through a similar signaling mechanism. As the first wall-associated kinase-like protein implicated in resistance to P. polysora, ZmWAKL21 broadens the functional scope of the WAKL family and highlights its broader involvement in plant defense signaling.

AvrRppC’s effect on ZmWAKL21-mediated immunity was further examined by protein infiltration–challenge assays, which showed that AvrRppC markedly suppressed P. polysora-induced ZmPR1 expression in ZmWAKL21-overexpression positive segregants compared with wild-type and null segregants, whereas this suppression was significantly alleviated in ZmWAKL21 knockout mutants (Fig. 9). Unfortunately, owing to the obligate biotrophic nature of P. polysora, it can only be cultured on live maize plants, which limits the ability to conduct transgenic experiments. This technical limitation prevents the generation of AvrRppC overexpression or knockout mutants, thereby constraining direct genetic validation on the pathogen side.

Together, our study provides new molecular insights into the arms race at the host–pathogen interface, where plants activate defenses via proteins such as ZmWAKL21, while pathogens deploy effectors to counteract them. These findings advance our understanding of WAKL-mediated immunity in monocots and lay a foundation for future exploration of their signaling mechanisms in biotrophic interactions.

ZmWAKL21 serves as a potential target for maize P. polysora resistance breeding

In the past few years, the fungal pathogen P. polysora, responsible for southern corn rust, has been growing more common and widespread. It has surfaced as a significant menace to worldwide maize cultivation [1, 31]. Previous studies have identified two genes conferring resistance to P. polysora through map-based cloning of quantitative trait loci (QTL), which is the main approach for identifying genes related to SCR resistance. Both of these resistance genes cloned to date are NLR genes, RppC [8] and RppK [9]. Both genes exhibit substantial vertical resistance to SCR. However, the rapid evolution of P. polysora populations challenges the durability of resistance conferred by NLR genes. To improve the durability and effectiveness of disease resistance, it is essential to combine NLR genes with other types of resistance genes.

To this end, our study identifies ZmWAKL21 as a novel resistance-associated gene that expands the current understanding of SCR resistance beyond the NLR paradigm. While ZmWAKL21 is highly conserved at the structural level, minor allelic variations were detected among the parental lines of five major Chinese maize varieties cultivated in 2024 (MY73, Denghai605, Jingke968, Yufeng303, and Zhongkeyu505), suggesting its potential applicability in breeding programs (Additional file 5: Figs. S7–8). Despite these minor sequence differences, our allele-based assays showed that AvrRppC binds and suppresses the kinase activity of multiple ZmWAKL21 variants, indicating that this effector–target association is conserved across natural allelic diversity in maize (Fig. 5C–G).

Given its distinct mechanism of action, high-expression form ZmWAKL21 may serve as a stable immune backbone for combination with NLR genes such as RppC and RppK through transgenic breeding, offering an effective strategy to enhance SCR disease resistance. Although ZmWAKL21 is conserved among maize germplasm, optimizing its expression or activity may further strengthen resistance. Thus, our findings not only deepen the understanding of crosstalk mechanisms in the maize-P. polysora pathosystem but also offer a promising target gene for developing SCR-resistant maize cultivars through molecular breeding.

Conclusions

Southern corn rust (SCR), caused by Puccinia polysora, continues to pose a significant challenge to global maize production. Here, we provide multiple lines of evidence that ZmWAKL21, a wall-associated kinase-like protein, contributes to basal resistance against P. polysora. AvrRppC binds ZmWAKL21 and consistently suppresses its intrinsic kinase activity. During P. polysora infection, ZmWAKL21 positively increases the expression of ZmrbohD and ZmPR1, resulting in elevated production of reactive oxygen species (ROS). Notably, this study represents the first report of a WAK/WAKL protein involved in southern corn rust resistance and one of the rare examples of direct interaction between a WAKL protein and a fungal effector. These findings expand the functional scope of WAK/WAKL-mediated immune responses and provide new insights into cell-surface immune mechanisms in monocot crops.
