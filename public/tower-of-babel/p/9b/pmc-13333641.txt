Pulmonary Leukemic Infiltration in Relapsed Acute Myeloid Leukemia: A Case Report
Authors: Masoud Mortezazadeh, Seyyed Reza Safaei, Ali Majidi Nejad, Seyyed Taher Seyyed Mahmoudi, Saeid Haji Aghajani, Aysan Nozheh, Amir Motamed Chaboki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary leukemic infiltration (PLI) is a rare complication of acute myeloid leukemia often misdiagnosed as pneumonia. This case highlights a unique presentation of PLI in a relapsed patient without hyperleukocytosis, necessitating a high index of suspicion. Aggressive salvage chemotherapy followed by stem cell transplantation was effective in resolving the infiltration and achieving remission.Keywords: acute myeloid leukemia, cancer, extramedullary relapse, pulmonary leukemic infiltration, relapsed AML

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is an aggressive hematopoietic neoplasm defined by uncontrolled proliferation of myeloid progenitor cells, resulting in both systemic and extramedullary disease manifestations that substantially affect clinical outcomes. Pulmonary leukemic infiltration (PLI), an uncommon yet catastrophic complication, occurs when malignant blasts invade pulmonary parenchyma and vasculature [1]. Frequently misdiagnosed due to nonspecific symptoms mimicking infection or pulmonary edema, PLI demands histopathologic confirmation, often necessitating invasive sampling [2]. Its pathophysiology involves multifactorial mechanisms including leukemic cell endothelial adhesion, microenvironmental tropism, and tissue infiltration, which can develop irrespective of circulating blast counts [3]. Although rare, PLI carries grave prognostic implications, with scant literature to inform optimal management. Early detection and aggressive treatment are therefore essential to mitigate its high mortality risk. We describe an AML case complicated by PLI, illustrating diagnostic challenges, characteristic imaging features, and treatment response—emphasizing the need for clinical vigilance regarding this underrecognized entity.

Case Presentation

A 39‐year‐old male with no prior significant medical or surgical history was referred to the hospital. He initially presented with symptoms of an upper respiratory tract infection and gingival hypertrophy. An incidental complete blood count checkup revealed abnormal findings. Initial laboratory evaluation revealed mild anemia (hemoglobin: 11.5 g/dL), mild leukocytosis (white blood cell count: 11,900/μL), and thrombocytopenia (platelet count: 80,000/μL). Physical examination was notable for gingival swelling but was otherwise unremarkable for acute distress.

Differential Diagnosis

Based on the patient's presenting history of upper respiratory symptoms and gingival hypertrophy, along with physical examination findings of gingival swelling and laboratory abnormalities (anemia, leukocytosis, and thrombocytopenia), the differential diagnosis includes an acute leukemia, specifically AML or acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), given the concurrent cytopenia and leukocytosis. Gingival hypertrophy in this setting is a well‐reckonable extramedullary manifestation of acute leukemia, particularly of the monocytic lineage. Other considerations include a severe viral infection or drug reaction causing cytopenia, although these would not typically present with gingival hypertrophy. Myelodysplastic syndrome is less likely given the acute presentation with leukocytosis rather than progressive cytopenia alone. Thus, an acute leukemia remains the primary diagnostic suspicion requiring prompt bone marrow evaluation.

Investigation and Treatment

Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy demonstrated a markedly hypercellular marrow (> 90% cellularity) with more than 80% blasts. Flow cytometric analysis established a diagnosis of FAB M4 (acute myelomonocytic leukemia), confirmed to be non‐M3 (non‐APL) based on the absence of PML‐RARA rearrangement. Cytogenetic studies showed a normal male karyotype (46, XY), and molecular testing revealed an NPM1 mutation without evidence of FLT3‐ITD, FLT3‐TKD, or CEBPA mutations (ELN Favorable risk). Baseline chest CT imaging at the time of diagnosis showed no abnormalities (Figure 1). No MRD study was performed at the time of initial remission assessment. The patient started standard “7 + 3” induction chemotherapy (cytarabine 100 mg/m2/day for 7 days and daunorubicin 60 mg/m2/day for 3 days). Bone marrow evaluation on day 28 post‐induction confirmed complete remission (CR) with less than 5% blasts. He subsequently underwent consolidation therapy with four cycles of high‐dose cytarabine (HiDAC) administered at 3 g/m2 intravenously over 3 h every 12 h on days 1, 3, and 5 of each cycle. He was discharged without complications. Twenty‐three months following his initial diagnosis, the patient presented with recurrent fatigue. New laboratory results showed thrombocytopenia and anemia (WBC: 7.6 × 103/mm3, Hgb: 12.7 g/dL, PLT: 123 × 103/mm3). Peripheral blood differential at relapse revealed: neutrophils 34%, lymphocytes 42%, monocytes 6%, and myeloblasts 18%, with no severe neutropenia. Of note, at the time of initial diagnosis the peripheral blood differential had shown 52% circulating blasts. The presence of 18% circulating blasts at relapse rendered pulmonary leukemic infiltration a plausible diagnostic consideration alongside infectious etiology. A repeat bone marrow examination confirmed relapse with 60% myeloblasts (Figure 2). Concurrently, he developed respiratory symptoms including a nonproductive cough with hypoxia. Vital signs at presentation were: temperature 38.2°C, respiratory rate 22 breaths/min, blood pressure 118/74 mmHg, heart rate 98 bpm, and oxygen saturation 88% on room air. The combination of low‐grade fever, tachypnea, and hypoxia in a patient with prior cytotoxic chemotherapy exposure prompted additional diagnostic evaluation. Chest CT imaging revealed new bilateral ground‐glass opacities predominantly in the lower lobes, multiple air cysts (measuring up to 9 mm in diameter), a mosaic attenuation pattern with interlobular septal thickening in the right lung, and enlarged mediastinal lymph nodes (the largest measuring 11 mm in short‐axis diameter (SAD)) (Figure 3). Empirical antibiotics (vancomycin and meropenem) were initiated on Day 1 of admission for suspected bacterial pneumonia or sepsis. Vancomycin was selected to cover methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and other gram‐positive organisms given the patient's healthcare‐associated risk profile, while meropenem provided broad gram‐negative coverage including Pseudomonas aeruginosa , a relevant pathogen in immunocompromised hosts following cytotoxic chemotherapy. Over the subsequent 4 days (Days 2–4), there was no clinical improvement in respiratory symptoms and no radiographic improvement. Two sets of blood cultures (aerobic and anaerobic) were drawn at admission and returned no growth. Nasopharyngeal RT‐PCR for SARS‐CoV‐2 was negative. Evaluation for tuberculosis included three sputum AFB smears, Xpert MTB/RIF assay, and an interferon‐gamma release assay (IGRA), all of which were negative. Serum HSV‐1/2 IgM was negative. Both serum CMV IgG/IgM serology and quantitative plasma CMV DNA PCR were negative, and BAL fluid was also negative for CMV PCR. Serum Toxoplasma gondii IgG/IgM serology, included due to prior corticosteroid exposure, was negative. Sputum Gram stain and sputum Giemsa cytology were not performed, as the clinical decision was made early to proceed directly to bronchoscopy with BAL and TBLB as the primary diagnostic modality to evaluate the pulmonary infiltrates in the context of confirmed AML relapse. Bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was performed on Day 5 to investigate potential pulmonary complications. Cultures from the BAL showed no growth of bacterial or fungal microorganisms. However, Histopathology and immunohistochemical analysis of the transbronchial lung biopsy (TBLB) specimen on Day 8 following admission revealed myeloid blasts within alveolar spaces, confirming leukemic pulmonary infiltration (Figures 4 and 5). Repeat flow cytometric immunophenotyping, cytogenetic analysis, and extended molecular testing were not performed at relapse, which represents a clinical limitation of this case.

Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

The patient was started on salvage chemotherapy on Day 9 of admission with the 5 + 5 regimen consisting of mitoxantrone 10 mg/m2/day and etoposide 100 mg/m2/day, both administered intravenously for five consecutive days. The patient's oxygen requirements progressively decreased between Days 10 and 14. By Day 14, the patient was maintaining an oxygen saturation greater than 95% on a 1 L/min nasal cannula. At the Day 28 assessment, repeat chest CT imaging demonstrated complete radiographic resolution of the bilateral ground‐glass opacities. On Day 28, the regimen evaluation showed an excellent response to therapy, with resolution of pulmonary infiltrates on repeat imaging (Figure 3) and bone marrow biopsy demonstrating < 1% blasts, indicating a second CR. Given the relapsed nature of his disease and the presence of extramedullary involvement, the patient was deemed an appropriate candidate for allogeneic hematopoietic cell transplantation. However, the transplant process was delayed due to challenges in securing a suitable donor. As interim therapy, he received one additional cycle of high‐dose cytarabine at the previously described dose. Ultimately, the patient successfully underwent a haploidentical hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) from his brother, which was conducted following confirmatory flow cytometry cross‐matching to ensure compatibility.

Discussion

Pulmonary leukemic infiltration (PLI) is considered one of the rare clinical complications of AML. Mostly diagnosed post‐mortem through autopsy, this condition requires a high index of suspicion and prompt management as its course tends to be deteriorating and rapidly fatal [4].

The pathophysiology of PLI remains incompletely understood, with multiple mechanisms proposed to explain its development [5]. One hypothesis suggests that PLI arises from pulmonary leukostasis, driven by the accumulation of leukemic blasts in peripheral blood. These blasts, characterized by greater rigidity and reduced deformability compared to normal leukocytes, obstruct capillary‐sized vascular channels, forming leukocyte aggregates and thrombi. The resultant vascular distension and hypoxia precipitate endothelial damage and vessel rupture, contributing to pulmonary compromise [6]. The development of PLI at relapse, despite a lower peripheral blast count than at initial diagnosis, may be explained by tumor cell tropism. We hypothesize that phenotypic changes, such as the de novo expression of adhesion molecules like CD56 (NCAM) at relapse, could mediate extramedullary organ infiltration. Clinicians evaluating similar patients are encouraged to obtain repeat immunophenotyping, including CD56, at relapse. This site‐specific targeting may explain PLI in the absence of hyperleukocytosis, highlighting a blast count‐independent mechanism [7, 8]. Recent insights from Iizuka et al. further elucidate PLI's pathogenesis, describing two distinct pathways: systemic blast overload in hyperleukocytic states and direct tissue invasion by blasts crossing alveolar barriers, as evidenced by autopsy findings of blast clusters within alveolar spaces [5] These interdependent pathogenic mechanisms reflect the multifaceted nature of pulmonary leukemic infiltration, necessitating refined diagnostic strategies.

The risk factors predisposing to PLI remain poorly defined due to its low incidence, complicating efforts to predict its occurrence. Broadly, pulmonary infiltrates in AML, including PLI, have been associated with hyperleukocytosis, older age, preexisting respiratory disease, extramedullary involvement beyond the lungs, and smoking [9]. The absence of traditional risk factors in this case implies the existence of noncanonical pathways in PLI pathogenesis, warranting clinical suspicion even in atypical presentations.

Diagnosing PLI presents significant challenges due to its broad differential, encompassing infectious etiologies, pulmonary edema, and other leukemic complications. In the absence of hyperleukocytosis, PLI often manifests asymptomatically or with subtle symptoms, as seen in our patient, who presented with cough and mild hypoxemia [10]. In retrospect, several features of our patient's presentation were atypical for bacterial pneumonia and may serve as clinical clues for suspecting PLI: the non‐productive cough, low‐grade fever, bilateral ground‐glass opacities without lobar consolidation, temporal concurrence with AML relapse, absence of response to broad‐spectrum antibiotics, and low blast‐to‐infiltration discordance. PLI should be considered proactively in any AML patient presenting with pulmonary infiltrates at or around the time of relapse, without waiting for antibiotic failure as the trigger for suspicion. Furthermore, it is important to note that meropenem and vancomycin do not cover atypical pathogens; therefore, non‐response to this regimen cannot completely exclude all infectious etiologies. Chest computed tomography (CT) plays a pivotal role in evaluation, revealing a spectrum of findings, including ground‐glass opacities, air‐space consolidation, interlobular septal thickening, and broncho vascular bundle thickening [11]. Heyneman et al. identified interlobular septal thickening and broncho vascular bundle thickening as predominant features in PLI [12], although a larger study by Yoshifuji et al. reported these findings in only 44% of cases, proposing four distinct CT patterns: type A (discrete nodules or masses), type B (edema‐like opacities without nodules), type C (a combination of types A and B), and type D (pleural thickening without parenchymal abnormalities) [13]. Our patient's imaging aligned with type B, characterized by edema‐like opacities, consistent with leukemic infiltration. Heterogeneity in imaging features highlights the imperative to integrate radiologic, clinical, and histologic data for accurate diagnosis.

Although regarded as invasive diagnostic tools with complications, bronchoalveolar lavage and TBLB are commonly suggested for diagnosis of pulmonary complications in AML [14]. In our case, BAL with immunohistochemical analysis of TBLB revealed myeloid blasts within alveolar spaces, establishing the diagnosis. Emerging studies have recommended noninvasive tests for early detection of PLI, hopefully resulting in rapid diagnosis and mortality reduction. Giemsa‐stained sputum and next generation sequencing (NGS) from formalin‐fixed‐paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) specimens are currently studied methods [15, 16]. Such advances are critical, as PLI carries a poor prognosis, with a median overall survival of < 1 year, as highlighted by Yoshifuji et al. The clinical course of PLI, particularly in hyperleukocytic patients, is often marked by rapid deterioration, with respiratory failure manifesting as hypoxemia, fever, and dyspnea, accompanied by radiographic evidence of diffuse infiltrates and pleural effusions [3].

Treatment strategies remain poorly standardized due to the condition's rarity and diagnostic delays. Reported approaches include chemotherapy, mechanical ventilation, lung irradiation, and cytoreductive agents like hydroxyurea, which has shown efficacy in rapidly reducing blast counts in Hyper leukocytic PLI [2, 14, 17, 18] In our patient, salvage chemotherapy with a 5 + 5 regimen resulted in significant alleviation of respiratory symptoms, underscoring the potential for tailored chemotherapeutic interventions to alter the disease course. These findings highlight the urgency of early recognition and the need for prospective studies to establish evidence‐based therapeutic protocols.

Conclusion

In conclusion, PLI represents a complex and life‐threatening complication of AML, characterized by diverse pathophysiological mechanisms, nonspecific clinical and radiographic presentations, and diagnostic challenges. Our case illustrates the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion, leveraging advanced diagnostics, and pursuing aggressive treatment to improve outcomes in this rare entity. Further research is imperative to elucidate risk factors, refine diagnostic tools, and optimize management strategies for PLI, ultimately enhancing survival in affected patients.

The primary take‐away lessons from this case are as follows:

Clinical vigilance: PLI should be considered proactively in AML patients with pulmonary infiltrates at or around the time of relapse, based on an integrated assessment of leukemic disease activity, peripheral blast count, CT pattern, microbiological findings, and temporal relationship to relapse
