Evaluation of dental students’ tooth preparation skills using conventional phantom labs versus advanced simulation technologies: a comparative study
Authors: Burcu Kızılırmak, Mehmet Gözen, Neslihan Güntekin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreclinical training is essential in dental education for developing psychomotor and clinical skills. Conventional phantom models are widely used, though they lack real-time tactile feedback. This study aimed to compare the effectiveness of conventional phantom training with that of advanced simulation-based systems in improving dental students’ tooth preparation skills. MethodsThis randomized controlled trial included 40 s-year dental students (N = 40) with no prior experience in simulation. Participants were randomly assigned to either conventional phantom training or virtual reality (VR) based simulation. Both groups received 10 h of training. Pre- and post-tests involved preparing tooth 46 with a chamfer finish line. Performances were assessed using five criteria: parallelism, occlusal reduction, taper angle, finish line standardization, and presence of undercuts. Each was scored on a 0–10 scale by a blinded evaluator. Statistical analyses were performed using ANOVA with post hoc tests at a significance level of p < .05.ResultsBoth groups showed improvement in post-test scores The simulation group’s post-test performance was found to be significantly higher than that of the other groups. Post hoc analysis confirmed that the simulation group outperformed both its own pre-test and the conventional group’s post-test results.ConclusionsSimulation-based training led to significantly higher outcomes in tooth preparation compared to conventional methods. The integration of digital simulation into dental education may enhance clinical readiness by providing individualized, repeatable, and measurable learning environments.Keywords: Dental education, Haptic feedback, Simulation, Tooth preparation, Virtual reality

Background

One of the primary objectives of dental schools is to provide adequate education to their students and to graduate competent and well equipped professionals. This dental education comprises theoretical coursework and preclinical training [1, 2]. Preclinical training, which plays a key role in supporting students’ development of skills and knowledge throughout their professional careers, enables the acquisition of various psychomotor abilities. A substantial portion of the dental curriculum is dedicated to the enhancement of these motor skills [3, 4]. In conventional preclinical education, students typically practice on phantom models that replicate the human oral anatomy. These physical simulators provide a realistic environment that includes teeth, gingival tissue, and jaw structures, allowing students to safely develop their clinical skills. By performing various treatment procedures on these models, students gain experience in hand–eye coordination, motor skills, and fundamental anatomical knowledge [5, 6].

Although phantom models, commonly employed in preclinical dental education, contribute significantly to the development of psychomotor skills, they lack the tactile feedback characteristic of natural dental tissues and do not fully replicate clinical conditions. The use of extracted human teeth in training is limited by ethical concerns and challenges related to procurement, and their morphological variability complicates standardization in evaluations. While conventional phantom heads are devoid of haptic feedback, advanced digital dental simulators can deliver real time tactile sensations. Haptic dental simulators, designed to address these limitations, provide a more immersive and realistic educational environment by integrating visual, auditory, and haptic feedback within virtual and augmented reality-based platforms. These technologies not only enhance students’ clinical proficiency but also enable objective assessment of performance, establishing themselves as a valuable adjunct to conventional teaching methodologies [3, 4, 7].

Haptic technologies aim to facilitate interaction with the environment through tactile and proprioceptive perception, thereby enhancing realism in healthcare education. When integrated with VR, these systems offer dental students a safe and repeatable platform for clinical training. Haptic simulators provide three-dimensional representations of teeth and dental instruments, and voxel-based structures enable the delivery of force feedback specific to various tissue types. These systems allow students to realistically experience complex procedures such as tooth preparation [4, 8].

Furthermore, when used in combination with phantom heads, haptic simulators support the development of fine motor skills and enhance students’ clinical self-confidence. This technology enables students to acquire sufficient hands-on experience before treating real patients and holds great potential for improving the quality of dental education. Therefore, the widespread implementation of dental simulators incorporating virtual reality and haptic feedback is essential for professional development and the provision of safe and effective patient care [6, 8, 9]. In addition, VR technology provides dental students with opportunities for self-assessment and independent learning, thereby reducing reliance on instructor-led feedback and offering unlimited practice potential. It enables students to perform procedures in a low-risk environment while simultaneously enhancing the overall quality of dental education [10]. Digital simulation technologies in dental education have evolved across a wide range, from desktop computer-based applications to haptic systems, digitally developed phantom heads, and finally to dental simulators based on virtual reality (VR), augmented reality (AR), and mixed reality (MR). These systems, which are generally referred to as “VR-simulation” in the literature, have evolved significantly over time and can now be defined and classified. All these advanced technological developments are referred to as advanced simulation [11, 12].

The aim of this study is to evaluate the effectiveness of conventional phantom laboratories and simulation-based education in developing dental students’ tooth preparation skills, and to assess students’ performance in different educational processes based on specific criteria. The research hypothesis is that advanced simulation applications will significantly enhance dental students’ preparation competence compared to conventional phantom-based training.”

Methods

This clinical investigation was conducted at the Department of Prosthodontics, Faculty of Dentistry, Necmettin Erbakan University. Ethical approval was obtained from the Dentistry Drug and Non-Medical Device Research Ethics Committee of Necmettin Erbakan University. The required sample size for the study was calculated using G*Power software (version 3.1, Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany). The analysis was performed in accordance with a three-way ANOVA design. The effect size (f) was set at 0.25, with a significance level (α) of 0.05 and a statistical power (1–β) of 0.95.

This study was designed as a randomized, controlled, experimental, and prospective investigation. A total of 40 s-year dental students (N = 40) from the Faculty of Dentistry at Necmettin Erbakan University, who had no prior experience with dental simulators, voluntarily participated in the study. Students who had previously received simulation training or participated in similar studies were excluded.

Participants were randomly assigned into two groups (n = 20) using a computer generated random number sequence. The first group received training in a conventional phantom laboratory, while the second group was trained in a VR-based simulation laboratory. For the simulation applications, the SIMtoCARE Dente® system (SIMtoCARE B.V., Vreeland, The Netherlands) was used. This device offers real time force feedback and 3D visualization, and is equipped with sensors designed to assess manual dexterity. In addition, the tooth preparation modules of this simulator allow students to compare their preparations with predefined ideal models, providing color-coded visual feedback and enabling standardized assessment of parameters such as taper, occlusal reduction, and finish line standardization.

The test before the training was called the stage pre-test, and the test after the training was called the stage post-test. Prior to the training, both groups underwent a pre-test to assess baseline skill levels. In this test, students were instructed to perform a tooth preparation with a chamfer finish line on tooth 46. All preparations were anonymized by assigning identification codes to ensure participant confidentiality, and recordings were reviewed anonymously during the assessment process. Evaluations were conducted by an experienced moderator using a structured and standardized scoring form. The evaluation was conducted by an experienced moderator using a structured and standardized scoring form. The assessment was based on five main criteria:

Parallelism (P), amount of occlusal reduction (O), taper (T), finish line standardization (F), presence of undercut areas (U). Each criterion was scored on a scale of 0 to 10 points.

The training process was conducted simultaneously for both groups, and a total of 10 h of preclinical preparation training was provided in accordance with the defined curriculum, using either conventional methods or simulation-based systems. To ensure consistency, both the conventional and simulation-based training sessions were conducted by the same instructor. Following the training, participants were asked to perform a post-test by preparing tooth 46 once again. The evaluations were carried out by the same moderator, and to minimize evaluator fatigue, the assessments were scheduled on different days with a limited number of samples reviewed per session. The scores obtained for each criterion were subjected to statistical analysis.

Before performing statistical tests, the distribution characteristics of the data were examined. The assumption of normality was evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and the homogeneity of variances was assessed using Levene’s test. The results confirmed that the data were normally distributed and that the variances were homogeneous. Therefore, a Three-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used for intergroup comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), and the level of significance was set at p <.05.

Results

In this study, the effect of training provided in conventional phantom (C) and simulation (S) environments on dental students’ tooth preparation skills was evaluated by comparing pre-test and post-test results. The assessment was based on five key criteria: parallelism (P), occlusal reduction (O), taper angle (T), finish line standardization (F), and the presence of undercuts (U).

According to the results of the Three-Way ANOVA (Table 1), there were statistically significant main effects for the training method (Methods) and stage (Stage) (p <.001). In addition, the interaction effect between method and stage (Methods × Stage) was also statistically significant (p <.001). However, the differences among the evaluation criteria (Criteria), as well as their interactions with either method or stage, were not statistically significant (p >.05).

Discussion

In this study, the effect of simulation-based training on dental students tooth preparation skills was investigated. Based on the findings, the research hypothesis was accepted. The post-test performance of students trained with simulation was found to be statistically significantly higher than both the performance of those trained with conventional methods and their own baseline scores (p <.001).

In line with the demands of the digital age, significant changes are taking place in dental education. In particular, dental simulation and haptic technologies enable students to experience clinical scenarios in a risk-free environment during preclinical training. These systems contribute to the development of students’ procedural decision-making skills, enhance their technical competence, and better prepare them for irreversible clinical procedures. Considering the challenges associated with the transition from preclinical to clinical practice, haptic simulators offer valuable support, particularly in operative dentistry where millimetric precision and manual dexterity are essential. The importance of these technologies in dental education is steadily increasing. This shift underscores the growing need to integrate digital teaching and assessment approaches into dental curricula [4, 13–15]. According to the findings of our study (Table 3), the difference between simulation and conventional education was statistically significant (p <.001). This finding suggests that simulation-based education can provide a more effective learning experience compared to conventional methods. These results suggest that simulation-based training, particularly in the post-test phase, may enhance student performance more effectively than conventional methods. Previous studies have also reported that the use of dental simulation devices not only improves motor skills but also leads to significant increases in performance levels [2, 4, 16]. In their study, Mirghani et al. reported that dental simulation devices can be used to assess student performance and that training can be tailored based on the individual student’s level of experience [16]. Consistent with the literature, the findings of this study indicate that dental students trained with virtual reality simulators demonstrated superior practical skills in tooth preparation compared to those trained exclusively with conventional methods [10, 13]. There are those who support the integration of simulation training into conventional education, as well as those who argue otherwise. According to this view, they reported that simulation education does not make a difference compared to conventional education and can not replace conventional education [17, 18]. However, some studies have reported that students initially experience greater difficulty in simulation environments and feel more comfortable with conventional methods. This may be attributed to variations in the time required for students to adapt to digital systems. These findings suggest that a student-centered and gradual approach may be more effective than abrupt transitions in the integration of digital technologies into dental education. In this context, modular training structures have been shown to be more beneficial and feasible [19, 20]. The fact that post-training simulation performance was significantly higher than that of the conventional phantom method further supports this conclusion. In addition to the technical advantages of simulation systems, they may have increased student motivation and participation, contributing to the higher performance observed in the simulation-based education group [19].

For a successful and long-lasting restoration, adequate retention and resistance must be achieved, which requires an appropriate convergence angle. A taper angle between 2° and 6° is generally recommended. However, studies have reported that dental students often fail to achieve this ideal range during tooth preparation [21]. The post-test scores of the simulation group were also found to be significantly higher than both their own pre-test scores and the post-test scores of the conventional group (Table 3). These findings show that digital feedback systems increase accuracy not only in a single parameter of tooth preparation but in many dimensions. Similarly, Hattori et al. [22] reported that haptic VR simulators increase accuracy and consistency in tooth preparation tasks. Previous studies have reported that taper angles obtained using conventional simulators tend to be larger than those of reference models, whereas smaller angles have been observed in haptic simulators. This difference is thought to be related to the rotational control offered by haptic simulators, which, unlike standard phantom jaws, allow users to more easily manage their visual field in the buccolingual direction [22–25]. The higher scores for taper angle and finish line standardization observed in the simulation group may be attributed to the enhanced ability to perceive and control depth, made possible by the rotational functionality of the device.

Real-time feedback provided by simulation systems has the potential to accelerate the learning curve. In contrast, the manual control required in phantom laboratories may reduce the level of error awareness among students. This may be explained by the fact that simulation systems allow immediate recognition and correction of mistakes through objective, real-time feedback [6, 9]. The high performance in the simulation-based training group may have been contributed to by improvements in visual tracking skills and strengthening of muscle memory; this is consistent with studies reporting that hand-tool alignment in VR simulators can facilitate learning and that haptic VR can support psychomotor skills through repetitive practice [26, 27] On the other hand, in conventional phantom laboratory settings, such individualized and objective feedback is limited, which can result in a longer learning process and increased dependence on the instructor. The results of this study indicate that simulation-assisted training contributes more substantially to the development of clinical skills in dental students compared to conventional methods. While conventional training provides a basic foundation, simulation technologies offer a safe, repeatable, and measurable learning environment that enhances both the efficiency and effectiveness of the educational experience [7, 17]. Although simulation has shown superior results, there are some technical limitations. The system cannot fully reflect clinical variables such as patient movement or soft tissue resistance, and the tactile feedback provided is more limited than that of natural tooth tissue [11]. These limitations may explain the differences between simulation and clinical performance and indicate that simulation should be complemented by patient-based training. Within this context, the integration of digital simulation technologies into dental education programs represents a valuable opportunity to improve the quality and effectiveness of contemporary dental training.

The limitations of this study include the involvement of a specific group of students and a focus on short-term learning outcomes, which may restrict the generalizability of the results. Additionally, the technical limitations of the simulation system used should be taken into consideration. A further limitation is that the simulator provided real-time visual feedback, which may have facilitated faster error correction and contributed to the superior performance of the simulation-based training group. Future studies should be conducted across different educational levels, with larger and more diverse samples, and should include multicenter research designs aimed at evaluating the long-term effects of simulation-based training.

Conclusion

Simulation-based training significantly improved the tooth preparation skills of dental students. In the post-test evaluations, students in the simulation group consistently achieved higher scores across all assessed criteria. Notably, digital simulation proved to be particularly more effective in clinically critical parameters such as taper angle and finish line standardization. These results emphasize the importance of incorporating simulation technologies into conventional dental education, as they facilitate a more individualized and effective learning process.
