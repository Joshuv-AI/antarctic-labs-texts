The Role of Machine Perfusion for Liver Transplantation in Children
Authors: Sapana Verma, Maria Baimas George, George Mazariegos, Dionisios Vrochides, Andrea Schlegel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPediatric liver transplantation (PLT) is the only lifesaving procedure for children with advanced liver disease and tumors. Despite significant progress and overall success in PLT, the field continues to face challenges due to procedural complexity across a heterogeneous age group, especially in infants and small children. Despite the use of living donor grafts, waitlist mortality remains disproportionately high in these younger patients. The unique challenges in PLT—including limited donor availability and higher risk of complications—underscore the need for improved strategies to evaluate and optimize donor liver quality before implantation. Machine perfusion has emerged as a promising tool in this context. While the benefits of hypothermic oxygenated perfusion (HOPE) for whole and partial livers have been reported with applications during and after liver split, the evidence for normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) remains scarce. Since the first report in 2019, multiple case series and clinical studies support the efficacy of HOPE in reducing early graft dysfunction, biliary complications, and graft loss after liver transplantation in children. Despite this progress, there is a pressing need for coordinated research efforts to study how HOPE and NMP affect transplant outcomes in pediatric recipients, especially when expanding split criteria or using DCD grafts, which are vital for expanding the donor pool despite the known higher risk.Keywords: ex‐situ split, hypothermic oxygenated perfusion, pediatric liver transplantation

Introduction

Pediatric Liver transplantation (PLT) is considered a life‐saving treatment for children with end‐stage liver disease and unresectable hepatic tumors [1]. However, waitlist mortality remains high due to the shortage of appropriately sized grafts—particularly for small children—combined with the vulnerability of pediatric recipients and the underutilization of the technical variant allograft [2]. According to OPTN/SRTR annual reports, the number of pediatric liver transplant candidates listed and transplanted increased compared to the previous years, accompanied by a higher number of deceased and living donor liver transplantations (DDLT and LDLT) in 2023 [3]. While pretransplant mortality has declined following policy revisions prioritizing pediatric donors for pediatric recipients, 48 children still either died or were delisted due to medical deterioration that year. The overall pretransplant mortality rate for pediatric candidates in 2023 was 6.7 deaths per 100 patient‐years, which has increased from a nadir of 4.9 deaths per 100 patient‐years in 2020 [3, 4], Posttransplant mortality remained another concern, with cumulative death rates of 6.0% at 1 year, 8.2% at 3 years, 9.8% at 5 years, and 13.9% at 10 years. Similar to outcomes observed in adults, pediatric recipients of living donor grafts demonstrated superior 5‐year survival compared to those receiving deceased donor organs [3]. In addition to regulatory considerations and institutional experience, a range of social and religious factors impact the available graft types for pediatric recipients. To accommodate children of varying ages and body weights, several surgical techniques have been developed to adjust graft size, including split liver procedures, LDLT, and graft reductions including monosegments [5]. However, the discrepancy in demand and availability of size‐matched donors in the pediatric population (< 1 year and 1–5 years) in the United States continues to increase every year.

Rodriguez‐Davalos et al. recently presented a global survey on current practices in pediatric liver transplantation (PLT), which included responses from 108 pediatric transplant centers across 38 countries. Collectively, these centers performed 10 619 PLTs between 2014 and 2018, with a median of 50 transplants per center annually (range: 1–1078). The highest volumes were reported from Asia, with the largest single‐center activity found in China. Living donor liver transplantation (LDLT) remains the primary source of grafts for children in many parts of the world, especially in Asia, the Middle East, and Africa, where cultural and religious factors limit the availability of deceased donors. Countries like Japan, South Korea, India, and China have established LDLT as a standard practice, with excellent outcomes due to meticulous patient selection, thorough preoperative assessment, skilled anesthetic care, experienced surgical teams, and timely identification and management of postoperative complications [6, 7, 8]. In contrast, LDLT accounted for only 15.4% of pediatric liver transplants in the United States in 2021. In the Australia–Oceania region, centers more commonly rely on deceased donor split or reduced grafts rather than living donors. The landscape in Europe has also evolved over time. Prior to 2000, full‐size liver grafts were predominant, but the use of split grafts has steadily increased, rising from 10% in 2000 to 34% in 2009. A recent ELITA‐ESPGHAN report by Junge et al. found that 45% of deceased donor split grafts (32.8% of PLT) were used for transplantation in 2022 [9]. Split liver transplantation (SLT) involves a transection of a whole deceased donor liver into a left lateral segment (LLS), which is transplanted into a child, and the remaining extended right graft used for an adult or a larger child/adolescent recipient [10].

The graft selection for PLT is based on recipient body weight and includes the following graft types: a reduced left lateral segment (LLS) for children weighing less than 5 kg (including monosegments), standard LLS for those between 5 and 25 kg, the left lobe (segments II–IV including the middle hepatic vein) for 25 to 50 kg, and the right lobe (segments V–VIII without the middle hepatic vein) for recipients with 50 kg or more. Additionally, children, especially adolescents, may require a higher graft‐to‐recipient weight ratio (GRWR) than adults to avoid small‐for‐size syndrome [11].

As the liver possesses the unique ability to regenerate within a short period, deceased donor split liver transplantation (DD‐SLT) represents another excellent opportunity to expand the donor pool. Although DD‐SLT offers more liver volume, usually with a single bile duct and longer hepatic artery, the graft is often procured under less ideal conditions [12, 13]. Despite advancements in surgical techniques and improvements in medical and anesthetic management, strict acceptance criteria, commonly referred to as split policies, are still enforced globally to mitigate donor‐related risk factors. Despite the liver's ability to regenerate, only about 1% of deceased donor livers in the United States are split for transplantation. Traditionally, donor criteria for “splitable” livers included: donor age ≤ 40 years, transaminases < 3× normal, and minimal vasopressor support. However, these criteria vary internationally, incorporating additional factors such as donor body weight (e.g., > 40 kg in Italy, > 50 kg in the UK), donor BMI thresholds (< 28 or < 30 kg/m2), serum sodium levels (< 160 mmol/L), donor length of hospital stay (≤ 5 days), and cold ischemia time (CIT), particularly for urgent cases or recipients weighing < 6 kg [14, 15]. To enhance the availability of appropriate organs for PLT, split criteria have been iteratively reviewed and broadened internationally. The Italian approach is particularly notable. Brain‐dead donors aged 18 to 50 years, free from transmissible infections or malignancies, are prioritized for PLT unless there is a UNOS status 1 patient or an adult with advanced liver disease (laboratory MELD score ≥ 30 points) on the waiting list. The introduction of this mandatory Italian split liver policy led to a substantial decrease in waiting list duration—from 229 to 80 days—and to a reduction in waiting list mortality from 4.5% to 2.5% in children, while maintaining similar posttransplant outcomes compared to the period before the policy was implemented [14, 16].

Dynamic organ preservation techniques have gained considerable interest in expanding the donor pool by improving graft quality, reducing CIT, and reducing clinically relevant complications. An additional advantage is the potential to use viability assessments during both ex‐situ and in situ perfusion approaches to better predict post‐transplant outcomes. These dynamic preservation techniques, which are routinely implemented in adult liver transplantation, are somewhat lagging in the pediatric field. Three main techniques are currently applied. In situ normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) and ex‐situ normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) deliver oxygenated blood at body temperature. NRP is specifically used in donors after circulatory death (DCD), allowing organ evaluation by recirculating the donor's blood within the abdominal cavity [17]. Hypothermic oxygenated perfusion (HOPE) has been extensively studied in adult liver transplantation, with 9 RCTs demonstrating improved clinical outcomes with DCD and DBD livers [18, 19]. A pioneering case report by Werner et al. in 2019 introduced machine perfusion in PLT, supporting the feasibility and safety of HOPE in pediatric donation after cardiac death (DCD) liver grafts [20]. Based on the evidence for HOPE from adult LT, an increasing number of case series and smaller studies have been reported with HOPE in all types of grafts for PLT. Of particular relevance is this dynamic preservation approach for DD‐Split grafts to reduce the impact of cold storage and logistic challenges. In general, reports of the use of NMP for split grafts remain scarce (Figure 1). This review summarizes the current literature on machine perfusion in pediatric liver transplantation, highlighting underlying mechanisms of injury and protection, technical aspects, logistics, and future challenges.

Types of SLT

Ex‐Situ and In Situ Split

SLT was the pinnacle of the stepwise progression of reduced liver transplantation and was first attempted in 1988. In deceased donor liver transplantation, in situ splitting involves dividing the liver within the donor before procurement, maintaining perfusion and minimizing cold ischemia. It offers better graft quality but requires experienced surgeons at the donor hospital. Ex‐situ splitting, done after procurement of the whole liver graft and transport at the transplant center, is logistically easier but may result in longer cold ischemia. Both techniques require surgical experience, and practice depends on the center policy and surgeons' experiences [21, 22, 23]. Due to prolonged ischemia and increased ischemia‐reperfusion injury (IRI) in very early reports of ex‐situ SLT, criteria for in situ SLT were proposed showing advantages over ex‐situ techniques [24]. Another early series showed slightly inferior overall patient and graft survival (74% vs. 96%, 61% vs. 81%) in ex‐situ SLT compared to in vivo SLT, and a similar trend was observed in the pediatric population (64% vs. 100%) [25]. However, with advanced experience and improved surgical techniques, a decade follow‐up report from UCSF showed excellent graft and patient survival with both splitting techniques [26]. One of the first reports from South Asia showed similar outcomes between both SLT techniques compared with LDLT, advocating for policy changes to facilitate organ sharing with more frequent use of SLT to reduce pediatric wait‐list mortality [27]. Although numerous reports have shown that in situ split techniques have successfully expanded the donor pool for pediatric recipients, these procedures do not eliminate IRI‐related complications and graft loss, particularly in a transplant world where the overall donor risk increases with older ages, more steatosis, and other metabolic risk factors allocating split grafts to the high‐risk group. Cold ischemia time (CIT) remains a critical factor even for in situ split procedures, and prolonged CIT has been linked to increased rates of primary nonfunction and hepatic artery thrombosis in pediatric recipients [25, 26, 28]. The current situation underscores the need for additional protective strategies such as machine perfusion [28]. During organ procurement, a lack of oxygen (hypoxia) disrupts mitochondrial function and ATP production. Even after the initial cold flush, metabolic activity like the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle continues, leading to further energy loss and metabolite accumulation until the graft is fully cooled. Prolonged cold ischemia can cause respiratory chain dys‐ and non‐function, with subsequent lack of energy, cell swelling, and activation of IRI‐injury pathways with apoptosis and necrosis and proinflammatory mediator release with recipient immune system activation [29, 30, 31].

A key mechanism during ischemia is the accumulation of toxic metabolites like succinate, especially in livers from metabolically compromised donors. Upon initial reperfusion, accumulated succinate drives uncoordinated electron flow at mitochondrial complex I, generating a burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS). This triggers the release of pro‐inflammatory signals (DAMPs, mitochondrial DNA, cytokines), initiating a cascade involving hepatocytes, endothelial cells, macrophages, and cholangiocytes. Toll‐like receptor activation and complement proteins expression further amplify sterile inflammation, neutrophil recruitment, and tissue injury. This process increases graft stiffness and hypoperfusion and later cell death given larger proportions of cells are very damaged. Targeting mitochondria is therefore essential in organ preservation to minimize IRI, sustain microvascular integrity, and prevent clinically relevant complications [31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 37, 38, 39]. Due to the cumulative effects of IRI‐induced metabolic stress including succinate accumulation, ROS generation, and sterile inflammation even when using in‐ or ex‐situ split grafts from deceased donors, there remains a significant risk of graft dysfunction or failure, particularly in marginal donors outside traditional split criteria.

The Role of Machine Perfusion

Hypothermic Oxygenated Perfusion

Hypothermic machine perfusion was introduced first in clinical practice in 2004 with the first clinical case series in adult LT by Guarrera et al. [40]. The HOPE concept was then promoted during the past decade and is usually performed after standard organ procurement, cold flush, and liver transport on ice at 8°C–12°C using a highly oxygenated (pO2 60–100 kPa) artificial perfusion solution, typically Belzer MPS. Initial studies applied HOPE for a median duration of 2 h using two main approaches: portal vein (PV) perfusion at 3–5 mmHg pressure, or dual HOPE (D‐HOPE), which involves simultaneous PV and hepatic artery (HA) perfusion, with HA pressures of 25 mmHg. HOPE is now supported by 9 RCTs, over 15 systematic reviews, and real‐world data from more than 1200 transplants in Europe. Studies consistently show that HOPE reduces early allograft dysfunction (EAD), reduces biliary and overall complications, shortens hospital stay, and improves graft survival [41, 42, 43]. HOPE works by minimizing IRI and the associated immune activation, preserving mitochondrial function and endothelial integrity [44]. This technique also improves liver perfusion quality and reduces stiffness, which is especially beneficial for small grafts and vessels, enhancing suitability in marginal and pediatric settings [45].

In 2019, the first successful transplantation from a pediatric DCD liver graft after ex‐situ HOPE was reported [20]. This waved a path for the use of HOPE for different pediatric graft types from DBD and DCD donors (Table 1, Figure 1). The first case series with HOPE showed reduced post‐transplant complications and better graft survival with perfusion [46]. Similarly, significantly lower rates of moderate to severe IRI were observed (grade ≥ 2), with fewer infiltrating neutrophils and absence of post‐reperfusion syndrome in HOPE‐perfused livers. The degree of IRI was comparable between HOPE‐treated split grafts (from deceased donors) and grafts from living donors [47]. The result of the most recent retrospective study compared HOPE SLT to static cold storage SLT and LDLT delineated that HOPE attenuated the risk of early IRI in pediatric recipients compared to cold storage SLT, thus proceeding towards results seen with LDLT [47]. Portal vein HOPE is of particular benefit due to its easy application and the untouched hepatic artery. With current devices, perfusion during ex‐situ split is easy and reliable. Figure 2 shows an example of a standard split of extended RL and LLS as routinely done for PLT. During HOPE, the mitochondrial metabolism behaves differently because oxygen is reintroduced under cold conditions, where mitochondria function more efficiently. Experimental studies across species have shown reduced tissue succinate levels and minimal ROS generation when reperfusion occurs at temperatures below 15°C. Under hypothermic conditions, mitochondrial complexes resume coordinated function, enabling directed electron flow and ATP reloading (Figure 3). These effects are enhanced by high oxygen concentrations in the perfusate during HOPE [29, 44, 48, 49, 50, 51]. Consequently, HOPE has been associated with improved graft survival and fewer complications in adults and now also in pediatric populations [44, 52, 53, 54]. Additionally, by dampening inflammation and the innate immune response, HOPE was reported to reduce the number and severity of acute cellular rejections [55, 56], an important benefit in PLT. The recent validated mitochondrial biomarker Flavin mononucleotide (FMN) quantified from HOPE perfusate could be beneficial to identify metabolically favorable livers outside current split criteria for children [57].

Normothermic Machine Perfusion

Normothermic machine perfusion (NMP) aims to provide a “near physiologic environment” with a perfusion at 37°C using a blood‐based perfusate [58, 59]. While this is of interest and provides an advantage over SCS to assess liver function and cope with logistics, NMP triggers the entire IRI injury cascade [60]. Previous RCTs have shown improved early post‐transplant outcomes, that is, significant reduction in peak AST, EAD rates, and post‐reperfusion syndrome in livers treated with NMP compared to SCS [61, 62, 63]. Despite several studies showing that NMP enables assessment and transplantation of organs that would have been discarded, end‐ischemic NMP did not reduce the rate of non‐anastomotic biliary strictures (NAS) in high‐risk DCD organs [64, 65].

Comparative studies between upfront and endischemic NMP, including grafts from risk‐matched donors, will help to understand the impact of SCS duration prior to NMP. Organs that are split ex situ under static cold storage benefit from HOPE during or after split, which has been shown to reduce cold ischemia‐related injury and improve early graft outcomes [46, 47, 66]. Grafts split in situ at the donor center might be suited to continue with NMP, allowing continuous perfusion with only short cold flush and storage without prolonged cold ischemia time associated with back‐to‐base logistics [67, 68, 69]. Despite the increasing application of NMP in adult LT, particularly in the United States, the reported experience with the use of NMP for whole and partial grafts for children and splits remains scarce. Brockmann et al. reported that the successful liver split during NMP, with possible minimization of cold ischemia, enabled safe liver transport and preservation; however, without implantation [70]. Since then, NMP was tested by Gao et al., demonstrating the feasibility of ex‐situ split during NMP for clinical SLT [71]. Since then, several cases and case series have been done with reports awaited. Figure 2 shows a whole donor graft and an eRL split before NMP with subsequent cannulation and connection to the NMP‐device. Despite the benefit of a shorter cold ischemia time compared to cold storage SLT, NMP triggers the IRI cascade on the machine (Figure 3), and better viability tests are required, particularly during prolonged NMP. One of the latest series of 10 splits in discarded livers during NMP showed functional viability, bile production, lactate clearance, as well as synthesis of coagulation during a continued 24 h research NMP after split [67]. Similarly, the newly published preclinical study by Niu et al. evaluated the feasibility of using ex‐situ NMP for pediatric liver grafts, specifically left lateral segment grafts (LLSGs). The group found that over nearly 6 days of perfusion, LLSGs maintained stable hemodynamics, low endothelin‐1 levels, and showed no histological signs of portal hyperperfusion injury. These findings support the safety of NMP in LLGs and suggest starting to test NMP for clinical PLT [72]. Robust viability testing during NMP is, however, required to successfully increase the donor pool and expand split criteria with the use of machine perfusion [73]. The severity of graft injury, however, is influenced by the individual metabolic profile of each donor liver, which is shaped by donor risk factors, medical history, lifestyle, management, procurement techniques, and preservation methods, and is further exacerbated when prolonged cold ischemia precedes NMP (Figure 3) [45, 65, 74].

Normothermic Regional Perfusion

NRP has emerged as a valuable technique to assess liver grafts donated after circulatory death (DCD) [75]. By restoring oxygenated blood flow in situ following circulatory arrest, NRP reduces time pressures during procurement and enables functional assessment of the perfused compartment prior to procurement. While extensively studied in adult liver transplantation showing benefits with fewer biliary complications [76, 77], its application in pediatric DCD donors with subsequent transplantation in pediatric recipients (or even split) remains limited and incompletely characterized. Recent U.S. registry data show that among 629 pediatric DCD donors, only 3.2% underwent thoracoabdominal NRP (TA‐NRP), resulting in 13 liver transplants, all into adult recipients [78]. These NRP livers had high utilization rates and demonstrated comparable 180‐day graft survival, hospital length of stay, and early graft function relative to direct recovery with cold flush and storage of DCD grafts. Similarly, a multicenter Spanish study reported the successful recovery and transplantation of 11 livers from 13 pediatric DCD donors using NRP, with one‐year graft survival reaching 90.9%, including successful use in both adult and pediatric recipients (Figure 1, Table 1) [75].

Despite these encouraging outcomes, the adoption of NRP in pediatric liver transplantation faces significant challenges. Ethical concerns regarding death determination and cerebral perfusion exclusion remain central, particularly in TA‐NRP. In addition, many transplant centers lack the experience, resources, and infrastructure needed to safely and consistently perform NRP [79].

The limited data we currently have is, however, encouraging. In a national study using the UNOS registry, Zhou et al. found that TA‐NRP was employed in only 3.2% of pediatric DCD donors, yet every recovered liver was successfully transplanted, albeit exclusively into adult recipients. These grafts showed favorable short‐term outcomes, with reduced delayed graft function and discard rates compared to direct recovery DCD grafts. Similarly, the Spanish multicenter experience offers complementary insights [75] demonstrated that NRP in pediatric donors (ages 2–17) enabled the recovery of 11 livers without the use of additional ex‐situ machine perfusion, five of which were transplanted into pediatric recipients, achieving 1‐year graft survival rates exceeding 90%. These data support the feasibility and safety of NRP alone in selected pediatric liver DCD.

Despite promising early results, current data on pediatric donor liver procurement with NRP are limited by small sample sizes, retrospective designs, and variability in practice. Most NRP‐recovered pediatric grafts have been used in adult recipients, making it difficult to generalize outcomes to children, who have distinct immunologic and developmental needs [79]. Ethical concerns—especially around death determination and cerebral reperfusion—remain a major barrier to broader adoption, particularly in pediatric cases where transparency and public trust are critical [80, 81].

Benefit and Cost Barriers

Machine perfusion, particularly HOPE, has been demonstrated to improve outcomes and reduce complications in SLT for pediatric recipients [45]. These young liver recipients often require prolonged post‐transplant in‐hospital care, including frequent visits and long‐term immunosuppression, leading to substantial healthcare costs [82]. While both HOPE and NMP increase upfront costs, analyses in adults suggest end‐ischemic NMP may be cost‐neutral and HOPE even cost‐effective by reducing post‐transplant complications, related interventions, and required costs [83, 84]. Other recent economic analyses demonstrated significant additional costs using upfront NMP [85]. Globally, reimbursement policies vary, with some centers absorbing costs or securing partial coverage, highlighting access differences [86]. In adults, HOPE has demonstrated cost‐effectiveness, reducing early allograft dysfunction and biliary complications, and reducing the number of interventions needed with lower total medical costs within the first year post‐transplant despite using DCD grafts [84, 87]. Of note, this recent study by Enzo et al. also considered relevant device‐related expenses, including staff training and maintenance in addition to upfront device and consumable costs. Overall, the cost benefits of these new preservation technologies rely heavily on a consistent outcome assessment after liver transplantation, which is often lacking [88]. Cost data and benefit analyses remain scarce today, and the current literature underscores the need for standardized, multicenter economic evaluations to assess the feasibility and long‐term benefits of MP in adult and even more in pediatric liver transplantation across different healthcare systems.

Discussion

The various organ perfusion techniques are continuously endeavored in organ transplantation. Two primary approaches at normothermic and hypothermic temperatures have been adopted and approved in select countries for use in livers with defined risk profiles [89]. Recent studies have shown the benefit of both, NMP and HOPE, in mitigating the short‐term outcome compared to cold storage alone. Although there is increasing evidence for the importance of MP in adult LT, the number of clinical studies with pediatric grafts remains limited, particularly with normothermic techniques. So far, no large cohort study or RCT has been performed in children. Various case reports, case series, and a retrospective study have reported the benefit of HOPE in PLT and for split grafts, showing comparable results with HOPE splits and LDLT grafts with lower post‐reperfusion syndrome and fewer post‐transplant early complications compared to SCS SLT [47].

Most series performed HOPE through the portal vein alone, an approach described with identical effects, both experimentally and in clinical studies compared with dual HOPE with additional perfusion through the hepatic artery (or celiac trunk) [90]. Single portal vein perfusion appears advantageous for split procedures. While HOPE was used for split grafts with transplantation in pediatric recipients in various countries, other perfusion techniques, including NMP, remain experimental with very limited reports. The most recent case summary illustrated that NMP minimized CIT during split. Graft perfusion to optimize donor–recipient pairing. Although very important steps were made with NMP, transplantation of such grafts is largely lacking.

In contrast, NRP is specifically associated with DCD donors, allowing for organ evaluation by recirculating the donor's own blood within the abdominal cavity [17, 76, 91]. Compared to static cold storage alone, early reoxygenation through NRP in DCD donors has shown promise in reducing biliary complications and improving graft survival [17, 74, 91]. NRP has the potential to increase the quality and number of viable liver grafts in pediatric DCD, but its place in clinical practice is not yet identified because DCD grafts are traditionally high risk. Addressing the logistical, ethical, and evidentiary gaps will require multicenter collaboration, inclusion of younger donors and recipients in future studies, and the development of pediatric‐specific NRP guidelines. Until then, the use of NRP in PLT remains promising but premature as a standard of care.

While such perfusion techniques are promising and of significant interest, further data are needed to determine optimal perfusion parameters tailored to liver lobes of varying sizes and weights, especially in the context of different surgical split techniques. Currently, no comprehensive analysis exists evaluating perfusion variables such as flow rates and pressures in relation to graft weight and quality. Additional results from PLTs with machine perfusion are awaited in upcoming months and years by a European split consortium [66].

Summary and Ongoing Challenges

Pediatric liver transplantation has gained popularity with excellent results. However, waitlist mortality remains a challenge. Machine perfusion serves as a valuable strategy for utilizing and assessing higher‐risk donor organs for transplantation and minimizing CIT. HOPE SLT remains the most tested MP for children so far. While this method mitigates IRI, its use has also been found to show similar outcomes compared to LDLT. Among larger cohort reports, the HOPE‐split study from France is currently the only retrospective study that was highly effective in showing comparable results as observed with LDLT, which supports the broader, more systematic adoption of this perfusion technique. Given the heterogeneity of PLT, demonstrating robust benefits of machine perfusion comparable to results observed in adults will require substantial time and dedicated effort. The wide range of graft types, recipient ages and weights, as well as diverse underlying liver conditions, may necessitate the testing of various perfusion protocols including flow rates, pressures, and perfusates across many cases to generate reliable data. Although NMP and NRP appear promising for PLT, their effectiveness remains limited by cold ischemia time and donor type. As demonstrated with the HOPE approach, the attenuation of immediate IRI‐related inflammation forms the basis for improved liver perfusion quality, enhanced regenerative capacity, decreased graft stiffness, and, ultimately, a lower incidence of vascular and biliary complications. This may potentially broaden the acceptance criteria for all donor types, including both deceased and living donors. To achieve this, future research is needed, guided by standardized and transparent criteria for risk factors and outcome reporting, along with close collaboration among all key stakeholders.
