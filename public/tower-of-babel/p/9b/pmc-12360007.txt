Epidemiology of globe injury repair mechanisms in the TriNetX US collaborative network database: retrospective cohort study
Authors: Matthew D Spangler, Nila Kirupaharan, Camellia Edalat, Meghan K Berkenstock
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo analyze demographics, incidence, and prevalence of globe injury repair mechanisms in the TriNetX United States population from 2013 to 2023.MethodsRetrospective cohort study of globe injury repair mechanisms in the TriNetX United States Collaborative Network database from 2013 to 2023. CPT codes from the ICD-10 classification system were utilized to identify repair methods for globe injuries (65275, 65280, 65285, 65286). Demographics were analyzed using two-factor ANOVA for age and chi-squared tests for race, ethnicity, and sex. Incidence and prevalence were analyzed yearly and cumulatively.ResultsOf the 119,347,853 subjects in TriNetX, 231,286 cases of globe injuries were identified. Demographics varied by repair category for age and sex ratios. An ANOVA showed a significant difference between age distribution over the four subtypes (mean age 47.2 ± 21.6 years, p < 0.001). The most common procedure was repair of non-perforating corneal lacerations with or without foreign body removal (n = 182,193) and was the only cohort with a female-predominant population (n = 119,990; 65.89%). Chi-squared tests revealed significant differences by sex (p < 0.001), ethnicity (p < 0.001), and race (p < 0.001). Incidence showed variation from 2013 to 2019; however, a large decrease in incidence was noted from 2019 to 2021.ConclusionsOur findings align with previous research indicating decreased globe injury incidence during the SARS-CoV-2 pandemic, except for perforating corneal and/or scleral lacerations without uveal tissue involvement. Our study reveals a shift towards higher affected female population than previous literature.Keywords: Eye (globe), Trauma, Epidemiology, SARS-CoV-2, Treatment surgery

Background

In the United States, the leading cause of monocular vision loss is due to ocular trauma, with approximately 2.4 million injuries every year [1–3]. Notably, one-third of these cases ultimately result in blindness [1, 4]. Globally, an estimated 55 million eyes are affected by ocular trauma each year, leading to monocular blindness in 18 million individuals and complete blindness in 1.6 million individuals [3, 5–8]. Ocular trauma is a risk factor for globe rupture and perforation [1, 4]. Despite both eyes occupying 0.2% of the body’s surface area, they are the third most frequently affected organ by traumatic injuries [9, 10]. Given that ocular trauma and subsequent globe rupture are largely preventable causes of blindness in the United States (US), examining trends and patterns in ocular trauma is imperative to inform preventative strategies and reduce the incidence of blindness.

Globe lesions are classified into two primary types: open and closed. By definition, open globe injuries are full-thickness disruption of the corneoscleral envelope, while in closed globe injuries, the corneoscleral structure remains intact [11, 12]. Lacerations are further classified into penetrating (a single entry site) and perforating wounds (both an entry and an exit site) [9, 11, 13]. Surgical intervention is often necessary in cases of open globe injuries to prevent permanent vision loss and protect ocular structures [14].

Prior epidemiological studies have reported the incidence and prevalence of globe injury range from 3.4–12.7/100,000 and 1.58–7.62/100,000 persons, respectively [1, 3, 6, 8, 14–16]. This variation underscores differences in study populations and geographic locations. Prior to this study, a comprehensive examination of incidence and prevalence of globe injuries at a national level has been limited. Leveraging the TriNetX database, a de-identified, multicenter network encompassing over 65 healthcare networks and 117 million patients across the United States, this study provides a broad analysis of demographics, incidence, and prevalence trends in globe injury repair mechanisms from 2013 to 2023.

Methods

Study cohorts

Individuals with globe injury repairs were identified using the International Classification for Disease, 10th Edition (ICD-10) codes. For diagnoses prior to 2016, the TriNetX software converted ICD-9 coded information into their equivalent ICD-10 codes. The codes utilized for the four globe injury repair categories examined in this study were the Current Procedural Terminology (CPT) codes of: repair of laceration; cornea, non-perforating, with or without removal foreign body (65275), repair of laceration; cornea and/or sclera, perforating, not involving uveal tissue (65280), repair of laceration; cornea and/or sclera, perforating, with reposition or resection of uveal tissue (65285), and repair of laceration; application of tissue glue, wounds of cornea and/or sclera (65286). Data were collected in September 2024 for the study period of 2013 to 2023.

Demographics

The demographics collected were age, sex, race, and ethnicity. An analysis of variance (ANOVA) test was run for average age and a chi-squared analysis was run for sex, race, and ethnicity, excluding other or unknown categories, to assess if there was a statistical difference between each of the globe injury repair mechanisms.

Incidence and prevalence

Incidence and prevalence of each subtype of globe injury were extrapolated from the TriNetX US Collaborative Database. Incidence refers to the number of new cases of globe injury repair documented within a given year. Incidence was reported yearly during the study period and cumulatively with the units of cases per 100,000 person-years. Prevalence was calculated over the study period of 2013–2023 and reported with the units of cases per 100,000 persons.

About the TriNetX database

TriNetX US Collaborative Network database with natural language processing was utilized in this study. This database is a multicenter-research network that utilizes deidentified patient data and is compliant with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule as defined in Section > > 164.514(b)(C). The natural language processor is a TriNetX program that is used to convert data from physician notes and clinical reports into ICD-10 coded information [17]. This study was deemed exempt by the Drexel University College of Medicine Institutional Review Board (IRB Protocol Number: 2412010921) and compliant with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Chi squared analysis showed that for sex (p < 0.001), ethnicity (p < 0.001), and race (p < 0.001), there were statistical significance between the repair mechanisms.

Incidence and prevalence

Cumulative incidence and prevalence of globe injury repair mechanisms were highest over the study period for repair of a non-perforating cornea lacerations with or without foreign body removal at 36.81 cases per 100,000 person-years and 174.00 cases per 100,000 persons, respectively. Repair of perforating cornea and/or scleral lacerations without uveal tissue involvement had the lowest incidence and prevalence of 0.68 cases per 100,000 person-years and 3.10 cases per 100,000 persons, respectively. Discussion

Most epidemiological studies examining globe injuries are limited to regional populations or datasets with restricted generalizability due to the nature of the databases utilized [1, 6, 8, 14–16]. Subsequently, the reported incidence of globe injuries in these studies has varied with incidence ranging from 3.4 to 12.7 cases per 100,000 person-years. In this study, we utilized a more granular approach by stratifying data by repair mechanisms and providing a detailed examination of the variation in incidence among these subtypes of globe injury repairs. This methodology allows for a deeper understanding of each globe injury repair subtype, which has not been extensively explored in the literature. Furthermore, the use of the TriNetX database, which in prior work by our group has been shown to be largely similar to that of the United States population, enhances the generalizability of our findings on a national scale [18].

The highest incidence and prevalence in this study were observed for non-perforating cornea lacerations with or without foreign body removal, reaching 36.81 and 174.00 cases per 100,000 persons, respectively. Past studies showed similar trends to this finding as their research identified non-perforating injuries as the most common type of ocular trauma [7, 16, 19]. In contrast, the lowest incidence and prevalence in this study were seen with perforating cornea and/or scleral lacerations without uveal tissue involvement, which mirrored findings in the Intelligent Research in Sight (IRIS) Registry [14]. This study found that closed globe injuries had the highest rate of recurrence at 27.68%, while recurrence rates for open globe injuries ranged from 3.00 to 9.81%. Due to limitations inherent to the TriNetX database, we are unable to verify through chart review whether these recurrences represent multiple interventions for the same incident or new, distinct injury events. Although there is limited prior research on recurrent globe injuries, some studies have reported recurrence rates as high as 22.7% [20]. Regarding open globe injuries specifically, Agrawal et al. reported that 27% of cases required two interventions, while Knyazer et al. reported a mean of 1.5 surgeries per injury [21, 22]. The recurrence rates found in our study for closed globe injuries is slightly higher than reported for past globe injuries, and our open globe recurrence is much lower than those in prior studies. The differences may be attributed to our use of a much larger and more diverse patient population, as opposed to previous studies that focused on smaller, regionally confined cohorts.

An important finding was the decline in incidence of globe injury repair mechanisms starting in 2019 with a gradual recovery beginning in 2021. Past research documents a similar trend of decreasing ocular trauma during the SARS-Co-V2 pandemic due to reduced exposure to high-risk environments and activities during lockdowns [23–26]. Factors such as school closures, workforce restrictions, adoption of social distancing protocols, and decreased willingness of patients to present to emergency departments due to the risk of SARS-Co-V2 exposure contributed to this decline [23–26]. Liang et al. reported that ocular injuries from sports and outdoor activities decreased from 10.4 to 6.9%, and occupational injuries decreased from 44.2 to 12.0% during the pandemic; while Pande et al. reported road traffic accidents decreasing 22% and outdoor assaults decreasing 45.1% [27, 28]. The subsequent rise in incidence following 2021 reflects the resumption of activities; however, the incidence still lags behind previous values, potentially indicating the lasting effect of a shift from outdoor activities to smartphone and social media use and many workforce positions remaining remote [26, 28]. The only subtype of globe injury repair that did not decrease was perforating cornea and/or scleral lacerations without uveal tissue involvement; this could be attributed to an increase in home improvement projects, domestic abuse, increased alcohol consumption, and continued falls at home [27–29].

Demographic analyses reaffirmed the predominance of males in the globe injury population in three of the four subtypes, a consistent finding across numerous studies [1, 4, 8, 9, 11, 16, 30, 31]. In the past, this pattern is often attributed to higher occupational exposure and engagement in high-risk recreational activities among men. A study at Massachusetts Eye and Ear Infirmary concluded that men were more likely to sustain open globe injuries; while younger men were more likely to sustain their injuries from projectile objects secondary to work or home-improvement, women in the older population were shown to more likely experience globe injuries from a fall [32]. Another study on globe rupture in a tertiary hospital further noted that men under 40 had a higher rate of globe injury due to assaults [33]. Interestingly, our study identified an exception in non-perforating cornea lacerations with or without foreign body removal subtype where women were overall more commonly affected. It is important to note that this subtype of globe injury repair was the most common with over 100,000 more patients than the other cohorts combined. This notion that men are more likely to sustain globe injuries due to higher risk behaviors may be evolving with changes in sex demographics due to changing occupational and gender roles in society [34]. Additionally, domestic violence against women increased during the pandemic and remained higher post-pandemic leading to increased ocular injuries, including globe trauma [28, 35]. Previous research illustrates this increase in domestic violence with Liang et al. reporting an increase from 16.8 to 43.1% in the proportion of domestic ocular trauma during the pandemic and Pande et al. reported that sexual assaults increased from 0.2 to 1.7% and female trauma overall increased by 18% during lockdown [27, 28].

Age trends in previous studies highlight the bimodal phase of globe injury with the population of patients being most affected by this injury peaking at 21–40 years old and again at 70 years old [3, 7, 14, 30]. This results from younger individuals associating themselves with more hazardous situations and risk-seeking behaviors, while the older population is subjected to age-related physiological changes of reduced tissue elasticity, slower healing rates, and increased fall rate, leading to globe injuries [1, 3, 4, 32, 33, 36]. The age distribution of our study resembles the bimodal peak structure with the average age in the middle of the peaks, ranging from 41.5 to 54.2 years old. Despite higher rates of globe trauma in black or Hispanic populations, the largest percentage of patients treated for globe injury are of the non-Hispanic, white population [8, 14, 31]. Our study strengthens these findings that the non-Hispanic, white population has the highest percentage of globe injury. Iftikhar et al. and Ojuok et al. highlight these disparities and attribute them to access to care, leading to poorer outcomes for non-white groups [8, 31]. All of these findings emphasize the need to address systemic barriers to equitable healthcare access and counseling for the prevention of globe injuries.

While the TriNetX database offers extensive, de-identified patient information, there are several limitations. This database relies on coded data, precluding chart review to assess clinical outcomes, such as pre-operation and post-operation visual acuities. Additionally, we are unable to chart review to establish whether patients with multiple repair operations were from the same or different globe injuries. Our demographics section is limited to the information provided by the database. Factors like occupation, which may help with the interpretation of the data are unable to be queried. TriNetX is also a fluid database that is constantly having information added by the expanding healthcare networks impacting incidence and prevalence rates.

Conclusions

The results of this study underscore the evolving epidemiology of globe injuries in the United States, highlighting shifts in demographics and alterations in exposure to occupational hazards during and following the SARS-Co-V2 pandemic. These results emphasize the importance of implementing targeted preventative measures and educational initiatives for high-risk populations. Future research should focus on examining the sociocultural factors influencing trends prospectively in globe injuries and evaluating the visual outcomes associated with various repair mechanisms. Overall, this study demonstrates the utility of leveraging the TriNetX database to analyze globe injury repair mechanism trends on a national scale, providing critical insights that can inform strategies to enhance patient care and outcomes.
