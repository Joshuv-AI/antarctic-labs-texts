Oxidative stress and cognitive function in Chinese patients with major depressive disorder: the mediating role of depression severity
Authors: Rui-jie Peng, Chao Sun, Yu Fan, Jin Li, Ju Gao, Wen-xi Sun, Xiao-bin Zhang, Qing Tian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOxidative stress is increasingly implicated in the pathophysiology of major depressive disorder (MDD), which is often accompanied by significant cognitive impairments. However, the interplay between oxidative stress markers, cognitive function, and the severity of depression symptom, particularly the potential mediating role of severity, requires further investigation. This study aimed to examine alterations in oxidative stress markers in Chinese patients with MDD, their association with cognitive performance, and whether depression and anxiety severity mediates this relationship.MethodsForty-four patients meeting DSM-5 criteria for MDD and 47 matched healthy controls (CON) were recruited. Cognitive functions were assessed using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) and the Stroop Color-Word Test. Depression and anxiety severity were evaluated using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD-24) and Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA). Plasma levels of oxidative stress markers, including malondialdehyde (MDA) and catalase (CAT), were measured via ELISA. Group comparisons (controlling for education), correlation analyses, and mediation analyses (using bootstrapping) were performed.ResultsCompared to healthy controls, patients with MDD exhibited significantly higher plasma MDA levels (1.9 ± 0.4 vs. 1.6 ± 0.5 nmol/mL, P = 0.025) and lower CAT activity (0.7 ± 0.2 vs. 0.9 ± 0.3 U/mL, P = 0.020). The MDD group showed significant cognitive deficits in immediate memory (P = 0.002), attention (P < 0.001), delayed memory (P = 0.010), and the RBANS total score (P = 0.002). Plasma MDA levels negatively correlated with immediate memory (r = − 0.34, P = 0.033) and delayed memory (r = − 0.36, P = 0.021), and positively correlated with both HAMD (P = 0.041) and HAMA scores (P = 0.048). Mediation analyses revealed that HAMD and HAMA scores significantly mediated the relationship between plasma MDA levels and immediate memory performance (Indirect effect P < 0.05 for both).ConclusionsChinese patients with MDD demonstrate heightened oxidative stress (elevated MDA, reduced CAT) and impaired cognitive function, particularly in memory domains, compared to healthy individuals. Higher levels of oxidative stress marker MDA are associated with greater depression/anxiety severity and poorer memory performance. Furthermore, symptom severity partially mediates the link between oxidative stress and immediate memory deficits, suggesting a complex interplay among these factors in MDD pathophysiology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-025-07478-9.Keywords: Depression, Oxidative stress, Cognitive functions, Mediating effects

Introduction

Depression is a very common psychiatric disorder, clinically characterized by significant and persistent affective downturns, diminished interest, and reduced volitional activity [1]. In the WHO’s assessment of the global burden of disease, depression ranked third in 2008, and it is projected to rank first by 2030 [2]. The global average annual prevalence of depression is roughly 6%, while the lifetime prevalence is as high as 15%–18%, with almost one in five people experiencing a depressive episode at some point in their lives. Depression is characterized by a high suicide rate, with a prospective follow-up study of more than 10 years confirming a suicide rate of 4.0%–10.6% and a lifetime suicide risk of 6.0% [1]. Depression has become a major cause of disability worldwide; in the Global Burden of Disease 2019 study, depressive disorders were the second-leading cause of years lived with disability (YLDs) globally [3, 4], and the number of suicide deaths it ranks first among all mental disorders [5].

Patients with depression exhibit cognitive dysfunction of varying degrees in multiple domains, including perception, memory, learning, and executive functioning, alongside their characteristic affective symptoms such as depressed mood and anhedonia. During a depressive episode, most patients experience cognitive dysfunction such as impaired thinking and attention indecisiveness. Furthermore, this relationship often follows a clear dose-response pattern, where the severity of depressive symptoms is strongly correlated with the degree of cognitive impairment; for instance, one study found the prevalence of cognitive deficits rose from 22.9% in mild depression to 41.8% in severe depression [6]. A growing number of studies have shown that effectively improving cognitive symptoms can help reduce the disability in patients [7, 8]. Given the importance of cognitive symptoms in the functional impairment of depressed patients, the US FDA Expert Working Group on Drug Evaluation and Research has recommended cognitive symptoms as one of the intervention targets for depression treatment [9].

At present, there are many studies on the pathophysiological mechanisms of depression, such as the monoamine neurotransmitter hypothesis, the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis (HPA) dysfunction hypothesis, and the neuroinflammation hypothesis, with growing evidence supporting the oxidative stress hypothesis [10]. Oxidative stress is defined as an imbalance between the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the body’s antioxidant defense systems. The brain is particularly susceptible to oxidative damage due to its high oxygen consumption rate and lipid-rich composition. This imbalance leads to the damage of key biological molecules, including lipids (lipid peroxidation), proteins, and DNA. Key indicators of this process include products of lipid peroxidation, such as malondialdehyde (MDA), and antioxidant enzymes that metabolize ROS, such as catalase (CAT).

Structural brain imaging studies in depressed patients have consistently shown that patients have reduced volumes in the prefrontal cortex and hippocampus [11]. These findings are consistent with post-mortem studies, which have revealed significant decreases in the number and density of glial cells and neuronal damage in these cortico-limbic regions [12]. These neuronal alterations are attributed to elevated oxidative stress, which contributes to a process of progressive neurodegeneration linked to the stage of the illness. The hippocampus, a brain region critical for memory and cognitive processes, is known to be particularly vulnerable to this oxidative stress-induced damage, which in turn leads to a reduction in crucial molecules like Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor (BDNF) [13, 14]. This establishes a clear pathological pathway where oxidative stress leads to structural damage in brain areas fundamental for cognition.

This link is further supported by clinical evidence showing that peripheral oxidative stress markers are elevated in patients with depression [15, 16]. Oxidative stress damage is particularly severe in the hippocampus and amygdala of depressed patients [17, 18]. Crucially, several studies have directly connected these markers to specific cognitive deficits. For instance, one study in patients with recurrent depressive disorder explicitly found that elevated plasma MDA levels were significantly associated with impairments in visual-spatial and auditory-verbal working memory, short-term declarative memory, and delayed recall declarative memory [11]. Other studies have also shown that interventions that successfully lowered plasma MDA levels also led to significant improvements in episodic memory and in executive function, as measured by the Trail Making Test (TMT) [19]. Another study in patients with early-onset schizophrenia reported that higher levels of both MDA and the antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase (SOD) were significantly correlated with slower information processing speed [20]. Animal models also confirm that a primary deficit in the antioxidant system is sufficient to induce depressive-like behaviors, researchers found that elevated levels of oxidative stress (e.g., MDA and 8-hydroxydeoxyguanosine) induced by intermittent hypoxia during sleep induced degenerative changes in the hippocampus, leading to spatial learning deficits in adult mice [21]. In addition, given the high comorbidity of depression and anxiety, it is critical to consider the role of anxiety as well. It is well-established that anxiety is independently associated with increased impairments in cognitive domains such as memory and executive function [22].

In the current study, plasma levels of oxidative stress markers, including MDA, peroxidase (POD), Cu/Zn-SOD, Mn-SOD, and CAT from depressed patients and healthy controls were measured to investigate peripheral oxidative stress alterations in depression. In addition, while cognitive dysfunction is a core diagnostic feature of MDD, a growing body of evidence indicates that these deficits, particularly in executive function, can persist even after mood symptoms remit, establishing them as a partially independent domain crucial for long-term functional outcomes [23, 24]. This partial independence motivates the investigation of shared biological mechanisms, such as oxidative stress, that may underlie both mood and cognitive symptoms. Therefore, we used the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS) to rate cognitive function alterations and to elucidate a possible correlation between oxidative stress markers and cognition in depressed patients, a relationship that has been relatively under-explored in previous studies. We also used statistical methods to investigate whether the severity of depression played mediated the relationship between oxidative stress markers and cognitive function.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was conducted at the Department of Psychiatry, Suzhou Guangji Hospital (Suzhou, China) from January 2022 to December 2023. Following institutional ethics approval, 44 patients with major depressive disorder (MDD) and 47 healthy controls were enrolled. The healthy control group was recruited to be comparable to the patient group in terms of age and gender distribution. All participants provided written informed consent. The MDD group met DSM-5 diagnostic criteria for major depressive disorders. The diagnosis of MDD for all patients was confirmed by two senior psychiatrists according to the DSM-5 criteria, using the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-5 (SCID-5) [25]. Demographic information and clinical characteristics were collected using standardized questionnaires.

Clinical and cognitive assessments

Depression and anxiety symptoms were evaluated using the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD 24-item version) [26, 27] and the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale (HAMA) [27, 28], respectively. HAMD scores ≥ 35 indicate severe depression, ≥ 8 indicate mild to moderate depression, and < 8 indicate no depression. HAMA scores ≥ 29 indicate severe anxiety, ≥ 21 indicate definite anxiety, and < 7 indicate no anxiety.

Cognitive assessments were conducted in a quiet, temperature-controlled environment using the RBANS [29] and the Stroop Color-Word Test [30]. RBANS comprises five cognitive domains: immediate memory (list learning and story memory), visuospatial/constructional (figure copy and line orientation), language (picture naming and semantic fluency), attention (digit span and coding), and delayed memory (list recall, list recognition, story recall, and figure recall). The Stroop Color-Word Test assessed executive function and cognitive processing speed through word reading, color naming, and interference conditions.

Measurement of oxidative stress markers

Blood samples (5 mL) were collected from participants’ antecubital veins before 8:00 AM, centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. Oxidative stress markers, including MDA, POD, total SOD (T-SOD), Cu/Zn-SOD, Mn-SOD, and CAT were measured by ELISA.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.1.2). Group differences were analyzed using chi-square tests for categorical variables (e.g., gender, smoking status, and alcohol use) and independent t-tests for continuous variables (e.g., age, education years, duration of illness, and number of episodes).

Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) with 10,000 permutation tests, controlling for education years, was used to compare cognitive function and oxidative stress markers between groups. A post-hoc power analysis was conducted using the pwr package (version 1.3-0.3, https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/pwr/index.html) in R to evaluate the statistical power for detecting the group differences in the main variables included in our mediation analysis (MDA, Immediate Memory, HAMD, and HAMA). The analysis confirmed that the study was adequately powered (all > 0.85) to identify these group differences. Pearson correlation analyses were performed to examine the relationships between oxidative stress markers, cognitive function, and clinical symptoms. Mediation analyses using bootstrapping with 10,000 resamples, conducted with the bruceR package (version 2024.6, https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/bruceR/index.html) in R, were performed to examine whether depression (HAMD) and anxiety (HAMA) symptoms mediated the relationship between MDA and memory performance. The indirect effects were considered significant (P < 0.05) when the 95% confidence interval (CI) excluded zero. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05.

Results

Demographic, clinical, cognitive and oxidative stress marker comparisons

Demographic analysis (Table 1) showed no significant differences between the MDD (N = 44) and healthy control (N = 47) groups in gender distribution, age, smoking status, or alcohol use (all P > 0.05). However, the MDD group had significantly fewer years of education compared to the control group (12.9 ± 3.4 vs. 14.5 ± 2.4 years, P = 0.026). As expected, the MDD group showed significantly higher scores on both the HAMD (32.4 ± 6.3 vs. 3.0 ± 2.6, P < 0.001) and HAMA (22.2 ± 6.6 vs. 2.6 ± 2.3, P < 0.001) compared to the control group.

Correlations among clinical characteristics, cognitive functions, and oxidative stress markers

Correlation analyses were performed on variables that showed significant between-group differences. As shown in Fig. 1A, significant negative correlations were observed between depression/anxiety symptoms and cognitive functions. Specifically, immediate memory was negatively correlated with both HAMD (r = − 0.49, P = 0.002) and HAMA scores (r = − 0.52, P = 0.001). In addition, attention showed negative correlations with HAMD (r = − 0.47, P = 0.001) and HAMA scores (r = − 0.54, P < 0.001), whereas delayed memory was negatively correlated only with HAMA scores (r = − 0.33, P = 0.033). RBANS total scores demonstrated negative correlations with both HAMD (r = − 0.48, P = 0.001) and HAMA scores (r = − 0.51, P = 0.001). In contrast, word interference time showed positive correlations with both HAMD (r = 0.42, P = 0.006) and HAMA scores (r = 0.51, P = 0.002).

Regarding oxidative stress markers, as illustrated in Fig. 1B, plasma MDA levels showed significant negative correlations with immediate memory (r = − 0.34, P = 0.033) and delayed memory (r = − 0.36, P = 0.021). However, plasma CAT activity showed no significant correlations with the RBANS total score, immediate memory, attention, delayed memory, or Stroop task performance (all P > 0.05).

Furthermore, Fig. 1C demonstrates that plasma MDA levels were positively correlated with both HAMD (r = 0.32, P = 0.041) and HAMA scores (r = 0.33, P = 0.048), suggesting a potential relationship between oxidative stress and clinical symptoms.

Mediating effects of depression and anxiety symptoms on the relationship between MDA and memory performance

Our initial analyses revealed that MDA levels were positively correlated with HAMD and HAMA scores, while MDA, HAMD, and HAMA scores were all negatively correlated with memory performance. Based on these findings, we conducted mediation analyses to explore the potential mechanisms underlying these associations. Specifically, we employed bootstrapping analyses with 10,000 resamples to test whether depression (HAMD) and anxiety (HAMA) symptoms mediated the relationship between oxidative stress (MDA) and memory performance.

For immediate memory performance, significant mediating effects were observed for both depression and anxiety symptoms. As shown in Table 4; Fig. 2A, HAMD scores significantly mediated the relationship between MDA and immediate memory (indirect effect = − 3.982, 95% CI [− 8.545, − 0.696], P = 0.048), with both significant total effect (− 13.711, P < 0.001) and direct effect (− 9.729, P = 0.026). Similarly, Table 5; Fig. 2B show that HAMA scores demonstrated significant mediation (indirect effect = − 4.011, 95% CI [− 8.044, − 0.862], P = 0.030), with both significant total effect (− 14.694, P < 0.001) and direct effect (− 10.683, P = 0.010).

For delayed memory performance, the mediating effects were less pronounced. As presented in Supplemental Table 1, HAMD showed no significant mediating effect (indirect effect = − 2.062, 95% CI [− 4.856, 0.032], P = 0.102), although both total effect (− 11.030, P < 0.001) and direct effect (− 8.968, P = 0.006) remained significant. Supplemental Table 2 demonstrates that HAMA showed a marginally significant mediating effect (indirect effect = − 2.181, 95% CI [− 4.760, − 0.240], P = 0.060), with both significant total effect (− 11.311, P < 0.001) and direct effect (− 9.130, P = 0.005).

These findings suggest differential mediating patterns for immediate and delayed memory performance. Both depression and anxiety symptoms partially mediated the relationship between MDA and immediate memory (Tables 4 and 5; Fig. 2A and B), while their mediating effects on delayed memory were less robust, with anxiety showing only marginally significant mediation and depression showing no significant mediating effect (Supplemental Tables 1 and 2).

Discussion

The aim of this study was to explore the changes in plasma oxidative stress marker levels in depressed patients and to further investigate the relationship between oxidative stress levels and cognitive function. We showed that, compared to healthy controls, patients with depression had altered plasma levels of oxidative stress markers, such as increased MDA levels and decreased CTA activity in depressed patients, which suggests that these markers may serve as potential biomarkers for depression. Furthermore, higher plasma MDA levels were associated with more severe symptoms of depression and anxiety. Finally, we found that depression and anxiety symptoms may partially mediate the relations between oxidative stress and cognitive function.

Changes in plasma oxidative stress markers in patients with depression

Plasma MDA levels in the MDD group were significantly higher than those in the healthy control group, while plasma CAT activity in the MDD group was significantly lower than in the healthy control group. Overall, these results indicated that antioxidant enzyme activities were reduced while the levels of oxidative stress products were increased in patients with MDD. A review of changes in oxidative stress in depressed patients showed that antioxidant enzyme activities (SOD, CAT, and GPx) and glutathione (GSH) levels were significantly lower, while lipid peroxides and inflammatory cytokines were significantly higher in these population [31]. Therefore, antioxidants hold potential therapeutic value for the treatment of depression.

It is noteworthy that while our MDD group showed significantly higher MDA and lower CAT levels, we did not observe significant group differences in other antioxidant enzymes like POD, T-SOD, Cu/Zn-SOD, or Mn-SOD. This finding, rather than being an outlier, aligns with the recognized inconsistencies in the literature regarding these specific markers. For instance, while some studies have reported a decrease in SOD activity in depression [32], others, like our results, found no significant change [33]. These discrepancies across studies may be attributed to differences in measurement methods for oxidative stress markers or the age of enrolled subjects. In the future, measuring oxidative stress marker levels in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) could provide more direct insights into central nervous system oxidative stress, as CSF is in direct contact with brain tissue and may better reflects the neurological microenvironment.

Cognitive function and demographic changes in patients with depression

Depressed patients in the current study had fewer years of education than healthy controls and had significantly higher HAMD and HAMA scores. A study examining depression among students during the COVID-19 pandemic [34], it was shown that the prevalence of depression was lower among those with more years of education, which is consistent with our findings. While several studies that support our findings in terms of the positive correlation between fewer years of education and increased depression [35–37], other studies have found the opposite results [38]. A possible reason for this discrepancy may be related to differences in the economic level of the study area or population investigated.

We found that immediate memory, attention, delayed memory, and total RBANS scores were reduced in the MDD group compared to healthy controls. Furthermore, results from the Stroop Color-Word Test showed that the MDD group took longer to complete the Stroop task. Our findings are consistent with previous studies indicating that significant decreases in memory and attention and longer reaction times to incongruent stimuli in depressed patients [39–41].

Correlation of oxidative stress markers with clinical scales and cognitive function

We found that plasma MDA levels were positively correlated with greater levels of depression and anxiety patients in the MDD group. This is consistent with previous studies that found a positive correlation between the levels of oxidative stress markers (e.g., GSH and F2-isoprostane) and the severity of depression and anxiety [42]. This relationship may be related to increased oxidative stress in depressed patients, which leads to an increase in oxygen free radicals in the brain, resulting in atrophy of neuronal and glial cell populations. In turn, this atrophy may exacerbate depressive symptoms, which can be viewed as a result of altered neuronal plasticity [11]. Therefore, it may be clinically useful to monitor the levels of oxidative stress markers to track changes in patients’ depressive symptoms.

We also found a negative correlation between plasma MDA levels and immediate and delayed memory in the MDD group. Supporting this finding, researchers who generated transient oxidative stress stimuli in rats found that repeated exposure induced various long-term changes in cognitive and behavioral functioning [43]. Interestingly, immunohistochemical staining of postmortem human brain tissue suggested that the hippocampus, which plays a primary role in spatial memory and cognition in humans [44], was severely damaged by oxidative stress in depressed patients [45]. We therefore hypothesize that increased oxidative stress in depressed patients likely resulted in neuronal damage and glial cell inactivation in the hippocampal region, which leading to subsequent memory and cognitive impairments. Studies have found that elevated plasma MDA levels in depressed patients were associated with impaired visual-spatial, auditory-verbal, immediate, and delayed memories [46], a finding similar to that of the current study. Another study showed that antioxidant scores (consisting of plasma levels of oxidative stress markers (oxidized GSH) and antioxidants (reduced GSH, GPx, and vitamin C) were significantly and positively correlated with total brain volume and the CA3/DG hippocampal subregion volume in depressed patients [47], which further supports our findings. Crucially, these studies reveal that cognitive deficits may not merely be transient effects of low mood, as they can persist long after emotional symptoms resolve, demonstrating a partial independence from mood [8, 24]a. This persistence underscores the importance of investigating shared underlying mechanisms, such as oxidative stress, which may drive both symptom severity and lasting cognitive decline. Future studies could obtain more precise results by directly measuring central nervous system oxidative stress, for example, by assaying markers in cerebrospinal fluid or measuring GSH levels using magnetic resonance spectroscopy.

Overall, the results suggest that decreasing oxidative stress marker levels in depressed patients may reduce symptom severity and cognitive impairment, which may be helpful for their clinical prognosis.

Depression and anxiety severity partially mediate the interaction between MDA levels and cognitive function

We not only confirmed that increased cognitive impairment and elevated levels of oxidative stress markers were present in depressed patients but also that a correlation existed between the two. We therefore further investigated a potential mediating role in this relationship.

Our results indicated that depression and anxiety symptoms partially mediated the relationship between oxidative stress and cognitive function. Specifically, increased oxidative stress in depressed patients was associated with more severe symptoms of depression and anxiety, which in turn was linked to a significant decline in cognitive function (especially memory). We therefore postulate that reducing the symptoms of depression and anxiety in depressed patients may partially improve cognitive impairment. Clinically, the correct use of antidepressants and appropriate psychotherapy can help to improve the cognitive impairment and reduce the levels of oxidative stress in depressed patients.

Another key finding of our study is that anxiety severity, as measured by the HAMA scale, significantly mediated the relationship between the oxidative stress marker MDA and immediate memory impairment. This suggests that elevated oxidative stress might impact memory not only directly, but also indirectly by exacerbating anxiety symptoms, which in turn negatively affect cognitive performance. This finding is consistent with a well-established neurobiological model where chronic anxiety leads to dysregulation of the Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal (HPA) axis and sustained high levels of cortisol [48, 49]. Brain regions crucial for cognition, such as the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex, are rich in glucocorticoid receptors, making them particularly vulnerable to this cortisol-mediated damage [50, 51]. This damage can manifest as structural remodeling, including hippocampal atrophy, and directly impairs performance on memory and executive function tasks [52, 53]. Therefore, our results, viewed through this lens, highlight the importance of assessing and treating anxiety symptoms in patients with depression, as this may be a key pathway to mitigating oxidative stress-related cognitive decline.

Overall, we found that oxidative stress (e.g., MDA levels) affected cognitive functioning (e.g., immediate memory) not only directly but also indirectly, with this relationship being partially mediated by the severity of depression and anxiety symptoms. In support of this finding, a recent a review [54] showed that supplementation with antioxidants not only improved depressive and anxiety symptoms in patients with depression but also reduced cognitive impairment, indicating a possible role for antioxidants in the clinical treatment and prognosis of depression.

Conclusion

We found that the levels of oxidative stress markers were elevated in depressed patients and were positively correlated with depression severity and negatively correlated with cognitive function (mainly memory). We hypothesize that this may be due to increased oxidative stress levels in depressed patients that led to neuronal damage in the hippocampus, which in turn exacerbated memory and cognitive function impairments. In addition, the severity of depression severity significantly mediated the relationship between oxidative stress and immediate memory. In the future, the use of antioxidants to improve depressive symptoms and cognitive impairments in patients may provide a new therapeutic avenue to improve their quality of life.

The current study has several limitations. First, our cross-sectional design precluded the investigation of causal relationships and changes over time. While we identified significant associations between oxidative stress, symptom severity, and cognitive function, this design cannot establish whether oxidative stress leads to cognitive deficits or vice versa. It also precluded the investigation of changes in these variables following antidepressant treatment. Therefore, we plan to conduct a longitudinal cohort study to investigate changes in clinical scale scores, cognitive function, and peripheral oxidative stress markers levels, and their relationships after antidepressant treatment in depressed patients. Second, we only measured peripheral oxidative stress levels; future studies should use methods such as measuring oxidative stress markers in the cerebrospinal fluid to more accurately assess oxidative stress alterations in the central nervous system. Finally, a significant limitation is the heterogeneity of medication use within the MDD group. While we noted that 23 of 44 patients were on medication, detailed information regarding the types, dosages, and duration of these treatments was not systematically collected. As antidepressants can influence both oxidative stress levels and cognitive function, this represents a potential confounding factor. Future studies should aim to recruit drug-naïve patients or systematically control for medication effects.
