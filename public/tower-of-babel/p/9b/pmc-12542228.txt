A comparative analysis of palpatory acuity in chiropractic students between 2015 and 2024
Authors: Alice Cade, Kirk Stevens, Jens Duehr, Katie Marten, Imran Khan Niazi, Rasmus Wiberg Nedergaard, Lisa Baptista, David Russell, Heidi Haavik, Kelly Holt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo explore differences in palpatory acuity between two independent student cohorts from 2015 to 2024, and to assess the potential effect of pedagogical changes in the teaching of palpation skills that emphasize sensory awareness over anatomical identification at the New Zealand College of Chiropractic (NZCC).MethodsThis study was a comparative cross-sectional study using two independent student cohorts. In 2015, palpatory acuity was assessed in 199 chiropractic students (42.5% female, mean: 25.8 ± 7.4 years) from 4 different cohorts. In 2024, 154 students (54.5% female, mean: 23.6 ± 5.6 years) over 4 cohorts were assessed. Palpatory acuity was measured by the participant’s ability to accurately locate a 0.1 mm nylon monofilament under a variable number of 80gsm white copying paper sheets while blindfolded. Unpaired Wilcoxon rank sum tests were used to assess differences between timepoints (2015 and 2024) and year groups.ResultsThe 2024 group (mean: 67.8 ± 22.5 pages) demonstrated significantly higher palpatory acuity compared with the 2015 group (mean: 31.1 ± 22.4 pages, p < .001). All cohorts in 2024 outperformed their 2015 counterparts (p < .001). In both groups, acuity improved notably between cohorts in years 2 and 3 but showed minimal further gains in the final year. In the 2015 group, year 3 (mean: 38.0 ± 24.4) and year 4 (mean: 37.5 ± 20.1) students outperformed year 1 (mean: 25.1 ± 19.8, p = .028–0.040) and year 2 students (mean: 26.3 ± 22.6, p = .039–0.040). In the 2024 group, year 3 students (mean: 81.7 ± 15.2) performed significantly better than year 1 (mean: 59.8 ± 22.5, p < .001) and year 2 students (mean: 70.6 ± 17.5, p = .023).DiscussionThese findings suggest that emphasizing sensory experience in palpation training may enhance student performance. Educators may consider integrating more structured haptic exercises early in curricula, while future research should explore longitudinal outcomes and the neural mechanisms underlying skill acquisition to guide further educational reform.ConclusionCurricular and pedagogical changes at NZCC were linked to significantly greater palpatory acuity in students. Palpatory acuity appears to improve early-to-midway through training (regardless of the method used to teach it), with limited gains thereafter. Practical experience and repetition contribute to improving acuity, but this study highlights the value of sensory-focused practice in curriculum design for chiropractic education.

Introduction

Palpation is an essential component of patient examination for chiropractors, and many other health care professionals [1], yet due to its subjective nature, remains one of the more difficult skills to teach and develop in students [2]. Static and motion palpation of a patient’s spine are some of the most commonly used methods of chiropractic spinal assessment and are reliable indicators of spinal dysfunction [2, 3].

Palpatory acuity, defined as the ability to detect and interpret tactile information [4], is fundamental to chiropractic practice, with recent research suggesting that palpation of the spine is reliable between chiropractors [5]. Current research further suggests the ability to distinguish between functional and dysfunctional (commonly described as ‘subluxated [6]’ by chiropractors) areas of the spine, via palpation, is fundamental to finding where to provide an adjustive thrust in the spine that changes brain function [7]. Given the importance of palpatory skill, identifying when changes in ability occur during training may allow for more targeted and effective practical teaching.

Early research suggests a steady improvement in palpatory ability in chiropractic students from first to final year of study, then to practicing chiropractors, with the greatest improvement between the first and final year of study [4, 8]. However, there is still little clarity on whether the shift in palpatory skill level happens gradually or in more distinct stages. Previous research suggests that time, experience, focus, and stress all affect the ability to upskill palpatory acuity [9–11], and that the change in skill level is brain-based, with experienced people showing significant cortical reorganization [12, 13].

In 2015, the palpatory acuity of 199 chiropractic students and 13 experienced chiropractors was assessed. These data showed palpatory acuity appeared to improve 2 years after entering a chiropractic training program but did not significantly increase thereafter [14]. Based on these findings, the NZCC undertook a curriculum review of the technique program, implementing changes designed to accelerate the development of palpation skills. The 2024 year-4 cohort included in the present study, however, had not yet received the updated training that was introduced for lower year groups.

The revised curriculum introduced haptic perception exercises (where the participant actively moves their hand to explore a surface or object [15]), such as the use of “feely boxes” or bags containing unseen objects for tactile exploration, as well as exercises involving different textures. One of the main pedagogical changes involved reducing the emphasis on immediately identifying or naming palpated structures. It was believed that students may have focused too heavily on naming what they felt, rather than exploring the sensory input itself and how it changed across different parts of an object. Research suggests that trying to identify multiple features of an object while palpating can be hindered by cognitive overload, whereas focusing on a single feature, such as texture, can improve perception [16]. Palpation of the human spine and associated tissues is a multi-feature process, and it was hypothesized that the additional cognitive load of also naming a structure may have slowed down palpatory skill acquisition.

This study aimed to assess palpatory acuity in chiropractic students in 2015 and compare them to a 2024 cohort. The 2015–2024 comparison hoped to assess the impact of altered psychomotor skill training and further inform teaching practices as during the 9-year period between timepoints the chiropractic-training program underwent significant changes to its technique skills acquisition courses.

Methods

Participants

All year groups (cohorts) of NZCC students were invited to take part in this study in 2015 and 2024. A snowball sampling [17] method was used, and all students were invited via email and word-of-mouth. Informed consent to be involved in educational research, as part of normal NZCC classes, had previously been gained. Participants were excluded if they had any known neurological deficits such as peripheral nerve lesions, neurological damage affecting hand/finger sensitivity, or any local skin injury.

The current NZCC chiropractic training program consists of five years of specialized chiropractic education. In 2015, the program structure included a pre-requisite year of health science courses that were taught externally, followed by four years of chiropractic training delivered at NZCC. By 2024, the five-year program had been introduced for the first time, with all years delivered internally. At the time of data collection, both Year 1 and Year 2 students in the new program were in their first year at NZCC, having entered directly into chiropractic training. Neither group had received palpation instruction prior to enrolling at the NZCC, and both were at the same novice level of palpatory skill and training. For the purposes of analysis, these two cohorts were combined and labeled as Year 1 to reflect their shared status as first-year students at the college. To ensure consistency and ease of comparison, all student groups in both 2015 and 2024 were labeled as Years 1 to 4.

Ethical approval

The study was reviewed by the NZCCs Research Committee. As previously stated, informed consent had already been gained, however, in 2015 the Heath and Disabilities Ethics Committee (HDEC) of New Zealand was also contacted to gauge whether further ethical approval should be sought. The HDEC recommend no further ethical approval was needed as data collection was performed as part of a course review process. Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Palpatory testing apparatus

Palpatory acuity was measured by assessing each participant’s ability to locate a 0.1 mm thick nylon monofilament (NMF) with their dominant hand under multiple stacks of paper sheets made up of variable numbers of pieces of paper. The participants were blindfolded with an eye mask and instructed to palpate with a pressure of approximately 500 g downward pressure (see below for training protocol).

The paper used was 80gsm standard white copying paper in portrait orientation, with an initial stack of fifteen sheets of paper, with each subsequent stack increasing in increments of 15 sheets through to 90 sheets for the last stack. Each of the six stacks of paper was fixed onto a table platform to ensure an easy-to-use testing station. The NMF was cut into pieces the width of a standard A4 paper and stretched tensely and taped down onto the table underneath each stack of paper and up to the border of the paper to ensure that participants were not able to use the presence of the tape to judge the location of the NMF, see Fig. 1. Each stack’s filament was placed at random height levels to ensure no recognizable pattern was present. For ease of identification of the location of the NMF/palpation zone by the researcher, a dark line will be marked with a highlighter on the second page from the top of each stack of paper, 5 mm either side of the MNF.

Pre-testing practice/Introductory demonstration of apparatus

Prior to commencing actual data collection, participants were given an opportunity to practice and familiarize themselves with the process. Participants were given two practice attempts to palpate the NMF (non-blindfolded) under a stack of 15 paper sheets as well as a stack of 45 paper sheets in order to get an idea of how filament felt under different thicknesses of paper. These practice stacks were not used for actual data collection. The paper stacks were also placed on an electronic scale, and the participants were instructed to palpate with a pressure of up to approximately 500 g of pressure. This was given as an opportunity to self-calibrate to the requested palpatory pressure. After the two practice attempts, participants were blindfolded for palpatory testing. A time frame of 5 s was allowed for palpation for the NMF. Participants were allowed to palpate freely, using any kind of palpation strategy (up and down or left to right motion).

When the participant indicated that they had located the NMF or when the time ran out, they maintained their finger position to indicate to the researcher where they perceived the NMF to be. If the participant correctly located the NMF within the palpation zone, and the researcher visually confirmed their findings, they were moved onto the next stack of paper sheets. The first unsuccessful attempt to palpate for and locate the NMF resulted in the completion of the assessment for that individual participant. Data recorded included sex, age, year of study and best score for paper stack thicknesses successfully palpated/completed. The data collection procedure, after introduction and practice attempts, took approximately 60 s per participant.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using R software (version 4.0.3) in RStudio [18]. Descriptive statistics such as unadjusted means, standard deviations, and counts were used to summarize the baseline characteristics of the data, and Shapiro-Wilk tests assessed for normality. Between-cohort (2015, 2024) differences in age were assessed within each Year group using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, while sex distributions were compared using Chi-squared tests. Data were analyzed using unpaired Wilcoxon rank sum tests to assess for differences between timepoints (2015 and 2024) and year cohorts. Statistical significance for all tests was set at p <.05 and adjusted using Holm multiplicity correction as appropriate [19]. All calculations were performed with unrounded data.

Results

Data did not follow a normal distribution, and while results are presented in-text as mean with standard deviation (mean (SD)) to better represent the change in time point and year cohorts, it is important to note that the pages palpated through were discrete stacks of 15, 30, 45, 60, 75, and 90 sheets. In 2015, 199 students (42.5% female, 25.8 (7.4) years, year 1 n = 64, year 2 n = 56, year 3 n = 43, year 4 n = 36) participated in this study, with a mean number of pages palpated through of 31.1 (22.4) pages. In 2024, 154 students (54.5% female, 23.6 (5.6) years, year 1 n = 63, year 2 n = 34, year 3 n = 27, year 4 n = 30) participated with a mean number of pages palpated through of 67.8 (22.5). Wilcoxon rank-sum and Chi-squared tests showed no significant differences (p >.05) in age or sex distributions between 2015 and 2024 cohorts across Year groups.

Initial analysis revealed the 2015 group (31.1 (22.4) pages) showed poorer palpatory acuity than the 2024 group (67.8 (22.5) pages, p <.001). Further analysis showed all cohorts, matched for years in the 2024 group were palpating through a greater number of pages than the 2015 group (p <.001, Table 2; Fig. 2).

Comparing cohorts, in 2015, Year 3 students (38.0 (24.4)) and Year 4 students (37.5 (20.1) demonstrated significantly greater palpatory acuity than Year 1 students (25.1 (19.8), p =.028–0.040) and Year 2 students (26.3 (22.6), p =.039–0.040). In 2024, Year 3 students (81.7 (15.2)) also showed a significant increase in the number of pages successfully palpated through compared to both Year 1 students (59.8 (22.5), p <.001) and Year 2 students (70.6 (17.5), p =.023; Table 2; Fig. 2).

Discussion

Key findings

This study found that palpatory acuity develops unevenly throughout the NZCC training program. In 2015, a significant increase in palpatory ability occurred between cohorts in years 2 and 3, with Year 3 and 4 students performing better than Year 1 and 2 students. A similar pattern was observed in 2024, where acuity peaked in Year 3, with little to no further improvement seen in the final year. These findings suggest that palpatory skill development is not linear but instead occurs in distinct stages, likely linked to shifts in clinical exposure and practical experience.

Overall, students in the 2024 group demonstrated significantly greater palpatory acuity than those in the 2015 group. This improvement may represent an advance or improvement in the teaching of practical skills and palpation due to changes within the NZCC curricula. This change in technique teaching consisted of including haptic perception games, where the participant actively moves their hand to explore a surface or object, where students tried to identify common items (paper clips, coins, or other items) underneath layers of fabric, foam, or paper. The teaching change also pivoted to emphasizing simply palpating, rather than identifying and naming structures, as the instructors thought the act of naming what was palpated may have interfered with a student’s learning. Simply put, intuition of sensation was emphasized early, and naming of structures came later in a student’s academic career. Interestingly, the 2024 group’s year 4 cohort was the last class to be taught in the old identify-and-name way of palpatory instruction, which may explain their poorer results than the lower year cohorts from this 2024 group of students.

Previous research has identified several factors that contribute to palpation skill development, including central sensory processing, the practitioner’s focus, and frequency of practice [1, 10, 11]. Repetition and experience are known to drive neuroplastic changes in sensory pathways [20], which may account for the sharp increase in acuity seen in the middle years of training. It is reasonable to assume that this neuroplastic component of learning may explain the significant increase in palpatory acuity between the lower year levels and the higher year levels, the latter having a substantial increase in practical experience and repetition of the sensory input involved in palpation. It is also possible that the cohort tested in 2025 had other reasons for showing improvements, like an increase in self-directed study or practice, different instructors, or learning prior to NZCC, but further research is needed to tease out any other influencing factors.

There are no similar studies to this one, testing acuity at two different timepoints after a pedagogical shift in teaching, but haptic perception, in relation to learning skills, has been well studied. Haptic perception does differ with experience, age, and environmental stressors, responding positively to the former and negatively to the two latter [9]. Haptic perception reduces with age, which may explain why faculty performed similarly or worse than their less experienced students, but task-specific experience and increased intensity of task use may offset these deficits [12, 13]. People with task-specific experience combined with frequent task use, such as professional musicians and Braille readers, show significant reorganization of their somatosensory cortices which may relate to haptic, tactile, or spatial palpatory acuity [12, 13, 21, 22]. As chiropractic is a task-specific, frequently performed palpatory skill, it may be that chiropractors also show significant cortical reorganization due to palpatory skill, but this needs to be explored in future research. Taken together, these data suggest that it’s not the act of palpation, but the processing of sensory information that is key to learning how to palpate and, most importantly, to understanding what is palpated. Future research could include brain-based measurements of novice and experienced palpators to further advance our understanding of why these leaps on palpatory acuity occur.

Implications for education

The findings provide evidence that changes to the palpation teaching curriculum were associated with improved outcomes in student performance. This supports the continued development of psychomotor training strategies that prioritize sensory experience before structural identification. Educators may wish to consider how accumulated practice hours, repetition, and clinical integration can be optimized to accelerate the development of palpatory skills.

Recent commentary in the health professions has advocated for a return to thorough training in hands-on assessment and psychomotor skills [23, 24]. Strengthening palpatory training early in a student’s education may improve diagnostic efficiency, reduce reliance on costly investigations, and ultimately enhance patient care. The teaching strategies described here may serve as a model for other manual therapy programs seeking to strengthen foundational clinical skills.

Strengths and limitations

This study contributes to the understanding of when and how palpatory acuity improves during chiropractic education. It also highlights the potential impact of targeted curricular changes. However, the study has some limitations. Participant numbers within year cohorts were relatively small, which may have influenced statistical comparisons. Additionally, the study provides two cross-sectional snapshots in time, rather than tracking one cohort over time. Due to the nature of the degree program, it was not possible to include a formal control group, which is a limitation. Lastly, the results of this study cannot be completely explained by pedagogical change. Factors such as differing student characteristics, other institutional changes, and post-COVID educational or quality of life changes [25], and societal change may have influenced these results. Future research could build on this by tracking individual students longitudinally throughout their training and into clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that palpatory acuity was substantially greater in 2024 students compared with their 2015 counterparts, suggesting that pedagogical changes may have contributed to improved performance over time. Within each program, palpatory acuity appeared to improve in distinct jumps, particularly early-to-midway through training, before plateauing in the final year. These findings are consistent with previous research indicating that palpation is a brain-based, cortically reorganizing skill shaped by repetition and experience. Taken together, the results highlight the potential influence of curriculum design on the pace of skill acquisition, while underscoring the need for further research to disentangle the effects of pedagogical reform from other contextual factors.
