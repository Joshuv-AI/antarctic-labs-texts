Recruitment of patients, carers and members of the public to advisory boards, groups and panels in public and patient involved health research: a scoping review
Authors: Meghan Gilfoyle, Carolyn Melro, Elena Koskinas, Jon Salsberg

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe objectives of this scoping review are to: (1) identify the distribution of and context of the recruitment strategies used, (2) explore the facilitators, benefits, barriers and ethical issues of the identified recruitment strategies, (3) distinguish the varying terminology for involvement (ie, panels, boards, individual) and (4) determine if the individual recruitment strategies used were to address issues of representation or bias.DesignA scoping review.SettingThis scoping review follows the framework by Peters et al. Seven electronic databases were explored including Scopus, Medline, PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Cochrane Library and PsycINFO (conducted July 2021). The search strategy was codeveloped among the research team, PPI research experts and a faculty librarian. Two independent reviewers screened articles by title and abstract and then at full text based on predetermined criteria.Primary and secondary outcome measuresExplore recruitment strategies used, facilitators, benefits, barriers and ethical issues of the identified recruitment strategies. Identify terminology for involvement. Explore recruitment strategies used to address issues of representation or bias.ResultsThe final sample was from 51 sources. A large portion of the extracted empirical literature had a clinical focus (37%, n=13) but was not a randomised control trial. Background

Public and patient involvement (PPI) is an important approach in health and social care research. PPI is recognised as a moral, methodological and policy imperative, and has become mandated across many jurisdictions and funding bodies globally, driving a rich, diverse and international evidence base.1–4 Specifically, evidence shows that involving patients and the public throughout the research process (ie, from research conceptualisation to dissemination) improves the research process and outcomes, such as the relevancy, credibility and effectiveness of knowledge being produced.5–8 For example, a systematic review by Baldwin et al7 evaluated the impact of involving older adults in health and social care research as coresearchers and found an array of benefits for the academic researchers (eg, new learning from coresearchers experiences and perspectives), older adult coresearchers (eg, gaining new knowledge and skills), as well as benefits for the research quality and impact (eg, richer data). Another systematic review and meta-analysis by Crocker et al9 found that PPI in clinical trials was beneficial for both enrolment and retention rates in the trial.9 This influx of evidence has underscored the impact of meaningful PPI as an approach that enhances the research quality and applications while also ensuring heterogeneity in study designs and practices for public and patient care.2 4 10

Given the heterogeneity in study design and practice, as well as the context-specific nature of PPI, a variety of approaches to PPI exist. Common approaches include advisory boards, stakeholder groups or patient panels that are active, ideally, throughout all stages of research. However, this involvement process is not easily achievable when recruitment strategies are neither effectively carried out nor well understood.11 12 As described by Morgan et al,11 variation exists pertaining to how PPI representatives are selected, including the characteristics of these individuals. For example, those who are members of ethnic minorities, or have a lower socioeconomic status are deemed ‘harder to reach’ populations and thus may not be represented.11 13 This is especially problematic for research in health prevention where health inequalities are often identified, and therefore, ‘harder to reach’ populations are a primary target for interventions.11 Indeed, more creative approaches that may require additional time and resources to recruit such populations proactively may be required, which in itself can act as a barrier to meaningful involvement.11 14

There is a lack of comprehensive guidance available for the recruitment of patients, carers and members of the public for advisory boards, groups and panels across study designs and contexts.12 For example, Conneely et al15 proposed and comprehensively described the use of a partnership-focused framework to establish a stakeholder panel, but within the context of older adults and health services research. Additionally, Arkind et al16 described the challenges and lessons learnt in recruiting individuals to their Oregon Community Health Information Network patient engagement panel (PEP).

Thus, although studies that discuss varying strategies for recruiting patients, carers and members of the public for advisory boards, groups and panels exist, the breadth of and optimal strategies for recruiting for such have not yet been extensively explored.12

Purpose of conducting the scoping review

As described in the protocol,12 this scoping review sought to explore the breadth of approaches employed for involving patients, carers and members of the public as partners on health research advisory boards, groups or panels within the context of health and social care services research. Preliminary searches identified papers discussing selection and engagement strategies in PPI specific to stakeholder groups and advisory groups, but did not reveal any literature reviews outlining the breadth of, and optimal strategies used for such recruitment.17–19 Therefore, the objectives of this scoping review were to:

Review question

What strategies are used to recruit patients, carers, and other members of the public as co-researchers, peer-researchers, steering committee members, or research advisors, to advisory boards, groups, or panels within participatory, public, and patient involved health research?

For the purpose of this review, we defined ‘recruitment’ as the identification of and invitation to join boards, panels and committees as project partners or advisors, not as study participants, enrollees or subjects. Research partners include but are not limited to, ‘knowledge users, decision-makers, stakeholders, end-users, service-users, consumers, community members, community of interest, citizens, industry, groups, funders engaged in the research process.’20

Search strategy

We began by conducting a preliminary search in MEDLINE (PubMed) to ensure the search terms that would be used across all databases adequately captured the keywords and subheadings. Iteratively, we searched and revised our search terms to capture the most appropriate body of literature. Next, we sought input from a broad community of experts via formal and informal conversations. This was important for framing our search criteria and helped to identify additional keywords for consideration. These search terms were then applied across all of the databases explored, including Scopus, Medline, PubMed, Web of Science, CINAHL, Cochrane Library and PsycINFO. We also included grey literature captured via Scopus, such as theses/dissertations and reports. Finally, we reviewed the references from articles selected for the review and extracted any relevant articles.12 The search strategy for all databases is outlined in online supplemental file 2. The searches for each database were conducted in July 2021 and were limited to articles between 1995 and 2021 (described in the eligibility criteria above). For the purposes of this scoping review, we reproduced terms used in the source material, as defined by the authors. This was done to ensure we captured the authors’ intention of using the term without imposing our overarching definitions, particularly given the conceptual variability.

Source of evidence screening and selection

The resulting literature from the seven databases explored was uploaded to the systematic review software ‘DistillerSR’ (https://www.evidencepartners.com/products/distillersr-systematic-review-software/), where duplicates were removed. Following this step, two independent reviewers screened the articles by title and abstract (MG and EK) and then at full-text (MG and CM) based on the predetermined eligibility criteria outlined in table 1.

The reviewers (MG, EK and CM) met at multiple stages throughout the reviewing process to discuss any discrepancies and changes in eligibility criteria that emerged. Any existing discrepancies regarding which articles to include or exclude, or why were deemed a ‘conflict’ and were sent to a third independent reviewer (JS) for arbitration. See figure 1 in the Results section below for the PRISMA22 flow diagram, which includes the finalised numbers of what was included and excluded at each stage of the review process.

Data extraction

The data extraction form was developed following the guidance of Peters et al,23 with additional context and concept-specific questions aligning with our research objectives and from informal discussions with other researchers in the field. Patient and public involvement

Before developing the protocol, informal consultations took place with community, organisational and other expert partners to guide the development of the research objectives, research question and the selection of search terms used in preliminary searches. However, patients and/or members of the public were subsequently not directly involved in the execution of this review.

Results

Search results

The search strategy used generated a total of 7439 references, with 3190 duplicates identified. A total of 4249 references were eligible for screening at the title and abstract stage, 4035 references were removed as they did not meet our eligibility criteria described in table 1. This left 214 articles to be screened at full text. A total of 163 articles were excluded after being reviewed at full text. The reasons for excluded articles can be found in figure 1, with the most common reason for exclusion being that it was a poster presentation or abstract (35%). We extracted data from the remaining 51 sources.

Inclusion of sources of evidence

The literature was predominantly based in the UK (35%, n=18),24–41 USA (24%, n=12)16 42–52 and Canada (20%, n=10).53–62 The remaining studies (22%, n=11) were based in multiple countries (8%, n=4),63–66 Australia (4%, n=2),67 68 Denmark (4%, n=2), Ireland (2%, n=1),69 Japan (2%, n=1)70 and Norway (2%, n=1).71 Almost all the literature were published since 2015 (96%, n=49), while the remaining 4%, n=2 were published in 201239 and 2006.37 Of the extracted literature, 69% (n=35) were empirical, and the remaining 31% (n=16) were non-empirical. Objective #2: distinguish the varying terminology for involvement (ie, panels, boards, individual)

The terms used for involvement varied across the extracted literature with only four terms being used more than once. Specifically, ‘advisory group’25 46 51 and ‘patient and public involvement group’35 66 74 were each used in three studies, respectively, ‘patient engagement panel’ was used in two studies,16 43 and ‘public and patient involvement panel’ in two studies.27 39 All other terms were distinct, although slightly, such as ‘advisory group’25 46 51 or ‘research advisory group’.24

Objective #3: explore the benefits, facilitators, barriers and ethical issues of the identified recruitment strategies

Below we present a summary of the findings for objective #3 with further details of the benefits, facilitators, barriers and ethical issues illustrated via extracted quotes from sources mentioned found in online supplemental file 4.

Benefits

Of the included studies (n=51), 25% n=13 sources16 24 27 37 38 44 47 49 50 61 63 65 73 reported benefits from their chosen recruitment strategies specifically in reference to human networks (16%, n=8)16 37 44 49 50 61 63 65 or previously involved in work (12%, n=6).24 27 38 44 47 73 One source44 discussed benefits for both human networks and those previously involved in work. When individuals were recruited because they were previously involved in the work, listed benefits surrounded familiarity with the research process. Individuals drew on this when contributing to research development and planning24 and this helped to ensure the appropriate representation of both the condition as well as diversity of panel members being explored.44

When individuals were recruited from human networks, benefits discussed were surrounding the ability to purposively sample. This ensured diversity in experience37 and opinions,50 while recruiting highlight motivated individuals who were already engaged in their communities, enhancing the collaborative process.49

Facilitators

Fourteen per cent (n=7) of sources26 27 36 38 48 61 72 discussed facilitators of their chosen recruitment strategies. The most common facilitators discussed were pertaining to human networks (6%, n=3),26 36 61 and previously involved in work (4%, n=2).27 38 Some of the networks included clinic contacts26 61 or patients from a clinic with a specific condition (ie, spinal inflection clinic patients).36 In addition, Grier et al36 circulated emails to a specific network (ie, the foundation trust members), which they noted had the biggest numerical response compared with other recruitment strategies used. For instance, Grier et al36 also used ‘other’ recruitment strategies, including a letter to clinic patients with spinal infection. Høeg et al,72 circulated flyers, which were useful for explaining the role of the patient panel. Finally, Portalupi et al48 discussed the phone and a phone guide as a recruitment facilitator, where ‘the (phone) guide (when recruiting) was designed to introduce the concept of the research advisory panel, learn about candidates’ experience with and illness, gauge interest in helping to steer the nascent programme and identify individuals who are authentic and empowered rather than advocates in order to avoid conflict of interest.’48

Barriers

Within sources (18%, n=9)24 26 33 40 48 50 54 66 72 that discussed barriers of their chosen recruitment strategies, the most common recruitment strategy referenced was human networks (14%, n=7).26 33 40 48 50 54 72 One source54 discussed the difficulties with recruiting from certain networks for a specific health condition. In this instance, people living with HIV were recruited and some candidates withdrew due to their challenges with mental health. Another source72 underscored barriers to recruitment from the clinical network setting (ie, patients in the practice). Specifically, the barriers in recruitment with conflicting nurse schedules and ability to approach patients before they left the practice,72 as well as the lack of infrastructure to support recruitment, especially for certain medical conditions (ie, pressure ulcers) that do not neatly fit into existing charitable or national structures.26

Another study highlighted issues with inviting people from a network without a formal recruitment process,33 or being very selective of criteria to take part in the panel, such as needing panel members who had relevant experience across diverse contexts.50 Salmon et al40 discussed issues of recruiting from certain networks, where managers acted as gatekeepers to recruiting women through existing community groups. Further, two sources26 66 considered barriers to recruit those who were previously involved in work/research. These barriers included not being able to participate given the nature of their health condition26, or where previous involvement may have impacted how people report their understanding in a trial, that is, rating their understanding as higher because of their investment in it, which could impact the findings, such as minimising differences between those in the intervention and control groups.66 Finally, one study24 considered the barriers of recruiting through flyers, including time to recruit, noting recruitment in this way took too much time.

Ethical issues

Six per cent (n=3) of sources26 72 73 reported ethical considerations/issues from their chosen recruitment strategies. Two studies26 72 discussed ethical issues/considerations regarding recruiting from human networks. Specifically, Nixon et al26 noted issues with clinical teams approaching their own patients to recruit, potentially blurring boundaries between clinician and researcher roles which they attempted to mitigate by involving a recruitment officer to act as a neutral party.

In addition, Høeg et al72 discussed recruiting those who may be vulnerable from their networks, such as those with poor hearing, who may not have fully understood the implications of participating which was realised and then addressed by the nurse doing the recruitment. Høeg et al72 also highlighted the issue of breaching study protocol when recruiting patients, specifically when the nurse was carrying out the recruitment process. Further, Nissen et al73 discussed ethical concerns with recruiting from those previously involved in work/research who could only be contacted as part of that study/work and not afterwards. Nissen et al73 also highlighted the need to recruit relevant individuals as ethically challenging given only assigned doctors are granted access to patient medical records to identify potentially relevant patients for recruitment.

Objective #4: determine if the individual recruitment strategies used were to address issues of representation or bias

Forty-one per cent (n=21) of studies discussed recruitment strategies or considerations to address issues of representation or bias. The recruitment strategies and discussed strategies for addressing issues of representation or bias are explored below. Further details from sources discussing recruitment strategies used to address issues of representation or bias, specifically extracted quotes from sources, can be found in online supplemental file 5.

Human networks

Issues of representation or bias were most frequently discussed (25%, n=13) regarding recruitment via human networks. Given the breadth of discussion in this area, we organised findings into categories outlined below.

Purposefully recruiting

Sixteen per cent (n=8) of the sources16 27 37 40 45 49 50 65 discussed recruiting in a purposeful way to reach populations that were representative, including difficult to reach populations. Such populations could include those living in a rural area, for example, where Brys et al45 focused their recruitment to ensure those who do not typically engage with researchers had the opportunity to take part regardless of their geography.

Arkind et al16 also highlighted that those who self-selected to take part in their PEP had the motivation and direction which provided the necessary momentum for them to lead on recruitment and purposefully recruit, for example, identifying recruitment outlets. Although these strategies were helpful in diversifying the PEP, authors16 noted the persistent challenges in recruiting specific groups like those experiencing homelessness, who are single parents, who are living with a disability, or who have a low socioeconomic status.16

Six sources27 37 40 49 50 65 also recruited in a purposeful way with particular attention given to diversity in perspective and/or inclusion of marginalised groups. For instance, Wright et al37 contacted patient forums from 40 cancer networks in the UK, yet noted their struggles to recruit representatives from ethnic minoritised backgrounds or in the palliative stage of their illness.37 Meanwhile, Salmon et al40 purposefully liaised with local children centres in specific areas of Exeter and Bristol in the South West of England that served diverse ethnic and socioeconomic populations to seek broader involvement from women with different perspectives.40 Further, Brunsdon et al65 found through feedback of the steering group that a gap in representation existed and sought to purposefully recruit to fill these gaps via Twitter adverts and personal networks (eg, direct contacts).65

Evans et al27 also considered the challenges with recruiting what they termed ‘service users’ in their study who were recruited from ‘two Welsh networks: one supporting public involvement in research (Involving People) (and) the other coordinating support for people with experience of chronic conditions (the Long Terms Conditions Alliance-Cymru)’.27 Interested individuals self-selected, which authors27 discussed as challenging as they tend to be those who seek involvement opportunities, and thus, ‘may not be representative of the average patient or carer, even when their diagnosis or care experience is commonly shared’.27

Evette et al50 mentioned purposefully recruiting patients from three groups including; the American Chronic Pain Association (ACPA), Advocates for the Reform of Prescription Opioids (ARPO), and Group Health to ensure experiences were reflected on their advisory committee, especially those with diverse opinions pertaining to opioid management for chronic musculoskeletal pain. Authors50 also selected members ‘who could: work effectively in a group context (and) voice their opinions and concerns’.50 For ACPA and ARPO, the organisation’s director nominated patients based on the aforementioned criteria, while Group Health patients completed questionnaires pertaining to the desired criteria.50 The project manager then selected five individuals for an interview to learn more about their interest and group work experiences.50

Finally, Bridges et al49 recruited patient advocates for their patient action committee from a network called LUNGevity—‘a patient advocacy organisation in the USA that is committed to increasing quality of life and survivorship of patients with lung cancer’.49 Bridges et al49 also mentioned that they included only the patient perspective of those who survived lung cancer, highlighting that ‘engaging family members of deceased individuals could have provided (them) with a proxy for the perspective of patients for whom treatment might not have been as successful and whose experiences might therefore have been very different’.49

Recruitment in clinics

Another area of human network recruitment was via clinics (6%, n=3).54 56 61 This presented challenges for bias, such as a bias towards including those that are more highly educated or have a favourable attitude toward the clinic.56 Haesebaert et al56 recognised these issues of bias in the limitations of their pilot study underscoring the need for a more diversified sample in a future study. Meanwhile, Garnett et al61 discussed the benefits of a patient advisory council to ensure a formal mechanism for patients to provide feedback on clinic initiatives and quality improvement. Garnett et al61 further underscored how the focus for recruitment was on expertise and experience as opposed to representational diversity (eg, culture or sex) given ‘the priority (was) that (the) small group serve an advisory role for clinic leadership regarding clinic processes and innovation, rather than a purely representational or advocacy role’.61 Furthermore, Lessard et al54 recruited 10 individuals with HIV from community and clinical settings in Montreal (eg, AIDS Community Care Montreal and McGill University Health Centre) and noted that ‘more could (have been) done to reach and include marginalised individuals, especially recently diagnosed, young or disengaged people living with HIV, given that studies show marginalised people living with HIV are often willing to participate when asked’.54

Recruiting broadly

Eight per cent (n=4) of sources33 55 62 69 discussed ensuring broadness in recruitment strategies to help establish inclusiveness and representation. For instance, O’Donnell et al69 ‘aimed for an inclusive and wide representation of patient and public collaborators’69, which was done via advocacy and non-govermental organisations (NGOs) representing older adults living with a disability in the Irish health and social policy setting.69

Ogourtsova et al62 used purposeful recruitment techniques, including word of mouth and snow ball sampling, for recruiting patient advisory group members (ie, parents of children with disabilities). For addressing issues of lack of diversity in the patient advisory group, authors62 proposed ‘recruiting additional members with diverse characteristics’, which could be done by “broadening (their) current recruitment strategies to reach and attract fathers of disabled children.62”

A technique used by Needham et al55 was to recruit broadly across Canadian patient communities, ensuring diversity in professional backgrounds and personal experiences for their Canadian Cancer Trials Group network.55 The recruitment process was formal, including a position posting with specified role attributes, followed by a panel interview process.55

Finally, Slade et al33 discussed challenges to representation within their ‘Lived Experience Advisory Group’, which composed of individuals who were predominantly well educated and confident speaking in groups. Slade et al33 suggested a more representative approach would have been to advertise the role more publicly specifically to less represented groups by using a formal process, rather than approaching and inviting people.33

Previously involved in research

Similar categories were noted for 4% (n=2) of sources38 73 that recruited from individuals previously involved in research.

Nissen et al’s study was built on previous projects and recruited patient representatives that were participants in prior projects or from a local patient association.73 They highlighted the importance of finding patient representatives who were willing to engage in collaboration on the project and were selected. They also noted challenges in recruiting from marginalised populations, such as those from low-income and low socioeconomic status groups.73

Troya et al38 recruited patient public involve and engagement members from a previous study who had suggested the topic for the current study. Challenges were also mentioned in regard to recruiting minority groups, such as ‘people from ethnic minorities and lesbian, gay, bisexual and transsexual (LGBT) groups’38 whose voices were not captured in their study, yet also engage in self-harm behaviour, the topic of their research.38

Social media

Two sources35 65 explored using social media, specifically Facebook35 and Twitter,65 for recruiting to ensure a more diverse sample was reached. For instance, McMillan et al35 noted that they ‘attempted to obtain a more diverse sample by advertising the workshop through Facebook and posters around the local area’.35 Meanwhile, Brunsdon et al65 discussed Twitter as a way to fill a gap of representation in their recruitment approach for their Steering Group.

Other

Lastly, Grier et al36 touched on the challenges of the panel reflecting the true diversity of the local population in regard to ethnicity and socioeconomic status.

However, they did note the usefulness of an expression of interest form to ensure representativeness in the panel in terms of experience, age and background for the PPI panel.36

Summary of findings

In summary, the majority of the extracted literature that was empirical in nature had a clinical study focus (39%, n=14) which was not an RCT. This is not surprising as a lack of formal guidance exists pertaining to involvement in trials,75 as well as a lack of flexibility once the trial has begun.76 Further, just over half of the literature used more than one recruitment strategy, with the predominant recruitment strategy being human networks such as word of mouth,45 foundation affiliation,47 existing networks,37 58 clinics51 64 or personal contacts.65 This, as the dominant recruitment strategy, could be due to the broad nature of what was deemed appropriate for this category, creating a higher count of applicable studies.

Benefits, facilitators, barriers and ethical issues were almost always pertaining to recruiting via human networks or previous involvement in the work. This is not surprising as these were the predominant recruitment strategies used. Some key benefits when individuals were recruited because they were previously involved in the work were surrounding familiarity with the research process. Evans et al27 suggested this provided a sense of ‘confidence, research awareness and experience to enable them to effectively contribute to research activities’.27 Meanwhile, when individuals were recruited from human networks, benefits discussed were: (1) surrounding ability to purposively sample, ensuring diversity in both experience,37 opinions50 and (2) individuals who were already highly engaged in their community, and thus motivated, enhancing the engagement process.49

Barriers for recruitment predominantly via human networks were discussed in four ways. First, managers can act as community gatekeepers, especially when seeking individuals from different ethnic backgrounds or those who are socially diverse.40 Second, a lack of a formal recruitment process has been discussed as a barrier.33 Although guides do exist77–79 to aid in the recruitment process, a gap remains regarding the persisting barriers and challenges highlighted in this review, such as a lack of demographic diversity.50 73 Specifically, Evette et al50 recruited panel members reflecting their diversity of opinions and clinical experience and not about demographic diversity. Third, one’s clinical condition was deemed a barrier for both human networks and previously involved in work.26 54 In particular, a clinical condition could make it challenging for individuals to participate and could lead to attrition.54 Finally, although considered to be both a benefit and facilitator (as described above), recruitment via a clinical network can create barriers for recruitment. Notably, barriers involve scheduling conflicts with clinical staff schedules, and patients not turning up for appointments.72

The terms used for involvement varied across the extracted literature with only four terms being used more than once. Furthermore, considerations to address issues of representation or bias were commonly discussed in the literature (n=21, 41%), with human networks as the most predominant recruitment strategies explored when discussing issues of representation or bias. Recruitment via clinics was mentioned as an issue, inherently selecting the ‘usual-suspects’,80 most of which were highly educated.56 These individuals are often selected as they have more experience and expertise, and not for representational diversity.61 There were some suggestions made throughout the literature to combat this issue, often involving purposeful sampling specifically targeting select groups, for example, through specific advocacy organisations69 or specific centres relevant to the population of interest.37 40 Other suggestions included recruiting broadly, such as publicly, and not limiting recruitment geographically.16 45

Furthermore, important ethical challenges were also presented, specifically when recruiting via clinical networks. The issue of blurring of professional boundaries when clinicians or nurses recruited patients was discussed. Nixon et al26 highlighted that ‘clinical members of the team recruiting and supporting their own patients could potentially blur these boundaries and create unequal relationships within the team’.26

Discussion

Understanding the breadth of and optimal strategies for recruiting patients, carers and members of the public for advisory boards, groups and panels addresses an overt gap in the collaborative research space. This gap has left research teams grasping at straws when recruiting for such boards, groups and panels, ultimately hindering progress in the commencement and development of their PPI objectives. The intention of this review has been to begin bridging this gap. Indeed we explored the various strategies used to recruit patients, carers and other members of the public as coresearchers, peer-researchers, steering committee members or research advisors, to advisory boards, groups or panels within participatory and public and patient involved health research. Across the four research objectives, a few commonalities were presented along with considerations/recommendations for future research.

Commonalities across the different study contexts were seemingly more obvious across the noted barriers, ethical issues, recruitment strategies and challenges of representation or bias. Specifically, who was being recruited/volunteering for the panels, boards, and groups. It was evident that a lack of individuals from minority and often marginalised groups (eg, LGBTQI+, low socioeconomic status and/or ethnic minorities 38 73) existed. As highlighted by Reynolds et al80 those recruited/who get involved are often ‘the ‘usual suspects’ typically come from more advantaged groups in society, usually white and of higher socioeconomic status, often retired and from professional backgrounds, and thus have more capacity to become involved and articulate their views’. In turn, this has prompted a larger question of ‘why’? Although not the predominant focus of this review and an important area for future research, we can speculate.

Articulated in the seminal text by Wallerstein et al,81 we still work within contextual realities where health and social care inequities are on the rise, where structural racism exists at an institutional level (eg, healthcare82), and ultimately where years of research ‘abuse or misuse all too often hav(e) fostered mistrust of research within communities who faced ‘helicopter’ or ‘drive-by’ research when data is solicited, taken and not returned to the community’.83 Indeed, recruiting a truly diverse and representative patient and public panel, group or board will require more than just recruiting broadly62, more publicly33 or using several methods of recruitment.26

In addition to the challenges with ‘who’ has been recruited/getting involved, we also reflected on the challenges of the ‘how’ people are being recruited. Particularly, the ethical challenges presented when recruiting within the context of collaborative research approaches. For instance, when considering ethical principles and guidelines in participatory approaches to research there has been ‘specific challenges and opportunities that may not be adequately addressed by traditional institutional frameworks for ethical conduct in research’.84 For example, the blurring roles between researchers and those who contribute data.84 The issue of blurring of professional boundaries when clinicians or nurses recruited patients was discussed26, which presented potential issues in regard to the power dynamics between providers and patients, that can impede the participatory process.85 86 For instance, the inherent power statuses that exist within various healthcare structures can reinforce power imbalances between patients and paid professionals (eg, clinicians).87 Nixon et al26 sought to overcome these barriers by including a neutral PPI officer where the patient could go to for further information and discussion about the study, similar to the institutional ombudsperson regularly assigned by institutional review boards (IRBs). When discussing power dynamics in community-based participatory research (PR), Wallerstein et al86 highlighted that ‘core relational practices for challenging power inequities were identified as those that fostered deliberative process of reflection and bidirectional communication that fostered respect and mutual trust based on their actions with each other’.86

We also considered the lack of consistency in terminology used and the potential challenges this creates in terms of reporting and understanding the nature of involvement. As described by Macaulay88 ‘the terminology is vast and confusing and arising from the long evolution of PR from different countries, disciplines and with different research goals’. Although choice of terms is not necessarily crucial, the diversity of terms has implications for comprehensively searching and accessing the literature, as well as in practice for avoiding confusion among teams and PPI contributors. The term used should reflect the needs and values of the project and team and be culturally and contextually appropriate. For example, use of the term patient or user or community, etc, may vary based on culture, context, setting or need. Likewise, the use of panel, group, board or committee, etc, may also be a reflection of how the group self-identifies. Thus, as previously proposed by Macauley,88 partnerships should consider discussion surrounding terminology to help with clarity and agreement about which to use and when. Further, it would be useful to report the rationale for the terminology used when discussing and disseminating collaborative research. A lack of and consistency in reporting more generally is also warranted, underscored in this review by a clear lack of discussion pertaining to facilitators, benefits, barriers and ethical issues to be extracted from the captured literature. This is not a new issue, and a call for researchers to consider reporting in a consistent way was highlighted by Gilfoyle et al.5 Researchers should strongly consider utilising frameworks like the ‘Guidance for Reporting Involvement of Patients and the Public 2’ (GRIPP2),89 which would help address this gap.

Finally, the lack of reported use of social media as a recruitment strategy was surprising. Only 6 (12%) of studies25–27 35 36 65 used it, and only two sources35 65 explicitly discussed its use to reach a broader and more diverse sample. This was surprising given the uptake of social media by adults across age ranges in the last 17 years.90 A study in the USA found that 84% ages 18–29, 81% ages 30–49, 73% ages 50–64 and 45% ages 65 and older reported using at least one social media site in 2021.90 Thus, in the extracted literature, almost all of which was published since 2015 (96%, n=49), social media seemed to be an untapped resource for recruitment, presenting opportunities for future research.

Recommendations

Based on the findings from this review, the authors propose the following recommendations:

Although recruiting via clinics can be a tremendous resource, we recommend carefully considering who is doing the recruiting to avoid personal and/or professional biases and to ensure that professional boundaries are not being blurred. A third-party recruiter might be considered.

Think creatively about how to recruit. Reaching those who do not typically engage in research remains an issue. The use of social media outlets appears to be underused (or underreported) and we recommend their use for recruitment.

Strengths and limitations

This review addressed an important gap in the literature as the first review exploring the breadth of and optimal strategies used for recruiting patients, carers and members of the public to panels, boards and groups. Although various guides and tools exist to help in such recruitment,77–79 most of these are based on local experiences or strategies, and do not attempt to systematically identify and address commonly encountered barriers and difficulties. In addressing this gap, we have highlighted some important considerations and suggestions for current and future research in this space, while underscoring the need for further discussion and reporting surrounding recruitment strategies, particularly in addressing the most commonly encountered issues.

There are also some important limitations to discuss. First, recommendations are based on a limited sample of literature, and thus, may not be applicable to a wide range of contexts. Second, we excluded papers that did not discuss recruitment in title/abstract/keywords. However, if not mentioned in the abstract it is likely it was not a predominant focus of the paper. Further, we excluded studies discussing the recruitment of patients, carers and public as direct coresearchers, not to boards, panels or committees. This is because the extracted data contained few substantive examples of this type of involvement. We propose future research expand on this review to include patients, carers and public as coresearchers. Further, we recognise that the category ‘human networks’ may exhibit variation when considering different types of human networks (ie, professional, patient or carer networks). This could be further extrapolated in future study but was beyond the scope and resources of this review.

Conclusion

In conclusion, human networks and individuals previously involved in research were the most applied and discussed strategies of recruitment. A general lack of discussion pertaining to facilitators, benefits, barriers and ethical considerations of recruitment strategies was apparent. Still, the use of multiple strategies of recruitment was present in the majority of the literature. The issue of representation and bias, specifically involving vulnerable groups and more diverse groups, persists. Nevertheless, recruiting broadly and purposefully were strategies to help address this issue. The use of social media appeared to be an underused resource for recruitment and should be explored in future research. We also proposed that researchers consider reporting their recruitment strategies more explicitly to strengthen our understanding of optimal recruitment strategies in this space, leading to more guidance and concrete recommendations.
