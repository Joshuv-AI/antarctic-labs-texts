Spontaneous and chaperone‐assisted metal loading in the active site of protein phosphatase‐1
Authors: Gerd Van der Hoeven, Sarah Lemaire, Xinyu Cao, Zander Claes, Spyridoula Karamanou, Mathieu Bollen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractProtein phosphatase PP1 has two active‐site metals (Zn2+/Fe2+) that are essential for catalysis. However, when expressed in bacteria, PP1 has two Mn2+‐ions in its active site, indicating that the incorporation of Zn2+/Fe2+ depends on additional eukaryotic component(s). Here, we used purified, metal‐deficient PP1 to study metal incorporation. Fe2+ was incorporated spontaneously, but Zn2+ was not. Mn2+‐incorporation at physiological pH depended on the co‐expression of PP1 with PPP1R2 (Inhibitor‐2) or PPP1R11 (Inhibitor‐3), or a pre‐incubation of PP1 at pH 4. We also demonstrate that PPP1R2 and PPP1R11 are Zn2+‐binding proteins but are, by themselves, not able to load PP1 with Zn2+. Our data suggest that PPP1R2 and PPP1R11 function as metal chaperones for PP1 but depend on co‐chaperone(s) and/or specific modification(s) for the transfer of associated Zn2+ to PP1.Keywords: metal incorporation, metallo‐enzyme, PP1, PPP1R11, PPP1R2, protein phosphatase, RIPPO

Protein phosphatase 1 belongs to the PPP family of protein Ser/Thr phosphatases [1, 2]. It is broadly expressed in eukaryotes and catalyzes a sizeable fraction of all protein dephosphorylation events. PP1 has two metals in its active site that are essential for catalysis [3, 4, 5]. The active‐site metals activate a water molecule for nucleophilic attack of the phosphorus atom of the incoming phospho‐monoester substrate. In addition, these metals also bind to the negatively charged oxygens of the phosphate group, which positions the phosphorus atom for nucleophilic attack and increases its electrophilicity. The active‐site metals of native PP1 are Zn2+ and Fe2+ [6]. However, PP1 expressed in bacteria has two Mn2+‐ions in its active site [4, 7], despite the presence of Zn2+ and Fe2+ in the culture broth, indicating that the incorporation of native metals depends on additional eukaryotic component(s). Bacterially expressed, Mn2+‐loaded PP1 is active but has enzymic properties that are very different from those of native, Zn2+/Fe2+‐loaded PP1 [6, 8, 9, 10]. Thus, only bacterially expressed PP1 can dephosphorylate tyrosine‐phosphorylated proteins and is inhibited by vanadate, an inhibitor of authentic protein tyrosine phosphatases. In addition, only bacterially expressed PP1 is inhibited by metal chelation with EDTA, indicating that Mn2+ is more loosely bound than Zn2+/Fe2+.

In vivo, PP1 is always associated with 1 or 2 regulatory polypeptides or RIPPOs, for Regulatory‐Interactors‐of‐Protein‐Phosphatase‐One [11]. RIPPOs control associated PP1 through (a) occlusion of the active site or a substrate‐binding channel, (b) extension of a substrate‐binding groove, (c) recruitment of substrates via a dedicated domain, and/or (d) targeting to a specific subcellular compartment. The large majority of RIPPOs have an unstructured PP1‐binding domain with short linear motifs (SLiMs) that mediate binding to the catalytic domain of PP1. More than a dozen distinct PP1‐interacting SLiMs have already been identified and some of these SLiMs, such as the RVxF motif, are shared by many RIPPOs [1, 2]. Most RIPPOs combine several SLiMs to form a large, unique PP1‐interaction interface. Vertebrates express about 200 structurally unrelated RIPPOs, enabling the assembly of a huge diversity of highly selective PP1 holoenzymes. The three first evolved RIPPOs, referred to as ancient RIPPOs, are SDS22, PPP1R11/Inhibitor‐3 (R11) and PPP1R2/Inhibitor‐2 (R2), which have key functions in the assembly and stabilization of PP1 holoenzymes. SDS22 and R11 form an inactive complex with nascent PP1 [12, 13]. This ternary complex is a substrate for the AAA+‐type ATPase p97/VCP, which co‐extracts R11 and SDS22. During this process, released PP1 is transferred to a canonical RIPPO, thereby forming functional PP1 holoenzymes. R2 stabilizes a subset of mature PP1 holoenzymes by its simultaneous interaction with PP1 and an associated RIPPO (Lemaire et al., unpublished data). Preliminary data suggest that R2 and R11 may have an additional role in metal loading during PP1 biogenesis or recycling (reviewed in [13, 14]). Thus, R2 forms an inactive, heterodimeric complex with PP1 that can be re‐activated by transient phosphorylation of R2 at Thr73, but also by Mn2+ addition following the removal of R2 [15], indicating that the re‐activation of PP1:R2 involves metal incorporation. R11 is potentially linked to metal loading of nascent PP1 by its ability to bind Zn2+ via a tri‐cysteine sequence (CCC‐motif) that docks into the active site of PP1 [16].

There are no systematic studies on the mechanism by which metals are incorporated into the active site of PP1. Here, we report on spontaneous and R2/R11‐guided metal incorporation in the active site of bacterially expressed, metal‐deficient human PP1. We show that Fe2+ is incorporated spontaneously, while Mn2+ incorporation at physiological pH depends on the prior co‐expression of PP1 with R2 or R11, or a pre‐incubation of PP1 at pH 4. We also demonstrate that R2 and R11 are both Zn2+‐binding proteins that are incapable, however, of independently transferring associated Zn2+ to the active site of PP1.

Protein expression and purification

Human PP1α, N‐terminally His‐tagged and C‐terminally intein‐tagged, was cloned into the pETDuet‐1 vector. HA‐tagged R11 (both WT & RVxFm), Myc‐tagged R2 (both WT & HYNEm), His‐tagged NIPP1‐(143–225) and Flag‐tagged SDS22 were cloned into the pRSFDuet‐1 vector. PP1α (pETDuet‐1) was expressed alone or co‐expressed with a RIPPO (pRSFDuet‐1) in BL21 Gold cells at 37 °C in LB medium with 50 μg·mL−1 ampicillin, 50 μg·mL−1 kanamycin and 1 mm EDTA until an OD600 of ≈0.6. Protein expression was induced at 18 °C by addition of 1 mm isopropyl β‐d‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). Cells were pelleted by centrifugation for 15 min at 6000 g and lysed with 10 volumes 20 mm Tris at pH 7.4, 0.3 m NaCl, 0.2% Tween‐20, and 5 μg·mL−1 leupeptin. After sonication, the lysates were cleared by centrifugation for 20 min at 15 000 g and the supernatant was loaded for 1 h at 4 °C on chitin beads. The beads were washed three times with 10 column volumes of TBS and the Intein‐tag was cleaved by overnight incubation in 2 column volumes of TBS containing 200 mm DTT and leupeptin. The beads were pelleted by centrifugation and the supernatant was supplemented with 60% glycerol and stored at −20 °C.

His‐tagged proteins for ITC experiments (human His‐R11, bovine His‐NIPP1 and mouse His‐R2 fragments) were cloned into the pET16b vector and expressed in BL21 Gold cells at 37 °C in LB medium with 100 μg·mL−1 ampicillin until an OD600 of ≈0.6. Protein expression was induced at 18 °C with 1 mm IPTG. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation for 15 min at 6000 g and lysed with 10 volumes 6 m urea, 0.5 m NaCl, 0.5% Triton X‐100, and protease inhibitors (1 mm PMSF, 1 mm benzamidine, 5 μg·mL−1 leupeptin). Cell lysis was completed with one freeze‐thawing cycle. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation for 20 min at 15 000 g and the supernatant was loaded for 1 h at 4 °C on HisPur™ Ni‐NTA Agarose. The beads were washed with 20 column volumes of a buffer containing 20 mm Tris at pH 7.9, 0.5 m NaCl and 50 mm imidazole, and the retained proteins were eluted in the same buffer but with 400 mm imidazole. The eluate was dialyzed overnight in 20 mm Tris at pH 7.4, 0.5 m NaCl and 0.5 mm TCEP. The purified proteins were either used immediately (ITC experiments) or supplemented with 60% glycerol and stored at −80 °C. The purity of the proteins was verified by Coomassie staining after SDS/PAGE (4–12% Bis‐Tris gels, NuPAGE Invitrogen).

Glycogen phosphorylase was purified from rabbit skeletal muscle and radioactively phosphorylated as previously described [17].

Characterization of purified PP1‐EDTA and PP1‐Mn2+

Freshly prepared PP1‐EDTA and PP1‐Mn2+ were injected on a Superdex‐75 column equilibrated in TBS. The absorbance at 280 nm was measured (Fig. S1A) and 0.5 mL fractions were collected for activity assays (Fig. S1B). To determine melting temperatures (T m; Fig. S1C), PP1‐EDTA and PP1‐Mn2+ were diluted in the presence of 0.3 mg·mL−1 denatured BSA (5 min at 90 °C). For PP1‐Mn2+ this mixture was supplemented with 1 mm MnCl2. Diluted protein mixtures were divided over PCR strips and heated on a Biometra‐T professional thermocycler for 3 min, using a temperature gradient of 41–80 °C. A reference sample was kept at 30 °C. Phosphatase activities were measured at pH 7 after a pre‐incubation at pH 4 in the presence of MnCl2 and using DiFMUP as substrate (fluorescence readout).

Trypsin treatment

500 ng of purified PP1‐EDTA, PP1‐Mn2+, R2 or R11 were incubated for 15 min at 37 °C in the absence or presence of trypsin (2.5 μg·mL−1). Sample buffer was added and after boiling samples were separated on a 10% gel, using MOPS as running buffer (Fig. S3). Proteins were visualized using Coomassie stain.

In vitro metal loading of PP1‐EDTA

PP1‐EDTA was incubated at room temperature in 20 mm Tris at pH 7.0 or 20 mm NaOAc/HOAc at pH 4.0, supplemented with 1 mg·mL−1 BSA, 0.1 m NaCl, 5 mm DTT, 0.05% Tween20, and the indicated metals. The added concentrations of MnCl2 (1 mm), FeCl2 (1 mm) and ZnSO4 (5 μm) were chosen based on their maximal effect on PP1‐EDTA activity in dose–response experiments. After 15 min, the pH was neutralized by addition of a buffer containing 20 mm Tris at pH 7.0 or 100 mm MES/Tris at pH 7.5, supplemented with 1 mg·mL−1 BSA, 0.1 m NaCl, 5 mm DTT and 0.5% Tween20.

Immunoblotting

SDS/PAGE was performed with 4–12% Bis‐Tris gels in MOPS buffer and the separated proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes using wet transfer in 50 mm Tris and 50 mm boric acid for a minimum of 2 h at 40 V. Membranes were blocked by 5% Milk in TBS supplemented with 0.05% Tween20. Primary antibodies were diluted in blocking reagent and incubated overnight at 4 °C while gently shaking. After washing the membranes three times with TBS and 0.05% Tween20, HRP‐coupled secondary antibodies were diluted in blocking reagent and incubated with the blot for 1 h at room temperature. Subsequently, the membranes were washed three more times and the signal was visualized on an imagequant LAS 4000 system using ECL reagent.

Phosphatase activity assays

Fluorescent phosphatase activity assays with 6,8 difluoro‐4‐methylumbelliferyl phosphate (DiFMUP) were performed after a trypsin treatment (125 μg·mL−1 for 5 min at 30 °C) to remove associated RIPPOs. During the assay DiFMUP and PP1‐EDTA were present at final concentrations of 40 μm and 0.25 nm, respectively. Reactions were carried out at 30 °C in white, 384‐well, flat‐bottom microplates (Greiner) on a Tecan Spark® reader. The reaction product was quantified at an excitation of 358 nm and emission of 455 nm, and measured every 4 min for 28 min. Colorimetric phosphatase activity assays with H3T3ph peptide (AR‐pT‐KQTARKS) or DiFMUP as substrate were carried out in clear, 384‐well, flat‐bottom microplates (Greiner). These substrates and PP1‐EDTA were present at a final concentration of 300 μm and 0.75 nm, respectively, in an assay volume of 20 μL. After 30 min at 37 °C, the reactions were stopped by addition of 40 μL of BIOMOL® Green, and free phosphate was quantified after a further incubation for 30 min at room temperature with a Tecan Spark® reader at 650 nm. Phosphatase activity assays with radioactively labeled glycogen phosphorylase a as substrate were performed as previously described [17].

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC)

All ITC metal‐binding experiments were performed at 22 °C on a MicroCal ITC 200 microcalorimeter (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA). The indicated protein was loaded in the cell at a concentration of 10–20 μm, the metal in the syringe at a 10‐fold (for R11 and NIPP1) or 50‐fold (for R2) higher concentration than the protein. The metals were injected 20 times with a 160‐s spacing interval while stirring at 750 r.p.m. To account for heat generation of metal addition, all experiments were corrected by subtraction with values from a control experiment using buffer in the cell and metal in the syringe, and carried out under the same experimental conditions. Analysis of the data was performed on microcal origin (OriginLab, Norhthampton, MA, USA) software version 7. A NonLinear Curve Fitting was used to fit a theoretical curve to the obtained data and to derive the fitting parameters.

Results

Metal incorporation in bacterially expressed human PP1

Human His‐tagged PP1α was expressed in bacteria in the presence of the metal‐chelator EDTA (PP1‐EDTA) to preclude metal incorporation in the active site. Size‐exclusion chromatography showed that PP1‐EDTA migrated as a protein of 37–39 kDa, corresponding to the calculated mass of PP1α (Fig. S1A,B). This showed that PP1‐EDTA was not aggregated. Moreover, the melting temperature of PP1‐EDTA was similar to that of PP1 prepared in the presence of Mn2+ (PP1‐Mn2+), indicating that it was not (partially) unfolded (Fig. S1C). Affinity‐purified PP1‐EDTA was assayed at pH 7 with three different substrates, namely a small‐molecule substrate that only interacts with the active site (DiFMUP), a peptide substrate (residues 1–10 of histone H3 phosphorylated at Thr3, H3T3ph) and a protein substrate (glycogen phosphorylase a). As expected, PP1‐EDTA was completely inactive with all three tested substrates (Fig. 1A–C). We subsequently used these phosphatase activity assays at pH 7 as a readout for in vitro metal incorporation in the active site of PP1‐EDTA. PP1‐EDTA remained inactive after Mn2+ addition at pH 7. However, we serendipitously found that PP1‐EDTA could be activated by Mn2+ addition after a pre‐incubation of the phosphatase at pH 4 (Fig. 1A–C). Importantly, while loading of PP1‐EDTA with Mn2+ depended on a pre‐incubation of the phosphatase at pH 4, it did not matter whether Mn2+ was already added during incubation of PP1‐EDTA at pH 4 or only during the subsequent phosphatase assay at pH 7 (Fig. S2). PP1‐EDTA was also activated by Fe2+ addition at pH 4, and even better at pH 7. Activation of PP1‐EDTA by Mn2+ or Fe2+ is further evidence that purified PP1‐EDTA was properly folded. Finally, PP1‐EDTA was not re‐activated at all by Zn2+ addition after pre‐incubation of the phosphatase at either pH 4 or pH 7. These results showed that Fe2+, in contrast to Zn2+, binds spontaneously to the active site of PP1‐EDTA, and that Mn2+ is only spontaneously loaded into the active site of purified PP1‐EDTA after a pre‐incubation of the phosphatase at pH 4, indicating that Mn2+ loading depended on a prior conformational change.

Co‐expression of PP1‐EDTA and PPP1R2 or PPP1R11 enables Mn2+‐loading at pH 7

Next, we examined whether Mn2+ incorporation in the active site of bacterially expressed PP1‐EDTA is affected by co‐expression with RIPPOs. While separately expressed PP1‐EDTA pre‐incubated at pH 7 could not be activated by Mn2+ addition, PP1‐EDTA co‐expressed with either wildtype (WT) R2 or R11 was activated by Mn2+ after prior pre‐incubation at pH 7 (Fig. 2A,B). In the latter experiments, purified PP1‐EDTA was assayed after trypsinolysis to destroy co‐purified R2 and R11, without affecting the integrity and activity of the catalytic domain ([18]; Fig. S3). The stimulatory effect of co‐expressed R2/R11 on Mn2+ incorporation at pH 7 was lost after mutation (m) of R2/R11 in key PP1‐anchoring motifs. More specifically, R2 was mutated in its HYNE sequence that docks to the active site of PP1 (147HYNE150 → AAAA; R2‐HYNEm), while R11 was mutated in its PP1‐binding RVxF motif (40KVEW43 → KAEA; R11‐RVxFm). The latter data demonstrated that the effect of co‐expression of PP1 with R2/R11 on subsequent Mn2+ loading was critically dependent on a direct PP1:R2/R11 interaction. This effect was also specific for R2 and R11, as it was barely detectable after co‐expression of PP1‐EDTA with another ancient RIPPO (SDS22) or a canonical RIPPO (NIPP1). These data indicated that R2 and R11 maintain co‐expressed PP1‐EDTA in bacteria in a state, equivalent to that of purified PP1‐EDTA at pH 4, that enables spontaneous Mn2+ incorporation. Our findings provide further evidence for a role of R2 and R11 as metal chaperones for PP1 (see Introduction). Importantly, the addition of R2 or R11 to separately expressed PP1‐EDTA did not allow spontaneous Mn2+ incorporation at pH 7.0 (Fig. 2C), indicating that R2 and R11 cannot convert purified PP1‐EDTA to a Mn2+‐receptive state.

PPP1R2 and PPP1R11 are Zn2+‐binding proteins

Next, we examined to which extent R2 and R11 contribute to the incorporation of Zn2+ in the active site of PP1‐EDTA. R11 was recently identified as a Zn2+‐binding protein [16]. We confirmed these data using Isothermal Titration Calorimetry (ITC) assays (Fig. 3A) and also confirmed that R11 binding to Zn2+ was lost after mutation of its tri‐cysteine‐motif (60CCC62 → SSS) that docks to the active site of PP1 (Fig. 3B). In addition, we found that R2 also binds Zn2+ with a similar Kd, in the low micromolar range (Fig. 3C). However, NIPP1 did not bind Zn2+ (Fig. 3D), showing that Zn2+ binding is not a general property of RIPPOs. R2 did not bind to Mn2+ (Fig. 3E) or Fe2+ (Fig. 3F), demonstrating at least a partial selectively for metal‐ion binding. As R2 lacks a tri‐cysteine motif, we used deletion mutagenesis to map the region(s) of R2 that mediate binding to Zn2+ (Fig. 4A). ITC assays with the purified R2‐deletion mutants identified two distinct N‐terminal fragments (residues 1–50 and 73–123) that bind Zn2+ with a similar affinity as full‐length R2 (Fig. 4B–F), indicating that R2 has at least two Zn2+‐binding sites. Alanine mutation of histidines and acidic residues within these fragments (8H → A; 37DEE39 → AAA; 48DE49 → AA; 76H → A + 81DDED84 → AAAA) did not affect their binding affinity for Zn2+ (Fig. 4G–J), indicating that Zn2+‐binding is not mediated by these charged residues. Hence, the detailed molecular mechanism of Zn2+ binding to R2 remains unclear. Finally, we examined whether the co‐expression of PP1 and either R2 or R11 enabled the spontaneous incorporation of Zn2+ in purified PP1‐EDTA, as detected for Mn2+ (Fig. 2A). However, this was not the case (not illustrated). Hence, our data showed that R2 and R11 are Zn2+‐binding proteins, but are not able to independently transfer associated Zn2+ to the active site of PP1.

Discussion

Here, we have explored in vitro metal incorporation into the active site of purified PP1‐EDTA. As metals are essential for catalysis, we used phosphatase activity assays as a readout for successful metal incorporation. PP1 is only catalytically active when both metal‐binding sites are occupied, implying that activation of PP1‐EDTA by metal addition only reports on the loading of both sites. Two Mn2+ ions were incorporated spontaneously when PP1‐EDTA was pre‐incubated at pH 4, but not at pH 7 (Fig. 1). We speculate that a pre‐incubation of PP1‐EDTA at pH 4 induces a conformational change that enables spontaneous Mn2+ incorporation. This Mn2+‐receptive conformation may mimic PP1 during or shortly after its translation in bacteria when Mn2+ is spontaneously incorporated. Intriguingly, Mn2+ could be spontaneously incorporated in purified PP1‐EDTA without prior pre‐incubation at pH 4 when it was co‐expressed with either R2 or R11, indicating that these RIPPOs maintain PP1‐EDTA in a Mn2+‐receptive state. At present, it is not clear what this Mn2+‐receptive state entails as the conformation of free and R2/R11‐complexed PP1 are largely identical [16, 19], indicating that it may involve a subtle conformational change.

Fe2+ was incorporated spontaneously in PP1‐EDTA pre‐incubated at pH 4 and even more at pH 7 (Fig. 1). As this endowed PP1‐EDTA with catalytic activity, we can conclude that Fe2+ filled up both metal‐binding sites. These data are in accordance with a recent report showing spontaneous Fe2+ incorporation in bacterially expressed PP1 when the culture medium was supplemented with an excess (0.65 mm) of Fe2+, in the absence of added Mn2+ [20]. Together, these data demonstrate that Fe2+ loading of PP1 occurs spontaneously in nascent and mature PP1, and does not need a specific metal chaperone. In contrast, we have not been able to activate purified PP1‐EDTA by Zn2+ addition, even after co‐expression with R2 or R11 (Fig. 1). This was an unexpected finding, in particular since R2 and R11 are both Zn2+‐binding proteins and interact with the active site of PP1 (Figs 3 and 4). We hypothesize that the loading of the active site of PP1 with Zn2+ depends on a hitherto unknown co‐chaperone or an allosteric/covalent modification of PP1 or R2/R11 that enables the transfer of R2/R11‐associated Zn2+ to the active site. Zn2+ has been identified as metal 1 in the active site of PP1 and the loading of this metal during the early steps of PP1 biogenesis is prevented by associated SDS22 [21]. Hence, Zn2+ loading emerges as a rate‐limiting step for PP1 activation and likely occurs during or shortly after p97/VCP‐mediated co‐extraction of R11 and SDS22 from nascent PP1. Further investigations are needed to delineate the exact contribution of R2/R11‐associated Zn2+ to the activation of nascent PP1 or mature PP1 that has lost its catalytic‐site Zn2+ ion.
