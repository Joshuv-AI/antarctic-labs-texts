Source Apportionment and Health Risk Assessment of Heavy Metals in Groundwater Around Typical Karst Region of the Lower Yellow River, China
Authors: Guan Guomeng, Liu Chunwei, Xu Qingyu, Yu Dalu, Ma Hekuan, Qi Huan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe Pingyin‐Changqing section of the Yellow River serves as an important groundwater recharge area in the lower reaches of the Yellow River, and its groundwater quality safety holds significant importance for the regional ecology and the health of its residents. This study systematically collected groundwater samples from this area, detected concentrations of six heavy metals (Mn, Zn, Ni, Pb, As, and Cd), and comprehensively applied mathematical statistics, spatial mapping analysis, source apportionment, and health risk assessment methods to systematically investigate the characteristics, distribution patterns, source mechanisms, and health risks of groundwater heavy metals in the region. The results indicate that (1) the mean concentrations of heavy metals in groundwater of the study area, in descending order, are Mn > Zn > Ni > Pb > As > Cd; compared against the Class III standard limits of the National Standard for Groundwater Quality, Mn shows the highest exceedance rate, followed by Pb, with exceedance rates of 18% and 5%, respectively; (2) heavy metal concentrations exhibit considerable spatial variability, with Mn exceedance points uniformly distributed in upstream and downstream areas on both banks of the Yellow River, while Pb concentrations demonstrate higher values downstream than upstream, with exceedance points mainly concentrated in the junction of Qihe and Dong'e; (3) sources of groundwater heavy metal pollution are identified as follows: Zn, Ni, and Cd primarily originate from different industrial sources, Pb mainly from traffic sources, As primarily from agricultural sources, and Mn predominantly from natural sources; (4) groundwater ingestion was identified as the dominant exposure pathway. Pb was the major contributor to noncarcinogenic risk, whereas As was the primary contributor to carcinogenic risk. Children exhibited higher noncarcinogenic risk levels than adults, reflecting their greater susceptibility to heavy metal exposure. Although both carcinogenic and noncarcinogenic risks remained within acceptable limits overall, relatively elevated risk values were concentrated at the junction of Changqing, Pingyin, Dong'e, and Qihe. Through the technical route of “pollution identification—spatial mapping—source apportionment—risk assessment,” this study provides a scientific basis for groundwater safety protection in the Yellow River Basin. It is recommended to strengthen dynamic monitoring of Pb pollution and implement comprehensive prevention and control measures in high‐risk areas of this region.

Introduction

Groundwater, as an essential freshwater resource, occupies a crucial position in the global water supply structure, especially in the densely populated North China Plain, where it serves as a major source of water for domestic use, agricultural irrigation, and industrial purposes (Foster and Chilton 2003; Jiang et al. 2019). With the rapid development of industrialization, urbanization, and agricultural activities, groundwater is facing increasing pollution threats, among which heavy metal pollution has attracted considerable attention due to its high toxicity, persistence, and bioaccumulation potential (Rezaei et al. 2019; Hassen et al. 2016). Numerous studies have demonstrated that long‐term consumption of groundwater contaminated with heavy metals (such as arsenic, cadmium, lead, and chromium) may lead to a variety of chronic health effects, including cancer, neurological damage, and hepatic and renal dysfunction (Vaishaly et al. 2015; Sharma and Kumar 2025).

The health risk assessment model developed by the US Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA), serving as a core technical approach for identifying pollution hazards and quantifying pollution risks, has been extensively applied in heavy metal pollution research in groundwater and soil (Zhang et al. 2021; Wcisło and Bronder 2022; Lupu et al. 2026). This framework, through the following four key components: hazard identification, exposure assessment, dose–response assessment, and risk characterization can effectively evaluate carcinogenic and noncarcinogenic risks to populations exposed to pollutants via drinking water intake and dermal contact (USEPA 2011). In recent years, this model, combined with Monte Carlo simulation, principal component analysis, and other methods, has been applied to groundwater heavy metal pollution assessment in various regions of China, such as the Yellow River Basin, northern Zhejiang, and Wuhan, providing important scientific basis for groundwater environmental management and public health protection (Liu et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2025; Xiang et al. 2022).

The Pingyin–Changqing section of the Yellow River is located in western Shandong Province, situated in the alluvial plain of the lower Yellow River. Numerous groundwater source areas are distributed along both banks of the Yellow River, and groundwater constitutes one of the primary drinking water sources for local residents. In recent years, intensive industrial and agricultural activities in this region, coupled with substantial fertilizer and pesticide application and untreated wastewater discharge from small industrial enterprises, have intensified environmental pressures on groundwater (Zhang et al. 2025; Tian et al. 2025; Tong et al. 2022). According to groundwater pollution risk assessment results for Jinan City, the Changqing urban area‐Xiaoli Town‐An Town in Pingyin County, influenced by industrial and mining enterprises, has been classified as a high‐risk zone for karst groundwater pollution (Gao et al. 2020); nitrate analysis and water supply source area investigations in Dong'e County jointly indicate that shallow groundwater in this locality has suffered varying degrees of pollution from agricultural production (Sheng et al. 2025; Yu, Lv, et al. 2023). Although preliminary investigations of groundwater quality in this region have been conducted (Chen et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2023), a systematic assessment of heavy metal pollution characteristics, sources, and potential human health risks in the cross‐municipal Pingyin‐Changqing‐Dong'e‐Qihe groundwater system remains limited. Therefore, based on field sampling and laboratory analysis combined with the USEPA health risk assessment model, this study aims to identify the pollution levels, spatial distribution characteristics, and human health risks of typical heavy metals in groundwater of this region, thereby providing scientific support for regional groundwater pollution prevention and drinking water security.

Materials and Methods

Regional Overview

The study area is located on both banks of the Yellow River at the junction of Pingyin County and Changqing District in Jinan City (Shandong Province), situated in the lower reaches of the Yellow River. The main administrative divisions include Pingyin County, Dong'e County, Changqing District, and Qihe County. The study area belongs to the temperate monsoon climate zone with four distinct seasons, an annual mean temperature of 12.8°C, and a multiyear average precipitation of 661.47 mm (1959–2024). The maximum annual precipitation is 1283.4 mm and the minimum is 320.7 mm; precipitation is concentrated from June to September (Chen et al. 2021), accounting for 65% of the annual total, with historical maximum daily rainfall mostly occurring in July (Chen et al. 2023). The main rivers in the area include the Yellow River, the Nanda Sha River, the Beida Sha River, the Xinzhaoniu River, and the Langxi River. Among them, the Yellow River primarily recharges groundwater in the study area (Tong et al. 2022; Li et al. 2022), while other rivers recharge groundwater during wet seasons and receive groundwater discharge during dry seasons (Yang et al. 2025).

The study area is located on the eastern margin of the North China Plain. The topography is dominated by the alluvial‐proluvial plain of the Yellow River, with low mountains and hills in the southeast. The terrain slopes from southeast to northwest, with elevations ranging from 12 to 650 m. The main strata in the area are divided into Quaternary unconsolidated sediments and Cambrian‐Ordovician carbonate rocks. The Quaternary deposits thicken progressively from southeast to northwest, with bedrock on the northwest side of the Yellow River completely covered by Quaternary deposits, consisting primarily of clay and fine sand. The underlying bedrock comprises Cambrian‐Ordovician carbonate rocks, with strata distributed from older to younger from south to north, forming a monocline structure. The main lithologies include limestone, dolomite, and shale (Wu et al. 2025). The study area is bounded by the Xiaolipu Fault to the east and the Dong'e Fault to the west; however, it does not constitute a closed hydrogeological unit and maintains close hydraulic connection with the downstream Baotu Spring Basin (Yu, Yu, et al. 2023; Cao et al. 2025).

Based on hydrogeological conditions, the study area can be divided into carbonate fissure‐karst aquifer groups and unconsolidated porous aquifer groups (Figure 1) (Wang et al. 2017). Specifically, the southeast side of the Yellow River is dominated by carbonate fissure‐karst aquifer groups, with well‐developed karst fissures and strong hydraulic conductivity, which facilitate groundwater recharge, runoff, and enrichment; the groundwater type is karst water. The northwest side of the Yellow River comprises multiple aquifer layers: The upper layer is Quaternary phreatic water, the middle layer is Quaternary confined water, and the lower layer is karst water. The thickness of Quaternary unconsolidated sediments ranges from 20 to 500 m, and the burial conditions are primarily controlled by the migration of paleo‐channels of the Yellow River (Zhi et al. 2021). Groundwater in the study area is mainly recharged by atmospheric precipitation and lateral seepage from rivers; the general groundwater flow direction is from southeast to northwest, with the main discharge modes being artificial abstraction and evaporation. The hydrochemical facies is predominantly HCO₃‐Ca.

Location of the study area and aquifer groups.

The study area features a well‐developed industrial and agricultural sector. Agriculture is dominated by characteristic cultivation and breeding industries, with high‐quality roses, Chinese medicinal herbs, and fruits and vegetables as the main agricultural products, demonstrating a relatively high level of intensification and industrialization. The industrial system comprises pharmaceutical chemicals, high‐end manufacturing, and food processing as pillar industries. The three regions exhibit complementary and synergistic industrial structures, jointly promoting the development of a characteristic economic belt in the Yellow River Basin and achieving high‐quality economic development.

Sample Collection and Analysis

Based on the climatic and hydrogeological conditions of the study area, the sampling period was scheduled for May to June 2025, prior to the onset of the rainy season, when precipitation is relatively low and evaporation is high, concentrated spring irrigation water use occurs, and regional groundwater levels are at their annual low with relatively stable hydrodynamic conditions, representing the dry season. Sampling during this period was selected for two primary reasons: first, to avoid the dilution effects of heavy precipitation and intense hydrodynamic mixing during wet seasons, thereby facilitating the identification of point‐source enrichment characteristics and cumulative pollution conditions of heavy metals, and second, as May–June coincides with the peak period of local agricultural fertilization and pesticide application, enabling greater capture of potential pollution risks from agricultural nonpoint source inputs.

A total of 44 samples were collected from the plain areas on both banks of the Pingyin‐Changqing section in the lower reaches of the Yellow River, including 24 karst water samples mainly collected from Pingyin County to Changqing District on the southeast side of the Yellow River, 17 pore water samples primarily obtained from Dong'e County to Qihe County on the northwest side, and three surface water samples collected from the Jianggou and Wangkoushan sections of the Yellow River as well as from a main irrigation canal as reference samples; the specific distribution of sampling points is shown in Figure 2.

Distribution of sampling points in the study area.

Groundwater sample collection and transportation were conducted in accordance with the Technical Specifications for Environmental Monitoring of Groundwater (HJ 164‐2020). Prior to sampling, the wells were purged by pumping to remove stagnant water, and samples were retrieved using a bailer or submersible pump and stored in 250 mL polyethylene bottles that had been sequentially rinsed with distilled water and raw water, with 2.5 mL of 50% HNO₃ solution added to each bottle. Upon completion of collection, all samples were delivered to the laboratory of Shandong Provincial Bureau of Geology and Mineral Resources (801 Institute of Hydrogeology and Engineering Geology) for analysis within 14 h. As was determined using a PF5‐2 atomic fluorescence spectrophotometer; Pb and Cd were analyzed using an iCap Q inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometer (ICP‐MS), and Zn, Mn, and Ni were measured using an ICAP 7200 inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP‐OES). The detection limits for As, Pb, Cd, Zn, Mn, and Ni were 0.0003, 0.00009, 0.00005, 0.004, 0.004, and 0.007 mg/L, respectively.

Research Method

PMF Source Apportionment

PMF (positive matrix factorization) source apportionment is a multivariate statistical analysis method widely applied in environmental science, atmospheric chemistry, and pollution source identification (Paatero and Tapper 1994). Proposed by Paatero and Tapper in 1994, this method decomposes the original data matrix X into a source contribution matrix G, a source composition profile matrix F, and a residual matrix E (Guo et al. 2024; Jiang et al. 2023), while minimizing the objective function Q, as shown in Equations (1) and (2): (1) Xij=∑k=1pGikFkj=+Eij (2) Q=∑i=1n∑j=1mEijUij2 where i is the number of samples, j is the number of heavy metal elements, k is the number of factors, and U is the uncertainty of the jth component in the ith sample, calculated segmentally based on the detection limit and signal‐to‐noise ratio.

Self‐Organizing Map Neural Network

Self‐organizing map (SOM) is an unsupervised neural network capable of nonlinearly mapping high‐dimensional data onto a low‐dimensional (typically two‐dimensional) grid while preserving the topological structure of the data (Kohonen 1982; Kohonen 1990). Its core advantage lies in strong visualization ability, enabling visualization of data clustering distributions and feature correlations, and it has been widely applied in pattern recognition, data mining, and environmental science (Wang et al. 2025; Tran et al. 2003).

Health Risk Assessment

USEPA Health Risk Assessment Model

Human exposure to groundwater inevitably involves contact with contaminants present in groundwater; for instance, heavy metals in groundwater can enter the human body through dermal contact and food chain intake. Most heavy metal elements possess certain toxicity—As, Cd, Cu, Zn, Pb, and Hg can cause a series of chronic diseases in humans, resulting in noncarcinogenic health risks, whereas As and Cd additionally present carcinogenic risks to human health (Saleem et al. 2025).

Human health risk assessment was assessed using the US Environmental Protection Agency (USEPA) Exposure Factors Handbook. Individuals are primarily exposed to environmental contaminants through two main nondietary pathways: hand‐to‐mouth ingestion and dermal contact. According to this handbook, the daily intake doses for adults and children under these two exposure pathways are calculated using Equation (3) and (4), respectively. (3) ADDing=C×IRing×EF×EDBW×AT (4) ADDderm=C×CF×SA×PC×EF×ED×ETBW×AT where ADD ing is the average daily dose through ingestion, in units of mg/(kg·d); ADD derm is the average daily dose through dermal contact, in units of mg/(kg·d), and C represents the concentration of chemical substances in water, that is, heavy metal content, in units of mg/L. Uncertainty Analysis Based on Monte Carlo Simulation

Monte Carlo simulation is a commonly employed computational method for uncertainty analysis in risk assessment. In this approach, it assigns probability distributions to deterministic parameters used in health risk calculations, randomly sampling values from within these parameter distributions to conduct simulation experiments. After 10,000 iterations, the frequency distribution of health risk outcomes is ultimately obtained (Mianeh et al. 2025). Data Processing

This study employed the multivariate geographic information fusion function of ArcGIS 10.8 for spatial distribution characteristic analysis of heavy metal concentrations and health risks. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted using Python (SciPy). Source apportionment of heavy metal elements was performed using EPA PMF 5.0. SOM neural network analysis was carried out using MATLAB combined with the SOM toolbox. Uncertainty analysis based on Monte Carlo simulation was implemented using Excel 2010 integrated with Oracle Crystal Ball.

Results and Discussion

Statistical Analysis

The heavy metal concentration data obtained from groundwater testing in the study area are presented in Table 4. The results show that the mean concentrations of heavy metals in groundwater follow the order: Mn > Zn > Ni > Pb > As > Cd. Compared with the Class III criteria of the Standard for Groundwater Quality (GB/T 14848‐2017), Mn exhibits the highest exceedance rate (18%) among all water samples, with a maximum value four times the standard limit, representing the primary heavy metal pollution risk; Pb shows localized exceedance (5%), with a maximum value 12.8 times the standard limit, constituting a secondary risk; As, Zn, Cd, and Ni all remain within standard limits with relatively low risks. Notably, all heavy metal concentrations in surface water are within standard limits and at low levels, indicating that heavy metal sources in groundwater are unrelated to surface rivers and can serve as reference samples. Porous water demonstrates higher heavy metal pollution risks, with Mn and Pb exceedance rates of 35.29% and 11.76%, respectively, whereas karst water shows only 8.33% Mn exceedance and no Pb exceedance (0%), indicating that porous water is more susceptible to contamination.

The distribution characteristics of heavy metal elements vary significantly. Pb, Zn, As, and Cd exhibit pronounced leptokurtic right‐skewed distributions with a small number of high‐value outliers; Mn shows a moderately leptokurtic and moderately right‐skewed distribution; Ni presents a platykurtic slightly right‐skewed distribution with the most uniform data distribution. Overall variability of all elements is relatively low, with Pb showing the highest coefficient of variation (5.10) indicating obvious localized pollution; Mn exhibits a lower coefficient of variation but the highest exceedance rate, suggesting a systematic pollution risk; Ni has the lowest coefficient of variation (1.20) with the most stable concentrations, being minimally disturbed by external factors.

Spatial Distribution Characteristics

The heavy metal concentrations at each sampling point in the study area were spatially interpolated and mapped using GIS techniques to generate distribution maps for each metal (Figure 3). The spatial distribution maps indicate distinct distribution patterns among the heavy metals. As concentrations exhibit a middle‐high, two‐ends‐low trend, with enrichment zones primarily distributed at the junction of Pingyin, Changqing, and Qihe. Pb concentrations are generally higher in the northeast than in the southwest, with localized enrichment sites (ZB081 and ZB083) located at the junction of Changqing and Qihe on the north bank of the Yellow River. Zn concentrations are relatively uniform overall, generally higher on the south bank than on the north bank of the Yellow River. Cd and Ni show relatively uniform concentration distributions along the Yellow River with low concentrations. Mn concentrations are also uniformly distributed, with exceedance points (QNQ, LYC, D1, LGQ, XZ30, XZ31, XZ33, and ZB080) identified on both banks of the Yellow River from southwest to northeast.

Spatial distribution of heavy metal concentrations in groundwater of the study area.

Based on the statistical analysis results in Section 2.1, the distribution characteristics of heavy metals appear to be associated with regional hydrogeological conditions. The southeast side of the Yellow River is dominated by carbonate fissure‐karst aquifer groups, with well‐developed karst fissures and strong hydraulic conductivity; groundwater runoff velocity is high, and water‐rock interaction is dominated by leaching‐dissolution, facilitating the dilution and dispersion of pollutants. In contrast, the northwest side of the Yellow River comprises unconsolidated porous aquifer groups in the upper layer and carbonate fissure‐karst aquifer groups in the lower layer; the burial conditions are controlled by the migration of Yellow River paleo‐channels, with relatively fine sediment particles and low hydraulic conductivity, resulting in sluggish groundwater runoff that is more prone to pollutant accumulation and enrichment. These conditions are reflected in the statistical results: The Mn exceedance rate in porous water is as high as 35.29%, significantly higher than 8.33% in karst water; the Pb exceedance rate in porous water is 11.76%, whereas no exceedance is detected in karst water. However, the influence of hydrogeological environment on heavy metal distribution is only a secondary factor, with heavy metal sources being the primary controlling factor; therefore, the following discussion will focus on the sources of groundwater heavy metals in the study area.

Heavy Metal Source Analysis

Spearman Correlation Analysis

Given the high dispersion of heavy metal concentrations in the study area, Spearman correlation coefficients were employed for correlation analysis, with results presented in Figure 4. The analysis indicates generally weak correlations among the heavy metal elements. Moderate positive correlations are observed between Pb‐As and Ni‐Zn, while a weak positive correlation exists between Mn‐As, suggesting that these heavy metal elements may share common sources or mixed sources. A moderate negative correlation between Pb‐Ni, combined with the spatial distribution patterns of heavy metals, indicates substantially different pollution sources for these two elements. Overall, correlations among heavy metal pollution sources in the study area remain relatively weak, with no strong associations observed. Combined with the distribution analysis results, it is inferred that the sources of various metal elements in groundwater are weakly associated.

Spearman correlation matrix of heavy metal concentrations in groundwater.

PMF Source Apportionment

This study applied the PMF model to quantitatively apportion the source contributions of six heavy metals (As, Pb, Zn, Cd, Mn, and Ni) in groundwater of the study area. Concentration and uncertainty data were imported into EPA PMF 5.0 software, and iterative computations were performed with factor numbers ranging from 2 to 6. The optimal model convergence was achieved with five factors: Q Robust/Q True stabilized and reached its minimum value, with sample residuals primarily distributed within the range of −3 to 3, indicating reliable PMF model performance (Sun et al. 2025; Mao et al. 2025); the results are presented in Figure 5.

Contribution rates of heavy metal pollution sources from PMF model.

The results indicate that heavy metal contamination in regional groundwater is primarily driven by anthropogenic activities, with industrial, traffic, and agricultural sources identified as key contributors.

Factor 1 exhibits the highest contribution to Pb (90.6%), with minor contributions to As and Mn. The high Pb contribution likely represents traffic emission sources (leaded gasoline residues and vehicular exhaust particulates) or industrial Pb emissions (Zeng et al. 2022; Al‐sabbagh and Shreaz 2025), with a total contribution rate of 16.3%.

Factor 2 shows a highly concentrated contribution to Zn (97.9%), indicating the presence of high‐intensity industrial point sources in the area. Local small‐scale bearing galvanizing and hardware hot‐dip galvanizing factories may represent the primary sources of Zn pollution; untreated or leaking process wastewater can readily cause sharp increases in local groundwater Zn concentrations (Fan et al. 2023). Therefore, Factor 2 is identified as an industrial pollution source, contributing 17.5%.

Factor 3 exhibits a 93.6% contribution to As, accompanied by minor contributions from Zn and Cd, which primarily points to the superposition of historical agricultural activities and modern aquaculture. First, the alluvial plain agricultural areas in the lower Yellow River have a long history of arsenical pesticide application; inorganic arsenic formulations (such as calcium arsenate and lead arsenate) were widely used for pest and disease control in orchards and economic crops from the 19th century to the mid‐20th century (Alloway 2012), and their soil residues exhibit persistence and cumulative characteristics, with long‐term leaching release serving as an important source of groundwater arsenic (Peryea and Creger 1994). Second, investigations indicate that roxarsone‐containing feed additives have been reported to be used in broiler farming in the northern Shandong Plain; the accumulation of chicken manure has led to significantly elevated arsenic contents in surrounding soils and the vadose zone, with enrichment in silty clay layers causing groundwater As concentrations to exceed the Standard for Groundwater Quality (Liu et al. 2017; Fisher et al. 2015). The study area is located in the western Shandong agricultural belt along the Yellow River, where economic crop cultivation (roses, Chinese medicinal herbs) and livestock‐poultry farming coexist; under the leaching effects of precipitation and irrigation, these factors jointly constitute an important source of groundwater As pollution (Liu et al. 2013). Therefore, Factor 3 is identified as an agricultural‐aquacultural pollution source, contributing 17.6%.

Factor 4 exhibits significant co‐enrichment of Cd (87.2%) and Ni (82.9%), with minor contributions from Pb and Zn. This element assemblage is highly specific to small‐scale electroplating industries, particularly supporting nickel‐plating operations that may exist within the local bearing and hardware industrial chains. Small‐scale electroplating workshops often utilize inferior nickel plates containing Cd impurities or Cd‐containing brighteners; illegal wastewater discharge or leakage from treatment ponds constitutes the primary cause of combined Cd‐Ni pollution. Additionally, alloy processing or improper disposal of industrial wastes containing Ni and Cd may also contribute partially. Therefore, Factor 4 is identified as a second industrial pollution source, contributing 30.3%.

Factor 5 shows a 91.5% contribution to Mn and a 10.7% contribution to Ni. This indicates predominantly natural geochemical processes in groundwater, possibly with minor perturbation from local industries. Under anoxic reducing conditions, Mn oxides in the aquifer are readily dissolved, leading to significantly elevated Mn concentrations (Mcmahon et al. 2019); simultaneously, pickling wastewater from small‐scale metal processing industries and anaerobic decomposition of aquaculture waste may additionally release minor amounts of Mn and Ni, superimposing upon natural processes. However, Mn pollution is widespread locally and clearly distinct from point‐source factory pollution; therefore, Factor 5 is identified as a natural pollution source, contributing 18.3%.

SOM Clustering Analysis

This study employed the SOM neural network to preprocess heavy metal (As, Pb, Zn, Cd, Mn, and Ni) concentration data from 44 sampling points in the study area. The data were input into the SOM model for training, projecting high‐dimensional heavy metal data onto a two‐dimensional hexagonal topological space to obtain a 6 × 7 neuron structure. In summary, this study integrated Spearman correlation analysis, PMF source apportionment, and SOM clustering to comprehensively apportion groundwater heavy metal sources in the study area. The mutual verification among these methods demonstrates the reliability of the conclusions. The study reveals that in nontraditional industrial zones, difficulties in regulating traditional agriculture, aquaculture, and small‐scale industries, combined with backward production methods, have become important sources of groundwater heavy metal pollution. Groundwater sampling on the northwest side of the Yellow River is predominantly porous water, which is readily susceptible to surface contamination sources.

Based on the relevant conclusions of this study, it is recommended to take the following measures in the future for regional groundwater governance or improving the health of residents' drinking water: Conduct a detailed investigation of pollution sources in Pb‐polluted areas where pollution is relatively concentrated, and treat contaminated soil and water sources; implement standardized management in agricultural and aquaculture areas with high As content, strictly control the use of pesticides and feed additives, and strengthen the harmless treatment of livestock and poultry manure; concentrate small‐scale industrial enterprises in parks in areas with high Cd, Ni, and Zn content, and provide supporting centralized sewage and waste residue treatment facilities. Finally, long‐term monitoring wells should be installed downstream of key pollution sources and in sensitive areas, and a long‐term risk warning system for groundwater pollution should be established.

According to the data analysis results, carcinogenic risks from groundwater heavy metals in the study area are within safe limits, with no significant carcinogenic risks currently present.

Comparative analysis of noncarcinogenic and carcinogenic health risks from groundwater heavy metals in the study area reveals that children exhibit higher noncarcinogenic health risk indices compared to adults, indicating greater susceptibility to noncarcinogenic health hazards from groundwater heavy metal pollution. This phenomenon primarily stems from children's distinctive physiological characteristics and behavioral patterns: On one hand, children's water intake per unit body weight is typically higher than adults, making them more likely to ingest contaminated groundwater through oral exposure in daily life (Pang et al. 2024); on the other hand, their immune and metabolic systems are not yet fully developed, resulting in higher sensitivity to pollutants and weaker detoxification and excretion capacities (Sly and Carpenter 2012), thereby making them more vulnerable to harm under identical exposure levels.

Given that groundwater serves as an important source of drinking water and domestic water supply, the potential threat of heavy metal pollution to children's health cannot be overlooked. Therefore, it is recommended to strengthen health protection for children, such as ensuring access to safe drinking water sources and avoiding direct contact with or accidental ingestion of untreated groundwater. Simultaneously, groundwater environmental supervision should be intensified, particularly in industrial zones, mining areas, and regions with severe agricultural nonpoint source pollution, where strict control of pollutant infiltration is necessary to prevent groundwater quality deterioration. Local ecological environment departments should improve groundwater monitoring networks, conduct timely health risk assessments, and formulate targeted prevention and control measures to effectively reduce the health impact of groundwater heavy metal pollution on children.

Uncertainty Analysis Based on Monte Carlo Simulation

To enhance the scientific rigor and predictive accuracy of the assessment conclusions, this study incorporated a Monte Carlo random coupling simulation to validate the evaluation results. Based on the mathematical models for noncarcinogenic risk (HI) and carcinogenic risk (TCR), probability distribution simulations of core predictive variables were conducted under conditions of 10,000 iterative samplings and a 95% confidence level (Xu et al. 2017), thereby generating cumulative probability curves to achieve health risk evaluation.

The simulation results are presented in Figure 9. The mean HI shows (noncarcinogenic health risk) values for adults and children are 1.28E‐01 and 2.75E‐01, respectively, both below the safety threshold established by USEPA (HI = 1). These simulation results are generally consistent with the previous findings, demonstrating the reliability of the simulation. Furthermore, the probability distribution characteristics indicate that children's exposure risks fall entirely within the safety interval, confirming that groundwater heavy metal pollution in the study area has not yet caused identifiable noncarcinogenic health threats to children. However, the cumulative frequency distribution for adult HI shows that the cumulative probability of risk values below 1 is 94%, representing a 6% probability that a randomly selected adult individual in the study area faces noncarcinogenic risks exceeding acceptable levels. For adults, therefore, groundwater heavy metal pollution in the study area poses a certain noncarcinogenic health risk.

Cumulative probability curves of noncarcinogenic (HI) and carcinogenic (TCR) risks for adults and children.

The mean TCR (carcinogenic health risk) values for adults and children are 3.49E‐06 and 1.98E‐06, respectively. According to the probability distributions, TCR values for both adults and children remain within the safe range (TCR = 1.00E‐04); thus, carcinogenic risks from groundwater heavy metals in the study area are within acceptable limits, with no significant carcinogenic risks currently present. The simulation results are consistent with the calculated results in Table 5, indicating credible simulation performance.
