Altered proprioceptive weighting and trunk muscle activation in individuals with recurrent low back pain: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Tadanobu Suehiro, Hiroshi Ishida, Tomotaka Ito, Kenichi Kobara, Hiroshi Osaka, Kosuke Oku, Ayaka Yakusue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIndividuals with low back pain (LBP) rely more on ankle proprioception and have a reduced capacity for upweight proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles. However, whether this impairment persists during low-symptom phases in patients with recurrent LBP remains unclear. Individuals with recurrent LBP show different patterns of trunk muscle activation when performing lifting tasks. However, little is known about the association between trunk muscle activation patterns during lifting tasks and proprioceptive weight changes during postural control tasks. We aimed to compare proprioceptive weighting between individuals with recurrent LBP and healthy controls and examine the relationship between proprioceptive weighting and trunk muscle activation patterns during a lifting task.MethodsTwenty-five patients with recurrent LBP and 20 healthy controls were recruited. Electromyography (EMG) was employed to assess the onset time, EMG amplitude, and overall activity of trunk muscles during the lifting task. Proprioceptive postural control was evaluated by applying vibrations to the triceps surae or lumbar multifidus muscles while participants stood on an unstable force plate, and the relative proprioceptive weighting (RPW) was determined. Differences in muscle activation patterns during the lifting task and RPW in the proprioceptive postural control test were compared between both groups. The relationship between RPW during the proprioceptive postural control test and the pattern of trunk muscle activation during a lifting task was assessed.ResultsRPW values were significantly higher in the recurrent LBP group than in the healthy control group (p = 0.03). During the lifting task, the onset of muscle activation in the multifidus, erector spinae, and transversus abdominis/internal oblique muscles was significantly delayed in the LBP group (p < 0.001), whereas the overall trunk muscle activity was higher (p = 0.04). RPW in the proprioceptive postural control test was significantly and positively correlated with the onset of multifidus activity during the lifting task in individuals with recurrent LBP (r = 0.41, p = 0.04).ConclusionsIndividuals with recurrent LBP demonstrate a reduced ability to upweight proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles and tend to rely more on ankle proprioception. The reduced ability to utilise proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles is associated with delayed activation of the multifidus muscle during lifting tasks.

Background

Low back pain (LBP) is a global health concern [1]. It is the leading cause of work absence and activity limitation and causes enormous social and economic burden [2, 3]. The complete recovery of patients with LBP episodes within 1 year range from 54 to 90% [1]. In total, 50% of the individuals with LBP experience recurrent episodes within 12 months [4]. Recurrent episodes of LBP are costly and have a higher proportion of work disabilities than the first episode [5].

Optimal postural control is essential for performing daily activities. For optimal control, reliable sensory inputs from the somatosensory, visual, and vestibular systems are identified and weighed by the central nervous system (CNS) [6]. One method of assessing proprioceptive deficits in individuals with LBP is to measure the displacement of the centre of gravity when the triceps surae and multifidus muscles vibrate separately. Vibration of the triceps surae or multifidus muscles induces a muscle-lengthening illusion and shifts the centre of gravity while standing. Therefore, changes in proprioceptive weight can be evaluated by measuring the amount and ratio of the centre of gravity deviation during the vibration of each muscle. Using muscle vibration, Brumagne et al. [6, 7] found that people with LBP depended more on ankle proprioception and had a reduced ability to enhance proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles for optimal standing postural control as postural tasks became more challenging. Furthermore, Claeys et al. showed a decreased ability to use feedback from the paraspinal muscles in sitting and standing positions [8]. However, it is unclear whether the ability to enhance proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles continues to decrease during low-symptom phases in patients with recurrent LBP.

Individuals with recurrent LBP in pain-free phases have less movement control of the lumbopelvic region and changes in trunk muscle activation patterns [9, 10] and may also have a reduced ability to upweight proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles. Decreased ability to use feedback from the paraspinal muscles has been associated with delayed onset of pelvic rotation during the preparatory phase of the sit-to-stance movement in individuals with non-specific LBP [11]. Precise movement control has been reported to rely heavily on the sensory components of the motor system [12]. Erroneous proprioceptive input may introduce inaccurate signals into the CNS, leading to distortion of the coordinate system for postural adjustment and alterations in motor control [13]. Individuals with recurrent LBP tend to exhibit delayed activation of local muscles and increased co-contraction of trunk muscles when performing lifting tasks [14], which are linked to a heightened risk of LBP [15]. This delay in local muscle activity and increased co-contraction of trunk muscles may be related to the reduced ability to enhance the proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles. However, little is known about the association between trunk muscle activation patterns during lifting tasks and proprioceptive weight changes during posture control.

This study had two purposes. The first purpose of this study was to compare proprioceptive weighting changes between individuals with recurrent LBP during a low-symptom phase and healthy controls and to identify any changes in proprioceptive weighting in individuals with recurrent LBP. The second purpose was to clarify the association between the activation patterns of trunk muscles during the lifting task and proprioceptive weighting changes in postural control. We hypothesised that individuals with recurrent LBP would demonstrate reduced ability to upweight proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles. In addition, we hypothesised that trunk muscle activity patterns during lifting are related to changes in proprioceptive weighting.

Methods

This study used a cross-sectional design.

Participants

The sample size was determined using G*Power, with a power (1 − 𝛽) of 0.80, an alpha level of 0.05, an effect size difference of 0.8, and a ratio of healthy participants to those with LBP of 1:1.25. Forty-two participants were required, with 19 and 23 in the control and LBP groups, respectively. To account for potential dropouts and missing data, we recruited 20 healthy individuals and 25 individuals with LBP, aged 20 to 50 years, through poster advertisements at our university. To qualify for participation, individuals with recurrent non-specific LBP needed to have experienced at least two instances of LBP within a year, each lasting at least 24 h, situated above the gluteal folds and below the 12th thoracic vertebra, and severe enough to interfere with work, leisure activities, or sports participation [16, 17]. At the time of testing, participants with recurrent LBP were required to be in a pain-free state from their LBP symptoms, with a Numerical Rating Scale (NRS) score of 0. Exclusion criteria encompassed the presence of red flags (such as spinal tumours or neoplastic diseases, infections, inflammatory diseases, and fractures); vestibular or neurological conditions; psychiatric, rheumatological, and/or cardiac diseases; signs of radiculopathy, lumbar spinal stenosis, or spondylolisthesis; a history of fractures or lumbar injuries resulting from trauma, such as falls; and any history of surgery on the spine or hip joints. The Ethics Committee at Kawasaki University of Medical Welfare granted approval for this study (approval number: 23–024). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

LBP and disability assessment

Pain severity experienced over the past week were assessed using an NRS from 0 (‘no pain’) to 10 (‘worst possible pain’). Current LBP-related interference with activities of daily living was assessed using the Oswestry Disability Index, a 10-item questionnaire with scores ranging from 0 to 100 [18], with higher scores indicating greater disability.

Proprioceptive postural control test

Muscle vibration, which stimulates the Ia afferents, was used to directly investigate the function of proprioception in maintaining posture [19]. Two muscle vibrators (MD-0105; Asahi Seisakusho, Tokyo, Japan) were attached to both sides of the triceps surae and lumbar multifidus muscles. The lumbar multifidus muscles and triceps surae are involved in multi-segmental and ankle strategies, respectively [6]. The vibration amplitude was approximately 0.5 mm. The vibration frequency was set at 60 Hz, which created the illusion of muscle-lengthening and joint movement [20]. A greater directional sway is expected when the CNS uses vibrating muscle signals for postural control.

For the proprioceptive postural control assessment, a force plate (T.K.K 5810; Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Niigata, Japan) was used to evaluate the characteristics of postural sway. The force plate data were sampled at a frequency of 100 Hz. The participants stood barefoot on a balance pad (AMB-ELITE; AIREX Co., Switzerland) placed on a force plate with their arm to the side. Both heels were separated by 10 cm, and the participants were instructed to wear blindfolds. To create an unstable support surface and reduce the reliability of ankle proprioceptive signals, a balance pad was employed (Fig. 1). This condition required the CNS to rely more on other proprioceptive inputs to maintain posture [21]. Muscle vibrations began 15 s after the start of the test and continued for 15 s.

The centre of pressure (COP) displacements collected from the force plate were analysed in two epochs: 15 s before muscle vibration and 15 s during muscle vibration. Negative values indicate backward displacement of the COP, and positive values indicate forward displacement. The sway path length, which is the total length of the COP that moved during each epoch, was also analysed. Here, ‘absolute TS’ refers to the absolute value of the mean COP displacement in the anterior–posterior direction during triceps surae vibration, subtracted by the absolute value of the mean COP displacement during the pre-vibration period. The ‘Absolute MF’ represents the absolute value of the mean COP displacement in the anterior–posterior direction during multifidus vibration minus the absolute value of the mean COP displacement in the pre-vibration period. An RPW value of 0 signifies complete dependence on multifidus proprioceptive signals, whereas an RPW value of 1 indicates full reliance on proprioceptive signals from the triceps surae [11, 22]. The proprioceptive postural control test was performed once.

Trunk muscle activation pattern during lifting task

Following the protocol established in our previous study [14], participants performed a lifting task using a 5-kg box, repeated three times. Standing upright, the participants held a 5-kg box on a table in front of them, with their elbows fully extended and shoulders flexed at 40°, using both hands. A pressure sensor, synchronized with an electromyograph, was positioned at the base of the box to detect when it was lifted off the table. An indicator bar was set up to limit shoulder flexion to 90°. Upon the activation of a diode lamp at eye level, the participants swiftly raised the box to a 90° shoulder flexion, while keeping the elbows extended (Fig. 2). This final posture was held for 3 s. Participants practiced lifting several times before the test. During the lifting task, electromyography (EMG) data were recorded using a wireless surface electromyograph (Logical Product Inc., Fukuoka, Japan) with a pair of Ag-AgCl surface electrodes at a sampling frequency of 1000 Hz. The skin was cleaned with alcohol, sanded, and shaved, if necessary. The electrodes were placed 2.5 cm apart in the direction of the muscle fibres over the following muscles [9]: lumbar multifidus, erector spinae (ES), transversus abdominis/internal abdominal oblique (TrA/IO), external oblique (EO), and anterior deltoid. For electromyographic recordings, electrodes were placed as follows: for the lumbar multifidus, along a line from the posterior superior iliac spine to the L1–L2 interspace, at the level of the L5 spinous process; for the ES, two finger widths lateral to the L1 spinous process; for the TrA/IO, 2 cm inferior and medial to the anterior superior iliac spine; for the EO, 15 cm lateral to the umbilicus; and for the anterior deltoid, one finger width distal and anterior to the acromion. In the control group, electrodes were placed on the right side, while in the group with recurrent LBP, they were positioned on the side where pain had been more intense during previous episodes.

Lifting task after receiving the visual signal, the participants quickly lifted the box until they reached 90° of shoulder flexion. I, initial position; II, end position

An angular rate sensor (MVP-RF10; Microstone, Japan) attached to the wrist was used to assess the peak angular velocities of shoulder movement during lifting. The data on angular velocity were collected at a frequency of 1000 Hz.

For EMG data, raw data were processed using the BIMUTAS-Video software (Kissei Comtec Inc., Nagano, Japan) with full-wave rectification and band-pass filtering from 20 to 500 Hz. The EMG amplitudes were averaged over a 3-s period starting when the box was lifted off the table. The average amplitude was normalised to the maximum voluntary contraction (MVC). Participants performed 3-s MVCs of the trunk muscles against manual resistance. For the EO and TrA/IO, MVC trials were conducted in the supine position, during which participants isometrically rotated their trunk to the left and right sides, respectively, against resistance [23]. For the lumbar multifidus and ES, MVC trials were performed in the prone position, with participants extending the trunk against resistance [23].

After normalisation, we combined the amplitudes of the multifidus, ES, TrA/IO, and EO muscles to evaluate the overall activity of the trunk muscles using the following equation [14]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Overall}\;\mathrm{activity}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{the}\;\mathrm{trunk}\;\mathrm{muscles}\:=\:\mathrm{multifidus}\;\mathrm{activity}\;(\%\mathrm{MVC})\:+\:\mathrm{ES}\;\mathrm{activity}\;(\%\mathrm{MVC})\:+\:\mathrm{TrA}/\mathrm{IO}\;\mathrm{activity}\;(\%\mathrm{MVC})\:+\:\mathrm{EO}\;\mathrm{activity}\;(\%\mathrm{MVC})$$\end{document}

The onset of muscle activation was identified by visually observing when the EMG rose above the baseline and remained elevated for at least 50 ms [24]. In cases where visual analysis made it challenging to pinpoint the onset, the amplitude surpassed 2 standard deviations from the baseline level [25]. The following formula was used to determine when the trunk muscles activated in relation to the anterior deltoid, the prime mover [26–28]:\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathrm{Relative}\;\mathrm{onset}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{trunk}\;\mathrm{muscle}\;\mathrm{activation}\:=\:\mathrm{onset}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{trunk}\;\mathrm{muscle}\;\mathrm{activation}\:-\:\mathrm{onset}\;\mathrm{of}\;\mathrm{anterior}\;\mathrm{deltoid}\;\mathrm{activation}$$\end{document}

Thus, when the values are negative, it means the trunk muscles activate before the deltoid muscle.

Statistical analyses

Data analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 23 (Armonk, USA). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of all continuous variables. To compare the recurrent LBP group with the healthy group on each COP outcome measure during the proprioceptive postural control test, as well as EMG amplitudes and shoulder movement speed during the lifting task, an independent-samples t-test was used. If the data did not follow a normal distribution, the Mann–Whitney U test was used. A multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) was conducted to compare the relative onset of trunk muscle activation between groups during the lifting task. When the MANOVA revealed statistically significant results, post hoc analyses were performed using univariate ANOVA. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient was used to examine the association between the RPW during the proprioceptive postural control test and the trunk muscle activation pattern during the lifting task.

Speed and muscle activation pattern of the lifting task

A comparison of shoulder movement speed during the lifting task showed no significant differences between the groups (control group: 128.8 ± 32.2 deg/s, recurrent LBP group: 120.0 ± 33.6 deg/s, p = 0.38, effect size d = 0.27, power = 0.13).

The results of the comparison of the relative onset of trunk muscle activation are shown in Table 3. MANOVA revealed a significant difference in the relative onset of trunk muscle activation between the groups (p < 0.001, partial η2 = 0.748). Post-hoc analysis revealed that the onset of the multifidus, ES, and transversus abdominis/internal oblique (TrA/IO) muscles significantly occurred later in the recurrent LBP group than in the healthy control group (p < 0.001). No significant difference was observed in the onset of EO between the two groups.

The results of the comparison of trunk muscle EMG amplitudes are presented in Table 4. The EMG amplitudes of the multifidus and the overall activity of the trunk muscles were greater in the recurrent LBP group than in the healthy control group (p = 0.04). No significant differences in the EMG amplitudes of the ES, TrA/IO, and EO were found between the two groups.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate potential differences in proprioceptive weighting changes between individuals with recurrent LBP in a low-symptom phase and healthy participants. We found that the RPW value was higher in individuals with recurrent LBP than in healthy controls. The results align with our hypothesis that individuals with recurrent LBP demonstrate reduced ability to upweight proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles.

Based on the RPW results of this study, the CNS in healthy individuals appeared to increase reliance on proprioceptive input from the paraspinal muscles (i.e. multifidus) while decreasing reliance on input from the ankle muscles when standing on an unstable surface, such as a balance pad, to maintain postural stability. In contrast, individuals with recurrent LBP tended to rely predominantly on ankle proprioception and exhibited difficulty in reducing this dependency, even when proprioceptive input from the triceps surae became unreliable under unstable conditions. One potential explanation for the reduced dependence on back muscle proprioception in individuals with LBP is the lower density of muscle spindles in the lumbar multifidus. Lumbar multifidus atrophy and increased fatty infiltration are evidenced in individuals with LPB, and these morphological changes can reduce density of muscle spindles and affect spindle function [8, 29–31]. Consequently, lumbar multifidus vibrations may have had a smaller effect. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that in individuals with LBP, the area of the primary somatosensory cortex responsible for processing proprioception or individual sensory signals moves medially [32]. A recent study has shown that individuals with a history of LBP, but no current pain, exhibit increased grey matter in the primary somatosensory cortex corresponding to the somatotopic representation of the lower back [33]. Increased grey matter in this region among individuals with a history of LBP has been associated with reduced acuity of lumbar perception [34]. This regional reorganisation and increased grey matter affect cortical processing and proprioceptive perception [34]. Pijnenburg et al. [35] found that individuals with nonspecific LBP exhibited reduced white matter integrity in the superior cerebellar peduncle. This condition is associated with a heightened dependence on proprioceptive signals from the ankle muscles, even when on an unstable support surface, which leads to a disregard for proprioception related to position and motion in the lower back [35]. Therefore, it is possible that a reduced ability to utilise proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles may be associated with reorganisation and increased grey matter of the somatosensory cortex as well as decreased integrity of the superior cerebellar peduncle.

In this study, the onset of the multifidus, TrA/IO, and ES activities occurred significantly later in the recurrent LBP group than in the healthy control group during lifting. In addition, the EMG amplitudes of the multifidus and the overall activity of the trunk muscles were higher in the recurrent LBP group than in the healthy control group. These findings align with those of an earlier study that observed a delay in the activation of the multifidus and TrA/IO muscles, along with heightened activity in the trunk muscles during lifting tasks in individuals recurrent LBP during their asymptomatic periods [9]. These altered muscle recruitment patterns may not be solely the result of peripheral factors but could also be influenced by CNS adaptations, such as changes in motor planning and control [10, 36, 37]. Although participants were asymptomatic during testing, it is plausible that previous pain episodes induced persistent compensatory strategies, including excessive co-activation of trunk muscles. This may reflect cortical reorganisation processes within the motor system [38, 39]. Supporting this interpretation, neurophysiological studies have shown that individuals with a history of LBP exhibit less clearly defined representations of trunk muscles within the motor cortex—a phenomenon often described as “smudging” [37, 39, 40]. In particular, overlapping cortical representations of the deep multifidus and longissimus muscles have been observed in people with recurrent LBP [39, 40], indicating reduced motor specificity and potentially contributing to the delayed activation patterns identified in this study. Although the current study did not directly assess cortical mechanisms, the neuromuscular alterations observed during the low-symptom phase in individuals with recurrent LBP may reflect persistent changes in central motor control mechanisms.

In addition, delayed activity of local muscles, such as the multifidus and TrA/IO, reduces segmental stability of the lumbar region [12], whereas increased overall activity of trunk muscles contributes to spinal stiffness [41]. The increased overall activity of trunk muscles found in individuals with LBP has been suggested to be a motor control strategy of stabilization of the lumbopelvic region [42, 43]. Furthermore, increased engagement of superficial trunk muscles has been considered a compensatory response to dysfunction of the deeper stabilizing muscles [12, 42, 44]. Taken together, the observed changes in trunk muscle activity patterns could reflect a compensatory response to reduced segmental stability, potentially resulting from delayed activation of the multifidus and TrA/IO muscles. This response may involve increased overall activation of the trunk muscles. However, further research with a larger sample size is warranted to confirm these findings.

The RPW in the proprioceptive postural control test was significantly and positively correlated with the onset of multifidus in this study. In other words, individuals who rely on ankle proprioceptive sensation and have a reduced ability to utilise proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles are involved in delayed activity of the multifidus. According to Hodges et al., precise movement control relied heavily on sensory components of the motor system [12]. They suggested that inaccurate afferent signals affected all aspects of motor control, ranging from basic reflex responses triggered by muscle mechanoreceptor stimulation to intricate movements that depend on a precise ‘internal model of body dynamics’, which allows the CNS to predict interactions between external and internal forces [12]. In addition, Mousavi-Khatir et al. [45] reported that the delay in the activity of the cervical ES muscles during rapid arm flexion after static neck flexion was due to changes in proprioceptive information. Kasai et al. [13] also showed that erroneous proprioceptive input injected erroneous signals into the CNS, leading to distortion of the coordinate system for postural adjustment and alteration of feedforward control. Therefore, as a factor in the correlation between the multifidus and RPW, we believe that patients with LBP with a reduced ability to utilise proprioceptive sensory feedback from paraspinal muscles were more likely to distort the coordinate system and internal model for postural adjustment and delay the activity of the multifidus.

The results of this study showed delayed activity of the multifidus, TrA/IO, and ES muscles and increased overall trunk muscle activity during the lifting task and elevated RPW during the proprioceptive postural control test in individuals with LBP compared to healthy participants. Increased trunk muscle activity during lifting movements induces a greater spinal compression and early muscle fatigue, which may contribute to the chronicity and recurrence of LBP [46–49]. Therefore, it is important to modify the muscle activity patterns in individuals with recurrent LBP. As this study revealed a relationship between trunk muscle activity patterns and lumbar proprioceptive function, it may be necessary to approach lumbar proprioception in addition to motor control exercises to improve muscle activity patterns in individuals with LBP.

This study has some limitations. First, the participants with recurrent LBP were young adults. Therefore, the generalisability of the results to middle-aged and older individuals is limited. Second, the muscle activity patterns during lifting movements were measured using surface EMG. Therefore, it was not possible to assess TrA and IO or superficial and deep multifidus activities separately. However, even when surface electrodes were used, differences in the onset times of TrA/IO and multifidus activity were detected between patients with LBP and healthy participants [14, 26], supporting the results of this study. Third, although the RPW result was statistically significant with a medium effect size, the statistical power was relatively low (0.58). Therefore, these findings should be interpreted with caution, and further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm the observed relationship. Fourth, the weight of the box used for the lifting task was standardized at 5 kg and was not normalized to the participants’ body weight. This may have resulted in variations in the effort required to lift the box among participants. However, since there was no significant difference in body weight between the groups, the impact on the study results is likely to be minimal.

Further studies are required to determine whether interventions focused on enhancing proprioceptive reweighting ability and muscle activation patterns can aid in the prevention of LBP recurrence

Conclusion

Individuals with recurrent LBP demonstrate a reduced ability to upweight proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles and tend to rely more on ankle proprioception. The reduced ability to utilise proprioceptive feedback from the paraspinal muscles is associated with delayed activation of the multifidus during lifting tasks, highlighting the need for targeted proprioceptive and motor control interventions.
