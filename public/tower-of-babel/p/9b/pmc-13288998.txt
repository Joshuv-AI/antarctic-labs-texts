Comparative efficiency of Asian surgical systems in COVID-19 response: a multi-level longitudinal benchmarking study
Authors: Sean Shao Wei Lam, Ashish Kumar, Yao Ge, Sze Ling Chan, Ahmad Reza Pourghaderi, Ma Wai Wai Zaw, Aamer Zaman Khan, April Camilla Camilla Roslani, Chunying Selvakumaran, Razman Jarmin, Diep Ho Ngoc, Dina Kumala Sari, Dipika Dey, Dung Nguyen Thi-Ngoc, Duy Tan Dao, Duy Nguyen Vu Minh, Farhana Raduan, Giang Vu Son, Hoang Le, Inke Nadia Diniyanti Lubis, Kamran Khalid, Khamsiah Nawawi, Koong Jun Kit, Laisham Trinity Meetei, Mariam Fatima, Muhammad Fahdy, Muhammad Ichwan, Nehar Sinam, Sanjib Singh Nepram, Nia Kania, Nipanka Goswami, Nomal Chandra Borah, Pandji Winata Nurikhwan, Mei Yen Phang, Purabi Sarkar, Duc Thanh Bui, Thanh Do Pham Nguyet, Van Thanh Nguyen, Quoc Viet Tran, Zhenzhi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSurgical systems in Southeast Asia were already experiencing significant supply shortages before the COVID-19 pandemic, which further exacerbated these deficits. With the aim of identifying modifiable factors for improvement, our study benchmarks surgical system efficiencies of eight Southeast Asian Hospital and Health Systems’ (HHS) in combating COVID-19, with an analysis spanning three levels: country, city and HHS.MethodsBased on 18-month data (January 2020 to June 2021), we developed a two-stage frontier benchmarking approach that applies efficiency benchmarking techniques to evaluate how close organisations operate relative to an efficiency frontier (representing the maximum potential output achievable with a given set of inputs). Stage 1 involves data envelopment analysis, which yields monthly national efficiency scores for five countries based on country-level panel data. Stage 2 involves stochastic frontier analysis, followed by evaluating HHS-level efficiency with HHS-level data over 64 possible model configurations, adjusting for stage 1 national-level efficiency scores as well as city-level responses.ResultsAmong the 64 possible stage 2 model configurations, two model clusters comprising 36 plausible models demonstrated superior fit to the observed data, thereby providing robust insights through majority voting. Policy measures related to access control and workplace closure were positively associated with recovery rates. Elective surgery volumes showed a negative association and emergency surgery volumes a positive association with efficiency. Our analysis indicates lags of 0–1 month between input changes and effects on the surgical system.ConclusionsInsights from the benchmarking of HHS efficiencies will help inform surgical system policy and responses. Access control and workplace closure policies are associated with COVID-19 recovery rates. Emergency surgery volumes are associated with higher efficiency and elective surgery volumes with lower efficiency. System responses to policy measures can manifest with lags of up to a month. Considering insights from multiple plausible models makes these conclusions more robust.Keywords: Public Health, COVID-19, Data Collection, Health Services Accessibility, methods

Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has resulted in over 750 million cases and over 7 million deaths in 2023.1 As the pandemic turns endemic, there is a need to draw lessons for the future from the catastrophe.2 Studies on comparative efficiency in managing the COVID-19 pandemic, focusing on national,3,8 regional9,12 and hospital-level13,15 policy measures and their efficiencies have been reported in the literature. Despite these studies, hospital and health system (HHS)-level studies are relatively sparse because of the paucity of detailed HHS-level data.

Surgical care was already under strain before the pandemic, with millions lacking access to essential surgery, particularly in low-income and middle-income countries. The Lancet Commission on Global Surgery found that five billion people did not have adequate access to surgical and anaesthesia care in 2014.16 Out of the economic losses from 2015 to 2030 in low-income and middle-income countries due to surgical system deficits, about half occur in Southeast Asia, East Asia and Oceania; South and South-East Asian countries have a large unmet surgical services needs.17 India, Indonesia and Pakistan were among the countries with the highest excess mortality rates during the pandemic period.18 Apart from Singapore, many Southeast Asian countries suffered from higher morbidity and mortality due to weaker healthcare systems and low levels of vaccination when the Delta variant of COVID-19 struck.19 Within HHS, the surgical workforce faced ‘distinct challenges’ in care delivery, given the inability of remote care to completely substitute for surgical services.20 COVID-19 forced a drastic reorganisation of surgical services, resulting in postponement of surgical care that was not time-critical, increased waitlist lengths and increased patient anxiety.21 22 There were also changes to pathways and models of care and workforce redeployment policies.

This study benchmarked the performance of eight Southeast Asian HHS to derive insights from policy measures undertaken and results achieved during the COVID-19 pandemic. Frontier benchmarking (FB) methods, which evaluate the efficiencies of organisations in terms of their performance relative to an efficiency frontier where the maximum potential outputs can be derived with a given set of inputs and available resources,23 were used. With a two-stage FB 2-FB) approach, we address this research question: what were the dynamics and drivers of HHS efficiency in providing surgical services while combating COVID-19, after adjusting for national efficiency and city-level responses? We control for the diversity of settings by looking at three levels of governance (national, city and HHS). This study is grounded in detailed proprietary HHS-specific data obtained through multilateral collaborations with regional HHS.

Data and methods

The 2-FB approach summarised in figure 1 is used to evaluate the relative efficiencies of HHS. This framework integrates the consideration of national with city and HHS level policies. Stage 1 employed Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) using the National Response Index (NRI), International Health Regulations Core Capacity Score (IHRCCS), population density and Gross Domestic Product (GDP) per capita as inputs and COVID-19 new cases per 1000 population and deaths per 10 000 population as outputs. The scales for the outputs are different as new cases have a much higher incidence than deaths. The resulting national efficiency scores were then incorporated as inputs in the stage 2 panel Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA), alongside City Response Indices (CRIs), hospital beds, numbers of doctors and nurses, emergency and surgical case volumes per bed and Hospital Response Indices (HRIs). The output variable in stage 2 was the recovery rate, computed as one minus the ratio of cumulative COVID-19 deaths to cumulative admissions.

Study data

In stage 1, panel data from five countries (Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, India and Pakistan) were analysed for the period from January 2020 to June 2021. National-level efficiency scores were calculated based on the NRI derived from the Oxford COVID-19 Government Response Tracker (OxCGRT),24 IHRCCS,25 GDP per capita and population density. Data were also sourced from the World Bank database.26 27 The reciprocal of population density (area per 1000 population) was used.28 The output variables were COVID-19 new cases per 1000 population and deaths per 10 000 population. Data were sourced from the WHO COVID-19 Dashboard.29 The output variables were adjusted based on y^ita=Ga−yita where Ga is a suitably large constant. yita represents the output variable a in time t for HHS i. Such an adjustment is commonly applied for the frontier models when a higher value of output is undesirable.30 31

In stage 2, site-specific data were obtained from eight HHS across five countries (Singapore, Malaysia, Indonesia, India and Pakistan). These HHS were part of a regional collaborative initiative aimed at strengthening health system resilience in response to the COVID-19 pandemic. They represent a diverse cross-section of health systems across South and Southeast Asia with varying levels of resources, governance structures and pandemic response capacities. The HHS included were as follows: the Singapore General Hospital (SGH) in Singapore, Hospital Canselor Tuanku Muhriz (HCTM) Universiti Kebangsaan Malaysia and University Malaya Medical Centre (UMMC) in Malaysia, Ulin General Hospital (UGH) and Adam Malik Hospital/Universitas Sumatera Utara (USU) in Indonesia, Guwahati Neurological Research Centre (GNRC; four sites in Guwahati city) and Regional Institute of Medical Sciences (RIMS) in India and Fatima Jinnah Medical University (FJMU) in Pakistan. These HHS served both patients with COVID-19 and general patients with varying bed capacities during the study period (online supplemental figure S1.1).

The HHS constituted a diverse regional reference set for the analysis. Our approach in stage 2 was adjusted for national-level and city-level demographic and response variables; the national-level efficiency scores computed in stage 1, the CRI and HRI were integrated to model hospital level efficiency in the stage 2 model. Both the HRI and CRI were adapted from the OxCGRT24 and validated through qualitative interviews and surveys with the partnering HHS.32 table 1 summarises the survey items for computing CRI and HRI. The development of the CRI and HRI was informed by a preceding qualitative study involving surgical leaders from tertiary healthcare institutions across South and Southeast Asia, which identified common institutional priorities and response strategies during the COVID-19 pandemic.33 The codebook for CRI and HRI is given in online supplemental file S2. Individual survey items were aggregated into response index subgroups for the analysis. These subgroups cover city-level and hospital-level responses. Detailed methodology given in online supplemental file S2 is adapted from the OxCGRT approach.24 COVID-19 beds, doctors deployed for COVID-19, nurses deployed for COVID-19, emergency and elective surgery volumes are also asked for in the surveys.32 These data are then normalised against the bed capacity of each corresponding HHS. The code book for the institutional survey is given in online supplemental file S3, comprising of four worksheets (General Information, Manpower, Facility, Volumes and Outcomes). Data collection was carried out from October 2021 to April 2022. Summary statistics for the data used in this study are provided in online supplemental table S1.1. The list of specialties included in the survey is given in online supplemental file S4. Intensive online meetings were organised with the HHS surgical leaders throughout the study period to clarify and validate the monthly responses received from partnering institutions.

2-Stage frontier benchmarking model

A simplified explanation of the 2-FB approach is presented here for clarity; a comprehensive, mathematically detailed description is provided in online supplemental file S5. Stage 1 of the 2-FB framework used the DEA approach to generate a national-level efficiency score. For this, four inputs were considered: NRI scores, IHRCCS scores, GDP per capita and population density. The two output variables were new COVID-19 cases per 1000 population and COVID-19 deaths per 10 000 population. In DEA, a Production Possibility Set defines the feasible universe of performance, that is, the set of all input–output combinations that are feasible based on the benchmarked entities. Thus, there is a ‘frontier’ of maximum achievable efficiency where the efficiency score will be one for benchmarked entities who are on this ‘frontier’. Efficiency scores are then computed per time period for all entities, assuming constant returns to scale and input orientation.34 The national efficiency score for each country was computed with the Benchmarking package of R.35

In stage 2, we considered a stochastic production function (SPF) for panel data of HHS i at time t to be represented by a stochastic production frontier. The inefficiencies of the benchmarked HHS are then derived based on the decomposition of inefficiency and error components with respect to this production frontier.36,38 The inefficiency and error terms are assumed to be independently distributed.37 The SPF can be estimated with the maximum likelihood estimation approach.23 We evaluated the output elasticities of the policy responses at the hospital and city levels and evaluated the effects on inefficiencies related to manpower deployed for COVID-19, the utilisation of COVID-19 beds and surgery volumes. The production frontier was modelled as a function of the CRI and HRI, with efficiency deviations influenced by resource allocation, hospital size and national-level performance. The production frontier of HHS i at time t was determined by (1) CRI; (2) HRI 1 – Access Control policies (HRI Group 1); (3) HRI 2 – COVID-19 manpower policies (HRI Group 2); (4) HRI 3 – Communication and monitoring policies (HRI Group 3); (5) HRI 4 – Surgical systems policies (HRI Group 4). Inefficiency effects were evaluated across the (1) number of beds used for patients with COVID-19, (2) number of doctors deployed for COVID-19 management, (3) number of nurses deployed for COVID-19 management, (4) emergency surgery volume, (5) elective surgery volume and (6) national efficiency scores computed in stage 1. The output variable is the case recovery rate. Given the case fatality rate (CFR), the case recovery rate is defined as 1-CFR. By clearly distinguishing the elasticities based on the hospital-level and city-level responses and inefficiencies related to the resource-related covariates, we can evaluate the impact of the local policy responses on the production frontier while adjusting for the national context using the efficiency scores from stage 1.

where dit represents the number of deaths in HHS i at time t and pit represents the number of inpatient admissions in HHS i at time t. We considered a lead effect, κ, of the output variables to ensure the plausible delayed effects for the interventions39 40: Yitm,κ=yi,t+κm or Yitc,κ=yi,t+κc for κ ∈ {0, 1, …, 5}. Aggregated statistics associated with five families of policy measures (CRI, HRI.1, …, HRI.5) were used for the input variables, xit∈R1×|K|.

Policy measures are dynamic and could change across months.24 To evaluate the plausible accumulation of policy effects, we further considered various moving average lags, ζ ∈ {0, 1, …, 5}, of the policy measures defined as Xijtζ=1ζ+1∑k=0ζxij,t-k,∀j∈K. We can utilise either the mean or median statistics for these policy measures. The number of clinicians deployed can also be evaluated at both the aggregated level (where we considered the sum of nurses and doctors) or separately. The roles of these clinicians may be distinct in the HHS, but they were all diverted to serve the needs of patients with COVID-19 regardless. Given the possible variants of input variables, various model structures were estimated across the combination of input and output variables as listed in table 2. The various combinations of model features resulted in 64 plausible parametric forms. Evaluating multiple model structures is like the bagging concept in ensemble learning as we aggregated the results of the stronger learners through majority voting.41 The approach can allow us to account for both model and parametric uncertainties.42 Models are then selected based on those with the better goodness-of-fit statistics. Goodness-of-fit is evaluated with the maximum log-likelihood (MLL).34 The significance of the effects is assessed at the 0.01 significance level. K-means clustering43 is then used to identify the group of models that are used to evaluate the significance of effects associated with the output variable. Results

While online supplemental table S1.1a shows the descriptive statistics for the stage 1 inputs and outputs over the 18- month study period (January 2020–June 2021), we summarise the main trends here. GDP per capita showed a notable skew, with a median of 0.378 and a mean of 1.588, ranging from a high of 6.718 in Singapore to a low of 0.141 in Pakistan. There was also a significant variation in area per 1000 population, with a maximum of 9.896 and a minimum of 0.124. IHRCCS scores ranged from 0.92 in Singapore to 0.52 in Pakistan. The NRI spanned from a low of 0.537 in Pakistan to a high of 0.652 in India, with India exhibiting the greatest fluctuation in NRI over the study period, as indicated by an SD of 0.219.

Similarly, online supplemental table S1.1b shows the overall descriptive statistics for stage 2 response and explanatory variables during the study period. HHS-wise descriptive statistics are provided in online supplemental table S1.2. The variables defining the production function are the CRI and HRI. Notably, this reference set of variables displays a diverse range of COVID-19 response actions. HRI.3 (communication and monitoring) has the highest mean (mean 76.82, SD 76.67), whereas HRI.5 (vaccination policy) has the lowest (mean 21.29, SD 10). All response indices demonstrate significant variability across the HHS and study period except for HRI.5 (vaccination policies).

With regard to the drivers of HHS inefficiency, HCTM shows the highest normalised surgical volume (mean 1.40, SD 0.43) (online supplemental table S1.2). In contrast, FJMU and GNRC have the highest normalised emergency (mean 1.08, SD 0.22) and elective (mean 1.13, SD 0.36). ULin has the lowest normalised total surgery (mean 0.34, SD 0.22). USU exhibits the lowest normalised emergency volume (mean 0.050, SD 0.02). For COVID-19 bed utilisation, ULin reports the highest (mean 0.30, SD 0.15). The lowest is FJMU (mean 0.06, SD 0.04). GNRC deploys the highest number of clinicians to COVID-19-related operations (mean 1.37, SD 0.86). As with the total clinicians deployed, GNRC shows the highest value (mean 0.96, SD 0.60) of nurses deployed. Both the cumulative response and marginal response show similar trends, with SGH having the highest cumulative response (mean 0.998, SD 0.004), whereas USU has the lowest cumulative response (mean 0.51, SD 0.21).

MLL values (which reflect how plausible it is that the data observed are explained by the hypothesised model) and γ parameters (which reflect how much of the variation is attributable to inefficiency) for the 64 parametric forms exhibited bimodal distributions (online supplemental figure S1.2a,b), indicating distinct groups. K-means cluster analysis utilising normalised MLL and γ further identified three distinct clusters, with two clusters belonging to the higher MLL group. Online supplemental figure S1.3 shows the calibration for the K-means clustering algorithm. Out of the three clusters, two clusters of 36 models with higher MLL values is defined as the reference set. The reference set is further differentiated into the high γ group (28 models, MLL ≥70%, mean γ=0.873, SD=0.066, range 0.746–0.954) and the low γ group (eight models, MLL<70%, mean γ=0.548, SD=0.020, range 0.515–0.584).

From the reference set, effect directions are summarised in table 3. Online supplemental figure S1.4 shows the distributions of the mean effect sizes in the form of kernel density estimates. Notably, policy measures related to access control (HRI.1) and workplace closure (HRI.2) are positively associated with the recovery rates (p<0.01). Conversely, volumes of elective surgery showed significant positive association as a source of inefficiency, whereas emergency surgery volumes were significantly negatively associated as a source of inefficiency (p<0.01). This implies that higher elective surgical volumes are associated with lower efficiencies, whereas higher emergency volumes are associated with higher efficiencies. Similar patterns were observed between the low and high γ subsets of models, with HRI.1 showing positive significance (online supplemental tables S1.3, S1.4; for the effect visualisation, see online supplemental figures S1.5–S1.8).

Discussion

This study derived insights into the effects of policy measures undertaken for the management of COVID-19 by eight HHS in Asia, adjusting for inefficiencies arising from national efficiency, city-level response stringency and other inefficiency covariates. Our analysis has implications for both methodology and policy.

In terms of methodology, we applied the idea of many-model thinking44 by drawing our insights from a set of acceptable models using an ensemble learning method applied on a parametric production function. We considered model uncertainty to generate insights via majority voting process across multiple well-performing models. This is similar to the bagging idea in ensemble learning in the machine learning literature.45 46 A number of other studies have looked at the drivers of effective policy responses in COVID-19 at the country-level (eg, 3,5), some of them have utilised similar benchmarking methodologies with the consideration of policy levers.7 11 None of the existing studies have leveraged on a many-model thinking approach in deriving insights on the effectiveness of non-pharmaceutical interventions (NPIs) during the pandemic. The study also contributed additional evidence on efficiency drivers through data contributed by eight HHS in five different countries in Asia.

The setting of this study exhibits epidemiological and system uncertainty.47 Maier et al48 recommend that under conditions of uncertainty, plausible scenarios should be examined rather than relying on a single best guess, which may lack robustness. In this study, we may not be able to a priori identify which outcomes may be most relevant, or whether there is a lag effect before any policy impact is observed.7 49 Cumulative and marginal responses, described in Equation 1(a) and (b), may also result in differing policy effects. The high degree of uncertainty makes it ‘easy for honest people to come to different conclusions’.50 Our many-model ensemble learning approach helps to draw inferences on significant associations across multiple (36) high-performing models, rather than one single model. Such a many-model approach has been shown to be a valid means of generating robust insights in situations of uncertainty.42 51 The approach of iterating through 64 possible models and drawing inferences based on majority voting from a subset of 36 plausible models is simple and easily replicable methodology that provides more robust inferences under model uncertainty.

From a policy perspective, there are lags between HHS actions being taken and system responses becoming visible. System response is associated with HHS actions with a response lag of 0 to 1 month for most of the reference set models (see online supplemental figure S1.9). This finding is similar to what was found from a study in Germany,52 where the effects of NPIs were associated with reduced intensive care unit cases with a lag of not less than 5 days, with the main effect being after 10–15 days. System response is lagged in the majority of the reference set models (see online supplemental figure S1.10). These lags imply that contextual knowledge and political will are necessary to facilitate NPIs, even in the absence of immediately visible results. For future novel pandemics or other health crises, response and moving average lags may have dynamics that are different from the case of COVID-19. Policymakers will need to do the right things with the knowledge that results will take time to show up.

Policy measures such as access control (HRI.1) and workplace closure (HRI.2) are positively correlated with recovery rates. This is similar to several empirical studies reporting the impact of business closure and mobility restrictions on COVID-19 incidence rates.53 A crucial finding from our study is the negative association of emergency surgery volumes with HHS inefficiency. Together with the positive association of elective surgery volumes, this presents additional evidence on the role of surgical system volumes and their relationship to efficient management of COVID-19. This finding also underscores the differential impact of elective vs emergency procedures on health system performance during the pandemic. It also highlighted the importance of managing elective surgical volumes carefully to avoid its negative impact on efficiencies. This is consistent with published evidence from outside the reference set countries, for example, Canada54 and Switzerland,55 underlining the complex interplay between policy implementation, surgical management, and their combined effects on health system efficiency during a global health crisis.

CRI, the city-level response index, exhibits a significant negative association with the efficiency frontier, implying that more stringent responses in a city lowered the efficiency frontier for an HHS in the city. This is counterintuitive, but it may reflect the phenomenon that high CRI values prevailed during times of greater crises, when recovery rates of the HHS were undermined by high stress in the national and regional systems. National-level efficiency in dealing with COVID-19 is negatively associated with HHS inefficiency (ie, the ability of the HHS to be closer to the efficiency frontier increases when national efficiency is higher), in line with an earlier study that found national policy actions to be significant in the successful management of the COVID-19 pandemic.56 Neither physical (hospital bed) nor human resources (doctor and nurse) dedicated to COVID-19 management have a significant effect on inefficiency; this may be because these resources were stretched beyond their usual limits.57 Unmeasured factors such as resource utilisation, work intensity and other non-linear dynamics may be at play in driving inefficiencies. We further recognise that residual confounding factors may make the analyses susceptible to Simpson’s paradox,58 whereas non-linear relationships may not be adequately captured by the models.

Efficiency can evolve dynamically across changes in policy measures and the COVID-19 situation throughout the 18-month study period. Nonetheless, we found that the efficiency rankings were stable across the benchmark period. Significant variations in efficiency scores were observed in four institutions: GNRC (Guwahati, India), RIMS (Imphal, India), Ulin Hospital (South Kalimantan, Indonesia) and USU Hospital (North Sumatra, Indonesia). In contrast, the other four HHS maintained relatively consistent mean efficiency scores. This consistency may reflect effective management practices as the policy measures keep pace with increased knowledge and learning through the pandemic.59 60 These trends are shown in figure 3. The countries of the HHS studied were able to quickly and effectively pivot in novel situations when the number of COVID-19 cases and CFR evolved.

We acknowledge that not all potential covariates or features are included in our analysis. For example, the longer-term effects of surgical volumes were not evaluated. The HHS studied are all urban. This is a limitation of this study; however, surgical services are concentrated in urban areas in the reference set countries.17 Further, in stage 2 of our 2-FB approach, we have also assumed a parametric production function based on the Cobb–Douglas model with exogenous inefficiency covariates. Our results should be viewed only within the context of our study objective. The reliance on self-reported policy measures may have also introduced self-reported bias. We have attempted to mitigate the bias to achieve face validity by adopting a mixed methods approach. This approach included extensive stakeholder interviews prior to the quantitative benchmarking and prospective validation of both the survey responses and results. Details of the qualitative study have been reported earlier.33

Conclusion

This study utilised a 2-FB approach to systematically evaluate the performance of surgical systems across eight HHS in Asia. Notably, the framework addressed both model and data uncertainties through a many-model approach that avoids drawing conclusions from a single ‘best’ model. Despite its limitations, this study provides valuable insights into the dynamics of surgical service delivery during the COVID-19 pandemic, adjusted for national-level and city-level responses and exogenous factors contributing to inefficiencies. We hope that this study offers a methodologically sound framework that can be applied broadly for robust insight. The extensive regional collaboration among Southeast Asian partners further underscores our study’s unique contribution to strengthening regional healthcare system performance during crisis conditions.
