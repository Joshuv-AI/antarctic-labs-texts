Introduction

The health of people is negatively impacted by emergencies and catastrophes, and many lives are lost as a result. The guidelines for humanitarian action during times of crisis, whether brought on by armed war or natural disasters, are addressed by the humanitarian principles. Humanitarian principles can be defined as a useful instrument for gaining access to the underprivileged population by winning over local communities and stakeholders. Fundamental principles of impartiality, independence, neutrality, and humanity are at the center of all humanitarian operations. Norwegian Refugee Council defined humanitarian principles as “principles that govern the practicability level of majorily humanity, impartiality, neutrality, and independence which are under increased scrutiny and pressure” [1]. The principles have their origins in International Humanitarian Law (IHL), a body of legislation that governs armed conflict and aims to mitigate its consequences. Moreover, IHL specifies the requirements that parties to a conflict may place on anyone attempting to help those suffering [2]. Humanitarian aid must be founded on the following four legal grounds as per international humanitarian law:

Humanity is a principle with two equal dimensions. It implies that everyone has a responsibility, both domestically and globally, to stop and lessen human suffering wherever it occurs. Furthermore, it implies that all people’s dignity needs to be respected and preserved [1].

Impartiality is a principle where aid for humanitarian issues must be provided only based on significance, without taking into account the differences in need across or within various social groups. Therefore, discrimination on the grounds of race, nationality, religion, class, or political convictions is prohibited. On the other hand, the requirements of those in need should be the only factors driving humanitarian help, with the most urgent situations receiving precedence [1, 2].

Therefore, discrimination on the grounds of race, nationality, religion, class, or political convictions is prohibited. On the other hand, the requirements of those in need should be the only factors driving humanitarian help, with the most urgent situations receiving precedence [1, 2].

Neutrality is a principle that requires that humanitarian aid not unfairly treat participants in conflicts. Therefore, the impartiality and neutrality principles are not all that dissimilar, and this is especially evident when humanitarian help is disbursed in circumstances including political, racial, religious, or ideological disputes [1, 3].

Therefore, the impartiality and neutrality principles are not all that dissimilar, and this is especially evident when humanitarian help is disbursed in circumstances including political, racial, religious, or ideological disputes [1, 3].

Independence is the other principle whereby humanitarian aims must stand alone from political, economic, military, or other interests. By definition, humanitarian aid is used to lessen or stop the suffering of those affected by humanitarian disasters. Humanitarian agencies must, therefore, develop and carry out their policies independently of governmental policies and activities and only operate in conformity with the core humanitarian values to uphold the concept of independence [2].

Though the concept of humanitarian principles is well-established, research on the value of impartial, independent, and neutral humanitarian action has been done in several nations. The findings indicate that it can be difficult to comprehend and put humanitarian principles into practice [3].

Selectively, impartiality is not always considered a principle, according to research done in Colombia on the knowledge of these humanitarian ideals in the populations impacted by emergency events. Additionally, the study demonstrated how the lack of humanitarian availability in the most troublesome locations leads to the distribution of humanitarian supplies mostly toward places with easier access. In reality, though, access is frequently determined by the authorities, who may recommend or extend invitations to humanitarians to assist in particular areas of strategic importance. As per the authorities, enhancing communication with community leaders and authorities to verify and validate needs-related information can enhance impartiality [1, 4].

For humanitarian organizations, the continual challenge is to apply the humanitarian principles of humanism, impartiality, neutrality, and independence in a relevant way in real-world operational contexts. The International Committee of the Red Cross (ICRC), constrained by these standards, has gradually created throughout time a logical framework that enables its leadership and local staff to act following these principles while creating customized solutions and realistic approaches [4].

Natural disasters are a worldwide issue that, due to their abrupt onset, can have catastrophic consequences. When a tragedy strikes in this way, inequality and unfairness are likely to influence how relief supplies are distributed to impacted populations, favoring some ethnic or religious groups over others. This makes it more difficult to uphold the established humanitarian action norms. It was noted that this was the case in 2004 during the Asian tsunami. One instance of a conflict in which military personnel were charged with carrying out a humanitarian intervention is the United States (US) invasion of Afghanistan in 2003. This led to the instrumentalization of aid for military reasons. In one instance, government forces pretended to be locals and helped the people in exchange for their telling the soldiers wearing disguises where the insurgents were [5].

In general, humanitarian principles are not observed in a desirable pattern in every sector, and some are not clearly understood. For example, beneficiaries and humanitarians have differing interpretations of what impartiality and independence entail. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to evaluate how the staff and clients at Tikur Anbessa Specialized Hospital (TASH) felt about the applicability of the four humanitarian principles in the healthcare settings where they serve emergency cases and the real difficulties they encounter when responding to emergency conditions. The study context for this research is a hospital humanitarian setting where principles of impartiality, independence, neutrality, and humanity have the tendency of applicability whether the setup is found in low- or middle-income countries (LMIC).

Methodology

Study setting

The study was conducted in TASH, located in Lideta Sub City, Addis Ababa, Ethiopia. TASH is the highest referral teaching hospital seeing approximately 370,000-400,000 patients a year but the exact number is not known. TASH was established in 1972 and has about 800 beds, 169 specialists, and 65 non-teaching Doctors. It is administered by Addis Ababa University found in the capital city. The emergency departments see around 80,000 patients a year.

The hospital provides health care services for clients found all over the country since it is the highest referral hospital. Due to this, several cases are investigated with great burden and workload. One of the departments giving the services is an emergency department where several humanitarian issues are encountered. Because of the wide range of services provided by TASH; the practicability of the humanitarian principles would probably be a relevant issue of investigation in this study setting.

Study design and approach

Facility-based descriptive and explanatory cross-sectional study designs were employed in this study. Both quantitative and qualitative methods were used to gain insights into the perceptions, experiences, and challenges regarding the humanitarian principles in emergency cases in TASH. Hence, a convergent parallel type of mixed methods approach was used to obtain deeper information on the survey where both the quantitative and qualitative data were collected at the same time.

The variables explored by the quantitative analysis include principles of humanity, impartiality, neutrality, and independence applicability from the perspective of the registrar, pharmacist, physician, laboratory technologists, and staff in charge of ancillary services during emergency cases in TASH. The variables explored by the qualitative analysis include the identification of challenges hindering the practicability of the four humanitarian principles from the perspective of registrar, pharmacist, physician, laboratory technologists, and staff in charge of ancillary services during emergency cases in TASH. Hence, the level of practices is best addressed with quantitative approaches whereas challenges are best described using qualitative approaches.

Sampling technique and instrument

The quantitative study used a simple random sampling technique to collect data with structured questionnaires from a total of 209 individuals in TASH. Of 209 participants 105 included staff and the rest 104 included clients (patients and caregivers in the emergency room) of TASH (Fig. 1). The participants were selected randomly based on their willingness and the data was collected from July 08–22, 2023. Structured questionnaires were developed in English and the questionnaires were translated into Amharic, the working language of the country. The questionnaire was developed for this study and has not been published elsewhere. The process of translation involved two researchers whose local language is Amharic. The instrument was independently translated word by word into the native language by these two researchers. Online dictionaries were used to help convert difficult words that have compatibility issues in both languages.

Study population

The qualitative study used interview guide developed to identify challenges hindering the practicability of the four humanitarian principles in TASH. Similar to the questionnaire; the interview guide was developed in English and the guide was translated into Amharic by two researchers whose native language is Amharic, the working language of the study area. The interview included study participants purposively selected from registrar, pharmacist, physician, laboratory technologists, and staff in charge of ancillary services and clients in emergency room. The number of participants for the interview was limited based on the data saturation gathered from the respondents since the responses given by the participants became very similar after several interviews conducted continuously.

Data collection

The data collection process for the quantitative study involves study participants from both staff (i.e., registrar, pharmacist, physician, laboratory technologists, and staff in charge of ancillary services) and clients (i.e., patients and caregivers) are included in the study because they are the relevant contributors to the survey conducted. The questionnaire disseminated to the study participants was about the perception of the practicability of the principles of impartiality, independence, neutrality, and humanity in the TASH setup. Hence, to gather the relevant responses for the assessment, the staff and clients are the primary sources of the data. In one way or another, all these stakeholders experience such practices of humanitarian services in the hospital setup.

The qualitative interview questions focus about major challenges hindering the practicability of impartiality, independence, humanity, and neutrality principles during emergency service provisions in TASH. The interview was conducted by a researcher who received some training in qualitative research methods. The qualitative data was collected from participants of the interview which include stakeholders from staff (i.e., registrar, pharmacist, physician, laboratory technologists, and staff in charge of ancillary services) and clients (i.e., patients and caregivers). The interviews were carried out ranged from 20 to 30 min in duration. The researcher ensured that conversational behavior was avoided during the interviews to maintain the integrity of the data collection process. The purpose of the interviews was to gain a deeper understanding of the research topic and to gather relevant information from the study participants using specified themes.

Data analysis

The questionnaire for the quantitative study involves a 5-point Likert scale. The quantitative data were analyzed using the tallying method and scientific calculator, and then frequencies and percentages were determined. The result of the study was presented by tables and pie charts. Statistical Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 23 was used for analysis. Descriptive methods (mean, standard deviation, and percentages) and inferential statistics (correlation) were used to analyze the quantitative data.

The qualitative data was analyzed thematically where four themes emerged. In this phase, study participants were interviewed until data saturation of the responses were achieved. The interview transcript coding software called NVivo was used for analysis. Eventually, four themes emerged which include; challenges related to humanity, challenges related to impartiality, challenges related to neutrality, and challenges related to independence. For respondents not willing to be audio recorded narrative analysis technique was used to interpret the responses of the participants.

The findings of the study are reported in three sections. In the first part, socio-demographic characteristics are summarized; in the second part quantitative results are examined finally qualitative results are reported.

Among 209 participants 76 (36.4%) were clients, 28 (13.4%) were relatives of the clients and the rest staff. The majority, 128 (65%) of the study participants were males and half of the study participants were in the age group of 20–29 years. Regarding the work experience around 42% of the staff has 1–3 years of experience and only 1.6% has more than 20 years of work experience. 64% of the respondents were general practitioners (Table 1).

Qualitative findings

The qualitative findings focused on the challenges encountered related to the four humanitarian principles practicability. Challenges hindering the practicability of the principle of humanity are poor knowledge of Health Care Practitioners (HCPs) about their responsibilities and lack of a positive mind (personal attitude). Most of the HCPs are not well aware of their duties of client services other than the routine professional activities they perform in the hospital setting.

Challenges hindering the practicability of the principle of neutrality are ethnic differences among clients and HCPs, language differences, and unavailability of laboratory equipment resulting in illegal referral to private diagnostic centers. Especially, ethnic background of the clients was influencing the level of treatment provided by the HCPs in the hospital. A wide range of people are biased by ethnic differences in various institutions.

Challenges hindering the practicability of the principle of independence are unfavorable conditions in the health facility, political and governmental influences, policies that do not consider the needs of HCPs, and referral systems. Hence; pharmaceutical product prescribing, dispensing, and counseling services to its clients with emergency cases violate the independence principles of humanitarian operation.

Challenges hindering the practicability of the principle of impartiality are ethnic differences among clients & HCPs and language differences. The HCPs and other staff of the hospital show partiality especially in emergency conditions based on clients’ ethnic backgrounds, financial capability, and language barriers which are the main causes for partial thinking of the HCPs and other ancillary service providers. These practices violate the impartiality principles of humanitarian operations.

Based on the interview responses of the study participants; four broad themes emerged which include challenges of humanity, challenges of neutrality, challenges of independence and challenges of impartiality. Discussion

This study aimed to investigate the practicability and challenges of humanitarian principles including humanity, neutrality, independence, and impartiality in a referral hospital setup during the provision of emergency interventions. Accordingly, the most relevant stakeholders were involved in the study including staff (registrar, pharmacist, physician, laboratory technologists, and staff in charge of ancillary services) and clients (patients and caregivers) of TASH.

Hence, the principle of humanity is practiced better whereas impartiality is the least practiced principle in the hospital. Moreover, the correlation analysis shows that there is no significant association between the demography of the clients and the practicability of the four humanitarian principles. Several challenges that hinder the practicability of humanitarian principles like ethnicity, language barrier, lack of facility, economic and political challenges were identified. Most of the findings hold true with studies performed by Alameldeen and Terry where the details are provided in the following sections. [5, 6].

The demographic factors (age, gender, status, profession, work experience, and familiarity with the humanitarian principles) were not significantly associated with the applicability of humanitarian principles in a healthcare setting in case of emergency. This finding complies with the Brunei Darussalam Pharmacy Board Code of Ethics, which states that all HCPs shall treat patients without prejudice of race, age, gender, sexual orientation, nationality, religion, disability, or socio-economic status; and not allow personal beliefs to influence the management of patients [5–7].

Additionally, regardless of a person’s nationality, ethnic background, political or ideological beliefs, or social standing, the ultimate and only goal of humanitarian action is to prevent and alleviate suffering, protect life, and ensure respect for the dignity of people in desperate situations as a result of conflict or disaster [7]. Similarly, based on this study there is no association between demography and applicability of humanitarian principles meaning that the humanitarian principles are being practiced regardless of the demography of the clients in hospitals.

Concerning the applicability of the humanitarian principles; the effect of being liaison office staff, pharmacists, physicians, laboratory technicians, or registrar on rendering service according to humanitarian principles is non-significant which holds with the code of medical ethics for doctors in Ethiopia. The code of medical ethics states that in case of emergency, HCPs shall extend all possible assistance to the patient without fail [6–9] which also involves staff in charge of ancillary services.

Relief and aid organizations (NGOs) working in impoverished communities are guided by four main principles, regardless of the nature of the situation [3]. However, there is ongoing controversy and disagreement about the applicability, intent, and interpretation of the principles in humanitarian aid organizations [6]. Similarly; in this study, from the four humanitarian principles humanity (mean = 3.5) is practiced well whereas impartiality (mean = 3.2) is poorly practiced in TASH which is consistent with other health facilities worldwide.

Moreover, some of the principles of these humanitarian organizations are beginning to act against them. For example, neutrality might be interpreted as a lack of boldness or political intelligence, as well as apathy, suspended morality, or giving up on change. It is possible to interpret refusing to choose a side as placing all sides on an equal moral plane because those who choose that course do not allow themselves to think about the reasons behind the violence or their responsibility for it [10–12]. Qualitative findings of this study show that the practicability of humanitarian principles is highly challenged due to several factors as mentioned by the Norwegian Refugee Council and Handicap International, which states that there was a perception among many Syrian nationals that neutrality is impossible within such a polarized conflict [1, 3]. It was however recognized that perhaps neutrality was more feasible for non-Syrian staff and International Non-Governmental Organizations (INGOs). In addition to neutrality being understood as not favoring one party to the conflict over another, it was also associated with not discriminating against any groups [13, 14].

Generally, the seeming simplicity of humanitarian principles and the challenges of effectively implementing them in practical settings present a certain paradox. Putting them into practice is undoubtedly more of an art than a science. Applying principles necessitates understanding them in a dynamic environment. In its efforts to provide aid and protection in conflicts or other violent situations, the International Committee of the Red Cross still faces paradoxes, moral quandaries, and occasionally rejection even after decades of experience. Even with the best of intentions and meticulous preparation, success is not always guaranteed [12].

Conclusions

From the results of this study, it can be concluded that the state of being a staff (HCPs and those providing ancillary services) and client of TASH has nothing to do with developing a certain perception towards the practicability of the four humanitarian principles (i.e., humanity, impartiality, neutrality, and independence).

As a recommendation, the Ethiopian Federal Ministry of Health (MOH) should provide on-the-job training towards improving awareness of staff of health facilities about humanitarian principles; policymakers (i.e., FMOH and regulatory bodies) should provide a suitable working environment to mitigate staff frustration in applying humanitarian principles; health facilities should build their capacity to mitigate the issue of discrimination during provision of their services.
