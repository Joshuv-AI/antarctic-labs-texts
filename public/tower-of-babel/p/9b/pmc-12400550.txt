A study on physical activity and sedentary behavior patterns among Tibetan and Han adolescents in Xizang
Authors: Bowen Song, Chenyu Liu, Meng Yuan, Feng Peng, Xiaotong Zhang, Ming Li, Shuangshuang Sun, Desheng Li, Yi Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe global decline in adolescent physical activity (PA) levels has become a major public health concern, contributing to an increased risk of non-communicable diseases and impaired psychosocial development. Emerging evidence indicates significant ethnic disparities in PA patterns and sedentary behavior (SB) profiles among adolescents. Within the unique geographical and cultural context of the Tibetan Plateau, understanding behavioral differences between Tibetan and Han Chinese adolescents is crucial for designing effective, targeted health interventions. Therefore, this study aimed to examine ethnic-specific differences in PA levels and SB patterns among Tibetan and Han Chinese adolescents residing in Xizang.MethodsA total of 1,256 Tibetan and Han Chinese adolescents participated in this study. PA and SB were objectively measured using accelerometers. Data collection was segmented into four distinct periods: weekdays versus weekends, and in-school versus out-of-school time on weekdays. Ethnic differences in activity patterns were analyzed using one-way analysis of covariance, adjusting for age, body mass index (BMI), socioeconomic status (SES), and accelerometer wear time.ResultsOn weekdays, Tibetan boys engaged in significantly more moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) (44.1 ± 16.3 min/day) than Han Chinese boys (35.3 ± 10.9 min/day; p = 0.011, η2 = 0.011). Tibetan girls also exhibited significantly more sedentary time (654.1 ± 123.2 min/day) than Han Chinese girls (613.0 ± 103.8 min/day; p = 0.001, η2 = 0.018). On weekends, Tibetan boys recorded more MVPA (43.0 ± 27.1 min/day) than Han Chinese boys (35.3 ± 20.2 min/day; p = 0.003, η2 = 0.014) and greater sedentary time (561.0 ± 172.8 min/day) compared to Han Chinese boys (514.1 ± 175.6 min/day; p < 0.05). During school hours, Tibetan boys spent less time in SB (423.7 ± 112.2 min/day) than Han Chinese boys (450.8 ± 102.0 min/day; p = 0.011, η2 = 0.011). Out of school, Tibetan boys engaged in more MVPA (20.0 ± 7.9 min/day) than Han Chinese boys (15.2 ± 6.1 min/day; p < 0.01, η2 = 0.088), and Tibetan girls showed higher MVPA levels (20.5 ± 8.3 min/day) than Han Chinese girls (15.8 ± 6.2 min/day; p < 0.01, η2 = 0.060). Both Tibetan boys (645.5 ± 117.2 min/day) and girls (672.5 ± 123.8 min/day) had significantly more sedentary time than Han Chinese boys (639.4 ± 117.7 min/day) and girls (594.0 ± 102.9 min/day; p < 0.01, η2 = 0.180; p < 0.01, η2 = 0.207).ConclusionThis comparative analysis revealed clear ethnic differences in activity patterns. Tibetan adolescents exhibited higher overall PA levels and total sedentary time compared to Han Chinese adolescents. However, Tibetan adolescents tended to interrupt sedentary periods more frequently, whereas Han Chinese adolescents were more likely to engage in prolonged, uninterrupted SB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24290-y.

Background

Physical activity (PA) refers to any bodily movement produced by skeletal muscles that requires energy expenditure [1]. Moderate PA plays a critical role in adolescents’ psychological and physiological development [2], and the world health organization (WHO) recommends at least 60 min of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) daily for this age group [3]. However, studies indicate that over 80% of adolescents worldwide fail to meet this guideline [4]. In China, the average daily MVPA time among adolescents is 41.1 min [5], with only about 30% meeting the recommended level [6]. Rapid urbanization has contributed to this decline, with evidence showing that PA time is negatively correlated with residential density [7].

Most of adolescents’ daily time is dominated by sedentary behavior (SB), defined as low metabolic equivalent (MET < 1.5) activities such as sitting or reclining while awake [8]. The average daily sedentary time among Chinese youth is 529.8 min [5], primarily due to educational demands and screen use. WHO recommends limiting recreational screen time to under two hours per day and interrupting sedentary study periods every 30 min [3]. However, Chinese adolescents generally exceed these limits. On average, they spend 0.5 h daily on after-school tutoring during weekdays [9] and about 10 h per day in sedentary school activities. Additionally, 58.3% of primary and secondary students use electronic screens for over two hours daily, and 63.4% report continuous electronic device use for non-study purposes exceeding 15 min per session [10]. Excessive screen time (over two hours daily) has been significantly associated with lower academic performance in Chinese, mathematics, and English, showing a dose-response effect [11]. Research on Shanghai middle school students also identified a negative correlation between SB and learning engagement. Insufficient PA combined with prolonged SB (PSB) has been linked to reduced physical fitness, poor academic performance, and increased risks of metabolic disorders, obesity, and cardiovascular diseases in adolescents [12]. These trends underscore the urgent need to examine PA and SB patterns among Chinese adolescents to promote their physical and mental well-being.

According to the ecological model of health behavior, adolescents’ PA and SB are shaped by their environmental and temporal contexts [13]. Understanding these patterns across different settings and timeframes is essential for designing effective interventions to combat sedentary lifestyles. Empirical studies offer valuable insights into these behaviors. Russell et al. [14] used accelerometers to assess 14-year-old adolescents’ activity levels, identifying significant gender differences in all time periods except Sundays, especially during evening hours. Sarah et al. [15] found that adolescents’ sedentary time notably increased during school, after-school, and weekend periods, accompanied by decreased light PA (LPA), whereas MVPA remained stable. Xie et al. [16] reported that secondary school students exhibited more SB than elementary students, with both groups showing increased sedentary time after school and on weekends compared to weekdays. While numerous studies have explored age and gender differences in PA and SB, little research has focused on ethnic or racial disparities. Traditional culture and religious beliefs may lead to notable ethnic differences in activity patterns [17]. Exploring these variations could enhance understanding and inform culturally appropriate interventions.

Xizang, situated on the Qinghai-Tibet Plateau, presents a unique high-altitude environment that contributes to physiological distinctions between Tibetan and Han Chinese populations. Moreover, differences in religious beliefs and cultural practices may shape divergent PA and SB patterns among adolescents. Using accelerometry, Nie et al. [18] compared PA levels between Tibetan adolescents living at high altitudes and Han adolescents from lowland areas, revealing that while Tibetan youth had lower PA guideline compliance, their overall activity levels and daily MVPA duration were significantly higher. However, studies directly comparing PA and SB patterns between Tibetan and Han adolescents in Xizang remain limited, particularly concerning the specific characteristics of SB among Tibetan adolescents.

Therefore, this study employed accelerometer-based measurements to objectively assess PA and SB patterns among Tibetan and Han Chinese adolescents, focusing on temporal variations across weekdays versus weekends and in-school versus out-of-school periods. The primary aim was to examine these behavioral patterns and identify potential ethnic differences between the two groups to inform tailored health interventions.

Methods

Research design

This is a cross-sectional study, which aimed to investigate the PA and SB patterns of Tibetan and Han adolescents aged 12–18 years in Lhasa, Xizang Autonomous Region. Objective measurements were used to assess these behaviors, and ethnic differences between Tibetan and Han adolescents were compared.

Participants

Figure 1 shows the process diagram for the inclusion and exclusion of participants. This study was conducted in Lhasa from June to December 2020. Using convenience sampling, the Eighth Middle School of Lhasa and Lhasa Middle School were selected as study sites. Subsequently, a random cluster sampling method was employed to randomly select several classes of Tibetan and Han students from each grade to recruit participants. Considering that the ratio of Tibetan to Han students in these schools was approximately 3:1, to prevent sample size imbalance, three Tibetan classes and two Han classes (which had fewer students) were randomly selected from each grade.

Participant Inclusion and Exclusion Process Diagram

The inclusion criteria were as follows: enrollment in junior or senior high school; aged between 12 and 18 years; in good health with no physical disabilities; and voluntary participation with written informed consent provided by parents. After enrollment, participants were provided with accelerometers and questionnaires to assess daily PA, SB, and socioeconomic status (SES). Anthropometric measurements were also recorded.

A total of 1,275 students were initially recruited. After validity checks of the accelerometer data, 19 participants with invalid data were excluded, yielding a final sample of 1,256 students: 485 Tibetan boys, 465 Tibetan girls, 154 Han Chinese boys, and 152 Han Chinese girls. The management and quality control of accelerometer data were overseen by Dr. Sun Yi.

Measures

PA and SB measurement

PA and SB were objectively assessed using ActiGraph GT3X + triaxial accelerometers (ActiGraph, Pensacola, FL, USA). Participants were instructed to wear the device continuously for seven consecutive days (five school days and two weekend days), removing it only for sleeping, swimming, and bathing. The accelerometer was positioned on the right hip, with data collected in 5-second epochs. Data collection began at 9:00 AM on the day of distribution and ended at 9:00 AM on the seventh day, after which all devices were retrieved by the investigators. Raw data were downloaded and processed using ActiLife software (version 6.10.2).

Data were deemed valid based on the following criteria: (1) a minimum of two valid weekdays and one valid weekend day; and (2) at least 600 min of wear time per day [19].

The Evenson cut-point classification method [20] was applied to categorize activity intensity levels, with thresholds defined as follows: SB (0-100 counts per minute [cpm]), LPA; 101-2,295 cpm), moderate PA (MPA; 2,296-4,011 cpm), and vigorous PA (VPA; ≥4,012 cpm). A threshold of 20 min was used to distinguish prolonged sedentary bouts; sedentary periods exceeding this duration were identified as PSB [21]. These cut-points have been validated in previous research and demonstrate high reliability and validity in assessing PA levels among children and adolescents [22].

The monitoring period was further categorized into four distinct temporal segments: weekdays versus weekends, and school hours versus out-of-school hours on weekdays. Weekdays were defined as Monday through Friday, and weekends included Saturday and Sunday. For middle school students, school hours spanned 9:00 to 18:00, with out-of-school hours defined as 18:00–24:00 and 0:00–9:00. For high school students, school hours were from 8:00 to 22:00, with out-of-school hours defined as 22:00–24:00 and 0:00–8:00.

It should be noted that although the Xizang Autonomous Region is geographically located in the sixth time zone (approximately two hours behind Beijing, which is in the eighth time zone), the entire country operates on Beijing Standard Time. Therefore, local students’ rest schedules are adjusted in accordance with natural daylight patterns to accommodate the geographical environment.

Body mass index (BMI)

Height measurements were obtained following standardized protocols. Participants stood barefoot on a stadiometer base plate with their backs against the vertical column. They were instructed to maintain an upright posture with their torso straight and head positioned in the Frankfort horizontal plane. Measurements were recorded in centimeters, precise to one decimal place, with a tolerance of ± 0.5 cm.

Body weight was measured with participants wearing light clothing and standing barefoot on the center of a calibrated weighing platform. Participants were instructed to stand naturally and maintain a stable posture. Weight was recorded in kilograms, precise to one decimal place, with an accuracy of ± 0.1 kg.

After obtaining height and weight measurements, BMI was calculated using the formula: BMI = Weight (kg)/Height (m)2.

Socioeconomic Status (SES)

In this study, family SES was assessed using a questionnaire[the questionnaire guide can be found in Supplementary Document 1] that measured parental education level, parental occupation, and household income, following the method outlined by Guo et al. [23]. Parental education was scored based on the number of years of schooling completed. Parental occupation was classified according to the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO). Household monthly income was categorized into four income brackets; each assigned a score: ≤2,000 RMB = 2 points; 2,001–5,000 RMB = 5 points; 5,001–8,000 RMB = 8 points; 8,000 RMB = 10 points.

The collected data underwent screening and transformation, with missing values addressed appropriately. All variables were standardized before performing principal component analysis to derive the composite SES score.

Statistical analyses

The normality of the data was evaluated using histograms and Q-Q plots, which confirmed that the data followed a normal distribution. Consequently, a one-way analysis of covariance was conducted to examine ethnic differences in PA and SB patterns between Tibetan and Han Chinese adolescents, with age, BMI, SES, and accelerometer wear time included as covariates. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The significance level was set at p < 0.05. Effect sizes were interpreted according to established benchmarks: small (η2 ≤ 0.01), medium (0.01 < η2 ≤ 0.06), and large (η2 > 0.14) [24].

Results

Description of study participants

Table 1 presents the demographic variables of the 1,256 study participants. The gender distribution was almost equal, with 50.8% of participants being male, and Tibetan adolescents (77.3%) constituting the majority. The mean age of participants was 15.3 ± 1.8 years. Regarding PA, the mean level of LPA among Tibetan adolescents was 63.3 ± 21.7 min. Tibetan boys spent more time in LPA (66.4 ± 59.5 min) compared to Han Chinese boys (59.5 ± 18.3 min), with a statistically significant difference (p < 0.01). Similarly, Tibetan girls had more LPA (63.5 ± 23.5 min) than their Han Chinese counterparts (56.7 ± 15.3 min), and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.021).

The overall mean moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) for adolescents in Xizang was 35.7 ± 12.7 min. Tibetan boys engaged in more MVPA (38.6 ± 12.1 min) than Han Chinese boys (33.6 ± 11.9 min), and this difference was statistically significant (p < 0.01). Similarly, Tibetan girls participated in more MVPA (34.8 ± 12.6 min) than Han girls (31.4 ± 10.3 min), with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.038).

Regarding SB, the overall sedentary time for adolescents in Xizang was 583.9 ± 111.4 min. Tibetan boys had more sedentary time (594.4 ± 115.9 min) than Han Chinese boys (570.0 ± 107.5 min), and the difference was statistically significant (p = 0.001). The overall PSB time was 516.4 ± 176.1 min, with Tibetan boys spending more time in PSB (548.4 ± 16.6 min) compared to Han boys (537.4 ± 177.5 min), a statistically significant difference (p = 0.01). Tibetan girls also exhibited more PSB (486.5 ± 176.1 min) than Han girls (485.2 ± 192.6 min), with a statistically significant difference (p = 0.046).

Total amounts and patterns of PA and SB on weekdays and weekends

Tables 2 and 3 present the PA and SB profiles of Tibetan and Han boys and girls during the workday. For PA, Tibetan girls engaged in more LPA than Han girls (69.8 ± 23.9 vs. 65.5 ± 15.3 min), a difference that reached statistical significance (p = 0.012, η2 = 0.01). Tibetan boys accumulated more moderate-to-vigorous PA (MVPA) compared to Han boys (44.1 ± 16.3 vs. 35.3 ± 10.9 min), with the analysis revealing a significant difference (p = 0.011, η2 = 0.011). Moreover, total PA (TPA) was higher among Tibetan boys (102.4 ± 30.8 min) than their Han peers (96.5 ± 22.2 min), as indicated by a meaningful statistical difference (p = 0.046, η2 = 0.006). A similar pattern was observed in girls, with Tibetan girls engaging in more TPA than Han girls (103.5 ± 32.1 vs. 95.9 ± 21.5 min), a result that was statistically reliable (p = 0.009, η2 = 0.018). In terms of SB, Tibetan boys spent less time in PSB than Han boys (495.8 ± 125.7 vs. 569.2 ± 156.4 min), a finding that was statistically confirmed (p = 0.014, η2 = 0.014). Conversely, Tibetan girls spent more time in overall SB than Han girls (654.1 ± 123.2 vs. 612.0 ± 103.8 min), with the comparison yielding a significant difference (p = 0.001, η2 = 0.018).

Tables 4 and 5 summarize the PA and SB characteristics of Tibetan and Han students on weekends. During the weekend, Tibetan boys exhibited significantly greater LPA than Han boys (64.7 ± 30.7 vs. 54.4 ± 29.7 min, p = 0.002, η2 = 0.015). They also engaged in more MVPA (42.9 ± 27.1 vs. 35.3 ± 20.2 min), a difference that proved statistically meaningful (p = 0.003, η2 = 0.014). Furthermore, their TPA levels were considerably higher than those of Han boys (107.5 ± 53.1 vs. 89.7 ± 45.9 min, p = 0.001, η2 = 0.017). For SB, Tibetan boys spent more time being sedentary than Han boys (561.0 ± 172.8 vs. 514.1 ± 175.6 min), and this difference was statistically notable (p = 0.011, η2 = 0.01). They also recorded higher levels of PSB (601.0 ± 289.1 vs. 505.6 ± 338.2 min), with results supporting a significant difference (p = 0.002, η2 = 0.015). Lastly, the frequency of SB interruptions was greater among both Tibetan boys (0.7 ± 0.6) and girls (0.6 ± 0.5) compared to their Han counterparts (boys: 0.3 ± 0.4; girls: 0.3 ± 0.4). These differences were statistically robust (boys: p < 0.01, η2 = 0.072; girls: p < 0.01, η2 = 0.059).

Discussion

This study used accelerometers to assess the PA and SB patterns of Tibetan and Han adolescents in Lhasa City, Xizang Autonomous Region. The results showed that Tibetan adolescents engaged in significantly higher levels of MVPA and LPA compared to their Han counterparts across weekdays, weekends, and after-school periods. In addition, Tibetan adolescents exhibited a higher frequency of sedentary breaks and accumulated more sedentary time during weekends and after-school hours than Han adolescents. Conversely, Han adolescents showed higher LPA levels than Tibetan adolescents during school hours, except on weekends. Overall, the behavioral patterns of Tibetan adolescents were marked by longer durations of both PA and SB throughout all time periods, suggesting potential lifestyle differences influenced by cultural, environmental, or socioeconomic factors.

The average daily sitting time of teenagers in Lhasa has reached 583.9 min, surpassing the national average of 529.8 min for Chinese children and adolescents [5]. This elevated sedentary level may be attributed to the unique environmental characteristics of the Tibetan Plateau, such as low atmospheric pressure, hypoxia, aridity, and intense solar radiation [25]. Further analysis revealed that Tibetan adolescents had higher sedentary time than Han adolescents during weekends and out-of-school hours but lower sedentary time during school hours. This difference may be associated with traditional Tibetan religious practices, including meditation, sutra chanting, and mantra recitation—which are typically sedentary [26]. These cultural activities likely contribute to the increased sedentary time observed among Tibetan adolescents outside school.

Interestingly, despite spending more time sedentary overall, Tibetan adolescents demonstrated more frequent sedentary interruptions than Han adolescents in Lhasa. Prior studies suggest that such interruptions can improve serum lipid profiles [27] and reduce metabolic risks related to PSB [28]. Therefore, although Tibetan adolescents accumulated more sedentary time, the potential health risks may be mitigated by these frequent breaks. Additionally, PSB during school hours accounted for only 23.8% of total SB—the lowest proportion among all measured periods. In general, Tibetan adolescents displayed a sedentary pattern dominated by extended sedentary durations during all periods except school hours, emphasizing the need for targeted interventions to reduce sedentary time, particularly outside school.

This study found that the average daily MVPA among Tibetan adolescents in Lhasa was 35.7 min, lower than the national average of 41.1 min for Chinese adolescents. However, Tibetan adolescents demonstrated higher MVPA levels during weekdays, weekends, and out-of-school periods compared to their Han Chinese peers. A key factor underlying this difference is the superior physiological adaptation of Tibetans to the plateau environment. Over generations, Tibetans have developed genetic mutations that support high-altitude living, resulting in traits such as greater infant birth weights and stable arterial oxygen saturation [29]. In contrast, large-scale Han migration to Xizang only began after the 1970 s, offering insufficient time for comparable physiological adaptation [30].

Cultural and religious practices also contribute to higher MVPA among Tibetan adolescents. Traditional activities such as sutra-transferring, mountaineering, prayer wheel rotation, dancing, horse racing, tug-of-war, and wrestling often involve MVPA-level exertion [31], providing additional opportunities for PA outside school hours. MVPA accounted for 33.4% of total PA on weekdays and 38.6% on weekends among Tibetan adolescents, with significantly higher proportions on weekends. This pattern reflects the “weekend warrior” phenomenon, where individuals concentrate PA into weekends. Previous research suggests that meeting MVPA guidelines, even when limited to 1–2 days per week, can reduce obesity and mortality risks compared to inactivity [32]. Given the academic pressures adolescents face during weekdays, promoting MVPA on weekends offers a practical strategy to support healthy physical development. For Tibetan adolescents, whose cultural practices favor higher MVPA during non-school periods, such approaches could be especially effective. Policymakers and educators should consider integrating cultural activities and weekend opportunities to further enhance PA engagement in this population.

Recently, China issued the Outline for the Construction of a Stronger Education State (2024–2035), recommending that primary and secondary school students engage in no less than two hours of daily PA. In this study, Tibetan adolescents accumulated an average TPA of 109.4 min per day, higher than their Han counterparts, but still below the recommended standard. Notably, in-school TPA accounted for 14.7% of total TPA, while out-of-school TPA comprised 21.5%, indicating that although school hours provided more absolute activity time, adolescents were proportionally more active outside school. This suggests the need for measures to increase in-school TPA in Xizang.

Recess represents an important opportunity to enhance in-school PA. One study demonstrated that a targeted recess intervention increased students’ recess TPA by 4.7 min [33], highlighting the potential of structured recess to boost PA levels. Additionally, improving the quality of physical education (PE) instruction is essential. Research indicates that when students participate in engaging and meaningful games during PE classes, their PA time increases by 20% compared to free-play PE sessions [34]. Thus, schools must enhance PE teaching quality, maximize the use of each class period, and create opportunities for structured PA to raise in-school TPA effectively.

A principal strength of this study was its use of accelerometry to objectively assess PA and SB patterns among Tibetan and Han adolescents in a high-altitude region. Device-based measurements provided accurate, reliable data reflecting daily activity patterns more authentically than self-reported methods. This approach offers valuable insights into behavior patterns in the distinct geographical and cultural context of Xizang. However, the study has two main limitations. First, the accelerometer’s inability to distinguish between static postures (e.g., standing vs. sitting) may have led to an overestimation of sedentary time. Second, the device lacked contextual data on activity types and settings. Future research should adopt multimodal methods combining accelerometry with self-reports and posture sensors to enable richer, more precise behavioral analysis through data triangulation. These improvements will help deepen understanding of PA and SB patterns in culturally distinct populations.

Conclusions

Tibetan adolescents demonstrated higher total PA time, sedentary time, and frequency of sedentary breaks compared to Han adolescents, whose SB was more prolonged. Despite these differences, the overall distribution patterns of PA and SB were largely similar between the two groups. Based on these characteristics, targeted interventions should be designed to increase PA levels, reduce daily sedentary time, and promote the physical and mental health of Tibetan adolescents.
