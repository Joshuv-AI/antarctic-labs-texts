Mitochondrial DNA replication is regulated by endoplasmic reticulum-mitochondrial contact sites, the mitochondrial calcium uniporter, and manganese
Authors: Amaia Lopez de Arbina, Angelica Zamudio-Ochoa, Mikel Muñoz-Oreja, Diego Perez-Rodriguez, Laura Mosqueira-Martín, Rebecca Lasalandra, Marina Villar-Fernandez, Uxoa Fernandez-Pelayo, Laura Rodriguez-Gomez, Seungtae Lee, Francisco Gil-Bea, Nerea Osinalde, Ainara Vallejo-Illaramendi, Dmitry Temiakov, Antonella Spinazzola, Ian J Holt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondrial DNA replication occurs at contact sites between the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) and mitochondria (ERMCS). Beyond the known role of the tubular ER protein RTN4, the factors regulating this process are poorly defined. Here, we show that repressing the ER protein ERLIN2 in human fibroblasts depletes ER–mitochondrial contact sites and inhibits mitochondrial DNA replication, as does silencing RTN4 or the ER–mitochondrial tether GRP75. GRP75 or RTN4 scarcity also decreases the level of the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU), whose inhibition blocks mitochondrial DNA synthesis. Because ERMCS depletion did not diminish mitochondrial calcium, and MCU complex can transport manganese, we tested whether manganese could bypass these defects. Manganese supplementation restored mitochondrial DNA replication in cells lacking ERMCS or with inhibited MCU, identifying manganese as a critical mediator. We then considered mitochondrial transcription as a potential manganese target, since it provides both transcripts for gene expression and primers for DNA replication. In vitro, manganese inhibits transcription re-start and stimulates RNA synthesis at the light-strand origin of replication. These findings support a model in which ER–mitochondrial contact sites, in conjunction with MCU, deliver manganese from the ER to mitochondria to promote DNA replication, potentially by modulating mitochondrial RNA polymerase activity.

Introduction

Energy production by mitochondria depends on 13 proteins encoded by the small circles of DNA located inside the organelles. The mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) molecules form nucleoprotein complexes, or nucleoids, that are closely and tightly associated with the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM) [1–6]. It has been proposed that the mtDNA is supported in the IMM by cholesterol microdomains, as the small amount of cholesterol associated with mitochondria chiefly co-fractionates with the mtDNA after mitochondrial lysis with mild detergent [7]. Moreover, cholesterol appears to be functionally important for mtDNA metabolism, as pharmacological or genetic perturbation of cholesterol homeostasis induces mtDNA abnormalities [8]. Likewise, cholesterol’s yeast counterpart, ergosterol, is essential for mtDNA maintenance [9]. Cholesterol-rich microdomains are also integral to the contact sites formed between the mitochondria and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) [10, 11]. ER–mitochondrial contact sites, or ERMCS, facilitate mitochondrial fission [12–14] and impact mtDNA replication and segregation [15, 16]. However, little is known about the ERMCS proteins that contribute to mtDNA maintenance, nor how the ER regulates mtDNA replication. ERLIN2 (ER lipid-raft associated 2) is a protein component of ER lipid microdomains [17], with cholesterol-binding properties [18], and a canonical marker of ERMCS [10, 19]. Hence, we hypothesized that ERLIN2 would be needed to form stable ERMCS and so would support mtDNA metabolism.

Here, we show that repressing the expression of ERLIN2 markedly inhibits mtDNA synthesis, as does gene silencing of the ER tubular protein reticulon 4 (RTN4), or the ERMCS bridging protein GRP75, or inhibiting the mitochondrial calcium uniporter, MCU. MCU complex transports manganese as well as calcium, and the former restores mtDNA replication in cells with few ERMCS. In vitro, manganese inhibits re-initiation of the mitochondrial transcription machinery, which would favour the production of primers for DNA replication over polycistronic transcripts for expression. Therefore, we propose that the ER regulates mtDNA replication by controlling manganese availability in the mitochondria.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and cell culture

All chemicals, unless otherwise stated, were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich with respective CAS number: thapsigargin (67526-95-8), tunicamycin (11089-65-9), 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU) (59-14-3), 5-ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) (61135-33-9), EdU Click-it® Thermo Fisher (C10337), MCU-i4 (HY-138620, MedChemExpress), MnCl2 (13446-34-9), cyclohexamide (66-81-9), actinonin (13434-13-4), HPG Click-it® Thermo Fisher (C10186). Primary human fibroblasts were routinely maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM, Gibco) containing 25 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 5% penicillin and streptomycin (Gibco), 5% CO2, at 37°C. All the cell lines were regularly confirmed free of mycoplasma, using the Venor Gem Classic Mycoplasma PCR Detection Kit (Minerva Biolabs).

For RNAi experiments, primary human fibroblasts were transfected twice (the second one, 72 h after the first) with Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX (Invitrogen) and 10 nM double-stranded RNA targeting gene of interest with following catalogue # and IDs: RTN4, catalogue #4392420, ID s32767 and s32768; ERLIN2, the catalogue #4427037, ID s22014 and s22015; GRP75, catalogue #4427037, ID s6989 and s6990; SOD2, catalogue #4427038, ID s13267 and s13269; Scrambled, catalogue #1022076. (Thermo Fisher Silencer Pre-designed siRNAs), and a pool of 30 distinct short interfering RNAs (siRNAs) targeting RTN4 in a single reagent # si-G020-57142 (siTOOLs, Biotech). Analysis of proteins and nucleic acids in situ, or on extracted material was performed 144 h after the first transfection.

Fibroblasts were grown on 96-well black microplates (Ibidi GmbH) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 20 min at room temperature (RT). Pulse labelling of mitochondrial and nuclear DNA with bromo-deoxyuridine (BrdU, Sigma) was essentially as previously described [20]. Briefly, cells were incubated with BrdU 50 µM for 8 h before fixation. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), the cells were permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 for 30 min and treated with 2N HCl for 30 min at 37°C. After five washes of 10 min with PBS, cells were blocked for 1 h at RT with 10% goat serum in 0.1% Triton X-100, PBS (PBST). Cells were incubated with primary antibodies in PBST overnight at 4°C. After three washes of 5 min with PBST, cells were incubated with the secondary antibody for 1–2 h at RT, before washing with PBS and mounting with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) nuclear stain (Ibidi GmbH). For image staining other than BrdU, the same protocol was followed omitting the acid treatment. Deoxynucleotide incorporation into newly synthesized DNA was also monitored via Click-chemistry with EdU. Cells were incubated with 50 μM EdU for 8 h and the incorporated EdU detected according to the manufacturer’s Click-it® protocol (Thermo Fisher, C10337). For pulse-chase experiments, fibroblasts were pulse-labelled for 14 or 26 h with 50 µM EdU, after which the cells were washed twice with PBS and incubated with fresh medium containing 3 mM dTTP and subjected to chases of 4–24 h.

Follwing two washes of 5 min each with WB-A, cells were returned to the chamber for 30 min for the ligation step, and after a further two washed, the amplification step with the secondary antibodies lasted 100 min. Finally, cells were washed twice for 10 min with WB-B followed by 0.01× WB-B for 1 min and covered with mounting medium containing 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) nuclear stain.

Fractionation and immunodetection of proteins

Cells were trypsinized and lysed with RIPA buffer [150 mM NaCl, 1.0% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulphate (SDS), 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0] 1× Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (Thermo Scientific). After a 40-min incubation on ice, the samples were centrifuged for 20 min at 15 000 × g at 4°C to remove cellular debris. Protein concentration was determined by DC protein assay kit (Biorad). Protein samples were prepared in 1× Laemmli loading buffer (Biorad) with dithiothreitol and resolved on 4%–15%, 4%–20%, or 12% Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Gels (Biorad) run in Tris/Glycine/SDS running buffer (Biorad). After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred overnight to Low-Fluorescence PVDF Transfer Membranes (Thermo Scientific) at 20 V, at 4°C. Membrane was washed with PBS for 5 min and incubated with No-Stain™ Protein Labelling Reagent (Thermo Fisher) for 15 min and visualized via an iBright FL1500 Imaging System. After washing with PBS for 2 min, the membrane was blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS, 0.1% tween for 1 h at RT. Membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies in BSA 3% in PBS, 0.1% tween, at 4°C and, after washing, incubated with secondary antibody for 1 h at RT. Proteins were detected using standard ECL™ Western Blotting Analysis System (GE Healthcare) or SuperSignal® West Dura (Thermo Scientific), and acquired and quantified using the iBright FL1500 Imaging System.

Quantification of mtDNA

For DNA isolation, cells were lysed in a buffer of 50 mM Tris, 100 mM NaCl, 10 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), 0.5% SDS and 300 μg/ml proteinase K, and incubated at 56°C for 1 h. DNA was then precipitated with 100% ethanol and 150 mM NaOH, washed with 70% ethanol, and resuspended in 10 mM Tris, 1 mM EDTA buffer. The DNA concentration was determined using a NanoDrop 1000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher), with A260/280 ratios ranging from 1.8 to 2.2, and the mtDNA copy number quantified via real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction, in triplicate on 384-Well Reaction Plates (Applied Biosystems) in a final volume of 13 μl per reaction. Each reaction contained 15 ng of DNA template, 1× Power SYBRGreen PCR Master Mix (Applied Biosystems), and 0.5 μM of forward and reverse primers. Mitochondrial and nuclear DNA were amplified using primers specific to regions of human t-RNALeu(UUR) and B2 microglobulin genes, respectively. t-RNALeu(UUR) forward sequence (5′-CACCCAAGAACAGGGTTTGT-3′) and reverse sequence (5′- TGGCCATGGGTATGTTGTTA-3′), GenBank: KY408791.1. B2 microglobulin forward sequence (5′-TGCTGTCTCCATGTTTGATGTATCT-3′) and reverse sequence (5′-TCTCTGCTCCCCACCTCTAAGT-3′), NCBI reference sequence NM_004048.4. Changes in the mtDNA copy number were determined using the 2−ΔΔCt method [21] and represented as fold-change relative to the mean value for vehicle/non-target silencer treated cells analysed in parallel [22].

Mitochondrial translation assay

Cells were incubated in methionine-free medium [DMEM without L-methionine and L-cysteine (Cat. no. 21 013)] supplemented with L-cysteine dihydrochloride (Alfa Aesar), pyruvate (Gibco), GlutaMAX (Gibco), and 0.1% or 10% FBS, at 37°C and 5% CO2 for 30 min to deplete methionine reserves, 20 min with or without a cytosolic protein synthesis inhibitor (CHX, 50 μg/mL), and then for 3 h with 50 μM Click-iT® HPG (L-Homopropargylglycine) (Invitrogen). In some cases, actinonin was added together with cycloheximide to inhibit mitochondrial as well as cytosolic protein synthesis and thereby establish background signal, and in other cases, HPG was omitted from the protocol as a further control. For cell imaging, cells plated on 96-well microplates (Ibidi GmbH) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 20 min at RT, rinsed with 3% BSA in PBS and then permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 in PBS for 20 min. The Click-it reaction between HPG alkyne and Alexa Fluor™ 555 Azide Triethylammonium Salt was applied using the reaction buffer supplied with the Click–iT® kit (Invitrogen). Parallel immunostaining (e.g. of the mitochondria network with anti-TOMM20 antibody) was performed as normal.

Thapsigargin is an inhibitor of the ER calcium ATPase (SERCA) that prevents calcium uptake from the cytosol, allowing an estimation of the ER Ca2+ pool [25].

Results

The ER protein ERLIN2 supports mitochondrial DNA replication via ERMCS

A landmark study in 2016 indicated that ER connections with mitochondria play a role in mtDNA replication and segregation [16]. Lewis et al. assigned a critical role in the process to tubular ER by showing that ER tubule versus ER cisternae abundance was proportional to the rate of mtDNA synthesis [16]. Here, applying another genetic manipulation, we silenced the critical ER tubular factor RTN4, in primary human fibroblasts with siRNAs. One round of transfection had only a modest effect on the steady-state level of Reticulon 4 protein, whereas two rounds produced an 90% decrease compared to controls (Fig. 1A). The two rounds of RTN4 silencing had no adverse effect on cell viability, assessed via SYTOX™ and Hoescht staining (Supplementary Fig. S1A and B), whereas mtDNA synthesis fell 67% (Fig. 1B and C, and Supplementary Fig. S2A), suggesting that the tubular ER supports mtDNA replication in primary cultured human cells, as well as in osteosarcoma and COS cells [16]. Application of a pool of siRNAs targeting RTN4, which lowers the concentration of each siRNA substantially, thereby minimizing the risk of off-target effects [27], also decreased mtDNA synthesis 70% (Supplementary Fig. S2A).

As ERLIN2 is a component of ER cholesterol micro-domains and a canonical marker of ERMCS [19], we next assessed whether ERLIN2 is functionally important for mtDNA synthesis. ERLIN2 silencing decreased the expression of the target protein by 80% (Fig. 1D), and the incorporation of BrdU into cytoplasmic foci (mtDNAs) decreased by 63% (Fig. 1E and F, and Supplementary Fig. S2A). As no such decrease in mtDNA synthesis was evident in cells transfected with a non-target RNA (Supplementary Fig. S2A and C), we infer that the decrease in BrdU incorporation is indicative of a marked inhibition of mtDNA synthesis owing to ERMCS depletion (Fig. 1G). Neither RTN4 nor ERLIN2 silencing altered the mtDNA copy number (Supplementary Fig. S2D and E), which suggested mtDNA stasis; i.e. both mtDNA degradation and synthesis are inhibited in the absence of ERMCS, which was confirmed by pulse-chase experiments (see below). Seeking further evidence that ERMCS are fundamental to mtDNA synthesis in human fibroblasts, we repressed another core component of ER-mitochondrial junctions, GRP75, via RNA interference, and found that this decreased BrdU incorporation in newly synthesized mtDNA similar to the repression of ERLIN2 and RTN4 (Supplementary Fig. S2A–C).

To assess whether ERMCS abundance was decreased by siRTN4 and siERLIN2, as expected, we performed PLA [28] with antibodies to two components of the junctions, inositol 1,4,5-trisphosphate receptor type1 (IP3R1) and glucose-regulated protein 75 (GRP75), localized in the ER and mitochondria, respectively [29, 30]. IP3R1 is widely dispersed in the cell, yet is most concentrated close to the nucleus, and overlaps with the ER protein KDEL, whereas GRP75 co-localizes with the mitochondrial marker TOMM20 (Fig. 2A). In control cells, the PLA yielded many cytoplasmic foci, consistent with IP3R1 and GRP75 being juxtaposed at ERMCS. RTN4 silencing diminished the GRP75/IP3R1 PLA signal by 88% (Fig. 2B), as expected given that RTN4 is a core component of the tubular ER that supports ERMCS formation [31]. ERLIN2 silencing produced a similar marked decrease in the PLA signal (Fig. 2B). Hence, the decreases in mtDNA synthesis resulting from siRTN4 and siERLIN2 are associated with a substantial decrease in ER-mitochondrial junctions.

Although many stresses cause protein misfolding, eliciting the unfolded protein response (UPR), neither RTN4 nor ERLIN2 repression induced the UPR or altered the shape or distribution of the ER cisternae (Supplementary Fig. S3A and B). Likewise, there was no mitochondrial fragmentation or other visible perturbation of the mitochondrial network that are frequent indicators of mitochondrial stress (e.g. Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S3B). Nor did silencing RTN4 affect mitochondrial translation, which is a highly energy demanding process (Supplementary Fig. S3C). Conversely, repressing the mitochondrial protease AFG3L2 altered the mitochondrial morphology, without inhibiting mtDNA synthesis (Supplementary Fig. S4). Hence, the arrest of mtDNA synthesis appears to be a specific effect of ERMCS depletion that does not stem from a generic stress response.

Depletion of ER ion stores inhibits mtDNA replication

Calcium transfer occurs via ERMCS, and calcium is known to stimulate various aspects of mitochondrial function, such as the tricarboxylic acid cycle [30, 32]. Therefore, we investigated the effect of depleting ER calcium on mtDNA replication using Thapsigargin, which irreversibly inhibits the SERCA pumps that transport cations into the ER [25]. Human fibroblasts were treated with and without 200 nM thapsigargin for 8 h, in the presence of BrdU. Thapsigargin decreased the ER calcium load as expected (Supplementary Fig. S5A), and this was accompanied by a 59% decrease in mtDNA synthesis, as measured by BrdU incorporation in cytoplasmic foci within the mitochondrial network, compared to untreated cells (Fig. 3A). Although thapsigargin induces the UPR [33], like the canonical UPR activator tunicamycin (Supplementary Fig. S5B), the latter did not appreciably alter mtDNA synthesis (Supplementary Fig S5C). Hence, ER-stress is not responsible for thapsigargin’s effect on mtDNA synthesis.

Inhibition of MCU impedes mitochondrial DNA synthesis

The MCU plays a major role in mitochondrial calcium homeostasis [34], and so was the prime candidate for regulating entry of calcium, or a related cation, into the mitochondria from the ER. With the hypothesis that MCU is the conduit for the ER factor that stimulates mtDNA replication, we applied the MCU inhibitor, MCU-i4 [35], at a concentration of 10 µM for 16 h and found it decreased nascent mtDNA synthesis 82% in fibroblasts, like ERMCS depletion (Fig. 3B versus Fig. 1). Considering this result, we next assessed the abundance of MCU in RTN4 and GRP75 silenced cells, which displayed decreased ERMCS and inhibited mtDNA synthesis (Figs 1 and 2, and Supplementary Fig. S2A), and recorded decreases of 65% and 75% in the steady-state level of MCU protein, respectively (Fig. 3C). These results suggest that ERMCS and MCU cooperate to support mtDNA synthesis.

Manganese supplementation restores mtDNA synthesis when ERMCS are scarce

Although the strong correlation between ERMCS, MCU, and mtDNA synthesis suggested calcium flux from the ER to the mitochondria might support mtDNA synthesis, calcium persists in mitochondria in the absence of MCU in vivo [36]. Moreover, mitochondrial calcium stores are not depleted by RTN4 or ERLIN2 silencing, evidenced by the amount of calcium released to the cytosol following mitochondrial depolarization (Supplementary Fig. S6A). Therefore, we next considered the other metal cation that is transported into mitochondria via MCU complex, manganese [37, 38]. To determine whether manganese scarcity might be responsible for the decrease in mtDNA synthesis in the absence of ERMCS, we again repressed RTN4 and ERLIN2 and supplemented the cells with and without manganese chloride. Although some cell types have an LD50 as low as 13 µM for manganese chloride [39], others tolerate 200 µM for at least three days (e.g. [40]). We initially tested a dose of 50 µM MnCl2, on primary human fibroblasts, and observed no change in cell number or morphology after 16 h. However, 50 µM manganese increased mtDNA synthesis in RTN4 and ERLIN2 silenced cells 2.5-fold and 2.4-fold, respectively (Fig. 4A and B, and Supplementary Fig. S6B). Subsequently, a lower dose of 10 µM manganese was found to increase mtDNA synthesis 2–7-fold after RTN4 or ERLIN2 silencing (Supplementary Fig. S6B). Manganese supplementation did not interfere with the gene silencing, as it had no effect on RTN4 abundance after silencing (Supplementary Fig. S6C), nor did it have an appreciable effect on mtDNA synthesis in control cells (Fig. 4A and B). In contrast to manganese supplementation, adding 3 mM magnesium, a metal cation involved in diverse aspects of nucleic acid metabolism, had little impact on repressed mtDNA synthesis (Supplementary Fig. S6D).

As well as restoring mtDNA synthesis in ERMCS repressed cells, manganese augmented mtDNA synthesis 3.7-fold in cells treated with the MCU inhibitor MCU-i4 (Fig. 4A and B). Given that manganese can restore mtDNA synthesis when ERMCS are scarce or MCU is inhibited, we infer manganese is the ER factor that ordinarily transfers from the ER to the mitochondria to promote mtDNA replication, and that it crosses the ion-impermeable, IMM via MCU. The MCU inhibitor MCU-i4 acts on the MICU1 subunit and so the high levels of manganese provided as a supplement may enter mitochondria by MCU complexes lacking this component, or other mitochondrial transporters albeit at lower efficiency than the ERMCS–MCU axis (Fig. 4C). Candidate transporters that could compensate for deficient ion transport via this axis include SLC11A2 that localizes to mitochondria in human cells (https://www.proteinatlas.org/search/slc11a2), and can transport manganese, as well as other metal cations [41]. Additionally, the mitochondrial carrier protein MTM1 appears to be critical for manganese supply to the organelle in yeasts [42], and the homologous mitochondrial carrier protein SLC25A39 has been linked to ion, specifically iron, homeostasis, although its principal substrate is glutathione [43].

Manganese-dependent superoxide dismutase regulates mitochondrial DNA replication

Manganese is an essential co-factor of superoxide dismutase 2, a protein that has been found associated with mitochondrial nucleoids [44]. SOD2 has also been linked to the activities of mtDNA polymerase (POLG), although hitherto this was assumed to reflect a protective role against mtDNA damage [45]. In yeasts, activation of SOD2 is dependent on manganese entry into mitochondria via MTM1, and the loss of MTM1 causes mtDNA abnormalities [42]. To determine whether SOD2 affects mtDNA synthesis in human cells, we measured BrdU incorporation in the mtDNA after silencing SOD2 and found that the mtDNA synthesis decreased 85% (Fig. 5 and Supplementary Figa S2A and S6B versus Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S2A). When SOD2 was scarce, manganese supplementation restored mtDNA synthesis, reaching 120%–160% of the level of untreated controls: a 5–15-fold increase with respect to SOD2 silenced cells without manganese (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Fig. S6B). SOD2 silencing produced similar effects in glutamatergic human neurons derived from iPSCs: lower mtDNA synthesis after siSOD2 compared to the non-target silencer, and increased mtDNA synthesis when siSOD2 neurons were supplemented with 50 µM manganese (Supplementary Fig. S7). These findings suggest that SOD2 regulates the supply of manganese for mtDNA replication, as when SOD2 is scarce, elevated manganese stimulates mtDNA synthesis in multiple cell types.

RTN4 and SOD2 silencing slow mtDNA degradation

As there was no mtDNA depletion as a result of siRTN4 and siERLIN2 silencing and only a modest decrease in mtDNA copy number in the case of SOD2 silencing (Supplementary Fig. S2E), we assessed mtDNA turnover, via pulse-chase labelling experiments. EdU incorporation in mtDNA was evaluated after a 26-h pulse, and again 4 and 24 h after its removal. After chase periods of 4 and 24 h, the mtDNA-EdU signal fell 37% and 80%, respectively, in the non-target transfected fibroblasts; in contrast, there was no decrease in the EdU signal in the siRTN4 and siSOD2 transfected cells at either time point (Fig. 5D). Hence, we conclude that RTN4 and SOD2 silencing result in downregulation of mtDNA synthesis and degradation. Although downregulation of mtDNA degradation will not prevent mtDNA depletion via cell division, the already relatively slow proliferation rate of the fibroblasts (compared to immortalized cell lines, such as HeLa and HEKs) was further reduced by the transfection regime (Supplementary Fig. S8). And in the SOD2 silenced neurons that also displayed impaired mtDNA replication, there was no possibility of mtDNA depletion via cell division, as these were terminally differentiated cells (Supplementary Fig. S7).

Manganese favours short over long transcripts in vitro

A priori manganese is not a good candidate to stimulate DNA replication directly, as it reduces the fidelity of DNA polymerases [46], and so we hypothesized that manganese might affect DNA replication indirectly via transcription. Mechanistically this could be straightforward for mtDNA, as the mitochondrial RNA polymerase POLRMT plays major roles in transcription and replication [47–50]. POLRMT generates both the polycistronic transcripts that give rise to the messenger, transfer, and ribosomal RNA products of mammalian mtDNA and primers for DNA replication [50–52].

To investigate the effect of manganese ions on mitochondrial transcription in vitro, we first performed primer extension experiments using an RNA–DNA scaffold, which allowed POLRMT to form a functional elongation complex (EC). We found that manganese had no impact on the ability of POLRMT to extend an RNA primer (Supplementary Fig. S9), suggesting that its presence has no significant effect on POLRMT catalytic activity, within the timeframe of the experiment. Next, we used increasing concentrations of manganese to determine its effect on transcription using templates containing the light-strand promoter, LSP (Fig. 6A). In the presence of a limited set of NTPs (no CTP), most of the transcription complexes were halted after synthesizing 89–98-nucleotide-long RNAs, and the addition of manganese strongly increased the abundance of these truncated RNAs (Fig. 6B). The maximum stimulation (~4-fold) was observed at 0.5 mM manganese chloride (Fig. 6B, lanes 5 and 6), whereas further increases in manganese concentration led to a significant reduction in RNA synthesis (Fig. 6B, lanes 7–9). To determine whether the increase in RNA synthesis was due to instability of the halted transcription complexes, resulting in higher turnover rates, or a general stimulation of transcription, we performed a run-off assay by adding CTP to facilitate extension of the elongation complexes stalled at deoxyguanosine residues in the template. In the absence of manganese, we observed accumulation of the 500-nucleotide run-off product, indicating that most of the stalled ECs resumed transcription (Fig. 6C, lane 1). However, with increasing concentrations of manganese, there was a marked decrease in run-off products and accumulation of the 89–98 nt RNAs, with the most marked effect at 0.25–0.5 mM MnCl2. These results suggest that manganese destabilizes ECs, leading to their dissociation and impaired resumption of transcription, which, if it occurs in mitochondria, would shift the balance from processive transcription to the synthesis of shorter RNA products. Since the mitochondrial replisome uses short RNA primers for replication, these findings suggest a putative mechanism for mtDNA stimulation when manganese concentration increases in mitochondria.

Synthesis of replication primers at the origin of replication, OriL, involves the formation of a short hairpin and transcription by POLRMT (Fig. 6D) [47]. When provided with a limited NTP set lacking UTP, POLRMT synthesizes ~28 nucleotide RNAs equivalent to those used by mtDNA polymerase γ, as primers for DNA replication (Fig. 6E). Manganese markedly stimulates the generation of such species, with maximum stimulation observed at 0.25 mM manganese (Fig. 6E). These data are consistent with the effect of manganese on halted EC stability and suggest a general mechanism by which the presence of manganese can stimulate mtDNA synthesis, as discussed further below.

Discussion

The current study suggests a model of how the ER supports the replication of mtDNA and identifies key regulatory and structural elements (Fig. 7A). In addition to RTN4 [16], the ER lipid raft protein ERLIN2, and the mitochondrial ERMCS bridging protein GRP75 contribute to mtDNA replication (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S2), as ERMCS abundance is directly proportional to mtDNA replication (Figs 1 and 2, and Supplementary Fig. S10). We infer that on the mitochondrial side of the junctions, MCU propagates the ER signal, as its inhibition reduces mtDNA replication similar to depleting ERMCS (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Fig. S3 versus Figs 3B and 5C), and calcium transfer from the ER to mitochondria occurs principally via MCU in normal circumstances. Other recent studies also link MCU and mtDNA: in one, an RNAi screen identified MCU as a factor involved in nucleoid morphology [53], while analysis of the MCU channel interactome identified several factors related to mtDNA replication [54], which suggests the channel physically interacts with the mitochondrial nucleoid directly or via other proteins. All the evidence points to manganese as the specific ER signalling factor that stimulates mtDNA replication. Manganese is an established substrate of MCU complex [37, 38], a co-factor of SOD2 (aka manganese-dependent superoxide dismutase) [55], and most compellingly manganese supplementation rescues mtDNA synthesis when ERMCS or SOD2 are scarce, or MCU is inhibited (Figs 4 and 5). Manganese has also been shown to prevent mtDNA abnormalities in yeast [42], which together with the current study suggests manganese has a conserved role in mtDNA metabolism. Notwithstanding all of the above, ERMCS and MCU dysfunction might indirectly affect mitochondrial import of nucleotides or other critical factors for mtDNA replication; even then the rescue effect (Figs 4 and 5) would suggest manganese is a key regulator of their entry into mitochondria that is controlled by the concerted action of ER and MCU.

ERMCS regulate mtDNA replication via manganese. (A) Reticulon 4 (RTN4) is a component of tubular ER (TER), which is required to form connections with the mitochondria (ERMCS), and RTN4 abundance is proportional to mtDNA synthesis (Fig. 1 and [16]). ERLIN2 is a fundamental component of ER lipid rafts, on which ERMCS depend (Fig. 2), and so it too supports mtDNA synthesis (Fig. 1). Equally, because GRP75 is a bridging component of ERMCS [28], its repression inhibits mtDNA replication (Supplementary Fig. S2). The abundance of the MCU correlates with that of RTN4 and GRP75 (Fig. 3C), and MCU inhibition represses mtDNA synthesis (Fig. 3B). Hence, mtDNA synthesis depends on ERMCS and MCU. Manganese is inferred to be the ER signalling factor that stimulates mtDNA replication, as it restores mtDNA synthesis after silencing of ERMCS factors and is a recognized substrate of MCU (Fig. 4). SOD2 silencing also inhibits mtDNA replication and is rescued by manganese (Fig. 5), suggesting it relays manganese entering via MCU to the mitochondrial nucleoid, where one proposed role of manganese is: (B) to shift the equilibrium slightly from long polycistronic transcripts to shorter RNAs that can serve as primers for DNA replication, based on manganese’s effects on the properties of the mitochondrial RNA polymerase, POLRMT (Fig. 6). IMM and OMM: inner and outer mitochondrial membranes, respectively; VDAC: voltage dependent anion channel, aka porin, POLG: mtDNA polymerase γ, LSP: light strand promoter, TER: transcription termination site, OriR: origin of replication.

Given that manganese inhibits transcription re-start and stimulates RNA synthesis at Ori-L (Fig. 6), manganese might play a regulatory role by tilting the balance from polycistronic transcription to replication by favouring the synthesis of short RNAs that can serve as primers for DNA replication (Fig. 7B). Although the conditions that strongly favour short transcripts in the in vitro assays used concentrations of manganese higher than found in cells (Fig. 6), these conditions represent an extreme version of the proposed model. Transcription greatly exceeds that of replication, and so a modest decrease in polycistronic transcripts could boost replication several fold. Moreover, channelling manganese to the mtDNA via MCU could achieve a considerably higher local manganese concentration than the average for the cell or the mitochondrial matrix. Alternatively, or additionally, manganese is likely to affect other aspects of mtDNA and RNA metabolism. Previously it has been implicated in the disruption of the mitochondrial RNA processing machinery (aka mitochondrial RNA granules) [55], although this too could be attributable to the mitochondrial RNA polymerase to which it is closely tied, and the effects of manganese on mtRNA metabolism could affect mtDNA replication via the transcript-dependent replication mechanism [57].

Considering ERMCS can profoundly impact mtDNA metabolism, this process merits investigation in all the contexts where aberrant ER-mitochondrial connections have been linked to disease, such as neurodegenerative disorders [58]. More specifically, mutations in ERLIN2 are established causes of motor neuron disease [59], and so dissection of mtDNA metabolism could provide important insights into the pathogenesis of these disorders. Another important corollary is that one or more of the widely reported consequences of calcium perturbation in neurological and neurodegenerative disorders could stem from changes in mitochondrial manganese availability and the consequent adverse effects on mtDNA replication. Equally, wherever MCU and SOD2 abundance or activity are altered, the impact on mtDNA replication needs to be considered in light of the new findings. MCU forms a multi-protein complex comprising ERME, MICU1, and MICU2, as well as MCU itself; hence, these partners may well play important roles in modulating the activity of the transporter, as it relates to manganese entry into mitochondria and thus mtDNA replication.
