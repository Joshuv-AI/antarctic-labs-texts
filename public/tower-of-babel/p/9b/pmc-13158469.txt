A risk-adapted approach to reTURBT in high-grade T1 NMIBC
Authors: Kamil Malshy, Edward M Messing
Published: 2026

Despite long-standing guideline recommendations, 1 the optimal use of repeat TURBT (reTURBT) in high-grade T1 non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) remains an area of real clinical uncertainty. The rationale for reTURBT is clear: to reduce understaging and eliminate residual disease; however, applying it universally to a biologically heterogeneous population comes at a cost. It is an invasive procedure with non-trivial morbidity, 2 contributes to healthcare burden, and perhaps most importantly, may delay the initiation of intravesical therapy. In a landscape increasingly focused on precision and de-intensification, there is a growing need to move away from reflexive second resections toward a more selective, patient-centered approach.

The prospective multicenter HuNIRe trial by Contieri and colleagues, recently published in European Urology Oncology, 3 offers a thoughtful attempt to address this gap. The study enrolled a selected group of patients with T1 NMIBC who had undergone a complete initial TURBT with detrusor muscle present, essentially isolating a population in whom the quality of the index resection was not in question. Patients were reassessed early, with urine cytology at 3 to 4 weeks and cystoscopy, including narrow-band imaging, at 4 to 6 weeks. Rather than performing reTURBT routinely, the investigators reserved it for those with positive cytology or suspicious cystoscopic findings, while the remainder proceeded directly to Bacillus Calmette–Guérin (BCG) induction.

Out of 107 eligible patients, 90 were ultimately enrolled, with a median age of 75 years and 76% presenting with primary tumors. Using this response-guided approach, reTURBT was avoided in 71% of patients (64/90), while 29% required repeat resection based on early findings, including 11 (12%) with positive urine cytology and 15 (17%) with suspicious cystoscopy findings. Among those undergoing reTURBT, residual disease was heterogeneous, including carcinoma in-situ (31%), high-grade Ta (23%), and persistent T1 disease (15%), while 27% had no residual tumor. Importantly, no patient was upstaged to muscle-invasive disease. At a median follow-up of 26 months, 2-year recurrence-free survival was 69% (95% CI 56 to 80%) and progression-free survival was 91% (95% CI 80 to 96%). There were no significant differences in outcomes between patients who underwent reTURBT and those who did not (recurrence-free survival of 74% vs 69%, p≈0.7; progression-free survival of 91% vs 93%, p≈0.6). Finally, outcomes were comparable to a historical cohort managed with routine reTURBT, suggesting that this selective strategy does not compromise short- to intermediate-term oncologic control.

What makes these findings particularly relevant is how they intersect with a practical issue we all deal with but rarely formalize: the timing of BCG. reTURBT is intended to optimize outcomes, but in reality, it often delays the start of intravesical therapy. Guidelines encourage early BCG initiation, ideally within a few weeks of TURBT (3–4 weeks per NCCN, not specified in AUA), yet once a second resection is added, that timeline becomes difficult to maintain. The question is whether that delay actually matters, and the data suggest that it does. In a cohort of over 400 patients, Cai and colleagues showed that the interval between TURBT and BCG was independently associated with recurrence, with delays beyond about 26 days linked to worse outcomes (HR ∼1.8, p = 0.017). 4 They also identified 26.5 days as a practical cutoff, and patients treated within this window had better recurrence-free survival, a finding that held up after propensity matching (p = 0.009). Overall, about one in five patients recurred and 10% progressed, placing the impact of timing into context. Taken together, timing clearly matters. This raises a practical concern: if reTURBT consistently shifts patients beyond this therapeutic window, its net effect on outcomes may be more complex than traditionally assumed.

Even beyond timing, there is the question of how much reTURBT actually fixes the problem it is meant to address. It certainly improves staging, but it remains, at its core, a sampling procedure. Data from Justin Mehr and colleagues illustrate this limitation well. 5 Despite reTURBT, up to 35% of patients are still upstaged at cystectomy, and a “negative” reTURBT carries only about a 66 to 69% negative predictive value for excluding muscle-invasive disease. In other words, even after two resections, a substantial proportion of patients remain misclassified. At the same time, contemporary data suggest that the clinical impact of reTURBT may be more limited than previously assumed. Contemporary data show that among patients undergoing reTURBT, residual disease is identified in ∼44% and ∼10% are upstaged, yet this does not consistently translate into improved recurrence or progression outcomes. 6 In other words, reTURBT finds more disease, but whether that improves outcomes remains unclear.

More broadly, this reflects a deeper issue. We rely on imperfect tools, including cystoscopy, cytology, initial TURBT characteristics, and repeat resection, to answer what is fundamentally a biological question. The next step is likely to come from better risk stratification rather than more procedures.

For residual disease, urinary tumor DNA represents a promising approach. Recent data suggest that utDNA can detect minimal residual disease with an overall accuracy of around 90%, with sensitivity of approximately 80% and specificity exceeding 95%, and may predict recurrence months before it becomes visible cystoscopically. 7 Trials such as TRUCE-LB01 are now evaluating whether reTURBT can be guided by molecular evidence rather than applied universally. 8

A separate but equally important challenge is identifying occult muscle-invasive disease. Multiparametric bladder MRI using VI-RADS provides direct assessment of local invasion, with reported sensitivity of approximately 83% and specificity of 90% for detecting muscle-invasive disease. 9 However, its performance is limited by interobserver variability, qualitative interpretation, and reduced accuracy in distinguishing lamina propria from muscularis propria invasion, particularly in the post-TURBT setting where resection-induced changes may confound staging. Although pre-TURBT MRI may offer more accurate assessment, this approach is not routinely feasible due to cost and would subject many patients with ultimately Ta or T1 disease to unnecessary imaging and potential delays.

More advanced deep learning approaches have demonstrated promising accuracy in differentiating non–muscle-invasive from muscle-invasive disease, 10 although these remain investigational and require external validation before clinical adoption.

While none of these approaches fully overcome the limitations of current staging, and reTURBT remains the standard, the emerging data call into question its routine use in all patients. Alongside prospective data from studies such as HuNIRe, and the growing recognition that BCG timing matters, this supports a more selective, risk-adapted approach after initial TURBT. In practice, a more tailored strategy may better match staging intensity to the underlying biology while avoiding unnecessary procedures and delays.
