Vascularized Brain Organoids: Advances in Engineering Human Neurovascular Models
Authors: Hee Young Kim, Jung Eun Lee, Seung Ho Yang, Jai Ho Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain organoids have emerged as an important platform for studying human neurodevelopment and neurological disorders, yet their physiological relevance remains limited by the absence of a functional vascular network. Recent efforts therefore focus on developing vascularized brain organoids (vBOs) to better recapitulate neurovascular interactions. Here, we investigate current strategies for vBO engineering, discussing its potential implications such as disease modeling, drug screening, and regenerative therapies along with persistent challenges in reproducing physiologically mature neurovascular features. This review outlines a rapidly evolving framework that enhances neurovascular modeling and supports emerging translational research in neurological disorders.Keywords: Organoids, Pluripotent stem cells, Neurovascular coupling, Blood-brain barrier, Cerebrovascular disorders

Introduction

Brain organoids (BOs) are self-assembled three-dimensional (3D) aggregates featuring cell types and cytoarchitectural properties of the brain. First developed by Lancaster et al. (1), human BOs have emerged as a groundbreaking platform for exploring previously untapped aspects of the brain. Together with the spinal cord, the human brain constitutes the central nervous system (CNS), which plays a crucial role in body regulation and cognitive functions. Research on the human brain has faced hurdles due to its structural complexity, highlighting the need for practical in vitro models.

Human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs), including human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) (2) and human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) (3, 4), have become widely used for their ability to differentiate into any cell type in the body. There have been successful efforts to derive various human brain cells such as neurons, astrocytes (ACs), and oligodendrocytes (OCs) from hPSCs (5-7), and moreover, aggregate them to BOs.

Protocols have been developed to replicate cell composition (8-11) and to identify neural network formation (12) in BOs. Specific regional organoids with functional circuits, such as the forebrain, cerebellum, brainstem, and thalamus, have been created (13-16). Assembloids have been generated as a self-organized combination of different organoids or cell types to explore interregional and intercellular interactions (17-20). BOs are a versatile tool for mimicking the dynamic progress of human fetal brain development and incorporating patient-specific characteristics. They provide a foundation for studying neurodevelopmental processes (21) and disease pathology (22, 23).

The physiological relevance of BOs faces persistent limitations, most notably the lack of a neurovascular system. The brain vasculature, essential for nutrient, oxygen, and drug delivery, supports neural cell survival, maturation, and maintenance of in vivo brain physiology. Consequently, the absence of vasculature leads to hypoxia and core necrosis in BOs and restricts versatile human brain model establishment (24, 25).

The importance of vascularized in vitro brain models is increasing, emphasizing the need for a more sophisticated biomimetic platform to study neurological and cerebrovascular diseases and to conduct clinical practices such as drug screening. In this review, we bind insights from blood-brain barrier (BBB) and neurovascular unit (NVU) models with recent protocols for generating vascularized brain organoids (vBOs), addressing developmental biology reconstituted in engineered platforms. This perspective highlights translational applications of vBOs including cerebrovascular disease, neurodegeneration, and regenerative medicine along with the persisting challenges and prospects of this field.

Brain Organoid Development

Efforts have been made to replicate neuroepithelium development in vitro. Inhibition of the dual suppressor of mothers against decapentaplegic (SMAD) pathway resulted in the rapid and uniform differentiation of hPSCs into neural progenitor cells (NPCs) (5). Subsequently, Lancaster et al. (1) established the first human BO with hPSC-derived embryoid bodies (EBs) which were embedded in Matrigel to promote the formation of 3D brain structures. This technique primarily relies on intrinsic development and requires minimal patterning, resulting in a large, complex 3D aggregation of brain components that replicates discrete regions from the retina to the hindbrain. To improve reproducibility, protocols were structured to sequentially introduce signaling molecules at specific stages of culture (10), further guiding the development of region-specific BOs.

Various protocols developed to derive BOs with precise in vivo characteristics and functionality. Cerebral organoids exhibit synaptogenesis-derived electrophysiological activity, further enhanced by the presence of ACs that support neuronal maturation (7, 10). Myelinated oligocortical spheroids were developed by inducing OC differentiation using endogenous growth factors (8). Assembloids, such as human cortical-subpallidum (13), medial ganglionic eminence-cerebral (20), and cortico-motor spheroids (17, 26), serve as a platform to study internal brain communications.

Despite promising perspectives, human BOs lack high-fidelity cell types and maturation, particularly in non-neural cell types, which are essential for brain homeostasis. To address this issue, researchers developed BOs including microglia using PU.1 gene expression (9) and progenitor co-culture methods (11). Cells resembling outer radial glia, which contribute to the formation of upper layer neurons, were also developed in cerebral organoids (27). Long-term culture of BOs showed signs of fetal and postnatal development in gene expression and RNA editing, although their maturation remains largely insufficient, primarily due to the short duration of culture periods (28). Limitations continue in structural and functional developments. The formation of cortical layers, comprising the ventricular zone, outer subventricular zone, and cortical plate, was observed in sliced neocortical organoid culture methods (21). While these structures partially mimic early brain development, they do not fully replicate the complex biological layering and regional interactions. Moreover, inadequate vascularization also leads to complications such as hypoxia and necrosis in the inner regions of the organoids, thereby compromising their survival and quality control.

Development of Brain Vasculature and the Neurovascular Unit

CNS vasculature performs multiple roles beyond oxygen and nutrient delivery, influencing the neuronal microenvironment. Oxygen and nutrient supply regulate the balance between neural stem cell (NSC) proliferation and differentiation, with hypoxic conditions favoring NSC proliferation and oxygenation promoting differentiation (29). Blood vessels also provide physical scaffolds for neuronal migration and axon guidance. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and Wnt signaling directs the migration of NPCs, OC precursors, and neurons (30).

The development of the CNS and its vasculature proceed in parallel, involving complex communication. The neural plate folds into the neural tube around embryonic days 7.5∼8.5 (E7.5∼E8.5) in mice, and analogously 3∼4 gestational weeks (GWs) in humans (31). Mesoderm-derived angioblasts migrate to the surface of the neural tube and assemble into the perineural vascular plexus (PNVP), the initial vascular network that envelopes the neural tube. Around E9.0∼E10.0 (≈GW 5∼6), the neural tube undergoes regionalization and dorsoventral patterning, initiating proliferation, differentiation, and migration of NPCs (29). Secondary angiogenesis occurs as vessel sprouts from the PNVP penetrate the neural tube to reach the ventricular area, driven by angiogenic growth factors, especially VEGF produced by NPCs and differentiated neurons (32, 33). As initial neural networks form, NPC-derived Wnt ligands activate Wnt/β-catenin signaling during E10.0∼E12.0 (≈GW 6∼8). This molecular program induces brain-specific vascular properties, including the upregulation of tight junction proteins and transporters such as glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1), thereby laying the foundation of the BBB before ACs mature and contribute to barrier refinement later in gestation (30, 34). Conversely, during early human cortical development, endothelial and meningeal cues including VEGF, Wnt, and CXC motif chemokine ligand 12 support NPC proliferation, migration, and differentiation (33, 35, 36) (Fig. 1).

The NVU plays a crucial role in maintaining BBB integrity and supporting neural function, comprising neurons, endothelial cells (ECs), pericytes (PCs), ACs, and their extracellular matrix (ECM) (37). Interactions within the NVU facilitate proper communication and signaling between neural and vascular components. Consequently, the vasculature continuously remodels and matures in tandem with CNS tissue development until the BBB reaches completion, with Notch signaling sustaining the development and integrity of vascular smooth muscle (38).

Establishment of In Vitro Brain Vasculature Models

Brain vasculature models simulate the vascular dynamics of the brain, providing a new basis to explore the complexities of neurovascular coupling. Insights from the differentiation of hPSCs into blood vessel cells underscore the potential to develop brain-specific vascular structures critical for organoid functionality. These models, including the BBB and NVU, offer a controlled environment to study the complex interactions between neural and vascular components, which are vital for maintaining brain health and integrity. Combination of cellular and molecular techniques drives the ongoing refinement of these models to enhance their physiological relevance.

Cellular and molecular architecture of the blood-brain barrier and neurovascular unit

The BBB primarily consists of ECs, PCs, and ACs, which collaboratively regulate delivery of substances between the blood and the brain. ECs create tight junctions through proteins like claudin-5 and occludin to ensure selective permeability (39). PCs, situated in the basement membrane, manage blood flow and support the BBB’s integrity, whereas ACs contribute to biochemical support by encasing blood vessels with their end-feet and secreting substances such as glial cell line-derived neurotrophic factor (GDNF) (40). Moreover, transport proteins like GLUT1 and efflux pumps such as P-glycoprotein (P-gp), alongside enzymatic barriers including monoamine oxidase, enhance the selective transport and metabolic barrier functions of the BBB (41). The NVU, incorporating BBB components and neurons, functions synergistically to uphold the CNS’s integrity. PCs, strategically located next to ECs, maintain BBB stability, regulating blood flow and responding to injuries (42).

Human pluripotent stem cell differentiation to endothelial phenotypes

Previous studies have concentrated on differentiating hPSCs into brain microvascular endothelial cells (BMECs). Stepwise differentiation processes (Table 1) have been developed, targeting signaling pathways for mesodermal induction, including the canonical Wnt signaling pathway (43, 44). Lee et al. (45) demonstrated the direct reprogramming of human dermal fibroblasts into ECs which exhibited typical endothelial characteristics. Spontaneous barrier properties vary depending on culture conditions and cellular composition. BMEC monoculture systems generally exhibit transepithelial/transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER), representing barrier integrity, of approximately 2,500∼4,700 Ω·cm2. Co-culture with ACs and PCs or use of B27 supplementation may substantially enhance such value (∼2,000∼8,000 Ω·cm2) (46, 47) (Table 2). However, the long-term stability of these methods has not yet been explored.

In vitro blood-brain barrier models

Transwell models (Fig. 2a) of the BBB generally use a simple chamber setup in which brain ECs are cultured on a semi-permeable membrane, facilitating the study of molecular transport across the BBB. Initially limited to EC monoculture, the model has evolved to include co-cultures with ACs and PCs with enhanced physiological relevance by mimicking cellular interactions and migration (48, 49). Studies have provided insights into improving barrier properties by optimizing cell culture conditions, medium composition, and cellular microenvironment. Modifications such as the addition of glucocorticoids have augmented tight junction protein expression, resulting in increased barrier tightness, increasing TEER to 600∼1,000 Ω·cm2 and reduced permeability to 3×10−7cm/s (50, 51).

(a) Transwell models. Endothelial cells (ECs) are grown on a porous membrane to study transport across the barrier. Co-culture models add astrocytes (ACs) or pericytes (PCs) on the opposite side, improving barrier properties. Glucocorticoids such as dexamethasone or hydrocortisone further tighten the barrier by increasing junction protein expression and transepithelial/transendothelial electrical resistance. (b) Dynamic microfluidic models. Two-dimensional (2D) models use microchannels with flowing medium to apply shear stress to ECs, which strengthens barrier function. Three-dimensional (3D) models embed ECs with ACs, PCs, and neural cells in extracellular matrix gels, creating vascular-like networks that better mimic the 3D structure and function of the blood−brain barrier. Dynamic microfluidic models (Fig. 2b) facilitate precise control and simulation of the cellular microenvironment, EC migration, and angiogenesis, effectively replicating the shear stress on ECs and the intricate architecture of the BBB (52). Dynamic models introduce fluid flow to simulate physiological blood circulation, activating mechanotransduction pathways in brain ECs. These models, combined with co-culture systems, demonstrate reduced permeability and increased TEER approaching ∼1,000 Ω·cm2 which significantly exceeds those observed in conventional transwell models (53, 54). 3D dynamic models further incorporate complex cell configurations and ECM components to approach concise BBB structure, exhibiting enhanced selective permeability and barrier integrity (55). These models employ technologies such as 3D biofabrication (56) and self-assembly (57) to construct structures that emulate the complex vascular networks of the brain. This provides insights into the dynamics of neurovascular coupling, indicating the potential of a more contiguous model to in vivo conditions (Table 2).

Three-dimensional neurovascular unit models

Functional 3D models of the NVU were successfully generated from co-cultures of BMECs, ACs, and PCs (58) to transwell models, enabling observation of neuronal influences on the barrier function. Stone et al. (59) cultured human BMECs in NVU transwell setups to study responses to ischemic conditions, demonstrating how neuronal presence can alter endothelial barrier properties under stress stimuli. Microfluidic systems were also applied to create dynamic, controlled environments that simulate the mechanical and chemical cues found in vivo. Combination of multiple cells such as BMECs, ACs, PCs, and neurons in microfluidic NVU chips has demonstrated enhanced barrier properties with TEER values reaching approximately 1,000∼1,500 Ω·cm2, and functional interactions between cell types (60, 61) (Table 2). Furthermore, advances in microfluidic technology have enabled gradient generation and real-time monitoring of cellular responses, utilizing multiple channels for culture, which facilitates detailed studies of cellular interactions and BBB permeability under various conditions (62). 3D engineering techniques have also been applied in NVU models to form perfusable vascular networks (63, 64).

Vascularized Brain Organoids

Directed differentiation for vascular induction in brain organoids

Sequential protocols were developed to guide hPSCs through specific developmental stages, leading to the formation of complex, functional vascular networks within cerebral organoids (Table 3). Initiated by hPSCs differentiation into mesodermal progenitors, self-assembled human vascular organoids were established in 3D matrices with exposure to VEGF, fibroblast growth factor 2, and a low-oxygen environment (5% O2). These vessel organoids display interconnected networks of CD31＋ endothelial tubes with proper PC localization, as indicated by platelet derived growth factor receptor beta (PDGFRβ) and calponin 1 markers, also enveloped by a basement membrane (65). Ham et al. (66) demonstrated that long-term treatment with VEGF and WNT7A results in the enhanced formation of vascular structures surrounded by PC-like cells expressing alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA). RNA sequencing and bioinformatics analysis revealed that VEGF treatment upregulates genes associated with vasculogenesis, tube formation, and BBB characteristics, as well as genes involved in brain development and neurogenesis.

Several studies have utilized a combination of directed differentiation and vBO culture techniques. Cakir et al. (67) induced the expression of ETV2 in hESCs, forming EBs followed by culture in cerebral organoid medium, which resulted in functional vascular networks with BBB characteristics, supporting neuronal differentiation and maturation. Dailamy et al. (68) overexpressed NEUROD1 for neural differentiation, and MYOD1 and BAF60C for muscle differentiation in hESCs to introduce parenchymal cell types into blood vessel organoids. Mesodermal induction with bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4) and Wnt-activator molecule CHIR, followed by vascular induction with VEGF and forskolin, was supplemented with brain-derived neurotrophic factor and neurotrophin-3 to support neural maturation. In this study, induced vBOs exhibited endothelial function and in vivo perfusion upon transplantation into immunodeficient mice. Treatment with CHIR and BMP4 was also applied to generate choroid plexus organoids demonstrating selective permeability and cerebrospinal fluid production (69).

Multicellular co-culture approaches to reconstruct neurovascular interactions

Co-culture of human BMECs, PCs, glial cells, and neurons from primary sources and hPSCs led to structured organization into organoids. This model not only expressed BBB proteins such as zonula occludens-1, P-gp, and GLUT1, but also demonstrated selective permeability, neurotoxicity protection, and high cell viability maintenance (∼21 days) (70). Mixed cultures of hiPSC-derived mesodermal progenitors, NPCs, and other parenchymal cells formed well-organized vascular structures and functional parenchymal tissues (71). Osaki et al. (72) co-cultured human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) with motor neuron spheroids in a collagen gel, resulting in enhanced neurite elongation, synaptogenesis, improved overall neuronal activity, and vascular formation promoted by neuronal networks.

Microfluidic systems for functional perfusable vascularization

Applying flow systems enables precise control over the cellular microenvironment and facilitates the formation of complex, perfusable vascular networks within 3D tissue models. Co-culturing neuronal spheroids and ECs in microfluidic devices led to the formation of interpenetrating vascular and neuronal networks, with blood vessels displaying an average lumen diameter of approximately 60 μm (72). Confocal imaging revealed that neurites from neuronal organoids aligned with the vascular networks and extended towards them, which is crucial for establishing a patterned architecture in the co-culture of cells such as ACs and PCs (73). Microfluidic systems allowed perfusability evaluation by flowing 1 μm fluorescent beads through the networks (72, 73). These platforms, coupled with electronic sensors, enable real-time, non-invasive monitoring of changes in oxygen metabolism (74).

Figarol et al. (75) merged human BMECs, ACs, and PCs with ECM mixture gel in pressure-driven microphysiological systems. Flow conditions enhanced the definition and organization of capillary-like networks resembling natural micro-vessels, primarily growing horizontally perpendicular to the flow direction. Enhanced expression levels of tight junction proteins, transporters, carriers, and genes such as CLDN5, TJP1, SLC2A1, TFRC, ABCG2, and ABCB1 were observed, as compared to those in static culture.

3D printing techniques applied to microfluidics enabled sacrificial writing into functional tissue with hiPSC-derived organ building block matrices, which, after removal, leave behind perfusable functional vascular channels. Improved cellular density, microarchitecture, and overall function in these 3D structures marks the importance of vascular networks for maintaining tissue viability and function at therapeutic scales (76).

Assembloids

Recent studies have developed methods to assemble cerebral organoids with vascular spheroids. Kook et al. (19) created vascular spheroids by co-culturing HUVECs, human umbilical cord blood-derived mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), and human dermal fibroblasts, which were subsequently fused with hiPSC-derived cerebral organoids. These assembloids demonstrated stable vascular structure maintenance up to 60 days, accompanied by a significant reduction in apoptotic cell death. Additionally, up-regulation of the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway was observed, suggesting enhanced neuronal proliferation and differentiation. Song et al. (77) demonstrated triculture assembly methods with sequential fusion of human NPCs, ECs, and MSCs. MSCs effectively migrated towards the spheroid center in response to CXC motif chemokine receptor 4), enhancing the structural integrity and functionality of the assembloid. Kong et al. (78) developed fused cortical-blood vessel organoids (fCBOs) by co-culturing cortical organoids and blood vessel organoids separately derived from hiPSCs, producing vascularized cortical tissue containing ECs, PCs, and increased glial population. These fCBOs exhibited activated inflammatory signaling, microglia/macrophage expression, and oxidative stress pathways upon SARS-CoV-2 infection. Infected fCBOs also captured Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathologies including β-amyloid plaques and tau hyperphosphorylation, modeling neuroinflammation-associated neurodegenerative mechanisms.

In vivo transplantation and organoid invasion of vasculature

Intracerebral transplantation protocols involve implanting in vitro-generated BOs into the brain of immunodeficient mice, where host tissues fuse with the graft organoid. Mansour et al. (79) transplanted selected cerebral organoids to the retrosplenial cortex of NOD-SCID immunodeficient mice. The organoids exhibited robust incorporation and survival in the mouse brain, displaying well-defined regional and morphological identities along with enhanced differentiation and maturation of neurons. Axonal outgrowth from the organoids into the host brain was noted, while host microglia inversely infiltrated the grafts, indicating integration into the host immune system. Pham et al. (80) also conducted transplantation to immunodeficient mice, where the organoid revealed robust vessel penetration and tubular structure formation, merging with the mouse brain as marked by human-specific markers STEM121 and CD31. Compared to non-vascularized organoids, both structural integrity and survival were significantly enhanced.

Comparative analysis of vascularized brain organoid generation strategies

As discussed above, multiple methods have been developed to generate vBOs (Fig. 3, Table 4), spanning in vitro differentiation to in vivo models. Directed differentiation induces endothelial and neural lineages simultaneously, yielding self-assembled vascular structures within organoids (67). Multicellular co-cultures combine neurons, ECs, ACs, and PCs in 3D matrices or chips, generally producing simpler capillary clusters with only modest barrier maturation (80). Microfluidic perfusion systems use controlled flow to create highly organized capillary networks aligned by shear stress, achieving very high TEER (approximately ∼1,000 to 4,000 Ω·cm2) that reflect strong barrier function (55, 81). Fusion-based assembloids merge endothelial components into neural tissue while extending stable viability duration up to ∼60 days (19, 82). Finally, in vivo transplantation of BOs into a host brain delivers host-derived vascular invasion and true physiological perfusion throughout the graft, achieving the highest vascular maturity of all methods (79).

These approaches thus occupy complementary positions on physiological resemblance and experimental practicability. Directed differentiation and assembloids tend to demonstrate moderate-to-high vascular complexity with internally generated vessels, whereas microfluidic systems impose the most realistic flow conditions and barrier characteristics (83, 84). On the other hand, co-culture models produce only rudimentary networks (85), and only in vivo transplantation achieves native degree of vasculature and perfusion (79).

Future Perspectives and Challenges

BOs replicate key aspects of the human brain in a 3D context, providing crucial insights into neurological disorders, therapeutic strategies, and studies of brain development and physiology. Anticipated as an advanced platform to mimic in vivo environment, vBOs may offer a revolutionary approach to extensive fields.

Modeling neurovascular dysfunction in cerebrovascular and neurodegenerative disease pathology

Growing evidence suggests that neurovascular dysfunction contributes to a range of brain disorders. NVU dysfunction, especially BBB alteration is a key mechanism in the pathogenesis of cerebrovascular disorders including ischemic stroke and cerebral small vessel disease (86, 87). Classical neurodegenerative diseases likewise display vascular contributions, where BOs have proven valuable for modeling dopaminergic neuron degeneration in Parkinson’s disease (23), and amyloid/tau pathology in AD (88).

Also, human cerebrovascular disease phenotypes are often difficult to capture in animal models due to human-specific interactions among vasculature and neuroimmune systems. Moyamoya disease models with RNF213 knockout mouse did not reflect the characteristic features such as gross malformations of the circle of Willis (89). In contrast, human iPSC-derived vascular ECs from RNF213 R4810K carriers/patients showed downregulated securin and reduced angiogenesis when RNF213 R4810K overexpressed (90), marking the need of human-specific models.

Recent models recapitulate neurovascular interactions and barrier dysfunction in disease pathology (82). NVU models can reproduce stroke pathophysiology, as oxygen-glucose deprivation and perfusion termination induce BBB disruption and neuronal injury (91). vBOs have emerged as a prominent platform for investigating disease-related phenotypes. Ji et al. (92) developed hPSC-based vascularized neuroimmune organoids consisting of microglia and ACs, which post-exposure of sporadic AD patient brain extracts demonstrated multiple hallmark AD pathologies and pathway disruption. Furthermore, drug-response measurements of anti-amyloid β drug Lecanemab in reducing amyloid burden and vascular side-effects were observed. Dao et al. (84) derived cerebral cavernous malformation (CCM) BBB assembloids from CCM1 mutant patient iPSC-derived cerebral and vascular organoids. These models expressed in vivo cavernoma-like phenotypes, CCM-related angiogenesis, altered neurovascular interaction, and impaired MSC differentiation, reflecting disease pathology. Together, vascularized models integrate human neurovascular physiology to investigate vascular mechanisms in various brain disorders.

High-throughput and precision pharmacological screening

Derived from hPSCs, BOs offer a promising platform for high-throughput screening (HTS) and personalized disease modeling. Durens et al. (93) developed an HTS platform using BOs, applying high-content imaging, multi-electrode arrays, and calcium imaging to consistently evaluate drug effects on neuronal activity and morphology across experiments. This method facilitates rapid, detailed analysis of the influences of drugs on neural networks, providing a scalable solution for drug discovery.

vBO platforms enable systematic interrogation of BBB transport, drug delivery pathways, and neurotoxicity. Simonneau et al. (94) generated >3,000 patterned human BBB organoids with ECs, ACs, and PCs based on hydrogel micro-scaffolds. This high-throughput array was combined with CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing to reveal clathrin dependence for transferrin receptor-mediated transcytosis, suggesting a screening platform for brain delivery mechanisms. Nzou et al. (70) generated human BOs with functional BBB, performing neurotoxicity screening with mercury chloride and Parkinsonian neurotoxins, quantified by adenosine triphosphate−based cell viability assays.

In the context of neurodegenerative diseases, Park et al. (22) employed AD patient-derived cerebral organoids to develop a logical network-based drug screening platform. This system, which employs mathematical modeling through pathological features, enabled identification of several FDA-approved drugs as potential candidates for drug repositioning. These advancements highlight the impact of vBOs in enhancing drug screening processes, offering more accurate and human-relevant models for developing new therapeutics.

Regenerative therapies

Patient-specific platforms derived from hPSCs facilitate optimized therapeutic approaches in regenerative medicine (95). Vasculogenesis enhances neuronal maturation and survival (67, 80), improving the relevance of testing trophic effects of cell therapies such as the MSC secretome, which shows dopaminergic neuroprotective effects on PD models (96). Engineered NVU-on-a-chip platforms enable evaluation of neurorestorative capacity of candidate stem cell therapies post-ischemic brain injury (97). Transplantation studies also show BOs fusing with host neural circuits, contributing to structural repair of infarcted brain tissue and improved functional outcomes of stroke (98). Thus vBOs, which better capture neurovascular coupling and BBB functions (24), are expected to provide a more elaborate environment to assess regenerative therapies targeting vascular stability, inflammation, and metabolic support.

Investigation of human brain development and physiology

BOs can model various stages of brain development, offering insights into their cellular and molecular processes which are valuable for exploring neurodevelopmental processes and disorders (21). They enable further examination of region-specific development, complex neural circuitries, and interneuron migration (11, 20). Also, several diseases such as microcephaly (99) and autism spectrum disorder (100) have been associated with reduced cerebral perfusion during brain development. BO vascularization can promote organoid survival and maturation while enabling long-term culture systems to study epigenetic, transcriptomic, and physiological transitions during early postnatal brain development (28), as well as the contribution of vascular dysfunction to neurodevelopmental disorders (82).

Challenges and potential improvements

Although vBOs represent a significant advancement in modeling human brain development and diseases, they have notable limitations. One primary constraint is their insufficiency to replicate complex brain architecture and function. While they can imitate certain aspects, BOs often lack distinct cortical layers, functional neuronal circuitry, and proper gyrification, essential for accurate modeling of physiology and disorders (101, 102). Furthermore, most vBOs fail to develop a fully functional and perfused vascular network capable of meeting metabolic demands. As a result, persistent cellular stress, hypoxia, and necrosis occur within deeper organoid regions, limiting their viability and long-term functional maturation (102, 103).

Another limitation is incomplete cell maturation and diversity. Although recent protocols introduce glial cells and vascular cells, their spatial organization and physiological coupling remain underdeveloped (101, 102). This is particularly evident in efforts to reconstruct interactions between neuronal and non-neuronal components, such as the NVU or BBB (82). Barrier properties of vBO-derived endothelium remain immature with reported TEER values (∼180 to 350 Ω·cm2) (67) far below the in vivo BBB estimates (∼8,000 Ω·cm2) (104). Moreover, limited cell variety and regional organization restrict their applicability for modeling neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders (105).

Consequently, substantial methodological refinement is required to enhance physiological relevance and versatility of vBO models. Microfluidic organ-on-chip platforms using bioengineering approaches such as 3D bioprinting or noninvasive real-time imaging can improve regulation of the organoid microenvironment (106-108). Angiogenic signaling pathways modulation—including the VEGF–HIF1A–AKT axis and angiogenic factor cysteine-rich angiogenic inducer 61 and hepatoma-derived growth factor—and endothelial transcription factor programming may facilitate vascularization and alleviate diffusion-limited hypoxia (109, 110). Multicellular NVU assembly with PCs and ACs, along with mesodermal progenitor integration may further improve endothelial maturation, barrier integrity, and cellular diversity (71, 111). Collectively, introducing perfusable bioengineering platforms with targeted vascular induction and multicellular approaches may substantially improve the structural and functional fidelity of vBO systems.

Also, as BOs continue to increase in complexity, ethical considerations have emerged regarding research oversight, donor consent, and the theoretical possibility of neural activity associated with sentience or consciousness. Although current BOs remain far from such levels of organization, ongoing ethical oversight and discussion are recommended as the field advances (112).

Conclusion

In this review, we discuss the advancements in brain vasculature models focusing on vBOs, which replicate the complex structure and function of the human brain. Transwell systems and microfluidics have significantly contributed to building functional BBB structures in brain vasculature models, while various differentiation and culture methodologies have developed to establish vBOs. Challenges remain in achieving full maturation and cell diversity, with the complex architecture of the brain continuing to pose significant obstacles. Moreover, the long-term stability and functionality of vBOs require further refinement to enhance their physiological relevance and applicability. Future research should focus on optimizing these models to fully exploit their potential in studying neurological diseases, drug screening, regenerative therapies, developmental processes, and brain physiology. Evolution to physiologically faithful BOs holds promise for redefining our understandings of these aspects.
