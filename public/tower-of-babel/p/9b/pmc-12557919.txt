Accuracy of two electronic apex locators in mandibular mesial canals and the influence of root canal anatomy: a micro-CT-based study
Authors: Zübeyde Gökçe Ürün, Berkan Çelikten, Ali Cemal Tınaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to compare working length measurements obtained using two different electronic apex locators (EALs), Propex Pixi and Woodpecker, at two distinct measurement points, and to evaluate how anatomical complexities affect their accuracy. The working length data from these EAL measurements were compared with gold-standard values determined by micro-computed tomography (micro-CT).MethodsMesial roots of 46 mandibular molars were included. Actual canal lengths (RCL) were determined under an operating microscope. The real working length (RWL) was calculated by subtracting the distance between the apical foramen and apical constriction, as determined by micro-CT, from the RCL. All teeth were embedded in an alginate model. Measurements were taken at two points: at the ‘0.5’ point (recorded as electronic working length 1, EWL1) and at the ‘0.0’ point, from which 0.5 mm was subtracted clinically to obtain electronic working length 2 (EWL2). All specimens were scanned with micro-CT to assess the location, diameter, and morphology of the apical constriction and apical foramen, the distance between them, and the presence of accessory canals. Categorical data were analyzed with Chi-Square tests; measurement values with repeated measures ANOVA and Tukey’s post-hoc tests. The significance level was set at p ≤ 0.05.ResultsBoth EAL devices measured significantly longer working lengths compared to the actual values (p < 0.0001). Significant differences were found among measurement methods, with EWL2 being closer to RWL than EWL1 (p < 0.05). Both devices showed higher precision at EWL2, while AF location and accessory canals had no significant effect. An AF–AC distance greater than 0.5 mm increased errors at EWL1 with Propex Pixi (p = 0.005).ConclusionBoth devices tended to overestimate the working length, and subtracting 0.5 mm from the ‘0.0’ reading improved measurement accuracy. Apical foramen location and accessory canals did not significantly affect results.

Background

Accurate determination of the correct working length is one of the most crucial steps for successful endodontic treatment. A suitable apical point must be identified to fully reach the root canal, shape and fill it correctly, and prevent injury to the surrounding periapical tissues [1, 2]. The apical foramen (AF) can be described as the region where the root canal system opens into the periodontal tissues. The position of the AF may vary due to various factors, such as age, certain pathologies, such as external root resorption, and the function of the tooth [3]. The apical constriction (AC) is considered the narrowest part of the root canal. Beyond the AC point, there is an increase in vascularity. Completion of the preparation in this region results in a smaller wound area, providing optimal conditions for healing [4]. However, there may be accessory and lateral canals near this region, and if the treatment is terminated 1–2 mm shorter than the AC point, there will be an area of 2–4 mm that remains uncleaned with the presence of the accessory canals. This may be sufficient for the development of reinflammation [5].

There are many methods for determining the working length, and among them, the use of electronic apex locators (EALs) provides dependable outcomes [6]. Recently, EALs have played a crucial role in determining working length and are recommended for use alongside radiographs by the European Society of Endodontology (ESE) [7]. Since their invention, electronic apex locators have evolved through multiple generations, and numerous studies have been conducted comparing their accuracy in measuring canal length [8–12]. Moreover, EAL devices can detect perforations and root fractures, as they function by establishing electrical communication with the periodontal ligament [13]. This circumstance also implies that canal complexities, such as isthmus and accessory canals, may affect the precision of EALs. Some studies indicate that the diameters of the AF and AC, as well as the position of the AF, can also impact the accuracy of EALs [14].

The Propex Pixi is a pocket-sized EAL. The Propex Pixi uses multiple frequencies and calculates the root mean square (RMS) values of electrical signals [15]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the Propex Pixi produces results consistent with the gold standard Root ZX device [16, 17].

EAL devices are essential tools for every clinician who performs root canal treatment. Woodpecker's DTE Dpex III apex locator device is a 5th generation, accessible tool that provides an indication of the position of the AF by utilising advanced multifrequency impedance measurement technology. It utilises automatic calibration for measurement. Although this device is widely used in clinics and frequently recommended at endodontic conferences, only a limited number of studies evaluating its performance were identified in the literature review. Although Propex Pixi and Woodpecker devices are frequently used in clinics, the Woodpecker device is more affordable for most clinicians. The Propex Pixi device was chosen due to its performance being in line with gold standard outcomes in previous studies. The Woodpecker device was chosen due to its frequent use in clinical settings, the relatively limited number of studies available on it based on our review, and its greater accessibility in many countries [18].

Currently, micro–computed tomography (micro-CT) imaging systems are methods that provide accurate results in determining the anatomy of the root canal system [19]. With this high-resolution and non-invasive method, the position of the AC can be precisely determined, allowing for EAL accuracy [10, 14]. Moreover, it enables the examination of complex anatomy in the apical third [20].

Many studies have reported that the presence of the isthmus and accessory canals in the mesial roots of mandibular molars is very common [19, 21, 22]. However, few studies have investigated whether these anatomical features affect the precision of apex locators [14, 23].

While most studies evaluating the accuracy of apex locators calculate the distance between the AF and the AC using an average value, the unique contribution of our study lies in precisely measuring this distance through micro-CT analysis rather than relying on estimated averages. This allows for more accurate assessments of where each device actually measures relative to the AC, helping clarify whether they truly identify the AC as claimed. In addition, there are only a limited number of studies that explore the influence of anatomical variations on the accuracy of apex locators. By addressing this aspect as well, our study aims to make a significant contribution to the literature.

Therefore, the aims of the present in vitro study were as follows:To evaluate the accuracy of two electronic apex locators (Propex Pixi and Woodpecker) in determining the working length in mandibular mesial canals by comparing their measurements—taken at different reference points on the device displays—with actual canal lengths obtained through micro-CT and microscopic analysis.To investigate the influence of root canal anatomical features—such as curvatures, isthmuses, and accessory canals—on the accuracy of electronic apex locators using a micro-CT-based approach.

Materials and methods

Ethical clearance and sample size calculation

Before the study, a power analysis was conducted, and a total of 46 teeth were used, with a statistical power of 80%, an effect size of 25%, and a significance level of 0.05 for Type 1 error [9]. This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee at the Gazi University Faculty of Dentistry (GÜDHKAEK.2020.17/3).

Tooth selection and preparation

Forty-six mandibular mesial roots that will be included in the study were randomly selected from the extracted lower molars. The mesial roots of mandibular molars were selected for this study because, according to previous research, they exhibit the highest frequency of anatomical complexities among root canals [19, 21]. After the teeth were kept in 5.25% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl) for two hours, they were kept in saline until the experimental stage. The selected teeth were examined with a dental operating microscope (Labomed Prima DNT Dental Microscope, © Labo Inc., USA), and periapical radiographs were obtained. Teeth with internal/external root resorption, calcified canals, immature apices, cracked/fractured teeth, and teeth with previous root canal treatment were excluded from the study. The crowns of 46 mandibular molar teeth were decoronated at the enamel-cement level under water cooling. Afterwards, the mesial roots of the samples were separated from their distal roots. Mesial roots were used in the study. The coronal surfaces of the mesial roots were smoothed with sandpaper to provide standard measurements and reduce the margin of error.

Visual measurement

The rubber stop was removed from the file. The actual length of the file was determined by measuring the distance between the tip of the file and the plastic handle three times with a digital calliper and calculating the average. The apically resistant hand file (8, 10, and 15 K-file) (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) was advanced into the canals until the tip was observed at the AF under × 25 magnification. The distance from the reference point on the flat surface of the tooth to the handle of the file was measured three times with a digital calliper, and the average was taken. By subtracting this distance from the entire length of the file, the canal length of the tooth was determined. This value was recorded as the real root canal length (RCL) for each sample.

EAL measurements

For this study, the Propex Pixi (Dentsply Maillefer, Ballaigues, Switzerland) and the Woodpecker DTE Dpex III (Guilin Woodpecker Medical Instrument Co., Guilin, China) apex locators were used. The roots were immersed in the alginate model to mimic the electrical conductivity of the periodontal ligament [24]. The root canals were filled with 5.25% NaOCl, and the excess NaOCl was removed from the tooth surface via cotton pellets. Electronic measurements were taken on all samples via both EALs. One end of the two-electrode cable of the Propex Pixi device was connected to the original lip clip, whereas the other end was attached to the apically resistant K-file inserted into the canal. The file was advanced into the canal until the'0.0'point on the apex locator screen was displayed. The file was then fixed at the'0.0'point using flowable composite resin, and the distance between the tooth surface and the handle of the file was measured via a digital calliper. Each measurement was repeated three consecutive times, and the average was recorded. The measured distance was then deducted from the length of the file itself and recorded as the electronic canal length for the Propex Pixi device (ERCLp) (Fig. 1). Next, the K-file was withdrawn to the'0.5'point in the same canal and the distance between the tooth surface and the handle of the file was measured. The obtained value was subtracted from the length of the file itself and recorded as an electronic working length 1 for the Propex Pixi device (EWL1p). Subsequently, 0.5 mm was subtracted from the electronic canal length value obtained at the'0.0'point. The electronic canal length 2 value was recorded for the Propex Pixi device (EWL2p).Fig. 1Experimental setup

Experimental setup

The Woodpecker device was subsequently attached to its original lip clip, and the measurements were repeated on all the teeth. The measurements obtained at the'0.0'point were recorded as the electronic canal length for the Woodpecker device (ERCLw) (Fig. 1). The measurements obtained at point'0.5'were recorded as electronic working length 1 for the Woodpecker device (EWL1w). The value obtained by subtracting 0.5 mm from the measured value at the'0.0'point was recorded as the electronic working length 2 for the Woodpecker device (EWL2w). Electronic measurements were considered valid when the file remained stationary at the desired points for 5 s. In order to achieve measurement standardization, a single experienced endodontist performed all EAL measurements, while another experienced endodontist, blinded to EAL measurements, conducted the micro-CT analyses.

Micro-CT imaging measurements

After electronic measurements, all the samples were scanned with a micro-CT device (Bruker Micro-CT SkyScan 1173, Kontich, Belgium). The X-ray tube of the device was set to 8 W, 80 kV, and 125 μA, and a 1.0 mm Al filter was used. Each image was exposed for 20 ms, and the noise ratio was reduced by taking 3 images from each angle and combining them. The scanning time was set to 40 min for each group. As a result of the scans, raw images with a pixel size of 13 μm were obtained for each sample. The samples were reconstructed via NRecon (ver. 1.6.5.2, SkyScan, Kontich, Belgium) software. The obtained sections were subsequently transferred to the Data Viewer (SkyScan, Kontich, Belgium) and Ctvox (ver. 2.0.6., SkyScan, Kontich, Belgium) programs. All micro-CT analyses in the study were performed by a single operator. The AC and AF locations, diameters, and morphologies were examined and recorded via programs. The AC and AF diameters were compared, and the distance between them was measured. The presence of the isthmus and accessory canal in all teeth was detected (Fig. 2). Evaluation of the measurements

The actual canal length measured under the microscope (RCL) and the values measured at the'0.0'points of the two apex locator devices (ERCLp, ERCLw) were compared.

The real working length (RWL) of the canal was determined by subtracting the actual distance between the AF-AC measured in the micro-CT device from the RCL value. The EWL1p and EWL1w values were measured at the 0.5 points of both apex locator devices. The EWL2p and EWL2w values were obtained by subtracting 0.5 mm from the measurements taken at the 0.0 points. All values were compared with the RWL. When the EWL1 and EWL2 values obtained with apex locators are subtracted from the RWL value, it is considered precise if the result is ± 0.5 mm or less, acceptable if it falls within the range of ± 0.5–1 mm, and mistaken if it exceeds 1 mm [9, 14, 25].

When the value measured by the electronic apex locator was subtracted from the actual canal length, a positive result indicated that the device measured a shorter length, whereas a negative result indicated that the device measured beyond the actual canal length. Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all variables. For qualitative variables, frequencies and percentages were calculated. For quantitative variables, arithmetic means and standard deviations were calculated. Bland–Altman analysis was performed to assess the agreement between each electronic apex locator and the reference radiographic working‐length (RWL). For each pair, we computed the mean of the two measurements (Mi) and the difference (Di), plotted Di against Mi, and calculated the mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (mean_bias ± 1.96·SDD). An a priori ± 5% limit was used to define clinically acceptable agreement. The Pearson Chi-Square test was used to examine the associations between categorical evaluations of the devices, as well as the AF position (axial vs. lateral), accessory canals (present vs. absent), and AF‒AC distances (less than 0.5 mm vs more than 0.5 mm). Repeated measures analysis of variance (RM-ANOVA) was used to compare the measurements of the devices. For significance, Tukey’s multiple comparison post-hoc test was performed to investigate the statistical significance of pairwise differences. SPSS (Version 20.0 for Mac) and GraphPad Prism (Version 9.0 for Mac) software were used for all the statistical calculations and analyses. Microsoft Excel (version 16.77.1 for Mac) was used to draw bar charts. The level of significance was 0.05 for the study.

Discussion

This study was conducted to clarify the uncertainty regarding the optimal reference point for measurements with electronic apex locators and to investigate the impact of anatomical variations on their accuracy. Although the in vitro models may be different from those used in clinical conditions, the primary advantage of evaluating the accuracy of EALs in vitro is the ability to use actual measurements of the tooth as the gold standard. Consistent with previous studies, RCL was determined under an operating microscope at × 25 magnification. The analysis was done by advancing the canal file until it became visible through the apical foramen, then retracting it until it was no longer seen [9, 14, 26]. In this observation-based method, the apical region was observed under an operating microscope from different angles and performed by a single investigator.

In this study, after placing the file inside the canal, the distance from the reference point on the tooth to the file handle was measured with a digital calliper accurate to 0.01 mm [9, 27]. By using this method, it becomes possible to evaluate two different EALs consecutively while also preventing errors that may occur with the positioning or displacement of the rubber stopper.

Various materials such as alginate, saline, floral foam, saline-impregnated gauze, agar–agar, and gelatin are used in in vitro studies with EALs. Alginate was used in this study, as the authors indicated that as a colloidal gel, alginate met the electroconductivity and root contact requirements more adequately than other experimental media did [24]. In addition, embedding the teeth in alginate prevents the practitioner from seeing the tooth roots, thus preventing possible biased evaluations [28].

While AFs can be easily viewed under an operating microscope, the most accurate method currently available for identifying ACs is micro-CT scanning to analyse sections along the axis of the canal [10, 14, 18, 20, 29–31]. Micro-CT is a noninvasive method that enables imaging and evaluation of hundreds of sections simultaneously [32]. Using micro-CT, the exact positions of the AF and AC can be determined, and the actual distance between them can be calculated [9, 18]. In this study, similar to previous studies, the AC position and the distance to the AF were obtained via micro-CT scans. RWL was obtained by subtracting the actual AF-AC distance from the RCL [10, 14]. This method ensures that the misconceptions arising from the fact that the AC position can vary in each tooth are avoided [14, 20].

Choosing between the'0.0'and'0.5'points in determining the working length mostly seems to depend on the personal experience and preference of the practitioner. Most electronic apex locators use the'0.0'or'APEX'indicator to indicate AF, and the'0.5'point is usually indicate the location of the AC [9]. However, readings at the'0.5'point may simply indicate an area slightly behind the apical foramen rather than the actual AC [27]. Additionally, the AC is not present in every tooth [33, 34]. In these cases, the'0.5'point can lead to inconsistent measurements [27]. Only a few studies have compared the difference between these two points, possibly due to difficulties in accurately determining the AC point [10, 14, 27]. In this study, we compared the measurements at both points.

Both apex locators demonstrated a tendency to measure slightly beyond the actual working length; however, the deviations remained within clinically acceptable limits. These results align with those of Yolagiden et al., who also reported accurate measurements at the 0.5 point across multiple apex locator models [35].

The measurements taken at the 0.0 point with a clinical subtraction of 0.5 mm (EWL2) showed smaller deviations compared to direct measurements at the 0.5 point (EWL1). Although the difference between RWL and EWL2 was also statistically significant, the numerical deviation was considerably smaller compared to the difference observed between RWL and EWL1. These findings emphasize the importance of selecting appropriate measurement strategies, especially in cases with anatomical complexities that could compromise electronic readings. In accordance with our results, De-Deus et al. also emphasized the need to clinically subtract 0.5 mm from the ‘0.0’ mark to minimize the risk of over instrumentation [18].

In this study, similar to other studies, measurements within the range of ± 0.5 mm are considered'precise,'whereas those within the range of 0.5–1 mm are considered'acceptable'[9, 14, 25, 36]. Due to the diversity in the shape of the apical region and the anatomical differences of the AC, a tolerance of ± 1 mm is considered clinically acceptable [37]. Our findings confirmed that measurements at the 0.0 point with a clinical subtraction of 0.5 mm (EWL2) substantially improved precision rates compared to direct 0.5 point measurements (EWL1). While Piasecki et al. reported no significant difference in accuracy between apex locators, our results suggest that both devices may vary in performance depending on the measurement strategy employed [9]. This highlights the importance of technique selection in maximizing the reliability of electronic apex locators, particularly in anatomically complex cases.

Electronic readings that deviate by more than 1 mm should be considered'mistaken'since the deviation would clinically overestimate or underestimate the canal length [2]. In certain studies, the percentage of measurements deviating by more than 1 mm was reported to be less than 10% [9, 38, 39]. However, our results indicate a higher incidence of ‘mistaken’ measurements, particularly at the EWL1 point. This discrepancy may stem from the methodological difference in our study, where actual anatomical data from micro-CT scans were used rather than relying on an assumed average distance between the apical foramen and apical constriction. Incorporating precise anatomical variations likely contributed to a more stringent assessment of measurement accuracy, reflecting real-world complexities that can challenge electronic apex locator performance.

Numerous studies have indicated that the isthmus and accessory canals are frequently present in the mesial roots of mandibular molars and exhibit complex anatomies [19, 21, 22]. Researchers have reported significant variations in the apical region and noted that the isthmus is commonly present in the apical third, even at the 1 mm level [19] (Fig. 8). Owing to their complex anatomy, mandibular molar teeth were selected as the focus of this study. Tahmasbi et al. reported an isthmus rate of 69.6% in mesial roots, with the majority located in the apical third at a rate of 44.3% [22]. In support of these findings, our study revealed an isthmus presence of 91.30%, 45.23% of which were located in the apical third. However, only a few studies have investigated whether these anatomical variations impact the accuracy of apex locators [14, 23]. Piasecki et al. reported that the presence of an accessory canal and isthmus slightly reduced the accuracy of electronic apex locators. However, the difference was not statistically significant [14]. Similarly, in this study, the absence of an accessory canal at both the EWL1 and EWL2 points led to a decrease in the number of measurements falling into the'mistaken'category. This difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). It is possible that this decline in acceptable measurements in the presence of an accessory canal is due to blockage of anatomical complexes by organic tissue debris. When the location of the apical foramen, whether axial or lateral, is examined, certain studies have indicated that the location of the apical foramen being lateral statistically reduces the accuracy of apex locators [14, 40]. In contrast, Ding et al. reported that measurements were statistically closer to the real working length when the apical foramen was positioned laterally [23]. Consistent with this, our study also revealed an increase in measurements in the'precise'category when the apical foramen was located laterally. However, these differences were not statistically significant. Discrepancies between studies could be attributed to variations in the types of teeth analysed. In our study, we focused on the mesial roots of mandibular molar teeth with complex anatomical structures, whereas other studies may have examined single-rooted teeth.

The distance between the apical constriction and the apical foramen has been measured in many studies, yielding different results [20, 41]. These inconsistencies between studies may be attributed to variations in evaluation methods, such as longitudinal or cross-sectional approaches, as well as different reference points. In line with our study, Piasecki et al. also reported via micro-CT of mandibular molars that the average distance from the AF to the AC was 0.46 mm [9].

Our findings suggest that an increased distance between the AF and the AC may influence the accuracy of electronic apex locators, as evidenced by the differing measurement performances of the Propex Pixi device at various points. One possible explanation is the variation in tissue composition, particularly the presence of cementum, which could affect the electrical impedance and dielectric properties that apex locators rely on. The denser structure of cementum compared to dentin may disrupt the device’s algorithmic measurements, leading to more inconsistent readings when the AF-AC distance is larger. Similarly, Barakat et al. suggested that the overestimation tendency observed in one of the devices could be related to algorithmic limitations and potential electromagnetic interference, supporting the notion that internal device mechanisms significantly influence measurement reliability [31]. While this hypothesis provides a plausible rationale, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear, highlighting the need for further studies to better understand the factors affecting electronic measurement accuracy.

In addition, several methodological limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. Intra-assessor variability could not be fully assessed, as the three repeated EAL measurements were performed consecutively in a single session rather than at separate time points. This may have reduced the natural variability between measurements and should be considered as a limitation. Furthermore, inter-assessor variability could not be evaluated, as EAL and micro-CT measurements were performed by different, blinded operators, each responsible for only one measurement method.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this study, both the Propex Pixi and Woodpecker apex locators demonstrated a tendency to record measurements beyond the actual working length. Adjusting the measurement by subtracting 0.5 mm from the ‘0.0’ reading resulted in more accurate and clinically acceptable values for both devices. Furthermore, neither the location of the apical foramen (axial vs. lateral) nor the presence of accessory canals had a significant effect on the accuracy of measurements obtained with either apex locator.
