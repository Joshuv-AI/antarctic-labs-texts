Analysis of severity in COVID-19 patients by using longitudinal immune profiles
Authors: Woobeen Jeong, Kyulhee Han, Taewan Goo, Taesung Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryCOVID-19 severity varies due to immune heterogeneity, complicating treatment; therefore, we modeled oxygen saturation, CRP, and blood urea as severity markers for pulmonary, inflammatory, and renal functions. We analyzed cytokine, T cell receptor (TCR), and B cell receptor (BCR) expressions in 106 patients (NIH, Korea) across three stages (T1–T3) and applied a joint modeling framework, using linear mixed-effects models to identify immune features associated with severity. Models incorporating correlations among severity variables outperformed models assuming independence. IL-6 was significantly associated with all markers (negatively with oxygen saturation and positively with CRP and blood urea), whereas IL-8 was linked to oxygen saturation, and IGLV1-44 was consistently associated with CRP. Overall, accounting for correlations improved model performance and highlighted IL-6, IL-8, and IGLV1-44 as candidate immune features that may help refine severity assessment in heterogeneous COVID-19 cases.Subject areas: health sciences

Introduction

Since the World Health Organization (WHO) declared COVID-19 a pandemic on March 11, 2020, there have been approximately 778 million infections and over 7.1 million deaths worldwide as of February 2025.1 To guide treatment strategies, the WHO introduced a standardized 0–10 severity scoring system based on clinical symptoms, imaging findings, and oxygen saturation.2 However, patient conditions often fluctuate due to individual variability in immune responses, manifesting a wide spectrum of symptoms.3 This variability highlights the need for a detailed analysis of adaptive immune profiles, which significantly decrease during the early onset of COVID-19 in severe patients but recover during the recovery stage, thereby providing an indicative biomarker for predicting disease progression and recovery.4 Cytokines, T cell receptors (TCRs), and B cell receptors (BCRs) have emerged as key adaptive immune profiles for predicting severity and guiding treatment.5 While conventional models have identified biomarkers linked to the WHO severity scoring system, they often fail to capture the multifaceted nature of severity changes, limiting early intervention and effective management of complications such as renal or cardiac dysfunction.6,7 Recent approaches have shifted toward analyzing changes in multiple severity response variables, such as oxygen saturation, C-reactive protein (CRP), and blood urea, over time to better understand their associations with immune responses.8

This study investigates the associations between three severity response variables (oxygen saturation, CRP, and blood urea levels) and adaptive immune profiles across early, middle, and late hospitalization stages. We hypothesize that incorporating correlations between the three severity response variables can improve model fitting and efficiency, which refers to how closely an estimator approximates the true parameter.9 To evaluate this assumption, the analysis compares four modeling approaches. The first approach uses the linear mixed-effects model (LMM), which does not account for correlations between the three severity response variables, while the other approaches incorporate these correlations using a joint modeling framework.10 Specifically, we evaluate models with different covariance structures ranging from a baseline model that assumes no correlations to more complex ones to identify the model with the best fit. Our objective is to estimate the impact of immune profiles linked to the three severity response variables by evaluating the fit and performance of these models. This, in turn, allows for more comprehensive interpretation of severity, considering not only pulmonary dysfunction but also immune and renal abnormalities, and provides insights for refining treatment strategies in COVID-19 patients.

Based on the likelihood ratio test (LRT), the model assuming common correlations (model 2) demonstrated a significant improvement in model fit compared with model 1 (p < 0.001) as shown in Table 1. In contrast, model 3 did not show a statistically significant benefit over model 2 (p = 0.164), nor did model 4 improve upon model 3 (p = 0.962). Given that model 2 also presented the lowest Akaike information criterion (AIC) value (5298.15), it was selected as the final model offering the optimal balance of fit and simplicity. Diagnostic plots for the normality and homoscedasticity assumptions of model 2 are provided in Figures S7 and S8.

Oxygen saturation exhibited the largest reduction in prediction errors (RMSE from 26.64 to 18.758; MAE from 13.887 to 10.961), while CRP and blood urea levels also showed smaller but significant improvements. To validate the best fit achieved by model 2, we examined the estimated parameters such as variance and correlation coefficients and compared them with the observed correlation coefficients (Tables 2 and S6). In this model, we assumed a time-invariant correlation pattern across time points for each pair of severity response variables (oxygen saturation, CRP, and blood urea). This implies that the correlations between these markers are sustained throughout the disease course, reflecting a persistent systemic inflammatory response, rather than transient random fluctuations. As shown in Table 2, model 2 estimated correlation coefficients of −0.24, −0.52, and 0.34 for the oxygen-CRP, oxygen-urea, and urea-CRP pairs, respectively. These estimates match the observed patterns in Figure S6, which confirms that the direction of these correlations remained consistent over time. Significant predictors in the LMMs with and without correlations

We evaluated how incorporating correlations between the three severity response variables into the random effects of the LMMs influenced the identification of significant immune profiles as predictors. While models 2, 3, and 4 showed significant improvements in model fit compared with model 1, models 2, 3, and 4 exhibited similar goodness of fit. Among them, model 2 demonstrated the best predictive performance. To control for multiple testing, we applied a Bonferroni correction at a significance threshold of α = 0.05, resulting in an adjusted threshold of α = 3.39 × 10−4.

When evaluating the −log10 p values of variables across the three severity response variables in models 1 and 2, variables significant at the 95% confidence level (p value <0.05) were identified and are illustrated in Figure 1. For CRP, model 2 identified 10 significant variables, of which 4 were not significant in model 1 (Figure 1E). Newly significant variables in model 2 included TNF-α, IGLV4-69, and IGLV1-47 (Figure 1B). For blood urea, model 2 identified 10 significant variables, of which 4 were not significant in model 1 (Figure 1D). Newly significant variables in model 2 included MMP1, TNF-α, IGLV1-47, and B cell activating factor (BAFF) (Figure 1F).

To mitigate overfitting and assess coefficient stability, we performed bootstrap resampling at the individual level (1,000 resamples).11 For each bootstrap sample, model 2 was refitted to calculate the empirical selection probability of each variable. The analysis revealed that our key identified features were consistently selected across the majority of resamples, with selection probabilities exceeding 0.7 (Table S4). This high frequency of selection demonstrates that the identified associations are robust signals rather than artifacts of overfitting to the specific dataset structure.

Discussion

Our analysis shows that incorporating correlations among random effects improves model fit and performance when predicting longitudinal severity response variables. Models 2, 3, and 4, which account for correlations between response variables, consistently outperformed model 1 by achieving lower AIC and error-based metrics such as RMSE, MAE, and Mahalanobis distance. This highlights the importance of considering correlations between severity response variables in modeling. Although models 2, 3, and 4 exhibited similar performance, model 2, which assumes common correlations between response variables across time points, demonstrated the highest predictive performance. In contrast, model 1, which lacks these correlations, had higher prediction errors and lower goodness-of-fit, demonstrating the limitations compared to models with correlations. Additionally, the ability of model 2 to account for more individual variability across severity response variables is supported by its higher ICC. The increases in marginal and conditional R2 further confirm that model 2 captures more variance explained by both fixed and random effects. Beyond statistical metrics, the common correlations across time point structure in model 2 offers clinical implications, particularly given our sampling timeline (T1: 0–3 days; T2: 7 days; T3: 14–56 days). This structure implies that the correlation between respiratory function (oxygen saturation), systemic inflammation (CRP), and renal stress (blood urea) remains consistent regardless of the time points.

Biologically, the correlations captured by model 2 reflect the fact that COVID-19 affects the entire body. As the infection leads to a cytokine storm, it causes lung damage while increasing CRP and urea levels due to systemic physiological stress and kidney dysfunction. This coordinated deterioration is consistent with clinical observations in severe COVID-19 patients, where hyperinflammation (elevated CRP) is strongly associated with acute respiratory distress (reduced oxygen saturation) and acute kidney injury (elevated blood urea levels).12 Previous studies have reported that these markers do not decline in isolation but exhibit strong inter-dependencies that reflect the severity of multi-organ involvement.13 Thus, model 2 better reflects the clinical reality that inflammation affects multiple organs consistently throughout the disease course.

The analysis revealed key biological and clinical insights through significant predictors identified for oxygen saturation, CRP, and blood urea. These three severity response variables capture complementary aspects of systemic disease progression in COVID-19. Oxygen saturation reflects pulmonary injury and gas exchange failure, CRP represents the hepatic acute-phase response to systemic inflammation, and blood urea levels indicate renal involvement and global metabolic stress. Circulating cytokines measured in plasma are well-established mediators linking immune activation to multi-organ dysfunction, while TCR and BCR repertoire features reflect the adaptive immune response that sustains or modulates this systemic inflammatory state. Therefore, modeling these immune features jointly with correlated severity outcomes provides a biologically coherent framework for capturing multi-organ disease severity. All identified cytokines, along with TCR and BCR sequences, were validated against the COVID-19 Drug and Gene Set Library.14

Among these, IL-6 emerged as a central factor, uniquely demonstrating significant associations with all three severity markers (oxygen saturation, CRP, and blood urea) in model 2. Specifically, higher IL-6 levels were associated with reduced oxygen saturation (β = −8.16, p value = 2.25 × 10−4), elevated CRP (β = 2.82, p value = 2.82 × 10−6), and increased blood urea levels (β = 3.21, p value = 3.07 × 10−4) in model 2 (Table 3). Unlike other cytokines, IL-6 not only stimulates the liver to synthesize CRP and induces vascular permeability, leading to lung injury (low oxygen saturation), but is also a known driver of acute kidney injury through systemic hemodynamic instability and direct renal tubular toxicity.4,15

In contrast, IL-8 was significantly associated with lower oxygen saturation (SpO2: β = −0.84, p value = 3.08 × 10−4) and higher CRP (β = 5.02, p value = 1.74 × 10−7) but not with blood urea.

It recruits neutrophils to pulmonary tissues, thereby promoting intra-alveolar inflammation. However, its association with renal dysfunction is less established, as there is insufficient histopathological consistency regarding its direct involvement in neutrophil-mediated microvascular damage in the kidneys.16,17

Meanwhile, IGLV1-44 showed a specific association only with lower CRP (β = 14.12, p value = 5.68 × 10−7), emphasizing the role of B cell activation in COVID-19 progression.18,19 Because IGLV1-44 is linked to non-neutralizing antibodies, its presence suggests an ineffective humoral response that fails to clear the virus, leading to persistent systemic inflammation (high CRP).20 Consequently, IGLV1-44 could play a crucial role in the immune response of COVID-19 patients with severe systemic inflammation. This is supported by the observation that IGLV1-44 is highly expressed alongside IGHV5-51 in the E3 epitope group, which exhibits weak neutralization.21 Instead of directly neutralizing the virus, these antibodies likely contribute to non-neutralizing immune response that could amplify systemic inflammation. Together, these findings highlight how cytokines and BCR segments contribute uniquely to different aspects of disease severity.

While model 2 demonstrates the best fit and performance, its clinical value extends beyond statistical accuracy. Our framework effectively captures the common correlation structure among the severity markers across time points (T1–T3). This structural consistency implies that our model is highly applicable for early risk prediction. Specifically, by integrating multiple severity indicators simultaneously, we expect this approach to more accurately detect high-risk patients who might otherwise be overlooked by single-marker assessments. We also acknowledge the inherent limitations of the linear framework employed in this study. Biological immune responses often exhibit complex non-linear dynamics, such as threshold effects or saturation points, which our current LMM may not fully capture. Therefore, future studies should explore non-linear modeling approaches to better account for these complex biological behaviors.

Limitations of the study

Data were collected in South Korea with complete TCR, BCR, and cytokine measurements across all three time points. Because the cohort was drawn from a single country and the sample size was limited, the findings may not fully generalize to populations with different racial, ethnic, or socioeconomic backgrounds. Validation in larger and more diverse cohorts will be important to assess broader applicability.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

This study utilized data from the Clinical and Omics Data Archive (CODA), which included 459 COVID-19 patients between August 2020 and January 2022. We selected 106 hospitalized patients with complete adaptive immune profiles and complete values of the severity markers. Samples were collected at three time points (T1–T3): T1 corresponds to the first 3 days from symptom onset, T2 is within 7 days of hospitalization, and T3 is between 14 and 56 days of hospitalization. To assess potential bias due to missing data, we compared complete (N=106) and incomplete (N=69) groups across clinical variables and found Cohen’s d values <0.2.

Participant characteristics

The final analytic cohort included 106 participants (42 males, 39.6%; 64 females, 60.4%) with a mean age of 52.0 ± 16.8 years. Using clinically motivated thresholds, low oxygen saturation was defined as a minimum peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO2) ≤ 93%, high urea as a peak blood urea level > 20 mg/dL, and high inflammation as a peak C-reactive protein (CRP) level > 10 mg/L. Under these definitions, 27 participants met the criterion for low oxygen saturation, 20 for high urea, and 19 for high CRP. Demographic characteristics of participants meeting each criterion are summarized in Table S5. Race, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status were not collected. Note that the dataset originates from hospitals in South Korea, caution is warranted when generalizing findings to populations with different demographic or socioeconomic backgrounds.

Method details

Data acquisition and preprocessing

Data for this analysis were obtained from the Clinical and Omics Data Archive (CODA) provided by the National Institutes of Health (NIH), which included 459 COVID-19 patients with alpha to delta variants confirmed to be SARS-CoV-2 positive via PCR testing between August 2020 and January 2022.22 To investigate adaptive immune responses over time, we selected 106 hospitalized patients with complete adaptive immune profiles, excluding individuals with missing values, at three key time points: T1, T2, and T3. To assess potential systematic bias due to missing data, we compared the severity markers (oxygen saturation, CRP, and blood urea) between the complete (N=106) and incomplete (N=69) datasets. We calculated Cohen’s d to quantify the magnitude of the differences. As shown in Figure S1, all Cohen’s d values are below the threshold of 0.2, indicating negligible differences between the two groups. This suggests that the availability of data was not associated with the severity of the patient's condition. Accession numbers and access conditions for the analyzed CODA datasets are provided in the resource availability section.

T1 corresponds to the first 3 days from symptom onset, T2 is within 7 days of hospitalization, and T3 is between 14 and 56 days of hospitalization. These specific intervals capture critical phases of the disease, with T1 as the early stage of infection, T2 as disease progression, and T3 as recovery and long-term immune responses.

A total of 530 adaptive immune profiles were analyzed, including 163 cytokines, 190 TCR repertoires (TRA and TRB), and 177 BCR repertoires (IgG, IgM, IgK, and IgL). All datasets were obtained from the CODA, which applies standardized normalization and batch-correction procedures during preprocessing.22 Cytokine levels were quantified using a customized multiplex bead–based immunoassay panel based on the Luminex MAGPIX platform (Luminex Corporation, USA). Plasma samples derived from CPT tubes were used for the assay, and all measurements were performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The TCR and BCR repertoires were profiled using the SMARTer® Stranded RNA-Seq Kit. Quantification was performed based on VDJ usage, which was determined by analyzing read counts corresponding to each variable (V), diversity (D), and joining (J) sequence. Only sequences with a Phred quality score of ≥ 20 were included in the analysis to ensure reliable results.23 These immune profiles, combined with three severity response variables, including oxygen saturation (%), C-reactive protein (CRP, mg/dL), and blood urea levels (g/L), provided insights into dynamic immune processes and their associations. Oxygen saturation was assessed using non-invasive methods, while CRP and blood urea levels were measured in blood serum, reflecting respiratory function, systemic inflammation, and kidney function, respectively.

We performed 5-fold cross-validation with cv.glmnet, applying the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm for each severity response variable. We then selected 96 variables that were chosen by at least one of the severity response variables, consisting of 36 cytokines, 23 TCR repertoires, and 37 BCR repertoires.

Linear mixed model architectures

We examined the associations between adaptive immune profiles and three longitudinal severity response variables measured at three time points by employing a linear mixed-effects model (LMM). This approach handles longitudinal outcomes by incorporating fixed effects, which capture overall population trends, and random effects, which capture variability unique to each individual.24 Specifically, we compared four models based on different assumptions about how the three severity markers (oxygen saturation, CRP, and blood urea) are correlated during COVID-19 progression.

Model 1 assumes there are no correlations among the markers. It suggests that a worsening or improvement in each of the markers is treated as an independent event.

Model 2 allows the three markers to be correlated and assumes that these pairwise correlations (Oxygen-Urea, Oxygen-CRP and Urea-CRP) are time-invariant. In this setting, while the strength of correlation varies across different marker pairs, the same correlation matrix is applied to all time points.

Model 3 allows the pairwise correlations to be time-variant. Unlike Model 2, it estimates a unique correlation matrix at each time point, enabling the model to capture how the associations between markers evolve across different clinical phases.

Model 4 assumes that correlations exist not only within the same time point but also across different times. This captures delayed correlations, meaning that the state of a marker at an earlier time can be associated with another marker at a later time.

To improve model fit and more accurately capture correlations among the severity response variables, we adopted a joint modeling framework that integrates three separate LMMs by adding correlation terms between the response variables within a covariance structure.25 We defined independent random effect terms uij for each longitudinal outcome yij, where j=1 for oxygen saturation, j=2 for CRP, and j=3 for blood urea. The overall outcome vector for each individual, yi∈R9×1 was constructed by stacking the three outcome-specific vectors yij∈R3×1 that correspond to three severity response variables measured at each of the three time points. Explanatory variables included time-invariant variables xif, time-varying variables xiv, measurement time indicators I(T=t) with time points t=1, 2, 3, and interaction terms between time-varying predictors and measurement time. Error terms ϵij from each longitudinal outcome were assumed to follow a compound symmetry structure, while residual errors across different severity response variables were considered independent.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Model evaluation

To determine the overall fit and predictive performance of the proposed models, we first conducted a likelihood ratio test (LRT) to evaluate whether each subsequent model offered a statistically significant improvement in fit compared to the model with no correlations. We then compared log-likelihood, restricted log-likelihood, Akaike information criterion (AIC), root mean square error (RMSE), mean absolute error (MAE), and Mahalanobis distance (dM). AIC evaluates model fit with model complexity, whereas RMSE and MAE assess predictive accuracy, with RMSE emphasizing larger errors and MAE being less sensitive to outliers.26 The Mahalanobis distance dM is computed by weighting the difference between observed and predicted values with the inverse of the total covariance matrix. By including both random effects Σu and residual covariances Σϵ, it provides a comprehensive prediction error that accounts for correlations between severity response variables.27

Following the overall comparison, we conducted a more detailed evaluation for each of the three severity response variables, comparing the best-fitting model against the model with no correlations. We focused on both error-based metrics, including RMSE and MAE and goodness-of-fit metrics, including intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), marginal R2 (Rm2), and conditional R2 (Rc2), as specified in the following equations. In these equations, σϵ02 represents the variance of the error in a model with only fixed effects and no random intercept. In contrast, σu2 and σϵ2 denote the variance of the random effects and the error, respectively, in a model that includes the random intercept. The ICC reflects the proportion of variance attributed to random effects, while marginal R2 and conditional R2 reflect how much variance in the severity response variables is attributed to fixed effects alone, and both fixed and random effects, respectively.28,29
