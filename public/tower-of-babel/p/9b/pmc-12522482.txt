Antibiotic resistance and virulence of bacteria in spices: a systematic review
Authors: Sahar Torki Baghbadorani, Ebrahim Rahimi, Amir Shakerian, Faham Khamesipour
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpices, widely valued for their flavor, color, and antioxidant properties, are increasingly used in culinary and food industries. Despite their benefits, spices may act as carriers for antibiotic-resistant and potentially pathogenic bacteria, posing a threat to food safety and public health.MethodsThis systematic review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive search of six databases (Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, and Embase) was conducted for English-language articles from inception to 2023, focusing on bacterial contamination, antibiotic resistance, and virulence in spices. Inclusion was limited to peer-reviewed articles, and methodological quality was assessed using the JBI checklist.ResultsOf the 3,458 initially identified articles, 16 met the inclusion criteria. Most studies originated from Asia (n = 5) and the Americas (n = 4). Bacteria commonly isolated from spices included Bacillus cereus, Escherichia coli, Salmonella spp., and Staphylococcus aureus. High resistance levels were observed against ampicillin (83.3%) and penicillin (82.1%), while most isolates were susceptible to polymyxin B and cephalothin. Resistance genes such as bla, tetK, and ermB were frequently detected, along with virulence genes like nheA, hblC, cytK, and tpeL.ConclusionSpices may serve as reservoirs for multidrug-resistant and virulent bacteria. Improved handling, processing, and decontamination practices are essential to mitigate foodborne risks and curb the spread of antimicrobial resistance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s42522-025-00172-6.

Introduction

The global demand for flavorful and health-promoting ingredients has led to increased use of spices in culinary and industrial food applications. Spices such as cinnamon, turmeric, black pepper, garlic, and ginger not only enhance palatability but also offer antioxidant and antidiabetic properties due to their bioactive compounds [1, 2]. However, despite their low moisture content, spices are vulnerable to microbial contamination at multiple points in the supply chain—from cultivation to packaging [3, 4]. Several studies have identified pathogenic and spoilage bacteria in spices, including Salmonella spp., Bacillus cereus, Escherichia coli, and Staphylococcus aureus, which are capable of surviving in dry environments and potentially causing foodborne outbreaks [5–7]. Alarmingly, these bacteria may carry antimicrobial resistance (AMR) traits and virulence genes, compounding the public health risk posed by contaminated spices [8, 9].

While the antimicrobial properties of spices are well-documented, their role as vectors for resistant pathogens has been underexplored. This review aims to synthesize current evidence on the prevalence, resistance profiles, and virulence traits of bacteria isolated from spices. By integrating findings from microbiology, food safety, and public health, this review identifies contamination trends, antibiotic susceptibility patterns, and gene prevalence to inform future research, regulation, and mitigation strategies.

Methods

Search strategy and selection criteria

This review was conducted following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive literature search was performed using six databases: Web of Science, PubMed, Scopus, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, and Embase. The search included studies published in English from database inception through December 2023.

The search strategy employed Boolean operators and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) where applicable. Keywords included: “bacterial contamination,” “antibiotic resistance,” “spices,” “spice microbiota,” “spice safety,” “virulence factors,” and “resistance genes.” The search strings were tailored to each database’s indexing system. Reference lists of eligible studies were also screened to identify additional articles.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Studies were included if they: (i) reported original research on bacterial contamination in spices; (ii) included data on antibiotic resistance and/or virulence traits; (iii) were peer-reviewed and published in English; (iv) Included food-grade or commercially distributed spices.

Studies were excluded if they: (i) were abstracts, reviews, editorials, or non-English publications; (ii) lacked primary data or methodological details; (iii) focused solely on spice extracts without evaluating contamination.

Study screening and data extraction

After duplicate removal, two reviewers independently screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text review for eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus.

Quality assessment

The Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool was used to assess study quality and risk of bias. Each study was evaluated for methodological clarity, sampling strategy, diagnostic techniques, and outcome reporting. The overall quality of included studies was rated as moderate to high. A summary table of the quality assessment is included in Supplementary Material 1 [10, 11].

Results

Study selection

The initial search yielded 3,458 articles. After removing 582 duplicates, 2,876 records remained. Title and abstract screening excluded 2,732 articles for irrelevance. Of the remaining 144 full-text articles, 128 were excluded for reasons including inadequate data (n = 37), lack of focus on spices (n = 52), or poor methodological quality (n = 39). A total of 16 studies were included in the final analysis. A PRISMA flowchart is presented in Fig. 1.

Antibiotic resistance patterns

Resistance data from 14 studies showed high prevalence of resistance to β-lactam antibiotics (Ampicillin: 83.3% and Penicillin: 82.1%). By contrast, low resistance was observed to Polymyxin B: 1.2% and Cephalothin: 1.2%.

Many isolates were also susceptible to kanamycin, neomycin, streptomycin, oleandomycin, chloramphenicol, imipenem, and nalidixic acid. Figure 2 presents resistance frequencies across tested antibiotics. Multi-drug resistance (resistance to ≥ 5 antibiotics) was reported in isolates from black pepper, curry powder, chili, and white pepper.

Discussion

This systematic review synthesizes global evidence on the bacterial contamination, resistance traits, and virulence profiles of microbes isolated from commonly used spices. The findings raise significant public health concerns, particularly given the frequent detection of multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria in widely consumed spices like black pepper, cumin, and curry powder. The microorganisms identified are associated with foodborne illness. For example, B. cereus is known to cause vomiting and severe diarrhea; E. coli can cause urinary tract infections, neonatal meningitis, and in some cases, hemolytic uremic syndromes; Salmonella spp. is a well-documented cause of typhoid fever and gastroenteritis; and S. aureus can produce toxins that cause toxic shock syndromes and food poisoning [21, 22].

The results for antibiotic resistance patterns reveal a high prevalence of resistance to ampicillin (83.33%) and penicillin G (82.14%). This strong resistance of Bacillus cereus to beta-lactam antibiotics, such as penicillin and ampicillin, has been documented several times in the scientific literature [23, 24]. High levels of resistance to β-lactams (ampicillin and penicillin) may reflect overuse of these antibiotics in agriculture and food production chains. The detection of bla, tetK, and ermB resistance genes further corroborates phenotypic resistance patterns. These genes are known to be plasmid-borne, raising the risk of horizontal gene transfer to other pathogenic bacteria.

The ability of Bacillus cereus to produce beta-lactamase enzymes, which are responsible for this resistance, is well established and has been confirmed by multiple studies. However, it is interesting to note that almost all isolates were shown to be susceptible to a diverse range of other antibiotics, including polymyxin B, cephalotin, kanamycin, neomycin, streptomycin, oleandomycin, chloramphenicol, imipenem, and nalidixic acid. These observations corroborate those of Fiedler et al., (2019) [25], who also noted the susceptibility of B. cereus strains to antibiotics such as chloramphenicol and imipenem.

Analysis of multidrug-resistant patterns revealed that some isolates from black pepper samples were resistant to up to eight different antibiotics. Similarly, isolates resistant to at least five distinct antibiotics were identified in samples of black pepper, chilli, curry powder, and white pepper. These findings are consistent with findings from other studies, such as those conducted by Luna et al., 2007 [26] and Hwang et al., 2002 [27], who also reported multi-drug resistant patterns in some isolates. The spread of these resistant bacteria, especially in spices, which are widely used in kitchens around the world, can facilitate the transmission of resistance genes between microorganisms, exacerbating the problem of antibiotic resistance. It is therefore imperative to step up efforts to monitor and limit the spread of antibiotic resistance throughout the food system, in order to preserve the effectiveness of antibiotics for future generations [28].

When toxin-coding genes were found in isolated bacteria, it was found that more than half of the samples had one, two, or three different forms of nhe. This outcome is consistent. with Jovanovic et al. who discovered a significant percentage of nhe gene carriers in isolates [29]. These genes confirm that among isolates, they are the most common toxin-coding genes [7, 27]. The genes that were most prevalent were nheA, nheB, and nheC. In Lindback et al.‘s opinion [30], the most hazardous genes are those listed. Virulence gene detection, particularly nheA, cytK, and hblC, indicates the potential for enterotoxin production. Bacillus cereus isolates harboring multiple virulence genes could contribute to spice-associated foodborne outbreaks, particularly when spices are added post-cooking.

Geographic differences in contamination levels and gene carriage suggest regional variation in agricultural practices, hygiene standards, and post-harvest processing. For example, studies from Asia and Africa more frequently reported MDR isolates, potentially reflecting differences in regulation or monitoring of spice production [14, 15, 21, 31].

While spices are known for their antimicrobial effects, their role as reservoirs for harmful bacteria highlights the need for enhanced safety controls. Inadequate washing, improper drying, and unhygienic packaging increase microbial risks, especially in informal markets.

Limitations

This review is limited by the heterogeneity of included studies in terms of spice types, diagnostic methods, and antimicrobial panels tested. The absence of quantitative meta-analysis also limits the ability to generate pooled prevalence estimates. Furthermore, data were restricted to English-language publications.

Conclusion

This systematic review reveals that spices can harbor multidrug-resistant and virulent bacteria, including B. cereus, E. coli, Salmonella spp., and S. aureus. Resistance to commonly used antibiotics such as ampicillin and penicillin was frequently observed, along with carriage of key resistance and virulence genes.

These findings underscore the urgent need for improved spice decontamination methods, standardized microbial safety regulations, and routine monitoring of antimicrobial resistance in spice products. Public health interventions should include the implementation of good agricultural and manufacturing practices (GAPs and GMPs), targeted consumer education, and research into effective decontamination techniques (e.g., irradiation, steam treatment, or ozone exposure).

Future studies should include genomic surveillance of spice-associated microbes, longitudinal tracking of resistance trends, and region-specific risk assessments to mitigate the global threat of antimicrobial resistance originating from the food chain.
