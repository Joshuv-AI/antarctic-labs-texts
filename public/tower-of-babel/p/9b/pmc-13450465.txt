Breaking the immune barrier: construction of cartilaginous organoids using alpha-1,3-galactosyltransferase-deficient pig cartilage-derived particles
Authors: Hongyu Jiang, Sice Wang, Zhengrui Zhou, Jiajie Chen, Cheng Huang, Wei Liu, Zhibo Jia, Jiazhou Wu, Jianting Ye, Yingjie Xiong, Tao Qian, Yanbin Wu, Yanjun Guan, Wenxin Huang, RuiChao He, Changyan Ma, Jiang Peng, Aiyuan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArticular cartilage injuries exhibit limited self-repair capacity due to avascularity and other intrinsic properties, leading to high disability rates and posing clinical challenges. In recent years, cartilaginous organoids (CORGs) have emerged as a promising tissue engineering strategy, offering new hope for cartilage regeneration and repair. However, biomaterials used in constructing CORGs still face challenges such as immunogenicity and biocompatibility during clinical translation. In particular, xenogeneic biomaterials, which contain natural antigens like the α-1,3-galactose (α-Gal) epitope, are prone to trigger severe host immune rejection. Gene editing technology, especially knocking out the gene encoding α-1,3-galactosyltransferase (GGTA1), holds promise for producing low-immunogenicity donor animals, such as α-Gal knockout pigs (α-Gal KO pigs).Main bodyIn this study, through in vitro and in vivo experiments, a humanized mouse subcutaneous implantation model was established to verify the low immunogenicity of cartilage tissues from α-Gal KO pigs. Subsequently, cartilage-derived particles (CDPs) were prepared from α-Gal KO pig cartilage and further used to construct CORGs. Finally, the ability of these CORGs to form new cartilaginous tissue ectopically was validated in a nude mouse subcutaneous model.ConclusionsThis study explores the low immunogenicity of gene-edited porcine cartilage tissue and constructs a novel xenogeneic dECM-based cartilage organoid with ectopic chondrogenic capability. This approach is expected to overcome immune rejection barriers and pave a novel strategic avenue for the clinical translation of cartilage organoids.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08263-2.Keywords: Cartilaginous organoids; Xenotransplantation; Alpha-1,3-galactosyltransferase-deficient pig; Cartilage-derived particles; Cartilage regeneration

Introduction

Articular cartilage injuries are relatively common in clinical practice, and their common causes include trauma, inflammation, autoimmune diseases, or tumors [1–5]. Due to the avascular and aneural anatomical characteristics, cartilage tissue’s regenerative and reparative capabilities are limited, often resulting in patient disability and severely affecting daily life [6, 7]. Commonly used clinical techniques, such as microfracture surgery, autologous chondrocyte implantation (ACI) [8], or matrix - induced autologous chondrocyte implantation (MACI) [9], all have limitations and cannot achieve optimal results. The neocartilage formed after microfracture surgery is mostly fibrocartilage, which cannot fulfill the load-bearing and lubrication functions of hyaline cartilage. ACI/MACI requires two surgical procedures, and the biological scaffolds currently used in MACI suffer from poor bioactivity. Additionally, 2D cell culture of autologous chondrocytes leads to chondrocyte dedifferentiation [10]. The development of biomaterials and tissue-engineered organs has provided new strategies for cartilage repair [11]. According to whether a scaffold is required, organoids can be classified into scaffold-based and scaffold-free [12]. In scaffold-based approaches, the use of biomaterials that mimic the microenvironment of the target organ/tissue can enhance the regenerative and reparative effects [13].

Biomaterials can interact with the internal environment and exert an influence on it; therefore, regulatory approval for these biomaterials is necessary to ensure their safety and effectiveness. Notably, the acceptance degree of these biomaterials in the host tissue, their impact on gene expression and signal transduction, their short-term and long-term effects on local microenvironments (such as inflammation, foreign body reaction, fibrosis or rejection), and the safety of their degradation products all need to be evaluated [14]. Despite these advancements, achieving the compatibility of current biomaterials with Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP) standards still poses challenges, which hinders their application in clinical settings [15].

Biomaterials derived from human tissues or organs are highly regarded due to their extremely low immunogenicity, making allogeneic transplantation a standard treatment method for certain diseases. However, human tissues are often scarce and limited in supply [15].

Given the limited differences between the extracellular matrix (ECM) components of xenogeneic tissue (collagen, laminin, fibronectin, elastin, and growth factors) and their human counterparts, xenogeneic ECM may be able to replace human ECM in clinical applications [16–18].

In particular, due to the similarities in size, bloodline, and extracellular matrix structure between pigs and humans, as well as ethical considerations, pigs have become a suitable alternative to human donors [19].

Porcine-derived biomaterials are generally preferred over those from other animals because they are more suitable for the attachment and growth of human cells [20, 21]. However, there are also some issues within this, such as the biosecurity risks posed by porcine endogenous retroviruses (PERV) [22]. These viruses may trigger immune responses and diseases in humans if they are transplanted along with tissues or biomaterials [19, 23, 24]. Additionally, the biggest obstacle to the clinical application of xenogeneic tissues is the severe immunological problem [25]. One major challenge is the presence of α-gal epitopes (Structural domains catalyzed by alpha-1,3-galactosyltransferase synthesis) [18, 26–28], which are abundant in xenogeneic biomaterials and can trigger hyperacute rejection reactions in recipients. Before the clinical application of porcine tissues, these problems must be addressed [29]. Decellularization treatment is an effective strategy to reduce the immunogenicity related to α-gal epitopes. Many decellularization methods can be used to eliminate α-gal epitopes associated with the extracellular matrix [30]. However, there is conflicting data regarding the degenerative effects of the residual α-gal epitopes present in decellularized tissues [18, 26, 31, 32]. Therefore, researchers have introduced gene editing technology into the processing of xenogeneic biomaterials [20, 33, 34]. Given the role of α-gal epitopes in immune rejection, gene-edited pigs with knocked-out genes such as GGTA1 (encoding α-1,3-galactosyltransferase) have become the consensus [35–37]. Additionally, PERVs can also be eliminated through gene editing techniques (such as CRISPR-Cas9) to enable gene-edited pig-derived biomaterials to overcome the immune barriers and biosecurity concerns, thereby boosting their clinical application potential. Therefore, directly using humanized porcine cartilage tissue that has undergone gene editing to construct organoid models can not only realistically reproduce the microenvironment of natural cartilage tissue, but also avoid the immune rejection reaction triggered by α-gal epitopes.

In terms of cell selection, human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells(hUCMSCs), with their excellent multidirectional differentiation potential and low immunogenicity, have become one of the most promising allogeneic cell sources in the field of regenerative medicine and are widely used across multiple domains [38, 39].

This study aimed to develop low-immunogenicity cartilaginous organoids (CORGs) suitable for clinical applications using xenogeneic biomaterials. Initially, we validated the gene-editing strategy for α-Gal KO pigs by assessing the immunogenicity of their cartilage tissue through in vitro experiments. Subsequently, we further evaluated the immune rejection of the cartilage tissue via subcutaneous implantation in humanized mice. Chondrogenic microparticles were prepared from the cartilage tissue of α-Gal KO pigs to construct low-immunogenicity CORGs, and the ectopic cartilage regeneration capacity of these CORGs was ultimately assessed. The whole experimental design is shown in Fig. 1. The CORGs developed in this study are expected to demonstrate the potential to overcome immune barriers, paving a new strategic pathway for their clinical translation.

Experimental section

Immunological characterization of α-Gal KO pig cartilage and cells

Histological staining

Cartilage was isolated from α-Gal KO pigs and wildtype pigs (WT pigs), leaned with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS).

Histological staining

The cartilage specimens were cut into 10-µm-thick frozen sections and stained with H&E, Safranine O, Alcian blue, and Masson’s trichome stain.

The remains of α-Gal KO pig was donated by key Laboratory of Animal Gene Editing and Animal Cloning in Yunnan Province (Yunnan Agricultural University, Kunming, China).

Lectin staining

The specificity of lectins for galactose has been demonstrated in previous studies [40, 41]. Here, an isolectin B4 (BSI-B4), FITC conjugate (L2895, Sigma, USA) was diluted to 25 µg/mL in PBS. Frozen sections of cartilage from α-Gal KO and WT pigs were incubated with this lectin conjugate for 60 min at room temperature. After washing with PBS, the sections were stained with DAPI (C0065, Solarbio, China) for 15 min, washed with PBS, and sealed. Then, they were imaged using a confocal slide scanner (PANNORAMIC, 3DHISTECH).

Evaluation of human serum IgG and IgM binding capacity

In order to evaluate the binding of human serum IgG and IgM to α-Gal KO and WT pig cartilage specimens, the two groups of specimens were sectioned. The sections were fixed and then incubated with 15% AB human serum for 1 h, followed by F(ab’)2-goat anti-Human IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, FITC (1:250, A24471, Invitrogen) and goat anti-human IgM Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody, DyLight™ 488 (1:200, SA5-10102, Invitrogen) for IF. Cell nuclei were stained using DAPI. The sections were sealed and imaged using a confocal digital slide scanner (PANNORAMIC, 3DHISTECH).

Flow cytometry analysis

Isolectin B4 (IB4): Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were extracted from the peripheral blood of α-Gal KO and WT pigs. The cells were counted and incubated with IB4 (1:500, L2895, Sigma) in the dark at 4 °C for 30–45 min. An on-line assay was performed using a BD Fortessa flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and the data were analyzed with Diva analysis software.

IgG and IgM: PBMCs were counted and incubated with inactivated AB human serum for 30 min. Then, they were blocked with 10% goat serum for 30 min. The cells were subsequently incubated with IgG and IgM antibodies in the dark at 4 °C for 30 min. The on-line assay was performed using a BD Fortessa flow cytometer to detect IgG (1:500, A18806, Invitrogen) and IgM (1:500, A21249, Invitrogen) binding, and the data were analyzed with Diva analysis software. Samples treated with antibodies alone were used as the controls. The results were expressed as the relative mean fluorescence intensity (mean fluorescence intensity).

The use of human blood samples was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Fourth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital(No.2020KY036-HS001).

Gene expression analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the PBMCs of both α-Gal KO and WT pigs, and qRT-PCR was performed after reverse transcription. The expression of target genes was normalized based on GAPDH expression, and the 2−ΔΔCt method was used to calculate the relative expression of the target genes. The gene primers are listed in Supplementary Table S1 (Supporting Information). All samples were run in triplicate to ensure technical consistency.

Subcutaneous implantation experiments in humanized mice

Subcutaneous implantation

The animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhongyan Zichuang (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., LTD (No. ZYZC202404019S). Female humanized mice [huPBMC-(M-NSG) mice (Cat. NO. NM-NSG-015), 6–8 weeks of age] were sourced from Shanghai Model Organisms Center, Inc. The mice were housed under SPF conditions with unrestricted movement and ad libitum access to food and water. These mice were randomly divided into three groups (Sham, α-Gal KO and WT).

α-Gal KO pig and WT pig cartilage specimens of the same thickness were taken, trimmed into 1 × 1 cm2 pieces, and sterilized using 60 Coγ irradiation. Mice were anesthetized using 4.5% isoflurane. A longitudinal incision (diameter: 1 cm) was made on the dorsal side. Blunt dissection was performed on the right and left sides of the dorsal midsection, and the two different cartilage tissue specimens were implanted subcutaneously. Finally, the skin was sutured using 6 − 0 sterile surgical absorbable sutures and sterilized with the iodophor disinfectant after surgery. A sham-operated group was also set up at the same time (n = 3). After 7 days, retroorbital blood samples were obtained from the mice for flow cytometry and biochemical analysis. All mice were euthanized by an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals, and the skin was incised to gain subcutaneous tissue for gross visualization. The tissues were finally placed in 4% paraformaldehyde for overnight fixation before subsequent experiments.

Flow cytometry

Peripheral blood samples from mice were assayed using flow cytometry on a BD Fortessa flow cytometer, and the data were analyzed with Diva analysis software. Specifically, the samples were incubated with APC-Cy™7 rat anti-mouse CD45 (BD, Cat: 557659), Spark Violet™ 538 anti-human CD45 (BioLegend Cat: 982320), FITC anti-human CD8a (BioLegend, Cat: 300906), PerCP mouse anti-human CD3 (BD, Cat: 552851), and PE-Cy™7 mouse anti-human CD4 (BD, Cat: 560649) antibodies at the recommended concentrations.

After incubation, the samples were vortexed and mix for 15 min. Then, 1 mL of hemolysin containing fixative was added, and the samples were again vortexed and mixed for 10 min. Finally, the samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min before discarding the supernatant. Then, 1 mL of 1× PBS was added, the samples were adequately vortexed and mixed, and the samples were centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the cells was resuspended in 200 µL of 1× PBS before analysis on the flow cytometry machine.

Biochemical analysis

The remaining blood samples were centrifuged at 3500 prm for 10 min, and the upper layer of clear bright serum was collected. Serum samples were biochemically assayed using a veterinary blood biochemistry analyzer (BS-240VET, Mindray, China). Use the ELISA kit (GEH0038-96T; GEH0001-48T; GEH0004-48T; GEH0006-48T, Servicebio, China) to analyze the serum.

Histological examination

Tissues were dehydrated in sucrose solutions. Frozen tissue sections were used for H&E staining to observe the subcutaneous implants and the surrounding lymphocyte infiltration. The same field of view area was taken for each group and the inflammatory cell density = number of inflammatory cells/field of view area was calculated.

Construction of CORGs based on α-Gal KO/WT pig cartilage-derived particles

Cell acquisition and culture

Second-generation research-grade human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hUCMSCs) (RC02003) were purchased from Nuwacell Biotechnologies Co., Ltd. The hUCMSCs were cultured to the fourth and fifth generation for experiments.

Acquisition of cartilage-derived particles and construction of CORGs

The α-Gal KO pig cartilage specimens were crushed and sieved to obtain α-Gal KO pig cartilage-derived particles (α-CDPs) with diameters between 150 mm and 300 mm were suspended in PBS, sterilized with 60 Coγ irradiation, and stored at 4 °C. Using the same method, we obtained WT pig cartilage-derived particles (CDPs). CDPs/α-CDPs were placed on glass slides, observed under a microscope (Nikon, Japan), and their particle sizes were analyzed. Additionally, total collagen and glycosaminoglycan (GAG) content in CDPs/α-CDPs were quantitatively measured. The GAG content was examined using a dimethylmethylene blue (DMMB) colorimetric quantitative detection kit (GMS19239.2, GENMED SCIENTIFICS INC., USA). The total collagen content was estimated using a hydroxyproline assay kit (A030, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Organoids were constructed using hUCMSCs loaded onto α-CDPs and CDPs scaffolds. α-CDPs or CDPs (20 mg) were combined with hUCMSCs (5 × 10^5 cells) in a 15 mL conical tube and centrifuged at 600 rpm for 5 minutes. The resulting aggregates were subsequently cultured statically in ncMission hMSC Medium (RP02010, Nuwacell) for 3 days to form spherical structures. These spheroids were then transferred to ultra-low attachment 24-well plates and cultured in chondrogenic induction medium (HUXUC-90041, Ceye) for 14 days. During this differentiation period, the medium was refreshed 2–3 times per week. The CORGs were dehydrated using ethanol gradients of 75%, 85%, 90%, 95% and 100% and placed in a supercritical carbon dioxide extractor for treatment. The particles were sprayed with gold for 120 s and then placed in a field emission scanning electron microscope (Thermo Scientific Apreo 2 S, USA) for observation.

Gene expression analysis

The CORGs were analyzed using qRT-PCR on days 7 and 14, as described earlier. In brief, RNA was extracted from CORGs on days 7 and 14, and qRT-PCR was performed after reverse transcription. The expression of target genes was normalized based on GAPDH expression, and the 2-ΔΔCt method was used to calculate the relative expression of the target genes. The gene primers are listed in Table S2 (Supporting Information).

Gross observation and histological and IF analysis

The gross morphology of the 7- and 14-day CORGs was observed using a stereomicroscope (Nikon, Japan). Then, 7- and 14-day CORGs were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and 10-µm-thick frozen sections obtained on a cryostat (CM1950, Leica, Germany) were used for histologic examination. Their morphology and structure were evaluated using H&E staining, and the content of glycosaminoglycans was examined using toluidine blue staining. Images were obtained using a microscope (Nikon, Japan).

For IF staining, anti-SOX9 primary antibody (1:200, ab185230, Abcam), ACAN (1:200, NB600-504, Novus) and COL Ⅱ (1:200, NB600-844, Novus) were used for immunofluorescence staining. Finally, imaging was performed using a confocal slide scanner (PANNORAMIC, 3DHISTECH).

CORGs Subcutaneous implantation experiments in nude mice

Implantation

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Zhongyan Zichuang (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., LTD (No. ZYZC202404019S). All animals were nude mice (BALB/c Nude, male, six to eight weeks of age), sourced from Spfanimals (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The mice were kept in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) setting, where they had free range of movement and unlimited access to both food and water. The nude mice were randomly separated into two groups: the α-CDP group and the WT group.

Before the implantation process, nude mice received anesthesia using 4.5% isoflurane. A subcutaneous injection of Meloxicam (2 mg/kg) was given before the operation. On the dorsal side, a longitudinal incision (5 mm in diameter) was created, and a single subcutaneous pouch was formed in the dorsal midsection via blunt dissection. Subsequently, CORGs were placed into this pouch. At the end of the procedure, the skin was closed using 6 − 0 sterile surgical absorbable sutures and disinfected with an iodophor disinfectant. One month later, all mice were euthanized by an overdose of pentobarbital sodium (150 mg/kg, intraperitoneal injection), in accordance with the AVMA Guidelines for the Euthanasia of Animals. An incision in the skin allowed for gaining the subcutaneous implant. The fibrous tissue covering the implant’s surface was stripped off and then placed in PBS for macroscopic observation. Following this, the implant was fixed overnight in 4% paraformaldehyde, preparing it for histological, immunohistochemical, and IF analysis.

Gross observation and histological, immunohistochemical, and IF analysis

The implant’s macroscopic features were scrutinized with a stereomicroscope (Nikon, Japan). Subsequently, the implant underwent fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde, and histological evaluation utilized frozen sections. Cartilage development within the implants was observed via H&E staining. Additionally, Safranin O and Toluidine blue staining were performed on the sections to determine the extracellular matrix content of the newly formed cartilage.

For immunohistochemical staining, sections were treated with an antigen recovery solution, blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 30 min, and incubated with 10% goat serum for 2 h. Then, the sections were incubated with a primary antibody against COL II (1:200, NB600-844, Novus) overnight at 4 °C. After washing in PBS, the sections were incubated with goat anti-mouse IgG H&L (HRP) (1:1000, ab6789, Abcam) for 1 h at 25 °C. After incubation with the chromogenic DAB solution for 10 min, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 2 min. An ethanol gradient was utilized for section dehydration, xylene for transparency, and a slide scanner (PANNORAMIC, 3DHISTECH) for imaging.

For IF staining, sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 5 min, followed by antigen retrieval using Frozen Section Antigen Repair Solution (Solarbio, C1035) for 5 min. After blocking with 10% goat serum for 2 h, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary anti-SOX9 antibody (1:200, ab185230, Abcam). On the following day, cells were washed three times with PBS and then incubated for 1 h with a goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) Cross-Adsorbed Secondary Antibody (Alexa Fluor 488, 1:500, A-11008, Invitrogen). Nuclei were then counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. Parallel IF staining for Human Nuclear Antigen (HNA, 1:200, NBP3-1391, Novus) was performed using the same protocol. All images were acquired using a confocal slide scanner (PANNORAMIC, 3DHISTECH).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses of parametric data from two groups were performed using Student’s t-test. Statistical analyses of parametric data from multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s test. Statistical analyses comparing different genotypes with different procedures were performed using two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s test. The rank-sum test was applied to analyze data with a nonhomogeneous variance. The data were subjected to normality tests, and all analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). All data are reported as the means ± SDs of the means. Significance was defined as a p-value < 0.05. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001, n.s. represents no significant difference.

Results

Immune characterization of α-Gal KO pig cartilage and cells

The knockout of GGTA1 was achieved via CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing. First, we characterized the immune properties of α-Gal KO porcine cartilage tissues and cells. Histopathological comparisons of cartilage specimens from α-Gal KO and WT pigs were performed (Fig. 2A). These analyses showed that the tissue structure of α-Gal KO pig cartilage was not altered after CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing (Fig. 2B).

(C) Representative flow cytometric results of BSI-B4 (BSI-B4) antibody-labeled α-Gal KO pig and WT pig PBMCs. To confirm the genetic stability of the donor pigs, we first performed a detailed analysis of the GGTA1 locus at both the genomic and protein levels. qPCR analysis revealed that GGTA1 mRNA was undetectable (Undetermined) in the tissues of α-Gal KO pigs, whereas high levels of GGTA1 expression were observed in WT controls ( Figure S1, Supporting Information). This complete deficiency at the genomic level provides the molecular basis for the reduction in immunogenicity. To further validate the transition from genomic to protein levels, we performed flow cytometry and IF staining to detect the α-Gal epitope. IB4 has a unique affinity for terminal α-D-galactose residues and can therefore detect α-Gal epitopes. The flow cytometric analysis of PBMCs showed that α-Gal KO pig PBMCs exhibited extremely low levels of binding to IB4 in comparison to PBMCs from WT pigs. This indicates that α-Gal has been obviously deleted in the α-Gal KO pigs (Fig. 2C).

Importantly, this distinct molecular transition—from the genomic deficiency of GGTA1 to the complete elimination of the α-Gal antigen—serves as the fundamental basis for the diminished immunogenicity of the donor cells, as it effectively prevents the binding of naturally occurring anti-Gal antibodies. To confirm these findings, IB4-based IF analysis was performed.

The results revealed the α-Gal KO pigs does not express α-gal protein. In contrast, WT pig cartilage was rich in α-Gal protein expression. Furthermore, minimal α-Gal protein expression remained detectable in decellularized WT pig cartilage specimens (Fig. 2D and G).

In addition, the binding ability of PBMCs to human IgG and IgG was also explored using flow cytometry. PBMCs from α-Gal KO pigs had a weaker binding affinity to human IgG and human IgM than those from WT pigs, indicating the weak binding capacity of tissues from α-Gal KO pigs to nonspecific human IgG and IgM (Fig. 2E). Subsequently, the cartilage specimens were incubated with AB-type human serum, and IF analysis was performed to detect the binding affinity to human IgG and IgM. The results showed that α-Gal KO pig cartilage had a reduced ability to bind human IgG and IgM than WT pig cartilage. Similarly, due to the removal of immunogenicity through decellularization, decellularized WT pig cartilage had low IgG and IgM binding ability, but it was still stronger than that of the cartilage of α-Gal KO pigs (Fig. 2F and G).

Immune response to α-Gal KO and WT pig cartilage in humanized mouse models

Humanized peripheral blood mononuclear cell (hPBMC) mice are a “humanized immune system” model constructed by transplanting peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from healthy human donors into severely immunodeficient mice. Their primary advantage lies in their ability to rapidly reconstitute a functionally active human immune system, making them an effective model for immunogenicity testing. In these mice, the human T cells within the engrafted PBMCs proliferate in response to stimulation by mouse xenogeneic antigens, while other cell types remain at low levels or diminish prematurely. As a result, the human immune cell population is predominantly composed of T cells, which enables this model to partially simulate the human immune environment and immune responses, making it suitable for various studies requiring human T cell-mediated immune reactions.

In this study, we subcutaneously implanted α-Gal KO pig cartilage and WT pig cartilage in humanized mice (Fig. 3A) to model post-implantation immune responses in humans. Seven days after subcutaneous implantation, blood was collected from the mice for peripheral lymphocyte typing via cell flow cytometry. The remaining blood was used for serological testing. Notably, although mouse-derived leukocytes (mCD45+) were still detected in the blood samples obtained from humanized mice, we focused on the fraction of human immune cells (hCD45+).

The implantation of both α-Gal KO pig cartilage and WT pig cartilage in humanized mice induced a higher proportion of cytotoxic T cells (hCD3+hCD8+) and a lower proportion of T helper cells(hCD3+hCD4+) than sham surgery (Fig. 3B and D). This suggested that both types of cartilage were at risk of varying degrees of immune respond after implantation. However, relative to the WT pig cartilage group, the α-Gal KO pig cartilage group had more T helper cells and fewer cytotoxic T cells, resulting in a higher T helper/cytotoxic T cell ratio. This was indicative of a pro-remodeling phenotype and also suggested that α-Gal KO pig cartilage implantation may be associated with lower host rejection due to adaptive immune responses in humans (Fig. 3B and D).

Further biochemical analysis of serum samples was also performed. The total C3 complement levels were higher in both the α-Gal KO pig group and the WT pig group than in the sham group. However, they were lower in the α-Gal KO pig group than in the WT pig group. The total IgM levels were the highest in the WT pig group. Although these levels were higher in the α-Gal KO pig group than in the sham group, this difference was not significant. The total IgG levels in both the WT and α-Gal KO pig groups were higher than those in the sham group. Although total IgG levels in the WT pig group was higher than those in the α-Gal KO pig group, this difference was not significant (Fig. 3F). ELISA analysis showed that the levels of human IL-2, IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ in the α-Gal KO pig group were all lower than those in the WT pig group (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Collectively, combined with the flow cytometric analysis of peripheral blood T-lymphocyte subsets from humanized mice, these findings showed that α-Gal KO pig cartilage had a lower host rejection risk in humans than WT pig cartilage.

The subcutaneous tissue around the implantation site was isolated from each humanized mouse and histologically analyzed. The foreign body reaction was evaluated by the degree of cellular infiltration (Fig. 3C and E). The degree of inflammatory cell infiltration in the WT pig group and the α-Gal KO pig group was more severe than that in the sham group. However, the degree of inflammatory cell infiltration in the α-Gal KO pig group was much lower than that in the WT pig group. This suggested that although the implantation of xenobiotic biomaterials may still cause immune rejection in humans, the knockdown of the α-Gal epitope can considerably lower the risk of immune rejection.

Construction of CORGs based on CDPs

Based on the results of our previous experiments, we speculated that the use of α-Gal KO pig cartilage as a source biomaterial for CORGs may lower the risk of immune rejection. Therefore, we constructed low-immunogenicity CORGs using cartilage-derived particles from α-Gal KO pigs. We performed particle size analysis of CDPs/α-CDPs, and their sizes were 230.5 ± 43.04 μm and 230.8 ± 61.22 μm, respectively (Figure S3, Supporting Information). Meanwhile, the cartilage-specific matrix components were also analyzed, and the results showed that the GGTA1 knockout gene-editing strategy did not affect the cartilage matrix composition (Figure S4, Supporting Information).

To analyze the differentiation of CORGs, qRT-PCR was performed to examine biomarkers associated with chondrogenic developmental stages. The chondrogenic transcription factor SOX9 showed consistently increased expression with prolonged induction time, suggesting the transition of CORGs from early to mature chondrocyte phenotypes during this stage. Meanwhile, as chondrocyte differentiation progressed, the expression of chondrocyte phenotypic markers COL II and ACAN increased by day 14 compared to day 7 (Fig. 4C).

(B) Histological staining of day7 and day14 CORGs (H&E and Toluidine blue). Scale bars: 1 mm. (C) qRT-PCR analysis of biomarkers related to chondrogenic development (SOX9, COL Ⅱ and ACAN) (n = 3)

From a macroscopic perspective, the CORGs at day 7 and 14 appeared as smooth, translucent spherical structures (Fig. 4A). H&E staining revealed relatively homogeneous chondrocytes and collagen surrounding the α-CDPs/CDPs in the α-Gal KO/WT group. Toluidine blue staining demonstrated abundant cartilage matrix components and chondrocytes arranged around the α-CDPs/CDPs in the α-Gal KO/WT group. Compared to day 7, CORGs at day 14 showed increased cartilage-specific matrix components around both α-CDPs and CDPs, indicating continuous extracellular matrix secretion by the CORGs over time (Fig. 4B). Combined with qPCR results, this suggests the progressive maturation of CORGs. SEM observation revealed abundant filamentous extracellular matrix components within the CORGs (Figure S5, Supporting Information).

Immunofluorescence analysis further evaluated the changes in specific markers (SOX9, COL Ⅱ, ACAN) in both groups of CORGs at different time points. At day 7, both groups expressed these specific markers, indicating chondrogenic differentiation of the spherical aggregates. By day 14, DAPI staining revealed increased cell distribution throughout the CORGs, accompanied by elevated numbers of SOX9+, COLⅡ+, and ACAN+ cells, reflecting the progressive maturation of CORGs (Fig. 5A and B).

Representative fluorescence images of CORGs cultured for 7 and 14 days after incubation with renewed medium. (A) Representative images of the SOX9/ACAN (Green/Red) immunofluorescence. (B) Representative images of the SOX9/COL Ⅱ (Green/Red) immunofluorescence. Scale bars: 200 μm

In summary, we successfully constructed CORGs based on both α-CDPs and CDPs.

CORGs promotes cartilage formation in vivo

Next, we sought to validate whether the in vitro-engineered cartilage tissue spheroids indeed exhibit in vivo cartilage-forming potential. Concurrently, we aimed to investigate whether gene editing of donor pigs for cartilage microparticles impacts their chondrogenic capacity. For the subcutaneous chondrogenesis assay, BALB/c-nu mice were selected as the model animals. These mice harbor a congenital thymic deficiency, resulting in a lack of mature T lymphocytes and impaired cellular immune function. While their B lymphocyte population remains intact, their overall immune competence is compromised, characterized by low IgG secretion levels. Consequently, BALB/c-nu mice are widely acknowledged as an appropriate model for transplantation studies. In our previous research, we have demonstrated that 3D cell spheroids constructed from CDPs of WT pigs possess chondrogenic capacity. Therefore, these cell spheroids were used as controls to evaluate the in vivo chondrogenic potential of 3D cell spheroids constructed from CDPs of gene-edited pigs.

The CORGs were subcutaneously implanted in BALB/c-nu mice and retrieved after 1 months for gross observation. The CORGs were observed to have formed translucent cartilaginous tissue subcutaneously (Fig. 6A and B). H&E staining showed the presence of relatively homogeneous chondrocytes and collagen around the α-CDPs in the α-Gal KO group. Safranin O and Toluidine blue staining showed that a large number of cartilage matrix components and chondrocytes were arranged around the α-CDPs in the α-Gal KO group. Finally, immunohistochemical staining showed the presence of abundant collagen fibers strongly positive for type II collagen in CORGs of α-Gal KO group. IF staining for SOX9 revealed a large number of SOX9-positive cells in the subcutaneous ectopic tissues in the both of α-Gal and WT group (Fig. 6C). These results were consistent with the findings of the histological analysis, demonstrating that a large number of chondrocytes were present in the subcutaneous ectopic tissues formed by the both groups of CORGs.

CORGs promotes cartilage formation in vivo. A-C) Histological (H&E, Safranine O, Toluidine blue and COL Ⅱ) and immunofluorescence staining (SOX9 and HuNu) of new formed cartilaginous tissue. Scale bars: 1 mm (above), 200 μm (below)

Consistent with previous findings, similar results were observed in the WT pig group. A substantial number of cells and secreted extracellular matrix were present around the cartilage particles, indicating that the gene-edited pig cartilage tissue exhibited similar in vivo chondrogenic activity to that of the WT pig cartilage tissue (Fig. 6A and B).

Interestingly, since human cell lines were used to construct the CORGs, we further verified the origin of the cells in the neonatal cartilaginous tissue based on human nuclear (HuNu) staining. We found a large number of both HuNu-positive and HuNu-negative cells. Thus, we speculated that in addition to the cells from the CORGs, some cells from BALB/c-nu mice were also involved in the formation of new cartilaginous tissue (Fig. 6C ).

Overall, the results suggested that CORGs based on α-CDPs/CDPs both can successfully form new cartilaginous tissue in vivo. Notably, gene editing of cartilage microparticle donors reduces their immunogenicity without compromising their chondrogenic capacity.

Discussion

The field of cartilage tissue engineering is currently at a critical stage for translating novel therapeutic approaches into clinical applications within the coming years [11]. The progress achieved in recreating the functional characteristics of native tissues using tissue-engineered cartilage constructs is encouraging, particularly with the development of CORGs [12]. Numerous CORGs for articular repair have been reported to date. For instance, a previous study utilized hyaluronic acid/hydroxyapatite/gelatin microspheres for the in situ self-assembly of CORGs to repair canine cartilage defects6. Another research effort constructed a cartilaginous organoid hydrogel based on the aging marker miR-24/synovial mesenchymal stem cells (SMSCs), which effectively attenuated chondrocyte senescence and repaired cartilage defects in the osteoarthritic microenvironment of rats [42]. Furthermore, a multi-gradient cartilaginous organoid was developed to mimic the natural structure of cartilage by controlling collagen fiber orientation within the hydrogel through molding compression technology and incorporating a chondroitin sulfate gradient design [43]. Despite these advancements, the clinical translation of such materials has remained challenging due to issues such as limited biomimetic bioactivity and the production of harmful degradation byproducts.

Therefore, a crucial aspect in advancing the translational applications in this field lies in developing strategies that endow constructs with bioactivity while avoiding the production of harmful degradation byproducts. Concurrently, to better simulate the native biomimetic environment, multiple disciplines have engaged in the development and research of animal-derived biomaterials.

Decellularized rat kidney ECM has been utilized as a natural scaffold, where co-culturing with endothelial cells and immature epithelial cells formed kidney organoids that were transplanted in situ to mimic renal functions [44]. Mechanistically, research has shown that tissue-derived ECM scaffolds can promote tissue regeneration by increasing IL-4 expression to induce tissue repair [45]. In other applications, porcine bladder ECM was successfully employed to repair muscle defects in mice [46]. Furthermore, vascularized liver organoids have been constructed by combining decellularized animal-derived liver ECM with endothelial cells and fetal liver cells to simulate liver functions [47]. Other investigations successfully generated intestinal organoids in vitro using porcine small intestinal ECM hydrogels [48]. Advances in biofabrication have also led to the development of ECM-based bioinks for bioprinting dual-chamber structured heart organoids loaded with human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs) [49]. Notably , a bilayer biomimetic scaffold fabricated from porcine ECM—incorporating UCMSCs and goat primary chondrocytes—has been applied to repair articular cartilage defects in goat knee joints [50].

In a previous study, we explored the construction of CORGs using decellularized extracellular matrix microcarriers derived from wild-type porcine cartilage. These CORGs successfully repaired cartilage defects, demonstrating excellent cartilage regenerative capacity [51]. Although this research revealed the significant therapeutic potential of CORGs constructed from animal-derived biomaterials, their clinical translation remains challenging due to immune barriers. The potential induction of adverse immune responses may compromise the survival of these organoids in humans, hindering the clinical application of animal-derived biomaterials [20, 31, 52].

Specifically, xenogeneic biomaterials contain antigenic, particularly α-gal epitopes. This antigen is a major source of immunogenicity in xenografts because α-gal epitopes are present on proteoglycans, glycoproteins, and glycolipids in all mammals except old world monkeys, apes, and humans. These epitopes are catalyzed by alpha-1,3-galactosyltransferase [53], which is absent in primates and humans.

Although decellularization can eliminate α-gal epitopes to some extent, the clinical application of many dECM materials is still limited due to significant immunological barriers [25]. The impact of various decellularization methods on the immunogenicity of the final xenograft product is not fully understood. Additionally, differences in tissue and organ origin can affect the presence of residual α-gal epitopes after decellularization. For example, decellularization treatment can completely eliminate α-gal epitopes from the porcine small intestinal mucosa (SIS) [54, 55]. The decellularized porcine SIS mainly consists of collagen fibers. Thus, it has minimal carbohydrate chains and is effectively cleared of α-gal epitopes after decellularization. The low levels of these epitopes prevent immune responses and thus do not affect regenerative outcomes [56]. In contrast, tissues such as cartilage contain high amounts of proteoglycans and consequently more α-gal epitopes. This makes the removal of α-gal epitopes with decellularization more challenging. Even after extensive decellularization, the retention of α-gal epitopes in cartilage can still lead to immune rejection, potentially compromising the effectiveness of regenerative therapy [53, 56].

To overcome the immune rejection triggered by xenogeneic biomaterials, researchers have recently achieved breakthrough progress in the genetic editing of donor animals for xenotransplantation. In particular, clinical trials involving highly vascularized organs (such as the heart and kidneys) have provided crucial insights into graft immune tolerance. These highly vascularized organs face severe immunological challenges, typically necessitating multiplex gene editing to counteract the complex human immune defense system. For instance, in the cases of pig-to-human heart transplantations approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2022 and 2023 [36], as well as the pig-to-human kidney transplantation conducted in 2024 [58], the donor pigs underwent between 10 and 69 genetic modifications.

The Gal epitope, encoded by the GGTA1 gene, serves as the primary xenoantigen triggering hyperacute rejection (HAR) and is the core target for eliminating immediate post-transplantation rejection and prolonging graft survival. Furthermore, Neu5Gc (synthesized by CMAH) and Sda (synthesized by B4GALNT2) expressed on the surface of porcine cells are also crucial antigens recognized by human natural antibodies; their knockout can minimize the risk of post-transplantation HAR. Consequently, triple-gene knockout (3TKO) pigs targeting GGTA1, CMAH, and B4GALNT2 currently represent a mainstream strategy in genetic editing. Notably, although multiplex gene editing (e.g., the 3TKO approach) has become a predominant strategy for whole-organ transplantation, the confounding effects it introduces into specific tissues remain incompletely understood. Previous studies have demonstrated that in certain cell types (such as islet cells), the additional knockout of CMAH and B4GALNT2 (alongside GGTA1) did not lead to a significant reduction in antigen-specific antibody binding. This indicates a profound diminishing marginal utility regarding clinical benefits, while concurrently increasing the complexity of genetic modifications and potential biosafety risks. More importantly, the excessive ablation of carbohydrate antigens alters cell surface glycosylation patterns, which may expose previously masked porcine endogenous proteins (cryptic antigens), thereby creating novel targets for immune system recognition [57].

In overcoming the immunological barriers of xenotransplantation, the introduction of humanized regulatory proteins is equally crucial. Complement activation is a central pathway leading to graft damage; CD46, CD55, and CD59 act synergistically to protect xenogeneic cells from complement-mediated lysis [58, 59]. Additionally, to inhibit macrophage phagocytosis in the innate immune response, the introduction of humanized CD47 to activate the SIRP signaling pathway has emerged as a key strategy. However, the role of CD47 in xenotransplantation remains controversial. Its ubiquitous expression and complex signaling pathways can lead to unintended consequences, including altered cellular metabolism [60]. Heme oxygenase-1 (HO-1) is also considered an indispensable component of multiplex gene editing strategies due to its potent anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic properties, as well as its critical protective role in mitigating graft ischemia-reperfusion injury [36].

Although coordinated multiplex gene expression (e.g., TKO combined with hCD46/hCD55/hCD47) has demonstrated exceptional anti-rejection efficacy in complex organ transplantations, articular cartilage, by contrast, is a typical avascular tissue with relatively low immunogenicity. This avascular nature severely restricts the direct infiltration of immune cells and antibodies from the host’s systemic circulation. Given this specific physiological context, we opted for a strategy based on the single-gene editing of GGTA1 rather than blindly pursuing multiplex gene stacking, thereby minimizing the uncontrollable biological risks associated with excessive genetic modification.

In this study, we first characterized the immunological properties of PBMCs and cartilage tissue from α-Gal KO pigs based on their binding to IB4, human IgG and human IgM. Since gene knockout was performed during the embryonic stage, both PBMCs and cartilage tissues were expected to lack α-gal epitope expression. Subsequent experiments with PBMCs and cartilage tissues demonstrated the success of gene editing owing to observed reduction in their binding ability to IB4, human IgG and human IgM. Given the ethics issues surrounding human trials, we used a humanized mouse model and simulated the human immune responses to cartilage from α-Gal KO and WT pigs.Our preliminary findings confirm that, specifically for cartilage, single-gene editing of GGTA1 is sufficient to effectively enhance immunocompatibility.

In clinical translation applications, the direct transplantation of cartilage derived from humanized gene-edited pigs into human cartilage defect areas imposes stringent requirements for immediate donor tissue acquisition and precise matching. This strategy not only necessitates harvesting cartilage tissue from donor surgeries that closely matches the recipient’s defect morphology and dimensions to maintain joint surface congruity and prevent secondary post-traumatic osteoarthritis, but also requires coordinating two simultaneous surgical procedures, resulting in complex logistics and limited clinical feasibility. Furthermore, as a single humanized gene-edited pig represents a valuable biological resource with potential for multi-organ (e.g., heart, liver, kidney, and cornea) simultaneous transplantation, maximizing the medical value of a single donor necessitates coordinated utilization across various tissue and organ repair applications [61]. In contrast, processing porcine cartilage into particles and further constructing CORGs can not only flexibly adapt to cartilage defects of varying morphologies, reducing surgical coordination challenges and resource consumption, but also enable single-acquisition, multiple-use approaches that enhance overall treatment efficiency and resource utilization. This approach additionally allows for standardized storage and immediate availability of cartilage repair materials, thereby establishing a novel on-demand treatment model in clinical practice that is unconstrained by donor matching time limitations.

In this study, we attempted to construct low-immunogenicity CORGs using low-immunogenicity genetically modified porcine cartilage tissue as biomaterials. In contrast to decellularized WT porcine cartilage, the use of genetically edited porcine cartilage completely eliminates α-gal antigen epitopes that may trigger immune rejection. Additionally, using genetically edited porcine cartilage circumvents the biosafety risks associated with toxic chemical decellularizing agents, while preserving critical extracellular matrix components essential for cartilage regeneration and repair that are often compromised by harsh chemical treatments. Firstly, α-Gal KO pig cartilage-derived particles were prepared via physical pulverization of low-immunogenicity cartilage. Based on these particles, we constructed three-dimensional cellular aggregates and verified their chondrogenic phenotype through qPCR and IF assays, demonstrating the expression of cartilage-related markers and confirming the successful construction of CORGs. Finally, we subcutaneously implanted the CORGs into nude mice to validate their capacity for ectopic cartilage formation in vivo.

Both the α-Gal KO and WT groups formed translucent cartilaginous tissue subcutaneously. Through H&E staining, cartilage matrix-specific staining (Toluidine blue and Safranin O), and COL Ⅱ immunohistochemical staining, it was demonstrated that the constructed CORGs successfully formed cartilage tissue ectopically in vivo. Positive immunofluorescence staining for chondrogenic markers such as SOX9 and COL Ⅱ further confirmed the robust chondrogenic capacity of the CORGs in vivo. Via HuNu staining, we observed that in addition to the implanted human-derived cells, host-derived cells also participated in the formation of the CORGs. This suggests that the CORGs may recruit endogenous stem cells to participate in chondrogenesis and cartilage formation in vivo. The underlying mechanisms require further investigation to be fully elucidated.

In summary, these findings demonstrate that CORGs constructed from cartilage-derived particles can form new cartilaginous tissue in vivo. Utilizing low-immunogenicity humanized gene-edited porcine cartilage as the source biomaterials holds significant promise for achieving cartilage regeneration and repair. While our study revealed promising findings that have significant therapeutic potential, some challenges in translating animal-derived biomaterials into clinical settings remained due to immune rejection, such as chronic immune rejection leading to disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) [62–64]. However, we believe that with the continuous advancement of genetically engineered animal technologies, we will be able to obtain xenogeneic biomaterials that are immunologically non-rejectable by humans, ultimately achieving the status of being immunoprivileged.

Despite achieving encouraging results, several limitations remain, which provide direction for our future work. First, due to the limited observation window in animal models, we only validated host immune rejection responses at 7 days post-implantation. Longer-term in vivo studies are still required to fully assess immunogenicity before further translation. Second, the absence of humanized gene-edited large animal models precludes in situ repair test of the newly constructed organoids. However, in our previous experiments, CORGs constructed using the same methodology demonstrated excellent cartilage repair efficacy. Future studies will verify the immune response in primate models, while evaluating the stability, integration and matrix remodeling of CORGs derived from gene-edited porcine cartilage, in order to comprehensively assess their therapeutic efficacy.

As our understanding of tissue-specific immunogenicity deepens, future studies will further validate the long-term efficacy of multiplex gene editing strategies. Additionally, we plan to utilize immune-reconstituted animal models such as the RGD pig [65], to more faithfully mimic the response of the human immune environment to xenografts, thereby providing a more comprehensive theoretical foundation for the clinical translation of xenogeneic tissue-engineered products.

Conclusion

In this study, we first characterized the low immunogenicity of cartilage tissue from α-Gal KO pigs through in vitro and in vivo experiments. Subsequently, CDPs were prepared from this source and utilized to construct CORGs. Finally, subcutaneous implantation in nude mouse models demonstrated that these organoids can facilitate cartilage neogenesis in vivo, demonstrating promising potential for cartilage regeneration and repair.

In conclusion, this work established a proof-of-concept for “breaking the immune barrier” in cartilage tissue engineering.
