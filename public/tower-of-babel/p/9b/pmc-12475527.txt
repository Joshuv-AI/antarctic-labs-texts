Ultrasound-activated piezoelectric scaffolds target dual Ca2+/NF-κB signaling pathways to orchestrate immunomodulation and osteogenesis for accelerated bone regeneration
Authors: Hui Zheng, Pengfei Yan, Peng Liu, Chang Yan, Mengqi Zhao, Zuyong Wang, Xiaofei Zheng, Huajun Wang, Rongkai Zhang, Luke P Lee, Swee Hin Teoh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCritical-sized bone defects fail to heal due to dysregulated inflammation and impaired osteogenesis. To overcome the limitations of static biomaterials, we engineered 3D-printed polycaprolactone scaffolds incorporating polydopamine coated BaTiO3/β-TCP nanoparticles (10 %PBT) for dynamic ultrasound (US) activation. Integrated in vitro/in vivo analyses revealed that US-activated piezoelectricity triggered two synergistic pathways: (1) Voltage gated calcium channel (VGCC) dependent Ca2+ influx specifically in osteoblasts (inhibited by ω-Hexatoxin-HV1A), activating Ca2+/NFAT signaling and direct mineralization; and (2) Suppression of NF-κB p65 phosphorylation and nuclear translocation in macrophages, driving anti-inflammatory M2 polarization. Crucially, M2 macrophages secreted pro-regenerative factors (BMP-2, VEGF), enhancing osteoblast differentiation and angiogenesis via paracrine signaling. In rat critical defects, 10 %PBT + US achieved 3 fold higher bone volume (BV/TV), mature collagen, increased CD31+ vessels, and elevated Runx2/BMP-2 expression. This work unveils a paradigm of targeted electro immunoengineering: wireless US dynamically orchestrates VGCC mediated Ca2+ osteoinduction and NF-κB inhibited M2 polarization with BMP-2/VEGF secretion, providing potent, growth factor free spatiotemporal control of Ca2+ and inflammatory signaling for precision bone regeneration.Keywords: Bone regeneration, Piezoelectric materials, 3D printing, Macrophage regulation, Ultrasound stimulation

Introduction

Critical sized bone defects (>5 cm in humans) caused by trauma or pathological resection present formidable clinical challenges, with over 30 % of cases progressing to nonunion even with current interventions [1,2]. Despite advancements in clinical treatment, the morbidity and disability rates associated with large bone defects and non-union fractures severely impact patient quality of life [3]. Bone defect healing is one of the most complex physiological processes, requiring a series of precisely coordinated biological events, including initial immune modulation, angiogenesis, osteogenic differentiation, and biomineralization [4]. While autologous bone grafting remains the gold standard, its limitations are profound: donor site morbidity occurs in 20–30 % of cases [5]. Moreover, the presence of uncontrolled persistent inflammation, impaired osteogenesis/angiogenesis leading to interrupted oxygen supply, and oxidative stress (ROS) overload further complicate bone defect healing [6].Utilizing bone tissue engineering scaffold materials that can provide a microenvironment conducive to bone regeneration represents an effective alternative strategy. Most artificial bone materials have limited biological activity in regulating immune response and guiding endogenous cell recruitment, so the osteogenic effect is not ideal. In order to develop advanced bone biomaterials for enhancing bone regeneration, extensive research has been conducted to integrate immunomodulatory molecules such as interleukin (IL)-4, bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP-2), or vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) into artificial bone materials. To effectively regulate immune response, vascularization, and bone marrow mesenchymal stem cell (BMSC) recruitment [7,8]. While this is an attractive and highly effective growth faction-based therapy, the biologics used to date have serious side effects (such as inflammatory and immune responses, hyperosteogenesis, and ectopic osteogenesis) that hinder its further application [9]. Given the complexity of bone regeneration and the above dilemmas, these treatments have encountered difficulties in clinical practice, with unknown side effects, biosafety concerns, and limited biological activity (such as immunomodulation and osteogenesis), leading to multiple complications in patients [10]. In addition to the addition of traditional bioactive molecules, emerging research has found that multiple physiological stimuli, including mechanical, electrical, and magnetic stimulation, represent a new prospect for bone repair by influencing cell fate and biological processes associated with bone regeneration [11].Electrical signals play a crucial role in bone formation and fracture healing. The endogenous piezoelectric nature of bone tissue generates surface potentials (0.7–2.3 mV) under physiological loading through collagen fibril deformation, creating electrokinetic microenvironments that regulate osteogenic differentiation and matrix mineralization [12]. It is worth noting that electrical stimulation therapy has been used clinically as an adjunct to promote fracture healing, as it has been reported to promote the proliferation, migration, and differentiation of cells associated with bone formation, such as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCS) and bone progenitor cells, osteoblasts, and endothelial cells [13]. By combining externally applied physical stimuli with intrinsic cues from metals and scaffolds, the new strategy can synergistically modulate bone regeneration [14]. Piezoelectric biomaterials, such as poly-L-lactic acid(PLLA), collagen, silk, and potassium sodium niobate, can provide a physiological electrical microenvironment, enabling programmed electromechanical stimulation that enhances MSC osteogenesis and angiogenesis playing a significant role in enhancing metabolic activity [15,16]. The potential mechanisms through which electrical stimulation promotes osteogenesis include the upregulation of intracellular Ca2+ concentrations, the opening of voltage-gated Ca2+ channels, and the promotion of osteogenesis through upregulation of calmodulin signaling pathways [17]. Ultrasound Activation Mechanism of BaTiO3 Scaffolds Piezoelectric BaTiO3 nanoparticles embedded in PCL scaffolds undergo dipole reorientation under non-invasive ultrasound. Mechanical stress waves induce lattice distortion in BaTiO3's tetragonal perovskite structure, generating surface potentials of 0.7–2.3 mV mimicking endogenous bone piezoelectricity [18]. This transient electric field:1.Opens voltage-gated Ca2+ channels (VGCCs) on osteoblasts and macrophages.2.Depolarizes cell membranes (ΔΨ = −15 to −30 mV)0.3.Triggers Ca2+ dependent signaling cascades post stimulation [19],Therefore, piezoelectric materials are promising for implants, as they can convert, mechanical forces into electrical signals to provide local electrical stimulation to surrounding bone tissues without the need for surgically implanted electrodes. Barium titanate (BTO) was the first piezoelectric ceramic used in bone regeneration studies and has gained considerable attention as a lead free implant material with good biocompatibility. Studies have shown that BTO exhibits excellent biocompatibility, bioactivity, and osteogenic potential, with BTO containing composites demonstrating significant potential as bone substitutes both in vitro and in vivo [20] (see Scheme 1).

Local inflammation and the immune microenvironment strongly mediate bone homeostasis and fracture healing [21,22].Macrophages exhibit high plasticity and are typically classified into pro-inflammatory (M1) and anti-inflammatory (M2) subtypes based on their functions. Macrophages have been shown to respond to electric fields and enhance electrical conduction to promote healing in myocardial, skin, and bone tissues [20,23].Furthermore, in vivo electrical signals can modulate macrophage behavior, including migration, phagocytic activity, and cytokine production [24]. Based on these findings, many studies have focused on endowing new materials with the ability to regulate their interactions with macrophages to reduce inflammation and improve osteogenic propertie. However, few studies have examined the effects of electrical stimulation and piezoelectric scaffolds on macrophage-mediated bone repair.

Bone regeneration is a complex process involving angiogenesis and osteogenesis. In the initial phase of bone defect repair, the lack of blood vessels limits nutrient and oxygen supply, affecting cell proliferation and tissue regeneration. Since bone is a vascularized tissue, vascularization is crucial for its regeneration. Therefore, materials that can promote angiogenesis, in addition to inducing bone formation, are highly desirable for bone tissue engineering. 3D printing technology can create scaffolds with fixed dimensions and structures and fabricate composite materials with various properties [25].Constructing porous, smart piezoelectric scaffolds through 3D printing that match the size of large bone defects and facilitate vascularization is particularly important for bone regeneration.

Here, we propose the development of a scaffold that effectively combines osteoinduction with local immune modulation to achieve efficient bone regeneration.as schematically illustrated in Fig. 1. Composite scaffolds containing BTO are manufactured using melt extrusion 3D printing technology. Poly(ε-caprolactone) (PCL), a biodegradable polymer commonly used in 3D printing, serves as the printing material. The solvent free 3D printing process ensures homogeneous dispersion of β-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) within polycaprolactone/barium titanate (PBT) scaffolds, eliminating thermal hotspots while providing uniform bioactivity. Under ultrasound stimulation (1 MHz, 2.5 W/cm2), BTO nanoparticles generate localized piezoelectric fields (0.7–2.3 mV) that electrochemically accelerate β-TCP dissociation through interfacial water splitting: anodic reactions (2H2O→4H++O2+4e−) produce protons which attack the β-TCP lattice (Ca3(PO4)2+2H+→2CaHPO4+Ca2+), while cathodic reactions (2H2O+2e−→H2+2OH−) facilitate hydroxyl-mediated mineralization. This process is further enhanced by ultrasound cavitation, where microbubble collapse generates microstreaming shear forces that erode β-TCP surfaces. The resultant Ca2+ and PO43− ions undergo electromigration PO43− attracted to positively charged BTO regions and Ca2+ repelled into surrounding tissues—creating a sustained ionic flux that primes the microenvironment for rapid biomineralization. The PBT scaffolds exhibit significant piezoelectric properties. These scaffolds significantly alleviate inflammatory responses and promote angiogenesis in the local tissue environment, substantially accelerating cranial bone healing in SD rats. Thus, 3D printed PBT scaffolds show great promise as implant materials for the reconstruction of extensive bone defects.

Synthesis and characterization of BTO@PDA-TCP NPs

PDA surface modification of BTO particles: First, 0.1g of dopamine was dissolved in deionized water to obtain a solution with a concentration of 2.0 g/L. Secondly, 0.06 g tris is added to the above solution until the pH of the solution reaches 8.5. Then 6 g BTO particles were added to the above dopamine solution and mechanically stirred for 24h. Finally, the BTO particles modified with PDA solution were washed with distilled water and then dried in a vacuum oven at 45 °C. Dang et al. used sensitive surface plasmonic resonance (SPR) technology to monitor the formation process of PDA coating in real time under different pH values, initial dopamine concentration and deposition time. The results show that the coating thickness of PDA reaches the maximum when pH is 8.5 [26]. Therefore, we chose a pH of 8.5 for dopamine in the paper.

Preparation of BTO@PDA-TCP particles: Firstly, 0.64g β-TCP and 1g BTO@PDA particles were mixed in 800 mL deionized water at 50 °C for about 8 h to obtain BTO@PDA-TCP particles. Then the grafted BTO@PDA-TCP particles were washed with distilled water and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C. The morphology of NPs was observed by scanning electron microscope (TESCAN (Czech Republic) MIRA4 LMH). Its composition and crystal structure were analyzed by Energy dispersion spectrometer (EDS) (UltimMax 40, Oxford, UK) combined with Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Fisher, Nicolet 6700, USA) observed by SEM.

Fabrication and characterization of 3D printed scaffolds

PCL and NPs were fully mixed in an open mill (XH-401B Dongguan, China) to obtain PCL/NPs blend. The open mill temperature was set at 100 °C and the drum spacing was 4 mm. Using fused deposition molding (FDM) technology, the PCL/NPs blend was put into an FDM printer (Foshan MPB02, China) to obtain a 3D structured porous scaffold, which was then printed layer by layer by extruding the blend into fibers, building the original scaffold layer by layer. The scaffold is printed at 90° between two successive layers. The extrusion pressure is set to 30 bar and the printing speed is maintained at 40mm/s. The gap between adjacent layers in the z direction is about 0.1 mm. Print temperature set to 140 °C. According to the added NPs, PCL, PCL/5 %PBT and PCL/10 %PBT scaffolds were fabricated. For simplicity, PCL, PCL/5 %PBT, and PCL/10 %PBT scaffolds are represented below as PCL, 5 %PBT, and 10 %PBT scaffolds, respectively.

Characterization of piezoelectric scaffolds: Scanning electron microscopy (TESCAN (Czech Republic) MIRA4 LMH) was used to characterize the morphology of the piezoelectric scaffold, and energy dispersion spectrometer (UltimMax 40, Oxford, UK) was used to scan the distribution of Ca, Ba and Ti elements in the piezoelectric scaffold. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA, Q-50, USA) was used to assess the actual content of inorganic components in the composite scaffold by heating the sample in air from room temperature to 650 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. Water contact Angle is measured with an automatic contact Angle measuring instrument (SL200 A/B/D, Solon Tech. China). The volume of the drop is 1–2 μL. After the image is captured, the baseline and vertex of the bottom of the drop are found, and the value of the contact Angle is automatically calculated using the software provided by the instrument. Compression tests on these supports were carried out on a universal material testing machine (Instron 5500R, UK) equipped with a 500 N load cell. The beam speed is set at 1 mm/min and the total strain is set at 3 % of the height of the support. Polarization-electric field hysteresis loops were tested using a ferroelectric tester (RTI-MultiFerroic, Radiant, USA).

Biocompatibility study

Cell viability:The scaffolds with a side length of 5 mm and a height of 0.6 mm was soaked in 75 % ethanol for 6 h and sterilized by ultraviolet irradiation, and could be used after cleaning with PBS. According to ISO 10993 and GB/T 16886.5, L929 fibroblasts are the recommended cell line for biocompatibility assessment of in vivo implants (purchased from Procell). L929 was inoculated on different scaffolds at a density of 3 × 103 cells/scaffold, and CCK-8 detection was performed after 1, 3 and 5 days of culture. CCK-8 solution was added to each well (10 μl per well), and the cells were incubated at 37 °C and 5 % CO2 for another hour. The absorbance of the solution was recorded at 450 nm using an enzyme-labeled instrument (Thermo Field,USA). On days 1 and 3, cells on the culture plates were stained with a live and dead staining kit (Abbkine, China). Fluorescence images were captured with confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, Opera Phenix Plus, USA) to assess their live/dead status (green fluorescence: alive; Red fluorescence: dead). To assess apoptosis in the piezoelectric scaffold, L929 was harvested and then stained with Annexin V and propyl iodide (PI). Finally, the percentage of living cells in the different groups was measured by flow cytometry (FACSCelesta, Becton, Dickinson, USA). To further evaluate cell proliferation in the piezoelectric scaffold, EdU staining was performed using the Abbkine KTA2030 kit.

Cell adhesion:L929 was inoculated on different scaffolds at a density of 3 × 103 cells/scaffold, cultured for 3 days and then stained with nucleus and F-actin. The method is: The cells on the scaffold were first fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde solution (Solarbio, China) for 30min, washed with PBS, permeated with 0.1 %Triton X-100 for 15 min, then closed with 1 %BSA solution for 1h, and FITC-labeled Sigma-Aldrich was added. F-actin was stained at room temperature for 1 h, and then DAPI was added to stain the nucleus. The cytoskeleton was observed by CLSM.

In vitro hemolysis rate: Blood compatibility was studied by acute hemolysis test, which was performed in different scaffold sample extracts. In brief, 1 ml of fresh anticoagulant whole blood (taken from SD rats) was suspended with 10 ml of PBS to obtain a uniform suspension. After incubating the prepared scaffold extract with the above-mentioned diluted blood suspension at 37 °C for 1 h, the sample was removed and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The 540 nm absorbance of the supernatant was measured and the hemolysis ratio was calculated.

The formula is: In vitro hemolysis ratio (%) = (H1-H0)/(H2-H0) × 100 %, where H0, H1 and H2 represent the absorbance values of the negative control (PBS),sample, and positive control (deionized water), respectively.

Osteoblast cell isolation

Suckling mouse were sterilized in 75 % ethanol for 5 min. After decapitation, calvaria were dissected, stripped of adherent fibrous tissues, and rinsed repeatedly with PBS. The bones were minced into 2–5 mm2 fragments, digested with 0.25 % trypsin for 30 min at 37 °C, and centrifuged (1000×g, 5 min). Supernatants were discarded, and pellets were further digested with 0.1 % collagenase type I for 1 h at 37 °C. Cell suspensions were centrifuged (1000×g, 5 min) and plated for subsequent experiments.

Angiogenesis assay using chicken embryo chorioallantoic membrane model

Fertilized chicken embryos were pre-incubated at 37 °C with 60 % humidity for 3 days. Then, carefully open the egg's air chamber to expose the allantoic membrane. To assess the formation of blood vessels, various scaffolds are placed on the allantoic membrane. Close the egg with a sealing film and incubate for another 3 days. Visualized on a stereo microscope (VISION, TY10Mantis Elite, Korea). AngioQuant software (version 1.33, Mathwork Inc, Houston, Texas, USA) quantitatively analyzed the tubular length, size and total number of connections of the formed vessels.

In vitro immunomodulation of macrophages

In the study, murine-derived macrophage cell line RAW 264.7 was provided by Procell. RAW 264.7 cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10 % FBS and 1 % P/S solution in an incubator at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. Change the medium once a day.

First, immunofluorescence was used to observe the morphological changes of macrophages co-cultured on different scaffolds. In short, after 2 days of co-culture, the sample was stained with F-actin and F4/80 and photographed using confocal microscopy. Next, for phenotypic polarization, surface markers of M1 macrophages (CD86) and M2 macrophages (CD206) were detected using flow cytometry. RAW 264.7 cells were first treated with lipopolysaccharide (LPS)50 ng/ml for 24 h as a simple in vitro stimulation of the inflammatory microenvironment during bone healing. The harvested cells were then inoculated to each sample in a 6-well tissue culture plate at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After 2 days of culture, the cells were digested and collected with trypsin, and then closed with 1 % bovine serum albumin (BSA) for 1h. After two rinsions with PBS, surface antigen staining was performed for 30 min using FITC-coupled CD86 (105005, BioLegend, China) and PE-coupled CD206 (141705, BioLegend, China). The treated cells were analyzed by flow cytometry (BD, USA) using FlowJo software (Tree Star Inc, USA). For immunofluorescence staining, after incubation for 2 days, cells were fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde, permeated with 0.5 % Triton X-100, and sealed with 1 % BSA. After that, antibodies against iNOS (Abcam, ab210823) were incubated with mouse monoclonal antibodies and then incubated with Cy3-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies (Servicebio, GB21303). The nuclei were then re-stained with DAPI and observed through confocal microscopy. For M2 markers, the same method was used to stain CD206 (Invitrogen, PA5-101657) antibodies. Finally, the stained cells were observed by CLSM. The polarization of macrophages was analyzed by immunofluorescence staining and qPCR. In addition, gene expression levels of the M1 surface marker (iNOS,TNF-α) and M2 surface marker (CD206,Arg-1) were detected by qPCR.

The secretion levels of bone forming factor BMP-2 and VEGF in macrophages were observed by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay. After 2 days of co-culture, the supernatant of each group was collected according to the previously reported protocol for further study. The concentrations of cell-secreted IL-1β(Bioss bsk12015), IL-10(Bioss bsk12007), BMP-2 (Fine Test EM0874) and VEGF (Fine Test EM0205) in the collected supernatants were measured using a commercially available ELISA kit.

In vitro evaluation of angiogenesis

For the Transwell chamber assay, HUVEC were inoculated in the upper chamber. Macrophage culture medium was added to the lower chamber. The migrating cells on the lower surface were stained with 0.1 % crystal violet solution for observation. For the scratch test, a straight scratch is formed when the HUVEC reaches 90 % confluence. Macrophage culture medium was added to stimulate HUVEC migration. HUVEC were immobilized and then migration observed under an inverted microscope.

Angiogenesis tests were measured with HUVEC in different macrophage cultures to induce angiogenesis. In short, HUVEC were inoculated in a matrix gel (Matrigel, 356262, Corning, USA) and incubated with different macrophage supernatants. The tubular structure was then observed and quantitatively analyzed. In addition, after 7 days of culture, immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect new blood vessel formation, and the expression of VEGF and angiopoietin-1 (Ang-1) was measured by RT-qPCR. In vivo bone defect repair capacity

The animal experiment was approved by the Animal Protection and Use Committee of Hunan University (No: HNU-IACUC-2024-114). To establish the animal model of skull bone defect injury, firstly, the rats were anesthetized by inhaling isoflurane (R510-22) through a gas anesthesia machine (Rayward, china) at 2L/min. The skin was dissected after disinfection, and then two critical-size full-thickness bone defects (5 mm diameter) were created on both sides of the sagittal suture of the parietal bone in each rat using a saline-cooled small animal skull trephine. A scaffold was implanted to fill the defect, and a 3-0 suture suture was used to close the wound. The entire skull was collected for evaluation at 4 and 8 weeks after implantation. Twenty-four male rats (200–220g) were randomly divided into control group, PCL group, 5 %PBT group and 10 %PBT group. Three days after surgery, ultrasound stimulation (WED-101,China) was given at 1MHZ,2.5W/cm−2 every other day for 5 min until sampling. At 4 and 8 weeks after operation, the rats were killed with CO2, and the whole skull was scanned by microCT system (NEMO, NMC-200, China) with the following Settings: voltage, 80 kV; Current, 0.06 mA, scanning accuracy 35 μm. Bone tissue volume/total tissue volume (BV/TV) and bone mineral density (BMD) were analyzed based on reconstructed microCT images. After CT scan, the samples were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde at 4 °C for 48 h, and then immersed in 10 % EDTA at 37 °C for about 4 weeks of decalcification. After embedding it in paraffin, paraffin sections with a thickness of 5 μm were prepared. In order to evaluate the local inflammatory response after implantation, iNOS and CD206 were selected as specific markers for double-label immunofluorescence staining to observe the phenotype of infiltrating macrophages. Immunofluorescence staining of pro-angiogenic factor VEGF/CD31 and bone differentiation promoting factor bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP-2), Runx2 and COLІ was further performed to evaluate the effects of piezoelectric scaffolds on bone vascularization and osteogenic differentiation during bone healing. Immunohistochemical staining for α-smooth muscle actin (a-SMA) was performed to evaluate the effect of the piezoelectric scaffold on fibrosis during bone healing. Finally, histological analysis was performed. Tissue samples were sectioned and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE) to assess bone repair capacity. After that, Masson three-color staining was performed to observe the maturity of regenerated bone tissue in the defect area. For Masson tricolor staining, red areas are treated as mineralized collagen (mature bone matrix), while blue areas are treated as non-mineralized collagen (immature bone matrix). All stained samples were imaged microscopically and semi-quantitatively by the ImageJ software.

Transcriptome analysis

RNA sequencing was performed to study gene expression profiles of macrophages cultured on different scaffolds. In short, RAW 264.7 macrophages (5 × 106 cells/ml) were co-cultured on different scaffold samples in a 6-well plate for 2 days. The total RNA of each group of macrophages was then collected using the TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer's instructions. The RNA samples were stored at −80 °C before sequencing. Statistical analysis

All experiments in this study were conducted at least three times. All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and charted using Origin 2018 software (Origin Lab Corporation, USA). Analysis was performed using the Student t-test (unpaired and two-tailed), one-way or two-way ANOVA, and then Tukey post hoc analysis. For two groups, unpaired t-test with Bonferroni correction when appropriate. P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Fabrication and characterization of PBT

The surface of BTO NPs is modified with polydopamine to prevent aggregation in the polymer matrix. A thin amorphous coating of about 3 nm can be observed on the surface of BTO NPs (Fig. 2A). The thickness of BTO modified by PDA coating is significantly higher than that of the original BTO nanoparticles, which proves the successful synthesis of BTO@PDA nanoparticles. Because the PDA layer is negatively charged, the zeta potential of BTO NPs decreases with the coating of PDA, while TCP is positively charged. The presence of TCP NPs leads to the increase of zeta potential of BTO@PDA-TCP, and the change of potential of these materials directly reflects the step modification process of BTO NPs (Fig. 2B). Fig. 2C shows TEM images of BTO@PDA-TCP nanoparticles. A large number of point-like particles are distributed on the surface of TCP, indicating that BTO can be deposited on the surface of TCP nanoparticles through the PDA layer. EDS element mapping also proves the successful modification of BTO@PDA-TCP (Fig. 2C). The functional groups (Fig. 2D) on the composite nanoparticle samples were further analyzed by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, and the sharp bands observed at 1258 cm−1 were attributed to the C-O bond in the PDA, indicating that the BTO surface had been successfully coated with PDA. A wide and strong band appears at about 961 cm−1, which is attributed to the PO43−stretching vibration pattern derived from β-TCP [27].X-ray diffraction (XRD) shows that compared with BTO, the characteristic diffraction peaks exist at 27.7°and 34.3°, corresponding to the crystal plane diffraction peaks of TCP(Fig. 2E). These results indicate that BTO@PDA-TCP nanocomposites have been synthesized successfully. Three-dimensional (3D) printing has been widely used to manufacture scaffolds with customizable shapes and internal structures in bone tissue engineering [28]. Poly (ε-caprolactone) (PCL) is biodegradable, non-toxic, has a low melting point (conducive to forming during manufacturing), and is suitable for bone tissue regeneration, which is widely used in tissue engineering [29]. We used fused 3D printing to construct a porous PCL/BTO@PDA-TCP(PBT) as a bone repair scaffold material. The temperature of the liquefier was set at 140 °C. In this study, 5 % and 10 % mass percentage BTO@PDA-TCP were used to make the PBT scaffold, and 10 %PBT scaffold showed the best mechanical properties (Fig. S1A, Supporting Information). The incorporation of different NPs was verified by TGA. The results show that the weight residual fraction of PCL, 5 %PBT and 10 %PBT brackets after thermal decomposition is 1.01 wt %, 5.3 wt % and 10.1 wt %, respectively (Fig. 2F). We evaluated the piezoelectric properties of the polarized scaffold by exposing it to cyclic loads and measured the piezoelectric constant d33. Fig. 2G shows the piezoelectric response of different scaffolds. With the increase of BaTiO3 content, the d33 piezoelectric coefficient of PBT composite increases. It is noteworthy that compared with the unmodified PCL sample, the piezoelectric response of BaTiO3 inclusions up to 5 vol% is gradually increased to 2.4 pC/N. However, when the BaTiO3 exceeds 10 wt%, the piezoelectric response increases significantly to 2.8 pC/N(Fig. S3, Supporting Information). The device derived from the PBT piezoelectric substrate outputs 80 mV voltage when exposed to mechanical stimulation, indicating that the PBT-based substrate has a high electromechanical response sensitivity(Fig. S4A, Supporting Information). The P-E measurement result also confirmed ferroelectricity (Fig. S4B, Supporting Information). Several previous reports have observed similar results with BaTiO3 [30].Due to the close packing density of piezoelectric BaTiO3 particles in the sintered sample, some HA-BaTiO3 composites exhibit very high d33 values (>50 pC/N). The PBT composite scaffold in the study would never exhibit such a high piezoelectric response because the BaTiO3 particles are not densely packed in the polymer matrix. Furthermore, bone regeneration does not require such a high scaffold-mediated piezoelectric response, as the bone itself exhibits a piezoelectric response [31]. The d33 value of the 3D-printed PBT composite in this study is close to the piezoelectric response of bone. Therefore, the piezoelectric response of PBT composites observed in this study highlights its great potential in bone remodeling processes and osteogenesis, as well as in accelerating the treatment of orthopedic defects. In addition, water contact Angle tests were performed on all printed scaffolds (Fig. 2H), where the water contact Angle of scaffolds made of hydrophobic PCL was >90°, which decreased with the addition of inorganic NP. It is generally believed that the matrix on hydrophilic surfaces is more conducive to cell attachment and growth than that on hydrophobic smooth surfaces [32]. Next, we evaluated the in vitro degradation characteristics of PBT. The degradation rate was assessed by placing the scaffold in 1M sodium hydroxide and calculating the weight loss at specified time points. The addition of BTO increased the degradation rate of PBT, which might be due to the enhanced hydrophilicity of BTO (Fig. S1B, Supporting Information). It is noteworthy that the increase in degradation rate did not lead to the rapid collapse of the scaffold structure. Our results indicate that by the 60th day, the scaffold still maintained a distinct structure. As shown in the corresponding SEM and EDS element mapping images, the printed wires are stacked to form a porous structure, displaying uniform Ca, Ba, and Ti signals similar to the wire morphology (Fig. 2I). Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show the microstructure of PBT (Fig. S2, Supporting Information).

Biocompatibility of piezoelectric scaffold

The US can serve as an external pressure source to remotely activate the BTO nanoparticles, and the cavitation effect of the US waves enhances the electrical output of the piezoelectric microspheres [13].US will cause significant thermal effects. To prevent local tissue damage, we conducted strict in vitro temperature measurements and real-time temperature monitoring during the ultrasound stimulation process. The results confirmed that the heat was extremely small (temperature difference ΔT = 0.9 ± 0.2 °C) (Fig. S5A, Supporting Information). To reach the pressure threshold required for the electrical output of the piezoelectric microsphere, we chose 2.5 W cm2 as the application intensity of US. This intensity is lower than the FDA diagnostic limit (3 W/cm2) and previous studies [33]. The live/dead staining and CCK-8 results also indicated that the group with an intensity of 2.5 W/cm2 had the best cell survival rate (Fig. S5C, Supporting Information). The US with a frequency of 1 MHz demonstrated potential therapeutic effects and high penetration ability [13],And the influx of calcium ions reached its peak at 1 MHz (Fig. S5B,Supporting Information). The selected parameters achieved a good balance in terms of efficacy, safety, and piezoelectric specificity. We investigated the cellular compatibility of the piezoelectric scaffold by measuring the survival, proliferation and adhesion of L929 cells. Before the cell culture experiment, all scaffolds were soaked in alcohol and sterilized with UV for 6 h. Exploring the cellular compatibility of the piezoelectric scaffolds using live/dead cell staining, L929 grew well on all scaffolds (emitting green fluorescence) and adhered tightly to the surface of the scaffolds (Fig. 3A), thus indicating excellent biocompatibility of all scaffolds. The cytocompatibility of the piezoelectric scaffold with L929 cells was measured by the CCK8 kit, and the number of L929 cells increased significantly with the prolongation of cell culture time (Fig. 3B), indicating that no cytotoxicity was generated after the introduction of BTO and TCP. Normal cells and early apoptotic cells have intact cell membranes, and PI, as a nucleic acid dye, cannot penetrate the intact cell membranes. In apoptotic and necrotic cells, PI can cross the cell membrane and bind to the nucleus. PI was labeled with Annexin V-FITC to distinguish apoptotic, necrotic and normal cells. In the bivariate flow cytometry scatter plot, the first quadrant is necrotic or late apoptotic cells. The third quadrant showed normal cells, and the fourth quadrant showed early apoptotic cells. The sum of the number of cells in the first and fourth quadrants was related to the apoptosis rate. Quantitative apoptosis assessment by flow cytometry showed that L929 in all groups had a high proportion of living cells (>95 %) (Fig. 3C), indicating that the piezoelectric scaffold had good biocompatibility and little cytotoxicity to L929. The addition of BTO and TCP had no adverse effect on the biocompatibility of piezoelectric scaffolds, but promoted the proliferation and survival of cells. The EdU staining results of L929 after 24h co-culture on various scaffolds, as shown in Fig. 3D, verified the significant improvement in cell proliferation brought about by the addition of BTO and TCP. Compared with pure PCL group, the proliferation rate of cells cultured on piezoelectric group was higher, especially in 10 %PBT group. In order to observe the adhesion and extension behavior of L929 cells on the piezoelectric scaffold, the adhesion of L929 cells on the piezoelectric scaffold was studied by immunofluorescence staining. L929 in the pure PCL group was fusiform (Fig. 3E). In contrast, the cells in the 5 %PBT and 10 %PBT groups were polygonal in shape, with filamentous pseudopods, and the cytoskeleton and cell clusters were more developed, indicating strong adhesion. Piezoelectricity can help cells enter the differentiation stage quickly and increase the cell proliferation rate [34].Together, these results suggest that the prepared piezoelectric scaffold can promote the adhesion and proliferation of L929 cells. This may be because the piezoelectric scaffold contains cell adhesion PDAs, in addition, the improved surface hydrophilicity as well as the charged substrate surface help to absorb proteins and various bioactive molecules from the medium through electrostatic interactions, thus further promoting cell adhesion, proliferation, and differentiation [35].This conclusion has also been confirmed in osteoblasts(Fig. S6,Supporting Information).Artificial bone regeneration scaffold materials used in clinical applications inevitably come into contact with blood, so the blood compatibility of piezoelectric scaffolds is studied by testing their hemolytic activity. Both optical images and quantitative analysis of hemolysis rate showed similar results as the negative control group (PBS)(Fig. 3F), indicating a safe hemolysis rate (less than 5 %) [36]. And the BCI rate exceeds 80 % (Fig. S7, Supporting Information). This good blood compatibility is mainly attributed to the nanoparticle PDA coating. Studies have shown that PDA coating has been shown to have a positive effect on reducing the hemolytic activity of biomaterials [37].These results show that the prepared piezoelectric scaffold material is non-hemolysis and compatible with blood cells.

Macrophage Polarization of Piezoelectric scaffold Regulation In Vitro

In the process of bone repair, inflammation, oxidative stress, angiogenesis, osteogenesis and other processes are closely related, and the first stage of immune response after scaffold implantation is crucial for bone tissue repair. A sustained immune response during bone repair can delay bone regeneration and eventually lead to non-union. In general, disruption of this inflammatory cascade directly impedes stem cell proliferation and differentiation, thus hindering subsequent repair processes [38]. In response to bone injury, the early response of innate immune effector cells (especially macrophages) comes into play [39]. The timely transition from pro-inflammatory M1-like macrophages to pro-regenerative M2-like macrophages represents a critical stage in normal bone regeneration [40]. In addition, the transformation of macrophage phenotype during inflammation affects a series of subsequent events that determine the fate of scaffold implantation. To study the immunomodulatory properties of piezoelectric scaffolds. Fig. 4A is immunofluorescence staining of F-actin and F4/80 in RAW 264.7 murine-derived macrophage cell line on a piezoelectric scaffold. F-actin and F4/80 double-standard immunofluorescence images showed that macrophages adhered to the surface of the piezoelectric scaffold. The cells in the control group were fried eggs with more filamentous pseudopodia, and the macrophages in the 5 %PBT and 10 %PBT groups were fusiform. Macrophages in the PCL and control groups showed M1-like morphology and large spread area, while macrophages in the 5 % PBT and 10 % PBT groups showed fuses, which is considered to be a typical M2 phenotype. Through its immunomodulatory effect, the macrophages in the piezoelectric scaffold group were more easily polarized into M2 macrophages than those in the non-piezoelectric scaffold group. At the same time, the number of CD206+ M2 macrophages in the 5 %PBT group and the 10 %PBT group increased compared with the control group and the PCL group. The results of the immunofluorescence assay indicated that the number of CD206+ M2-type macrophages increased in the piezoelectric group (Fig. 4B). Flow cytometry was used to evaluate the polarization of macrophages. Flow cytometry results showed that the piezoelectric scaffold significantly decreased the expression of surface marker CD86 in M1 macrophages and enhanced the expression of surface marker CD206 in M2 macrophages. The expression of CD86 in macrophages of control group and PCL group was higher than that of 5 %PBT group and 10 %PBT group (Fig. 4C). The mRNA expression levels of polarimetric genes (including CD206,Arg-1,TNF-α and INOS) in macrophages were measured by qRT-PCR. Similar to the staining results, M2 macrophage gene expression levels in 5 %PBT group and 10 %PBT group were higher than those in CON group and PCL group (Fig. 4D). In addition, the contents of IL-1β, IL-10, BMP-2 and VEGF in the supernatant of macrophages were detected by ELISA. The secretion of IL-1β, IL-10, BMP-2 and VEGF indicated that the electric microenvironment enhanced the regulatory effect and accelerated the secretion of pro-regenerative cytokines by macrophages (Fig. 4E). These results suggest that the electrical signal and microenvironment provided by the piezoelectric scaffold can regulate immunity, promote the polarization of M2 macrophages and the secretion of pro-healing cytokines by macrophages, and promote bone tissue and blood vessel regeneration through paracrine mechanism. Through targeted co-culture blocking experiments, we conclusively demonstrate that BMP-2 and VEGF secreted by M2-polarized macrophages are indispensable factors for piezoelectric scaffold-enhanced osteogenesis and angiogenesis. The functional neutralization of these factors (via anti-BMP-2/VEGF antibodies) abolished of the osteogenic activity and angiogenic capacity (Fig. S8, Supporting Information), effectively reversing the pro-regenerative effects of M2-conditioned media. This paracrine axis, orchestrated through piezoelectricity-driven immunomodulation, establishes macrophage-mediated cytokine secretion as the central mechanism underlying biomaterial-enabled bone repair.

Piezoelectric scaffold promotion angiogenesis in vitro

To investigate the chemotactic response of macrophage supernatant to HUVEC(Fig. 5A), Transwell migration and cell scratch tests were performed (Fig. 5B and C). The supernatant promoted HUVEC migration and infiltration, and the HUVEC migration capacity in the piezoelectric group was higher than that in the control group (P < 0.05), The supernatant of macrophages has a positive effect on cell migration, possibly because the piezoelectric scaffold can promote the secretion of VEGF factor by M2 macrophages(Fig. 4E). The 10 %PBT piezoelectric scaffold was the most effective in promoting HUVEC migration and infiltration. The ability of blood vessels to form after bone injury plays a crucial role in the reconstruction of bone defect tissue, as they provide blood, nutrients, and oxygen to the damaged tissue and relieve the uncontrolled inflammatory response at the damaged site [41]. VEGF gene is a gene related to angiogenesis, and its expression profile is closely related to angiogenesis [42,43]. After 1 week of co-culture, VEGF immunofluorescence staining was further performed to evaluate the angiogenesis of the piezoelectric scaffold. When HUVEC was co-cultured with macrophage supernatant, VEGF expression in 5 %PBT and 10 %PBT groups was significantly up-regulated compared with the control group and PCL group (Fig. 5D), suggesting that the piezoelectric scaffold had good angiogenic bioactivity in vitro. In addition, we used the chicken embryo allantoic membrane (CAM) model to assess the vascular formation situation [44,45].The three scaffolds were placed directly on the allantoic membrane and photographs were taken to examine the formed blood vessels that invaded the area where the scaffold was located. As shown in Fig. 5E, Based on these images, a quantitative analysis of blood vessel length, size, branching index and number of connections was performed using ImageJ. The most abundant angiogenesis was observed in 10 %PBT scaffolds, followed by 5 %PBT scaffolds, while only limited angiogenesis was observed in all PCL scaffolds (Fig. 5E,Fig.S9, Supporting Information).HUVEC in the piezoelectric scaffold group showed more blood vessels, and some incomplete tubular structures were observed in the PCL group, suggesting that piezoelectric scaffolds binding to macrophages can promote angiogenesis in vitro(Fig. 5F). The results of qRT-PCR analysis were consistent with those of immunofluorescence staining. As shown in Fig. 5G, the expression levels of VEGF and Ang-1 in HUVEC in 10 %PBT group were significantly higher than those in control group and PCL group. Therefore, the piezoelectric scaffold can effectively promote angiogenesis by regulating the immune microenvironment. These data indicate that piezoelectric scaffolders can provide an excellent electrical microenvironment for bone vascularization. It has shown great potential for bone angiogenesis in bone regeneration.

Piezoelectric scaffold promotion osteogenesis in vitro

In order to verify the hypothesis that piezoelectric scaffolds induced macrophages to polarize and secrete osteogenic factors to promote osteogenesis (Fig. 6A), MC3T3-E1 cells (MC3T3s) and osteoblasts cultured in M2 macrophage-conditioned media were analyzed at key differentiation stages: ALP activity (Day 7) and mineralization (Day 21). ALP, as an important osteoblastic marker of cell maturation and calcification, is widely accepted as an indicator of early differentiation of osteoblasts [46], The expression intensity of ALP in 5 %PBT and 10 %PBT groups was the highest, followed by PCL and con groups (Fig. 6B,Fig.S10B, Supporting Information). Multiple studies have reported that piezoelectric BaTiO3 improves biocompatibility in polymer matrices, playing a crucial role in promoting ALP activity and matrix mineralization [47]. In addition, calcium deposition and collagen secretion are also the building blocks of bone formation, which can be measured by alizarin red (ARS) staining. Corresponding to ALP staining, a significantly positive staining of alizarin red ARS (Fig. 6C) was detected in cells cultured in both the 5 %PBT group and the 10 %PBT group, indicating significant calcium nodules during osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3s. The improvement of osteogenic activity of MC3T3s is mainly due to the secretion of osteoblast-related cytokines by M2 macrophages. To further investigate the effect of macrophage supernatant on MC3T3s and osteoblasts osteogenesis, mRNA expression levels of osteogenesis related genes, including Runx2 and BMP-2, were determined using qRT-PCR. Similar to the staining results, 5 %PBT group and 10 %PBT group showed higher levels of bone gene expression than CON group and PCL group (Fig. 6D). Subsequently, the expression levels of Runx2 and BMP-2 in MC3T3 cells and osteoblasts were detected by immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 6E, F, Fig.S10C, D, Supporting Information). The expressions of Runx2 and BMP-2 in MC3T3s and osteoblasts in 5 %PBT group and 10 %PBT group were significantly higher than those in CON group and PCL group. Taken together, these findings suggest that piezoelectric scaffolding significantly promotes early osteogenic differentiation and mineralization of MC3T3s and osteoblasts by regulating the immune microenvironment, which can be considered as a therapeutic platform for accelerating bone regeneration. Calcium ion (Ca2+) related signaling pathways are crucial for the osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3s induced by ultrasound-activated piezoelectric scaffolds (Fig. 6G). Therefore, the intracellular concentration of Ca2+ was measured. Fig. 6H shows that the intracellular Ca2+ concentration significantly increased in the piezoelectric scaffold group treated with ultrasound(US). This indicates that the piezoelectric potential generated by the ultrasound activated piezoelectric scaffolds promoted Ca2+ influx. We demonstrated that the piezoelectric signal generated by ultrasound activation triggered Ca2+ influx, which was subsequently transduced through calcium related membrane receptors. Among the potential calcium related receptors on the membrane, Voltage-Gated Calcium Channels (VGCCs),Piezo-type mechanosensitive ion channel component 1 (Piezo1), and Transient Receptor Potential Canonical 1 (TRPC1) are well-known to receive physical signals and induce calcium influx [48].To explore which channel plays a key role in piezoelectric stimulation, inhibitors were used to block each of the three channels mentioned above. incubation with inhibitors for Piezo1 and TRPC1 (GSMTx4) did not affect calcium influx or the osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3s (Fig. 6I,J,K). Conversely, Blocking VGCCs with ω-Hexatoxin-HV1A (HV1A) significantly inhibited calcium influx and suppressed the osteogenic differentiation of MC3T3s in the PBT + US group (Fig. 6L,M,N). The above results indicate that VGCCs are the key channel transducing the piezoelectric signal into intracellular calcium signaling.

Evaluation of bone regeneration capacity in vivo

In terms of biological properties, piezoelectric scaffolds significantly enhanced osteogenesis through a variety of biochemical and biophysical properties, and effectively induced pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype transition. To further evaluate the role of piezoelectric scaffolds in bone repair, Critical-sized calvarial defects (5 mm diameter) were created in 24 Sprague-Dawley rats (male, 200 ± 20 g) randomized into four groups (n = 6): (1) Blank control, (2) PCL,(3)5 %PBT,(4)10 %PBT (Fig. 7A,Fig.S11, Supporting Information) [40]. scaffolds (PCL, 5 %PBT, and 10 %PBT) were implanted at the defect site. The rats that did not receive any scaffold were the blank control group. Micro-CT showed that the healing effect of the 10 %PBT group was the best at 4 and 8 weeks after surgery, which exceeded that of the control group and the PCL group (Fig. 7B). The data showed that more new bone was formed after implantation of the piezoelectric scaffold compared with the control group and the PCL group. Sagittal microCT analysis further confirmed that the piezoelectric scaffold had relatively high bone tissue regeneration ability(Fig. S12, Supporting Information).At 8 weeks after implantation, BV/TV in the 10 %PBT group was significantly higher than that in the PCL and CON groups. The site where the 10 %PBT piezoelectric scaffold was implanted also showed more mature bone with a higher density. The BMD data showed the same trend as the BV/TV results. Critical-sized rat calvarial defects treated with ultrasound-activated 10 %PBT scaffolds exhibited significantly accelerated bone regeneration.This enhancement directly correlated with ultrasound-triggered ionic release. Released Ca2+ and PO43− ions supersaturate local fluids, accelerating hydroxyapatite (HA) nucleation. Dissolved ions adsorb onto the scaffold, forming a CaP rich layer that guides new bone growth. In summary, the 10 %PBT piezoelectric scaffold demonstrated better osteogenic behavior in cell experiments, and its enhanced ability to promote healing bone reconstruction was also validated in rat models. In order to further study the effect of piezoelectric scaffold on skull defect repair, H&E staining and Masson tri-color staining test were used for histological evaluation. H&E staining image analysis revealed more new bone plates and islands in the 10 %PBT group (Fig. 7C). The red stain in Masson tri-color stain represents mineralized collagen and mature bone matrix, while the blue stain represents non-mineralized collagen and immature bone matrix. In this study, the 10 %PBT group showed rich red staining and more collagen deposition, indicating that the regenerative skull of the piezoelectric group was calcifying and most of it had matured (Fig. 7D). In addition, similar results were obtained by COL1A1 immunofluorescence staining of bone tissue (Fig. S13, Supporting Information). In order to evaluate osteogenesis in vivo, Runx2 and BMP2 immunofluorescence staining were further performed on bone tissues, and the results showed that the proportion of positive cells in the 10 %PBT group was significantly higher than that in the control group (Fig. 7E and F), indicating that the piezoelectric scaffold could accelerate the expression of osteogenesis related genes. The expression of these proteins can continuously activate osteogenesis and ECM deposition. These results suggest that piezoelectric scaffolds can promote bone regeneration and collagen deposition in vivo.

In further experiments, we explored the polarization of macrophages and neovascularization in bone defects. Macrophage infiltration was detected by tissue immunofluorescence. It was observed that the expression of CD206 in the skull tissue of the 10 %PBT group was higher than that of the control group, while the expression of iNOS in the 10 %PBT group was lower than that of the control group (Fig. 8A). This result was the same as in vitro experiment, indicating that M2 type macrophage infiltration near the bone defect increased after implantation of the piezoelectric scaffold. As mentioned earlier, VEGF is not only an indicator of vascular endothelial cell function, but also a mediator of angiogenesis osteogenic coupling. Immunohistochemical fluorescence staining showed that VEGF expression was the highest in 10 %PBT group (Fig. 8C). Subsequently, tissue immunofluorescence staining was performed to measure the expression of CD31, which is expressed at the close junction between endothelial cells. CD31, as a marker of H-type blood vessels, is the main blood vessel involved in osteogenesis, and most bone progenitor cells were selectively located near H-type blood vessels [49].The results showed that the expression of CD31 was highest in the 10 %PBT group (Fig. 8B).The immunohistochemical results of a-SMA showed that only a slight degree of fibrosis was observed in the 10 % PBT group, thereby confirming the long-term biocompatibility. Together, these findings suggest that piezoelectric scaffolders can promote bone defect repair in vivo because of its ability to modulate immune invasion and vascular regeneration. Favorable immune microenvironment not only enhances osteogenic differentiation, but also promotes the formation of new blood vessels. The newly formed blood vessels provide the nutrients needed to maintain osteogenic differentiation and osteoblast proliferation.

Molecular mechanism by which the piezoelectric scaffolds promote bone regeneration

After in vivo and in vitro experiments, the molecular mechanism of polarization of macrophages by piezoelectric scaffold was determined by RNA sequencing. A total of 11312 differentially expressed genes (DEG) were obtained between the 10 %PBT piezoelectric scaffold group and the CON group, of which 130 up-regulated genes and 393 down-regulated genes were detected (Fig. 9A). The clustering results of differentially expressed genes are shown in Fig. 9B. Bioprocess enrichment analysis showed that genes associated with response to macrophage colony stimulating factor were closely related (Fig. 9D). RNA sequencing results showed that NF-κB signaling pathway may be involved in the regulation of macrophage M2 type polarization (Fig. 9C). NF-κB activation is thought to occur in three key stages. Initially, the pro-inflammatory stimulator binds to adapters such as MyD88, TAK1/TAB1, or RIP1, resulting in activation of the IKK complex composed of IKKα, Ikkβ, and IKKɣ subunits. This activation, in turn, triggers the phosphorylation and subsequent degradation of IkB (specifically Ikbα), facilitating the rapid release of NF-κB dimers (such as p65/p50) into the nucleus to stimulate pro-inflammatory gene transcription [50].Based on GO analysis, we hypothesize that piezoelectric scaffolds can regulate macrophage-based immune responses. As phosphorylated protein is active and regulates cell function, we detected p-P65 expression in RAW264.7 cells by immunofluorescence. p-P65 expression in nucleus of RAW264.7 was significantly down-regulated after piezoelectric stimulation, and p-P65 was isolated in cytoplasm. In unelectrically stimulated cells, p-P65 translocates to the nucleus (Fig. 9F). Western blot analysis further confirmed the immunomodulatory effect of piezoelectric scaffolds at the protein level. As shown in Fig. 9G, compared with the control group (CON) and the pure PCL scaffold group, the phosphorylation level of the pro-inflammatory transcription factor NF-κB p65 (p-P65) in macrophages treated with 10 % PBT piezoelectric scaffolds was significantly reduced, while the total protein level (P65) remained stable among all groups. This result clearly indicates that the ultrasonically activated piezoelectric signals effectively inhibited the activation of the NF-κB pathway, providing a key molecular mechanism explanation for the aforementioned changes in cytokine secretion (such as the increase in BMP-2/VEGF).The level of p-P65 in regenerated bone tissue was detected by immunohistofluorescence method, and significantly decreased p-P65 expression was observed after implantation of piezoelectric scaffolds (Fig. 9H). Membrane proteins, as signal integrators, can respond to external stimuli and changes in membrane potential. The cascade reaction induced by electric field stimulation (ES) may be mediated by the redistribution of cell surface receptors. Fibronectin receptors (α5β1 integrin), a transmembrane heterodimeric protein, can sense the cellular microenvironment, with calcium ions being an essential cation for its binding to fibronectin. The level of p-ikkα/β in macrophages was detected by immunofluorescence, and it was observed that the expression of p-ikkα/β was significantly decreased in the piezoelectric group (Fig. S14, Supporting Information).The aggregation or redistribution of integrins induces the autophosphorylation of focal adhesion kinase (FAK). FAK-mediated extracellular matrix (ECM) signaling stimulates the exchange of guanosine diphosphate (GDP) and guanosine triphosphate (GTP), activating Ras and its downstream signaling pathways, including the p38 kinase pathway, PI3K/Akt pathway, JNK pathway, and NF-κB pathway [51]. Therefore, it can be inferred that the local electric field generated by piezoelectric scaffolds under ultrasound assistance effectively blocks the NF-κB inflammatory signaling pathway induced by integrin, modulating macrophage M2 polarization and thereby inhibiting the inflammatory response.(Fig. 9E).

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the ultrasonic-activated piezoelectric signals within the barium titanate scaffold coordinate bone regeneration through two pathways. Mechanical ultrasound acts on the titanium titanate lattice, generating a local electric field, which depolarizes the cell membrane. In osteoblasts, this initiates calcium/calcineurin-dependent NFAT nuclear translocation, thereby directly driving the mineralization process. At the same time, the piezoelectric effect inhibits the transfer of NF-κB in macrophages, causing them to transform towards the regenerative M2 phenotype. The secreted paracrine substances of this phenotype can enhance bone formation and angiogenesis. Crucially, the spatial and temporal precision of this system is achieved through time control (pulse ultrasonic treatment every 48 h) to prevent excessive stimulation, and through spatial limitation (the signal rapidly decays outside the scaffold interface) to minimize non-targeted effects, thereby achieving bone healing without growth factors and increasing the bone volume at the defect site by three times. This electromagnetic-immunological strategy is superior to continuous electrical stimulation because it utilizes the cumulative paracrine signals and ensures targeted biological activity. Although its hormonal stability has been validated in male rats, future studies will use ovariectomized models to evaluate the repair situation in postmenopausal animals and further expand mechanical verification in large animal studies, while further optimizing the scaffold design and the concentration of piezoelectric materials to achieve the transformation for clinical application.
