Loss of PINK1 causes age-dependent mitochondrial trafficking deficits in nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons via aberrant p38 MAPK activation
Authors: Jingyu Zhao, Yuanxin Chen, Lianteng Zhi, Qing Xu, Juan Subiry, Zebang Chen, Shiquan Cui, Hui Zhang, Chenjian Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMutations in PTEN-induced putative kinase 1 (PINK1) cause early-onset, autosomal-recessive Parkinson’s disease (PD). While previous studies have shown age-related declines in dopamine release and ATP levels in Pink1−/− mice, the mechanisms remain unclear. Using a novel TH-Mito-Dendra2 transgenic mouse model to label dopaminergic neuron mitochondria, we show that PINK1 loss leads to age-dependent defects in axonal mitochondrial trafficking in acute brain slices. These deficits are characterized by reduced anterograde transport and increased mitochondrial stalling. Pharmacological induction of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and calcium release impaired mitochondrial mobility. Consistent with this, Pink1 knockout mice exhibited elevated mitochondrial calcium, oxidation levels, and p38 MAPK hyperactivation. Treatment with a calcium channel blocker and p38 inhibitor SB202190 restored mitochondrial motility and increased anterograde transport. Together, our findings suggest that PINK1 loss disrupts mitochondrial trafficking by disturbing calcium and redox homeostasis via the p38 pathway, contributing to PD pathogenesis.Subject terms: Cell biology, Neuroscience

Introduction

Parkinson’s disease (PD) is a major neurodegenerative disorder affecting people above 65 years old, characterized by dopamine neuron loss in the substantia nigra pars compacta (SNc), leading to striatal dopamine deficiency1. While the pathogenesis of PD is complicated, mounting evidence implicates mitochondrial dysfunction as a key initiating factor2. Mitochondrial abnormalities in PD include altered morphology, impaired energy production, increased oxidative stress, dysregulated dynamics and calcium homeostasis, and disrupted mitophagy. However, it remains unclear whether these mitochondrial defects are primary causes or downstream consequences in PD. The identification of PINK1 as a PD gene provided direct evidence linking mitochondrial dysfunction to PD pathogenesis3. PINK1 regulates key mitochondrial processes like mitophagy, calcium homeostasis, and fission in neurons, underscoring its relevance in studying mitochondrial dysfunction’s role in PD. PINK1 loss-of-function models exhibit multiple mitochondrial defects, including increased fission4, depolarization5, impaired electron transport chain complexes I and III6,7, and calcium overload8. Additionally, PINK1 interacts with Miro/Milton, implicating its role in mitochondrial trafficking9.

Neurons, especially synapses, are highly energy-demanding. A human cortical neuron utilizes ~4.7 billion ATPs/second at rest10, underscoring the importance of mitochondrial trafficking. Following evidence that PINK1 can interact with trafficking adapter proteins9, several studies have explored the relationship between PINK1 and mitochondrial trafficking in different models. One study demonstrated that overexpression of PINK1 or PARKIN in rat hippocampal axons significantly reduced mitochondrial motility. For impaired mitochondria, PINK1 phosphorylates Miro, leading to PARKIN-dependent Miro degradation and subsequent dissociation of kinesin from mitochondria, thus sequestering depolarized mitochondria11. Another study demonstrated that D-AKAP1/PKA (D-AKAP1, dual-specificity A Kinase Anchoring protein 1; PKA, Protein Kinase A) can restore the mitochondrial trafficking deficiency in cortical neurons from PINK1 knockout (KO) mice12. While these studies support the critical role of the PINK1/PARKIN pathway in regulating mitochondrial trafficking13, most research utilizes in vitro systems or Drosophila models, which do not fully recapitulate mammalian in vivo conditions. Moreover, many studies lack specificity for dopaminergic neurons. From this perspective, investigations that more closely reflect in vivo physiology and directly target dopaminergic neurons remain needed.

Here, we generated a TH-Mito-Dendra2 transgenic mouse model to selectively label mitochondria in dopaminergic neurons and established a new approach to directly visualize mitochondrial trafficking along the nigrostriatal pathway in acute brain slices from Pink1−/− mice. Using this system, we identified an age-dependent impairment of mitochondrial motility and anterograde transport in PINK1-deficient dopaminergic neurons, associated with aberrant p38 signaling. These findings provide a potential mechanistic basis for our previous observations of reduced striatal dopamine release and ATP production in Pink1−/− mice during early middle age14.

Results

Generation and characterization of TH-MitoDendra2 BAC transgenic mice

To specifically label mitochondria in dopaminergic neurons, we generated a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) transgenic mouse model. A mitochondria-targeting photo-convertible fluorescent protein Dendra2 gene15,16 was inserted in the Th exon 1 (Fig. 1A). This design places Mito-Dendra2 expression under the control of the Th promoter regulatory elements, ensuring specificity to dopaminergic neurons without creating a TH-Dendra2 fusion protein. The co-localization of Mito-Dendra2 and the immunofluorescence for TH confirmed the specificity of Mito-Dendra2 in dopaminergic neurons within the SNc and ventral tegmental (VTA) area (Fig. 1B–E). We also verified the specificity of Mito-Dendra2 signals at the dopaminergic terminals in the striatum and olfactory bulb (Fig. S1), and the results showed Mito-Dendra2 was only expressed in TH-positive neurons and neurites. Moreover, the co-localization of Mito-Dendra2 signals with MitoTracker staining verified the subcellular localization of Mito-Dendra2 in mitochondria (Fig. 1F). Quantitative co-localization analysis yielded a Pearson’s correlation coefficient of R = 0.49, with a Costes’ randomization P value of 1.00, indicating a significant spatial overlap between Mito-Dendra2 and the mitochondrial marker. Using this model, we can visualize the continuous and intact projection bundles from dopaminergic neurons in the SNc to the striatum in the sagittal view (Figs. 1E and S2). These results demonstrate that Mito-Dendra2 is specifically localized in the mitochondria of dopaminergic neurons and their projections, providing a novel and powerful tool for studying mitochondrial trafficking in these neurons.

Investigation of mitochondrial trafficking in acute brain slices with two-photon laser microscopy (2PLSM)

To evaluate mitochondrial trafficking in Pink1−/− mice, we crossed them with Mito-Dendra2 transgenic mice to generate Pink1−/− /MD+ transgenic mice (Fig. 2A). We used acute sagittal brain slices (Fig. 2A), a preparation that preserves native neuronal circuitry, to monitor mitochondrial trafficking. Two-photon laser scanning microscopy (2PLSM) was employed to track mitochondrial movement. Time-lapse imaging allowed direct visualization and recording of mitochondrial trafficking along nigrostriatal axon bundles (Figs. 1E and 2A, B). Mitochondrial movement was primarily classified into three states: stationary, anterograde (moving towards the terminals), and retrograde (moving towards the cell body). As depicted in Fig. 2B and Video S1, we clearly captured mitochondrial movement at sequential time points. After collecting mitochondrial trafficking data, we used the software Fiji with two plugins, KymographDirect and KymographClear17, to analyze the differences in the percentage and velocity of moving mitochondria (Fig. 2C).

Age-dependent mitochondrial axonal transport deficits in Pink1−/− mice

After establishing the imaging approach, we evaluated the alterations in mitochondrial trafficking between Pink1−/− mice and age-matched WT (Pink1+/+) littermates at different ages (Fig. 3, Tables S1–S3). The percentages of stationary, anterograde, and retrograde mitochondria were similar in 2–4-month-old Pink1−/− mice and WT littermates (Fig. 3A1, A2, B1, B2, C, Table S1). The average velocities of anterograde and retrograde movement were also comparable (Fig. 3D, Table S1). Approximately 84-85% of mitochondria were stationary in both genotypes at 2–4 months, which is higher than typically reported in cultured cells13,18. Correspondingly, ~11% of mitochondria exhibited anterograde movement and ~4–5% retrograde movement, differing from the more balanced anterograde and retrograde trafficking (~15% each) commonly observed in cultured cells18.

In 5–7-month-old Pink1−/− mice, the proportion of stationary mitochondria increased significantly (90.8 ± 0.7% (Pink1−/−) vs. 85.2 ± 0.7% (Pink1+/+), P < 0.0001). Anterograde movement decreased from 10.0 ± 0.9% to 5.6 ± 0.8% (P = 0.0019), while retrograde movement showed a slight decrease (3.6 ± 0.5% (Pink1−/−) vs. 4.9 ± 0.8% (Pink1+/+), P = 0.1624; Fig. 3A3, A4, B3, B4, E, Table S2). Additionally, the anterograde velocity decreased significantly (0.52 ± 0.02 µm/s (Pink1−/−) vs. 0.60 ± 0.03 µm/s (Pink1+/+), P = 0.0335) (Fig. 3F, Table S2). This reduction in anterograde movement may result in less mitochondrial content in axonal terminals.

In Pink1−/− mice older than 10 months, the percentage of static mitochondria increased significantly compared to WT littermates (91.7 ± 1.1% (Pink1−/−) vs. 86.6 ± 1.9% (Pink1+/+), P = 0.0371), while anterograde movement decreased to 2.5 ± 0.6% (P = 0.008) (Fig. 3A5, A6, B5, B6, G, Table S3). In these older Pink1−/− mice, retrograde mitochondrial transport exceeded anterograde transport, reaching 6.2 ± 1.0% (Fig. 3G, Table S3). Furthermore, the anterograde velocity was significantly decreased to 0.36 ± 0.03 µm/s (P = 0.004) compared to age-matched WT controls (0.48 ± 0.02 µm/s), while retrograde velocity remained unchanged (Fig. 3H, Table S3).

In summary, Pink1−/− mice exhibited age-dependent deficits in mitochondrial axonal trafficking. These deficits, which were not present in 2–4-month-old mice, emerged around 5 months of age and persisted beyond 10 months of age. The trafficking abnormalities included an increased proportion of static mitochondria, a decreased proportion of anterograde motile mitochondria, and slower anterograde velocities. These findings differ from previous reports in cultured neurons11 and Drosophila19, highlighting potential differences between ex vivo and in vitro models, as well as between invertebrate and vertebrate systems. Notably, aging may play a critical role, as neuronal cultures largely reflect a developmentally young neuronal state.

Mitochondrial calcium elevation and transport deficits in Pink1−/− mice

Mitochondrial axonal transport is a tightly regulated process involving many factors. Cytosolic Ca2+ can modulate mitochondrial motility via the Miro protein20. Furthermore, mitochondria themselves play a pivotal role in calcium homeostasis21. We first evaluated mitochondrial calcium levels in around 3–4 months old Pink1−/− mice and WT littermates using AAV9-TH-mito-GCaMP6, a modified GCaMP6 virus that is specifically expressed in mitochondria in dopaminergic neurons. Figure 4A, B shows that the relative [Ca2+]mt (relative fluorescence intensity) was significantly increased in Pink1−/− mice (78.6 ± 4.3%) compared to WT littermates (35.7 ± 4.6%, P = 0.0001). Next, we examined whether elevated mitochondrial calcium directly affects mitochondrial trafficking. Acute brain slices from 3–4-month-old WT mice were incubated with 5 µM CGP37157, an inhibitor of the mitochondrial Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger (NCLX) that induces mitochondrial overload22. Mitochondrial motility was significantly decreased after 2 h of CGP37157 incubation (Fig. 4C, D, Videos S2 and S3). The proportion of static mitochondria increased from 85.3 ± 0.7% to 90.3 ± 1.2% (P = 0.0048), while anterograde movement declined from 8.7 ± 1.1% to 5.6 ± 0.9% (P = 0.0432) (Fig. 4D).

To further test the relationship between calcium influx and mitochondrial transport, we examined the effects of the L-type calcium channel blocker isradipine (20 µM). In contrast to CGP37157, isradipine treatment significantly reduced the proportion of stationary mitochondria and restored mitochondrial trafficking in Pink1−/− mice (Fig. S3), consistent with a rescue effect mediated by reducing calcium influx. Together, these results support the idea that dysregulated calcium homeostasis contributes directly to the mitochondrial trafficking defects observed in Pink1−/− dopaminergic neurons.

Importantly, the trafficking abnormalities induced by CGP37157 in WT neurons phenocopied the age-dependent mitochondrial motility defects observed in 5–7-month-old and >10-month-old Pink1−/− mice, further supporting a shared calcium-dependent mechanism underlying these deficits. In contrast, neither CGP37157 nor isradipine significantly altered the velocities of moving mitochondria in either the anterograde or retrograde direction (Fig. 4E), suggesting that calcium dysregulation primarily affects the proportion of motile mitochondria rather than transport speed itself.

Reduced mitochondrial anterograde movement by ROS elevation

Mitochondrial calcium overload impairs normal mitochondrial function, leading to increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and decreased ATP production23,24. To elucidate the relationship between mitochondrial calcium homeostasis and ROS generation in acute brain slices, we utilized CGP37157 to induce mitochondrial calcium overload in brain slices derived from roEGFP transgenic mice (4–5-month-old) and evaluate the ROS alteration25 after treatment (Fig. 5). Our results showed a significant increase in [Ca2+]mt, which led to an elevation in oxidation levels (P = 0.0005, Fig. 5C).

Subsequently, to investigate the impact of elevated ROS levels on mitochondrial trafficking dynamics, we exposed acute brain slice preparations from WT mice (5–7-month-old) to 50 μM menadione (Fig. 6A–D). Menadione is a widely employed compound known to exacerbate endogenous ROS production in cultured cell systems26. Following a 20-min incubation with 50 µM menadione, the proportion of stationary mitochondria significantly increased from 88.7 ± 1.5% to 96.8 ± 0.9% (P = 0.0036, Fig. 6C). Anterograde-moving mitochondria were reduced by nearly 70%, from 6.7 ± 1.1% to 2.2 ± 0.7% (P = 0.0202), while retrograde movement also declined (4.6 ± 0.7% (aCSF control), 1.1 ± 0.2% (MED), P = 0.0177, Fig. 6A–C, Videos S4 and S6). The average velocity of both anterograde and retrograde movement remained unchanged after MED treatment (Fig. 6D). To rule out the possibility that decreased motility was due to DMSO toxicity, we also evaluated the effect of DMSO treatment on mitochondrial trafficking. The results showed no significant change in the percentage of moving mitochondria and their velocity between aCSF control and DMSO treatment (Fig. 6C, D, Videos S4 and S5). Additionally, we examined the effect of H2O2 treatment, another reagent commonly used to induce ROS generation, and observed similar results on mitochondrial trafficking (Fig. S4). These findings suggest that artificially increasing endogenous ROS levels in WT brain slices can impair mitochondrial motility, particularly affecting anterograde movement, similar to the deficits observed in Pink1−/− mice.

Next, we evaluated oxidation levels in Pink1−/− mice. Manganese superoxide dismutase (MnSOD), a crucial mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme, detoxifies oxygen-free radicals generated during mitochondrial respiration. Evidence indicates that oxidative stress significantly upregulates MnSOD expression levels27. We quantified MnSOD expression levels in the striatum and SNc regions of Pink1−/− mice and WT littermates. Western blot analysis revealed a significant upregulation of MnSOD levels in 5–7-month-old Pink1−/− mice compared to WT littermates in both the striatum and SNc regions (Fig. 6E, F), with a significant increase in both striatum (P = 0.0428) and substantia nigra (P = 0.0277). Complementary immunostaining corroborated these findings, showing significantly higher MnSOD immunoreactivity in dopaminergic neurons from Pink1−/− mice compared to WT controls (Fig. 6H, I). Quantitative analysis revealed an approximate 4-fold increase in relative MnSOD fluorescence intensity in Pink1−/− mice (P < 0.0001). To further assess mitochondrial ROS levels in dopaminergic neurons of Pink1−/− mice, we performed MitoSOX staining in acute brain slices. Consistent with the MnSOD findings, MitoSOX fluorescence intensity was significantly elevated in both dopaminergic neuronal cell bodies (P < 0.0001) and axon terminals (P = 0.0008) compared with WT controls (Fig. 6J, K). Our previous findings demonstrated decreased mitochondrial coupling efficiency starting at 3–4 months of age and continuing through 14 months14. This reduced coupling efficiency directly correlates with increased oxidative stress. Together, these results provide compelling evidence of elevated oxidative levels in PINK1 knockout mice. The excessive ROS generation in acute brain slices from WT mice induced by menadione treatment was associated with impaired mitochondrial motility. These findings underscore a potential pathogenic link between PINK1-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction, dysregulated redox homeostasis, and disrupted mitochondrial trafficking, which may contribute to the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in PD.

p38 MAPK pathway hyperactivation by loss of PINK1

We have demonstrated that PINK1 loss causes mitochondrial calcium overload in dopaminergic neurons, resulting in a significant increase in oxidation levels. Oxidative stress plays a pivotal role in dopaminergic neuronal death. Environmental toxins like paraquat and rotenone induce ROS production, which activates the p38 MAPK pathway. This activation not only contributes to mitochondrial dysfunction and apoptosis but also creates a feedback loop that exacerbates oxidative stress, accelerating neurodegeneration. Interventions targeting p38 MAPK have shown promise in disrupting this deleterious cycle28. However, the specific mechanisms by which excessive ROS affect mitochondrial movement in neurons remain to be fully elucidated. Previous studies have shown that intracellular ROS can inhibit the function of the motor-adapter complex that regulates mitochondrial motility through the p38 MAPK pathway, thus reducing mitochondrial motility in vitro26.

We analyzed p38 phosphorylation level in Pink1−/− mice (6–7-month-old) (Fig. 7A, B). Immunoblot results showed that p38 phosphorylation levels were approximately threefold higher in Pink1−/− mice than in WT mice in the striatum (P = 0.0286). The substantia nigra region exhibited a similar increase in p38 phosphorylation levels (P = 0.0079). Elevated p38 phosphorylation indicates heightened activation of the p38 pathway. Collectively, these results suggest hyperactivation of the p38 MAPK pathway in Pink1−/− mice.

We further examined the activation status of other MAPK family members. Phosphorylated JNK levels were also increased in Pink1−/− mice (Fig. S5), whereas ERK phosphorylation remained unchanged (Fig. S6). Given that both p38 and JNK are key mediators of cellular stress responses, their selective hyperactivation is consistent with elevated oxidative stress in PINK1 deficiency and aligns with our earlier observations (Fig. 6H).

Improved mitochondrial motility in Pink1−/− mice by inhibiting p38

To investigate whether inhibiting p38 activity could alleviate mitochondrial trafficking deficits in Pink1−/− mice, we treated acute brain slices from 7 to 8 months old Pink1−/− mice with the p38 inhibitor SB202190 (Fig. 7C–F, Videos S7 and S8). Incubating Pink1−/− mice brain slices in aCSF containing 50 μM SB202190 for 1 h significantly increased mitochondrial motility in dopaminergic neurons (Fig. 7E). The proportion of immobile mitochondria decreased from 90.9 ± 0.5% to 84.6 ± 0.6% (P < 0.0001). The percentage of anterograde-moving mitochondria significantly increased from 5.0 ± 0.9% to 11.1 ± 1.4% (P = 0.0037). Retrograde mitochondrial movement was not significantly affected (aCSF: 4.1 ± 0.8% vs. SB202190: 4.3 ± 1.2%). Additionally, SB202190 treatment did not alter the velocity of either anterograde or retrograde mitochondrial movement (Fig. 7F). Consistent with a broader role for p38 signaling in regulating mitochondrial transport, SB202190 treatment of WT brain slices similarly increased mitochondrial motility, primarily by enhancing anterograde transport (Fig. S7A).

We also verified the effectiveness of SB202190 treatment in Pink1−/− mouse brain slices through immunoblot analysis, which showed a significant ~62% reduction in p38 phosphorylation (Fig. S7C). This substantial decrease in p38 phosphorylation led to a significant rescue of mitochondrial motility defects in dopaminergic neurons of Pink1−/− mice, particularly by increasing the percentage of anterograde movement. However, SB202190 treatment did not improve the decreased anterograde velocity in Pink1−/− mice.

We next examined the protein expression involved in axonal mitochondrial transport (Fig. S8). The adapter protein Miro1 showed a significant reduction in Pink1−/− mice compared to WT littermates: approximately a 15% decrease in the striatum (P = 0.0286) and approximately a 24% decrease in the substantia nigra (P = 0.0317). This reduction in Miro1 may lead to decreased mitochondrial attachment to microtubules and reduced motility. However, we observed no significant changes in the expression of the motor proteins kinesin and dynein in Pink1−/− mice (Fig. S8). We also assessed mitofusin 2 (MFN2) expression and detected no significant differences between genotypes (Fig. S9).

Defective mitochondria at dopaminergic neuron terminals in Pink1−/− mice

Pink1−/− mice exhibited an age-dependent decrease in striatal dopamine release and ATP levels14. This observation, along with the impaired mitochondrial axonal transport in Pink1−/− dopaminergic neurons, led us to hypothesize that reduced anterograde movement of mitochondria in Pink1−/− dopaminergic neurons might result in a depletion of healthy mitochondria at their axon terminals. To test this hypothesis, we employed tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) immunogold electron microscopy to directly assess mitochondrial morphology and quantify mitochondrial numbers. In WT mice (8–9-month-old), dopaminergic neurons exhibited relatively intact mitochondrial cristae, indicating overall healthy mitochondria (Fig. 8A). In contrast, Pink1−/− mice displayed notable mitochondrial abnormalities suggestive of fragmentation and swelling. Compared to the organized, laminar cristae and dense matrix observed in WT mice, mitochondria in Pink1−/− terminals displayed disorganized or absent cristae and electron-lucent cytoplasmic vacuoles indicative of matrix swelling. (Fig. 8B, indicated by the red arrows). In Pink1−/− mice, there was a significant decrease in the proportion of healthy mitochondria within dopaminergic terminals (P = 0.0079). However, the overall percentage of healthy mitochondria in the striatum remained unchanged (Fig. 8C, P = 0.4524). Compared with WT mice, the circularity (P < 0.0001) and aspect ratio (P < 0.0001) of mitochondria in the dopaminergic neurons of Pink1−/− mice were significantly changed (Fig. 8D), indicating that the mitochondria at the terminals of dopaminergic neurons in Pink1−/− mice tended to be more rounded and smaller.

Taken together, our findings indicate that loss of PINK1 leads to elevated mitochondrial calcium levels in SNc dopaminergic neurons by disrupting calcium regulatory mechanisms. Perturbation of mitochondrial calcium homeostasis is accompanied by increased oxidative stress and ROS production, which in turn engages p38 MAPK signaling. Previous studies have demonstrated that p38 can directly phosphorylate kinesin heavy chains, thereby inhibiting axonal transport26,29–32. Based on these observations, we propose a working model (Fig. 8E): the increase in oxidative level caused by loss of PINK1 activates the p38 MAPK pathway, leading to p38-mediated phosphorylation of the anterograde motor protein KIF5. This phosphorylation disrupts the mitochondrial anterograde transport complex, reducing the percentage of mitochondria undergoing anterograde movement. Consequently, diminished anterograde trafficking is predicted to limit the delivery of functional mitochondria to dopaminergic terminals, potentially resulting in reduced ATP availability and impaired dopamine release.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a novel approach that synergistically combines Mito-Dendra2 mice, acute brain slice preparations, and 2PLSM to investigate mitochondrial axonal transport within dopaminergic neurons. This approach enables direct interrogation of mitochondrial trafficking within an ex vivo context that preserves key features of neuronal architecture and circuit organization, thereby providing a physiologically relevant framework for investigating PD–related mechanisms. This strategy offers several advantages. (i) Cell-type specificity: We focused specifically on the nigrostriatal dopaminergic neurons, which are the most vulnerable neuronal population in PD pathogenesis. (ii) Preserved neuronal network: Utilization of acute brain slices from mice provides a more integrated and physiologically relevant neuronal network that closely mimics in vivo conditions. Notably, the relative thickness of these slices helps maintain the coherence of the neuronal circuitry, including interactions among neurons, astrocytes, and glial cells, for a considerable duration, despite potential mechanical damage from sectioning. Importantly, an advantage of our model is that it allows investigation of mitochondrial trafficking phenotypes across different stages of aging. (iii) This preparation remains amenable to pharmacological manipulation, enabling functional interrogation of mitochondrial dynamics within an integrated tissue environment.

Using this method, we identified age-dependent impairments in mitochondrial axonal transport in dopaminergic neurons in the Pink1−/− mice. These defects were characterized by an increased proportion of stationary mitochondria, reduced anterograde movement, and slower anterograde velocities, emerging in midlife and persisting with age. These transport abnormalities provide a plausible mechanistic framework for our previously reported age-dependent reductions in striatal dopamine release and ATP production in Pink1−/− mice14. Consistent with our findings, prior studies have reported that loss of Pink1 in mouse cortical and midbrain neurons reduces the proportion of mitochondria undergoing anterograde movement along dendrites12. Importantly, the relatively high fraction of stationary mitochondria observed in our study (~85%) is consistent with the physiological properties of mature axons rather than indicative of tissue compromise. In vivo imaging studies have demonstrated that mitochondrial motility declines markedly with neuronal maturation, with stationary fractions exceeding 90% in cortical axons of young adult mice33. Thus, the stationary fraction observed in our acute slice preparations aligns well with established in vivo baselines. While reduced motility can reflect pathological stress in some experimental contexts, the magnitude and consistency of our measurements support the interpretation that these values reflect the intrinsic stability of mitochondria in mature dopaminergic axons.

Notably, our findings differ from reports in other model systems, including Drosophila and dissociated hippocampal neuron cultures, in which PINK1 loss has been associated with increased mitochondrial motility11,19. Several factors may contribute to these discrepancies. First, differences in model systems, species, and neuronal populations likely influence the phenotypic outcomes of PINK1 loss. Second, our use of acute brain slices across multiple age stages enables investigation of the age-dependent progression of mitochondrial trafficking defects, which may not be fully captured in dissociated culture systems that typically represent relatively immature or “young” neurons. Importantly, although the PINK1 mutation is present throughout life, dopaminergic neuronal dysfunction and degeneration in PD are strongly age-dependent processes. In addition, the experimental preparations differ substantially. Previous studies primarily utilized dissociated neuronal cultures, whereas our acute brain slice preparation preserves local neuronal circuitry, axonal architecture, and glia-neuron interactions within a more physiologically relevant environment. This approach may reveal mitochondrial transport deficits that are masked or compensated for in isolated culture systems. Furthermore, dopaminergic neurons are among the most selectively vulnerable neuronal populations in PD, suggesting that their mitochondrial dynamics and calcium handling properties may differ substantially from those of other neuronal types. Together, these findings suggest that the effects of PINK1 deficiency on mitochondrial dynamics are highly context-dependent, varying across species, neuronal subtypes, developmental stages, and experimental conditions. In this context, our ex vivo approach provides a physiologically relevant framework for studying mitochondrial trafficking in dopaminergic neurons within their native cellular and circuit architecture, which may be particularly important for understanding PD pathophysiology.

With respect to mechanism, our data support a model in which mitochondrial calcium overload functions primarily as a stress signal in PINK1-deficient dopaminergic neurons. We observed elevated mitochondrial calcium levels, increased oxidative stress, and hyperactivation of p38 MAPK signaling in Pink1−/− mice. Rather than enhancing metabolic output, chronic mitochondrial calcium accumulation is likely to promote ROS generation and activate stress-responsive signaling pathways. Consistent with this framework, pharmacological elevation of mitochondrial calcium or ROS impaired mitochondrial motility, whereas inhibition of L-type calcium channel and p38 signaling restored transport. This mechanism aligns with emerging evidence from other PD models, including LRRK2- and Parkin-associated pathways, in which mitochondrial calcium dysregulation and stress kinase activation converge on impaired axonal transport34,35. These findings suggest that calcium-induced arrest of mitochondrial trafficking may represent a shared vulnerability across genetically distinct forms of PD, ultimately compromising synaptic bioenergetics.

Previous studies have shown that p38 MAPK can inhibit axonal transport without altering kinesin protein abundance, instead acting through phosphorylation of KIF5 motor proteins to reduce binding affinity to microtubules26,29–32. Although we were unable to directly measure p38-dependent phosphorylation of KIF5 in brain tissue, the restoration of mitochondrial motility following p38 inhibition supports the functional relevance of this pathway in PINK1 deficiency. Further work will be required to define the precise molecular targets linking p38 activation, Miro1 regulation, and the mitochondrial transport machinery in dopaminergic neurons.

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, although we provide convergent evidence for increased oxidative stress using MnSOD expression and direct MitoSOX measurements combined with two-photon imaging, we did not comprehensively quantify individual ROS species using multiple dedicated biochemical or genetically encoded probes. Therefore, our conclusions are focused on altered mitochondrial oxidative/redox status rather than precise quantification of specific ROS species. Second, we did not directly measure mitochondrial membrane potential or respiration in dopaminergic neurons; therefore, inferences regarding mitochondrial depolarization or respiratory efficiency remain indirect and hypothesis-generating. Third, direct quantification of mitochondrial density at dopaminergic axon terminals was beyond the scope of the current study due to technical resolution constraints. Finally, although acute brain slices preserve native architecture, they cannot fully recapitulate the chronic in vivo environment, including long-term circuit activity and systemic influences.

In summary, our study introduces a physiologically relevant and adaptable approach for investigating mitochondrial trafficking in dopaminergic neurons in PD mouse models. Application of this strategy to Pink1−/− mice revealed an age-dependent mitochondrial transport phenotype that differs from prior observations in other systems, underscoring the importance of cellular and experimental context. Beyond advancing understanding of PINK1-associated PD, this platform can be extended to examine the trafficking of additional organelles or proteins across disease models. More broadly, our findings highlight stress-responsive pathways such as calcium and p38 MAPK as potential modulators of axonal bioenergetics, providing a conceptual framework for future studies aimed at preserving dopaminergic function in PD.

Methods

Animals

Mice were housed in a temperature and humidity-controlled specific pathogen-free facility under a 12-h light/dark cycle schedule with food and water available ad libitum. Mice were on the FVB/NTac mouse background (Taconic). All experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committees of Thomas Jefferson University, Peking University, and the University of Georgia.

Construction, modification, and confirmation of TH-MitoDendra2-BAC

BAC clone covering the entire tyrosine hydroxylase (Th) gene was obtained from GENSAT RPCI23-350E13 (http://genome.ucsc.edu/cgi-bin/hgTracks?clade=mammal&org=Mouse&db=mm9&position=RP23-350E13).

Dendra2-SV40 polyA from Clontech pCMV vector was ligated with a mitochondrial-targeting sequence (MTS)15. The MTS was from human subunit 8A of cytochrome c oxidase (COX8A): 5’ATGTCCGTCCTGACGCCGCTGCTGCTGCGGGGCTTGACAGGCTCGGCCCGGCGGCTCCCAGTGCCGCGCGCCAAGATCCATTCGTTGGGGGATCCACCGGTCGCCACC3’. Mito-Dendra2 was inserted into the exon 1 of the Th gene with homologous recombination. Homologous arms, A arm (642 bp) and B arm (538 bp), from the first exon of Th were ligated to 5′ and 3′ ends of the Mito-Dendra2 cassette by PCR amplification, respectively, and inserted into the shuttle vector following the standard protocol16. The shuttle vectors were introduced into the RP23-350E13 competent cells to construct the co-integrated BACs using electroporation. Recombinants were identified by chloramphenicol and ampicillin resistance selection followed by PCR analysis16. Purified TH-MitoDendra2 BACs were used to generate TH-MitoDendra2 transgenic mice.

TH-MitoDendra2-BAC transgenic mice

TH-MitoDendra2-BACs were used for pronuclear injection in the Taconic FVB/NTac background. Founders and progeny carrying the TH-MitoDendra2 transgene were identified by PCR analysis of tail DNA and direct sequencing. The expression pattern of Mito-Dendra2 was confirmed using TH immunofluorescence in TH-MitoDendra2-BAC transgenic mice. The transgenic mice were maintained by breeding with wild-type (WT) Taconic FVB/NTac mice.

Immunofluorescence

Mice were anesthetized with ketamine/xylazine (100 mg/10 mg per kg) and subsequently perfused with ice-cold 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and post-fixed for 24 h. Coronal sections, 30 μm thick, were obtained using a Thermo Scientific Microm HM550 Cryostat. Serial sections were collected into 10 wells, with each well containing every 10th section. Floating sections were stored in antifreeze buffer (30% glycerol and 30% ethylene glycol in PBS) at −20 °C. The sections were stained with a polyclonal anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) antibody (Millipore, MAB5280) at a 1:1000 dilution for 36 h at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a Dylight 594-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500, Life Technologies). Fluorescent images were captured using a Leica SP8 laser scanning confocal microscope.

Acute sagittal slice preparation

Animals were anesthetized with either isoflurane (1–2%) or ketamine/xylazine (100 mg/10 mg per kg) and underwent transcardiac perfusion with ice-cold, pre-oxygenated cutting solution containing (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 26 NaHCO3, 3.7 MgSO4, 0.3 KH2PO4, and 10 glucose, pH 7.4. The brains were immediately dissected out in cold, pre-oxygenated cutting solution. Sagittal slices containing the nigrostriatal projection were cut at a thickness of 300 μm using a vibrating microtome (VT1200, Leica, Solms, Germany) with an angle of approximately 21°. The slices were recovered in oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing (in mM): 125 NaCl, 2.5 KCl, 26 NaHCO3, 2.4 CaCl2, 1.3 MgSO4, 0.3 KH2PO4, 10 glucose, 2 HEPES, pH 7.4, at room temperature for >30 min14.

Two-photon laser microscopy imaging of mitochondrial trafficking in brain slices

Parasagittal slices containing nigrostriatal projections were obtained from TH-MitoDendra2 transgenic mice. The brain slices were positioned within a recording chamber and superfused with oxygenated aCSF at a flow rate of 1.5 mL/min. Optical imaging of Dendra signals was acquired using an Ultima multiphoton laser scanner (Bruker) for a BX51/61 Olympus microscope with a Titanium-sapphire laser (Chameleon-Ultra2, Coherent Laser Group), equipped with a 20× 1.0 NA water immersion objective (XLUMPLFL20XW, Olympus). The Dendra2 protein was excited at a wavelength of 900 nm, and its fluorescence was collected at 500–560 nm. Images were captured in 8-bit resolution, and 4 μs dwell time, with regions of interest set at 512 × 512 pixels. The sampling rate ranged from 0.2 to 0.5 s per frame. All experiments were conducted at 33–35 °C. Laser power was carefully controlled (below 10 mW) and kept at the minimum required for reliable signal detection, but with no effect on the mitochondrial trafficking.

Images were processed using the open-source software Fiji (NIH) and MATLAB (Version 8.5.0 R2015a, MathWorks). Intensity-based alignment was performed to register images at different time points. To measure mitochondrial motility velocity, the plugins KymographClear and KymographDirect were used to generate kymographs representing moving distance versus moving duration. The number and percentage of mitochondria in different states—static, retrograde, and anterograde—were quantified and measured. For each experiment, multiple kymographs were generated from the same field in order to capture most moving mitochondria. Overall velocity was determined by calculating the ratio of total displacement to observation duration. For mitochondria exhibiting bidirectional movement, comprehensive trajectory analysis was performed using kymographs, where directional changes were identified by slope inversions, enabling velocity calculations for individual directional segments.

Stereotaxic injection of AAV

Three-to-four-month-old male mice of Pink1+/+and Pink1−/− were anesthetized with isoflurane (1–2%) or ketamine/xylazine (100 mg/10 mg/kg). A small craniotomy, approximately 0.8 mm in diameter, was made over the right midbrain (−3.4 mm caudal, +1.0 mm lateral). To achieve expression of mito-GCaMP6 in dopaminergic neurons, 0.1–0.2 µl of AAV-TH-mito-GCaMP6 virus (1013 vg/ml, a gift from Dr. James Surmeier’s lab at Northwestern University) was pressure-injected through a syringe into the midbrain at a depth of −4.2 mm ventral from the dura surface. Following the injections, the incisional wound on the skull was sealed with tissue adhesive (3M Vetbond). The mice were placed on a heating pad until they fully recovered and returned to the isolation room. Carprofen (5 mg/kg, Sigma-Aldrich) was administered intraperitoneally once per day for at least 3 days to reduce pain.

2PLSM mito-GCaMP6 imaging in acute brain slices

Mitochondrial Ca2+ levels were measured with the mitochondrially targeted Ca2+-sensitive probe TH-mito-GCaMP636. Two to three weeks after AAV virus injection, mice were prepared for coronal brain slice sectioning following transcardiac perfusion with ice-cold, oxygenated cutting solution. After a recovery period of 30–45 min, the coronal brain slices were placed in a chamber perfused with oxygenated aCSF. To measure free Ca2+ levels in mitochondria, the slices were sequentially treated with the following solutions: (1) regular aCSF, (2) 500 µM EGTA + aCSF without Ca2+ for 30–40 min, (3) 500 µM EGTA + 1 µM ionomycin + aCSF without Ca2+ for 30 min, and (4) 1 µM Ionomycin + aCSF with high Ca2+ (3 mM) for 20 min. Time series images were acquired every 5 min to determine the minimal and maximal fluorescence intensity. The fluorescence intensity at the end of treatment (2) was defined as the minimum fluorescence (Fmin), and the fluorescence intensity at the end of treatment (4) was defined as the maximum fluorescence (Fmax). The relative free calcium level was calculated using the equation: (F−Fmin)/(Fmax−Fmin).

MitoSOX staining and measurement in acute brain slices

To assess mitochondrial superoxide levels in acute brain slices, 250 μm acute brain slices were recovered in oxygenated artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) at 36 °C for 1 h following slicing. Slices were subsequently incubated in ACSF containing 2 μM MitoSOX Red (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 19 min at 36 °C to label mitochondrial superoxide. Following incubation, slices were washed in fresh aCSF for 30 min to remove excess dye. MitoSOX Red fluorescence was imaged by two-photon microscopy with 760 nm excitation.

Immunoblot

Tissues were homogenized and lysed with RIPA buffer (Millipore Sigma, R0278) supplemented with fresh protease inhibitors (Roche, 11873580001) and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, 04906845001). The homogenate was further lysed for 1 h at 4 °C and cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were collected, and protein concentrations were determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Scientific Pierce, 23227). Protein samples (30–50 μg) were separated using Mini-PROTEAN® TGX™ Precast Gels (Bio-Rad). After blocking, the blots were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. Following washing with 1× TBST (0.1% Tween-20), the blots were incubated with secondary antibodies (1:10,000, IRDye® 800RD, Li-COR 926-32211; IRDye® 680RD, Li-COR, 926-68070) for 1 h and visualized using the Odyssey Infrared Imager (Li-COR, 9120). The relative protein abundance was quantified using Image Studio software (Li-COR).

TH-Immunogold labeling and electron microscopy

Mice were perfused intracardially with freshly prepared fixatives. The fixative consisted of 3.75% acrolein and 2% paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.4 (PB). After fixation, brains were sectioned at 50 µm in PB using a vibratome, and subsequently fixed at 4 °C overnight in 4% PFA/PB. Sections were treated with 50 mM glycine in 1× PBS for 30 min to reduce free aldehydes and rinsed extensively with PB three times. The sections were subsequently permeabilized with Tris-buffered saline containing 3% goat serum, 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA), and 0.04% Triton X-100 for 30 min. The primary mouse anti-TH antibody (#MAB5280, Millipore Sigma) was diluted 1:100 in PBS with 3% goat serum and 1% BSA. The sections were incubated with the primary antibody at 4 °C for 48 h. After washing three times (10 min each) with PBS, the sections were labeled with 0.8 nm gold-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibody (#800.177, Aurion) diluted in a buffer containing 3% goat serum, 0.8% BSA, and 0.1% cold fish gelatin in PBS. The sections were incubated with the secondary antibody at 4 °C for 48 h. Following one wash with dilution buffer and six washes with PBS, sections were post-fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde in PBS. After several washes with PBS, the sections were exposed to Enhancement Conditioning Solution (ECS; #500.055, Aurion) diluted 1:10 with ddH2O, for four times at 10 min each. Ultra-small gold particles were enlarged using R-Gent SE-EM Silver Enhancement Reagents (#500.044, Aurion) for 150 min. The sections were rinsed in ECS before being transferred to PBS. Tissue sections were transferred to PBS in Coors dishes and fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide in PB for 60 min on ice. After rinsing in ddH2O, the sections were sequentially dehydrated in 30%, 50%, 70%, 85%, and 95% ethanol solutions, followed by two exposures to 100% ethanol and two exposures to acetone. Tissue sections were incubated overnight in a 1:1 mixture of epoxy resin (#14120, EMbed 812, Electron Microscopy Sciences) and acetone before being transferred to pure EMbed 812 for 3 h (three times, 1 h each). The sections were embedded in EMbed 812 and polymerized for 24 h at 65 °C. Plastic blocks were trimmed and sectioned with a Leica ultramicrotome (EM UC7, Leica) equipped with a 35° diamond knife (Diatome). Serial ultrathin sections (75 nm) were collected on single-slot formvar-coated copper grids. After post-staining with uranyl acetate and lead citrate, the grids were examined under a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai G2 Spirit BioTWIN, FEI) equipped with a digital camera (Orius 832, Gatan) at 120 kV.

Mitochondrial morphometric analysis

To quantify mitochondrial morphology in dopaminergic terminals, digital electron microscopy images were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH). Mitochondria were included in the analysis only if they were located within axonal profiles containing visible TH-immunogold particles. The aspect ratio was calculated for each mitochondrion using the formula: Aspect Ratio = Major Axis/Minor Axis. A value of 1.0 indicates a perfect circle (fragmented/swollen), while higher values indicate elongated (healthy) mitochondria. Measurements were performed by an investigator blinded to the genotype.

Data and statistical analysis

Data were presented as mean ± SEM unless otherwise specified. Statistical analyses were conducted using Prism 9.0 (GraphPad), including the unpaired t-test, Mann–Whitney test, and one-way ANOVA. Differences were considered statistically significant when P < 0.05. Statistical analysis was based on the number of slices or experiments unless otherwise noted. “N” denotes the number of mice, and “n” denotes the number of experiments.
