Outcomes of ATA Low-Risk Pediatric Thyroid Cancer Patients Not Treated With Radioactive Iodine Therapy
Authors: Mya Bojarsky, Julia A Baran, Stephen Halada, Amber Isaza, Hongming Zhuang, Lisa States, Frederick D Grant, Stephanie Robbins, Lindsay Sisko, Julio C Ricarte-Filho, Ken Kazahaya, N Scott Adzick, Sogol Mostoufi-Moab, Andrew J Bauer
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextThe American Thyroid Association (ATA) Pediatric Guidelines recommend patients not receive radioactive iodine therapy (RAIT) for differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) confined to the thyroid. Since publication, there is ongoing concern whether withholding RAIT will result in a lower rate of remission.ObjectiveThis study explores whether ATA low-risk patients treated with and without RAIT achieved similar remission rates.MethodsMedical records of patients <19 years old diagnosed with DTC and treated with total thyroidectomy between 2010 and 2020 were reviewed. Multivariate logistic regression was performed to evaluate factors influencing RAIT administration and remission rate.ResultsNinety-five patients with ATA low-risk DTC were analyzed: 53% (50/95) and 47% (45/95) were treated with and without RAIT, respectively. RAIT was used to treat 82% of patients before 2015 compared with 33% of patients after 2015 (P < .01). No significant difference in 1-year remission rate was found between patients treated with and without RAIT, 70% (35/50) vs 69% (31/45), respectively. With longer surveillance, remission rates increased to 82% and 76% for patients treated with and without RAIT, respectively. Median follow-up was 5.8 years (IQR 4.3-7.9, range 0.9-10.9) and 3.6 years (IQR 2.7-6.6; range 0.9–9.3) for both cohorts. No risk factors for persistent or indeterminate disease status were found, including RAIT administration, N1a disease, and surgery after 2015.ConclusionWithholding RAIT for pediatric patients with ATA low-risk DTC avoids exposure to radiation and does not have a negative impact on remission rates. Dynamic risk stratification at 1-year after initial treatment is a suitable time point to assess the impact of withholding RAIT for these patients.

Before 2015, children and adolescents diagnosed with differentiated thyroid cancer (DTC) were treated with total or near-total thyroidectomy (TT) followed by radioactive iodine therapy (RAIT) with iodine-131 (1). Disease remission was considered a negative postablative whole-body RAI scan and undetectable serum thyroglobulin (Tg) level. In 2012, several adult studies investigated the use of lower dose or no RAIT to treat patients with low-invasive DTC, resulting in recommendations to withhold RAIT administration for adult DTC with disease grossly confined to the thyroid (2–4).

In an effort to reduce exposure to radiation for pediatric patients with DTC having low-invasive behavior, the inaugural American Thyroid Association (ATA) Pediatric DTC Guidelines recommended a risk stratification system for the use of RAIT. The ATA Guidelines separated patients into a 3-tiered risk level system based on the American Joint Commission on Cancer (AJCC) TNM classification to define the risk of persistent postsurgical disease, advising RAIT be withheld from treatment for DTC categorized as low risk (5). ATA low-risk category describes patients with DTC that is grossly confined to the thyroid without lymph node (LN) metastases or with a small number of central neck LNs positive for metastasis. Despite these recommendations, there are currently no published data evaluating the effect of stratified RAIT administration on disease remission rates for pediatric patients with ATA low-risk DTC (5).

This study explores remission rates of patients with ATA low-risk DTC treated with and without RAIT at a high-volume pediatric thyroid center to (1) document the change in clinical practice before and after publication of the inaugural ATA Pediatric Guidelines and (2) determine whether there was inferiority in achieving remission when not administering RAIT.

Material and Methods

Data Acquisition

A retrospective chart review was conducted of patients who were diagnosed with DTC <19 years old at the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia (CHOP) between January 2010 and December 2020. The clinical registry of CHOP's Thyroid Center was accessed to analyze information pertaining to patient demographics, medical history, surgical approach, pathology, somatic oncogene alterations, and postoperative surveillance, including Tg, Tg antibodies (TgAbs), and radiological imaging. Surgeries were performed at a single center by 2 high-volume thyroid surgeons (K.K., N.S.A.), defined as completing 25 or more thyroid surgeries annually (6).

Clinical Variables

Primary tumor (T), regional LNs (N), and distant metastasis (M) were assigned using the 8th edition of the AJCC classification for DTC (7). Low-risk DTC was defined in accordance with the ATA Pediatric Guidelines and adjusted based on recent data: disease grossly confined to the thyroid (AJCC T1-T3a staging) with AJCC N0/NX disease or with minimal AJCC N1a disease (metastasis to ≤5 central neck LNs) (5, 8, 9). Two-stage thyroidectomy describes patients who underwent initial lobectomy followed by completion thyroidectomy.

In accordance with the 2015 ATA Pediatric Guidelines, unstimulated Tg and neck ultrasound were used to determine clinical response to therapy. Table 1 outlines the definitions used for dynamic risk stratification at 1 year after initial treatment (±6 months), including excellent response, biochemical incomplete response, structural incomplete response, and indeterminate response (10, 11). Neck ultrasound was used to confirm radiological remission. Negative imaging was defined as having (1) no sonographic evidence of persistent papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) in the thyroid bed, with visualized LNs in the central and lateral neck having benign morphology, and (2) fusiform shape with echogenicity and blood flow limited to the hilum. Indeterminate imaging was defined as (1) having any thyroid bed lesion >5 mm that is not normal-appearing residual thyroid tissue, or (2) having any LN that did not retain normal morphology but without overtly suspicious characteristics such as microcalcifications, cystic degeneration, or peripheral blood flow on Doppler imaging.

Somatic Driver Oncogene Analysis

Somatic driver alterations were detected using (1) CHOP's Comprehensive Solid Tumor Panel (CSTP), (2) Asuragen miRinform Thyroid Test, or (3) ThyGeNEXT. CSTP is a next-generation sequencing panel encompassing 238 genes and 600+ fusions (12, 13). Asuragen miRinform Thyroid Test analyzes the presence of common variants in BRAF, HRAS, KRAS, and NRAS, and fusion transcripts in RET/PTC1, RET/PTC3, and PAX8-PPARγ in pathologic specimens (14). ThyGeNEXT utilizes targeted next-generation sequencing on cytologic specimens to detect common fusion transcripts and DNA mutation variants (15). Somatic oncogenic drivers were categorized according to genotypic variations correlating to differences in disease behavior: low-invasive (PTEN, DICER1, RAS, TSHR, and PAX8-PPARγ fusion) and high-invasive drivers (BRAFV600E mutation and RET, NTRK1/NTRK3, ALK, MET, or BRAF fusions) (16–19). Genetic profiling for a portion of the samples has previously been reported (20). Patients with germline mutations in thyroid tumor predisposition syndromes were excluded from this study.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were run in R 4.1.0 and R Studio 1.4.1717 using packages tidyverse and rstatix (19–21). Median (interquartile range [IQR] Q1 - Q3) were reported for parametric and nonparametric variables. Categorical variables were compared using the 2-tailed Fisher exact test or chi-squared test depending on test sample size. Continuous variables were compared using Mann–Whitney U for nonparametric data. Well-established risk factors for receiving RAIT administration and persistent disease at 1 year after initial treatment, including LN metastasis, tumor size, and year of surgery, were evaluated by multivariate logistic regression (OR; 95% CI). Remission rate of patients with low-invasive and high-invasive drivers were compared using the 2-tailed Fisher exact test. P ≤ .05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Study Population

A retrospective review of 263 patients diagnosed with DTC between 2010 and 2020 was conducted. Of these patients, 168 (168/263, 64%) were excluded due to being diagnosed with ATA intermediate- or high-risk DTC (n = 109), lack of information regarding remission (n = 35), undergoing thyroid lobectomy (n = 14), or undergoing surgery >19 years old (n = 10). A final cohort of 95 patients (78 females; 17 males) diagnosed with DTC meeting ATA low-risk criteria who underwent TT at <19 years of age were included in the study (Table 2). Patients were diagnosed with DTC at a median age of 15.5 years (IQR 14.0-17.1, range 10.2-18.6). Median endocrinology follow-up following DTC diagnosis was 5.1 years (IQR 2.8-7.2, range 0.9-10.9; Table 3).

RAIT Administration

Of the 95 patients, 53% (50/95) and 47% (45/95) were treated with and without RAIT, respectively. RAIT was used to treat 82% (31/38) of patients before 2015 compared with 33% (19/57) of patients after 2015. Between 2014 and 2015, a 37% decline in RAIT administration was observed (Fig. 1). Large tumor size (OR 8.7, 95% CI 1.5-71.7, P = .022), minimal N1a disease (OR 2.8, 95% CI 0.9-9.4, P = .084), and surgery performed before 2015 (OR 10.2, 95% CI 3.5-33.9, P < .001) were factors influencing RAIT administration. Fourteen patients treated with RAIT demonstrated positive TgAb levels (median 5.95 IU/mL; IQR 2.08-60.00) compared with 14 patients treated without RAIT (median 3.40 IU/mL; IQR 1.55-15.75).

Clinicopathologic Features and Remission Rates

Clinicopathologic features of pediatric patients with low-risk DTC treated with and without RAIT are presented in Table 3. Patients receiving RAIT presented with larger tumors (19.5 mm; IQR 12.0-30.0 mm vs 12.0 mm; IQR 7.0-17.5 mm; higher AJCC T status; P < .001); however, AJCC N status was not significantly different between groups (P = .694). No significant differences were observed between age at the time of thyroid surgery (P = .503), surgical approach (P = .127), or LN dissection (P = .564) among patients treated with and without RAIT (Table 3).

Remission rates for patients treated with and without RAIT were not statistically different at 1-year after initial treatment (P = .089) and at last clinical evaluation (P = .534; Table 3). Of the 95 patients who underwent TT, 70% (35/50) and 69% (31/45) of patients treated with and without RAIT, respectively, achieved remission at 1 year after treatment. At last clinical evaluation, remission was achieved in 82% (41/50) and 76% (34/45) of patients treated with and without RAIT, respectively. There were no patients found to have persistent structural disease (Table 3).

Risk Factors for Persistent Disease

RAIT administration, minimal N1a disease, and surgery performed after 2015 were evaluated by univariate and multivariate logistic regression for persistent disease at 1 year after initial treatment. RAIT administration (OR 0.8, 95% CI 0.3-2.4, P = .724), minimal N1a disease (OR 1.5, 95% CI 0.5-4.1, P = .445), and surgery performed after 2015 (OR 1.1, 95% CI 0.4-3.4, P = .857) were not significant risk factors for the development of persistent disease at 1 year after treatment (28/95 indeterminate or persistent disease). No additional risk factors were identified for RAIT administration and persistent disease at 1 year after initial treatment.

Somatic Driver Alterations

Of the 95 patients, 56 underwent somatic oncogene analysis using CHOP's CSTP (40/56), Asuragen miRinform Thyroid Test (17/56), and/or ThyGeNEXT (1/56). Of these patients, 32 demonstrated somatic driver variants on CHOP's CSTP (n = 25), Asuragen miRinform Thyroid Test (n = 6), and ThyGeNEXT (n = 1), with 25 (25/32, 78%) patients harboring high-invasive alterations, and 7 (7/32, 22%) patients harboring low-invasive alterations (Table 4). Identified somatic alterations included 14 (14/32, 44%) BRAFV600E, 9 (9/32, 28%) RET/NTRK3 fusions, 5 (5/32, 16%) RAS (3 NRASQ61R and 2 KRASG12V), 2 (2/32, 6%) DICER1 (1 p.E1813G and 1 p.D1709N), and 2 (2/32, 6%) BRAF fusions. Of these patients, 44% (11/25) of patients harboring high-invasive drivers and 43% (3/7) of patients demonstrating low-invasive drivers were treated with RAIT. At 1 year after treatment, 56% (14/25) of patients with high-invasive alterations achieved remission compared with 86% (6/7) of patients with low-invasive alterations. Of the patients who demonstrated persistent biochemical disease at 1 year after initial treatment, 3 tumors had the somatic BRAFV600E oncogenic variant. Of the 18 patients not treated with RAIT, 64% (9/14) of patients with high-invasive alterations achieved remission compared with 75% (3/4) of patients with low-invasive alterations. No significant difference was found in remission rate at 1 year after initial treatment (P = .212) and at last clinical evaluation (P = .150) between patients harboring high-invasive alterations and low-invasive alterations (Table 4; Fig. 2).

Discussion

This study demonstrates comparable remission rates for ATA pediatric low-risk patients treated with and without RAIT at 1 year after initial treatment (P = .089) and at last clinical evaluation (P = .534; Table 3). Excellent response remission rates (Table 1) at 1 year after TT were similar (70% vs 69%), with rates increasing by 12% (70-82%) and 7% (69-76%) with continued surveillance in patients treated with and without RAIT, respectively (Table 3). Based on this initial study, 1 year after TT appears to be an accurate timepoint for initial assessment of remission in the ATA pediatric low-risk patient population. The difference in remission rate at last evaluation is likely attributed to the varying lengths in median follow-up for patients treated with (5.8 years) and without RAIT (3.6 years), with the majority of patients treated with RAIT before 2015 having a resultant longer period of surveillance (Table 3). This is the first comprehensive analysis to show noninferiority in withholding RAIT for pediatric patients with ATA low-risk DTC and is in line with a study assessing remission rates for adult patients with low-risk thyroid cancer who did not receive RAIT (22).

Over the course of the study, annual RAIT administration gradually declined in accordance with the release of the 2015 ATA Pediatric Guidelines, with 100% (6/6) of patients being treated with RAIT in 2010 compared with 0% (0/7) of patients in 2020 (Fig. 1). Larger tumor size, N1a status, and surgery before 2015 were found to be independent factors for administering RAIT; however, no significant risk factors, including withholding RAIT administration, were found to influence remission status at 1 year after treatment. Interestingly, even in this ATA low-risk cohort, patients harboring high-invasive alterations were less likely to achieve remission compared with patients harboring low-invasive alterations, although the difference did not achieve statistical significance (P = .212; Table 4). Future prospective studies are warranted to explore whether high-invasive somatic oncogenic driver alterations are a risk factor for persistent disease in ATA low-risk patients who do not receive RAIT.

The 2015 ATA Pediatric Guidelines recommended that pediatric patients with low-invasive DTC could achieve remission without the use of RAIT: in other words, there was no benefit to administering RAIT to patients with DTC “grossly confined to the thyroid” (Recommendation 15) (5). This approach mirrored the risk stratification system from the 2009 ATA Adult DTC Guidelines and the clinical applicability of the ATA pediatric risk level stratification system to predict disease status after initial therapy has been supported by a number of pediatric-specific studies (11, 23–27).

In contrast to this approach, the recently published European Thyroid Association (ETA) Pediatric Guidelines recommend that the majority of pediatric patients with DTC undergo TT (Recommendation 10B) followed by RAIT (Recommendation 14A) (28, 29). In addition, the ETA Guidelines do not recommend prophylactic central neck LN dissection for all patients to assess invasive behavior of disease and do not include the ATA pediatric risk levels, or any other classification system, to stratify which patients who undergo TT could achieve remission without RAIT. With the majority of patients receiving RAIT, the ETA Guidelines recommend that patients should be counseled on the potential risk for RAI-associated salivary gland dysfunction, second primary malignancies, and decreased fertility, with consideration for semen preservation in post-pubertal males (Recommendation 28). In practice, the risk of side-effects from RAIT are relatively low. However, in pediatrics, the risks and the long-term consequences of treatment complications are realized over a longer period when compared with the adult DTC population, supporting ongoing efforts to limit the use of RAIT when possible (30, 31).

This study is limited by its single-center retrospective design and variance in the length of clinical follow-up. Our current data are also not powered to discern how ATA risk level, RAIT administration, and oncogenic driver influence the risk for persistent disease. Investigation of clinical outcomes of pediatric DTC patients at 5 years after initial treatment as well as the incorporation of larger, multicenter cohort studies are required to confirm our conclusions (32). To understand and further improve clinical practices for managing pediatric DTC, the authors have created the Child and Adolescent Thyroid Consortium, an international, pediatric registry designed to enhance collaborative efforts across institutes. Through this effort, larger multicenter studies will be conducted to further assess clinical risk factors that may increase the utility of dynamic risk stratification as well as provide improved stratification for surgical and medical therapy of pediatric patients with DTC. Nevertheless, results from the present study fill a needed gap in research and support the 2015 proposed change in clinical practice: to avoid exposure of ATA low-risk pediatric patients to RAIT when there is no clinical benefit.

Conclusion

Withholding RAIT for ATA low-risk pediatric patients who undergo TT does not appear to have a negative impact on remission rates. Dynamic risk stratification at 1 year after initial treatment appears to be the earliest time point to assess the impact of withholding RAIT for ATA low-risk pediatric patients. Continued surveillance is recommended to ensure stable, long-term remission.
