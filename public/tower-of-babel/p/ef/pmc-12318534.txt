Ex vivo fluorescence-guided resection margin assessment in breast cancer surgery using a topically applied, cathepsin-activatable imaging agent

ABSTRACT
Up to 40 % of breast cancer patients have a tumor-positive resection margin (TPRM) – defined as cancer cells at the surface of the resected specimen – after breast-conserving surgery (BCS), necessitating re-resection or boost radiation. To prevent these additional treatments, intraoperative near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging with the topically applied, cathepsin-activatable imaging agent AKRO-6qcICG might be used to detect TPRMs and guide additional resection. Here, to validate its performance, the agent is topically applied to all surfaces of freshly resected breast cancer specimens (n = 11 patients) and to 3–5 mm thick tissue slices of the specimens (n = 26 patients). NIR fluorescence images of the resection surfaces and tissue slices are acquired and correlated to final histopathology. AKRO-6qcICG detects TPRMs with a sensitivity, specificity, PVV, and NPV of 100 %, 67 %, 10 %, and 100 %, respectively. On the tissue slices, the fluorescence signal has a median tumor-to-background ratio of 1.8. These findings indicate that topically applied AKRO-6qcICG can visualize TPRMs ex vivo with a high sensitivity and NPV, with sufficient contrast to adjacent healthy breast tissue.

Introduction

Breast cancer remains the most frequently diagnosed malignancy and the leading cause of cancer-related mortality in women worldwide [1]. Most newly diagnosed breast cancer patients can be treated with breast-conserving surgery (BCS) – also known as lumpectomy - combined with adjuvant radiation therapy [2,3]. Although the survival rate with such treatment is equivalent to that of total mastectomy, BCS has a greater risk of tumor-positive resection margins and hence local recurrence [4–6]. A tumor-positive resection margin after BCS occurs in approximately 10–40 % of patients and necessitates re-resection or boost radiation therapy [7–10]. These additional treatments are associated with increased morbidity, negative impact on cosmetic outcome, complication risks, healthcare costs, and adverse psychological effects [11].

Numerous pathology and imaging methods for intraoperative guidance and margin assessment have been evaluated to decrease tumor-positive resection margin rates after BCS, but most have significant clinical and technical limitations that have precluded wide-spread adoption [12]. An ideal method for margin assessment during BCS would be capable of detecting residual malignancy on the surgical cavity surface rapidly, non-invasively, and in real-time. Preferably it would also be low-cost and easy to use. A technique that could meet these requirements is intraoperative tumor-targeted near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging. This technique allows real-time optical imaging by selectively highlighting tissues that overexpress certain molecular targets. It combines the administration of a tumor-targeted fluorescence contrast agent with the use of a fluorescence-sensitive camera system, matched to operate in the range of NIR fluorescence light (700–900 nm) [13].

To date, most targeted contrast agents under investigation for fluorescence-guided BCS are administered intravenously, necessarily days or hours prior to surgery [14,15]. As an alternative to systemic delivery, contrast agents could be topically applied onto the surgical cavity after the lumpectomy is performed, to visualize potential residual tumor tissue and guide its resection. This topical approach would result in much faster tumor visualization, better integration into the surgical workflow, lower risk of systemic side effects and would be more patient-friendly and cost-effective compared with systemic administration. Several topical fluorescent imaging agents have been tested in vivo in a preclinical setting and ex vivo on resected human breast cancer specimens, but these agents were not able to adequately distinguish malignant from benign breast tissue [16,17].

A promising topically applied contrast agent for fluorescence-guided BCS is the fluorescently quenched, protease-activatable imaging agent AKRO-6qcICG, whose molecular structure is composed of indocyanine-green (ICG) and a NIR quencher conjugated to a peptide Cbz-Phe-Lys core [18]. It is a so called “smart” imaging agent that only produces a fluorescent signal when processed by a tumor-specific enzyme, essentially targeting ICG – which normally lacks specificity – to tumor tissue, reminiscent to the action of some ICG-nanoparticles [19]. AKRO-6qcICG is cleaved and activated by the cysteine cathepsin L and — to a lesser extent — cathepsins B and S, all proteolytic enzymes that are highly upregulated in breast cancer compared with healthy breast tissue [20–22]. The cellular sources of these cathepsins are primarily tumor-associated macrophages, and to some extent breast cancer cells as well [20]. AKRO-6qcICG exploits a latent lysosomotropic effect (LLE) that causes accumulation of the fluorescent fragments of the imaging agent in lysosomes, resulting in increased signal strength and duration [18]. In a preclinical study, AKRO-6qcICG has successfully been used as a thermosensitive gel formulation comprising poloxamer 407 and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) that is liquid at room temperature and semi-solid at body temperature, which consequently prevents pooling and ensures uniform distribution of the imaging agent to tissue [23]. In this formulation, AKRO-6qcICG could clearly and rapidly differentiate between breast cancer and healthy breast tissue in vivo in an orthotopic mouse model, and ex vivo in the freshly resected breast cancer specimen of one patient [23].

The current study aims to validate the ability of AKRO-6qcICG to visualize human breast cancer and to further investigate its potential for margin assessment during BCS. To this end, the imaging gel was topically applied ex vivo onto the resection surfaces and tissue slices of freshly resected breast cancer specimens of an extended patient cohort.

Patients and methods

Patients

Female patients with invasive breast cancer or ductal carcinoma in situ (DCIS) scheduled to undergo surgery at the Haaglanden Medical Center (HMC), The Hague, The Netherlands were enrolled in the study between December 2021 and October 2022. HMC, a large teaching hospital, performs 350–400 breast cancer surgeries annually. To prevent the chance of absence of tumor cells in the resection specimen, patients undergoing a re-resection or who received neoadjuvant treatment were excluded. Due to logistic infeasibility, patients who received intraoperative radiotherapy were excluded. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Patients’ written informed consent was obtained before any study-related procedure was performed. IRB approval was waived by the ethics committee of the HMC due to the ex vivo methodology of the study (N21.077). All human tissues used in this study were procured in line with WHO Guiding Principles on Human Cell, Tissue and Organ Transplantation.

Ex vivo topical application of AKRO-6qcICG and macroscopic NIR fluorescence imaging

The study workflow is graphically summarized in Fig. 1A. All breast cancer surgeries were performed according to standard-of-care. Immediately after resection, the breast cancer specimen was marked with sutures for orientational purposes according to hospital protocol and brought to the Pathology Department. The thermosensitive AKRO-6qcICG imaging gel – an investigational drug product - comprised of AKRO-6qcICG and poloxamer 407 (Pluronic® F-127, Sigma-Aldrich, St Louis, MO USA or Poloxamer 407, Duchefa Farma, Haarlem, NL), solubilized in saline or water for injection and DMSO.

Different concentrations of the poloxamer and DMSO were tested, ranging between 13 % and 19 % mass/volume and 0–17.5 % volume/volume respectively, in a total of 26 patients enrolled in the study. Two different batches of AKRO-6qcICG were used. The first was produced at Stanford University (patients 1–16), the second at the Leiden University Medical Center (LUMC) (patients 17–26). Table S1 summarizes the composition of the gel used for each individual patient. AKRO-6qcICG was originally developed as 6qc-ICG by Dr. Bogyo at Stanford University and licensed to Akrotome Imaging Inc. and hence renamed AKRO-6qcICG [18].

In patient 16–26, following a standardized and validated procedure, one mL of 50 μM imaging gel was topically applied onto each resection surface (cranial, caudal, ventral, dorsal, lateral, and medial) of the freshly resected specimen directly upon arrival in the Pathology Department using a cotton-tipped applicator, and the tissue incubated for 10 minutes at room temperature. AKRO-6qcICG has been shown to fluoresce mildly in its inactivated form [23]. Therefore, to diminish background signal, the imaging gel was washed off five times with 100 mL of sterile saline and the tissue was dried with sterile gauze. During incubation and after each washing step, NIR fluorescence images of the resection surfaces were acquired with the Pearl® Trilogy Imaging System (LI-COR, Lincoln, NE, USA) in the 800 nm wavelength channel. This “black box” NIR fluorescence camera system was chosen for this validation study as it allows for keeping variables such as ambient light, imaging angle, and distance constant. In all 26 patients, the resected specimen was (subsequently) inked, cooled at −80 °C for 5 minutes, and sliced into 3–5 millimeter-thick slices according to hospital protocol. One mL of AKRO-6qcICG imaging gel was applied onto the entire surface of the tissue slice with the macroscopically largest tumor diameter and the two adjacent slices using a cotton-tipped applicator. After 10 minutes of incubation at room temperature, the imaging gel was washed off 3–5 times (washing 5 times was not always possible due to the friable nature of breast tissue. The tissue slices had to remain intact for proper standard of care histopathological assessment). During incubation and after each washing step, NIR fluorescence images of the tissue slices were acquired using the Pearl® Trilogy Imaging System in the 800 nm wavelength channel. Different AKRO-6qcICG concentrations (20, 50 and 100 μM) were tested on the tissue slices (Table S1). In the first three patients, while validating the procedure and logistics, a uniform high concentration of 100 μM was used. In the subsequent four patients, a different concentration was used for each tissue slice. Since the 50 μM concentration optically provided the most optimal signals, it was selected for all remaining patients.

Histology

The 3–5 mm thick tissue slices were cut in smaller parts that were fixed with formalin and embedded in paraffin (FFPE). All FFPE tissue blocks (n = 372) were cut into sequential 4 μm thick tissue sections that were mounted on adhesive slides. The first 4 μm tissue section of each FFPE tissue block, from the side on which the imaging agent was topically applied, was reserved for later microscopic NIR fluorescence analysis. The next sequential tissue section was stained for routine hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) histology. The next two sequential sections were reserved for immunohistochemistry. On the H&E slides, tumor-positive regions were delineated by a board-certified pathologist (H.M. H.) blinded for the fluorescent signal. Resection margins were assessed according to the hospital’s protocol. A tumor-positive resection margin was defined as ink on tumor (i.e., the presence of pathology ink at the surface of the surgical specimen on either invasive tumor cells or DCIS at microscopic evaluation) [24]. For each resected specimen, the extent and location of the tumor-positive margin(s) or the width and location of the smallest tumor-free margin was recorded as per standard of care. All H&E slides were scanned using the Philips IntelliSite Pathology Solution (Philips Electronics, Eindhoven, The Netherlands).

NIR fluorescence signal analysis

All macroscopic NIR fluorescence images of the resection surfaces and 3–5 mm thick tissue slices acquired with the Pearl® Trilogy Imaging System were analyzed using the QuPath software version 0.2.3 (University of Edinburgh, Edinburgh, Scotland, Great Britain) and MeVisLab software version 3.4.1 (MeVis Medical Solution, Bremen, Germany), as described previously [25,26]. The original NIR fluorescence images of the resection surfaces and tissue slices were imported into QuPath. Based on the pathology report and delineated H&E slides, tumor-positive areas were manually annotated. Using the expand annotation function, the 0.5 cm border area around the tumor was automatically computed as background. Finally, the whole tissue slice was also then annotated to prevent the standardized 0.5 cm background rim from protruding outside the tissue. The three annotated areas (tumor, background, and whole tissue slice) were exported into the MeVisLab network.

The mean fluorescence intensity (MFI), defined as the total fluorescent signal divided by the number of pixels, was calculated for each annotated area. The tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) was defined as the MFI of the tumor area divided by the MFI in the background rim area.

An example of the NIR fluorescence signal analysis performed is shown in Figure S1. In line with clinical fluorescence-guided surgery trials, areas that showed a fluorescence signal with a TBR ≥ 1.5 were defined as fluorescence-positive and areas that had a TBR < 1.5 as fluorescence-negative [27–29]. Using the same quantification method in QuPath and MeVisLab, signal-to-background ratios (SBR) of fluorescent areas on tumor-negative resection surfaces were calculated. Since the surfaces of the resection surfaces were larger compared to the tissue slices, a 1 cm border around the tumor was used as background to include more potential false-positive areas.

Immunohistochemistry for cathepsin L and CD68

Two sequential 4 μm tissue sections of each FFPE tissue block were immunohistochemically stained for respectively cathepsin L and CD68. The tissue sections were deparaffinized using xylene and rehydrated in decreasing concentrations of ethanol. Slides were subsequently rinsed with demineralized water, and endogenous peroxidase was blocked with 0.3 % hydrogen peroxidase (Merck Millipore, Darmstadt, Germany) for 20 minutes at room temperature. Slides were then rinsed with demineralized water, and heat-mediated antigen retrieval was performed in the PT Link (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using Target Retrieval Solution pH 6.0 at 95°C. After rinsing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4), slides were incubated overnight at room temperature with primary antibodies, diluted in 1 % bovine serum albumin/PBS, against cathepsin L (clone ab6314, Abcam, dilution 1:3600, mouse monoclonal) and CD68 (clone KP1, Dako, dilution 1:10000, mouse monoclonal). After three PBS washing steps, the slides were incubated with a horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-labelled secondary antibody against either mouse or rabbit IgG (EnVision, Agilent, Santa Clara, USA) for 30 min at room temperature. Binding of the antibody was visualized using 3,30-diaminobenzidine (DAB) (Agilent, Santa Clara, USA). All slides were counterstained with hematoxylin for 10–15 seconds, dehydrated at 37°C, and mounted with pertex. All immunohistochemically stained slides were scanned using the Pannoramic 250 Flash III scanner (3D Histech, Budapest, Hungary).

Quantification of immunohistochemical staining

The expression of cathepsin L in tumor and adjacent healthy tissue in all tumor-positive 4 μm tissue slides (n = 143) was quantified as a mean immunohistochemical staining intensity (MSI) by means of a previously described objective scoring method using the ImageJ software (version 1.53 h, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) [30,31]. In each slide, one region of interest (ROI) containing the tumor tissue and one containing all the healthy tissue was drawn, based on the annotations by the pathologist on the H&E slides. The MSI of the ROIs was measured as follows: 1) the plugin colour_deconvolution2 was used to remove hematoxylin staining, to make sure only the DAB staining was analyzed, 2) the image was converted to grey-scale, and 3) the image was inverted. ImageJ subsequently calculated an MSI value between 0 (black, no staining signal) and 255 (white, maximal staining signal) for each ROI and determined its area. The MSI in tumor and normal tissue from the individual 4 μm slides of each tissue slice were combined and corrected for area to create a weighted average MSI per tissue slice (n = 66). For each tissue slice, the MSI ratio was calculated by dividing the weighted average MSI in tumor tissue by the weighted average MSI in healthy tissue. Consequently, the fluorescence signal intensities in the tissue slices could objectively be compared to the cathepsin L expression in the same tissue slice.

Microscopic NIR fluorescence imaging

The first cut 4 μm tissue section of each FFPE tissue block was deparaffinized and scanned using the NIR fluorescence-sensitive Odyssey CLx (LI-COR, Lincoln, Nebraska USA) in the 800 nm wavelength channel. Subsequently, 21 of these slides that together represented four tissue slices of four different patients were mounted with EverBrite Hardset with DAPI (Biotium, Fremont, CA, USA) and scanned with the ZEISS Axio Scan.Z1 fluorescence slide scanner (ZEISS, Oberkochen, Germany), using the 735 nm excitation laser and >760 nm detection filter.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 29 software (SPSS, IBM Corporation, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA). To investigate differences in MFI between tumor tissue and adjacent healthy breast tissue on the resection surfaces and tissue slices, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used. The same test was used to investigate differences in cathepsin L expression between tumor tissue and healthy breast tissue. The Kruskal-Wallis test was performed to investigate differences in TBR between the three different imaging agent concentration groups. The Spearman’s correlation coefficient was determined to investigate the correlation between the MFI of the whole resection surface and the margin width, the correlation between the TBR and the tumor size, and the correlation between the TBR and the cathepsin L MSI ratio in the tissue slices. All statistical tests were performed two sided and results were considered statistically significant at the level of p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics are depicted as mean (standard deviation, sd) or median (interquartile range, IQR). Data are summarized in graphs generated by GraphPad Prism 9. Figures were generated with biorender.com.

Results

Patient and tumor characteristics

Patient and tumor characteristics are summarized in Table 1. In total, 26 patients with histologically proven breast cancer who underwent surgery between December 2021 and October 2022 were enrolled. Median age at surgery was 68 years (IQR 54–73.5). Twenty patients (77 %) had invasive cancer, one (4 %) had pure DCIS, and five (19 %) had both. All invasive tumors were estrogen receptor (ER)-positive and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2)-negative. Twenty-one (81 %) invasive tumors were progesterone receptor (PR)-positive. Histologic analysis after surgery showed Bloom-Richardson histology grade 1 in eleven tumors, grade 2 in twelve tumors, and grade 3 in two tumors. The median Ki-67 labelling index was 5 % (IQR 2–6). The median size of the tumor in the greatest diameter, as determined by a pathologist, was 14 mm (IQR 9.75–20.5). Three patients (12 %) had a tumor-positive resection margin. Only two of these tumor-positive resection surfaces were imaged, as imaging of the resection surfaces was only performed in patient 16–26.

In patients 16–26, both the resection surfaces (cranial, caudal, medial, lateral, anterior, and posterior) and 3–5 mm thick tissue slices of the freshly resected specimen were imaged after topical application of AKRO-6qcICG. In patients 1–15, only the tissue slices of the resected specimen were imaged. Table S1 provides an overview of the resection surfaces and tissue slices that were imaged in each patient, the imaging agent concentrations that were used and the number of washing steps performed. In total, 63 resection surfaces and 78 tissue slices were imaged. Two (3 %) of the 63 resection surfaces that were coated with imaging gel and imaged were tumor-positive (i.e., a tumor-positive surgical margin). Sixty-six (85 %) of the 78 tissue slices contained tumor tissue.

Topically applied AKRO-6qcICG imaging gel detects tumor-positive resection surfaces ex vivo

The ex vivo topical application workflow is summarized in Fig. 1A. In 11 patients, a total of 63 resection surfaces were imaged after topical application of AKRO-6qcICG (in one patient, three resection surfaces could not be imaged due to the shape of the specimen). Two of the 63 resection surfaces that were imaged were tumor-positive. Both these tumor-positive resection surfaces were fluorescent (tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) ≥ 1.5). Forty-two of the 61 tumor-negative resection surfaces were not fluorescent (signal-to-background ratio (SBR) < 1.5). This corresponds to 2 true positives, 42 true negatives, 19 false positives, and 0 false negatives. Therefore, the sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value of topically applied AKRO-6qcICG for the detection of tumor-positive resection surfaces in these patients was 100 %, 67 %, 10 %, and 100 %, respectively. To illustrate, Fig. 1B1 and B3 show the white light and NIR fluorescence images of respectively a tumor-negative and tumor-positive resection surface of two different patients. As shown in Figure S2, a strong correlation was found between the MFI of the resection surface and the resection margin width (r = −.74, p < 0.01).

Topically applied AKRO-6qcICG imaging gel visualizes breast cancer with adequate contrast to healthy tissue on 3–5 mm thick tissue slices

For histopathological correlation of the fluorescence signal, three 3–5 mm thick tissue slices of each resected specimen were imaged after topical application of AKRO-6qcICG. Fig. 1B2 and B4 show the white light, NIR fluorescence, and corresponding H&E histopathology slides of two 3–5 mm thick tissue slices. The fluorescence signal overlaps with the area annotated by the pathologist as tumor.

Of the 66 tumor-containing 3–5 mm thick tissue slices that were imaged, 53 (80 %) showed a TBR ≥ 1.5 after the final washing step. The histopathologically-proven viable tumor in the tissue slices showed a significantly higher fluorescence signal intensity compared with adjacent healthy breast tissue in the same slice (median MFI = 0.041 in tumor vs. 0.030 in healthy tissue, p < 0.05) (Fig. 1C). The batch of AKRO-6qcICG produced at the LUMC gave a higher absolute fluorescence signal intensity in both tumor and healthy tissue, compared with the batch produced at Stanford University, as illustrated by the two groups in Fig. 1C. However, the TBRs did not differ significantly between the two batches (median TBR = 1.76 in the Stanford batch vs. 1.85 in the LUMC batch, p = 0.785). This difference in absolute fluorescence intensities between the two batches could be explained by prolonged storage (> 4 years) of the Stanford batch before use. The LUMC batch was used within a year after production.

The median TBR after the final washing step in all 66 tumor-positive tissue slices was 1.8 (IQR 1.6–2.1). There was no significant difference in TBR between the three different AKRO-6qcICG concentrations that were tested (median TBR = 1.57 for 100 μM vs. 1.85 for 50 μM vs. 1.91 for 20 μM, p = 0.38) (Fig. 1D). Additionally, the tumor size did not correlate significantly with the TBR (r = 0.074, p = 0.555).

Increased number of washing steps improved the tumor-to-background ratio

Forty-six tumor-containing tissue slices were washed 5 times (not all tissue slices could be washed 5 times due to the friable nature of breast tissue). The median MFI in tumor tissue and adjacent healthy breast tissue (or background) both decreased with each washing step (Fig. 2A). However, because the background signal in most patients had a relatively larger decrease with consecutive washing steps, the median TBR increased with each washing step (Fig. 2B). After 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 washing steps the median TBR was 1.22, 1.62, 1.64, 1.67, 1.70, and 1.80, respectively. In Fig. 2C, the effect of multiple washing steps on the fluorescence signal in one tissue slice is shown.

Levels of cathepsin L expression correlate with the intensity and localization of the AKRO-6qcICG fluorescence signal in patient samples

All breast tumors in this study showed cathepsin L expression to some extent. The median mean staining intensity (MSI) of cathepsin L in tumor tissue and adjacent healthy breast tissue was 12.2 (IQR 7.9–20.0) and 6.8 (IQR 5.1–9.0), respectively. Fig. 2D shows the cathepsin L MSI in the tumor and adjacent healthy breast tissue for each tumor-containing tissue slice (n = 66). Cathepsin L expression was significantly higher in tumor tissue compared with adjacent healthy tissue (median cathepsin L MSI = 12.2 in tumor vs. 6.8 in healthy tissue, p < 0.05). The median cathepsin L MSI ratio between tumor and healthy tissue was 1.9 (IQR 1.2–2.6). As shown in Fig. 2E, the cathepsin L MSI ratio as determined by immunohistochemistry correlated significantly with the fluorescence signal intensity on the tissue slices, defined as TBR (r = 0.40, p < 0.05). Discussion

The near real-time detection of residual tumor during BCS using topically applied, tumor-targeted NIR fluorescence imaging agents has the potential to reduce tumor-positive resection margin rates and prevent additional treatments with inherent morbidities. Our validation study demonstrates that AKRO-6qcICG allows for clear ex vivo breast cancer visualization. Topical application of the imaging agent resulted in a significantly higher fluorescence signal in tumor tissue compared to adjacent healthy breast tissue, with a median TBR of 1.8. It allowed the detection of all tumor-positive resection surfaces and 80 % of all tumor tissue in the tissue slices.

It has previously been described that a thick layer of quenched (i.e., unactivated) AKRO-6qcICG imaging gel generates a fluorescent signal [23]. Therefore, to flush off excess, inactive imaging gel, multiple washing steps were performed after the 10 minutes incubation time, and the effect on the TBR was investigated. We demonstrate that already after one to two washing steps an adequate contrast between tumor and healthy breast tissue is established. The TBR further increased with each consecutive washing step. These findings correspond to the optimal number of washing steps found for a different Pluronic-based, nerve specific, NIR fluorescence imaging gel [32]. Since each step takes less than one minute, washing the gel off up to five times would be feasible in a clinical setting. Based on this ex vivo validation study, we anticipate that the total added time for in vivo use during BCS, including probe application onto the surgical cavity walls, incubation, washing, and imaging, would be approximately 15–20 minutes.

In BCS, tumor-targeted NIR fluorescence imaging for tumor-positive margin detection could be used both in vivo on the resection cavity surfaces and ex vivo on the resected specimen [14,15,33]. However, due to the soft, pliable nature of a resected breast cancer specimen, its geometry frequently does not accurately correspond to that of the resection cavity [34]. Consequently, correlating an ex vivo detected tumor-positive resection surface to the in vivo location of the residual tumor that needs to be excised could be difficult. Therefore, detection of residual tumor tissue on the surgical cavity walls seems to be the most promising approach. Additionally, it’s important to consider that the surgical cavity temperature may be (well) below 37°C, which is a critical factor in selecting the final formulation of the thermosensitive gel. Future clinical trials with topically applied (activatable) tumor-targeted NIR imaging agents, such as AKRO-6qcICG, should focus on a combination of ex vivo and in vivo use. As AKRO-6qcICG features the FDA-approved fluorophore ICG, the imaging agent is highly compatible with virtually all existing approved clinical imaging systems.

In our patients, nineteen (31 %) of the 61 tumor-negative resection surfaces showed a false-positive fluorescence signal, resulting in a moderate specificity and low positive predictive value, comparable to that reported for an intravenously applied cathepsin-activatable imaging agent, that was recently approved by the FDA [35,36]. Cathepsin-activatable imaging agents detect upregulated cathepsin expression and/or activity in any tissue type, not just in the tumor. Our immunohistochemical analysis indicates that the false-positive AKRO-6qcICG signal in this study may be caused by cathepsin L expression in connective tissue surrounding the tumor. This is in line with our finding that there is a significant negative correlation between the fluorescence signal intensity of a resection surface and the resection margin width, which has also been observed by others [35]. We hypothesize that the compressing stress that the expanding tumor mass exerts on the surrounding tissues induces extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling in those tissues [37]. Consequently, cathepsins - proteolytic enzymes that play a major role in protein turnover - may be upregulated to facilitate the ECM remodeling [21]. The resulting false-positive fluorescence signal could complicate accurate assessment of the margin status using this imaging technique.

We also found that the tumor tissue in 13 tissue slices could not be detected (TBR < 1.5) using topically applied AKRO-6qcICG. In these tumors the cathepsin L expression ratio between tumor and healthy tissue was lower compared with the tissue slices in which the tumor could be detected (1.4 vs. 2.0). This was mainly due to higher cathepsin L expression levels in the surrounding healthy tissue (8.8 vs. 6.6) and suggests the ratio of cathepsin L expression between tumor and adjacent normal tissue is a determinant for detectability. This is supported by our finding that the TBR moderately correlates with the cathepsin L MSI ratio. However, these TBR differences could also (partly) be explained by variations in cathepsin activity levels between those tumors, which cannot be detected with immunohistochemical staining.

For the detection of residual tumor in the surgical cavity, a high sensitivity and negative predictive value are most important. This way, no residual tumor will be missed and fluorescence negative areas can be left untouched. In contrast, a false-positive signal caused by cathepsin activity in normal tissue, if not omnipresent, is manageable, as the additional resection of a small volume of tissue after BCS does not seem to greatly affect cosmetic results, especially when compared to a second surgery in case of tumor-positive resection margins [38,39]. However, the additional resection of large quantities of tissue based on potentially false-positive fluorescence signals could result in prolonged surgery times, impose additional workload on the pathology department, and adversely affect cosmetic results. These factors must be considered during the cost-benefit analysis of future clinical trials.

The TBRs found in these patients are not extremely high compared to other tumor-targeted fluorescent imaging agents. This could partly be due to the inactivation of cathepsins after resection and during the freeze-thaw cycle. Additionally, it could be the result of the TBR calculation method used. The 0.5 cm border around the tumor was used as background, because this area represents the contrast that is relevant intraoperatively (i.e., the way a surgeon would interpret a resection margin as tumor-positive or tumor-negative using the fluorescence images). Due to scattering, the border around the tumor has a relatively higher fluorescence signal intensity compared to the other healthy tissue in the tissue slices. This is reflected by the fact that the median TBR in all tumor-containing tissue slices is 2.5 (IQR 1.9 – 3.1) when the whole tissue slice is used as background instead of only the 0.5 cm border. Additionally, using the whole tissue slice as background decreases the false-negative rate (i.e., the percentage of tumor-containing tissue slices with a TBR < 1.5) from 20 % to 9 %. However, the ‘border’ method used in the current study is more appropriate for final clinical translation and implementation. No significant difference in TBR between the different imaging agent concentrations investigated were found.

Although our results are promising, the current study has some limitations. First, cooling of the resected specimen at −80 °C before slicing, and the time taken between resection and topical AKRO-6qcICG application (approximately 5–10 minutes for the resection surfaces and 25–30 minutes for the tissue slices) could result in a decreased cathepsin activity, and consequently a moderated imaging agent activation and NIR fluorescence signal. With future in vivo use by application to the resection cavity walls, these issues are mitigated, and the NIR fluorescence signal intensities will potentially be much higher. Secondly, there is a margin of error intrinsic to the drawing of tumor-positive areas for the MFI and immunohistochemistry intensity calculations. To minimize variability, the tumor delineations drawn by the pathologist on the HE slides were copied in detail onto the NIR fluorescence images of the tissue slices and the background area was automatically generated. Still, minor differences in delineations could occur since the drawing of the tumor delineations is a manual process. Finally, expression of cathepsins as measured here is not indicative of activity, which is the read out of imaging agent activation (cathepsins are pro-enzymes that require further post-translational cleavage to elicit protease activity). Therefore, the immunohistochemistry may over-estimate active protease associated with the fluorescence signal. Nonetheless, a correlation between fluorescence and protease expression was found.

The results of the current study demonstrate the potential of AKRO-6qcICG to detect residual tumor in the surgical cavity during BCS in near real-time. The feasibility of the in vivo use of AKRO-6qcICG will be investigated in an upcoming phase I/II clinical trial. Furthermore, provided that the imaging agent translates successfully to the clinic, this topical imaging agent could also be used for NIR fluorescence imaging of other cathepsin overexpressing cancer types, such as lung and colorectal tumors [21,40,41].
