Cell-type-specific dysregulation of mitochondrial calcium signaling in Alzheimer’s disease
Authors: Shatakshi Shukla, Ashlesha A. Kadam, Shanikumar Goyani, Natasha Jaiswal, Kunal Samantaray, Darpan Raghav, Shiridhar Kashyap, Dhanendra Tomar, Pooja Jadiya
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by amyloid plaques, tau tangles, and synaptic dysfunction. Despite decades of research, effective disease-modifying therapies remain elusive, highlighting the need for alternative therapeutic targets. While neurons have traditionally been the focus of AD studies, increasing evidence underscores critical roles for glial cells particularly microglia and astrocytes in disease progression. Mitochondrial calcium (mCa2+) dysregulation has emerged as a key contributor to neurodegeneration, yet how mCa2⁺ signaling varies across brain cell types and contributes to AD pathology remains poorly understood.MethodsWe developed stable human SH-SY5Y (neuroblastoma-derived cells), HMC3 (microglial-like cells), and SVGp12 (astrocytic-like cells) immortalized cell lines expressing APP mutations (Swedish, Florida, and London; APPswe/F/L). We assessed mitochondrial calcium uniporter (mtCU) expression, mCa2+ flux using ratiometric calcium (Ca2+) indicators, and evaluated calcium retention capacity (mito-CRC) as a readout of mitochondrial permeability transition pore opening. Bioenergetic parameters including ATP, NADH, membrane potential, and oxygen consumption rate (OCR) were measured alongside structural mitochondrial changes, ROS levels, and cell death using imaging and biochemical assays.ResultsAPPswe/F/L expression induced mitochondrial dysfunction across all brain immortalized cell types, with neuroblastoma-derived cells exhibiting the highest susceptibility to mCa2+ overload, energy failure, and cell death. Conversely, neuroblastoma-derived cells displayed faster mCa2+ uptake at low Ca2+ levels, indicating distinct regulatory thresholds. Glial-like cells exhibited more elaborate mitochondrial networks and enhanced metabolic capacity, yet all cell types showed impaired mitochondrial structure, reduced membrane potential and respiration, and increased ROS under mutant APP expression.ConclusionsThis study reveals cell-type-specific differences in mCa2+ signaling and mitochondrial function in AD, uncovering unique vulnerabilities in neuroblastoma-derived and glial-like cells. These findings highlight the need for cell-targeted strategies to restore mCa2+ homeostasis and mitochondrial function in AD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12964-025-02460-0.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder marked by extracellular amyloid-beta (Aβ) plaque accumulation, tau-associated neurofibrillary tangles, synaptic dysfunction, and cognitive decline. Despite decades of research, disease-modifying therapies remain elusive, and recent approaches targeting Aβ and tau have shown only limited clinical success [1–3]. This highlights a critical need to identify earlier cellular events and alternative therapeutic targets. While neuronal degeneration is a hallmark of AD, increasing evidence indicates that glial dysfunction, particularly involving microglia and astrocytes plays a central role in disease onset and progression [4–7]. These glial cells maintain brain homeostasis through immune surveillance, synaptic support, and metabolic coupling with neurons [5, 6], and their dysfunction is associated with gliosis, neuroinflammation, and neuronal loss [6, 8, 9]. However, the molecular basis for cell-type-specific vulnerability in AD remains poorly defined.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is one of the earliest and most consistent features of AD, preceding overt Aβ and tau pathology [10–12]. As key regulators of energy production and cell survival, mitochondria are critical for maintaining neuronal activity and glial support. Mitochondrial impairment disrupts ATP generation, increases oxidative stress, and weakens neuron–glia communication—factors that contribute to synaptic failure, inflammation, and neurodegeneration [13]. Yet, how mitochondrial function is differentially regulated across brain cell types in AD remains unclear. Mitochondrial calcium (mCa2+) plays a central role in coupling cytosolic signaling to mitochondrial metabolism and cell fate decisions. Ca2+ enters the mitochondrial matrix via the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (mtCU), a multi-subunit complex in the inner mitochondrial membrane (IMM), driven by membrane potential (Δψ) [14–16]. The core pore-forming subunit, MCU [14, 17, 18], is supported by regulators such as MCUB (a dominant-negative homolog of MCU), MCUR1 (MCU regulator 1), EMRE (Essential MCU Regulator Element), and Ca2+-sensing proteins MICU1, MICU2, and MICU3, which control the threshold and kinetics of Ca2+ uptake [15, 16, 19–25]. Proper mCa2+ uptake promotes oxidative phosphorylation and mitochondrial dehydrogenase activity [26–29], while excessive uptake (mCa2+overload) leads to mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening, membrane depolarization, oxidative stress, and cell death [27, 30, 31].

Evidence from AD mouse models and human datasets shows early disruption of mCa2+ homeostasis in neurons [11]. For example, neuron-specific loss of NCLX, the mCa2+ efflux transporter, exacerbates Aβ pathology and cognitive decline in the 3xTg-AD mouse model, while its overexpression mitigates neurodegeneration [11]. Increased neuronal mCa2+ levels have also been correlated with amyloid plaque deposition in the APP/PS1-Tg mouse model [32]. In addition, MICU3 downregulation in Tg-SwDI mice correlates with neuronal loss and glial activation [33]. However, most of these studies have focused on neurons, overlooking glial mitochondria despite their critical roles in metabolism, immune regulation, and neuroprotection [34]. Transcriptomic data from AD patient brains suggest widespread dysregulation of mtCU components [32], but how mCa2+ signaling differs across neurons, microglia, and astrocytes and how these differences contribute to AD pathogenesis remains unclear.

Mitochondrial function is highly heterogeneous across brain regions and cell types. Proteomic and transcriptomic studies have shown that mitochondrial composition, respiratory capacity, and metabolic specialization vary significantly between neurons and glia [21, 35–37]. For example, neurons rely primarily on oxidative phosphorylation, while astrocytes favor glycolysis [38]. Microglia exhibit unique redox and immunometabolic profiles. Astrocytes express more peroxisomal and fatty acid metabolism proteins, and brain mitochondria differ from those in peripheral tissues in their mtCU regulation and mCa2+ uptake thresholds [37, 39, 40]. This diversity may influence each cell type’s response to metabolic stress and contribute to their selective vulnerability in AD. We hypothesized that mCa2+ regulation and mitochondrial function differ fundamentally across brain cell types and shape their responses to AD pathology. To test this, we generated immortalized human neuroblastoma-derived cells (SH-SY5Y), microglial-like cells (HMC3), and astrocytic-like cells (SVGp12) stably expressing familial AD-linked APPswe/F/L mutations. These cell lines were used to systematically profile mtCU expression, mCa2+ flux, mitochondrial structure, respiration, oxidative stress, and susceptibility to cell death. We acknowledge that these experiments were conducted in isolated cell culture systems using immortalized cell lines, which do not fully replicate the complex cellular crosstalk and physiological characteristics of primary neurons, astrocytes, and microglia in the brain. This represents a key limitation of study. However, despite these constraints, the use of well-characterized cell models allows for controlled, cell-type-specific investigation of mCa2+ dysregulation in the context of AD-linked mutations. These findings provide valuable mechanistic insights and lay the groundwork for future studies in more physiologically relevant systems, including primary cultures and in vivo models. Ultimately, this study contributes to identifying potential therapeutic targets tailored to the distinct metabolic demands and vulnerabilities of different brain cell types in Alzheimer’s disease.

Methods

Plasmids and chemicals

APPswe/F/L plasmid was purchased from Vecto Builder (Cat# VB220324-1227aqs) and pCMV6-A-Puro (Cat# PS100025) was purchased from Origene. Plasmid transfection was performed using FUGENE transfection reagent (Promega, Cat# E5911) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix was purchased from Thermo fisher Scientific (Cat# A25777). Carbonyl cyanide-p-trifluoromethoxyphenylhydrazone (FCCP) was obtained from MilliporeSigma (Cat# C2920-10MG). Fura-FF (Cayman Chemicals, Cat# 20415) was used for calcium measurements. RIPA lysis buffer was purchased from Merck (Cat# 20–188). Digitonin (MilliporeSigma, Cat# 300410-1GM) and Thapsigargin (MilliporeSigma, Cat# 586005-1MG) were used for cell permeabilization and ER calcium depletion, respectively. Aβ1−42 preformed fibrils (rPeptide, Cat# AF-1002-2) were used for amyloid treatments.

Cell culture and stable cell lines

To develop AD immortalized cell models, human neuroblastoma- derived cells (SH-SY5Y), microglial-like cells (HMC3), and astrocytic-like cells (SVGp12) purchased from ATCC were used as wild-type (WT) cells. WT cells were transfected with a construct overexpressing human amyloid beta precursor protein (APP) carrying Swedish, Florida, and London (APPswe/F/L) mutations fused with a Flag tag. Stable cell lines were generated using puromycin selection (2 µg/ml) for one week, resulting in SH-SY5Y-APPswe/F/L (AD-neuroblastoma-derived cells), HMC3-APPswe/F/L (AD- microglial-like cells), and SVGp12-APPswe/F/L (AD- astrocytic-like cells). Cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM) and Minimal Essential Medium (MEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% penicillin/streptomycin, 1% non-essential amino acids (NEAA), and 1 mM sodium pyruvate. Cultures were kept at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. APPswe/F/L-transfected cells underwent puromycin selection (2 µg/ml) 48 h post-transfection and were expanded after one week of selection. Only cells with a passage number below 20 were utilized. For imaging studies, cells were seeded onto 35 mm poly-D-lysine-coated optical dishes.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To quantify extracellular Aβ peptides Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42, culture supernatants were collected from WT and APPswe/F/L-expressing cells and analyzed using Aβ-specific ELISA kits (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation, Cat# 292–62301, 298–62401) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Standards and samples (100 µL) were incubated overnight at 4 °C in pre-coated plates. After washing, plates were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody for 1 h at 4 °C, followed by incubation with TMB substrate for 30 min at room temperature. The reaction was terminated with a stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm with a microplate reader.

Detection of misfolded protein aggregates

Protein aggregates were detected using the PROTEOSTAT® Aggresome Detection Kit (Abcam, Cat# ENZ-51023), which employs a fluorescent probe to identify misfolded protein cargo and proteins involved in aggresome formation. Cells were grown on the glass slides until reaching ~ 80% confluency. Cells were washed twice and fixed using 4% formaldehyde for 30 min at room temperature. After fixation, cells were washed twice with 1X PBS and permeabilized using 1% Triton X-100 with 0.5 M EDTA, incubated on ice for 30 min. Following permeabilization, cells were washed twice with 1X PBS, incubated with Dual Detection Reagent for 30 min at room temperature in the dark, and washed again to remove excess reagent. Coverslips were mounted on microscope slides, and stained cells were analyzed using confocal microscopy (60X magnification).

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR)

Total RNA was isolated using the RNeasy kit (Qiagen, Cat# 74104), and 1 µg of RNA was reverse-transcribed using the cDNA Reverse Transcription Kit-IV (Applied Biosystems). qPCR was conducted using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems) according to manufacturer’s protocol. RPS13 was used as an internal control gene and all reactions were run in three biological replicates. 2−ΔΔCt (fold change) was calculated to determine mRNA expression relative to WT control and fold change was normalized to SH-SY5Y-WT condition. The list of all the primer sequences can be found in Supplementary Table 1. Heat maps were generated using Morpheus software (Broad Institute).

Western blot analysis

Cells were lysed in 1x RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with SIGMAFAST™ protease inhibitor cocktail and centrifuged at 4 °C for 30 min. Protein concentrations were determined using the Bio-Rad assay at 660 nm. To ensure consistency and minimize technical variability, equal numbers of cells were seeded for each condition, and all cultures were maintained under identical media, incubation times, and treatment protocols. Protein isolation was performed simultaneously across all samples using the same lysis buffer and standardized procedures. Equal amounts of total protein were resolved by electrophoresis on SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to a PVDF Immobilon-FL membrane (EMD Millipore, Cat# IPFL00010). The PVDF membrane was incubated with blocking buffer (Rockland, Cat# MB-070) at room temperature for 2 h. The membrane was incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight. The following primary antibodies were used in the study: Flag (1:1000) (MilliporeSigma, Cat# F1804), Tubulin (1:1000) (Proteintech, Cat# 66031-1-Ig), MCU (1:1000) (Proteintech, Cat# 26312-1-AP), MCUB (1:250) (Proteintech, Cat# 20387-1-AP), MCUR1 (1:1000) (Thermofisher scientific, Cat# PA5-32164), MICU1 (1:1000, custom made antibody), MICU2 (1:1000) (Bethyl laboratories, Cat# A300-BL19212), MICU3 (1:1000) (MilliporeSigma, Cat# HPA024771), NCLX (1:1000) (Proteintech, Cat# 21430-1-AP), EMRE (1:500) (Bethyl laboratories, Cat# A300-BL19208), and ATP5a (1:1000) (Proteintech, #66037-1-Ig). After incubation with primary antibody, the membrane was washed three times in every 10 min with TBS-T (TBS containing 0.1% Tween20) and then incubated further with specific secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature. Licor IR680 and IR800 secondary antibodies were used at dilutions 1:1000. All blots were imaged on a Biorad ChemiDoc system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ, with background subtraction, and normalized to ATP5a, which served as a consistent loading control across all samples. This normalization allowed for reliable relative comparisons of protein expression, even across different cell types. Full-length Western blots are provided in Supplementary Fig. 8. Heat maps were generated using Morpheus software (Broad Institute).

Quantification of mCa2+ flux

To evaluate mCa2+ uptake at physiological Ca2+ levels, assays were performed using a 10 µM Ca2+ concentration. For each experiment, 2.5 million cells were transferred to an intracellular-like medium containing 120 mM KCl, 10 mM NaCl, 1 mM KH₂PO₄, 20 mM HEPES-Tris, 1.5 µM thapsigargin, 20 µg/ml digitonin, and supplemented with 2.5 mM succinate and adjusted to pH 7.2. All solutions were treated with Chelex 100 to remove trace Ca2+ (MilliporeSigma). Digitonin-permeabilized cells were gently stirred, and 1 µM Fura-FF (Invitrogen) was added to monitor extra-mitochondrial Ca2+. After acquiring baseline recordings, a 10 µM Ca2+ bolus was added at 300 s. Fluorescent signals were monitored at 380/510 nm excitation/emission using a spectrofluorometer (Delta RAM, Photon Technology Int.). Uptake rate was calculated for 50-second intervals following Ca2+ addition at 300 s. At 400 s, 1 µM Ru360 was added to inhibit mCa2+ uptake and to measure efflux. Efflux rate was calculated for 50-second intervals after Ru360 addition at 400 s. At the completion of the experiment, the protonophore FCCP was added to disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential, causing rapid release of mCa2+ into the matrix. All experiments were conducted at 37 °C and recorded on a PTI spectrofluorometer. Ca2+ release was calculated by subtracting the average of last 10 readings after FCCP addition from the average of initial 10 readings before Ca2+ addition at 300 s. A custom MATLAB-based approach was used to evaluate mCa2+ uptake and efflux regions. Specifically, a MATLAB script (MathWorks, R2024a) was employed to extract kinetic parameters. Uptake rate was determined by identifying the peak Fura-FF signal within the 250–350 s window post Ca2+ addition, followed by fitting a monoexponentially decay to the subsequent 50 s. Efflux rate was extracted analogously, using the 400–450 s trough region after Ru360 addition. The negative slopes of log-transformed decay fits were used to quantify uptake and efflux rates, respectively. To assess relative changes, fold-changes were calculated by normalizing each condition to the mean of three SH-SY5Y-WT replicates (used as baseline control). MATLAB was further used to generate annotated dual-panel figures for each replicate showing both full Ca2+ trace and highlighted kinetic regions.

Quantification of MICU1/2-dependent mCa2+ flux

To evaluate MICU1/2-dependent mCa2+ uptake, assays were performed using 3 µM and 20 µM Ca2+ concentrations to assess uptake rates at low and high Ca2+ levels, respectively. For each experiment, 2.5 million cells were transferred to an intracellular-like medium (ICM buffer) as described above, containing 1.5 µM thapsigargin, 20 µg/ml digitonin and 2.5 mM succinate, adjusted to pH 7.2. Digitonin-permeabilized cells were gently stirred, and 1 µM Fura-FF (Invitrogen) was added to monitor extra-mitochondrial Ca2+. After acquiring baseline recordings, a 3 µM or 20 µM Ca2+ bolus was added at 300 s. FCCP was subsequently added to disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential, causing a rapid release of mCa2+ into the matrix. Fluorescent signals were monitored at 380/510 excitation/emission in a spectrofluorometer (Delta RAM, Photon Technology Int.). Uptake rate was calculated for 50-second time intervals after Ca2+ addition at 300 s. Ca2+ release was calculated by subtracting the average of last 10 readings after FCCP addition from the average of initial 10 readings before Ca2+ addition at 300 s. MATLAB based approach was used to evaluate mCa2+ uptake regions similarly as discussed in the previous methodology section. To assess relative changes, fold-changes were calculated by normalizing each condition to the mean of the three SH-SY5Y-WT replicates (used as baseline control). MATLAB was further used to generate annotated dual-panel figures for each replicate showing both full Ca2+ trace and highlighted kinetic regions.

Quantification of mCa2+ retention capacity

mCa2+ retention capacity (CRC) was evaluated to determine the cell-type specific mCa2+ overload. Cells were collected by trypsinization and washed with intracellular-like medium (120mM KCl, 10mM NaCl, 1mM KH2PO4, 20mM HEPES-Tris, pH 7.2) devoid of any Ca2+ traces using Chelex 100 (MilliporeSigma). The plasma membrane of cells was permeabilized using digitonin (20-µg/ml) and sarcoendoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA) mediated Ca2+ was blocked using thapsigargin (1.5 µM) to get mitochondria specific Ca2+ levels and succinate (2.5 mM). For evaluation of mCa2+ content: 2.5 million cells were added with the ratiometric Ca2+ reporter Fura-FF (Invitrogen, 1µM) to monitor mCa2+ retention capacity. At 300 s, 5µM Ca2+ boluses were added at the indicated time points until a plateau was reached, indicating no more mCa2+ uptake was observed, at this point 1 µM FCCP was added to release out all mCa2+. FCCP was added to disrupt mitochondrial membrane potential, causing a rapid release of mCa2+ into the matrix.

ATP assay

Equal numbers of cells (20 × 10³) were plated in 96-well plates. After treatment with the oxidizing agent tert-butyl hydroperoxide (200 µM, 1 h), ATP levels were measured using the CellTiter-Glo® 2.0 Cell Viability Assay (Promega #G9241), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, the culture medium was replaced with Opti-MEM, and an equal volume of ATP reagent was added directly to the wells. Plates were incubated for 10 min at room temperature in the dark. Luminescence was measured using a plate reader set to a sensitivity of 2000. ATP levels were normalized to total DNA content using the CyQuant™ assay (Promega). For DNA quantification, the CyQuant reagent (Promega #C7026) was added directly to cells in complete medium, incubated in the dark for 30–60 min, and fluorescence was measured at excitation/emission maxima of 508/527 nm using a standard plate reader.

NADH assay

Equal numbers of cells (20 × 10³ per well) from all groups were plated in a 96-well plate and treated with the oxidizing agent tert-butyl hydroperoxide (TBH, 200 µM) for 1 h. After treatment, the cells were washed and replaced with DPBS, followed by incubation with NAD/NADH-Glo™ reagent (Promega™G9071) for 1 h at room temperature in the dark. Luminescence was measured using a plate reader and normalized to DNA content using the CyQUANT™ assay (Promega™). For the CyQUANT™ assay, the reagent was added directly to cells in complete medium and incubated in the dark for 30–60 min, and fluorescence was measured using a standard plate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of 508/527 nm.

Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) analysis

SH-SY5Y were grown at a cell-density of 30 × 103 while HMC3 and SVGp12 cell lines (both WT and APPswe/F/L) were grown at a cell-density of 20 × 103 for 24 h were subjected to OCR measurement at 37 °C in an XF96 extracellular flux analyzer (Seahorse Bioscience). The cell numbers were standardized to keep constant oligomycin, FCCP and rotenone/antimycin A concentration in all cell-lines. Cells were plated in XF media pH 7.4 supplemented with 25 mM glutamax, 25 mM glucose and 1 mM sodium pyruvate and sequentially exposed to oligomycin (2 µM), FCCP (2 µM), and rotenone plus antimycin A (0.5 µM). Quantification of basal respiration (base OCR – non-mito respiration (post-Rot/AA), ATP-linked respiration (post-oligo OCR − base OCR), Max respiratory capacity (post-FCCP OCR − post-Rot/AA), Spare respiratory capacity (post-FCCP OCR − basal OCR) and Proton leak (post-Oligo OCR − post-Rot/AA OCR) was performed. Readings were normalized to cell number and DNA content using Cyquant (Promega™). For DNA quantification, CyQUANT™ reagent was added directly to the cells in complete medium, incubated in the dark for 30–60 min, and fluorescence was measured using a standard plate reader at excitation/emission wavelengths of 508/527 nm.

Membrane rupture assay

SYTOX Green (Thermofisher Scientific, Cat #S7020) nucleic acid stain was used to examine membrane rupture. Increased SYTOX Green fluorescence is associated with higher number of dead cells as it intercalates in DNA of cells with compromised plasma membrane. In a 96-well plate, equal numbers of cells density (20 × 103) from all groups were treated with Ionomycin (5 µM for 1 h) and stained with SYTOX stain (1 mM) to incubate at 37 °C for 30 min. SYTOX fluorescent intensity was measured at 504 nm excitation and 530 nm emission spectra and the readings were normalized to DNA content using Cyquant (Promega™). The reagent is added directly to cells in complete medium and incubated in dark for 30 to 60 min and fluorescence read on standard plate readers at an excitation and emission maxima of 508/527 nm.

Quantification of mitochondrial ROS

MitoSOX Deep Red (Thermofisher Scientific, Cat# M36008) was used to analyze the production of mitochondria-specific superoxide production, which enhances red fluorescence under higher ROS production. In a 96-well plate, equal numbers of cells (20 × 103 per well) from all groups were treated with Antimycin A (20 µM for 30 min) as a positive control. A kinetic fluorescence assay was performed using a microplate reader, with excitation and emission wavelengths set to 410/610 nm. DNA content was quantified using the CyQUANT™ assay (Promega™), and the time point showing the maximal kinetic difference in MitoSOX fluorescence was selected for plotting and analysis. or imaging studies, cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 per well for all cell lines. After incubation with MitoSOX™ Deep Red as described above, live-cell imaging was performed at 100x magnification using an FV4000 confocal microscope. MitoSOX fluorescence intensity per cell was quantified using ImageJ software.

Quantification of mitochondrial membrane potential and structure

Mitochondrial membrane potential and structural analysis were performed using confocal microscopy in cells stained with TMRM (Thermofisher Scientific #T668). All cell lines were plated at a consistent density of 1 × 10⁵ cells per 35 mm optical-bottom dish and cultured under identical conditions. Cells were incubated with TMRM stain (100 nM for 30 min) followed by imaging using a FV4000 confocal microscope at 100x magnification. Mitochondrial membrane potential was quantified as mean fluorescence intensity per cell using ImageJ software, ensuring consistent thresholding and background subtraction across all samples. To assess mitochondrial structure, we used the MiNA (Mitochondrial Network Analysis) plugin in ImageJ, which enables objective quantification of mitochondrial morphology even in densely packed networks. Parameters analyzed included mitochondrial count, total area, perimeter, mean branch diameter, mean branch length, and number of branches. This method has been validated in previous studies [41–44] and allows for reliable comparison of mitochondrial architecture across cell types and conditions. All images were acquired under identical settings, and multiple fields per condition were analyzed to ensure reproducibility. Skeletonized images and quantitative outputs were used to support conclusions regarding mitochondrial structural differences.

Treatment with pre-formed Aβ1−42 fibrils

To analyze the effect of pathological Aβ fibrils, SH-SY5Y cells were treated with pre-formed Aβ1−42 fibrils (rPeptide, Cat# AF-1002) at a final concentration of 5 µM for 24 h, as previously described [45]. SH-SY5Y were seed at 1 × 106 cell density in 100 mm dish and next day at ~ 60% confluency treated with Aβ1−42 fibrils for 24 h. After treatment, the culture media from the treated SH-SY5Y cells were collected and analyzed for Aβ levels using an Aβ-specific ELISA kit (100 µl/well) as detailed in the previous section. SH-SY5Y cell pellet from the same dish was analyzed for Mitochondrial calcium retention capacity (CRC) as described above, comparing SH-SY5Y-WT cells, SH-SY5Y-WT-pCMV6 cells, and SH-SY5Y cells treated with pre-formed Aβ₁₋₄₂ fibrils.

Statistics

Statistical analyses and graphical representations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.0. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Each experiment was independently replicated a minimum of three times. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were employed. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was utilized for single-factor comparisons across multiple groups. For analyses involving multiple factors, two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was applied to evaluate statistical significance. The specific statistical methods used for each dataset are detailed within the corresponding figure legends. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05, corresponding to a 95% confidence interval.

Results

Immortalized brain cells show differential expressions of mCa2+ flux components in AD

Building on our previous findings of altered mtCU components in human AD brains and mouse models [11, 46], we developed a cell-type-specific in vitro model system using immortalized human neuroblastoma-derived (SH-SY5Y), microglial-like (HMC3), and astrocytic-like (SVGp12) cell lines stably expressing APPswe/F/L mutations. Western blot analysis confirmed successful expression of Flag-tagged APPswe/F/L mutant in all three cell types (Fig.S1A–B). Aggregated protein content, visualized using Proteostat staining, showed significantly increased aggregate accumulation in APPswe/F/L-expressing cells compared to WT (Fig.S1C-E). Quantitative analysis demonstrated a marked increase in aggregate count per cell (Fig.S1F). To assess amyloidogenic processing of APP, we quantified extracellular Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 levels using ELISA. All APP-expressing cell lines secreted significantly higher levels of Aβ1-40 and Aβ1-42 levels and Aβ42/40 ratio compared to WT (Fig.S1G-I). These data confirm successful generation of AD-like phenotypes in neuroblastoma- derived cells, microglial-like cells, and astrocytic-like cells models, providing a robust platform for studying cell-type-specific mCa2+ signaling in AD.

We first assessed the mRNA expression of core mtCU components (MCU, MCUB, EMRE, MCUR1, MICU1/2/3) and the Ca²⁺ efflux transporter NCLX. Under basal conditions, SVGp12-WT exhibited higher baseline mRNA levels of MCU, MCUB, and MICU isoforms compared to HMC3-WT and SH-SY5Y-WT, suggesting stronger dependence on mCa2+ uptake (Fig. 1A & S1J). In APPswe/F/L -expressing cells, MCUR1 transcript levels were elevated across all cell types, while MICU2 was significantly increased in neuroblastoma- derived cells and astrocytic-like cells, and MICU3 was specifically upregulated in neuroblastoma- derived cells (Fig. 1A & S1J). APPswe/F/L expression led to elevated MCUR1 mRNA across all cell types, with MICU2 significantly increased in neuroblastoma-derived and astrocytic-like cells, and MICU3 specifically upregulated in neuroblastoma-derived cells. These data indicate transcriptional upregulation of select mtCU modulators in AD-like conditions. Protein-level analysis revealed partial concordance with mRNA trends and also uncovered distinct post-translational regulation. In WT cells, MCU protein levels were higher in microglial-like and astrocytic like cells than in neuroblastoma-derived cells (Fig. 1B-C & S1K). APPswe/F/L expression increased MCU protein in neuroblastoma-derived cells and microglial-like cells, with no significant change in astrocytic-like cells. MCUB protein was highest in WT astrocytic-like cells followed by microglial-like cells compared to neuroblastoma-derived cells; APPswe/F/L expression led to MCUB downregulation in neuroblastoma-derived and astrocytic- like cells. EMRE levels were elevated in WT astrocytic like versus neuroblastoma-derived and microglial-like cells, with no consistent AD-associated change (Fig. 1B-C & S1K). Although MCUR1 mRNA was increased in all APPswe/F/L -expressing cells, MCUR1 protein was reduced in APPswe/F/L neuroblastoma-derived and astrocytic-like cells, suggesting translational repression or increased degradation. MICU1 protein was decreased in WT microglial-like and astrocytic-like cells relative to neuroblastoma-derived cells and was further reduced in APPswe/F/L -expressing neuroblastoma-derived and astrocytic-like cells. MICU2 protein showed not major difference in both WT and APPswe/F/L -expressing all cell-types. MICU3 protein expression level was highest in neuroblastoma-derived compared to microglial-like and astrocytic-like cells under baseline conditions. APPswe/F/L expression increased MICU3 in neuroblastoma-derived but decreased it in astrocytic-like cells. NCLX protein expression was relatively higher in WT neuroblastoma-derived compared to microglial-like and astrocytic-like cells but was significantly reduced in APPswe/F/L-expressing neuroblastoma-derived and astrocytic-like cells. Together, comparison of mRNA and protein levels indicates that transcriptional upregulation (e.g., MCUR1 mRNA) does not always translate to increased protein abundance, suggesting post-translational regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 1D). Brain cell types exhibit distinct mCa2+ handling capacities under physiological and AD conditions

mCa2+ uptake and release are essential for regulating cellular energy metabolism, redox balance, and survival, especially in the brain, where energy demands are tightly coupled to function. However, the cell type–specific regulation of mCa2+ flux and its dysregulation in AD remains poorly characterized. Our findings indicated differential expression of mtCU components among various immortalized brain cell types, prompting us to directly assess mCa2+ flux in neuroblastoma- derived cells (SH-SY5Y), microglial-like cells (HMC3), and astrocytic-like cells (SVGp12), under both physiological and AD conditions. To investigate this, we measured mCa2+ uptake and efflux rates using ratiometric Ca2+ (Fura-FF) and membrane potential (JC-1) indicators in WT and APPswe/F/L-expressing cell lines. Maintaining precise calcium concentrations in the cytosol and mitochondria is critical for cellular function. While calcium influx into mitochondria supports ATP production, excessive uptake can be toxic. Calcium efflux, primarily mediated by the mitochondrial sodium-calcium-lithium exchanger (NCLX) and the calcium-hydrogen exchanger (CHE), is essential for releasing surplus calcium and resetting mitochondrial calcium levels. Pathological conditions can lead to mCa2+ overload, triggering mPTP opening, release of apoptotic factors like cytochrome c, and necrotic cell death. Studying mCa2+ influx primarily mediated by MCU helps define the threshold for mPTP opening, providing insights into the mechanisms of cell death. Experiments on calcium efflux and influx are used to study the conditions under which mitochondria lose their capacity to regulate calcium and trigger cell death. Assays were first performed at a physiological Ca2+ concentration (10 µM). Under baseline conditions, microglial-like cells exhibited significantly higher mCa2+ uptake (Fig. 2B & D) and efflux (Fig. 2B & E) rates compared to both neuroblastoma- derived cells (Fig. 2A & D) and astrocytic-like cells (Fig. 2C & D). In contrast, astrocytic-like cells demonstrated the lowest mCa2+ flux, including reduced uptake (Fig. 2C & D) and efflux rates (Fig. 2C & E), as well as diminished Ca2+ release (Fig. 2C & F) following FCCP treatment, suggesting a more restricted mCa2+ handling capacity at rest. These findings highlight intrinsic, cell type–specific differences in mCa2+ regulation. Microglia, as the brain’s resident immune cells, may require elevated mCa2+ cycling to support rapid ATP production and ROS generation necessary for immune surveillance and phagocytic responses. MICU1 functions as a critical suppressor of Ca2+ entry at resting cytosolic Ca2+ concentrations and facilitates rapid mCa2+ uptake when Ca2+ levels rise, particularly in cooperation with MICU2 [47, 48]. This “on-off” switching mechanism ensures that mitochondria respond appropriately to changes in cytosolic Ca²⁺, preventing overload while enabling bioenergetic responsiveness. To examine how this regulatory mechanism varies across brain cell types and is altered in AD, we measured mCa2+ uptake and release kinetics using a low (3 µM) Ca2+ bolus, a range where MICU1/2 coordination is especially critical. Mitochondrial morphological remodeling in AD reflects cell-type-specific structural vulnerability

Mitochondrial morphology serves as a key indicator of cellular bioenergetic capacity and metabolic health. Structural features including mitochondrial count, area, perimeter, and branching complexity provide insights into cristae density, mitochondrial interconnectivity, and fusion/fission dynamics. For instance, increased mitochondrial number and area correlate with enhanced cristae surface and ATP production, while greater perimeter and branching indicate elevated fusion capacity and network responsiveness under energetic stress [49]. To assess how AD-associated mutations affect mitochondrial architecture, we performed confocal imaging using TMRM staining to assess mitochondrial membrane potential (Δψm) in neuroblastoma-derived, microglial-like, and astrocytic-like cell lines under both WT and APPswe/F/L-expressing conditions. In WT cells, astrocytic-like and microglial-like cells exhibited higher TMRM fluorescence intensity than neuroblastoma-derived cells, indicating enhanced membrane potential and metabolic activity (Fig. S5A). Conversely, APPswe/F/L -expressing microglial-like and astrocytic-like cell lines displayed significantly reduced TMRM signal, indicating mitochondrial depolarization and dysfunction. Quantitative analysis of mitochondrial structure revealed pronounced, cell-type-specific differences under both baseline and AD conditions (Fig. 5B–E, S5B–C). Under WT conditions, astrocytic-like cells showed the most complex mitochondrial networks, with the highest mitochondrial count, area, perimeter, and number of branches. These parameters were moderately lower in microglial-like and lowest in neuroblastoma-derived cells, aligning with their known bioenergetic demands and support functions. Quantitative analysis of mitochondrial structure revealed robust, cell-type-specific differences in both WT and APPswe/F/L -expressing cells (Fig. 5A–E, S5 B-C). In WT conditions, astrocytic-like cells exhibited the most complex mitochondrial networks, with the highest mitochondrial count, total area, perimeter, and number of branches. These values were moderately lower in microglial-like and lowest in neuroblastoma-derived cells. This ranking reflects known differences in cellular energy demands and mitochondrial activity across brain cell types, with astrocytic-like typically supporting neuronal metabolism. APPswe/F/L expression induced distinct structural disruptions in all three cell types. Astrocytic-like and microglial-like cells exhibited marked reductions in mitochondrial count, area, and perimeter, consistent with fragmentation and loss of cristae complexity (Fig. 5A–E, S5 B-C). Notably, APPswe/F/L-expressing microglial-like displayed longer (Fig. 5A & S5 B) but thinner branches (Fig. 5A & E) compared to WT microglial-like cells, suggesting compensatory remodeling in response to stress. In contrast, APPswe/F/L -expressing astrocytic-like cells exhibited shorter, less branched mitochondria (Fig. 5A & S5 C), reflecting impaired remodeling capacity and reduced fusion potential. Together, these findings demonstrate that APPswe/F/L mutations drive distinct, cell-type-specific disruptions in mitochondrial morphology. While glial cells, especially astrocytic-like and microglial-like cells exhibit more elaborate mitochondrial networks under physiological conditions, they also show greater structural vulnerability in the AD context. These data suggest that mitochondrial structural complexity is closely linked to functional output, and that APPswe/F/L expression disrupts this coupling in a cell-type-specific manner. The increased impairment observed in glial cells likely reflects their greater reliance on mitochondrial metabolism, positioning them as critical contributors to AD pathogenesis.

Cell-type-specific alterations in mCa2+ retention and susceptibility to stress-induced cell death in AD models

To investigate how mCa2+ handling influences cell-type-specific vulnerability to stress and death in AD, we assessed the mitochondrial calcium retention capacity (mito-CRC) in SH-SY5Y, HMC3, and SVGp12. Mito-CRC reflects the threshold at which mitochondria can buffer Ca2+ before initiating mitochondrial permeability transition (mPTP) opening, a key trigger of mitochondrial-mediated cell death. Using the ratiometric calcium indicator Fura-FF, we applied repeated 5 µM Ca2+ pulses while monitoring real-time mCa2+uptake. The opening of mPTP was identified by a collapse in mitochondrial membrane potential (ΔΨ) (Fig. 7A–C). Under WT conditions, astrocytic-like and microglial-like cells displayed significantly higher mito-CRC compared to neuroblastoma-derived cells (Fig. 7A-D), indicating greater resistance to Ca2+ overload and improved mitochondrial stability. In all APPswe/F/L-expressing cell models, mito-CRC was significantly reduced, indicating impaired mCa2+retention capacity and a lower threshold for mPTP opening. To account for transfection-induced stress, WT neuroblastoma-derived cells were transfected with the empty pCMV-6 vector followed by puromycin selection; no significant difference in mito-CRC was observed between transfected and non-transfected cells (Fig. S7B-C). Pre-formed Aβ 1–42 fibril levels in neuroblastoma-derived cells were validated through ELISA (Fig. S7A) and treated in SH-SY5Y-WT cells showed reduced CRC, suggesting that pathological concentration of Aβ carry out similar outcomes as the stable APP-swe/F/L expression (Fig. S7B-C). Upon APPswe/F/L expression and TBH treatment, all cell types exhibited elevated SYTOX fluorescence (Fig. 7E), indicating increased stress-induced cell death. This treatment showed increased cell death in neuroblastoma-derived compared to astrocytic and microglial-like cells, with further exacerbation in the presence of APPswe/F/L mutations (Fig. 7F). Discussion

This study identifies fundamental cell-type-specific differences in mCa2+ signaling and function in AD, using immortalized human neuroblastoma-derived, astrocytic-like, and microglial-like cell lines expressing pathogenic APPswe/F/L mutations. The observation reveals that neuroblastoma -derived cells are particularly vulnerable to mCa2+ overload, while glial-like cells, despite greater baseline buffering capacity and metabolic activity, also exhibit pronounced mitochondrial dysfunction under AD conditions. These findings underscore the need for cell-specific therapeutic approaches to restore Ca2+ homeostasis and mitochondrial health in AD. All experiments were conducted in isolated cell culture systems, which do not reflect the complex cellular crosstalk and metabolic diversity of primary brain cells. Therefore, while our findings offer mechanistic insights, they should be interpreted within the context of these limitations.

Under physiological conditions, astrocytic-like, and microglial-like cells displayed higher protein expression of mtCU components, including MCU and MCUB relative to neuroblastoma-derived cells. This likely supports more efficient mCa2+ uptake and retention, in line with their roles in metabolic support and immune regulation [50, 51]. Neurons showed enriched MICU3 expression, a regulator known to enhance rapid mCa2+uptake via MCU in response to synaptic activity [33, 36, 52]. While this enables fast metabolic coupling, MICU3-driven facilitation may also increase neuronal susceptibility to Ca²⁺ overload in disease. APPswe/F/L expression altered the mCa2+ regulatory landscape in a cell-type-specific manner. In APPswe/F/L expressing neuroblastoma-derived cells, MICU1 downregulation alongside MCU upregulation likely disrupted MCU gating, favoring excessive mCa2+ influx and overload. This is consistent with previous reports linking MCU activity to dendritic mitochondrial morphology, synaptic plasticity in hippocampal CA2 neurons, allowing fine-tuned mitochondrial responses to excitatory input [53]. Moreover, MCU activity has been linked to neuronal excitability across brain regions, underscoring its context-dependent role in neuronal physiology [54]. In contrast, APPswe/F/L-expressing neuroblastoma - derived cells and astrocytic-like cells exhibit a broad impairment in MCU gatekeeping mechanisms, accompanied by a downregulation of MICU1. Given that MICU1 and MICU2 normally suppress MCU activity at low cytosolic Ca2+ levels and facilitate uptake only when Ca2+ rises [55], their loss could lead to dysregulated or constitutive mCa2+ influx. Rather than reflecting a protective adaptation, this downregulation likely compromises mCa2+ homeostasis and increases cellular vulnerability. These findings align with previous studies highlighting the importance of MICU1/2 in maintaining mitochondrial integrity and supporting oxidative phosphorylation during cellular stress [31]. Furthermore, consistent downregulation of MCUB and NCLX was observed in most of APPswe/F/L expressing neuroblastoma -derived cells and astrocytic -like cells. Given that MCUB functions as a dominant-negative regulator of the MCU complex, limiting Ca2+ uptake [56, 57], its reduction may further exacerbate mCa2+ homeostasis. NCLX, the primary mitochondrial Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, is a key efflux pathway, and its dysfunction has been linked to AD pathogenesis through Aβ- and tau-mediated suppression, resulting in mCa2+ accumulation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial depolarization in neurons and astrocytes [11, 46, 58, 59]. Our data corroborates these findings, showing reduced NCLX expression in neuroblastoma - derived cells and astrocytic-like cells under AD conditions. NCLX inhibition has also been shown to exacerbate synaptic excitotoxicity and network hyperactivity [60], further supporting its critical role.

Functionally, APPswe/F/L-expressing neuroblastoma - derived cells displayed diminished Ca2+ efflux, reduced mito-CRC, and increased sensitivity to Ca2+-induced cell death. These changes were accompanied by lower ATP and NADH levels and increased mitochondrial ROS production, highlighting the reliance of these cells on tightly regulated mCa2+ dynamics for metabolic efficiency. Astrocytic-like cells showed high mCa2+ uptake at elevated Ca2+ concentrations in the healthy state, consistent with their roles in buffering and metabolic regulation. However, under APPswe/F/L expression, uptake was reduced, most prominently in microglial-like cells, suggesting greater sensitivity to mCa2+ stress. Our data confirmed that microglial-like cells had the highest baseline mCa2+ uptake capacity at physiological levels, while astrocytic-like cells exhibited the lowest. Astrocytic-like cells responded to Ca2+ perturbations by upregulating mtCU components, suggesting a unique, potentially protective response. Interestingly, we found that astrocytic-like and microglial-like cells responded more efficiently to high Ca2+ loads than neuroblastoma- derived cells, likely due to more effective MICU1-MICU2 cooperativity. These gatekeepers regulate Ca2+ entry based on cytosolic thresholds and are essential for protecting against Ca2+ overload [25]. Human MICU1 mutations are associated with neuromuscular and cognitive dysfunction, highlighting its clinical relevance [61].The greater mCa2+ retention capacity in astrocytic-like cells may also reflect structural differences; astrocytic-like cells in particular exhibited more extensive mitochondrial networks, consistent with high energy demands and buffering capacity. Spontaneous mCa2+ influxes in astrocytic-like cells observed in adult brain slices support our in vitro results [62]. Mitochondrial morphology changes further underscored cell-type-specific vulnerabilities. Astrocytic-like cells displayed more elaborate mitochondrial architecture under basal conditions, which deteriorated following APP expression, alongside loss of membrane potential and respiratory decline. In neuroblastoma- derived cells, high MCUR1 expression is shown to facilitate MCU-mediated Ca2+ uptake [24] may further increase susceptibility to Ca2+ overload and energetic collapse in AD.

Together, our findings support a model in which differences in the composition and regulation of the mtCU complex determine each brain cell type’s susceptibility to mitochondrial dysfunction in AD. This is consistent with prior work in Drosophila showing that MCU subunits are functionally non-redundant and must be tightly regulated for proper function [63]. These insights underscore the importance of developing cell-type-specific mitochondrial-targeted therapies to restore calcium balance and preserve mitochondrial health in AD.

Conclusion

This study provides compelling evidence that mCa2+ signaling and bioenergetic function are tightly regulated in a cell-type-specific manner in AD. Neuroblastoma - derived cells exhibit the greatest susceptibility to mCa2+ overload, loss of mito-CRC, and Ca2+-induced cell death. In contrast, glial-like cells (astrocytic and microglial models), despite possessing more robust mitochondrial structure and higher baseline metabolic capacity, also undergo pronounced mitochondrial dysfunction under AD conditions, characterized by reduced Ca2+ uptake, depolarization, oxidative stress, and impaired respiration. We acknowledge that these findings are based on immortalized cell lines, which do not fully replicate the metabolic and physiological characteristics of mature primary neurons, astrocytes, or microglia. Therefore, conclusions regarding basal metabolic differences between cell types should be interpreted with caution. Nonetheless, the use of well-characterized cell models allowed for controlled, cell-type-specific investigation of mCa2+ dysregulation in response to APPswe/F/L overexpression. These insights lay the groundwork for future validation in primary cultures and in vivo models. Importantly, our data reveal that components of the mtCU complex such as MICU1, MICU2, MICU3, EMRE, MCUR1, MCUB, and NCLX are differentially expressed and dysregulated across cell types in the AD context, identifying them as promising therapeutic targets for restoring calcium homeostasis and mitochondrial resilience.

Despite these insights, several key questions remain. How dynamic are mtCU component changes during disease progression in vivo, and are they reversible with therapeutic intervention? What is the role of inter-organelle Ca2+ communication particularly with the endoplasmic reticulum and lysosomes in shaping cell-type-specific responses? Furthermore, how do post-translational modifications influence mCa2+ regulation and remodeling in AD? Future studies should explore these mechanisms using in vivo models with cell-type-specific modulation of mtCU regulators (e.g., MICU1/2, NCLX). Integration of high-resolution Ca2+ imaging, single-cell transcriptomics, and spatial proteomics will be crucial to map regional and temporal mitochondrial responses during disease progression. Elucidating these pathways may uncover novel strategies to selectively protect vulnerable brain cell populations and halt the progression of neurodegeneration in AD.
