Novel reference method for precise determination of tryptophanase activity
Authors: Marwa M Ali, Inas J Mahdi, Hayder Obayes Hashim, Asad M Hadwan, Mahmoud Hussein Hadwan, Roaa Altaee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTryptophanase (TnaA; EC 4.1.99.1) is a pyridoxal 5′-phosphate-dependent enzyme that catalyzes the β-elimination of L-tryptophan into indole, pyruvate, and ammonia. It plays a key role in bacterial signaling and biofilm formation and is industrially relevant for L-tryptophan biosynthesis. Existing assay methods for TnaA activity measurement, including Kovács reagent colorimetry, HPLC, and NADH-coupled spectrophotometry, each carry limitations in cost, complexity, or analytical sensitivity that restrict their routine use. The current study aims to develop and validate a simple, cost-effective spectrophotometric method for quantifying TnaA activity using 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH) as a chromogenic reagent, targeting the pyruvate product of the enzymatic reaction. The DNPH-TnaA assay uses a stable hydrazone complex formed between pyruvate and 2,4-DNPH, which is measurable at 432 nm. It was applied to Escherichia coli lysates (ATCC 8739 and five clinical isolates). Performance was evaluated for linearity (5–500 µM), detection limits, intra- and inter-assay precision (CV%), spike recovery, selectivity against nine interference classes, and signal stability over 120 h. Validation included comparisons with Kovács colorimetric and HPLC methods, using Passing–Bablok and Bland–Altman analyses. The limits of detection (LOD) and quantification (LOQ) were 2.25 U/l and 6.7 U/l, respectively. Intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) was 1.19% (mean 270.83 ± 3.23 U/l), inter-assay CV was 2.65% (mean 273.55 ± 7.25 U/l), both acceptable. Selectivity errors were within ±3.33%. The colored hydrazone was stable at 25°C for 48 h. Passing–Bablok regression showed r = 0.99 with near-unity slopes and negligible intercepts, and Bland–Altman analysis indicated minimal bias. ANOVA found no significant differences among the methods at the .05 level. The DNPH-TnaA assay is a precise, accurate, and robust method for measuring TnaA activity, comparable to Kovács and HPLC methods. Its simplicity, low cost, and compatibility with common lab equipment make it ideal for routine diagnostics and research applications.

Introduction

Tryptophan indole-lyase (EC 4.1.99.1), also known as tryptophanase (TnaA), catalyzes the in vivo breakdown of L-tryptophan into indole, pyruvate, and ammonia through a β-elimination process [1]. This process relies on pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP, vitamin B6), and TnaA exhibits activity only when a PLP molecule is covalently bound to a lysine residue within the active site. TnaA exhibits promiscuous substrate specificity, degrading many other substituted L-amino acids and their analogs, including the colorimetric substrate analog S-(o-nitrophenyl)-L-cysteine [2]. TnaA can degrade D-tryptophan in the presence of elevated ammonium phosphate levels [3, 4]. It can also degrade diastereomeric combinations of cysteine S-conjugates [5]. The enzyme’s apparent lack of stereochemical selectivity has established it as a model system for engineering enzymes capable of degrading contaminants. TnaA is industrially significant because it enables the inexpensive in vitro synthesis of L-tryptophan and related amino acids using excess substrates [6].

TnaA is overexpressed and becomes a predominant protein in Escherichia coli under alkaline stress conditions [7]. The upregulation of the TnaA gene tnaA by stress factors enhances indole synthesis [8], which functions as an extracellular signaling molecule in E. coli [9] and in numerous other bacteria, including Proteus vulgaris and various Morganella species [10]. Chemical signaling in bacterial communication, known as quorum sensing, is a prevalent phenomenon. In several pathogens, including Pseudomonas aeruginosa, quorum sensing activates virulence gene expression [11, 12]. Similarly, the chemical signal indole promotes quorum sensing in Enterobacteriaceae under environmental stress and has been shown to influence biofilm formation in E. coli [13, 14].

Escherichia coli TnaA functions as a tetramer composed of four 52.8 kDa subunits; at the same time, the dimeric form of the enzyme can be isolated [15] or produced at low temperatures and remains inactive. PLP is covalently linked to Lys270 in the active conformation of TnaA; however, dialysis may remove this version of the enzyme, producing the apo form [16]. Diffraction-quality crystals [17] have made it challenging to clarify the molecular features of E. coli TnaA. Although the structure of E. coli TnaA remains unknown, the homolog from P. vulgaris has helped clarify the situation [18]. P. vulgaris and E. coli TnaA exhibit 51% sequence similarity, indicating that their 3D structures, active sites, and other essential residues are likely to be very similar. Alterations in the kinetic and spectral properties of the two enzymes suggest possible subtle structural changes [19]. Compared to the enzyme from P. vulgaris, spectroscopic studies suggest that E. coli TnaA has a more hydrophobic active site [20].

TnaA activity is quantified using two primary methods: colorimetric determination of indole production and spectrophotometric measurement of pyruvate formation. Each methodology offers distinct advantages and limitations, making them suitable for specific experimental contexts and research objectives.

The Kovács method represents a well-established biochemical assay for identifying and quantifying TnaA enzyme activity in bacterial cultures. The Kovács method uses p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde dissolved in hydrochloric acid and amyl alcohol. When TnaA cleaves L-tryptophan, releasing indole, the indole reacts with p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde in the acidic amyl alcohol layer to form a pink-to-red Schiff base complex, which is quantified spectrophotometrically at 570 nm. The method effectively differentiates between TnaA-positive and TnaA-negative bacterial species, providing valuable taxonomic information for bacterial classification systems [21]. A notable advantage is the method’s exceptional efficiency: it detects TnaA activity within 5–180 min, whereas conventional methods can take up to 24 h. This enables more efficient laboratory workflows and accelerated diagnostic outcomes in microbiological applications [22]. This rapid identification capability is indispensable for bacterial classification in clinical and environmental microbiology, enabling prompt identification of potentially pathogenic strains and providing insights into metabolic pathways and functional capabilities, thereby substantially contributing to understanding bacterial physiology and ecological relationships [23]. Beyond taxonomic applications, TnaA assays utilizing the Kovács method serve as valuable tools in fundamental research, enabling investigators to examine enzyme kinetics and explore the regulatory mechanisms governing TnaA expression across diverse bacterial species, thereby enhancing the understanding of bacterial metabolism under varying environmental conditions [24].

An innovative colorimetric approach measures indole production using p-dimethylaminocinnamaldehyde (PDACA) as a chromogenic reagent [25]. This method offers greater sensitivity and cost–effectiveness than the traditional Ehrlich reagent used in the Kovács method. The procedure involves extracting indole from aqueous solutions into light petroleum, followed by PDACA treatment to form a colored complex, which is quantified spectrophotometrically at 640 nm. The method demonstrates superior sensitivity and stability at an optimal PDACA concentration of 13.4 mM, and can detect indole concentrations exceeding 1 μg.

An alternative colorimetric method uses the reaction between indole and sodium nitrite in an acidic glycine–HCl buffer (pH 2.6) to produce a stable red compound [26]. This chromogenic reaction exhibits reproducible linearity across a broad range of indole concentrations (0.05–1.00 μmol) and remains stable for more than 1 week. The procedure involves mixing indole with sodium nitrite and glycine–HCl buffer, heating the mixture in boiling water for 5 min, and then measuring the red chromophore spectrophotometrically at 490 nm. The optimal sodium nitrite concentration is 0.04 g/l, providing superior linearity and sensitivity while eliminating the need for prior indole extraction, thereby simplifying the analytical process.

Chakravarti and Ghosh [23] developed a streamlined procedure for identifying TnaA-positive recombinant clones and regulatory mutants. This method employs a modified colorimetric assay based on the indole reaction with p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde in the presence of perchloric acid, yielding a stable pink product. The protocol involves in situ colony lysis on nitrocellulose membranes, followed by treatment with a chromogenic reagent after exposure to the TnaA substrate. This approach differentiates among TnaA-positive (tna+), TnaA-negative (tna−), and constitutive (tnac) mutants based on color changes, demonstrating high sensitivity and enabling simultaneous screening of thousands of colonies.

TnaA activity can be quantified by monitoring the decrease in absorbance at 340 nm, corresponding to NADH consumption during pyruvate production [27]. The reaction mixture comprises L-tryptophan, NADH, and lactate dehydrogenase. Lactate dehydrogenase couples the TnaA reaction to NADH oxidation, enabling quantification of activity by monitoring NADH concentration. This methodology provides sensitive and reliable quantification of TnaA activity by measuring the reduction in absorbance at 340 nm, which is directly proportional to the NADH consumed during the enzymatic reaction.

A direct spectrophotometric methodology utilizes S-o-nitrophenyl-L-cysteine (SOPC) as a novel chromogenic substrate with enhanced reactivity [28]. In the presence of TnaA, SOPC undergoes α,β-elimination, producing o-nitrothiophenolate. SOPC exhibits a visible absorption maximum at 370 nm with an extinction coefficient (ε) of 2700 M1·cm1, while the reaction product demonstrates maximal absorption at 412 nm. The enzymatic reaction can be monitored spectrophotometrically at either 370 nm (ε = 1860 M1·cm1) or 470 nm (ε = 626 M1·cm1). However, the pKa of o-nitrothiophenol is 5.4, necessitating corrections to the extinction coefficient when assays are conducted at pH values below 8.0.

A rapid and sensitive HPLC assay measures TnaA activity by fluorometric detection of indole, the enzyme-catalyzed product of tryptophan [29]. This method requires a total incubation time of 20 min, followed by rapid reversed-phase chromatographic separation, with indole eluting in ∼8 min. The assay demonstrates excellent reproducibility and sensitivity, detecting indole concentrations in the nanomolar range, which provides substantial advantages over traditional spectrophotometric techniques that require extended processing times and complex sample preparation. The HPLC assay was successfully validated for measuring TnaA activity in E. coli cells, yielding an average enzymatic activity of 6589.6 U/g of cells. Fluorometric detection of enzymatically liberated indole enhances sensitivity and selectivity by incorporating online identification, ensuring unambiguous peak assignments, and improving analytical reliability. Although initially demonstrated with E. coli, this assay can be readily adapted for other biological samples, providing a rapid, sensitive, and reliable alternative for evaluating TnaA activity and detecting tryptophan in various biological matrices.

The current study presents a simple, cost-effective spectrophotometric method for quantifying TnaA enzyme activity using 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH) as a colorimetric reagent. This assay is based on the biochemical principle that a stable chromogenic hydrazone complex forms in a 1:1 molar ratio between 2,4-DNPH and pyruvate, a primary product of the TnaA-catalyzed reaction. The methodology is straightforward and convenient to execute in a laboratory environment. Upon reaction with carbonyl compounds, DNPH forms hydrazone derivatives that can be readily quantified by spectrophotometry. This technique requires only affordable instrumentation, such as a single- or double-beam spectrophotometer or a plate reader, thereby making it suitable for routine laboratory applications.

Experimental

Chemicals

Sodium pyruvate was obtained from Sigma Aldrich. The following chemicals, pyridoxal-5′-phosphate, indole, disodium EDTA, p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde, amyl alcohol, tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane (Tris), and tryptophan were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. Monopotassium phosphate, sodium hydroxide (NaOH), 2,4-DNPH, hydrochloric acid (HCl), ethanol, and all other chemicals utilized in this study were sourced from Thomas Baker Pvt. Ltd.

Reagents

Buffer solution

A 100 mM phosphate buffer with a pH of 7.8 was prepared by dissolving 13.6 g of monopotassium phosphate (MW: 136.09 g/mol) and 3.6 g of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) in 1000 ml of distilled water. The pH was adjusted to 7.8 by carefully adding small amounts of HCl or sodium hydroxide (NaOH). This resulted in a 0.1 M phosphate buffer, which will be used for measuring TnaA activity. Subsequently, 20 mg of pyridoxal 5-phosphate (final concentration: 0.405 mM), 10 mg of glutathione (final concentration: 0.325 mM), 100 mg bovine serum albumin, and 0.1 g of potassium chloride (KCl) were added to the buffer solution. The mixture was stirred thoroughly to ensure complete dissolution and then stored in a dark container at 2–8°C.

Substrate solution

The substrate solution was prepared by dissolving 408.46 mg of tryptophan (20 mM) in 100 ml of potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8, 100 mmol/l), resulting in a final concentration of 10 mM. This solution was used as the substrate.

Color reagent

To prepare 5 mM of the 2,4-DNPH color reagent, dissolve 1 g of 2,4-DNPH in 1 l of a solvent mixture containing 40 ml of HCl, 460 ml of distilled water, and 500 ml of ethanol. The solution was thoroughly mixed to ensure homogeneity.

Finally, a 0.5 M NaOH solution is prepared by carefully dissolving 20 g of NaOH pellets in 1 l of distilled water. The solutions were stored in a colored bottle at 2–8°C and remained stable for up to one month.

Instrument

A Shimadzu UV-visible spectrophotometer model 1800 was used to measure UV-visible spectra using matched 1 cm quartz cells. A SpectraMax 190 Microplate Reader was used to perform the measurements in 96-well plates.

Bacterial isolates

The reference ATCC 8739 E. coli and four local E. coli isolates, which had previously been identified to the species level by means of the BioMérieux VITEK 2 system (Marcy-l′Étoile, France), were also used. TnaA activity of all five strains was tested by the indole test.

Source of TnaA

Escherichia coli was utilized as a source of TnaA activity, with a specific strain selected for testing and cultivation in tryptone water. Tryptone water (HiMedia, India) was prepared by dissolving 10 g of tryptone in 1 l of distilled water. The pH was adjusted to 7.2 ± 0.2. The medium was dispensed into test tubes containing 5 ml per tube and sterilized by autoclaving at 121°C for 15 min. Each tube was inoculated with a loopful of each bacterial isolate, which had been recently cultured overnight on nutrient agar. All isolates were cultured in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. The inoculated tubes were then incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24 h.

Cells were harvested from the cultures by centrifugation at 10 000g for 10 min, washed with 100 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), and resuspended in the same buffer. The cell suspension was sonicated on ice, and the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 10 000g for 5 min. The cell supernatant was mixed with 5 mM glutathione and incubated in a shaking water bath at 37°C and 150 rpm for 10 min.

The curve was constructed according to standard protocols using a 500 µM pyruvate stock solution. Absorbance was measured at 432 nm using the DNPH-TnaA method, and the curve was plotted as absorbance versus pyruvate concentration.

Pyruvate standard curve

Signal stability assessment

The stability of the colored hydrazone adducts, the final product of the DNPH-TnaA method, was carefully examined to ensure consistent and reliable measurements. Using a standard pyruvate concentration of 0.5 mM, absorbance was measured at 432 nm over an extended period. Readings were taken at set intervals: 15, 30, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 h, and then at 24, 48, 72, 96, and 120 h after the reaction. During stability testing, both measurement and storage conditions were maintained at 25°C to minimize temperature-related variability and accurately mimic typical laboratory conditions.

Linearity and sensitivity determination

Method performance was rigorously characterized by evaluating linearity and sensitivity across a comprehensive range of TnaA activities (0.05–100 U/l). Linear regression analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism (version 8; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) to determine the relationship between TnaA activity and absorbance. Direct comparison with the established Kovács method provided benchmarking against the current reference standard. Analytical sensitivity was quantitatively defined by the limits of detection (LOD), the minimum detectable TnaA activity, and the limit of quantitation (LOQ), the lowest activity level that can be reliably quantified with acceptable precision and accuracy. These parameters establish the operational range and detection capabilities of the DNPH-TnaA assay.

Analytical validation

Intra-day precision

For method validation, five bacterial samples were selected, and each was divided into multiple aliquots. To assess intra-day precision, TnaA activity was measured in triplicate for each aliquot on the same day. This approach enabled the evaluation of the assay’s consistency over a single day. Remaining aliquots were stored at −20°C for later use in inter-day precision assessments.

Inter-day precision

Inter-day precision was assessed by analyzing a single freshly thawed aliquot from each bacterial sample daily for 5 consecutive days. For each day, TnaA activity was measured in triplicate per sample. The reproducibility of the assay was evaluated by calculating statistical parameters, including the mean, standard deviation, and coefficient of variation (CV%), derived from these triplicate measurements.

Matrix effect evaluation, selectivity, and precision assessment

Biological matrices present a significant analytical challenge in enzymatic assays, as endogenous compounds can interfere with accurate analyte quantification. In the DNPH-TnaA assay, inherent carbonyl-containing compounds present in biological samples react with the DNPH reagent independently of TnaA activity, potentially generating false-positive signals that compromise measurement accuracy. To address this systematically, a control reaction was incorporated into the assay’s correction mechanism as an integral component.

The control reaction replicated all assay components and conditions, except for one critical modification: the exclusion of the TnaA substrate L-tryptophan from the control reaction. This design ensures that any absorbance measured in the control originates exclusively from matrix-derived interfering compounds rather than from enzyme-catalyzed product formation. By subtracting the control absorbance from the absorbance of the complete reaction, the contribution of endogenous carbonyl compounds is eliminated, isolating the signal that truly reflects TnaA enzymatic activity. This matrix-correction strategy enhances both the precision and accuracy of the assay by removing matrix-derived bias, thereby producing measurements that more faithfully reflect true TnaA activity levels in complex biological samples and improving the assay’s reliability for clinical and research applications in which sample matrices exhibit considerable variability.

The selectivity of the DNPH-TnaA assay was evaluated using a modified protocol adapted from Azeez and Hadwan [30]. To investigate potential interference from biomolecules commonly encountered in vivo, an interference study was conducted using nine PBS-based solutions. These included PBS alone as a negative control; carbohydrates (1 mM fructose, mannose, and galactose); amino acids (1 mM tyrosine, alanine, and serine); protein (0.1% w/v bovine serum albumin); metabolites (100 μM creatine and lactic acid); a Protease Inhibitor Cocktail at 10 μl/ml; and detergents (0.1% w/v Triton X-100 and Tween-20). Each solution was spiked with a known concentration of TnaA activity, and analyte recovery was calculated by comparing measured activity to the expected value. Relative error was determined using the formula: [(found activity − added activity)/added activity] × 100%.

Assay precision was assessed at three hierarchical levels in accordance with CLSI guidelines [31]. Intra-assay precision (repeatability) was evaluated by measuring replicate samples (n = 10) spanning a range of TnaA activities within a single analytical run, with results expressed as the CV%. Inter-assay precision was determined by analyzing quality-control samples across at least 5 separate days to assess measurement consistency over time. Inter-laboratory precision served as the final and most rigorous validation step, involving independent testing by different analysts across different laboratory settings. Acceptance criteria were defined as a CV% of ≤10% for intra-assay precision and ≤15% for inter-assay precision.

Together, this structured evaluation of selectivity against common biological interferents and multi-level precision testing provides comprehensive validation of the DNPH-TnaA assay’s capacity to accurately and reliably determine TnaA activity in complex biological samples.

Reference method

Spectrophotometric method

The activity of the TnaA enzyme was assessed by measuring indole production [20]. A 0.1 ml of the prepared cell-free TnaA lysate was combined with 2 ml of the reaction mixture and incubated at 37°C for 5 min to facilitate enzyme-substrate interaction. The reaction mixture was prepared in 100 ml of 0.1 M potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), containing 2.75 mg of pyridoxal-5′-phosphate as a cofactor and 19.6 mg of disodium EDTA as a chelating agent. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.2 ml of 0.02 M L-tryptophan, then incubated at 37°C for 10 min to promote indole formation. To terminate the reaction and enable colorimetric detection, 2 ml of Kovács reagent was added, followed by incubation at room temperature for 20 min. The mixture was subsequently centrifuged at 15 000g for 2 min to remove insoluble material, and the supernatant was analyzed spectrophotometrically at 570 nm. Indole concentration was quantified using a standard curve relating absorbance to known concentrations, and enzyme activity was expressed in units, with 1 unit corresponding to the production of 1 μmol of indole per minute under the assay conditions. For indole detection, Kovács reagent (5 mM) was prepared by dissolving 150 mg of p-dimethylaminobenzaldehyde in 50 ml of hydrochloric acid and 150 ml of amyl alcohol, then used. This reagent formed colored complexes with indole, thereby enabling quantification of indole to measure TnaA activity.

Indole standard curve

An 8.54 mM indole stock solution was prepared by dissolving 100 mg of indole in 100 ml of amyl alcohol. Serial dilutions were made from 5 µM to 500 µM to create a calibration curve. For colorimetric analysis, 0.1 ml of water was combined with 2 ml of each dilution, then 2 ml of Kovács reagent was added. The mixtures were incubated at room temperature for 20 min to facilitate color development, then centrifuged at 15 000g for 2 min. The clear supernatants were subsequently measured spectrophotometrically at 570 nm to detect the indole curve.

HPLC protocol

The assessment protocol for TnaA using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) involves several critical steps. Initially, ∼5 ml of E. coli solution is mixed with 5 ml of PBS (pH 7.0; 100 mM) and sonicated for 5 min to extract the enzyme. Following this, a portion of the extract (0.2 ml) is incubated with 0.2 ml of 100 µg/ml pyridoxal phosphate at 37°C for 10 min. Afterward, 0.2 ml of 0.5 M tryptophan is added, and the mixture is incubated for an additional 20 min. The reaction is terminated by adding 0.2 ml of trichloroacetic acid (TCA), and the acidic extract is neutralized with solid tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane. Figure 1 shows the direct correlation between incubation time and the resulting tryptophan.

After centrifugation at 3000g for 10 min, the supernatant is filtered through a 0.22 µm Millipore membrane filter to remove any particulates. The samples are then analyzed by HPLC under isocratic conditions with anhydrous methanol-water (1:1, v/v) at a flow rate of 1.0 ml/min, with fluorescence detection at 285 nm and a 320 nm cutoff filter. The chromatographic peaks are identified by retention time, co-chromatography with reference compounds, and excitation spectra to confirm peak identity.

The activity of TnaA is calculated from the concentration of indole produced using a specific formula that incorporates the peak area, the volume of the injected sample, and the weight of the E. coli sample. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 8. The data presented are the means ± standard deviations from three independent experiments, each conducted in triplicate. Comparisons between groups were made using Student’s t-tests, while correlations were assessed using Pearson correlation coefficients. A P <.05 was considered statistically significant for all comparisons.

Results and discussion

The present study introduces a novel, straightforward, and economical spectrophotometric assay for quantifying TnaA activity, employing 2,4-DNPH as the chromogenic reagent. The TnaA catalyzes a PLP-dependent α,β-elimination reaction on L-tryptophan. This reaction stoichiometrically yields three metabolites in equimolar amounts: indole (C8H7N), pyruvate (C3H3O3−, the ionized form of pyruvic acid), and ammonia (NH3).

The assay’s core principle exploits the 1:2 stoichiometric formation of the colored hydrazone complex between pyruvate and 2,4-DNPH, one of the three equimolar products of the TnaA-catalyzed reaction. This derivative exhibits a characteristic brown color due to extended π-conjugation within its aromatic structure, as depicted in Scheme 1.

The enzymatic reaction catalyzed by TnaA produces pyruvate. Subsequently, pyruvate reacts with the 2,4-DNPH reagent in the presence of sodium hydroxide to form a brown hydrazone product.

This hydrazone chromophore exhibits a maximum absorbance at 432 nm, enabling accurate spectrophotometric quantification. The absorbance intensity at 432 nm is directly proportional to the pyruvate concentration, providing an indirect yet precise measure of TnaA activity.

The assay’s core principle exploits the 1:2 stoichiometric formation of the colored hydrazone complex between pyruvate and 2,4-DNPH, one of the three equimolar products of the TnaA-catalyzed reaction.

As illustrated in Fig. 3, the color intensity and absorbance at 432 nm correlate linearly with the amount of pyruvate generated by TnaA activity. One unit of TnaA enzyme is defined as the quantity of enzyme that catalyzes the formation of 1 μmol of pyruvate per minute under standard assay conditions (pH 7.8, 37°C, with 0.02 mM L-tryptophan and 0.405 mM PLP). To enhance color development, sodium hydroxide (typically 0.5–1 M) is added post-reaction to convert the initial hydrazone adduct into a bright brown product with peak absorbance at 432 nm, ensuring high sensitivity and reproducibility.

The spectral properties of the brown-colored hydrazone product were linked with the pyruvate concentration produced by tnaA enzyme activity. The resulting product was measured spectrophotometrically at 430 nm. The pyruvate concentrations used were 250, 200, 150, 100, 50, and 0.0 µmol/l, respectively.

The 2,4-DNPH method offers several compelling advantages for quantifying TnaA activity. The protocol is straightforward and economical, requiring only a standard spectrophotometer for absorbance measurements at 432 nm and minimal specialized equipment, making it readily implementable across a wide range of laboratory settings without advanced instrumentation.

A particularly significant benefit is the assay’s capacity to provide a precise, quantitative assessment of enzyme activity through stoichiometric detection of pyruvate. By targeting pyruvate via the formation of a stable chromogenic hydrazone derivative, the method circumvents the need for direct indole measurement, which conventionally requires organic solvent extraction. This distinction is consequential: solvent-based extraction introduces additional procedural complexity, raises safety concerns about volatile organic compounds, and introduces potential variability arising from inconsistent solvent handling, all of which can compromise reproducibility and analytical reliability.

By eliminating these confounding steps, the DNPH-based approach streamlines the workflow while preserving high sensitivity, making it well-suited for enzyme kinetics studies. Overall, this method represents a meaningful advancement in TnaA activity quantification, offering an improved balance of reproducibility, operational safety, and analytical efficiency.

Signal stability

The study demonstrates that the colored compound remains highly stable at 25°C, with consistent absorbance at 430 nm over 2 days. Notable decreases of 14% after 24 h and 18% the following day indicate a gradual degradation.

Validation and comparative analysis of TnaA activity assays in bacterial samples

This study rigorously evaluated the analytical performance of the newly developed DNPH-TnaA assay for quantifying bacterial TnaA activity and systematically compared it against two well-established reference methods: the Kovács-TnaA colorimetric assay and HPLC-TnaA analysis. To ensure methodological equivalence and eliminate potential confounding variables, identical enzyme source, buffer system, substrate concentration, and reaction temperature (37°C) were maintained across all three platforms, with each method applied according to its own validated incubation protocol.

The validation protocol employed a diverse panel of TnaA-producing bacterial isolates spanning a clinically relevant range of enzymatic activity, ensuring a comprehensive assessment of assay performance under varied enzymatic conditions. Each bacterial isolate was analyzed in parallel using the DNPH-TnaA method, the conventional Kovács reagent method, and HPLC quantification. The resulting dataset was subjected to comprehensive statistical analysis, including correlation analyses and regression modeling to assess inter-method concordance and establish analytical equivalence.

Precision analysis demonstrated that the DNPH-TnaA assay delivers highly reproducible measurements under both intra-assay and inter-assay conditions (Table 2). Within-run precision, determined from 30 replicate measurements within a single analytical session, yielded a mean TnaA activity of 270.83 ± 3.23 U/l and a CV of 1.19%, well below the commonly accepted 10% threshold for enzymatic assays. Between-run precision, evaluated from 30 measurements across multiple days and analytical sessions, yielded a mean activity of 273.55 ± 7.25 U/l and a CV of 2.65%, confirming exceptional reproducibility and minimal day-to-day variation. These low CV values demonstrate that the DNPH-TnaA assay provides consistent, reliable results suitable for routine diagnostic and research applications.

Analytical parameters

Linearity and sensitivity assessment of the DNPH-TnaA method

Evaluation of linear dynamic range

The linearity of the DNPH-TnaA method was systematically evaluated across a pyruvate concentration range spanning 5 μM to 500 μM. Throughout this concentration range, the assay exhibited a robust linear relationship between pyruvate concentration and absorbance (Fig. 2). This linear response establishes the method’s effective dynamic range and confirms its suitability for quantitative enzymatic measurements across clinically and experimentally relevant activity levels.

Determination of analytical detection limits

The analytical sensitivity of the DNPH-TnaA method was rigorously characterized by determining the LOD and LOQ in accordance with established analytical chemistry principles. These critical performance parameters were calculated from the standard deviation (σ) of blank signal measurements, in accordance with internationally recognized guidelines. The LOD, representing the minimum detectable enzyme activity, was defined as 3× the standard deviation of blank measurements (3σ), while the LOQ, indicating the lowest reliably quantifiable activity, was established as 10× the blank standard deviation (10σ).

For bacterial TnaA activity measurements, baseline variability was assessed by performing 10 independent blank determinations containing all assay components except active enzyme (0 U/l). The LOD was calculated as the mean blank absorbance + 3 SDs, whereas the LOQ was determined as the mean blank absorbance + 10 SDs. To ensure reproducibility and confirm these threshold values, triplicate analyses of additional blank samples were performed across three independent experimental batches, each conducted on a separate day.

Under fully optimized assay conditions, the DNPH-TnaA method achieved exceptional analytical sensitivity with an LOD of 2.25 U/l and an LOQ of 6.7 U/l for bacterial TnaA activity. The LOQ of 6.7 U/l denotes the lowest enzyme activity concentration at which measurements maintain acceptable precision (CV ≤20%) and accuracy (recovery 80%–120%), thereby establishing the lower boundary of the method’s quantitative operational range. These low detection and quantification limits demonstrate the method’s capability to accurately measure TnaA activity even in samples with minimal enzymatic content, making it suitable for diverse applications, including bacterial identification, clinical diagnostics, and biochemical research.

Analytical recovery

To evaluate the accuracy of the DNPH-TnaA assay under conditions approximating routine analytical use, spike-recovery experiments were performed using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, 100 mM, pH 7.8) as the matrix. Known TnaA activities were added at three concentration levels spanning the clinically and experimentally relevant range: low (50 U/l), medium (200 U/l), and high (400 U/l). Each level was prepared in 10 independent replicates (n = 10) to ensure statistical robustness. Prior to recovery calculation, the spiked activities at each level were verified using the Kovács colorimetric method as the reference standard, establishing the accepted value against which DNPH-TnaA measurements were compared. Recovery was computed as the percentage of activity measured by the DNPH-TnaA assay relative to the Kovács-verified spiked value. Mean recoveries ranged from 96.4% to 103.5% across all three concentration levels, with CVs below 2.0% in all cases (Table 4). These results confirm low systematic error and demonstrate that the assay maintains high accuracy and reproducibility across a broad dynamic range in a defined biological matrix.

To validate the DNPH-TnaA assay for measuring bacterial TnaA activity, it was directly compared against two established reference methods: the Kovács colorimetric assay and an HPLC-based assay. Samples covering a broad activity range (10–400 U/l) were prepared by serial dilution and tested simultaneously across all three methods. Agreement, bias, and equivalence were assessed using Bland–Altman plots and Passing–Bablok regression, performed in GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Passing–Bablok regression demonstrated strong concordance in both comparisons. In the Kovács assay (Fig. 4B), the correlation coefficient was r = 0.99, with a regression equation of y = 1.0024x − 0.4785, indicating a near-unity slope and a negligible intercept. A comparable result was observed against HPLC (Fig. 4D), yielding r = 0.99 and y = 1.0019x − 0.1, further confirming proportional and constant agreement across the measured range.

Selectivity and precision of the DNPH-TnaA method

The selectivity of the DNPH-TnaA assay was evaluated to assess its accuracy in quantifying bacterial TnaA activity in the presence of potential interfering biomolecules common in biological samples. This study adapted a protocol from Azeez and Hadwan [30], with modifications to align with the assay’s conditions. The design mimicked complex biological matrices to ensure reliable TnaA measurement without substantial interference from endogenous or exogenous compounds.

Test solutions were prepared by mixing 1 ml of TnaA standard solution (300 U/l) with 1 ml of each interfering substance, resulting in a final TnaA concentration of 150 U/l in a 2 ml volume. Nine categories of potential interferents were tested: alternative buffers, carbohydrates, amino acids, proteins, metabolites, lipids, chelating agents, protease inhibitors, and detergents. Specific substances included Tris buffer (alternative buffer); a mix of mannose, glucose, and maltose (carbohydrates); a mix of histidine, tyrosine, glutamic acid, and arginine (amino acids); bovine serum albumin (BSA, proteins); uric acid (metabolites); oleic acid sodium salt (lipids); EDTA (chelating agent); a protease inhibitor cocktail; and sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS, detergent). PBS served as the negative control for baseline comparison.

Each mixture was assayed for TnaA activity using the DNPH-TnaA method and the established Kovács-TnaA reference method. Relative error was calculated as [(DNPH-TnaA result—Kovács-TnaA result)/Kovács-TnaA result] × 100%, highlighting any deviations due to interference. This comparison provided a robust evaluation of the method’s selectivity and potential biases.

Results (Table 5) demonstrated minimal interference across all categories, with relative errors ranging from −3.33% to +2.00%. The Tris buffer showed a −3.33% error, indicating a slight underestimation with negligible impact. The carbohydrate mix yielded −2.00%, confirming limited effects on assay performance. Amino acids produced +2.00%, while BSA caused +1.33%, both within acceptable limits. Uric acid had a minor −0.67% error. Lipids (oleic acid sodium salt) resulted in −3.33%, EDTA in +1.33%, the protease inhibitor cocktail in +2.00%, and SDS in −2.67%. All errors were within ±5%, with no value exceeding 3.33%, underscoring the method’s high selectivity.

These findings affirm the robustness of the DNPH-TnaA assay for TnaA quantification across diverse biological matrices. Its consistent performance across chemical classes, buffers, sugars, amino acids, proteins, metabolites, lipids, chelators, inhibitors, and detergents supports its practical utility for routine analysis without significant bias or loss of precision.

Conclusion

This study validates a novel spectrophotometric method for quantifying TnaA activity, based on the reaction between pyruvate, its enzymatic product, and 2,4-DNPH. Comprehensive validation results demonstrate that the DNPH-TnaA assay fulfills essential performance criteria for routine enzymatic analysis. It exhibits excellent linearity over the 5–500 µM pyruvate range, with a LOD of 2.25 U/l and a LOQ of 6.7 U/l, thereby confirming its efficacy in detecting TnaA activity, even at low enzyme concentrations. Intra- and inter-assay precision exceeded CLSI standards, with CVs of 1.19% and 2.65%, respectively. Spike-recovery experiments ranged from 96.4% to 103.5%, indicating minimal systematic error. Evaluation against nine classes of potential interferents revealed errors within ±3.33%, thereby affirming robustness in complex sample matrices. The stability of the hydrazone chromophore at 25°C was maintained for at least 48 h, supporting practical laboratory applications. The method’s equivalence was rigorously demonstrated through comparison with the Kovács colorimetric assay and HPLC, exhibiting a near-perfect correlation coefficient (r = 0.99) and biases of less than 1%. One-way ANOVA analyses confirmed the absence of significant differences among the six E. coli strains, supporting the assay’s compatibility with diverse sample types.

The DNPH-TnaA assay offers practical benefits over indole-based methods by eliminating the need for organic solvent extraction, simplifying the workflow, reducing exposure to volatile organic solvents, and lowering variability. Targeting pyruvate instead of indole avoids Kovács-based extraction issues and requires only basic equipment and inexpensive reagents. These features make it suitable for resource-limited settings without sacrificing quality.
