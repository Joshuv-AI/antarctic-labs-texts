A 59-Year-Old Man With a Pulmonary Cavity Containing Fungus Balls
Authors: Takafumi Kato, Hiroshi Igei, Mizuki Morota, Takuma Yotsumoto, Takeshi Fukami, Masashi Kitani, Akira Hebisawa, Junko Suzuki, Akira Watanabe, Nobuharu Ohshima, Yoshiteru Morio, Hirotoshi Matsui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Case PresentationA 59-year-old man living in the suburban area of Greater Tokyo presented to his local hospital with chronic productive cough persisting for 1 year and hemoptysis for the past 3 months. A chest CT scan revealed an uneven cavity containing multiple masses in his right upper lobe (Fig 1). Sputum culture turned positive twice for filamentous fungi, but species could not be identified. The patient had active tobacco use with a 25 pack-year smoking history. Additionally, he had a history of hypertension and untreated diabetes mellitus, with a hemoglobin A1C level of 12.2%. Consequently, he was referred to our hospital for further investigation and treatment.

The patient’s vital signs were normal: body temperature 36.9 °C, pulse rate 95 beats/min, BP 140/84 mm Hg, and oxygen saturation 97% in ambient air. Physical examination did not reveal any specific abnormalities. Blood test revealed normal blood counts with no significant abnormalities except for a hemoglobin A1C level of 8.4% and a mildly elevated C-reactive protein level (0.26 mg/dL). Negative test results included tumor markers, antinuclear antibodies, myeloperoxidase- and proteinase 3-antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies, interferon-gamma releasing assay (T-SPOT.TB), anti-Mycobacterium avium complex antibody, β-D glucan, antigens for Aspergillus and Cryptococcus species, and anti-Aspergillus IgG antibody.

Bronchoscopy was performed to collect a bronchial wash from the right upper lobe where fungus balls had enlarged (Fig 1). After bronchoscopy, voriconazole was administered based on a tentative diagnosis of pulmonary mycosis. Cultures from the bronchial wash turned positive, showing white woolly colonies that differed in appearance from those of Aspergillus species (Fig 2). Four months later, the patient experienced massive hemoptysis, estimated at 200 mL in volume, and was admitted to our hospital. A chest CT scan revealed enlarged fungus balls within the cavity, along with consolidations and bilateral ground glass opacities, presumably due to blood aspiration (Fig 1). The patient was treated with antibiotics and hemostatic agents for 1 week, followed by a surgical resection of the right upper lobe. Macroscopic examination of the resected lung revealed a cavity 4.5 cm in diameter, filled with dark brown fungus balls (Fig 3). Microscopic analysis showed fungal aggregates within the cavity, but no evidence of fungal invasion into lung tissue was observed (Fig 3). Subsequently, the result of species identification for the previously obtained bronchial wash culture returned, leading to a final diagnosis of this case.Figure 3A, Macroscopic and (B) microscopic images of resected right upper lobe. Diagnosis: Chronic noninvasive pulmonary Schizophyllum commune infection

Discussion

Clinical Discussion

S commune is an environmental basidiomycetous fungus (mushroom) that is distributed worldwide and commonly found in nature, especially thriving on rotting wood.1 Although rarely pathogenic to humans, it is considered an emerging fungal pathogen, with an increased number of reported cases of infections in human. A global case review study highlighted that of the 71 reported S commune infections, 45 (63%) were bronchopulmonary and 22 (31%) were sinusitis.2 In extremely rare occasions, S commune causes chronic fungus balls in lungs, whereas Aspergillus fumigatus is the predominant cause of fungus balls. To date, there have been only four cases of fungus ball formation in lungs by S commune, all of which had suspected underlying immunosuppressive conditions due to diabetes, cancer, or significantly low BMI.2, 3, 4, 5 In the present case, negativity of β-D glucan, Aspergillus antigen, and anti-Aspergillus IgG antibody, alongside the presence of white wooly colonies in bronchial wash cultures, prompted the submission of the specimen to the mycology research institute for further identification of the causative organism.

Molecular identification through sequencing of the polymerase chain reaction-amplified ribosomal internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region and D1/D2 region of the large subunit rRNA is recognized as the most widely used method for species identification.1 The ITS region, which includes ITS1 and ITS2 separated by the 58S rRNA gene, is highly variable between species but conserved within species, making it ideal for distinguishing a wide range of fungi, especially molds. The D1/D2 region, located at the 5' end of the 28S rRNA gene, offers a balance of conserved and variable sequences, providing high resolution for identifying yeast species.

Given the lack of widely accepted standard treatment for S commune infection, in the current case, voriconazole therapy was continued for 9 months after surgery until no recurrence was confirmed via chest CT scan. Additionally, serum anti-S commune IgG antibody levels were monitored before and after surgical resection.6 The antibody level, which was positive before surgery, turned negative and continued to decrease over time (Fig 4). To our knowledge, this report is the first to analyze serum levels and their transition of anti-S commune IgG for chronic S commune cavitary disease. Radiologic Discussion

As previously stated, A fumigatus is the predominant cause of fungus balls. Chronic cavitary pulmonary aspergillosis (CCPA) mainly occurs in patients with underlying structural lung abnormality (eg, sequelae of TB, nontuberculosis mycobacterial infections, COPD, lung cancer, bronchiectasis). Due to the high frequency of pulmonary TB in the upper lobes, CCPA predominantly occurs in these regions. Immunocompromising conditions, such as myelosuppression or severe diabetes as in the present case, predispose patients to an increased risk for aspergillosis or other fungal infections. To date, no radiologic features have been reported that distinctively differentiate fungus balls caused by S commune from those caused by Aspergillus species. Notably, the fungus balls caused by S commune were located in the upper lobes in all patients, regardless of their previous history of pulmonary TB. Radiologic characteristics of pulmonary fungus balls include intracavitary masses that may change position with changes in body posture, the air crescent sign characterized by a crescent of air outlining part of the fungus ball and the cavity wall, and typically, an absence of contrast enhancement. Differentiation between CCPA and subacute invasive pulmonary aspergillosis, formerly known as chronic necrotizing pulmonary aspergillosis, can be challenging with chest CT scan because both conditions share radiographic features (eg, thick-walled cavities, fungus balls, pleural thickening). However, subacute invasive pulmonary aspergillosis typically exhibits thicker and more irregular cavity walls, and more widely spread consolidations compared with CCPA.7

Pathologic Discussion

Chronic noninvasive pulmonary aspergillosis (aspergilloma) and S commune infection share pathologic characteristics, such as (1) fungal aggregates consisting of branching septate hyphae visualized by Grocott methenamine silver or periodic acid-Schiff staining, (2) cavitary lesions with fungus ball development, (3) granulomatous inflammation and fibrosis, and (4) absence of invasive fungal proliferation into the lung tissue. Some research reports that hyphae of A fumigatus exhibit dichotomous branching with acute angles, whereas S commune hyphae frequently bifurcate at right angles and acute angles without exhibiting dichotomous branching.8 Furthermore, the septate hyphae by S commune exhibit clamp connection.8 However, these findings may not be observed in tissue sections; therefore, differentiating S commune from A fumigatus through histologic investigations is often difficult, necessitating genetic investigation by DNA sequencing for diagnosis, as performed in the current case. In rare instances, particularly in immunocompromised individuals, S commune can cause invasive diseases (eg, pneumonia, brain abscess, otitis externa8, 9, 10). None of the reported cases of pulmonary fungus balls by S commune, including the current case, exhibited fungal invasion into the lung tissues (Table 1). Some of these cases had a previous history of pulmonary TB, which might have provided a suitable site for S commune infection. In the current case, the resected lung lobe exhibited emphysematous changes and multiple bullae. However, it was impossible to determine whether these underlying abnormalities caused the S commune infection.

Conclusions

First, chronic S commune infection should be considered when fungal cultures from patients with fungus balls produce white woolly colonies. Second, lobectomy was an effective intervention for controlling both hemoptysis and infection itself. Finally, the conversion of serum anti-S commune IgG antibody levels from positive to negative postsurgery highlights the potential utility of these antibodies in the diagnosis and monitoring of S commune infection.
