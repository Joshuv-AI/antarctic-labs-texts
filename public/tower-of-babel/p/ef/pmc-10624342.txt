Flagellar motors of swimming bacteria contain an incomplete set of stator units to ensure robust motility
Authors: Yuhui Niu, Rongjing Zhang, Junhua Yuan
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Flagellated bacteria, like Escherichia coli, swim by rotating helical flagellar filaments powered by rotary flagellar motors at their base. Motor dynamics are sensitive to the load it drives. It was previously thought that motor load was high when driving filament rotation in free liquid environments. However, torque measurements from swimming bacteria revealed substantially lower values compared to single-motor studies. We addressed this inconsistency through motor resurrection experiments, abruptly attaching a 1-micrometer-diameter bead to the filament to ensure high load. Unexpectedly, we found that the motor works with only half the complement of stator units when driving filament rotation. This suggests that the motor is not under high load during bacterial swimming, which we confirmed by measuring the torque-speed relationship by varying media viscosity. Therefore, the motor operates in an intermediate-load region, adaptively regulating its stator number on the basis of external load conditions. This ensures the robustness of bacterial motility when swimming in diverse load conditions and varying flagella numbers.

INTRODUCTION

Escherichia coli cells are propelled by the rotation of several flagellar filaments, each driven at its base in the cell membrane by the reversible flagellar rotary motor. Each E. coli cell contains approximately four to seven flagella, distributed randomly around the cell body (1). When all motors on a cell rotate counterclockwise (CCW), the flagellar filaments rotate as a helical bundle at one end of the cell and allow the cell to swim smoothly forward.

The function of the flagellar motor depends sensitively on the load it drives. A key property of the motor revealing its load dependence is the torque-speed relationship, showing how much torque it generates at different speeds. During CCW rotation, the motor torque is approximately constant (in the range of 1200 to 1500 pN·nm) up to a knee speed, beyond which it drops rapidly to zero (2–6). The region from zero to the knee speed is considered the high-load region, whereas that above the knee speed is the intermediate to low-load region. A motor can contain up to 11 functionally independent torque-generating units (stator units) (4), exchanging with a membrane pool of stator units on a timescale of 1 min (7–11). One end of each functional stator unit binds to the rigid peptidoglycan layer of the cell envelope, and the other end interacts with the rotor to generate the motor torque (12, 13). Because of the catch-bond nature of the stator binding with the peptidoglycan, the number of stator units in a motor varies depending on the load conditions (14–19), with 8 to 11 stators at high loads and a decreasing number at lower loads (14), to approximately 1 to 2 stators near zero load (20).

When bacteria swim in a free liquid environment, that is, they swim in a solvent with viscosity close to water and are away from any confining boundary, it is commonly believed that the flagellar motor is under high load operating in the constant torque region, as the flagellar rotation speed is typically lower than the knee speed (21–23). However, the motor torque measured for swimming bacteria falls within the range of 370 to 700 pN·nm (21, 22, 24, 25), substantially lower than the motor torque at high loads (1200 to 1500 pN·nm).

We suspected that the inconsistency may be because the flagellar motors of swimming bacteria are not under high load and do not contain a complete set of stator units. To resolve this puzzle, we carried out motor resurrection experiments by attaching a 1-μm-diameter bead suddenly to a rotating filament. We found that the rotation speed increased step by step, and revealed that the number of stator units is approximately five for the flagellar motor driving the rotation of a filament. This half-full complement of stator units explains the inconsistency between previous measurements and suggests that the flagellar motor is not under high load when bacteria swim in water. To directly test this, we measured the torque-speed relationship using swimming bacteria in media with various viscosities, demonstrating that the flagellar motor works in the intermediate-load region beyond the knee speed when bacteria swim in water.

Thus, bacteria swim with flagellar motors operating in the intermediate-load region and having a half-full complement of stator units. We further demonstrated that swimming bacteria can adaptively increase the number of stator units in flagellar motors when encountering a medium with high viscosity and that the rotation speed of a flagellar bundle saturates when the number of filaments in the bundle exceeds 2. Therefore, the half-full complement of stators in a flagellar motor ensures the robustness of bacterial motility when swimming in environments with different load conditions (e.g., different viscosities) and when swimming with different numbers of flagella.

RESULTS

Number of stators in a flagellar motor driving an unsheared filament

We suspected that the motor may not be under high load when driving the rotation of a filament and thus may not have a full complement of stators. To test this, we sought to carry out motor resurrection experiments by suddenly attaching a 1-μm-diameter bead to an unsheared filament to ensure a high-load condition. If the motor was not under high load before attaching the bead, then mechanosensitive recruitment of more stator units to the motor would increase the flagellar rotation speed step by step (14). We used the strain JY27, which rotates its motors exclusively CCW and has a cysteine point mutation FliCS353C on the filament protein to allow fluorescence labeling of the filaments or labeling of streptavidin-coated beads to the filament (26, 27).

To carry out the motor resurrection experiment, we first biotinylated the filament with maleimide–polyethylene glycol 2 (PEG2)–biotin. When preparing the cells, great care was taken to avoid shortening the filaments (see Materials and Methods). Cells with biotinylated filaments were adhered to the poly-l-lysine–coated surface of a flow chamber. Then, a solution of 1-μm- diameter streptavidin-coated beads was flowed to the chamber. Occasionally, a bead attaches to a filament and begins to rotate. An example of the rotating bead is shown in movie S1. We extracted the rotation speed of the bead as a function of time and clearly observed that the rotational speed increases in steps as more stators are incorporated into the motor (Fig. 1A). More example traces of the motor speed are shown in fig. S1. The mean speed change per stator could be obtained from the mean step sizes, and the number of stators bound to the motor at the start of the measurement was extracted by dividing the initial speed by the mean speed change per stator (see Materials and Methods). We measured 41 motors, and the distribution of stator numbers at the start of the measurements is shown in Fig. 1B. The average number of stators per motor was approximately five (4.88 ± 1.6). The stators bind to the motor on a timescale of approximately 1 min. To accurately determine the number of stators when the motor was driving the rotation of the filament (before the bead was attached), we ensured that the duration between flowing the bead solution and the start of each measurement was below 50 s, so that the average number of stators (4.88 ± 1.6) we obtained should not overestimate the actual number of stators before bead attachment by more than one.

Therefore, a flagellar motor driving the rotation of an unsheared filament contains only a half-full complement of stators. This also suggests that the motor was not under a high-load condition.

Flagellar motors driving the rotation of unsheared filaments work in the intermediate-load region

To directly determine the load region in which flagellar motors operate when driving the rotation of unsheared filaments, we aimed to measure the motor torque-speed relationship by varying the media viscosity. We fluorescently labeled the flagellar filaments (26) and adhered the cell body to an uncoated coverslip glass in a sample chamber. Some of the flagellar bundles dispersed when the cells were attached, allowing us to observe individual rotating filaments and filament bundles within the cell population. Some of the filaments and filament bundles rotated approximately in the focal plane of the microscope, and those were recorded with a high-speed camera. Typical videos of a rotating flagellar filament and a flagellar bundle are shown in movies S2 and S3, respectively. The rotational speed of the flagellar filament or flagellar bundle was obtained by calculating the power spectra for the change in fluorescence brightness over time as the flagella rotated (see Materials and Methods). An example of a flagellar filament image is shown in Fig. 2A, with a yellow box denoting the region for calculating the fluorescence brightness and a gray line denoting the fitted waveform of the filament. Examples of the power spectra for a rotating filament and a bundle are shown in Fig. 2 (B and C, respectively), from which the corresponding rotation speeds for the filament and the bundle could be extracted. We carried out the measurements in motility buffer (MB) supplemented with 0, 2, 3, 5, and 7% Ficoll (400 kDa from Sigma-Aldrich). The rotation speeds normalized by the value in 0% Ficoll, ω/ω0, at various values of 1/η (where η is the viscosity of the buffer) are shown in Fig. 3A, which does not exhibit a proportional relationship, suggesting that the motor torque was not constant as the viscosity (or load) changed (28, 29).

To calculate the motor torque, we measured the waveform parameters of the filaments and filament bundles, with the values shown in table S1 along with values of the cell body length and width. The values are consistent with those measured by Darnton et al. (21). The parameters remained constant in buffers of different viscosities. Using these values and the rotation speeds, we applied the resistive force theory (30, 31) to calculate the motor torque (see Materials and Methods for details). The average value we obtained for a rotating filament in 0% Ficoll was 300 ± 102 pN·nm, consistent with that obtained previously (21). To estimate the influence on the drag coefficient when the filaments or bundles rotate close to the glass surface, we used 1-μm-diameter polystyrene beads stuck to the glass coverslip to estimate the distance between the glass surface and the flagellar helical axis, with an average value of 292 ± 38 nm. Using a hydrodynamic model that includes the surface effect (32), we obtained the average torque for motors driving the rotation of individual filaments in 0% Ficoll, which was 475 ± 163 pN·nm. The torque-speed relationship was obtained by calculating the average torque in buffers of various viscosities, as shown in Fig. 3B for individual filaments and filament bundles. The rightmost data point for each dataset denotes the situation in MB with 0% Ficoll, demonstrating clearly that the motors are in the intermediate-load region beyond the knee speed.

The surface effect when calculating the torque is difficult to extract precisely because the distance between the rotating filaments and the surface is hard to measure accurately, and the rotating filaments are not strictly parallel to the surface. To eliminate the surface effect, we carried out additional experiments using optical tweezers to trap the bacteria away from the surface (more than 50 μm). The rotation speeds of the flagellar bundle and the cell body were obtained by analyzing the power spectra of the signal from the position-sensitive detector of the optical tweezers. To avoid light damage to the cells, the trap duration was less than 30 s, and only the first 10 s of the data for each cell was used to calculate the speeds. An example of the cell body rotation frequency as a function of time in the trap is shown in Fig. 4A. The power spectrum for the first 10 s is shown in Fig. 4B, clearly showing two peaks that correspond to the rotation of the cell body and the flagellar bundle. We carried out measurements in MB supplemented with 0 to 15% Ficoll, and the average bundle rotation rate as a function of 1/η is shown in Fig. 4C, which exhibits a nonlinear relation indicating that the torque is not constant. The corresponding torque-speed relation is shown in Fig. 4D, with the rightmost data point (on the dashed line, which is the load line connecting this data point with the origin of coordinates) corresponding to the situation in MB with 0% Ficoll, again demonstrating clearly that the motors are in the intermediate-load region beyond the knee speed.

Robustness of flagellar rotation against changes in number of flagellar filaments

When flagellar filaments form bundles, fluid-mediated interactions among the filaments decrease the viscous load on each flagellum, thereby increasing the flagellar rotation speed. As we found that the motors are not under the high-load (constant torque) region but in the intermediate-load region beyond the knee speed, the increase in speed with the number of filaments in the bundle should not be marked, which we sought to measure.

For the population of cells we measured in MB with 0% Ficoll, we measured the rotation speeds of individual filaments (number of cases: 285) and flagellar bundles (number of cases: 130), with the distribution of speeds shown in Fig. 5A. We also counted the number of filaments in the flagellar bundles, but the counting became unreliable if the number was larger than four. Thus, we did not count the number of filaments in flagellar bundles if it was more than four. The average rotation speed of a single filament and bundles with different numbers of filaments is shown in Fig. 5B. The rotation speed of the flagellar bundles formed by two filaments is approximately 45% higher than that of a single filament, but the increasing trend of flagellar rotation speed slows down as the number of filaments in the bundle increases. The rotation speed of the bundles with three filaments is basically the same as that of the bundles with four filaments.

Therefore, the flagellar rotation speed is robust against changes in the number of filaments in the flagellar bundle. We believe the robustness is due to three factors. First, the motors operate in the intermediate-load region such that the speed increases more slowly with the decrease of load (compared to the situation of high-load region with constant motor torque). Second, as the number of filaments increases, the decrease in load leads to mechanosensitive binding/unbinding of the stators, resulting in a smaller number of stators in the motors, which compensates for the speed increase. Third, the magnitude of the decrease in load per motor with more filaments decreases when more than two filaments are in the bundle, because of the influence of short-distance interflagella friction (33, 34).

Robustness of flagellar rotation against changes in load condition

As the motors operate in the intermediate-load region with an incomplete set of stators, when bacteria encounter environments with high-load conditions (e.g., higher viscosities or near a solid surface), mechanosensitive recruitment of stator units would allow the motors to increase the torque to compensate for the decrease in flagellar rotation speed. We sought to test this by subjecting the bacteria to a sudden increase in viscosity.

To rapidly change the viscosity experienced by the bacteria, we designed a microfluid chamber consisting of three separate streams with laminar flow (35). In this chamber, a “cell-injection” stream, a “Ficoll-injection” stream, and a “blank” stream merged into a central channel (Fig. 6B). The cross section of each stream inlet was 175 μm by 2000 μm, and the fluid flow speed was 17 μm/s. This ensured that the flow was laminar with minimal mixing, thereby creating well-defined boundaries along the direction perpendicular to the flow. The width of the boundaries was approximately 450 μm, as measured by using fluorescein as the marker (Fig. 6C). The viscosity of the Ficoll stream was 4.5 cP.

In a typical experiment, a swimming cell was captured from the cell-injection stream of the flow chamber using optic tweezers, and its steady-state swimming behavior was monitored for about 20 s. The trapped cell was then rapidly (under 30 s) positioned into the Ficoll-injection stream by moving the flow chamber using a motorized translation stage, and the bundle rotation speed was measured for up to 8 min. Following previous practices (35, 36), we used a trapping MB (TMB) instead of MB, which allowed long-term observation of flagellar rotation with minimal photodamage (fig. S2). We noted that the average speed of the bundle in TMB was 116 ± 16 Hz (mean ± SD, 115 cells), which was in agreement with previous observations (35, 36) and lower than the speed of 174 ± 27 Hz (mean ± SD, 90 cells) in MB.

An example of the flagellar rotation speed as a function of time when the bacterium experienced a stepwise increase in viscosity (from 0.99 to 4.5 cP) is shown in Fig. 6D, with more examples presented in fig. S3. In each figure, the first two data points (red circles) denote the flagellar rotation speed when the cell was in TMB without Ficoll. The following points (blue circles) show the sudden decrease and the slow recovery of speed when the cell experiences the stepwise increase in viscosity. We measured 21 cells and fitted individual recovery traces with the function a + b × {1 − exp[−(kon + koff) × t]} to obtain the sum of the rate constants kon + koff, where a and b are also fitting parameters. Derivation of the fitting function is presented in the Supplementary Materials. We obtained an average value of kon + koff = 0.009 ± 0.006 s−1 (mean ± SD), consistent with the value obtained previously (16, 17). Therefore, the timescale of the recovery is consistent with that of the stator turnover, indicating that the mechanosensitive recruitment of stator units to the flagellar motors allows the bacterium to recover its flagellar rotation speed when encountering an environment with high viscosity. The stepwise increase in speed, typically seen in the resurrection of a single motor driving the rotation of a flagellar filament (Fig. 1), is usually not observed in the recovery process because the recruitment of stator units to individual flagellar motors in the flagellar bundle is stochastic and not coordinated. As noted above, the waveform of the flagellar bundle did not change with increased viscosity, so the speed recovery was not due to any potential alteration in the waveform of the flagellar bundle.

DISCUSSION

Here, by measuring the torque-speed relationship for motors driving the rotation of individual flagellar filaments, we found that the motors work in the intermediate-load region instead of the high-load (constant torque) region as commonly assumed previously. Consistent with this, by suddenly attaching a 1-μm-diameter bead to the rotating filament to monitor motor resurrection, we found that flagellar motors driving the rotation of individual filaments contain, on average, five stator units, a half-full complement. Therefore, flagellar motors of swimming bacteria work in the intermediate-load region, containing an incomplete set of stator units. Our findings here resolved the long-time inconsistency between the motor torque obtained from swimming bacteria (21, 22, 24, 25) and that obtained from single-motor measurements (3, 4, 37).

In a natural environment, swimming bacteria may encounter different external load conditions, such as media with different viscosities and fluid-solid interfaces. The intermediate-load condition and the incomplete set of stator units in flagellar motors allow bacteria to swim robustly against changes in load conditions by mechanosensitive recruitment of stator units to the motors, which we demonstrated by subjecting the bacteria to a sudden increase in media viscosity.

Because of randomness in the assembly of flagella, multiflagellated bacteria have various numbers of flagella over a cell population, forming flagellar bundles with different numbers of flagellar filaments. The fluid-mediated interactions among the filaments decrease the load on each flagellum. Thus, the bundle rotation speed would increase with the number of filaments in the bundle if there were no compensating mechanisms. The intermediate-load condition and the incomplete set of stator units in flagellar motors we found here provide the compensating mechanism, thereby allowing bacteria to swim robustly against variations in the number of flagella.

Previous studies on bacterial swimming under different load conditions generally assumed that the flagellar motors of swimming bacteria work under high loads (23, 38–43), generating a constant torque despite changes in loads. Similarly, previous studies on the effect of flagella number on bacterial swimming speed also assumed that the flagellar motors work in the high-load (constant torque) region (21, 34, 44). In light of our findings here, those assumptions should probably be revisited.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cells and media

The strain JY27 (ΔfliC cheY) is a derivative of the E. coli K-12 strain RP437. The plasmid pBAD33FliCS353C expresses the mutant filament protein FliCS353C under an arabinose-inducible promoter. JY27 transformed with pBAD33FliCS353C was used for all experiments. Cells were grown in 3 ml of Luria-Bertani broth (1% w/v Bacto Tryptone, 0.5% w/v yeast extract, and 0.5% w/v NaCl) with appropriate antibiotics [chloramphenicol (25 mg/ml)] at 33°C overnight (for 16 hours). This bacterial solution was further diluted 1:100 with tryptone broth (TB) (1% w/v Bacto Tryptone and 0.5% w/v NaCl) and grown for approximately 4 hours at 33°C to the midexponential phase (optical density at 600 nm = 0.45 to 0.50). Arabinose (0.01%) was added at the start of incubation to induce protein expression. Ten milliliters of the TB cultivated culture was washed twice with MB [10 mM potassium phosphate, 67 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM EDTA, 1 mM methionine, and 10 mM lactic acid (pH 7.0)] by gentle centrifugation at 800g for 10 min and gently resuspended in 250 μl (for fluorescence staining) or 500 μl of MB (for motor resurrection experiments), to avoid shearing of the flagellar filaments. Ficoll 400 (F8016, dialyzed and lyophilized, Sigma-Aldrich) solutions were redialyzed to filter out possible monosaccharides from hydrolysis (45). The viscosity of Ficoll solutions at different concentrations was measured by a viscosity meter (NDJ-5 s; Shanghai Pingxuan Scientific) at a room temperature of 23°C.

Motor resurrection experiments with unsheared flagella

To biotinylate the flagellar filaments, 50 μl of the washed cell suspension was mixed with 5 μl of maleimide-PEG2-biotin (Thermo Fisher Scientific; 10 mg/ml in dimethyl sulfoxide) and stirred on a gentle gyro shaker for 20 min at room temperature. The mixture was then gently washed twice with 1 ml of MB and resuspended in 400 μl of MB. Then, 25 μl of cells was flowed into a sample chamber that was constructed with two double-sided tapes, a glass slide, and a poly-l-lysine–coated coverslip. The chamber was incubated for 3 min and rinsed with 100 μl of MB. The chamber was placed on the sample stage of a Nikon Ti-E phase-contrast microscope with a 40× objective, and the focal plane was set slightly above the position of the cell body. Next, 20 μl of 1-μm-diameter streptavidin-coated magnetic sphere (65001; Invitrogen Molecular Probes) diluted 25-fold were flowed into the chamber. In the field of view, some beads would attach to the filaments, and the rotation of the beads was recorded with a complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera (DCC1545M-GL; Thorlabs, Newton, NJ) at 50 frames/s. We ensured that the time between flowing the beads into the chamber and the start of recording the spinning beads was within 50 s. Each bead was usually measured for about 6 min. The bead rotation speeds were calculated using custom scripts in MATLAB as described previously (46). The speed traces were smoothed with a 10-point running average, resulting in a time resolution of about 0.2 s. The location and height of each speed step were determined using a step-finding algorithm described previously (11, 14). Two key parameters in the step-finding algorithm, the minimum step size in speed and the minimum length of the dwell time, were set as 0.3 Hz and 5 s, respectively. The number of stators at the initial time was obtained by dividing the initial speed by the mean speed increment per stator for the whole 6-min trace.

Fluorescent flagella imaging and analysis

We stained the flagellar filaments with a thiol-reactive dye (Alexa Fluor 568 C5 maleimide; Invitrogen Molecular Probes, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Then, 50 μl of the washed cell suspension and 5 μl of the fluorescent dye were mixed together and shaken at 80 rpm for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark (47). The mixture was then gently washed twice with 1 ml of MB to remove the excess dye and resuspended in 400 μl of MB. The labeled bacterial culture (25 μl) was sealed in a sample chamber that was constructed with two strips of double-sided tape, a glass slide, and a coverslip. We only looked for cells with bodies adhered to the coverslip surface but with flagella turning (movies S2 and S3). The flagella were imaged using the Nikon Plan Apo 100×/1.45 oil objective on a Nikon Ti-Eclipse inverted microscope equipped with a 75-W xenon lamp and a scientific complementary metal-oxide semiconductor camera (Prime95B; Photometrics). Images were captured at a rate of 496 frames/s and recorded for 10 s.

We fitted and measured the waveform parameters (length L, pitch P, and helical radius R) of the flagellar filaments and bundles using the Kappa plugin in ImageJ (https://imagej.nih.gov/ij/). The analysis of flagellar rotation speed was carried out using a custom code written in MATLAB. We chose a rectangular region that covered the entire filament or filament bundle and rotated the image so that the axis of the flagella helix was parallel to the horizontal direction. A region of interest (ROI) of 3 × 3 pixels was selected within the rectangular region, and the average intensity of the ROI was calculated for each frame of a video. The power spectrum of the average intensity within the ROI as a function of time was then calculated. This process was repeated for all possible positions of the ROI in the rectangular region, and the resulting power spectra were averaged (Fig. 2), from which the peak position could be obtained as the flagellar rotation speed.

The length of the filaments or bundles was in the range of 4 to 7 μm. We sorted the length into three groups: 4 to 5 μm, 5 to 6 μm, and 6 to 7 μm. The rotation speed of the filaments of bundles did not change much with the length (fig. S4).

Optical tweezer measurements

An individual cell was trapped using a commercially available optical tweezer setup (NanoTracker; JPK Instruments, Berlin, Germany) (48). The trapping light was provided by a 1064-nm laser through a 60× numerical aperture 1.0 water-immersion objective. Each cell was trapped with its body long axis along the beam axis. The signal from the back-focal-plane interference indicating the position of the cell body was recorded by a position-sensitive photodetector at a sample frequency of 1000 Hz.

To minimize photodamage, we used a trapping power of 100 mW for short-term trapping experiments measuring bundle rotation speeds in MB (Fig. 4A). For long-term trapping experiments using a three-channel flow chamber (see below), TMB {70 mM NaCl, 0.1 mM methionine, 100 mM tris-Cl (pH 7.5), 2% w/v glucose, and oxygen-scavenging system [glucose oxidase (80 μg/ml) and catalase (13 μg/ml)]} (49) was used to reduce trap-mediated oxidative damage to the cells (Fig. 6B), and the trapping power was set to 250 mW. The oxygen scavenging system was added 1 hour before use to reach a steady oxygen level.

The trap signal from the position-sensitive detector was analyzed using custom scripts in MATLAB. The rotational frequencies of the flagellar bundle and cell body were obtained by analyzing the power spectra of the x-direction signal of the position-sensitive detector. To monitor the change in rotation speed over time due to possible photodamage in MB or TMB, the power spectra of the signal in a running window with a width of 2 s and a slide of 0.8 s were calculated. The frequency region around the cell body rotation was then plotted as a heatmap (Fig. 4A and fig. S2A).

Three-channel flow chamber

The flow channel pattern was cut from double-sided tape and placed between two coverslips, one of which had custom-drilled holes (1-mm diameter) for inlets and outlets. In this chamber, the cross section of each channel entrance was 175 μm by 2000 μm. A fluid flow of 17 μm/s was maintained in the chamber using a syringe pump (Pump-22; Harvard Apparatus, Holliston, MA). The fluid flow was laminar, creating well-defined boundaries along the direction perpendicular to the flow. Fluorescein (46955-100G-F; Sigma-Aldrich) (100 μM) was used to quantify the boundary profile using fluorescence microscopy. A swimming cell was captured and observed for about 20 s from the cell-injection stream (viscosity of 0.99 cP) of the flow chamber and then positioned quickly into the Ficoll stream (viscosity of 4.5 cP) by moving the flow chamber using a motorized translation stage. The distance moved was at least 1000 μm, exceeding the width of the boundary mixing region (about 450 μm; Fig. 6C). The motile behavior in the Ficoll stream was observed for up to 8 min. We also performed control experiments to monitor the effect of fluid flow on the flagellar rotation speed. The flagellar rotation speed of trapped cells in 17-μm/s flow exhibited an approximately 10% decrease (from 116 ± 16 Hz to 104 ± 19 Hz) in TMB and a 3% decrease (from 77 ± 13 Hz to 75 ± 10 Hz) in higher-viscosity TMB compared to the no-flow conditions.
