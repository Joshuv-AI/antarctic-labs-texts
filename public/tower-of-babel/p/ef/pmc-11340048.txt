Health behavior inventory – short form: a Chinese validation study using classical test theory and item response theory
Authors: Lai Kun Tong, Mio Leng Au, Mu Rui Zheng, Yong Bing Liu, Guang Lei Fu, Yue Yi Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIt is imperative to use a validated tool to measure and understand health behavior as it greatly impacts health status. It has been demonstrated that the Health Behavior Inventory - Short Form (HBI-SF) is valid in several countries. The purpose of this study was to translate the HBI-SF into Chinese and test its psychometric properties.MethodsThis study employed a two-phase methodology. The phase one entailed the cross-cultural adaptation of the HBI-SF, while the subsequent phase evaluated the psychometric properties of the scale. This evaluation encompassed classical test theory (CTT) and item response theory (IRT) tests to assess reliability and validity. It involved 1058 participants, of whom 1036 completed the questionnaire successfully. Out of these, 518 were analyzed for CTT, along with 518 for IRT.ResultsThe S-CVI was found to be 0.935, while the I‐CVI ranged from 0.889 to 1.000. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis suggested the goodness-of-fit indices for the four-factor model was acceptable. Regarding the subscales, the average variance extracted (AVE) and Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio of Correlations (HTMT) matrix also met the cutoff values (AVE > 0.5 and HTMI < 0.85). Furthermore, the internal consistency and composite reliability indices of all factors were greater than 0.7. Infit and/or outfit values indicated that all items fitted the Rasch model. The Wright map revealed that the average person measures for the participants (mean = − 0.994, SD = 0.430) were comparatively lower than the average item measures (mean = 0.000, SD = 0.257). The person separation reliability values for the four factors ranged from 0.600 to 0.746, indicating an acceptable level of reliability. Two items showed differential item functioning.ConclusionsThe findings derived from both CTT and IRT analyses demonstrate favorable levels of reliability and validity for the Chinese version of the HBI-SF.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-19774-2.

Introduction

The health status of individuals is significantly influenced by their health behaviors. For example, previous research findings have demonstrated a direct correlation between the consumption of high sugar-sweetened beverages and weight gain [1], as well as an increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus [2]. Research on health behavior has far-reaching implications for public health practice and policy, as it can examine the association between specific behaviors and health outcomes [3]. Researchers can develop evidence-based interventions and strategies to promote healthy behaviors and prevent diseases by understanding and identifying the factors that influence health behaviors [4]. It is therefore essential to measure and understand health behavior with a validated tool.

There exists a multitude of measurement instruments that can be utilized for the evaluation of health behavior, encompassing both general and specific assessment tools. The utilization of generic measurement instruments facilitates the possibility of conducting comparisons and generalizing findings across diverse populations and contexts [5]. Various universal measurement tools for health behaviors are available, such as the Health Behavior Inventory – Short Form (HBI-SF; 12 items) [6], the Duke Health Profile (17 items) [7], the Health Promotion Lifestyle Profile Short Form (22 items) [8], the Health Risk Behaviors Inventory (28 items) [9], the Health Habits Scale (44 items) [10], the Health Promotion Lifestyle Profile (48 items) [11], the Health Promotion Lifestyle Profile II (52 items) [12], and among others. The HBI-SF, with its limited number of items, offers the advantage of facilitating accurate and comprehensive completion of the assessment by individuals. Consequently, this feature can enhance response rates and mitigate participant fatigue or frustration, which proves particularly advantageous in scenarios involving large sample sizes or time constraints [13, 14].

The development of the Health Behavior Inventory – 20 (HBI-20) aimed to assess health behaviors, which was subsequently simplified by Levant et al. into the 12-item HBI-SF [6]. The HBI-SF includes four subscales, two focusing on health-promoting behaviors (diet, and proper use of health care resources), and two focusing on health risk behaviors (anger and stress, and substance abuse). The HBI-SF demonstrates acceptable levels of reliability and validity, as evidenced by the Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranging from 0.62 to 0.83 for each subscale. Furthermore, the validity of the instrument was confirmed through factor analysis [6]. The HBI-SF has proven to be reliable and valid in its application to the United States, New Zealand, and Africa [6, 15, 16]. However, as far as we know, the HBI-SF has not yet been tested in the Chinese population.

The original English version of the HBI-SF has demonstrated robust psychometric properties, however, the cultural, linguistic, and contextual differences between English-speaking populations and Chinese communities necessitate the adaptation and validation of the HBI-SF to ensure its relevance and accuracy in the Chinese context. The importance of cultural adaptation extends beyond mere language translation; it involves addressing possible cultural biases and ensuring that the instrument’s psychometric properties are consistent across cultures [17]. Cultural bias in health assessment tools can result in the over- or under-representation of certain behaviors or attitudes, skewing the understanding of health-related issues within a population [18]. The adaptation of the HBI-SF for the Chinese population aims to bridge these gaps, providing a validated tool that accurately measures health behaviors in this demographic.

Previously, the assessment of psychological attributes of the HBI-SF relied on the classical test theory (CTT). However, the CTT has inherent limitations despite its widespread usage [19]. Notably, the psychometric properties of a CTT-based instrument are contingent upon the specific group being assessed. Conversely, the item response theory (IRT) ensures that item parameters (such as item difficulty and discrimination) remain unaffected by respondent groups, and likewise, group characteristics remain unaffected by the item sample [20]. Consequently, the objective of this study was to employ a combination of CTT and IRT to examine the psychological attributes of the Chinese version of the HBI-SF, aiming to acquire a more comprehensive comprehension of its psychological characteristics.

Methods

This study employed a two-phase methodology. The phase one entailed the cross-cultural adaptation of the HBI-SF, while the subsequent phase evaluated the psychometric properties of the scale. This evaluation encompassed CTT and IRT tests to assess reliability and validity.

The HBI-SF

The HBI-SF is a Likert scale consisting of 12 items and four subscales, with a 7-point rating system ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree) [6]. Items one to three and items seven to nine are reverse scored. To calculate subscale scores, participants’ responses are summed and divided by the number of items, after reverse coding, ensuring that higher scores on all subscales reflect a higher level of health risk [6].

Phase I: cross cultural adaptation process

The process of translating and culturally adapting the HBI-SF into Chinese adhered to a six-step procedure, in compliance with established guidelines and the consensus-based standards for the selection of health measurement instruments (COSMIN) [21]. Authorization for this procedure was acquired through email correspondence with the developer of the HBI-SF.

Stage 1: forward translation

Two individuals proficient in both English and Chinese, possessing medical expertise, undertook the independent translation of the HBI-SF from English to Chinese. Both bilingual individuals are native Chinese speakers, with one having completed a Chinese-English translation course and the other having successfully passed a publicly accessible English proficiency examination. Additionally, both bilingual individuals hold master’s degrees.

Stage 2: synthesis of the translations

The original version of the HBI-SF along with the two translated versions were synthesized by an expert committee in order to achieve a unified translation version. This committee comprised experts in the fields of Public Health, Primary Health Care, Nursing, Gerontology, Epidemiology, and Translation, all of whom are proficient in both English and Chinese, and included two translators at Stage 1.

Stage 3: back translation

Two bilingual experts, who were unaware of the objectives of this study and the HBI-SF, conducted independent back-translation of the synthesis version produced during the second stage into English. These two bilingual individuals differed from the bilinguals involved in stage 1. They possessed a master’s degree and held positions as English instructors at a university.

Stage 4: expert committee review

The primary responsibility of the expert committee was to amalgamate all versions of the HBI-SF and formulate the pre-final version for subsequent field testing. The committee had access to the initial version as well as each translated version, accompanied by accompanying written reports elucidating the reasoning behind decisions made during earlier phases. The committee was comprised of two translators at stage 3, in addition to the members at stage 2.

Stage 5: pretesting

According to the established principles of best practices for translating and culturally adapting patient-reported outcome measures, the recommended sample size for a pre-test usually ranges from 5 to 8 participants [22]. Pre-final translated version was tested with seven participants. After filling out the questionnaires, participants were interviewed face-to-face to get feedback. The participants’ ability in independently completing the questionnaire was observed, and they were encouraged to report any issues pertaining to wording, layout, or instructions. The expert committee then assessed these findings.

Stage 6: submission and appraisal of all the written reports by developers / committee

As the comprehensive documentation of the cross-cultural adaptation process, the final report was submitted to the committee along with all accompanying reports. The survey study employed the final Chinese version of the HBI-SF.

The translation process and strategies in this study effectively addressed equivalent translation issues [23]. The translators involved in Stage 1 and Stage 3 are proficient in both Chinese and English, and they have extensive experience in translating between these languages, providing assurance for vocabulary equivalence, idiomatic equivalence, and grammatical-syntactical equivalence. The experts involved in Stage 2 and Stage 3 are also proficient in both Chinese and English; in addition to translation experts, other domain experts are engaged in research related to health behaviors, providing assurance for experiential equivalence. The implementation in Stage 5 provided assurance for conceptual equivalence.

Phase II: psychometric evaluation

Study design and participants

The psychometric evaluation of the instrument was conducted through a cross-sectional study encompassing both the eastern and western regions of China. Specifically, the eastern region included Guangdong Province, Jiangsu Province, and Macao Special Administrative Region, while the western region consisted of Sichuan Province.

The online survey employed convenience sampling to recruit participants who satisfied the specified inclusion and exclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria encompassed individuals aged 18 years or older residing in the aforementioned regions, possessing the ability to comprehend, read, and write in Chinese, and willingly volunteering to partake. Conversely, the exclusion criteria encompassed individuals unable to independently complete the electronic questionnaire. A sample size of 200 subjects is considered ‘very good’ by the COSMIN Risk of Bias checklist [24]. The recommended sample size for CTT is 10 cases per item [25], equating to 120 in this study, while IRT requires a sample size exceeding 500 [25]. Our aim was to maintain the reliability and validity of the research outcomes while also considering practical implementation feasibility. Therefore, the sample size was determined as follows: N = max (CTT sample size, IRT sample size), resulting in a sample size of 500 for this study. Consequently, the sample size was determined using the formula N = max (CTT sample size, IRT sample size), resulting in a final sample size of 500 for this study.

Data collection

The research team disseminated research promotional posters through social platforms and chat groups, enabling participants to access online data collection platforms via QR codes or links provided on the posters. Data collection was conducted using Wenjuanxing (https://www.wjx.cn/), the predominant online data collection platform in China. In order to guarantee the quality of the data, each IP address or device was allowed to fill in the questionnaire only once. Data were collected between June 16, 2023 and July 1, 2023.

Statistical analysis

The data collected was evenly partitioned into two distinct segments, with one allocated for the purpose of conducting CTT analysis and the other designated for IRT analysis. This study employed both CTT and IRT for their complementary advantages. CTT offers a foundational framework for assessing the reliability and validity, but its assumption of equal item contribution can be limiting. In contrast, IRT estimates item difficulty and discrimination, offering a detailed view of each item’s role in measuring the latent trait [20]. By integrating both approaches, this study aimed to comprehensively evaluate the HBI-SF, ensuring a robust assessment of its psychometric properties and enhancing data interpretation accuracy. The data was analyzed utilizing the software jamovi 2.3.28 [26].

CTT analysis

The content validity of the scale was assessed using the Content Validity Index (CVI), which included both the Scale Level CVI (S-CVI) and the Item Level CVI (I-CVI). A total of nine experts from various fields (including Public Health, Health Education, Chronic Disease, Tobacco Control, Epidemiology, Community Nursing, Aged Care, and Family Counselling and Family Education) were invited to rate each item on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = not relevant, 4 = highly relevant). The minimum acceptable value for the S-CVI is 0.9 or above, while the I-CVI should be 0.78 or higher [27].

Confirmatory factor analysis was employed to validate the original scale’s four-factor structure. Given the advantages of robust maximum likelihood in providing less biased standard error estimates and accurately recovering population interfactor correlations [28], the confirmatory factor analysis in this study was employed this estimator. Various fit indices were used to evaluate the model’s adequacy to the sampled data, including the Goodness of Fit (GFI), Normed Fit Index (NFI), TLI, CFI, RMSEA, and SRMR. The following criteria were considered met for the acceptance of a model: GFI ≥ 0.90, NFI ≥ 0.90, TLI ≥ 0.90, CFI ≥ 0.90, RMSEA ≤ 0.10, SRMR ≤ 0.10 [29]. Convergence validity was evaluated using average variance extracted (AVE), with a threshold of 0.5 indicating satisfactory convergent validity. The discriminant validity was examined through the Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio of Correlations (HTMT) matrix (HTMI < 0.85) [30].

Internal consistency reliability was measured using Cronbach’s alpha, with a value of 0.7 considered acceptable [31]. The study revealed that Cronbach’s alpha is impacted by varying item loadings, leading to the superior performance of McDonald’s ωt [32]. Consequently, this study included an evaluation of McDonald’s ωt alongside the traditional Cronbach’s alpha. The assessment of construct reliability was conducted by evaluating the composite reliability (rho_c), with a threshold of ≥ 0.7 deemed acceptable [33].

IRT analysis

In IRT, the assumption of unidimensionality plays a crucial role. We evaluated unidimensionality through principal component analysis and verified that the variance proportion of the first factor exceeded 20% [34]. Specifically, the variance proportions for the four factors - Diet, Anger and stress, Proper use of health care resources, and Substance use - were found to be 79.2%, 69.2%, 70.1%, and 84.6%, respectively. This evidence suggests sufficient unidimensionality within each factor.

The Rasch model is not only applicable to dichotomous data, but also demonstrates efficacy in handling ordered polytomous data [35]. Rasch model for polytomous items was applied in this study. The adequacy of the model’s fit was assessed through the examination of infit and outfit statistics, where an acceptable range for the infit and outfit mean square (MNSQ) values fell between 0.6 and 1.5 [36].

The Wright map was employed to assess the appropriateness of the targeted between person measures and item measures. Persons with a greater susceptibility to health risks and items necessitating a higher level of health risk endorsement were positioned more prominently on the Wright map. Conversely, individuals with a lower susceptibility to health risks and items endorsed by those with a lower susceptibility were situated towards the lower extremity of the Wright map.

The measure of person separation reliability serves as an indicator of the extent to which the index effectively distinguishes between individuals. A reliable index is defined as having a person separation reliability value exceeding 0.7 [37], while a value above 0.6 is deemed acceptable for a construct comprising merely four items [38]. Differential item functioning (DIF) represents a manifestation of differential validity, employed to evaluate the likelihood of comprehending and responding to an item among individuals of varying genders. An item was deemed biased if the p-value was found to be less than 0.05 [39].

Results

Phase I: cross cultural adaptation process

In the forward translation and back translation stages, the translators’ renditions exhibited a high degree of similarity, with only a few instances of synonym usage. However, during the expert committee review stage, the experts proposed specific modifications, such as incorporating the clarification of “2 alcoholic drinks” for item 11 and introducing the example of “recreational drugs” for item 12. According to these recommendations, the research team diligently incorporated pertinent explanations and examples, aiming to facilitate a precise comprehension of the items among Chinese residents. In the pretesting stage, participants indicated that all items were easy to understand. Supplementary Table S1 presents the original version and Chinese version of the HBI-SF.

Phase II: psychometric evaluation

Samples description

A total of 1058 participants were enlisted for the study, with 1036 successfully completing the questionnaire. Out of these, 518 samples were utilized for CTT analysis, while an equal number of 518 samples were employed for IRT analysis. The majority of participants in both samples were female, with over half possessing college or undergraduate degrees (Table 1). Samples were aged 18 to 84 years for CTT analysis and 18 to 85 years for IRT analysis.

The person separation reliability values for the four factors ranged from 0.600 to 0.746, indicating an acceptable level of reliability (Table 4). Upon evaluating the DIF with respect to gender, it was observed that item 10 and item 12 exhibited a significant contrast in responses between males and females, with a discrepancy of 29.15 logits and 22.43 logits, respectively (Table 4). This discrepancy suggests that males reported a greater impact of these items. Despite the presence of DIF in item 10 and item 12, they were nevertheless retained in the analysis.

Discussion

This study adhered to the COSMIN guideline for the translation of the HBI-SF into Chinese. Within this study, both CTT and IRT were employed to assess the psychological attributes of the Chinese version of the HBI-SF. To the best of our knowledge, this study represents the initial endeavor to validate the health behavior measurement instrument utilizing both CTT and IRT methodologies. The findings derived from both CTT and IRT analyses demonstrate favorable levels of reliability and validity for the Chinese version of the HBI-SF.

This study adhered to the COSMIN guideline for conducting the translation and cultural adaptation of the HBI-SF [21]. The prescribed procedures were meticulously implemented, encompassing forward translation, synthesis of the translations, back translation, expert committee review, pretesting, submission and appraisal of all the written reports by developers/committee. Given that the health literacy levels of Chinese residents have shown an upward trend in recent years, it is noteworthy that in 2020, these levels remained below 24.0% [40]. Consequently, during the expert committee review stage, it was recommended by experts to incorporate explanations for item 11 and item 12, aiming to enhance the general comprehension of the items among Chinese residents.

As a final version, item 11 was revised to read ‘I drink more than 2 alcoholic drinks (i.e., more than 20 grams of alcohol) per day.’ Item 12 was revised to read ‘I use recreational drugs (e.g., cocaine, marijuana, and ketamine).’ The feedback received from participants in the pretest stage provided confirmation of the Chinese version’s efficacy in accurately communicating the intended message. Moreover, the evaluation of content validity yielded satisfactory S-CVI and I-CVI values, signifying that the instrument adequately encompassed pertinent content for the assessment of health behavior among the Chinese population.

This study also explored the structural validity of the HBI-SF through the utilization of confirmatory factor analysis. The findings demonstrated satisfactory model fit indices, suggesting that the four-factor structure of the HBI-SF reasonably represented the underlying construct of health behavior within the Chinese population. Moreover, the internal consistency, convergent validity, and discriminant validity of each factor were found to be favorable. In this study, the internal consistency of all factors exceeds that of the original scale [6]. Among the four factors under consideration, it is noteworthy that the person separation reliability of the Substance Use factor was comparatively suboptimal. This factor encompasses three items pertaining to smoking, drinking, and recreational drug use, a composition likely influenced by the higher prevalence of smoking and drinking in comparison to recreational drug use across various global regions. Furthermore, it is important to acknowledge that individuals engaging in recreational drug use within the Chinese population tend to conceal this behavior [41], thereby contributing to a lower person separation reliability for the Substance Use factor specifically within this demographic. While smoking and alcohol consumption can be classified as forms of substance abuse, it is imperative to acknowledge that recreational drug use poses significantly greater health risks compared to these behaviors [42]. Consequently, future research endeavors should incorporate a comprehensive evaluation of various health behaviors, assigning appropriate weights to reflect their respective health effects and accurately represent the genuine health hazards they entail.

The utilization of IRT analysis facilitated the evaluation of item fit for the Chinese version of the HBI-SF. The findings demonstrated favorable item fit statistics across all factors, suggesting the items effectively differentiated between various levels of the latent trait within each factor. Furthermore, the participants demonstrated a lower level of endorsement for items within the Anger and Stress factor, whereas items within the Substance Use factor received the highest level of endorsement. This finding aligns with the health behavior situation in China, where stress is reported as the most prevalent issue [43], while substance use is not widely observed [44]. The HBI-SF was not defective with regard to items, but in terms of assessing the appropriateness of targeted between person measures and item measures for Chinese populations, the tool needs to be improved. The health behavior items proved to be challenging for the participants due to the fact that many of the items requiring greater health risks were not endorsed by the participants. The HBI-SF encompasses four factors, namely diet, Anger and Stress, Proper Use of Health Care Resources, and Substance Use. However, it is noteworthy that physical activity, a crucial health behavior [45], was not incorporated into the HBI-SF. To enhance the suitability of the HBI-SF for the Chinese population, future research should contemplate the inclusion of physical activity-related items. Two items exhibited DIF based on gender. The presence of DIF by gender indicates that the two items possess dissimilar connotations for males and females. Specifically, in China, the prevalence of smoking among males exceeds 50%, whereas it remains below 3% among females [46]. Consequently, in comparison to males, females are more inclined to strongly disagree when responding to the statement “I use tobacco products”. Similarly, in China, the prevalence of drug use among males significantly surpasses that among females [47], leading women to be more prone to strongly disagree when asked about their usage of recreational drugs. DIF signifies the presence of a group impact on the outcomes of a measurement. It is important to note that DIF should not be equated with mere measurement bias. The findings of DIF analysis offer valuable insights for interpreting research outcomes, particularly within the group where DIF is observed, thereby promoting a more informed understanding of the results [48]. There exists a gender DIF in these two items; however, it aligns with the facts, thus justifying the retention of both items.

Limitation

This study has certain limitations. Firstly, the validity of the Chinese version of the HBI-SF was assessed using both CTT analysis and IRT analysis. However, criterion validity testing, specifically concurrent validity, was not conducted due to the absence of a gold standard for measuring health behavior. Secondly, data collection for this study was conducted online, which raises concerns about the reliability and validity of the paper scale. Thirdly, the sample exhibited a female-dominant distribution, which may introduce gender bias and limit the broader applicability of the findings. Future research should aim for a more diverse and representative sample to improve the generalizability of the study outcomes.

Conclusions

The Chinese version of the HBI-SF demonstrates good reliability and validity within Chinese populations. These findings hold substantial implications for both scholarly inquiry and clinical application. Researchers can confidently employ the HBI-SF as a tool to evaluate health behaviors among the Chinese populace, thereby enhancing comprehension of health-related matters within this specific cultural milieu. Moreover, clinicians can effectively employ the HBI-SF to inform interventions and treatment strategies customized to the unique requirements of Chinese individuals, thereby fostering favorable health behaviors.
