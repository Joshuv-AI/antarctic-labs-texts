Comorbidities and Events of Clinical Interest in Patients with Pulmonary Arterial Hypertension (PAH): Analysis of Real‐World Data
Authors: Julia R. DiBello, Dena Rosen Ramey, Alexandra G. Cornell, Harald Sinclair Mackenzie, Tammy Jiang, Michelle Skornicki, Dionna Andrean, Jackie Lucia, Ioana R. Preston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) patients frequently present with cardiometabolic comorbidities and experience substantial hematologic and cardiovascular events. In a retrospective U.S. linked claims‐EHR cohort (n = 16,749), hypertension, heart failure, diabetes, and CKD were common. Incidence rates (per 100 person‐years) for thrombocytopenia and major bleeding were 8.2 and 7.3, respectively.

Pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) is a rare, progressive disease characterized by remodeling of pulmonary vasculature and an increase in pulmonary vascular load, leading to hypertrophy and remodeling of the right ventricle [1]. PAH Group 1 can be classified as idiopathic (IPAH), heritable, or associated with other conditions including connective tissue disease (CTD), congenital heart disease, portal hypertension, human immunodeficiency virus, anorexigen exposure, or schistosomiasis [2].

Approximately 80% of PAH patients have cardiometabolic comorbidities at diagnosis and the presence of these conditions serve as prognostic markers of worse disease outcome [3, 4]. Patients with PAH are also at increased risk of many comorbidities due to their underlying disease and treatments. Anticoagulants are frequently prescribed to PAH patients due to an increased risk of thromboembolic events [5], as well as for various comorbidities, and these medications are associated with an increased risk of bleeding [6]. PAH medications (prostacyclins) have also been associated with increased risk of bleeding and thrombocytopenia [7].

There is limited literature in real‐world settings outside of PAH registries describing factors that affect treatment decisions and disease trajectory in PAH, such as cardiometabolic co‐morbidities and hematological abnormalities including thrombocytopenia and major bleeding events [3, 4, 8]. The primary objective of this study was to describe cardiometabolic comorbidities prior to or at the time of diagnosis among adult PAH patients and to estimate the incidence rate of thrombocytopenia, major bleeding, myocardial infarction (MI), and ischemic and embolic stroke after PAH diagnosis.

Methods

This retrospective study utilized linked administrative claims and EHR data from Optum's de‐identified Market Clarity Data (Optum® Market Clarity) including > 86 million patients in the United States. As there was no standard methodology to identify PAH patients from real world data (RWD) [9, 10, 11], a validated algorithm, modified to include echocardiogram as a PAH diagnostic, was utilized. This algorithm had the highest PPV (86%) among available algorithms and was reported to identify PAH patients with all underlying etiologies [11]. The PAH cohort included patients ≥ 18 years with ≥ 2 diagnosis codes for primary pulmonary hypertension (ICD‐9 416.0, ICD‐10 I27.0) who had ≥ 1 claim/record for right heart catheterization or echocardiogram within ± 12 months of index date (defined as the first occurrence of a PAH ICD diagnosis code from July 1, 2007 to March 1, 2021), ≥ 1 claim for PAH‐specific therapies (in the 6‐months before or any time after index date), and continuous enrollment/activity for 180‐days prior to and including index date. Patients were excluded if they had a history of pulmonary hypertension (PAH or any other type of pulmonary hypertension) in 180‐days before the index date or < 30 days of continuous enrollment/activity after index date.

Patients were followed from 1‐day after the index date until the first occurrence of an event of interest, death, disenrollment, or end of available data. Medication use was summarized as percent of patients with any record for the medications of interest. Comorbidities were assessed using ICD‐9/ICD‐10 codes within the 180‐day period before and on the index date. Events of interest (including thrombocytopenia, MI, major bleeding events, and ischemic and embolic stroke) were identified using ICD‐9/ICD‐10 diagnostic codes.

Results

The mean age at diagnosis of PAH (n = 16,749) was 61.9 years with 60.5% of patients ≥ 60 years of age. The majority of patients were female (58%), Caucasian (70%) and non‐Hispanic (83%). The most frequently observed cardiometabolic co‐morbidities were hypertension (78%), congestive heart failure (59%), diabetes mellitus (42%), and chronic kidney disease stage 3+ (30%).

Median follow‐up time was 1,049 (IQR: 447, 1,889) days. The most common PAH‐specific treatments were phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitors (73%) and endothelin receptor antagonists (29%); 19% of patients used prostacyclin analogues/prostacyclin pathway agonists and 79% of patients used anticoagulants/antiplatelets.

MI, ischemic and embolic stroke occurred in 5.6% (95% CI: 5.3, 6.0), 3.1% (95% CI: 2.8, 3.3), and 0.7% (95% CI: 0.6, 0.9), of patients, respectively, and incidence rates were 1.7 (95% CI:1.6, 1.8), 0.9 (95% CI:0.8, 1.0), and 0.2 (95% CI: 0.18, 0.26) per 100 person‐years. Approximately 23.7% (95% CI: 23.0, 24.3) and 21.6% (95% CI: 21.0, 22.2) of patients developed thrombocytopenia or major bleeding events, with incidence rates of 8.2 (95% CI: 8.0, 8.5) and 7.3 (95% CI: 7.0, 7.5) per 100 person‐years, respectively (Table 1). The occurrence of major bleeding events was consistent by sex with incidence rates (per 100 person‐years) of 7.4 (95% CI: 7.1, 7.7) and 7.1 (95% CI: 6.8, 7.5) for females and males, respectively. Some variability in major bleeding rates was observed by race; the lowest rates were observed in Asian patients (4.3 (95% CI: 2.7, 5.8)), followed by Caucasians (6.9 (95% CI: 6.6, 7.1)) with the highest observed rates in African American patients (8.5 (95% CI: 7.9, 9.1)). The incidence rate of thrombocytopenia was higher in males (8.9 (95% CI: 8.4, 9.3) compared to females (7.8 (95% CI: 7.4, 8.1)). The lowest incidence rate of thrombocytopenia was observed in Caucasian patients (7.9 (95% CI: 7.6, 8.2), followed by African American (8.8 (95% CI: 8.2, 9.4) and Asian patients 8.9 (95% CI: 6.4, 11.3).

Discussion

Consistent with the published literature [6, 12, 13], cardiometabolic conditions were common. The prevalence of hypertension and diabetes vary from 12% to 51% and 12%–29%, respectively based on published PAH registry data [6, 12, 13]. The lower prevalence observed in registries compared to our study may be due to the younger mean age in these samples compared to our sample and the time period of analysis (latest registry data from 2016) as the prevalence of cardiometabolic conditions has increased over time [14].

Phosphodiesterase‐5 inhibitors (PDE5i) were the most frequently prescribed PAH‐specific medication in this study, followed by endothelin receptor antagonists (ERAs), prostacyclin analogues/prostacyclin pathway agonists, and soluble guanylyl cyclase stimulators. Similar findings were reported in a recent publication based on 1,019 incident, treated PAH patients from the US based PHAR registry; nitric oxide pathway drugs (PDE5is or soluble guanylate cyclase) were the most frequently used medications, with 87% of patients receiving drugs in this class, followed by ERAs (55%), and prostacyclin pathway agents (34%) [15].

To our knowledge this is the first study estimating rates of major bleeding and thrombocytopenia in a large real‐world population of PAH patients outside of PAH registries. Our estimate for the rate of major bleeding, 7.3 per 100 person‐years, is consistent with the range of estimates provided in a previous single center study including IPAH and CTD‐PAH patients prescribed anticoagulant therapy, where incidence rates were reported as 5.4 per 100 and 19 per 100 patient‐years, respectively [16]. Thrombocytopenia occurred in a similar proportion of patients in our study (24%) compared to the proportion seen in the Mayo clinic PAH registry data (20%) [8].

The REVEAL registry characterized the primary reason for hospitalization in newly diagnosed PAH patients and reported 1.9%, and 0.9% were hospitalized for acute coronary syndrome, and ischemic stroke, respectively [17]. The proportions of patients hospitalized in REVEAL is lower than those found in our study likely due to differences in mean age (50 years vs. 62 years) and the recruitment and treatment of PAH patients at specialty centers within REVEAL versus the real‐world PAH population included in the current study.

A key strength of this study is the large sample of real‐world PAH patients allowing for robust estimation of co‐morbidity prevalence and frequencies of events of interest. However, there are limitations to this study inherent to working with RWD. There is no standard methodology to distinguish the clinical classifications of PH or subtypes of PAH using real‐world data. However, to reduce misclassification a validated algorithm for PAH was utilized. It should be noted that this algorithm may have missed PAH cases coded with ICD‐10 I27.21 in the later part of the study period (2018–2021) as this code was not released until 2017 after the development of the algorithm used in this study.

This study provides important information on the prevalence of cardiometabolic co‐morbidities and robust estimates of rates of clinically relevant events in a large real‐world population of PAH patients that are useful for the comprehensive management of PAH.

Ethics Statement

Ethical approval was waived for this retrospective study because it used de‐identified data. The study was a secondary analysis of existing, non‐identifiable information and did not involve human subjects or intervention.
