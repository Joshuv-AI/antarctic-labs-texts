Influence of increased slice thickness and sharpening filter application in the detection of bone graft loss around implants
Authors: Débora Costa Ruiz, Matheus Barros-Costa, Michelle Chang, Henrique Mateus Alves Felizardo, Hugo Gaêta-Araujo, Deborah Queiroz Freitas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeThis study investigated the effect of increasing slice thickness and applying sharpening filters on the diagnosis of bone graft loss in regions adjacent to zirconia implants using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT).Materials and MethodsTwelve zirconia implants were inserted into mandibles, followed by bone graft application. Graft loss was then simulated in half of the samples. CBCT scans were obtained using 2 devices (OP300 and Eagle 3D) with parameters set at 90 kVp, 8 mA, and a 133 µm voxel size. Five examiners assessed the scans under different conditions: original thickness, 1 mm, 2 mm, no filter, Sharpen 1×, and Sharpen 2×. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated and compared using 2-way analysis of variance (α=5%).ResultsFor the OP300 device, increasing slice thickness to 1 mm impaired AUC, sensitivity, and specificity metrics (P<0.05). Overall, filter application reduced this impairment (P<0.05). For the Eagle device, slice thickness did not affect diagnostic metrics, but specificity values increased with sharpening filter application on scans at original thickness or with 1 mm slices (P<0.05).ConclusionFor 1 device, a 1 mm increase in slice thickness impaired the diagnosis of bone graft loss in areas adjacent to zirconia implants. However, the application of sharpening filters helped mitigate this impairment.Keywords: Cone-Beam Computed Tomography, Dental Implants, Artifacts, Zirconium, Image Enhancement

Introduction

Dental implant therapy is one of the most widely used procedures for the rehabilitation of missing teeth and has gained significant popularity in dentistry.1 Its versatility allows for the replacement of single teeth as well as the support of prosthetic appliances.1 A fundamental property of the materials used in implant fabrication is biocompatibility, which reduces the likelihood of allergic reactions and supports favorable osseointegration.2,3

A considerable proportion of patients require bone grafting before dental implant placement. Because bone grafting adds a substantial financial burden, ensuring successful osseointegration is critical.4,5 Therefore, the early detection of bone graft loss is essential for treatment success. Accurate imaging techniques capable of detecting subtle graft changes are central to this process.6 In this context, cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT) has become widely adopted. Its ability to generate 3-dimensional visualization makes it superior to digital periapical radiography in certain diagnostic scenarios.7,8 However, despite these advantages, image artifacts can interfere with diagnostic interpretation.9 Such artifacts are caused by high-density materials with high atomic numbers that alter X-ray attenuation and scattering.10,11

Dental implants are composed of materials with high atomic numbers and physical densities. As a result, evaluating the peri-implant region may be significantly compromised by image artifacts, which can impair the detection of bone graft loss.12,13 The manifestation of these artifacts is influenced by the CBCT acquisition parameters and post-processing tools, including slice thickness adjustments and sharpening filter applications.14,15 Variations in slice thickness can affect spatial resolution and potentially impact the visibility of anatomical structures and subtle bone alterations. Similarly, sharpening filters, intended to enhance image quality, may improve visualization of critical details or, conversely, obscure important diagnostic features. Importantly, both of these tools can be applied without increasing the patient’s radiation exposure, making them favorable options for optimizing image quality.16,17,18

Despite their potential impact, no prior research has specifically examined how variations in slice thickness and sharpening filter application affect the detection of bone graft loss in CBCT scans. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to determine whether these parameters influence diagnostic accuracy for detecting bone graft loss in CBCT imaging.

Material and Methods

This study was approved by the local Institutional Research Ethics Committee under registration number CAAE: 66035322.8.0000.5419 and was conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration.

Sample preparation

Six dried human mandibles and a trephine drill (WF Cirúrgicos, São Paulo, Brazil) were used to prepare 2 implant sites in each posterior region per mandible, measuring 5 mm in depth and 8 mm in width. Twelve zirconia implants (Neodent Ceramic Implant System, Neodent, Curitiba, Brazil), sized 3.75 × 10 mm, were installed perpendicular to the occlusal plane at a depth of 10 mm by an implantologist following the manufacturer’s protocol and drill sequence (Neodent Control Stop Drills, Neodent, Curitiba, Brazil).

A particulate xenogenous bone graft (Bonefill Porous Fino, Bionnovation Biomedical, São Paulo, Brazil) was hydrated with saline solution and then used to fill the twelve implant sites up to the level of the cover screw. In 3 mandibles (6 sites), the graft material was removed up to the level of the third implant thread on the buccal side to simulate bone graft loss (Fig. 1). In the remaining 3 mandibles (6 sites), the graft material was maintained up to the level of the cover screw to simulate intact graft sites.

Mandibles with a graft and with graft loss. A. Particulate xenograft present. B. Graft removed to the third thread of the implant.

The mandibles were stabilized and placed in a plastic container. Ballistic gelatin was prepared and applied to cover the mandibles to mimic soft-tissue attenuation of X-rays, thereby replicating clinical imaging conditions.19,20 The mandibles were then stored under refrigeration until image acquisition.

Image acquisition

Two scans per mandible were obtained, each capturing the area of interest on 1 side of the mandible, using 2 CBCT devices: OP300 (Instrumentarium, Tuusula, Finland) and Eagle 3D (Dabi Atlante, Ribeirão Preto, Brazil). Imaging parameters were set at 90 kVp, 8 mA, 133 µm voxel size, and the smallest available field of view for each device (OP300: 6 × 4 cm; Eagle 3D: 5 × 5 cm). A total of 24 CBCT scans were acquired (2 CBCT devices × 12 mandible sites).

Image preparation and evaluation

The 24 scans were exported in DICOM format and renamed according to a random numerical sequence. Five specialists in Oral Radiology (each with ≥5 years of CBCT interpretation experience), blinded to the true condition and sample distribution, independently assessed the scans. Before assessment, all examiners received instructions on the evaluation criteria (presence or absence of bone graft loss) using scans not included in the study sample, as well as guidance on completing the answer spreadsheet.

The assessments were performed in a quiet, low-light room using OnDemand 3D software (Cybermed, Seoul, Korea). For each CBCT scan, examiners recorded their evaluation on a spreadsheet specifying slice thickness—original (0.13 mm, derived from the 133 µm voxel size), 1 mm, and 2 mm—and filter condition (no filter, Sharpen 1×, and Sharpen 2×) (Figs. 2 and 3). To replicate clinical practice, examiners were permitted to adjust brightness, contrast, and zoom. In total, 216 CBCT datasets were assessed (24 original scans × 3 slice thicknesses × 3 sharpening filter conditions).

A 5-point scale was used for diagnostic classification of each scan: (1) absence of bone graft loss, (2) probable absence of bone graft loss, (3) uncertainty, (4) probable presence of bone graft loss, and (5) presence of bone graft loss. Inter-examiner agreement was calculated across the 5 evaluators. Thirty days after the initial assessment, 30% of the sample (65 scans) was randomly selected and re-assessed to determine intra-examiner reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with a significance level of 5% (P<0.05). The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity were calculated by comparing examiner assessments with the actual condition of each site (presence or absence of bone graft loss). These values were calculated separately for each examiner across the tested variables. Results were compared using 2-way analysis of variance with the Tukey post hoc test. Inter- and intra-examiner agreement was assessed using the weighted kappa test and interpreted according to the Landis and Koch scale.21

Results

Table 1 presents the mean and standard deviation (SD) values for the diagnostic metrics related to bone graft loss, based on scans obtained using the OP300 device. Statistically significant influence was observed for both tested variables: the slice thickness and the filtering condition. When an increase of 1 mm in slice thickness was used, there was a significant decrease in AUC, sensitivity, and specificity metrics compared to the original slice thickness and to a 2 mm increase. However, this reduction in AUC, sensitivity and specificity metrics was not observed when the Sharpen 1× or Sharpen 2× filters were applied. In those cases where a reduction in the values was observed at 1mm thickness, the application of Sharpen 1× or Sharpen 2× resulted in significantly higher values, making the values for original, 1 mm and 2 mm thickness similar. For AUC and specificity at 1 mm thickness, values for Sharpen 1× and Sharpen 2× were higher than those for without a filter, but not different from each other; for sensitivity, Sharpen 2× was significantly higher than Sharpen 1×, which was significantly higher than without the filter condition.

Discussion

The present findings indicate that increasing slice thickness by 1 mm negatively affected the detection of bone graft loss in 1 of the CBCT devices evaluated. In contrast, the use of sharpening filters improved the visualization of the compromised area. Given the clinical importance of promptly detecting bone graft loss, particularly in peri-implant regions that are often affected by imaging artifacts, these results underscore the need to optimize image processing parameters. Specifically, while increasing slice thickness may hinder diagnostic accuracy, the application of sharpening filters can mitigate this limitation, thereby supporting more reliable interpretation when bone graft loss is suspected.

The 2 CBCT devices exhibited distinct responses to changes in slice thickness and filtering. For the OP300, both factors significantly influenced diagnostic performance, with a decrease in accuracy at 1 mm thickness—an effect mitigated by sharpening filters. In contrast, the Eagle 3D showed no significant variation in AUC or sensitivity, with improvements observed only in specificity when filters were applied. These differences may be related to variations in detector resolution and reconstruction algorithms.11 The OP300 appears more susceptible to resolution loss from slice thickness adjustments, whereas the Eagle 3D maintains greater image stability. Furthermore, sharpening filters were more effective in restoring diagnostic performance in the OP300, possibly due to differences in how each system processes edge detail.

Sharpening filters enhance the definition of anatomical structures by accentuating their boundaries.22,23 This effect facilitates the identification of subtle changes, such as bone defects or discontinuities, making them more apparent. Accordingly, it is not surprising that sharpening filters improved diagnostic accuracy for bone graft loss in this study. A previous investigation on vertical root fractures in teeth with metallic posts reported that sharpening filters, when combined with the metal artifact reduction (MAR) tool, enhanced visualization of fracture lines.24 This is consistent with the present findings. However, the MAR tool was not evaluated here, as earlier studies have shown that its use may reduce accuracy in other diagnostic tasks.6,25,26 Moreover, MAR performance varies substantially depending on device type and intensity settings, with extreme MAR adjustments on the Eagle 3D shown to impair visualization of diagnostic features such as bone graft loss.6 In addition, because MAR is not universally available across CBCT systems, other post-processing tools were prioritized.27

Interestingly, the negative effect of increased slice thickness on graft loss detection was observed only with a 1 mm increase. Contrary to expectations, a 2 mm slice thickness did not compromise visualization. One might assume that if a 1 mm increase reduces diagnostic accuracy, a 2 mm increase would exacerbate this effect. However, diagnostic performance at 2 mm thickness was similar to that at the original setting.

These findings suggest that the relationship between slice thickness and image interpretability may not be strictly linear, particularly in the presence of artifacts. In this study, the zirconia implant likely contributed to this effect. The proposed explanation involves the partial volume effect—a phenomenon in which structures of different densities are represented within a single voxel, making them appear similar on CBCT scans.11,28,29 Increasing the slice thickness by 1 mm may have led to a more pronounced overlap and concentration of artifacts caused by the zirconia implant, thereby worsening interpretability. At 2 mm thickness, however, the stronger partial volume effect, despite reducing spatial resolution, may have averaged density variations across a larger volume, attenuating artifact severity and preventing further diagnostic impairment.

A previous study on slice thickness and peri-implant bone defect detection reported that thickness variations did not significantly influence diagnostic performance.14 That study also used a zirconia implant and the same CBCT device (OP300) as in the present research. However, while the earlier study evaluated alveolar bone loss around implants, the current study specifically assessed the detection of bone graft material loss. This methodological distinction may account for the differing results and highlights the need for further research on slice thickness effects across different diagnostic tasks, such as root resorption or furcation defects.

Future investigations into sharpening filters are also warranted. Although some studies have reported that sharpening filters increase image noise, this did not compromise graft loss diagnosis in the present research.16 This finding suggests that the impact of increased noise may vary depending on the anatomical structure being assessed. In some cases, enhanced edge definition may outweigh the negative effects of additional noise, and radiologists may not perceive the noise as detrimental.

This study adopted an ex vivo design, which cannot fully replicate the complexity of real clinical scenarios. Factors such as patient movement during scanning and individual anatomical variability may introduce artifacts or variations absent in a controlled experimental setting. Moreover, the deliberate simulation of bone graft loss and the use of ionizing radiation preclude performing such a study in human subjects for ethical and practical reasons. Despite these limitations, the study was conducted under rigorously standardized conditions, ensuring methodological consistency. To enhance clinical relevance, the mandibles were covered with ballistic gelatin, simulating soft-tissue attenuation of the X-ray beam.30 In addition, all CBCT scans were randomized across slice thickness and filter application, ensuring that images were evaluated in an unpredictable sequence. This procedure minimized examiner bias from familiarity with previously assessed cases or from favoring specific conditions. As a result, the findings remain valid and clinically relevant for guiding CBCT interpretation in cases of suspected graft failure.

Furthermore, because this was a diagnostic accuracy study rather than a subjective evaluation of image quality, the authors believe that the examiners’ awareness of slice thickness and filter settings did not influence their judgments. Unlike preference-based studies, diagnostic assessments depend on clinical reasoning rather than perceptual preference, and are therefore less prone to bias.16 The intra- and inter-examiner agreement levels observed here ranged from fair to almost perfect and from fair to substantial, respectively. Notably, intra-examiner agreement values were higher than those reported in previous studies involving regions adjacent to high-density materials that generate artifacts.6,9,14,15,16 This improvement may be attributed to the standardized instruction session conducted prior to evaluation. During this session, examiners received detailed guidance on the imaging features to assess, proper use of the 5-point diagnostic scale, and the correct completion of the evaluation spreadsheet. This preparatory training likely contributed to the enhanced reliability observed.

In summary, the impact of slice thickness and sharpening filter application on bone graft loss diagnosis varies depending on the CBCT device. For 1 of the devices evaluated, increasing slice thickness to 1 mm impaired diagnostic accuracy in detecting graft loss adjacent to zirconia implants. In such cases, the application of a sharpening filter is recommended as a complementary tool to improve diagnostic performance.
