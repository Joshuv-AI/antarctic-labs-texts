Predictors and Long‐Term Clinical Impact of Heart Failure With Improved Ejection Fraction After Acute Myocardial Infarction
Authors: Kyung An Kim, Sang Hyun Kim, Kwan Yong Lee, Andrew H. Yoon, Byung‐Hee Hwang, Eun Ho Choo, Jin Jin Kim, Ik Jun Choi, Chan Joon Kim, Sungmin Lim, Mahn‐Won Park, Ki‐Dong Yoo, Doo Soo Jeon, Youngkeun Ahn, Myung Ho Jeong, Kiyuk Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLittle is known about the characteristics and long‐term clinical outcomes of patients with heart failure with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF) after acute myocardial infarction.Methods and ResultsFrom a multicenter, consecutive cohort of patients with acute myocardial infarction undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention, patients with an initial echocardiogram with left ventricular ejection fraction ≤40% and at least 1 follow‐up echocardiogram after 14 days and within 2 years of the initial event were considered for analyses. HFimpEF was defined as an initial left ventricular ejection fraction ≤40% and serial left ventricular ejection fraction >40% with an increase of ≥10% from baseline at follow‐up. Independent factors predicting HFimpEF were identified, and clinical outcomes of patients with HFimpEF were compared with those without improvement. From an initial cohort of 10 719 patients with acute myocardial infarction, 191 patients with HFimpEF and 256 patients with non‐HFimpEF who had initial and follow‐up echocardiographic data were analyzed. The median follow‐up duration was 4.5 (interquartile range, 2.9–5.0) years. The factors predicting HFimpEF were lower peak creatine kinase myocardial band, smaller left ventricular dimensions, lower ratio between early mitral inflow velocity and mitral annular early diastolic velocity ′, and the use of β blockers or renin–angiotensin system blockers at discharge. HFimpEF was associated with a significantly decreased risk of all‐cause death compared with non‐HFimpEF (hazard ratio, 0.377 [95% CI, 0.234–0.609]; P<0.001). In 2‐year landmark analysis, these findings were consistent not only before but also after the landmark point. Similar findings were true for cardiovascular death and admission for heart failure.ConclusionsPatients with HFimpEF after acute myocardial infarction showed distinct clinical and echocardiographic characteristics and were associated with better long‐term clinical outcomes.RegistrationURL: https://www.clinicaltrials.gov; Unique Identifier: NCT02806102.

Left ventricular (LV) dysfunction is an important predictor of adverse outcomes after acute myocardial infarction (AMI). 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 In the era of primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), the prevalence of LV systolic dysfunction after myocardial infarction (MI; as well as death and reinfarction) has seen a significant decline. 5 , 6 , 7 However, despite advances in interventional and pharmacological treatment, MI remains one of the leading causes of heart failure (HF) worldwide. 8 In clinical practice, LV systolic function is most commonly evaluated using LV ejection fraction (LVEF), with an LVEF ≤40% used as the definition of HF with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). 9 The new universal definition of heart failure introduces a new category of HF: HF with improved ejection fraction (HFimpEF), defined as a baseline LVEF of ≤40%, a ≥10% increase from baseline LVEF, and a second measurement with LVEF >40%. 10 Patients classified as having HFimpEF are at a decreased risk of all‐cause and cardiovascular death compared with those with chronic HFrEF. 11 , 12 , 13 Additionally, the recovery of LV function following MI is a known predictor of favorable prognosis. 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 However, there is no consensus on what criteria can define improvement of LV function after AMI. Therefore, it would be important to determine if the concept and criteria of HFimpEF can also be applied to changes in LVEF in the context of AMI. With this in mind, this study aimed to identify the prognostic factors associated with the development of HFimpEF, and to compare the clinical outcomes between patients with HFimpEF and those without sufficient LVEF recovery.

Methods

Data are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Population

This study was based on the COREA‐AMI (Cardiovascular Risk and Identification of Potential High‐Risk Population in Acute Myocardial Infarction) registry. The COREA‐AMI registry is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (study ID: NCT02806102), and details regarding the registry have been reported in previous publications. In brief, all consecutive patients who underwent PCI after a diagnosis of AMI at 9 major university hospitals located throughout Korea from January 2004 to August 2014 were retrospectively identified and enrolled, with follow‐up data obtained up to March 2019.

Coronary angiography was performed within 48 hours of admission for all patients. The revascularization strategy was at the discretion of the operating physician and performed according to standard guidelines. 19 The presence of coronary artery disease was defined as a stenosis of ≥70% as determined by angiography and ≥50% for the left main coronary artery. Optimal medical therapy, including antiplatelet agents, renin–angiotensin system blockers (RASBs), β blockers, and lipid‐lowering medications, was prescribed, taking into account final diagnosis, patient comorbidities, and procedural complexity.

Demographic, laboratory, angiographic, procedural, and follow‐up data were collected for each patient at 1, 6, and 12 months after discharge and annually thereafter by trained reviewers who were blind to the results. All clinical outcomes of interest were centrally adjudicated by a committee at Seoul St. Mary's Hospital, consisting of an independent group of reviewers who were unaware of the patient's status and confirmed by original source documentation. For those lost to follow‐up, mortality data were collected on the basis of disqualification from the National Health Insurance Service. Data were registered in a web‐based case report system shared by the participating hospitals, and the final data set was prepared by independent statisticians at the clinical research coordinating center. The institutional review board of each participating institution approved the study protocol and waived the requirement for written consent. This study is in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study End Points and Definitions

HFimpEF was defined according to the 2021 Universal Definition of Heart Failure: a baseline LVEF of ≤40% and a second measurement of LVEF >40%, with a ≥10% increase from baseline LVEF. 10 Non‐HFimpEF was defined as patients who had a baseline LVEF ≤40% and did not meet HFimpEF criteria. Echocardiography was performed at the time of initial admission for AMI according to standard guidelines. 20 LVEF was assessed using the biplane Simpson's method. The participating hospitals have a common echocardiography reporting format, facilitating data sharing and reducing intercenter variability. We included only the patients who underwent follow‐up echocardiography after 14 days, as the majority of cardiac functional recovery from myocardial stunning has been shown to occur before this period, while lower LVEF after 2 weeks is more reflective of adverse remodeling and is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular events. 18 , 21 For the purpose of the current analysis, we also excluded patients with a repeat echocardiogram >2 years after the initial examination to reduce heterogeneity in follow‐up and immortal bias. Patients with a prior history of MI or revascularization were also excluded.

The primary outcome of interest was all‐cause death during 5 years of follow‐up after index PCI for AMI. We also analyzed outcomes for 5‐year cardiovascular death (defined as death resulting from AMI, sudden cardiac death, HF, stroke, or other vascular causes) and admission for first HF.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables are presented as numbers and frequencies and were compared using the χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test. Ordinal variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Continuous variables are expressed as either mean±SD or median (interquartile range [IQR]), depending on variable distribution, and were compared using the Student's t test or Mann–Whitney U test as appropriate.

Logistic regression was performed to identify independent predictors for the improvement of LV systolic function. Variables with P<0.20 in univariable analysis were selected for multivariable analysis, and the stepwise Akaike information criterion method was used to select the final variables. Variables identified as predictors of improved LV systolic function in previous studies were kept during multivariable analysis: age, sex, initial LVEF, peak creatine kinase myocardial band (CK‐MB), ST‐segment–elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) versus non‐STEMI, left main/left anterior descending coronary artery involvement, and treatment with β blockers. The model was also adjusted for the duration to follow‐up echocardiography to correct for possible heterogeneity. Receiver operating characteristic curves were used to determine the optimal cutoff points for each significant variable. The discriminative power of the logistic regression model was assessed using the area under the curve and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test.

All‐cause death was visualized using Kaplan–Meier cumulative event curves and compared with the log‐rank test. Multivariable Cox regression analysis was used to correct for confounders with adjustment for the following variables: age, sex, body mass index, past history of hypertension and diabetes, Killip class, Global Registry of Acute Coronary Events risk score, hemoglobin, peak CK‐MB, NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide), STEMI versus non‐STEMI, multivessel disease involvement, initial LVEF, treatment with dual antiplatelet therapy, RASBs, β blockers, and mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs) at discharge. The proportional hazard assumption was validated by calculation of Schoenfeld residuals and plots of the log and minus log. A landmark analysis at the 2‐year point was done to eliminate immortal bias. Fine–Gray analysis was used to determine the effect of HFimpEF on cardiovascular death and admission for HF to account for competing risks.

A 2‐sided P value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using R Statistical Software version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) and SPSS version 25.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY).

Results

Baseline Characteristics

The patient selection process is shown in Figure 1. From a total of 10 719 patients in the COREA‐AMI registry, after excluding patients with in‐hospital death, no initial echocardiogram, and an initial LVEF >40%, there were 1192 patients with an initial LVEF in the HFrEF range. Among them, we further excluded 40 patients with a previous history of MI or revascularization and 452 patients (of whom 213 died) who did not have a second echocardiogram during the registry follow‐up period, as well as 141 patients for whom the follow‐up echocardiogram was within 14 days after the index PCI. For the purpose of landmark analysis, we also excluded 112 patients who underwent their second echocardiogram after 2 years, leaving 191 patients with HFimpEF and 256 patients with non‐HFimpEF for final analysis. The median time until repeat echocardiography was 8.9 (IQR, 4.1–13.5) months, and the total duration of follow‐up was 4.5 (IQR, 2.9–5.0) years.

The echocardiographic findings of the study population are presented in Table 2. In initial echocardiography, patients who improved to HFimpEF had significantly smaller LV size and lower ratio between early mitral inflow velocity and mitral annular early diastolic velocity (E/e′), but there was no significant difference in baseline LVEF between the 2 groups, with a median value of 35.0%. However, on follow‐up echocardiography, patients with HFimpEF had a median LVEF increase of 19.0% to a final LVEF of 53.0%, compared with a 2.0% increase and a final LVEF of 35.1% for the patients with non‐HFimpEF. Left atrium and LV dimensions were all significantly larger in the non‐HFimpEF group, as were E/e′ and right ventricular systolic pressure. The HFimpEF group also had a numerically longer duration to follow‐up echocardiography, but this did not reach statistical significance.

Predictors of HFimpEF

On univariable logistic regression, lower peak CK‐MB, treatment with β blockers at discharge, smaller LV end‐diastolic dimension (LVEDD), and lower E/e′ were associated with the development of HFimpEF (Table 3). After multivariable adjustment, higher peak CK‐MB (odds ratio [OR] per 100 ng/mL, 0.964 [95% CI, 0.936–0.993]; P=0.016), larger LVEDD (OR per 1 mm, 0.994 [95% CI, 0.990–0.999]; P=0.017), and higher E/e′ (OR per 1, 0.992 [95% CI, 0.985–0.998]; P=0.024) emerged as independent negative predictors of HFimpEF, while treatment with β blockers (OR, 1.163 [95% CI, 1.018–1.329]; P=0.026) or RASBs (OR, 1.098 [95% CI, 1.002–1.203]; P=0.045) was a positive predictor of HFimpEF. There was no significant association between the duration to follow‐up echocardiography and improvement to HFimpEF.

The optimal cutoff points for predicting HFimpEF were peak CK‐MB <191.3 ng/mL, E/e′ <15.0, and LVEDD <54.5 mm (Figure S1). The area under the curve for the logistic regression model was 0.693 (95% CI, 0.641–0.735 by DeLong's method; Figure S2), and the Hosmer–Lemeshow test indicated appropriate fit for the logistic regression model (P=0.857).

Clinical Outcomes of HFimpEF

During 5 years of follow‐up, all‐cause death occurred in 35 (18.2%) patients in the HFimpEF group, of which 15 (7.9%) occurred within 2 years of the initial MI and 20 (10.5%) occurred after 2 years. In the non‐HFimpEF group, 87 (34.0%) patients died, of whom 43 (16.8%) were within 2 years of the initial MI, and 44 (17.2%) were after 2 years. Kaplan–Meier event curves showed that all‐cause death was significantly lower in the HFimpEF group (P<0.001; Figure 2A), which was true both before and after the 2‐year landmark point (prelandmark, P<0.001; postlandmark, P=0.002; Figure 2B).

Discussion

In the present study, we compared 5‐year clinical outcomes between HFimpEF versus non‐HFimpEF in patients with AMI using data from a large multicenter observational study. The main findings are as follows. First, HFimpEF after AMI was associated with a lower risk of adverse outcomes compared with those with incomplete recovery. Second, prognostic factors predicting HFimpEF were lower peak CK‐MB, smaller LVEDD, lower E/e′, and the use of β blockers or RASBs at discharge. Third, this is the first study to confirm the efficacy of low E/e′ as an indicator of future LVEF recovery in patients with HFrEF after AMI.

Previous studies investigating the recovery of LVEF in patients with reduced LVEF after MI have reported that improvement of LVEF is associated with better prognosis. However, the criteria used to define LV systolic dysfunction and recovery have varied. Chew et al 14 described outcomes in patients with AMI with an initial LVEF of <40%. Subsequent LVEF increase was associated with a 3‐fold decrease in all‐cause death compared with no recovery, with a larger relative risk reduction for the subgroup with ΔLVEF >10%. Dauw et al 15 described outcomes in patients with STEMI with LVEF <50% at the index event and recovery to LVEF >50% and showed that patients with LV function recovery had comparable outcomes to those with normal baseline LVEF, as opposed to patients with chronic LV dysfunction, who had a >2‐fold higher risk of all‐cause death and 6‐fold risk of hospitalization. Otero‐Garcia et al 16 analyzed patients with STEMI with initial LVEF <50% and reported a 2.5 times higher all‐cause death and 8 times higher cardiovascular death for the patients without subsequent recovery to LVEF ≥50%. Parodi et al 22 defined LV systolic function recovery as ΔLVEF >10% or alternatively an LVEF increase to >50% and found that failure of LVEF recovery was associated with an HR of 2.8 for 5‐year all‐cause death. Wu et al 17 demonstrated that in young patients MI with an initial LVEF <50%, recovery to LVEF >50% was associated with an ≈8‐fold with reduction in all‐cause death. Finally, in an analysis of 3 historical cohorts, Chew et al 18 showed that no improvement in LVEF after MI was associated with a >3‐fold increased risk of all‐cause and cardiac death.

Our results are consistent with previous publications that show that the recovery of LVEF after AMI is associated with better prognosis. In addition, our study applies the criteria of HFimpEF to define recovery of LV systolic function and shows that it is associated with better clinical outcomes and has prognostic value in predicting death. Guidelines recommend follow‐up echocardiography within 6 to 12 weeks of STEMI for patients with LVEF <40% to identify those at risk of sudden cardiac death and eligible for implantable cardioverter–defibrillator implantation. 19 Our results provide additional support for follow‐up echocardiography after MI for the purpose of risk stratification, as changes in LVEF (or lack thereof) provide additional value to known risk factors in predicting prognosis.

Previous studies have variously identified higher initial LVEF, lower peak CK‐MB and troponin levels, NSTEMI, significant mitral regurgitation, nonanterior or no left main/left anterior descending coronary artery involvement, no multivessel involvement, no previous ischemic heart disease, female sex, and treatment with β blockers as predictors of improved LVEF after AMI. 14 , 17 , 18 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 Meanwhile, in the general population of patients with HFrEF, nonischemic pathogenesis, shorter duration of HF, female sex, absence of diabetes, no left bundle‐branch block, smaller LV dimensions, absence of late gadolinium enhancement on cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, lower NT‐proBNP, lower troponin, and treatment with neurohormonal antagonists such as β blockers, RASBs, and aldosterone antagonists have been reported as predictors of LV systolic function recovery that meets the standards of HFimpEF. 11 , 13 , 26 , 27

In our analysis, lower peak CK‐MB, smaller LVEDD, lower E/e′, and the use of RASBs and β blockers at discharge were associated with subsequent recovery of LVEF to HFimpEF. In previous studies, peak CK‐MB and troponin levels have been shown to predict improved LVEF after AMI and is thought to represent the degree of myocardial damage. 14 , 17 , 28 Meanwhile, smaller LV dimensions and treatment with RASBs and β blockers have been identified as predictors of HFimpEF in the general population, which may signify that the concept of HFimpEF can also be applied to post‐MI myocardial dysfunction.

RASBs and β blockers, along with MRAs, are an integral part of both acute and chronic HF and are recommended for patients after MI with LVEF <40%. 9 , 19 , 29 , 30 In our analysis, treatment with RASBs and β blockers was associated with a significantly higher probability of subsequent HFimpEF. RASBs and β blockers are well known to slow and even reverse the adverse remodeling found in HFrEF. 13 , 31 , 32 Use of RASBs and β blockers is also recommended for all patients after AMI, but the recommendation is strongest for those with systolic dysfunction because the mortality benefits associated with these medications have been best documented in this population. 19 Our results are therefore not surprising and further support the use of RASBs and β blockers in HF after AMI, both to reduce death and especially to improve LV systolic function. In contrast, treatment with MRAs at discharge was not associated with a significant improvement in LVEF. This may be partially due to the notably low proportion of patients on these medications (≈20% at discharge) in both groups. The concept of simultaneous initiation of guideline‐directed medical therapy was established fairly recently, 33 , 34 whereas previous guidelines recommended treatment with RASBs and β blockers with uptitration to maximal tolerated doses before initiation of MRAs. 35 , 36 This historic approach may have been the reason for the conservative MRA prescription practices among physicians.

Few studies have evaluated the impact of impaired diastolic function on the subsequent recovery of systolic function. In a study by Yoshimura et al, 37 baseline E/e′ predicted recovery to HFimpEF in patients with HFrEF, with 16.1 as the optimal cutoff point. Yang et al 38 reported that E/e′ <15 was a predictor of recovered LVEF in patients with atrial fibrillation with HF undergoing catheter ablation. In our analysis, lower baseline E/e′ also predicted recovery to HFimpEF, with 15.0 as the optimal cutoff point. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to identify lower E/e′ as a predictor of future LVEF recovery after AMI. In contrast to LV systolic dysfunction, diastolic dysfunction after AMI has received little attention. We found in a previous analysis using the same COREA‐AMI cohort that diastolic dysfunction as assessed by a HFA‐PEFF (Heart Failure Association Pretest assessment, Echocardiography and natriuretic peptide, Functional testing, Final etiology) score ≥4 was associated with a higher risk of adverse outcomes. 39 Our results demonstrate the need for more research on the relationship between systolic and diastolic dysfunction after the ischemic damage caused by AMI.

Interest in HFimpEF has recently increased with the discovery of newer drugs such as angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors and sodium–glucose transport protein 2 inhibitors, which have been shown to reverse remodeling in HFrEF. 13 , 40 , 41 , 42 Considering the potential benefits of applying contemporary HFrEF treatment regimens—comprising β blockers, angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors, sodium–glucose transport protein 2 inhibitors and MRAs—to our study population, it is highly plausible that such treatments could lead to more favorable outcomes. Integrating these therapies addresses key pathophysiological mechanisms involved in HFrEF, potentially resulting in greater recovery of LVEF and more patients with HFimpEF. Therefore, our study's findings should be viewed in light of the potential shift in treatment practices, particularly the limited use of angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitor and sodium–glucose transport protein 2 inhibitor treatments, and underscore the need for further research in a more contemporary population. Recent findings such as the DAPA‐MI (Dapagliflozin in Patients With Myocardial Infarction) and REDUCE‐AMI (Randomized Evaluation of Decreased Usage of Beta‐Blockers After Acute Myocardial Infarction) studies provide further insight into how contemporary HFrEF treatment might influence recovery patterns of cardiac function after MI and determine which patients are more likely to benefit. 43 , 44 Further studies focusing on subsets of patients after MI and exploring the relative impacts of each component of contemporary HF treatment on HFimpEF could help develop more tailored therapeutic strategies in HF following AMI.

Limitations

There are several limitations to this study. First, although the COREA‐AMI registry is a large, multicenter, prospective registry enrolling >10 000 patients, the number of patients with HFimpEF and persistent HFrEF was relatively small. However, it is similar to previous studies on the same subject and may reflect the decreased proportion of patients with LV systolic dysfunction in the era of primary PCI. Second, there was heterogeneity in the duration of follow‐up echocardiography, which may have led to immortal bias, although we used landmark analysis to correct this effect. A significant proportion of the patients died before a follow‐up echocardiogram could be obtained, which may have introduced selection bias. However, the direction of the bias is likely to favor our results as more persistent HF may have contributed to death; even after the exclusion of these patients with a potentially larger proportion of non‐HFimpEF, HFimpEF was associated with decreased adverse outcomes. Third, the study period predates the widespread adoption of newer treatments for HFrEF, such as angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors and sodium–glucose transport protein 2 inhibitors, and analyses regarding such medications were not available during the study. Fourth, we did not analyze medication treatment patterns beyond prescription at discharge. However, after AMI, the effects of RASBs and β blockers in attenuating adverse cardiac remodeling is known to be greatest within days to months after the initial event. 45 Finally, this study, by virtue of being a retrospective one, is subject to all the inherent biases that come with such analyses, although multivariable regression was used to control for confounders.

Conclusions

In a multicenter cohort of consecutive patients with AMI undergoing PCI, factors predicting recovery of LVEF to HFimpEF after an initial LVEF ≤40% were lower peak CK‐MB, smaller LVEDD, lower E/e′, and the use of β blockers or RASBs at discharge. HFimpEF after AMI was associated with a lower risk of all‐cause death, cardiovascular death, and admission for HF compared with those with incomplete recovery. Our study suggests that patients with HFimpEF after AMI have distinct characteristics compared with those with persistently decreased LVEF and have a decreased risk of adverse outcomes. Further research is needed to better characterize HFimpEF and to develop strategies to improve cardiac function after AMI.
