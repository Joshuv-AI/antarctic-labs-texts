Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT), comprising aspirin and a P2Y12 inhibitor, is a cornerstone of care after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI).1 Its effectiveness in reducing stent thrombosis and improving survival is well established.1 However, real-world adherence remains suboptimal, with multiple studies showing that delays in initiation or early discontinuation significantly increase morbidity, mortality, and health care use.2, 3, 4 Premature cessation of DAPT is among the strongest predictors of stent thrombosis,2,5 which is associated with high rates of myocardial infarction, urgent revascularization, and mortality.2,3 The risk is particularly pronounced in the first days to weeks after PCI, when endothelial healing is incomplete and thrombotic potential is highest.2

Barriers to DAPT initiation and adherence are multifactorial, encompassing high drug costs, lack of insurance coverage, prior authorization delays, limited pharmacy availability, and insufficient patient education.6,7 Although financial and insurance-related challenges are widely recognized, patient education remains an especially underappreciated factor.8 Many patients are unaware of the immediate and severe consequences of missed doses—most notably, acute stent thrombosis—and may underestimate the urgency of filling prescriptions or fail to notify the care team when medications are inaccessible.8,9

Pharmacy availability is another critical but often overlooked issue. Community pharmacies, particularly in smaller or rural areas, may not routinely stock sufficient quantities of newer P2Y12 inhibitors such as ticagrelor. This can result in multiday delays while medications are ordered, leaving patients unprotected during a period of heightened thrombotic risk. Such systemic barriers contribute not only to early stent thrombosis but also to increased rates of readmission for acute coronary syndromes, recurrent myocardial infarction, and other preventable complications.6, 7, 8, 9

Recognizing the persistent gap between guideline-directed therapy and real-world implementation, our institution developed a multidisciplinary project to address these barriers through a coordinated approach involving inpatient, outpatient, and transitions-of-care pharmacy services.

Project Rationale

Our hypothesis was that routing P2Y12 inhibitor prescriptions through the hospital's outpatient pharmacy, combined with bedside delivery and targeted financial assistance, would achieve the following objectives:

1.Eliminate pharmacy-related delays, including stock shortages common in community pharmacies.

We also recognized that extending hospital resources traditionally reserved for inpatient care to the outpatient PCI population could have long-term cost-saving implications. A core element of this approach was the 340B Drug Pricing Program, a federal initiative created by the U.S. Congress in 1992. The program requires pharmaceutical manufacturers that participate in Medicaid to sell outpatient drugs at substantially reduced prices to eligible hospitals and clinics, particularly those serving vulnerable or underserved populations. These discounts, often ranging from 25% to 50% off the standard cost, allow institutions to improve access to high-cost medications.

Intervention Design

The intervention design included several key components:

•Preferred routing: P2Y12 inhibitor prescriptions were incorporated into the post-PCI order set, with the hospital's outpatient pharmacy set as the default while still allowing patients the option to opt out. At discharge, patients received an initial 90-day supply, ensuring uninterrupted therapy during the critical early post-PCI period. For ongoing treatment, patients could choose to continue filling prescriptions through the hospital's outpatient pharmacy, transfer them to a community pharmacy of their choice, or enroll in our mail-order service for convenient home delivery.

Discussion

Our experience demonstrates that system-level barriers, rather than patient-level nonadherence alone, are major drivers of poor medication adherence after PCI.7,8 By proactively addressing these barriers through hospital-based resources, we can markedly improve the transition from inpatient to outpatient care.

Bedside delivery not only ensured immediate access but also served as a key educational touchpoint. The availability of financial assistance programs, particularly via 340B pricing, was transformative in making brand-only medications affordable for patients.10 Importantly, the multidisciplinary approach ensured buy-in across stakeholders and sustained adherence with the new workflow.

Since implementation, the program has been expanded beyond the post-PCI population to the broader inpatient setting through the creation of a transition-of-care pharmacy order set. This allows early pharmacist consultation for high-cost cardiovascular medications, particularly heart failure therapies such as sacubitril/valsartan, SGLT2i, and patiromer, enabling regimen optimization before discharge. By initiating cost assessments, completing prior authorizations during admission, and securing bedside delivery, we ensure that patients are better positioned to maintain adherence and avoid therapy gaps after leaving the hospital.

Limitations of this project include the single-center design, reliance on manual audits, and short-term adherence measurement. Further research is needed to evaluate long-term clinical outcomes such as rates of stent thrombosis, myocardial infarction, readmissions, and mortality.2, 3, 4, 5

Conclusions

This project, summarized in Figure 1, demonstrates that leveraging hospital outpatient pharmacy services, financial assistance programs, and bedside delivery can dramatically improve post-PCI DAPT access and adherence. Such models are scalable, sustainable, and capable of reducing adverse cardiovascular outcomes and health care costs.
