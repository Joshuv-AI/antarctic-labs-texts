Brain imaging screening in metastatic breast cancer: Is it time to rethink clinical guidelines and practice?
Authors: Sarah Sammons, Nayan Lamba, Nancy U Lin
Published: 2025

Brain metastases (BM) are common in patients with metastatic breast cancer (MBC). Over time, roughly 25–50% of patients with HER2-positive (HER2+) or triple-negative MBC will develop BM [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. BM are associated with poorer survival across breast cancer subtypes and significant quality of life (QOL) challenges, including emotional, physical, social, and cognitive impairments, inability to work, and increased disability claims [9]. Older, retrospective studies evaluating the impact of brain imaging screening (BIS) in asymptomatic patients with MBC have not reported convincing evidence of overall survival (OS) benefit; however, impacts on QOL or neurocognitive/neurological outcomes are difficult if not impossible to adequately capture in retrospective analyses. At the same time, there have been significant improvements in local therapies and systemic therapies over the >15 years since these studies were published, and a strong consensus that OS is not the only relevant endpoint that could justify BIS.

The role of BIS in patients with MBC remains highly controversial, as evidenced by conflicting recommendations across oncology guidelines. ASCO guidelines note that there are insufficient data to recommend for or against performing BIS, but that it could be considered based on shared decision making in those with HER2+ disease [10]. In contrast, ESMO guidelines suggest that BIS at diagnosis of MBC is potentially justified in patients with HER2+ and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), acknowledging this approach will result in a higher rate of detection of asymptomatic BM [11]. The 2025 NCCN guidelines recommend brain MRI only “if suspicious symptoms” or “as clinically indicated” [12]. The lack of a clear consensus also means that many insurers will not reimburse for BIS.

It is in this context that Matos and colleagues conducted a large international survey that highlights a critical disconnect between clinical guidelines, physician practices, and patient preferences. [13] The survey revealed that 65% of physicians (529 physicians from 50 countries; 80% European) already request BIS, particularly in cases of extracranial progression or high-risk subtypes such as HER2+ and TNBC. Among physicians who order BIS, QOL, treatment implications, and international guidelines were among the most frequent reasons cited. Among physicians who do not currently order BIS, 91% indicated they would adopt this practice if an OS benefit were demonstrated, 79% would order BIS if it changed treatment strategy, and 57% would adopt BIS if recommended by international guidelines. On the patient side, the data are even more striking: 85% of patients expressed a desire to undergo BIS, regardless of the uncertain clinical benefit, and 91% wanted more information about BM development—a topic that only 13% of physicians routinely address [13].

These findings highlight several critical issues. First, patients are overwhelmingly in favor of BIS, valuing the opportunity for early detection and intervention over the current paradigm of waiting for neurological symptoms to manifest. Second, the reluctance to implement BIS from clinicians is highly rooted in international practice guidelines, which themselves are heavily influenced by the lack of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) proving clinical benefit. Third, physicians and patients are open to endpoints beyond OS to support the implementation of BIS. Finally, both patients and physicians desire more information to inform decisions around this fraught topic.

The case for routine brain imaging screening in high-risk MBC

Screening for BM is already recommended for patients with Stage II-IV non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [14], small cell lung cancer (SCLC) of any stage [15], and Stage IIIC-IV melanoma [16]. This rationale is not based on RCTs but rather common-sense principles, including elevated incidence and the observation that timely intervention can improve QOL and modify treatment approaches. Sometimes, a strong rationale and supportive non-randomized data obviate the sole dependence on RCTs to drive clinical practice.

There are several compelling reasons to consider BIS in MBC patients at high risk for BM.

1.Screening for BM is associated with clinically relevant rates of detection of asymptomatic disease: In a Phase II single-arm, prospective clinical trial testing the impact of a baseline and 6-month screening brain MRI in patients with MBC, 25% of patients with TNBC, 24% of those with HER2+, and 23% of those with hormone receptor-positive/HER2-negative (HR+/HER2−) breast cancer were diagnosed with BM [17]. In a prospective study testing serial brain MRI (at diagnosis of MBC, and at time of switch to second- and third-line systemic therapy), asymptomatic BM were detected in 24% of patients with TNBC and 17% of patients with HER2+ MBC [18]. Somewhat soberingly, even with this screening schedule, a substantial proportion of additional patients presented with symptomatic BM between scheduled scans.

2.Screening for BM leads to diagnosis of smaller and fewer lesions, prior to the development of neurologic symptoms: A single-institution study compared the presentation, management, and outcomes of BM in patients with MBC (mostly unscreened) and NSCLC (mostly screened). MBC patients presented with larger (median diameter: 17 mm vs. 14 mm) and more numerous BM (38.5 % vs. 20.9 % with 5 or more BM) compared to NSCLC patients. MBC patients were more likely to be symptomatic at diagnosis (75.9% vs. 60.5%), experience seizures (16.9% vs. 11.4%), and receive whole brain radiation therapy (WBRT) as initial management (59.9% vs. 42.9%) [19]. These findings suggest that the absence of screening in MBC leads to delayed detection, resulting in larger and more numerous BM at presentation, which require more invasive treatments and are associated with worse neurological outcomes. In a large SEER and institutional analysis, on multivariable regression of the institutional cohort, more than four BM at diagnosis, presence of BM in a high-risk location such as the frontal lobe, and lack of local brain-directed therapy were also associated with greater risk of seizure development [20]. While prospective studies would need to validate this hypothesis, it is possible that BIS could reduce the incidence of seizures in MBC patients with BM, which could have significant impacts on patient QOL, given the need for anti-seizure medications as well as restrictions on driving and other activities associated with the occurrence of seizures.

3.Advanced radiation oncology techniques are safer and more effective for smaller central nervous system (CNS) lesions: Modern radiation oncology techniques, such as stereotactic radiosurgery (SRS) and stereotactic radiotherapy (SRT), are most effective and less toxic when treating smaller and fewer lesions. In a prospective registry of 200 patients with 1237 BM who underwent SRS between 2012 and 2015, local control rates were 100% for all subcentimeter intracranial metastases. With the caveat of selection bias, patients whose tumors at first SRS were less than 10 mm maximal diameter or a volume of 250 mm3 also experienced longer OS [21]. Furthermore, the risk of radiation necrosis with SRS/SRT increases with the size of BM [22,23]. Routine BIS may enable the identification of BM at a stage where these advanced modalities can be optimally utilized. In a real-world study of Stage IV NCSLC patients, the rate of SRS among patients who underwent screening was more than twofold those who did not [24]. Many randomized trials have shown QOL improvements with SRS versus WBRT [25]. Finally, the use of SRS/SRT is likely to further expand given a recent RCT demonstrating similar OS and better functioning compared to WBRT even in patients with 5–20 metastases [26]. The likely trend towards an expanded use of SRS/SRT further increases the potential benefit of identifying smaller lesions where the risk/benefit profile of localized RT is particularly favorable.

4.Treating patients with asymptomatic/occult lesions may result in better outcomes: Awaiting symptoms of BM to image the brain means waiting until patients develop headaches, seizures, nausea, vomiting, vision changes, balance problems, motor dysfunction, or cognitive changes. Many of these symptoms can be irreversible. In several retrospective series, the presence of neurological symptoms at diagnosis was independently associated with inferior OS [27,28]. Symptomatic lesions indicate advanced disease burden and urgency in treatment. While it is true that historical series have not convincingly demonstrated improvements in OS associated with BIS, these studies have important limitations. Miller and colleagues reported the incidence and impact of occult BM in MBC detected upon screening CNS imaging from clinical trial participants. Occult CNS metastases were found in 14.8 % of patients, most commonly in those with HER2 overexpression and multiple metastatic sites [29]. Survival was equally poor compared to patients with symptomatic CNS disease. However, the study included patients from 1998 to 2001, prior to the use of HER2-directed agents, antibody-drug conjugates, and many other modern therapies. Indeed, nearly all patients died of extracranial progression without further intracranial progression – quite distinct from contemporary experience. Furthermore, information on neurocognitive outcomes, neurologic deficits, or QOL were not collected. In contrast, a recent real world retrospective analysis of 609 newly diagnosed patients with stage IV NSCLC found that the use of brain MRI screening resulted in significantly longer median OS among patients with a good performance score [24]. Data from multiple ongoing prospective trials will provide more information on potential benefits (and harms) of BIS in patients with MBC and are expected to report out in the near future (Table 1).

5.Advances in systemic treatments have expanded treatment options and improved outcomes for patients with BM, particularly in HER2+ MBC: In HER2+ MBC, the therapeutic landscape has evolved significantly in recent years, with several systemic therapies demonstrating the ability to result in clinically meaningful rates of intracranial response and to improve both central nervous system progression-free survival (CNS-PFS) and OS in patients with BM [30,31]. Notably, agents such as tucatinib and trastuzumab deruxtecan have shown substantial OS benefits in this population, as evidenced by pivotal trials such as HER2CLIMB [32] and DESTINY-Breast03 [33,34]. The early identification of BM through routine BIS is particularly critical in HER2+ disease, as it enables prioritization of CNS-active therapies. By detecting BM at an asymptomatic stage, clinicians can tailor systemic treatment strategies to include agents with proven CNS efficacy, ultimately optimizing outcomes and extending survival in this high-risk group. Sadly, the same is not true for TNBC BM, as no therapies have been shown to improve OS in this population to date.

6.The identification of asymptomatic BM on BIS can lead to changes in treatment strategy: Notably, 78% of surveyed physicians responded that they would request BIS if it could change the treatment strategy. Between opening up options for safer and more effective SRS/SRT, facilitating avoidance of WBRT, and contributing to the prioritization of CNS-active systemic therapies that can prolong CNS-PFS and OS in patients with BM, the impact of BIS is likely to be quite different in the setting of the contemporary treatment landscape than it has been historically.

Potential harms and unanswered questions for BIS

Despite a compelling rationale for BIS in MBC, several important limitations remain. First, current evidence in MBC is limited to retrospective and small prospective studies, with no randomized trials showing improvements in survival, QOL, neurologic function, or cost-effectiveness. Second, routine BIS would inevitably identify more asymptomatic BM, which could lead to unnecessary interventions and potentially more treatment-related complications. For example, if BIS were performed on a schedule independent of the timing of systemic therapy switches, it is possible that newly detected BM may not be “new” at all, but responding to the systemic treatment. Yet, the discovery of the BM may prompt unnecessary local therapy and/or further switches of systemic therapy and spark considerable patient anxiety without any corresponding clinical benefit, and even potential harm. Third, the discovery of BM may lead to exclusion of patients from clinical trials. However, we would argue that the solution to this concern is to continue to push for more inclusive eligibility criteria to ensure that early detection of BM through BIS does not limit access to experimental therapies while still enabling optimal patient management. Fourth, BIS has the potential to identify non-oncologic incidental findings such as asymptomatic brain infarcts and cerebral aneurysms. These findings would require additional follow-up, which would contribute to increased health care resource utilization and potentially increase patient distress [35]. Fifth, the impact of BIS on patient QOL is unknown, and earlier diagnosis may bring not only opportunities for timely therapy but also increased anxiety, overtreatment, and health system burden. Sixth is the issue of costs and the burden of additional testing on the healthcare system [36]. Finally, both potential benefits and optimal schedules for BIS are likely subtype-dependent—underscoring the need for both prospective studies and real-world experiences to better define which patients are most likely to benefit and how to best implement BIS in clinical practice.

The way forward

BM in MBC cause profound morbidity, reduced survival, and devastating impacts on QOL. We believe that requiring definitive randomized trials to start thoughtfully including BIS in clinical guidelines and clinical practice is neither practical nor necessary—other cancers, such as NSCLC and melanoma, have adopted screening based on incidence, biology, and common-sense principles.

Several ongoing prospective studies (Table 1) are evaluating the impacts of BIS. We eagerly look forward to expected reporting for many of the trials in the next 1–2 years. We believe these data, while unlikely to be perfect, will provide information on multiple relevant endpoints of interest, and combined with existing retrospective and prospective evidence, will collectively likely provide sufficient rationale to implement BIS for high-risk populations, particularly HER2+ and TNBC patients, and prompt strong reconsideration of screening guidelines.

Early detection of BM has the potential to guide CNS-active therapies, enable less invasive local treatments, influence systemic therapy choices, and prevent irreversible neurologic decline either due to disease or its associated treatments. Real-world prospective studies can refine imaging frequency and timing, but adoption should not be delayed for the results of a definitive RCT. This is not only a matter of clinical utility but also of equity and patient-centered care: patients overwhelmingly desire screening, even understanding the limitations of the data, and as a medical community, we need to treat this issue with the urgency it deserves. By embracing proactive surveillance, we can shift care from late-stage crisis management to earlier intervention, offering patients a genuine opportunity for improved survival, preserved function, and better QOL.
