Linked color imaging for detection and characterization of gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors
Authors: Xin Pu, Yang Xu, Xueting Zhang, Zijin Yin, Yan Liu, Min Min
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsThe endoscopic detection of gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors (GI-NETs) remains clinically challenging. Linked color imaging (LCI), which enhances color contrast, may improve the identification of tumorous lesions. This study aims to evaluate the endoscopic characteristics of GI-NETs using LCI in comparison with white light imaging (WLI).MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed two pictures both with WLI and LCI for consecutive patients with GI-NETs who underwent endoscopic resection at our center. The endoscopic features and clinic characteristics were analyzed. The edges of lesions were identified using the Canny operator, and color differences (∆E) between lesions and surrounding mucosa in gastric and rectal NETs were measured using the CIE Lab* color space(CIE: Commission Internationale d’Eclairage). Three expert endoscopists and three non-experts independently assessed LCI and WLI images using a validated visibility scores scale.ResultsSixty patients were included in the analysis. The average ∆E values for LCI were significantly higher than those for WLI in both gastric and rectal NETs (P < 0.05). In rectal NETs, the increased contrast was predominantly driven by enhancement of the yellow-blue component (b*) (P < 0.001). LCI provided superior delineation of lesion edges and more detailed visualization compared to WLI. LCI significantly improves the visibility scores of GI-NETs compared to WLI among non-expert endoscopists (P < 0.001).ConclusionLCI significantly improves the endoscopic visibility of GI-NETs by enhancing color contrast between lesions and surrounding mucosa and optimizing edges visualization. These benefits may allow easy recognition of GI-NETs even for non-experts, suggesting that LCI may be a valuable tool for enhancing the endoscopic detection and characterization of GI-NETs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12876-025-04338-4.Keywords: Linked color imaging, Gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors, Visibility scores

Introduction

Gastrointestinal neuroendocrine tumors (GI-NETs) represent a rare and heterogeneous category of solid tumors with diverse morphologies and clinical behaviors. In recent years, the incidence of GI-NETs has steadily increased, primarily due to the advancements in diagnostic technologies and their widespread application [1–3]. Early diagnosis is critical for improving the prognosis of GI-NETs. However, detecting early-stage GI-NETs, where tumor invasion is limited to the submucosa, remains a significant challenge. This difficulty arises primarily from the limited diagnostic sensitivity of endoscopic biopsy and the insufficient understanding of the endoscopic characteristics of these tumors [4, 5]. Although recognizing these distinct endoscopic findings is essential for accurate diagnosis, they remain inadequately characterized, highlighting the urgent need for improved diagnostic tools to facilitate early detection.

Linked color imaging (LCI, Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan) is a novel image-enhanced endoscopy technique developed to overcome the limitations of conventional endoscopy [6–8]. LCI provides optimal brightness for mid-to-long-range observations and improves visibility by enhancing color contrast between tissue. By offering a clearer and more distinct view of lesion borders and intensifying the original color contrast of different tissue areas, LCI shows promise as a valuable tool for detecting tumorous lesions during endoscopy [9, 10].

Several studies have demonstrated LCI’s efficacy in enhancing color contrast for early gastric cancer (EGC), enabling better differentiation of lesions from the surrounding mucosa compared to WLI [11–13]. These findings establish the superiority of LCI over WLI in identifying tumorous lesions. Our prior research has further validated these advantages, showing that LCI outperforms WLI in detecting colorectal polyps, particularly diminutive lesions (≤ 5 mm) [14]. Moreover, we demonstrated that combining WLI with LCI images significantly improved the endoscopic diagnosis of minimal change esophagitis (MCE) [15]. This cumulative evidence strongly supports LCI’s enhanced capability for identifying subtle mucosal lesions that might be overlooked with conventional WLI. In clinical practice, gastric NETs typically present as reddish lesions, frequently arising in the context of atrophic gastritis. In contrast, rectal NETs are commonly characterized by yellowish subepithelial lesions that may mimic hyperplastic or adenomatous polyps, posing challenges for accurate endoscopic identification [16]. Commpounding these difficulties, many GI-NETs are classified as type IIb and are ≤ 5 mm in diameter, rendering them particularly challenging to detect. LCI enhances both the expansion and reduction of color information simultaneously, allowing for the improved differentiation-making reddish mucosa appear more intensely red and whitish mucosa appear whiter. Compared to blue laser imaging(BLI), LCI provides a color depth more closely resembling that of WLI, making it a more effective technique for differentiating mucosal colors [17]. Given these advantages, we conducted this study to systematically evaluate the endoscopic characteristics of GI-NETs and visibility scores using both LCI and WLI. Through retrospective image analysis, we specifically examined the color differentiation and edges evaluations as objectively indicator, lesion visibility score by endoscipists as subjectively indicator, with the ultimate goal of improving the endoscopic detection and characterization of GI-NETs.

Methods

Patients

This retrospective, single-center study was conducted at the Fifth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital, with approval from institutional ethics committee. The study protocol received approval from the Institutional Review Board. The data are anonymous, and the requirement for informed consent was therefore waived. The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. To ensure patient confidentiality, all data extracted from medical records and institutional databases were de-identified prior to analysis.Consecutive patients diagnosed with GI-NETs between January 2016 and March 2024 were identified from our pathology database. Diagnoses were based through histological examination of endoscopic biopsy or resected specimens [18]. The collected data included patient demographics (age, sex), endoscopic findings (using both WLI and LCI), and histological characteristics, such as preoperative biopsy diagnoses, tumor size and depth of invasion in endoscopic submucosal dissection specimens. Endoscopic finding were extracted from endoscopy reports and subsequently verified by the primary investigators to ensure data accuracy and consistency.

Acquisition of endoscopic images

Endoscopic images were retrieved from the image database of the Fifth Medical Center of Chinese PLA General Hospital, covering the period between January 2016 and March 2024. All procedures were performed using the LASEREO laser endoscope system (Fujifilm Co., Tokyo, Japan.) consisting of an EG-L590WR and EC-L590WM endoscope, a light source (LL-4450). The color enhancement for LCI and WLI, which we used in this study, was C1. All LCI and WLI images were captured using standardized settings: maintained at 3–5 cm from the lesion without magnification applied, wash systematic irrigation with 0.9% saline until complete mucosal visibility. The following cases were excluded from the analysis: those in which the lesion image was not captured at 3–5 cm distance in the two modes, and those where accurate mucosal color analysis was precluded due to attachment of mucus, blood, pus, halation, or shadowing.

Once the edges are detected, we calculate the number of edge pixels in both LCI and WL images, which gives us a measure of edge density for each image type. Using this data, we analyze the distribution of edge densities to compare the lesion contour proportions between the two imaging modes (Fig. 1).

The distribution of edge densities to compare the lesion contour proportions between WLI and LCI mode

To further evaluate and compare the capability of LCI and WLI in visualizing lesion edges, we employs the Entropy of Color Gradients Image (ECGI) as an evaluation metric which had been utilized in our previous research [19], which reflects the richness of texture and edge information as well as the contrast in an image. A higher ECGI score indicates a greater amount of information in the color gradient distribution, corresponding to more and clearer edges and textures.

Color difference analysis

The color difference between cancerous lesions and the surrounding mucosa was analyzed using the 1976 Lab* color space, as defined by the International Commission on Illumination (L*=light/dark; a*=red/green; b*=yellow/blue), as previously reported [20]. For each image in both WLI and LCI modes, a single independent endoscopist randomly selected five points from the cancerous lesion and five points from the surrounding mucosa (Fig. 2). To avoid selection bias as much as possible, the color difference was analyzed by another experienced endoscopist who was blinded to the clinical significant using a commercially available image analysis software (Adobe Photoshop CS4, Adobe Systems Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) as previous report [21]. The comparison between LCI and WLI was performed by calculating the average of the five median RGB values for the five sample points in each region. The L*a*b* values were derived from these average RGB values. The color difference (ΔE) between the cancerous lesions and surrounding mucosa was calculated based on the ΔL, Δa and Δb values. These ΔL, Δa, and Δb values were further analyzed to determine their individual contributions to overall color difference.

Visibility scores of endoscopic images

Three experts endoscopists (each with 10 years of experience) and three non-experts who were blinded to the histology evaluated the visibility of GI-NETs in 60 paired gastric and rectal NET images. For each lesion, we systematically selected one LCI and one WLI frame from the recorded endoscopic sequences to form matched image pairs. To minimize potential assessment bias, the paired images were presented to evaluators in randomized order with a normal-sized monitor at a fixed distance. Each endoscopist independently assessed the visibility of the GI-NETs in both WLI and LCI image using a 4-point visibility scale as previously reported [8, 22]: score 4, excellent (easily detectable); score 3, good (detectable with careful observation); score 2, fair (hardly detectable without careful examination); and score 1, poor (not detectable without repeated careful examination). Supplementary Fig. 1 show representative images for each score.

Outcome measurements

The primary outcome of this study was the quantitative color difference (ΔE) between GI-NETs and the surrounding mucosa under WLI versus LCI. ΔE was calculated using the the 1976 Lab* color space incorporating three-dimensional Lab* color space values (L*, a* and b*). Secondary outcomes included endoscopic visibility scores and edge delineation clarity.

Sample size evaluation

Due to the absence of published data specifically addressing color difference ΔE analysis and comparative evaluation using WLI and LCI in GI-NETs, sample size estimation was based on studies involving visually analogous lesions. For gastric NETs, referencing early gastric cancer [9], 15 subjects per group (2 images/subject, 1:1 allocation) provided 95% power (α = 0.05, two-sided t-test). For rectal NETs, based on sessile serrated lesions (SSLs) data [8], 44 subjects per group were required to achieve 90% power under identical statistical assumptions.

Statistical analysis

Clinical data were expressed as percentage, means, and ranges. Color differences were presented as mean (± SD) values. Comparison of color differences between WLI and LCI, as well as the L*, a* and b* values between cancerous lesions and surrounding mucosa, were a 2-tailed paired t-test with false discovery rate adjustment was used. Visibility scores were used as ordered categorical variables; comparisons between methods used Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Bonferroni correction. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software, version 17.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL).

Results

Patient characteristics

The clinical characteristics of 60 enrolled patients are summarized in Table 1. The cohort had a mean age of 54.9 years (range: 28–82). Of the 60 patients, 33 (55%) were male and 27 (45%) were female. Tumor locations were as follows: gastric lesions in 15 patients (25%), duodenal in 1 (1.7%), and rectal in 51 patients (73.3%). Histopathological grading classified 57 tumors (95%) as G1, and 3 (5%) as G2. In terms of tumor size, the majority were small, with 42 (70%) measuring less than 1 cm, while the remaining 18 (30%) ranged between 1 and 2 cm. In terms of morphology, the predominant type was 0-IIb(22 cases, 37%). The most frequent endoscopic appearance was yellow(47 cases,78.3%). Among gastric NETs, all lesions were located in the gastric body or fundus. All eight gastric NETs patients exhibited atrophic gastritis, characterized by mucosal atrophy and enhanced vascular visibility.

Objective edges evaluations

Histogram analysis revealed distinct differences in edge density distribution between WLI and LCI. In WLI images, edge pixels predominantly clustered within lower-density regions (0.025–0.050), representing the highest frequency of edge occurrence. In contrast, the LCI images have a broader distribution of edge densities, with a significant number of edge pixels extending into higher-density ranges. Furthermore, even in the lower-density range (0.000-0.025), LCI significant edge detail, while consistently outperforming WLI as edge density increased. The histogram clearly demonstrates that the lesion contours are more prominent in LCI mode, as indicated by a higher frequency of edge pixels in the higher-density regions of the histogram. This suggests that LCI is more effective at delineating lesion boundaries, providing a clearer and more detailed visualization of the lesion contours compared to the WLI. A comparative analysis of ECGI scores between the two imaging modalities LCI and WLI is presented in Supplementary Fig. 3. The mean ECGI values were significantly higher in LCI images compared to WLI images (4.69 ± 0.58 vs. 4.06 ± 0.27; p < 0.05). These results indicate that LCI imaging provides superior visualization of architectural details and margin delineation, consistent with its enhanced capacity to capture texture and edge information.

Objective color evaluations

A total of 60 paired images (15 gastric, 44 rectal, and 1 duodenal NETs) were obtained using both LCI and WLI for analysis. Representative endoscopic images demonstrating both modalities are presented in Fig. 2. In our observations, both gastric and rectal NETs exhibited characteristic color patterns, with central tumor regions appearing yellowish against a whitish surrounding mucosa. The color difference (∆E) values with LCI were significantly higher than those with WLI in both gastric NETs (20.47 ± 6.74 vs. 13.54 ± 9.22) and rectal NETs (20.88 ± 5.09 vs. 15.17 ± 7.39) (P < 0.05) (Supplementary Fig. 2). This increase was primarily attributed to a significant elevation in the yellow-blue component (b*) (13.56 ± 6.92 vs. 8.15 ± 6.92, P < 0.001) in rectal NETs (Fig. 3). No statistically significant differences were observed in the brightness (L*) or red-green component (a*) between gastric and rectal NETs when comparing LCI and WLI. Calculations of L*a*b* color values for cancer lesions and surrounding mucosa were performed in gastric and rectal NETs (Table 2). LCI demonstrated significantly higher L* values than WLI for both cancer lesions and surrounding mucosa in both gastric or rectal NETs (P = 0.001). In rectal NETs, a notable difference was observed in the a* and b* values between LCI and WLI for cancer lesions, with values of 11.19 ± 11.25 vs. 23.76 ± 9.29, (P < 0.0001) for a*, and 27.70 ± 8.04 vs. 33.0 ± 10.14 (P < 0.0045) for b*, respectively. In contrast, for cancer lesions of gastric NETs, the difference in the a* value between LCI and WLI was not statistically significant (P = 0.1055).

The color value of the cancer lesions had a significantly higher score than the surrounding mucosa in axes a* and b* in WLI and LCI in gastric NETs, but axis b* in rectum WLI and LCI (Fig. 3). This chromatic enhancement likely reflects the characteristic red or red-yellow hue frequently observed in gastric NETs, contributing to their improved endoscopic detectability. In contrast, rectal NETs demonstrated a more selective colorimetric profile, with only the b* component showing significant elevation relative to adjacent mucosa in both imaging modes (P < 0.05). This finding correlates with the predominant yellow coloration typical of rectal NETs, which appears to be the primary chromatic determinant in these lesions.

Visibility scores of endoscopic images

The mean (± SD)visibility scores of the six endoscopists were 2.82 ± 0.96 for gastric NETs and 2.46 ± 1.03 for rectal NETs using WLI, and 3.35 ± 0.75 for gastric NETs and 3.28 ± 0.86 for rectal NETs using LCI. The visibility score for LCI was significantly higher than that for WLI, with P < 0.001 for gastric NETs and P < 0.0001 for rectal NETs. For the experts, the visibility scores for the LCI were significantly higher than that of the WLI in rectal NETs (P < 0.001), but did not significantly differ in gastric NETs (P = 0.3). For non-experts endoscopists, the visibility scores for LCI were significantly higher than that for WLI in both gastric NETs (3.50(2.75,3.75) vs. 2.75(2.00,3.25), P < 0.001), and rectal NETs (3.25(2.50,3.50) vs. 2.50 (1.75,3.00), P < 0.001) (Table 3). There was no significant differences in gastric NET of LCI between expert and non-expert (P = 0.055), These findings demonstrate that LCI significantly improves the visibility scores of GI-NETs compared to WLI among non-expert endoscopists. Notably, for gastric NETs, the visibility achieved by non-experts using LCI was comparable to that of expert endoscopists.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first comprehensive evaluation comparing LCI and WLI for GI-NETs through a combination of objective and subjective analyses.Our study employed objective evaluated color differences and lesion edges between the cancerous lesions in GI-NETs and the surrounding mucosa, and demonstrate that LCI offers superior delineation of lesion edges and enhanced color contrast compared to WLI. Furthermore, both expert endoscopists and non-expert endoscopists achieved higher subjective visibility scores with LCI, highlighting its potential as a valuable diagnostic tool for GI-NETs.

A better understanding of the endoscopic features of GI-NETs could enhance the accuracy of targeted biopsies under LCI observation, thereby improving the clinical diagnosis of GI-NETs and increasing in the detection of the IIb and small lesions. Type 1 gastric NETs, the most frequent subtype, accounting for approximately 80–90% of cases and typically occur in the background of atrophic gastritis [23, 24]. In clinical practice, we have observed that most gastric NETs arise against a background of atrophic gastritis, endoscopically characterized by pale mucosa with loss of gastric folds. Since LCI enhances contrast by making red areas appear more vivid and white areas more distinct, gastric NETs typically appear red and LCI enhances red hue visualization, this technology enables detection of smaller type IIb lesions.Similarly, predominantly manifest as yellowish lesions, where LCI’s superior vascular pattern delineation allows identification of vascular interruption sites that often indicate the presence of polyps or type IIb NETs.These lesions are often small, multifocal and flat, making them difficult to detect with an endoscope, even by experienced endoscopists [25, 26]. Most colonic NETs are located in the rectum, and their detection is increasing though screening colonoscopy. Unfortunately, small rectal NETs (typically < 5 mm) are often misinterpreted as hyperplastic lesions and resected with cold snaring, which can result in incomplete resection [27–29]. In our study, we found that using the LCI not only facilitated the identification of type IIb NETs but also improved the visibility scores of NETs by highlighting the yellowish areas covered on the mucosal surface when compared to WLI.

Our study conducted a systematic quantitative evaluation of L*a*b* color values and ΔE differences between cancerous lesions and surrounding mucosa in gastric and rectal NETs. The color difference (ΔE) values were significantly higher when comparing LCI to WLI in both gastric and rectal NETs. Furthermore, theΔE is expressed according to the evaluation criterion of the National Bureau of Standards (NBS) units of color difference [30]. After an extensive review of the literature on color difference analysis using LCI, we found that recent studies on EGC reported ΔE values ranging from approximately 12.5 to 29.4 for LCI, and 12.9 to 18.6 for WLI [9, 31, 32]. In contrast, ΔE values reported in studies of colorectal polyps were generally lower, with LCI ranging from 5.7 to 18.0 and WLI from 4.5 to 12.9 [8, 33]. According to the evaluation criteria based on the NBS units for color difference, these values generally fall within the “Much” to “Very much” categories, indicating a perceptible and clinically meaningful improvement. These findings support that LCI consistently provides higherΔE values compared to WLI, particularly in upper gastrointestinal lesion detection, thereby demonstrating its superior visibility performance.

This enhancement appears primarily attributable to the marked increase in yellow-blue chromaticity (b*) observed in rectal NETs under LCI. Additionally, the L* value was significantly higher for LCI compared to WLI, both for the cancerous lesions and surrounding mucosa. Higher L* values with LCI indicate lighter coloration, which contributes to the improved visibility of lesions in comparison to WLI. One notable difference between LCI and the other IEE methods is brightness. Specifically, Δa* values were lower with LCI than with WLI in rectal NETs, indicating that the mucosa appears more highlighted in red under LCI. Furthermore, the Δb* values were significantly lower when comparing LCI and WLI for both cancerous lesions and surrounding mucosa in gastric and rectal NETs. These results suggest that the higher brightness of LCI compared to WLI results in a subdued vividness of both the cancerous lesions and surrounding mucosa, which enhances contrast and make the lesions more detectable.

Next, we compared the color characteristics of cancerous lesions and surrounding mucosa in both gastric and rectal NETs. In gastric NETs, irrespective of whether LCI or WLI mode was used, the a* and b* values of the cancerous lesions were significantly higher than those of the surrounding mucosa, corroborating our routine endoscopic observations.The diagnostic superiority of LCI became particularly evident in rectal NETs, where type IIb lesions were consistently detectable only under LCI examination. This enhanced detection capability likely stems from LCI’s unique capacity to amplify the yellow chromatic component (b*) of cancer lesion, thereby optimizing the contrast differential between lesions and normal mucosa. Therefore, based on these findings, we recommend activating LCI mode during pre-ESD evaluation to ensure accurate lesion margin demarcation, as conventional WLI may fail to reveal the complete extent of these subtle lesions.

In our previous study, we published the first research on the ECGI criterion to evaluate the performance of LCI on colonoscopy.The color gradient image was introduced as the extracted feature under the consideration of the texture, the edge, and the color [19]. We found that LCI improved the visualization of polyps edges and made them easier to detect compared to WLI. In this study, we continued to use computer assistance to extract the edges of NETs lesions. Our findings indicate that, similar to its role in detecting polyps, LCI offers clearer visualization of NETs compared to WLI. However, due to the generally larger diameter of polyps compared to NETs, the ability of LCI to delineate lesion edges becomes even more critical in clinical applications for detecting smaller NETs. This enhanced edge visualization is particularly important when dealing with lesions that are often difficult to identify using conventional WLI.

Lesion visibility score is a subjective indicator for endoscopists. Previous research has established the diagnostic advantage of in the visibility of early gastric cancer (EGC), with an increase of over 70% in both experts and non-experts endoscopists [10]. This finding highlights LCI’s potential utility in screening and surveillance endoscopy for the identification of EGC. A meta-analysis focusing on EGC detection additionally supported these observations, demonstrating a pooled relative risk (RR) of 2.0 for LCI compared to conventional imaging [34]. The endoscopic visibility scale used in this study, while previously validated for LCI, demonstrated strong clinical utility in our LCI/WLI comparative analysis. Importantly, for non-expert endoscopists, LCI significantly improved the visibility scores of gastric NETs to a level comparable with that of experts, with no significant difference observed between the two groups. This result highlights the potential of LCI to enhance lesion detectability for non-experts and thereby improve clinical detection rates. Among expert endoscopists, there was no statistically significant difference in the visibility scores for gastric NETs between WLI and LCI, suggesting that experts are able to detect these lesions effectively regardless of the imaging modality. This finding is consistent with clinical expectations.

This study had several limitations. First, its single-center, retrospective design and relatively small sample size attributable to the low prevalence of GI-NETs and limited global availability of LCI systems may restrict the generalizability of our findings. This study included only 15 patients with gastric NETs. Due to the low incidence of gastric NETs, sample size estimation was based on studies of early undifferentiated gastric cancer [9], as the color differences between cancerous and non-cancerous mucosa under WLI and LCI are similar in both conditions. However, with only 15 cases, the subgroup analysis is statistically underpowered and prone to bias. Future studies should involve larger sample sizes to better analyze the color characteristics of gastric NETs and improve detection rates. Multicenter prospective studies with larger cohorts are needed to validate LCI’s diagnostic utility for NETs. Second, while our image-based assessment clearly demonstrates LCI’s optical advantages, real-time endoscopic detection involves additional technical and cognitive factors not captured in static image review. Moreover, the majority of studies in this area have utilized magnifying endoscopy or close-up observations, which may provide more detailed views of the lesions. Therefore, future research incorporating magnifying endoscopy or other advanced imaging techniques may further improve the visibility of GI-NETs using LCI. Third, while our study demonstrated a significant improvement in visibility with LCI compared to other modalities, it was based on a relatively modest number of cases. Larger-scale studies will be essential to confirm the generalizability of these findings and to evaluate the effectiveness of LCI across different clinical settings. Due to the significant differences in color characteristics between LCI and WLI, this study could not adopt a blinded design. Finally, while LCI demonstrates clear advantages in visualizing mucosal abnormalities like NETs, its clinical utility for subepithelial lesions (e.g., GISTs or leiomyomas) remains limited due to their preservation of normal surface mucosa. For such lesions, techniques emphasizing submucosal structural assessment (e.g., EUS or contrast-enhanced endoscopy) may be more appropriate.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that LCI enhances the visibility of GI-NETs by providing a clear contrast between the lesions and the surrounding mucosa compared to WLI. LCI significantly improves the detection of these lesions, highlighting its potential as a valuable tool for the identification of GI-NETs. The future application of LCI in GI-NETs screening is promising, and the adoption of novel diagnostic modalities, such as LCI based on image-enhanced endoscopy (IEE), could transform diagnostic practices and follow-up care. To strengthen clinical evidence, randomized controlled multicenter trials should evaluate LCI’s impact on GI-NET detection rates and diagnostic accuracy compared to WLI. Further studies should correlate LCI-enhanced visibility with clinically meaningful endpoints, including histopathological confirmation and long-term outcomes. Standardized training protocols and objective assessment methods are needed to minimize inter-observer variability in future evaluations.
