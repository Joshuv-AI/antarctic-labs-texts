Artificial intelligence assistance improves endoscopist accuracy for gastric cancer dysplasia and intestinal metaplasia
Authors: Yoon Hee Lee, Gihong Park, Ji Yoon Kim, Byeong Yun Ahn, Dabin Jeong, Jong Kyoung Choi, Hyunsoo Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence (AI) can accurately classify gastric lesions, but its clinician-level impact in real-world practice remains uncertain. We compared endoscopists’ diagnostic performance with vs. without AI assistance using both still-image (M1) and video (M2) datasets. We analyzed 1,584 cases (226 cancer, 282 dysplasia, 90 non-neoplastic lesions [NNL], 649 intestinal metaplasia [IM], and 337 gastritis/normal). One representative still image per case was extracted for M1; edited five-second video clips formed M2. Six in-training endoscopists (< 3 years’ experience) independently read M1 and M2 with and without AI after a one-week washout. As a stand-alone model, AI achieved 91.31% (M1) and 92.51% (M2) accuracy for focal lesions (sensitivities 91.02% and 91.91%; specificities 95.50% and 96.12%). For IM, accuracy was 91.83% (M1) and 92.45% (M2). With AI assistance, overall reader accuracy increased from 74.92% to 86.66% in M1 (AUC 0.742 to 0.860) and likewise from 74.92% to 86.81% in M2 (AUC 0.796 to 0.900); all p < 0.05. By subtype (videos, M2), accuracy improved 80.01% to 89.85% for cancer (+ 9.84%), % 67.16%to 81.08% for dysplasia (+ 13.92%), 77.59% to 89.50% for NNL (+ 11.91%), and 68.95% to 85.34% for IM (+ 16.39%). Still-image results showed similar gains (e.g., dysplasia 67.16% to 81.32%, IM 68.95% to 79.22%, both p < 0.05). AI assistance significantly enhances endoscopists’ diagnostic accuracy across lesion types and modalities, with the largest benefits for dysplasia and IM—conditions prone to clinician-level variability. Introduction

Early detection of gastric cancer (GC) is closely associated with a reduction in cancer-related mortality, and assessment of dysplasia and intestinal metaplasia (IM) is crucial for the identification of high-risk groups for gastric cancer development. Despite advances in endoscopic screening programs, diagnostic performance remains variable and strongly dependent on the experience of the endoscopist, with a substantial proportion of gastric cancers and IM cases still being missed in routine practice1,2. These limitations underscore the need for adjunctive tools to enhance detection and diagnostic consistency.

With the increasing application of artificial intelligence (AI) in upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, diagnostic systems based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) trained on over 10,000 white-light endoscopic images have achieved multiclass classification of gastric lesions—including carcinoma, dysplasia, and benign entities—with diagnostic accuracy on par with or exceeding that of expert endoscopists3. Recent meta-analyses further demonstrate that AI-assisted endoscopy can detect intestinal metaplasia (IM) with pooled sensitivities and specificities exceeding 90%, outperforming human endoscopists on the same datasets4. These findings highlight AI’s potential as a reliable adjunct tool for identifying subtle or diffuse mucosal changes, particularly for diagnostic purposes.

However, majority of these studies have evaluated the stand-alone performance of AI systems or relied on retrospective analyses of still images. Moreover, robust evidence remains scarce showing that AI support translates into measurable improvements in endoscopists’ diagnostic accuracy in real-world settings. While several randomized controlled trials in colonoscopy have demonstrated clear clinical benefits of AI assistance—such as significant improvements in dysplasia detection rates5,6— similar high-quality evidence in gastric endoscopy is limited. Only a few small-scale studies have directly compared diagnostic accuracy with versus without AI assistance, and prospective multicenter validations remain rare. Therefore, we designed this study to evaluate the clinical impact of AI assistance on endoscopists’ diagnostic accuracy for a broad spectrum of gastric lesions. Both focal lesions (gastric cancer, dysplasia, and non-neoplastic lesions) and diffuse lesions (IM) were included, and diagnostic performance was assessed using still images as well as real-time endoscopic videos. By directly comparing accuracy with and without AI support, our study aimed to clarify the potential role of AI-assisted endoscopy in routine clinical practice.

Methods

In this study, Venotics-G (Prevenotics, Seoul, Korea), an AI system based on a customized Vision Transformer (ViT) framework, was employed for endoscopic image analysis. A ViT architecture was selected for its superior capability to capture long-range contextual information via self-attention mechanisms, which is particularly advantageous for detecting diffuse or poorly demarcated gastric lesions7,8. To ensure diagnostic reliability and reproducibility, the model was developed using a large-scale Training Set of 16,076 images collected at Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) between January 2014 and July 2022. This development set was further partitioned into training, validation, and internal testing subsets using an 8:1:1 ratio to optimize model hyperparameters. (Supplementary Table S1.)

For model training, we employed the AdamW optimizer with a learning rate of 1 × 10− 5 and a batch size of 32, with all input frames standardized to a resolution of 352 × 352 pixels.

As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. S1, the model is designed for multi-task parallel learning to simultaneously perform four specialized diagnostic tasks: (1) landmark classification, (2) abnormality detection, (3) abnormality classification, and (4) IM detection.

The IM detection engine was specifically configured to activate only when viewing the f and corpus, a design choice intended to fulfill two critical objectives. First, this localization ensures the system adheres to the standardized Operative Link for Gastric Intestinal Metaplasia Assessment (OLGIM) staging criteria, which determines gastric cancer risk based on the severity and distribution of intestinal metaplasia within these specific mucosal regions. Second, restricting detection to these areas reduces false-positive triggers caused by atypical mucosal patterns or complex light reflections frequently encountered in the cardia or fundus. Consequently, this targeted approach optimizes the balance between clinical accuracy and real-time diagnostic efficiency. To enhance model interpretability and support clinical decision-making, attention map visualizations are also incorporated into the real-time display.

Study design

We conducted an observer-based, reader-blinded, randomized case-order validation study across two centers to evaluate the clinical impact of Venotics-G (Prevenotics, Seoul, Korea). To maintain a rigorous experimental control, the term ‘randomization’ in this study design specifically refers to the shuffled presentation sequence of the 1,584 cases within each session, rather than the random allocation of participants or the chronological order of intervention.

All observers first performed a baseline interpretation without AI assistance. To eliminate potential recall bias, a one-week washout period was implemented before the second evaluation with AI assistance. For each session, the presentation order of the entire dataset was independently and randomly shuffled for each observer using the sample function in the R programming language with a fixed set.seed() value. This randomized case-order framework was strictly employed to mitigate sequential learning effects and ensure that the improved performance with AI was not a result of case familiarity or order bias.

Data collection, editing, and dataset composition

The endoscopic videos and still images were collected from patients aged 19 years or older, retrospectively from Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) and prospectively from the National Medical Center (NMC). At SNUH, upper endoscopic videos acquired between January 1, 2023, and November 1, 2024, were included. Only white-light imaging (WLI) videos were included, as we restricted the analysis to WLI to minimize variability associated with device-dependent image-enhanced endoscopy (IEE).

At NMC, video data were collected from January 1, 2024, to January 31, 2025, applying the same inclusion and exclusion criteria. (Supplementary Table S1.) Videos containing biopsy forceps, endoscopic caps, or those with poor focus and excessive noise that hindered lesion identification were excluded.

For neoplastic lesions, including gastric cancer and dysplasia, only histologically confirmed cases were included. In this study, we utilized the term ‘dysplasia’ to represent non-invasive epithelial neoplasia (Vienna Category 3 and 4). For the diffuse lesion category, the analysis was specifically targeted at high-risk IM (defined as OLGIM stage III or IV). This focus was chosen because OLGIM III/IV serves as a more reliable and reproducible histological gold standard for AI validation compared to atrophic gastritis, representing the most clinically actionable stage for gastric cancer surveillance. Throughout this manuscript, ‘IM’ refers strictly to this high-risk subset to ensure diagnostic relevance.

For other non-neoplastic lesions (NNL), which include benign gastric ulcer, erosion, polyp, gastritis and normal mucosa, when biopsy results were unavailable, the endoscopic images were reviewed by two experts (HC and JKC, each with > 10 years of experience), and only cases with concordant assessments were included to ensure the reliability of the reference standard.

The full-length endoscopic videos were reviewed, and 5-second segments were extracted where the lesions were clearly visualized and considered to represent their features best. These edited video clips were designated as the M2 dataset. In addition, from each video, one representative still image showing the most clearly visible lesion was extracted and referred to as the M1 dataset. The edited videos and still images were anonymized and assigned a unique screening ID. After collecting endoscopic video clips, each case was first categorized as either a focal or diffuse lesion based on its morphological pattern. Focal lesions were then divided into three diagnostic categories: gastric cancer, adenoma, or non-neoplastic lesions (NNL). Diffuse lesions were classified into two categories based on the presence or absence of intestinal metaplasia. An overview of the dataset composition is shown in Fig. 1.

AI application and comparative review

Each video was analyzed by the Venotics-G software to predict lesion presence and location. The same videos were then randomly assigned and reviewed by six endoscopists in training (less than three years of experience and not board-certified) in two phases: initial interpretation without AI assistance, followed by subsequent interpretation with AI assistance after a one-week washout period (Fig. 1).

The baseline (without-AI) evaluation was conducted using only the video dataset (M2), as dynamic observation better reflects real-world endoscopic workflows and enables assessment of clinical applicability under natural visual conditions. In contrast, both still-image (M1) and video (M2) datasets were used for AI-assisted evaluation to compare diagnostic consistency and modality-dependent effects.

Randomization was performed using the R programming language’s sample function with fixed set.seed() values for reproducibility. Reviewers were blinded to each other’s interpretations.

Outcomes and statistical analysis

Diagnostic performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), and lesion localization match rates for both focal lesions and IM, before and after AI assistance.

For the overall focal lesion analysis, reader diagnoses were dichotomized into focal lesions (gastric cancer or dysplasia) versus non-neoplastic lesions (NNL). For lesion-specific analyses, a one-vs-rest binary classification strategy was applied. Specifically, gastric cancer performance was evaluated against a combined control group of dysplasia and NNL cases; dysplasia was evaluated against cancer and NNL cases; and NNL was evaluated against cancer and dysplasia cases.

Accordingly, ROC curves were represented as step functions connecting (0,0), (1 − specificity, sensitivity), and (1,1). In this setting, the AUC is mathematically equivalent to balanced accuracy, calculated as (sensitivity + specificity)/2. Per-reader AUC values were calculated individually and averaged across readers.

Comparisons between pre- and post-AI assistance conditions were performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test for paired per-reader performance metrics. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.2.3 for Windows).

To simulate real-world differential diagnostic conditions, negative control groups for each lesion category were proportionally sampled from the remaining lesion types. Target sample sizes were determined based on predefined superiority margins derived from prior lesion-specific diagnostic performance studies9–14, together with a standardized effect size of 0.3 according to established statistical methodology15. This corresponded to clinical tolerance margins (δ) of 0.0397 for gastric cancer and 0.0111 for dysplasia. The study was powered at 80% to detect statistically significant improvements, with a 5% dropout rate incorporated into the final sample size estimation.

Ethics statement

This study used a mixed design, including retrospective data collected from Seoul National University Hospital (SNUH) and prospective data collected from the National Medical Center (NMC). All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. 2410-035-1575). Written informed consent was obtained from patients enrolled prospectively at NMC, while the requirement for informed consent was waived for the retrospective dataset collected at SNUH due to the use of anonymized data.

Results

Overview of dataset

A total of 1,584 endoscopic video clips were collected and converted into still images and video clips for subsequent analysis. Of these, 226 cases (14.3%) were gastric cancers (including 163 EGC and 63 AGC), 282 (17.8%) were dysplasias (including 280 LGD and 2 HGD), and 649 (41.0%) were NNLs, all categorized as focal lesions (Supplementary Table S2). Intestinal metaplasia (IM) was identified in 649 cases (41.0%) (Fig. 1).

AI performance: image dataset and video dataset

The standalone diagnostic performance of the AI model is presented in Table 1. For focal lesions, the system achieved an accuracy of 91.31%, with a sensitivity of 91.02% and a specificity of 95.50% in M1. In the M2 dataset, the system demonstrated an accuracy of 92.51%, with a sensitivity of 91.91% and a specificity of 96.12%. Overall, the AI model’s performance was slightly higher in the video-based assessment (M2) than in the still-image assessment (M1). Subtype analysis showed that the model maintained a stable diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV and NPV all exceeding 90% for gastric cancer, dysplasia, and NNL across both modalities. Specifically, the system reached its peak accuracy for NNL at 94.80% in M2.

Endoscopist diagnostic performance on still image (M1): with and without AI

The diagnostic performance of the endoscopists in the still-image-based assessment (M1) is summarized in Table 2, with Figs. 2 and 3 presenting the ROC curves for each lesion type. The overall diagnostic accuracy for focal lesions improved from 74.92% (95% CI: 71.25–78.58%) without AI to 86.66% (95% CI: 84.50–88.82%) with AI support (p < 0.05). This comprehensive enhancement was characterized by significant increases across all evaluated metrics, including sensitivity (from 65.49% to 81.07%), specificity (from 82.74% to 91.16%), and both positive and negative predictive values (all p < 0.05). Furthermore, the narrowing of the 95% CI for accuracy and sensitivity reflected an increase in diagnostic precision, while the AUC expanded from 0.742 to 0.860 (p < 0.05).

In the subtype analysis, the baseline accuracy was highest for gastric cancer among neoplastic lesions at 80.01%. AI assistance led to a statistically significant improvement in accuracy (88.38%) and sensitivity (from 68.25% to 83.78%, p < 0.05), but the specificity for gastric cancer did not show a significant change (from 89.73% to 92.53%, p > 0.05).

In contrast, the baseline accuracies for dysplasia and IM were lowest at 67.16% and 68.95%, respectively. The most substantial improvements in accuracies were shown in these categories. AI assistance drove the accuracy for dysplasia to 81.32% (a 14.16% point increase; p < 0.05) and for IM to 79.22% (a 10.27% point increase; p < 0.05). AI assistance improved the diagnostic performance for both subtypes, although the underlying drivers differed. For dysplasia, the gain was primarily driven by a surge in sensitivity, which rose by 20.09% points (from 44.86% to 64.95%), although this did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.063). In contrast, the improvement in IM performance was characterized by a significant gain in specificity (from 67.95% to 90.86%, p < 0.05). Additionally, the highest diagnostic accuracy among all categories was achieved for NNL at 90.28%, with statistically significant improvements across all parameters.

In the subtype analysis, gastric cancer showed a trend consistent with M1, but with higher overall values; AI assistance significantly improved both sensitivity (86.73%) and accuracy (89.85%) (both p< 0.05). For IM, a more comprehensive improvement was observed compared to M1; while M1’s accuracy was primarily driven by specificity, this video-based test showed significant gains in both sensitivity and specificity, leading to a markedly higher accuracy of 85.34% (p < 0.05).

In contrast, dysplasia showed significantly improved performance with AI, but the magnitude of the increase was relatively modest compared to the still-image test, with accuracy reaching 81.08%. For NNL, although diagnostic performance improved, the gains in specificity and PPV did not reach statistical significance, resulting in a slightly lower increase in accuracy compared to the results from the still-image assessment (89.50% vs 90.28%; p < 0.05).

Discussion

In this multicenter validation study, we developed an AI system that detects and diagnoses various gastric lesions with high accuracy, demonstrating stable performance across both still-image and video-based evaluations. This finding suggests that the system can effectively recognize lesions despite the differing detection thresholds required for variations in color tone, border clarity, and surface morphology.

Our results align with previously reported AI performance. For gastric cancer, prior studies reported accuracies of 60.8–94%16. Our model achieved results within the upper range of these values. For dysplasia, earlier studies reported accuracies of 92–99%, consistent with our findings17–21. Similarly, for non-neoplastic lesions, our accuracies (92.40% in M1 and 94.80% in M2) were comparable to previous reports (~96%)17,22. For IM detection, previous meta-analyses and large-scale studies reported pooled sensitivity and specificity around 93–99%23–25. Our model achieved slightly lower but balanced results (accuracy 91.8–92.5%; sensitivity 90.9–91.6%; specificity 92.6–93.2%)26, confirming robust performance in practical settings.

Unlike prior single-center or still-image–based analyses28,29, our investigation integrated real-time video data from multiple institutions, allowing evaluation under realistic diagnostic conditions. Based on this robust validation framework, we demonstrated that AI assistance significantly improved diagnostic accuracy across all lesion categories—including both focal and diffuse types—in both still-image (M1) and video-based (M2) assessments (all p < 0.05).

Without AI, the endoscopists demonstrated relatively high and consistent performance in diagnosing gastric cancer (mean accuracy: 80.01 ± 2.58%), likely reflecting their familiarity with the distinct visual patterns of malignancy emphasized during training. In contrast, baseline accuracy was notably lower for dysplasia (67.16 ± 6.04%) and IM (68.95 ± 5.48%). These lower figures, accompanied by wider 95% confidence intervals and lower inter-observer agreement, highlight that subtle mucosal changes and ill-defined margins remain inherently challenging for in-training endoscopists.

With AI assistance, these diagnostic gaps were substantially bridged. The results demonstrate that AI serves as an effective ‘equalizer’ by elevating overall diagnostic accuracy and reducing inter-observer variability. Notably, while substantial gains in specificity remained a common driver of improvement for IM in both modalities, the nature of this enhancement evolved in the video-based assessment. In the M1 test, the accuracy gain for IM was almost exclusively driven by a significant increase in specificity (67.95% to 90.86%, p < 0.05). In contrast, the M2 assessment demonstrated a more synergistic improvement; while specificity gains remained high (67.95% to 91.71%, p < 0.05), they were accompanied by a notable rise in sensitivity (70.15% to 77.78%, p = 0.063). This dual improvement led to a markedly higher accuracy of 85.34%, suggesting that the temporal and spatial information provided by video data allows AI to not only rule out false positives but also better assist endoscopists in identifying diffuse mucosal patterns.

However, it is noteworthy that for certain subtypes such as dysplasia and IM, the increase in sensitivity did not reach statistical significance. This lack of significance is likely attributable to the high inter-observer variability among in-training endoscopists at baseline. The large standard deviations (SD) in their initial performance resulted in wide and overlapping 95% confidence intervals, which masked the statistical impact of the observed sensitivity gains. Nevertheless, the substantial and consistent rise in overall accuracy, driven by significant gains in specificity (e.g., 86.15% to 95.82% for dysplasia), suggests that the AI’s primary clinical value currently lies in enhancing diagnostic certainty and ruling out false-positive interpretations rather than merely increasing detection.

Furthermore, an interesting contrast was observed in NNL performance. While AI improved all metrics in M1, the gains in specificity and PPV for NNL did not reach statistical significance in the video-based assessment (p = 0.094). Notably, this occurred despite the AI achieving its highest standalone accuracy for NNL (94.80% in M2). This discrepancy suggests that in dynamic observation, real-time AI cues may increase the cognitive load on in-training endoscopists. To avoid false negatives, less experienced practitioners may over-interpret benign inflammatory changes as potential lesions. These findings suggest that while AI enhances precision, clinicians must maintain balanced judgment to avoid unnecessary biopsies. Whether this increased scrutiny impacts overall procedural efficiency warrants further investigation in prospective live settings.

These findings are consistent with, and in several metrics align with the upper performance range of, previously reported CNN-based studies. They further suggest that AI assistance enhances diagnostic accuracy, particularly for challenging lesion types, and may help reduce inter-observer variability, translating algorithmic precision into clinically meaningful improvements in endoscopic interpretation28,30–32.

n video-based evaluation, the AI outperformed still-image analysis, likely because the temporal aggregation framework helped mitigate frame-level noise and enhanced diagnostic stability. The system’s four-engine parallel architecture achieves rapid processing (~25 ms per frame), enabling comprehensive analysis—including simultaneous focal lesion detection and IM grading—within approximately 750 ms (Supplementary Video S1). While such computational performance is designed to facilitate smoother integration into clinical workflows, its actual impact on procedural efficiency remains to be confirmed in real-world practice. Notably, our system demonstrates comparable accuracy to previously reported high-speed frameworks26 while utilizing fewer computational resources. This relative efficiency suggests a potential advantage for deployment in diverse clinical settings, though further prospective validation is necessary to fully determine its practical utility.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be addressed. First, although this was a dual-center study, the majority of data were collected retrospectively from a single center, which may not fully represent the diverse endoscopic findings across different clinical settings. Second, the use of curated five-second video clips may have created an optimized diagnostic environment; thus, these results might reflect the upper bound of the AI’s performance rather than routine practice with more visual noise.

Third, while AI served as an effective’equalizer’ for in-training endoscopists, these findings may not directly generalize to expert-level practitioners. Fourth, the one-week washout period was relatively short and could potentially introduce recall bias. However, the use of a large dataset comprising 1,570 cases combined with a randomized case-order design effectively mitigated this risk, ensuring that the diagnostic improvements were not driven by case familiarity.

Fifth, our IM definition was strictly limited to high-risk stages (OLGIM III/IV) to ensure pathological reproducibility, which may limit the system’s applicability to focal or low-risk mucosal changes. Furthermore, as an exploratory study, no formal multiplicity adjustment was applied; results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating. Given the pilot nature of this clinical validation, we prioritized the detection of potential AI-driven improvements across diverse lesion types over the stringent control of type I error, which warrants confirmation in subsequent larger-scale trials. Lastly, the long-term impact on clinical workflow and patient outcomes remains to be clarified through prospective longitudinal studies.

Future research should focus on expanding multicenter, prospective validation in diverse clinical settings to incorporate heterogeneous endoscopic systems and real-world conditions. Beyond diagnostic accuracy, integrating AI into daily workflows must be accompanied by comprehensive evaluations of usability, cost-effectiveness, and procedural efficiency. Longitudinal outcome studies are warranted to determine whether AI-assisted diagnosis contributes to improved therapeutic decision-making and, ultimately, better patient survival. Furthermore, ensuring the reliability of AI-driven outputs and fostering user trust will be essential for the successful integration of these systems into routine clinical practice.

Conclusion

AI assistance consistently improved clinicians’ diagnostic accuracy across lesion types and modalities, with the greatest gains for dysplasia and intestinal metaplasia—conditions prone to interpretation variability. These benefits reflected concurrent increases in sensitivity and specificity rather than a trade-off, supporting true enhancement of clinical decision-making. Our findings support integrating AI into routine upper endoscopy to standardize interpretation and enable earlier recognition of clinically significant lesions. Prospective multicenter validations across diverse endoscopic systems should further define effects on workflow and patient outcomes.
