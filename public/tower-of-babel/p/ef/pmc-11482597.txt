Lead Acetate Exposure and Cerebral Amyloid Accumulation: Mechanistic Evaluations in APP/PS1 Mice
Authors: Huiying Gu, Luqing L Liu, Alanna Wu, Yongqi Yu, Uzay Emir, Stephen J Sawiak, Paul R Territo, Matin R Farlow, Wei Zheng, Yansheng Du
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:The role of environmental factors in Alzheimer’s disease (AD) pathogenesis remains elusive. Mounting evidence suggests that acute and past exposure to the environmental toxicant lead (Pb) is associated with longitudinal decline in cognitive function, brain atrophy, and greater brain β-amyloid (Aβ) deposition. However, the nature of Pb-induced amyloid deposition and how it contributes to AD development remain unclear.Objectives:This study investigates the role of Pb in the pathogenesis of cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and whether plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) contributes to this process in the APP/PS1 mouse model.Methods:Female APP/PS1 mice at 8 wk of age were administered either 50mg/kg Pb-acetate (PbAc) (i.e., 27mg Pb/kg) or an equivalent molar concentration of sodium acetate (NaAc) via oral gavage once daily for 8 wk. Amyloid deposition and vascular amyloid were determined by immunostaining. In addition, Aβ perivascular drainage, vascular binding assay, and microglial endocytosis were examined to determine underlying mechanisms. Furthermore, magnetic resonance imaging demyelination imaging was performed in vivo measure the level of demyelination. Finally, Y-maze and Morris water maze tests were assessed to evaluate the cognitive function of mice.Results:APP/PS1 mice (an AD mice model) exposed to PbAc demonstrated more vascular amyloid deposition less neocortical myelination, and lower cognitive function, as well as greater vascular binding to Aβ40, higher Aβ40/Aβ42 ratios, strikingly lower Aβ40 levels in the perivascular drainage, and microglial endocytosis. Importantly, exposure to a specific PAI-1 inhibitor, tiplaxtinin, which previously was reported to lower CAA pathology in mice, resulted in less CAA-related outcomes following PbAc exposure.Discussion:Our findings suggest that PbAc induced CAA/AD pathogenesis via the PAI-1 signaling in the APP/PS1 mouse model, and the inhibition of PAI-1 could be a potential therapeutic target for PbAc-mediated CAA/AD disorders. https://doi.org/10.1289/EHP14384

Introduction

Despite being an occupational hazard, environmental exposure to lead (Pb) has been and will continue to be a major public health concern, because this metal is widely present in air, drinking water, household products, plastics, and painted materials.1 The presence of high Pb levels were detected in diffuse neurofibrillary tangles,2 suggesting a possible link between Pb exposure and Alzheimer’s disease (AD).3 In other studies, Pb was not found to accumulate substantially in blood, cerebral spinal fluid, and postmortem brains, possibly because of its fast turnover rate in human fluid and soft tissues.4–7 Recently, it was demonstrated that in a healthy male population the cumulative adult bone Pb levels had a biological half-life of 20–30 y8 and were associated with faster declines in cognition, suggesting that Pb exposure can accelerate cognitive aging.9 Moreover, another study also showed an increased association between tibia lead and resistant hypertension.10 Recent evidence in rodents10 and monkeys11,12 suggests that past Pb exposure might lead to latent expression of amyloid precursor protein (APP), thus resulting in amyloid formation later in life. Alternatively, Pb exposure could also directly stimulate lifelong amyloid deposition when young without affecting APP expression, as demonstrated in mice baring the human APP gene.13,14 However, the nature of Pb-induced amyloid deposition and its contribution to the development of AD and related dementia (ADRD), as well as the underlying pathogenic mechanism remain to be determined. It should be noted that by 2050, dementia cases have been projected to triple among the population over the age of 75 y (born before 1975).15 The reference blood Pb level (BLL) of children recommended by the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (US CDC) in 1975 was 30μg/dL,16 rather than current 3.5μg/dL (5 μg/dL for adults).16,17 In addition, the public health guidance from the US Surgeon General in 1971 recommended intervention at BLL 60–80μg/dL.18 Therefore, two different populations at risk should be targeted: a) those exposed to Pb at high dosages when young; and b) individuals who were continuously exposed to dosages below the reference BLL. Even between 2011 and 2018, a significant number of children had BLLs >20μg/dL.19 Therefore, this study was specifically designed to focus on the first population. Aggregation of Aβ peptide in senile plaques is considered a major hallmark and central event in AD pathogenesis.20,21 It is interesting that Aβ not only deposits in the brain parenchyma but also in the cerebral vasculature, where the latter results in cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and contributes to blood vessel damage.22–24 In contrast to parenchymal amyloid, which is largely composed of Aβ42, CAA contains predominately Aβ40, with a higher Aβ40/Aβ42 ratio.25 It has been postulated that CAA-induced neurodegeneration can result from either ischemic demyelination and microinfarction or from hemorrhagic injury.26 These pathways cause both gray (GM) and white matter (WM) atrophy, along with disconnecting brain networks. Epidemiological data show the prevalence of CAA among older patients with dementia is 60%, higher than those without dementia.27 More than 90% of patients with AD have pathologic evidence of CAA, but this varies widely depending on methods used to determine CAA formation.28–30 In addition, because a small vessel disease contributes to subcortical vascular dementia (SVD) syndrome, CAA was shown to be common in vascular dementia (VaD) (∼30%) and was associated with cortical microinfarcts in VaD.31 Smaller percentages of CAA were observed for VaD and might in many instances be incidental, as seen in significant numbers of cognitively normal patients.28 It is noteworthy that both AD and VaD represented 85% of dementia cases in one study31 and are often clinically overlapped.32 However, research into the exact role of CAA in the pathogenesis of AD and ADRD is still under development.33 It is interesting that the coexistence of CAA with AD was observed to result in more pronounced decline in cognitive performance in comparison with AD alone, even after accounting for variables such as age, neurofibrillary tangles, amyloid plaques, and infarctions.34 Cerebral microvascular amyloid deposition, rather than parenchymal amyloid accumulation, was more frequently associated with dementia in individuals afflicted with AD and spontaneous CAA disorders.35,36 This association was also observed in mouse models.37 An important factor is that myelin water fraction (MWF) imaging studies demonstrated that the myelin loss that usually occurs early in vascular dysfunction-induced neuronal injury is also associated with neurocognitive function loss and aging in patients with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD.38

It is important to note that CAA was shown to be induced by the dysfunction of pathways underlying the perivascular drainage and microglial endocytosis mechanisms that clear Aβ from the brain along cerebrovascular walls.39 Previous mouse studies discovered that several CAA risk factors, including apolipoprotein E4 (apoE4), apolipoprotein J (apoJ), aging, and high-fat diet,40–42 affected microglial clearance and perivascular clearance drainage of Aβ. Plasminogen activator inhibitor type-1 (PAI-1)43 is the principal inhibitor of plasminogen activators including the fast-acting inhibitor of tissue-type PA (tPA) and urokinase-type PA (uPA) that are responsible for converting plasminogen to plasmin, a strong proteolytic enzyme that contributes to fibrinolysis,44 Aβ degradation,45 and increased thickness of vascular basement membranes.46 Important relevant findings from previous studies are that accumulation of PAI-1 promotes the development of PAI-1–dependent perivascular fibrosis47 that could enhance Aβ binding to the cerebrovasculature,48 and disrupted perivascular drainage pathways, leading to CAA formation in mouse models.49 Significant findings in studies in mice using an inhibitor of PAI-1, tiplaxtinin (TIP), have shown that PAI-1 played an important role in CAA formation, likely independent of the plasmin mechanism, as in comparison with its negative effect on parenchymal amyloid deposition.50

Therefore, we predict that Pb, as an important environmental risk factor for both amyloid pathology and cardiovascular injury, may specifically contribute to ADRD pathogenesis by stimulating vascular amyloid or CAA via PAI-1.

Materials and Methods

Animals and Treatments

APP/PS1 heterozygous mice with a C57BL/6C3H genetic background were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Stock No. 004462) and bred in the Laboratory Animal Resource enter (LARC) at Indiana University School of Medicine. Wildtype (WT) littermates (nonAPP/PS1 carrier) were used as controls. Mice were housed 3–5 per cage, fed with mouse diet (2018SX; Envigo) and water ad libitum, and maintained in a 12-h light/dark cycle facility.

All solutions were prepared using double-distilled water and their pH was adjusted to a pH of 7.2 before gavage. Pb-acetate (PbAc, 467863; Sigma–Aldrich) solution was stored at 4oC and freshly prepared every week. In addition, mouse diet (2018SX; Envigo) amended with 12mg/kg PAI-1 inhibitor, TIP51 (BOC Sciences), was used to evaluate the role of PAI-1 in outcomes related to PbAc exposure in select groups. The diet was stored at 4oC and replenished in the cages bi-weekly.

APP/PS1 mice and WT mice at 8 wk of age were administered either 50mg/kg PbAc (i.e., 27mg Pb/kg) or an equivalent molar concentration of sodium acetate (NaAc) via oral gavage once daily. Atomic Absorption Spectrometry (AAS) Analysis

Six WT mice were administered 50mg/kg Pb-acetate via oral gavage once daily for 8 wk. Blood was drawn from CO2 anesthetized mice via the cardiac puncture and collected with heparin tubes (365965; BD). Pb concentrations in whole blood were determined by a flameless graphite furnace AAS using the procedure of Fernandez and Hilligoss.52–54 The blood samples (200μL/sample) were digested with nitric acid in the oven at 55oC overnight. Digested samples were diluted 50-fold with 1.0% (vol/vol) ultrapure HNO3. An Agilent Technologies 200 Series AA Spectrometer with a GTA 120 graphite tube atomizer (Agilent Technologies 200 Series) was used to quantify Pb concentrations. The detection limit for this method was 1.35 ng Pb/mL of assay solution. The standard curves were prepared daily at concentrations of 0, 4, 8, 12, 16, and 20μg/L, with correlation coefficient of R2 value exceeding 99.5%. The intraday and interday precisions of the method were 1.5% and 2.9%, respectively.

Histology and Immunohistochemistry

Left hemibrains from APP/PS1 mice (see above) were rapidly frozen by plunging into liquid nitrogen, and the right hemibrains were ice-chilled immediately after harvest. To determine how Pb exposure affected amyloid plaque and vascular amyloid deposition, left hemibrains were sectioned with a vibratome at 30μm for plaque quantification. Brain vessels were blocked with 1% BSA (A7906; Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h at room temperature and then stained with an anticollagen IV antibody (1:80; PA5-104508, Invitrogen) for 24 h at 4oC, followed by an alkaline phosphatase (AP) goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (1:1,000; ab6722, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature and detected by AP staining kit (red) (ab242286; Abcam), following the manufacturer’s instructions. Parenchymal plaques and vascular amyloid depositions were double stained with 1% Thioflavin S (T1892; Sigma-Aldrich) for 8 min, washed twice in 80% and 95% ethanol for 3 min, and then washed with distilled water three times. Slides were observed using a high-content imaging system (ImagXpress Micro Confocal) with 10×/NA=0.45 and 60×/NA=0.94 Nikon objectives.14 Confocal images were collected using a z-increment of 3μm and projected via maximal projections. For each animal, any size visible plaques were counted in nine sections (spaced 210μm apart) from one hemibrain.14 Nine brain regions were randomly imaged for the quantification of vascular associated amyloid deposition and amyloid-associated vessels by an observer blinded to the treatment paradigm. Brain sections were also stained with rabbit anti-NeuN antibody (1:500; ABN78, EMD Millipore) or rabbit anti-myelin basic protein antibody (MBP, 1:500; ab218011, Abcam) for 24 h at 4oC, respectively. An anti-rabbit FITC antibody (1:1,000; ab6717, Abcam) was used as a secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature. Results were visualized using confocal microscopy.55 The analysis was performed on three randomly selected sections from hippocampus and cortex area. Fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ (Image Processing and Analysis in Java; National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA). All assessments were done by an observer blind to the experiment. Three or four mice per group were used, and experiments were repeated at least once.

Isolation of mouse cerebral microvasculature and brain parenchyma.

Ice-chilled right hemibrain tissues were weighed and put in a 1mL tissue glass grinder (Wheaton) with 3 times its volume (g/mL; brain weight/HB volume) of ice-cold HB-I buffer (HEPES 10 mmol/L, NaCl 141 mmol/L, KCl 4 mmol/L, MgSO4 1.0 mmol/L, NaH2PO4 1.0 mmol/L, CaCl2 2.5 mmol/L, and glucose 10 mmol/L, pH 7.4) and homogenized with eight up and down strokes of the pestle. Four times the volume of ice-cold 30% Dextran-70 solution was then added, and the solution was homogenized with another three up and down strokes. The homogenate was transferred to another tube and centrifuged at 5,400×g at 4oC for 15 min. The supernatant (vasculature-depleted brain parenchyma) was carefully removed from the pellet by pipetting for ELISA. The pellet was washed with a mixed solution of HB-I and 30% Dextran-70 (3:4) twice to remove the brain parenchyma. The cerebral microvasculature in the pellet and the vasculature-depleted supernatant were confirmed via light microscopy (Leica Galen III Microscope) examination.56–58 The cerebral microvasculature was used for western blot and ELISA assay. The parenchyma was used for ELISA assay.

Quantification of Aβ by ELISA.

Levels of Aβ were assayed by sandwich ELISA as described previously.13,14 In brief, isolated mouse cerebral microvasculature and brain parenchyma were extracted with a 5-M guanidine buffer. The hippocampal and cortical tissues were processed in 96-well ELISA plates that had been coated with antibodies 2G3 and 21F12 (1:300; generous gifts from Eli Lilly and Co.)59–61 for 24 h at 4oC to determine Aβ40 and Aβ42, respectively. After incubation of plates with casein buffer [0.25% casein and 0.05% sodium azide in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)] for 2 h, samples were loaded 24 h at 4°C. Biotin-3D659–61 (1:50; a generous gift from Eli Lilly and Co.) was incubated for 1 h at RT, followed by horseradish peroxidase (1:3,000; 434323, ThermoFisher Scientific Inc.) for 1 h. After incubation with 3,3′,5,5′-Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB; Sigma-Aldrich), the plates were read at the FlexStation 3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader (Molecular Devices) for absorbance at 450 nm. Total protein concentrations in the brain were determined using BCA protein assay, and the concentration of Aβ in the tissues was reported as ng/mg of total protein.

Cell Culture of BV2 Cells

The immortalized BV2 microglial cell62 was derived from C57BL/six mice and purchased from Accegen. In brief, the cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 Media supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% L-glutamine, and 1% penicillin/streptomycin in a humidified incubator with 95% air and 5% CO2 at 37oC and passaged twice a week. All supplies were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific Inc. Next, 2×104 cells were seeded at a 96-well cell culture plate containing 100μL cell culture media for 24 h and then treated with or without 1μM PbAc for 24 h (n=4). The viability of BV2 cells was quantified by MTT [3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] assay. In brief, BV2 cells were washed with PBS and replaced with fresh cell culture media, incubated with 5mg/mL MTT (M2128, Sigma–Aldrich) at 37oC for 4 h in a cell culture incubator. Then after washing cells three times, 100μL dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO) was added into the culture plate to dissolve MTT. Read absorbance at 570 nm using the FlexStation 3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader. Values are expressed as a percentage of control cells. Data are represented as the mean±standard deviation (SD) and repeated at least once.

To measure the expression level of LRP1, 2×105 BV2 cells were seeded in a 12-well cell culture plated for 24 h and then treated with or without 1μM PbAc for 24 h (n=6). At the end of the experiment, cells were washed and lysed with the radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing protease inhibitors (1:25; Roche). Scraped cell extracts using cold plastic cell scrapers together with the RIPA buffer were collected in microcentrifuge tubes and incubated on ice for additional 30 min. After centrifugation at 16,000×g for 10 min at 4oC, levels of PAI-1 and LRP1 in the supernatant were assayed by western blot.

To test in vitro microglial endocytosis of Aβ, 2×105 BV2 cells were seeded in a 12-well cell culture plated for 24 h and pretreated with or without 10μM TIP for 1 h, followed by 1μM PbAc treatments for an additional 24 h; 1μM external Aβ40 or Aβ42 (J66277 and J66387; ThermoFisher) was then added into culture media for another 3 h. At the end of the experiment, culture media were removed, and cells were washed and lysed with the RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (1:25; Roche). Scraped cell extracts using cold plastic cell scrapers together with the RIPA buffer were collected in microcentrifuge tubes and incubated on ice for additional 30 min. After centrifugation at 16,000×g for 10 min at 4oC, intracellular levels of Aβ40 and Aβ42 in the supernatant were determined by ELISA as tissues.14 Six wells per group were assayed for each experiment that were repeated at least once.

Western Blot Analysis

Isolated cerebrovascular tissues and cultured cells were homogenized in the RIPA buffer containing protease inhibitors (1:25; Roche) and incubated on ice for additional 30 min before centrifugation at 16,000×g for 10 min at 4°C to analyze specific target proteins. The whole protein concentration in supernatants separated from tissue and cultured cell debris was determined by the BCA protein assay (ThermoFisher). The supernatants (20μg of protein, from either tissue or cell extraction) were loaded onto a 4%–12% Bis–Tris gel, electrophoresed, and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Blots were then blocked in 5% skim milk for 2 h at room temperature and probed at 4°C for 24 h with rabbit anti PAI-1 antibody (1:1,000; ab222754, Abcam), anti-LRP1 antibody (1:20,000; ab92544, Abcam), or mouse anti GAPDH (1:1,000; sc-32233, Santa Cruz). After washing three times, blots were incubated with secondary IRDye 680RD goat antimouse or IRDye 800CW goat anti-rabbit antibody (1:5,000; 925-68070 or 925-32211, LI-COR Biosciences) for 1 h at room temperature and imaged with an Odyssey Imaging System. Band intensities were quantified, and results were reported as a ratio of the target protein to GAPDH.14

Ex Vivo Aβ Binding Affinity Assay

The cerebrovasculature was isolated from female wild-type (WT) mice following the methods of isolation of mouse cerebral microvasculature as mentioned above. Next, 200μg of vessels per reaction were pretreated with or without 10μM TIP for 1 h, followed by 1μM PbAc treatments for additional 24 h in the Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) buffer supplemented with 0.5% BSA at room temperature with gentle rotation. Then, 1μM Aβ40 or Aβ42 was added to the buffer for additional 30 min. Vessels were then washed three times in the HBSS buffer with 0.5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and lysed with 5M guanidine-HCL for 1 h at room temperature. The level of the Aβ bonded to vasculature was assessed by ELISA as described earlier in the “Materials and Methods” section. Four samples per group were used for each experiment, each of which was repeated at least once.

Perivascular Drainage Assay

Mice 16 wk of age received PbAc or NaAc for 8 wk, and treatments were restrained in a stereotaxic device after IP injections with ketamine (75mg/kg) and xylazine (10mg/kg). Injection capillaries were then inserted through predrilled holes and into the left dorsal hippocampus (coordinates from Bregma: AP=−2mm; ML=1.8mm, and DV=1.6mm). All animals received a 0.5μL Fluor488-labeled Aβ40 (AnaSpec; final concentration was 10μM) over a 10-min period to allow for adequate perivascular drainage while minimizing Aβ clearance across the BBB or via uptake by microglia or macrophages. Injected capillaries were left in situ for 2 min to prevent the reflux of the fluospheres along the injection track but allowed for diffusion of Aβ in the brain parenchyma. Mice were then transcardially perfused with PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) before removal of the brains. Brains were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and sectioned with a vibratome at 30μm. To quantify perivascular drainage, brain vessels were stained with an anticollagen IV antibody (1:80; PA5-104508, Invitrogen) for 24 h at 4oC, followed by an alkaline phosphatase (AP) goat anti-rabbit IgG antibody (1:1,000, ab6722, Abcam) for 1 h at room temperature, to label and count the number of arteries colocalized with fluorescent-labeled Aβ. Photos of double-labeling immunohistochemistry were captured from tissue sections between the injection site and 100-μm posterior using a Leica confocal microscope. The level of Aβ associated with cerebrovasculature between the injection site and 100-μm posterior was quantitated by measuring the fluorescence intensity using ImageJ (Image Processing and Analysis in Java).41 Three mice per group were used, and experiments were repeated at least once.

MRI Demyelination Imaging

Brains from PbAc-treated mice were examined by MRI using ultra-short echo time (UTE) with a Rosette k-space pattern to evaluate brain myelination alterations with or without PAI-1 treatment. All MRI examinations were conducted at the Small Animal MRI Facility in the Bindley Bioscience Center at Purdue University, Lafayette, Indiana, USA. Mice were anesthetized by 1.5% isoflurane for maintenance and scanned in a 7-Tesla horizontal-bore small animal MRI system (BioSpec 70/30; Bruker Instruments) equipped with a gradient insert (maximum gradient: 660 mT/m; maximum slew rate: 4,570 T/m/s), a volume transmit and receive 1H radiofrequency (RF) head coil. Multiparametric mapping (MPM) sequences were based on a multigradient echo sequence with RF spoiling at 117°.63 Echoes were obtained from 2.41 ms, with a spacing of 2.1 ms. For the three contrasts, weightings magnetization transfer (MT)/proton density (PD)/T1 parameters were: Repetition time (TR)=25/25/18 ms, fractional anisotropy (FA)=6/6/40°, and the number of echoes 6/8/6. The matrix was 256×160×128 with a field of view 30×22×30mm, yielding an isotropic spatial resolution of 109×175×175 μm3. For MT images, a single 4 ms [bandwidth (BW) 685 Hz] Gaussian preparation pulse 2kHz off-resonance of 10μT was applied. With an acceleration of 1.6 achieved by zero-filling in the phase encoding directions, the total scan time for the MPM sequences was 17 min and 46 s. Processing followed to produce maps of T1 and MTsat for each animal to calculate myelin water fraction.63 Images were preprocessed using SPM12 (University College London, London, UK; leased on 1 October 2014) with the SPMMouse toolbox.64 The mouse GM and WM masks from SPMMouse were used to calculate summary statistics of parameters at each age. In brief, the images were processed by manually removing the nonbrain tissue using masks. Then, the images underwent bias correction and were transformed into the SPMMouse template space using SPM affine transformations. All images were realigned to match the SPMMouse template with regard to orientation and resolution. After preprocessing, myelin water fraction measures were modeled in a general linear model (GLM) to test hypotheses. A suitable model for this study is a two-sample Student’s t-test between groups. p-Values <0.001 were considered to indicate statistical significance, and two-sided tests were used for all statistical analyses. Three or four mice per group were used for each experiment.

Morris Water Maze Test

The water maze was performed 1 wk before Y-maze assays in a 1.2-m diameter light yellow cylindrical pool filled with 26°C water, rendered opaque with 150mL of nontoxic white tempera paint. It included a 20-cm diameter hidden Plexiglas escape platform submerged 0.5cm below the surface of the water. The water maze was situated in an experimental room with abundant environmental cues, such as a window, door, experimental equipment, and tables. Following a 7-wk PbAc exposure, mice performed visible (platform with a flag) and invisible (platform) trials, as well as a probe trial (without platform). Initially, the platform was placed in the pool’s second quadrant, and mice were gently placed at the fourth quadrant facing the wall. For subsequent trials, mice were randomly placed in any quadrant except the one containing the platform. An observer timed how long each mouse took to find the platform, with trials capped at 60 s. Mice failing to find the platform within this timeframe were placed on it (in which case 60 s was recorded at the time). After each trial, mice rested on the platform for 30 s. Mice were given 18 training (six times per day/3 d) trials, during which their latency to find the platform was measured up to a maximum of 60 s. Following visible platform tests, the flag was removed for the invisible platform experiment conducted in the same manner. The flag was removed, and the trials were repeated as described. After 3 d of training, tests were performed on each of day 4, day 5, and day 6. The final assessment, termed a probe trial, is a measure of both cognitive reasoning and memory. This test occurred immediately following the invisible platform tests, during which the platform was removed from the pool. Mice were assessed for the amount of time spent searching in the quadrant previously containing the platform.14,65 Five or more mice per group were used for each experiment that were repeated at least once.

Y-Maze Spontaneous Alternation

After Morris water maze test, mice underwent a spontaneous alternation by allowing mice to explore all three arms of the Y-maze. This Y-maze consisted of three identical arms (30 cm in length, 5 cm in width, and 12-cm high opaque walls) that converged in the center at 120° angles from each other. Mice were allowed 3-d of habituation in the experimental environment and were acclimated to the Y-maze 5 min before the test. At the beginning of the test, each mouse was placed at one end of an arm and allowed to explore freely for 8 min. The frequency with which the tail of each mouse fully entered each arm was counted. The number of times each mouse entered the arm one after another (in A, B, or C sequence) was counted as actual alternation. After the behavioral test, mice were returned to their home cage. The inside of the Y-maze was wiped with 70% ethanol between different animal trials. All behavioral procedures were recorded by a video camera, and the sequence of entry was manually counted. Statistical Analysis

Unless otherwise indicated, statistical analysis to evaluate the differences between groups were conducted using a one-way analysis of variance with post hoc comparisons using Dunnett’s test. All data are presented as mean±SD. Differences between two means were considered significant at <0.05.

Results

Cortical and Hippocampal Aβ Pathology in 16-Wk-Old Female APP/PS1 Mice Exposed to PbAc for 8 Wk in the Presence or Absence of TIP

We exposed 8-wk-old female and male APP/PS1 mice to 50mg/kg PbAc or an equivalent molar concentration of NaAc for 8 wk. In this study, by performing a double staining with both thioflavin S and anticollagen IV (Figure 1A) in brain sections followed by stereological quantification, we found that in comparison with the NaAc-treated group, female APP/PS1 mice exposed to 50mg/kg PbAc (i.e., Pb at 27mg/kg) for 8 wk exhibited markedly greater levels of vascular Aβ formation in both the hippocampus and the neocortex by almost 300% (7.0±1.0 vs. 27.3±5.4, p<0.01) at 16 wk of age (Figure 1C). In contrast, those exposed to PbAc had more total beta amyloid plaques than NaAc-treated group (258.0±89.7 vs. 481.8±86.2, p<0.05; Figure 1B). The ratio of vascular Aβ deposits to parenchymal amyloid deposits was greater by almost 100% (0.03±0.008 vs. 0.06±0.013, p<0.05; Figure 1D) in Pb-exposed vs. Na-exposed female mice. Consistently, amyloid-positive vessels in PbAc-treated female APP/PS1 mice were greater than those NaAc-treated mice (16.3±3.5 vs. 7.0±1.0, p<0.01; Figure 1E). Consistent with the data of amyloid deposition, ELISA analyses revealed that the concentrations of Aβ1–40 and Aβ1–42 in cortical parenchyma of PbAc-treated mice were higher by 94.8% (1.65±0.35 vs. 3.21±0.38, p<0.01) and 33.9% (4.33±0.99 vs. 5.80±1.36, p>0.05) than those of NaAc-treated mice, whereas those in the vasculature of PbAc-treated female mice were much greater by 610.4% (57.39±11.76 vs. 8.08±2.62, p<0.01) and 64.5% (115.14±20.00 vs. 70.02±18.18, p<0.05) than NaAc-treated mice (Figure 1F,G). As expected, after PbAc treatments, the ratios of Aβ1–40/Aβ1–42 in PbAc-treated groups were greater at 0.58±0.21 in the parenchyma and 0.50±0.10 in the brain vasculature than the ratio in the NaAc-treated groups at 0.39±0.09 and 0.11±0.02, respectively (Figure 1H).

Vascular PAI-1 Expression, Perivascular Drainage of Aβ, and Ex Vivo Aβ Binding Affinity to Isolated Cerebrovasculature in PbAc Exposed Female APP/PS1 Mice

To elucidate the possible mechanism underlying the shift of Aβ deposition predominately from the parenchyma to the cerebrovasculature in PbAc-treated female APP/PS1 mice, we investigated PAI-1 expression in the cerebrovasculature of female mice exposed to chronic PbAc at 27mg/kg (PbAc at 50mg/kg), because PAI-1 was shown to induce vascular Aβ formation.68 As expected, PAI-1 expression was significantly greater in cerebrovasculature isolated from mice treated with 27mg/kg of Pb (PbAc at 50mg/kg) for 3 d in comparison with control (1.35±0.18 vs. 1.00±0.12, p<0.05, Figure 3A). To look at the mechanism, we next isolated cerebrovasculature from WT mice, exposed them to NaAc or PbAc at 1μM for 24 h, and then evaluated the binding affinity to Aβ40 using ex vivo binding affinity assay. Significantly higher levels of Aβ40 (32.02±2.21 vs. 23.63±2.68 ng/mg, p<0.05) and Aβ42 (31.93±1.17 vs. 32.78±2.79 ng/mg, p=0.62) bound to the vasculature were observed in PbAc- than levels in the NaAc-treated groups (Figure 3B). The ratio of Aβ40/Aβ42 was also greater in PbAc-treated blood vessels than in Na-treated ones (1.00±0.03 vs. 0.73±0.13, p<0.05) (Figure 3C). In addition, we sought to investigate whether PbAc exposure could dampen perivascular drainage. To evaluate perivascular drainage in PbAc-exposed subjects, mice were injected with Fluor488-labeled Aβ40 (AnaSpec) into the left dorsal hippocampus, and colocalization of Aβ40 was evaluated in arteries between the injection site and a site 100-μm posterior to the injection site 60 min after injection. We found a 2.9-fold stronger Aβ40 fluorescent intensity surrounding arteries in PbAc-treated APP/PS1 mice than in NaAc-treated APP/PS1 littermates (5.90±1.38 vs. 17.00±2.63, p<0.01) (Figure 3D,E).

CAA, Myelination Expression, and Cognitive Function in 16-Wk-Old Female APP/PS1 Mice Exposed to PbAc for 8 Wk in the Presence or Absence of TIP

Given that cerebrovascular PAI-1 was greater in APP/PS1 mice exposed to PbAc and PAI-1/tPA was specifically shown to contribute to amyloid formation and related cognitive deficits, we investigated whether TIP, a PAI-1 inhibitor, could specifically lower CAA in comparison with parenchymal amyloid deposition and affect cognition in PbAc-treated APP/PS1 mice. As expected, mice exposed to TIP at a dosage of 12mg/kg in chow exhibited markedly lower parenchymal amyloid deposition in APP/PS1 mice also exposed to PbAc (349.7±40.5), in comparison with mice exposed to PbAc alone (481.8±82.6, p<0.05) (Figure 1B). Mice exposed to TIP also had markedly lower levels of vascular Aβ formation in both hippocampus and neocortex (15.0±1.0 vs. 27.3±5.4, p<0.05) (Figure 1C). Consistent with the behavioral results below, TIP in mice exposed to NaAc did not show any effect on vascular amyloid formation (15.0±1.0 vs. 14.6±3.0, p>0.05). Mice exposed to PbAc with TIP had a lower ratio of vascular Aβ deposits to parenchymal amyloid deposits by almost 33.3% (0.04±0.003 vs. 0.06±0.013, p<0.05) (Figure 1D). APP/PS1 Mice exposed to PbAc and TIP also had less amyloid-positive vessels than those exposed to PbAc alone (15.0±1.0 vs. 27.3±5.4, p<0.05) (Figure 1E). Moreover, PbAc-exposed mice treated with TIP also exhibited more myelin in the neocortex (31.75±4.98 vs. 42.47±2.81, p<0.05) (Figure 5E). For the further confirmation, we performed an MRI imaging for in vivo detection of myelin and showed that mice exposed to Pb and TIP had significantly more myelin in the cortical white matter in comparison with those exposed to PbAc alone, as measured using the myeline water fraction (0.35±0.02 in Pb+TIP vs. 0.32±0.03 in control, p<0.01) (Figure 6A). Furthermore, we assessed mouse memory in APP/PS1 mice using a Y-maze spontaneous alternation test that examined spatial working memory66,67,73 and Morris water maze.14 As expected, mice exposed to PbAc for 8 wk had a significantly lower percentage of arm alternations (46.7±8.4%), in comparison with NaAc-treated mice (62.5±13.1%, p<0.01) (Figure 6A). In contrast, those also exposed to TIP had dramatically greater memory in comparison with those exposed to PbAc alone (64.1±11.0%, p<0.01) (Figure 7A). Consistently, PbAc-exposed APP/PS1 mice exposed to TIP also had to greater spatial learning and memory as assessed by the Morris water maze. In the water maze, TIP-treated mice exposed to PbAc performed significantly better than mice exposed to Pb alone. Both TIP with PbAc and with NaAc–treated groups required 7.0±4.6 s and 11.9±13.0 s to find the platform during the first trail of the visible platform test and shortened to 5.6±1.4 s and 5.4±3.5 s by the third trail. Notably, NaAc-treated mice took 14.4±7.8 to find the submerged platform on the third attempt (p<0.05) (Figure 7C). In the probe test, PbAc-treated mice spent 23.1±6.7 in comparison with 28.9±5.1 s in NaAc-treated mice (p<0.05) in the quadrant previously containing the platform, whereas TIP-treated PbAc exposed mice spent 38.0±10.1 s in the correct quadrant (p<0.01) (Figure 7D). To confirm that PbAc exposure did not cause cognitive deficits in regular mice, WT control mice exposed to PbAc and NaAc were also subjected to the Morris water maze test. The results indicated no significant performance differences between these two groups in three different types of trials (p>0.05; Figure 2A–D). Furthermore, the performance of APP/PS1 mice treated with TIP alone was not different from that of the control group (p>0.05) (Figure 7A–D).

Finally, we sought to investigate the mechanisms underlying outcomes related to TIP and PbAc-related CAA outcomes. Consistent with vascular amyloid data, APP/PS1 mice exposed to PbAc demonstrated significantly lower Aβ40 vascular binding affinity (24.50±4.52 ng/mg vs. 32.02±2.21 ng/mg, p<0.05) (Figure 3B) and a ratio of Aβ40/Aβ42 (0.27±0.11 vs. 1.00±0.03, p<0.05) (Figure 3C) in TIP-treated groups than groups exposed to PbAc alone. Discussion

The APP/PS1 mouse line is a well-characterized and widely used AD model with extensive parenchymal Aβ plaques, but less pronounced CAA.74–76 This model has been widely used to investigate the vascular risk factors such as stroke or hyperhomocysteinemia that promote CAA by shifting parenchymal Aβ into vasculatures.77,78 Although the role of two types of amyloids (parenchymal and vascular amyloids) in AD pathogenesis remains to be defined, CAA is increasingly recognized as a major player in AD development (observed in more than 80% of AD cases).33 In patients with AD, CAA is closely associated with a higher burden of parenchymal amyloid and tau pathology.79 An important factor is that CAA also contributes to amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (both ARIA-E&H)80,81 and intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), two main dose-limiting adverse events in immunization treatments against amyloid for AD.33,82 The data presented in this paper thus highlight the effect of PbAc exposure within the APP/PS1 AD mouse model to gain insight into the complex etiologies of Pb-mediated ADRD. Because our previous study did not find that PbAc exposure affected Aβ production in another APP transgenic mouse model,13 this work suggests that PbAc exposure was associated with a shift from parenchymal amyloid plaques to vascular-associated amyloid plaques by rapidly inducing amyloid formation around the cerebral vasculature via specific inhibition of the clearance of Aβ40 via overactivation of the PAI-1 mechanism. The data provided new evidence for understanding the central roles of the environmental toxin Pb in the pathogenesis of AD and related dementia. Our results suggested that early past exposure of PbAc could stimulate vascular amyloid accumulation/CAA formation that possibly further stimulated vascular injury such as ischemia, hypoperfusion, and hemorrhage; we hypothesized this could lead to ADRD at elderly mice. It was postulated that CAA can further exacerbate parenchymal amyloid deposition33 and tau pathology.83 Overall, we have come to three major novel conclusions: a) PbAc preferentially and rapidly stimulated vascular amyloid deposition in young female APP/PS1 mice accompanied by significant cortical demyelination and cognitive deficits; b) PbAc exposure was associated with vascular and microglial PAI-1 signaling that resulted in greater Aβ40 binding to vasculature, less perivascular drainage of Aβ40, and less LRP-1-mediated microglial Aβ40 uptake—we propose that these led to an elevated Aβ40 to Aβ42 ratio, thus promoting CAA formation; and c) we further propose that the PAI-1 inhibitor, TIP, which was shown to powerfully inhibit vascular-associated amyloid deposition and its related behavioral changes in APP/PS1 mice exposed to PbAc, could block PbAc-induced CAA/amyloid pathogenesis and underlying mechanisms, demyelination, and cognitive deficits, further supporting the hypothesis that PbAc exposure induced demyelination and cognitive deficits, most likely resulting from PbAc-induced PAI-1 mediated vascular amyloid deposition. Our findings are relevant to environmental and ADRD research because individuals were previously exposed to high levels of Pb, which is currently recognized as a dangerous toxicant. Early vascular amyloid may further potentiate amyloid accumulation leading to pathological changes in old age. In this paper, we specifically focused on whether high PbAc exposure at a young age stimulated vascular amyloid deposition/CAA that may further potentiate late ADRD pathogenesis. It should be noted the current US CDC reference standard for Pb exposure is much lower than before, and this study was conducted using a Pb level that represented Pb exposure in a population born before 1975. In this study, the dose of 27mg/kg gavaged daily for 8 wk produced a BLL (29.5±4.9μg/dL, ∼1.4μM) in mice that is comparable to the reference BLL (30μg/dL) suggested by the US CDC in 1975.16 In addition, we used 1μM of PbAc for the in vitro and ex vivo studies, which was comparable to the in vivo BLL, although one study suggested that it might be necessary to use higher concentrations of Pb in vitro.84 We did not observe a loss of body weight in APP mice gavaged daily with 27mg/kg Pb for 8 wk, consistent with a previous study.14 This Pb dose regimen did not induce cognitive deficits in wild-type mice.14 Further study on lifelong exposure to lower doses of Pb is required and underway.

Currently, it remains unclear how Pb exposure induces amyloid formation and pathology. In our previous mouse study, we demonstrated that PbAc exposure did not directly induce Aβ production by affecting its secretase activities on APP procession.14 Instead, PbAc influenced the transportation or clearance of Aβ through blood brain barriers. Here, our findings suggested that PbAc exposure affected vascular binding to Aβ40 and perivascular drainage of Aβ40, both of which were shown to specifically contribute to CAA formation in the APP/PS1 mouse model.85 In addition, because microglia also play a key role in CAA formation,86–88 in this study we demonstrated that after Aβ was added to the culture media, PbAc significantly inhibited intracellular levels of Aβ40, but not Aβ42, in BV2 cells, suggesting that PbAc preferentially inhibited microglial endocytosis of Aβ40 and this process was likely via the inhibition of LRP-1 by Pb, because a higher binding affinity of Aβ40 to LRP-1 than for Aβ42 was found.89 All of these mechanisms might drive the increased ratio of Aβ40/ Aβ42 that stimulate CAA formation.25 It was observed that a lack of proper LRP-1-mediated phagocytosis caused Aβ to be deposited in the vessel wall, ultimately increasing CAA.90 Taken together, our results suggest that PbAc exposure induced the ratio of Aβ40/Aβ42, potentiating a deposition of Aβ40 in cerebral blood vessels, in comparison with parenchymal amyloid formation that needs a lower ratio of Aβ40/Aβ42. It should be noted that our results differed from previous studies showing a full shift from parenchymal amyloid to CAA formation in mice with no microglial or genetically alterations.86–88 In our study, although PbAc-exposed mice exhibited a greater difference in vascular-associated amyloid deposition in PbAc-exposed vs. control, these mice still exhibited moderately higher parenchymal amyloid deposition following PbAc exposure, which might additionally support the observations that CAA enhanced parenchymal amyloid deposition.33 It is also quite possible that PbAc also affected other clearance pathways to increase overall brain Aβ levels instead of simply inhibiting a specific process to directly induce CAA formation. It is interesting to consider the possibility that CAA and parenchymal amyloid might each promote the formation of the other.91

Furthermore, to confirm and understand the mechanism underlying Pb-induced vascular associated amyloid deposition, we investigated the role of PAI-1, a potent regulator of vascular injury and inflammation in the central nervous system92 that had been implicated in cerebral amyloid deposition and AD pathogenesis.44 Initially, it was suggested that PAI-1 stimulated amyloid deposition by inhibiting plasminogen activators and downstream plasmin, ultimately resulting in reduced Aβ degradation.45,93 However, most recently, it appeared PAI-1 particularly contributed to CAA development independent of the plasmin mechanism,94 although it remained unclear how PAI-1 played such a specific role in vascular amyloid deposition. By using the Pb-APP/PS1 model to address this issue, our findings suggested that Pb, via PAI-1, enhanced binding between Aβ40 and the cerebral blood vessels and impaired perivascular drainage of Aβ40,95,96 and that both processes were responsible for CAA formation.33,85 In this study, we found that Pb-exposed mice had significantly greater PAI-1 expression in the cerebrovasculature and microglia in comparison with control mice. By using a PAI-1 inhibitor, TIP, also known as PAI-039, which was shown to inhibit CAA formation, CAA-related vascular function, and cognitive deficits without affecting parenchymal amyloid deposition in mice,50 we demonstrated that TIP-exposed mice had less Pb-associated CAA-related pathology, less parenchymal amyloid deposition, less vascular binding to Aβ40, and less perivascular drainage than those exposed to Pb alone. In addition, this compound recovered the Pb-associated impairment of Aβ40 endocytosis in microglial cells, although its underlying mechanism remains unclear. One possibility was that the inhibition of PAI-1 increased tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) that was an agonist for LRP-1 and the facilitator of Aβ endocytosis.50,93 We note that BV2 cells are not a complete replacement for in vivo microglia,97 and further in vivo evaluation of our in vitro data obtained from this cell line is necessary.

Because Aβ is deposited in both the parenchyma and vasculature, and the parenchymal amyloid contains predominately Aβ42, whereas CAA is composed primarily of Aβ40, this results in a very high Aβ40/Aβ42 ratio.25 The vascular dysfunction resulting from amyloid deposition in the cerebral vessel walls is currently considered an active player in the mechanism of neurodegeneration and a major contributor to disease pathogenesis.89,95,96,98,99 The vascular dysfunction resulting from CAA, specifically CAA-related cerebral ischemia and microhemorrhage, may directly contribute to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline.100 In APP/PS1 mice, the discrepancy between the effects of parenchymal and vascular amyloid on cognitive alteration has been described. In these observations, it appeared that CAA or vascular amyloid contributed much more to cognitive deficits.50,86 A recent paper specifically linked neuronal loss to CAA but not to parenchymal amyloid.86 In our study, although we have not seen neuronal loss at such a young age in females, we did observe less myelin in the cortex as determined by immunohistochemistry, accompanied by significantly more vascular-associated amyloid plaques and differences in measures of short-term learning and spatial recognition following Pb treatments. Demyelination has been shown to contribute to cognitive deficits.72 Here, the mitigation of Pb exposure–related vascular-associated amyloid plaque formation by TIP was associated with more myelin and better measures of short-term learning and spatial recognition. However, it should be noted that although Pb-associated myelin outcomes were accompanied by greater levels of vascular amyloid deposition and poor outcomes related to short-term learning and spatial recognition, further direct evidence is required to determine whether and how vascular amyloid, independently or dependently of parenchymal amyloid, contributes to these outcomes and possible subsequent neurodegeneration. In addition, it is also necessary to investigate whether and how observed early vascular amyloids finally develop into severe CAA pathology in older individuals. Furthermore, whether Pb-associated demyelination is reversible or eventually induces neuronal losses and cognitive deficits in AD models remains to be determined. An interesting finding is that we observed Pb-associated demyelination in the cortex but not in the hippocampus, consistent with observations that CAA was commonly associated with cortical ischemic demyelination.101 However, a further study is required to elucidate this finding.

Collectively, given the striking role of Pb exposure in vascular pathology,102 amyloid deposition,13,14 neurotoxicity, and its strong association with AD and dementia,103 we conducted this study using young female APP mice and identified that Pb exposure accelerated vascular amyloid formation in comparison with parenchymal amyloid and related poorer outcomes associated with short-term memory and spatial recognition. Our results suggest that Pb exposure resulted in a shift of parenchymal amyloid to vascular-associated amyloid plaque formation, and that this was related to PAI-1 signaling. It is notable that novel translational MRI imaging, although it is still under development and data are pilot, together with our immunohistochemical work, provided evidence that PAI-1 inhibition mitigated the Pb exposure–associated cortical demyelination, which can support future in vivo studies to gain further insight into additional mechanisms underlying the role of Pb in the pathogenesis CAA and ADRD. Thus, our findings point to how Pb exposure acts as risk factor for dementia, including both AD and VaD with CAA pathology, and the potential therapeutic implications that could result from these findings.
