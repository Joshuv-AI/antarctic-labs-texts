A systematic review of water, sanitation, and hygiene practices in developing countries: exploring the effects on public health
Authors: Etagegn Shacho, Kassahun Eba, Samuel Fekadu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLack of proper access to safe Water, Sanitation, and Hygiene (WASH) is a pervasive cause of preventable disease and mortality in developing countries, disproportionately impacting the most vulnerable portions of the population. Despite activity globally, much still needs to be accomplished regarding expansion of WASH coverage, and the issue still remains a defining public health challenge. This systematic review synthesizes available evidence to assess the current status of WASH practices, associated health outcomes, and the barriers to effective safe WASH behaviors in developing countries. .MethodThe systematic review examined WASH practice and access conditions in the developing world. Of a total of 1273 records identified via Google Scholar, Scopus, MEDLINE, Embase, and PubMed, 44 studies were incorporated that meet the inclusion criteria through a rigorous screening process according to PRISMA guidelines. Quality of studies was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2), and reviewed by three authors. RoB 2 tool was applied using five primary domains randomization, deviation from interventions, missing data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting to critically assess the methodological quality of included studies.ResultsAccording to the reviewed papers, approximately 73% of the study focused on children and newborns under the age of five, indicating their high prevalence of WASH-related illnesses. Up to 69% of households do not treat their drinking water and only one-third of caregivers practice consistent handwashing at important periods. In children aged 6–23 months, soap-based handwashing significantly lowers diarrhea and respiratory illnesses. Access to clean and safe water remains limited, with just 16–50% of populations in some countries (e.g., Bangladesh, Tanzania, Ethiopia, and Chad) having sufficient access. Improved water sources are frequently available, although household-level treatment and storage remain low (4.7%-34% in rural areas). Water availability is heavily influenced by socioeconomic factors such as income, female education, and political stability. Several countries (e.g., Nigeria, Bangladesh, Ethiopia, Kenya, and India) have low sanitation coverage, whereas others (China and South Africa) do better. Improved sanitation facilities limit pathogen exposure, improve child nutrition by reducing intestinal infections, and increase economic and environmental sustainability.ConclusionThe evaluated research shows that WASH-related illnesses disproportionately impact children under the age of five, who make up roughly three-quarters of the study population. Despite progress in upgrading water sources, access to safe and clean water remains extremely limited, particularly in low-income and politically insecure areas. Household water purification and safe storage methods are woefully inadequate, leaving households vulnerable to preventable infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12889-026-28396-9.

Introduction

The health of the individual and the community depends on water supply, sanitation, and hygiene (WASH) practices. Having access and quality clean drinking water, adequate sanitary services, and regular hygiene practices are essential for reducing the transmission of illness, enhancing general health, and encouraging the performance of health policies [1]. Globally, billions of people still do not have access to adequately managed water supply, sanitation, and hygiene services, despite tremendous advances [2]. As of 2024, an estimated 3.4 billion individuals lacked access to safely manage and proper sanitation services, while 2.1 billion lacked access to drinking water [3].

In 2024, 1.7 billion people did not have access to basic hand washing supplies, including 611 million individuals lacked access to soap and water, in their homes [3]. In 2024, approximately 74% of the global population, used a safely managed and drinking water service, representing substantial progress since 2015; however, major inequalities remain between urban and rural populations [3]. An additional 1.5 billion individuals have had access to basic services, where an improved water source was located within a 30-minute round trip [4]. The poorest populations and those in rural areas are least likely to have access to basic services, with over half of the 703 million people in 2022 were without basic drinking water in sub-Saharan Africa [4].

One of the most basic human needs and an essential necessity for health and human well-being is access to clean water, sanitary facilities, and hygienic practices (Harrold, 2025; W. UNICEF, 2020). As a result, improving WASH conditions can save 1.4 million lives annually [2].

WASH is essential for preventing cholera, typhoid, hepatitis A, and diarrheal illnesses [2, 5, 6] According to Ritchie, Spooner [7], unsafe water alone causes more than a million fatalities every year. An estimated 829,000 individuals die from diarrhea per year as a result of contaminated drinking water, poor hand hygiene, and unsafe WASH practice [8]. By addressing these issues, 395,000 teenagers under the age of five could not die each year [6]. According to Silva, Vieira [9], one of the easiest and most economical ways to prevent infectious illness is to wash your hands with soap. Additionally, better infrastructure for practice lessens the spread of illnesses like schistosomiasis, intestinal worms, and trachoma [10].

Given the serious public health risks associated with inadequate WASH, particularly for vulnerable groups like the urban poor in developing countries, systematic reviews of WASH practices in developing nations are essential [11]. Everyone agrees that having safe water and sanitation is one of the basic human right and that the Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) target 6.2 aims for everyone to have good sanitation [12], however; about two billion people still lack proper sanitation facilities, and more than 600 million people still excrete on the open, space that can spreads diseases and hinders economic growth [13].

Systematic reviews consistently indicate that WASH practice are closely linked with public health outcome [14, 15]. Numerous studies have shown that improved WASH practices are closely linked with reductions in several health problems, including infectious diseases and malnutrition [16, 17]. Evidence also shows that WASH practices can substantially reduce the occurrence of diarrheal diseases [18, 19]. For example, a meta-analysis of 136 impact evaluations reported reduction the risk of diarrheal and enteric diseases by approximately 13% [18]. Improvements in drinking water quality especially through higher-quality water sources or point-of-use filtration were associated with reductions in diarrhea risk of up to 50% [19]. Similarly, access to basic sanitation services without sewer connections was linked to a 21% decrease in diarrhea risk, while sanitation systems connected to sewers showed a 47% reduction [19]. Promotion of handwashing practices, often combined with hygiene education, contributed to about a 30% decline in diarrheal incidence [19]. Inadequate WASH conditions are also strongly associated with child undernutrition, particularly stunting among children under five years of age in several developing Asian countries [16]. Limited water supply and lack of safe drinking water increase the likelihood of stunting, while the use of unsafe groundwater further raises the risk of undernutrition outcomes such as stunting, underweight, and wasting among children [16]. Additionally, poor WASH environments facilitate the transmission of a wide range of infectious diseases, including typhoid, dysentery, and cholera, as well as neglected tropical diseases such as soil-transmitted helminth infections and trachoma [20, 21]. The goal of conducting systematic reviews in this context is to fill the knowledge gap regarding WASH conditions, particularly during major health crises like pandemics, which exacerbate pre-existing vulnerabilities [22, 23]. Inadequate WASH facilities adversely affect women and girls, who are primarily responsible for the family’s WASH requirements and face more sanitary issues because of customs and constraints from the environment. WASH facilities are crucial for preventing illness and promoting wellness [24, 25].

In developing countries, there is rapid urbanization, which has caused expansion of slum houses. These unofficial houses are known by overcrowded and insufficient WASH infrastructures, thus escalating public health risks [26]. There is shared use of water sources as well as sanitation facilities in such communities and this circumstance exacerbates their risk to diarrheal and sanitation-related stress [26, 27]. This condition highlights the urgent need for finding solution and this systematic research work on WASH practices and their health impacts identifying gaps and recommend solutions [22]. This systematic review synthesizes evidence from multiple data sources to provide a robust understanding of WASH practices in order to informing policy and looking the existed programmatic responses to improve public health in developing countries [28, 29]. For example, the strong link found between poor sanitation and child health, which underscores the need to incorporate sanitation into health promotion policies [9]. The study findings from systematic reviews can support the global agenda (Sustainable Development Goal 6.2), which aims for universal access to adequate sanitation [11]. It also reinforces and emphasizes the need for safe water and sanitation facilities and mentioning them as a fundamental human rights, thus it needs cooperative efforts for sustainable improvements [11].

Water and sanitation services are emphasized in the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs), which include key indicators for WASH. SDG 6.1.1 focuses on safely managed drinking water, SDG 6.2.1a on sanitation services, and SDG 6.2.1b on access to handwashing facilities with soap and water [30, 31]. These parameters are essential for evaluating the world’s efforts to make sure that everyone has access to clean water and sanitary facilities [31, 32]. According to the SDG goal 6, the world’s population uses of drinking WASH achievement of the world population were 74%, 58%, ad 80%, respectively [3].

Objectives of the study

The objective of this systematic review was to examine WASH practices in developing nations, access to improved WASH services, and the impact of inadequate WASH on public health. Additionally, this review also assessed challenges to achieving water and sanitation for public health.

Methods of the review

This systematic review frequently highlights vulnerable groups in developing countries as well as exposing disparities in healthcare and particular challenges seen [33]. According to Moher, Liberati [34], the study adhered to Preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses guidelines to ensure that the relevant information was included. To find relevant literatures on the study subject, the search cover the databases of Web of Science, Google scholar, PubMed, MEDLINE, Embase, and Scopus databases. The following search terms were used in the literature search: “effect of water, sanitation, and hygiene,” “impact of water, sanitation, and hygiene,” “effect of WASH,” “impact of WASH,” “effect of sanitation facilities,” “impact of sanitation facilities,” “effect of hygiene,” “impact of hygiene,” “water, sanitation, and hygiene interventions,” “improved water, sanitation, and hygiene,” “access to WASH,” “hand washing,” “hygiene,” “sanitation,” “WASH.” Only English-language studies with human subjects that were published between January 2015 and February 2026 were included. The review concentrated on papers published after the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) timeframe to include evidence generated during the SDGs era.

The rationale here was to look after the implementation of the SDGs and also laws addressing the impact of WASH on public health are relatively new in a number of developing countries. As a result, this study was used to identify current trends and practices of WASH. Our initial search yielded a total of 1273 research articles in English language, but only 44 of them selected for further analysis.

Eligibility criteria

The PECOS framework was utilized to organize the review and search process. The population (P) included households, women, children, and other vulnerable groups living in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The exposure (E) consists of water distribution, sanitation services, hygiene practices, hand washing, water treatment, and safe water storage. The Comparator (C) comprised all baseline or unimproved WASH conditions. The Outcomes (O) focused on public health indicators such as diarrhea, infectious diseases, mortality, morbidity, and related health outcomes. The study designs included cross-sectional, randomized controlled trials (RCTs), quasi-experimental, cohort, and case-control studies. Additionally, only full-text papers published in English between January 2015 and February 2026 were included in the review.

Study selection

Publication search was limited to those literatures reporting on the WASH practice and service access in LMICs, which was classified by the World Bank in 2017 from 2015 to 2026. Literatures were selected using a two-stage review process. First, the titles and abstracts of the identified publications were assessed and studies that meet this criterion were included for further assessment. Second, three reviewers (ES, KE and SF) independently assess the full text of the final list of published articles using a standard protocol.

All of the studies collected from the databases were transferred to Endnote 7, and duplicates were removed. To assess eligibility of those literatures, first the researchers independently reviewed each study’s title and their abstract. Those literatures which were including the required information either in their title and/or abstract, then the full texts were assessed.

When disagreement concerning the eligibility of a study occurs, the third author (KE) was sought and the literature included or excluded after consensus was reached. Finally, a list of eligible studies was identified; the required data were extracted from the each identified study using a standard protocol.

Data extraction

The three authors (ES, SFH and KE) extracted data independently using a prepared spreadsheet. The articles included the following information: author, year of publication, study area, research design, sample size, and outcome. The collected data were organized and shown in tabular, graphical, and textual formats, including crucial results such as the effects on public health of various types of WASH practices, the study location, and the year of publication.

Quality assessment

The risk of bias and quality of included studies were critically appraised with the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool for randomized trials (RoB 2) [35, 36]. The tool was selected because, despite various frameworks having been created, one standardized tool for quality assessment has not been developed for use solely in WASH specific quality [37].The RoB 2 tool evaluates five categories: [38] bias because of the process of randomization; bias because of deviations from those planned interventions; (3) bias due to of missing data on the outcome; (4) bias in assessing the outcome; and (5) bias in reporting the selected result. Reporting within the review was in accordance with PRISMA guidelines.

In addition to RoB 2, this systematic literature used Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) as very low, low, moderate and high quality. The GRADE quality assessment is used and help to rate the quality or certainty of evidence of the selected literature, where the definitions are present in Table 1. The approach rate the quality of evidence begins with the quality of study design (which is randomized trails grouped as high quality and observational studies as low), and then consider of five reasons to possibly downgrading the quality of study evidence [40]. The approach, GRADE, criteria for grading evidence present in the selected literature encompasses of limitation of the study, inconsistency, imprecision, publication bias, indirectness, and other issues. When certain criterion exist, the study is grouped either as serious (which leads to downgrading it by one level) or very serious (downgrading the study by two levels). The GRADE system grouped into four categories, which are high, moderate, low, and very low [39].

Literature searching was conducted using the Google scholar, EMBSE, MEDLINE, Scopus, and PubMed databases, which yielded an initial 1273 records. After removal of 361 duplicates, 912 individual records were screened for further assessment using their title and abstract and the screening eliminated 829 studies that were not relevant, review articles, unpublished theses, review articles, and letters to editors. Latter after the 83 full texts of the articles were read, a total of 39 articles were excluded in which16 articles due to outside of the developing countries list based on the 2017 world category, 18 articles due to lack of original data and 5 as they did not focus on WASH practices. Operational definitions

Developing country” refers to countries that have a lower Human Development Index (HDI) than developed countries and a less established economic base [41].

Public health refers to the science and art of avoiding disease, extending life, and promoting health via coordinated societal initiatives and well-informed decisions [42].

Improved drinking-water sources- are referring water sources which are free from external contamination, particularly free of fecal matter. An improved water sources included household water source, public standpipes, protected springs, protected dug wells, boreholes, as well as rainwater collection [43].

Unimproved water sources refers to unprotected wells, springs, and surface water bodies (e.g. river, dam or lake), water supplied by vendor, bottled water (except water used for other purposes is accessible from an improved source) and water delivered by tanker trucks [43].

Improved sanitation facilities are described as those which are effectively and hygienically protected from human waste contact. Improved sanitation includes flush or pour-flush to piped sewer system, septic tank, and ventilated-improved pit latrines, or pit latrines with slab or composting toilets [43].

Unimproved sanitation: sanitation facilities characterized by pit latrines that lacks a slab or platform, hanging latrines, and bucket latrines [44].

Results

Study characteristics of WASH practice in developing country

The design of WASH studies ranging from randomized controlled trials to cross-sectional surveys and quasi-experimental designs strongly impacts outcomes and their interpretation. For example, cluster-randomized trials and controlled before-and-after studies allow better assessment of causality, whereas cross-sectional studies mainly provide snapshots of prevalence and practices without causal inference [45, 46]. According to Table 2, WASH studies in developing nations use a variety of study designs, including cross-sectional, cluster randomized controlled trial, quasi-experimental, and intervention studies. However, more than half of the study used cross sectional study design. WASH practices all target reducing disease burden and improving health, the specific outcomes are domain-specific and influenced by socioeconomic, cultural, and infrastructural factors in developing countries [91, 92]. Understanding these characteristics and outcomes is essential for designing integrated and context-sensitive WASH interventions that achieve sustainable improvements in public health [93]. Though improving WASH facilities in developing countries help to reduce impacts of infectious diseases, most developing countries are lacking improved WASH facilities. In least developed countries, the supply of safe water is a challenge and nearly 1.2 billion people are in severe sanitation risks [94].

Assessment of access to improved WASH practices and their effectiveness in developing countries

Improved WASH facilities are essential for preventing waterborne diseases mainly diarrhea, cholera, and intestinal worms, are heavily affected vulnerable populations especially children under five. Adequate WASH access contributes to better nutritional outcomes by reducing stunting and under nutrition, as poor hygiene and sanitation are linked to malnutrition [16]. Furthermore, access to clean water and sanitation enhances economic growth and gender equality by empowering women and girls with safer environments and reduced health burdens [21].

Access to safe drinking water is vital for human health as it reduces illnesses such as cholera, diarrhea, and other waterborne diseases, which disproportionately impact children below five years age in most developing countries [28, 100]. Improved water access also supports education, especially for girls, and promotes gender equality by reducing the burden on women who often travel long distances to collect water [101]. As indicated in Table 3, access of improved water in developing nations showed variations; for instance, countries such as Bangladesh, Indonesia, Tanzania, Chad, and Ethiopia from 16 to 50% of the people have access to adequate drinking water. In these countries, more than half of the individuals have no access to clean and safe drinking water. According to the study findings, the availability of drinking water in sub-Saharan African countries (SSA) is lower than 70% and ranges 28% in Tanzania to 68% in Nigeria. This has similarity with the report of Rahut, Singh [94], where 16 countries out of 23 SSA countries had a drinking water facilities below 70%.

In developing nations such as Nigeria, Bangladesh, Benin, Ethiopia, Kenya and India had poor sanitation practice. However, some countries such as China and South Africa had better sanitation practice (Table 3).

Access of improved WASH practice and their effectiveness in developing country

Improved sanitations such as facilities utilized hygienically separate human excreta’s contact, offers numerous benefits. These facilities often involve closer access, reduced waiting times, and safer disposal of waste [102]. Improved sanitation practices are fundamental for improving public health, enhances socio-economic development, and sustainability in environment in developing countries [21].

The result confirmed the existence of variation in access and quality of basic WASH services, which is access to drinking water, sanitation, and hygiene services. In sum, it is worthwhile to indicate that having any of the WASH facilities is linked with access to other WASH practices. For example, households who have better sanitation and hygiene facility have had better drinking water supply. In the same way, having access to basic sanitation facility increased the likelihood of having access to basic drinking water and/or sanitation facilities.

According to the review result, low-income countries particularly SSA countries may not be in a position to achieve the intended SDGs goals unless a comprehensive WASH approach is implemented, that is., ensuring universally availability of the basic WASH services, mainly for rural settlers. Therefore, the SDGs goals of the UN emphasize the significance of improving universal access to basic drinking water source. However, SSA is in high risk due to a high population size increment and limited hygiene and sanitation facilities. As a result, the region is known for hygiene-related disease [103]. To meet the SDG goals not to “leave no-one behind” require prioritizing those individual in remote areas and disadvantaged people (which include children, elder, displaced people and people with disability). Decision-makers should make decision following adequate evidence on the current status of the WASH facilities [104].

The impact of inadequate WASH on public health in developing country

Poor water, sanitation, and hygiene services and practices caused a substantial challenges on the public health, which is impacting approximately one-third of the world people [21]. Globally in 2019, the existence of inadequate water, sanitation, and hygiene facilities become a cause for the death of 1.4 million people and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for 74 million [105]. In 2016 in LMICs, poor WASH practices resulted human deaths, which was estimated to be over 1.6 million and a cause for approximately 105 million DALYs, however; they could be prevented through adequate WASH services [21, 106].

In Africa, according to WHO 2019 report, WASH related diseases caused deaths for 1, 035,000 and 54,590,000 DALYs, in which 273,000 were below five years age. Acute respiratory infectious disease is linked with poor hand-hygiene. It has resulted death for 356,000, DALYs for 16. 5 million and 112,000 death from under-five year age [21].

Diarrheal diseases are the leading cause of WASH-attributable deaths and DALYs [6]. In 2016, an estimated of 829,000 WASH-attributable deaths as well as about 49.8 million DALYs were resulted from diarrheal diseases, accounting for 60% of all diarrheal related deaths [106]. Unsafe WASH practices caused 395,000 deaths and 37 million DALYs for children under five years age in 2019, of which 273,000 of these deaths were due to diarrhea [6]. Diarrheal diseases are main cause of child morbidity in developing countries [107, 108]. Unsafe WASH services were attributed to a significant portion of all diarrhea incidences, accounting for 22,768 in India [61], 6494 in Kenya [57] and 4820 in Indonesia [70] 98 in Ethiopia [53], and 133 in Ghana [71] cases (Fig. 2). Studies have consistently shown an improved water and sanitation services can reduce the probability of occurrence of child diarrhea [108]. For instance, an intervention in rural Bangladesh demonstrated a reduction in the overall prevalence of waterborne diseases, including diarrhea, from 7 to 10%, and specifically in children under five from 13 to 22% [109]. According to Gera, Shah [110], particularly hand washing practice hygiene reduce the chance of developing acute respiratory infections, cough, influenza and conjunctivitis by 24%, 10%, 50%, and 51%, respectively. However, evidence on the direct impact of hygiene interventions on mortality reduction remains limited and inconsistent.

Challenges to achieving water and sanitation for public health

Socioeconomic disparities influence access to all or part of the WASH services [21, 111]. Poverty, malnutrition, and illiteracy are major challenges to WASH implementation in Africa [21]. Households who have low economic status, less educational status, and those individuals living in rural areas have less chance to access basic WASH facilities [112]. In Pakistan, for example, from the lowest wealth category only 4.8% of households have had access to clean water sources. From the two highest wealth strata, 44% of the households were in a position to used an improved sanitation facilities [113]. The “urbanization of poverty” also exacerbates sanitation issues in densely populated areas, straining existing systems [21]. Cultural beliefs and existence of limited knowledge on practicing of improved sanitation and hygiene practices can also affected the adoption of healthy WASH behaviors. Some cultural norms lead to resistance to new infrastructure and differing attitudes towards hygienic habits, especially in rural communities [21].

Inadequate WASH infrastructure is a pervasive challenge in developing countries [111, 114]. In Africa, over 70% of rural water schemes are inoperable or intermittently functional [21, 111]. This compromises human well-being and hinders access to potable water [111]. The major challenges were poor water supply, inadequate toilet facilities, poor toilet facility emptying practices, and a lack of cooperation among different sanitation components. For instance, from the 14 studied towns in Ethiopian, the mean water shortage was nearly 35%, and 17% of households had no toilet facilities. In addition, only nearly 20% of the households had flushed toilets, and about 5% practiced in an open defecation. Many towns also lack wastewater dumping sites and have poor or fragmented collaboration among stakeholders [115]. Aged infrastructure and limited investment in WASH infrastructure further exacerbate these problems [21, 111].

Governmental and non-governmental organizations (NGOs) are actively addressing WASH challenges, but significant obstacles remain [21, 116, 117]. Their efforts were helping the local people by strengthening of their water governance using transparency, responsibility, and community participation [114]. In addition, expanding climate-resilient WASH facilities and promoting community education on hygiene programs are also crucial for enhancing of equitable access to WASH services [114]. Water Aid, an NGO, emphasizes the need for governments to declare WASH a top priority, set explicit mandates, increase funding, encourage gender-responsive approaches, and integrate WASH into health policy [21]. However, challenges persist due to poor government involvement, with many hygiene efforts spearheaded by NGOs, which impedes holistic planning and budget allocation [21]. Limited capacities at the local level and inadequate data also hinder effective WASH governance [118]. Collaboration is essential between governmental bodies, the private sector, and civil society organizations for investing in infrastructure, promoting proper hygiene practices, as well as conducting community education programs [116]. Decentralizing WASH efforts can empower communities and foster self-reliance in managing services [21]. The effectiveness of WASH initiatives depend on integrating community skills to use clean water and hand-washing practices [21, 100].

Study quality assessment and risk of bias

The risk of bias in the selected studies was assessed using the RoB2 tool. Regarding the randomization process, nearly 78% of studies demonstrated a low risk, while the remainder showed some concerns. Deviations from intended interventions carried a low risk in 80% of studies, missing outcome data in 80%, outcome measurement in 95%, and selective reporting result in 95%. Overall, about 80% of the studies were grouped as a low risk of bias (Fig. 3; Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

There is a gap in the expansion of the WASH practices in those low income countries. This review work identified 44 published articles covering the WASH practices in developing countries published from 2015 to 2025. In the study region, numerous studies were conducted and most countries give more coverage for the WASH services, however; to sufficiently achieve the 2030 SDG, the countries in developing countries should work more to maximize the effective implementation of the WASH practices by considering of the access and sustainability as a vital point.

The synthesis is based on the available evidence from observational studies. Though observational studies are highly vulnerable to internal validity than those of randomized controlled trails (RTCs), it can provide evidence. Properly conducted RTCs provide adequate evidence concerning the causal relation between intervention and an outcome. Applying RTCs on WASH practice is not simple due to its financial and ethical challenge. Thus, observational studies can be done on a wider context and different social and ecological settings [119].

The review identified important gaps seen in the WASH practices. For example, from the total included articles, all of the studies investigated the water access (n = 44), and the articles covered access to sanitation (n = 43), but those cover the hygiene was lower (n = 34).

Improved water practices in developing countries significantly enhance public health, primarily by reducing the incidence and severity of waterborne diseases, especially diarrheal illnesses that disproportionately affect children under five [120, 121]. Access to improved drinking water, particularly microbiologically safe water at the point of use, has been shown to decrease morbidity and mortality rates linked to contaminated water sources [120, 122]. Improved access to water in developing countries is influenced by socioeconomic indicators such as gross national income, female education, political stability, and control of corruption, with significant variations depending on the water source and income group analyzed [123]. Thus, in Malawi and Burkina Faso, involves prioritizing safe drinking water, implementing sound policies, securing funding, and effectively managing water resources to accommodate urban population growth while enhancing hygiene and reducing water-borne diseases [124].

Annually, approximately 1.7 million people die from diarrheal diseases, 90% of whom are children under 5 in developing countries, and 88% of these cases are linked to unsafe water, inadequate sanitation, and poor hygiene [102]. Therefore, higher rate of infection were common in the young age children from less than 8 years than those greater than 8 years age [125]. Improved sanitation, coupled with safe water and hygiene, can prevent various infectious diseases like diarrhea, malaria, dengue, and worms [94]. Moreover, increased access to sanitation can reduce child malnutrition [126]. The synthesis findings showed that diarrhea due to lack of clean water availability caused a serious death rate in most developing countries, but no strong evidence showing that the spread of disease were linked with poor hygiene.

Strength and limitation of the review

This systematic literature review produced evidences on WASH from studies conducted in developing countries using review protocol of PRISMA. The study assesses the quality of each identified study using RoB2 and GRADE quality assessment tools. The study findings can help to show the need for improved WASH policies, health education and sanitation and hygiene practices. The stud can also enable scholars, educators, and health practitioners to promote proper implementation of WASH. However, the systematic literature review access large data sources, and thus there are limitations in this study as it is covered only journal articles from limited databases. Furthermore, during the search procedure, a large number of reports from governmental and non-governmental organizations were found; however, these sources of grey literature were not included in this systematic review. To make sure methodological consistency, scientific rigor, and dependability in quality assessment, the review particularly concentrated on peer-reviewed articles. However, because research with negative or non-significant results is less likely to be published in peer-reviewed journals, the lack of grey literature may have caused publication bias.

Conclusion

This systematic examination shows that access to safe water, better sanitation, and hygiene services is still severely lacking in poor nations, where WASH practices are still quite inconsistent. Although study formats varied from cross-sectional surveys to randomized controlled trials, the majority of cross-sectional studies restrict the ability to draw conclusions about causality, even if they offer important information about public health risks and current behaviors. Vulnerable groups, especially children under five, continue to be disproportionately affected by poor WASH conditions, which raise the prevalence of infectious diseases, malnutrition, and diarrheal disorders. The critical need for integrated interventions is highlighted by the fact that many households still lack appropriate sanitation, clean drinking water, and good hygiene practices, despite progress in some areas.

Effective WASH programs have shown significant gains in child health outcomes and decreases in disease burden, particularly when they combine water, sanitation, and hygiene components. However, the primary barriers to accessibility for all continue to be socioeconomic inequality, cultural norms, poor infrastructure, and a lack of governmental ability. Implementing context-sensitive, equitable, and evidence-based WASH initiatives is crucial to achieving lasting public health improvements and achieving the UN lasting Development Goals, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa and other low-income regions. To lower health risks, enhance nutrition, and advance general well-being in developing nations, cooperation between governments, non-governmental organizations, and communities is essential, as are investments in infrastructure, education, and governance.
