Foundation models for X-ray interpretation: a narrative review of current techniques and future perspectives in diagnostic imaging
Authors: Isah Salim Ahmad, Rabiatu Bako Suleiman, Tian Yu, Rongbo Lin, Cailei Zhao, Jianxiang Liao, Benqing Wu, Yaoqin Xie, Xiaokun Liang, Haifeng Wang, Zhanqi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectiveThe scarcity of high-quality annotated data is a primary bottleneck in developing artificial intelligence (AI) for chest X-ray (CXR) interpretation. Foundation models (FMs), trained on broad datasets via self-supervision, present a transformative solution. This narrative review analyzes the current state of vision and vision-language foundation models (VLFMs) specifically for CXR, evaluating their potential to bridge research and clinical practice through a novel analytical framework.MethodsFollowing PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we conducted a narrative review of literature from 2021–2025. We introduced and applied a novel four-pillar analytical taxonomy to structure the field: (I) core model architecture; (II) training framework; (III) clinical application adaptation; and (IV) quality assurance (QA). This framework guided the synthesis of evidence from over 150 studies, enabling a structured analysis of model designs, training paradigms, adaptation strategies, and evaluation metrics.Key Content and FindingsThe four-pillar taxonomy provides a critical lens to deconstruct the FM development pipeline. Our analysis reveals distinct maturity levels across pillars: while architectural innovation and training strategies are advanced, rigorous QA and adaptation for real-world heterogeneity (e.g., portable vs. stationary imaging, device/site shifts) are underdeveloped. We identify domain-specific challenges, including bias propagation from limited public datasets, label noise in report-mined supervision, and a significant gap between retrospective benchmark performance and prospective clinical utility. Evidence-based recommendations are provided for model selection, efficient adaptation via parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT), and the implementation of stratified evaluation.ConclusionsCXR-FMs offer a path toward more generalizable and data-efficient diagnostic AI. However, successful clinical translation is contingent upon coordinated advances across all four pillars of the proposed framework, moving beyond architectural scaling to prioritize data diversity, robustness auditing, and seamless workflow integration. This taxonomy provides researchers and clinicians with an actionable framework to develop and evaluate models whose impact will be determined by demonstrated fairness, reliability, and utility in diverse clinical environments.

Introduction

Medical imaging is a cornerstone of contemporary healthcare (1), driving diagnostics, treatment planning, and disease monitoring (2). A persistent challenge in leveraging artificial intelligence (AI), particularly for chest X-ray (CXR) analysis, is the acute scarcity of high-quality, expertly annotated data (3). This bottleneck is exacerbated by privacy constraints (4), the specialized skill required for labeling, and the cost of annotation. Consequently, conventional supervised learning paradigms (4), which are fundamentally dependent on vast labeled datasets (5), face significant limitations in effectiveness and scalability within medicine.

The emergence of foundation models (FMs) presents a transformative paradigm shift (6). Trained on extensive and diverse datasets (7), FMs learn universal representations that can be efficiently adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks (8). In medical imaging, this capability offers a powerful solution to data scarcity. By leveraging large-scale (9), often unlabeled, datasets through self-supervised pre-training, FMs can achieve robust performance on specialized tasks like abnormality detection and report generation when fine-tuned on smaller, targeted datasets. However, the successful integration of FMs into clinical CXR workflows is fraught with challenges. Model performance is inextricably linked to the quality and representativeness of training data, where embedded biases can affect generalizability across populations and institutions (10,11). Furthermore, the clinical translation of these complex models is hindered by the “black-box” problem (12), raising concerns about interpretability and trust, alongside persistent issues of data privacy, rigorous validation, and seamless workflow integration (13-15).

Application of FMs in X-ray imaging

FMs are defined as models trained on broad data using self-supervision at scale and adaptable to a wide range of downstream tasks (16), a concept popularized by Stanford HAI (17). In medical imaging, FMs are developed to overcome the scarcity of labeled data by leveraging large unlabeled datasets (18). The core technical components of FMs include large-scale pretraining with architectures like convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or vision transformers (ViTs) (19), self-supervised learning (SSL) methods such as contrastive learning (20), masked image modeling (MIM), or distillation, and adaptation strategies ranging from prompt engineering and linear probing to parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) and full fine-tuning (21).

In radiology, particularly for X-ray imaging, vision-language foundation models (VLFMs) such as chest X-ray agent (CheXagent) (22) and radiology foundation model (RadFM) (23) are designed to handle CXRs by aligning images with textual radiology reports, enabling tasks like zero-shot disease classification and automated report generation (24). Vision-only FMs, such as RAD-DINO (25) and Ray-DINO (26), utilize SSL on large-scale X-ray datasets to learn general visual representations suitable for detection, segmentation, and registration without relying on paired text (27).

Challenges in developing X-ray FMs include limited access to large-scale clinical datasets, the need for high-quality image-text pairs, and ensuring robustness across different imaging devices and institutional protocols (20). Clinical integration also requires addressing explainability, fairness, bias, and regulatory concerns. FMs are positioned as transformative tools that enhance generalization (26), reduce annotation dependency, and offer versatile adaptation in X-ray-based diagnostic applications (19).

Methods

A comprehensive narrative review was conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. The objective was to synthesize the development, application, and challenges of FMs in CXR interpretation. The methodology was structured around a novel four-pillar analytical framework designed to clarify the progression from algorithmic innovation to clinical tool deployment.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion was limited to original research on vision or VLFMs primarily applied to CXR analysis. Reviews, studies focused on other modalities without a CXR component, and non-English publications were excluded.

Data extraction and synthesis

Data from included studies were extracted independently by two reviewers using a standardized form. Extracted data were organized thematically to enable a comparative analysis and narrative synthesis of trends and challenges across the reviewed literature.

A four-pillar analytical framework for FMs in CXR interpretation

The burgeoning field of FMs for CXR analysis demands a structured framework to transcend mere model cataloging and enable critical evaluation and principled design. This review proposes and applies a four-pillar analytical taxonomy encompassing core model architecture, training framework, clinical application adaptation, and QA to systematically deconstruct and assess CXR-FMs. This framework, illustrated in Figure 3, maps the journey from algorithmic conception to viable clinical tool, critically examining the interdependencies and trade-offs that ultimately dictate a model’s real-world efficacy and limitations.

Pillar 1: core model architecture—foundational choices and performance implications

The architectural backbone is a primary determinant of a model’s representational capacity, computational demands, and suitability for specific tasks.

Synthesis of reviewed evidence

Transformer-based architectures (e.g., ViT, Swin) excel at modeling long-range, global contextual relationships within an image, which is crucial for understanding holistic anatomical layout. However, this capability typically comes with high computational cost and a high demand for extensive pre-training data (28,29).

CNNs (e.g., ResNet, DenseNet), foundational to earlier models, offer strong inductive biases for local feature and texture extraction. This makes them inherently data-efficient and often more robust for tasks focused on localized abnormality detection (30). Hybrid Architectures represent a strategic synthesis, combining CNN layers for efficient local feature extraction with transformer modules for global context integration. This design aims to balance computational efficiency with comprehensive image understanding (31).

Analytical insight & author interpretation

The architectural choice is strategic, not merely technical. Our analysis indicates that models prioritizing high-resolution, pixel-wise tasks (e.g., segmentation) often leverage CNN-heavy or hybrid designs. Conversely, models focused on global image classification or semantic report generation are increasingly built upon pure transformer or hybrid backbones, benefiting from their superior capacity for integrating complex, distributed visual information (32).

Pillar 2: training framework—the data-centric determinant of generalization

The training framework defined by data foundation and pre-training objective is the most critical factor shaping a model’s robustness, bias profile, and generalization potential.

Synthesis of reviewed evidence

Data foundation

Heavy reliance on large public datasets like MIMIC-CXR and CheXpert introduces a concentration risk. Consequently, many models inherit and propagate the demographic (e.g., predominantly U.S. critical-care populations) and linguistic (English reports) biases inherent to these sources, a key factor explaining common performance degradation during external validationv (33,34).

Pre-training strategy

A clear functional dichotomy exists. Generative objectives (e.g., masked autoencoding) build a rich, intrinsic understanding of anatomical structure and normality, excelling in tasks requiring detailed reconstruction or unsupervised anomaly detection. Contrastive objectives (e.g., image-text alignment) optimize for semantic representation, enabling powerful zero-shot classification and retrieval, but can sometimes lack fine-grained spatial awareness.

Analytical insight & author interpretation

There is no universally optimal training paradigm. The choice must be application-led: contrastive strategies are well-suited for semantic/classification tasks, while generative strategies are advantageous for detailed perceptual tasks (35). Across all strategies, the quality, diversity, and representativeness of the training data remain the paramount bottleneck, directly determining the ceiling for model fairness and robustness (36).

Pillar 3: clinical application adaptation—bridging capability and clinical workflow

This pillar examines the methods and trade-offs involved in tailoring a general-purpose FM to specific clinical tasks and integrating it into practical workflows.

Synthesis of reviewed evidence

Adaptation spectrum

Techniques range from efficient but often limited prompt engineering and linear probing, to high-performance but resource-intensive full fine-tuning (which risks “catastrophic forgetting”). A strong trend toward PEFT methods like LoRA is evident, as they enable effective adaptation with minimal new parameters, preserving pre-trained knowledge (37).

Task-specific demands

The adaptation pathway is dictated by the target task. Disease classification may only require a new classifier head, while report generation necessitates complex integration with a large language model (LLM), and segmentation demands specialized pixel-level decoders (38).

Analytical insight & author interpretation

The “adaptation gap” the performance loss when moving from a pre-trained FM to a specialized clinical tool, is a critical but underreported metric. Efficient adaptation via PEFT is not merely a technical convenience but a prerequisite for sustainable, scalable deployment across diverse and resource-constrained healthcare environments.

Pillar 4: QA framework measuring what matters for clinical trust

This pillar focuses on the evaluation protocols necessary to translate technical performance into credible assessments of clinical readiness and trustworthiness.

Synthesis of reviewed evidence

Beyond aggregate metrics

While papers consistently report aggregate metrics like area under the curve (AUC), rigorous QA requires stratified evaluation across patient demographics, disease prevalence, and imaging equipment. Performance frequently degrades on external datasets or for patient subgroups underrepresented in training data, a fragility that aggregate scores mask.

The validation loop

Current literature heavily emphasizes retrospective bench testing. True clinical QA necessitates prospective real-world validation and the integration of continuous clinician feedback to identify edge cases, calibrate uncertainty, and align model outputs with clinical reasoning pathways a process poorly documented in existing FM research.

Analytical insight & author interpretation

Benchmark performance is a poor proxy for clinical utility. A rigorous QA framework, applied through this analytical lens, must mandate evaluation of fairness, robustness, and workflow integration. These elements, more than peak accuracy, are the true determinants of successful and ethical clinical translation.

Technical architecture of medical imaging FMs

FMs represent a paradigm shift in medical image analysis, defined as models trained on broad, often unlabeled datasets using self-supervision at scale and subsequently adapted to a wide range of downstream tasks. Their development is guided by scaling laws, where performance predictably improves with increased model size, dataset breadth, and computational resources. This scalability can lead to emergent properties, capabilities like enhanced contextual understanding not seen in smaller models. Originating from LLMs, this concept has expanded into computer vision, giving rise to vision foundation models (VFMs) and VLFMs. For medical imaging, FMs offer a crucial advantage: they overcome the acute scarcity of expensive, expert-annotated data by learning generalizable representations from vast unlabeled datasets (39), thereby improving generalization across diverse clinical domains and imaging modalities compared to models pre-trained on natural images (40).

The technical architecture of these models rests on three core pillars, as illustrated in a foundational figure (see Figure 4). First, large-scale pre-training employs encoder architectures like CNNs or ViTs to distill raw data into general-purpose embeddings (41). CNNs leverage inductive biases for efficient local feature extraction, while ViTs use a self-attention mechanism to model global dependencies across image patches, making them highly scalable. Second, SSL is essential, deriving supervisory signals from the data’s inherent structure instead of manual labels. SSL methods are broadly categorized into discriminative approaches, such as contrastive learning [e.g., simple framework for contrastive learning of visual representations (SimCLR)] and distillation (e.g., DINO), and generative approaches like MIM, including masked autoencoders (MAE), which learn by reconstructing masked portions of the input. For multimodal data combining vision and language, specialized SSL strategies like image-text contrastive learning (ITC) align representations across modalities (42).

The third pillar is the efficient adaptation of these pre-trained models to specific clinical tasks. Adaptation strategies exist along a spectrum of computational demand. Lightweight methods include prompt engineering (e.g., zero-shot or in-context learning) and linear probing, where only a final classifier is trained. Mid-weight approaches involve adding task-specific heads or employing PEFT techniques like LoRA or adapters, which modify only small subsets of parameters. For maximum performance, full fine-tuning updates all model weights, often enhanced by instruction tuning or reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) to align model behavior with complex clinical reasoning and human preferences. The power of FM in medical imaging stems from a synergistic pipeline: scalable architectures pre-trained on extensive data via self-supervised objectives create rich, transferable embeddings (42,43). These embeddings are then efficiently tailored to downstream tasks from diagnosis to segmentation using a flexible hierarchy of adaptation techniques. This paradigm reduces dependency on labeled data while promoting robust, generalizable AI tools for clinical application (29).

How to train an FM?

Training paradigms for medical imaging FMs: generative and contrastive pre-training

The construction of a robust FM for radiographic interpretation hinges on the strategic selection and implementation of pre-training paradigms. These paradigms enable the model to learn transferable, semantically rich representations from vast, often unlabeled, collections of medical images. Two dominant and complementary strategies are generative pre-training and contrastive pre-training. Each approaches the learning objective from a distinct perspective: one learns by synthesizing data, the other by discriminating between data points.

Generative pre-training: learning the data manifold through reconstruction

Generative pre-training operates on the principle of teaching a model the underlying data distribution by tasking it with reconstructing or generating coherent data samples. This forces the model to internalize crucial anatomical and pathological features, fostering strong generalization capabilities essential for downstream diagnostic tasks, as shown in Figure 5.

Auto-regressive modeling

Inspired by successes in natural language processing [e.g., generative pre-trained transformer (GPT)], this approach sequences image patches (e.g., in raster order) and trains the model to predict the next patch conditioned on all previous ones. For X-rays, this teaches the model the spatial dependencies and contextual relationships between anatomical structures, building a coherent understanding of the image as a structured whole.

Masked autoencoding

This powerful technique, akin to bidirectional encoder representations from transformers (BERT) in natural language processing (NLP), involves randomly masking a significant portion (e.g., 60–80%) of input image patches. The model is then trained to recover the original signal from the remaining context. In radiology, this compels the model to develop a profound understanding of normal anatomy and its variations, as it must infer missing content based on global context, a skill directly analogous to identifying anomalies.

Probabilistic & diffusion-based methods

Variational autoencoders (VAEs) learn a latent distribution, enabling not only reconstruction but also controlled generation and anomaly detection by measuring the likelihood of a given scan. Denoising diffusion probabilistic models (DDPMs) represent the state-of-the-art, learning to iteratively denoise a corrupted image back to its original state. This process captures highly detailed data distributions. Crucially, text-conditioned diffusion models can generate clinically relevant X-ray images guided by radiographic reports or findings, effectively learning a bidirectional alignment between imaging features and textual descriptions.

Contrastive pre-training: learning by semantic alignment and discrimination

In contrast to generative methods, contrastive pre-training focuses on learning representations by bringing semantically similar data points closer in an embedding space while pushing dissimilar ones apart. This paradigm excels at learning invariance to irrelevant perturbations and aligning data across different modalities or views, as shown in Figure 6.

Frameworks for contrastive pre-training for chest X-ray analysis. This schematic illustrates the core contrastive learning paradigms used to train foundation models on chest radiographs. The principle trains a model to semantically cluster similar data while separating dissimilar samples within a learned representation space. (A) Image-image contrastive learning: a single chest X-ray and its augmented view (e.g., rotated, cropped) form a positive pair, teaching the model invariant recognition of thoracic anatomy. A radiograph from a different anatomical domain (e.g., a hand X-ray, shown as a generic example of a dissimilar image) acts as a deliberate negative pair. This contrast sharpens the model’s ability to extract features unique to chest pathology, ensuring it distinguishes relevant thoracic patterns from irrelevant visual information. (B) Vision-language contrastive learning: this framework aligns medical imaging with textual data. A chest X-ray is embedded close to its corresponding radiological text report (positive pair) and pushed apart from an unrelated report (negative pair). This process builds a unified semantic space where visual findings are directly linked to clinical language, enabling models to ground image features in descriptive diagnostics and generate preliminary impressions.

Image-image contrastive learning

Techniques like SimCLR generate positive pairs by applying different data augmentations (e.g., random cropping, rotation, intensity adjustment) to the same X-ray. The model is trained to recognize that these augmented views represent the same underlying scan, learning features that are invariant to these non-diagnostic transformations. Methods like BYOL achieve similar goals using only positive pairs through a siamese network architecture with a momentum encoder.

Vision-language contrastive learning

This is particularly potent for medical imaging. Models like contrastive language-image pre-training (CLIP) are trained on large datasets of image-text pairs (e.g., X-rays and their corresponding radiology reports). The model learns a shared embedding space where a CXR showing pneumonia is embedded close to the text “consolidation” or “opacification”, and far from an unrelated report. Specialized derivatives like MedCLIP enhance this by constructing semantically meaningful positive pairs across patients based on shared clinical concepts or label metadata, strengthening the model’s grasp of biomedical semantics.

Synthesis for FM training

An effective FM for medical imaging often benefits from a hybrid or multi-stage pre-training strategy that leverages the strengths of both paradigms. For instance, a model may first undergo generative pre-training (e.g., via masked autoencoding) to build a comprehensive low-level and anatomical feature library. This is followed by contrastive pre-training (e.g., vision-language alignment on reports) to refine these features into semantically organized representations that align closely with clinical reasoning. This combined approach yields an FM that possesses both a deep generative understanding of radiographic structure and a discriminative ability to link visual patterns to clinical language, making it exceptionally adaptable to tasks like classification, retrieval, report generation, and zero-shot anomaly detection.

Radiology

Radiological imaging employs various modalities, each providing distinct data essential for clinical diagnosis. Among these, plain radiography, particularly X-ray imaging, is a foundational technique (44,45). X-rays utilize ionizing radiation to produce two-dimensional projectional images, which are critical for the initial assessment and detection of conditions such as fractures (46), infections, and thoracic abnormalities. This modality remains a cornerstone of diagnostic imaging due to its accessibility, speed, and clinical utility (47).

In clinical practice, the output of these imaging studies is typically a narrative radiology report. These reports offer a rich, expert interpretation that details observations, diagnoses, severity assessments, and comparisons with prior examinations. This coupling of extensive visual data with descriptive, albeit unstructured, text renders radiology an ideal domain for VLFMs. However, the heterogeneity in reporting styles, terminology, and clinical detail across institutions presents significant challenges for automated processing and model training (48).

Early advances in radiology models

Relative to other medical fields like pathology, the development of FM in radiology has progressed more gradually. Initial approaches predominantly utilized transformer architectures (29) and CNN-transformer hybrids for tasks such as automated report generation (40) and image segmentation. While pioneering, these models often lacked scalable SSL frameworks (21). Subsequent research shifted towards contrastive vision-language pretraining to better align medical images with their textual descriptions. Early SSL efforts, such as Contrastive Learning of Medical Visual Representations from Paired Images and Text (ConVIRT) (49) and global and local representation alignment (GLoRIA) (50), focused on improving global and local semantic alignment between images and reports. Later adaptations like Bio-ViLT incorporated temporal dynamics, yet these methods remained constrained by dataset scale compared to contemporary FM (42). Generalist VLFMs

This category emphasizes scalability across multiple medical imaging domains, including radiology, pathology, and ophthalmology. Models like PubMed-CLIP, PMC-CLIP, BiomedCLIP, and MEDVInT leverage large-scale, paired datasets from sources such as PubMed to perform multimodal tasks including disease classification, image-text retrieval, and medical visual question answering (Med-VQA). Instruction-tuned models like LLaVA-Med and Med-Flamingo further specialize in Med-VQA. While evaluated on radiological data, these models are not radiology-specific and typically process 3D volumes [computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)] as 2D slices, foregoing crucial spatial context (60).

CXR VLFMs

CXR analysis has emerged as a particularly active subdomain due to the modality’s clinical ubiquity, availability of large public datasets (61), and reduced computational complexity relative to 3D imaging. A central challenge is procuring large, high-quality paired image-text datasets (62). To address data scarcity, models like MedCLIP employ semantic matching on unpaired data. Other strategies focus on enhancing data quality (63): CheXzero uses contrastive learning on report “impressions”, CXR-CLIP employs radiologist-designed prompts, and KAD integrates external medical knowledge graphs. Frameworks like UniChest and MedKLIP improve generalization through novel training paradigms that isolate dataset-specific biases and enable fine-grained alignment. Recent models, including ELIXR and MAIRA-1/2, utilize parameter-efficient adapters to align visual encoders with LLMs for report generation (40). A significant shift is exemplified by Google’s CheXAgent, which leverages millions of instruction-image-answer triplets, demonstrating the power of large-scale, instruction-tuned learning for clinical tasks (60). Extending to 3D modalities

Applying FMs to inherently 3D modalities like CT and MRI presents distinct challenges. Early attempts, such as CLIP-LUNG, processed 3D scans as 2D slices, losing vital spatial information. Subsequent models like M3FM and MedBLIP began incorporating 3D patches but did not fully exploit volumetric context (63). A notable step was RadFM, which processes full 3D volumes and was trained on a large multimodal dataset, though its capabilities in dense prediction tasks like segmentation were limited (23). Recent models, including M3D-LaMed and SAT, have made strides in 3D medical image segmentation. Specialized CLIP extensions for 3D data, such as Rad-CLIP and CT-CLIP, and comprehensive frameworks like Merlin, which integrates EHR data, highlight ongoing progress. However, the development of a truly generalist, fully 3D-aware FM for radiology remains an open area of research (64).

VFMs

While VLFMs dominate report-related tasks, VFMs are gaining traction for tasks requiring precise spatial understanding, such as detection and segmentation. Early 3D approaches used multi-view or sequential processing but were limited. Modern VFMs often employ advanced SSL (65). Knowledge distillation techniques, as seen in DeSD and SMIT, and graph-based SSL methods like those in LVM-Med, have shown promise (64). The adaptation of frameworks like DINOv2 to radiology, exemplified by RADDINO and RayDINO, a large-scale VFM trained on nearly one million X-rays demonstrates strong performance in classification and segmentation without textual supervision. Other advances include VISION-MAE for masked autoencoding on 3D data and cancer-specific models trained on 3D CT volumes. The adaptation of the segment anything model (SAM) to medical imagery has also been widely explored, revealing both its versatility and the challenges of domain transfer (66).

Concluding remarks

The development of FMs in radiology, while progressing, has not matched the pace set in pathology. Early progress was led by vision-language models for classification and report generation (29). Current trends highlight two parallel paths: the scaling of data and model size (e.g., CheXAgent) and the refinement of training techniques to maximize information from available datasets (62). Simultaneously, vision-only models are being actively developed to address spatial tasks like segmentation, offering an alternative that circumvents the need for large, curated text-image pairs (63).

Key challenges persist, including the scarcity of large-scale, high-quality 3D datasets, the computational demands of 3D model training, and the need for models that seamlessly integrate volumetric reasoning with clinical language understanding (64). The future likely lies in a synergistic approach, leveraging the strengths of both vision-only and vision-language paradigms. Continued research focused on scalable 3D architectures, efficient multimodal alignment, and robust clinical validation is essential to translate these foundational advances into reliable tools for clinical workflows.

Critical analysis of adaptation, reproducibility, and benchmarking challenges

The emergence of FMs presents a dual reality: unprecedented capability alongside significant challenges to scientific openness and reproducible progress. This tension is acute in medical AI. While open-source models like LlaMA (91) and SAM (27) enable community validation and iteration, the most capable models, such as GPT-3 and GPT-4, are often accessible only as opaque services via commercial application programming interfaces (APIs). More critically, their massive, curated training datasets are almost never released. This creates a profound reproducibility crisis; without access to the data, it is impossible to audit for harmful biases, understand failure modes, or independently verify performance claims, a situation ethically untenable for clinical tools (64). This opacity entrenches a resource barrier, concentrating advanced FM development within well-funded entities and limiting broader academic scrutiny and innovation.

In response, PEFT methods like low-rank adaptation (LoRA) and prompt engineering have become essential for adapting large, frozen FMs to specific clinical tasks with limited labeled data (81). However, this adaptation process itself reveals limitations. Performance is highly sensitive to the quality and bias of the small adaptation dataset. Furthermore, studies often report only aggregate metrics on standard benchmarks, failing to document performance degradation on rare conditions, specific patient subgroups, or images from unseen institutions critical limitations for clinical deployment.

Benchmarking, data biases, and the illusion of generalization

Benchmarking is the primary method for evaluating CXR-FMs, yet standard practices often obscure more than they reveal. Common metrics, AUC, sensitivity, specificity, provide a necessary but insufficient view of performance (46). A critical shortcoming in the literature is the frequent omission of subgroup analysis. A model may achieve a high aggregate AUC on the CheXpert (92) benchmark yet fail systematically on pediatric cases, portable radiographs, or manifestations of tuberculosis, which are underrepresented in dominant training sets like MIMIC-CXR (93).

The quality and composition of the benchmark dataset itself are the most significant determinants of reported performance. “High quality” must extend beyond size and the presence of expert labels (94) to encompass representational fairness and pathological diversity. As detailed in our analysis (Table 4, Figure 7), public datasets exhibit severe demographic and linguistic skews. Models trained predominantly on data from U.S. intensive care units (MIMIC-CXR) will inevitably encode those patients’ age, disease prevalence, and even reporting style, degrading performance in outpatient or global health settings. This is not a theoretical concern; studies have documented performance drops of >15% in AUC when models are validated on external, geographically distinct datasets (95).

Consequently, the pursuit of higher benchmark scores on biased datasets can be counterproductive. The field requires a shift toward more meaningful benchmarking that includes:

Evaluation metrics for CXR-FMs: a framework for critical analysis

The rigorous evaluation of CXR-FMs requires a multifaceted approach, employing distinct metric categories tailored to specific downstream tasks. Crucially, the interpretation of any metric is contingent upon a clear specification of the experimental context. This includes the dataset version and split (e.g., MIMIC-CXR v2.0 test set), the label source and handling of uncertainty (e.g., CheXpert competition labels), the adaptation method employed (e.g., zero-shot, linear probing, or full fine-tuning) (46), and the evaluation protocol (e.g., image-level vs. patient-level aggregation). Direct comparison of reported values across different studies is highly problematic without accounting for these methodological differences, which can lead to invalid conclusions in the absence of standardized benchmarks (93). Within a defined context, these metrics serve as the essential empirical tools for this review, enabling the comparative analysis of model capabilities, the identification of performance limitations, and the assessment of translational progress (96).

Classification and detection metrics

For discriminative tasks such as disease classification, metrics must account for dataset imbalance. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) remains the standard for evaluating a model’s overall ranking and discrimination ability between classes (93). For imbalanced conditions, such as the detection of rare pathologies, the area under the precision-recall curve (AUPRC) provides a more informative measure of performance where the positive class is of primary interest. Complementary metrics include F1-scores, available in Macro-F1 (which gives equal weight to each class, critical for evaluating performance on rare conditions) and Micro-F1 (which aggregates contributions across all labels, providing an overall measure for multi-label settings) (97). Fairness metrics

Fairness metrics evaluate whether a diagnostic AI model performs equitably across patient subgroups defined by attributes such as age, gender, race, and institutional provenance. By measuring performance disparities such as differences in AUC, F1-score, or false negative rates, these metrics are essential for auditing the real-world reliability and safety of a model. Their critical application in this review is to assess potential biases in CXR-FMs, many of which are trained on public datasets (e.g., MIMIC-CXR) that may not represent global patient diversity. Disparities revealed by these metrics directly challenge a model’s generalizability and clinical readiness (101).

Report generation

Automated report generation in CXR-FMs is evaluated using metrics adapted from machine translation, such as the bilingual evaluation understudy (BLEU), recall-oriented understudy for gisting evaluation (ROUGE), and Consensus-based Image Description Evaluation (CIDEr). These n-gram-based scores quantify the lexical and syntactical similarity between AI-generated impressions and reference radiology reports. While useful for benchmarking the fluency and thematic coverage of models like ELIXR or CheXagent, they are limited proxies for clinical accuracy, factual correctness, and diagnostic priority. Therefore, in line with rigorous QA, these automated scores must be supplemented by expert clinical review (102).

Methodological recommendations for development and adaptation: bridging strategy and evidence

Addressing these challenges necessitates informed choices in the FM pipeline, guided by a critical reading of performance evidence:

❖ For pre-training: the optimal strategy depends on the task and data. Contrastive (e.g., DINOv2) or MIM (MAE) pretraining is highly effective for learning robust, general-purpose visual features from large collections of unlabeled or weakly labeled images, bypassing text noise. When the goal is semantic tasks like zero-shot classification or report generation, contrastive vision-language pretraining (CLIP-style) is necessary but must be applied to carefully curated image-report pairs to mitigate label noise; studies like MedCLIP demonstrate that meticulous pair construction leads to measurable gains in zero-shot accuracy over methods using noisy automatic pairings (113).

The translational pathway: from validation to clinical integration

Future progress hinges on a paradigm shift from reporting benchmark scores to ensuring reproducible, clinically relevant validation.

❖ Confronting the reproducibility crisis: many high-performing models, especially commercial ones, are inaccessible “black boxes” with undisclosed training data. This makes independent auditing for harmful biases and verification of performance claims impossible, and an ethically untenable situation for clinical tools (115). The field must prioritize open-source models and data.

Discussion

The systematic analysis conducted through our four-pillar taxonomy reveals a distinct and uneven landscape in the development of FMs for CXR interpretation. This structured perspective allows us to move beyond broad generalizations to identify specific maturity levels, critical interdependencies, and CXR-specific gaps that must be addressed for clinical translation.

Pillar 1 (core architecture) and Pillar 2 (training framework) represent the most mature areas of research. The architectural evolution from CNNs to hybrids and ViTs is well-documented, as is the superiority of large-scale, in-domain self-supervised pre-training. However, our synthesis identifies a critical CXR-specific gap: the frequent neglect of 3D context. Models trained on 2D slices or projections fail to leverage the volumetric nature of thoracic anatomy, a limitation that hinders performance in tasks like localization of lesions across views. Furthermore, the dominance of a few large public datasets (e.g., MIMIC-CXR) within the training framework has created a concentration risk, where models inherit and propagate demographic and acquisition biases, directly explaining common failures in external validation.

Pillar 3 (clinical application adaptation) is rapidly advancing, with PEFT emerging as a best practice. Yet, the field often overlooks the “adaptation gap” the performance drop from pre-training to fine-tuned clinical task as a key metric. A CXR-specific challenge here is the adaptation for portable versus stationary radiographs, which have fundamentally different technical characteristics and disease prevalences, requiring tailored adaptation strategies rarely discussed in current literature.

Pillar 4 (QA) is the most underdeveloped. While aggregate metrics are universally reported, our analysis shows a severe deficit in rigorous, stratified evaluation. There is a pronounced lack of benchmarking across different scanner types, patient subgroups, and clinical settings. Consequently, the robustness of models to real-world distribution shifts remains largely unproven. The pathway to clinical trust requires this pillar to evolve from retrospective metric reporting to a framework encompassing prospective validation, uncertainty quantification, and continuous monitoring for performance drift.

In conclusion, the taxonomy exposes a misalignment in progress: significant investment in architectural and training scale (Pillars 1 & 2) is undercut by immature and inconsistent validation practices (Pillar 4). The path forward requires a rebalancing of effort, where advances in data diversity and efficient adaptation (Pillars 2 & 3) are rigorously stress-tested within a CXR-specific QA framework that prioritizes fairness and real-world robustness.
