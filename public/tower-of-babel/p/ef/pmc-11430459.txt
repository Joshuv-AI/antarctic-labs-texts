A neural circuit for alcohol withdrawal–induced hyperalgesia in a nondependent state
Authors: Xin-Lu Yang, Wei Gao, Wan-Ying Dong, Changjian Zheng, Sheng Wang, Hong-Rui Wei, Yanli Luo, Zhi Zhang, Yongquan Chen, Yan Jin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Alcohol use disorder is highly prevalent worldwide, with characteristically severe pain sensitivity during withdrawal. Here, we established a mouse model of hyperalgesia during ethanol withdrawal (EW) before addiction to investigate the window for onset and underlying mechanisms. Viral tracing with in vivo microendoscopic and two-photon calcium imaging identified a circuit pathway from dorsal hippocampal CA1 glutamatergic neurons (dCA1Glu) to anterior cingulate cortex glutamatergic neurons (ACCGlu) activated in EW mice with hyperalgesia. Chemogenetic inhibition of this pathway can alleviate hyperalgesia in EW mice, whereas artificial activation recapitulates EW-induced hyperalgesia in naïve mice. These findings demonstrate that the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu neuronal pathway participates in driving EW-induced hyperalgesia before ethanol dependence in mice.

INTRODUCTION

Alcohol consumption is known to have complex and contradictory effects on pain, and although excessive alcohol consumption has been associated with the onset and severity of a variety of pain conditions (1–3), acute alcohol treatment produces analgesia (4). Previous studies of alcohol-associated hyperalgesia have generally focused on alcohol dependence state. For example, clinical investigations and animal studies have shown that people with alcohol use disorder and rodents with chronic ethanol exposure display heightened pain sensitivity during alcohol withdrawal (5, 6). Individuals with alcohol use disorder commonly resume consumption to relieve withdrawal-associated pain, increasing the risk of further relapse (7–11); however, although a few studies reported that withdrawal-induced hyperalgesia occurs before the development of alcohol dependence statement (12–14), the underlying neural mechanisms of this phenomenon remain unclear.

Previous studies have shown that both acute and chronic alcohol exposure produce progressive neural maladaptive alterations that influence the activity of various brain regions (e.g., cortex, striatum, and hippocampus), which, in turn, drive disorders such as alcohol dependence, withdrawal syndromes, and relapse (15–17). Neural activity of the hippocampus, which is a key component of the limbic system (18), is known to be highly sensitive to alcohol exposure and withdrawal (19). Using magnetic resonance imaging, one previous study showed that higher glutamate markers are associated with lower gray matter volumes in the hippocampus of alcoholics in early abstinence (20). In addition, studies suggest that the CA1 undergoes dynamic changes in neural activity at different stages of alcohol exposure (21, 22). However, whether and how the CA1 region may be involved in the process of alcohol withdrawal–induced hyperalgesia remains unknown.

In the present study, we established a murine model of nondependent ethanol withdrawal (EW)–induced hyperalgesia to investigate the neural circuit mechanisms of pain sensitization associated with ethanol cessation. Through in vivo fiber photometry, two-photon and microendoscopic calcium imaging, in vitro electrophysiology recording, viral tracing, and other standard chemogenetic methods, we found that EW leads to decreased mechanical pain thresholds in mice from short-term ethanol consumption before the formation of dependence. In this pathological state, glutamatergic neurons in the dorsal hippocampal CA1 (dCA1Glu) project to and activate glutamatergic neurons in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACCGlu). Moreover, chemogenetic inhibition of this pathway could alleviate pain sensitization caused by EW. Our results thus uncover a dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway that participates in the development of hyperalgesia in the period following short-term alcohol consumption before alcohol dependence.

RESULTS

EW-induced hyperalgesia in mice under a nondependent state

The methods that previously used to establish mouse models of alcohol dependence were through chronic intermittent ethanol exposure, in which animals received at least four repeated cycles of ethanol vapor exposure for 1 month (on average) or by ad libitum consumption of liquid ethanol for at least 3 weeks (23, 24). Thus, to explore the effects of withdrawal from short-term alcohol consumption before dependence on pain sensitivity in mice, we shortened the exposure time and established a murine model of continuous ethanol consumption by allowing mice to freely consume 20% ethanol for 2 weeks with a 24-hour withdrawal period every 6 days, which was a modified form of the method for model establishment described by the previous studies (12, 13) (Fig. 1A). To avoid the social communication of pain via visual or olfaction cues in the present study, we kept the mice in an exhaust-ventilated closed-system cage rack feeding system and housed in two separate experimental spaces for measurement of ethanol consumption. At 24 hours after the second withdrawal from ethanol consumption, we found that mice in the EW group (EW mice) had significantly lower mechanical thresholds than vehicle-treated mice upon stimulation of the right hindpaws with a series of von Frey filaments (Fig. 1B). By contrast, there was no significant difference between EW mice and controls in thermal thresholds assessed by Hargreaves tests (Fig. 1C). The reduction in mechanical thresholds we observed during EW could persist for as long as 3 days. In addition, no changes were observed in mechanical or thermal thresholds in the vehicle control mice (Fig. 1, B and C).

As anxiety has been usually identified as a phenotypic disorder characteristic of alcohol withdrawal that indicates alcohol dependence in humans (25). We then tested whether withdrawal from 2 weeks of ethanol consumption was also accompanied by anxiety-like emotional disorders using open field test (OFT) and elevated plus maze (EPM) tests. The results showed no significant differences between vehicle-treated and EW mice in either the frequency of entries to the center area or the distance traveled in OFT tests (Fig. 1, D and E) nor any obvious differences in frequency of entries or time spent in the open arms in EPM tests (Fig. 1, F and G). To further test whether EW mice developed alcohol dependence or remained in a nonaddicted state, we conducted preference tests by providing two bottles, containing 20% ethanol in tap water versus water or 3% saccharin solution versus water, to both groups of mice after 24 hours of water deprivation during the second withdrawal period following 2 weeks of ethanol consumption. The results showed that both EW and vehicle control mice showed a high preference for saccharin and very low consumption of ethanol, while we found no differences in the intake of saccharin or ethanol between EW and vehicle mice (Fig. 1, H and I), suggesting that EW mice retained a high preference for saccharin comparable to that of control mice, displaying no obvious preference for ethanol after 2 weeks of continuous ethanol consumption. In addition, we also quantified ethanol consumption in mice under this paradigm and found that ethanol intake did not change over 2 weeks (Fig. 1J), whereas increased alcohol intake over time is a known hallmark of alcohol dependence (26).

We also tested the plasma ethanol concentrations by collecting the tail vein blood of EW mice and found that there were no differences in plasma ethanol concentrations immediately after the first and second withdrawals, suggesting that the plasma ethanol concentration remained in a relatively stable state after continuous ethanol exposure (Fig. 1K). In addition, the plasma ethanol concentration gradually decreased at 2 hours, being significantly lower than immediately after the second withdrawal, and could not be detected 8 hours after the second EW (Fig. 1K). Moreover, we found that the EW mice exhibited no differences in the growth trend in the body weight compared with the vehicle mice (Fig. 1L), suggesting that this 2-week continuous ethanol exposure in the current study did not affect the health status of the mice. These results collectively suggested that withdrawal from 2 weeks of ethanol consumption could induce pain sensitization, but not ethanol dependence, in mice.

dCA1Glu neuronal activity is enhanced in EW mice

As a subregion of the limbic system, the hippocampus, especially but not exclusively the dorsal CA1, is a sensitive target for alcohol in the brain and may be involved in diseases related to acute or chronic alcohol consumption or withdrawal (27–31). To investigate whether and how the dCA1 may play a role in EW-induced hyperalgesia, we first examined c-Fos expression in this region through immunofluorescence staining, which revealed a robust increase in c-Fos signals in the dCA1 of EW mice compared to that in vehicle mice (fig. S1A); furthermore, the c-Fos signal mainly colocalized with signal from glutamate antibody (fig. S1B). To test the neuronal excitability of dCA1Glu neurons, we conducted in vitro electrophysiological recordings in brain slices of control and EW transgenic Thy1–yellow fluorescent protein (YFP) mice (Fig. 2A). Compared to the vehicle mice, dCA1Glu neuronal activity of EW mice was significantly elevated, with increased firing rate and decreased rheobase of current-evoked action potentials (Fig. 2, B and C, and fig. S1C) and no obvious change in resting membrane potential (fig. S1D). In addition, we also conducted microendoscopic experiments to visually observe dCA1Glu neuronal activity in vivo by injecting adeno-associated virus (AAV)–calcium- and calmodulin-dependent protein kinase IIα (CaMKIIα)–calmodulin-based genetically encoded fluorescent calcium indicator (GCaMP) 6m into the dCA1 of EW and vehicle control C57 mice (Fig. 2, D and E, and movie S1). In line with our above data, the calcium transient frequency and intensity of dCA1Glu neurons were significantly higher in EW mice than controls (Fig. 2, F to H). These results together revealed that dCA1Glu neuronal activity is increased during withdrawal from short-term ethanol consumption before the formation of dependence in mice.

To further explore how dCA1Glu neurons participate in pain sensitization, we used a chemogenetic inhibitory virus [AAV-CaMKIIα–human M4 muscarinic DREDD receptor coupled Gi (hM4Di)–mCherry] and its ligand clozapine-N-oxide (CNO) to selectively inhibit dCA1Glu neurons in EW and vehicle control mice (Fig. 2I). Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings showed that resting membrane potential was significantly hyperpolarized in hM4Di-mCherry–expressing dCA1Glu neurons after bathing in CNO (10 μM), thus confirming virus expression in dCA1Glu neurons (fig. S2, A to C). Behavioral tests showed that mechanical thresholds were significantly higher in EW mice after chemogenetic inhibition of dCA1Glu neurons by intraperitoneal (ip) injection of CNO compared to EW mice infected with a control virus in the dCA1, while chemogenetic inhibition of dCA1Glu neurons had no effects on the mechanical thresholds in hM4Di-vehicle mice compared with mCherry-vehicle mice (Fig. 2J).

In addition, we also chemogenetically activated the unilateral dCA1 of naïve C57 mice by microinjection of hM3Dq-expressing virus (AAV-CaMKIIα-hM3Dq-mCherry) (Fig. 2K and fig. S2, D to F). In these naïve mice, specific activation of dCA1Glu neurons led to reduced mechanical thresholds of contralateral hindpaws (Fig. 2L), with no change in the total distance of OFT tests (fig. S2, G to I), compared to that of mice infected with control virus. Further, according to the Racine scale, which is widely used to evaluate seizure severity (32, 33), we looked into epileptoid behaviors in behavioral recordings, such as mouth and facial movements, head nodding, forelimb clonus, rearing, and falling, whereas almost no epileptoid behaviors were observed in mice during chemogenetic activation of dCA1Glu neurons (movies S2 and S3). These results supported that dCA1Glu neurons indeed contribute to hyperalgesia associated with EW in mice.

An excitatory dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway

To identify the downstream projection regions of dCA1 neurons, we injected an AAV–CaMKIIα–enhanced YFP (EYFP) virus into the dCA1 of C57 mice (Fig. 3, A and B, and fig. S3A). Three weeks later, we detected fibers in several brain regions with a strong EYFP-expressing signal, such as the ACC, lateral mediodorsal thalamic nucleus, angular thalamic nucleus, and ventral CA1, while observing very few or no EYFP-expressing fibers in the periaqueductal gray and the primary somatosensory cortex (Fig. 3C and fig. S3, B and C). As the ACC is known to integrate sensory and affective components of pain, we next dissected the anatomical structure of this potentially distinct dCA1 → ACC pathway. To this end, we used an anterograde monosynaptic tracking strategy in which AAV-hSyn-Cre virus was injected into the dCA1 and AAV–DIO–enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP) virus was injected into the ACC of naïve C57 mice (Fig. 3D). At 3 weeks after injection, EGFP-expressing neurons could be observed in the ACC (Fig. 3E) and were predominantly colocalized with glutamate antibody (Fig. 3F). These results thus verified the presence of a direct dCA1 → ACC pathway.

Next, we used a monosynaptic rabies–based retrograde tracing system to identify the targets of dCA1Glu projections in the ACC. In this experiment, Cre-dependent helper viruses [AAV–elongation factor-1α (EF1α)–DIO–tumor virus receptor A (TVA)–GFP and AAV-EF1α-DIO–rabies virus glycoprotein (RVG)] were infused into the ACC of CaMK2-Cre mice (which specifically express Cre recombinase in glutamatergic neurons), while the rabies virus (RV) EnvA-pseudotyped RV-ΔG-DsRed was injected into the same site 3 weeks later (Fig. 3, G and H, and fig. S4A) so that helper viruses could facilitate retrograde RV transmission across monosynapses. After virus expression, we observed abundant DsRed-expressing neurons in the dCA1 (Fig. 3I), as well as the secondary motor cortex, locus coeruleus, the contralateral ACC, and some subregions of the thalamus (fig. S4, B and C). In addition, immunofluorescence staining showed that DsRed-labeled neurons in the dCA1 colocalized with signals from glutamate antibody, but not from γ-aminobutyric acid (GABA) antibody (Fig. 3, I and J). These results suggested that dCA1Glu neurons project to ACCGlu neurons.

To examine the functional connections of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway, we performed optogenetic experiments in which AAV-DIO–channelrhodopsin-2 (ChR2)–mCherry was injected into the dCA1, while AAV-DIO-EYFP was injected into the ACC of CaMK2-Cre mice (Fig. 3, K and L). Electrophysiological recordings in brain slices showed that brief light stimulations of ChR2-containing dCA1Glu terminals in the ACC could reliably elicit excitatory postsynaptic currents (EPSCs) in the ACCGlu neurons and that these EPSCs could be eliminated by administration of the AMPA receptor antagonist, 6,7-dinitroquinoxaline-2,3(1H,4H)-dione (DNQX; 20 μM) (Fig. 3, M and N). These data thus demonstrated that dCA1Glu neurons send functional, monosynaptic, excitatory glutamatergic projections to ACCGlu neurons.

Hyperactivity of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway in EW mice

To visually observe and quantify the activity of dCA1Glu projections to the ACC, we conducted whole-cell patch-clamp recordings in C57 mice injected with a retro-AAV–CaMKIIα–EGFP virus in the ACC. The results showed that the firing rate of dCA1Glu neurons was elevated, while the rheobase of current-evoked action potentials was decreased in EW mice compared to that in vehicle controls at 24 hours after the second EW (fig. S5, A to E). In addition, in vivo microendoscopic calcium imaging in C57 mice given an ACC injection with a retro-AAV-CaMKIIα-Cre virus and a dCA1 injection with an AAV-DIO-GCaMP6m virus showed that the calcium transient events and intensity of dCA1Glu neurons were both significantly higher in EW mice compared to the corresponding neurons of vehicle mice at 24 hours after the second EW (fig. S5, F to K). Together, these results indicated that dCA1Glu neurons innervating the ACC show increased activity during withdrawal from short-term ethanol consumption before the formation of dependence in mice.

In view of the above findings, we hypothesized that if ACC neurons are innervated by dCA1Glu neurons in an excitatory state, then increased innervation of this pathway during withdrawal from short-term ethanol consumption should lead to increased excitability of ACC neurons. Immunofluorescence staining showed that c-Fos expression was significantly higher in the ACC of EW mice than in the ACC of vehicle control animals (fig. S6, A and B). In addition, in vitro electrophysiological recordings in brain slices from EW and vehicle-treated Thy1-YFP mice revealed that the activity of ACCGlu neurons was enhanced, as indicated by increased firing rate and decreased rheobase of current-evoked action potentials relative to that in vehicle mice (fig. S6, C to F). To visualize changes in ACCGlu neuronal activity at the single-cell level, we performed in vivo two-photon calcium imaging, which revealed that calcium signal intensity of detection events observed in GCaMP6m-labeled ACCGlu neurons was increased significantly of EW mice at 24 hours after the second EW, compared that with vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 4, A to E).

To examine sensitivity to mechanical stimuli in ACCGlu neurons following withdrawal from continuous short-term ethanol consumption, we then applied in vivo fiber photometry to record calcium signals in freely moving C57 mice expressing AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m in the ACC to determine whether ACCGlu neurons were sensitized to subthreshold stimuli (Fig. 4, F and G). The results showed that calcium signals in ACCGlu neurons increased rapidly following 0.07 g of von Frey stimuli to the right hindpaws of EW mice at 24 hours after the second EW (Fig. 4, H to J). To then determine the effects of mechanical stimuli on ACCGlu neuronal activity at the single-cell level, we conducted in vivo microendoscopy recordings of calcium transient frequency and intensity of ACCGlu neurons of freely moving mice injected with an AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m virus into the ACC (Fig. 4, K and L). The results showed that both calcium signal events and intensity of GCaMP6m-labeled ACCGlu neurons of EW mice significantly increased at 24 hours after the second EW, compared that with vehicle-treated mice (Fig. 4, M to O). Notably, this calcium activity rapidly increased in response to 0.07 g of von Frey stimuli to the contralateral hindpaws in EW mice at 24 hours after the second EW, while it was not found in vehicle mice (Fig. 4, P to S, fig. S7, and movies S4 and S5). Collectively, these results suggested that the sensitized ACCGlu neurons contribute to the effect of withdrawal from short-term ethanol consumption before the development of dependence in mice.

Functional role of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway in EW-induced hyperalgesia mice under a nondependent state

In light of this evidence showing enhanced inputs in the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway of mice with EW-associated hyperalgesia, we next examined the influence of excitatory dCA1Glu inputs on ACCGlu neuronal hyperactivity in EW mice. By contrast, in vivo two-photon calcium imaging indicated that the increased fluorescence intensity in GCaMP6m-labeled ACCGlu neurons could be suppressed by selective inhibition of dCA1Glu neuronal activity in EW mice, but not in EW mice infused with the control virus (Fig. 5, A to D). Further, in EW Thy1-YFP mice with selective inhibition of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway by microinjection of CNO into the ACC (Fig. 5, E and F), the increased excitability of ACCGlu neurons was significantly abolished (Fig. 5, G to I). Meanwhile, behavioral tests showed that specific chemogenetic silencing of this pathway activity also alleviated the hyperalgesia in EW mice at 24 hours after the second EW, as indicated by an increase in mechanical thresholds (Fig. 5J). Chemogenetic silencing of this pathway had no effects on the mechanical thresholds in vehicle control mice (Fig. 5J).

In addition, in vivo two-photon calcium imaging indicated that chemogenetic activation of dCA1Glu neurons could increase the fluorescence intensity in ACCGlu neurons of naïve C57 mice (Fig. 5, K to M). At the same time, we found that microinjection of CNO into the ACC to activate dCA1Glu terminals in the ACC resulted in decreasing mechanical thresholds in naïve C57 mice (Fig. 5, N and O). Together, these cumulative results demonstrated that excitation of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway plays a causal role in the development of withdrawal-induced hyperalgesia in mice before ethanol dependence.

The dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway controls prolonged hyperalgesia in EW mice under a nondependent state

Given that the reduced mechanical thresholds we observed during the EW period could persist for up to 3 days, we also examined the possible role of this dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway in the development of prolonged hyperalgesia associated with EW. For this purpose, we recorded dCA1Glu neuronal activity at 72 hours after the second EW. Whole-cell recordings of ACC-projecting dCA1Glu neurons in brain slices, visualized by ACC infusion with retro-AAV–CaMKIIα–EGFP (Fig. 6A), revealed a significant increase in firing rate with concomitant decrease in the rheobase of current-evoked action potentials in EW C57 mice (Fig. 6, B to D). To obtain stable imaging data of this specific circuit, we performed in vivo microendoscopic recordings after the second EW following injection of a retro-AAV–CaMKIIα–Cre virus into the ACC and AAV-DIO-GCaMP6m virus into the dCA1 of mice (Fig. 6E). We found that the calcium transient frequency and intensity of dCA1Glu neurons specifically projecting to the ACC were significantly enhanced at 72 hours after the second EW (Fig. 6, F to H).

In view of the above findings, we examined whether the heightened activity of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway, driven by this increased dCA1Glu neuronal activity, is required to maintain hyperalgesia during the EW period. To test this possibility, we conducted microendoscopic recordings at 72 hours after the second EW in EW and vehicle control mice with AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m infused into the ACC (Fig. 6I) and found that both the calcium transient frequency and intensity of ACCGlu neurons were enhanced in these EW mice (Fig. 6, J to L). Further, these recordings, with concurrent nociceptive threshold tests, showed a robust increase in calcium signal frequency and intensity of ACCGlu neurons at 72 hours after the second EW following 0.07 g of von Frey stimuli to the contralateral hindpaws of EW mice, while this was not observed in vehicle control animals (Fig. 6, M to P, fig. S8, and movies S6 and S7). We also noted that chemogenetic inhibition of dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway activity by CNO injection in the ACC could partially restore mechanical thresholds in EW mice at 72 hours after the second EW (Fig. 6, Q and R). Together, these results suggested that enhanced activity of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway plays a role in regulating the prolonged hyperalgesia associated with EW in mice under a nondependent state.

DISCUSSION

This study defines a dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway through which hyperalgesia is induced by withdrawal from short-term ethanol consumption in nondependent mice. Central to this process is a circuit mechanism involving aberrantly high inputs from dCA1Glu neurons to ACCGlu neurons during EW.

Alcohol withdrawal syndromes comprise a range of physical and psychological disorders, especially hyperalgesia (34), which is reportedly an important indicator of relapse in clinical (11, 35). Previous studies have commonly focused on withdrawal-induced hyperalgesia caused by chronic alcohol exposure in the addiction phase (5, 34, 36, 37), but few studies have investigated the underlying neural circuit mechanisms and critical time window for inducing pain sensitization during the cessation from short-term alcohol consumption in the nondependent stage. In this study, we established a mouse model of withdrawal-induced hyperalgesia through 2 weeks of short-term alcohol consumption before the development of alcohol dependence. Note that we could already observe an increase in pain sensitivity during withdrawal even at this early stage, which is similar to previous findings in which mice and voles developed hyperalgesia after withdrawal from 2 to 3 weeks of alcohol consumption (12–14). Here, on the basis of the “two-bottle paradigm,” as reported in these previous studies (12–14), we used a “one-bottle paradigm” in which mice were allowed to drink voluntarily. Anxiety-like behavioral assays, blood ethanol measurements, and ethanol preference tests in mice experiencing a second alcohol withdrawal together confirmed that this alcohol withdrawal–induced hyperalgesia could occur early in addiction development in the nondependent stage. As reported in other animal studies, ethanol resumption can restore withdrawal-associated hyperalgesia, in turn, increasing the risk of further relapse (12, 38). Hence, a clear understanding of the fundamental neural mechanisms responsible for withdrawal-induced hyperalgesia before alcohol dependence may eventually lead to the development of strategies for treating or even preventing alcohol use disorder.

The hippocampus is well known to be susceptible to the effects of alcohol (39–44). However, the function of the hippocampus in alcohol withdrawal–induced hyperalgesia has remained unclear. Results in our current study demonstrate that dCA1Glu neuronal activity remarkably increases upon withdrawal from short-term alcohol consumption before the development of alcohol dependence in mice and that chemogenetic inhibition of these excited neurons can alleviate the hyperalgesia resulting from this increased activity. Although previous studies have shown that the hippocampus plays an important role in the formation and maintenance of pain (39–41), the hippocampus is not a typical region for processing pain information in the brain. The analgesic effects achieved by silencing dCA1Glu neuron activity likely involved other brain regions, leading us to uncover projections from dCA1Glu neurons to the ACC, a critical area for integrating sensory and affective pain components (44), during withdrawal-induced hyperalgesia in the present study. Here, we found that enhanced excitatory activity of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu neural pathway obviously contributes to the development of mechanical hyperalgesia in the period after alcohol consumption, whereas selective inhibition of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway can alleviate this hyperalgesia resulting from EW, suggesting that the hippocampus can regulate the response to nociceptive stimuli in mice with EW-induced hyperalgesia. Similarly, selective activation of the hippocampus also results in hyperactivity of ACC neurons, inducing pain sensitization to nociceptive stimuli even in naïve mice. However, these findings do not imply the presence of nociception-encoding neurons in the hippocampus, nor do they explain whether the hippocampus is involved in directly encoding nociceptive processes in other nonclassical types of pain. In addition, further investigation is necessary to determine whether the hippocampus innervates other neural circuits that participate in alcohol withdrawal–induced hyperalgesia.

The ACC is a critical area wherein nociceptive information is received from the medial thalamus and somatosensory center (45–47) and transmitted to a wide range of brain regions (48–50). A growing body of studies has reported that inhibiting ACC neuronal activity could alleviate pain sensitivity under a variety of pain conditions, such as inflammatory pain, neuropathic pain, and cancer pain (45, 51, 52). Previous studies have also reported that the ACC is not only involved in regulating EW-induced hyperalgesia in rodent models but also contributes to olfaction-induced socially transferred hyperalgesia (44). Here, our findings further demonstrate that enhanced dCA1Glu activity drives an increase in ACCGlu neuronal activity, subsequently promoting the development of hyperalgesia during withdrawal from short-term ethanol consumption, before ethanol dependence, in mice. Moreover, we also found that selective chemogenetic silencing of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway did not affect nociceptive mechanical thresholds in vehicle control mice, indicating that inactivation of this neural circuit does not interfere with the signal processing of external nociceptive stimuli under physiological conditions, at least in our study. We thus propose that this dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway selectively mediates hyperalgesia in response to EW under a nondependent state. However, further investigation is necessary to determine whether other neural circuits may participate in alcohol withdrawal–induced hyperalgesia before the development of alcohol dependence.

In summary, our study illustrates the functional role of the dCA1Glu → ACCGlu pathway in promoting withdrawal-induced hyperalgesia following short-term ethanol consumption, and this physiological link between nondependent alcohol consumption and pain sensitization thus adds to a growing repertoire of intricate, network interactions among different neural systems in the brain that collectively mediate the development of alcohol dependence. As the current clinical treatments for alcohol use disorder remain unsatisfactory, this study can guide further development of targeted interventions, which may ultimately lead to improved strategies for prevention of alcohol dependence, especially among patients in the early stages.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

C57BL/6J and CaMK2-Cre male mice weighing 20 ± 5 g were purchased from the Jackson Laboratories at 8 to 12 weeks of age. Thy1-YFP mouse lines were gifts from J. N. Zhou. All animals were kept in the exhaust-ventilated closed-system cage rack feeding system (Optimice System, Animal Care Systems, USA) with an independent ventilation system. The mice were group-housed with five per cage in a colony in a stable environment (22 ± 2°C ambient temperature and 50 ± 10% humidity) unless a cannula, optic fiber or gradient index (GRIN) lens was implanted. They were maintained under a 12-hour light-dark cycle (lights on from 7:00 to 19:00) and were able to access to food and water ad libitum. The animals were acclimated to the housing conditions at least 5 days before the experiments initiated. The experiments were carried out in line with ARRIVE guidelines and Ethics Guidelines of Animal Care and Use in the University of Science and Technology of China. All mouse experiments were approved by the Animal Care Committee of the University of Science and Technology of China (IACUC permit: USTCACUC24120123041).

Ethanol exposure

The one-bottle paradigm used to establish the mouse model of EW in the nondependent state was modified from the two-bottle paradigm described in previous studies (12). In this paradigm, mice treated with ethanol exposure were given only one bottle containing 20% (v/v) ethanol, diluted from 200 proof anhydrous ethanol (Merck, Rahway, NJ, USA), in 3% saccharin solution (Macklin, Shanghai, China) for 2 weeks. Vehicle control mice were supplied with one bottle containing 3% saccharin control solution. After 6-day interval of continuous ethanol availability, the solutions were replaced with tap water for 24 hours as a withdrawal period, with two withdrawal periods during the 14-day experimental course.

Measurement of ethanol consumption

Mice were placed in polycarbonate cages (25 cm by 15 cm by 10 cm) at least 24 hours before presentation of 20% (v/v) ethanol drinking solution or 3% saccharin control solution and given free access to food along the top of the cage in the rooms for modeling. The mice that received vehicle solution were housed in a different room from the mice that received the ethanol during the experiment of ethanol consumption measurement. The bottles were weighed 24 hours after the ethanol or vehicle was given. To control for potential spills during handling or evaporation, the average value of daily “drops” (loss of fluid in the cage with no animal present) was subtracted from individual fluid intakes. The mice were weighed before every ethanol drinking session to calculate the grams of ethanol intake per kilogram of body weight.

Ethanol preference

To assess ethanol preference, during the second withdrawal period, mice were deprived of water for 24 hours and then exposed to one bottle of 20% ethanol or 3% saccharin solution and one bottle of tap water for 2 hours in the experimental room with bottle positions switched after 1 hour. The weight of the fluid in each bottle was measured. The amounts (in weight) of ethanol or saccharin (Wt) and water (Ww) consumed were used to calculate relative ethanol preference for each mouse as follows: 100% × [Wt / (Wt + Ww)].

Ethanol concentration

The tail vein blood of mice was collected in the corresponding time points. The samples were centrifuged at 2000g for 15 min at 4°C, and then the supernatant was stored at −80°C until assayed. Then, the plasma ethanol concentration was tested by the Ethanol Assay Kit (MAK076, Sigma-Aldrich) in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations.

Pain testing

von Frey filament test

Mice were placed individually in a Plexiglas chamber on the top of a wire grid floor and allowed to habituate to the environment and experimenter for 30 min each day at least 2 days before testing. To precisely assess changes in mechanical nociceptive thresholds, we applied a range of von Frey stimuli (0.02, 0.04, 0.07, 0.16, 0.4, 0.6, and 1.0 g) to the hindpaws of mice, which we expected would refine the threshold of that determined by Deuis et al. (53) (0.4, 0.6, 1.0, 1.4, and 2.0 g). Mechanical thresholds were recorded upon elicitation of hindpaw withdrawal in three measurements, with a minimum 5-min interval between each test.

Hargreaves test

Mice were assessed for thermal hypersensitivity as described as previous study (51). Mice were placed in transparent plastic chambers on a glass plate maintained at a constant temperature (30°C) and allowed to acclimatize for at least 30 min each day at least 2 days before testing. The nociceptive heat sensitivity was measured by focusing a beam of light on the plantar surface of the paw using the Hargreaves apparatus (IITC Life Science, USA). The latency to paw withdrawal from the heat source was recorded as paw withdrawal latency, where a maximal cutoff time of 20 s was used to prevent potential tissue damage. Measurements were taken three times with an interval of at least 5 min between two tests.

Anxiety-like behavioral tests

The OFT and the EPM test routinely used to assess general locomotor activity and anxiety-like behaviors of rodents (54, 55). For all behavioral tests, mice were transported from the holding to the experimental room and habituated approximately 2 hours before tests. Dim light (~20 lux) was used to minimize the anxiety of the animals. The behavioral setups were cleaned with 75% ethanol and clean water after each test to remove olfactory cues.

OFT test

Mice were gently placed in the center of an open field apparatus that consisted of a square area (25 cm by 25 cm) and a marginal area (50 cm by 50 cm by 25 cm). They were allowed to freely explore the arena. The movement trajectories of mice were recorded by camera for 6 min. “Frequence to center,” which represents the frequency of entries into the center area, and “total distance,” representing the total distance traveled by each mouse within the apparatus, were both calculated using EthoVision XT 14 software.

EPM test

The EPM consists of two open arms (30 cm by 6 cm by 0.3 cm), two closed arms (30 cm by 6 cm by 20 cm), and a central platform (6 cm by 6 cm). The apparatus was elevated above the floor (100 cm), and the mice were placed on the central platform facing one of the open arms. The mice were allowed to freely explore the maze for 6 min, and their movement trajectories were recorded by camera. “Time in open arms” and “frequence to open arms,” representing the time spent in and the frequency of entries to the open arms of the apparatus, were calculated using EthoVision XT 14 software.

Virus and trace injection

For stereotactic surgeries, the mice were anesthetized using isoflurane with oxygen (3% for induction and 1.5 to 2% for maintenance) and placed in a stereotactic apparatus (RWD Life Science Inc., China). A heating pad was used to maintain the core temperature at 36°C. A volume of different viruses varied from 100 to 300 nl were injected into brain regions depending on the viral titer and expression potential at a rate of 30 nl/min using a microsyringe pump (UMP3, World Precision Instruments, USA). The pipette remained in the injection site for 12 min at the end of infusion to avoid virus overflow. All viruses were purchased from BrainVTA (Wuhan, China). The coordinates of stereotaxic injection were measured in anterior-posterior (AP) from bregma, medial-lateral (ML) from the midline, and dorsal-ventral (DV) from the pial surface of the brain (dCA1: ML = −1.0 mm, AP = −1.5 mm, DV = −1.55 mm; ACC: ML = −0.35 mm, AP = +0.25 mm, DV = −1.40 mm).

For monosynaptic anterograde tracing, recombination AAV (rAAV)–hSyn-EGFP-P2A-Cre–woodchuck hepatitis virus posttranscriptional regulatory element (WPRE)–poly(A) (pA) (AAV-hSyn-Cre; AAV2/1, 1 × 1013 vg/ml, 300 nl) was injected into the dCA1 of C57 mice to allow the virus to drive Cre-dependent transgene expression in the postsynaptic neurons. Simultaneously, rAAV-EF1α-DIO-EGFP-WPRE-pA (AAV-DIO-GFP; AAV2/9, 1.95 × 1012 vg/ml, 300 nl) was injected into the ACC. After 3 weeks, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (20 mg/kg, ip), and brain slices (40 μm) were harvested for tracing the EGFP signal or costaining with glutamate-specific or GABA-specific antibodies in the ACC.

For retrograde monosynaptic tracing, a 200-nl volume of helper viruses containing rAAV-EF1α-DIO-H2B-EGFP-T2A-TVA-WPRE-hGH-pA (AAV-DIO-RVG; AAV2/9, 4.59 × 1012 vg/ml) and rAAV-EF1α-DIO-RVG-WPRE-pA (AAV-DIO-EGFP; AAV2/9, 5.56 × 1012 vg/ml) (1:2) were injected into the ACC of CaMK2-Cre mice. Three weeks later, 300 nl of RV-EnvA-ΔG-DsRed [2 × 108 infectious units (IFU)/ml] was injected into the ACC at the same site. Seven days after the last injection, mice were anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (20 mg/kg, ip), and brain slices (40 μm) were harvested for tracing the DsRed signal or costaining with glutamate-specific or GABA-specific antibodies in the dCA1.

For chemogenetic manipulation, a 300-nl volume of the rAAV-EF1α-CaMKIIα-hM4D(Gi)-mCherry-WPRE-pA (AAV-CaMKIIα-hM4Di-mCherry; AAV2/9, 3.69 × 1013 vg/ml) or the rAAV-EF1α-CaMKIIα-hM3D(Gq)-mCherry-WPRE-pA (AAV-CaMKIIα-hM3Dq-mCherry; AAV2/9, 2.69 × 1013 vg/ml) virus was injected into unilateral dCA1 of C57 mice. Three weeks later, CNO (5 mg/kg, ip; Sigma-Aldrich) was given 1 hour before the behavioral tests and electrophysiological recordings. rAAV-EF1α-CaMKIIα-mCherry-WPRE-pA (AAV-CaMKIIα-mCherry; AAV2/9, 8.93 × 1012 vg/ml) was used as the control. For intra-ACC microinjection, a guide cannula (O.D.0.41 mm-27 G/M3.5, RWD, Shenzhen, China) was bilaterally implanted above ACC site. CNO (5 μM) diluted in standard artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF; 300 nl) was infused bilaterally into the ACC for more than 2 min through the cannula in CaMKIIα-hM4Di–treated or CaMKIIα-hM3Dq–treated mice.

For optogenetic manipulation, rAAV-EF1α-CaMKIIα-hChR2(H134R)-mCherry-WPRE-pA (AAV-CaMKIIα-ChR2-mCherry; AAV2/9, 1.63 × 1013 vg/ml, 300 nl) was used for optogenetic activation. Three weeks later, for electrophysiological recordings, blue laser light (470 nm, 20-ms duration, 5 and 10 Hz) was delivered on brain slice containing the dCA1 or ACC, and then the membrane potential and EPSCs of glutamate neurons were recorded.

In vivo fiber optic calcium recordings

Mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane and fixed in a stereotactic frame (RWD, Shenzhen, China). A 200-nl volume of rAAV-EF1a-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m-WPRE-pA (AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m; AAV2/9, 2.53 × 1012 vg/ml) was injected into the ACC of C57 mice for induction of calcium indicator expression 2 weeks before the optic fiber (diameter of 200 μm; Newdoon; 0.2 mm above the site of virus injection) implantation. While optical fiber recordings were performed using multichannel fiber photometry device (INPERC1-3C, Inper Ltd., Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China) 5 to 7 days after the implantation. A core numerical aperture 0.48 patch cords (Inper Ltd) were connected to the optic fiber cannula implanted in mice, and then the mice were placed on a wire mesh grid individually in a Plexiglas chamber to adapt the testing environment for 30 min. A 470-nm laser was delivered by an indicator of DA sensor (DA4.4) for measuring the activity of glutamate neurons. The values of calcium transients change (ΔF/F) from −3 to 10 s (0 s represents the onset of each stimulus) were calculated as (F − F0)/F0 for each trial, where F is the GCaMP6m signal in each image and F0 was defined as the mean of the GCaMP6m signal before von Frey stimulus initiation subtracted by autofluorescence background. Typical traces were formed using a custom MATLAB script that was developed by ThinkerTech.

In vivo two-photon calcium imaging

Cranial window surgery

Mice were anesthetized with 2.5% isoflurane and fixed in a stereotactic frame. Mice were provided with the carprofen [10 mg/kg, subcutaneously (sc)], dexamethasone (20 mg/kg, sc), and enrofloxacin (125 mg/kg, ip) before surgery and every 24 hours for 4 days after surgery. For the dCA1 → ACC pathway, 200 nl of AAV-CaMKIIα-hM4Di-mCherry was injected into the dCA1 unilaterally, and 250 nl of AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m was injected into the ACC ipsilaterally in C57 mice. A 3-mm by 3-mm piece of skull above the ACC was removed, and then this area was covered with 1.2% agarose; afterward, a round cover glass (3 mm; Bellco Glass Inc.) was cemented to the skull. Custom-designed aluminum head bar was glued to the skull with dental cement and 3 M Vetbond.

Two-photon imaging

Imaging was performed using an upright two-photon microscope (FV30S-SW, Olympus) at a frame rate of 512 pixels by 512 pixels for 200 circles in the x-y plane 3 weeks after cranial window surgery. Live images were acquired using a macro water-immersion objective (×25/0.8 numerical aperture) equipped with an infrared laser (excitation wavelengths of 800 nm). The typical average power (20 to 30 mW) was used for imaging GCaMP6m-expressing neurons in the ACC.

Data processing and analysis

Time-series data were exported to ImageJ for further analysis. Imaging data were corrected for mechanical drift using TurboReg. Sequential images were used to generate time-lapse videos. These corrected data were analyzed using an open-source routine, which is available via GitHub (https://github.com/flatironinstitute/CaImAn-MATLAB). Briefly, the calcium image was segmented into individual cell using a MATLAB script, and component extraction was performed according to prespecified descriptions of the spatial footprint (location and shape) and the activity trajectory of the source. Traces of ΔF/F versus time were automatically generated for each GCaMP6m-expressing neuron.

Microendoscope imaging and data processing

For microendoscopy experiments, AAV-CaMKIIα-GCaMP6m was delivered into the dCA1 (200 nl) or ACC (200 nl) of C57 mice. To record dCA1 neurons that specifically project to the ACC, retro-AAV-CaMKIIα-Cre was delivered into the ACC (200 nl), and AAV-DIO-GCaMP6m was delivered into the dCA1 (200 nl) ipsilaterally of C57 mice. After virus injection, microendoscopic GRIN lenses (4.1 mm in length and 0.5 mm in diameter; Inscopix, USA) were implanted into the dCA1 or ACC of the anesthetized mice. After GRIN lens implantation, mice were allowed to recover for at least 3 weeks. Before data acquisition, mice were briefly anesthetized with 3% isoflurane, and an integrated miniature fluorescence microscope (Inscopix, USA) was attached to the baseplate. The mice were allowed to acclimate to the weight of the microscope and rectangular recording chamber during three daily 15-min sessions a day. Imaging data were collected at 20 Hz for 15 min, and the mice were allowed to move freely.

For data analysis, fluorescence videos were processed offline by the Inscopix data processing software. First, the original data were 2× temporally downsampled, and motion was corrected using default settings. The fluorescence signals were then normalized to the time-averaged mean (ΔF/F calculation), the normalized movie was then used for semiautomatic extraction of the calcium fluorescent signals associated with individual cell based on the principal and independent component analysis. On the basis of ΔF/F and the image (cell morphology), the regions of interest identified by principal and independent component analysis were visually selected as the candidate cells. The somatic calcium events were identified by spike inference and calcium values above the automatically calculated threshold. To calculate the z-score, identical neurons were identified across all imaging sessions using a cell registration method (56). The method used a probabilistic approach to determining whether pairs of neighboring cells from different sessions are the same entity, which is based on the centroid distance and the spatial correlation. After cell registration, ΔF/F values of each neuron across all sessions were exported and further standardized as z-score using the mean and SD in baseline session: z-score = (ΔF/F − mean)/SD. To examine neuronal activity after von Frey stimulation, neuronal calcium transients were normalized to z-scores for the designated periods. Specifically, the moment of von Frey stimulation was defined as 0 s, and the data were normalized using −5 to 0 s as the baseline. z-scores were calculated for the period before (−5 to 0 s) and after (0 to 10 s) von Frey stimulation.

In vitro electrophysiological recordings

Brain slice preparation

The anesthetized mice were perfused through the left ventricle with ice-cold oxygenated N-methyl-d-glucamine (NMDG)–based ACSF (NMDG ACSF), which contained 20 mM Hepes buffer, 93 mM NMDG, 25 mM glucose, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 30 mM NaHCO3, 0.5 mM CaCl2, 5 mM Na-ascorbate, 10 mM MgSO4, 3 mM Na-pyruvate, 3 mM glutathione, and 2 mM thiourea (pH 7.3 to 7.4; osmolarity, 300 to 305 mosM). The brain was then extracted and sectioned into coronal slices (300 μm) using a vibrating microtome system (VT1200s, Leica). All brain slices (including dCA1 and ACC) were initially incubated in oxygenated NMDG ACSF at 33°C for 10 min and then recovered in oxygenated Hepes-buffered ACSF at 28°C for at least 1 hour, which contained 2.5 mM KCl, 92 mM NaCl, 2 mM CaCl2, 1.2 mM NaH2PO4, 2 mM CaCl2, 30 mM NaHCO3, 3 mM Na-pyruvate, 5 mM Na-ascorbate, 20 mM Hepes, 2 mM MgSO4, 25 mM glucose, 2 mM thiourea, and 3 mM glutathione (pH 7.3 to 7.4; osmolarity, 300 to 310 mosM). Brain slices were subsequently transferred into a slice chamber (Warner Instruments) for electrophysiological recording and continuously perfused with oxygenated standard ACSF at 32°C, which contained 3 mM KCl, 20 mM NaHCO3, 129 mM NaCl, 2.4 mM CaCl2, 1.3 mM MgSO4, 3 mM Hepes, 1.2 mM KH2PO4, and 10 mM glucose (pH 7.3 to 7.4; osmolarity, 300 to 310 mosM).

Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings

An infrared–differential interference contrast microscope (BX51WI, Olympus, Japan) equipped with fluorescent fittings was used to visualize neurons in dCA1 or ACC slices. mCherry- or GFP-labeled neurons were identified under a water immersion objective (×40) microscope during whole-cell patch-clamp recording. The recordings were performed by using patch pipettes (5 to 8 megohms) that filled with potassium gluconate–based internal solution, containing 130 mM K-gluconate, 5 mM KCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 10 mM Hepes, 0.6 mM EGTA, 0.3 mM Na–guanosine 5′-triphosphate, and 2 mM Mg–adenosine 5′-triphosphate (pH 7.2; osmolality, 285 to 290 mosm) and pulled from borosilicate glass capillaries (VitalSense Scientific Instruments Co. Ltd., Wuhan, China) with an outer diameter of 1.5 mm on a four-stage horizontal puller (P-1000, Sutter Instruments, USA). The current-evoked firing was recorded in current-clamp mode, and the action potential threshold current was defined as the minimum current to induce an action potential.

Light-evoked response

Light-evoked action potentials were recorded while the ChR2-expressing dCA1Glu neurons activated by a blue laser light (473 nm, 10 V, 20 ms) through an optical fiber positioned 0.2 mm above the brain slices containing dCA1. To verify the synaptic functionality of dCA1Glu → ACCGlu neurons, light-evoked EPSCs were recorded in ACCGlu neurons with a holding potential of −70 mV while photostimulating (473 nm, 20 ms, 5/10 Hz) ChR2+ dCA1Glu terminals in the ACC. The AMPA receptor antagonist DNQX (20 μM) was used to verify monosynaptic excitatory glutamatergic projections. The signals were recorded by a patch-clamp amplifier (MultiClamp 700B Amplifier, Digidata 1440A analog-to-digital converter, USA) and pClamp 10.7 software (Axon Instruments/Molecular Devices, USA), and analyzed using Clampfit software version 10.7 (Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA).

Immunofluorescence

Mice were deeply anesthetized with pentobarbital sodium (20 mg/kg, ip) and then perfused with 0.9% saline, followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. The brains were removed and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde in phosphate-buffered saline at 4°C overnight and then incubated in 20 and 30% sucrose solution overnight for dehydration. Coronal slices (40 μm) were cut on a cryostat microtome system (CM1860, Leica). For immunofluorescence, the sections with dCA1 or ACC were incubated with blocking buffer (0.5% Triton X-100 and 10% normal donkey serum in phosphate-buffered saline) for 1 hour at room temperature and then treated with primary antibodies, including anti–c-Fos (1:500; rabbit, Synaptic Systems), anti-glutamate (1:200; mouse, Sigma-Aldrich), and anti-GABA (1:500; rabbit, Sigma-Aldrich) with 0.3% Triton X-100 and donkey serum at 4°C for overnight, followed by corresponding fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Invitrogen) for 2 hours at room temperature. Fluorescence signals were visualized using a Zeiss LSM880 microscope.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 8.0 (CA, USA) was used for statistical analysis and graphing. The data are expressed as the means ± SEM. Histograms and QQ plots were used to assess whether the data conformed to a normal distribution. The unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test and nest t test were used for comparisons between two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni test was used for multiple comparisons. Otherwise, the nonnormally distributed data were analyzed by a Mann-Whitney U test. Statistical significance was accepted at P < 0.05.
