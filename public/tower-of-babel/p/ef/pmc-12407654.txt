AoVps18 regulates sporulation, trap morphogenesis, and nematode predation by modulating vacuole assembly and attractant synthesis in Arthrobotrys oligospora
Authors: Meichen Zhu, Yankun Liu, Yi Chen, Qiyan Hu, Dake Zhao, Wenjie Wang, Jinkui Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVacuoles are essential organelles in eukaryotic cells, playing key roles in cellular homeostasis through nutrient sensing, osmoregulation, and autophagy. In filamentous fungi, vacuole dynamics are crucial for mycelial growth, stress response, and pathogenicity. The vacuolar functions and their regulation in nematode-trapping (NT) fungi remain poorly understood. Here, we characterized a vacuolar protein sorting (Vps) protein Vps18 (AoVps18) in a typical NT fungus Arthrobotrys oligospora, which is required for the proper regulation of mycelial growth, trap formation, and sporulation. Through integrated phenotypic and molecular analyses, we established that AoVps18 physically interacts with core mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling components (AoSte12 and AoFus3) to coordinate predation-related cellular processes, including vacuole assembly, mitochondrial dynamics, and lipid droplet accumulation. Notably, we identified a TGAAAC regulatory motif in the Aovps18 promoter, suggesting direct transcriptional control by the MAPK effector, AoSte12. RNA sequencing and metabolomics further revealed that AoVps18 is involved in regulating multiple cellular processes and synthesizing compounds critical for the chemotaxis of nematodes toward A. oligospora. Overall, these findings elucidate the regulatory mechanisms by which AoVps18 coordinates vacuolar function with trap morphogenesis and mycelial growth in NT fungi, advancing both the fundamental understanding of Vps proteins regulation and potential biocontrol applications against plant-parasitic nematodes.KEYWORDS: Vacuolar protein sorting protein, vacuole assembly, attractant, sporulation, nematode predation

Introduction

Vacuoles, also known as lysosomes in animal cells, are essential organelles involved in regulating cellular homeostasis in fungi. They play crucial roles in nutrient sensing, osmoregulation, and autophagy, thereby influencing various aspects of cell biology [1,2]. In plant cells, vacuoles are vital for development, reproduction, and stress responses [3]. In yeast, vacuolar morphology is regulated by proteins such as the lipid kinase Fab1, which controls vacuole size under osmotic stress [4]. In the filamentous fungus Aspergillus flavus, the Fab1 homolog AfFab1 maintains vacuolar size and cellular morphology [5]. Similarly, ergosterol-β-glucosidase Egh1 contributes to cellular homeostasis in Saccharomyces cerevisiae [6], while plasma membrane protein Nce102 and its homologue Fhn1 regulate vacuolar morphology and function [7]. Proper functioning of vacuoles heavily relies on vacuolar sorting and assembly, which ensures appropriate transport of various cargo (such as proteins and lipids) to the specific area [8]. Vacuolar sorting involves recognition and trafficking of cargo proteins, whereas vacuolar assembly involves construction of the vacuolar membrane, packaging of its contents, and maintenance of vacuolar stability [8,9]. These processes are crucial for maintaining cellular integrity and function. They are orchestrated by various proteins and protein complexes, including small GTPases, vacuolar protein sorting (Vps) proteins, coat proteins, tethering proteins, and fusogenic proteins [10,11].

Small GTPases, particularly Rab5 and Rab7, play crucial roles in vesicle-vacuole fusion and vacuole morphogenesis. These small G proteins interact with the HOPS (homotypic fusion, protein sorting) and CORVET (class C core vacuole/endosome tethering) complexes to regulate vesicle aggregation during endosomal trafficking [2,12]. Both complexes share several subunits, including Vps11, Vps16, Vps18, and Vps33, with CORVET uniquely containing Vps3 and Vps8, and HOPS containing Vps39 and Vps41 [13]. In many organisms, including fungi, defects in vacuolar sorting and assembly can lead to various diseases and developmental abnormalities [10]. The Vps proteins are essential for vesicle trafficking, organelle function, and fungal pathogenicity. For example, in S. cerevisiae, vps18 knockout impairs Golgi function and increases temperature sensitivity [14], whereas in Aspergillus nidulans, AnDigA (a homolog of Vps18) is involved in nuclear translocation, mitochondrial morphology, and polar growth [15]. In nematode-trapping (NT) fungi, such as Drechslerella dactyloides, vacuole assembly and trap morphology are regulated by the SNARE protein DdVam7 and transcription factor DdaSte12 [16,17]. Further, the SNARE protein, AoSec22, regulates vacuole assembly, trap formation, and stress responses in Arthrobotrys oligospora [18]. Meanwhile, AoRab7A interacts with Vps41 and Vps35 to regulate mycelial growth and pathogenicity [19]. Despite these insights, the roles and regulatory mechanisms of Vps proteins, particularly Vps18, in NT fungi remain poorly understood.

NT fungi, such as A. oligospora, represent a unique class of carnivorous fungi that capture, immobilize, and consume nematodes using specialized traps, such as adhesive knobs, networks, and constricting rings [20,21]. Owing to their ability to prey on nematodes, NT fungi are being explored as potential biological control agents [22]. Among these, A. oligospora is the most extensively studied NT fungus, forming adhesive networks to capture nematodes and secreting extracellular enzymes to infect and digest them [23]. Trap morphogenesis is a critical process in this predatory behavior. Recent genomic and proteomic studies on A. oligospora have revealed multiple cellular processes involved in mycelial development and trap formation, including signal transduction [24–28], autophagy [29], peroxisomal biogenesis [30,31], and the role of transcription factors [32,33]. Several compounds are critically involved in regulating the predatory behavior of NT fungi. A recent study revealed that 6-methylsalicylic acid (6-MSA), a polyketide synthase-derived metabolite and biosynthetic intermediate of arthrosporols, acts as a morphogen that suppresses trap formation in Arthrobotrys flagrans (syn. Duddingtonia flagrans) [34]. Notably, 6-MSA exhibits functional similarities to quorum-sensing molecules, suggesting its potential role in mediating fungal behavioral responses through chemical signaling. Intriguingly, when either AOL_s00215g283 (encoding 6-MSA synthase) or AOL_s00215g281 (encoding the decarboxylase for 6-MSA) was deleted, a pronounced increase in trap formation was observed within 6 h of nematode exposure [35].

Mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), which are conserved signaling cascades in eukaryotes, play crucial roles in fungal growth, stress response, and pathogenicity. For example, AoFus3, a homologous Fus3 in S. cerevisiae, is essential for hyphal fusion and trap morphogenesis in A. oligospora. Deletion of Aofus3 leads to the formation of hyphal coils instead of typical three-dimensional networks, owing to the loss of hyphal fusion [24]. AoSte12, a downstream transcription factor of AoFus3, also regulates hyphal fusion and trap formation, whereas inactivation of Aoste12 results in enhanced hyphal fusion and an increased number of mycelial rings in the traps [36]. Therefore, Fus3-MAPK and Ste12 play indispensable roles in trap morphogenesis of NT fungi. Despite these advances, the interacting proteins of Ste12 involved in mycelial development and trap morphogenesis remain unclear.

In this study, we investigated the role of AoVps18, a homolog of S. cerevisiae Vps18, in regulating mycelial growth, trap formation, and pathogenicity of A. oligospora. Given the conserved roles of Vps18 homologs in vacuolar function, we hypothesized that AoVps18 is essential for maintaining vacuolar integrity in A. oligospora. We further postulated that AoVps18 deficiency would disrupt core cellular processes (such as vacuole assembly, mitochondrial dynamics, lipid metabolism, and vesicular transport) ultimately impairing mycelial growth, conidiation, trap morphogenesis, and secondary metabolism, and thereby compromising nematode predation efficiency. To test this hypothesis, we furtherly investigated its interactions with key regulatory proteins, including AoSte12 and AoFus3. Collectively, our study provides novel insights into the potential regulatory network involving AoVps18, shedding light on its role in the MAPK signaling pathway and its pleiotropic effects on cellular processes in NT fungi.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

The fungus A. oligospora (strain ATCC 24,927, purchased from the American Type Culture Collection [ATCC]) and its derived mutants (ΔAovps18–1 and ΔAovps18–2) were cultured on potato dextrose agar (PDA, potato 200 g/L, glucose 20 g/l, agar 20 g/L) medium at 28°C. S. cerevisiae FY834 strain (Testobio, Ningbo, China) and Escherichia coli DH5α strain (Takara, Dalian, China) were used to construct knockout vectors, which were cultured in yeast extract peptone dextrose (YPD, yeast extract 10 g/L, tryptone 20 g/L, glucose 20 g/L, agar 20 g/L) and lysogeny broth media, respectively [37].

Sequence analysis and target gene deletion

AoVps18 (AOL_s00215g688) was identified in A. oligospora by homologous sequence alignment. Conserved domains of Vps18 were predicted using the Pfam website (http://pfam-legacy.xfam.org) [36]. To compare AoVps18 with its homologs, proteins from various fungi were searched from GenBank database, and sequence similarity was assessed using DNAman software (Version 6). A phylogenetic tree of Vps18 homologs was constructed using the neighbor-joining method in MEGA 7 software [38].

Homologous recombination was used to create an Aovps18 knockout strain. Specifically, the 5′ and 3′ flanking regions of the Aovps18 gene were amplified from the A. oligospora genome, and the hph gene was amplified from plasmid pSCN44 (a gift from Dr. KA Borkovich, University of California) using specific primers (Table S1). These components, including the homology arm sequences, hph cassette, and linearized pRS426 backbone (a gift from Dr. KA Borkovich), were co-transformed into S. cerevisiae FY834 competent cells using a lithium acetate/polyethylene glycol (LiAc/PEG) chemical transformation protocol. And recombinant plasmids (pRS426-Aovps18:hph) were subsequently selected on SD/-Ura media [39,40]. The alternative fragment was subsequently transformed into A. oligospora protoplasts, and transformants were selected on tryptone-yeast extract-glucose agar (TYGA, tryptone 10 g/L, glucose 10 g/L, yeast extract 5 g/L, molasses 5 g/L, agar 20 g/L) medium supplemented with hygromycin B (200 μg/mL) [41]. Positive transformants were further verified using PCR and Southern blotting [42].

Analyses of mycelial growth, stress response, and conidiation

Mycelial growth was assessed by inoculating WT and ΔAovps18 mutant strains on PDA, tryptone-glucose, and TYGA media, with colony diameters measured at 24-h intervals. For stress response analyses, strains were grown on TG medium supplemented with oxidative agents (menadione and H₂O₂), a cell wall-disturbing agent (sodium dodecyl sulfate, SDS), and osmotic agents (NaCl and sorbitol); colony diameters were then recorded after 5 days at 28°C, and relative growth inhibition was calculated, the colony diameter on TG was used as control [43].

Conidia yield was quantified by growing strains on cornmeal-molasses-yeast (CMY) medium (corn 20 g/L, yeast extract 5 g/L, agar 20 g/L) for two weeks at 28°C. Conidiophore formation was observed under a light microscope [44,45]. Mycelial and conidial morphologies were observed after 7 days of incubation on CMY medium using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) following fixation in 4% glutaraldehyde [46]. For organelle structure analysis, mycelia and traps were collected from water agar (WA) medium, fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde, and observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM) [45].

Staining observations

Intracellular organelles were stained using specific dyes. Mycelia were collected after 5 days of incubation on PDA medium. Mycelial nuclei were stained with 20 μg/mL DAPI (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and cell walls and septa with 20 μg/mL calcium fluoride white (CFW, Sigma-Aldrich) [37]. Lipid droplets (LDs) were visualized with 10 μg/mL boron dipyrromethene (BODIPY, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and autophagosomes were observed with 100 μg/mL monodansylcadaverine (MDC, Sigma-Aldrich) [40]. Vacuoles were stained with 5 μM 7-amino-4-chloromethylcoumarin (CMAC, Thermo Fisher Scientific) [19], and reactive oxygen species (ROS) were detected using 10 µg/mL dihydroethidium (DHE, Sigma-Aldrich) [47]. The treated mycelial samples were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Trap induction, nematode mortality, and proteolytic activity

Mycelial discs of WT and ΔAovps18 mutant strains were inoculated on 6 cm WA plates at 28°C. After 4 days, 300 Caenorhabditis elegans were added, with WA plates containing nematodes serving as controls. The trap number and nematode mortality were recorded every 12 h [48].

To assess proteolytic activity, WT and ΔAovps18 strains were cultured in 100 mL of PD broth at 28°C and 180 rpm for 2 days. Next, 25 mL of skimmed milk was added, and fermentation was continued for 3 days. The fermentation supernatant was harvested, and proteolytic activity was detected using skim milk plates [41].

RNA sequencing and reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) analysis

The WT and mutant strains were incubated on PDA medium, and 300 nematodes were added when the colonies covered 3/4 of the plate to induce trap formation. Mycelia (including traps) were collected at 0, 12, and 36 h post-induction (hpi), frozen in liquid nitrogen, and sent to Meiji Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) for RNA extraction and sequencing [49]. RNA-seq data were analyzed on a cloud platform (https://cloud.majorbio.com/) [33]. The transcript levels of candidate genes were verified by RT-PCR using a LightCycler 480 and SYBR Green I master mix (Roche, Basel, Switzerland). Specifically, the RNA of the WT and mutants was isolated using the AxyPrepTM multisource total RNA miniprep kit (Axygen, USA). The cDNA was synthesized from 1 µg of RNA using the PrimeScriptHRT reagent kit (TaKaRa, Japan). RT-PCR was performed using cDNA as a template with specific primers (Table S2) for the target genes, with β-tubulin (AOL_s00076g640) as an internal reference. Relative transcript level was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [50].

Analysis of secondary metabolites

Fungal strains were cultured in PD broth at 28°C with shaking at 180 rpm for 7 days. Mycelia were separated from the fermentation broth, which was then ultrasonicated three times (30 min per cycle), and extracted with 250 mL of ethyl acetate. After vacuum-drying, the samples were dissolved in chloroform (1:1 v/v), filtered by a 0.22 µm membrane, and analyzed using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Xcalibur software (version 3.0, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to compare the metabolic profiles of WT and mutant strains, and Compounds Discoverer software (version 3.0, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to analyze the metabolic data [37,51].

Nematodes chemotaxis assay

WT and ∆Aovps18 mutants were cultivated in PD broth at 28°C and 180 rpm for 7 days. The fermentation broth was used for chemotaxis assays [34]. Chemotaxis was tested on 6 cm WA plates and divided into three sections. WT and ΔAovps18 mutant fermentation broths, as well as PD broth, were placed in separate sections, and nematodes were supplemented to the center of the plate. After 90 min, the nematode distribution across the plates was observed and counted. Volatile organic compounds (VOCs) involved in nematode chemotaxis were identified by analyzing the differentially expressed VOCs from metabolomic data, and their attractiveness to nematodes was tested using DMSO as a control [52]. And the concentration of each tested compound was uniformly maintained at 0.1 M.

Yeast one-hybrid (Y1H) and yeast two-hybrid (Y2H) assay

For the Y1H assay, the cDNA of Aovps18 and Aoste12 was amplified from A. oligospora using specific primers (Table S3). The plasmids pGADT7 (Coolaber, Beijing, China) and pAbAi (Coolaber, Beijing, China) were linearized using EcoRI, and the recombinant plasmids pAbAi-Aovps18 and AD-AoSte12 plasmids were constructed in E. coli DH5α. The linearized pAbAi-Aovps18 plasmid was transformed into S. cerevisiae Y1H Gold cells (Weidibio, Shanghai, China) and plated on SD/-Ura and SD/-Leu media containing 1000 ng/mL aureobasidin A (ABA; Coolaber, Beijing, China). Next, AD-AoSte12 was introduced into pAbAi-Aovps18 transformants, which were then plated on SD/-Ura and SD/-Leu plates with 1000 ng/mL ABA and incubated for 3 days [48]. pGADT7-Rec-p53/p53-AbAi was used as the positive control, and pAbAi-Aovps18/AD was used as the negative control.

The proteins interacting with AoVps18 were predicted using the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/). For the Y2H assay, the cDNA of Aovps18, Aoste12, Aofus3 (AOL_s00110g154), Aovps16 (AOL_s00004g466), and Aovps41 (AOL_s00054g461) was amplified using specific primers (Table S3). The fusion plasmids AD-AoFus3, AD-AoSte12, AD-AoVps16, AD-AoVps41, BD-AoFus3, and BD-AoVps18 were constructed using pGADT7 (AD) and pGBKT7 (BD) vectors. The AD-target and BD-target plasmids were co-transformed into Y2H Gold competent cells using a chemical transformation method. The transformed cells were plated onto three selective media: SD/-Trp/-Leu, SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade, and SD/-Trp/-Leu/-His/-Ade supplemented with X-α-Gal (20 μg/mL). Protein-protein interactions were assessed based on colony growth under these conditions. The pGADT7-T + pGBKT7–53 plasmid pair served as a positive control, while pGADT7-T + pGBKT7-Lam was used as a negative control [46,53].

Statistical analysis

Prism 8.0 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for data analysis, employing one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated thrice.

Results

Sequence analysis of AoVps18

AoVps18 is a protein with a 959-amino acid sequence, an isoelectric point of 5.36, and a molecular weight of 108.43 kDa. It contains multiple conserved domains, including the Pep3_Vps18 and RING-H2_Pep3p-like domains, as well as a clathrin superfamily domain. Sequence alignment revealed that AoVps18 shares high similarities (81.62–92.49%) with homologs from other NT fungi, particularly Arthrobotrys flagrans (92.49%). In contrast, the similarity with homologs from other fungi ranged from 47.76–51.87% (Figure S1A). Phylogenetic analysis indicated that Vps18 homologs from NT and other filamentous fungi formed two distinct branches (Figure S1A), suggesting potential functional and mechanistic differences between these groups.

AoVps18 modulates hyphal growth, stress response, and conidiation

Two independent mutant strains were generated via homologous recombination (Figure S1B) and confirmed using PCR and Southern blotting (Figure S1C and D). Deletion of Aovps18 significantly impaired mycelial growth. The colony diameters of the ΔAovps18 mutant strains were 1.98 cm on PDA, 2.33 cm on TG, and 3.08 cm on TYGA, compared to 6.42 cm, 6.05 cm, and 5.70 cm for the WT strain, respectively (Figure 1(A, B)). Furthermore, the ΔAovps18 mutants exhibited irregular colony morphology with minimal aerial hyphae (Figure 1(C)). Additionally, the ΔAovps18 mutants displayed a marked reduction in both cell length and the number of nuclei per cell, with average cell lengths of 21.08 μm (WT, 49.2 μm) and an average of 4 nuclei per cell (WT, 10) (Figure 1(D–G)).

Aovps18 deletion also affected the response to various chemical stresses. ΔAovps18 mutants exhibited reduced sensitivity to osmotic reagent, with RGI values of 19.66% at 0.2 M NaCl and 10.06% at 0.5 M sorbitol, compared with 55.14% and 57.01% in the WT, respectively. In contrast, the mutants were more sensitive to oxidative stress, with an RGI of 37.79% at 0.02 mM menadione, compared to 4.98% in the WT. Additionally, the ΔAovps18 mutants exhibited increased sensitivity to SDS, with RGIs of 86.38% compared to 69.16% in the WT (Figure S2A, B). The relative transcript levels (RTLs) of oxidative stress-related genes (thd, ccp1, ctt1, per, gstA, and ctl) were significantly reduced in ΔAovps18 mutants (Figure S2C). Similarly, the RTLs of genes associated with cell-wall biosynthesis (gfpa, csg, hex, trs, and gls) were downregulated (Figure S2D).

Conidiation, a critical trait for NT fungi and other filamentous fungi, was completely abolished in ΔAovps18 mutants. No conidia were observed in the conidiophores using light microscopy or SEM, and quantitative analysis confirmed the absence of conidial production (Figure 1(H–J)). The RTLs of sporulation-related genes (flbA, fluG, abaA, hyp1, veA, velB, stuA, brlA) were significantly down-regulated, with hyp1 showing the most prominent decrease (Figure 1(K)).

AoVps18 is indispensable for trap formation and pathogenicity

After nematode induction, the WT strain exhibited an increasing number of traps over time, whereas the ∆Aovps18 mutants failed to form traps until 48 hpi (Figure 2(A, B)). The nematode predation ability of the ∆Aovps18 mutants was remarkably impaired, with predation rates of 67.33% (WT strain) and 19.67% (mutants) at 12 hpi, and 92.67% (WT strain) and 20.67% (mutants) at 24 hpi (Figure 2(C)). Despite reduction, the ∆Aovps18 mutants retained some predation ability at 12 and 24 hpi (Figure 2(C)). Nematode predation by NT fungi involves attraction, capture, adhesion, and digestion (Figure 2(D)) [54]. Therefore, the differences in these steps between the ∆Aovps18 mutants and WT strain were investigated further. The chemotaxis assay showed that fewer nematodes were attracted to the fermentation broth and fungal mass of ∆Aovps18 mutants compared to that of the WT strain (Figure 2(E), Figure S3A, B). TEM images revealed a remarkable reduction in the thickness of adhesive layer of ∆Aovps18 mutants compared to the WT (Figure 2(F)). In addition, the RTLs of genes associated with adhesion proteins (AOL_s00007g5 and Aomad1; Aomad1 has been confirmed to be associated with trap formation and pathogenicity [55,56] as well as the lectin AofleA) were significantly downregulated in ∆Aovps18 mutants (Figure 2(G)). Proteolytic activity in the fermentation broth of ∆Aovps18 mutants was slightly decreased compared to that with the WT strain. Correspondingly, the RTLs of genes associated with cuticle-degrading proteases (spb, aoz1, and alp1) were significantly downregulated, whereas those of kex and slp were upregulated (Figure 2(H, I)). In summary, deletion of Aovps18 impairs the fungal ability to attract (via volatile organic compounds (VOCs) in the fermentation broth), capture (trap formation), adhere (adhesive layer formation), and digest (proteolytic activity) nematodes, thereby reducing its pathogenicity.

AoVps18 influences nematode attraction by modulating VOC synthesis

To determine the mechanism by which nematode attraction was decreased by ΔAovps18 mutants, the secondary metabolites in the fermentation broth extract of WT and ΔAovps18 mutants were analyzed using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC). The results revealed significant differences in metabolic profiles between the WT and ΔAovps18 mutant strains, particularly in the 16–36 min retention time (Figure 3(A)). Compared with the WT strain, 6965 compounds were upregulated, and 4044 compounds were downregulated in the ΔAovps18 mutants (Figure 3(B)). Among downregulated VOCs in ΔAovps18 mutants, several were identified as attractants for nematodes, including benzaldehyde (BA), 6-methoxysalicylic acid (6-MOSA), and salicylic acid (SA). In contrast, the synthesis of p-aminosalicylic acid (PASA) and p-aminobenzoic acid (PABA) was markedly upregulated (Figure 3(C)). Chemotaxis assays showed that BA (75.09%), 6-MOSA (61.16%), and PASA (54.29%) elicited significantly higher CI values compared to SA (33.37%) and PABA (29.34%) (Figure 3(D)). Moreover, the fermentation broth of ΔAovps18 mutants showed a significant decrease in nematode attraction, with CI values of 35.45% for the WT strain and 15.29% for the mutants (Figure 3(E)). Additionally, the content of arthrobotrisins, which are compounds unique to NT fungi like A. oligospora [57], was markedly reduced in the ΔAovps18 mutants (Figure 3(F)). These results suggest a potential indirect role for AoVps18 in modulating VOC production, which in turn affects the ability of A. oligospora to attract nematodes.

AoVps18 involves in vacuole assembly, mitochondrial morphology, ROS, and LD accumulation

To investigate the effect of Aovps18 on vacuole assembly, vacuole morphology was examined using TEM and CMAC staining. Deletion of Aovps18 impaired vacuole fusion, resulting in small fragmented vacuoles (Figure 4(A, B)). Meanwhile, mitochondrial morphology was altered in the ΔAovps18 mutants, with mitochondria predominantly exhibiting an elongated shape (Figure 4(C)). The average mitochondrial length was 0.5 μm in the mutants, compared with 0.3 μm in the WT strain (Figure 4(D)). Further, the number of mitochondria was reduced from eight in the WT strain to four in the mutant strains (Figure 4(E)). These alterations in mitochondrial number and morphology likely affected cellular energy production, as ATP content was remarkably decreased in the ΔAovps18 mutants (0.33 μmol/g) compared with that in the WT strain (0.62 μmol/g) (Figure 4(F)). Mitochondria are known to generate ROS during ATP production, while deletion of Aovps18 resulted in decreased ROS accumulation (Figure 4(G, H)). Peroxisomes act as key organelles in maintaining cellular redox balance and preventing the oxidative stress caused by excess ROS. Analysis of genes (pex11, pex12a, pex13, pex14, and pex23) involved in peroxisome function showed significantly altered expression levels in the ΔAovps18 mutants (Figure 4(I)). Furthermore, LD morphology was altered, with ΔAovps18 mutants exhibiting smaller and more numerous LDs (Figure 4(J, K)). Except for sur2, the RTL of six genes involved in fatty acid synthesis (gns1, sdeA, nd1, ker, gig30, and cem1) was significantly downregulated in ΔAovps18 mutants (Figure 4(L)).

AoVps18 mediates pleiotropic gene regulation

To explore the mechanisms underlying the cellular processes influenced by Aovps18, we determined the transcriptome profiles of the WT and ΔAovps18 mutants. These results revealed that Aovps18 deletion leads to widespread changes in gene expression. Compared to the WT strain, 3860 genes were differentially expressed at 0 h (1959 upregulated, 1901 downregulated), 2364 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified at 12 h (1344 upregulated, 1020 downregulated), and 1887 DEGs were identified at 36 h (1055 upregulated, 832 downregulated) (Figure 5(A)). Venn analysis revealed 202 genes related to nematode induction (Figure 5(B)). Among these, 71 were significantly enriched in the intrinsic component of the membrane and 70 were significantly enriched in the integral component of the membrane (Figure S4).

Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of the DEGs at each time point revealed distinct functional patterns. At 0 h, the DEGs were markedly enriched in metabolic processes, biosynthetic processes, oxidoreductase activity, ribosomes, and translation. At 12 h, DEGs were markedly enriched in component of membrane and transporter activity, and at 36 h, DEGs were enriched in component of membrane, peptidase, and oxidoreductase activity (Figure 5(C)). The above analysis showed that membrane-related genes were significantly enriched during the trap formation stages (12 h and 36 h) compared with those at the vegetative development stage (0 h). Similarly, KEGG analysis showed that numerous pathways were enriched during the vegetative development stage, such as ribosomes, autophagy, endocytosis, fatty acids, the MAPK signaling pathway, the TCA cycle, and peroxisomes; notably, 94 DEGs were enriched in ribosomes. Interestingly, the MAPK signaling pathway, autophagy, proteasome, and peroxisome were remarkably enriched during the vegetative development and trap formation stages. In contrast, the number of DEGs enriched in ribosomes decreased remarkably at the trap formation stage (Figure 5(D)). This was related to the sensing of nematodes and their subsequent predation.

Further, 15 KEGG pathways were co-enriched in the transcriptome and metabolome, including oxidative phosphorylation (43 DEGs and 2 differentially expressed metabolites (DEMs)), fructose and mannose metabolism (22 DEGs and 11 DEMs), amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism (30 DEGs and 9 DEMs), multiple amino acid metabolism (70 DEGs and 96 DEMs), glutathione metabolism (17 DEGs and 7 DEMs), pyrimidine metabolism (17 DEGs and 3 DEMs), starch and sucrose metabolism (30 DEGs and 12 DEMs), unsaturated fatty acid biosynthesis (8 DEGs and 12 DEMs), lipoic acid metabolism (3 DEGs and 1 DEM), pentose phosphate pathway (15 DEGs and 3 DEMs), phenylalanine metabolism (10 DEGs and 30 DEMs), and glycolysis/glycogenesis (19 DEGs and 4 DEMs) (Figure S5). These co-enriched pathways provide further insights into the molecular changes induced by Aovps18 in response to nematode interactions.

Vps18 contributes to membrane dynamics through interactions with Vps16 and Vps41

Transcriptome analysis revealed that many DEGs were significantly enriched in membrane-related processes. Further, RT-PCR validation confirmed that the transcription patterns of DEGs involved in steroid synthesis and membrane function were consistent with the transcriptomic data (Figure S6). To explore the potential interactions between AoVps18 and DEGs, a protein interaction network was analyzed using the STRING database, in which 10 proteins potentially interacted with AoVps18, including AoVps8, AoVps11, AoVps16, AoVps33A, AoVps41, AoVps39, AoVti1 (vesicle transport v-SNARE protein), AoAvaB (vacuolar morphogenesis protein), AOL_s00054g460, and AOL_s00188g85 (Figure 5(E)). Subsequently, the Y2H assays confirmed that AoVps18 interacted with AoVps16 and AoVps41 (Figure 5(F)). Notably, the transcription of AoVps16 and AoVps41 genes was remarkably upregulated in the ΔAovps18 mutants (Figure 5(G)).

AoVps18 is regulated by the MAPK signaling pathway

In our previous study, AoSte12 was confirmed to interact with AoVps18 [36]. Further investigation revealed that AoSte12 also interacted with AoFus3 and that AoVps18 interacted with AoFus3 (Figure 6(A, B)). Notably, deletion of Aovps18 did not affect the transcript levels of Aoste12 or Aofus3 (Figure 6(C)). However, deletion of Aofus3 significantly increased the transcript levels of Aovps18 (Figure 6(D)), whereas markedly reduced in Aovps18 expression was observed in the ΔAoste12 mutant (Figure 6(E)). Additionally, DAP-seq analysis of AoSte12 revealed a conserved TGAAAC motif in the Aovps18 promoter (Figure 6(F, G)), which is a known binding site for AoSte12. Furthermore, the Y1H assay supported direct DNA-protein binding between the AoSte12 and Aovps18 promoters (Figure 6(H)).

Discussion

In the present study, we elucidated the role of AoVps18 in regulating key cellular processes in A. oligospora by focusing on its involvement in vacuole assembly, mitochondrial morphology, LD accumulation, and VOCs. Our results highlight that AoVps18 is modulated by the MAPK signaling pathway, particularly through interactions with AoSte12 and AoFus3. In addition, AoVps18 directly interacted with AoVps16 and AoVps41. The deletion of Aovps18 led to significant alterations in cellular morphology, including fragmented vacuoles, elongated mitochondria, and an increase in the number of smaller LDs, suggesting a disruption in intracellular organelle dynamics. Additionally, our findings show that A. oligospora utilizes VOCs to attract nematodes, and that the absence of Aovps18 impairs VOC production, thereby reducing the nematode-attraction ability of the fungus. These changes in cellular processes subsequently affect the formation of traps and conidia as well as mycelial growth (Figure 7). Taken together, these results underscore the crucial role of AoVps18 in coordinating cellular functions that maintain cell integrity as well as enable the fungus to effectively interact with its environment.

The proposed regulatory mechanisms by which AoVps18 influences trap formation, spore production, and mycelial development in A. oligospora. Deletion of Aovps18 impairs nematode attraction by altered the VOC production in A. oligospora. AoVps18 is regulated by AoSte12 and AoFus3, and interacts with AoVps41 and AoVps16, influencing key biological functions such as vacuole and mitochondrial dynamics, ATP production, lipid droplet formation, and overall energy regulation. These interactions modulate trap formation, spore production, and mycelial growth, highlighting the crucial role of AoVps18 in the A. oligospora lifecycle.

We found that AoVps18 was modulated by the MAPK signaling pathway, particularly through its interaction with AoSte12 and AoFus3. Critically, our data support a model where AoSte12 directly regulates Aovps18 transcription, evidenced by: (1) identification of a functional TGAAAC motif (Ste12-binding site) in the Aovps18 promoter (Figure 6(F, G)), (2) confirmed physical binding of AoSte12 to this promoter via Y1H assays (Figure 6(H)), and (3) significant downregulation of Aovps18 in ΔAoste12 mutants (Figure 6(E)). This supports AoSte12 May function as a transcriptional activator upstream of subcellular organelle dynamics including vacuolar sorting. MAPK signaling pathways are well-known for their role in regulating cellular responses to environmental stimuli, including stress responses, development, and pathogenicity in fungi [58]. Our results suggest that Aovps18 serves as a key downstream effector in this pathway, mediating responses to environmental cues necessary for nematode predation. The interactions among AoVps18, AoSte12, and AoFus3 likely regulate multiple cellular processes, including the maintenance of cell wall integrity and the ability to form traps, which are crucial steps in the predatory behavior of A. oligospora. This AoSte12-AoVps18 regulatory axis mechanistically links developmental signaling to organelle dynamics. Deletion of Aofus3 results in the formation of non-fused hyphal loops instead of mature traps, whereas the absence of Aoste12 leads to the formation of numerous fused multi-ring traps in A. oligospora [36,59]. The interaction between Ste12 and Fus3 has been confirmed [60]; however, their interaction with AoVps18 has not been reported previously. In ΔAovps18 mutants, multiple cellular processes were impaired, suggesting that these interactions may also extend to other signaling networks, further implicating AoVps18 in a complex regulatory network required for survival and nematode interaction.

Deletion of Aovps18 induced pronounced morphological alterations in intracellular organelles. Y2H analysis revealed direct interactions of AoVps18 with both AoVps16 and AoVps41 (Figure 4(F)). Intriguingly, ΔAovps18 mutants exhibited fragmented vacuoles, elongated mitochondria, and an increased abundance of smaller LDs (Figure 4). These observations align with the following established mechanisms in eukaryotic membrane trafficking: (1) In animal cells, Vps16 mediates lysosome-endosome-autophagosome fusion by recruiting Vps33A to the HOPS complex [61], and (2) yeast Vps41 mutations cause characteristic phenotypes, including iron-limited growth defects, vacuolar morphology abnormalities, and defective sorting of membrane-associated/soluble vacuolar proteins [62]. These findings establish AoVps18 as a pivotal regulator of membrane dynamics, which orchestrates organelle morphology and function through interactions with core trafficking machinery. Further, vacuoles are essential for various cellular functions, including nutrient storage, detoxification, and maintenance of cellular homeostasis [1]. Vacuole fragmentation suggests a disturbance in vacuole fusion and membrane dynamics, which could compromise the overall cellular environment [63]. Similarly, the observed elongation of mitochondria and alterations in LDs indicate the role of Aovps18 in mitochondrial dynamics and lipid metabolism, which are critical for cellular energy balance and stress response [64–67]. These changes align with the findings of previous studies in other organisms, where Vps18 homologs have been implicated in regulating membrane trafficking, vesicle formation, and the maintenance of organelle integrity. For example, deletion of vps18 impairs Golgi function and increases the temperature sensitivity of S. cerevisiae [14], while inhibition of Vps18 function in a single vacuole partner results in a 15–35% reduction in fusion activity [68], resulting in many fragmented vacuoles instead of large compartments [69]. In A. nidulans, DigA (a homolog of AoVps18) regulates nuclear migration, mitochondrial morphology, and polar growth [15]. In AndigA mutants, the vacuoles are highly fragmented, and their distribution extends to the hyphal tip. Similarly, in A. oligospora, AoVps35 and AoVps41 are involved in vesicle aggregation and multiple cellular processes, whereas deletion of Aovps35 and Aovps41 causes failure of vesicle assembly, resulting in a fragmented state [19]. Further, vesicles in ∆Aosec22 mutants were deformed, leading to weakened mycelial growth and trap formation [18].

The deletion of Aovps18 led to a reduced ability of A. oligospora to attract nematodes, was consistent with the altered levels of VOCs (BA, SA, and 6-MOSA) detected in the mutants. While this correlation strongly suggests AoVps18 influences VOC profiles, the precise molecular pathway connecting vacuolar sorting/assembly to the biosynthesis of specific volatiles remain to be fully elucidated. The observed enrichment of DEGs in metabolic pathways (Figure 5(C, D)) provides candidate routes for this regulation that warrant future investigation. Deletion of Aovps18 resulted in DEGs enriched in component of membrane, metabolic process, and MAPK signaling pathway (Figure 5(C, D)), suggesting that AoVps18 May influence VOC production through its regulatory effects on cellular morphology and metabolic processes. Studies have showed that VOCs play a pivotal role in mediating nematode attraction, and their production is regulated by various cellular factors, including those involved in signal transduction, metabolism, and membrane trafficking [70,71]. A. oligospora produced a small group of volatile furanone and pyrone metabolites involved in lifestyle transition and deletion of AOL_s00079g496 resulted in increased amounts of furanone attractants and enhanced attractive activity, whereas it caused decreased trap formation [70]. The chemotaxis experiments revealed the following order of chemotaxis: BA > 6-MOSA > PASA > SA > PABA (Figure 3(D)). Metabolomics analysis also showed that PASA and PABA were upregulated in ∆Aovps18 mutants, suggesting PASA and PABA are also involved other cellular process. Among these VOCs, BA and SA have previously been proven to be chemotactic agents [72], whereas the attraction ability of 6-MOSA, PASA, and PABA to nematodes has not been reported.

In addition to its effects on cellular morphology and nematode attraction, deletion of Aovps18 significantly impaired trap formation, conidial production, and mycelial growth. Trap formation is a hallmark of the predatory behavior of A. oligospora, and its regulation is tightly linked with the proper functioning of intracellular organelles, including vacuoles [19], autophagosome [29,73,74], peroxisome [30,31], and mitochondria [75,76]. The deletion of related genes (e.g. Aovps35, Aopex1, and Aoatg11) impairs trap formation and pathogenicity. The morphological alterations observed in ΔAovps18 mutants, including disrupted vacuole fusion and mitochondrial elongation, likely contribute to the impairment of trap formation. However, the nematode predation rate of the ∆Aovps18 mutants reached 20.67% at 24 h (Figure 2(C)). This may be related to extracellular protease activity and adhesive layer in the outer ring of the mycelium. Although the expression of genes encoding protease, adhesions, and lectins (Aospb, Aoaoz1, Aoalp1, Aofle, Aomad1, Ao7g5) were downregulated in ∆Aovps18 mutants, four genes (Ao54g276, Ao76g207, Aosed, Aospd) were expressed normally, and two genes (Aokex, Aoslp) were significantly upregulated (Figure 2(G, I)). The extracellular proteolytic activity was similar to that of the WT, indicating that the mutant retained some pathogenicity.

Furthermore, defects in conidial formation showed that AoVps18 plays a crucial role in the differentiation process required for reproductive success and pathogenicity. Similar observations in other fungal species have shown that disruption of membrane trafficking and organelle dynamics impairs conidiation. In S. cerevisiae, cells bearing pep3 (vps18) disruption alleles were viable but incapable of sporulating [69]. In A. nidulans, the vesicle trafficking gene dnfD is crucial for sporulation; ∆dnfD mutants produce misshapen conidiophore vesicles, which are defective in metulae formation [77]. Similarly, in Aspergillus oryzae, conidiation is severely impaired in ∆vps24 mutants, with no conidia or aerial hyphae being formed when cultured on PD plates at 30°C [78]. In Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici, deletion of vps9 caused a reduction (over 50.0%) in conidiophore production [79]. Collectively, these findings underscore the evolutionarily conserved role of Vps proteins in regulating conidiation across diverse fungal species.

Further, the deletion of Aovps18 significantly impaired mycelial growth. Staining results revealed a markedly reduced cell length in the ∆Aovps18 mutant. Notably, significant differences were found in the enriched GO terms between the vegetative hyphae (0 h) and trap formation stages (12 and 36 h). At the vegetative growth stage (0 h), DEGs were predominantly enriched in biosynthetic and metabolic processes, whereas KEGG pathway analysis highlighted enrichment in ribosome function and the cell cycle. These results suggest that Aovps18 is required for mycelial growth, a function conserved across other fungal species. For example, the absence of pep3 (vps18) severely inhibits growth [69] and the deletion of vps18 leads to temperature-sensitive growth defects in S. cerevisiae [14]. The deletion of vps24 abolished the formation of conidia and aerial hyphae in A. oryzae [78]. In F. oxysporum f. sp. lycopersici, the absence of vps9 resulted in colony diameter reductions of 53.9 and 44.1% on CM and PDA plates, respectively [79]. The growth defects observed in ∆Aovps18 align with the conserved role of Vps proteins in maintaining hyphal expansion and cellular homeostasis in fungi.

Conclusions

Our study revealed that AoVps18 modulates essential cellular functions as well as plays a key role in fungal interactions with the environment, particularly in nematode predation. A. oligospora utilizes VOCs to attract nematodes, and deletion of Aovps18 significantly reduces VOC production, impairing nematode attraction. Furthermore, AoVps18 is crucial for vacuole assembly, mitochondrial morphology, and LD dynamics, and its function is tightly regulated by the MAPK signaling pathway. These disruptions hinder trap and conidial formation, and reduce mycelial growth, underscoring the importance of AoVps18 in coordinating these cellular processes (Figure 7). Our findings highlight that AoSte12 and AoFus3 interact with AoVps18, suggesting a complex regulatory network in which these MAPK pathway components modulate AoVps18 expression and the associated cellular processes. The TGAAAC motif in the Aovps18 promoter, identified by DAP-seq and validated using Y1H assays, indicates direct transcriptional regulation by AoSte12. These findings enhance our knowledge of the regulatory mechanisms underlying organelle dynamics and the stress responses in this system, offering new insights into how cellular signaling networks maintain organelle integrity and function during trap and conidial formation.
