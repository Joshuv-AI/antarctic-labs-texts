Cap 2′-O-methyltransferase CMTR2 regulates male meiosis independent of its methyltransferase activity
Authors: Bo Zheng, Wenxin Gao, Hao Wu, Dan Zhao, Tiantian Wu, Haoyue Hu, Bing Jiang, Wenying Qu, Yuxuan Feng, Yuting Liang, Jun Yu, Xiaoyan Huang, Xia Chen, Kehan Wang, Fei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-obstructive azoospermia represents one of the most intractable forms of male infertility, with its onset frequently associated with meiotic abnormalities. To date, the pivotal molecular mechanisms governing meiosis remain incompletely understood. This study demonstrates that CMTR2 (Cap methyltransferase 2) exerts a critical regulatory function in male germ cell meiosis through a mechanism independent of its methyltransferase activity. Germ cell-specific Cmtr2 knockout mice (Ddx4-GcKO) exhibited meiotic prophase I zygotene arrest and male sterility. Ddx4-GcKO spermatocytes displayed phenotypic abnormalities, including defective DNA double-strand break repair and compromised crossover formation and recombination. Subsequent investigations revealed a marked reduction in the messenger RNA (mRNA) stability of key meiotic genes, Speedy/RINGO cell cycle regulator family member A (Spdya) and moloney leukemia virus 10 Like 1 (Mov10l1), in Ddx4-GcKO testes. Mechanistic studies indicated that CMTR2 directly interacts with the 5′ untranslated regions of these genes. Importantly, CMTR2 selectively mediates 2′-O-methylation of Spdya mRNA, whereas its regulation of Mov10l1 mRNA stability is independent of its enzymatic activity. This study is the first to uncover the non-canonical role of CMTR2 in meiotic regulation, enhancing our comprehension of meiotic molecular mechanisms and offering novel insights into the etiology of male sterility.

Introduction

Infertility affects approximately 15% of couples worldwide, with male factors accounting for nearly 50% of cases [1]. Male sterility arises from diverse etiologies including testicular dysfunction, endocrine disorders, and lifestyle choices [2]. Spermatogenesis, comprising sequential mitosis, meiosis, and spermiogenesis, represents an exquisitely regulated developmental cascade [3]. Central to this process is meiosis, which ensures genomic haploidization through a single DNA replication followed by two successive divisions (meiosis I and II). The extended prophase I of meiosis (leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, diplotene, and diakinesis) facilitates critical chromosomal events including programmed double-strand break (DSB) formation, homologous recombination, and synaptonemal complex assembly [4]. Disruption of these meiotic processes frequently underlies impaired spermatogenesis and consequent infertility. Among the various manifestations of male infertility, nonobstructive azoospermia (NOA) is particularly severe, characterized by a complete absence of sperm in the semen due to testicular dysfunction [5]. Although recent studies have uncovered additional monogenic causes of NOA, the full spectrum of its underlying pathogenic factors remains incompletely elucidated.

The CMTR family, belonging to the FtsJ family of RNA methyltransferases, comprises two distinct proteins: CMTR1 and CMTR2 [6, 7]. In mammals, CMTR1 and CMTR2 function to catalyze 2′-O-ribose methylation at the first and second nucleotides downstream of the 5′ end N7-methylguanosine (m7G) cap structure of messenger RNAs (mRNAs), respectively [8]. The roles of CMTR1 in mammals have been preliminarily elucidated. Mice with a ubiquitous deficiency of Cmtr1 exhibit embryonic lethality [9]. CMTR1 is also involved in neuronal development, as brain-specific conditional knockout of Cmtr1 in mice results in reduced cortical size and abnormal dendritic morphology. Furthermore, CMTR1 is crucial for maintaining fertility. Mice with germ cell-specific knockout of Cmtr1 in males exhibit a complete loss of germ cells and sterility, whereas females with oocyte-specific knockout show reduced fertility [9]. Despite reports indicating that homozygous deletion of Cmtr2 in mice also results in pre-weaning lethality, the role of CMTR2 in postnatal mouse growth and development, particularly its specific functions in the reproductive system, remains unclear [9, 10].

Current understanding of CMTR molecular functions primarily derives from their catalytic roles in modifying the mRNA 5′ cap structure, a highly conserved feature essential for mRNA stability, translation efficiency, and gene expression regulation across eukaryotes and numerous viruses [11, 12]. All eukaryotic and most viral mRNAs contain a 5′ cap0 structure formed by m7G [13]. CMTR1 methylates the first nucleotide, while CMTR2 can methylate both the first and second nucleotides [7, 14, 15]. By remodeling the mRNA-protein interaction network, the cap1 structure, on one hand, reduces its affinity for proteins associated with the RNA degradation pathway and, on the other hand, enhances its binding capacity to translation initiation factors, thereby synergistically promoting specific gene expression [8, 16]. The absence of cap1 modification renders mRNA susceptible to recognition by decapping enzymes, leading to degradation. In vertebrates, such unmodified transcripts can also activate the type I interferon pathway [17–19]. Current evidence suggests that cap2 contributes to the stability of specific transcript classes, such as ribosomal protein and histone mRNAs, and may also enhance translation efficiency [15, 20, 21]. Nevertheless, whether CMTRs are involved in other molecular regulatory pathways beyond these well-established functions of cap modifications remains to be elucidated.

In this study, germ cell-specific deletion of Cmtr2 results in complete male sterility, while Sertoli cell-specific ablation showed no phenotypic consequences, demonstrating that CMTR2 is essential for germ cell development. Further analysis established that Cmtr2 deficiency disrupts the first wave of spermatogenesis by arresting meiotic progression at the zygotene stage of prophase I. Mechanistic studies revealed that CMTR2 can directly bind to the 5′ untranslated region (5′ UTR) regions of meiotic regulator genes Spdya and Mov10l1, thereby preventing their mRNA degradation. Notably, in vitro methyltransferase assays showed that CMTR2 exhibits 2′-O-ribose methylation activity toward Spdya mRNA but not toward Mov10l1 mRNA, suggesting that CMTR2 may regulate the expression stability of these two genes through distinct mechanisms. In conclusion, this study provides novel insights into the role and mechanisms of CMTR2 in maintaining spermatogenesis and male fertility.

Materials and methods

Mice and ethics

The conditional knockout mice and knock-in mice were obtained from Cyagen Biosciences. Briefly, for generating the conditional knockout model, CMTR2flox/flox mice were first created by inserting loxP sites in the same direction flanking exon 2 of the Cmtr2 gene (Cre recombinase-mediated recombination is expected to excise the intervening genomic region). The Amh-Cre, Ddx4-Cre, and Stra8-Cre transgenic lines express the Cre recombinase from the Amh, Ddx4, and Stra8 promoters, respectively. We crossed CMTR2flox/flox mice with Amh-Cre, Ddx4-Cre, and Stra8-Cre mice to generate Cmtr2 conditional knockout mice (AmhCre/+; Cmtr2flox/flox, Ddx4Cre/+; Cmtr2flox/flox and Stra8Cre/+; Cmtr2flox/flox), in which Cre recombinase is expressed in Sertoli cells, germ cells, and spermatogonia, respectively. CMTR2loxP/loxP mice were used as the control group. For generating the knock-in mice, CMTR2-KI mice were first created by inserting the 3× Flag tag sequence at the 3′ end of exon 2 of the Cmtr2 gene. We used wild-type C57BL/6J mice as the control group for the CMTR2-KI mouse study. The sgRNA target sequences are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The primer sequences used for genotyping are listed in Supplementary Table S2. Wild-type C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the animal core facility of Nanjing Medical University. Mice were maintained under a 12/12 h dark-light cycle at 22°C with free access to food and water. The study was conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and was approved by the Animal Ethical and Welfare Committee of Nanjing Medical University (license number: 2402015).

Plasmid construction and siRNA synthesis

Plasmids (CMTR2WT-Flag and CMTR2MUT-Flag cloned in pcDNA3.1 vectors) for transfection of GC-2 cells were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Inc. (Shanghai, China). Cmtr2 small interfering RNA (siRNAs) were synthesized by GenePharma (Suzhou, China), and the sequences of siRNAs are listed in Supplementary Table S3. The siRNA powder was then diluted with DEPC water to a storage concentration of 20 µM and stored at − 80°C for use.

Cell culture and transfection

GC-2 cells were maintained in DMEM medium (Cytiva, SH30243.01) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. For plasmid DNA overexpression assays in GC-2 cells, plasmid DNA was combined with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, L3000015) in Opti-MEM reduced serum medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 31985070) and incubated at ambient temperature for 15 min. The plasmid DNA-to-Lipofectamine 3000 ratio was determined based on the manufacturer’s guidelines. This transfection complex was subsequently added to the cell culture medium, followed by incubation at 37°C with 5% CO₂ for 48 h. Post-incubation, samples were harvested for downstream analyses including immunoblotting, immunoprecipitation, or immunofluorescence staining. For RNA interference experiments in GC-2 cells, siRNA was mixed with Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 13778075) in Opti-MEM medium and incubated at room temperature for 5 min. The siRNA-to-Lipofectamine RNAiMAX ratio was calculated according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting siRNA-lipid complex was then introduced into the cell culture medium to proceed with subsequent experimental procedures.

Isolation of mouse primary Leydig cells, Sertoli cells, and germ cells

Primary Leydig cells, Sertoli cells, and germ cells were separated following previously validated protocols [22–24]. Initially, testes were dissected out, and the outer tunica albuginea was stripped away under the stereomicroscope. The remaining testicular tissue was then subjected to enzymatic digestion in 10 ml of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10010023), supplemented with 2 mg/ml of collagenase IV (GLPBIO, GC19591) and 1 mg/ml of DNase I (Sigma–Aldrich, DN25). This digestion process was carried out at 37°C for 25 to 35 min, with gentle shaking to ensure uniform digestion.

To isolate Leydig cells, the digested tissue suspension was diluted with PBS to reach a final volume of 50 ml. After allowing the suspension to settle under normal gravity for 30 min, the supernatant, which contained Leydig cells, was carefully collected and centrifuged at 250 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The resulting cell pellet was then resuspended in 10 ml of M199 medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11150067) and carefully layered onto a discontinuous Percoll gradient (Sigma–Aldrich, P4937). This gradient consisted of four 10 ml layers with densities of 1.035, 1.076, 1.085, and 1.095 g/ml, all prepared in PBS. Centrifugation of the gradient at 800 × g for 30 min at 4°C separated the cells based on density. The fraction located between the 12 and 13 ml marks was collected and centrifuged again at 250 × g for 10 min to obtain a pellet of purified Leydig cells, which were then ready for further experimental use.

For the isolation of Sertoli cells, seminiferous tubules devoid of Leydig cells were first obtained by centrifuging the tissue at 100 × g for 30 s at 4°C and then washing it three times with PBS. These tubules were subsequently digested in a solution containing 1 mg/ml of collagenase IV, 1 mg/ml of DNase I, and 1 mg/ml of hyaluronidase (Sigma–Aldrich, H3506) for 20 min at 37°C with shaking. After sedimentation and washing, the tubules underwent further digestion with 2 mg/ml of hyaluronidase and 1 mg/ml of DNase I for 15 to 20 min at 37°C to eliminate peritubular myoid cells. Following repeated washes, the tubule fragments, now primarily containing germ and Sertoli cells, were treated with 1 mg/ml of trypsin (DIFCO, 215250) and 1 mg/ml of DNase I for 5 min at 37°C, with the reaction being terminated by the addition of FBS. The cell suspension was then filtered through 70 and 40 μm strainers and centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The cell pellet was resuspended in DMEM/F12 medium (Cytiva, SH30023.01) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10270106), transferred to culture plates, and incubated for 1 h at 37°C under 5% CO₂. Non-adherent germ cells were removed, and fresh culture medium was added to the plates to continue culturing the adherent Sertoli cells. After 24 h, a brief hypotonic treatment (20 mM Tris, pH 7.4) was applied for 2 min to lyse any remaining germ cells, and the Sertoli cells were then collected for subsequent experiments.

Germ cell populations at distinct developmental stages were isolated from C57BL/6J mice using the STA-PUT method, which separates cells based on sedimentation velocity at unit gravity [24]. Specifically, spermatogonia were harvested from P6 mice, while spermatocytes and spermatids were collected from adult mice.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

Total RNA was isolated from testis tissues or cellular samples utilizing the Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme, product number R401-01), strictly adhering to the guidelines provided by the manufacturer. Subsequently, the isolated RNA underwent reverse transcription to synthesize complementary DNA (cDNA) employing the Reverse Transcription Kit (Vazyme, R211-01). For the quantification of relative mRNA expression levels, the Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q311-02) was utilized, with reactions conducted on an Applied Biosystems 7500 real-time PCR instrument. The expression data were analyzed via the 2–ΔΔCT computational method, employing 18S ribosomal RNA as the endogenous control for normalization. The sequences of primers employed in this study are detailed in Supplementary Table S2.

Fertility test

To assess male fertility, adult male mice from control, Ddx4-GcKO, and ScKO groups were individually mated with two wild-type female mice per cage. Continuous breeding was maintained for at least three months. Throughout this period, daily checks were performed for the presence of vaginal plugs, which are formed after mating and indicate successful copulation. The number of newborn mice was tallied within two days after their birth. For each pair of mice, a single reproductive cycle was monitored.

Spermatozoa analysis

Sperm cells were isolated from the cauda epididymis through a gentle extrusion process and subsequently suspended in human tubal fluid medium that had been enriched with 10% FBS. Following this, a 10 µl sperm sample was incubated at a constant temperature of 37°C for a duration of 10 min. After the incubation period, computer-assisted semen analysis (CASA, Hamilton-Thorne Research, USA) was carried out. The purpose of this analysis was to determine the total sperm count and assess motility-related parameters, including the quantity of motile sperm and the number of sperm exhibiting forward progression.

H&E staining

Initially, the mouse testes and the cauda epididymis were surgically removed and promptly placed into a 10% Hartman’s fixative solution (Sigma–Aldrich, H0290) at room temperature for a 24-h fixation period. Subsequently, the fixed tissues were subjected to paraffin embedding. After embedding, tissue sections with a thickness of 6 μm were generated. These tissue sections were then deparaffinized by undergoing two separate 10-min treatments with xylene. Following deparaffinization, the sections were rehydrated. This rehydration process involved sequential immersion in absolute ethanol twice, with each immersion lasting 10 min, then in 95% ethanol for 10 min, and finally in 70% ethanol for 10 min. Next, the slides carrying the tissue sections were rinsed with water twice. After that, they were stained with hematoxylin for 5 min, followed by a 10-min wash with running water. The sections were then briefly treated with 0.1% hydrochloric acid for 5 s and further washed until they acquired a blue color. Afterwards, the slides were counterstained with eosin for 20 s. Then, they were dehydrated. The dehydration steps included immersion in 70% and 90% ethanol, each for 15 s, followed by 2-min treatments with absolute ethanol and 15-min treatments with xylene. Finally, the slides were mounted using neutral balsam, left to dry overnight at 65°C, and examined under a Nikon-112 microscope (Nikon, Japan).

Immunoblotting

Testis samples or alternative tissues were subjected to lysis using RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0013D) that incorporated protease inhibitors (MedChemExpress, HY-K0010). The lysates were then kept on ice for 20 min. Subsequently, protein loading buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0015) was added to the lysates, and the mixtures were denatured by immersion in 95°C water for 5 min. For each sample, around 20 μg of proteins were loaded and separated via electrophoresis (Bio-Rad, USA). Following electrotransfer (Bio-Rad, USA), PVDF membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 88518) were blocked in 5% non-fat dry milk for 1 h. After that, they were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. The membranes were washed three times with TBST (Beyotime Biotechnology, ST677) and then incubated with secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, protein bands were visualized using ECL (Beyotime Biotechnology, P0018) and immunoblotting detection systems (GE Healthcare, USA). Relevant information about the antibodies used in immunoblotting is provided in Supplementary Table S4.

Immunofluorescence and TUNEL staining in testicular sections

Testes were harvested from both control and cKO mice, followed by fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) at 4°C for 12 h. Subsequently, each testis was bisected using a razor blade and subjected to an additional 12-h fixation in 4% PFA at 4°C. The testes were then dehydrated through a sequential series of 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose solutions, embedded in optimal cutting temperature compound (SAKURA, 4583), and subsequently cryosectioned. After obtaining 6-μm cryosections, the samples underwent three rounds of washing with pre-chilled PBS. Next, the samples were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Sigma–Aldrich, T8787) for 10 min and blocked in a 3% BSA/PBS solution for 1 h at room temperature. Primary antibodies, diluted in the blocking solution, were applied to the samples and incubated overnight at 4°C. Following three washes with PBS as required for staining, secondary antibodies were diluted in the blocking solution and applied to the samples for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33342 (Life Technologies, H3570) at room temperature for 15 min, and immunofluorescence signals were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (LSM 800; Zeiss, Germany). Relevant information about the antibodies used in immunofluorescence is provided in Supplementary Table S4. For Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling (TUNEL) staining, testis or cell samples were labeled using the TUNEL Apoptosis Detection Kit (Yeasen Biotechnology, 40307ES60), and the number of TUNEL-positive cells in each seminiferous tubule was counted.

Spermatocyte spreading and immunofluorescence staining

Testes from both control and cKO mice were dissected, and the seminiferous tubules were rinsed in PBS. Following rinsing, the tubules were treated with a hypotonic extraction buffer (composed of 50 mM sucrose, 17 mM trisodium citrate dihydrate, 5 mM EDTA, 0.5 mM DTT, and 1× protease inhibitor cocktail, pH 8.2) for 45 min on ice. The tubules were then minced in 100 mM sucrose (pH 8.2) on a clean glass slide and subjected to gentle pipetting to facilitate germ cell release. The resulting cell suspensions were applied to fresh slides coated with 1% PFA and 0.15% Triton X-100 and incubated overnight at 4°C in a humidified chamber. After incubation, slides were rinsed twice with 0.4% Photo-Flo 200 (Kodak, 2393), air-dried at room temperature, and stored at –80°C for future use. Subsequent immunofluorescence staining was performed using the same tissue immunofluorescence protocol as previously described [25].

Single-cell suspension and sequencing library preparation

The single-cell suspension preparation and sequencing library construction procedures were performed as previously established [26]. Testicular tissues from three biological replicates were immediately placed in GEXSCOPE™ Tissue Preservation Solution (Singleron Biotechnologies, China) on ice after collection and three washes with cold PBS. Following three additional washes with HBSS, tissues were enzymatically digested in 2 ml GEXSCOPE™ Tissue Dissociation Solution using the Singleron PythoN™ Automated Tissue Dissociation System at 37°C for 15 min. The digested cell suspension was centrifuged at 500 × g for 5 min and resuspended in PBS. Cell viability was determined by trypan blue exclusion assay prior to loading single-cell suspensions into microfluidic chips using the Singleron Matrix® Single Cell Processing System. scRNA-seq libraries were prepared with the GEXSCOPE® Single Cell RNA Library Kit (Singleron Biotechnologies, China), normalized to 4 nM, pooled, and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with 150 bp paired-end configuration.

Data processing for scRNA-seq

The computational analysis of scRNA-seq data followed established methodologies [27–29]. Raw sequencing reads were processed through a customized bioinformatics pipeline to generate gene expression matrices. Reads containing identical cell barcodes were grouped, and unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) were aggregated to quantify transcript counts per gene per cell. Cell quality control was performed using the following thresholds: exclusion of cells with either >71 000 UMIs or mitochondrial gene content exceeding 20%, and removal of cells containing <240 or >8500 detected genes. Clustering and cell type identification were conducted with Seurat (v3.1.1), with cell populations annotated according to established marker gene expression patterns. Multiple characteristic markers were utilized to define each cell identity. Differential expression analysis identified upregulated genes in each cluster using thresholds of ln (fold change) >0.25 and P-value <0.01. Transcripts meeting fold change >1.5 with P-value <0.05 were considered statistically significant differentially expressed genes.

Single cell trajectory construction

The partition-based graph abstraction (PAGA) graph was constructed from the preprocessed Seurat object following established methodologies [30]. Briefly, PCA embeddings derived from Seurat analysis served as input for the scanpy.pp.neighbors function (Scanpy v1.6.0). We then executed scanpy.tl.paga using Seurat-defined clusters as the grouping variable, with subsequent visualization via sc.pl.paga_compare. This approach generates topology-preserving embeddings by initializing a fine-grained graph layout with coarse-grained cluster coordinates. ForceAtlas2 visualization with PAGA initialization produced readily interpretable cell distributions. Pseudotemporal ordering was automatically computed for all cells following manual specification of the root cell, implementing the diffusion pseudotime algorithm. Gene expression dynamics along the pseudotime axis were analyzed using the Rfast2 package, with significant temporally regulated genes identified at a false discovery rate threshold of <1 × 10-7. These genes were subsequently grouped according to their expression pattern similarities across pseudotime.

FISH analysis

Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) was performed utilizing a FISH Kit (RiboBio, Guangzhou, China) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. Custom-designed probes targeting Spdya and Mov10l1 were synthesized by RiboBio. Mouse monoclonal anti-SCP3 antibody (Supplementary Table S4) was employed for spermatocyte labeling. Confocal fluorescence images were captured using a Zeiss LSM800 laser scanning confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss).

RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation assay

The RIP assay was conducted utilizing the Magna RIP™ RNA-Binding Protein Immunoprecipitation Kit (Millipore, 17-704) strictly following the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were lysed using RNA binding protein immunoprecipitation (RIP) lysis buffer at 4°C for a duration of 30 min. Subsequently, the resulting cell lysates were incubated with magnetic beads coated with an anti-Flag antibody. Gapdh mRNA was used as a negative control. The relative abundance of target RNA was quantified via real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR). The primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

For cross-linking and immunoprecipitation (CLIP) assays, cells were first irradiated with 254 nm UV light (400 mJ/cm²) to crosslink RNA-protein interactions in situ [31]. Subsequent steps mirrored the RIP protocol, including lysis, anti-Flag antibody incubation, and RNA recovery, but utilized CLIP-specific stringent wash buffers (0.1% SDS and 0.1% Triton X-100) to reduce background. Both assays were performed in triplicate, and data were analyzed as mean ± SD.

Actinomycin D assay

The cells subjected to transfection were exposed to Actinomycin D (MedChemExpress, HY-17559) at a final concentration of 1 μg/ml for designated time intervals before RNA isolation using Trizol reagent. Subsequently, alterations in RNA expression levels were assessed by RT-qPCR.

Luciferase reporter assay

The genomic regions spanning from −2000 bp to +100 bp relative to the transcription start sites of the Spdya and Mov10l1 genes were individually cloned into the pGL6 luciferase reporter vector (Beyotime). Subsequently, the constructed plasmids were co-transfected into GC-2 cells together with the specified siCmtr2. The Renilla luciferase vector was employed as an internal control. Luciferase activity was measured using the Luciferase Reporter Assay System, following the previously described protocol [31, 32].

Synthesis of recombinant CMTR2 protein

The coding sequence of mouse Cmtr2 was amplified by PCR from mouse testis-derived cDNA and cloned into a pET-28a(+) vector, resulting in a construct that expresses an N-terminal 6× His-tagged fusion protein. The recombinant plasmid, pET-28a(+)-6× His-CMTR2, was verified by sequencing and subsequently transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) competent cells for protein expression. The transformed bacteria were grown in LB medium supplemented with 50 μg/ml kanamycin at 37°C until the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached approximately 0.6–0.8. Protein expression was then induced by adding 0.5 mM isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside, and the culture was incubated at 16°C with shaking at 180 rpm for 14–16 h. Bacterial cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer, and lysed by sonication on ice. The soluble fraction containing the 6× His-tagged CMTR2 protein was collected by centrifugation and applied to a nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid affinity chromatography column (TransGen Biotech, DP101-02). After extensive washing, the bound protein was eluted with an imidazole-containing buffer. The purified protein was concentrated, and its integrity and purity were assessed by SDS–PAGE and Coomassie blue staining. Aliquots of the purified CMTR2 protein were flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at −80°C for subsequent in vitro methyltransferase activity assays.

In vitro methyltransferase assays

The CMTR2 protein was synthesized using the aforementioned method. The first 60 nt of Spdya and Mov10l1 RNAs were subjected to an in vitro methyltransferase assay (Supplementary Table S5). Of note, the start sites of both Spdya and Mov10l1 are confirmed by analyzing the CAGEseq data [33]. The DNA templates were synthesized with T7 Class II promoter at 5′ ends and the RNAs were made via in vitro transcription (IVT) according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific, AM1354). After IVT, we purified the transcripts with G-25 spin column (GE Healthcare, 27-5325-01) and applied 1 μg of each transcript to perform capping assay via the Vaccinia Capping System (NEB, M2080S). After that, the capped RNAs were further purified with G-25 spin column and subjected to the following in vitro methylation assay. To perform the in vitro methyltransferase assay, 250 nM CMTR2 protein (dissolved in the methyltransferase buffer: 5 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 50 mM KOAc, 0.5 mM MgOAc, and 0.5 mM MnCl2),10 µM RNA fragments, and 1 µl 14C-SAM were incubated at 37°C for 30 min. After column purification, the products were digested by Nuclease P1 (1 unit) and resolved by spotting 10 µl of reaction mixture on TLC PEI Cellulose F glass plates (Merck, 1.05725) and developed in 0.45 M (NH4)2SO4 in a TLC chamber for 60 min. For 2D-TLC, the plates were rotated 90° and dissolved for another 60 min. Reaction products were visualized by exposure to storage phosphor screens (GE Healthcare, USA) and scanned on a Typhoon FLA 9500 scanner (GE Healthcare, USA).

Pulse labeling

Pulse labeling experiments were conducted as previously reported with minor modifications [34, 35]. Briefly, GC-2 cells were incubated with 500 μM 4-thiouridine (4sU; Sigma, T4509) for 10 min. After three washes with cold PBS, cells were incubated with fresh medium. Following the indicated chase time, cells were harvested, and total RNA was immediately extracted using the Total RNA Extraction Reagent (Vazyme). Biotinylation was performed by assembling a 1 ml reaction mixture containing 100 μg of 4sU-labeled total RNA, 100 µl 10× biotinylation buffer (100 mM HEPES and 10 mM EDTA), and 400 µl of biotin-HPDP (1 mg/ml dissolved in dimethylformamide; Thermo Fisher Scientific, 21341). The reaction was incubated for 1.5 h at room temperature in the dark with gentle agitation. Biotinylated RNA was captured using 100 µl Dynabeads MyOne Streptavidin C1 beads (Invitrogen, 65002). Finally, the newly transcribed RNA was eluted with 100 µl of 100 mM DTT and recovered by ethanol precipitation. RNA expression levels were calculated by RT-qPCR.

Correlation analysis

Gene set correlation analysis was performed according to established methods [36]. Feature set scores were computed using Seurat’s PercentageFeatureSet function based on average expression values of defined gene groups. Pairwise correlations among these gene signatures were then quantified through Pearson correlation analysis applied directly to the expression matrix. To ensure analytical robustness, cells exhibiting zero expression across all measured features were excluded from the analysis due to their uninformative transcriptional profiles.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 7.0 (La Jolla, USA). Each experiment was repeated at least three times. For comparisons between two groups, Student’s t-test was used when the data met assumptions of normality and equal variance; otherwise, Welch’s t-test was applied. For comparisons among three or more groups, one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used. All data are presented as mean ± SD. P < 0.05 is considered to indicate a significant result.

Result

Deletion of the Cmtr2 in germ cells rather than sertoli cells results in sterility

To characterize the expression profile of Cmtr2 in mice, we first assessed its transcript distribution across multiple tissues and observed ubiquitous expression (Supplementary Fig. S1A). We next evaluated Cmtr2 expression during testicular development by performing RT-qPCR on testes collected at postnatal days (P) 7, 14, 21, 28, 35, 42, 49, and 56. These time points correspond to key stages of spermatogenesis, including spermatogonial proliferation and differentiation (P7), pachytene spermatocyte presence (P14), round spermatid formation (P21), elongating spermatid emergence (P28), and full spermatogenesis (P35 onward). Cmtr2 transcript levels were relatively high at P7 and P14 but gradually declined thereafter (Supplementary Fig. S1B). To further resolve its cellular expression, we purified spermatogonia, spermatocytes, round spermatids, elongating/elongated spermatids, Sertoli cells, and Leydig cells from mouse testes. Consistent with the developmental timecourse, RT-qPCR analysis revealed higher Cmtr2 expression in spermatogonia and spermatocytes, with lower levels detected in spermatids and somatic cells (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

The seminiferous epithelium, serving as the core functional unit of the seminiferous tubules in the testis, plays a pivotal role in spermatogenesis. It is composed of Sertoli cells and germ cells at various developmental stages (spermatogonia, spermatocytes, and spermatids). Through precise intercellular coordination and microenvironmental regulation, it orchestrates the entire developmental process of spermatozoa, from stem cell proliferation to maturation [37]. To investigate the role of CMTR2 in spermatogenesis, we generated Sertoli cell- and germ cell-specific Cmtr2 conditional knockout mice by crossing Cmtr2flox/flox mice with Amh-Cre or Ddx4-Cre mice, respectively. Amh-Cre drives Cmtr2 deletion specifically in Sertoli cells, whereas Ddx4-Cre drives Cmtr2 deletion in germ cells beginning at the primordial germ cell stage. The resulting AmhCre/+; Cmtr2flox/flox and Ddx4Cre/+; Cmtr2flox/flox mice are hereafter referred to as Amh-ScKO and Ddx4-GcKO (Supplementary Fig. S1D and E [38, 39].

Fertility tests were first performed on control and conditional knockout (cKO) mice at 8 weeks of age. Interestingly, while Ddx4-GcKO mice were completely sterile, Amh-ScKO mice exhibited fertility comparable to the control group (Fig. 1A). We then collected the testes from 8-week-old mice and observed significantly reduced testis size and testis-to-body weight ratio in Ddx4-GcKO mice compared with both control and Amh-ScKO mice (Fig. 1B and C). This testicular atrophy in Ddx4-GcKO mice indicated impaired spermatogenesis, whereas normal spermatogenesis was maintained in Amh-ScKO and control mice. Morphological examination of the epididymis revealed a complete absence of sperm in Ddx4-GcKO mice, whereas abundant spermatozoa were observed in both Amh-ScKO and control mice (Fig. 1D). Subsequent sperm quantification confirmed non-obstructive azoospermia in Ddx4-GcKO mice (Fig. 1E). To elucidate the etiology of non-obstructive azoospermia in Ddx4-GcKO mice, we performed histological analysis of testicular sections using hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. Morphological examination revealed severe seminiferous tubule disorganization with extensive vacuolization in Ddx4-GcKO mice, accompanied by a complete absence of both round and elongated spermatids. In contrast, testicular morphology in Amh-ScKO mice was indistinguishable from control mice (Fig. 1H). Our findings demonstrate that CMTR2 expression in germ cells is essential for normal seminiferous epithelium function, playing a critical role in spermatogenesis through the regulation of germ cell development. In contrast, CMTR2 appears to be functionally redundant in Sertoli cells, as its ablation in these cells showed no discernible impact on spermatogenesis.

CMTR2 is required for the progression of spermatogenesis

Spermatogenesis occurs in three distinct phases where spermatogonia undergo mitotic proliferation to form primary spermatocytes, these primary spermatocytes complete meiotic division to generate haploid spermatids, and finally, the spermatids undergo morphological transformation into mature spermatozoa [3]. To clarify at which stage spermatogenesis is arrested in Ddx4-GcKO mice, we quantified the numbers of different types of germ cells using markers specific to germ cells at various developmental stages. We first confirmed that the number of TRA98-positive germ cells was significantly reduced in Ddx4-GcKO mice compared with the control group (Fig. 2A and B) [40]. In contrast, no significant difference was observed in the number of SOX9-positive Sertoli cells between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. S2A and B [41]. We next employed PLZF and LIN28 to label undifferentiated spermatogonia and KIT to label differentiated spermatogonia [42–44]. The results demonstrated comparable numbers of undifferentiated and differentiated spermatogonia between Ddx4-GcKO and control mice (Fig. 2C and D and Supplementary Fig. S2C–F). Given that DMRT1 is expressed in undifferentiated spermatogonia, differentiated spermatogonia, and Sertoli cells [45], we used DMRT1 and TRA98 double staining to label total spermatogonia and likewise found no significant difference between control and Ddx4-GcKO mice (Supplementary Fig. S2H and G. Subsequent labeling of primary spermatocytes with DMC1 and SCP3 revealed significantly reduced spermatocyte numbers in Ddx4-GcKO mice relative to controls (Fig. 2E–H) [46]. Furthermore, it was difficult to identify round and elongated spermatids in the seminiferous tubules of Ddx4-GcKO mice (Fig. 1H). To investigate spermatocyte depletion, TUNEL staining confirmed substantially elevated apoptosis levels in Ddx4-GcKO testes (Fig. 2I and J). Collectively, although meiosis is initiated and spermatocytes are observed, no postmeiotic germ cells were detected, which indicates that the deletion of CMTR2 results in meiotic arrest, underscoring the essential role of CMTR2 in male meiosis.

CMTR2 is dispensable for spermatogonial proliferation

Since CMTR2 is highly expressed in spermatogonia, and to determine whether the meiotic arrest and spermatocyte reduction caused by Cmtr2 deletion are secondary to defects in spermatogonial proliferation, we generated a spermatogonia-specific conditional knockout model (Stra8Cre/+; Cmtr2flox/flox, hereafter “Stra8-GcKO”) by crossing Cmtr2flox/flox mice with Stra8-Cre mice (Supplementary Fig. S1D and E [47]. Morphological analysis revealed that the seminiferous tubules of Stra8-GcKO testes were severely disorganized, accompanied by extensive vacuolization and a complete absence of both round and elongated spermatids (Supplementary Fig. S3A).

To determine the stage at which germ cell loss is initiated in Stra8-GcKO mice, we next performed a quantitative analysis of germ cell populations. The number of total germ cells (TRA98-positive cells) was significantly reduced in Stra8-GcKO testes compared with controls (Supplementary Fig. S3B and C, whereas the number of Sertoli cells (SOX9-positive cells) remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S4A and B. Further analysis showed that the numbers of undifferentiated spermatogonia (PLZF-positive and LIN28-positive cells), differentiating spermatogonia (KIT-positive cells), and total spermatogonia (DMRT1 and TRA98 double-positive cells) were comparable between Stra8-GcKO and control mice (Supplementary Fig. S4C–J). In contrast, spermatocyte (DMC1-positive and SCP3-positive cell) numbers were markedly decreased, and neither round nor elongated spermatids were observed (Supplementary Fig. S5A–D). Taken together, Stra8-GcKO and Ddx4-GcKO mice display highly comparable phenotypes. This finding suggests that the meiotic defects observed in Ddx4-GcKO mice do not stem from abnormalities in spermatogonial proliferation, but rather reflect a direct and essential function of CMTR2 in progressing through meiosis.

Deletion of Cmtr2 leads to defective meiosis

To elucidate the molecular defects in spermatocytes from germ cell-specific Cmtr2 knockout mice during meiosis, we employed stage-specific markers to analyze meiotic progression. The distinct substages of prophase I (leptotene, zygotene, pachytene, and diplotene) were identified based on the dynamic localization patterns of the synaptonemal complex (SC) component SCP3 and DNA DSB marker γH2AX [48, 49]. In normal spermatocytes, γH2AX signals were diffusely distributed throughout the nucleus during leptotene and zygotene stages, reflecting the presence of unrepaired DNA DSBs. During pachytene and diplotene stages, however, γH2AX signals became restricted to the XY body, while their disappearance from autosomes indicated successful repair of DSBs in these regions [50]. We prepared meiotic spreads using the testes of control mice and Ddx4-GcKO mice, followed by immunofluorescence staining for γH2AX and SCP3. While control and Ddx4-GcKO spermatocytes showed comparable diffuse nuclear γH2AX distribution during leptotene and zygotene stages, we observed abnormal γH2AX retention on autosomes during pachytene and diplotene stages in Ddx4-GcKO mice, in addition to the expected XY body localization (Fig. 3A). Quantitative analysis revealed a significant increase in the proportion of zygotene spermatocytes but a reduction in the proportion of diplotene/diplotene-like spermatocytes in Ddx4-GcKO mice compared to controls (Fig. 3B). Although the proportion of pachytene/pachytene-like spermatocytes was similar between groups, Ddx4-GcKO mice exhibited a significantly higher proportion of abnormal pachytene-like spermatocytes (Fig. 3C). These findings demonstrate that germ cell-specific Cmtr2 deletion impairs autosomal DNA damage repair during pachytene and causes meiotic arrest at the zygotene stage.

Following DNA break formation, homologous chromosome invasion and crossover events occur through the recruitment of the MLH1/MLH3 heterodimer during the mid-to-late pachytene stage [51]. Our analysis revealed significantly diminished MLH1 localization in Ddx4-GcKO pachytene-like spermatocytes, demonstrating defective crossover formation (Fig. 3D and E). The replication protein A (RPA) complex, consisting of RPA1, RPA2, and RPA3 subunits, plays a critical role in meiotic recombination through its single-stranded DNA binding activity [52]. Using RPA2 as a marker for the RPA complex, we observed distinct focal localization at DSB sites from leptotene to pachytene stages in control spermatocytes. However, the number of RPA2 foci is significantly reduced in zygotene and pachytene-like spermatocytes of Ddx4-GcKO mice (Fig. 3F and G). Collectively, these results demonstrate that a subset of Ddx4-GcKO spermatocytes exhibit defective resolution of DNA breaks, impaired crossover formation, and incomplete meiotic recombination. These meiotic defects explain the increased apoptosis in spermatocytes and the meiotic prophase I arrest observed in Ddx4-GcKO mouse testes.

The deletion of Cmtr2 results in the arrest of the first wave of spermatogenesis at the zygotene spermatocyte stage

The first wave of spermatogenesis is initiated postnatally. By postnatal day 10 (P10), spermatogonia differentiate into spermatocytes. Subsequently, zygotene and pachytene spermatocytes sequentially emerge within the seminiferous epithelium on P12 and P14, respectively. Ultimately, on P35, mature spermatozoa are released into the epididymis, signifying the end of the first wave of spermatogenesis [53]. To determine whether the zygotene arrest caused by Cmtr2 deletion in germ cells occurs during the first wave of spermatogenesis, we initially performed immunofluorescence staining on testes from postnatal days 6, 10, 12, 14, and 18 using the germ cell marker DDX4 and the Sertoli cell marker SOX9, followed by quantification of germ cell numbers [54, 55]. Our results demonstrated that germ cells in Ddx4-GcKO mice began to decline starting from P12, when zygotene spermatocytes predominated in the seminiferous tubules, consistent with prior findings from spermatocyte counts in adult testes (Fig. 4A and B). Notably, consistent with the Ddx4-GcKO mice, we observed that germ cells in the Stra8-GcKO mice also began to decline starting from P12 (Supplementary Fig. S6A-B). To further characterize the type of germ cell loss, we conducted immunofluorescence staining for LIN28, PLZF, KIT, DMRT1, DMC1, and SCP3 in testes from P12 Ddx4-GcKO mice. Our results revealed no significant differences in the numbers of undifferentiated spermatogonia (PLZF-positive and LIN28-positive cells), differentiating spermatogonia (KIT-positive cells), and total spermatogonia (DMRT1 and TRA98 double-positive cells) between Ddx4-GcKO and control mice, whereas the numbers of spermatocytes (DMC1-positive and SCP3-positive cells) were significantly reduced in Ddx4-GcKO mice (Fig. 4C–J and Supplementary Fig. S7A–D). TUNEL staining further confirmed substantial apoptosis of germ cells in Ddx4-GcKO mice (Fig. 4K–L). In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that the deletion of Cmtr2 in germ cells leads to the arrest of the first wave of spermatogenesis at the zygotene stage of prophase I of meiosis.

CMTR2-associated cellular landscape in testes

To elucidate transcriptional regulatory networks at single-cell resolution, we performed single-cell RNA sequencing on testicular tissues from control and Ddx4-GcKO mice at P12. After stringent quality control, 69 362 high-quality cells were retained for downstream analysis. The sequencing depth averaged 3620 median UMIs and 1992 median nGene per cell. Unsupervised clustering combined with uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) revealed 12 distinct cell populations, including all major expected testicular cell types such as undifferentiated spermatogonia (Undiff.SPG), differentiated spermatogonia (Diff.SPG), pre-leptotene spermatocytes, leptotene spermatocytes, zygotene spermatocytes, Sertoli cells, Leydig cells, peritubular myoid cells, endothelial cells, APOE⁺ macrophages, CD14⁺ macrophages, and T cells (Fig. 5A). Analysis of three biological replicates per group confirmed consistent clustering across samples, with minimal batch effects and preservation of genotype-specific differences. UMAP visualizations clearly delineated the major cell populations in both control and Ddx4-GcKO mice testes (Fig. 5B). Expression patterns of canonical marker genes were further illustrated using dot plots and feature-level UMAPs, demonstrating their distribution across identified clusters (Fig. 5C and D). To identify cluster-enriched transcripts, we visualized the top five most highly expressed genes per cell type in a heatmap (Fig. 5E). Together, these analyses establish a single-cell transcriptomic atlas of the postnatal testis in the context of Cmtr2 deficiency, providing a foundational resource for identifying cell type-specific regulatory targets.

Spdya and Mov10l1 are core targets for CMTR2 during meiosis

We next applied PAGA to germ cell populations from control and Ddx4-GcKO testes to characterize CMTR2-dependent germ cell features. This approach provides an interpretable graph representation of cellular dynamics, based on the underlying data structure [30]. Pseudotime analysis with accompanying box plots illustrated the predicted progression of germ cell differentiation under normal conditions (Fig. 6A). Reconstruction of developmental trajectories further suggested potential lineage relationships among germ cell subtypes (Fig. 6B). In Ddx4-GcKO mice, signature scoring of gene sets related to stem cell maintenance and differentiation indicated no significant changes in spermatogonia from Ddx4-GcKO mice (Supplementary Fig. S8A and B. In contrast, meiotic and spermatogenesis-related transcript sets were markedly altered (Supplementary Fig. S8C and D. Transcripts of regulatory genes involved in spermatogonial maintenance and differentiation remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. S9A–D). Comparative trajectory analysis revealed a differentiation blockade at the zygotene spermatocyte stage in Ddx4-GcKO testes (Fig. 6C). These results further support a role for CMTR2 in the regulation of meiotic progression rather than spermatogonial differentiation.

To delineate the molecular basis of CMTR2 function in meiosis, we systematically analyzed DEGs across the three spermatocyte subpopulations affected by Cmtr2 deletion. The analysis revealed comprehensive transcriptional alterations, with distinct upregulation and downregulation patterns (Fig. 6D). We specifically focused on meiosis-associated genes potentially regulated by CMTR2. From the downregulated DEGs, we identified nine genes with established roles in meiotic division (Fig. 6E and F and Supplementary Fig. S10). It is noteworthy that we observed significantly downregulated expression of Spdya and Mov10l1, specifically in spermatocytes of the Ddx4-GcKO group compared to controls, in contrast to other genes associated with meiosis (Fig. 6G and Supplementary Fig. S11). The expression patterns of Spdya and Mov10l1 were further visualized using UMAP across the primary populations of the control, Ddx4-GcKO, and their merged groups (Fig. 6H). The analogous expression profiles of Spdya and Mov10l1 were also mapped onto the differentiation trajectory fitted by PAGA in germline populations (Supplementary Fig. S12). Building upon this data, we have identified Spdya and Mov10l1 as fundamental meiotic targets regulated by CMTR2.

To investigate the molecular mechanism by which CMTR2 regulates Spdya and Mov10l1 expression, we compared their mRNA levels between control and Ddx4-GcKO mice in whole P12 testes, as well as in purified spermatogonia and spermatocytes. Subsequently, we performed fluorescence in situ hybridization experiments using fluorescently labeled Spdya and Mov10l1 nucleic acid probes, and the results showed a substantial downregulation in the expression of Spdya and Mov10l1 in SCP3-positive spermatocytes from the testes of Ddx4-GcKO mice (Fig. 7C–F). Immunoblotting also demonstrated that the protein expression levels of SPDYA and MOV10L1 were significantly decreased in the testes of Ddx4-GcKO mice (Fig. 7G–I).

CMTR2 is an mRNA cap-binding protein that utilizes its inherent methyltransferase activity to act on the 5′-cap structure of mRNAs, thereby mediating the 2′-O-ribose methylation of the mRNA cap2 structure [9]. Therefore, we hypothesized that CMTR2 stabilizes Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs by mediating their 2′-O-ribose methylation. To clarify the interaction between CMTR2 and Spdya/Mov10l1 mRNAs, we overexpressed wild-type CMTR2 and a CMTR2 mutant lacking the Adrift-type SAM-dependent 2′-O-MTase domain in the spermatocyte-like cell line GC2 and performed RIP experiments. Wild-type CMTR2 bound to the 5′ UTRs of Spdya and Mov10l1, whereas the binding ability of the mutant CMTR2 to Spdya and Mov10l1 was significantly reduced (Fig. 7J and K). CLIP assays further demonstrated that wild-type CMTR2, but not the CMTR2 mutant lacking the adrift-type SAM-dependent 2′-O-MTase domain, specifically enriched the 5′ UTR fragments of both Spdya and Mov10l1 (Fig. 7L and M).

Due to difficulties obtaining reliable CMTR2-specific antibodies, we generated a knock-in mouse model (KI) with a Flag tag at the CMTR2 C-terminus to study CMTR2-mRNA interactions in vivo (Supplementary Fig. S14A–C). Immunoblotting confirmed successful Flag tagging of CMTR2 in the testis of KI mice (Supplementary Fig. S14D). Analysis of testis samples from KI mice of different ages and cell types showed that CMTR2-Flag expression matched the CMTR2 mRNA pattern we observed in wild-type mice (Supplementary Fig. S14E–G). RIP assay demonstrated that CMTR2-Flag could bind 5′ UTRs of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs in testes (Fig. 7N and O). Our findings indicated that full-length Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs, but not 5′ UTR-deletion mutants, efficiently retrieved CMTR2-Flag in the KI testes. Collectively, these in vitro and in vivo results corroborate the structural data and establish that CMTR2 could bind to the 5′ UTRs of both Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs.

CMTR2 maintains the stability of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs and mediates 2′-O-ribose methylation of Spdya mRNA

Next, we used GC2 cells to investigate whether CMTR2 stabilizes Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs. When Cmtr2 was knocked down in GC2 cells, RT-qPCR showed significantly reduced expression of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs, along with shorter mRNA half-lives (Fig. 8A–D). In contrast, overexpression of wild-type CMTR2, but not mutant CMTR2, in GC2 cells increased Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNA levels and extended their half-lives (Supplementary Fig. S15C–F). Furthermore, using 4-thiouridine pulse labeling, we demonstrated that Cmtr2 knockdown significantly shortens the half-lives of both Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs (Supplementary Fig. S15G and H. These results indicate that CMTR2 binds to and stabilizes Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs. Next, to investigate whether CMTR2 regulates the stability of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs through its methyltransferase activity, we performed in vitro methyltransferase assays. We generated capped RNA substrates encompassing the first 60 nucleotides of each transcript, ensuring their transcription start sites matched the endogenous ones (Supplementary Fig. S17A and B. After incubating these substrates with recombinant CMTR2 and ¹4C-SAM, thin-layer chromatography analysis revealed that CMTR2 catalyzed the 2′-O-ribose methylation of Spdya mRNA. Surprisingly, under the same conditions, CMTR2 did not exhibit detectable methyltransferase activity toward Mov10l1 mRNA (Fig. 8E). Next, to further test whether the catalytic activity is required for transcript stabilization, we constructed catalytic-dead point mutants of CMTR2 (K117A, D235A, and K275A) and performed Actinomycin D chase assays [6]. These mutants, while enzymatically inactive, fully retained the ability to stabilize both Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs (Fig. 8F and G). This suggests that CMTR2 binds to and regulates Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNA in a manner independent of its methyltransferase activity.

Discussion

In human cells, the formation of cap1 and cap2 structures is catalyzed by the specific methyltransferases CMTR1 and CMTR2, respectively. The cap1 structure accounts for 80%–100% of transcripts, while the cap2 structure is present in approximately 40%–50% [7, 20]. Previous studies have demonstrated that depletion of CMTR1 in male germ cells results in meiotic arrest, implying non-redundant roles for CMTR1 and CMTR2 during germ cell development [9]. Transcriptome sequencing analysis of mice with a systemic deficiency in Cmtr1 and Cmtr2 revealed a lack of significant overlap in the gene sets regulated by these two genes, indicating that they modulate largely distinct transcriptomes. This functional dichotomy appears to be evolutionarily conserved, as a similar non-overlapping specificity in target transcript regulation has been demonstrated for CMTR1 and CMTR2 in Drosophila [15]. Based on these findings, this study aimed to characterize the functional role of CMTR2 in the male reproductive system using conditional knockout mouse models with targeted deletion of Cmtr2 in male germ cells or Sertoli cells. The results demonstrate that deletion of Cmtr2 in germ cells leads to phenotypic defects similar to those caused by Cmtr1 deletion, including meiotic arrest and failure of spermatogenesis. Interestingly, Sertoli cell-specific knockout of Cmtr2 did not affect spermatogenesis or male fertility in mice, suggesting that CMTR2 is not required for the establishment or maintenance of Sertoli cell function. Given that Sertoli cells exit mitosis and undergo terminal differentiation until postnatal day 15 in mice, while Amh-cre-mediated gene deletion occurs during embryonic stages, the loss of Cmtr2 in Sertoli cells appears to have no detectable impact on their mitotic activity [38, 56]. Furthermore, in this study, we also found that deletion of Cmtr2 in germ cells did not affect the population sizes of undifferentiated and differentiated spermatogonia. These findings indicate that CMTR2 is dispensable for mitotic regulation.

DSB repair defects represent one of the core causes leading to meiotic arrest, a finding that has been thoroughly validated in multiple gene knockout models [57–59]. Notably, this study confirmed that Ddx4-GcKO mice exhibited similar meiotic arrest at the zygotene stage and accumulation of DSBs. Moreover, we found that the absence of CMTR2 significantly reduces the mRNA stability of the meiotic regulatory genes Spdya and Mov10l1. SPDYA, a key regulatory factor for DSB repair, reduces the formation of H2AX foci, whereas its knockdown promotes DNA damage signaling [60]. Spdya-deficient germ cells arrest at the zygotene stage of meiosis [61]. MOV10L1, a key enzyme for piRNA processing, has decreased expression that leads to abnormal increases in LINE-1 transposon activity, thereby inducing DNA damage [62, 63]. Mov10l1 deficiency in germ cells similarly leads to meiotic arrest [64]. These findings collectively indicate that CMTR2 maintains meiotic progression by regulating the mRNA stability of Spdya and Mov10l1. Its deficiency triggers excessive DSB accumulation and repair defects. Since DSB repair defects are often accompanied by abnormalities in homologous chromosome crossover and recombination [65], we observed abnormal meiotic crossover and recombination in Cmtr2-deficient spermatocytes.

The stability of mRNA has long been recognized as a central aspect of the precise regulation of gene expression [66]. Among various mechanisms, multiple mRNA methylation modifications have been established as key regulatory strategies [67, 68]. Additionally, studies suggest that internal 2′-O-methylation mediated by the 2′-O-methyltransferase fibrillarin may stabilize peroxidasin mRNA [69]. Cap1-containing RNAs exhibit significant resistance to the decapping enzyme DXO, indicating that 2′-O-methylation of the first nucleotide may sterically hinder enzymatic access to the cleavage site, thereby enhancing the structural integrity of the 5′ end [18]. Recent research has revealed that cap2 contributes to mRNA stability, suggesting a role for CMTR2-mediated methyltransferase activity in regulating mRNA stability [20]. In this study, we propose that the RNA 2′-O-methyltransferase CMTR2 targets the 5′ UTRs of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs via its adrift-type SAM-dependent 2′-O-MTase domain and regulates the stability of both transcripts. Surprisingly, in vitro methyltransferase assays showed that only Spdya mRNA, but not Mov10l1 mRNA, undergoes 2′-O-methylation by CMTR2. This indicates that CMTR2 can stabilize specific mRNAs in a methyltransferase activity-independent manner in male germ cells. Certain RNA-binding proteins (RBPs) enhance mRNA stability by specifically recognizing sequence or structural elements within the 5′ UTR. For example, ELAVL1 binds the 5′ UTR of NDUFB6 mRNA, a gene relevant to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, to regulate its stability [70]. Although CMTR2 has not been classified as a canonical RBP and is primarily known for catalyzing methylation of the RNA cap2 structure, this study reveals that CMTR2 also acts as an RBP by binding the 5′ UTRs of mRNAs and thereby contributes to their stability.

Collectively, cap1 and cap2 modifications play complementary and hierarchical roles in protecting mRNAs. In terms of conferring resistance to nuclease degradation, existing evidence indicates that 2′-O-methylation of the first nucleotide (cap1) constitutes a sufficient and decisive barrier against the decapping and exonuclease activities of the DXO protein [18]. Therefore, cap1 modification serves as the foundation for safeguarding the intracellular structural integrity of mRNA. Conversely, in avoiding inappropriate activation of innate immunity, the modifications exhibit a clear hierarchy of effectiveness. Cap0 RNA acts as a high-affinity ligand and potent activator of RIG-I. Although cap1 substantially weakens this binding, it still carries a pro-inflammatory risk when present at elevated global levels. In contrast, the dual methylation of cap2 provides a higher-order capacity for immune evasion, further suppressing RIG-I recognition and downstream signaling [20]. Thus, while cap1 ensures the structural integrity of mRNA, cap2 functions more as a sophisticated “self” marker, offering a deeper layer of immune tolerance at the transcriptome level. This “division of labor” may explain the universal presence of cap1 versus the selective nature of cap2, as essential basal protection is indispensable, whereas advanced immune stealth is dynamically regulated based on mRNA lifespan or cellular context.

Several questions remain for future investigation. First, although our results indicate that germ cell-specific deletion of Cmtr2 does not alter the numbers of undifferentiated or differentiated spermatogonia, the reduced spermatocyte population in GcKO testes, together with the lack of spermatogonial stem cell transplantation assays, precludes the formal exclusion of a contribution of CMTR2 to spermatogonial differentiation or meiotic initiation. Therefore, the meiotic defects observed in GcKO spermatocytes may originate from earlier disruptions, including impaired spermatogonial differentiation and defective meiotic entry. Second, cap2 can shape gene expression, especially for long-lived transcripts, to further enhance their stability in cells and increase their protein expression levels [20]. Our research has found that CMTR2 can maintain the stability of Mov10l1 mRNA in a manner independent of its methyltransferase activity. However, since Spdya mRNA can be modified through 2′-O-methylation by CMTR2, we cannot precisely determine the specific regulatory mechanism by which CMTR2 affects the stability of Spdya mRNA. The results of the actinomycin D assays indicate that both Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs have similar half-lives, each lasting approximately 12 h. This suggests that neither of them belongs to the category of long-lived transcripts. Furthermore, since the methyltransferase-inactive mutant of CMTR2 can still maintain the stability of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs, it is more plausible that CMTR2 maintains the stability of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNA through direct binding to its 5′UTR rather than via 2′-O-ribose methylation. Third, emerging evidence indicates that Cap2 functions beyond mRNA processing and plays a critical immunomodulatory role. Although RIG-I is primarily activated by uncapped or Cap0 RNA, Cap1 RNA can still weakly bind and activate RIG-I at high concentrations. Recent studies show that the dual methylation of Cap2 further reduces this binding affinity, thereby attenuating the inappropriate activation of RIG-I by endogenous RNAs and helping to maintain immune homeostasis [20]. However, our single-cell transcriptome sequencing analysis suggested that Cmtr2 depletion in germ cells does not lead to significant alterations in the RIG-I-like receptor signaling pathway. We hypothesize that this phenomenon can be attributed to the distinct immune-privileged microenvironment of male germ cells, especially spermatocytes [71]. This specialized environment is actively maintained by Sertoli cells, which function to create a protective niche. Furthermore, the innate immune signaling pathways intrinsic to germ cells exist in a suppressed condition, thereby resulting in a less sensitive surveillance of cytoplasmic RNAs compared with that exhibited by somatic cells [72].

In conclusion, the present findings unequivocally establish that CMTR2 is a meiosis regulatory gene that functions during meiotic prophase. It plays a pivotal role in orchestrating crucial meiotic events, encompassing DSB repair, homologous recombination, and crossover generation. Furthermore, this study confirms that CMTR2 enhances the stability of mRNAs encoding meiotic prophase regulators through a unique mechanism independent of its intrinsic enzymatic activity, underscoring its indispensable role in the progression of meiosis (Fig. 9).

Schematic diagram illustrating the crucial role of CMTR2 in the progression of prophase I during male meiosis. CMTR2 binds to the 5′ UTRs of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs, which encode key regulatory proteins functioning in meiotic prophase I, and stabilizes their expression. The stabilization of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs by CMTR2 is indispensable for ensuring the efficiency of DNA DSB repair, crossover formation, and homologous recombination during meiotic progression. In the absence of CMTR2, the stability of Spdya and Mov10l1 mRNAs is compromised, resulting in their accelerated degradation and reduced transcript half-lives, impaired DNA repair, and aberrant crossing over and recombination. Consequently, meiosis is arrested at the zygotene stage of prophase I.
