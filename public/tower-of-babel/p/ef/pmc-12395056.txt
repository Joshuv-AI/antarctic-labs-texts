Effect of Blue Covarine Toothpastes on Tooth Colour: A Randomised Crossover Study
Authors: Lu Zhang, Andrew Joiner, Ashok K Gupta, Matteo Barili, Suzanne Platten, Matthew D McDonald, Wen Luo, Jane R Matheson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo measure the effect of toothpastes containing blue covarine on tooth whiteness and yellowness.MethodsThis was a randomised, double-blind, 4-period, crossover design study conducted at a Chinese clinical research facility. Sixty-six participants with 2 natural upper central incisors (UCIs) with Vita tooth shade in range 2M2 to 3M3, without visible damage, gum recession, restorations or extrinsic/intrinsic stain, were allocated to evaluate 4 test toothpastes in random order using Williams square approach. The toothpastes were (1) silica-based fluoride toothpaste containing clay, charcoal, blue covarine and green covarine (BC+GC), (2) silica-based fluoride toothpaste with blue covarine (BC1), (3) silica-based fluoride toothpaste with a higher level of blue covarine (BC2) and (4) silica-based fluoride toothpaste (control). Tooth colour was measured using a video-digital imaging system. At each test period, static images of UCIs were taken before and after a single brushing with allocated toothpaste. Tooth whiteness index (WIO; primary outcome) and tooth yellowness index (YIO) were calculated from Commission Internationale de l’Eclairage (CIE) XYZ colour values derived from the images. All variables were analysed using paired t-test for comparison within product and ANCOVA for comparison between products (P < .05).ResultsAll toothpastes significantly increased tooth whiteness and reduced tooth yellowness immediately after brushing (P < .0001). BC + GC (n = 61), BC1 (n = 63) and BC2 (n = 63) showed significantly greater increases in WIO and reductions in YIO compared to control (n = 63) (p < .0001) with WIO mean differences ± SE of 5.51 ± 0.40, 3.79 ± 0.40 and 5.00 ± 0.39 and YIO mean differences of –4.37 ± 0.34, –3.20 ± 0.34 and –4.28 ± 0.34, respectively.ConclusionThe toothpastes containing blue covarine effectively whiten teeth and reduce yellowness after a single brushing.Clinical significanceThe toothpaste containing blue and green covarine plus clay and charcoal and two toothpastes containing blue covarine demonstrated tooth whitening and reduction in yellowness immediately after one brush.Study retrospectively registeredNCT06800443 (Clinicaltrials.gov).KEY WORDS: Tooth colour, Perception, Blue covarine, Green covarine, Charcoal, Digital imaging

Introduction

The colour and whiteness of teeth are of aesthetic concern and importance to many individuals. Discontentment with tooth colour has been widely reported in many different adult populations, with percentages ranging from 19.6% to 65.9%.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 For example, 56.2% of adults from a Malaysian population5 and 52.6% of adults from a Chinese population were unhappy with their tooth colour.6 This dissatisfaction, together with a strong desire for whiter teeth, has driven interest among consumers for tooth-whitening solutions.

Tooth discoloration can be intrinsic or extrinsic in origin.8 Intrinsic tooth colour is influenced by both internal morphology and external texture of dentine and enamel.9,10 Extrinsic discoloration is determined by the adsorption of chromogenic components from certain foods, drinks, medications and tobacco products on the tooth surface.11, 12, 13, 14

Tooth colour assessment plays a critical role in both clinical and aesthetic dentistry, and can be performed either visually using shade guides or instrumentally with digital devices.1,15,16 Instrumental methods, such as spectrophotometer and colorimeter, are valued for their reliability and accuracy in colour matching17, 18, 19; however, their contact-based nature may introduce errors due to tooth curvature and translucency and pose challenges for effective cross-infection control during in vivo use.1,16 Non-contact digital imaging systems offer a practical alternative, can minimise the systematic error stemming from translucency and surface curvature,20 while also eliminating the need for physical contact with the tooth surface, thereby significantly reducing the risk of cross-contamination in clinical settings. Moreover, they provide a visual record of how tooth colour changes over time.16

Most digital imaging systems typically rely on Commission Internationale de l'Éclairage L*a*b* (CIELAB) colour space.1,21 The CIELAB colour space defines colours in 3 dimensions associated with 3 perceptual attributes: L*, a* and b*.22 L* represents perceptual lightness and ranges from 0 (black) to 100 (white). The a* and b* coordinates represent red–green and yellow–blue components, respectively. However, CIELAB was not designed to measure perceived tooth whiteness, and its complexity makes it challenging to directly correlate changes in its 3-dimensional values to perceptual whiteness or yellowness. The tooth whiteness index (WIO) was proposed specifically for quantifying tooth whiteness based on human visual perception of the Vita 3D Shade Guide.23 It is a 1-dimensional colour index specifically tailored to predict the perceptual whiteness of teeth under daylight conditions.24 When compared to other whiteness indices, such as WIC, the WIO index was shown to be the most robust,25 and it has been used in a number of clinical studies.26, 27, 28, 29, 30 More recently, a yellowness index (YIO) has been developed for quantifying tooth yellowness based on human perception of shade guide tabs.31 As part of this research, a psychophysical study showed that YIO had a higher correlation with perceptual yellowness than b*.31

A non-contact video-based digital imaging system (VDIS) has been developed for measuring tooth colour in vitro and in vivo.32 This system calculates tooth colour, including WIO32 and YIO,31 from RGB data derived from the tooth images, offering a practical and reproducible solution for research applications.32

There are many toothpastes on the market designed to meet the increasing cosmetic demands of tooth whitening. These toothpastes are formulated with a range of chemical, abrasive and optical actives that can achieve tooth whitening through different modes of action.33,34 Blue covarine is a blue pigment incorporated into toothpaste to alter the optical properties of teeth. It can deposit onto the tooth surface and shift the reflected colour of teeth from yellow to blue, thus leading to a whiter appearance.35 In vivo and in vitro studies have demonstrated that toothpastes containing blue covarine can increase tooth whiteness immediately after brushing.26, 27, 28 In vitro studies have also shown that blue covarine can remain on the tooth surface for several hours, with stable effects observed for up to 120 minutes under aqueous conditions.35 Blue covarine has been shown to deposit uniformly and non-specifically on enamel using time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry.35 Importantly, studies have demonstrated that toothpaste containing blue covarine does not permanently stain restorative materials or their margins,36,37 making it a suitable option for individuals with dental restorations.

There is an increasing number of toothpastes available commercially that contain activated charcoal.38,39 Charcoal has the potential to deliver tooth-whitening benefits based on its abrasiveness and high capacity to adsorb and retain stain chromophores.38 Activated charcoal is highly porous and has an extremely high surface area,39 which can help in the effective and progressive cleaning of the dentition. A review reported that one-third of the charcoal-containing toothpastes also included clay.39 In formulations containing both charcoal and clay, the clay may enhance the binding capacity of charcoal by holding deposits within the pores of both materials.38 While the tooth-whitening benefit of blue covarine is well established,35 the whitening effect of blue covarine in a full toothpaste formulation containing green covarine, clay and charcoal needs to be evaluated. In addition, two new toothpaste formulations with blue covarine alone have been developed for which tooth-whitening benefits need to be assessed.

The objective of the current research was to evaluate the effects of these toothpastes containing blue covarine on tooth whiteness (using WIO) and tooth yellowness (using YIO) immediately after a single brushing, in comparison to a control toothpaste containing no optical actives.

The null hypothesis was that the toothpastes containing blue covarine would not give an increase in tooth whiteness compared to a control toothpaste immediately after a single brushing.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a double-blind (participant/image analyst/statistician/investigator), 4-product, crossover design study conducted at a clinical research facility in Shanghai, China in December 2018 and January 2019. Prior to initiation, ethical approval was obtained from the Institution Ethics Committee for Clinical Research. All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines. The study was designed to measure change in tooth colour using a non-contact VDIS32 immediately after brushing with toothpastes containing optical actives, compared to a control toothpaste. This study is reported according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials statement (CONSORT 2010) as extended to randomised crossover trials.40

Recruitment and eligibility criteria

Participants were recruited by a local recruitment agency. All participants voluntarily gave their written informed consent before any study procedures were conducted, and the privacy rights of all participants were maintained. Eligible participants were those aged 18 years and above, in good general health, with 2 natural upper central incisors within the colours of 2M2, 3R1.5, 2R2.5, 3L1.5, 2L2.5, 4M1, 2M3, 3M2, 4R1.5, 4L1.5, 3L2.5, 3R2.5, 5M1, 4M2 and 3M3 from VITA 3D-MASTER Shade Guide as calculated from images captured using VDIS, that were free from extrinsic and intrinsic stain. Participants with an upper central incisor that had cap, crown, veneer, crack, excessive gum recession or restorations on the distal, mesial, buccal or incisor edge were excluded from the study. In addition, participants were excluded if they had used tooth-bleaching products within 6 months preceding screening, had received a scale and polish or used tooth-whitening products within 3 months of screening, or had orthodontic bands, removable partial dentures, untreated caries, severe gingivitis or periodontitis, diabetes or any other medical condition deemed unsuitable by the dentist. Current smokers, including those who used e-cigarettes, were also ineligible.

Study products

Four study products were tested, specifically a silica-based toothpaste containing charcoal, clay, blue covarine, green covarine and fluoride as sodium fluoride (BC + GC); two silica-based toothpastes containing different levels of blue covarine and fluoride as sodium monofluorophosphate (BC1 having the lower level and BC2 containing the higher level of blue covarine) and a control silica-based toothpaste containing fluoride as sodium fluoride and no blue or green covarine. The washout toothpaste was the same formulation as the control toothpaste to ensure no additional confounding variables were introduced during the washout period.

Study procedures

The study comprised a screening visit followed by 4 test visits, with a washout period of 1 week between test visits. At the screening visit, participants who provided written informed consent were given an oral soft tissue (OST) exam and assessed against study inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible participants were given a soft-bristled, flat-trim toothbrush (Systema, Lion) and washout toothpaste and instructed to brush twice daily at home for the duration of the study. To minimise any immediate staining effects from food and standardise oral conditions, participants were asked to refrain from eating and drinking (water as an exception) for at least 1 hour before each test session and from brushing their teeth for at least 4 hours prior to test visits.

At the first test visit (following a minimum of a 3-day washout period after a screening visit), participants were randomised based on order of attendance at the site to the sequence in which they would receive the 4 toothpastes (1 study toothpaste per visit) by clinical staff not involved in image analysis. The randomisation schedule was generated by the statistician using Williams design approach suitable for a 4-product, 4-period crossover design. The randomisation list was not accessible to those involved in tooth imaging or image analysis. At each test visit, compliance was confirmed, and an OST exam was undertaken. Digital static images of the upper central incisors were taken using a VDIS before and immediately after brushing with toothpaste. Participants brushed for 90 seconds using 1.5 g of toothpaste followed by water rinse using participants’ normal brushing technique. To collect an image, the participant’s teeth were exposed through use of cheek retractors, and the participant positioned their head on a chin rest and forehead rest to ensure minimal movement. The VDIS operator collected an image of the participant’s teeth and a reference white tile. Using live video, the pre-brushing image served as a guide to enable identical positioning of the teeth for post-brushing imaging. The average RGB values of the upper central incisors were derived from the VDIS images, then converted into CIEXYZ values and CIELAB values using the camera characterisation model and the CIE colour model, respectively.32 WIO values were calculated using equation (1)32 whereas YIO values were calculated in a post hoc analysis using equation (2).31

A full description of the VDIS has been reported elsewhere.32 Both the VDIS operator and image analyst were blinded to product allocation.

Oral soft tissue examinations were conducted throughout the study period. Any adverse events observed were recorded. Clinical judgment was used to determine the relationship between any adverse event and the study product.

Sample size calculation

At least 66 eligible participants were planned to be randomised to ensure 60 completed the study. Sixty participants were sufficient to detect a product difference of at least 1 WIO unit (between test toothpastes and control) with 80% power and 5% level of significance, assuming 2.7 units as the standard deviation of paired differences as calculated from a previous VDIS multi-period crossover design study.

Product blinding

All toothpastes were packed in identical white tubes which were only differentiated by a unique alphanumeric code shown on the product label. The linkage of product code to specific toothpaste formulation was retained in a sealed envelope throughout the study. This code was broken after the statistical analysis was completed.

Statistical analysis

All participants received the toothpastes as per the randomisation, and therefore data were analysed as per their original assigned groups. The outcome measures per participant were the average values from 2 upper central incisor teeth. The primary outcome variable was WIO with CIE L*a*b* as secondary endpoints. YIO was calculated and analysed post hoc. Paired t-tests were used to test for significant differences between the pre- and post-brushing values for each product. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed for comparison between groups, keeping product and visit as fixed effects, participant as random effect, product and visit as interaction terms, and baseline as covariate. The significance level was set at 5%.

Results

Sixty-eight participants were recruited for the study, with 66 participants randomised into the product test period and 61 completing the study. Seven participants withdrew from the study for personal reasons. During the blind data review, all data from one participant were excluded due to tooth stains being visible in the first pre-brushing tooth image, and data from the first test visit for another participant were excluded due to a red mark being visible on the tooth. Discussion

This study demonstrated that the silica-based fluoride toothpaste containing charcoal, clay, blue covarine and green covarine and both toothpastes containing blue covarine gave statistically significant increases in tooth whiteness (WIO) and reductions in yellowness (YIO) immediately after one brush versus baseline, as well as versus the control toothpaste. Thus, the null hypothesis is rejected for WIO. A previous clinical study demonstrated that a single brushing with toothpaste containing blue covarine resulted in significant tooth whitening compared to a negative control.26 Two additional studies reported measurable whitening effects for toothpaste containing blue covarine post single brushing, though both studies were limited by the absence of a negative control.27,28 These studies reported WIO improvements of 2.28-5.51 units following a single brushing with toothpastes containing blue covarine.26, 27, 28 The current study confirmed that blue covarine–containing toothpaste can deliver whitening benefits with WIO improvements in the range of 5.14 to 6.8 units, and that the benefit can be delivered from toothpaste formulations containing additional ingredients such as charcoal and clay. As tooth colours are similar globally1 and instant whitening with blue covarine provides a comparable increase in WIO irrespective of initial tooth color,26 the results from this study are considered generalisable to the broader population.

A crossover study design was selected for this study to control for confounding variables. By having the same individual receive all toothpastes, we could assess the efficacy of each product more accurately. One potential downside of this design is the carryover effect, where the impact of one product might influence the next. To address this, we included a 1-week washout period between each test phase to eliminate any carryover, and the effectiveness of this approach was confirmed during statistical analysis.

Digital imaging methods are a viable approach to assessing tooth colour16 and help to reduce the bias that may be present in studies where a human assessment of tooth colour is used.41 Indeed, several clinical studies have been described using non-contact camera-based digital imaging methods to measure tooth colour, and these methods are sensitive enough to quantify changes in tooth colour following use of whitening toothpaste and strips.26, 27, 28,42,43 The VDIS used in this clinical study has previously been shown to be a reproducible and highly robust means of measuring tooth colour and whiteness.32 The results of this clinical study confirm that the VDIS is sensitive enough to determine changes in tooth colour following product use and able to discriminate between products.

WIO was developed to predict perceptual whiteness of teeth under daylight conditions. A psychophysical study was conducted using digitally modified tooth images to investigate the impact on perceived whiteness resulting from changes to individual CIELAB parameters and changes in specific CIELAB values caused by blue covarine. The study showed that increases in L* and reductions in b* and, to a lesser extent, a* drive the overall perception of tooth whiteness, and for changes resulting from blue covarine, a WIO change of greater than 1.99 units is perceivable to the human eye.44 All the toothpastes tested in the current study gave a change in WIO greater than 2 units, which demonstrates that these toothpastes give a noticeable increase in tooth whiteness. In contrast, the control toothpaste only had a change in WIO of 1.39 units, which is below the visible perception threshold. All the toothpastes containing blue covarine showed significant changes in the three CIELAB colour components compared to control toothpaste. There were significant reductions in a* and b* values for all toothpastes containing blue covarine from pre-brushing, indicating that the whiteness of teeth increased, whereas the L* values were only slightly reduced (Table 2). This is similar to findings in previous clinical studies27,28 where the perceived change in whiteness from blue covarine was mainly driven by changes in a* and b* rather than changes in L*.

Yellowness is also one of the key components in the perception of dental aesthetics. In terms of b*, the current study demonstrated all toothpastes containing blue covarine presented a decrease in b*, a trend shifting from yellow to blue. A perceptual yellowness index (YIO) was published after the study completion.31 To understand the impact of blue covarine on tooth yellowness more accurately, YIO values were calculated from the CIE XYZ data and analysed post hoc. Reductions in YIO were observed for participants using toothpastes containing blue covarine as compared to control toothpaste, indicating a reduction in tooth yellowness. From a previous psychophysical study it was found that YIO performed better than b* in correlation with the perceptual tooth yellowness, though there was no statistically significant difference.31

Activated charcoal is proposed to bind to surface deposits on teeth, including plaque, bacteria and stained material.38 This binding action is potentially facilitated by clay in formulations that contain it.38 Although this study was not designed to explore the whitening capability of either charcoal or clay, the observation of a statistically significant increase in whiteness after one brush for a toothpaste containing these ingredients in addition to blue covarine and green covarine is extremely encouraging. A separate in vitro study on extrinsic tooth stain removal45,46 demonstrated that a silica-based fluoride toothpaste containing clay and charcoal gave significantly greater stain removal than a silica-based fluoride toothpaste containing clay, and both produced significantly greater stain removal than a silica-based fluoride control toothpaste. These in vitro results suggest that in addition to the increase in whiteness and decrease in yellowness after one brushing, a toothpaste containing blue covarine, green covarine, charcoal and clay also has the potential to deliver progressive tooth stain removal.

A limitation of this study is the short evaluation period, which captures only immediate whitening effects of a single brushing. Further studies could include longer-term follow-up to assess the longevity of benefits.

Conclusions

The present study demonstrated that a toothpaste containing blue and green covarine plus clay and charcoal and two toothpastes containing blue covarine produced statistically significant increases in tooth whiteness and reductions in tooth yellowness immediately after a single brushing.
