Cathodoluminescence Enhancement Mechanisms in Silica Microspheres
Authors: Hadar Aharon, Zahava Barkay, Sophie Meuret, Ofer Kfir
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCathodoluminescence (CL) enables optical‐frequency analysis of samples with nanometer resolution, originating from the interaction of a focused electron beam with radiative electronic states or directly with the optical modes of the sample. Here, we decompose the various mechanisms underlying CL generation and emission from an archetypal spherical resonator using its spectral, angular, and spatially resolved features. We investigate the radiation of optical whispering‐gallery modes in regimes of coherent and incoherent luminescence. The use of different experimental regimes allows us to disentangle the different contributions to the CL in spheres, namely photon absorption, generation, and radiative leakage, and conclude that the photon generation occurs precisely on the sphere's surface. In addition, the spheres serve as high‐NA collimating lenses for CL, resulting in a mode quality unprecedented for CL in free space. We believe that such collimated and directed CL in free space will enhance existing quantum measurements of CL and facilitate new ones, such as high‐rate electron‐photon entangled pairs, CL from quantum emitters, and homodyne analysis of CL.Keywords: cathodoluminescence, microspheres, whispering‐gallery modes

Introduction

Electron microscopes are ubiquitous analytical tools for the ultrasmall, enabling imaging, diffraction, and spectroscopy of materials down to the atomic scale [1, 2, 3]. The ability to pinpoint the electron beam with an accuracy well below an optical wavelength is used to quantify local optical responses that would otherwise be smeared by the light diffraction limit [4, 5, 6, 7], such as the mapping of evanescent fields, dark modes [8, 9], high momentum states [10, 11, 12], and more. Capturing photons emitted from an impinging electron, namely cathodoluminescence (CL), was applied to characterize plasmonic nanocavities and surface plasmon‐polaritons [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19], resonant dielectric nanostructures [20, 21, 22], mineral coloration in geoscience [23, 24], thin films in semiconductors [25], etc., providing access to their spectral, spatial, polarization, and angular‐resolved emission properties [26, 27, 28]. An alternative approach for probing photon generation instead of emission is EELS [29, 30], which stands for electron energy loss spectroscopy, used in a complementary manner to probe the correlations and entanglements formed by the electron‐photon coupling [31, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. The high resolution of CL is particularly powerful for analyzing the properties of discrete modes within optical resonators. By exciting the sample with a focused electron beam, CL probes the local density of optical states (LDOS) of photonic crystals and defects, both spatially and angularly [22, 29, 37, 38, 39]. For example, CL spectroscopy has been used to study one‐dimensional pillar resonators [40], angle‐resolved CL in plexcitonic crystals has revealed coupling‐induced modifications of the band structure and a redistribution of optical states between loss channels [41, 42], and in topological photonic lattices, CL has directly probed the LDOS of bulk and edge states while visualizing their spatial field distributions [43]. Similar approaches have also been applied to optical whispering‐gallery modes (WGMs) circulating on the edge of dielectric microcavities [21, 32, 44, 45, 46, 47]. These modes are typically characterized by global properties such as their quality factor (Q‐factor, or Q), modal volume (V), polarization (TM or TE, for transverse magnetic or electric polarization), radiation loss, and absorption [44, 48]. But in the context of their interaction with an electron and the generation of CL, local properties become important, as well as the particular coupling mechanism. One can divide the electron‐sample interaction into coherent and incoherent processes. Coherent CL stems from integrating over the electron trajectory in space and time in a manner that matches the dynamics of the optical mode's field, sometimes referred to as phase matching. It includes transition radiation [26, 27, 49, 50], Cherenkov radiation [39, 51, 52, 53, 54], and Smith–Purcell radiation [55, 56]. In incoherent CL, the generated charge carriers populate intrinsic material excitations, such as defects, excitons, and higher bands [57, 58, 59], etc., followed by spontaneous emission [22], for which the phase is undetermined. In practice, the generated CL photons need to reach a detector, which typically requires their out‐coupling in its direction, making the analysis of optical cavities more complex. On one hand, a good resonator may have a high Q‐factor and density of states, but the confinement of light to it inherently means that radiation to the environment is inhibited. On the other hand, a poor resonator would emit broadly spectrally and angularly, suffering from a low signal‐to‐noise ratio, and may be dominated by other optical signals of the sample. These are particularly harsh tradeoffs for quantum optical measures, where one ideally wants a narrowband emission with a well‐defined radiative mode [60, 61, 62, 63, 64].

In this work we investigate the CL properties and enhancement mechanisms of individual silica microspheres. We use a focused electron beam in a scanning and in a transmission electron microscope (SEM and TEM) with beam energies of 30 and 200 keV, respectively, and use spheres with several diameters. We show that different photon generation and radiation phenomena are separable spatially and angularly. Varying the electron beam energy and the sphere's diameter disentangles CL generation mechanisms, radiative efficiency, and material absorption. As an example of competing luminescence phenomena, we address the collimation of external CL, showing that its dominance under some parameters may hinder the analysis or be used as a beneficial feature for out‐coupling CL with a good spatial mode.

Another regime is apparent in Figure 4, analyzing the CL's polar angle θCL. There is a monotonic trend of θCL versus re up to the sphere's edge. In principle, one expects that for re→0, θCL would nullify, but the CL distribution is too wide to show that clearly. However, for re>Rsphere, the angular distribution shrinks, forming a sharp spatial mode as in Figure 3d. For re>Rsphere, the trend in Figure 4 is no longer monotonic. Therefore, two regimes can be clearly distinguished: re<Rsphere and re>Rsphere as the WGM‐regime and collimation‐regime, respectively. CL in the collimation regime originates from outside the sphere. In our case, CL emanates from carbon; either from the laces holding the sphere (see Figure 2a,b) or from the film that covers the holes between them intermittently. Figure S3 in the Supporting Information S1 shows that no CL is detected for re≳Rsphere in the absence of an underlying layer. Thus, the sphere acts as a collimating optic for emitters on the surface underneath.

In fact, the on‐sample collimation enabled by the silica sphere could be a beneficial feature for downstream applications of CL (see Figure S5 in the Supporting Information S1 for an optics ray‐tracing illustration). First, it collects a solid angle of 1.84 sr, centered at an inclination of around 45° (see illustration in Figure 1 and data in Figure 3d). For an omnidirectional emission, the collection efficiency would then be 1.84/4π=14.6%. Second, the outgoing mode has a low‐divergence, with a nearly transform‐limited Gaussian profile. The measured standard deviation of the angular distribution in Figure 3d is σθ≈σϕ=5°. Assuming that the mode has an initial size equal to the sphere's diameter, we estimate that its emittance is 0.17 μm⋅rad, corresponding to M2≈2 [66]. For some context, consider the constraints of the CL‐collection mirrors in TEMs, which protrude into the narrow objective‐lens gap [67, 68, 69, 70]. The collimation enables the suppression of possible aberrations in the mirror, resulting in a mode with sufficient purity for quantum measurements, or an efficient coupling to a single‐mode fiber [61, 71].

Discussion

In the following, we analyze the angular‐independent spectra to disentangle their features and evaluate the origin of the CL photon generation. The CL signal inherently convolves the contributions of the (i) generation of a photon in the resonator, (ii) radiative coupling to free space, and (iii) material absorption. To identify these components separately, we use spheres with several diameters and tune the electron‐beam energy. These degrees of freedom assist in distinguishing the different contributing phenomena and analyzing each of them in the regime where it dominates. We note that spatially filtered spectra are key, since they allow us to omit angular regions for which the CL signal is dominated by an irrelevant source of CL. In Figure 2d, the signal near θCL→−90° has a higher background due to a residue of collimated, non‐WGM luminescence.

We begin by addressing the competition between the loss mechanisms, namely photon absorption and radiation to free space, through their contributions to the quality factor of the microresonator. After addressing the loss, we analyze the generation processes of light in the resonator.

Let us consider the processes following the population of a WGM, regardless of how it was excited. The interplay between material absorption and radiation is reflected in their respective lifetimes, τmat and τrad. These are typically expressed in terms of the quality factors, Qmat=ωτmat and Qrad=ωτrad, where ω is the optical angular frequency of a chosen spectral peak. The balance between the decay mechanisms varies with the sphere's size. As the diameter increases from 2.1 μm, through 4.4 μm to 62 μm (Figure 2a–c), the radiative loss diminishes. Thus, the balance between the radiative and absorptive mechanisms in the 2.1 μm spheres shifts to an absorption‐dominated regime in the 4.4 μm spheres, whereas the CL in the largest spheres is entirely suppressed. To give a quantitative example, the theoretical radiative lifetime for TM‐polarized modes is 28 fs and 104 fs in the 2.1 and 4.4μm microresonators, respectively. These values are calculated for similar wavelengths, 503 nm and 505 nm. The lifetime is the inverse linewidth calculated using the WGMode package for MATLAB [72], for a given refractive index (1.45) and the diameters mentioned above. For all the identifiable peaks in the spectrum, the quality factor of a peak centered at a wavelength λ is extracted from its full‐width‐at‐half‐maximum (FWHM) bandwidth, Δλ, by Qtot=Δλ−1λ [44]. All spectral peaks were fitted with a Lorentzian line shape using MATLAB. Representative fits are shown in Figure S1b of the Supporting Information S1, and the full fitting procedure and uncertainty analysis are described in Section S1 of the Supporting Information S1.

The absorption‐dominated regime allows us to focus only on the material contribution to the quality factor. For the 4.4 μm sphere, the quality factor extracted from the experimental spectrum in Figure 5a is Qtot∼102. Since the calculated radiative component is Qrad∼104, its contribution to Equation (1) is indeed negligible, and one can assume that Qtot≅Qmat. Hence, the measured quality factor across the entire spectrum quantifies the absorption. Using the full CL spectrum acquired with a 30 keV beam in the SEM (Figure S1a in the Supporting Information S1), we extract Qmat from the larger spheres for both TM and TE resonances at wavelengths above 470 nm, where the peaks are well resolved. Below 470 nm, the signal‐to‐noise ratio decreases, and the resonances cannot be reliably separated for linewidth extraction. To access Qmat at shorter wavelengths, we perform a CL spectrum measurement with a 200 keV beam in the Hitachi HF2000 TEM, using a tangential electron trajectory re≈Rsphere to enhance phase‐matched excitation of WGMs. At 200 keV, the electron velocity approaches the phase velocity of the optical mode, enabling efficient coherent CL generation (see schematic illustration in Figure 5d). This phase‐matching channel is strongly suppressed at 30 keV (by at least seven orders of magnitude in our calculation, Figure 5c). Indeed, the CL spectrum from the TEM shows a propensity for TM‐polarization. For microspheres prepared under comparable conditions and exhibiting similar surface quality (see Figure S6 in the Supporting Information S1), we combine the values of Qmat extracted from the SEM and TEM into a unified dataset, from which we can interpolate a wavelength‐dependent Qmat(λ).

Although typically the CL‐photon generation depends on the electron energy, we use the calculated modes to assess that the photons in our experiment form strictly at the surface of the sphere. For the low‐energy electrons impinging on silica spheres, the emission is incoherent due to the lack of phase matching, and the electron velocity at 30 keV is ve≈0.3c, whereas the optical phase velocity in silica is vph≈0.69c, where c is the speed of light. Moreover, we observe no clear signatures of transition radiation (TR) at 30keV, which would primarily contribute a broadband background rather than discrete peaks. Any TR is expected to be weak at this low electron velocity and is likely obscured by the stronger WGM‐related emission [26, 27]. Thus, the cathodoluminescence is mediated by material excitations, whose radiation according to Fermi's golden rule is governed by the local density of optical states (LDOS) [22, 73], which is the energy density of the WGM per photon [72]. One can see that this form represents the competition between loss and radiation. In the limit of a non‐absorptive material, Qmat→∞, NCL→Γ, and all the generated photons radiate. For the spectral peaks centered at 509 nm and 524 nm with an impact parameter of re≈Rsphere (see Figure 2e), we find that ΓTE/ΓTM=0.84±0.25, where the error represents the standard deviation of ΓTE/ΓTM emanating from its multiple experimentally measured input variables. In the semi‐analytical calculation of the WGMode package, the ratio of ΓTE/ΓTM exhibits a sharp discontinuity at the sphere's boundary, dropping from 3.5 to 1 inside and outside of the sphere, respectively (see Figure 5g). This behavior follows from the modal electric field profiles used in the calculation (Figure 5f), which show that the TE‐polarized mode dominates near the boundary since it is better confined [44, 72]. To enable a direct comparison with the experiment, we define a region of interest (ROI) corresponding to the electron excitation impact parameter, taken at a depth of 140nm from the surface of the sphere (see the shaded region in Figure 5g). It is a conservative estimate for an impact parameter of re≈Rsphere−70nm, and is symmetrically biased toward and away from the surface. In practice, the surface would contribute more. Averaging over this ROI, we find that 〈ΓTE/ΓTM〉ROI≈2.7. This value is higher by 7 standard deviations than our measurement of 0.84.

However, it seems that our measured photon generation ratios at 30 keV include an inherent contradiction. On one hand, the electron energy is too low for coherent CL (Figure 5c), allowing only CL mediated by excitations in the material. On the other hand, the measured ratio of the generation rates, ΓTEΓTM=0.84±0.25, befits CL emission right outside the sphere, that is, in vacuum, where the calculated value is ΓTEΓTM=0.93. In other words, the CL is emitted from the material positioned in the vacuum. In reality, the sphere is imperfect and may exhibit some ovality or surface roughness (Figure S6 in the Supporting Information S1). Thus, the smearing of the interface between material and vacuum smooths the sharpness of the calculated transition of ΓTEΓTM. However, this seeming contradiction verifies that the CL generation is located right at the sphere's boundary. This surface sensitivity is granted here due to the edge‐localization of the optical WGMs, their edge field‐discontinuities, and the spectral separability of the TE‐ versus TM‐polarized modes. Typically, CL is dominated by the bulk, where the optical density of states at the surface is small, making the surface contribution negligible [22, 39, 74]. The surface sensitivity is further enhanced by the geometry of the near‐edge electron incidence. When the electron trajectory is tangent to the sphere re≲Rsphere, it is parallel to the surface and remains superficial over hundreds of nanometers. Thus, the surface propensity of the electron incidence and the photonic modes enable the selective sensing of surface phenomena via CL without resorting to low electron energies [75]. This highlights the role of geometry and fabrication precision in shaping the CL response and sets the stage for engineering mode selectivity, polarization control, and directional output through careful tuning of the beam parameters and resonator dimensions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we investigated cathodoluminescence in silica microspheres acting simultaneously as resonant cavities for optical whispering‐gallery modes and as collimating lenses for external emission. Although the various contributions to the CL are inherently convolved, we disentangle them by analyzing their spectral, spatial, and angular measurements. The properties of on‐sample lensing of the sphere for CL originating in its vicinity are characterized according to its angular directionality. For the nondirectional CL from WGMs, we apply a spectral analysis to disentangle photon generation, radiative out‐coupling, and absorptive loss mechanisms. Specifically, we find the quality factor related to material absorption and radiative whispering‐gallery loss, Qmat and Qrad, respectively. This decomposition is used to quantify photon generation rates and deduce the surface sensitivity of WGM CL. Altogether, our results establish silica microspheres as model systems to probe electron‐photon interactions with mode selectivity while also serving as practical elements for improving the CL signal in advanced spectroscopy, exemplified by two particularly helpful aspects of nanoscopic photonics. First, it offers a path to investigate CL of surface electronic excitations by combining the edge‐parallel electron irradiation and surface photonic modes. Second, the on‐sample and high‐NA collimation by the sphere shapes CL into a low‐divergence and nearly diffraction‐limited beam and does so with high efficiency. Thus, it could enable low‐NA and otherwise constrained CL‐collection optics to produce a good spatial mode compatible with fiber coupling or quantum measurements [36, 61, 71].
