Newly isolated human-derived Lactobacillus fermentum strain stimulates IFN-γ-secreting CD8+ T cells for enhanced anti-cancer immunity
Authors: Tae-Young Kim, Seungil Kim, Sohyeon Lee, Su-Hyun Lee, Jiyoung Yu, Kyunggon Kim, Mi-Na Kweon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe gut microbiota represents a promising target to enhance cancer immunotherapy, yet the microbial molecules mediating anti-tumor immunity remain poorly defined. Here, we identify a human-derived strain, Lactobacillus fermentum MI37, that augments anti-tumor immune responses and synergizes with anti-PD-1 therapy. MI37 induces robust IFN-γ production with minimal IL-10, increases tumor-infiltrating cytotoxic T lymphocytes, and upregulates inflammatory gene programs in a syngeneic mouse model. Notably, heat-killed MI37 retains its immunostimulatory and anti-tumor effects, indicating a structural microbial component mediates activity. Fractionation identifies lipoteichoic acid (LTA) as the key effector and purified Staphylococcus aureus-derived LTA recapitulates MI37-induced IFN-γ production and CD8+ T cell activation. Genomic and proteomic analyses further reveal MI37-specific features in teichoic acid biosynthesis, including a distinctive sulfatase N-terminal domain-containing LTA synthase. These findings establish LTA-producing probiotics as potential microbial adjuvants to improve cancer immunotherapy efficacy.Subject areas: Cancer, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have transformed cancer therapy, yet their clinical benefit remains limited, with overall response rates averaging ∼30% in solid tumors.1,2,3 Tumors are often classified as “hot” or “cold” based on the degree of immune cell infiltration within the tumor microenvironment (TME). “Hot” tumors, enriched for T cell infiltration and immune activation, are more responsive to ICIs, whereas “cold” tumors—characterized by immune exclusion or immune desert phenotypes—are typically resistant.4,5,6 Strategies that convert “cold” tumors into “hot” tumors by enhancing T cell infiltration represent a promising approach to overcome this resistance.7

The gut microbiota is a pivotal regulator of host immune homeostasis and has become a major focus in the development of microbiota-based therapeutics.8 Notably, the composition of the commensal gut microbiota differs markedly between responders and non-responders to cancer immunotherapy, indicating that microbial profiles can profoundly influence clinical outcomes and therapeutic efficacy.9,10,11 Recent studies further demonstrate that specific gut microbes can modulate the TME and shape responses to immune checkpoint blockade, supporting the concept that the gut microbiota may serve as both a biomarker and a therapeutic target in cancer treatment.12 Approaches under investigation include antibiotic modulation, dietary interventions, fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT), and probiotic administration.13 Notably, FMT from ICI-responsive donors has been shown to enhance therapeutic efficacy,14,15 and several gut microbial species have been directly associated with improved ICI outcomes.16,17 However, the microbial mechanisms and specific effector molecules responsible for these beneficial effects remain largely unknown, underscoring the need for mechanistic and standardized approaches to advance microbiota-based cancer therapies.

Gut microbes produce diverse metabolites and structural components that can modulate immunity. Metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), bile acid derivatives, and tryptophan catabolites influence the differentiation, activation, and function of both innate and adaptive immune cells.18,19,20 Structural components, including peptidoglycan (PGN), lipopolysaccharide (LPS), lipopeptides, teichoic acids, DNA, and RNA, function as microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), detected by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as Toll-like receptors (TLRs) and Nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors (NLRs), triggering innate immune activation.21,22 Although these microbial signals are increasingly recognized as immune modulators, their role in promoting systemic anti-tumor immunity remains poorly understood.

In this study, we investigated the strain-specific immunostimulatory properties of gut microbes derived from healthy human donors and explored the molecular mechanisms by which these strains enhance anti-cancer immune responses. Our findings aim to provide mechanistic insight into how specific microbial molecules can serve as adjuvants to improve ICI therapy.

Results

Gut commensal L. fermentum MI37 enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy

To identify novel probiotic bacterial strains capable of stimulating anti-cancer immunity, we screened 268 Lactobacillus isolates from the feces of healthy human volunteers23 (Table S1). In cancer immunology, IL-10 and IFN-γ play complex, often opposing roles: IL-10 generally exerts immunosuppressive effects, whereas IFN-γ promotes anti-tumor activity.24,25 In particular, previous clinical evidence has shown that a higher IFN-γ/IL-10 balance is positively associated with response to anti-PD-1 therapy in melanoma.26 We therefore prioritized strains that induced high IFN-γ and low IL-10 production in co-cultures of mesenteric lymph node (mLNs) and splenic immune cells (Figure 1A). Three Lactobacillus fermentum (L. fermentum) strains met these criteria. Thus, among 268 Lactobacillus strains, we identified three that induced high IFN-γ and low IL-10, all classified as L. fermentum (Figure 1A). We then evaluated the in vivo anti-cancer activity of these strains by administering them in combination with anti-PD-1 in an MC38 syngeneic tumor mouse model (Figure 1B). Although two L. fermentum strains failed to enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1, the MI37 strain successfully reduced tumor volume (Figure 1C) and weight (Figure 1D), suggesting potentiation of this therapeutic effect.

L. fermentum MI37 enhances the T cell-mediated anti-cancer response to anti-PD-1 therapy

T cells are central mediators of anti-cancer immunity. To assess whether MI37 augments anti-tumor T cell responses, we examined tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes in treated mice. Co-administration of anti-PD-1 antibodies with MI37 significantly increased intratumoral CD8+ T cell numbers but did not alter CD4+ T cell abundance (Figures 2A and 2B). In addition, the population of CD8+ and CD4+ T cells expressing IFN-γ within the TME was elevated (Figures 2C and 2D). Among myeloid-lineage cells, mice receiving combined MI37 and anti-PD-1 treatment exhibited increased macrophage abundance within the TME compared with mice treated with anti-PD-1 alone, whereas dendritic cell (DC) levels remained unchanged (Figures S1A and S1B). Transcriptome profiling further revealed a clear separation between the two groups based on hierarchical clustering and principal-component analysis (PCA) (Figures S2A and S2B). Notably, the MI37 and anti-PD-1 combination upregulated genes involved in inflammatory and immune-activating pathways, including NF-ĸB and necroptosis signaling (Figures 2E and S2C). In addition, transcripts associated with T cell activation—such as CD69, ICOSLG, lymphotoxin-alpha (LTA), and KCNA3—and CXCL1, a chemokine known to recruit T cells and natural killer (NK) cells,27,28 were markedly increased in the combination group (Figure 2E). Furthermore, the combination therapy of MI37 and anti-PD-1 significantly increased intratumoral expression of IFN-γ, TNF-α, and the cytotoxic effector protease granzyme B (Figure 2F). Together, these findings demonstrate that the gut commensal L. fermentum MI37 potentiates T cell-mediated anti-cancer immunity and improves the therapeutic efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade.

MI37 stimulates effector CD8+ T cells through both DC-dependent and DC-independent mechanisms

Antigen-presenting cells (APCs) are essential for initiating anti-cancer T cell responses, primarily through IL-12 production and antigen presentation, which together drive potent cytotoxic T lymphocyte (CTL) activity.29 To determine whether MI37 influences APC-mediated IL-12 production in vitro, we co-cultured bone marrow (BM)-derived DCs with MI37 or the type strain KCTC3112. While KCTC3112 failed to induce IL-12p70 production, MI37 significantly stimulated IL-12p70 secretion in BM-DCs (Figure 3A). To better approximate in vivo priming conditions, BM-DCs were first exposed to MI37, washed, and then co-cultured with CD8+ T cells for 3 days under suboptimal conditions for CTL induction. Compared with KCTC3112-primed DCs, MI37-primed DCs induced higher expression of IFN-γ and TNF-α in CD8+ T cells (Figures 3B and 3C). Notably, MI37 also directly enhanced IFN-γ expression in CD8+ T cells in the absence of DCs (Figure 3D), suggesting that MI37 promotes CTL function through both DC-dependent priming and DC-independent pathways. Together, these results indicate that MI37 enhances T cell-mediated anti-cancer immunity via dual mechanisms: indirect activation through DCs and direct modulation of T cell activity.

HK MI37 retains the ability to enhance anti-cancer immune responses

Previous studies have shown that microbiota-derived metabolites, such as inosine and butyrate, exert beneficial effects on anti-cancer immunity.30,31 To explore the mechanisms underlying MI37-mediated anti-cancer responses, we first examined whether metabolites produced by MI37 could stimulate IFN-γ production. Cell-free culture supernatants (CFCSs) were isolated from MI37 or KCTC3112 and applied to splenocytes treated with various concentrations of LPS. CFCSs did not enhance IFN-γ production; rather, it suppressed IFN-γ compared with LPS treatment alone (Figure 4A). Growing evidence indicates that bacterial structural components, in addition to metabolites, play important roles in modulating anti-cancer immunity.32,33 We therefore tested whether structural components of MI37 contribute to its immunostimulatory effect. Heat-killed (HK)-MI37, but not HK-KCTC3112, induced IFN-γ production to a level comparable with live MI37 and increased the frequency of CD8+IFN-γ+ T cells (Figures 4B and 4C). Furthermore, oral administration of HK-MI37 in combination with anti-PD-1 therapy in the MC38 syngeneic tumor mouse model produced a synergistic effect, leading to greater tumor suppression and significantly elevated IFN-γ expression compared with the anti-PD-1 monotherapy (Figures 4D and 4E). These results suggest that structural components of MI37 are key mediators of its ability to enhance anti-tumor immunity.

LTA regulates the effector function of CD8+ T cells via a TLR2-mediated mechanism

To identify structural components of MI37 responsible for modulating anti-cancer immunity, we fractionated MI37-derived subcellular components and extracted crude materials from each fraction. IFN-γ production was significantly increased in response to the pellet fraction, whereas the supernatant fraction had no such effect (Figure S3A). We then isolated crude PGN and wall teichoic acids (WTAs) from the pellet fraction but neither reproduced the IFN-γ-inducing activity (Figure S3B). Lipoteichoic acid (LTA), a major membrane-anchored component of the Gram-positive bacterial cell wall, has been reported to critically modulate host immune responses.34,35,36 We confirmed the presence of LTA in MI37 by confocal imaging (Figure 5A). In parallel, crude LTA isolated from MI37 induced robust IFN-γ production, with markedly higher activity compared with crude LTA obtained from KCTC3112 (Figure 5B). However, because MI37-derived LTA could not be fully purified, we used Staphylococcus aureus-derived LTA (sLTA), whose structural and immunostimulatory properties are well characterized,37,38,39 to assess the potential role of purified LTA in our system. sLTA significantly increased IFN-γ production at 10 μg/mL (Figure 5C). According to previous reports, sLTA is a well-established mediator of TLR2 signaling.34 In our in vitro mononuclear cell culture system, we compared the ability of sLTA, HK-MI37, and HK-KCTC3112 to activate pattern-recognition receptors by assessing the expression levels of TLR2 and TLR4. TLR4 expression decreased across all treatment groups, whereas TLR2 expression was significantly increased in the sLTA- or HK-MI37-treated groups compared with the vehicle control (Figure S4). Notably, HK-KCTC3112 induced an even higher level of TLR2 expression than both sLTA and HK-MI37 (Figure S4). Despite this strong induction of TLR2, HK-KCTC3112 failed to elicit IFN-γ production (Figure 4B). To determine whether the IFN-γ-inducing activity of sLTA and HK-MI37 was dependent on TLR2, we pretreated cells with the TLR2 inhibitor C29. As shown in Figure 5D, C29 completely abolished IFN-γ induction by both sLTA and HK-MI37, demonstrating that their immunostimulatory activity is TLR2-dependent. The heightened TLR2 activation by HK-KCTC3112 may reflect the presence of additional TLR2-interacting ligands within its cell envelope, such as PGN-associated molecules or bacterial lipoproteins. However, although KCTC3112 strongly induces TLR2 expression, it may not effectively engage the downstream cytokine signaling pathways required for IFN-γ production. Given that HK-MI37 induced IL-12p70 production in DCs, we next examined whether sLTA exerted a similar effect. Indeed, sLTA stimulated IL-12p70 secretion by DCs in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 5E). Moreover, sLTA-treated DCs enhanced the frequency of CD8+IFN-γ+ T cells and CD8+TNF-α+ T cells in DCs–CD8+ T cell co-culture (Figures 5F and 5G). We next examined whether sLTA could directly augment CTL function. Indeed, direct treatment with sLTA substantially increased the frequency of CD8+IFN-γ+ and CD8+TNF-α+ T cells (Figures 5H and 5I). Together, these findings identify LTA as a potent immunostimulatory component that promotes IFN-γ production and CD8+ T cell activation and suggest that LTA may be a key mediator of the anti-cancer immunity associated with MI37.

MI37-specific SNtp encodes a domain involved in LTA biosynthesis

To identify molecular features of MI37 associated with LTA, we performed whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and comparative genomics against the type strain KCTC3112. Clusters of orthologous groups of proteins (COG) enrichment analysis revealed that MI37 harbors a greater number of genes associated with cell wall, membrane, and envelope biogenesis compared with KCTC3112 (Figure 6A). These MI37-specific genes were enriched for functions in exopolysaccharide (EPS) and LPS biosynthesis, transporters, teichoic acid metabolism, and glycosyltransferases (Figure 6A). To further probe LTA-related components, we compared the cell wall- and membrane-associated proteomes of MI37 and KCTC3112. Proteomic profiling identified 208 proteins upregulated in MI37 and 177 upregulated in KCTC3112 (Figure 6B). Notably, proteins involved in the biosynthesis and modification of teichoic acid, including TagE, DltB, and BacA, were elevated in MI37 (Figures 6B and 6C). To verify the molecular differences between MI37 and KCTC3112 at the gene and protein levels, we isolated crude LTA from both strains using the n-butanol extraction method. A larger amount of crude LTA was recovered from MI37 than from KCTC3112 (Figure S5A), and the crude LTA isolated from MI37 exhibited a higher phosphate content relative to that from KCTC3112, indirectly indicating a greater abundance of LTA in MI37 (Figure S5B). In addition, each strain expressed unique proteins absent from the other: MI37 contained 34 unique proteins, while KCTC3112 harbored 47 (Figure 6D). Among the MI37-specific proteins, we identified a sulfatase N-terminal domain-containing protein (SNtp), associated with cell wall, membrane, and envelope biogenesis (Figure 6E). Since SNtp has not been functionally characterized, we analyzed it using NCBI BLAST-Conserved Domain Database (CDD). CDD analysis revealed multiple conserved domains, including a sulfatase domain, an MdoB superfamily domain, and, importantly, a predicted LTA synthase domain within the SNtp sequence (Figure 6F). The presence of this LTA synthase-related domain suggests a dual role in sulfate metabolism and LTA biosynthesis, potentially conferring strain-specific properties to MI37. Simple modular architecture research tool (SMART) analysis further supported these findings, identifying multiple transmembrane helices and a sulfatase domain (Pfam Sulfatase) within SNtp (Figure 6G). Notably, this sulfatase domain is also present in lipoteichoic acid synthase (LtaS), a well-established enzyme involved in LTA biosynthesis in Gram-positive bacteria.40 Taken together, these results suggest that MI37 harbors molecular determinants associated with teichoic acid synthesis and modification, including the novel SNtp, which may enhance LTA biosynthesis and contribute to its distinct immunomodulatory properties.

Discussion

In this study, we identified a human-derived L. fermentum MI37 strain that induces CTL effector functions through an in vitro screening system. MI37 significantly enhanced the therapeutic efficacy of anti-PD-1 treatment in the MC38 syngeneic tumor model. Combination therapy reprogrammed the TME toward a “hot” immune phenotype, characterized by increased infiltration of IFN-γ+ T cells and elevated cytotoxic activity. Among microbial structural components, LTA emerged as a key mediator of effector T cell responses. These findings highlight the potential of MI37 to augment immune checkpoint blockade and suggest that LTA may contribute to anti-cancer immunity by remodeling the tumor immune landscape.

Interestingly, MI37 promoted anti-tumor T cell responses through both DC-dependent and DC-independent mechanisms, ultimately promoting robust CTL effector functions. MI37 not only stimulated IL-12 production—an essential cytokine for CTL priming—but also directly activated CD8+ T cells in the absence of DCs. This dual mechanism is consistent with previous reports showing that Bifidobacterium breve and Hominenteromicrobium enhance IL-12 production and promote DC recruitment, thereby facilitating CD8+ T cell infiltration into tumors.41,42 Moreover, the ability of MI37 to directly activate T cells aligns with reports that certain bacterial components can modulate T cell function independently of APCs.35,43 Collectively, these findings suggest that MI37 functions as a multifaceted immune modulator, engaging both innate and adaptive pathways to potentiate anti-cancer immunity, highlighting its promise as a microbial adjuvant to improve immune checkpoint therapy.

Here, we show that HK-MI37 retains the capacity to stimulate IFN-γ production through a TLR2-dependent manner and to synergize with anti-PD-1 therapy, indicating that structural components—rather than secreted metabolites—drive its immunostimulatory activity. Growing evidence supports the concept that bacterial structural components can act as potent immunoregulatory factors, exerting either pro- or anti-inflammatory effects to shape host immunity. For instance, the lipid a15:0-i15:0 phosphatidylethanolamine (PE), a diacyl PE with two branched chains from the gut commensal Akkermansia muciniphila, induces pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-6 in DCs.44 In contrast, a cell surface polysaccharide from Bifidobacterium bifidum promotes Foxp3+ regulatory T cell differentiation and exerts strong anti-inflammatory effects in a T cell transfer colitis model.45

In this study, we attempted to purify MI37-derived LTA using hydrophobic interaction chromatography (HIC); however, no phosphate-specific signal was detected by the inorganic phosphate assay, indicating that LTA could not be recovered in measurable amounts. Accordingly, we used sLTA as an alternative reagent to assess the capacity of LTA to modulate anti-tumor immune responses. Our data demonstrate that sLTA recapitulates the immunostimulatory effects of MI37 by inducing IL-12 production in DCs and augmenting CD8+ T cell effector function, directly or indirectly. Together, these results highlight LTA as a central effector molecule mediating the anti-tumor immune activity of MI37.

LTA is a key regulator of host immune responses, with immunological effects that vary substantially depending on the bacterial source. LTA stimulates innate immune cells, such as DCs and macrophages, primarily through PRRs, including TLR2.46,47,48 These interactions activate inflammatory signaling pathways and induce the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-12, TNF-α, and IL-6.34,49,50 However, not all LTAs are equally immunostimulatory. For example, LTA from Lactobacillus plantarum KCTC10887BP induces minimal nitric oxide and TNF production in macrophages and shows weaker TLR2 activation than LTA from Staphylococcus aureus.51 This strain-specific LTA also reduces mortality in LPS-induced endotoxemia and attenuates Shigella flexneri PGN-induced inflammation in monocytes.52,53 The immunological diversity of LTAs is largely attributed to their structural heterogeneity.54 For instance, LTA from L. plantarum contains additional acyl chains, unsaturated fatty acids in its glycolipid moiety, and distinct sugar substitutions on the glycerol phosphate (Gro-P) backbone compared with S. aureus LTA. In addition, LTAs undergo D-alanylation, a modification known to influence immune responses. Disruption of D-alanine incorporation into LTA in L. plantarum and Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG (GG; named after its original isolators, Goldin and Gorbach) confers anti-inflammatory properties and provides protection in colitis models.55,56

MI37 exhibits elevated expression of dltB, a gene involved in the D-alanylation of LTA, compared with the reference strain KCTC3112. We also identified an MI37-specific SNtp, absent in KCTC3112, that harbors a sulfated domain homologous to LtaS, suggesting a potential role in LTA biosynthesis. These findings indicate that MI37 harbors distinct molecular machinery for teichoic acid modification, which may contribute to its unique immunomodulatory properties.

Taken together, our findings demonstrate that the newly isolated L. fermentum MI37 enhances anti-PD-1 efficacy by promoting robust anti-tumor immune activation, positioning MI37 as a promising microbiome-based adjuvant for cancer immunotherapy. We further identified two distinctive molecular features—elevated dltB expression and an MI37-specific single nucleotide target polymorphism (SNtp)—which likely contribute to specialized LTA biosynthesis and may underlie the strain’s potent immunostimulatory capacity. These mechanistic insights provide a strong rationale for the therapeutic enhancement observed in vivo. Although strain-level assessment of MI37 prevalence in human ICI responders is not currently feasible using available public microbiome datasets, future clinical studies incorporating MI37-specific genomic markers (e.g., SNtp and LTA-associated loci) will enable targeted detection of MI37-like strains in patients undergoing immunotherapy. Such translational analyses will be essential for determining whether MI37 colonization or its structural signatures correlate with clinical responsiveness.

Limitations of the study

In this study, we did not analyze the potential influence of sex or age on the experimental outcomes, as only 7–8-week-old female mice were used. This represents a limitation in the generalizability of our findings. Further research including both sexes and various age groups is required to determine whether these results are consistent across different biological contexts.

This study demonstrates the potent anti-tumor efficacy of MI37 in the MC38 colon cancer model; however, evaluation in a single syngeneic model represents a key limitation. Because microbiome-based interventions can vary substantially depending on host immune status and the immunologic landscape of different tumor types, validation across additional tumor models will be essential to establish the generalizability and translational potential of MI37.

A second limitation concerns the role of IFN-γ signaling in vivo. Although our results clearly show increased IFN-γ-producing CD8+ and CD4+ T cells following combination therapy, the functional requirement of IFN-γ was not directly tested. Definitive evidence would require experiments using IFN-γ-neutralizing antibodies or IFN-γ receptor-deficient mice, which we identify as important future work.

Finally, while our in vitro immune-cell co-culture assays strongly suggest that MI37-mediated induction of IFN-γ is driven by its cell-wall structural component LTA, we were unable to directly confirm this mechanism using LTA-targeted approaches such as biosynthesis inhibition or genetic knockout. Nonetheless, fractionation studies demonstrated that crude LTA isolated from MI37 potently induced IFN-γ production in splenic mononuclear cells, whereas the crude PGN and WTA fractions failed to elicit comparable responses. These observations support LTA as the most plausible immunostimulatory effector, but direct biochemical and genetic validation will be essential for future mechanistic studies.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human fecal samples

Healthy human fecal samples were collected from the Human Dock Center of Asan Medical Center with approval from the Institutional Review Board (Approval No. A20201614). A total of 77 donors were involved in this study. All donors were screen-selected for the absence of gastrointestinal diseases and recent antibiotic use to ensure a representative healthy gut microbiota. Detailed information regarding donor, including their sex, age, and the number of isolated Lactobacillus strains, is provided in Table S1.

Isolation and culture of Lactobacillus strain

To identify novel probiotic bacterial strains capable of modulating anti-tumor immune responses, we screened Lactobacillus strains isolated from the feces of healthy human volunteers, as in previous studies.23 In brief, total fecal samples were obtained from the human dock center of Asan Medical Center. Fecal samples were suspended in PBS and then seeded onto MRS agar (BD Bioscience). The fecal cultures were maintained at 37°C under anaerobic conditions generated using a GasPak 100 system (BD Bioscience). Colonies were selected from MRS plates and tested using PCR for the Lactobacillus genus with the primer set 5′-AGCAGTAGGGAATCTTCCA-3′ and 5′-ATTYCACCGCTACACATG-3′.

Mice and ethics statement

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Asan Institute for Life Sciences (Approval No. 2024-20-172). All procedures adhered to the appropriate institutional guidelines and regulatory requirements. C57BL/6N mice were purchased from Orient Bio (Seongnam, South Korea). Experimental mice were 7–8-week-old females fed sterile food and water ad libitum housed in the animal facility of Asan Medical Center (Seoul, South Korea) and kept under specific pathogen-free conditions. The influence of sex and age on the study results was not specifically evaluated, and this is acknowledged as a limitation of the study.

Cell lines

The MC38 cell lines were purchased from Kerafast. The cell lines were authenticated by the supplier via STR profiling and were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination before use.

Method details

In vitro immuno-stimulatory assay

Lactobacillus strains were cultured under anaerobic conditions in MRS medium for 18–20 h. Spleen and mLNs were harvested from C57BL/6N mice, and mononuclear cells were isolated by mechanical disruption. Cells were resuspended in RPMI (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Gibco), 100× non-essential amino acids (Gibco), 20 mM HEPES (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco), and 55 μM β-mercaptoethanol (Gibco). A total of 2×105 cells were plated in 96-well plates and co-cultured with bacterial strains at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 1:20 (cells:bacterial CFU) for 3 days in the presence of 100 μg/mL gentamicin. Cells were also co-cultured with MI37-derived subcellular fractions or sLTA (MedChemExpress). After 72 h, culture supernatants were collected for cytokine analysis, and cells were re-stimulated with PMA (50 ng/mL, Sigma), ionomycin (2 μM, Sigma) in the presence of GolgiStop (BD Biosciences) for 4 h prior to intracellular cytokine staining by flow cytometry. Mouse IL-10 and IFN-γ concentrations in supernatants were measured using an ELISA kit (IL-10; Invitrogen, IFN-γ; BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To block TLR2, cells were pre-treated with the 100uM TLR2 inhibitor C29 for 1 h before being treated with sLTA or HK-bacteria.

Culture of the MC38 cell line

The MC38 cell line was cultured in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS, 2 mM glutamine, 0.1 mM nonessential amino acids, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, 10 mM HEPES, 50ug/ml gentamycin sulfate, and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

In vivo tumor model

For syngeneic tumor experiments, female C57BL/6N mice were subcutaneously injected with MC38 colon cancer cells (2×105). Beginning on day 5, mice received oral administration of MI37 (2 × 109 CFU) five times per week for the duration of the experiment. Mice were considered successfully engrafted when palpable tumors were detected within 7–9 days after MC38 cell inoculation and tumor volume reached approximately 30–80 mm3 prior to ICI treatment initiation. Anti-PD-1 monoclonal antibody (100 μg) was administered intraperitoneally twice weekly from days 7–21. Tumor size was measured three times weekly, and tumor volume was calculated as length × width2 × 0.5. Mice were euthanized at indicated time points or when tumor volume reached the humane endpoint of 1,500 mm3. The initial group size was six mice per group; however, animals were occasionally excluded from analysis based on predefined criteria, such as unsuccessful tumor engraftment or early humane endpoint criteria not related to treatment effects, resulting in final group sizes of five to six mice.

Tumor homogenization

Tumors were excised, weighed, and homogenized in Tris-EDTA buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 1 mM EDTA, pH 7.4) containing 0.05% sodium azide, 1% Tween-80, and protease inhibitor cocktail. Homogenates were centrifuged at 11,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was collected for IFN-γ measurement using a mouse ELISA kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Isolation of tumor-infiltrating leukocytes

Tumors were minced into small fragments and digested in 10 mL of RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 units/mL collagenase I, 1 mg/mL collagenase IV, 50 μg/mL DNase I, and 100 U/mL penicillin–streptomycin for 45 min at 37°C with magnetic stirring. The digested tissue was resuspended in 10 mL HBSS containing 5 mM EDTA and passed through a 100-μm cell strainer. Cell suspensions corresponding to 0.5 g of the original tumor weight were subjected to 40–80% Percoll gradient centrifugation. Lymphocytes and myeloid cells were collected, washed in RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS, and stimulated with 50 ng/mL PMA and 1 μg/mL ionomycin in the presence of Golgistop for 4 h at 37°C.

Flow cytometry analysis

Cells were washed with PBS with 1% BSA before staining. For Fc receptor blocking, cells were incubated with anti-CD16/32 (BD Biosciences). For surface staining, cells were incubated with specific antibodies at 4°C for 20 min. For intracellular staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized with the BD Cytofix/Cytoperm kit for 20 min at 4°C. Live/dead discrimination was performed with the Invitrogen staining kit. The following antibodies were used: anti-CD45 (clone Ly-5, APC/cy7, BD Biosciences), anti-CD3 (clone 17A2, PerCP/cy5.5, BioLegend), anti-CD4 (clone RM4-5, PE-cy7, Invitrogen), anti-CD8 (clone Ly-2, FITC, BD Biosciences), anti-IFN-γ (clone XMG1.2, APC, BD Biosciences), anti-TNF-α (clone MP6-XT22, PE, BioLegend), anti-B220 (clone RA3-6B2, PE, BD Biosciences), anti-CD11c (clone HL3, FITC, BD Biosciences), anti-MHCII (clone M5/114.15.2, eflour 450, eBioscience), anti-CD11b (clone M1/70, PE-cy7, eBioscience), and anti-F4/80 (clone BM8, APC, eBioscience).

Real-time qPCR

Total RNA was isolated from tumors using the RNeasy Mini Kit (QIAGEN). RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA with Superscript II reverse transcriptase and oligo (dT) primers (Thermo Fisher). Real-time qPCR was performed using SYBR Green chemistry (Affymetrix) on an ABI 7500 Real-Time qPCR System (Applied Biosystems). The primer sequences is indicated in Table S2.

BM-DC differentiation and stimulation

BM cells were harvested from the femurs and tibias of C57BL/6N mice. Following red blood cell lysis, cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% Antibiotic–Antimycotic (Gibco), 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, non-essential amino acids, β-mercaptoethanol, and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF, 20 ng/mL, Peprotech) to generate BM-DCs. On days 7–8, CD11c+ BM-DCs were purified using CD11c MicroBeads Ultrapure (Miltenyi Biotec) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. BM-DCs were seeded in 96-well plates and incubated for 18 h with MI37 or KCTC3112 at an MOI of 1:20, or with sLTA at the indicated concentrations. Culture supernatants were collected for IL-12p70 quantification using ELISA. All BM-DC experiments were conducted in complete medium supplemented with 10 ng/mL GM-CSF.

IL-12p70 ELISA

Samples were added to a 96-well microplate pre-coated with a capture antibody and incubated at room temperature (RT) for 2 h. After washing, the detection antibody was applied, followed by streptavidin-HRP (R&D). The following antibodies were used: IL-12p70 (clone 48110, R&D) as the capture antibody and biotinylated anti–IL-12 (clone P43432, R&D) as the detection antibody.

DCs-dependent CD8+ T cell activation

Primed BM-DCs were washed twice with PBS to remove residual bacteria. For CD8+ T cell priming assays, naive murine splenic CD8+ T cells were isolated using MACS microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec). Naive CD8+ T cells (2.5×105) were co-cultured with primed BM-DCs (5×104) for 3 days with 10 ng/mL anti-CD3 (BioLegend) and 10 ng/mL IL-2 (R&D). After 72 h, supernatants were collected, and cells were re-stimulated with PMA (50 ng/mL, Sigma) and ionomycin (2 μM, Sigma) in the presence of GolgiStop (BD Biosciences) for 4 h, followed by intracellular cytokine staining and flow cytometry.

Preparation of HK bacteria and bacterial culture supernatant

L. fermentum MI37 and KCTC3112 were cultured in MRS broth for 16 h. Cultures were centrifuged, and the supernatants were filtered through a 0.2-μm syringe filter (PALL) before treatment of mononuclear cells in the presence of 500 ng/mL LPS. Bacterial pellet was washed twice with PBS, resuspended in PBS, and heat-inactivated at 95°C for 30 min.

MI37 subcellular fractionation

MI37 cultures in the exponential growth phase were centrifuged and washed twice with PBS.

To increase permeability, cells were heat-inactivated. The turbid supernatants obtained after 20 min of sonication in 0.1M sodium citrate buffer (pH 4.7), followed by removal of unbroken cells, were considered MI37 cleavage products. Ultrasonicated lysates were centrifuged at 13,000 × g for 10 min, and the resulting pellet fraction, containing cell-wall and membrane fragments, were collected for subsequent extraction of structural components. Both the pellet and supernatant fractions were lyophilized and re-suspended in PBS. For in vitro immuno-stimulatory assays, mononuclear cells (2×105) were co-cultured with 10 μg/mL of MI37-derived subcellular fractions in the presence of 100 μg/mL Normocin (Invivogen).

Isolation of cell wall–derived structural components from MI37

Crude PGN and WTA were isolated as previously indicated with minor modifications.57 MI37 of exponential phase was first subjected to ultrasonic disruption to obtain crude cleavage products. To remove membrane-associated materials and lipoproteins, these pellet products were treated with 0.5% SDS at 60°C for 30 min and subsequently washed with PBS. The resulting pellet was subjected to digestion: first, with 50 μg/mL DNase and 50 μg/mL RNase in 1M Tris-HCl (pH7.0) for 2 h at RT, and then with 200 μg/mL Trypsin and 50 mM CaCl2 for 24 h at 37°C with shaking. After washing with PBS, the pellet was incubated in 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) at RT with shaking for 24 h to separate WTA. Following centrifugation at 20,000 × g for 10 min, whole PGN was sedimented. WTA was recovered from the supernatant by performing TCA extraction with two volumes of diethyl ether repeatedly until the sample pH reached 7.0; the final WTA solution was then lyophilized and reconstituted in D.W. Simultaneously, the whole PGN precipitates was washed twice with acetone and then with D.W until the pH reached 7.0. To obtain soluble PGN, the lyophilized PGN was enzymatically digested with 1 mL of 5,000 U/mL mutanolysin at 37°C for 16 h with shaking. The enzyme was inactivated by incubation at 100 °C for 10 min, and soluble PGN was subsequently collected from the supernatant after centrifugation. To isolate the crude LTA fraction, we extracted bacterial LTA using an n-butanol–based method originally described by Pradhan et al.58 with minor modifications. The pellet obtained after ultrasonication was mixed with an equal volume of n-butanol and stirred at room temperature for 30 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 10 min, and the lower aqueous phase containing the crude LTA was collected and lyophilized.

Inorganic phosphate assay

LTA levels were indirectly quantified using the PiPer Phosphate Assay Kit (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For sample preparation, sLTA or crude LTA isolated from bacterial cells was reconstituted in distilled water. To release inorganic phosphate from LTA, samples were mixed with HCl and boiled at 98 °C for 2 h prior to performing the assay.

Confocal imaging of LTA

MI37 was cultured to the exponential phase in MRS broth, harvested by centrifugation, and washed with 1 mL of PBS. Cells were fixed in 4% formalin and digested with 50 U/mL of mutanolysin for 30 min at 37°C. After washing, cells were attached to silane-coated glass slides for 15 min, then sequentially permeabilized with ice-cold methanol and acetone (5 min each, −20°C). For antibody staining, cells were incubated with a monoclonal anti-LTA IgG antibody (clone 55, Hycult Biotech) in PBS supplemented with 1% BSA, followed by incubation for 1 h with goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500, Invitrogen) in the dark. Bacterial membranes were counterstained with 1 μg/mL FM5-95 (Invitrogen) in H2O for 10 min. Negative controls were processed identically, omitting the primary antibody. After mounting, fluorescence images were acquired using an LSM 710 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss).

Whole-genome sequencing

Genomic DNA (gDNA) from MI37 was extracted using the MagAttract HMW DNA Kit (Qiagen), following the manufacturer’s instructions. DNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentration was determined with Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Invitrogen). For shearing, 5 μg of gDNA was processed using a Megaruptor3. A SMRTbell library was prepared using the SMRTbell Express Template Preparation Kit (PacBio). Genome and library sizes, along with Qubit concentrations, were entered into the PacBio calculator for pooling. Sequencing was performed using the Sequel Sequencing Kit v3.0 and SMRT Cell 1 M v2. De novo genome assembly was generated from PacBio reads, and sequencing analyses were performed by CJ Bioscience, Inc. Gene prediction and functional annotation of the assembled genome were conducted using the EzBioCloud genome database. Comparative WGS analysis was performed by calculating average nucleotide identity based on BLAST (ANIb) using the ANI calculator from Kostas lab (http://enve-omics.ce.gatech.edu/ani).

RNA-seq analysis

Total RNA was extracted from tumor tissues using the RNeasy mini kit (QIAGEN). RNA-seq libraries were prepared from 1 μg of total RNA per sample using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA LT Sample Prep Kit (Illumina). The protocol included poly(A) selection, RNA extraction, RNA fragmentation, random hexamer-primed reverse transcription, and paired-end (101 nt) sequencing on a NovaSeq6000 platform (Illumina). Library quantification was performed using qPCR according to the qPCR Quantification Protocol Guide (KAPA Library Quantification Kit for Illumina Sequencing platforms) and quality assessed with a 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies). RNA-seq and statistical analyses were conducted by Macrogen Inc. (Seoul, South Korea). Reads were pre-processed to remove adapters and low-quality sequences, then aligned to the Mus musculus genome (mm10) using HISAT v2.0.5. Transcripts were assembled and quantified with StringTie v1.3.3b. Filtered expression data were log2-transformed and normalized by quantile normalization. For gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), raw count matrices were normalized, and enrichment was performed with GSEA software using a pre-ranked gene list. Nominal P-values were estimated by 1000 permutations.

Preparation of bacterial cell membrane sample for proteome analysis

Lyophilized bacterial cell membrane fractions were resuspended in 5% SDS buffer containing 50 mM TEAB (pH 8.5) for protein denaturation. Disulfide bonds were reduced by adding 20 mM dithiothreitol and incubating at 95°C for 30 min, followed by alkylation with 40 mM iodoacetamide in the dark at RT for 30 min. For digestion conditioning, samples were acidified with a 10-fold dilution of 12% phosphoric acid, and suspension trap (S-Trap) binding buffer (90% methanol, 100 mM TEAB, pH 7.55) was added to a final volume of 500 μL, according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The mixture was loaded onto an S-Trap spin column (ProtiFi, Long Island, New York, USA), washed twice with 150 μL of S-Trap binding buffer, and centrifuged at 4,000×g for 30 s after each wash. Proteins were digested with A trypsin/Lys-C mix (protein-to-enzyme ratio, 50:1; Promega, Madison, WI) dissolved in 50 mM TEAB (pH 8.5), and the column was incubated at 37°C for 12 h without shaking.59 Peptides were sequentially eluted with 40 μL of 50 mM TEAB, and 40 μL of 0.2% formic acid/50% acetonitrile, with centrifugation at 1,000×g (first two elutions) or 4,000×g (final elution) for 1 min each. Eluted peptides were dried and stored at −80 °C until LC-MS analysis.

LC-MS analysis and database search

Peptide mixtures were reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid containing 0.005% n-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM). Separation was performed on an Ultimate3000 RSLC system coupled to a Q Exactive HFx mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The liquid chromatography gradient and data-dependent acquisition parameters followed previously published protocols.60,61 MS spectra were searched using Sequest HT within Proteome Discoverer (version 2.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) against the Limosilactobacillus fermentum proteome (strain ATCC 14931/DSM 20052/NCIMB 11840) (Uniprot, https://www.uniprot.org/proteomes). Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification. Identified proteins and label-free quantification values were extracted for downstream analysis. Protein identification was performed using the SequestHT on Proteome discoverer (version 2.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) against the UniProt protein sequence database of Limosilactobacillus fermentum (Proteome ID: UP000001697). Briefly, precursor mass tolerance was set to ±10 ppm and MS/MS tolerance was set at 0.02 Da. The search parameters were set as default including cysteine carbamidomethylation as a fixed modification and n-terminal acetylation and methionine oxidation as variable modifications with two miscleavages. The false discovery rates were set at 1% for the peptides in each analysis using “Percolator”. From the SEQUEST search output, peptide filters that included peptide confidence, peptide rank, score versus charge state, and search engine rank were set at the default values for the proteome discoverer. Label-free quantitation was performed using the peak intensity for the unique and razor peptides of each protein and excluded peptides including methionine oxidation. The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE62 partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD071250.

COG enrichment pathway

Genes and proteins identified from WGS and proteomics datasets were functionally annotated using EggNOG-mapper v2.63 The annotated features were subsequently categorized according to COG functional classifications.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0.2 (GraphPad Software). Comparisons between two groups were made using two-tailed unpaired t-tests. Multiple group comparisons were performed by one- or two-way ANOVA followed by Bonferroni’s post hoc test (∗, p < 0.05; ∗∗, p < 0.01; ∗∗∗, p < 0.001). Statistical details of experiments can be found in the figure legends. The value of n indicates the number of mice in each group, as specified in the figure legends.
