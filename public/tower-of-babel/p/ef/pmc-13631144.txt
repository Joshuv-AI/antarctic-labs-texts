Extracellular DNA Alters Detection of Subtle Bacterial Responses to Soil Rewetting
Authors: H A Kittredge, K M Dougherty, Kathryn Glanville, Sarah E Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobial communities are often characterized using DNA-based sequencing, but these approaches also capture extracellular DNA (exDNA) released from dead cells, potentially altering inference about microbial responses to environmental change. This may be especially important during pulse disturbances, such as soil drying–rewetting, which can increase microbial mortality and transient necromass pools. We assessed whether exDNA altered inference about bacterial responses to drying–rewetting (an 80 mm simulated rainfall event following a 28-day drought) in conventionally tilled corn and perennial switchgrass soils. We quantified bacterial abundance (16 S rRNA gene copies), alpha diversity, and community composition in paired soil samples with exDNA included (+ exDNA) and in samples treated with propidium monoazide (PMAxx) to reduce amplification of exDNA (− exDNA). At our level of replication (n = 4), PMAxx treatment did not significantly alter overall temporal response patterns (i.e., no significant main effect of DNA treatment or DNA × time interaction). However, PMAxx treatment increased sensitivity to detect some pairwise temporal changes in bacterial abundance and community composition in corn soils following rewetting. exDNA pools were proportionally highest immediately after rewetting in corn soils, suggesting transient extracellular DNA may contribute to masking during disturbance recovery. In contrast, PMAxx treatment had comparatively small effects in switchgrass soils, which exhibited weaker temporal responses overall. Inclusion of exDNA also changed which taxa appeared most responsive to rewetting. Together, our results suggest that exDNA does not uniformly bias soil microbial inference, but may reduce detectability of subtle disturbance-driven shifts in certain soils. Introduction

Large pools of extracellular DNA (exDNA) can accumulate in the environment as bacteria die [1–6]. This exDNA is included in DNA-based characterizations of ‘live’ communities, and it can alter estimates of bacterial abundance, diversity, and community composition [7–14]. exDNA can also obscure temporal changes in microbial communities (Carini et al. [8]), particularly after pulse disturbances, which can cause non-random microbial mortality and increase compositional differences between extracellular and intracellular DNA pools [9]. In addition, a sudden increase in the amount of necromass produced with pulse disturbances may cause underestimates of bacterial mortality and misrepresentations of community shifts [15–18]. This could hide dynamics by rare or previously low-abundance taxa that DNA-SIP studies show can increase under rewetting [19, 20]. Here, we investigate soil drying-rewetting, a well-characterized soil disturbance [21], asking whether exDNA obscures estimates of bacterial abundance (16S copy number with quantitative PCR), alpha diversity, and community composition (MiSeq sequencing of 16S rRNA gene, Supplemental Methods).

We characterized these bacterial dynamics in a drying-rewetting experiment [22] in two different cropping systems in the Great Lakes Bioenergy Research Center’s cropping system experiment at Kellogg Biological Station in Michigan, USA. The experiment induced a longer drought and larger rewetting event than the historical average. Repeating this experiment in two cropping systems (corn agriculture and perennial switchgrass monoculture) allowed us to examine how exDNA dynamics changed with variation in organic matter and other soil properties. We collected soil and DNA at 3 timepoints: after 28 days of no precipitation (~ 6 h before rewetting, timepoint 0 or t0), 1 h after a field-simulated 80 mm rewetting event (t1), and 18 h after rewetting (t2) (Fig. 1a, N = 4 per crop type). At each time point we removed exDNA from one of two paired soil samples using the chemical propidium monoazide (PMAxx) which binds to exDNA and prevents amplification. We used a PMAxx method modified from Carini et al. [8], and optimized both PMAxx concentration (0.12 mM) and light conditions (Fig. S1, Supplementary Methods) to try and overcome some weaknesses of this method [23].

We first asked (i) how exDNA concentration varied over time and with changing soil moisture, predicting that exDNA concentration would increase in the first hour after rewetting due (t1) to an increase in bacterial death [16], and decline thereafter (t2) as exDNA decays [24, 25]. Second, (ii) we asked whether including exDNA influenced conclusions about microbial community response to changes in soil moisture. We predicted that exDNA would obscure estimates, and would do so most strongly after soil is rewet (t1) because of the release of exDNA. Finally, (iii) we asked how soil properties altered response to moisture and exDNA dynamics. We predicted that exDNA dynamics would differ between intensively managed corn and perennial switchgrass soils due to differences in soil structure, moisture retention, microbial activity, and disturbance regime [26–29]. Switchgrass soils may promote longer exDNA persistence through moisture buffering and greater organic matter stabilization, but may also promote faster exDNA turnover due to greater microbial activity.

Results and Discussion

Relic exDNA has been shown not only to inflate richness estimates [7, 9], but also to dampen detection of temporal turnover in soil microbial communities [8], suggesting that total DNA pools may underestimate dynamic responses. This may be especially true during pulse disturbances when temporal trends may be subtle, exDNA is released from death [27, 30], and exDNA pools are more dynamic. PMAxx can remove some of this relic DNA, but whether this is needed to detect temporal microbial dynamics is unclear. For our first question, we predicted that exDNA pools would increase following soil rewetting due to osmotic lysis [16]. We found partial support for this prediction. In absolute terms, exDNA concentrations trended highest at the end of the 28-day drought (t0), not t1 (Fig. 1b). It is likely drought induced some mortality, but perhaps this also reflects slower exDNA degradation under dry conditions [24, 25, 31]. When examining the proportional response of exDNA, relative to total 16S rRNA gene copies, we found proportion of exDNA was greatest immediately following rewetting in corn soils (Fig. S2), supporting our prediction that exDNA is high during early rewetting, which may be more important for obscuring the live proportion. Because PMAxx efficiency may vary under changing soil conditions [23], and because our sampling times (1 h and 18 h post-rewetting) may have missed transient exDNA pulses, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously. To help predict when and where exDNA could lead to masking, we need a better general understanding of microbial turnover and necromass stability under fluctuating moisture (but see [32]), particularly studies that use multiple methods (e.g., PMAxx and isotopic labeling).

Our second question was whether failing to apply PMAxx masked our ability to detect important changes in microbial communities over time, not only under stable conditions [8], but after a drying-rewetting disturbance. Drying–rewetting cycles may increase mortality and transient relic DNA, conditions predicted by theory to increase the potential for exDNA bias [9]. In our study, masking was minimal and context dependent. In the broadest sense, applying PMAxx (removing exDNA) did not significantly modify temporal response patterns (i.e., no significant main effect of DNA, or time x DNA interaction, Table 1, S1). Although unsurprisingly, inclusion of exDNA consistently inflated estimated copy number (Abundance DNA term: p < 0.1). Nevertheless, when we conducted pairwise sensitivity analyses we found that treating with PMAxx allowed us to detect some statistically different temporal changes in abundance and community composition (Table S2, Fig. 1b, d) in corn soil, and particularly when comparing before rewetting to after, consistent with what we predicted. But it should be emphasized that even where exDNA inclusion altered statistical significance (Table S2), it did not change the direction of the effect (higher or lower) and did not change temporal trends (Fig. 1b–e). Thus, exDNA in our study did not affect overall temporal dynamics, but exDNA removal may help increase statistical power effects are subtle or replication is low, as it was in this study.

We found support for our third prediction: soil properties altered responses to moisture and exDNA inclusion. This is consistent with theoretical [9] and empirical studies demonstrating context-dependent relic DNA effects [8], although soil properties may also influence PMAxx effectiveness [23]. Overall, exDNA removal and moisture changes (Fig. 1a) had larger effects in corn than switchgrass soil. Corn soils had lower organic matter, root biomass, and water holding capacity, which may have increased microbial stress and amplified death and transient exDNA effects following rewetting. In contrast, switchgrass soils appeared comparatively buffered, showing smaller soil moisture changes and proportional exDNA effects, and fewer changes in ecological inference after PMAxx treatment. Although exDNA removal altered paired community composition in switchgrass soils (Fig. 1d), these shifts did not substantially alter overall conclusions. Together, these findings suggest that exDNA effects may become strongest in disturbed soils undergoing rapid moisture transitions. Perhaps greater transient cell death in disturbed corn soils with lower moisture buffering capacity leads to stronger short-term exDNA effects, which outweigh the potentially greater exDNA persistence promoted by higher organic matter in switchgrass soils.

Our taxa-specific analysis showed that exDNA inclusion can alter which taxa appear responsive to rewetting (Fig. 2). Several taxa, including Mizugakiibacter, Bryobacter, and Luteolibacter, exhibited strong transient post-rewetting increases only when total DNA was analyzed, suggesting that exDNA may inflate apparent microbial responses to rewetting disturbance. In contrast, some taxa (e.g., Massilia) showed stronger responses following PMAxx treatment, which may have been masked without this treatment. The identity and magnitude of affected groups also differed between corn and switchgrass soils (Fig. 2, Fig. S3), consistent with previous studies showing that relic DNA effects vary across taxa and environmental contexts. Because PMAxx may not comprehensively remove all exDNA, nor remove it at the same effectiveness for every taxa, it may not be reliable to compare taxon dynamics in PMA-treated soil. Still, PMAxx treatment likely provides a closer approximation of live microbial dynamics than total DNA alone, and studies interested in responses of specific taxa should be cautious about exDNA masking this.

In conclusion, under a pulse disturbance, we found that exDNA inclusion did not uniformly bias soil microbial inferences, but could obscure subtle responses to disturbance under specific soil conditions. By experimentally imposing a well-characterized hydrological pulse, we extend prior work on temporal exDNA masking into a disturbance ecology framework. Our results suggest that exDNA removal may be most important in disturbed soils undergoing rapid environmental change, particularly when evaluating responses of individual taxa or subtle community shifts. In contrast, exDNA effects may be less important when treatment effects are large or statistical power is high. Future work integrating complementary approaches for distinguishing living and relic DNA, alongside improved understanding of microbial mortality, extracellular DNA persistence, and degradation, will help predict when exDNA is most likely to meaningfully influence ecological inference.
