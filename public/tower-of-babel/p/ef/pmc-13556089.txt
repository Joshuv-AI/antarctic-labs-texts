Prevalence and clinical correlates of avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder in children and adolescents with Celiac disease: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Halenur Teke, Aydan Sogut, Hilal Tuter Mavi, Selcuk Teke, Yasin Maruf Ergen, Birce Izgi Akcay, Seda Bozduman Celebi, Mehmet Ayhan Congologlu, Necati Balamtekin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAvoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID) may represent a clinically important problem in celiac disease (CD), where lifelong dietary restriction, ongoing gastrointestinal symptoms, and food-related anxiety may promote food avoidance beyond gluten-containing foods and contribute to maladaptive eating patterns. Although adult studies have reported high rates of ARFID symptoms in CD, pediatric data are limited. This study aimed to evaluate the prevalence and clinical correlates of ARFID in children and adolescents with CD using a multidisciplinary assessment approach involving pediatric gastroenterology and child and adolescent psychiatry specialists, including anthropometric, nutritional, bone health, psychological, and health-related quality-of-life (QoL) correlates.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted at a tertiary-care center involving children and adolescents aged 8–17 years with biopsy-confirmed CD (n = 101). Participants were assessed using a combination of clinical interviews and psychometric scales, including the Nine Item ARFID Screen (NIAS), the Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale (RCADS), and the Pediatric QoL Inventory (PedsQL). ARFID diagnosis was established through a DSM-5-guided clinical evaluation by two child and adolescent psychiatrists, and clinical and anthropometric data were collected alongside laboratory results. Multivariable regression models were used to identify factors independently associated with ARFID and clinical outcomes.ResultsThe prevalence of ARFID in the cohort was 42.6%. Children with ARFID exhibited significantly lower weight and BMI z-scores (p < 0.001 and p = 0.002, respectively), a higher prevalence of osteopenia/osteoporosis (p = 0.005) and micronutrient deficiencies (p < 0.001), poorer QoL (p < 0.001), and higher anxiety and depressive symptom scores (p < 0.001 and p = 0.003, respectively). Presence of gastrointestinal symptoms and higher anxiety score were independently associated with ARFID in children and adolescents with CD.ConclusionIn this cohort, ARFID appeared to be a clinically relevant comorbidity in pediatric CD, with adverse physical and psychosocial correlates. Restrictive eating extending beyond gluten avoidance should be recognized during clinical assessment, particularly in children with gastrointestinal symptoms, prominent anxiety symptoms, poor weight gain, or persistent micronutrient deficiencies. Longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the clinical course of ARFID symptoms and to inform targeted multidisciplinary care in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40337-026-01699-3.

Introduction

Celiac disease (CD) is a chronic, immune-mediated systemic disorder triggered by gluten ingestion in genetically predisposed individuals, and its cornerstone therapy is a lifelong strict gluten-free diet (GFD) [1]. Although clinical control can be achieved in most cases with a GFD, the diet’s rigidity and the constant vigilance required to avoid inadvertent gluten exposure through cross-contact impose a substantial treatment burden [2]. Particularly during childhood and adolescence, these requirements may restrict eating in social settings, increase isolation, disrupt family mealtime routines, and reinforce food-related anxiety [3]. Collectively, these factors may contribute to maladaptive eating patterns [4, 5].

Avoidant/Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID) is an eating disorder characterized by persistent restrictive eating driven by sensory sensitivity, lack of interest in eating, or fear of aversive consequences of eating—without concerns about weight or body shape—and may lead to nutritional deficiency, impaired growth/weight gain, and/or marked psychosocial dysfunction [6] Across community-based pediatric and adolescent samples, reported ARFID prevalence varies widely, ranging from 0.3% to 15.5% [7]. In CD, ongoing gastrointestinal symptoms, anxiety about gluten exposure, negatively conditioned responses to food, social difficulties related to adherence to a GFD, and psychiatric comorbidities may increase vulnerability to ARFID, potentially promoting avoidance that extends beyond gluten-containing foods to other medically safe items [5, 8–11].

Consistent with these mechanisms, evidence—particularly from adult samples—suggests that ARFID is more prevalent in individuals with CD than in the general population and is linked to exacerbated nutritional deficiencies, increased anxiety and depression, as well as deteriorated social functioning [5, 10, 11]. However, findings from adult cohorts cannot be directly extrapolated to children and adolescents, because restrictive eating during this developmental period may have additional implications for growth, weight gain, pubertal development, micronutrient status, bone health, and family-based dietary management. Therefore, pediatric data are needed to evaluate the clinical relevance of restrictive eating that extends beyond medically required gluten avoidance in children and adolescents with CD. We hypothesized that ARFID would be frequent in pediatric CD and would be associated with gastrointestinal symptoms, higher anxiety and depressive symptom burden, poorer nutritional and anthropometric status, lower bone mineral density, and impaired health-related quality of life (QoL). Accordingly, using a multidisciplinary assessment by pediatric gastroenterology and child and adolescent psychiatry, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of ARFID and its main clinical and psychosocial correlates in children and adolescents with CD.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted between September 2025 and February 2026 at a tertiary-care center, in the Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition and Child and Adolescent Psychiatry outpatient clinics. Eligible participants were children and adolescents aged 8–17 years with CD, either newly diagnosed at the time of assessment or previously diagnosed and followed in the pediatric gastroenterology clinic. CD diagnosis had been established by pediatric gastroenterologists according to the ESPGHAN 2012 or 2020 diagnostic recommendations, depending on the date of diagnosis. To ensure diagnostic consistency, all included participants had histopathologic confirmation of CD on small intestinal biopsy obtained during upper gastrointestinal endoscopy; no biopsy-free diagnoses were included. Marsh–Oberhuber type 2 or type 3 lesions were considered histologically compatible with CD in the appropriate clinical and serological context [12, 13].

Participants were excluded only if they had a previously diagnosed or clinically evident neurodevelopmental disorder, such as autism spectrum disorder or intellectual disability, that precluded reliable participation in the psychiatric interview or completion of questionnaire-based assessments. This decision was based on available medical history, parent-reported developmental and educational history, and clinical judgment during the psychiatric assessment.

Eligible participants were consecutively recruited from the Pediatric Gastroenterology outpatient clinic during routine follow-up or diagnostic visits, where they underwent standard clinical evaluation for CD. Participants whose parents/legal guardians provided informed consent and who themselves provided assent were referred for a same-day study assessment in the Child and Adolescent Psychiatry clinic, including a psychiatric interview and questionnaire administration.

ARFID diagnosis

All participants underwent a face-to-face clinical assessment with the child and at least one parent. ARFID was evaluated jointly by two child and adolescent psychiatrists according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5) criteria [14]. The diagnosis of ARFID was assigned only when both clinicians reached consensus that the DSM-5 criteria were met. The NIAS-P was not used as a diagnostic cutoff for ARFID; rather, it was administered to characterize ARFID symptom severity. The diagnosis was based on the overall DSM-5-guided clinical evaluation, integrating child and parent reports, eating history, current food avoidance patterns, medical history, anthropometric and nutritional consequences, and psychosocial functioning.

To minimize misclassification with medically required dietary treatment for CD, avoidance limited to gluten-containing foods was not classified as ARFID. Similarly, temporary food restriction during acute gastrointestinal symptoms, physician-recommended dietary restriction, or avoidance that was proportional to active CD-related symptoms was not considered sufficient for an ARFID diagnosis. ARFID was diagnosed only when restrictive eating extended beyond gluten-containing foods to medically safe gluten-free foods and was associated with DSM-5-defined clinically significant consequences, including weight/growth impact, nutritional deficiency, need for supplementation or feeding support, and/or marked psychosocial impairment. Marked psychosocial impairment was considered present when restrictive eating substantially interfered with family meals, school eating, eating outside the home, peer activities involving food, or caused persistent distress or family conflict around eating.

Restrictive eating attributable to limited food availability, culturally sanctioned practices, weight- or shape-related concerns, or a pattern better explained by another medical or psychiatric condition was not diagnosed as ARFID.

Data collection and clinical variables

Study data were obtained from a study-specific sociodemographic/clinical form and hospital records. The form captured basic child/family and parental sociodemographic characteristics, gastrointestinal symptoms within the previous 3 months, supplement use, and comorbidities. Hospital records provided Marsh-Oberhuber histopathological grade at diagnosis [15], serum anti-tissue transglutaminase immunoglobulin A (tTG-IgA) level measured on the day of study assessment, time since CD diagnosis, anthropometric measurements, micronutrient status, and DXA-based BMD. tTG-IgA positivity was defined as ≥ 1× the upper limit of normal (ULN); this threshold was used only to classify current serologic status and was not used as a diagnostic criterion for CD. Height and weight were measured by nursing staff at the index visit, and height z-score, weight z-score, and BMI z-score were calculated according to Neyzi reference standards [16]. Time since CD diagnosis was recorded in months, with newly diagnosed cases coded as 0 months.

GFD adherence was assessed by the pediatric gastroenterology team only in previously diagnosed participants who had initiated a GFD, based on child-parent report and serologic follow-up. Participants were classified as non-adherent if dietary lapses were reported or if tTG-IgA positivity persisted beyond 12 months after diagnosis [17]. Micronutrient deficiency was defined as at least one documented vitamin or mineral deficiency within the preceding year. BMD was recorded using the most recent dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) measurement obtained within 12 months before study assessment and expressed as a height-age-adjusted z-score using Turkish pediatric reference curves. BMD categories were defined as normal BMD (z-score > − 1), osteopenia (− 2 < z-score ≤ − 1), and osteoporosis (z-score ≤ − 2) [18, 19].

Psychometric assessments

All instruments were administered individually, and completed forms were reviewed on-site to minimize missing data. ARFID symptom severity was assessed using the Nine Item ARFID Screen—Parent Report (NIAS-P), comprising three subscales (selective eating, appetite/interest, and fear of aversive consequences) and a total score; higher scores indicate greater ARFID symptom severity (developed by Zickgraf and Ellis; Turkish validation by Akçay et al.) [20, 21].

Anxiety and depressive symptoms were assessed using the Revised Child Anxiety and Depression Scale (RCADS), which yields six subscale scores (separation anxiety, social anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder, panic disorder, generalized anxiety, and major depressive disorder). In this study, RCADS raw scores were used to assess dimensional anxiety and depressive symptom burden, including the total anxiety score, depression score, and RCADS total score (developed by Chorpita et al.; Turkish validation by Görmez et al.) [22, 23].

Health-related QoL was assessed using the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) Generic Core Scales, a generic, non-disease-specific pediatric health-related QoL instrument applicable across pediatric populations, including children and adolescents with chronic medical conditions. The scale yields physical, psychosocial, and total QoL scores on a 0–100 scale, with higher scores indicating better QoL. The Turkish versions have demonstrated validity and reliability in children and adolescents (developed by Varni et al.; Turkish validation by Memik et al.) [24–26].

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethics Committee of the University of Health Sciences, Gülhane Training and Research Hospital (Decision No: 2025/182, Decision Date: 11.09.2025). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their parents or legal guardians after providing a comprehensive explanation of the study aims and procedures. All study procedures adhered to the ethical standards set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test together with visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. Continuous variables with an approximately normal distribution were summarized as mean ± SD, whereas non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median (IQR). Categorical variables were reported as n (%). Between-group comparisons were performed using the independent-samples t test for approximately normally distributed continuous variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for non-normally distributed continuous variables. The chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Because the cohort included both newly diagnosed and previously diagnosed participants, outcomes were also compared according to time since CD diagnosis (< 12 months vs. ≥ 12 months). This cutoff was selected to reflect the clinically relevant first-year period after diagnosis, during which serologic response, mucosal recovery, and nutritional improvement are expected to occur in many children with CD [17]. Associations between continuous variables were examined using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). To examine independent associations, multivariable regression models were constructed according to outcome type: multivariable logistic regression was used for binary outcomes (ARFID status and osteopenia/osteoporosis vs. normal BMD), and multivariable linear regression was used for continuous outcomes (BMI z-score and PedsQL total score). Variables for each multivariable model were selected a priori based on the main study aims, clinical relevance, and variables considered most relevant from the univariable comparisons, while keeping the number of covariates appropriate for the sample size. Effect estimates were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI) for logistic regression and β coefficients for linear regression. Multicollinearity in multivariable models was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIFs), with VIF > 5 considered indicative of potential multicollinearity. For logistic regression models, goodness of fit was evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test. For linear regression models, overall model fit was assessed using the F test and R² values, and residual independence was evaluated using the Durbin-Watson statistic. A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Participant characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the study cohort (n = 101). The mean age was 11.70 ± 3.03 years, and 51 participants (50.5%) were female. DSM-5-defined ARFID was identified in 43 participants (42.6%). Among participants with ARFID, symptom profiles included selective eating in 34 (79.1%), lack of appetite or interest in eating in 30 (69.8%), and fear of aversive consequences of eating in 13 (30.2%). GFD adherence was assessed only in participants with a prior CD diagnosis who had initiated a GFD (n = 87); 14 newly diagnosed participants were not assessed for GFD adherence.

Comparisons by ARFID status

The ARFID group had a higher prevalence of osteopenia/osteoporosis (p = 0.005). Weight z-score and BMI z-score were lower in the ARFID group (p < 0.001 and p = 0.002, respectively), whereas height z-score did not differ between groups. Gastrointestinal symptoms within the previous 3 months, micronutrient deficiency, and supplement use were more frequent in children with ARFID (p = 0.006, p < 0.001, and p < 0.001, respectively). In contrast, tTG-IgA positivity, time since CD diagnosis, Marsh-Oberhuber histopathological grade, and self-reported GFD adherence among those assessed were comparable between groups (Table 2).

Psychometric measures also differed between groups. Children with ARFID had lower total, physical, and psychosocial PedsQL scores (all p < 0.001), higher NIAS total and subscale scores (all p < 0.001), and higher RCADS total, depression, and total anxiety scores (p < 0.001, p = 0.003, and p < 0.001, respectively) than those without ARFID. Anxiety subdomain scores were also higher in the ARFID group (Table 2).

Sociodemographic and parental characteristics were generally comparable between groups. However, maternal history of psychiatric disorder was more common in the ARFID group than in the non-ARFID group (23.3% vs. 6.9%, p = 0.039). Detailed sociodemographic and parental characteristics are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Comparison by time since CD diagnosis

Table 3 compares outcomes by time since CD diagnosis (< 12 months vs. ≥ 12 months). ARFID prevalence was 55.2% in the < 12-month group and 37.5% in the ≥ 12-month group, and did not differ significantly between groups. Compared with the < 12-month group, the ≥ 12-month group showed lower tTG-IgA positivity (p < 0.001), a more favorable BMD category distribution (p = 0.019), and higher BMD z-score, weight z-score, and BMI z-score (p = 0.002, p = 0.016, and p = 0.003, respectively). PedsQL and NIAS scores were comparable between groups. RCADS total score was higher in the < 12-month group (p = 0.043), whereas total anxiety and depression scores did not differ significantly (Table 3).

Correlations between ARFID symptom severity and clinical, anthropometric, and psychometric outcomes

Spearman’s rank correlation analyses showed that higher ARFID symptom severity (NIAS total score) was associated with lower weight z-score (ρ = −0.398, p < 0.001) and BMI z-score (ρ = −0.305, p = 0.002), with a borderline inverse correlation with BMD z-score (ρ = −0.195, p = 0.050) (Fig. 1). NIAS total score was also positively correlated with RCADS total score (ρ = 0.307, p = 0.002) and inversely correlated with PedsQL total score (ρ = −0.656, p < 0.001) (Fig. 1). NIAS total score also correlated positively with total anxiety score (ρ = 0.306, p = 0.002) and depression score (ρ = 0.234, p = 0.019), and inversely with PedsQL physical score (ρ = −0.681, p < 0.001) and psychosocial score (ρ = −0.626, p < 0.001).

PedsQL total score was also inversely correlated with RCADS total score (ρ = −0.383, p < 0.001), total anxiety score (ρ = −0.366, p < 0.001), and depression score (ρ = −0.384, p < 0.001).

Factors associated with ARFID

A multivariable logistic regression model (McFadden pseudo-R² = 0.229; n = 101) was constructed using age, gender, depression score, total anxiety score, tTG-IgA positivity (≥ 1×ULN), maternal history of psychiatric disorder, and presence of gastrointestinal symptoms. Presence of gastrointestinal symptoms (OR 3.37, 95% CI 1.22–9.29; p = 0.019) and higher total anxiety score (OR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01–1.09; p = 0.02) were independently associated with ARFID, whereas no significant associations were observed for the other variables included in the model (all p > 0.05). Model diagnostics showed no evidence of multicollinearity, with VIF values ranging from 1.07 to 1.77. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated acceptable model fit (p = 0.377).

Factors associated with BMI z-score

A multivariable linear regression model including ARFID, tTG-IgA positivity, age, gender, and time since CD diagnosis was fitted to identify factors independently associated with BMI z-score. Longer time since diagnosis was independently associated with a higher BMI z-score (β = 0.02 per month; p < 0.001), whereas ARFID (β=−0.62; p = 0.004) and older age (β=−0.08; p = 0.015) were independently associated with lower BMI z-scores. Gender (p = 0.110) and tTG-IgA positivity (p = 0.873) were not significantly associated with BMI z-score. The overall linear regression model was statistically significant (R² = 0.286, p < 0.001). Model diagnostics showed no evidence of multicollinearity, with VIF values ranging from 1.06 to 1.31, and the Durbin-Watson statistic was 1.915.

Factors associated with osteopenia/osteoporosis

To examine factors associated with osteopenia/osteoporosis, we performed a two-step multivariable logistic regression analysis. In Model 1, adjusted for age, gender, ARFID, tTG-IgA positivity, and time since CD diagnosis, ARFID and tTG-IgA positivity were independently associated with higher odds of osteopenia/osteoporosis (p = 0.006 and p = 0.025, respectively; McFadden pseudo-R²=0.155) (Table 4).

In Model 2, after additional adjustment for BMI z-score and micronutrient deficiency, higher BMI z-score was independently associated with lower odds of osteopenia/osteoporosis (p = 0.003), whereas micronutrient deficiency was not statistically significant (p = 0.081; McFadden pseudo-R²=0.315). After inclusion of these nutritional indicators, the associations of ARFID and tTG-IgA positivity were attenuated and no longer statistically significant (Table 4).

Model diagnostics showed no evidence of multicollinearity in either model, with VIF values ranging from 1.06 to 1.31 in Model 1 and from 1.11 to 1.51 in Model 2. The Hosmer-Lemeshow test indicated acceptable model fit for both Model 1 (p = 0.985) and Model 2 (p = 0.326).

Factors associated with total QoL (PedsQL)

A multivariable linear regression model including age, gender, ARFID, time since CD diagnosis, RCADS total score, BMI z-score, and monthly household income was fitted to identify factors independently associated with total QoL (PedsQL). ARFID showed the strongest independent negative association with total QoL (β=−13.59; p < 0.001), and higher RCADS total scores were also independently associated with lower total QoL (β=−0.16; p = 0.009). Time since diagnosis showed a borderline association (β=−0.08; p = 0.056), whereas age, gender, BMI z-score, and income were not significantly associated with total QoL (p > 0.05). The overall linear regression model was statistically significant (R² = 0.464, p < 0.001). Model diagnostics showed no evidence of multicollinearity, with VIF values ranging from 1.01 to 1.43, and the Durbin-Watson statistic was 2.301.

Discussion

This study provides a multidisciplinary evaluation of the prevalence and clinical correlates of ARFID in children and adolescents with CD followed at a tertiary-care center. We identified ARFID in 42.6% of participants. Reported ARFID prevalence in individuals with CD varies widely (14–57%), largely depending on methodology and sampling [5]. More recently, adult studies have reported high rates of screen-positive/probable ARFID (49% and 57%) [10, 11]. However, these estimates rely primarily on screening instruments such as the NIAS, potentially inflating prevalence estimates by conflating medically required dietary restriction with pathological avoidance. A key strength of our study is that ARFID ascertainment was not based solely on questionnaire scores; rather, diagnoses were established through face-to-face DSM-5-based clinical interviews conducted by a child and adolescent psychiatrist, with careful differentiation between obligatory CD-related gluten restriction and restrictive eating that extends beyond gluten-containing foods. The prevalence in our cohort likely reflects the enhanced diagnostic sensitivity of our multidisciplinary approach, combined with the complex case profile of a tertiary-care setting. These findings reinforce that ARFID is a clinically meaningful comorbidity in pediatric CD, warranting targeted awareness and multidisciplinary evaluation.

Consistent with prior reports in celiac populations, the ARFID group exhibited higher anxiety-depression symptom scores and lower QoL levels [5, 11]. ARFID symptom severity correlated positively with anxiety and depression scores and showed a strong inverse relationship with QoL. Notably, in our multivariable analysis, higher total anxiety score was independently associated with ARFID. This association is consistent with the clinical relevance of fear of aversive consequences and conditioned negative responses in ARFID-related restrictive eating [5, 7]. However, given the cross-sectional design, the directionality of this relationship cannot be determined. Anxiety symptoms may contribute to food-related avoidance, particularly through symptom experiences after gluten exposure or anticipation of symptoms; alternatively, anxiety may arise in response to restrictive eating, dietary burden, or shared vulnerability [8, 27]. In addition, maternal psychiatric history was more common in the ARFID group than in the non-ARFID group (23.3% vs. 6.9%). This finding may reflect shared familial vulnerability or family-level feeding dynamics, including heightened food-related vigilance and overprotective feeding practices, which have been discussed in relation to avoidant eating behaviors in children [3, 28–30]. These findings support the importance of considering parental factors during pediatric CD follow-up and adopting a family-centered approach when clinically indicated [31].

Studies in adult celiac cohorts have generally not found meaningful differences in BMI, bone health, or micronutrient deficiencies according to ARFID status [10]. In contrast, in our cohort, children with ARFID had lower weight and BMI z-scores and a higher frequency of osteopenia/osteoporosis and micronutrient deficiency. These findings should be interpreted cautiously, as growth- and nutrition-related impairment form part of the DSM-5 clinical consequences of ARFID and may therefore reflect, at least in part, the broader clinical profile of children meeting ARFID criteria. While ARFID in adults with CD has been more consistently associated with social burden and psychological symptoms [10, 11], developmental differences may partly explain why nutritional and anthropometric correlates were more prominent in our pediatric cohort. During childhood and adolescence, ongoing linear growth, pubertal development, bone mineral accrual, and higher nutritional requirements may make restrictive dietary intake more likely to affect weight gain, micronutrient status, and bone health [32, 33]. In our stepwise multivariable analysis, the association between ARFID and osteopenia/osteoporosis was attenuated after additional adjustment for BMI z-score and micronutrient deficiency, suggesting that nutritional status, particularly BMI z-score, may partly account for this association. This pattern is consistent with pediatric CD bone literature, in which low BMI is a key risk factor for low BMD [34]. However, because of the cross-sectional design, this finding should not be interpreted as evidence of causality or formal mediation. Taken together, these findings suggest that children with CD who meet ARFID criteria may represent a nutritionally more vulnerable subgroup, warranting careful monitoring of growth, bone health, and micronutrient status alongside psychosocial assessment.

Another finding of our study was that ARFID was not associated with better reported GFD adherence or serologic control among children and adolescents with CD. This observation should be interpreted cautiously, given the self-reported component of adherence assessment, but it aligns with reports from adult celiac cohorts in which ARFID has not been associated with better disease control or improved dietary adherence [5, 10]. Nevertheless, our findings suggest that restrictive eating in ARFID should not be assumed to represent optimal implementation of the GFD; rather, it may coexist with psychosocial burden and nutritional vulnerability. Clinicians should therefore differentiate medically necessary strict GFD adherence from pathological restriction during follow-up, particularly in patients with nutritional vulnerability or psychosocial burden [5].

When outcomes were examined according to time since diagnosis (< 12 vs. ≥12 months), the ≥ 12-month group showed more favorable BMI, BMD, and serologic profiles, consistent with expected improvements after GFD initiation [35, 36]. In contrast, ARFID prevalence did not differ significantly between the two groups, suggesting that restrictive eating patterns may be observed at comparable frequencies in both early and later stages after CD diagnosis. In early disease or before GFD initiation, gluten-triggered gastrointestinal symptoms and conditioned aversive responses may precipitate ARFID, whereas in more chronic cases, prior negative experiences, the restrictive nature of the GFD, cross-contact concerns, and socially driven hypervigilance may contribute to symptom maintenance [8, 9, 27, 37, 38]. In line with previous reports, in our cohort, gastrointestinal symptoms were more frequent in the ARFID group and independently associated with ARFID, suggesting that persistent symptoms may reinforce conditioned avoidance [5]. This association may not be unique to CD and may instead reflect a broader link between ongoing gastrointestinal symptom burden and ARFID across pediatric GI disorders [9, 37, 39, 40]. Nevertheless, CD may confer additional vulnerability because symptom-related food avoidance occurs in the context of a lifelong medically prescribed restrictive diet, persistent vigilance regarding gluten exposure, and the potential for fear of adverse consequences to generalize beyond gluten-containing foods [8, 27]. Adult studies likewise report no association between GFD duration and ARFID, further suggesting that ARFID may represent a chronic clinical phenotype requiring longitudinal attention in CD care [10].

A major strength of this study is the multidisciplinary assessment by pediatric gastroenterology and child and adolescent psychiatry specialists, with ARFID diagnosed through face-to-face DSM-5-guided clinical evaluations rather than screening tools alone. The inclusion of objective outcomes, including BMD, tTG-IgA, and micronutrient deficiencies, further strengthens the clinical relevance of our findings. However, several limitations should be considered. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. GFD adherence and current gastrointestinal symptoms were based partly on patient/family report, introducing potential recall and reporting bias. In particular, GFD adherence was assessed using child-parent report and serologic follow-up rather than a validated adherence questionnaire; therefore, adherence misclassification cannot be excluded. Consecutive recruitment from a single tertiary-care center may have resulted in overrepresentation of children with more complex clinical, nutritional, or psychosocial problems, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader pediatric CD population. Although ARFID diagnoses were established through DSM-5-guided joint clinical evaluations by two child and adolescent psychiatrists rather than questionnaire cutoffs, a fully structured ARFID-specific diagnostic interview was not used. This may limit diagnostic reproducibility and objectivity and should be considered when interpreting the prevalence estimate.

The cohort included both newly diagnosed and previously diagnosed patients, introducing heterogeneity in disease duration, GFD exposure, serologic response, and nutritional recovery. Although we performed stratified analyses by time since diagnosis and adjusted for disease duration in relevant multivariable models, the sample size did not allow fully stratified multivariable analyses within all disease-duration subgroups. In addition, although tTG-IgA positivity was included in the multivariable model, tTG-IgA status is an indirect marker of current serologic activity and does not fully capture mucosal healing or symptom activity; therefore, residual confounding related to active or recently untreated CD cannot be completely excluded. All psychometric measures were self- or parent-reported, and the parent-reported NIAS-P may be susceptible to proxy-report bias, particularly because parental psychological distress may influence the perception and reporting of child eating behaviors. Because multiple exploratory comparisons were performed, particularly across psychometric subscales, the possibility of type I error cannot be excluded. Neurodevelopmental exclusions were not based on standardized screening tools, which may have introduced measurement bias. The same-day psychiatric referral model may also have introduced non-response bias by limiting participation among families with greater socioeconomic, logistical, or psychological burden. Finally, detailed dietary intake, the specific food groups involved in selective eating, and the temporal onset of restrictive eating patterns were not systematically characterized, limiting a more granular distinction between ARFID and longstanding picky eating behaviors. The absence of a healthy or non-celiac gastrointestinal comparison group also limits interpretation of the observed prevalence and precludes conclusions regarding the specificity of these findings to CD.

Conclusions

Overall, our findings suggest that ARFID may represent a clinically relevant comorbidity in pediatric CD, with adverse physical and psychosocial correlates. Restrictive eating that extends beyond gluten avoidance should be recognized during clinical assessment, particularly in patients with persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, prominent anxiety symptoms, poor weight gain, or persistent micronutrient deficiencies. In such cases, multidisciplinary evaluation and treatment involving pediatric gastroenterology, child and adolescent psychiatry, dietetics, and psychology may be considered. Further longitudinal studies are needed to clarify the clinical course of ARFID symptoms and to determine whether targeted multidisciplinary approaches improve outcomes in this patient population.
