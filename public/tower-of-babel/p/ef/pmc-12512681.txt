The genetic influence of sex on gene expression for blood in pigs
Authors: Qing Lin, Junxiong Huang, Tianru Zhou, Teddy Tinashe Chitotombe, Jinyan Teng, Jiaqi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPigs are one of the most important farm animals in the agrifood industry. Many complex traits and patterns of gene expression exhibit sexual dimorphisms in pigs. However, the impact of sex on gene expression remains poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we utilized the gene expression data of blood tissue derived from PigGTEx project to explore the genetic influence of sex on gene expression in pigs. Differential gene expression analysis identified 116 male-biased and 248 female-biased genes. Sex-combined and sex-stratified cis-heritability (cis-h2) were highly positively correlated, while the low correlation were observed between male-stratified and female-stratified cis-h2. Sex-interaction expression quantitative trait locus (eQTL) mapping identified 16 genes with at least one sex-biased eQTL (sb-eGenes) in blood, including 7 female-specific eGenes and 4 male-specific eGenes. Notable examples included the immunology-associated male-specific eGene SLC4A1 and the female-specific eGene PRR14, illustrating sex-specific regulation of gene expression in blood. We further found that sb-eGenes were associated with various complex traits through distinct genetic regulation mechanisms. For example, the male-specific gene SLC4A1 was associated with average daily gain with the identical effect, while the female-specific gene MFGE8 exhibited opposite effect.ConclusionsThis study revealed sex-biased gene expression patterns and sex-dependent regulatory effect of gene expression of blood tissues in pigs. Additionally, this study found the sexually dimorphic regulation of gene expression underlying complex traits. These findings provided a comprehensive insight and resource and advance our understanding of sexual dimorphism in genetic mechanism underlying complex traits in blood.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-025-12029-3.

Background

Understanding the genetic basis of complex traits, such as growth and reproductive traits, is essential for genetic improvement in pigs [1]. Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) provide comprehensive insights into uncovering the genetic architecture of complex traits [2, 3]. However, most trait-associated variants are located in the non-coding region [4], hindering the interpretation of genetic mechanism on complex traits. Functional genomics, like Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) [5] and the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE) [6], have offered integrated resources to interpret the regulatory mechanism of complex traits in human. In farm animals, initiatives such as the Functional Annotation of ANimal Genomes (FAANG) project [7] and Farm Animal Genotype-Tissue Expression (FarmGTEx) project [8–11] also make the functional genomics resource available, advancing our understanding on the genetic mechanism underlying complex traits. For instance, the PigGTEx resource facilitated the investigation of the genetic architecture of complex traits in pigs [1, 12]. Nevertheless, previous study have reported systematic differences of genetic effects between gene expression and complex traits [13], highlighting the complicated regulation on complex traits and the need for more precise genomics information.

The genetics and environment jointly regulated gene expression and formation of complex traits [14, 15]. Sex represents a major biological factor influencing both gene expression and complex traits [16, 17]. For instance, the previous studies have explored the impact of sex-interaction genetic effect on gene expression [18, 19]. In addition, almost all of complex traits and diseases exhibited a certain degree of sexual dimorphism [17]. A UK Biobank (UKB) study of 530 complex traits revealed subtle yet widespread differences between male and female [20]. The PigGTEx project primarily explored the genetic effect of breed- and cell type-interaction expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) [9], while ignoring the impact of sex on gene expression in pigs. The gene expression of blood tissue is typically associated with immune response, which also demonstrated sex differences [21]. However, the genetic influence of sex on gene expression in pig blood remains poorly characterized.

In this study, we utilized the gene expression data of blood tissue derived from the PigGTEx resource to explore the impact of sex on gene expression in pig. We first identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of gene expression between male and female in blood. Next, we estimated the sex-combined and sex-stratified cis-heritability (cis-h2) of gene expression to investigate the difference of genetic variance between sexes. Then, we performed the sex-combined and sex-stratified eQTL mapping to compare genetic effect between male and female. Finally, we conducted the sex-interaction eQTL mapping to identify the sex-biased eQTL (sb-eQTL) and explored the potential links between the sb-eQTL target genes (refer as sb-eGenes) and complex traits in pigs.

Material and methods

Data summary

We collected 386 blood samples with matched genotype and gene expression data from the PigGTEx project [9]. The details about these blood samples were provided in Table S1. We predicted the missing sex information using MetaPred (https://github.com/FarmGTEx/metadata-prediction-v1) based on gene expression data. For the prediction of sex information, we utilized the gene expression data (transcripts per million, TPM) derived from PigGTEx project and utilized the 20-fold × 5-repeat times to obtain the prediction probability of sex information for each sample. Then, we retained the samples with the prediction probability of sex information higher than 95% for downstream analysis. For the genotype data, the low-density single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) were called from the RNA-seq data using GATK (v4.0.8.1) [22]. After that, the low-density genotypes were imputed to high-density genotypes using Pig Genomics Reference Panel by Beagle (v5.1) [23]. After quality control, 2,833,585 common bi-allele SNPs (minor allele frequency (MAF) > 0.05) across samples were maintained for further analysis. The details about the processing of genotype data could be found in the PigGTEx project [9]. After quality control, 384 samples with known sex information (including 156 males and 228 females) were kept for the downstream analysis.

Differentially expressed genes analysis

Low expression gene (mean read counts < 10 across all samples) were filtered out. Then, we normalized the gene expression level to counts per million (CPM) using edgeR R package (v 4.2.2) [24]. Next, we yielded the surrogate variables (confounding factors) of normalized gene expression data except the sex using smartSVA R package (v 0.1.3) [25]. The procedure about the adjustment of confounding factors was as follow. First, we utilized the num.sv() function grouped by sex information to calculate the number of surrogate variables (including 2 surrogate variables). Next, we utilized the smartsva.cpp(n.sv = 2) function to compute the surrogate variables. After that, we adjusted the surrogate variables for normalized gene expression data using removeBatchEffect() function from limma R package (v3.60.6) [26]. We leveraged the adjusted gene expression data to perform differentially expressed genes analysis using Wilcox test method. Finally, we set the false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and then |log2(fold change)|> 1.5 as the threshold to obtain the sex-biased genes.

Sex-combined and sex-stratified cis-h2

To explore the similarity and difference of genetic variance of gene expression between male and female, we estimated the sex-combined and sex-stratified cis-h2 of gene expression in blood using OmiGA (v1.0.4.250515_beta2) [27]. For the processing of gene expression data, we first maintained the genes located on autosome. Next, we removed the genes with TPM < 0.1 and counts < 6 in less than 20% samples. After that, we normalized the gene counts across samples using the trimmed mean of M-value (TMM) method [28] using edgeR R package, and then performed inversed normal transformation of the TMM. To estimate sex-combined cis-h2, we utilized the entire dataset to estimate cis-h2. To estimate sex-stratified cis-h2, we separated the genotypes and gene expression data into male and female-stratified dataset to estimate male-stratified and female-stratified cis-h2, respectively. Phenome-wide association study (PheWAS) in pig and human

To investigate the genetic connection between sb-eQTL/eGenes and complex traits, we first performed PheWAS analysis using PigBiobank [30]. We utilized the top sb-eQTL of sb-eGenes to obtain the association of GWAS meta-analysis across 25 complex traits in pigs (Table S2). The detailed information of GWAS meta-analysis could be found in the previous study [1]. In addition, we also performed the PheWAS of sb-eGenes based on ortholog genes derived from BioMart in human complex traits using GWAS atlas [31] (https://atlas.ctglab.nl/PheWAS). We considered the Bonferroni correction (P = 0.05/number of traits) as the significant threshold.

Results

The sex-biased gene expression analysis

After quality control, we retained 384 RNA-seq samples of whole blood tissue with known sex information from the PigGTEx project [9] for downstream analysis. To identify the sex-biased genes, we performed differentially expressed gene analysis between male and female using Wilcoxon test method. In total, we identified 116 male-biased and 248 female-biased genes on autosomes, and 1 male-biased and 5 female-biased genes on X chromosome (Fig. 1a-b, Table S3). The t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE) of sex-biased genes showed that the sex-biased genes located on X chromosome performed better sex clustering than sex-biased genes located on autosomes (Fig. 1c- d). The sex-biased genes located on autosomes enriched in distinguished functional pathways between male and female (Figure S1a). Sex-stratified and sex-combined cis-h2

To investigate similarities and differences of additive genetic variance of gene expression between sexes in blood, we estimated the sex-combined and sex-stratified cis-h2 of gene expression in blood. We found a significantly positive correlation between sex-combined cis-h2 and male-stratified (Figure S2a, Pearson’s r = 0.55, P < 1.00 × 10–300) or female-stratified (Figure S2b, Pearson’s r = 0.67, P < 1.00 × 10–300) cis-h2. In addition, the correlation of stratified cis-h2 was positive between male and female (Figure S2c, Table S4, Pearson’s r = 0.41, P = 1.00 × 10–273), though weaker than their correlations with sex-combined cis-h2. We further found that the distribution of cis-h2 showed a slight difference between male (median cis-h2[Male] = 0.052) and female (median cis-h2[Female] = 0.061). In summary, these results indicated a high degree of similarity of genetic variance between sexes.

Sex-biased cis-eQTL and sb-eGenes

To further explore the genetic effect of sex on gene expression in blood, we first investigated the correlation between estimated genotype or phenotype principal components (PCs) and known confounders, such as age and breed. We found that the genotype or phenotype PCs had a high correlation with known confounders (Figure S3), indicating the effective correction of confounding factors. Next, we explored the statistical model for sex-interaction cis-eQTL mapping. We found a highly positive correlation (Figure S4a, Pearson’s r = 0.82, P < 1.00 × 10–300) between LM and LMM. We utilized the LMM result that overcame the genetic correlation between individuals for downstream analysis. We discovered a high concordance between sex-combined and male- (Figure S4b, Pearson’s r = 0.84, P < 1.00 × 10–300) or female-stratified (Figure S4c, Pearson’s r = 0.93, P < 1.00 × 10–300) cis-eQTL mapping. In addition, the sex-stratified cis-eQTL mapping also showed a high correlation between male and female (Figure S5a, Pearson’s r = 0.74, P < 1.00 × 10–300). Next, comparing with the sex-stratified cis-eQTL mapping, we found low correlation between sex-interaction and male-stratified (Pearson’s r = 0.24, P = 1.00 × 10–177) or female-stratified (Pearson’s r = 0.26, P = 1.00 × 10–218) cis-eQTL mapping (Figure S5b-c), indicating the different genetic regulation of gene expression between sex-stratified and sex-interaction in blood.

In total, we identified 16 sb-eGenes (FDR < 0.1) derived from LMM in blood (Fig. 2), including 7 female-specific genes, 4 male-specific genes, 3 genes with significant in both sexes (Both) and 2 genes with not significant in either sex (Neither). Compared with the sex-combined cis-eQTL mapping, ten out of 16 sb-eGenes were exclusively sex-biased, indicating the specific genetic regulation of sex-by-genetics in blood. For instance, SLC4A1 gene showed significance in sex-interaction cis-eQTL mapping, while it showed no significance in sex-combined cis-eQTL mapping (Fig. 3a). The further integration with sex-stratified cis-eQTL mapping found that SLC4A1 gene was the male-specific gene in blood (Fig. 3a), and the effect size of top cis-eQTL showed the opposite direction between male and female (Figure S6). The PheWAS of SLC4A1 gene also showed links with immunological traits in human (Figure S7a-b). Similarly, PRR14 gene was significant only in sex interaction cis-eQTL mapping (Fig. 3b). We further found that the PRR14 gene was a female-specific gene by integrating the sex-stratified cis-eQTL mapping in blood (Fig. 3b). Discussion

In this study, we explored the genetic impact of sex on gene expression in blood tissue. Through differential gene expression analysis, we found 116 male-biased and 248 female-biased genes in blood, and these sex-biased genes enriched in different functional pathways in male and female. In addition, the sex-combined and sex-stratified cis-h2 showed a highly positive correlation, indicating the similar genetic regulation of gene expression between male and female. Furthermore, we discovered 16 sb-eGenes, including 7 female-specific eGenes and 4 male-specific eGenes. By incorporating the complex traits in pigs, we found that sb-eGenes were associated with various complex traits by different genetic regulation. These results help us to understand the impact of gene expression in blood.

The gene-by-environment interaction regulates gene expression and complex traits [32]. We primarily investigated the impact of sex on gene expression and identified a small part of sex-biased genes in blood. The male-biased and female-biased genes enriched in different pathways, which provided an essential insight on regulation of gene expression. In addition, the small-effect sex-biased genes (FDR < 0.05 and |log₂(fold change)|< 1.5) might also play an essential role in the biological function in blood. Furthermore, we explored the correlation of cis-h2 between male and female, and found the moderately positive correlation in both sexes. However, the distribution of cis-h2 showed slight difference between male and female (median cis-h2[Male] = 0.052, median cis-h2[Female] = 0.061), indicating the genetic similarity and difference between male and female. Besides, the estimated stability of cis-h2 was partially influenced by the sample size and the coverage of genotypes, which might cause the low correlation between male and female.

The LM yielded greater statistical power of sb-eQTL mapping, while it ignored the genetic architecture or genomic relationship. In fact, farm animals, such as pigs, existed a strong population structure or genomic relationship because of the strong selective pressure. The LMM could overcome the impact of population structure or genomic relationship by incorporating the genomic relationship matrix into the statistical model [33]. We exploited the genetic effect of sex-by-genotype interaction and identified 16 sb-eGenes. For instance, the male-specific eGene SLC4A1 was associated with sex-specific susceptibility to osmotic hemolysis that impact red blood cell function and hemolysis [34], supporting the discovery of the sex-biased gene. Besides, the female-specific eGene PRR14 was associated with Parkinson's disease showing different effects between male and female in blood [35].

Although this study explored the impact of sex on gene expression in blood, several limitations might influence the discovery in this study. Firstly, sample size represents a key limiting factor that primarily influenced the statistical power of cis-eQTL mapping and sex-genotype interaction because of the small interaction effect. For instance, the sample size partially affected statistical power of sex-stratified cis-eQTL mapping, which affected the definition of sex-biased genes. Therefore, the extension of sample size, such as meta-analysis, could enhance the power of molecular QTL mapping. Secondly, the dataset derived from public domain might introduce the population heterogeneity, which might affect the foundation of sex-biased genes. The designed dataset is beneficial to avoid the impact of population heterogeneity. Thirdly, because of the genetic difference between male and female, it is necessary to perform sex-stratified or sex-interaction GWAS for further interpretation of complex traits in pigs. Finally, the links between blood sb-eGenes in pigs and immunological traits in human indicated the potential similarity of genetic regulation underlying complex traits between human and pigs. Therefore, collecting orthologous traits between human and pigs would be beneficial to investigate the potential common regulation underlying complex traits.

Conclusion

This study revealed sex-biased gene expression patterns in blood tissue. Additionally, the sex-dependent regulatory effect of gene expression could perform sexually dimorphic genetic regulation underlying complex traits in pigs. These finding provided a comprehensive insight for understanding the genetic influence of gene expression in blood and offered a blood sb-eGene to reveal the genetic impact of sex underlying complex traits in pigs.
