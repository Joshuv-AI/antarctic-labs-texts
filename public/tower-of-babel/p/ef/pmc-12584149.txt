Feasibility of endovascular treatment for fetal-type posterior cerebral artery aneurysm with P1 segment aplasia: illustrative case

The fetal-type posterior cerebral artery (FPCA) is a common anatomical variant of the circle of Willis, in which the posterior cerebral artery (PCA) originates from the internal carotid artery (ICA). This variant is observed unilaterally in 5.6%–7.2% and bilaterally in 2.1%–9% of the population on routine clinical imaging.1–3 FPCAs can be classified as either partial type, with a hypoplastic P1 segment, or complete type, in which the P1 segment is absent. The latter is relatively uncommon, occurring in approximately 3.5%–7.9% of cases.4,5 In FPCA, the PCA arises from the ICA via a dominant posterior communicating artery and supplies both the thalamus and occipital lobe. In contrast to the adult-type PCA, the FPCA can only be accessed via the ICA, which significantly restricts both access routes and device options during endovascular treatment (EVT). Furthermore, FPCA aneurysms are most often fusiform or dissecting in morphology. Given these anatomical and technical constraints, parent artery occlusion (PAO), with or without bypass, is often selected as the primary treatment approach.6–17 However, in cases of P1 segment aplasia, proximal occlusion of FPCA may compromise both distal perfusion and perforating arteries, increasing the risk of thalamic infarction.18 Therefore, treatment strategies that prioritize preservation of FPCA flow may be necessary.

Here, we present a rare case of a ruptured FPCA aneurysm with ipsilateral P1 segment aplasia, successfully treated with stent-assisted coil embolization while preserving the anterograde FPCA flow. We also review the existing literature to characterize FPCA aneurysms and discuss optimal endovascular strategies in anatomically constrained cases.

Illustrative Case

A 79-year-old woman was brought to our hospital in cardiopulmonary arrest. Return of spontaneous circulation was achieved after resuscitation, and she regained a Glasgow Coma Scale score of 11 (E3V3M5). Noncontrast CT imaging revealed subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) with intraventricular hemorrhage (Fig. 1A). CT angiography demonstrated a fusiform aneurysm with a bleb arising from the left FPCA, along with absence of the ipsilateral P1 segment (Fig. 1B). The aneurysm measured 7.3 mm at the neck, with a dome size of 7.1 × 7.4 mm. The parent artery diameter measured 2.4 mm proximally and 2.1 mm distally (Fig. 1C).

Given the complexity and risk of direct surgical intervention, an endovascular approach was selected. The aneurysm’s sidewall and saccular-like morphology suggested that stent-assisted coil embolization would be feasible. However, because of the narrow diameter of the FPCA, the use of two microcatheters was deemed unsafe. Therefore, a strategy involving neck bridging stent deployment followed by transcellular coil embolization was planned. Given the severity of the patient’s clinical condition, we prioritized stabilization of vital signs during the acute phase following rupture. Oral dual antiplatelet therapy (100 mg of aspirin and 75 mg of clopidogrel) was initiated, and EVT was subsequently performed under general anesthesia 2 weeks after the aneurysm rupture.

An 8-Fr OPTIMO Flex guiding catheter (Tokai Medical Products) was placed in the left ICA, and a 6-Fr Esperance distal access catheter (Japan Lifeline) was advanced to the C4 segment of the ICA. A microcatheter (SL-10, 45° preshaped, Stryker) was navigated into the FPCA, distal to the aneurysm (Fig. 1D). A Neuroform Atlas stent (3.0 × 15 mm, Stryker Neurovascular) was deployed across the aneurysm neck (Fig. 1E). Target XL 360 soft coil (5 × 15 cm, Stryker) was used as a framing coil, followed by insertion of i-ED Complex Infini (3–5 × 15 cm, Kaneka Medix) into the aneurysm sac (Fig. 1F). During the treatment, heparinization was maintained with an activated clotting time of 250–300 seconds.

Postoperative MRI confirmed the absence of ischemic complications. Digital subtraction angiography performed 1 month later demonstrated complete aneurysm obliteration with persistent perfusion of the FPCA (Fig. 1G). An Allcock test confirmed continued absence of the ipsilateral P1 segment (Fig. 1H). After 2 months of rehabilitation, the patient was discharged home with a modified Rankin Scale score of 3.

Literature Review

We conducted a systematic review of the literature on EVT of FPCA aneurysms accessed via the ICA (Table 1). Searches were performed in the PubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science databases using combinations of the following keywords: “aneurysm,” “fetal-type,” “posterior cerebral artery,” “coil embolization,” “stent-assisted,” “endovascular,” and “subarachnoid hemorrhage.” English-language case reports and case series with sufficient clinical detail were included. Articles lacking clear anatomical information or procedural context were excluded. For each case, we extracted data on patient demographics, P1 segment status, aneurysm location and morphology, rupture status, treatment strategy, and clinical outcomes.

Discussion

Aneurysms of the FPCA are rare, and their management is complicated by anatomical variation and hemodynamic factors. The absence or hypoplasia of the P1 segment results in sole perfusion of the PCA territory by the ICA, increasing hemodynamic stress and possibly predisposing to aneurysm formation at vessel bends. Table 1 summarizes our literature review of previously reported cases of FPCA aneurysms treated with endovascular approaches. Most reported FPCA aneurysms were fusiform or dissecting in morphology and were managed with PAO, suggesting that parent artery preservation is often considered unfeasible with EVT. PAO cases combined with direct revascularization procedures such as superficial temporal artery–PCA bypass were able to avoid ischemic complications. Nonetheless, such revascularization surgeries are technically demanding and not always feasible. In contrast, ischemic infarctions occurred in 10 of 15 cases treated with PAO alone, most of which involved not only the occipital lobe but also the thalamus. Therefore, preservation of FPCA flow is ideal whenever feasible.

Observations

In our case, the FPCA aneurysm was successfully treated with stent-assisted coil embolization while preserving parent artery patency. However, unlike aneurysms in the adult-type PCA, access to FPCA aneurysms is limited to the ICA route. Furthermore, the small caliber of the FPCA restricts the choice and maneuverability of endovascular devices, often precluding the use of dual microcatheter techniques. In our extensive review, only 5 FPCA aneurysms including the present case were successfully treated with selective coil embolization while preserving anterograde FPCA flow. These cases shared some characteristics: the aneurysms were located at the P1 or P2 segment and had a saccular-like configuration. Such morphology likely facilitated device navigation in the aneurysm and enabled effective coil placement. When these anatomical and morphological features are present, selective coil embolization with preservation of the FPCA should be considered a feasible treatment option.

Because of the small caliber and tortuosity of the FPCA, the transcellular technique was selected over the jailing technique. An open-cell stent (Neuroform Atlas) was chosen to facilitate microcatheter passage through the stent struts, making the transcellular approach more feasible in this anatomical setting. To minimize the risk of coil herniation into the parent artery, a relatively large framing coil was first deployed to ensure stable intrasaccular support, followed by a long coil to achieve dense packing and further reduce the risk of coil protrusion. Throughout the procedure, meticulous attention was paid to maintaining stable microcatheter positioning to avoid dislodgment from the FPCA.

Lessons

We report a rare case of a ruptured aneurysm arising from the FPCA with ipsilateral P1 segment aplasia, successfully treated with stent-assisted coil embolization via a transcellular approach while preserving anterograde FPCA flow. Given the high incidence of thalamic and occipital lobe infarction following PAO, preservation of FPCA flow should be prioritized whenever feasible.
