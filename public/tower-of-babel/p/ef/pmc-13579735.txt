Association of postoperative day 1 procalcitonin levels with short-term mortality after liver transplantation: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Tong Chen, Hantao Mai, Haoran Li, Xiaoxin Zhong, Ning Wang, Peng Hao, Ruilian Guo, Hongwei Han, Kelin Zhang, Guifang Zhong, Huihua Cheng, Benhua Jiang, Sicong Zhu, Suyuan Zhuang, Yantao Chen, Qing He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPostoperative complications, especially infections, are important causes of poor prognosis after liver transplantation. Procalcitonin (PCT) has been proven to be a sensitive marker of infection and inflammation in many diseases. As a result, the purpose of this study was to determine the connection between early postoperative PCT levels and mortality in liver transplant patients.MethodsWe included 205 liver transplant recipients who underwent orthotopic allogeneic liver transplantation at Sun Yat-sen University’s Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital. We grouped the patients according to the tertile of PCT levels on postoperative day one (POD 1) and divided the patients into three groups: Q1 (≤ 2.0 ng/ml), Q2 (2.1–5.0 ng/ml), and Q3 (≥ 5.1 ng/ml). To analyze the curvilinear association between PCT levels and 90-day mortality and in-hospital mortality, we used Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. The Kaplan-Meier method was used to assess survival rates at 30, 60 and 90 days after liver transplantation. The Log-rank test was used to analyze differences in survival rates between different PCT groups.ResultsHigher POD1 PCT levels were associated with increased 90-day and in-hospital mortality. Furthermore, this study revealed a statistically significant decrease in ICU-free days at 28-day, hospital-free days at 28-day, CRRT-free hours at 28-day, and hospital-free days at 60-day among patients in the group of high PCT level.ConclusionsHigher POD1 PCT levels were associated with worse short-term postoperative outcomes after liver transplantation. However, POD1 PCT should be interpreted as a potential early risk-associated biomarker rather than as an established independent prognostic factor.Keywords: PCT, Mortality, Liver Transplant Patients, Retrospective study

Background

Numerous patients have been able to extend their lives through liver transplantation, which is considered to be the ultimate form of treatment for end-stage liver disease [1]. Compared to decades before, the prognosis of liver transplant recipients has greatly improved because of increased surgical competence, better patient selection, and superior postoperative care of complications and immunosuppressive medication [2]. However, postoperative complications, especially infections, are still important causes of poor prognosis [3]. The management and monitoring of postoperative patients are crucial, especially in the early stages, which may affect the overall prognosis.

Procalcitonin is the 116 amino acid precursor of hormone calcitonin, which is normally synthesized by the thyroid parafollicular C cells and then released in the blood. However, under specific inflammatory stimuli, PCT is continuously generated in many extra-thyroid tissues (e.g., liver, kidney, gut, lung and leukocytes), resulting in a significant increase in measurable PCT concentration in blood [4]. It has been demonstrated that PCT is a sensitive indicator of infection and inflammation in many diseases and clinical settings [5]. In addition to PCT, C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukins (ILs) are also commonly used infectious markers in clinics. However, numerous studies show that PCT has a higher sensitivity and specificity. In addition, PCT levels are not affected by corticosteroids or immunosuppressants [6, 7]. Multiple studies have shown that the increase in PCT is connected with greater mortality and the occurrence of various complications [7–14]. Accordingly, we speculate that PCT may play a role in the diagnosis of early infection and the prediction of prognosis in patients after liver transplantation.

Although there is accumulating evidence evidence indicating that PCT might be a valuable marker for predicting prognosis of solid organ transplant patients, research in liver transplant patients is still limited [15]. Considering that liver transplant patients have many physiological and pathological characteristics different from others, it is critical to assess the effects of PCT in this context. Thus, retrospective research was conducted to explore the relationship between PCT levels on POD 1 and prognosis in liver transplant patients, emphasize the importance of monitoring early PCT levels in patient management, and draw clinicians’ attention to it.

Methods

Study participants

We included 205 liver transplant recipients who underwent orthotopic allogeneic liver transplantation at Sun Yat-sen University’s Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hospital from June 2019 to September 2022 and met the inclusion and exclusion criteria (detailed results can be found in Fig. 1).

The STROBE flow chart of the study. Abbreviations: STROBE, Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology; PCT, procalcitonin. A total of 205 patients met the inclusion criteria and had complete POD1 PCT data

Inclusion criteria

Consecutive patients who completed liver transplantation in our hospital from June 2019 to September 2022.

Exclusion criteria

Patients under the age of 18, those who had a second liver transplant, and those with insufficient clinical data at our institution before surgery were excluded.

The immunosuppression (IS) regimen in this study was standardized in all patients and followed the guidelines of European Association for the Study of the Liver [16].

This study was designed and reported in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in reporting observational research.

Study design

The general information and preoperative clinical indicators of the recipients included age, gender, Body Mass Index (BMI), history of alcohol consumption, history of hypertension or diabetes, primary liver disease, Model for End-stage Liver Disease (MELD) score, Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation (APACHE) II score, operation duration and anhepatic phase, postoperative ultrasonic arterial resistance index (RI), primary nonfunction (PNF), early allograft dysfunction (EAD), PCT on POD 1, IL-6, lactate, hemoglobin, total bilirubin (TB), international standard ratio (INR), albumin (ALB), serum creatinine (Cr) and partial pressure of oxygen (PO2).

PCT measurement

PCT was measured in the routine laboratory in the morning and evening of the first day after liver transplantation, with higher values being recorded. Duration of measurement was dependent on the clinical course of the patient. For this reason, we included only patients with a complete dataset of PCT on POD 1 in the study. Preoperative PCT was not routinely measured at our institution.

Outcome indicators included

30-day postoperative mortality, 60-day postoperative mortality, and 90-day postoperative mortality; in-hospital mortality; CRRT-free hours in 28 days after transplantation; ICU-free days in 28 days after transplantation; hospital-free days in 28 and 60 days after transplantation.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was conducted using R software version 4.0.2 and Python version 3.8. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median and interquartile range, according to their distribution. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Differences across POD1 PCT groups were compared using one-way analysis of variance or the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

Missing data were assessed for all variables included in the analysis. POD1 PCT and all mortality outcomes were complete for all included patients. Missing data were limited to selected covariates, including IL-6, hepatic artery resistance index, and operation duration. Because missingness was minimal, k-nearest neighbors imputation was used only for these covariates and was not applied to the primary exposure or mortality outcomes. Complete-case sensitivity analysis was performed to assess robustness. The primary outcome was 90-day all-cause mortality after liver transplantation. Other outcomes, including 30-day mortality, 60-day mortality, in-hospital mortality, CRRT-free hours within 28 days, ICU-free days within 28 days, and hospital-free days within 28 and 60 days, were considered secondary or exploratory outcomes.

POD1 PCT was analyzed both as tertiles and as a continuous variable after logarithmic transformation, log(PCT + 1). The tertile-based categorization was exploratory and was not considered a clinically validated threshold. Kaplan-Meier curves and log-rank tests were used to compare survival across PCT groups. Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to evaluate the association between POD1 PCT and 90-day mortality. The adjusted model included MELD score, operation duration, and early allograft dysfunction. Hazard ratios and 95% confidence intervals were reported. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals. All statistical tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Missing data and sensitivity analysis

Among the 205 included patients, postoperative day 1 PCT and all mortality outcomes were complete. Missing data were observed only in selected covariates: IL-6 was missing in 5 patients, hepatic artery resistance index in 2 patients, and operation duration in 1 patient. Complete-case sensitivity analyses excluding patients with missing covariate data yielded results generally consistent with the primary analysis.

Comparison of clinical data across PCT groups

A total of 205 liver transplant recipients were included in the study. We grouped the patients according to the tertile of PCT levels on the first postoperative day and divided the patients into three groups: Q1 (≤ 2.0 ng/ml), Q2 (2.1–5.0 ng/ml), and Q3 (≥ 5.1 ng/ml). As indicated in the table, among the baseline data compared across the three groups, only age, BMI, MELD score, IL-6, and TB had p-values less than 0.05. Specifically, the Q1 group had a higher average age, with a mean of 54.6 ± 8.8. Significant differences were not observed in the remaining variables such as gender, APACHE II score, ultrasonographic hepatic artery resistance index, PNF, EAD, and related laboratory tests, with p-values all exceeding 0.05 (see Table 1 for results).

Preoperative liver disease proportions in liver transplant patients

Liver transplant patients often have multiple underlying liver conditions preoperatively. In our hospital, the most common combination of diseases before transplantation was hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and liver cancer, accounting for 40.5% of the patient population. This was followed by patients with concurrent hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, and liver failure, accounting for 16.1%, and those with hepatitis, liver cirrhosis, liver cancer, and liver failure, making up 9.3% of the population. An exploded pie chart was used to specifically present sections representing less than 5% to provide a clearer depiction of the distribution characteristics of indications before liver transplantation (detailed results can be found in Fig. 2).

Curve analysis of PCT levels on POD 1 with 90-day mortality and in-hospital mortality

The relationship between POD1 PCT levels and 90-day mortality, as well as in-hospital mortality, was visualized using logistic regression. An increase in PCT was disproportionately related to an increase in the 90-day mortality, and this relationship was nonlinear. The Spearman correlation coefficient was 0.161, with a p-value less than 0.05, indicating a statistically significant correlation between PCT values on POD 1 and the 90-day mortality (see Fig. 4A).

Cox regression analysis for 90-day mortality

Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to evaluate the association between POD1 PCT and 90-day mortality. In the unadjusted model, log-transformed POD1 PCT was significantly associated with increased 90-day mortality, HR 1.56, 95% CI 1.14–2.15, P = 0.006. After adjustment for MELD score, operation duration, and EAD, the association was attenuated and did not reach conventional statistical significance, HR 1.38, 95% CI 0.96–2.00, P = 0.086. When POD1 PCT was analyzed according to tertiles, Q3 showed a higher estimated hazard of 90-day mortality than Q1, but the association was not statistically significant, HR 1.86, 95% CI 0.55–6.28, P = 0.317. The proportional hazards assumption was not violated in the adjusted models (see Table 3).

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort study of adult liver transplant recipients, higher POD1 PCT levels were associated with worse short-term postoperative outcomes, including increased 90-day mortality and fewer ICU-free days, CRRT-free hours, and hospital-free days. However, the correlation between POD1 PCT and mortality was weak. In the unadjusted Cox regression model, log-transformed POD1 PCT was associated with an increased risk of 90-day mortality. After adjustment for MELD score, operation duration, and EAD, this association was attenuated and did not reach conventional statistical significance. These findings suggest that POD1 PCT may reflect early postoperative risk and perioperative clinical severity, but should not be interpreted as an established independent prognostic factor.

Procalcitonin is the precursor molecule of human calcitonin, which is present in very low concentrations in normal individuals. During bacterial infection, fungal infection, or parasitic infection, it will be significantly elevated. However, there is no significant change in PCT concentration during viral infection and autoimmune response. As a result, the high PCT level is usually the symptom of a bacterial infection [15, 17]. Moreover, PCT has the advantages of significantly increasing in the early stages of infection, reaching a half-life of 24 h in vivo, and being not easily degradable in vitro, which has been used as a diagnostic indicator for bacterial infections in clinical practice [18]. Several studies on the relationship between PCT and the prognosis of various diseases have been published over recent decades, but the results remain contradictory [7–15, 17, 19, 20]. A prospective cohort study on kidney transplantation suggested that postoperative PCT level was independently associated with both graft failure and mortality [14]. Similar findings were made by prospective single-center research in Spain with 100 individuals, which discovered that a PCT cutoff of < 2 ng/ml within 24 h had a strong predictive value for ruling out ICU death (negative predictive value 100%) and grade 3 primary graft malfunction after 72 h (96.5%) [11]. The current study confirmed the findings of the previous studies by finding a correlation between PCT and in-hospital and 90-day mortality. Nonetheless, the findings of the current study are not entirely supported by the findings of two other studies. Based on the existence of ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI), 110 liver transplant patients were split into IRI and non-IRI groups in a retrospective single-center research carried out at the University Hospital of Zurich. PCT levels were used to separate IRI patients into two groups: those with PCT > 15 mcg/L and those with PCT < 15 mcg/L. In terms of 90-day and 12-month graft and patient survival, the study results showed that IRI patients with PCT > 15 mcg/l had outcomes similar to patients without IRI, but the outcome in patients with IRI and PCT < 15 mcg/l was considerably poorer [19]. An additional study on the correlation between PCT level and infection following liver transplantation revealed that there was no significant difference in hospital stay at PCT cutoff values of ≥ 9 ng/ml or < 9 ng/ml [15]. The difference between the studies may be due to the insufficient sample size and less inclusion of confounding variables in the above studies. Our study included 205 patients, a larger sample size than those of the above studies. Furthermore, by comparing preoperative MELD scores and APACHEII scores of liver transplant patients in different PCT groups, this study found similar preoperative disease severity in patients in all groups. This indicates that the results of this study are more convincing. The observation period of some previous studies is short. However with the development of extracorporeal life support technology, the sensitivity of short-term mortality in critically ill patients is gradually decreased, and then the prolonged observation time is more beneficial to evaluating the relationship between exposure and outcome. In our study, the survival curves were not significantly different between the three PCT groups at 30 days and 60 days, while the survival varied at 90 days.

There are two potential explanations for this association. First, a high PCT level might indicate the occurrence of postoperative infection [10, 21–23]. Additionally, an increase in PCT would speed up cell deterioration and create a vicious cycle. A dose-dependent cell lysis can be induced by serum PCT at extremely low levels, according to research by Sauer and Doss. Furthermore, the study demonstrated that PCT might influence every aspect of cellular function, including metabolism, induction of cell death, proliferation, microalbumin production, and detoxifying ability, in a dose-dependent way [24]. Thus the interaction between high PCT levels and cell damage would lead to liver failure. Meanwhile, according to studies, the degree of liver failure is associated with a significant death rate [25]. It can be concluded that high PCT levels are associated with poor postoperative outcomes.

The present study found that CRRT-free hours in 28days after liver transplant decreased with an increase in PCT concentration. With an incidence rate varying from 5.6% to 60.5%, acute kidney injury (AKI) is one of the most frequent complications following liver transplantation [26–29]. In addition to being related to longer ICU hospitalizations, more infectious problems, and higher hospital costs, postoperative AKI in transplant recipients has also been connected with the survival of both the recipients and the transplanted organs [30]. According to research conducted in the US, individuals who required renal replacement therapy after surgery had a higher 90-day mortality rate and a worse 1-year survival rate than those who did not [31]. In addition, studies have found that PCT has good predictive value for renal adverse outcomes in patients undergoing cardiac surgery [8]. Receiving postoperative CRRT treatment was crucial for liver transplant recipients to preserve homeostasis and improve their body’s pathological status [32]. PCT levels on POD1 may be used for prediction of AKI after liver transplantation, which will facilitate the early initiation of CRRT to improve the prognosis.

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective cohort study, it cannot establish causality between POD1 PCT levels and postoperative outcomes, and the generalizability of the findings may be limited. Although missingness in the present cohort was minimal and was restricted to selected covariates, while POD1 PCT and mortality outcomes were complete for all included patients, potential bias related to missing or unmeasured variables cannot be fully excluded. K-nearest neighbors imputation was used only for covariates with limited missingness and was not applied to the primary exposure or mortality outcomes. Complete-case sensitivity analysis yielded generally consistent findings. Second, although multivariable Cox regression was performed, the number of 90-day mortality events was limited; therefore, adjusted estimates may be unstable and should be interpreted cautiously. Third, donor-related variables were not systematically collected in the retrospective dataset and therefore were not included in the analysis, which may have introduced residual confounding related to donor and graft quality. Fourth, preoperative PCT was not routinely measured, and this study only evaluated POD1 PCT rather than serial postoperative PCT changes. Therefore, we could not determine whether elevated POD1 PCT reflected pre-existing inflammation, perioperative stress, early infection, graft injury, or a combination of these factors. Fifth, the tertile-based categorization of POD1 PCT was exploratory and was not based on validated clinical thresholds, which may have introduced information loss and limited generalizability. Although POD1 PCT was additionally analyzed as a log-transformed continuous variable, the optimal clinical cutoff remains uncertain. Finally, several secondary outcomes were examined without formal multiplicity adjustment, and these findings should therefore be interpreted as exploratory. Future multicenter prospective studies with larger sample sizes, systematically collected donor-related variables, preoperative and serial postoperative PCT measurements, and longer follow-up are needed to validate these findings and clarify the clinical utility of PCT after liver transplantation.

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective cohort study, higher POD1 PCT levels were associated with worse short-term postoperative outcomes after liver transplantation, including higher 90-day mortality and fewer days free from ICU care, CRRT, and hospitalization. However, the association between POD1 PCT and 90-day mortality was attenuated after adjustment for clinically relevant covariates. Therefore, POD1 PCT should be interpreted as a potential marker associated with early postoperative risk rather than as an established independent prognostic factor.
