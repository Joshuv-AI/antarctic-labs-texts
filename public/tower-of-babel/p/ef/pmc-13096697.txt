Evaluation of antigen and antibody assays for surveillance of Lassa virus in peri-domestic rodents in southeastern Sierra Leone: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Allison R Smither, James Koninga, Franklyn B Kanneh, Momoh Foday, Matthew L Boisen, Nell G Bond, Mambu Momoh, John D Sandi, Lansana Kanneh, Foday Alhasan, Ibrahim M Kanneh, Mohamed S Yillah, Donald S Grant, Duane J Bush, Diana K S Nelson, Kaitlin M Cruz, Raphaëlle Klitting, Matthias G Pauthner, Kristian G Andersen, Jeffrey G Shaffer, Luis M Branco, Robert W Cross, John S Schieffelin, Robert F Garry
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundLassa fever, caused by Lassa mammarenavirus (LASV), is a rodent-borne disease endemic to west Africa. Zoonotic surveillance of LASV provides epidemiological insights into its potential presence. We aimed to assess LASV antigen and antibody prevalence in peri-domestic rodents in southeastern Sierra Leone.MethodsIn this cross-sectional study, small mammal trapping was conducted in Kenema District, Sierra Leone, from Nov 8, 2018 to July 10, 2019. LASV antigen was detected with a nucleoprotein antigen rapid diagnostic test at necropsy, then compared with gold-standard quantitative RT-PCR in a subset of specimens to assess specificity and sensitivity. LASV IgG antibodies against nucleoprotein and glycoprotein were tested using a semi-quantitative ELISA in Mastomys and Rattus species.FindingsOf the 373 specimens tested, 74 (20%) were positive for LASV antigen. Compared with qRT-PCR, antigen testing showed a specificity of 61·9% (95% CI 52·5–70·6) and sensitivity of 100·0% (95% CI 2·5–100·0%). 40 (11%) specimens tested positive for LASV nucleoprotein-specific IgG, whereas an additional 12 (3%) were only positive for LASV glycoprotein-specific IgG, respectively. Simultaneous antigen and IgG antibody presence was observed in some Mastomys sp specimens (odds ratio 0·43 [95% CI 0·24–0·73], p=0·0011). However, the relative strength of antigen response did not explain the variability in the IgG response to either glycoprotein (ρ 0·04 [95% CI −0·34 to 0·41], p=0·85) or nucleoprotein (ρ −0·23 [95% CI −0·52 to 0·11], p=0·17).InterpretationAlthough further evaluation is needed, the tools used in this study can aid in generating valuable public health data for LASV surveillance and in prioritising specimens for further downstream analyses.FundingNational Institute of Allergy and Infectious Diseases of the National Institutes of Health.

Introduction

Lassa mammarenavirus (LASV), the causal virus of Lassa fever, is a public health threat for much of west Africa, with over 900 000 cases of Lassa fever annually and an estimated 5000–15 000 deaths.1,2 LASV is primarily transmitted from infected rodents to humans via inhalation or ingestion of rodent excreta, although human-to-human and nosocomial transmission does occur.3 Although most LASV infections result in asymptomatic or mild disease, about 20% of infections progress to a severe viral haemorrhagic fever.3 With no licensed therapeutics or vaccines currently available for Lassa fever, rodent exclusion and pest management programmes are recommended to prevent contracting LASV.

Although the multimammate mouse, Mastomys natalensis, is the primary host reservoir for LASV based on available data, two other closely related species, Mastomys erythroleucus and Hylomyscus pamfi, have been identified as additional host reservoirs of LASV in Nigeria and Guinea, respectively.4 LASV IgG has also been detected in other common rodent species in LASV-endemic areas, such as Rattus rattus and Mus musculus.5–7 Despite the near-ubiquitous presence of M natalensis in sub-Saharan Africa, LASV is only known to circulate in west Africa. Nonetheless, climate change, land-use change, and population growth might expand LASV’s ecological niche and the risk of Lassa fever into central and eastern Africa.8 Detection of active infections and previous exposures of LASV in rodents can inform public health risk assessment in endemic and at-risk areas.

ELISAs have been developed for LASV antigen and antibody serology in humans; rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) have also been created for LASV antigen, with similar concordance to antigen ELISAs.9–11 Although ELISAs must be performed in a laboratory, an RDT can be performed nearly anywhere and provide results in as little as 15 min. Meanwhile, rodent surveillance for LASV IgG has traditionally been performed using indirect immunofluorescence assays.6 However, immunofluorescence assays for LASV must be performed in Biosafety Level 4 facilities, making them impractical for in-country surveillance. In 2022, Soubrier and colleagues12 adapted a human LASV IgG ELISA for the detection of LASV IgG in Mastomys sp using anti-mouse reagents, with similar concordance to immunofluorescence assays. However, this assay only qualitatively indicates the presence or absence of nucleoprotein IgG in Mus-related sp.

LASV is a bi-segmented ambisense RNA virus encoding four proteins: the nucleoprotein, the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase, a matrix RING-finger zinc-binding protein, and the glycoprotein precursor. Nucleoprotein is the most abundantly expressed and immunogenic protein and has been shown to dampen the innate immune response by degrading aberrantly generated double-stranded RNAs upon virus infection;3 antibodies to nucleoprotein do not exhibit neutralising activity.13 Conversely, glycoprotein-specific IgG antibodies are crucial for antibody-mediated neutralisation of LASV, as glycoprotein is the sole antigen displayed on the virion surface.14 Although ELISAs cannot determine the neutralising activity of antibodies, glycoprotein IgG-positive specimens could be prioritised for neutralisation assays to better understand rodent antibody responses to LASV. Furthermore, neonatal rodents infected with LASV or other mammarenaviruses develop persistent infections and continually shed viruses and antigens, despite the low-level presence of antiviral antibodies.15,16 Mastomys sp infected with mammarenaviruses as adults, however, rapidly clear the virus and develop high levels of antiviral antibodies.17,18 Understanding the relationship between concurrent antigen and antibody presence might further elucidate the dynamics of the rodent LASV response.

The primary aims of this cross-sectional study were to assess LASV antigen and antibody prevalence in peridomestic rodents in southeastern Sierra Leone, and to understand the relationship between their presence. Rodent serosurveillance data in Sierra Leone are scarce, with only one study published since the 1991–2002 Sierra Leone Civil War.19,20 We used commercially available RDTs to quickly assess the potential presence of LASV nucleoprotein antigen in peri-domestic rodents. Specificity and sensitivity of the RDT results were then compared with gold-standard PCR in a subset of specimens. We modified a semi-quantitative human ELISA that detects LASV IgG from the four major LASV lineages (I–IV) to detect nucleoprotein-specific and glycoprotein-specific IgG in Mastomys-related and Rattus-related spp. Last, we examined the relationship between nucleoprotein-specific and glycoprotein-specific IgG responses, as well as between antigen and IgG presence. Together, these tools and data provide epidemiological insight into potential previous LASV exposures and current LASV infections in southeastern Sierra Leone rodents.

Methods

Study design and small mammal collection

This cross-sectional study was conducted in Kenema District, Sierra Leone between Nov 8, 2018, and July 10, 2019, as part of ongoing Lassa fever surveillance activities at Kenema Government Hospital (Kenema, Sierra Leone). Communities were selected in a non-randomised manner based on known Lassa fever incidence and proximity to Kenema Government Hospital (within a 3-h drive so that samples would be close to the laboratory). The communities were ones that were known to the Kenema Government Hospital outreach team as having Lassa fever, as patients from these communities have come to Kenema Government Hospital for treatment and were familiar with the Kenema Government Hospital outreach team and their surveillance activities. Each community was sampled once. After obtaining consent from the community chief, Sherman live traps were placed inside homes to capture peri-domestic rodents—defined as rodents commonly found in human dwellings, including Mastomys sp, Rattus sp, Mus sp, and related species—as well as other small mammals. Non-mammalian captures (eg, frogs, toads) were released and excluded from the specimen count to avoid harming non-target species and to preserve local ecosystems. The number of trap nights, traps set per home, and homes trapped per community were recorded. Ethical approval was obtained from the Sierra Leone Ethics and Scientific Research Committee (no protocol number assigned) and the Tulane University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (ID 1034).

Procedures

Specimens were identified to the genus level based on morphometric features upon collection. Specimen processing was performed as previously described for virological testing of small mammals, with Biosafety Level 3 field precautions taken to prevent inhalation or ingestion of rodent excreta.21 Non-rodent small mammals and specimens yielding less than 35 μL of serum, which was insufficient for serological testing, were excluded from the final analysis. Cytochrome b PCR was used to confirm species identity for a randomly selected subset of specimens, as previously described.22 Not all samples were tested due to shipping and laboratory constraints during the COVID-19 pandemic.

ReLASV Pan-Lassa Antigen RDTs (Zalgen Labs, MD, USA) were deployed and scored during field collections at necropsy according to the manufacturer’s instructions, using 30 μL of serum.

RNA extracted from randomly selected serum and organs (ie, liver, lung, kidney, and spleen) was used for quantitative RT-PCR to quantify LASV viral copies as previously described.23 RNA extraction, experimental controls, and procedures are detailed in appendix 2 (p 1). Not all samples were tested due to shipping and laboratory constraints during the COVID-19 pandemic.

ELISAs were performed using the Zalgen ReLASV Pan-LASV Prefusion-glycoprotein and nucleoprotein IgG kits as previously described, with adaptations for detecting rodent antibodies.24 Anti-mouse and anti-rat secondary antibody solutions replaced the anti-human secondary antibody solution. Mouse and rat positive and negative controls were used instead of human controls. All other kit components were used according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Specimens identified in the field as Mastomys-related (Mastomys sp, Praomys sp, and Hylomyscus sp) were tested with anti-mouse reagents, and Rattus sp specimens were tested with anti-rat reagents. Details on positive and negative controls, antibodies, ELISA procedures, and assay cutoffs determination are described in appendix 2 (pp 1–2).

Outcomes

The primary outcomes of this analysis were to calculate the prevalence of LASV antigen and IgG antibodies in the study population, and to compare the specificity and sensitivity of antigen RDT results with those of qRT-PCR. The secondary outcomes were to examine the relationship between LASV antigen presence and IgG antibody presence and assess the correlation between nucleoprotein IgG response and glycoprotein IgG response, as well as between antibody strength and RDT score.

Statistical analysis

The final study population for analysis included Mastomys-related or Rattus-related spp with sufficient serum for both antigen and antibody testing. Proportions of antigen positive, nucleoprotein-specific IgG, glycoprotein-specific IgG, and any IgG were reported by species. Sensitivity and specificity of the RDT compared with the gold-standard qRT-PCR were calculated for serum samples tested by both methods. McNemar’s exact test with odds ratios (ORs) and 95% CIs were used to compare the proportion of antigen-positive specimens with qRT-PCR-positive specimens. Differences in nucleoprotein-specific and glycoprotein-specific IgG for Mastomys sp and Rattus sp were assessed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test (paired) for Mastomys sp and the Mann–Whitney U test (unpaired) for Rattus sp, with 95% CIs determined by bootstrapping (1000 iterations). Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) with 95% CIs determined by bootstrapping (1000 iterations) were used to assess correlations between glycoprotein-specific and nucleoprotein-specific IgG in IgG-positive Mastomys sp specimens, and between mean IgG relative values versus RDT scores in IgG-positive Mastomys sp specimens. Antibody values too low to be calculated were reported as 0 U/mL for correlation analyses; no other values were missing or imputed. McNemar’s exact test with OR and 95% CI was used to compare the proportion of Ag-positive and IgG-positive specimens in Mastomys sp and Rattus sp. All analyses and visualisations were performed in R (v4.1.3) and RStudio (v1.4.1717), with R package descriptions provided in appendix 2 (p 2).

Role of the funding source

The funder of this study had no role in in study design, data collection, data analysis, data interpretation, or writing of the report.

Results

534 small mammals were captured across 25 communities, 864 homes, and 18 349 trap nights (figure 1; appendix 2 p 3). We excluded shrews (Crocidura sp; n=8) and specimens with insufficient serum volume for testing (n=153); 373 rodent specimens were included in the final analysis. Genus-level identification was based on morphometrics; a subset (n=205) was typed to the species level using Cytochrome b PCR (appendix 2 p 3).

The presence of LASV nucleoprotein antigen was assessed in serum at necropsy using the Zalgen ReLASV Pan-Lassa Antigen RDT. 74 (20%) of 373 specimens tested positive for antigen (table 1). Of these, 70 (95%) were Mastomys sp, three (4%) were Rattus sp, and one (1%) was Praomys sp. Serum from antigen-positive (n=46) and antigen-negative (n=73) specimens with sufficient volume for RNA extraction was tested by qRT-PCR to evaluate specificity and sensitivity. Notably, only one antigen-positive specimen tested positive by qRT-PCR, impacting specificity and sensitivity results. Although the RDT has been shown to have a sensitivity of 83·3% (95% CI 73·2–90·8) and a specificity of 92·8% (95% CI 87·8–96·2) compared with the gold-standard qRT-PCR in human serum,10 our subset of rodent serum had a specificity of 61·9% (52·5–70·6) and a sensitivity of 100·0% (2·5–100·0; appendix 2 p 4). Organs (ie, liver, lung, kidney, and spleen) from antigen-positive specimens (n=68) with tissues available for RNA extraction were also tested by qRT-PCR. Tissues from antigen-negative specimens were not available for testing due to shipping and laboratory constraints during the COVID-19 pandemic (from March, 2020 to February, 2023). Of the 68 tested specimens, 32 (47%) had at least one tissue test positive by qRT-PCR (appendix 2 p 4).

We compared LASV nucleoprotein-specific and glycoprotein-specific IgG responses in mouse-related and rat-related species using ELISA. 52 (14%) of 373 specimens (42 Mastomys sp and ten Rattus sp) tested IgG positive to at least one protein (table 1). Of the 23 specimens positive for both nucleoprotein-specific IgG and glycoprotein-specific IgG, 21 had higher nucleoprotein-specific IgG than glycoprotein-specific IgG relative values. Of the 22 Mastomys specimens that were IgG positive for both proteins, the mean nucleoprotein-specific IgG relative value (1531·48 U/mL [95% CI 1006·94–2056·02]) was higher than the mean glycoprotein-specific IgG relative value (434·27 U/mL [260·73–607·82]; figure 2, difference of 1097·21 U/mL [95% CI 607·76–1677·17], p<0·0001). Glycoprotein-specific IgG variability was moderately explained by nucleoprotein-specific IgG (ρ 0·58 [95% CI 0·31–0·76], p<0·0001; appendix 2 p 5). By contrast, seven of ten IgG-positive Rattus sp specimens had only glycoprotein-specific IgG or had a higher glycoprotein-specific IgG relative value than nucleoprotein-specific IgG relative value. However, the mean difference between glycoprotein-specific IgG (11·62 U/mL [95% CI −4·00 to 20·67) and nucleoprotein-specific IgG (8·33 U/mL [−2·48 to 20·75]) was not statistically significant (difference of 3·29 U/mL [95% CI −14·01 to 17·40], p=0·16; figure 2).

Simultaneous presence of LASV antigen and LASV IgG was observed in the Mastomys sp population, in which 21 of 42 IgG-positive specimens were also antigen positive (OR 0·43 [95% CI 0·24–0·73], p=0·0011; table 2). However, variability in antibody relative values was not explained by antigen relative values, as indicated by the RDT score, for either nucleoprotein or glycoprotein (figure 3). Spearman’s correlation coefficients showed no statistically significant relationship with regard to nucleoprotein-specific IgG (ρ −0·23 [95% CI −0·52 to 0·11], p=0·17) and glycoprotein-specific IgG (ρ 0·04 [95% CI −0·34 to 0·41], p=0·85). In contrast with the Mastomys sp specimens, none of the ten IgG-positive Rattus sp specimens tested positive for antigen (OR 3·33 [95% CI 0·86–18·85], p=0·092; appendix 2 p 4).

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic reinforced the utility of RDTs for on-demand diagnostics. Their portability, ease of use, and stability make RDTs well suited for field-based pathogen surveillance. In this study, we showed the feasibility of using commercially available LASV RDTs as a potential tool for in-country rodent serosurveillance, pending further validation. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use LASV antigen RDTs to rapidly assess potential presence and relative strength of LASV antigen in rodents. Additionally, although most LASV IgG surveillance studies in rodents rely on immunofluorescence assays, we developed a semi-quantitative ELISA capable of detecting LASV nucleoprotein-specific and glycoprotein-specific IgG in both Mastomys and Rattus species.

Of the 373 specimens examined, 74 (20%) tested positive for LASV nucleoprotein antigen. The proportion of antigen-positive specimens in our study might be influenced by selection bias, as communities selected for trapping were not chosen at random, nor were seasonal reproduction dynamics and specimen age considered in the interpretation of results. These factors might limit the extrapolation of true LASV prevalence in the broader rodent population. The proportion of antigen-positive specimens in our study is higher than that reported in the only previous study5 assessing LASV antigen in peri-domestic rodents, in which approximately 5% of the study population tested antigen positive. We detected antigen in Mastomys sp, Rattus sp, and Praomys sp; notably, LASV antigen has not been previously reported in Praomys sp.

LASV antigen tests often perform as well as, or better than, nucleic acid tests for detecting active LASV infections due to substantial genetic diversity at the nucleotide level among lineages and strains, while protein sequences remain relatively conserved within intra-lineage strains.9 Furthermore, RNA is more prone to degradation, a challenge frequently encountered in real-world settings compared with protein and one that might contribute to differences in test performance. Accordingly, more specimens tested antigen positive than qRT-PCR positive. However, several limitations must be considered regarding our qRT-PCR results. First, serum might not be the optimal medium to assess viraemia in rodent hosts. Only one antigen-positive specimen tested positive by qRT-PCR in serum, whereas other studies assessing viral presence in rodents often use whole blood. Second, sparse data skew the interpretation of sensitivity and specificity. Third, we were unable to test organs from antigen-negative specimens to compare RDT performance with qRT-PCR due to shipping and laboratory constraints during the COVID-19 pandemic. Further qRT-PCR testing of organs from antigen-negative specimens, as well as testing for viral presence using whole blood or isolated red blood cell pellets, is needed to fully evaluate RDT specificity and sensitivity. Altogether, the RDT might be better suited as an initial diagnostic tool to triage specimens for further testing, as antigen presence alone cannot confirm an active LASV infection.25

After evaluating LASV nucleoprotein antigen detection by RDT, we examined the prevalence of LASV nucleoprotein-specific and glycoprotein-specific IgG by ELISA, following the adaptation of a commercial ELISA assay to detect Mastomys-related sp and Rattus-related sp. 52 specimens (14%) were IgG positive for at least one protein, a result consistent with other seroprevalence studies of similar scope and size.5,6 We did not detect LASV IgG in Praomys sp or Hylomyscus sp specimens. LASV IgG has been previously detected in Praomys sp (Praomys daltoni)26, and H pamfi has been identified as a novel LASV reservoir in Nigeria and as a host of a related LASV-like virus in Côte d’Ivoire.7,27 Given that previous studies used anti-mouse IgG secondary antibodies to detect LASV IgG in both Praomys sp and Hylomyscus sp, it is plausible that too few specimens were tested to detect LASV exposure in these species.

In IgG-positive Mastomys sp specimens, the mean nucleoprotein-specific IgG relative value was higher than the mean glycoprotein-specific IgG relative value. Conversely, in IgG-positive Rattus sp specimens, the mean nucleoprotein-specific IgG relative value was lower than the mean glycoprotein-specific IgG relative value; however, the difference was not statistically significant and might have been influenced by the small sample size of IgG-positive Rattus sp specimens. Unexpectedly, more Rattus sp specimens tested positive for glycoprotein-specific IgG than nucleoprotein-specific IgG, despite that nucleoprotein is more highly expressed and immunogenic than glycoprotein.14 Although no documented studies have examined IgG responses in rats infected with LASV or other old-world (OW) mammarenaviruses, one study that infected C57/BL6 mice, hamsters, Mastomys sp, and gerbils with similar concentrations of lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV) found significant variation in nucleoprotein-specific IgG ELISA responses across rodent species.28 Although limited by the relatively small sample size, our findings suggest that further investigation of the Rattus sp antibody response to LASV and other OW mammarenaviruses is warranted.

A linkage between viral antigen presence and IgG presence is expected in a reservoir host if a mechanism of persistent infection allows for sustained viral replication. In mice persistently infected with LCMV, low-level viraemia (and thus antigen) is maintained despite early and consistent generation of non-neutralising antibodies.29 Similarly, viral antigen might persist towards the end of, or even after, an acute viral infection, even as IgG antibody production against the pathogen begins. In our study, we observed a strong association between simultaneous antigen and IgG presence in Mastomys sp specimens, whereas Rattus sp specimens were either antigen positive or IgG positive. However, little variability in antibody relative values was explained by the relative amount of antigen present in Mastomys sp specimens. Our results contrast with those of Demby and colleagues in Guinea,5 who found that antigen and antibody presence in Mastomys sp were mutually exclusive. Differences in assay sensitivity and specificity might account for the discrepancy, as ELISA results can vary based on numerous factors, including antibody selection, antigen optimisation, and operator characteristics. Nonetheless, the strong antigen–IgG association in Mastomys sp suggests at least some degree of chronic infection.

An important limitation to our study is the potential impact of sparse data bias, particularly in Rattus sp specimens. This bias is also evident in the sensitivity and specificity testing of serum antigen by RDT compared with qRT-PCR. Additionally, specimens were sampled at a single timepoint, making it impossible to determine the exact stage of infection. Antigen-positive and IgG-negative Mastomys sp could represent different stages of infection, ranging from early antigen production before viraemia to recovery after infection, before the detection of IgG antibodies. Although the Zalgen Pan-LASV RDT exhibits minimal cross-reactivity with other recombinant OW mammarenavirus nucleoprotein antigens and has a low limit of detection for LASV nucleoprotein antigen (L Branco, personal communication [data available on request]), the IgG ELISAs have yet to be validated against positive human controls with previous exposure to other OW mammarenaviruses. Thus, it is plausible that related mammarenaviruses might cross-react with the IgG ELISAs, leading to false positives. Conversely, false negatives might occur when antigen or antibody concentrations decrease below the assay’s limit of detection. The potential for cross-reactivity with LCMV and other OW mammarenaviruses, as well as further characterisation of assay detection limits, should be assessed in both human and rodent populations before broader use of these assays.

In this study, we used an in-field LASV antigen RDT and adapted a semi-quantitative human ELISA to assess the prevalence and relative quantity of LASV IgG in wild-caught rodents in southeastern Sierra Leone. Although our sampling strategy and use of these tests do not resolve many of the broader challenges surrounding LASV surveillance and human infection risk assessment, our findings contribute valuable zoological surveillance data on LASV prevalence in the region. Notably, our study does not account for seasonal variations or changes in species abundance over time, but these data can serve as a foundation for future longitudinal studies of LASV prevalence. We also evaluated IgG responses in Mastomys sp and Rattus sp to both nucleoprotein-specific and glycoprotein-specific IgG, which, to our knowledge, has not been previously demonstrated. This is particularly relevant as Mastomys sp and Rattus sp account for nearly 95% of peri-domestic rodents collected in Sierra Leone and can cohabitate in human dwellings.30 Our ELISA assays, as well as those developed by Soubrier and colleagues,12 might support studies investigating the natural history and infection dynamics of LASV in rodent hosts. The ability to screen rodent specimens for potential presence and previous exposures to LASV, using accessible in-country techniques, will enhance LASV surveillance in rodents and aid in prioritising specimens for downstream analyses to advance Lassa fever research and therapies.

Supplementary Material

Research in context

Evidence before this study

A literature review was conducted in PubMed using the search terms (“mastomys natalensis lassa”, “lassa serology”, “lassa IgG”, “lassa“, “lcmv serology”, “lcmv IgG”, and “lcmv”) to identify relevant studies published before Sept 15, 2022, with updates on Aug 8, 2024, and Feb 7, 2025. This review determined the number of Lassa mammarenavirus (LASV) rodent serology studies conducted in west Africa, the availability of LASV diagnostics, and relevant diagnostic and mechanistic studies on lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus, a surrogate mammarenavirus. Despite the high burden of LASV and Lassa fever in Sierra Leone, only two LASV rodent serology studies have been conducted in the country, although at least three studies have examined LASV phylogeny using rodent-derived sequences. By contrast, more than ten rodent serology studies have been conducted in Guinea and Nigeria. Regarding LASV diagnostics, only one other study has specifically developed assays for rodent serology.

Added value of this study

Nearly all rodent LASV surveillance studies rely on indirect immunofluorescence assays to detect IgG, a qualitative method that indicates previous LASV exposure but does not assess the strength of the IgG response. Additionally, immunofluorescence assays require advanced biocontainment facilities that are unavailable in west Africa, substantially increasing the cost and logistical burden of such studies. The methods described in this study, performed entirely in-country, enable the rapid detection of LASV antigen and IgG in rodents while also assessing the relative strength of these responses. Furthermore, this study also contributes much-needed LASV zoonotic surveillance data from southeastern Sierra Leone.

Implications of all the available evidence

The ability to rapidly identify rodents with current or past LASV exposure could aid public health officials in outbreak investigations and zoonotic surveillance. The diagnostic tools presented in this study can help prioritise LASV antigen-positive and IgG-positive specimens for downstream analyses, enabling further research into LASV dynamics within the host reservoir.
