Cr-Doped CuTi-Layered Double Hydroxide Nanoarchitectures Grown on 3D Ni Foam: Boosting Charge Separation for Photocatalytic Mineralization of Refractory Organics
Authors: Hanrui Li, Yanming Zhou, Xiaotong Wang, Xingxiang An, Zheming You, Jinyu Tang, Keli Chen, Wanliang Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Immobilized photocatalytic membranes that integrate efficient charge separation with structural robustness are highly desirable for the sustainable removal of refractory organic pollutants, yet they remain challenging to construct due to limited interfacial activity and sluggish carrier transport. Herein, Cr-doped CuTi-layered double hydroxide (Cr-CuTi-LDH) nanoarchitectures are rationally grown in situ on three-dimensional nickel foam (NF) via a facile one-step hydrothermal strategy, yielding a self-supported photocatalytic membrane with intimate interfacial contact and enhanced electronic conductivity. Chromium incorporation induces pronounced nanosheet refinement and ordered stacking, which effectively regulates nucleation kinetics, maximizes accessible surface sites, and promotes the separation and migration of photogenerated charge carriers. Systematic photoelectrochemical analyses, including electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, transient photocurrent response, and Mott–Schottky measurements, reveal that Cr doping significantly reduces charge-transfer resistance and facilitates electron–hole separation without substantially altering the bandgap structure. Benefiting from these interfacial and electronic advantages, the optimized Cr-CuTi-LDH/NF membrane exhibits significantly enhanced photocatalytic activity for the mineralization of Rhodamine B and Methylene Blue under simulated solar irradiation. It degraded 82.8% and 76.9% of Rhodamine B and Methylene Blue, respectively, within 180 min, while demonstrating excellent reaction kinetics and outstanding cycling stability. Mechanistic investigations indicate that the synergistic participation of photogenerated holes and superoxide radicals (•O2 –), promoted by Cr-mediated charge regulation, dominates the degradation pathway. This work not only elucidates the critical role of heteroion doping in governing interfacial charge dynamics within LDH-based membranes, but also provides a scalable design paradigm for high-performance, recyclable photocatalytic systems for water purification.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of modern industry has significantly increased the discharge of refractory organic pollutants into aquatic environments, posing severe threats to ecological systems and human health − Conventional wastewater treatment technologies, − including physical adsorption and chemical oxidation, , often suffer from limited mineralization efficiency, secondary pollution, or high operational costs, particularly when treating chemically stable organic contaminants. − In this context, photocatalysis has emerged as a promising green technology − that utilizes solar energy to drive the mineralization of organic pollutants into benign products such as CO2 and H2O under mild conditions. ,

Despite these advantages, the practical deployment of photocatalytic systems remains severely hindered by the intrinsic limitations of conventional powder catalysts. These include difficult recovery, particle aggregation, and potential secondary contamination, which collectively impede long-term operation and scalability. − Immobilized photocatalytic membranes have therefore been proposed as an effective alternative, offering structural stability, easy recyclability, and minimized catalyst loss. However, the development of high-performance photocatalytic membranes still faces several critical challenges: (i) insufficient interfacial coupling between active materials and substrates, (ii) limited exposure of catalytically active sites, and (iii) inefficient charge separation and sluggish carrier transport across heterogeneous interfaces.

Layered double hydroxides (LDHs), a class of two-dimensional anionic clays with the general formula [M1–x II M x III (OH)2] x+[(A n‑) x/n yH2O] x‑, have emerged as attractive photocatalytic materials owing to their tunable composition, abundant surface hydroxyl groups, and capability to generate reactive oxygen species under light irradiation − In particular, the incorporation of high-valence cations such as Ti4+ into LDH frameworks has been shown to enhance redox activity and light utilization. , Nevertheless, pristine LDHs generally suffer from rapid recombination of photogenerated charge carriers and poor electrical conductivity, which fundamentally limit their photocatalytic efficiency. , While immobilizing LDHs onto conductive substrates is considered a promising strategy to overcome these limitations, the rational integration of structural design and charge regulation within LDH-based membranes remains insufficiently explored. ,

Nickel foam, with its three-dimensional porous framework, high electrical conductivity, and excellent mechanical robustness, provides an ideal platform for constructing self-supported photocatalytic systems , In situ growth of LDH nanosheets on nickel foam can establish strong interfacial contact and continuous electron transport pathways, thereby promoting charge separation. , Meanwhile, heteroion doping has emerged as a powerful approach to tailor the electronic structure and carrier dynamics of LDHs − , Chromium ions, in particular, possess multiple valence states and redox flexibility, enabling them to act as electron mediators that capture and shuttle photogenerated carriers, thereby suppressing recombination. However, current studies mainly focus on either structural immobilization or elemental doping individually, while the synergistic coupling of heteroion regulation with in situ membrane constructionespecially in CuTi-based LDH systemsremains largely unexplored. More importantly, a unified strategy that simultaneously optimizes nanoscale architecture, interfacial coupling, and charge transport within a single integrated system is still lacking.

To address these challenges, herein we propose a synergistic design paradigm that integrates heteroion doping, two-dimensional LDH nanoarchitecture, and three-dimensional conductive scaffolds into a unified photocatalytic membrane system. Specifically, Cr-doped CuTi layered double hydroxide (Cr–CuTi-LDH) nanosheets are directly grown in situ on nickel foam via a one-step hydrothermal method, forming a self-supported membrane with intimate interfacial integration. The introduction of Cr species not only refines the nanosheet morphology and increases accessible active sites, but also serves as an efficient charge regulator to facilitate electron–hole separation and migration, without disrupting the intrinsic LDH crystal framework. Meanwhile, the conductive nickel foam substrate provides rapid electron transport pathways, enabling efficient interfacial charge extraction. As a result, the as-prepared Cr–CuTi-LDH/NF membrane exhibits significantly enhanced photocatalytic mineralization performance toward representative organic pollutants, such as Rhodamine B and Methylene Blue, along with excellent stability and recyclability. This work not only demonstrates a previously underexplored integration of heteroion doping and in situ membrane engineering, but also establishes a generalizable strategy for coupling structural and electronic regulation in LDH-based photocatalytic systems, offering new insights for the design of high-efficiency, scalable water purification technologies.

Experimental Section

Materials

Copper nitrate trihydrate (Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O, Macklin, 99%), ammonium fluoride (NH4F, Macklin, 98%), urea (CO­(NH2)2, Kermel, 99%), titanium tetrachloride (TiCl4, Macklin, 99%), chromium­(III) nitrate nonahydrate (CrN3O9·9H2O, Macklin, 99%), methylene blue (MB, Macklin, 98%), rhodamine B (RhB, Macklin), and nickel foam (NF, Sheng’er Nuo). All experimental materials used in this study were of analytical grade and were not further purified.

Synthesis of CuTi-LDH and Cr-CuTi-LDH Membranes

The CuTi-LDH precursor solution was prepared by dissolving 0.242 g of Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O, 0.600 g of CO­(NH2)2, and 0.074 g of NH4F in 40 mL of deionized water, followed by the dropwise addition of 110 μL of TiCl4 under continuous stirring. The resulting mixture was ultrasonicated for 10 min to ensure complete homogenization. Subsequently, the precursor solution was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, in which a piece of nickel foam substrate was placed at an inclined position to enable in situ growth of the CuTi-LDH membrane. The hydrothermal reaction was carried out at 130 °C for 8 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature, the obtained membrane was washed thoroughly with deionized water and dried in a vacuum oven at 60 °C for 12 h, yielding the Cu1Ti1-LDH membrane. For comparison, CuTi-LDH membranes with different Cu/Ti molar ratios were synthesized by adjusting the amounts of Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O and TiCl4. Specifically, Cu1Ti3-LDH and Cu3Ti1-LDH membranes were obtained using 0.121 and 0.363 g of Cu­(NO3)2·3H2O, together with 164 and 54.8 μL of TiCl4, respectively, while keeping the other reaction parameters unchanged.

Cr-doped CuTi-LDH membranes were prepared by introducing Cr­(NO3)3·9H2O (0.02 g) into the precursor solution, corresponding to a nominal Cr doping content of 2.5%. The subsequent hydrothermal synthesis and post-treatment procedures were identical to those described above, resulting in the formation of Cr-CuTi-LDH membranes.

Characterization

The surface and cross-sectional morphologies of the membranes, as well as the spatial distribution of constituent elements, were examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300, Germany). The crystalline structures of the samples were identified using X-ray diffraction (XRD, Bruker D8 Advance, Germany). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS20, USA) was employed in conjunction with XRD analysis to elucidate the chemical composition of the membranes. The elemental composition and chemical states of the surface species were analyzed by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-α, USA). Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES, model: Agilent 5110, USA) quantitatively analyzed the elements in the samples. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEOL JEM-F200, Japan) was used to investigate the microstructural features, including lattice fringes and heterointerfacial architectures. Optical absorption properties were measured using a UV–visible spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-2700i, Japan) equipped with an integrating sphere. Photoelectrochemical measurements, including electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, Mott–Schottky analysis, and transient photocurrent response, were conducted on an electrochemical workstation (CHI660E, CH Instruments, China) using 0.5 M Na2SO4 aqueous solution as the electrolyte. Steady-state/transient fluorescence spectrometry (model: Edinburgh FLS1000, UK) was used to test PL spectra and TRPL spectra of the samples to understand fluorescence intensity and fluorescence lifetime. Electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy (EPR, model: Bruker EMXplus-6/1, Germany) was employed to characterize the types and concentrations of free radicals in photocatalysis.

Degradation Experiment

Photocatalytic degradation experiments were performed using Rhodamine B (RhB) as a model organic pollutant. In a typical test, 30 mL of RhB aqueous solution with an initial concentration of 5 mg L–1 was used, and the size of each photocatalytic membrane was fixed at 2.0 cm × 3.0 cm. The reaction temperature was maintained at 25 °C using a circulating water bath to exclude thermal effects. Prior to light irradiation, the membranes were immersed in the RhB solution and magnetically stirred in the dark for 60 min to achieve adsorption–desorption equilibrium. Photocatalytic reactions were initiated under irradiation from a 300 W xenon lamp (CEAU LIGHT, China). In the experiment, no UV-cutoff filters were used, and the spectrum covered the full UV–vis-I. During the degradation process, 2 mL aliquots were withdrawn from the reaction solution at 30 min intervals. The residual concentration of RhB was determined by measuring the absorbance at 554 nm using a UV–visible spectrophotometer. The degradation efficiency was calculated according to the following equation surplusratio(%)=CtC0×100% Where C 0 and Ct are the concentrations of RhB solution before and after t min of photocatalytic degradation, respectively.

To further validate the photocatalytic performance of the membranes, RhB solutions with different initial concentrations (3, 5, and 8 mg L–1) were employed. Comparative photocatalytic experiments were also conducted using CuTi-LDH membranes with different Cu/Ti ratios under identical conditions. The stability and recyclability of the membranes were evaluated through three consecutive photocatalytic degradation cycles. After each cycle, the membrane was removed, dried, and reused in a fresh RhB solution under the same experimental conditions.

To evaluate the general applicability of the photocatalytic membranes toward organic pollutants, additional degradation experiments were carried out using methylene blue (MB) as a representative dye. Both RhB and MB solutions were prepared with an initial concentration of 5 mg L–1 and a volume of 30 mL. The residual concentrations of RhB and MB were determined by monitoring the absorbance at 554 and 664 nm, respectively. The degradation efficiencies were calculated using the same equation described above.

Optical Performance Testing

The optical properties of the prepared membranes were evaluated using an integrating sphere accessory attached to a UV–visible spectrophotometer. The absorption spectra of CuTi-LDH photocatalytic membranes and substrate foam nickel NF at different ratios were measured across the wavelength range of 200–800 nm. The membranes were circular with a diameter of 2 cm. Based on the absorption spectra, the bandgap width (E g, eV) of the membranes was calculated using the following detailed formula: αhv=A(hv−Eg)n/2 Where A represents the proportionality constant, α denotes the absorption coefficient, h signifies the Planck constant, and ν represents the frequency of light.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Detailed Characterization of Photocatalytic Membranes

The CuTi-LDH photocatalytic membrane was fabricated via a facile one-step hydrothermal strategy, as schematically illustrated in Figure a. Nickel foam (NF) was employed as the conductive substrate and positioned at an inclined orientation within the Teflon-lined autoclave containing the Cr-doped CuTi-LDH precursor solution, enabling the in situ growth of LDH nanosheets on the substrate surface during the hydrothermal process. After the reaction, a uniform blue–green membrane was obtained and subsequently dried under vacuum to yield the Cr-CuTi-LDH/NF photocatalytic membrane. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to examine the surface morphology and structural features of the photocatalytic membranes.

(a) Schematic illustration of the preparation of Cr-CuTi-LDH/NF membranes; (b–d) SEM image of Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF; (e–g) SEM image of Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF.

As shown in Figure b–d, the undoped CuTi-LDH membrane exhibits a typical plate-like nanosheet morphology, with lateral sizes ranging from approximately 200 nm to 1 μm and thicknesses of about 20–50 nm. These nanosheets are randomly oriented and loosely stacked, forming a hierarchically porous architecture. In contrast, the Cr-doped CuTi-LDH membrane (Figure e–g) displays a markedly different morphological configuration. The nanosheets become noticeably smaller and more uniform, with reduced lateral dimensions of approximately 100–300 nm, and assemble into a more compact and regularly stacked layered structure. This pronounced morphological evolution indicates that the introduction of Cr species effectively regulates the growth behavior of CuTi-LDH during hydrothermal synthesis. Specifically, Cr incorporation is likely to suppress excessive lateral crystal growth while promoting more ordered vertical stacking, resulting in enhanced interlayer cohesion, likely due to altered nucleation kinetics and surface energy under hydrothermal conditions. At the same time, we also measured the BET surface area of Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF and Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF (Table S2). The BET surface area of the Cr–CuTi-LDH/NF sample is measured to be 6.82 m2 g–1, which is significantly higher than that of the undoped CuTi-LDH/NF (3.79 m2 g–1). These results indicate that Cr doping effectively refines the nanosheet structure, leading to increased surface area and more accessible active sites, which is consistent with the SEM and TEM observations.

X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to elucidate the crystalline structure and phase composition of the photocatalytic membranes. As shown in Figure a, In addition, a characteristic diffraction peak located at approximately 13°, 26°, 36°, 40°, 53°, 63°, can be assigned to the (003), (006), (012), (018), (110) plane of CuTi-LDH, − confirming the successful in situ growth of LDH phases on the conductive substrate. Notably, no additional impurity phases are detected upon Cr incorporation, indicating that Cr doping does not alter the overall LDH crystal structure. Simultaneously, the incorporation of Cr caused a certain degree of peak shift. Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was further employed to probe the chemical bonding environment of the membranes.

As shown in Figure b, the CuTi-LDH samples exhibit a broad and intense absorption band centered at approximately 3310 cm–1, which is attributed to the stretching vibrations of O–H groups associated with hydroxyl layers and interlayer water molecules. Compared with undoped Cu–Ti LDH, a slight red shift of the O–H stretching band is observed, which can be reasonably attributed to the modulation of O–H bond electron density induced by Ti4+ incorporation. The pronounced band broadening is indicative of strong hydrogen-bonding interactions between interlayer anions and confined water molecules. Furthermore, the absorption bands in the range of 500–900 cm–1 correspond to the lattice vibration modes of M–O and M–OH bonds, , characteristic of layered double hydroxide frameworks. These spectroscopic features collectively confirm the formation of a typical LDH structure, in which positively charged metal hydroxide layers are charge-balanced by interlayer anions.

Figure f displays the Cr 2p XPS data, showing two characteristic peaks at approximately 576 and 586 eV, corresponding to the Cr 2p 3/2 and 2p 1/2 orbitals. Figure S1a displays the XPS data for Ni 2p, with Ni 2p1/2 and Ni 2p3/2 peaks at 873.4 and 855.6 eV, respectively. Photocatalytic Degradation of Organic Pollutants

The photocatalytic performance of the photocatalytic membrane was evaluated by degrading organic pollutants such as Rhodamine B (RhB) and Methylene Blue (MB). Rhodamine B is a red fluorescent dye used in industries like glass manufacturing, mining, and steel production, posing significant health risks including cerebral venous congestion, myocardial fiber rupture, and stripe loss. Similarly, Methylene Blue, employed in dyeing, pharmaceuticals, and aquaculture, causes issues such as red blood cell alterations and necrotic granulomas. Therefore, addressing the degradation of organic pollutants like RhB and MB is an urgent priority.

Figure a demonstrates the application of Cr-doped CuTi-LDH photocatalytic membranes in degrading RhB solution (5 mg/L, 30 mL). During the photocatalytic reaction, after 180 min of illumination, the degradation efficiency of RhB on the Cr-doped CuTi-LDH photocatalytic membrane with a copper–titanium ratio of 1:1 reached 82.8%, while the degradation efficiencies for the 1:3 and 3:1 membranes were 60.1% and 44.2%, respectively. Figure b illustrates the degradation kinetics of the photocatalytic membranes. The data reveal that the kinetic constant of the 1:1 Cr-doped photocatalytic membrane is significantly higher than those of the 1:3 and 3:1 membranes, highlighting its superior performance. To simulate common RhB concentrations, degradation tests were conducted at 3 mg/L, 5 mg/L, and 8 mg/L on Cr-doped CuTi-LDH membranes with a 1:1 Cu:Ti ratio, as shown in Figure c. Lower RhB concentrations exhibited higher degradation rates, In contrast, higher concentrations exhibited reduced adsorption, making photocatalytic degradation the primary factor in concentration reduction.

The undoped membranes demonstrated significantly lower performance than Cr-doped membranes, with degradation rates of 74.8%, 59.5%, and 42.9% after 180 min of irradiation for the 1:1, 3:1, and 1:3 membranes, respectively. This improvement is attributed to the successful Cr doping. 1:1, 3:1, and 1:3 membranes exhibited degradation rates of 82.8%, 60.1%, and 44.2%, respectively, after 180 min of illumination. This improvement is attributed to the successful Cr doping, which significantly enhances the photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants. To evaluate the recyclability of the photocatalytic membranes, the photocatalytic degradation of RhB and MB (5 mg L–1, 30 mL) was repeated for five consecutive cycles. The results show that the degradation efficiency remains at a relatively high level after multiple cycles, with only a slight decrease compared to the initial performance, demonstrating good cyclic stability of the material. In addition, SEM images of the samples after cycling (Figure S3a,b) reveal no noticeable morphological changes, confirming the excellent structural stability of the membrane.

These results collectively indicate that the Cr–CuTi-LDH/NF photocatalytic membrane possesses good long-term stability and reusability.

Photocatalytic Membrane Photovoltaic Testing and Mechanism Study

To further investigate the membrane’s photovoltaic properties and photocatalytic mechanism, a series of tests and mechanism studies were conducted on the membrane. Initially, the light absorption capacity of the photocatalytic membranes was measured using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV–vis), as shown in Figures a and S4a,c. The absorption ranges of membranes with and without Cr doping were concentrated between 465–475 nm, indicating that the membranes could absorb not only ultraviolet light but also a small amount of visible light, confirming their effective response to visible light. As shown in Figures b and S4b,d, the bandgap of both Cr-doped and undoped membranes ranged from 2.69 to 2.92 eV, exhibiting minimal variation. This limited shift likely stems from the low Cr doping concentration, which exerts a negligible influence on the bandgap. To elucidate the separation and transfer efficiency of photogenerated carriers in the samples, transient photocurrent spectroscopy and EIS measurements were performed on the photocatalytic membranes. As shown in Figure c (electrochemical impedance spectroscopy), the arc diameter corresponding to the 1:1 Cu:Ti ratio membrane was significantly smaller than those of other Cr–Cu1Ti3-LDH/NF, Cu1Ti3-LDH/NF, and Cr–Cu3Ti1-LDH/NF, Cu3Ti1-LDH/NF membranes (as shown in Figure S5), indicating that Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF exhibits lower resistance. This suggests superior electrical conductivity, charge transfer, and photogenerated carrier separation efficiency. Figures d and S6 display the transient photocurrent spectra of Cr–Cu3Ti1-LDH/NF and Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF, respectively, revealing that Cr–Cu3Ti1-LDH/NF exhibits a higher photocurrent density compared to Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF. This indicates that Cr–Cu3Ti1-LDH/NF possesses more lone pair electrons participating in photocatalytic reactions, attributed to the successful chromium doping that enhances the separation efficiency of photogenerated carriers.

(a) UV–vis diffuse reflectance spectra of Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF and Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF; (b) relationship between photon energy (hv) and Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF and Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF; (c) EIS spectra of Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF and Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF; (d) photocurrent response spectrum of Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF; (e, f) Mott–Schottky (M-S) plots of Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF and Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF.

Figure e,f depict the Mott–Schottky (M-S) plots with and without Cr doping, respectively, used to determine the flat-band potential (E fb) of the photocatalytic membranes and calculate their conduction band potential (CB). The positive slope of the M-S curve confirms its n-type semiconductor nature, with an Efb value of −1.17 eV (relative to the Ag/AgCl electrode) in the Cr-undoped state. Converting these values to standard hydrogen electrode (NHE) potentials using the equation E(NHE) = E(Ag/AgCl) – E θ + 0.059 pH, where E θ(Ag/AgCl, pH = 7) = 0.197 V, yields a CB potential of −0.954 eV. Therefore, its valence band potential VB was calculated as 1.776 eV using the equation E g = E VB – E CB. Similarly, the E fb value for Cr-doped material was calculated as −1.15 eV (relative to Ag/AgCl electrode), determining its CB potential as −0.934 eV and its valence band potential VB as 1.926 eV.

To gain deeper insight into the charge carrier dynamics in the photocatalytic membranes, photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) measurements were performed for Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF and Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF samples (Figure S7a,b). As is well-known, the PL emission intensity is closely related to the recombination rate of photogenerated electron–hole pairs; a lower PL intensity generally indicates more efficient charge separation. As shown in Figure S7a, the Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF film exhibits a much stronger fluorescence signal compared to the Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF sample, suggesting a higher recombination rate in the former. In contrast, the significantly quenched PL intensity of Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF indicates suppressed recombination and more efficient charge carrier separation. Furthermore, TRPL measurements (Figure S7b) reveal that Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF exhibits a longer average carrier lifetime than Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF, indicating prolonged survival of photogenerated charge carriers. This extended lifetime facilitates more effective participation of charge carriers in surface redox reactions. These results collectively demonstrate that Cr incorporation effectively modulates the charge transfer dynamics, leading to enhanced charge separation and prolonged carrier lifetime, which are crucial for the improved photocatalytic degradation performance.

To elucidate the photocatalytic degradation mechanism of the Cr–Cu1Ti1-LDH/NF membrane, electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy was performed using 5, 5-dimethyl-1-pyrroline N-oxide (DMPO) and 2, 2, 6, 6-tetramethylpiperidine-N-oxyl (TEMPO) as spin-trapping agents (Figure S8a). No characteristic DMPO–•O2 – signal was observed under dark conditions, whereas distinct signals appeared upon light irradiation, indicating that superoxide radicals are generated only under photoexcitation. This result confirms that photogenerated electrons are effectively transferred to adsorbed oxygen molecules, reflecting efficient charge separation. Similarly, the TEMPO–h+ signal is significantly enhanced under light irradiation, demonstrating the active involvement of photogenerated holes in the reaction process. The increased signal intensity suggests that more charge carriers are successfully separated and participate in surface redox reactions. These findings indicate that both •O2 – radicals and h+ play dominant roles in the photocatalytic degradation process. This conclusion is further supported by radical scavenging experiments (Figure S8b), which confirm the synergistic contribution of these reactive species. Overall, the EPR results provide direct evidence for efficient charge separation and active species generation in the Cr–CuTi-LDH/NF system, which is crucial for its enhanced photocatalytic performance.

Based on the spatial arrangement of the valence band (VB), conduction band (CB), and involved reactive species, we describe the mechanism of Cr-doped CuTi-LDH photocatalytic degradation of pollutants. As shown in Figure , under illumination, electrons in CuTi-LDH are excited from the VB to the CB. With the cyclic promotion of chromium ions, this facilitates the reduction of oxygen molecules to •O2 –. Ultimately, the synergistic action of photogenerated holes (h+) and superoxide anion radicals (•O2 –) converts organic pollutants into harmless small-molecule products such as CO2 and H2O. In summary, the Cr-doping strategy in CuTi-LDH membranes effectively promotes the separation of photogenerated electrons and holes, enabling high-performance, synergistic adsorption and photocatalytic degradation of organic pollutants.

Conclusions

In summary, Cr-doped CuTi layered double hydroxide (CuTi-LDH) was successfully grown in situ on nickel foam substrates via a one-step hydrothermal process, forming a self-supported photocatalytic membrane. The direct growth of Cr–CuTi-LDH nanosheets on the three-dimensional conductive nickel foam ensures strong interfacial coupling, structural integrity, and efficient charge-transport pathways, thereby overcoming the limitations associated with powder photocatalysts and weakly immobilized systems. Systematic structural and spectroscopic analyses demonstrate that Cr incorporation does not disrupt the intrinsic layered double hydroxide framework but effectively regulates nanosheet morphology and interfacial electronic states. The resulting membrane architecture combines the advantages of two-dimensional CuTi-LDH active layers and a three-dimensional conductive scaffold, leading to enhanced separation and utilization of photoexcited charge carriers. Consequently, the Cr-CuTi-LDH/NF photocatalytic membrane exhibits improved degradation performance toward representative organic pollutants, such as Rhodamine B and Methylene Blue, together with good stability and recyclability. This work highlights that in situ growth of Cr-doped CuTi-LDH on nickel foam is an effective strategy for constructing immobilized photocatalytic membranes, in which heteroion doping and substrate coupling cooperatively regulate charge dynamics and interfacial reactions. The proposed membrane design provides a feasible and scalable pathway for developing advanced LDH-based photocatalytic systems for environmental remediation.
