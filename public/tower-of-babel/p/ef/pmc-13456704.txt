Introduction

Congenital granular cell tumor (CGCT), also known as congenital epulis of the newborn, is a benign soft tissue tumor that typically originates from the gingiva of the maxillary or mandibular alveolar ridge at birth. It is a rare tumor, first described by Ernst Christian Neumann in 1871, with an estimated incidence of only 0.0006%[1,2]. Clinically, congenital epulis appears as a protruding, pedunculated mass that can interfere with feeding and respiration, often causing significant distress and esthetic concern for the parents. Early recognition and prompt surgical intervention are therefore pivotal, as these lesions do not spontaneously regress, though no recurrence has been reported post-excision to date[3]. While the alveolar ridge is the typical site, extra-alveolar occurrences are extraordinarily rare. Here, we report an atypical case of a CGCT arising from the lower gingival buccal sulcus with extension to the lower lip in a newborn female, successfully managed with surgery. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria[4].

Case summary

A single-term female child, born at 39 + 1 weeks of gestation with a birth weight of 2840 grams via lower segment cesarean section (performed for a previous cesarean section) presented with a large lower lip mass since birth. An oral mass was identified on prenatal ultrasound, though dynamic imaging was unfortunately unavailable for inclusion. On postnatal examination, a 5 × 5 cm soft, friable, irregular, pedunculated mass was observed, strictly originating from the lower gingival buccal sulcus and extending to the lower lip. The mass was pink to red in color, with visible blood vessels over its surface (Fig. 1A). No other mass lesions were identified in the oral cavity. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the oral cavity showed a T2 high signal intensity exophytic pedunculated soft tissue lesion measuring 3.9 × 3.5 × 3.7 cm arising from the median and right paramedian lower gingival buccal sulcus (Fig. 2). The lesion had a base measuring 13 mm with encasement of the sockets of the lower central incisors. It showed a maintained interface with the tongue posteriorly, as well as the upper lip and upper gingiva buccal sulcus superiorly. No obvious intralesional suppression was noted in the short tau inversion recovery sequence. On day 3 of life, surgical excision of the mass was performed under general anesthesia with an endotracheal tube fixed to the left corner of the mouth. A pediatric surgeon performed the surgery, and the case was reviewed in our pediatric multidisciplinary meeting before the intervention. The primary surgeon had no prior experience with this specific rare lesion. The lesion was excised using electrocautery. Because the central incisor was encased in the mass, it was also removed, and the base was repaired with a Polyglactin 3/0 round body (RB) suture.

The gross specimen was approximately 5 × 5 cm, presenting as a soft, friable mass, white to pink in color, with ulceration on the surface. On the cut section, there was a solid grayish-white component within the mass. Histopathology of the mass showed solid nests and sheets of polygonal tumor cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm and round to ovoid nuclei, separated by thin vascular and collagenous septa, consistent with a CGCT (Fig. 3). The patient was managed in the neonatal ward, and breastfeeding was initiated on the second postoperative day (Fig. 1B). The patient was discharged on the third postoperative day. After a month of follow-up, the child is feeding well and doing well overall, with no signs of recurrence.

Discussion

CGCT, first described by Neumann in 1871, is a rare, benign soft-tissue growth that appears in newborns, almost always arising from the alveolar mucosa[1]. It is also known as a congenital epulis or Neumann’s tumor. These lesions are either sessile or pedunculated, may appear smooth or lobulated, are pink, and range in size from a few millimeters to several centimeters[5]. CGCT affects female infants 8–10 times more often than it does males, suggesting a possible hormonal influence on its development. The tumor is more commonly found in the maxilla than the mandible, particularly in the anterior region[5,6].

The defining novelty of this case is the extra-alveolar extension of the mass. Although the origin was within the gingival buccal sulcus, the significant extension, mimicking a primary lower lip tumor, is highly atypical. Most global case reports detail CGCTs protruding directly from the maxillary or mandibular ridges; lip involvement necessitates a careful differential diagnosis, including adult granular cell tumor, congenital hemangioma, melanotic neuroectodermal tumor of infancy (MNTI), embryonal rhabdomyosarcoma, and infantile myofibroma[7,8].

Because of these differentials, postoperative histopathological evaluation is the gold standard for diagnosis. The tumor consists of large, round cells with abundant eosinophilic granular cytoplasm. While immunohistochemistry (IHC) using markers such as S100 and CD68 is highly valuable for definitively ruling out differential diagnoses, a limitation of this case report is that IHC was not performed due to resource constraints at our center. The diagnosis was established based on the classic morphological appearance on H&E staining, which correlated strongly with the clinical presentation.

The prenatal detection of an oral mass, as occurred in this case, can be valuable, allowing parents and healthcare teams to prepare for the birth and manage the newborn appropriately[9]. Large tumors can obstruct the airway and interfere with early feeding. Surgical excision is the definitive treatment[10]. A review of the literature shows that the timing of surgery varies[11,12]. Still, early intervention (often within the first few days of life, as in our day-3 excision) generally yields excellent respiratory and feeding outcomes without increased surgical morbidity. The histopathological confirmation after removal helps reduce family anxiety and ensures that radical surgery is avoided.

Finally, the presence of a tumor protruding from a newborn’s mouth can be highly distressing for parents. In regions like Nepal, where superstition may influence perception, such congenital abnormalities can create significant social stigma and aesthetic concerns. Counseling the family on the benign nature of the lesion is as critical as the surgical intervention.

Another limitation of our study is the short follow-up duration of 1 month. CGCT is characteristically benign, and recurrence or malignant transformation has never been documented in the literature; however, long-term monitoring remains advisable to ensure continued normal oral development.

Conclusion

CGCT, though typically an alveolar lesion, can present atypically with extension to the lower lip in newborns. These masses interfere with feeding and respiration, making early, complete en bloc surgical excision the recommended treatment. While recurrences are rare and the chance of malignancy is exceptionally low, long-term follow-up remains necessary.
