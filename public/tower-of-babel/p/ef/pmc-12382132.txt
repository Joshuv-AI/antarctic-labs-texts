The gut-retina axis: association of dietary index for gut microbiota with diabetic retinopathy in diabetic patients—a cross-sectional study from NHANES 2009–2018
Authors: Yining Zeng, Mingxue Gao, Xiyue Tan, Hao Wu, Junjie Xiang, Aonan Liu, Jingqi Zhang, Yichen Yao, Tao Shen, Tao Zhang, Junguo Duan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe gut-retina axis, an emerging area of research, has uncovered the bidirectional link between the intestines and retina, offering new insights into ophthalmic disease management. Diabetic retinopathy (DR), a common diabetes complication with a digestive-related connection, lacks large-sample retrospective studies on the impact of gut microbiota-related diets. The association between the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) and DR requires further investigation.Methods1,285 diabetic patients from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) spanning the years 2009 to 2018 were analyzed. DI-GM, based on 14 foods or nutrients intake, served as the exposure variable. Associations were assessed via Spearman correlation, weighted logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis, and subgroup analyses.ResultsSpearman analysis revealed predominantly inverse correlations among DI-GM components. After adjusting for confounders, each 1-point increase in DI-GM was associated with a 12% lower DR risk (OR = 0.88, 95% CI 0.78–0.99, P = 0.039). The Q4 DI-GM exhibited a 67% reduced DR risk compared to Q1 (OR = 0.33, 95%CI 0.12–0.88, P = 0.028). RCS analysis identified a nonlinear dose-response relationship (P-nonlinearity < 0.01), with rapid risk reduction at DI-GM < 4 and diminishing returns thereafter. Subgroup analyses indicated that most subgroup associations showed no statistically significant differences (interaction P > 0.05), except for participants with long-standing diabetes, without hypertension and BMI < 25 (OR = 0.55, 95%CI: 0.33–0.93, P = 0.03).ConclusionsHigher DI-GM scores are linked to lower DR risk, supporting dietary optimization (e.g., increased whole grains/vegetables, reduced processed meat) as a potential strategy for DR prevention through gut microbiota modulation. These findings advance gut-retina axis theory and provide actionable dietary guidelines for diabetes complications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13098-025-01929-9.Keywords: Diabetic retinopathy, Dietary index for gut microbiota, Gut-Retina axis, Cross-sectional study, NHANES

Introduction

Diabetic Retinopathy (DR) is a common diabetes complication and a leading cause of blindness among the working-age population [1]. The International Diabetes Federation projected that the number of diabetes mellitus patients, reaching 463 million in 2019, will climb to 700 million by 2045. Due to the close connection between diabetes and DR, this sharp increase seriously threatens global visual health. In recent years, with the deepening of research on the gut microbiota, it has been found to be closely related to the development of various systemic diseases, including immune and metabolic disorders [2]. The connection between the gut microbiota and ophthalmic diseases has also gradually attracted attention [3], especially through the emerging concept of the “Gut-Retina Axis“ [4].

The gut-retina axis refers to the bidirectional regulatory mechanism between the gut and the retina through multiple pathways [5]. On one hand, dysbiosis of the gut microbiota can increase the permeability of the intestinal mucosal barrier, allowing gut microbes and their metabolites to enter the bloodstream and eventually deposit in the retinal tissue, triggering inflammatory responses that contribute to the development and progression of retinal diseases [6–8]. On the other hand, the retina and optic nerve, as part of the central nervous system, can indirectly affect gut function through the brain by sensing environmental information, thereby influencing the composition and function of the gut microbiota [9, 10]. This bidirectional regulatory mechanism provides new insights into the pathogenesis of ophthalmic diseases. The close connection between gut microbiota and DR has also been widely reported [11, 12].

Based on the aforementioned background, this study aims to investigate the relationship between the Dietary Index for Gut Microbiota (DI-GM) and DR. The DI-GM is a scoring system based on dietary intake that reflects the impact of diet on the gut microbiota. By analyzing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) from 2009 to 2018, this study seeks to elucidate the association between DI-GM and DR, providing scientific evidence for the prevention of DR through dietary interventions.

Materials and methods

Study population

Participants data for this study were derived from NHANES 2009–2018. Initially, 3,870 diabetic participants with DR diagnosis data were considered. Participants were excluded if they had missing data on the components of DI-GM (n = 445), education level (n = 44), PIR (n = 320), smoking and drinking status (n = 124), hypertension (n = 4), diabetes duration (n = 19), BMI (n = 52), or blood test indicators including red blood cell count (RBC), white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophils (NEUTS), hemoglobin (HGB), platelet count (PLT), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) (n = 1,577). Finally, 1,285 participants were included in the analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart

Exposure and outcome definitions

The exposure refers to the DI-GM score. Calculation of DI-GM was based on scoring system formulated by Kase et al. which contains 14 foods or nutrients [29]. The 24-hour dietary recall data from the 2009–2018 NHANES was utilized for the calculation of the DI-GM. For beneficial foods, if the intake reached or surpassed the sex-specific median, they were assigned a score of 1; otherwise, a score of 0 was given. Regarding unfavorable foods, when the intake met or exceeded the sex-specific median (or the fat-derived energy was above 40%), a score of 0 was assigned; otherwise, a score of 1 was applied. The overall DI-GM score ranged from 0 to 14, where beneficial foods could contribute 0–10 points and unfavorable foods 0–4 points. The components and scoring criteria of the DI-GM are presented in detail in Table S1.

The outcome was whether the participants had a history of DR. At present, the NHANES incorporates diabetes-related information into its questionnaire. Participants who responded “yes” to the DIQ010 question “Doctor told you have diabetes” were identified as having diabetes. Participants who responded “yes” to the DIQ080 question “Diabetes affected eyes/had retinopathy?” were identified as having a history of DR.

Covariates

Due to the complexity of the pathogenesis and risk factors of DR, as well as the correlations between the subjects’ daily dietary choices and their age, sex, race, income, daily living habits, underlying systemic diseases, and blood status, we incorporated covariates such as age, sex, race, education level, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, hypertension, diabetes duration, HbA1c, RBC, WBC, NEUTS, HGB, PLT, HDL-C, and LDL-C. This was to exclude the impacts of demographic characteristics, lifestyle habits, underlying diseases, and blood status on the correlation between DI-GM and DR, thereby enhancing the scientific validity of the research findings. Race was classified into Non-Hispanic White, Non-Hispanic Black, Mexican American, Other Hispanic, and Other Race. Education level was divided into less than 9th Grade, 9-11th Grade, high school grade, some college or AA degree, and college graduate or above. Smoking status was categorized into two groups (Yes: smoked more than 100 cigarettes in a lifetime; No: smoked less than 100 cigarettes in a lifetime). Drinking status was also classified into two groups (Yes: consumed alcohol more than 12 times in a year; No: consumed alcohol less than 12 times in a year). Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, a diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg, currently taking blood pressure-controlling medications, or self-reported doctor-diagnosed hypertension. All the above-mentioned information, including demographic data and questionnaire data, was collected through NHANES interviews. Blood test indicators were obtained by NHANES staff collecting specimens, which were then analyzed at the NHANES mobile examination center (MEC) and Collaborative Laboratory Services, Ottumwa, Iowa.

Statistical analysis

Complex sampling dietary weights (WTDRD1*1/5) from NHANES were applied to obtain reliable national estimates. The weighted baseline characteristics of the participants were statistically presented and displayed in two groups based on the status of DR. Participants’ characteristics were presented as median (IQR) for continuous variables and frequencies (weighted percentages) for categorical variables. The design-based Kruskal Wallis test was used to determine whether there were significant differences in continuous variables between with DR group and without group. Pearson’s X² (Rao & Scott adjustment) was employed to assess the differences in the percentages of categorical variables. The Spearman correlation model was utilized to explore the potential correlations among the intakes of 14 DI-GM components. A weighted multivariate Logistic regression model was adopted to analyze the association between DI-GM and DR. Model 1 was an unadjusted model. Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, race, education level, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, and BMI. Model 3, based on Model 2, was further adjusted for hypertension, diabetes duration, HbA1c, RBC, WBC, NEUTS, HGB, PLT, HDL-C, and LDL-C. Weighted restricted cubic spline (RCS) was used to analyze the dose-response relationship between DI-GM and DR. Subgroup analyses and interaction analyses were conducted to explore the different characteristics and interactions of the relationship between DI-GM and DR among different subgroups. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.1), and a two-sided P-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Weighted baseline characteristics of participants

The weighted baseline characteristics of participants were presented by DR status (Table 1). In total, 1285 participants were analyzed, among which 603 (49%) were female and 241 (17%) had DR. The age median was 61.00 years, the PIR had a median (IQR) of 2.49 (1.33, 4.62), and the DI-GM score had a median (IQR) of 4.00 (3.00, 6.00). There were no significant differences in age, sex, race, and education level between with DR and without DR group (P > 0.05), while significant differences were observed in DI-GM quantiles (P < 0.05). Moreover, those with DR typically had a longer diabetes duration, higher HbA1c levels, lower RBC and HGB levels, a lower DI-GM, and a lower beneficial DI-GM (P < 0.05).

Spearman correlations among 14 DI-GM components

Spearman correlation analysis showed various degrees of correlations among the 14 DI-GM components (Fig. 2). Overall, negative correlations were significantly more common than positive correlations among the intakes of the 14 DI-GM components. Except for the significant positive correlations between processed meat and red meat, between fat and fermented dairy, and between fat and refined grains, there was a lack of significant correlations among the remaining components. This indicates a close connection in the intake behaviors of these components, suggesting a characteristic of concurrent intake.

Spearman correlation analysis of intakes of 14 DI-GM components1

Logistic regression analysis between DI-GM and DR

After weighted multivariate logistic regression analysis, in the case of no adjustment for confounding covariates (Model 1), there was a negative association between DI-GM and DR (OR = 0.87, 95%CI: 0.79–0.95, P = 0.002). When analyzed by DI-GM quartiles, the risk of DR was significantly lower in the Q4 group (in Model 1, OR for Q4 group = 0.28, 95%CI: 0.13–0.61, P = 0.002). After adjusting for age, sex, race, education level, and all other confounding factors (Model 2 and Model 3), the negative association between DI-GM and DR remained significant (OR = 0.88, 95%CI: 0.78–0.99, P = 0.039), and the reduced risk of DR in the DI-GM Q4 group was still evident (in Model 3, OR for Q4 group = 0.33, 95%CI: 0.12–0.88, P = 0.028) (Table 2).

Dose-response analysis of DI-GM with DR

The dose-response relationship between DI-GM and DR risk was evaluated via a multivariable-adjusted RCS analysis (Fig. 3). Given that the P-value for nonlinearity was below 0.01 (P for nonlinearity < 0.01), the nonlinear association between DI-GM and DR risk was statistically significant. Moreover, with the threshold inflection point at 4, when the DI-GM score rises from a low level to 4, the DR risk drops sharply. Yet, when the score exceeds 4, the decline in DR risk slows, showing a diminishing-returns effect (P for overall < 0.01). Clinically, this finding offers crucial guidance. Clinicians can take a DI-GM score of 4 as a key threshold in managing diabetic patients. For those with a score below 4, adjusting the diet (increasing intake of beneficial foods like whole grains, veggies, and fruits while reducing processed meats and high-fat foods) to raise the DI-GM score can significantly lower DR risk. For patients with a score at or above 4, while further raising the score may still help, other factors like lifestyle changes (exercise, quitting smoking, limiting alcohol) and controlling blood glucose and pressure should be considered for better DR prevention.

Subgroup analyses

The association between DI-GM and DR varied across different subgroups defined by age, sex, race, education level, PIR, BMI, smoking and drinking status, hypertension, and HbA1c (Fig. 4). However, the P-value for interaction in most subgroups were not statistically significant (P for interaction > 0.05), indicating a relatively consistent overall association trend.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses among high-risk populations

To explore the different manifestations of the association between DI-GM and DR in high-risk populations (long-term diabetes, hypertension, and obesity), subgroup analyses on participants with diabetes duration ≥ 5 years, hypertension, and BMI ≥ 25 were conducted(Fig. 5). In high-risk populations, most subgroups did not show significant differences, and the interaction was also not significant (P for interaction > 0.05). However, in the long-term diabetic population, among those without hypertension and with a BMI < 25, there was a significant negative association between the DI-GM score and DR (OR = 0.55, 95%CI: 0.33–0.93, P = 0.03). This may suggest that good blood pressure control and weight management are beneficial for the gut microbiota to exert a greater preventive effect against DR.

Discussion

Through an in-depth analysis of the data from NHANES from 2009 to 2018, this study found that DI-GM was negatively associated with DR. This result is consistent with some previous research findings, further confirming the potential importance of gut microbiota health in the prevention and management of DR. In addition, by using Spearman correlation analysis, weighted restricted cubic spline analysis, subgroup analysis, and interaction analysis, this study further discovered and verified the partial positive correlation among the 14 components of DI-GM, the non-linear relationship between DI-GM and DR, as well as the differential manifestations of DI-GM in different subgroups.

At present, extensive evidence suggests a close link between gut microbiota and various retinal degenerative diseases, such as age-related macular degeneration(AMD), DR, and glaucoma [13–15]. Previous studies have shown that the gut microbiota influences the progression of DR through the microbiota-gut-brain-retina interactions by various mechanisms: an increase in the permeability and disruption of the function of the intestinal barrier, the activation of inflammatory factors, the breakdown of blood-retinal barrier (BRB), the activation of the stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway, and the impact of gut microbiota metabolites. Das et al. demonstrated that, compared with healthy individuals and patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), patients with DR had a reduction in anti-inflammatory bacteria, probiotics, and potential pathogens [16]. Changes in the gut microbiome can trigger pathological inflammation and accelerate the progression of DR [5]. Research revealed that alterations in the gut microbiota of diabetic mice are directly associated with DR characteristics such as retinal microglial activation, acellular capillary formation, and peripheral immune cell infiltration, suggesting the role of dysbiosis in the pathology of DR [15]. Metabolic and genetic risk factors induce gut dysbiosis and barrier disruption, promoting systemic endotoxemia (LPS/AGEs) that activates TLR/RAGE receptors and NLRP3 inflammasome-driven pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, TNF-α). This cascade triggers microglial/astrocyte activation, adhesion molecule (e.g., ICAM-1, VCAM-1) upregulation, and BRB breakdown, ultimately driving retinal inflammation, apoptosis, and neovascularization via immunosenescence and inflammaging pathways [17]. Intestinal dysbiosis leads to an increase in intestinal permeability and chronic low-grade inflammation, which is characterized by the increased production of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and VEGF-A, ultimately exacerbating pathological angiogenesis [18]. The gut microbiota may participate in the gut-retina interaction through the disrupted BRB [19]. Aberrant regulation of the STING pathway is crucial in inflammatory diseases [20]. In DR patients, gut dysbiosis can disrupt intestinal balance and barrier function via mis-accumulated STING. This allows microbial products to enter the blood, reach the retina through the damaged BRB, and chronically activate the retinal STING pathway, accelerating DR progression [21]. The metabolites of the gut microbiota can act on the brain through the bloodstream or neural pathways, thereby affecting the function and survival of retinal ganglion cells [22]. Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), including acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are small-molecule organic metabolites produced by the fermentation of dietary fiber and resistant starch. Studies have confirmed that SCFAs can play a positive role in DR through anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidative stress, regulation of glycolipid metabolism, and insulin resistance. For example, they can improve disorders of glucose and lipid metabolism as well as insulin resistance by regulating the endocrine system [22, 23]. Acting as signaling molecules, SCFAs activate downstream G protein-coupled receptors (GPRs) (e.g., GPR41, GPR43, GPR109a, and Olfactory receptor 78) expressed on specific cell surfaces, thereby participating in energy metabolism and blood pressure regulation [24]. Furthermore, SCFAs decrease insulin resistance by suppressing endotoxin-associated inflammation and safeguarding the intestinal mucosal barrier [25]. Lipopolysaccharide (LPS), from Gram-negative bacteria, is elevated in T2DM patients’ intestines and is key to microbial translocation [26, 27]. LPS can activate the MyD88-NF-κB pathway via the TLR4/MD-2 complex, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., TNF-α, IL-6), and impairs insulin signaling through JNK-dependent IRS-1 phosphorylation, thereby driving systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and the progression of diabetes [28, 29]. Thus, LPS can disrupt the intestinal barrier, activates inflammatory complexes, damages vascular endothelial cells, disturbs vascular function, and worsens DR [30–32].

In summary, there are close associations and intricate interaction mechanisms among the gut microbiota, diabetes and DR. However, given the limited influence of genetic factors on the composition of the host gut microbiota, diet becomes an effective means for individuals to improve their gut microbiota [5]. Research has confirmed that different dietary habits, such as high-fat diets and high-fiber diets, are associated with the abundances of different gut bacterial genera [33]. Diet al.so affects the production of gut microbiota metabolites such as SCFAs, LPS, and bile acids [34]. For example, research has shown that intermittent fasting can mediate alterations in the gut microbiota, restructuring it into species capable of producing neuroprotective tauroursodeoxycholate, and that this process achieves retinal protection and prevents DR by regulating the activation of its receptor, TGR5 [15]. Moreover, appropriate dietary adjustments and fecal microbiota transplantation can maintain intestinal homeostasis and improve the condition in patients with DM and DR [35]. Due to the great potential of high-fiber and anti-inflammatory foods in disease prevention, fiber-rich and anti-inflammatory foods that promote beneficial gut bacteria are good choices for dietary adjustments. For example, adding one avocado to participants’ daily diet led to an increase in the relative abundance of Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, which is associated with diabetes [36, 37]. Long-term intake of whole broccoli in mice reduced inflammation, enhanced lipid parameters and insulin sensitivity, and modified the gut microbiome [38]. α-Galactooligosaccharides from chickpeas significantly increased the numbers of Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus in the intestines of mice with metabolic syndrome induced by a high-fat diet [39]. Cranberry polyphenols can reshape gut microbiota, regulate mucosal markers for epithelial barrier repair, ease obesity-related gut dysbiosis and metabolic inflammation, and improve glucose balance [40]. Green tea has the potential to improve glucose metabolism by decreasing the abundance of P. vulgatus, which is related to higher blood glucose levels [41]. High consumption of processed red meat is linked to reduced microbial alpha-diversity. What’s more, microbes positively associated with it increase levels of creatine, carnitine metabolites, fasting insulin, blood glucose, C-reactive protein, apolipoprotein A1, triglycerides and blood pressure. This implies processed red meat may harm blood glucose and inflammation control via influencing microbial species and their metabolic pathways [42]. Therefore, modulating the composition and function of the gut microbiota through optimizing the dietary structure may represent a potential novel approach for the prevention and treatment of diabetes and DR. Thus, for individuals looking to optimize their gut microbiota for better health, especially those with diabetes or at risk of DR, it is recommended to incorporate a variety of these beneficial foods into their diet. To be specific, this includes regularly consuming avocados, adding broccoli to meals, using chickpeas as a protein source, enjoying cranberries in various forms like juices or dried snacks, drinking green tea daily and taking enough fiber-rich food. Additionally, it is advisable to moderately reduce the intake of processed red meat and fat. By making these dietary adjustments, people can potentially improve gut health and decrease the risk of diabetes and its complications such as DR. In addition to dietary adjustments, microbiota-targeted therapies (probiotics, prebiotics, and synbiotics) can regulate blood lipid levels, reduce fasting insulin, insulin resistance, and HbA1c levels, demonstrating great potential for blood glucose and lipid control. Thus, they are also means available for gut microbiota modulation [43–45].

In addition, although this study mainly focuses on DR, it is worth noting that DR, AMD, and glaucoma share a common pathological change, namely neurodegeneration. Moreover, multiple studies have confirmed that gut microbiota dysbiosis is also closely related to the development of AMD and glaucoma. LPS can enter the bloodstream through the damaged intestinal barrier and thus participate in the early pathophysiological process of AMD [46]. Chen et al. demonstrated that the commensal microbiota can pre-sensitize T cells. When the intraocular pressure (IOP) increases transiently, the pre-sensitized T cells will infiltrate into the retina. Even if the IOP returns to normal, this T-cell infiltration can still lead to the continuous degeneration of retinal ganglion cells [47]. Therefore, the findings of the relationship between DI-GM and DR in this study may also provide some references for the research and prevention of AMD and glaucoma. Further research is needed to verify this hypothesis in the future.

Strengths and limitations

This study, leveraging NHANES data, delved deep into the association between DI-GM and DR and achieved valuable outcomes. It boasts several strengths. The NHANES database, with its complex multi-stage stratified probability sampling, encompasses a wide range of individuals, ensuring the representativeness of the sample. This makes the research results highly generalizable across different populations. In data analysis, numerous confounding factors were taken into account, including age, gender, race, education, lifestyle, underlying diseases, and blood test indicators. The use of the weighted multivariate Logistic regression model, combined with methods like Spearman correlation and RCS analysis, effectively controlled these factors and provided a comprehensive exploration of the relationship between DI-GM and DR, revealing their complex associations.

Nonetheless, the study has its drawbacks. The cross-sectional design restricts the determination of a causal relationship between DI-GM and DR. We can’t tell if a high DI-GM reduces DR risk or if DR patients’ diets influence DI-GM. The reliance on 24-hour dietary recalls is subject to recall bias and dietary fluctuations, which may skew the DI-GM score, even with sampling weight adjustments. Additionally, although this study adjusted for multiple confounding factors, there are still potential confounders that may affect the results. Lifestyle factors like diet and exercise significantly impact both the gut microbiota and DR. Besides the dietary factors in DI-GM, other dietary behaviors such as excessive intake of high-sugar and high-fat foods can cause gut microbiota dysbiosis, exacerbate inflammation, and increase DR risk [48, 49]. Lack of exercise can also indirectly affect the gut microbiota and retinopathy development by influencing metabolic function [50, 51]. Moreover, the NHANES-based DR diagnosis via questionnaires lacks objective examination and classification, which may lead to inaccurate diagnoses and affect the research accuracy.

Conclusion

Our study suggests that a higher DI-GM is associated with a lower risk of DR. This indicates that DI-GM might be a potential dietary factor related to DR prevention. According to the gut-retina axis theory, optimizing the dietary structure of diabetic patients could potentially be a new approach to prevent DR. Notably, Clinicians should consider DI-GM thresholds (< 4 vs. ≥4) when guiding dietary modifications, particularly in high-risk populations. However, due to the study’s limitations, future prospective cohort studies are needed to track the long-term impact of dietary interventions on DR risk and clarify the causal relationship. Advanced dietary assessment techniques like biomarker detection can be used to accurately evaluate diet and gut microbiota. Researching dietary component interactions and their relationships with other factors will help understand diet’s role in DR, providing a solid basis for personalized prevention and treatment.
