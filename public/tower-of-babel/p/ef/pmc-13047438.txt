Impact of Health Insurance Type on Access to Biologics and Systemic Corticosteroid Exposure in Patients With Severe Asthma: A Real-World Study From the Korean Severe Asthma Registry (KoSAR)
Authors: Kyoung-Hee Sohn, Sun-Kyung Lee, Hwa Young Lee, So Ri Kim, Seung Eun Lee, Joo-Hee Kim, Woo-Jung Song, Sang-Heon Kim, On behalf of the Korean Severe Asthma Registry (KoSAR) Investigators
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe impact of socioeconomic status on asthma control has been widely investigated. However, evidence regarding the impact of health insurance type on biologic utilization and systemic corticosteroid (SCS) exposure among patients with severe asthma remains limited. This study aimed to evaluate the associations between health insurance type, biologic utilization, and SCS dose in South Korea.MethodsData from the Korean Severe Asthma Registry (KoSAR) between March 2022 and October 2023 were analyzed. Patients were categorized into 4 different groups based on their health insurance type: National Health Insurance (NHI) with private health insurance (PHI), NHI-only, and Medical Aid (MA) with PHI, and MA-only.ResultsAmong the 578 patients with severe asthma, 327 (56.6%) were classified as NHI+PHI, 192 (33.2%) as NHI-only, 22 (3.8%) as MA+PHI, and 37 (6.4%) as MA-only. Decline in lung function, asthma exacerbations, and impaired quality of life were significantly more prevalent in the MA+PHI and MA-only groups. Overall, 159 patients (27.5%) were identified as biologic users. The most frequently prescribed biologics were dupilumab (8.8%), reslizumab (8.5%), omalizumab (7.1%), followed by mepolizumab (2.3%). Biologic utilization varied significantly by insurance type, with rates of 30.9% in the NHI+PHI group, 27.1% in the NHI-only group, and only 8.1% in the MA-only group (P = 0.011). Conversely, dependence on SCS (defined as SCS use for more than one month) showed an inverse pattern, being most prevalent in the MA-only group (52.6%), followed by the NHI-only group (36.8%) and the NHI+PHI group (26.7%) (P = 0.017).ConclusionsOur study showed that differences in biologic and SCS use were associated with insurance type. These findings suggest that insurance coverage and out-of-pocket expenses are important factors influencing access to optimal care for severe asthma, emphasizing the need for policy interventions to promote health equity.

INTRODUCTION

In the current era of precision medicine for asthma, biologics have demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing asthma exacerbations, systemic corticosteroid (SCS) use, and lung function decline.123 Before the era of biologics, severe asthma was associated with up to 5 times higher direct healthcare costs compared with non-severe asthma.456 Although biologics have transformed the treatment of severe asthma, most research has focused on biological and clinical endpoints such as biomarkers and therapeutic efficacy. However, the social barriers to accessing biologic treatments remain poorly characterized.

Recent meta-analyses indicate that lower socioeconomic status (SES) is associated with higher secondary healthcare utilization, such as emergency department visits and hospital admissions, but not with increased rates of asthma exacerbations or mortality.7 These disparities suggest a potential therapeutic inequity between patients of lower and higher SES, which may be driven by multiple factors. A key contributing factor is the financial constraint, which renders high-cost biologics less accessible to those with low income. Given that insurance status is a known determinant of severe asthma control in South Korea,8 we hypothesized that insurance type exerts a significant influence on biologic use and SCS exposure among these vulnerable populations.

South Korea maintains a mandatory National Health Insurance (NHI) system, which has provided universal coverage since 1989. Under this system, all individuals accessing healthcare services, whether through public or private providers, are required to be enrolled in the NHI. Although South Korea has implemented universal public health insurance, 78.6% of the population additionally enrolls in private health insurance (PHI) to supplement out-of-pocket expenses not reimbursed by the national scheme.9 While the majority of the population is covered by NHI and/or PHI, low-income individuals are supported through the Medical Aid (MA) program, which was used in this study as a proxy for limited insurance coverage.10 Although MA beneficiaries are entitled to minimal copayments of up to 15% or no copayments for standard medical services, they often face significant system-level barriers and relatively higher financial challenges when accessing advanced, non-reimbursed therapies such as biologics.

Five monoclonal antibodies—omalizumab, mepolizumab, benralizumab, reslizumab, and dupilumab—have been approved for the treatment of severe asthma in Korea.1112 Despite the substantial annual costs of biologics, which can exceed 10,000,000 KRW per year (approximately 7,000 USD), potential disparities in biologics utilization across different insurance types, including NHI, PHI, and the MA program, have not yet been investigated. In the United States, biologic use has been reported to be more prevalent among privately insured patients, individuals with higher income, and those with better access to specialist care1314; however, the impact of Korea’s unique insurance system and strict reimbursement regulations on biologic use has not been investigated. We hypothesized that insurance type is associated with differences in biologics utilization and reliance on SCS. To test this hypothesis, we compared asthma control status, biologic use, and SCS exposure patterns across insurance types, including NHI, PHI, and MA, using data from a nationwide registry cohort.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study population

Patients data were obtained from the Korean Severe Asthma Registry (KoSAR), a nationwide, multicenter, prospective observational cohort, between March 2022 and October 2023. The KoSAR is an ongoing, nationwide prospective cohort study established in 2011 to assess the clinical characteristics and long-term outcomes of Korean patients with severe asthma.15 Although KoSAR was established in 2011, Phase 2 of the registry (KoSAR-2) was launched in 2022 to prospectively evaluate longitudinal outcomes in patients with severe asthma.8 This study analyzed data from KoSAR-2 participants who were diagnosed with severe asthma. Patients aged > 18 years were diagnosed with severe asthma and met any of the following criteria were included: 1) failure to consistently achieve a well-controlled status despite the use of medium- to high-dose inhaled corticosteroids (ICSs); or 2) well-controlled asthma after medium- to high-dose ICS use but with a history of more than one unscheduled urgent visit, ≥ 3 administrations of SCSs in the previous year, a near-fatal asthma attack, or worsening symptoms when oral corticosteroids (OCSs) or ICS were reduced to 25% at any point. For this analysis, 610 patients were enrolled, and 578 were included in the final analysis. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of each hospital, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants (IRB number: HYUH 2022-03-038).

Data collection and reimbursement regulation for biologics

Baseline demographic and clinical information, including age, sex, smoking status, body mass index, comorbid conditions documented in medical records, and emergency department visits for asthma exacerbations during the preceding year, was obtained using structured interviews and self-administered questionnaires and recorded in an electronic case report form.

Laboratory assessments included peripheral blood, sputum eosinophil counts, and skin prick test (SPT), as well as measurements of fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO). Pulmonary function testing was conducted using standardized spirometry equipment in accordance with current clinical guidelines.16

Health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and asthma control were evaluated at baseline using validated patient-reported outcome measures, including the Asthma Control Test (ACT), Severe Asthma Questionnaire (SAQ), and EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level instrument (EQ-5D-5L).17 For these instruments, higher scores represent improved clinical status.

Asthma-related medication use, including ICS, long-acting β2-agonists (LABAs), biologics and SCS, was assessed using prescription records from the previous 6 months. SCS dependence was defined as the continuous administration of OCS, either daily or every other day, for at least one month within the 6-month period preceding enrollment.

In South Korea, the MA program provides publicly funded health coverage for low-income individuals, functioning alongside the NHI system. Eligibility is generally determined by low household income (typically ≤ 40% of the standard median) and limited financial support capacity, with reduced copayments for covered services.18 PHI operates in a complementary relationship with the public health insurance system, providing supplemental coverage for approximately 30% of medical services that are not reimbursed by public insurance.19 Patients were classified into four groups (NHI+PHI, NHI-only, MA+PHI, and MA-only) according to insurance type and the expected level of financial burden (Fig. 1).

Since July 2020, omalizumab has been reimbursed under the Korean NHI scheme for patients with severe asthma, with a patient co-payment ranging from 30% to 60%. In 2023, the monthly out-of-pocket cost per vial ranged from approximately KRW 150,000 to 270,000, depending on insurance coverage and eligibility for manufacturer-sponsored co-pay assistance, although these costs vary over time with changes in drug pricing and reimbursement policies. Reslizumab and mepolizumab were listed for insurance reimbursement in November 2023, and benralizumab in July 2024. Patients enrolled before October 2023 were required to pay out-of-pocket for anti-IL-5 agents. However, even after reimbursement, the out-of-pocket costs for reslizumab, mepolizumab, and benralizumab remain approximately KRW 500,000–850,000 per month. In January 2026, dupilumab was approved for reimbursement for severe asthma.

Statistical analyses

Baseline demographic characteristics, clinical parameters, laboratory findings, HRQoL indices, and treatment patterns were compared according to insurance type: PHI, NHI, and MA. Insurance type was treated as an ordinal variable reflecting decreasing insurance coverage and increasing socioeconomic vulnerability.

Continuous variables were summarized using descriptive statistics, and categorical variables were presented as counts and percentages. The normality of continuous variables was assessed using graphical methods. Because several variables showed non-normal distributions and the MA group had a relatively small sample size, statistical inference was based on nonparametric methods.

To evaluate monotonic trends across ordered insurance groups, the Jonckheere-Terpstra test was used for continuous variables. This test assesses ordered differences across group distributions without assuming normality and is appropriate when a priori ordering of groups is hypothesized. For binary outcomes, including biologic utilization and SCS dependence, the Cochran-Armitage trend test was applied to assess linear trends in proportions across insurance categories.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, and a P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Given the exploratory nature of this registry-based study, adjustments for multiple comparisons were not applied; instead, emphasis was placed on the consistency and direction of observed trends across clinically relevant outcomes. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Study population and baseline characteristics

Among the 610 patients enrolled in the KoSAR registry, 578 were included in the final analysis after excluding 4 patients due to missing Global Initiative for Asthma treatment step information (n = 3), missing clinical data (n = 1), or enrollment outside the study period (n = 28). The study population comprised four groups: NHI+PHI (n = 327, 56.6%), NHI-only (n = 192, 33.2%), MA+PHI (n = 22, 3.8%), and MA-only (n = 37, 6.4%). Patients in the NHI-only and MA-only groups were significantly older than those in the NHI+PHI and MA+PHI groups (64.2 and 65.3 vs. 54.0 and 53.2 years, respectively; P < 0.001; Table 1). Asthma duration was longest in the MA-only group (19.7 years), followed by the NHI-only (17.2 years), MA+PHI (15.3 years) and NHI+PHI (11.7 years) groups (P < 0.001). There were no statistically significant differences in healthcare utilization among the 4 groups; however, emergency department visits and hospitalization were most frequent in the MA-only group. In addition, the MA-only group showed a higher prevalence of comorbidities, including hypertension, arrhythmia, osteoporosis, and depression.

Clinical parameters and HRQoL

The laboratory findings and HRQoL according to insurance types are summarized in Table 2. Lung function parameters, including forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1) and the FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio, differed significantly across the 4 insurance groups, with significantly lower values observed in the MA+PHI and MA-only groups compared with the NHI+PHI and NHI-only groups (P < 0.001). However, markers of type 2 inflammation, such as blood eosinophil counts, FeNO, SPT positivity and total Immunoglobulin E (IgE) level, did not differ significantly between the 4 groups. ACT scores were highest in the NHI+PHI group (18.9 ± 4.9), followed by the NHI-only (18.7 ± 4.6), MA-only (15.6 ± 4.3), and MA+PHI groups (14.9 ± 4.6, P = 0.006). SAQ scores were highest in the NHI+PHI group, followed by the NHI-only, MA-only, and MA+PHI groups (P = 0.025).

Biologic utilization and SCS dependence

Significant disparities were observed in the utilization of biologic therapies and reliance on SCS based on insurance type (Fig. 2, Supplementary Table S1). Biologic utilization differed significantly across the 4 groups. Biologic utilization was highest in the NHI+PHI group (30.9%), followed by the NHI-only (27.1%), MA+PHI (13.6%), and MA-only (8.1%) groups (P = 0.047) (Fig. 2A). The most frequently prescribed biologics were dupilumab (8.8%), reslizumab (8.5%), omalizumab (7.1%), followed by mepolizumab (2.3%). Among the 41 patients who received omalizumab, 11 (26.8%) were covered by NHI reimbursement, whereas the remaining 30 patients (73.2%) accessed omalizumab through PHI support or direct out-of-pocket payment. Overall, 32.3% of patients were identified as having SCS dependence, exhibiting an inverse pattern across insurance groups (Fig. 2B). Since PHI coverage may mitigate financial burden among MA beneficiaries, a subgroup analysis of SCS use and biologics utilization was conducted in the NHI+PHI, NHI-only, and MA-only groups (Table 3). Biologic use was lowest in the MA-only group (8.1%), compared to the NHI-only (27.1%) and NHI+PHI (30.9%) groups (P = 0.011) (Fig. 3A). Conversely, SCS dependence was highest in the MA-only group (52.6%), followed by the NHI-only (36.8%) and NHI+PHI (26.7%) groups (P = 0.017) (Fig. 3B). Consistently, the cumulative SCS dose over 6 months was significantly higher among MA-only (702.1 ± 623.0 mg) than among patients covered by NHI-only group (512.5 ± 314.2 mg) or NHI+PHI group (416.4 ± 420.0 mg; P = 0.013) (Fig. 3C). Meanwhile, no statistically significant differences were observed with respect to biologic use or SCS dependence between the NHI+PHI and NHI-only groups. Prescription rates of other maintenance therapies, including ICS/LABA/long acting muscarinic antagonist triple therapy and leukotriene receptor antagonist, did not differ significantly across the 3 groups. DISCUSSION

This study suggests a discrepancy between similar biological profiles and differing treatment patterns associated with insurance type. Notably, markers of type 2 inflammation, including blood eosinophil counts, FeNO, SPT, and total IgE level, were comparable across insurance groups, whereas the real-world administration of monoclonal antibodies differed significantly. This discrepancy suggests that the observed treatment gap is unlikely to be explained by underlying differences in asthma phenotypes or biological characteristics; instead, it may reflect structural and financial barriers within the current Korean healthcare system. The inverse association between biologic usage and SCS dependence suggests that insurance-related barriers may be associated with poorer clinical outcomes and increased steroid-related morbidity in vulnerable populations.

This phenomenon highlights the presence of “financial toxicity” within the Korean healthcare system, suggesting that public insurance coverage alone may be insufficient to ensure equitable access to biologics for severe asthma. Our results mirror trends observed in the United States, where biologic uptake is more prevalent among privately insured individuals and those with higher SES.71420 In the United States, patients with private insurance had approximately 3-fold higher rates of biologic therapy compared with those covered by public insurance. Notably, no visits for biologic treatment were observed among uninsured patients.14 Our 4-group analysis revealed a graded pattern of biologic utilization across insurance types (MA-only 8.1% < MA+PHI 13.6% < NHI-only 27.1% < NHI+PHI 30.9%). The lower biologic utilization in the MA+PHI group than in the NHI-only group suggests that MA status may constitute an additional structural barrier to accessing advanced therapies, even when supplemental private insurance offsets out-of-pocket costs. This implies that reducing out-of-pocket costs alone may be insufficient to ensure equitable access, and that reimbursement criteria and administrative processes within public insurance frameworks should be addressed to mitigate these inequities. Although previous studies indicate that patients with private insurance often achieve better asthma control,2122 our findings indicate that access to biologic therapies is influenced by both insurance type and socioeconomic vulnerability. Therefore, reducing out-of-pocket costs alone may be insufficient to ensure equitable access; reimbursement criteria and administrative processes within public insurance systems may also require reform to reduce these disparities.

A key insight from this study is the dissociation between “same biology” and “different treatment” in severe asthma patients. Notably, this insurance-driven inequity appears more pronounced in severe asthma than in other chronic inflammatory conditions in South Korea. Previous research on Korean patients with rheumatoid arthritis showed no difference in biologic utilization between MA and NHI patients.23 Under the current Benefit Extension System for rare and severe diseases in the Republic of Korea, patients with rheumatoid arthritis who fail standard treatment are eligible for a reduced statutory copayment rate of 10%. In contrast, patients with severe asthma remain subject to a significantly higher copayment rate of 30%–60%, even when meeting stringent clinical reimbursement criteria. Given that both conditions are chronic inflammatory diseases requiring high-cost biologics, this discrepancy suggests that reimbursement policies for severe asthma biologics may be more restrictive and highlights the need for enhanced financial protection for patients with severe asthma.

The clinical consequences of limited access to biologic therapy are significant. Patients in the MA-only group exhibited the highest prevalence of SCS dependence (52.6%) and significantly greater cumulative steroid exposure compared with those covered by PHI or NHI. This disproportionate reliance on SCS among the most socioeconomically vulnerable group represents a major clinical concern regarding potentially avoidable adverse outcomes, given its well-established association with steroid-related morbidity and adverse long-term outcomes.242526 Additionally, the MA group demonstrated significantly poorer asthma control, accompanied by worse lung function (FEV1 and FVC), higher rates of emergency department visits and hospitalizations, and markedly impaired HRQoL, as reflected by lower SAQ and EQ-5D index scores.17 Collectively, these findings suggest that restricted access to biologics is associated with suboptimal disease control as well as with a more hospital-centered and potentially inefficient pattern of care.27 Our results provide real-world evidence that insurance-related underuse of biologics is closely linked to worse clinical outcomes and increased healthcare utilization in severe asthma.

A major strength of this study is the use of nationwide, real-world data from the KoSAR registry, which reflects the current clinical status of severe asthma management in Korea. However, the study has several limitations. First, because of observational design, causal relationships between insurance type and asthma control could not be definitively established. Second, although insurance type serves as a reliable indicator for SES, it may not fully capture all individual socioeconomic nuances such as educational level, health literacy, and household wealth. Additionally, the MA and MA+PHI groups had relatively small sample sizes (n = 37 and 22, respectively) compared to the NHI and NHI+PHI groups. Therefore, subgroup analyses of individual biologic agents should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Nevertheless, the consistent trend toward lower overall biologic utilization in the MA patients suggests clinically meaningful disparities in access, warranting further investigation in larger, population-based studies. Lastly, given that KoSAR is based on tertiary referral centers, MA patients may be under-represented or selectively referred, potentially leading to an overrepresentation of patients with more complex, severe asthma. This may limit the generalizability of our findings.

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that individual insurance coverage and out-of-pocket expenses remain fundamental obstacles to achieving health equity in severe asthma care. Such barriers, particularly for MA beneficiaries, are associated with reduced biologic use, increased SCS dependency, and poorer clinical outcomes. Ensuring equitable access to biologics is essential to bridge the treatment gap between biological potential and clinical outcomes in the era of precision medicine.
