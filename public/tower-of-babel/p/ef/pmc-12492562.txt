A case of ADH5/ALDH2 deficiency combined with 3q29 microduplication syndrome
Authors: Shu Chenzhuo, Fu Lingling, Wei Ang, Chen Hui, Wang Ruixin, Yang Bixi, Li Hongmin, Ma Jie, Wang Tianyou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA case of ADH5/ALDH2 deficiency combined with 3q29 microduplication syndrome in the Department of Hematology, Beijing Children’s hospital was retrospectively analyzed. The child, a 3-year-old girl, had a history of recurrent cutaneous petechias, and multiple episodes of pancytopenia. She was examined for café au lait spots, growth retardation, and microcephaly. Fanconi anemia (FA) was highly suspected according to the clinical manifestations and laboratory tests of the child, and was diagnosed as Amed syndrome combined with 3q29 microduplication syndrome according to the genetic results. Amed syndrome is an autosomal recessive multisystem disorder characterized by global developmental delay, impaired intellectual development, bone marrow failure and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) in childhood, and global dysplasia and short stature. The disease is caused by combination mutations of ADH5 and ALDH2 genes, which belongs to a special type of inherited bone marrow failure syndrome (IBMFS). Therefore, for the repeated pancytopenia in childhood, we should be alert to the possibility of congenital bone marrow failure syndrome, improve genetic testing as soon as possible, and carry out hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) when necessary. 3q29 microduplication syndrome is mainly due to the delayed development of neurological symptoms. Background

IBMFS is mainly characterized by ineffective hematopoiesis and high risk to progress hematological malignancy [1–3]. FA is the most common type, which is associated the dysfunction of DNA damage repairment and characterized by onset hidden [1, 2]. Many children do not seek medical care, until developing MDS and acute myeloid leukemia (AML) [1]. IBMFS contains a wide range of overlapping clinical features, and its pathogenesis includes a variety of gene variants affecting telomeres, hematopoietic transcription factors, ribosome assembly, DNA damage response, and immune response. Many genes are related to IBMFS, such as FANCA, FANCG, FANCC, etc. related to FA; DKC1, TINF2, etc. related to Dyskeratosis congenita (DC); GATA1, TSR2, etc. related to Diamond Blackfa anemia (DBA); SBDS, SRP54, EFL1, etc. related to Shwachman Diamond syndrome (SDS) [1, 3, 4]. What’s more, GATA2, CEBPA, RUNX1, SAMD9, SAMD9L and DDX41 are associated with familial susceptibility to hematological malignancy [5]. For newly diagnosed children with bone marrow failure, the first step is to distinguish between inherited and acquired diseases before giving treatment[6]. The main treatment methods are immunosuppressive therapy and HSCT [6]. IBMFS often has poor response to immunosuppressants, and HSCT is the only radical treatment method [6, 7]. Previous studies have found that most children with IBMFS are caused by single gene abnormalities. Recent studies have discovered a novel IBMFS caused by dual gene mutations, involving dual allele mutations in ADH5 and homozygous mutations in ALDH2 [8, 9]. This special syndrome, ADH5/ALDH2 deficiency (AMeD syndrome), has been reported in literature as a case of ADH5/ALDH2 mutation in adolescent aplastic anemia (AA) patients who progress to MDS or leukemia and require HSCT [8, 9]. Its hematological phenotype (i.e. AA and MDS/leukemia), short stature, or skin pigmentation indicate similarity to FA, also known as Fanconi anemia like IBMFS [8–11].

3q29 microduplication syndrome, a rare genetic disease, is characterized by neurodevelopmental phenotype, including psychomotor delay, mild to moderate cognitive impairment, convergent strabismus, generalized hypotonia with joint laxity, brisk symmetrical reflexes, generalized clumsiness, genu valgum gait, etc [12–15]. With the growing of children, they also suffer from oppositional, provocative conduct and isolation, feelings of self-depreciation and inadequacy, etc [13]. In addition, these patients show congenital abnormalities such as cleft palate, conductive hearing loss, microcephaly, congenital heart disease and metabolic disorders such as obesity [12, 13, 15, 16].

Our center reported a case of ADH5/ALDH2 deficiency combined with 3q29 microduplication syndrome. Next generation sequencing and Sanger sequencing play an important role in clinical diagnosis.

Case presentation

General information

A female patient, aged 3 years, 10 months, and 6 days old, was admitted to our hospital on June 20, 2023 for further diagnosis due to a 3-year pancytopenia. On June 30, 2020, she was hospitalized at a local hospital three years before admission due to “skin ecchymosis”. She was found pancytopenia and white blood cell count of 2.4 × 109/L, neutrophils 1.1 × 109/L, hemoglobin 103 g/L, platelets 51 × 109/L. She showed slight improvement in hemoglobin and platelets after administering 15 g intravenous immunoglobulin and taking leucogen orally, but still below normal values. She was hospitalized in a local hospital for more than 2 years and 2 months before admission due to “cutaneous petechias and thrombocytopenia”. Blood routine tests showed a minimum granulocytes count of 0.53 × 109/L, minimum hemoglobin 75 g/L, minimum platelet 3 × 109/L, blood EB virus IgM positive and EBV-DNA amplification positive, bone marrow aspiration “decreased bone marrow hyperplasia, decreased granulocytic and erythrocytic proliferation, no complete megakaryocyte cell line”, bone marrow biopsy shows “decreased bone marrow hematopoietic tissue hyperplasia, decreased granulocytic proliferation, active erythrocytic proliferation, inverted granulocytic red ratio, approximately 1–2 megakaryocyte cell proliferation, scattered/focal distribution of lymphocytes”, administration of immunoglobulin 1 g/kg×2 days later, 1U of red blood cell infusion was used to correct anemia. Subsequently, routine monitoring showed that the absolute values of white blood cells and neutrophils slightly recovered, but platelets and hemoglobin were still low. The child has intermittent cutaneous petechias and no nasal bleeding. A blood routine examination 10 days before admission showed decreased proliferation of the erythroid and megakaryocyte lineages. Hemostatic drugs were administered intravenously and platelets were infused before discharge.

Personal history: Her intellectual and physical development is slightly behind that of normal peers, and she can walk at the age of 18-months old.

Family history: No dysmorphia or dwarfism in parents.

Physical examination after admission: Height: 98 cm (between P10-P25), weight: 13.6 kg (< P3), head circumference 46 cm, microcephaly (between − 1SD and − 2SD), mild anemia appearance, no white spots on the whole body skin, visible scattered bleeding points on the skin of the size of the needle tip, without fading and protruding the skin surface, ecchymosis can be seen on the right face, scattered café au lait spots can be seen on the whole body skin, without reticular pigmentation. The superficial lymph nodes of the whole body were not palpable and enlarged, the lips were slightly pale, the oral mucosa was free of bleeding points and white spots, the thorax was normal, the physiological curvature of the spine was present, there was no deformity, the nails of the limbs were well formed, and the rest of the physical examination showed no abnormalities.

Final diagnosis

The patient underwent a peripheral blood genetic examination and was validated by Sanger by MyGenostics. The child has a compound heterozygous ADH5 mutation (Table 2). Further analysis of the gene revealed a homozygous mutation in ALDH2 Gene (c.1510G > A, P.E504K) (Table 3). There was a suspected chimeric duplication of approximately 65.85 Mb in the long arm 3q22.1q29 of chromosome 3.

Treatment

On the fourth day of admission, the hemoglobin and platelets of the children were significantly decreased, and 1U of platelets and 1U of suspended red blood cells were injected to correct anemia. On the 6th day of admission, the patient’s temperature was normal, general condition was fair, the patient’s condition improved, and was allowed to be discharged.

Discussion and conclusions

IBMFS are genetic disorders, underscoring the critical role of family genetic counseling [1]. Next-generation sequencing (NGS), particularly whole-exome sequencing (WES), plays a pivotal role in diagnosing IBMFS by enabling simultaneous detection of all associated genes [3]. In this case, the patient presented with pancytopenia, microcephaly, low body weight, and café-au-lait spots, prompting an initial suspicion of IBMFS, notably Fanconi anemia (FA).

The diagnostic gold standard for FA involves assessing cellular hypersensitivity to DNA interstrand cross-linking agents, such as mitomycin C (MMC) and diepoxybutane (DEB) [1, 2, 19]. However, false-negative results may occur in patients with hematopoietic reversion or somatic chimerism, necessitating the use of primary skin fibroblasts for reliable testing [1, 2]. SCGE further aids in differentiating FA from acquired aplastic anemia (AAA). Ji K et al. [20] demonstrated significant differences in comet assay parameters—tail DNA percentage (TDNA%), tail moment (TM), and Olive tail moment (OTM)—between FA and AAA patients (P < 0.01). While MMC testing, telomere length analysis, and SCGE yielded negative results in this patient, NGS identified compound heterozygous mutations in ADH5 and ALDH2, alongside a 3q29 microduplication.

ADH5 and ALDH2 encode formaldehyde dehydrogenase and acetaldehyde dehydrogenase, respectively, which detoxify formaldehyde into less toxic formate [8, 10]. Formaldehyde induces genomic damage by impeding replication fork progression via DNA interstrand cross-links (ICLs) and non-enzymatic DNA-protein cross-links (DPCs) [8, 9]. Although humans possess two formaldehyde repair mechanisms [8], homozygous or compound heterozygous ADH5 mutations combined with the ALDH2 rs671 polymorphism (Glu504Lys) result in insufficient aldehyde dehydrogenase activity, leading to endogenous formaldehyde accumulation that overwhelms DNA repair capacity [8, 9]. Endogenous formaldehyde arises from histone/nucleic acid demethylation and one-carbon metabolism in the bone marrow hematopoietic system [9]. These defects disrupt hematopoietic cell division and differentiation, causing IBMFS—specifically AMeD syndrome, characterized by aplastic anemia, intellectual disability, and dwarfism [8, 10]. Patients with homozygous ADH5 mutations (e.g., p.E504K; Table 3) exhibit severe manifestations, including neurological deficits, motor dysfunction, and early mortality [8].

The ICL repair pathway, mediated by FANC proteins, is central to FA pathogenesis [8]. Overload of this pathway mimics FA phenotypes (e.g., café-au-lait spots, short stature, microcephaly) and increases susceptibility to MDS and AML [8–10]. However, AMeD syndrome is distinguished by severe dwarfism (height and head circumference < −4.0 SD) and intellectual impairment, whereas FA typically presents with radial ray anomalies, chromosomal fragility, and solid tumors [8]. The absence of marked dwarfism in this patient complicated clinical differentiation between FA and AMeD, highlighting the necessity of comprehensive genetic testing in atypical cases.

Overlapping clinical features among IBMFS subtypes (e.g., FA, dyskeratosis congenita [DC], Shwachman-Diamond syndrome [SDS]) and AMeD syndrome—such as growth retardation and hematopoietic dysfunction—necessitate genetic testing for definitive diagnosis, particularly in cases lacking a family history [11]. While AMeD syndrome predominantly manifests as aplastic anemia, rare cases present with macrocytic anemia. Formaldehyde-induced folate metabolism disruption further necessitates differentiation from megaloblastic anemia, which lacks congenital malformations and hyperpigmentation [11].

The patient’s microcephaly and gross motor delay partially align with 3q29 microduplication syndrome, a rare disorder with marked phenotypic heterogeneity [12–14].

The patient subsequently developed MDS. Although guidelines for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (HSCT) timing in 3q29 microduplication syndrome with IBMFS are lacking, early HSCT—supported by evidence from FA and SDS studies—is recommended to improve prognosis [21–23]. Caution is warranted when using cytotoxic conditioning regimens due to the patient’s concurrent PPM1D mutation, which confers resistance to apoptosis via p53 inhibition and is linked to therapy-related MDS/AML [24, 25]. The homozygous ALDH2 c.1510G > A (p.E504K) variant further portends a poor prognosis [8], necessitating vigilant post-HSCT monitoring for neurological deficits, infections, and disease progression.

Infants with recurrent bleeding, café-au-lait spots, and pancytopenia should raise suspicion for IBMFS. Genetic testing is critical to distinguish IBMFS from AAA and to identify rare variants. Tailored management—including HSCT—should be guided by subtype-specific protocols, with lifelong surveillance for hematologic malignancies and complications.
