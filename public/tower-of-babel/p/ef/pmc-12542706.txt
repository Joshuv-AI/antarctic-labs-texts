Carotid endarterectomy versus stenting in patients with carotid artery stenosis and type 2 diabetes: a nationwide cohort study from the USA
Authors: Yuqing Fan, Nan Peng, Linfeng Jiang, Shuo Zhang, Mengyao Xue, Dongning Yao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesPatients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) are associated with a higher risk of carotid artery stenosis and adverse postoperative outcomes compared with individuals without T2DM. The comparative effectiveness after carotid endarterectomy (CEA) versus carotid artery stenting (CAS) in this population remains unclear. This study aimed to compare the in-hospital outcomes of CEA versus CAS in patients with carotid artery stenosis and T2DM.DesignA retrospective cohort study using a doubly robust estimation model which incorporated an inverse probability weighting (IPW) model, followed by logistic regression to balance baseline characteristics.SettingNation-wide administrative hospitalizations in the US National Inpatient Sample, 2016–2021.ParticipantsAdults (≥18 years) with T2DM who underwent either CEA or CAS for asymptomatic or symptomatic carotid stenosis.Main outcome measuresIn-hospital mortality, postoperative stroke and myocardial infarction during the same admission.ResultsA total of 31,363 patients with asymptomatic carotid stenosis (CAS, n=6338; CEA, n=25,025) and 3710 patients with symptomatic carotid stenosis (CAS, n=1271; CEA, n=2439) were evaluated. Among asymptomatic patients, the CEA group had a lower risk of mortality (108 (0.4%) vs 58 (0.9%); adjusted OR 0.997, 95% CI 0.995 to 0.999) but a higher incidence of myocardial infarction (500 (2.0%) vs 151 (2.4%); adjusted OR (aOR) 1.005, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.008) compared with the CAS group. No significant difference in the incidence of postoperative stroke was observed. Among symptomatic patients, the CEA group was also associated with a lower risk of mortality (29 (1.2%) vs 56 (4.4%); aOR 0.973, 95% CI 0.961 to 0.985), with no significant differences in postoperative stroke or myocardial infarction.ConclusionsCEA was associated with lower in-hospital mortality in type 2 diabetic patients with symptomatic or asymptomatic carotid stenosis compared with CAS, but its use in asymptomatic individuals should be carefully considered due to potential cardiovascular risks.

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Carotid endarterectomy (CEA) is recognized as an effective intervention for reducing stroke risk in carotid stenosis populations, and the advent of carotid artery stenting (CAS) introduced a less invasive alternative, especially for patients deemed high-risk for surgery. Type 2 diabetes mellitus exacerbates atherosclerosis and impairs vascular healing, which may increase the risk of postoperative complications in these patients. Despite the high prevalence and potentially postoperative risk of type 2 diabetes in carotid stenosis populations, no prior randomized trials have specifically compared CEA and CAS in diabetic patients. Furthermore, real-world evidence comparing postoperative outcomes, such as mortality, between these procedures remains limited.

Introduction

Stroke, the fifth leading cause of death and a major cause of disability in the USA, is attributed to extracranial internal carotid artery stenosis in approximately 8–15% of ischemic cases.1 Additionally, patients with stroke tend to be younger, especially among those with diabetes, which may be linked to diabetes-related cardiovascular complications.2 Type 2 diabetes is associated with a 1.5-fold to twofold increased risk of ischemic stroke,3 4 and it is expected that the benefit of stroke prevention measures in this population might be higher due to their elevated baseline risk. Carotid endarterectomy (CEA), a revascularization surgery, was used to prevent stroke in patients with carotid artery stenosis. According to the Clinical Practice Guidelines for the Management of External Cervical Cerebrovascular Disease (2022) issued by the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS),5 for patients with a high risk of CEA, such as a history of neck radiation therapy or a history of serious heart disease, who cannot receive anticoagulant therapy, carotid artery stenting (CAS) may be a preferred treatment.

CAS has been increasing in use in the USA since it was introduced in 1980 as a minimally invasive alternative.6 Multiple high-quality randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared the efficacy and safety of these revascularization procedures in patients with carotid artery stenosis.7 A systematic review of RCTs indicated that CEA is associated with a lower risk of stroke or death in the perioperative period compared with CAS.8 Notably, the Stenting and Angioplasty with Protection in Patients at High Risk for Endarterectomy trial specifically examined outcomes in a high-risk patient population9 and came to the opposite conclusion that the cumulative incidence of stroke, myocardial infarction, or death within 30 days was higher for patients undergoing CEA compared with those receiving CAS. However, most RCTs excluded patients at high surgical risk or with significant comorbidities, such as diabetes, thus limiting the applicability of these findings to patients with diabetes who exhibit distinct vascular pathophysiology.

Diabetes mellitus has been cited by a considerable body of evidence as an independent predictor of increased prevalence of carotid artery stenosis or stroke, as well as postoperative risk,51012 but it is usually not paid enough attention as a clinical problem.13 Moreover, as far back as 2010, the SVS “carotid” guidelines emphasized the increased vascular event risk associated with diabetes mellitus.14 However, no RCTs have been specifically conducted to evaluate the safety and efficacy of CEA versus CAS in patients with diabetes mellitus. Current evidence on whether diabetic patients derive similar safety benefits from carotid revascularization as the general population remains limited and inconsistent, and the comparative safety of CEA versus CAS in diabetic patients remains uncertain.1519 Given the rising prevalence of diabetes and increased use of CAS in clinical practice, our study aimed to compare the in-hospital outcomes of CEA versus CAS in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), specifically evaluating three major endpoints: all-cause mortality, postoperative stroke, and myocardial infarction.

Methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study was conducted using data from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project National Inpatient Sample (NIS) database between 2016 and 2021.20 The NIS captures approximately 35 million hospital stays annually across the USA, representing about 20% of all inpatient admissions. As the database contains publicly available, de-identified information, Institutional Review Board approval was not required.21

Our patient population consisted of individuals diagnosed with T2DM and either asymptomatic or symptomatic carotid stenosis, all of whom underwent CAS or CEA. Diagnoses and procedures were identified using ICD-10-CM codes, which were defined in accordance with prior study as detailed in online supplemental table 1.22 All adult hospitalizations (age ≥18 years) were included in our study, while those with missing vital status data were excluded.

Primary exposure and outcome

The primary exposure was the type of carotid revascularization procedure received—CEA or CAS. While CEA remains more commonly performed overall, the use of CAS has increased in recent years.6 Each patient included in this study received only one procedure during the same hospital stay.

Primary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, postoperative stroke, and myocardial infarction, segregated by the type of carotid stenosis (asymptomatic and symptomatic). CEA and CAS are relatively high-risk procedures, with myocardial infarction and death being adverse events.7 8 In-hospital mortality and myocardial infarction were assessed as indicators of safety, while postoperative stroke was used to evaluate the procedural efficacy.23 Outcomes were identified by extraction using their respective ICD-10 codes as detailed in online supplemental table 1. Secondary outcomes encompassed various hospitalization-related endpoints, including hospitalization costs, length of stay, and routine discharge rates, which were directly obtained from the database.

Covariates

Covariate selection was guided by a comprehensive literature review.15 22 Variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex, race), comorbid conditions (obesity, hypoglycemia, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, heart failure, arrhythmia, liver disease, chronic lung disease, pneumonia, chronic kidney disease, atherosclerosis, diabetic neuropathy, malignancy), and healthcare-related variables (elective admission status, payer status, median household income for patient’s ZIP Code, hospital bed size, hospital teaching status, and hospital region). Comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 codes as listed in online supplemental table 1. Other variables were derived directly from NIS administrative records. Imaging-related variables (eg, carotid patency from ultrasound or angiography) were not available in the NIS.

Statistical analysis

All analyses incorporated NIS discharge-level weights to generate nationally representative estimates.24 Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages, and continuous variables were summarized using weighted means and SEs. The χ2 test was used for categorical comparisons, and the Student’s t-test was used for continuous variables.

To reduce baseline imbalances between the CEA and CAS groups, inverse probability weighting (IPW) based on propensity scores was applied. Propensity scores were derived from a multivariable logistic regression model including 22 prespecified covariates, encompassing patient demographics, comorbidities, and healthcare-related variables. Then, the inverse probability of treatment weights was leveraged based on propensity scores to approximate the average treatment effect.25 To evaluate the effectiveness of IPW in balancing the two groups, the imbalance of covariates for the original and the adjusted (weighted) cohorts was compared. The standardized mean differences (SMDs) between the CAS and CEA groups were calculated.26 A non-significant univariate analysis (p>0.05) coupled with SMD<0.1 signifies that the balance between the groups has been successfully achieved.27

To enhance the robustness of our findings, a multivariable logistic regression adjusting for the variables that remained unbalanced between the groups was then performed on the weighted cohort to estimate the correlation of an exposure on an outcome, thus the term doubly robust analysis. Conventionally, when estimating causal effects using regression models or propensity scoring models alone, both statistical models must be correctly specified to ensure unbiasedness. However, the doubly robust estimation method mitigates this requirement by combining these two approaches, such that only one of the two models needs to be correctly specified to obtain an unbiased effect estimate.28 29 Sensitivity analyses were conducted using multivariable logistic regression on the original (unweighted) cohort, adjusting for procedure type and all covariates included in the propensity model.

All statistical tests were two-sided, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R software, V.3.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Demographics and clinical characteristics

In our study, a total of 31,363 diabetic patients with asymptomatic carotid stenosis (CAS: n=6338; CEA: n=25,025) and 3710 diabetic patients with symptomatic carotid stenosis (CAS: n=1271; CEA: n=2439), who underwent carotid revascularization (CEA or CAS) between 2016 and 2021, were evaluated (figure 1). A higher proportion of patients chose CEA surgery in both asymptomatic and symptomatic groups, with CEA being four times more common in asymptomatic patients and twice as frequent as CAS in symptomatic patients (table 1). The average age of patients undergoing CAS or CEA was similar (70.82 vs 70.76, p=0.585 in asymptomatic patients; 69.82 vs 69.82, p=0.999 in symptomatic patients). More males than females underwent these procedures, and among males, the rate of CAS was higher than CEA (62.9% vs 59.4%, p<0.001 in asymptomatic patients; 67.3% vs 60.5%, p<0.001 in symptomatic patients). Patients undergoing surgery were predominantly white, and among white individuals, the proportion of CAS was lower than CEA in asymptomatic patients (77.5% vs 80.6%, p<0.001 in asymptomatic patients; 69.2% vs 73.3%, p=0.253 in symptomatic patients).

In asymptomatic patients, the CAS group had a higher prevalence of combined hypoglycemia (1.5% vs 1.1%, p=0.010), hyperlipidemia (79.6% vs 78.3%, p=0.031), heart failure (19.8% vs 14.9%, p<0.001), arrhythmia (22.5% vs 19.4%, p<0.001), chronic lung disease (26.4% vs 24.7%, p=0.007), chronic kidney disease (26.3% vs 24.2%, p=0.001), atherosclerosis (7.4% vs 5.1%, p<0.001), and malignant tumor (14.9% vs 13.5%, p=0.003). However, fewer patients in the CAS group were obese (20.4% vs 21.5%, p=0.046) or had liver disease (1.8% vs 2.3%, p=0.011). Among symptomatic patients, those receiving CEA had a higher prevalence of heart failure (20.1% vs 14.7%, p<0.001) and epidemic influenza/pneumonia (4.6% vs 2.4%, p<0.001), but a lower prevalence of hyperlipidemia (1.5% vs 1.1%, p<0.001) and diabetic neuropathy (14.3% vs 17.7%, p=0.010) (table 1).

Regardless of asymptomatic or symptomatic status, patients with heart failure had a higher proportion to choose CAS (19.8% vs 14.9%, p<0.001 in asymptomatic patients; 20.1% vs 14.7%, p<0.001 in symptomatic patients). In elective hospital admissions, the proportion of patients undergoing CAS was lower than CEA (73.1% vs 82.6%, p<0.001 in asymptomatic patients; 9.9% vs 17.0%, p<0.001 in symptomatic patients). In contrast, urban teaching hospitals performed a greater number of CAS surgeries (78.4% vs 66.8%, p<0.001 in asymptomatic patients; 82.3% vs 71.8%, p<0.001 in symptomatic patients).

IPW and doubly robust analysis for outcomes

Considering the imbalance in baseline characteristics between patients undergoing CAS and CEA, we employed IPW based on propensity scores to construct a weighted cohort. The distribution of covariates after IPW is presented in table 2. Most baseline characteristics of the weighted cohorts were well balanced between the CAS and CEA groups. Exceptions included race, hospital bed size, hospital teaching status, primary payment coverage, hospital region in asymptomatic patients, and hospital region in symptomatic patients.

Among symptomatic patients in the weighted cohort, the CAS group had significantly higher mortality (4.2% vs 1.5%, p<0.001), higher hospitalization costs ($168,875.85 vs $122,953.75, p<0.001), and a lower incidence of routine discharge (37.7% vs 49.8%, p<0.001) compared with the CEA group. However, the incidence of postoperative stroke was lower in the CAS group (1.1% vs 1.9%, p=0.024). The increased risk of myocardial infarction observed with CAS before IPW was no longer present afterward (3.7% vs 2.9%, p=0.186). In addition, the length of stay (7.93 vs 7.61, p=0.354) was similar between the groups.

Under the doubly robust estimation framework, multivariable logistic regression was applied to adjust for residual imbalances in the weighted cohort. Further modeling found that in asymptomatic patients, CEA was associated with lower mortality (OR 0.997, 95% CI 0.995 to 0.999, p=0.011) but an increased incidence of myocardial infarction (OR 1.005, 95% CI 1.001 to 1.008, p=0.007). Among symptomatic patients, CEA also associated with lower mortality (OR 0.973, 95% CI 0.961 to 0.985, p<0.001), while the increased risk of postoperative stroke observed with CEA in the IPW model was no longer statistically significant (table 4). The sensitivity analysis of the multiple logistic regression model did not substantially change the results of mortality and myocardial infarction (online supplemental tables 2–4).

Discussions

Our study used data from the NIS, a nationally representative database of hospital admissions in the USA, to compare the in-hospital outcomes of CEA and CAS, especially focusing on type 2 diabetic patients. We observed that CEA was associated with significantly lower in-hospital mortality in type 2 diabetic patients with carotid stenosis compared with CAS. However, in patients with asymptomatic carotid stenosis, CEA was also linked to a higher incidence of myocardial infarction, warranting careful consideration. Among symptomatic patients, CEA demonstrated a mortality benefit without increasing myocardial infarction risk, suggesting its potential clinical advantage in this subgroup. These observations were confirmed by a doubly robust analysis, which combined IPW and regression models to adjust for demographic and hospital characteristics. This method mitigated the risk of model misspecification as long as either model is correct, thus enhancing the robustness in empirical studies.

Previous studies assessing the efficacy and safety of CAS and CEA have largely focused on the general population, stratified by asymptomatic and symptomatic carotid stenosis. For example, an NIS-based study (2016–2020) reported that CEA reduced mortality while increasing myocardial infarction risk among asymptomatic patients, and reduced mortality with no significant effect on myocardial infarction among symptomatic patients.22 Although the CEA group showed a greater reduction in the incidence of death compared with our study, the findings regarding myocardial infarction only in the asymptomatic population and no statistically significant difference in stroke were consistent with our conclusion reached by double-robust analysis. Additionally, considerable evidence showed that patients with type 2 diabetes who underwent carotid revascularization had no significant difference in the risk of death and myocardial infarction after procedures compared with patients without type 2 diabetes during the perioperative period.30 31 This suggested that the comparative safety profile of CEA versus CAS may be similar across patients with type 2 diabetes and the general population.

The Carotid Revascularization Endarterectomy versus Stenting Trial (CREST trial),32 one of the largest studies to evaluate CAS and CEA for asymptomatic and symptomatic carotid stenosis, presented a lower risk of myocardial infarction in the CAS group but no significant differences in the incidence of mortality between the two groups during the perioperative period. Our findings regarding myocardial infarction in patients with type 2 diabetes were in alignment with the results obtained from the CREST trial. However, in the Asymptomatic Carotid Trial I (ACT I trial) regarding patients with asymptomatic carotid stenosis,33 the CAS and CEA groups exhibited no significant differences in the 30-day incidence rates of both death, stroke, and myocardial infarction. Notably, our findings demonstrated lower in-hospital mortality for CEA compared with CAS among asymptomatic patients. Similar to our study, a nationwide real-world analysis from Germany reported that CEA was associated with reduced mortality within 30 days and even up to 5 years, which also highlighted that absolute event rates in routine practice were substantially higher than those observed in RCTs, particularly among asymptomatic patients.34 Such discrepancies likely reflect that RCTs enrolled only a small proportion of highly selected, low-risk individuals, whereas real-world patients frequently present with multiple comorbidities, such as diabetes, who were largely excluded from trials. These findings underscore the complexity of outcome prediction and suggest that diabetic patients may respond differently. In our study regarding patients with diabetes, CAS was associated with higher in-hospital mortality but a lower risk of myocardial infarction among asymptomatic patients. Supporting this, a systematic review of asymptomatic patients35 indicated an increased risk of stroke or death after CAS compared with CEA groups.

There were also some studies specifically conducted among symptomatic patients. In the EVA-3S trial regarding patients with symptomatic carotid stenosis,36 CEA had lower mortality and stroke rates than CAS at 1 and 6 months. The International Carotid Stenting Study regarding patients with symptomatic carotid stenosis showed that the CAS group experienced three fatal procedural myocardial infarctions,37 whereas the CEA group had four non-fatal instances in 120 days. Our research indicated that patients undergoing CEA have a lower mortality rate, yet no significant difference was observed in the incidence of myocardial infarction. Our findings are also consistent with several retrospective real-world studies that reported lower in-hospital mortality after CEA.15 22 Conversely, a prospective cohort study demonstrated no significant differences in perioperative mortality or postoperative stroke between CEA and CAS, suggesting that short-term safety may be comparable in routine practice.38 This discrepancy may be explained by the fact that the latter was a single-center, non-randomized study, which limits the generalizability of its findings. Given these inconsistencies, likely driven by differences in patient populations and study settings, more research is still needed in the future to compare diabetic patients with the general population and to compare differences after procedures in patients with diabetes.

Evidence directly comparing CAS and CEA in diabetic patients remains scarce. A retrospective analysis from 2001 to 2009 revealed that the incidence of stroke and mortality was 3.4% in the CEA group and 2.1% in the CAS group (p=0.46) in diabetic patients.19 This suggested that there is no significant difference in stroke and mortality between CEA and CAS, whereas our study associated CEA with a lower mortality rate. However, that analysis relied on unadjusted comparisons, limiting its interpretability. The limited number of studies may compromise the reliability of the findings. To our knowledge, our study was the first one to compare the postoperative outcomes of CAS and CEA specifically in patients with diabetes. CEA was associated with lower mortality in both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals, although CAS was linked to a lower myocardial infarction rate in asymptomatic cases. These findings address a critical knowledge gap and provide valuable insight into treatment selection for diabetic patients—an area where randomized controlled trials are lacking and real-world data are essential. Regardless of the chosen procedure, optimal perioperative management of diabetes is critical. Strict glycemic control, comprehensive cardiovascular assessment, and appropriate preoperative optimization are essential to reduce the risk of perioperative events. Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that certain antidiabetic agents, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists like semaglutide, may offer additional stroke prevention benefits, whereas sulfonylureas may be associated with increased risk.39 40

Our study has certain limitations. First, as a management dataset, the NIS did not capture clinical elements such as vital signs, laboratory values, or imaging findings. This omission restricted the depth of clinical characterization and limited the ability to adjust for potentially relevant prognostic factors. Additionally, misclassification could occur because of potential miscoding possibilities. For example, if postoperative nosocomial strokes were not properly coded as “postprocedural cerebrovascular infarction following other surgery (I97.821),” the current analysis may miss or misclassify a certain percentage of postoperative stroke. However, it is logical that any potential misclassification will occur without bias against any particular group of programs. Second, despite the use of a variety of analytical methods, including IPW and doubly robust analysis, there remains the possibility of unmeasured confounders that could influence the outcomes of CEA and CAS. If these unmeasured variables are unevenly distributed among the treatment groups, they could introduce bias into the results. Third, the dataset only records in-hospital outcomes and does not provide longitudinal follow-up. This is a major limitation because the fundamental goal of carotid revascularization is to prevent long-term stroke and improve survival. Therefore, while our analysis enables comparison of perioperative outcomes between CEA and CAS, it cannot address differences in long-term effectiveness or definitively answer the question of which revascularization strategy is preferred.

Conclusions

During the period of this study, CEA in the USA was performed with a lower in-hospital mortality risk compared with CAS in diabetic patients with either asymptomatic or symptomatic carotid artery stenosis, but CEA was linked to a higher incidence of myocardial infarction in asymptomatic patients. Further randomized controlled studies with homogeneous cohorts comparing CAS and CEA in diabetic patients are warranted to validate these findings. In the absence of such clinical trials, real-world evidence remains essential for guiding clinical decision-making and optimizing care for patients with diabetes.
