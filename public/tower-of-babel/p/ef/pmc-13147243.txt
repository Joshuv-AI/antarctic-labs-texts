When to prefer MitraClip over cardiac surgery
Authors: Fausto Castriota, Angelo Squeri, Roberto Nerla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevere mitral regurgitation (MR) is associated with an unfavourable prognosis, characterized by high mortality and reduced quality of life. Mitral surgery remains the gold standard for degenerative MR in patients with low operative risk, ensuring durability and improved survival; however, in elderly or frail patients, perioperative risk limits the applicability of surgical treatment. In this context, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER), predominantly with MitraClip™, has assumed an increasingly important role. The 2025 ESC/EACTS Guidelines recommend TEER in patients with severe secondary MR who remain symptomatic despite optimized medical therapy and cardiac resynchronization, with evidence demonstrating reduced hospitalizations and improved quality of life. Randomized trials such as COAPT and RESHAPE-HF2 have consolidated these findings, while the MATTERHORN study has shown non-inferiority of TEER compared with surgery in terms of clinical efficacy, with a superior safety profile. In primary MR, ongoing studies (REPAIR-MR, MITRA-HR) are evaluating the effectiveness of TEER vs. surgery in patients at moderate or high surgical risk. In summary, MitraClip currently represents the preferred therapeutic option in patients with high-risk secondary MR, while surgery remains indicated for repairable primary MR in low-risk surgical candidates.Keywords: Severe mitral regurgitation, Mitral valve surgery, Transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER)

Introduction

Mitral regurgitation (MR) is the most common valvular heart disease,1 with an estimated prevalence of approximately 2% in the general population and increasing frequency in elderly individuals.2,3 Moderate-or-greater MR occurs in <1% of people younger than 50 years, but affects nearly one in ten individuals over 75 years of age.3

Primary MR (PMR) also called degenerative or organic MR is caused by abnormalities in one or more components of the mitral valve (MV), such as the leaflets, chordae tendineae, or papillary muscles.4 Secondary MR (SMR), also referred to as functional MR because the valve itself is structurally normal, results from annular dilatation and geometric distortion of the subvalvular apparatus secondary to left ventricular dysfunction and dilatation due to ischaemic or non-ischaemic heart failure, occurring in roughly 9 out of 10 cases.4

In approximately 10% of patients, SMR may be due to left atrial myopathy caused by long-standing atrial fibrillation. Severe MR is a strong predictor of adverse outcomes, including more frequent heart-failure hospitalizations, reduced quality of life, and shortened survival.5 Reported mortality rates reach 20% at 1 year and 50% at 5 years.6 Furthermore, a direct relationship exists between MR severity and mortality: 1-year mortality ranges from 45% to 57% among patients with moderate or severe MR.7,8

Although SMR can be surgically corrected through MV repair or replacement, surgery has never clearly been shown to alter the natural history of SMR or improve survival.8 A further major limitation is that up to 50% of patients may not meet eligibility criteria for surgery because of high operative risk associated with advanced age, impaired left ventricular function, and multiple comorbidities. This may explain why the majority of heart-failure patients with SMR continue to be managed conservatively.

Transcatheter treatment

Transcatheter repair provides a minimally invasive alternative to surgery for the treatment of MR, using a percutaneous approach.9 Currently, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) for SMR includes the MitraClip™ (Abbott Vascular, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and the PASCAL™ system (Edwards Lifesciences, Irvine, CA, USA). Among other percutaneous devices, the Carillon Mitral Contour System™ (Cardiac Dimensions, Kirkland, WA, USA) performs indirect mitral annuloplasty. Additional percutaneous mitral repair devices are still under regulatory evaluation, and their commercial use remains limited.

The new 2025 ESC/EACTS Guidelines10 have redefined the role of TEER. In primary MR, where surgery remains the gold standard in low-risk patients, TEER is considered in high-risk or non-surgical candidates (Class IIa, LoE B).

For secondary MR, TEER is now recommended to reduce hospitalizations for heart failure and to improve quality of life in symptomatic patients with severe ventricular SMR persisting despite guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) and cardiac resynchronization therapy (CRT), when indicated, provided that they meet specific clinical and echocardiographic criteria (Class I, LoE A). A growing role is also recognized for TEER in improving symptoms among patients who do not fully meet these criteria.

The MitraClip

The transcatheter MitraClip procedure is based on the surgical edge-to-edge technique described by Alfieri and allows MR repair by approximating the mitral valve leaflets, creating a double-orifice mitral valve through a percutaneous venous and transseptal approach. The MitraClip device enables real-time positioning and repositioning of the clip under transoesophageal guidance to optimize MR reduction.

The first-generation MitraClip has undergone multiple device iterations (Figure 1), which have expanded anatomic feasibility, improved procedural success, enhanced MR reduction, and increased durability of results, while also providing an indirect annuloplasty effect. The MitraClip received Ce marking in 2008, FDA approval for PMR in 2013, and approval for SMR in 2019. The device now has over 20 years of clinical experience, with more than 250 000 patients treated in more than 75 countries.

Evolution of the MitraClip system from generation (G) 1 to generation 5. Generation 2 introduced nitinol grippers to improve leaflet capture. Generation 3 (MitraClip NTR/XTR) added various clip arm lengths. Generation 4 introduced additional clip sizes and controlled grippers. Generation 5 features a redesigned delivery system with more intuitive controls to allow more precise positioning.

MitraClip and the surgical approach

The therapeutic choice between cardiac surgery and transcatheter treatment with MitraClip must be guided by a multidisciplinary Heart Team evaluation, integrating clinical, anatomical, and functional characteristics, as well as the most recent scientific evidence and the 2025 ESC/EACTS Guidelines. It is essential to differentiate the treatment strategy for degenerative (primary) mitral regurgitation (MR) from that for functional (secondary) MR, as the underlying mechanisms and supporting evidence differ substantially.

As noted, degenerative MR results from intrinsic structural abnormalities of the mitral leaflets, chordae tendineae, or subvalvular apparatus. Mitral valve prolapse, chordal rupture, and myxomatous degeneration are among the most common causes. In these cases, surgical mitral repair has long been the reference standard—especially in younger patients and those with low operative risk. When technically feasible, surgical repair ensures superior durability, improved survival, and a significant reduction in the progression of heart failure.

However, in elderly, frail, or comorbid patients, operative risk may be prohibitive. In such cases, MitraClip provides a valuable therapeutic alternative, capable of reducing MR severity and improving symptoms with a favourable safety profile.

The ongoing randomized clinical trial REPAIR MR11 (Percutaneous MitraClip Device or Surgical Mitral Valve Repair in Patients With Primary Mitral Regurgitation Who Are Candidates for Surgery) aims to compare clinical outcomes with MitraClip vs. surgical repair in patients with severe primary MR, moderate surgical risk, and anatomically favourable conditions for repair. The objective is to determine whether, in intermediate-risk patients with primary MR, MitraClip can serve as an effective alternative to surgery—achieving comparable clinical improvements in symptoms and quality of life, albeit with potentially reduced durability. This study has initiated a re-evaluation of the role of TEER in degenerative MR, particularly where surgery is less feasible or less acceptable to patients.

Another ongoing randomized study, MITRA HR (Multicentre Study of MitraClip in Patients With Severe Primary Mitral Regurgitation at High Surgical Risk), is investigating the effectiveness of MitraClip in high-risk surgical candidates with primary MR. Its aim is to provide further evidence supporting the non-inferiority of TEER in terms of efficacy—and its superiority in safety—compared with conventional mitral surgery in high-risk patients.

MitraClip in functional mitral regurgitation

In functional MR (FMR), the regurgitation results from left ventricular dilatation and dysfunction; traditional surgery has shown less favourable outcomes, with limited prognostic benefit and often high perioperative risk. In this context, MitraClip has assumed a central role, as demonstrated by the COAPT trial12 (Cardiovascular Outcomes Assessment of the MitraClip Percutaneous Therapy for Heart Failure Patients with Functional Mitral Regurgitation), which showed significant reductions in heart-failure hospitalizations and mortality in patients treated with TEER compared with medical therapy alone. These results were confirmed and expanded by subsequent trials, including the randomized RESHAPE-HF213 (Randomized Investigation of the MitraClip Device in Heart Failure), reinforcing the effectiveness of MitraClip in improving symptoms, functional capacity, and quality of life. The additional randomized study MITRADVANCE (MitraClip TEER + optimal HF therapy vs. optimal HF therapy alone) is ongoing.

Further insights into the comparative role of MitraClip and cardiac surgery are provided by the MATTERHORN trial14 (A Multicenter, Randomized, Controlled Study to Assess Mitral Valve Reconstruction for Advanced Functional or Ischemic MR), which directly compared TEER with surgical mitral intervention in patients with secondary MR and heart failure. The results demonstrated the non-inferiority of MitraClip to surgery in terms of clinical efficacy, with a superior safety profile. Although the sample size was limited and the non-inferiority margin was wide, the study supports the concept that MitraClip may represent a valid alternative to surgery even in operable patients—particularly when surgical risk or clinical frailty is significant.

Overview of the MATTERHORN trial

MATTERHORN represents a major step in the direct comparison between TEER with MitraClip and traditional cardiac surgery in patients with secondary MR and symptomatic heart failure. This multicentre randomized trial was designed to assess the non-inferiority of MitraClip compared with surgery in terms of clinical efficacy (Figure 2).

TEER with MitraClip proved non-inferior to surgery for reducing MR and improving NYHA functional class. Moreover, MitraClip demonstrated a notably superior safety profile, with fewer periprocedural complications—an especially relevant finding in a population already compromised by heart failure. Quality-of-life improvements, assessed by tools such as the Kansas City Cardiomyopathy Questionnaire (KCCQ), were similar between groups.

In the MATTERHORN population of 210 patients with secondary MR and symptomatic HF despite guideline-directed therapy, TEER was non-inferior to surgery regarding the primary composite end-point at 12 months. The end-point occurred in 16.7% of TEER patients vs. 22.5% of surgical patients (estimated difference −6% points; 95% CI −17 to 6).

Other outcomes

Quality of life (Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire) and functional capacity (6-min walk distance) improved in both groups without clinically significant differences. Hospital length of stay and ICU time were significantly shorter with TEER (median 4 days vs. 12; ICU 1 day vs. 3 days).

In summary, the MATTERHORN trial demonstrates that in patients with heart failure and secondary mitral regurgitation who are candidates for both strategies, transcatheter edge-to-edge repair represents a safe and effective alternative to surgery, with a markedly lower short-term risk profile, albeit with reduced long-term durability of valve correction. These findings provide randomized evidence that helps bridge the gap between European and U.S. guidelines and supports therapeutic decision-making based on the balance between perioperative risk and anatomical durability of the repair.

The trial does, however, present several limitations: the enrolled cohort was at relatively low risk, as shown by the overall lower incidence of primary endpoint events compared with other studies; participating centres were selected based on their high level of experience with both TEER and surgical mitral valve repair. As a result, the findings may not be generalizable to all centres.

In essence, MATTERHORN reinforces the concept that MitraClip can serve as a valid alternative to surgery in patients with secondary MR, particularly when operative risk is elevated or clinical frailty is significant.

Conclusions

The 2025 ESC/EACTS Guidelines have incorporated these findings, recommending MitraClip in patients with symptomatic secondary MR despite optimal medical therapy, provided that valve anatomy is favourable and left-ventricular dilatation is not excessive. For primary MR, surgery remains the first-line option in low-risk candidates, but TEER is considered in high-risk or non-surgical patients, supported by emerging evidence that may broaden its indication.

In conclusion, MitraClip today represents a preferential choice over cardiac surgery in patients with symptomatic secondary MR, high surgical risk, or clinical frailty. The 2025 ESC/EACTS Guidelines consolidate its role, supported by randomized trials such as COAPT and RESHAPE-HF2. Cardiac surgery remains indicated in low-risk patients with repairable primary MR, but TEER is increasingly positioned as an alternative for high-risk candidates, with further evidence expected in the coming years.

The management of mitral regurgitation therefore requires an integrated, personalized approach in which therapeutic choices are guided by scientific evidence, clinical guidelines, and the individual characteristics of each patient.
