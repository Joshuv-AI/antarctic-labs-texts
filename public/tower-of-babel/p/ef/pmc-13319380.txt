Initial Experience With Automated 3D Color Flow Quantification of Mitral Regurgitation
Authors: Serge C. Harb, Joseph Hajj, Abdelrahaman Ahmed, Amar Krishnaswamy, Samir Kapadia, Patrick Collier, Richard Grimm, Rhonda L. Miyasaka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Accurate quantification of mitral regurgitation (MR) is essential for preprocedural evaluation and intraprocedural decision-making during transcatheter mitral interventions. Conventional echocardiographic techniques, such as the proximal isovelocity surface area method, have numerous limitations. Proximal isovelocity surface area is sensitive to the timing of MR, orifice geometry, and jet eccentricity. Assumptions of hemispheric flow convergence and circular regurgitant orifices are often untrue, particularly in functional MR. As a result, nonholosystolic jets may lead to overestimation of MR severity, while multiple jets may cause underestimation.To overcome these limitations, we describe our initial experience with the Philips 3D Automated Color Flow Quantification tool, used to focus on optimal image acquisition strategies and common pitfalls. This software enables automated calculation of regurgitant volume directly from 3D color Doppler data, allowing assessment of multiple and noncircular MR origins. By integrating a fluid dynamics model with artificial intelligence–based segmentation, the tool performs frame-by-frame systolic analysis, capturing temporal variations in regurgitant flow throughout systole.Our study included patients with severe MR undergoing transcatheter edge-to-edge repair; MR quantification is important for both procedural planning and intraprocedural guidance with this population. Although further validation is needed before widespread adoption, accurate analysis is highly dependent on high-quality 3D color Doppler acquisition. This manuscript outlines key technical considerations for optimizing 3D color imaging—including balancing spatial resolution and temporal resolution, increasing frame rate, and addressing common analysis challenges—with illustrative figures and supplemental videos.

Accurate quantification of mitral regurgitation (MR) is crucial for preprocedural evaluation and intraprocedural decision-making during transcatheter mitral interventions. Conventional techniques such as proximal isovelocity surface area are limited by geometric assumptions and variable reproducibility (Figure 1). The method provides an instantaneous peak flow rate rather than an average value over systole, and measurements can vary depending on the timing of MR, orifice geometry, and jet eccentricity. For instance, it assumes a hemispheric flow convergence and a circular regurgitant orifice, conditions that are rarely present in functional MR. To address these limitations, we present our initial experience with the Philips 3D Automated Color Flow Quantification tool, used to focus on optimal image acquisition strategies and common pitfalls.

The software provides automated regurgitant volume measurements directly from 3D color flow data allowing assessment of multiple or noncircular MR origins (Figure 2). Moreover, it leverages a fluid dynamics model combined with artificial intelligence-driven segmentation to perform frame-by-frame systolic analysis, thereby integrating variations in regurgitant flow throughout systole. This is potentially a very clinically relevant tool that can provide quantitative metrics of MR severity, although this technique depends heavily on high-quality 3D color acquisition.Figure 2CFQ workflow. Accurate mitral regurgitation (MR) quantification using Philips 3D Automated Color Flow Quantification (CFQ) begins with high-quality 3D color Doppler acquisition. This requires clear visualization of the regurgitant orifice (high spatial resolution) and sufficient temporal resolution, ideally with a volume rate ≥15 to 20 Hz to capture dynamic systolic flow. A high Doppler scale (≥70 cm/s) is also essential to avoid aliasing. Next, automated mitral valve modeling is performed. The 3D data set must encompass key anatomical landmarks for accurate segmentation, although overly large volumes may compromise spatial and temporal resolution. The flow convergence zone is then reviewed and confirmed. Regurgitant volume (RVol) is calculated using a validated fluid dynamics model, with results displayed as a flow curve over time. In this case, posterior leaflet flail resulted in an initial RVol of 54 cc. Following successful transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (TEER) with one device, repeat CFQ analysis showed a marked reduction in MR severity. RVol decreased to 10 cc.Abbreviation: MV, mitral valve.

Our study included patients with severe MR undergoing transcatheter edge-to-edge repair; the tool may be particularly valuable for both preprocedural assessment and intraprocedural decision-making for this population. Although further validation is required before clinical implementation, the first step lies in understanding how to acquire high-quality 3D color data sets, an essential prerequisite for accurate software performance. As mitral regurgitation (MR) occurs during systole, capturing enough systolic frames is essential for accurate quantification. Low frame rates may miss key phases of systole and underestimate regurgitant volume. Multiple strategies can improve frame rate, although each carries tradeoffs that may affect spatial resolution or color fidelity. The left image shows an initial 3D data set acquired at 11 Hz. Optimization techniques include the following: (a1) selecting a color mode optimized for frame rate and increasing it to 19 Hz, but with potential resolution and color degradation (a2); (b1) using multibeat acquisition to reach 18 Hz, although it may introduce stitch artifact and incomplete jet visualization (b2, arrows); (c1) reducing the acquisition box to raise frame rate to 17 Hz but risking jet exclusion if the box is miscentered (c2). Careful technique selection is required to balance temporal resolution with adequate visualization of the regurgitant jet.Figure 6Analysis pitfalls. Accurate flow analysis requires isolating mitral regurgitation (MR) flow. (a) MR originates from a P3 flail. (b) Diastolic frames were inadvertently included at the start of systole, as detected by Philips 3D Automated Color Flow Quantification (CFQ) through pink line tracing and mesh overlay (arrows). (c) The flow curve shows high initial flow that actually represents diastolic inflow rather than MR. (d and e, arrows) Non-MR flow may also be detected from left ventricular outflow tract (LVOT) acceleration. (e, lower right, asterisk) CFQ enables exclusion of LVOT flow, shown as a grayed-out area on the model. (f, larger grayed-out region) This exclusion area can be manually expanded, enabling correct detection of the P3 MR origin (f, arrow). Careful exclusion of nonregurgitant flow and proper systolic frame identification are essential to avoid miscalculation of regurgitant volume.

Pearls and pitfalls of frame rate optimization. As mitral regurgitation (MR) occurs during systole, capturing enough systolic frames is essential for accurate quantification. Low frame rates may miss key phases of systole and underestimate regurgitant volume. Multiple strategies can improve frame rate, although each carries tradeoffs that may affect spatial resolution or color fidelity. The left image shows an initial 3D data set acquired at 11 Hz. Optimization techniques include the following: (a1) selecting a color mode optimized for frame rate and increasing it to 19 Hz, but with potential resolution and color degradation (a2); (b1) using multibeat acquisition to reach 18 Hz, although it may introduce stitch artifact and incomplete jet visualization (b2, arrows); (c1) reducing the acquisition box to raise frame rate to 17 Hz but risking jet exclusion if the box is miscentered (c2). Careful technique selection is required to balance temporal resolution with adequate visualization of the regurgitant jet.
