Preventive effects of crocin and meloxicam on morphine withdrawal symptoms in mice: behavioral and biochemical insights
Authors: Tahereh Eteraf-Oskouei, Adel Mahmoudi Gharehbaba, Sarah Majidpour, Bohloul Habibi Asl
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe development of opioid-induced dependency is associated with several mechanisms, including oxidative stress, increase in inflammatory cytokines, and the activation of the glutamatergic system. This study investigates the effects of crocin, a bioactive compound from saffron, and meloxicam, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, on morphine withdrawal symptoms in male albino mice.MethodsTen groups, each consisting of nine mice, were randomly assigned to receive different treatments once daily for four days. On the fourth day, to assess the impact of the drugs on morphine dependency, naloxone was administered two hours after the final morphine injection. Withdrawal symptoms, specifically the frequency of jumping and standing on feet, were recorded over a 30-minute observation period for each mice. Additionally, to evaluate the effects of these drugs on pro-inflammatory cytokines, blood samples were collected from the animals’ hearts, and tests for TNF-α levels were conducted.ResultsBehavioral assessments following naloxone-precipitated withdrawal showed that crocin and meloxicam (20 mg/kg) significantly reduced withdrawal behaviors, including jumping and standing. Additionally, serum TNF-α levels were significantly decreased in groups treated with crocin, indicating its potential anti-inflammatory effects.ConclusionThese findings indicate that crocin and meloxicam could potentially mitigate morphine withdrawal symptoms in mice, underscoring their promising therapeutic applications in the management of opioid dependence.Keywords: Morphine, Withdrawal, Dependency, Meloxicam, Crocin, Mice

Introduction

Opioids are recognized as the most effective medications for managing severe pain; however, prolonged use of these drugs can lead to the development of tolerance and dependence [1, 2]. Multiple mechanisms contribute to the development of dependence, increased in inflammatory cytokines [3], increased levels of nitric oxide (NO) in the central amygdala [4], upregulation of NMDA receptors [5], and increased levels of glucocorticoid receptors [6], as well as oxidative stress [7].

Morphine is classified among the most potent analgesics for managing various types of pain, particularly chronic conditions like neuropathic pain and cancer pain. However, its clinical effectiveness has progressively declined with repeated use.

The priority of pharmacology studies is research on new drugs with natural sources and few side effects. Saffron (Crocus sativus L.), belonging to the Iridaceae family [8], is cultivated in several countries, including India, Spain, Greece, China, Italy, Azerbaijan, Turkey, and Iran [9]. There is an increasing interest in the isolated compounds of saffron due to their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties that positively impact human health. These effects are mediated through mechanisms such as the reduction of lipid peroxidation, inhibition of diazinon (DZN), and decreased levels of inflammatory cytokines and neuronal damage markers, attributed to its free radical scavenging activity [10]. Research indicates that the primary bioactive constituents of saffron include safranal, crocetin, picrocrocin, and crocin [11, 12]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that crocin exhibits a range of beneficial properties, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory [13], renoprotective [14], anti-atherosclerotic [15], antidepressant [16], anti-platelet aggregation [17], and anti-cancer effects [18, 19]. By scavenging free radicals, crocin can effectively shield cells from damage caused by oxidative stress [20].

While previous studies, investigated the effects of saffron as a whole on morphine withdrawal syndrome in mice [21, 22], our study specifically focuses on crocin, a key bioactive component of saffron, to precisely evaluate its therapeutic potential. Additionally, unlike other studies, which employed a different dependency model, our research utilizes a standardized morphine withdrawal protocol in mice, providing a more targeted approach to understanding the mechanisms of symptom alleviation.

Meloxicam, an enol-carboxamide non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), has been utilized for an extended period to manage acute pain and inflammation. Unlike many other NSAIDs, it exhibits a stronger inhibitory effect on the inducible isoform of cyclooxygenase (COX-2) compared to the constitutive isoform (COX-1) [23]. The effects of meloxicam on reducing inflammatory factors have been previously investigated [24, 25].

This research investigated the effects of meloxicam and crocin on morphine withdrawal symptoms in mice, focusing on their combined use due to their shared anti-inflammatory mechanisms and the potential for synergistic effects in addressing pain and inflammation, which are often associated with opioid dependence.

Materials and methods

Materials

Morphine sulfate (10 mg/mL) was procured from Darou Pakhsh Pharmaceutical Company (Tehran, Iran). Meloxicam was sourced from Koshan Pharmed Pharmaceutical Company (Tehran, Iran). Naloxone was obtained from Tolid Daro Company (Tehran, Iran). Ketamine was purchased from Rotexmedica GmbH (Berlin, Germany). Additionally, crocin was acquired from Samisaz pharmaceutical company (Mashhad, Iran).

Animals and treatment

Male albino mice (20–30 g; Pasteur Institute, Tehran, Iran) were randomly divided into 10 distinct groups, with each group comprising 9 mice. The animals were kept under controlled environmental settings, with a standard temperature of 24 ± 0.5 °C and a lighting schedule of 12 h of light followed by 12 h of darkness. They had unrestricted access to food and water. The studies were conducted following the guidelines outlined in the Guide for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, (National Institutes of Health Publication No 85 − 23, revised 1985). The proposal was approved by the ethical committee of Tabriz university of medical sciences (IR.TBZMED.VCR.REC.1398.220).

The control groups received daily injections of either saline (10 mL/kg; ip), morphine (50 mg/kg; ip) or DMSO 25% (10 mL/kg; ip). Other groups were pretreated with different dosages of crocin (100-200-400 mg/kg, ip) [26–28], meloxicam (5-10-20 mg/kg, ip) [29, 30], as well as a combination of crocin (100 mg/kg, ip) and meloxicam (5 mg/kg, ip) to evaluate preventive effects on morphine-induced dependence. Morphine was injected in each group 30 min following the administration of the designated treatments. This protocol was maintained for a duration of four days, with daily injections being performed.

Withdrawal symptoms test

Mice were evaluated for the extent of dependence following a four-day regimen of drugs administration. The administration of naloxone (4 mg/kg, ip), two hours after the final morphine dose, induced significant withdrawal symptoms in the morphine-dependent mice. Each of the mice was placed individually on filter paper within an open plexiglass chamber (25 cm × 25 cm × 40 cm), and an observer recorded the frequency of jumps and instances of standing on their hind feet over a 30-minute observation period for each subject.

Behavioral scoring was performed by an observer blinded to treatment group assignments. To ensure objectivity, all video recordings were analyzed using randomized identification codes, with group information revealed only after completion of all behavioral quantifications.

Determination of TNF-𝛼 concentrations

All animals were deeply anesthetized via injection of ketamine (100 mg/kg, ip) to ensure complete unconsciousness prior to blood collection. Terminal cardiac puncture was then performed to obtain blood samples for TNF-α measurement, which simultaneously served as the method of euthanasia. This approach ensured rapid hypovolemic arrest while maintaining compliance with ethical guidelines, as animals remained fully anesthetized and insensible throughout the procedure. The blood samples underwent centrifugation at 3000 rpm for 10 min. Serum levels of TNF-α were quantified using the ELISA technique.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis of each data set was performed by Instat software. The results were shown as mean ± SEM for the various groups. The data were analyzed using the unpaired T-test and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey post hoc test. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Effects of crocin on withdrawal

Figure 3 illustrates the effects of different crocin doses (100, 200, and 400 mg/kg) on morphine withdrawal symptoms. The results demonstrated that crocin significantly reduced the frequency of both jumping and standing behaviors across all tested doses (p < 0.001). Specifically, the 400 mg/kg dose showed the most pronounced effect on jumping behavior, while the 100 mg/kg and 400 mg/kg doses exhibited strong efficacy in reducing standing behavior. Notably, the 200 mg/kg dose also significantly alleviated withdrawal symptoms, though to a slightly lesser extent compared to the other doses.

Discussion

Recent evidence reveals that morphine dependence develops through coordinated neuroimmune mechanisms involving both neuronal adaptations and glial cell activation. Specifically, chronic morphine exposure triggers microglial activation through Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) signaling, initiating a cascade of pro-inflammatory cytokine release including TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 [31]. These cytokines then disrupt normal neurotransmission by enhancing glutamatergic activity while suppressing GABAergic inhibition [32], creating the neuronal hyperexcitability that underlies withdrawal behaviors. Our observation of elevated TNF-α levels following morphine administration directly reflects this neuroinflammatory process.

TNF-α, a pro-inflammatory cytokine, plays a significant role in the neuroinflammatory mechanisms underlying morphine withdrawal syndrome. Chronic morphine exposure upregulates TNF-α expression in microglia and astrocytes, contributing to central nervous system (CNS) hyperexcitability and withdrawal-related behaviors such as hyperalgesia, anxiety, and autonomic dysfunction [33]. TNF-α enhances synaptic glutamate release and suppresses GABAergic inhibition, exacerbating neuronal hyperactivity during withdrawal [34, 35]. This glutamate/GABA imbalance particularly affects key addiction-related brain regions including the nucleus accumbens and amygdala, which may explain the specific withdrawal behaviors (jumping, standing) measured in our study [1].

The effectiveness of crocin in our study likely stems from its ability to interrupt this neuroinflammatory cascade at multiple levels. As a potent carotenoid antioxidant, crocin can neutralize the reactive oxygen species that activate NF-κB signaling upstream of TNF-α production [36]. Additionally, crocin may directly modulate microglial activation states through inhibition of TLR4 signaling pathways [37], reducing their release of pro-inflammatory cytokines. This dual mechanism would account for both the observed TNF-α reduction and behavioral improvements.

DMSO has been shown to modulate TNF-α expression and activity in various experimental models. Studies indicate that DMSO can exert anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing TNF-α production in immune cells, such as macrophages, likely through inhibition of nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-κB) signaling. However, the effects may be concentration-dependent, with low doses exhibiting minimal impact and higher doses significantly attenuating pro-inflammatory cytokine release. These properties make DMSO a useful solvent control in inflammation studies, though its intrinsic bioactivity necessitates careful interpretation of results. While we used a standardized, low concentration of DMSO (25%) to minimize solvent-related effects, we cannot rule out its potential contribution to the observed reductions in TNF-α levels or behavioral symptoms. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise molecular mechanisms underlying DMSO-mediated TNF-α regulation [38].

The inflammatory effects associated with morphine are partly due to the activation of the innate immune system and TLR4. TLR4 is present on the surface of microglia and, to a lesser extent, on astrocytes. When opioids bind to TLR4, they initiate an inflammatory response that promotes the release of cytokines such as TNF, interleukin 1β, and interleukin 6 [39]. This neuroimmune activation represents a key bridge between chronic morphine exposure and the withdrawal syndrome observed in our study. The time course of our protocol (4-day morphine exposure followed by naloxone challenge) is particularly effective at revealing this connection, as the acute withdrawal precipitates maximal cytokine release from primed glial cells [40].

In the current study, of the administration morphine (50 mg/kg, i.p.) over four days resulted in a notable increase in TNF-α levels among the subjects. Thus, the findings of this study regarding the impact of morphine on TNF-α elevation are consistent with previous research [41]. Moreover, immunomodulatory agents that mitigate or eliminate morphine tolerance also reduce TNF expression [42].

The heightened production of free radicals and reactive oxygen species, combined with insufficient elimination by antioxidants, results in damage to cell membranes and apoptosis. Extended use of morphine exacerbates oxidative stress through two primary mechanisms:1) Reduction of Antioxidant Enzymes: Morphine diminishes the activity of antioxidant enzymes, including superoxide dismutase, catalase, and Glutathione-S-transferase [43]. 2) Stimulation of Oxidative Metabolism: Morphine enhances the oxidative metabolism of dopamine and xanthine, leading to an increased generation of free radicals and reactive oxygen species [44].

The aim of this study was to assess the impact of crocin and meloxicam on morphine withdrawal symptoms in mice. The findings revealed that intraperitoneal administration of crocin significantly mitigated morphine withdrawal symptoms, including the frequency of jumps and instances of standing on feet.

The results of our study are in line with prior research that indicates crocin’s effectiveness in decreasing TNF-α levels [45, 46]. In this investigation, crocin significantly alleviated withdrawal symptoms and led to a notable reduction in TNF-α levels. Therefore, it appears that one mechanism underlying crocin’s inhibitory effects on morphine dependence may be its capacity to suppress TNF-α production.

In another part of this study, the administration of meloxicam (20 mg/kg, i.p.) led to a significant decrease in withdrawal symptoms, including the frequency of jumps and instances of standing on feet. However, at this dosage, no significance reduction in TNF-α levels was observed when compared to the group receiving DMSO + morphine. This apparent paradox - where meloxicam improved withdrawal behaviors without suppressing systemic TNF-α - may reflect several non-exclusive mechanisms: [1] COX-2 inhibition by meloxicam could preferentially target prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) signaling, which drives withdrawal behaviors independently of TNF-α [47, 48]; [2] higher meloxicam doses might shift arachidonic acid metabolism toward alternative inflammatory pathways (e.g., lipoxygenase products) [49]; or [3] TNF-α-independent CNS effects (e.g., modulation of glutamate transmission) may predominate at these doses [25, 50].

While our study focused on TNF-α as a key inflammatory mediator, the partial dissociation between meloxicam’s behavioral effects and TNF-α levels suggests the involvement of additional pathways. Meloxicam’s known COX-2 inhibitory effects may modulate withdrawal through prostaglandin-mediated mechanisms that complement cytokine signaling [51]. Notably, PGE2 has been shown to directly excite locus coeruleus neurons during opioid withdrawal [52], potentially explaining why COX inhibition improved behaviors despite unchanged TNF-α levels. This highlights the complexity of neuroimmune interactions in opioid withdrawal and suggests that optimal treatment may require multi-target approaches addressing both cytokine and prostaglandin pathways.

While meloxicam is generally considered to have limited BBB permeability under physiological conditions, with reported brain-to-plasma ratios of 0.1–0.3 in rodent studies [53], several factors may enhance its CNS access in our experimental paradigm. The neuroinflammatory state induced by morphine withdrawal is known to increase BBB permeability through downregulation of tight junction proteins [54] and morphine-induced vascular remodeling [55, 56]. This pathological BBB alteration could facilitate meloxicam’s entry into the CNS during acute withdrawal. Furthermore, even limited CNS penetration may be pharmacologically relevant given meloxicam’s high potency as a COX-2 inhibitor (nM-range IC50). Alternative mechanisms should also be considered, including meloxicam’s potential to reduce prostaglandin-mediated glial priming through peripheral COX-2 inhibition [57] or its possible modulation of central inflammation via vagal nerve signaling [58]. These pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic considerations support that meloxicam could exert both direct central and indirect peripheral-to-central anti-inflammatory effects in our withdrawal model.

While our TNF-α measurements reflect peripheral inflammation, the behavioral improvements suggest concomitant neuroinflammatory modulation. Future studies should directly assess CNS cytokine levels to confirm these central effects.

Regarding the co-administration of crocin and meloxicam, no significant differences in morphine withdrawal symptoms were noted. This observation may be explained by several factors. First, a pharmacodynamic saturation effect may be at play [59], where both compounds independently target overlapping inflammatory pathways, potentially reaching maximal efficacy at the tested doses. Since both agents suppress TNF-α and inflammatory mediators, their combined use may not provide additional benefit when these pathways are already maximally inhibited by either drug alone.

Second, the divergent primary mechanisms of action between these compounds could contribute to this phenomenon. While crocin predominantly modulates oxidative stress and glutamatergic signaling pathways, meloxicam primarily inhibits prostaglandin synthesis through COX-2 blockade. If morphine withdrawal symptoms in our model were driven predominantly by oxidative stress pathways, the additional anti-inflammatory effect of meloxicam might not significantly augment the therapeutic outcome.

Third, the specific dose ratio used in our combination therapy may require further optimization. It is possible that different dose combinations or administration schedules could reveal synergistic effects that were not apparent with the current regimen. This possibility is supported by previous work demonstrating that dose ratios critically influence the outcomes of combination therapies in neuropharmacological interventions.

We acknowledge that our current study design did not include mechanistic investigations to fully elucidate these potential interactions. Future studies incorporating dose-response analyses, measurements of additional inflammatory markers, and examination of central nervous system cytokine levels could provide more definitive answers regarding the nature of crocin-meloxicam interactions in this model.

Limitations

While jumping and standing behaviors are validated proxies for withdrawal severity, our study did not assess other somatic (e.g., ptosis, diarrhea) or affective (e.g., anxiety-like behaviors) symptoms. Future work could integrate additional measures like weight loss monitoring, open-field locomotion, or elevated plus-maze tests to capture withdrawal’s multidimensional nature. Such approaches would further bridge translational gaps to clinical withdrawal phenotypes.

Although DMSO is a widely accepted solvent for poorly water-soluble compounds like meloxicam, its inherent biological activity may introduce confounding variables. While our dose was selected based on prior literature showing minimal interference at this concentration, the use of alternative vehicles (e.g., saline with solubilizing agents) in future work would help validate our findings.

Conclusion

The findings of this study underscore the potential of crocin and meloxicam in alleviating morphine withdrawal symptoms in mice. Both agents demonstrated significant effects in reducing withdrawal behaviors, with crocin showing a notable decrease in serum TNF-α levels, which may contribute to its efficacy. While meloxicam also reduced withdrawal symptoms, its impact on TNF-α was less pronounced, suggesting alternative mechanisms may be at play. The lack of significant differences in withdrawal symptoms with the co-administration of crocin and meloxicam indicates the need for further investigation into their combined effects.

Overall, this research supports the exploration of crocin and meloxicam as adjunct therapies in managing opioid dependence, warranting further studies to elucidate their mechanisms of action and clinical applicability.
