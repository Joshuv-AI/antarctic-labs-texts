Modulating cortical excitability through transcranial direct current stimulation combined with therapeutic exercise for craniofacial myofascial pain: Randomized controlled trial
Authors: Ramesh Chandra Patra, Deepti Agrawal Garg, A Yashudas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTemporomandibular dysfunction (TMD) involves pain, motor impairment and psychological distress, often sustained by maladaptive central neuroplasticity. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) can modulate cortical excitability, enhance descending inhibitory control, and improve function, making it a promising adjunct to multimodal rehabilitation.ObjectiveTo examine the effects of tDCS combined with multimodal rehabilitation on pain, motor performance and psychosocial outcomes in TMD and to explore associated neurophysiological mechanism.MethodsIn this randomized, assessor-blinded trial, participants received either active tDCS plus rehabilitation or Sham tDCS plus rehabilitation. Assessments occurred at baseline, post-intervention (8 weeks) and at 3 and 6-month follow-up. Primary outcomes were EEG Alpha Modulation Index (AMI), Pressure pain threshold (PPT) and Jaw functional limitation scale (JFLS); secondary outcomes included neck disability index (NDI), patient global impression of change (PGIC) data were analyzed via repeated-measures ANOVA with intention-to-treat principles.ResultsActive tDCS with rehabilitation produced greater improvements than sham in all outcomes (p < 0.001). EEG Alpha Modulation Index increased (η2 = 0.24), indicating enhanced cortical excitability. PPT improved (η2 = 0.22) showed significant and sustained functional gains. PGIC scores indicated large perceived improvement (r = 0.72), maintained at 6 months. No serious adverse events were reported. Minor events (headaches, tingling, skin irritation) occurred in <10 % of sessions and were transient.ConclusiontDCS combined with therapeutic exercise safely enhances cortical excitability, pain modulation, function and patient outcomes in TMD, supporting Multimodal rehabilitation. Further research should refine protocals and confirm long-term benefits across populations.Keywords: Transcranial direct current stimulation, Temporomandibular dysfunction, Pain modulation, Neuroplasticity, Craniofascial Myofacial, Rehabilitation

Introduction

Background and rationale

Central sensitization in craniofacial myofascial pain syndrome

Craniofacial Myofascial Pain Syndrome (MPS) is a complex musculoskeletal disorder characterized by the presence of myofascial trigger points, localized muscle stiffness and referred pain with the head, neck and jaw regions.1,2 The Craniofacial variant often involves the temporomandibular joint, masticatory muscles and upper cervical structure, contributing to overlapping clinical manifestations such as headaches, facial pain and oromandibular dysfunction.3,4

Central sensitization, a maladaptive increase in the excitability and synaptic efficacy of central nociceptive neurons, is believed to be induced in chronic MPS by prolonged nociceptive input from active trigger points.5,6 This phenomenon causes receptive fields to widen, pain thresholds to change, and pain to persist even after peripheral input has been resolved.7 Studies employing neurophysiological markers like EEG and TMS have verified cortical reorganization, impaired sensorimotor integration, and increased activity in brain regions involved in pain processing in patients with craniofacial MPS.8,9 These results imply that in order to effectively treat peripheral pathology, the altered processing of the central nervous system should also be addressed.

Limitations of conventional therapy

Currently, manual therapies, dry needling, therapeutic exercises, and pharmaceutical interventions (such as muscle relaxants and NSAIDs) are the main methods used to treat craniofacial MPS.10,11 Despite providing temporary symptom relief, these modalities are not very effective over the long term, especially for patients who have chronic pain trajectories and central sensitization.12,13 Pharmacotherapy frequently produces short-term pain relief, but it can also have negative side effects and lead to dependency, particularly in chronic conditions. The hyperexcitability of the central nervous system may also not be adequately reversed by manual therapy and exercise alone.5,2

Additionally, conventional physical rehabilitation techniques rarely include tactics meant to alter central neural mechanisms; instead, they usually focus on postural correction, muscle reactivation, and pain management.14 Given that overlapping central mechanisms like thalamocortical dysrhythmia and impaired inhibitory control are now known to contribute to persistent pain and dysfunction, this gap is especially important in craniofacial MPS6,3. Thus, new methods that can alter the central and peripheral causes of pain are required.

Role of neuromodulation in and rehabilitation

Mechanisms of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a non-invasive method of brain stimulation that modifies synaptic plasticity and neuronal excitability by applying a low-intensity electrical current to specific cortical regions.15,16 Anodal tDCS can affect pain processing circuits, increase cortical excitability, and promote long-term potentiation (LTP)-like effects when applied over motor and prefrontal cortices.17,9 Modification of endogenous opioid pathways, NMDA receptor activity, and glutamatergic transmission mediate these effects.7,9

The analgesic potential of tDCS in chronic pain conditions like fibromyalgia, temporomandibular dysfunction (TMD), and orofacial pain has been shown in clinical and preclinical studies.17,3 For instance, after repeated sessions of tDCS applied to the primary motor cortex, Fricov'a et al.13 reported significant pain relief in patients with orofacial pain. Further supporting its central analgesic mechanisms, Kim et al.9 found that patients with TMD experienced reduced pain sensitivity and modulation of (μ)-opioid receptor availability after high-definition tDCS in a pilot randomized clinical trial.

Furthermore, recent studies show that tDCS can affect attentional and emotional modulation networks in addition to cortical pain processing regions by stimulating the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex.16 These results lend credence to the increasing body of evidence suggesting that tDCS is a viable treatment option for central dysfunction in long-term musculoskeletal disorders.

Synergistic potential with therapeutic exercise

Therapeutic exercise is still a mainstay of musculoskeletal rehabilitation, but when combined with tDCS, it may have synergistic effects by simultaneously modifying central excitability and activating peripheral motor pathways.15,8 Mechanisms like motor learning, elevated cortical connectivity, and BDNF upregulation promote exercise-induced neuroplasticity; these effects may be amplified by concurrent neuromodulation.3,16

According to Jobin et al. the combined tDCS and exercise therapy was safe and feasible in patients with cervicogenic headache, a condition whose pathophysiology overlaps with that of craniofacial MPS.12 They also showed encouraging trends toward pain reduction and functional improvement. Furthermore, research on populations with chronic TMD and fibromyalgia has demonstrated that multimodal approaches that combine central and peripheral interventions produce better results than monotherapy.17,9

It is possible that the combination of tDCS and exercise will improve descending pain inhibition, restore sensorimotor integration, and improve cortical reorganization.15,7 Given the presence of both muscular and cortical dysfunction in chronic craniofacial MPS, this integrative approach is particularly pertinent.

Evidence gap and need for the study

There are few high-quality randomized controlled trials assessing the combined effects of therapeutic exercise and tDCS in patients with craniofacial myofascial pain syndrome, despite mounting evidence in favor of their use separately. The majority of current research is either limited in its ability to measure central neurophys-iological changes, has small sample sizes, or has short follow-up periods.10,13 Moreover, many overlook neuroplasticity biomarkers like cortical excitability or sensorimotor EEG rhythms, functional outcomes, or patient-reported measures.7

The literature is severely lacking in controlled trials that specifically look at the synergistic use of tDCS and exercise in craniofacial MPS. Exploring innovative multimodal interventions that can both modulate central excitability and restore peripheral function is crucial as central sensitization is increasingly acknowledged as a therapeutic target in chronic pain syndromes.3,6 In clinical settings, it is also necessary to assess the approaches’ viability, safety, and tolerability.12

Objectives of the study

Primary objective

To evaluate the efficacy of tDcS combined with therapeutic exercise in reducing pain and improving functional outcomes in patients with craniofacial myofascial pain syndrome.

Secondary objectives

To assess changes in cortical excitability and central sensitization following the intervention using neurophysiological markers.

To evaluate feasibility, safety and patients-reported outcomes such as quality of life, disability, and satisfaction.

Methods

Ethical

The International Clinical Trials Registry received a prospective registration for the trial on December 20, 2021 (CTRI/2021/12/038734).

Trial design and setting

A prospective, double-blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT) with two parallel intervention arms was the design of this study. Assessing the effectiveness and safety of combining therapeutic exercise and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) in patients with craniofacial myofascial pain syndrome (MPS) was the main goal. The trial was carried out at Lovely Professional University's outpatient department in Punjab, India. The intervention period lasted 8 weeks, with follow-up assessments conducted immediately post-intervention, at 3 months and at 6 months after completion of the intervention.

Participants were randomly assigned to one of the following intervention groups:

Experimental Group A: Active tDCS combined with a standardized therapeutic exercise program.

Controlled Group B: Sham tDCS combined with the same therapeutic exercise program.

The trial was carried out in a clinical setting at Lovely Professional University's (Punjab, India) Out-patient Department under the direction of certified neurologists and physiotherapists. Methodological rigor, transparency, and reproducibility were ensured by the study's adherence to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 guidelines.

Participants

A total of 80 individual were screened at Lovely Professional University, Phagwara, India, from January to March of 2022. Individual of 68 agreed to participate and fulfilled the eligibility requirements. Using a double-blinded allocation process, participants were randomized to either the experimental group (active tDCS + Therapeutic exercise program; n = 34) or the control group (sham tDCS + Therapeutic exercise program; n = 34). During the intervention eight participants (4 per group) stopped participating, leaving 60 participants (Group A, n = 30 and Group B, n = 30 per group) for analysis. Recruitment process

Participants recruited through local community clinics, campus announcements, and outpatient referrals. To confirm the diagnosis of craniofacial MPS, each prospective participant underwent a pre-screening evaluation that included a physical examination and clinical interview. All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded to participate in the study if they had a history of neurological or psychiatric disorders, were pregnant or nursing, were taking centrally acting medications like antidepressants or anticonvulsants, had metallic devices implanted, cardiac pacemakers, or a history of seizures, or had previously been exposed to neuromodulation therapies like transcranial direct current stimulation or transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and participants with pre-existing dermatological conditions or skin lesions at electrode application sites were excluded due to the risk of tDCS-related skin irritation or burns. All eligible participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, in accordance with ethical research practices.

Randomization and blinding

A computer-generated block randomization sequence with permuted blocks of different sizes was used to randomly assign participants to either Group A or Group B, ensuring group balance. In order to preserve allocation concealment, an independent researcher created sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes (SNOSE).

A double-blind trial was used. The group assignments were hidden from the outcome assessors and participants. The tDCS device was set up to provide either sham or active stimulation. Stimulation in the sham condition mimicked the feeling of active stimulation while preserving blinding integrity by ramping up for 30 s and then turning off automatically. At study completion, participants were asked to guess whether they had received active or sham tDCS, to validate blinding integrity. The success of blinding was confirmed by chi-square analysis (p > 0.05).

Interventions

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS)

A CE-certified direct current stimulator, such as the Soterix Medical 1 × 1 or a comparable model, was used to administer tDCS. Study according to Kim et al.,9 incorporated this intervention.

Mechanism of cotical excitability modulation

Anodal stimulation increases excitability and promotes long-term potentiation-like plasticity by depolarizing the dendritic resting membrane potential of pyramidal neurons in the motor cortex. Dendrites are hyperpolarized by cathodal stimulation, which reduces excitability. These polarity-dependent effects affect motor learning pathways and cortical pain modulation networks.17

Electrode placement

The anodal electrode was positioned using the international 10–20 EEG system at the C3 position over the left primary motor cortex (M1).

The cathodal electrode was situated above the supraorbital region on the opposite side (Fp2).

Electrode specification

The intervention was administration using 5 × 7 cm sponge pads soaked in saline. Five sessions per week, lasting 20 min each, were used to administer stimulation at an intensity of 2 mA. Over the course of two weeks, ten sessions were held. The current for the sham group increased for 30 s before halting. The device's visual and auditory indicators replicated continuous stimulation in order to maintain blinding Jobin et al.,.12 At the conclusion of each session, participants were monitored for any negative consequences.

Therapeutic exercise program

For both groups, a 30-min therapeutic exercise program designed specifically for people with craniofacial MPS was administered. The physiotherapists who oversaw the program were blind to the group assignments, and it was founded on evidence-based multimodal rehabilitation frameworks. Outcome measures

Assessments were conducted at four time points: at baseline, prior to randomization; immediately after completion of the 8-week intervention period (post-intervention); at 3 months following the end of the intervention; and at 6 months following the end of the intervention. All evaluations were performed by outcome assessors who were blinded to group allocation.

Primary outcome

1.The EEG Alpha Modulation Index (AMI), It measures the percentage change in alpha power (8–13 Hz) between baseline and post-intervention, is a straightforward indicator of cortical excitability. Higher excitability is indicated by greater alpha suppression. Mean alpha power is extracted from a specified region of interest after the EEG is preprocessed with a 1–45 Hz band-pass filter and artifact removal. The 10–20 system is used to record the EEG at ≥ 500 Hz. Paired t-tests or repeated-measures ANOVA are used to analyze AMI, which is computed as (Baseline Post)/Baseline × 100. For a more comprehensive excitability profile, this result is simple, repeatable, and enhances time-resolved measurements like ERD/ERS.19

Adverse Event Following each session, participants reported potential adverse events (tingling, itching, dizzinness, headache, erythema). Events were graded for severity (mild, moderate, sever) and frequency. All events were transient, resolving within minutes without intervention.

Sample size calculation

G∗Power software was used to estimate the sample size based on an expected effect size (Cohen's d = 0.7) was derived from randamized tDCS studies in chronic pain conditions.12,13 It was necessary to have at least 52 participants (26 per group), assuming an α = 0.05 and power = 80 %. The final recruitment goal was set at 60 participants (30 per group), taking into account a possible 15 % dropout rate.

Statistical analysis

SPSS v27.0 (IBM Corp.) was used for all analyses, and p < 0.05 was chosen as the significance level. Using the Shapiro-Wilk test, the normality of continuous variables was examined. Two-way repeated measures ANOVA (time × group) was used to analyze between-group differences for VAS, NDI, JFLS, EEG, and PPT scores, and paired t-tests were used to evaluate changes within groups. PPT and EEG alpha modulation were assessed over time using mixed-model analyses. Missing data were handled using an intention-to-treat strategy that carried over from the previous observation. For the purpose of evaluating safety, adverse events were descriptively summarized.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

An equal number of sixty participants were assigned to the experimental group (n = 30) and the control group (n = 30). There were no significant differences in baseline values for the EEG Alpha Modulation Index (AMI), Pressure Pain Threshold (PPT), Jaw Functional Limitation Scale (JFLS), Neck Disability Index (NDI), Patient Global Impression of Change (PGIC), comorbidity profile, age, sex distribution, BMI, education, or symptom duration between the groups. All baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between groups (all p > 0.05).

All demographic, clinical history, and outcome variables shown in Table 2, were similar between the experimental (n = 30) and control (n = 30) groups at baseline. The experimental group's mean age was 42.3 ± 10.8 years, while the control group's was 43.7 ± 9.9 years (p = 0.62, Cohen's d = 0.14). Between groups, the percentage of men was comparable (40.0 % vs. 36.7 %, p = 0.79, OR = 1.15, 95 % CI 0.43–3.09). There was no significant difference in body mass index (25.4 ± 3.2 vs. 25.1 ± 3.5 kg/m2, p = 0.74, d = 0.09). The experimental group's median symptom duration was 14 months (IQR 9–20), while the control group's was 15 months (IQR 10–22) (p = 0.68, r = 0.07). There was no significant difference in the amount of education (14.1 ± 2.5 vs. 13.8 ± 2.7 years, p = 0.64, d = 0.11). Similar rates of medication use (33.3 % vs. 30.0 %, p = 0.79, OR = 1.17, 95 % CI 0.40–3.42) and comorbidity prevalence (26.7 % vs. 23.3 %, p = 0.77, OR = 1.20, 95 % CI 0.38–3.79) were also observed.

The following baseline outcome measures showed no significant differences: Jaw Functional Limitation Scale (42.1 ± 11.5 vs. 43.8 ± 12.2, p = 0.64, d = 0.14), Neck Disability Index (32.6 ± 8.7 vs. 31.8 ± 9.1, p = 0.73, d = 0.09), EEG Alpha Modulation Index (0.58 ± 0.12 vs. 0.56 ± 0.11, p = 0.41, d = 0.18), and pressure pain threshold (3.42 ± 0.48 vs. 3.39 ± 0.52 kg/cm2, p = 0.79, d = 0.06). The effect sizes were small (Cohen's d ≤ 0.18; ORs near 1.0; r ≥ 0.07) and all p-values were greater than 0.05, suggesting that there were very little practical differences between the groups. According to these results, randomization produced groups that were evenly distributed at the start of the study, reducing the possibility of baseline confounding in later analyses.

Primary outcome

EEG Alpha Modulation Index (AMI)

AMI in the experimental group increased significantly from baseline (0.58 ± 0.12) to post-intervention (0.67 ± 0.11), and this increase was mostly maintained at 3-month (0.66 ± 0.11) and 6-month follow-ups (0.65 ± 0.12). On the other hand, the control group's values remained consistent throughout all time points, ranging from 0.56 to 0.57. Time × group interaction was found to be significant (F(3,174) = 18.42, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.24, 95 % CI = 0.15–0.33), according to a two-way repeated measures ANOVA. Post-hoc comparisons showed that the experimental group's AMI improved significantly at all post-baseline assessments (p < 0.001), while the control group showed no significant changes (p > 0.05). At every post-intervention time point, the differences between the groups were significant (p < 0.001).

Pressure pain threshold (PPT)

PPT in the experimental group increased significantly from baseline (3.42 ± 0.48 kg/cm2) to post-intervention (3.94 ± 0.45), with only minor decreases at 3-month (3.90 ± 0.47) and 6-month (3.88 ± 0.50) follow-ups, staying well above baseline levels. Values in the control group varied between 3.39 and 3.44 kg/cm, indicating very little change. There was a significant time × group interaction (F(3,174) = 22.15, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.28, 95 % CI = 0.18–0.36), according to statistical analysis, and post-hoc tests only confirmed sustained PPT improvements in the experimental group (p < 0.001).

Jaw Functional Limitation Scale (JFLS)

The experimental group's JFLS scores (lower scores indicate improvement) dropped significantly from 42.1 ± 11.5 at baseline to 29.5 ± 10.8 after the intervention, with only slight increases at the 3-month (30.2 ± 11.1) and 6-month (30.8 ± 11.5) follow-ups. There were no discernible changes in the control group (43.8 ± 12.2 at baseline to 42.0 ± 12.4 at 6 months). There was a significant time × group interaction (F(3,174) = 19.87, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.26, 95 % CI = 0.16–0.35), with no significant change in the controls (p > 0.05) and improvements in the experimental group lasting over time (p < 0.001).

Secondary outcome

Neck disability index (NDI)

Post-intervention, the experimental group's NDI scores increased from 32.6 ± 8.7 at baseline to 23.2 ± 8.1. They showed slight increases at 3-month (23.7 ± 8.3) and 6-month (24.4 ± 8.5) follow-ups, but they were still much lower than baseline. There was little change in the control group's scores (31.8 ± 9.1 at baseline to 31.2 ± 9.4 at 6 months). A significant interaction between time and group was found to support this (F(3,174) = 16.54, p < 0.001, η2 = 0.22, 95 % CI = 0.13–0.31).

Patient global impression of change (PGIC)

The median PGIC of the experimental group increased from 0 [0–1] at baseline to 65, 6, 7 after the interven-tion, and it remained at this level at 3- and 6-month follow-ups, indicating a persistent sense of significant improvement. Following intervention, the control group showed a slight improvement to 3,2, 3, 4 but by 6 months, it had dropped to 2.2,3 At every post-baseline time point, the experimental group's PGIC ratings were significantly higher, according to Mann-Whitney U tests (e.g., post-intervention: U = 105.0, p < 0.001, r = 0.72).

Adverse events were mild and transient, resolving spontaneously within minutes. in Table 4, no severe or serious events occurred and no participant discontinued the intervention due to adverse events. Discussion

This randomized controlled study investigated the effectiveness of therapeutic exercise in conjunction with transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) for the rehabilitation of patients suffering from craniofacial myofascial pain syndrome (MPS). In addition to significant clinical gains in pain sensitivity (Pressure Pain Threshold, PPT), jaw function (Jaw Functional Limitation Scale, JFLS), neck disability (Neck Disability Index, NDI), and patient-reported global improvement (PGIC), our results showed that the combined intervention produced significant and sustained improvements in cortical excitability, as measured by the EEG Alpha Modulation Index (AMI).

The experimental group performed better than the control group large effect sizes at 3- and 6-month follow-ups as well as right after the intervention. Crucially, there were no severe side effects, demonstrating the intervention's safety and tolerability. The primary result, AMI, increased significantly from the beginning and remained steady over time. The secondary outcomes also showed this trend: PPT rose, JFLS and NDI scores fell, and PGIC ratings showed a consistent perceived improvement. These results imply that cortical neuromodulation and focused physical therapy can result in both immediate neurophysiological advantages and long-term functional enhancements.

Evaluation based on prior research

These results are consistent with earlier research showing that tDCS can improve cortical excitability and alter pain perception in temporomandibular and chronic musculoskeletal disorders.25,26,27 Our study's sustained improvement in AMI is in line with reports of neuroplastic changes resembling long-term potentiation (LTP) brought on by repeated tDCS sessions. These changes may prime neural circuits for more effective pain management and motor learning.15,17

Non-invasive neuromodulation techniques like tDCS have been shown by Silva et al.25 to significantly reduce pain and improve functional outcomes in temporomandibular dysfunction, particularly when incorporated into multimodal rehabilitation programs. Similarly, researches reported that high-definition tDCS can more accurately modulate the somatosensory system, improving motor output and sensory discrimination mechanisms that are probably related to the improvements in jaw and neck function that we saw.26,27,28

The improvement in PPT that we saw in our trial lends credence to the idea that tDCS reduces central sensitization by influencing descending pain inhibitory pathways.29,30 Increased sensitivity to peripheral stimuli and changed nociceptive processing in the central nervous system (CNS) are hallmarks of chronic myofascial pain. In pain-related brain regions such as the insula and anterior cingulate cortex, which are involved in both the sensory and affective aspects of pain, tDCS may lessen hyperexcitability by modifying cortical and subcortical networks.3,31,32

Additionally, functional gains in neck function (NDI) and jaw mobility (JFLS) show that neurophysiological alterations resulted in real advantages for day-to-day living. Cervical dysfunction and craniofacial myofascial pain frequently coexist, so treating both areas at the same time might have had a synergistic effect. Musculoskeletal pain mechanisms are multifactorial, involving both central and peripheral drivers, as highlighted by Graven-Nielsen.29,33 Interventions that target multiple levels of the pain pathway are likely to produce better results.

Mechanism of effect of the tDCS

Central neuroplasticity

Polarity-dependent alterations in cortical excitability are known to be induced by tDCS. Neuronal firing, synaptic plasticity, and intracortical facilitation can all be improved by anodal stimulation over the motor cortex. These modifications may combine with repeated motor practice to form longer-lasting neural network adaptations, which would maintain the benefits over several months.15,16,31,34.

Pain modulation

According to Gurdiel-A'lvarez et al.,26 Galhardoni et al.,31 and Hervik et al.,27 tDCS reduces central sensitization and abnormal nociceptive processing by changing activity in pain-related cortical and subcortical regions, including the anterior cingulate cortex, insula, and primary motor cortex. According to Puntillo et al.30 and Graven-Nielsen,29 these effects are in line with the larger pathophysiological framework of musculoskeletal pain, which involves maladaptive neuroplastic changes. Improved top-down control over nociceptive inputs and a move toward more effective sensorimotor integration could be reflected in enhanced AMI. Central sensitization, a major cause of chronic pain in myofascial syndromes, may be lessened by tDCS through enhancing corticospinal output and modifying thalamocortical rhythms.3,9,13,17,35,36

Improved motor control

Better recruitment and coordination of the cervical and craniofacial muscles were probably facilitated by therapeutic exercise. Motor learning is accelerated when given subsequent to cortical priming, and more effective techniques may take the place of compensatory movement patterns.15,16,37,38 tDCS can improve motor planning and execution by increasing excitability in motor cortical areas. This is due to increased synaptic plasticity within corticomotor pathways. These modifications help improve muscle coordination and lessen maladaptive motor patterns, which are frequently seen in chronic pain disorders.25,34

Psychosocial contributions

Consistently improving PGIC scores indicates that patients felt significant benefits, which may encourage self-efficacy and strengthen adherence to treatment. Because chronic pain is biopsychosocial, these elements are essential for long-term success.12,39,40,41 Prefrontal and limbic circuits can be modulated by tDCS, which can improve mood, perceived control, and the patient's overall perception of change while also influencing the affective and cognitive aspects of pain. These psychological advantages could support the functional recovery and physiological pain alleviation brought about by neuromodulation.25,27

Clinical implications

Treating craniofacial myofascial pain, the combination of tDCS and therapeutic exercise is a non-invasive, well-tolerated, and successful treatment option. The intervention may lessen dependency on pharmacological treatments, which frequently have negative side effects, by addressing both cortical and musculoskeletal causes of pain and dysfunction.

Since long-term symptom control in this population is still a significant challenge, the consistent improvements seen over a six-month period are especially pertinent. Our findings imply that an 8-week program of combined tDCS and exercise may be a practical, economical approach with long-lasting effects. Furthermore, even in protocols involving repeated sessions, the lack of significant adverse events highlights the approach's safety.

Strength of the study

The study combines peripheral rehabilitation (exercise) and central neuromodulation (tDCS), a novel strategy for treating musculoskeletal dysfunction and cortical excitability. The 30-s ramp-up/off sham approach improved blinding integrity and upheld methodological rigor. A randomized controlled design was used in this study to reduce selection and allocation bias and make sure that the observed differences between groups were due to the intervention and not confounding variables. In order to lessen measurement bias which is crucial when assessing subjective outcomes like pain perception and functional limitations blinded assessment was used. A thorough evaluation of the intervention's effects was obtained by combining several outcome domains, such as neurophysiological measures, clinical evaluations, and patient-reported outcomes. A common limitation in a large portion of the tDCS literature, where effects are frequently measured only in the short term, was addressed by including long-term follow-up periods, which showed the durability of treatment benefits.15,25 Furthermore, the validity and reliability of the results were improved by the application of strict statistical techniques, such as an intention-to-treat strategy.

Limitations and future directions

Notwithstanding its advantages, a few drawbacks should be taken into account. First, larger multicenter trials are required to confirm generalizability, even though the sample size was sufficient to identify clinically significant differences. Second, both groups received therapeutic exercise, which may have reduced between-group differences in certain outcomes, even though we used a sham control for tDCS. Nonetheless, this design decision supports findings regarding the superiority of cortical neuromodulation over conventional physiotherapy. Third, we omitted neurochemical and neuroimaging measurements, which might have offered more profound understanding of the mechanisms of change. The neural correlates of long-term AMI improvement may be clarified by future research using functional MRI or MR spectroscopy. Additional limitations, include reliance on a single-center sample, lack of multiplicity control for numerous secondary outcomes, and lack of EMG evaluation for the jaw and cervical muscles, which could offer complementary neurophysiological information.

Furthermore, it is still unknown what the ideal tDCS dosage parameters (such as current intensity, session frequency, and electrode montage) are for treating craniofacial myofascial pain. Clinical recommendations could be improved by comparative studies looking at various stimulation regimens. Finally, even though our follow-up lasted six months, longer-term research is needed to ascertain whether the effects continue after a year and whether booster sessions are still necessary. To improve generalizability, future studies should also use multicenter trial designs, include follow-up durations longer than 12 months, and incorporate EMG results.

Conclusion

This study offers compelling evidence that when tDCS and therapeutic exercise are combined, patients with craniofacial myofascial pain syndrome experience notable and long-lasting improvements in cortical excitability, pain sensitivity, jaw and neck function, and patient-perceived recovery. Central neuroplasticity, better pain management, better motor control, and psychosocial engagement are probably the mechanisms underlying these advantages. In several outcome domains, the intervention yielded significant effect sizes and was safe and well-tolerated. In line with previous research on temporomandibular dysfunction, somatosensory modulation, and musculoskeletal pain mechanisms, our findings lend credence to the inclusion of cortical neuromodulation in rehabilitation protocols for this condition. Subsequent research endeavors ought to enhance stimulation parameters, clarify fundamental neural mechanisms, and assess the methodology in more extensive and heterogeneous patient groups.
