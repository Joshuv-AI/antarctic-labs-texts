The association between empathy and artistic practice: a cross-sectional study with medical students
Authors: Martim Fornetti, Miguel Barbosa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEmpathy is essential to medical practice, and efforts to cultivate it often include incorporating liberal arts into medical curricula to promote it. However, the association between empathy and engagement in artistic practice beyond the medical curricula remains unclear. This study aims to assess the association between medical students´ empathy and their engagement in artistic activities.MethodsStudents enrolled at 11 Portuguese medical schools were invited to participate in an online survey. The survey included questions about their artistic practice, and the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) to measure empathy.ResultsA total of 450 medical students completed the survey. 158 students (35%) were actively engaged in the arts, 118 of which practiced music (75%). Participants involved in artistic activities demonstrated significantly higher scores in the Fantasy subscale compared to their peers. Participants engaged in multiple artistic activities scored significantly higher in overall IRI, as well as in Empathic Concern and Fantasy subscales, compared to those practicing only one artistic activity. Participants who began their artistic practice earlier tended to achieve higher scores in overall IRI and on the Empathic Concern subscale.ConclusionsThe present study highlights a significant positive association between artistic practice and empathy levels among medical students. Further research is recommended to clarify this association, potentially informing revisions to medical school curricula.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-06146-y.

Background

Empathy plays a crucial role in medical education as it is essential for the development of doctors who are more attuned to human needs and the practice of a more humanized medicine [1]. Several studies demonstrate that students with higher empathy scores during medical school also achieve higher empathy scores during residency [2]. Other studies show that the empathy of medical students decreases throughout their training [3], highlighting the importance of medical education programs that promote the ongoing development of empathy. Therefore, it becomes essential to discern the contributing factors to this decline and identify protective factors, particularly those capable of promoting empathy among medical students. High workloads and unsuitable learning environments emerge as primary stressors that can erode empathy [4]. Conversely, factors associated with elevated empathy scores include quality time spent with family, engagement in community service, and participation in arts-related activities [4].

Different artistic courses have been integrated into medical school curricula to enhance empathy. One of the most popular is “Healer’s Art”, an international medical school elective course that increases medical students’ empathy among themselves and toward future patients, using creative arts and storytelling to reflect on topics such as personal wholeness, grief, and loss [5]. Among the various art forms frequently employed in medical education are the performing arts, including theatre and music, the visual arts, encompassing painting and sculpture, and the literary arts, such as poetry and prose [6]. Studies involving several art forms simultaneously have demonstrated positive effects on medical student empathy [7, 8]. Additionally, focused interventions reveal the specific benefits inherent to each art form.

The performing arts

It has long been suggested that integrating an academic discipline of performance studies could help students to become more empathetic [9]. For example, theatre is one of the most prominent art forms in medical education and various studies have explored different ways of integrating it into the curriculum and evaluating its outcomes. Watching plays centred on the lives of terminally ill patients has been shown to enhance medical student empathy and insight towards them [10, 11]. Moreover, the inclusion of theatrical performance and improvisation techniques in medical school lectures is widely appreciated by students and has the potential to enhance empathy [12–15]. Similar approaches have yielded comparable results in residents [16]. Several studies show theatre can reduce student distress [17] and increase overall empathy scores [18–20]. However, this increase might not be sustained over time [21].

Music has emerged as a powerful resource in medical education. The act of simply listening to music was considered by medical residents as a valid approach to medical humanism training [22]. More recently, an interpersonal approach involving collaboration between students and patients in writing original songs has been shown to enhance positive attitudes about care and this approach might also provide a justice-oriented framework for the development of empathic capabilities [23]. However, there is still a lack of studies measuring the impact of music using validated empathy scales.

The visual arts

The visual arts have been intertwined with medicine for a long time, and several studies highlight their potential to promote empathy among medical students [24, 25]. Interpreting and discussing works of contemporary art has enabled medical students to apply their observational skills, develop a tolerance for ambiguity, and engage in otherwise challenging conversations about affective responses to vulnerable persons [26–29].

Despite abundant qualitative studies demonstrating these various benefits, focused visual arts interventions failed to enhance the overall empathy scores of medical students and residents [30–34].

The literary arts

Several studies have shown that literature promotes a sustained increase in medical student empathy [35]. Creative writing seems to be a particularly useful tool for achieving such results [36]. The integration of medicine-related prose and poetry into medical education has shown improvement in empathy for patients’ perspectives and colleagues alike [37–39]. However, literature-based interventions have yet to significantly increase medical student empathy scores [40, 41].

Not all artistic interventions have consistently proven effective in increasing and sustaining empathy scores [32–34, 40, 41]. Nonetheless, a clear positive correlation exists between exposure to the arts and empathy among medical students [6, 8, 42]. However, the role of arts-related activities prior to medical school admission, within student associations and in everyday life remains largely unexplored.

Although previous studies have shown a positive association between empathy and artistic exposure, many have focused on passive forms, such as attending performances or visiting museums, rather than on active artistic engagement like creating an art piece or performing [11, 22, 26]. Active participation may have a stronger impact on empathy due to deeper emotional and cognitive involvement, transitioning from mere spectator to artist [8]. Additionally, past research often overlooks key variables such as the frequency of artistic practice, the age of onset, and whether the engagement was continuous or intermittent. More studies are needed to explore these active forms and incorporate these variables for a more comprehensive understanding. Multicenter studies using validated empathy rating scales are particularly warranted to better grasp the potential of artistic practice in fostering empathy among medical students [6, 35].

The present study aims to assess the association between medical student empathy and their active engagement in artistic activities – artistic practice –, including variables such as the specific art forms in which students are engaged, the frequency of their artistic practice, the age at which they began, and whether their engagement was continuous or intermittent. These variables introduce new perspectives that address significant gaps in the current research, offering a deeper understanding of how various dimensions of artistic engagement may contribute to the development of empathy in medical students.

Methods

Participants

The sample includes medical students enrolled in Portuguese medical schools during the academic year 2023-24: [1] Escola de Medicina da Universidade do Minho [2], Instituto de Ciências Biomédicas Abel Salazar [3], Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade do Porto [4], Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade de Coimbra [5], Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade de Lisboa [6], Faculdade de Ciências Médicas da Universidade Nova de Lisboa [7], Faculdade de Ciências da Saúde da Universidade da Beira Interior [8], Faculdade de Medicina da Universidade Católica Portuguesa [9], Faculdade de Ciências da Vida da Universidade da Madeira [10], Faculdade de Ciências e Tecnologias da Universidade dos Açores, and [11] Departamento de Ciências Biomédicas e Medicina da Universidade do Algarve. In Portugal, an undergraduate student qualifies as a Doctor of Medicine (MD) by completing an Integrated Master’s Degree in Medicine, with a total duration of six years.

The required sample size for this study was determined through a power analysis using an online survey platform. Given the total population of 11,021 medical students, we aimed to achieve a power of 80% (β = 0.20) and a significance level of α = 0.05. Based on these parameters, the power analysis indicated that a minimum sample size of N = 372 was required to ensure adequate statistical power for the study.

Instrument

Demographic and background information

Gender, age, medical school and year of studies.

Artistic practice

To measure respondents’ artistic practice, we used a questionnaire measuring the following variables: the types of artistic activities practised, the frequency of practice, the age at which practice began, and whether the practice was continuous or intermittent. The questionnaire was developed for this study and is available in additional file 1. Specifically, we inquired about activities such as music, dance, theatre, painting, photography, prose, and poetry. For frequency, students rated their engagement on a scale of 1 (never), 2 (annually), 3 (monthly), 4 (weekly) and 5 (daily).

Measurement of empathy

The Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI) [43] is designed to measure empathy. In this study, a validated Portuguese version of the IRI was used [44] and is displayed in additional file 2. This 24-item self-report measure comprises four 6-item subscales: Perspective Taking (PT) – tendency to adopt the psychological point of view of others; Empathic Concern (EC) – feelings of sympathy and concern for others; Personal Distress (PD) – “self-oriented” feelings of personal anxiety and unease in tense interpersonal settings; and Fantasy (F) – tendency to transpose oneself imaginatively into the feelings and actions of fictitious characters in books, movies, and plays. Scores for each subscale are a mean score of the responses to its respective items. An overall IRI score is obtained by calculating a mean of the scores for all four subscales.

Procedure

The survey was sent via email to all medical student associations in Portugal and subsequently distributed to all medical students enrolled during the 2023–24 academic year. It was available for a period of 3 months. All students gave informed consent and responded anonymously. This project was approved by the ethical commission for the Lisbon Academic Medical Centre (CAML).

Data analysis

The data were analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics 29.0.2.0 for Mac (IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Initially, descriptive statistics including means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentages were calculated for sociodemographic and artistic practice variables. Secondly, independent t-tests were used to compare empathy scores between different students’ groups. Finally, Pearson correlations were calculated among sociodemographic, artistic practice variables for each main artistic activity, and IRI overall and subscales scores.

Results

Out of 11,021 students enrolled at the 11 participating institutions, 789 (7%) responded. Of these, 339 individuals failed to complete the survey. The remaining 450 respondents were included in the final analysis.

To assess the distribution of the data, normality tests were conducted to determine whether the variables followed a normal distribution, a key assumption for parametric testing. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was applied. It did not indicate a normal distribution. According to Kline, if normality tests do not indicate a normal distribution, one should examine the values of skewness and kurtosis. If these values are SK < 3 and Ku < 7, the deviations from normality can be considered relatively minor, and parametric tests can be used [45]. Skewness and kurtosis were calculated for the overall IRI scale and all subscales. The values of skewness were all inferior to 3. The values of kurtosis were all inferior to 7. Thus, we proceeded with parametric testing.

The respondents’ mean age, in years, was 22.15 (SD = 4.93). The majority of respondents identified as female (77%), which is representative of the population currently enrolled in Portuguese medical schools [46]. Other artistic activities reported by students included crochet (n = 10, 6%), sculpture (n = 8, 5%) and playwriting (n = 8, 5%).

The respondents’ average age at the onset of their artistic practice was 8.61 years (SD = 4.83). There was a statistically significant negative correlation between the age of start of artistic practice and overall IRI score (r = − .16; p = .045). There was a statistically significant negative correlation between the age of start of artistic practice and Empathic Concern score (r = − .27; p < .001). Thus, students who began their artistic practice earlier tended to achieve higher scores in overall IRI and on the Empathic Concern subscale. No other statistically significant correlations were observed between the age of start of artistic practice and overall IRI or other subscale scores.

Finally, out of the 158 students actively engaged in artistic activities, 99 (63%) reported continuous artistic practice. Students with continuous artistic practice scored significantly higher in the Perspective Taking subscale (M = 3.89; SD = 0.53) when compared to students with intermittent artistic practice (M = 3.72; SD = 0.47); t(156) = 2.13, p = .04. However, no significant differences were found in overall IRI or other subscale scores.

Discussion

The present study explored the association between artistic practice and empathy among medical students and investigated whether factors such as the number and type of artistic activities, as well as the frequency, continuity, and age of start of artistic practice, influenced empathy levels.

The results indicate a positive relationship between artistic practice and empathy among medical students, with the Fantasy subscale showing the strongest correlation. This finding aligns with previous research highlighting a positive association between arts-based programs and the Fantasy subscale [31], suggesting that engagement in the liberal arts enhances the ability to empathize by allowing individuals to immerse themselves in various perspectives - whether those of characters, composers, audience members, or collaborators. Therefore, we hypothesized that medical students who actively participate in the arts may develop a heightened capacity to imagine and understand the experiences of others, including seeing the world from another person’s perspective or mental state, feeling their emotional struggles, and being driven to take action to support those in distress.

Students engaged in multiple artistic activities displayed higher scores in overall IRI, as well as Empathic Concern and Fantasy subscales. Previous research has also highlighted the cumulative effect of engaging in various art forms, yielding similarly positive results [8]. These findings suggest that active engagement in multiple artistic activities is especially beneficial for enhancing empathy among medical students. Therefore, diversifying the range of artistic elective offers to medical students and encouraging diverse artistic expression within medical school settings could play a critical role in promoting and sustaining empathy throughout medical training and into their professional careers.

When examining each artistic activity, dance exhibited the strongest correlation with empathy, showing significantly higher scores in overall IRI, as well as Empathic Concern, Personal Distress – leading to prosocial behaviour [47] – and Fantasy subscales. Previous research has also confirmed the positive association between dance and empathy [7]. Additionally, theatre was the only other artistic activity positively correlated with the overall IRI score, a finding consistent with extensive studies on theatre’s impact on empathy [18–21]. We hypothesize that both dance and theatre, often practiced in groups, may inherently foster cooperation and collaboration among participants, thereby enhancing empathy. Moreover, theatre uniquely involves adopting a character’s viewpoint, requiring a deep understanding and immersion into their circumstances – an exercise that is central to developing empathy [9]. These activities not only promote emotional resonance and perspective-taking but also cultivate the social and interpersonal skills crucial for empathetic engagement, suggesting that incorporating dance and theatre into medical education could significantly enrich the empathetic capacities of medical students.

Our findings further indicate that students who begin artistic practice at an earlier age tend to achieve higher overall IRI and Empathic Concern scores, suggesting early engagement in the arts may foster a greater capacity for empathy and compassion towards others facing adversity. This aligns with evidence supporting the inclusion of creative activities in social-emotional learning curricula to directly promote empathy in children [48]. Moreover, students who maintain continuous engagement in artistic activities exhibit higher levels of Perspective Taking, which is crucial for developing a deeper understanding of others’ perspectives, particularly in clinical settings. These results have significant implications for medical education. While much of the existing research on medical student empathy focuses on electives artistic courses during undergraduate training [6], our study highlights the importance of early-life artistic engagement, particularly during childhood and adolescence. This early exposure may lay a stronger foundation for empathy that extends into medical practice, enhancing the ability of future healthcare professionals to connect with and understand their patients on a deeper level. Therefore, integrating arts education earlier in life and encouraging sustained artistic involvement could be instrumental in cultivating more empathetic and effective healthcare providers.

Limitations of the study

This study faced several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, the response rate was relatively low at 7% (n = 789), a limitation that is often observed in similar research designs [49]. Moreover, the exclusion of participants who did not fully complete the survey further reduced the number of valid responses from 789 to 450. Although we did not specifically assess the potential impact of these incomplete responses, the low overall response rate may have introduced selection bias, potentially affecting the generalizability of the findings. However, by including respondents from all Portuguese medical schools and across all stages of undergraduate training, we aimed to enhance the representativeness of our sample, which is considered more important than the response rate itself [49].

Another limitation is the inherent restriction of cross-sectional studies, which only allow for the identification of correlations rather than causal relationships. This design limits our ability to infer the long-term effects of artistic engagement during medical training. To address this gap, future research should employ longitudinal designs to investigate the ongoing impact of continuous artistic practice throughout medical school. Such studies could provide deeper insights into how sustained artistic activities influence medical students’ development over time. Moreover, intervention-based studies that actively incorporate artistic practice into the curriculum are necessary to clarify its potential benefits and to establish a stronger cause-effect relationship.

Lastly, our study did not explore the impact of passive forms of artistic engagement, such as reading literature or attending cultural events like concerts. Future research in this area could offer important insights into which specific aspects of artistic engagement might be most beneficial for integration into medical education. Understanding the broader spectrum of artistic involvement could help in developing a more holistic approach to enhancing the well-being and professional growth of medical students through the arts.

Conclusions

The findings of this study highlight a significant positive association between artistic practice and empathy levels among medical students, particularly in relation to the Fantasy subscale of the Interpersonal Reactivity Index (IRI). Students who engaged in multiple artistic activities demonstrated higher overall empathy scores, suggesting that a diverse range of artistic experiences may be especially beneficial in fostering empathy. Moreover, earlier initiation and continuous engagement in artistic practice were associated with higher empathy, indicating the potential long-term benefits of sustained artistic involvement.

These results have important implications for medical education. Integrating diverse forms of artistic expression within medical curricula could play a crucial role in enhancing the empathetic capacities of future healthcare professionals. Additionally, encouraging early and continuous engagement in the arts, both before and during medical school, may further strengthen students’ ability to empathize with patients.
