Modeling epithelial wound closure dynamics with AI: A comparative study across cell types
Authors: Xueyao Cai, Weidong Li, Wenjun Shi, Yuchen Cai, Jianda Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSkin wound healing exhibits complex spatiotemporal heterogeneity that challenges traditional static assessment methods. Current artificial intelligence (AI) approaches often treat segmentation and temporal modeling as disconnected processes, limiting dynamic quantification of healing trajectories across distinct cell types. We aim to develop an integrated AI framework combining enhanced segmentation with temporal modeling for quantifying in vitro wound closure dynamics in normal epithelial (MCF10A) and tumor (MCF7) cells, and to compare algorithmic performance across healing phenotypes.MethodsWe implemented an enhanced UNet++ model for wound segmentation in time-lapse images, benchmarked against Otsu thresholding. Temporal closure trajectories were modeled using polynomial regression, Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Regression (SVR), Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA), and Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN). Performance was evaluated via Dice/IoU (segmentation) and MAE/R2 (temporal modeling).ResultsUNet++ achieved significantly higher segmentation accuracy than Otsu thresholding (Dice: p = 8.841 × 10−49; IoU: p = 3.931 × 10−47) with consistent temporal robustness across healing phases. For closure trajectory modeling, RF achieved superior accuracy for MCF7 (mean absolute error [MAE] = 0.48 %, R2 = 0.968) and MCF10A (MAE = 1.73 %, R2 = 0.872), excelling in capturing nonlinear phase transitions and plateau behaviors. TCN showed promise for abrupt changes in MCF7 (MAE = 1.67 %, R2 = 0.698) but failed for near-stationary MCF10A trends. Significant cell-type differences emerged, with RF providing the most interpretable predictions.ConclusionThis integrated framework enables precise dynamic wound monitoring, holding clinical potential for chronic ulcer management and tumor margin surveillance, particularly through its ability to discern cell-type-specific healing phenotypes.Keywords: Wound closure, Artificial intelligence, Image segmentation, Cell migration, Random forest

Introduction

Skin wound healing is a complex, multi-phase process that includes hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling [1]. This process is tightly regulated by intracellular signaling networks and influenced by local microenvironmental factors, such as oxygen levels, extracellular matrix composition, and inflammatory mediator concentrations. Systemic conditions, like metabolic status and immune function, also play critical roles [2,3]. Under normal circumstances, most wounds can heal spontaneously. However, in individuals with diabetes, cancer, immune dysfunction, or advanced age, the healing process is often severely impaired [4,5]. These conditions create a heterogeneous local microenvironment, leading to disrupted cell migration, prolonged inflammation, and delayed tissue regeneration. As a result, healing times are extended, wound repair quality decreases, and severe cases may progress to chronic non-healing wounds [6,7]. Such chronic wounds not only significantly affect patients' quality of life but also place a considerable strain on healthcare systems, presenting a major challenge in modern clinical management [8].

Traditional methods for evaluating wound healing rely on endpoint indicators, such as total closure rates and healing duration, often using static images. These approaches fail to continuously quantify the dynamic changes during healing and overlook the temporal and nonlinear characteristics of wound repair [9]. As a result, they miss rhythmic fluctuations in cell migration and abrupt phase transitions, limiting the understanding of differences in migratory behaviors between tumor cells and normal epithelial cells. The spatiotemporal heterogeneity of wound healing poses a significant challenge to conventional assessment methods. Static analyses cannot accurately reflect the dynamic features of migration, proliferation, and remodeling, particularly during nonlinear fluctuations or phase-specific transitions [10,11]. Consequently, traditional methods offer limited support for clinical decision-making and regenerative medicine. To advance regenerative medicine and improve diagnostic and therapeutic systems, it is essential to extract continuous, multidimensional biological signals from the wound healing process and develop predictive dynamic models.

In recent years, advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) have created new opportunities for automating the recognition and dynamic modeling of wound healing processes, particularly through the application of deep learning in medical image analysis [12]. Deep learning models excel in tasks such as tissue boundary delineation, abnormal region detection, and temporal feature extraction [13,14]. Semantic segmentation networks have proven effective in analyzing medical images of skin, cornea, and tumors, serving as robust tools for identifying and quantifying wound regions [15,16]. Recent studies have demonstrated the efficacy of these architectures across various applications, including enhanced accuracy in tumor segmentation in MRI scans using DeepLabv3+ [17], the strong performance of Attention UNet in segmenting skin lesions [18], and the deployment of UNet++ for automatic segmentation in diverse medical imaging challenges [19]. Additionally, algorithms such as Random Forests (RF), Support Vector Regression (SVR) and Temporal Convolutional Networks (TCN) offer unique advantages in capturing the nonlinear dynamics of biological systems [20]. These models can reveal complex behaviors during wound healing, including phase transitions and temporal dynamics.

However, current research often treats semantic segmentation and temporal modeling separately, lacking a systematic framework that integrates spatial recognition with temporal prediction. This disjointed approach limits the ability to track the full course of wound healing and hinders mechanistic interpretation. Moreover, distinct cell types, such as normal epithelial and tumor cells, exhibit significant differences in migratory capacity and responsiveness to the microenvironment, which can affect the accuracy of AI models but has not been thoroughly evaluated [21,22]. In complex clinical scenarios, such as diabetic foot ulcers or postoperative chronic wounds, the wound environment often shows high variability and non-stationary evolution [23]. Traditional evaluation methods based on endpoint indicators are inadequate for real-time monitoring and stage-specific interventions [24]. Therefore, there is an urgent need for a novel AI framework that integrates image segmentation with temporal modeling. This approach should accurately extract morphological features while capturing the nonlinear evolution of cellular behaviors, providing a foundation for personalized wound management and intelligent therapeutic strategies.

In this study, we propose and validate an integrated AI analytical framework that combines image segmentation with temporal modeling. Utilizing an enhanced UNet architecture alongside various time-series modeling strategies, we quantitatively analyze and compare the migratory behaviors and healing trajectories of different cell types (MCF10A and MCF7) in in vitro wound repair assays. Our aim is to elucidate cell type-specific regulatory differences in wound healing dynamics and assess the applicability and robustness of different modeling algorithms for phenotypic prediction and clinical relevance. This research introduces a novel computational approach for dynamic wound monitoring and establishes a theoretical and methodological foundation for intelligent decision-support systems in complex clinical scenarios, including tumor margin surveillance and chronic ulcer management. Methods

Training an improved UNet-based model

We constructed a wound segmentation dataset based on the publicly available Wound Healing Assay Dataset (WHAD) (https://dx.doi.org/10.21227/f77j-j355), which includes grayscale images and corresponding binary masks indicating wound areas for two cell types (MCF7 and MCF10A). Images were normalized and formatted into a PyTorch dataset. We chose UNet++ as our base model due to its superior capability to capture fine-grained features and multi-scale contextual information through its nested skip connections [25], which is essential for precise wound boundary segmentation. To further enhance edge detection and robustness to scale variation, we implemented two additional architectural improvements. First, we incorporated spatial attention mechanisms to focus specifically on wound-edge regions. Second, we employed a multi-scale input strategy, feeding both original and downsampled images into the network to improve scale invariance. The output layer employed Sigmoid activation for binary predictions, and a composite loss combining Binary Cross Entropy and Dice loss was optimized with Adam and early stopping. Separate models were trained for each cell type, preserving the best-performing weights.

For each cell type, the model was trained for up to 150 epochs with a batch size of 4, using an initial learning rate of 1e-3 and employing ReduceLROnPlateau scheduling. An 80:20 train-validation split was utilized, and training was stopped early if the validation loss did not improve for 5 consecutive epochs. The UNet++ architecture incorporated up to three intermediate convolutional blocks within each nested skip pathway. Each block consisted of two 3 × 3 convolutions, ReLU activations, and batch normalization. Feature maps at different semantic levels were densely aggregated through concatenation, with the number of filters set to 64, 128, and 256 across varying encoding depths.

Segmentation-based prediction and wound area quantification

After training, the best-performing model was employed for frame-wise inference on test images, generating binary wound masks where pixel value 0 represents the wound area and 1 indicates cellular regions. The wound area was quantified by counting the pixels classified as wounds and converting this into physical units (μm2) using a pixel size of 0.65 μm × 0.65 μm. Temporal information was extracted from the filenames (e.g., t00, t01) to align the predicted wound areas with their corresponding time frames, thereby constructing a time-series dataset. The resulting wound closure curves were plotted and saved for further comparison between cell types.

Pixel-level evaluation of segmentation performance

To evaluate the effectiveness of our improved segmentation model, we compared its performance against a traditional Otsu thresholding method as a baseline [26]. For each image, Otsu's method was applied to generate a binary mask, with the wound region determined based on pixel intensity distribution. The predicted masks from both UNet and Otsu methods were compared against ground truth masks using Intersection-over-Union (IoU) and Dice similarity coefficient [27]. Paired t-tests were conducted to assess the statistical significance of differences in IoU and Dice scores between the two methods. Box plots and scatter plots were used to visualize overall performance, while time-resolved line plots were constructed to show mean IoU trends across experimental groups. These analyses provide insights into the robustness and temporal consistency of the deep learning model during the wound healing process.

Quantification and modeling of wound closure dynamics

Based on the wound area predicted by the UNet segmentation model, percent closure was calculated for each image frame as the relative reduction from the initial maximum area: (A0 − At)/A0, where A0 denotes the maximum wound area within each cell group (MCF7 or MCF10A) and At represents the area at time t. Inter-frame closure differences were also computed to quantify dynamic healing rates. To model temporal healing trajectories, frame-wise mean closure values were aggregated for each group. A third-degree polynomial regression was first applied to capture overall nonlinear trends. Additionally, RF was employed as a non-parametric alternative, using an 80/20 train-test split for evaluation. For both models, prediction accuracy was assessed using mean absolute error (MAE) and the coefficient of determination (R2). The fitted curves provided smoothed representations of the wound closure process for subsequent intergroup comparisons.

Multi-model regression analysis for wound closure prediction

To further assess the complexity of wound healing dynamics, we implemented a suite of regression models on the frame-wise mean wound closure values. The models included SVR with a radial basis function (RBF) kernel (with hyperparameters C = 10, gamma = 0.1, and epsilon = 0.5, selected based on preliminary grid search experiments), an Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) model (with fixed order (1,1,1), justified by ADF-based non-stationarity assessment and the short sequence length), and a TCN, designed for learning long-range dependencies in time-series prediction tasks. All models utilized frame ID as the temporal index. For the ARIMA model, fitting was conducted on the first 80 % of time points, with predictions evaluated on the remaining frames. The TCN model employed a sliding window strategy, using the past PAST_STEPS frames to predict the next closure value. Model performance was assessed using MAE and R2, allowing for consistent benchmarking. This multi-model comparison aimed not only to evaluate performance but also to explore the interpretability–complexity trade-off between classical statistical models and modern deep learning approaches in modeling wound healing progression.

Group-wise regression modeling of wound closure trajectories

To visualize and compare wound healing dynamics across experimental groups, we applied RF to the frame-wise mean closure values for each group (MCF7 and MCF10A). Frame ID served as the temporal index, allowing the model to capture nonlinear closure trends over time. For each group, the data were split into training and testing sets (80/20 ratio). Model performance was assessed using MAE and R2, evaluated on both full and test subsets. The fitted regression curves were overlaid on the original closure data to provide an intuitive representation of wound healing progression. This group-wise RF modeling enabled direct comparison of closure trajectories between experimental conditions, offering a robust and interpretable framework for summarizing cell-specific wound healing patterns.

Results

Overall performance comparison between UNet and Otsu

To evaluate segmentation performance, we compared UNet with the classical Otsu thresholding method using the Dice coefficient and IoU as metrics. UNet achieved significantly higher Dice scores than Otsu (p = 8.841 × 10−49), highlighting the advantage of deep learning-based methods in delineating wound regions (Fig. 2A). Similarly, UNet demonstrated superior IoU performance compared to Otsu (p = 3.931 × 10−47), confirming its overall accuracy improvement (Fig. 2B). To further examine temporal consistency, we plotted the mean IoU values of representative experimental groups over time. Across all time points, UNet consistently outperformed Otsu, indicating better robustness and stability throughout the wound healing process (Fig. 2C).

Segmentation performance comparison between UNet++ and Otsu methods. (A) Dice coefficients across all test images. (B) Intersection-over-Union (IoU) scores. (C) Temporal evolution of mean IoU for representative experimental groups. (D) Per-image IoU comparison. Points above the identity line (y = x) indicate higher UNet++ accuracy.

Image-wise evaluation confirms the superiority of UNet

In addition to group-level trends, we conducted a per-image analysis to directly compare UNet and Otsu segmentations. Each point in the scatter plot corresponds to a single image's IoU under both methods. Most points lie above the identity line (y = x), indicating that UNet produced more accurate segmentation masks than Otsu in most cases (Fig. 2D). This image-wise analysis reinforces the consistent performance advantage of UNet at the individual image level.

Polynomial regression captures group-wise closure trends

To capture the temporal trends of wound closure, we applied third-degree polynomial regression to fit the frame-wise mean closure values for MCF7 and MCF10A cells. As shown in Fig. 3A, the fitted curve for MCF7 successfully captured the nonlinear dynamics of closure, achieving a MAE of 3.18 % and a R2 of 0.406. For the MCF10A group, the polynomial model also revealed a general upward trend with moderate fluctuations, yielding an MAE of 6.52 % and an R2 of 0.456 (Fig. 3B). These results suggest that a third-degree polynomial model can provide a coarse but interpretable approximation of closure trajectories in both cell types. However, the relatively low R2 values suggest that polynomial regression alone may not be sufficient to characterize the full complexity of closure dynamics, motivating the exploration of more adaptive and non-linear regression approaches such as RF.

Polynomial regression of wound closure dynamics. (A) MCF7 group. (B) MCF10A group. True mean (blue circles) vs. polynomial fit (red solid line).

RF achieves higher fidelity fitting of closure dynamics

Building on this, we next assessed the performance of RF models. To further improve the fit of closure dynamics, we applied RF regression to model the frame-wise mean closure values. The RF regression demonstrated excellent agreement with the observed closure curve for the MCF7 group, achieving an MAE of 0.48 % on the full dataset and 0.76 % on the held-out test set, with corresponding R2 values of 0.968 and 0.935, respectively (Fig. 4A). For the MCF10A group, RF similarly yielded favorable results, with MAE values of 1.73 % (full) and 1.35 % (test), and R2 values of 0.872 and 0.801 (Fig. 4B). Compared to the polynomial regression approach, RF showed substantially improved accuracy and generalization capacity, particularly in capturing abrupt changes and nonlinear trends. These findings indicate that RF provides a more robust and interpretable model for summarizing group-level wound closure trajectories.

Random Forest (RF) regression of closure dynamics. (A) MCF7 group. (B) MCF10A group. True mean (blue circles) vs. RF predictions (green solid line).

SVR exhibits variable performance across cell types

To evaluate the performance of kernel-based regressors, SVR with an RBF kernel was applied to model closure dynamics. In the MCF7 group, SVR produced suboptimal results, with MAE values of 3.43 % (full) and 3.42 % (test), and relatively low R2 scores of 0.328 and 0.183, respectively (Fig. 5A). These results indicate that SVR failed to capture the non-linear oscillations present in the MCF7 closure trajectory. Interestingly, for the MCF10A group, SVR achieved a much lower test MAE of 1.29 % and a relatively high test R2 of 0.821, despite a negative R2 on the full set (−0.145), suggesting potential overfitting or dataset imbalance (Fig. 5B). These findings suggest that SVR may perform adequately in select scenarios, but its sensitivity to hyperparameters and reliance on kernel assumptions restrict its generalizability across diverse closure dynamics.

Support Vector Regression (SVR) performance. (A) MCF7 group. (B) MCF10A group. True mean (blue circles) vs. SVR predictions (purple solid line).

ARIMA fails to accurately model wound closure time series

To evaluate the applicability of classical time series models, we employed an ARIMA(1,1,1) model to fit the wound closure dynamics. In the MCF7 group, the ARIMA model demonstrated suboptimal performance, yielding a training MAE of 2.45 % and a negative R2 of −4.117. On the test set, performance further deteriorated, with an MAE of 4.06 % and R2 = −0.227 (Fig. 6A). The model failed to capture abrupt phase shifts or local fluctuations effectively. Similarly, in the MCF10A group, the ARIMA model performed poorly, with training and test R2 values of −42.503 and −0.298, respectively, despite an apparent fit on the training segment (Fig. 6B). These results indicate that while ARIMA is interpretable, it lacks the flexibility to model the complex non-stationary patterns observed in wound closure trajectories. Compared to other regression models evaluated, ARIMA struggled to accommodate the non-stationary and abrupt shifts in closure trajectories, highlighting its limitations in dynamic biological systems.

Autoregressive Integrated Moving Average (ARIMA) modeling results. (A) MCF7 group. (B) MCF10A group. True mean (blue circles) vs. ARIMA fit (red dashed line). Vertical dashed grey line marks train-test split (80/20).

TCN partially captures wound closure dynamics

To evaluate deep temporal modeling capabilities, we trained a TCN using 1D-CNN architecture with sliding window inputs. In the MCF7 group, the TCN model achieved a mean absolute error of 1.77 % and a R2 of 0.732, indicating a reasonably good fit to the complex nonlinear dynamics, including abrupt slope changes (Fig. 7A). However, in the MCF10A group, TCN underperformed, with an MAE of 5.11 % and a slightly negative R2 of −0.038, suggesting potential over-smoothing or limited responsiveness to flat trajectories (Fig. 7B). These results indicate that TCN may offer advantages in capturing certain dynamic features in highly variable data like MCF7 but lacks generalization when faced with near-stationary trends.

Temporal Convolutional Network (TCN) performance. (A) MCF7 group. (B) MCF10A group. True mean (blue circles) vs. TCN predictions (purple solid line).

RF consistently outperforms TCN across cell lines

Overall, RF and TCN outperformed all other models evaluated, with RF demonstrating the best performance (Table 1). In the MCF7 group, RF achieved a lower MAE of 0.48 % and an R2 of 0.968, while TCN had an MAE of 1.67 % and an R2 of 0.698. The green curve of RF closely followed the true closure curve, whereas TCN tended to overshoot or smooth certain dynamic fluctuations. In the MCF10A group, RF again outperformed TCN, achieving a lower MAE (1.73 % vs. 8.08 %) and a higher R2 (0.872 vs. 0.048), particularly in modeling plateau-like trajectories where TCN failed to generalize effectively.

Taken together, these findings highlight the superior robustness of RF under varied closure dynamics. Furthermore, they suggest that future enhancements to deep learning models may need to focus on architectural refinements or larger datasets to achieve the stability and accuracy demonstrated by traditional ensemble methods like RF.

Discussion

In this study, we developed an integrated analytical framework that combines automated deep learning with time-series modeling to accurately identify wound regions of two cell types from image data and track their temporal evolution. By incorporating an improved UNet segmentation model alongside various temporal modeling approaches, the framework achieved high-precision wound area segmentation while systematically comparing the performance of different algorithms in modeling wound healing dynamics. Importantly, our results indicate that RF outperformed other methods, highlighting its effectiveness in this context. This study is the first to systematically evaluate the generalizability of various algorithms in predicting wound healing kinetics, addressing a critical gap in developing prognostic tools for tissue repair. The AI models demonstrated the ability to capture phase-transition dynamics during the healing process, overcoming the limitations of traditional endpoint analyses. This advancement provides a robust methodological foundation for in silico screening of regenerative therapies and the intelligent design of personalized intervention strategies.

In terms of image segmentation, our enhanced UNet model integrates the UNet++ architecture, multi-scale input and a spatial attention mechanism, significantly improving edge sensitivity and scale robustness [28]. Compared with the traditional Otsu thresholding method, the modified UNet demonstrated substantial advantages in both Dice coefficient and IoU metrics [29]. It not only provided more accurate segmentation overall but also exhibited greater temporal stability across time-lapse sequences. These results validate the reliability of deep learning approaches in biological image semantic segmentation, particularly in dynamic scenarios that require continuous tracking of morphological changes.

Regarding model performance, we quantified the segmented wound regions into time-series data and explored the predictive capabilities of multiple modeling approaches. While polynomial regression offered a rough fit for nonlinear trends, its explanatory power was limited, particularly for dynamic changes seen in MCF7 cells, where it showed significant fitting errors. In contrast, the RF algorithm consistently demonstrated superior predictive performance across multiple datasets. The advantage of RF lies in its strong alignment with the biophysical characteristics of wound healing. This superiority is not only due to the algorithm's inherent robustness [30], but also to its ability to accurately capture the nonlinear dynamics of the healing process, such as phase-specific acceleration of wound edge migration and fluctuations during tissue remodeling. Unlike traditional time-series models that assume linearity or stationarity, RF can adapt to irregular biological rhythms, making it suitable for complex, non-stationary medical time-series data [31]. The recursive partitioning mechanism of RF effectively models abrupt changes in migration fronts and stochastic remodeling events, avoiding the limitations of classical regression's linear assumptions [32]. Additionally, its bootstrap aggregation mechanism provides strong noise resistance to biological variability, which is crucial for clinically deployable AI [33]. The clinical robustness of RF highlights its potential for future integration into intelligent medical systems, such as real-time clinical image monitoring platforms.

Among the models tested, SVR performed reasonably well with MCF10A cells but showed significantly reduced predictive capacity with the more complex healing patterns of MCF7 cells, indicating its sensitivity to data distribution. The classical time-series model ARIMA struggled to fit the healing process in either cell type, as evidenced by negative R2 values, highlighting its limitations in handling non-stationary and abrupt dynamics [34]. Notably, the negative R2 values observed in both SVR and ARIMA are indicative of their fundamental limitations rather than merely suboptimal parameter tuning. For ARIMA, the assumption of linear stationarity severely restricts its flexibility to adapt to abrupt healing phase transitions and nonlinear remodeling dynamics. This mismatch between model structure and biological process often results in predictions that perform worse than the baseline mean predictor, yielding negative R2. In the case of SVR, particularly on the MCF10A dataset, the negative R2 on the full dataset may stem from overfitting to the limited variance in near-plateau regions, as well as from sensitivity to kernel-specific hyperparameters. These observations emphasize that the choice of model architecture plays a critical role in wound healing prediction tasks, especially when handling non-stationary or low-variance sequences. On the other hand, TCN had some advantages in capturing dynamic fluctuations in MCF7 cells. However, they underperformed with the more stable healing patterns of MCF10A, likely due to overfitting or excessive smoothing from their sliding window mechanism when applied to gradually evolving processes [35]. Notably, while the overall prediction accuracy of TCN was slightly lower than that of RF, TCN succeeded in identifying multiple phase-transition points in the MCF7 healing trajectory. This highlights their strength in modeling irregular dynamics or disruptions in biological rhythms.

Additionally, TCN outperformed traditional linear models in capturing residual information across consecutive frames, making them well-suited for dynamic video-stream analysis [36]. This capability suggests significant potential for clinical applications in scenarios with complex temporal behaviors, such as chronic non-healing wounds or real-time drug response monitoring. From a cell-type-specific perspective, we observed notable differences in model performance between tumor cells (MCF7) and non-tumor epithelial cells (MCF10A). MCF7 cells, known for their higher invasiveness and dynamic proliferation [37], showed highly variable and unpredictable healing trajectories, making accurate modeling challenging. In contrast, MCF10A cells exhibited stable migration patterns and smoother trends [38], leading to much better model fitting across all evaluated methods. These performance differences reflect the AI system's sensitivity to variations in input data complexity and highlight the intrinsic differences in migratory behavior and dynamic responses between tumor and non-tumor cells [39]. This provides a biological basis for optimizing AI model architectures based on cellular behavioral phenotypes. Moreover, the findings support the idea of using AI models to simulate cell behavior and transitions in pathological states, emphasizing their potential for "bio-mimetic" representation of cellular dynamics.

Despite the promising results, several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the evaluation of the segmentation model was primarily benchmarked against the Otsu thresholding method. While this comparison highlights a significant advancement over classical techniques, assessing the model against a broader range of deep learning-based segmentation models, such as DeepLabv3+, Attention UNet, and FPN, would provide a more comprehensive evaluation of its performance. This represents an important direction for our future work to further validate and optimize the segmentation component. Second, the current dataset includes a relatively small number of temporal frames, which may limit the model's ability to capture long-term trends or identify delayed phase transitions during healing. Future work will aim to extend the imaging duration and tracking period to improve both resolution and generalizability. Third, the model was trained without explicit wound healing stage labels (e.g., inflammatory, proliferative, migratory phases), which may reduce its interpretability and clinical applicability. Incorporating phase-specific labels in future studies will enable the development of stage-aware predictive models, facilitating more precise therapeutic interventions. Finally, this study primarily focused on morphological indicators without integrating molecular-level information, such as cytokine profiles, protein expression, or metabolic markers. Future research will aim to create a multimodal framework that combines image-based data with omics-level information, providing a more comprehensive understanding of the wound healing process.

In the future, the AI-based modeling framework proposed in this study has significant potential for clinical translation [40]. With its high degree of automation and robustness in both image segmentation and temporal modeling, this framework can be integrated into high-throughput imaging systems for real-time assessment and visual tracking of wound healing. This integration could greatly enhance the efficiency of developing regenerative therapies and screening drug candidates. In the context of personalized medicine, this model may be used for continuous monitoring in chronic wound situations, such as diabetic foot ulcers, allowing for dynamic quantification of healing progress and treatment responses. Additionally, the model can help identify specific healing phases, acting as a decision-support tool for stage-specific clinical interventions. Looking ahead, further optimization by incorporating multimodal data and annotated healing stage labels is expected to improve the model's precision and interpretability, ultimately advancing the intelligent and personalized use of AI systems in wound management.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed an automated framework that combines image segmentation with temporal modeling for accurate quantification and dynamic analysis of the wound healing process. The enhanced UNet++ model, improved with spatial attention mechanisms, significantly boosted segmentation accuracy and temporal stability. For time-series modeling, the RF algorithm outperformed others across different cell types, particularly in capturing nonlinear dynamics and phase transitions during healing. The TCN also showed promise in identifying abnormal dynamic patterns, making it suitable for complex scenarios like chronic non-healing wounds. Notably, the different responses of the models between tumor and non-tumor cells highlighted the AI system's ability to capture cellular behavioral differences. This provides a strong foundation for designing personalized interventions in regenerative medicine.
