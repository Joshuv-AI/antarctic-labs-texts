Distinct filtering processes shape bacterial and fungal communities and their co-occurrence patterns across garlic-associated compartments
Authors: Tianshu Wang, Bangyan Niu, Shibo Xia, Dali Song, Yi Yang, Hailei Wei, Miao Gao, Wei Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGarlic (Allium sativum L.) is an important economic crop that is often subjected to continuous monoculture practices. Although the garlic rhizosphere is known to harbor growth‑promoting and disease‑suppressive potential, comprehensive investigations of microbial communities and interactions across the rhizosphere, roots, and leaves remain limited. In this study, bacterial and fungal communities in the rhizosphere soil, roots, and leaves of garlic plants grown under long-term monoculture in four major production regions of China were examined using amplicon sequencing, aiming to characterize the garlic‑associated microbiome and explore the potential pathogen‑antagonistic taxa under continuous monoculture.ResultsMicrobial communities and their predicted functions were more strongly influenced by the compartment niche than by the production area, with distinct bacterial and fungal community patterns in each compartment niche. Source-tracking analysis revealed distinct colonization routes for bacterial and fungal communities in the garlic leaf endosphere. Bacterial communities in leaves appeared to be predominantly derived from the root endosphere, while fungal communities in leaves showed a potential direct derivation from the rhizosphere soil rather than roots. Potential pathogenic fungi were more abundant in rhizosphere soil and leaves than in roots. In the bacterial co-occurrence network, two modules abundant in roots and characterized by Pseudomonas and Flavobacterium were negatively associated with pathogen relative abundance. In the cross-kingdom network, keystone taxa included potential pathogenic ASVs from Verticillium and Fusarium, and they were negatively associated with bacterial taxa such as Lysinimonas and Microbacterium.ConclusionsThese findings provide insights into the distinct colonization patterns of bacterial and fungal communities in garlic and their co-occurrence networks under long-term monoculture. The identification of bacterial taxa with antagonistic potential offers candidates for developing biocontrol agents to improve soil-borne disease management in garlic production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00870-2.Keywords: Garlic, Compartment niche, Endosphere, Filtering process, Fungal pathogen, Co-occurrence patterns

Background

Garlic (Allium sativum L.), an important economic crop that is rich in nutrients and has appealing flavors and antibacterial properties, has been developed worldwide [1, 2]. China has the largest garlic growing area, approximately 840,221 ha, with about 20.7 million tons of annual production [3]. Driven by the pursuit of higher productivity and economic profit, garlic is frequently subjected to continuous monoculture, which may result in continuous cropping obstacles such as yield decline and increased disease incidence [4, 5]. Microbial communities are distributed throughout different plant compartments, and play crucial roles in nutrient cycling and disease suppression, and are intimately related to plant growth and health [6]. An imbalance in the plant microbial community structure is often thought to be the main cause of microbe-induced plant diseases [7, 8]. Understanding the microbiome communities and their interactions in garlic can present a sustainable and effective strategy to enhance garlic health and yield.

The rhizosphere of garlic is characterized by a diverse array of beneficial microbes that possess disease-suppressive potential. Garlic roots secrete unique sulfur-containing compounds such as diallyl disulfide that can exert selective pressure on rhizosphere microbial communities, inhibiting stress-sensitive pathogens while recruiting stress-tolerant microbes [9, 10]. Intercropping garlic with cotton or rotating it with pepper has been shown to suppress soil-borne pathogens such as Phytophthora and Verticillium, and a synthetic microbial community comprising six Pseudomonas strains isolated from garlic rhizosphere has been demonstrated to promote radish growth [10–12]. Meanwhile, long-term monoculture often overwhelms these pathogen-defensive capabilities, leading to a reduction in bacterial diversity and the enrichment of pathogenic fungi such as Sclerotium cepivorum, Pythium spp., and Fusarium spp., potentially leading to garlic disease risks including root rot and white rot [5, 13, 14]. Since these pathogens often infect plants via root tissues and can colonize internal compartments, it is essential to characterize microbial communities in the root and leaf endospheres alongside the rhizosphere. Although the microbial communities in garlic rhizosphere have been well characterized [5, 11], comprehensive investigations spanning the rhizosphere, roots and leaves remain scarce.

For garlic cultivation, continuous monoculture is frequently practiced and does not always result in severe disease outbreaks or yield losses, which may be partly attributed to the interactions within microbial communities. Apart from the direct allelopathic suppression of pathogens by garlic root exudates [9], the adaptation of microbial communities to long-term cultivation likely plays a crucial role. While pathogen pressure triggers a “cry for help” response that recruits beneficial microbes [15, 16], prolonged and invariant cropping systems can also drive the soil microbiome to reach a stable ecological state. Zhang et al. [17]. revealed that while short-term soybean continuous cropping induced microbial dysbiosis, long-term monoculture eventually restored the pathogen-suppressive function of microbial communities. The self-stabilization of microbial communities was also reported under intensive radish monoculture [18]. However, whether continuous garlic monoculture can shape a balanced soil microbial community that potentially counteracts pathogens remains unclear. The topological properties of the microbial co-occurrence networks are indicators of the capacity of plant-associated microbiomes to resist or recover from certain stresses [19–21]. Exploring the intra- and inter-kingdom co-occurrence patterns in garlic-associated microbiomes can provide insights into eco-friendly strategies for managing garlic diseases under continuous monoculture.

In this study, the microbiomes of the rhizosphere soil, roots, and leaves of garlic plants grown under long-term monoculture in four main production areas (i.e., Sichuan and Yunnan in Southwestern China, Hebei in Northern China, and Xinjiang in Northwestern China) were analyzed. The objectives of this study are (i) to characterize and compare the bacterial and fungal community composition and distribution patterns across different plant compartments along the soil–plant continuum; and (ii) to explore the inter- and intra-kingdom co-occurrence relationships among microbes in garlic under continuous monoculture. We hypothesize that garlic plants exhibit distinct patterns in bacterial and fungal communities along the soil–plant continuum and that specific network interactions under long-term monoculture may provide hints for the identification of potential pathogen antagonists.

Methods

Sample collection

Soil and plant samples were collected from fields in four garlic production areas: Jianwei County in Sichuan Province of Southwest China (SC, 104° 04 ‘36 “E, 29° 25’ 11” N, humid subtropical climate zone, Ultisol), Longyang District in Yunnan Province of Southwest China (YN, 99° 22 ‘11.33 “E, 25° 53’ 19.59” N, subtropical highland climate zone, Ultisol), Daming County in Hebei Province of North China (HB, 115° 09’ E, 36° 18’ N, temperate continental monsoon climate zone, alluvial soil) and Jimusar County in Xinjiang Province of Northwest China (XJ, 89.18° 59 ‘56 “E, 39° 10’ 39” N, cold desert climate zone, Mollisol). All four experimental sites had been under continuous monoculture garlic cultivation for more than 10 years, with no severe disease outbreaks recorded. Garlic plants were sown in October 2020 at SC, YN and HB, and sown in April 2021 at XJ, at a density of approximately 600,000 plants per hectare, following standard local agronomic practices. The soil chemical properties at each site at the time of sampling are shown in Table 1.

Garlic rhizosphere, root, and leaf samples were collected at the maturing stage: April 2021 at SC and YN, May 2021 at HB, and late August 2021 at XJ. For sampling, eight 2 × 2 m sampling plots were established in the field, each separated by 10 m. In each plot, five garlic plants were randomly selected and excavated to a depth of 20 cm with their whole roots and surrounding soil using a shovel. Plants were gently shaken to remove loosely attached soil, and the soil tightly adhering to the roots was collected as rhizosphere soil. Roots were washed following established protocols [22] and collected as root samples. From each plant, 200 g of leaves were clipped and washed three times in sterilized phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) buffer using sonication (332.5 W, 2 s on / 1 s off for 5 min per wash) to remove epiphytic organisms; the washed leaf tissues were collected for subsequent endophyte analysis. Samples from the same niche within the same plot (i.e., the five plants) were pooled to obtain sufficient material and to reduce individual-plant variability, creating one representative mixed sample per niche per plot. All samples were stored at − 20 °C prior to microbial analysis.

DNA extraction, amplification, and sequencing

Rhizosphere soil DNA was extracted from 0.4 g of soil using the PowerSoil DNA Isolation Kit, and root and leaf DNA was extracted using the FastDNA SPIN Kit (MP Biomedicals, Solon, USA) following the manufacturer’s protocol, directly from samples stored at − 20 °C after collection. The DNA quality and quantity were detected by NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) for subsequent PCR procedures. The primer sets 799 F and 1193R, ITS1F and ITS2R were used to amplify the V5-V7 region of bacteria 16 S rRNA gene and the ITS1 region of fungi, respectively [23, 24]. PCR reactions for both bacteria and fungi were performed using the same 25 µL reaction system, which contained: 0.25 µL of Q5 high-fidelity DNA polymerase (New England Biolabs, MA, USA), 1 µL of each forward and reverse primer (10 µM), 2 µL of DNA template, 2 µL of dNTPs (2.5 mM), 5 µL of 5× Reaction Buffer, and 13.75 µL of nuclease-free water. A no-template negative control was included by replacing DNA template with the same volume of nuclease-free water. Thermal cycling conditions for bacteria consist of initial denaturation at 96 °C for 5 min; followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 96 °C for 20 s, annealing at 57 °C for 25 s, and extension at 68 °C for 45 s; with a final extension at 68 °C for 10 min. Thermal cycling conditions for fungi consist of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 52 °C for 15 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s; with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR amplification products were separated on a 2% agarose gel by electrophoresis to verify the expected fragment size and specificity. The target bands were then excised from the gel, and the DNA fragments were purified using the Axygen Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After quality control, quantification and normalization of the DNA libraries, 250-bp paired-end reads were generated using an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 system (Illumina Inc., CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The raw sequencing data were deposited in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) Database, BioProject ID: PRJNA1213685.

Bioinformatic analyses

Raw data were processed using QIIME2 (v2021.11) according to the official tutorials [25]. The raw sequence data were demultiplexed using the demux plugin, followed by primer removal with the cutadapt plugin [26]. The sequences were then quality filtered, denoised, merged, and chimeras were removed using the DADA2 plugin [27]. The unique bacterial and fungal ASVs were taxonomically assigned by the RDP classifier according to the SILVA database (v 138) and the UNITE database (v 8.0) respectively [28, 29]. The potential functional pathways in the bacterial communities were predicted using PICRUSt2 [30]. FUNGuild (v 1.2) [31] blast successfully annotated the functional guild for 1,518 ASVs (48.8% of all the fungal ASVs).

Statistical analyses

The Shannon index was calculated after rarefying all samples to sequencing depths of 42,342 reads for bacteria and 25,905 reads for fungi. Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on the Bray–Curtis distance was performed using the vegan package in the R environment (v 4.3.1; http://www.r-project.org/). To test the effects of site and niche on the microbial community composition, permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) with 999 permutations was carried out using the adonis function in the vegan package. To evaluate differences in the relative abundance of the top 10 classes and top 20 genera among niches, the non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis test was performed, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple pairwise comparisons. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg method, implemented with the rstatix and multcompView packages. The potential sources of microbial compositions among various niches were estimated using SourceTracker [32]. The rarefied datasets were used as input for SourceTracker analysis to ensure comparable sequencing effort among rhizosphere soil, root endosphere, and leaf endosphere samples. ASV enrichment and depletion were calculated using the edgeR package based on the negative binomial generalized log-linear model [33].

The bacterial and fungal co-occurrence networks were constructed and visualized using the ggClusterNet package [34]. For these networks, the 1,000 ASVs with the highest relative abundances were retained, and Spearman’s rank correlation was used as the co-association metric. Associations were considered significant when the correlation coefficient (r) was > 0.6 and the p-value was ≤ 0.05. Additionally, the Spearman correlation between the relative abundance of modules and predicted fungal functions was calculated using the corr.test function in the psych package. Fungi‒bacteria interdomain ecological networks (IDENs) were constructed using an integrated Network Analysis Pipeline (iNAP, http://mem.rcees.ac.cn:8081) [35]. In this analysis, only ASVs that appeared in more than four samples were retained to compute the co-association matrix using the SparCC metric, with default parameters used. The thresholds for generating regional IDENs were set at r > 0.4 and p ≤ 0.01. The resulting networks were visualized in Gephi (v 0.9.2) using the Fruchterman-Reingold layout. ASVs with within-module connectivity (Zi) > 2.5 and among-module connectivity (Pi) > 0.62 were defined as ‘keystone species’ [36]. The network properties of 100 random networks were also calculated at default parameter values in the iNAP.

Results

The garlic microbial diversity and community structure were more strongly affected by the compartment niche than by the production area

The Shannon index exhibited a consistent pattern across the four production areas in response to compartment niches, where the indices for both bacterial and fungal communities in garlic roots and leaves were significantly lower than those in rhizosphere soil, and those for bacteria were relatively greater in roots but lower in leaves (P < 0.05) (Fig. 1A). The variations in bacterial and fungal community composition (based on Bray-Curtis distance) were explained mainly by compartment niches (bacteria: R2 = 0.57, P < 0.001; fungi: R2 = 0.19, P < 0.001), followed by experimental sites (bacteria: R2 = 0.07, P < 0.001; fungi: R2 = 0.11, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1B).

Different letters indicate significant differences at p < 0.05. B: PCoA analyses of bacterial and fungal communities based on Bray-Curtis distance. C: The relative abundance of the top 10 most abundant classes in the bacterial and fungal communities. D: The relative abundance of the top 20 most abundant genera in the bacterial and fungal communities

The bacterial community was dominated by Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria and Actinobacteria (Fig. 1C). Certain classes, including Acidimicrobiia, Deltaproteobacteria, Thermoleophilia, and Gemmatimonadetes, showed higher relative abundance in the soil than in the roots and leaves, whereas Bacilli exhibited higher relative abundance in leaves than in roots and rhizosphere across the four sites (adjusted P < 0.05). The fungal community was consistently dominated by Dothideomycetes across all three compartments, with Sordariomycetes displaying greater relative abundance in the soil and roots than in leaves (adjusted P < 0.05). At the genus level, the abundant microbial taxa enriched in the rhizosphere soil, roots and leaves were distinct, and the top 20 most abundant genera also presented different relative abundances among the experimental sites (Fig. 1D). Among the top 20 most abundant bacterial genera, garlic leaves significantly enriched Corynebacterium, Pantoea, Serratia, and Streptococcus (adjusted P < 0.05); garlic roots significantly enriched Agromyces, Erwinia, Pseudomonas, and Sphingobacterium (adjusted P < 0.05); rhizosphere soil significantly enriched MB-A2-108. Among the top 20 most abundant fungal genera, garlic leaves significantly enriched Cutaneotrichosporon, Leveillula, Moesziomyces, Penicillium, Stemphylium, and Wallemia (adjusted P < 0.05); garlic roots significantly enriched Hypomyces, Setophoma, Tetracladium, and Urocystis (adjusted P < 0.05); rhizosphere soil significantly enriched Acremonium, Botryotrichum, Chaetomium, Humicola, and Plectosphaerella (adjusted P < 0.05).

Distinct filtering processes for bacterial and fungal communities in garlic

Source-tracking analysis based on all the samples from the four main production areas was conducted to identify the potential sources of the observed microbial communities in each niche (Fig. 2A). An abundance-weighted Bayesian estimate indicated that 54.7% and 81.1% of the bacterial and fungal community composition in roots was derived from rhizosphere soil, 61.1% and 6.5% of the composition in leaves was derived from roots, and 23.7% and 72.8% of the composition in leaves was derived from rhizosphere soil. These percentages represent estimated contributions to overall community composition, rather than proportions of individual ASVs. For fungal communities, rhizosphere soil rather than root endophytes was estimated to be the major contributor to leaf endophytes. Despite the rarefaction step applied prior to SourceTracker analysis to standardize sequencing depth, differences in community composition and richness among compartments may still influence source attribution.

The bacterial and fungal communities in garlic rhizosphere soil presented the most detected ASVs (Bacteria: 27027; Fungi: 2822), and they shared more ASVs with those in roots (Bacteria: 1511 shared; Fungi: 131 shared) than those in leaves (Bacteria: 147 shared; Fungi: 80 shared) (Fig. 2B). The bacterial community in leaves shared more ASVs with roots than with the soil (Roots: 257; Soil: 147), while the fungal community in leaves shared more ASVs with the soil than with roots (Roots: 66; Soil: 80). The numbers of unique ASVs detected in rhizosphere soil, roots, and leaves are 25,480, 13,317, and 1557 for bacterial communities, respectively, and 2663, 149, and 78 for fungal communities, respectively. The ternary plot showed similar patterns in which garlic leaves shared more bacterial ASVs with roots than with rhizosphere soil, but shared more fungal ASVs with rhizosphere soil than with roots (Fig. 2C). The bacterial ASVs that were significantly (adjusted P < 0.05) more abundant in leaves and roots when compared to the soil was attributed primarily to Gammaproteobacteria. The fungal ASVs that were more abundant in leaves and soil than in roots (adjusted P < 0.05) were mainly Dothideomycetes, whereas those showed greater relative abundance in roots and soil than in leaves (adjusted P < 0.05) were mainly Sordariomycetes.

Garlic compartment niches shaped potential microbial functions

PICRUSt2 generated predicted functional profiles for all bacterial ASVs (100% coverage). However, given that these profiles are based on phylogenetic inference rather than direct metagenomic sequencing, they reflect potential functional capabilities rather than confirmed metabolic activity. The potential functional pathways in bacterial communities differed more strongly between niches than within the same niche across sites, except for the HB rhizosphere soil that exhibited a distinct profile relative to the soils at the other three sites (Fig. 3A, Table S1). This indicated similar selective processes for taxa possessing requisite functions in garlic roots and leaves. Notably, pathways involved in the biosynthesis of streptomycin, polyketide sugar units, isoflavonoid, and vancomycin group antibiotics were depleted with niche ascension (i.e., from rhizosphere soil to roots and subsequently from roots to leaves) (Table S1).

Based on the FUNGuild database, functional guilds were successfully assigned to 1,518 ASVs (48.8% of the total fungal ASVs). The fungal community in the garlic system was classified by function guilds and traits, and their enrichment was shaped mainly by garlic compartment niches (Fig. 3B). The plant pathogen was the most abundant fungal guild and was present in a considerable proportion in garlic leaves and rhizosphere soil across the four experimental sites. The relative abundance of saprotrophic fungi was much lower than that of pathogens, and was higher in rhizosphere soil than in roots and leaves. Soft rot was the most abundant trait, accounting for the greatest relative abundance in garlic leaves, followed by rhizosphere soil.

Co-occurrence patterns of microbial taxa in garlic plants

The co-occurrence network analysis of the bacterial and fungal communities identified six and five main ecological clusters, respectively (Fig. 4A1 and A2). In the bacterial network, most of the main clusters were dominated by Gammaproteobacteria, Alphaproteobacteria and Actinobacteria (Fig. 4B1). In addition to these dominant classes, Gemmatimonadetes also exhibited a relatively high proportion in modules 1 and 3, which were found to have higher relative abundances in rhizosphere soil than in leaves and roots (adjusted P < 0.05). In the root-enriched modules (modules 2 and 5) (adjusted P < 0.05), the genera Rhizobium, Flavobacterium, and Pseudomonas were observed to have relatively high proportions, and Sphingobium and Lechevalieria were abundant in module 2 and 5, respectively (Table S2). The class Bacilli also showed a relatively high proportion in the leaf-enriched modules (modules 4 and 11) (adjusted P < 0.05). In the fungal network, the main clusters were dominated by Sordariomycetes and exhibited higher relative abundances in rhizosphere soil than in leaves and roots (adjusted P < 0.05). Dothideomycetes was the second most abundant class, with a greater proportion than Sordariomycetes in modules 1 and 2 in the leaves and modules 3 and 4 in the roots (Fig. 4B2). Taxa assigned to modules 4 and 8 were not detected in leaves, and taxa assigned to module 8 were not detected in roots except for those belonging to Leotiomycetes. The relative abundance of plant pathogens correlated positively with models 1, 3 and 11 but negatively with models 2, 4 and 5 in the bacterial network and positively with all five main clusters in the fungal network (P < 0.05) (Fig. 4C). The relative abundance of soft rot fungi was negatively correlated with modules 2, 3 and 5 in the bacterial network (P < 0.05).

The fungi‒bacteria interdomain ecological networks within the garlic rhizosphere soil, roots, and leaves were constructed to further investigate the co-occurrence patterns between bacterial and fungal taxa (Fig. 5). Among these microbial co-occurrence networks, the network in rhizosphere soil was the most complex, as evidenced by its greater number of nodes and edges, linkage density and links per species, average degree and average path length than those in roots and leaves (Fig. 5A, Table S3). Compared with those in the other two niches, the network in the roots presented the greatest number and diversity of compartments. Notably, although the network in leaves contained the fewest nodes and edges, it showed the highest connectance (the proportion of realized links among all possible links in the network) and nestedness (the tendency for taxa with fewer interactions to be connected to subsets of taxa with more interactions), reflecting that microbes are more tightly interconnected under the constrained leaf environment. The network in rhizosphere soil comprised 322 negative edges out of a total of 984 edges, whereas the root and leaf networks contained 29 out of 207 and 4 out of 54 negative edges, respectively.

Bacteria‒Fungi inter-kingdom bipartite networks (A) and keystone ASVs (B) in garlic rhizosphere soil, roots, and leaves. Edge colors represent the sign of the correlation, and node colors indicate ASV taxonomic classification

The within-module (Zi) and among-module (Pi) connectivity scores of each taxon were calculated to identify the keystone taxa in the fungi‒bacteria interdomain ecological networks at Zi > 2.5 and Pi > 0.62 in each compartment niche (Fig. 5B). In the rhizosphere soil network, six fungal ASVs were identified as module hubs, among which two ASVs were annotated as Solicoccozyma and Verticillum at the genus level (Table S4). Notably, while 19 bacterial ASVs were positively correlated with the fungal keystone ASV belonging to Verticillium, 2 bacterial ASVs belonging to the genera Microlunatus and Nitrospira were negatively correlated with it (r > 0.4, P < 0.01). In the root network, 11 fungal keystone species were identified as module hubs, among which five ASVs were annotated to the genus level and belonged to Alternaria, Fusarium, Hypomyces, Stemphylium and Volutella (Table S5). Negative correlations (r > 0.4, P < 0.01) were found between the potential pathogenic keystone species and bacterial ASVs belonging to several genera, such as Arthrobacter, Hydrogenophaga, Kineosporia, Nocardia, Ohtaekwangia, and Lysinimonas. In the leaf network, one fungal ASV and two bacterial ASVs (both belonging to the genus Pseudomonas) were identified as connectors (Table S6). Both bacterial keystone taxa were positively correlated with ASV_1967 (r > 0.4, P < 0.01), which belonged to the class Exobasidiomycetes, and was annotated as Golubevia pallescens.

Discussion

Distinct filtering processes for bacterial and fungal taxa in garlic plants

Both the bacterial and fungal communities in garlic under long-term monoculture were predominantly shaped by compartment niches (Fig. 1), which is similar to the maize microbiome as characterized by Zhang et al. [37]. This result indicated a stronger selective effect of garlic roots and leaves than of soil type, climate factors, or potential microbial dispersal from surrounding microbial communities. Compared with those in the rhizosphere soil, both the bacterial and fungal communities in garlic roots and leaves presented significantly lower diversity indices (Fig. 1A). This result aligns with the role of the rhizosphere as a microbial hotspot due to the abundant rhizodeposits and the strong selective filtering effects exerted by plant roots and leaves, as reported in previous studies [38]. The effects of compartment niches were more profound on bacterial communities than on fungal communities, whereas the effects of site were opposite (Fig. 1B). This was in line with Liu et al. [39], who demonstrated that prokaryotic communities across different compartments of grassland plants were subjected to stronger compartment-specific selection pressures than fungal communities were, whereas fungal communities were more impacted by geographical factors. For bacteria, genera (such as Pseudomonas and Streptococcus) that include species with potential plant growth-promoting rhizobacteria (PGPR) showed higher relative abundance in garlic endosphere niches [11, 40]. For fungi, Dothideomycetes and Sordariomycetes were abundant in soil but depleted in roots and leaves, respectively (Fig. 1C), both of which include common soil-borne pathogens of Allium plants as well as saprotrophs that play essential roles in the decomposition of plant residues [41–44]. The relatively high abundance of taxa assigned to Stemphylium in leaves and Fusarium in roots may indicate a potential risk of diseases such as leaf blight and dry rot (Fig. 1D), as these genera include well-known plant pathogenic species [45, 46].

The bacterial taxa in leaves were predominantly sourced from roots, with high-abundance shared ASVs accounting for most of the source-assigned composition in leaves, while the fungal endophytes in the roots and leaves were both predominantly sourced from the rhizosphere soil (Fig. 2). In this study, we used “filtering process” to refer to the process through which both host-associated and environmental selection pressures shape microbial community composition as microorganisms transition from one compartment (e.g., rhizosphere soil) into internal plant tissues such as roots and leaves. These results indicated distinct filtering processes in garlic plants for bacterial and fungal taxa. The gradual filtering process of bacterial taxa indicated a tiered compartment-specific selection that allows only certain bacteria to colonize the leaf compartment. This finding was in line with a previous study demonstrating that leaf endosphere microbial communities in cereal crops are sourced mainly from the root endosphere via vertical transmission [38]. Plant secondary metabolites such as benzoxazinoids in maize have been shown to influence microbial community assembly in roots, shoots, and the rhizosphere, and to selectively favor or exclude specific taxa [47]. Although the specific metabolites involved have not yet been characterized for garlic, such chemical-mediated filtering may also operate in garlic as its tissues are known to contain organosulfur compounds such as diallyl disulfide, many of which have documented antimicrobial or selective growth effects [10].

In contrast to the bacterial communities, for the fungal community, rhizosphere soil, rather than root endophytes, serves as the main potential source of leaf endophytes (Fig. 2A). This suggests a potential alternative fungal colonization route from the soil to the leaves that does not primarily involve passage through root tissues, but disperses from soil to the phyllosphere via wind or other vectors and enters internal leaf tissues instead [21, 48]. Compared with bacterial taxa, fungal taxa may be more strictly filtered when dispersed vertically from roots to leaves through internal tissues because of their complex interactions with the plant immune system as well as their body size [49, 50]. Meanwhile, ASVs from the two most abundant fungal classes, Dothideomycetes and Sordariomycetes, were predominantly shared by soil and leaves and soil and roots, respectively (Fig. 2B), suggesting that they may utilize distinct dispersal routes. For example, certain Dothideomycetes species, such as Alternaria spp., the causal agents of garlic purple blotch, are often dispersed aerially via spores and typically infect foliar tissues, whereas many Sordariomycetes species, such as Fusarium spp., which cause garlic basal rot, are commonly soil- or seed-borne and invade the plant via root tissues [41, 42]. In addition, it may also be possible that fungi travel internally through the endosphere from roots to leaves but only establish and become detectable in leaf tissues. It should be noted that SourceTracker estimates depend on the availability of representative source communities, whereas bulk soil outside the rhizosphere and phyllosphere communities were not sampled in this study. Consequently, taxa originating from these environments cannot be directly assigned to their true sources, and may appear as unknown or be assigned to the most compositionally similar sampled source, potentially inflating the apparent contributions of rhizosphere soil or roots to the leaf microbiome.

The potential functions of the bacterial communities of garlic plants revealed significant differences across the rhizosphere soil, roots, and leaves (Fig. 3A). Despite the marked differences in garlic rhizosphere soil, the potential functional pathways within the same niches were clustered together. This result indicated that garlic plants selected bacterial communities with similar functions despite the wide differences among the native communities at different sites. The prediction of fungal community guilds indicated a high relative abundance of putative pathogens in garlic leaves and rhizosphere soil (Fig. 3B). These taxa included genera comprising known garlic pathogens, such as Alternaria (purple blotch) and Stemphylium (leaf blight) [41, 45]. This result aligns with the results presented in Fig. 2, which demonstrate that rhizosphere soil, rather than root endophytes, serves as the primary potential source of leaf endophytes. Pathogenic fungi may migrate from the rhizosphere soil to the leaves through wind or rain splash, thereby entering the vulnerable leaf endosphere. Aside from pathogens, saprotrophic fungi represented another major functional guild, showing significantly higher relative abundance in the rhizosphere soil compared to the roots and leaves. This distribution pattern is consistent with their ecological role as decomposers in soil, occupying niches that are distinct from those of the plant-colonizing pathogens. It should be noted that these predictions are based on taxonomic assignments and public databases, which may not fully capture the functional variation within a genus or species. The specific disease outcomes and causal relationships require validation through targeted cultivation-dependent methods and pathogenicity assays.

Potential antagonistic modules and taxa against pathogenic fungi

The co-occurrence network analysis of bacterial and fungal communities in garlic suggested complex interactions between key ecological modules and pathogenic fungi, where root-enriched modules 2 and 5 were negatively correlated with the relative abundance of both plant fungal pathogens and taxa associated with soft rot traits (Fig. 4). Although these negative correlations could result from several ecological processes such as resource competition and niche differentiation, these results may also suggest the potential role of these bacterial modules in limiting the invasion of pathogenic fungi from the rhizosphere soil into garlic roots. Previous studies have shown that bacterial communities play a vital role in maintaining the balance of interkingdom microbial networks by protecting plants from pathogens [51, 52]. Certain species within the genera Pseudomonas and Flavobacterium have been reported to antagonize plant pathogens through competitive or inhibitory mechanisms [53, 54]. Their relatively high representation in modules 2 and 5 may therefore indicate a potential contribution to the maintenance of garlic plant health. In contrast, the main modules in the fungal network were positively correlated with the relative abundance of plant pathogens. This pattern may reflect pathogen build-up under continuous garlic cropping, suggesting that although no obvious disease symptoms were observed, the community structure already indicates an elevated risk of fungal disease outbreaks.

For the bacteria-fungi co-occurrence network, the rhizosphere soil hosted a more complex network compared with roots and leaves (Fig. 5A), which is consistent with its generally higher taxonomic richness. Although such complexity is expected, the rhizosphere network still provides a valuable framework for identifying potential antagonistic or keystone taxa relevant to pathogen suppression across different sites. The predominance of positive associations over negative ones in the endosphere (roots and leaves) indicated that these networks were dominated by cooperative interactions, rather than resource competition or direct antagonism [55, 56]. The cooperation between microbial taxa, particularly among plant growth-promoting endophytes, could be reinforced to achieve long-lasting beneficial effects on host plants.

Several soil-borne pathogenic fungi, such as Verticillium klebahnii and Stemphylium vesicarium, were identified as keystone species in rhizosphere and roots, respectively (Fig. 5). This could possibly reflect the accumulation of pathogens under long-term continuous cropping of garlic, as shown in the dominance of Dothideomycetes and Sordariomycetes in fungal communities, may have exerted persistent selective pressures that shaped microbial network and forced community adaptation to their presence, although direct temporal evidence was not obtained in this study. In the rhizosphere soil and root networks, a total of 12 bacterial ASVs belonging to several genera such as Microlunatus, Nitrospira, Arthrobacter, Hydrogenophaga, Kineosporia, Nocardia, Ohtaekwangia, and Lysinimonas were negatively correlated with potential pathogenic keystone species. This may indicate a possible role of these bacterial ASVs in limiting the activity or spread of pathogens, while such patterns could also arise from pathogen-driven suppression of bacterial taxa under long-term monoculture. Unlike the rhizosphere and root networks where all keystone species were fungi, two bacterial ASVs from Pseudomonas were identified as keystone species. Members of Pseudomonas have been shown to induce systemic resistance in plants, activating defense-related genes and providing protection against a broad spectrum of pathogens [54, 57]. The occurrence of Pseudomonas as keystone species may result from either selective enrichment during the filtering process or adaptation to the endosphere environment. Leveraging the natural antagonistic properties of bacteria against pathogenic fungi offers an opportunity to design synthetic microbial communities that enhance garlic resilience against diseases. Further in situ studies comparing community composition in healthy vs. diseased plants, as well as in monoculture vs. rotated cropping systems, are needed to better elucidate the processes underlying community assembly.

Conclusions

Our results revealed distinct filtering processes that shape bacterial and fungal communities differently across garlic compartment niches. The bacterial communities were more strongly influenced by the compartment niches, with a gradual filtration of taxa from the rhizosphere soil to the roots and leaves. In contrast, fungal communities in garlic leaves were predominantly sourced from the rhizosphere instead of roots. Two bacterial modules characterized by higher relative abundances of the classes Bacteroidia and Actinobacteria were negatively associated with the relative abundance of plant pathogens, suggesting their potential antagonistic effects against pathogenic fungi. Pathogenic fungi such as Verticillium and Fusarium along with growth-promoting Pseudomonas were identified as keystone taxa, indicating that long-term garlic continuous monoculture may have shaped microbial communities adapted to pathogen accumulation. These findings provide a comprehensive characterization of the garlic microbiome and offer insights into potential antagonistic taxa.
