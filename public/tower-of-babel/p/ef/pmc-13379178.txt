Computed Tomography and Sonography of Spleen-related Complications in Sickle Cell Anemia
Authors: Sahil Gupta, Adity Prakash, Sudhansu Sekhar Mohanty, Basanta M. Swain, Pooja Swami, Swati Khanna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Spleen, being the filter of blood, is affected in virtually all cases of sickle cell anemia (SCA). Therefore, imaging features of the spleen can provide valuable inputs regarding the course and complications of SCA. To evaluate the diagnostic utility of ultrasonography and computed tomography (CT) in detecting splenic complications in patients with SCA.Methods:Seven patients with SCA underwent abdominal ultrasound and contrast-enhanced CT of the abdomen. The imaging features of their spleen are elucidated through this case series.Results:The imaging spectrum varied from splenomegaly, extramedullary hematopoiesis, infarcts, fibrosis, calcifications, splenic sequestration, secondary hemochromatosis, and splenic abscess to autosplenectomy.Conclusions:Imaging was able to diagnose spleen-related complications in SCA and added a valuable dimension to patient management.

INTRODUCTION

Sickle cell anemia (SCA) is a common inherited blood disorder caused by a mutation in the β-globin gene of hemoglobin. It is most prevalent in Sub-Saharan Africa and the Indian subcontinent, where it poses a significant public health challenge.[1] Patients with SCA harbor unstable red blood cells (RBCs) that tend to polymerize when deoxygenated.[23] Sickling episodes, triggered by hypoxia, dehydration, fever, stress, or drugs, cause vessel occlusion, leading to pain crises, organ damage, stroke, or blindness.[45]

Although any organ can be affected in SCA, the spleen, being the filter of blood, is virtually involved in all cases.[6] Spleen gets involved in sickle cell as early as 6 months of age, and by 5 years, a substantial proportion of children develop functional asplenia.[7] Along with hematological workup, imaging of the spleen in SCA, especially ultrasound, is also often requested by referring clinicians. However, the imaging spectrum and role in characterizing various spleen-related complications in SCA have not been covered extensively in previous literature.

Through this case series, we aim to highlight the importance of imaging in characterizing various spleen-related complications of SCA. The authors believe that routine integration of splenic imaging in managing SCA patients will improve overall patient care and outcome.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Patients with SCA, referred to the Department of Radiodiagnosis, underwent abdominal ultrasonography (USG) and computed tomography (CT) for evaluation. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Abdominal USG was performed using a Voluson S10 machine with a curvilinear transducer (3–5 MHz). Triphasic abdominal CT scans were acquired on a GE Optima CT 660 multidetector scanner with a slice thickness of 1.25 mm and multiplanar reformats. Imaging phases included noncontrast, arterial (30–40 s), portal venous (60–70 s), and delayed (3–5 min). Nonionic intravenous contrast was administered at a dose of 1–1.5 mL/kg (maximum ~ 100 mL) at a rate of 3–5 mL/s.

RESULTS

The USG and CT of the spleen in seven cases of SCA have been described below.

Case 1

A 41-year-old man, sickle cell hemoglobin C (HbSC) disease variant, presented with acute pain in the abdomen and in the bilateral lower limbs. Abdominal USG showed an enlarged echogenic spleen. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) abdomen showed an enlarged heterogeneous spleen, and almost the entire spleen was nonenhancing, with only a few patchy residual enhancing parenchyma suggestive of massive splenic infarcts as shown in Figure 1a-c.

(a) Ultrasonography (USG) shows splenomegaly with heterogeneous echotexture. (b) Axial Noncontrast computed tomography (NCCT) shows hyperdense spleen with few hypodense areas (white arrow) within the splenic parenchyma. (c) Axial contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) shows large wedge-shaped nonenhancing area (white arrow) suggestive of a massive splenic infarct. Patchy residual areas of normally enhancing splenic parenchyma (*). (d) USG shows multiple, round hypoechoic nodules in the spleen (white arrow). Such nodules could represent either extramedullary hematopoiesis or microabscess or occasionally infarcts. (e) Axial NCCT shows hyperdense spleen with multiple round hypodense nodules (white arrow). (f) On axial CECT nodules become isointense to the splenic parenchyma (*), confirming that there is enhancement within the nodules. The nodules likely represent extramedullary hematopoiesis

Case 2

A 13-year-old male with HbSC variant presented with chest and extremity pain. Ultrasound showed a normal sized spleen with multiple rounded hypoechoic lesions within the splenic parenchyma. CECT showed a hyperattenuating spleen (CT HU: 95) with multiple hypodense rounded lesions, which on postcontrast phase showed diffuse central enhancement becoming isointense to the splenic parenchyma, indicative of extramedullary hematopoiesis, as shown in Figure 1d-f. Infarcts and microabscesses would have remained hypodense on the contrast phase, as they do not enhance.

Case 3

A 19-year-old male with homozygous sickle cell (HbSS) disease variant presented with severe abdominal pain and sudden drop in hemoglobin for the last 1 day. USG showed a heterogeneously hyperechoic globular enlarged spleen. The CECT abdomen showed blood-attenuating areas within the splenic parenchyma, suggestive of intraparenchymal bleed. In addition, multiple mildly enhancing hypodense areas were seen in the splenic parenchyma, as shown in Figure 2a-c. A clinical diagnosis of splenic sequestration was made.

(a) Ultrasonography (USG) of abdomen shows globular enlarged heterogeneous spleen. Axial Noncontrast computed tomography (NCCT) (b) and Axial contrast enhanced computed tomography (CECT) (c) shows massively enlarged globular spleen with peripheral reticular calcification and poorly enhancing areas (*). The patient had acute drop in hemoglobin with sudden onset massive splenomegaly, consistent with splenic sequestration. (d) USG shows small sized echogenic heterogeneous spleen. (e) Axial NCCT shows a solitary calcific focus (white arrow) in an atrophied hyperdense spleen. (f) On axial CECT, the spleen does not show any measurable enhancement (white arrow), suggestive of splenic infarct

Case 4

A 27-year-old male with sickle cell beta thalassemia (Hb S/ β Th) variant presented with complaints of bilateral elbow and knee joint pain for 1 day. USG showed heterogeneous splenic parenchyma laced with fibrous matrix. The CECT scan showed a solitary calcific focus within the spleen with nonenhancing wedge-shaped areas, suggestive of splenic infarcts, as shown in Figure 2d-f.

Case 5

A 27-year-old male with HbSS variant presented with complaints of fever, pain abdomen, back pain, and vomiting for 4 days. USG revealed splenomegaly with multiple subcentimetric hypoechoic lesions in the spleen. CECT showed splenomegaly with multiple poorly enhancing hypodense lesions in the spleen, as shown in Figure 3a-c. Given the history of fever, the diagnosis of splenic microabscess was considered.

(a) Ultrasonography (USG) shows heterogeneous splenic parenchyma with multiple hypoechoic round nodules (white arrow). (b) Axial Noncontrast computed tomography (NCCT) abdomen shows hyperdense, fibrosis-laced splenic parenchyma with multiple calcific foci and multiple round hypodense nodules (white arrow). (c) The nodules do not enhance on axial contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) (white arrow). (d) USG shows enlarged spleen with heterogeneous parenchyma. Axial NCCT (e) and coronal NCCT (f) show splenomegaly with diffuse reticular or lace-like calcifications within spleen (white arrow), suggesting functional asplenia. Coronal NCCT (g) and axial CECT (h) scan show a shrunken completely calcified spleen (white arrow), suggestive of autosplenectomy

Case 6

A 50-year-old male with HbSS variant presented with complaints of blood-tinged sputum and generalized weakness for 12 days. USG showed enlarged fibrosis-laced splenic parenchyma with areas of calcification. The CECT abdomen showed heterogeneous calcification throughout the splenic parenchyma, as shown in Figure 3d-f. An intraparenchymal air foci was also seen. The patient had functional asplenia.

Case 7

A 19-year-old male with HbSS variant presented with complaints of pain in the chest, abdomen, and joints for the last 2 days. Spleen was not visualized on USG. On CECT, the spleen was small in size, measuring 3 cm, and the entire spleen showed dense calcification, suggestive of autosplenectomy as shown in Figure 3g and h.

DISCUSSION

A number of complications can arise in the spleen in SCA, as illustrated through our case series. The most common is changes in spleen size. While early in the disease course, splenomegaly is more common, as the disease progresses spleen usually becomes shrunken and atrophied.[8] Splenomegaly is usually the result of extramedullary hematopoiesis, whereas a shrunken spleen indicates autosplenectomy with functional asplenia.[8]

Acute onset of splenomegaly is more dangerous than chronic splenomegaly. Normally, the spleen enlarges more along the long axis of the organ. However, a globular spleen with disproportionate increase in width rather than the length may indicate a sudden severe splenomegaly. This can occur when the splenic drainage vasculature is occluded by sickled cells, leading to pooling of blood within the splenic parenchyma.[910] This complication is called splenic sequestration crisis and is a dreaded complication of SCA.[10] On CT, sequestered RBCs can lead to intraparenchymal hemorrhage within the spleen, as was seen in one of our cases. A triad of acute drop in hemoglobin, sudden onset splenomegaly, and intrasplenic parenchymal hemorrhage is highly specific for sequestration crisis.[9] In severe cases, rupture of the spleen can also occur.[8]

Changes in splenic parenchymal echogenicity and attenuation are also commonly seen. Repeated blood transfusions in SCA lead to secondary hemochromatosis, characterized by iron deposition in the reticuloendothelial system.[11] Secondary hemochromatosis is seen on USG as an echogenic liver and spleen and on CT as a hyperdense liver and spleen with attenuation of more than 70 Hounsfield unit.[811] On magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), hemosiderosis is seen as low signal intensity of the spleen and liver on “in phase” images, with increased signal on “out phase” images.[812] Fibrosis within the splenic parenchyma leads to heterogeneous coarsened appearance of the spleen with heterogeneous enhancement on contrast CT. Gamna–Gandy bodies in the spleen are characterized by multiple punctate echogenic foci on USG, whereas on MRI, they manifest as a signal void on all pulse sequences, reflecting their hemosiderin-rich composition.[13]

Calcifications are quite common in sickle cell disease. Calcifications can range from few scattered calcific foci to arboriform patterns to dense calcification. Calcification usually indicates nonfunctional spleen.[12] Vaso-occlusive crisis can lead to splenic infarcts.[14] Massive infarcts can also occur. Infarcts are seen as wedge-shaped hypodensities that do not enhance. Splenic abscess and microabscesses can form due to a depressed immune system. Abscess typically shows strong peripheral enhancement.[12] However, microabscesses can be difficult to differentiate from extramedullary hematopoietic lesions and small splenic infarcts on imaging. In such cases, clinical and lab correlation, such as the presence of fever and leukocytosis with fine needle aspiration of splenic lesions, may be useful.

Thus, Imaging findings guide clinical management by detecting acute splenic crises requiring urgent intervention, characterizing complex lesions for targeted treatment, and averting unnecessary invasive procedures by confirming chronic benign changes.

Treatment of SCA is usually supportive. Blood transfusions are the cornerstone to treat episodes of crisis. To prevent infections, empirical antibiotic coverage and vaccination can be given. Drugs such as hydroxyurea and crizanlizumab help reduce the frequency of episodes of sickling crisis.[15] Stem cell transplantation can offer a complete cure; however, it is associated with significant risks and challenges, particularly in identifying a suitable donor. Recent researches are exploring the potential of gene therapy as a promising alternative.[16]

CONCLUSION

Imaging of the spleen plays a vital role in monitoring patients with SCA. USG and CT can effectively detect extramedullary hematopoiesis, splenic sequestration crisis, secondary hemochromatosis, splenic abscess, parenchymal fibrosis, calcifications, and autosplenectomy. Thus, routine integration of splenic imaging with hematological workup in the management of SCA will improve the overall patient care and outcome.
