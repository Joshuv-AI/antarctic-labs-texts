Therapy-induced senescence rewires the melanoma secretome to promote antitumor immune response and augment cell therapies
Authors: Marina Capece, Evdokiya Reshetnikova, Yinchong Wang, Nar B Katuwal, Daniel de Lima Bellan, Amrendra Kumar, Vijaya Bharti, Reese Watkins, Jordan Krull, Shaopeng Gu, Qin Ma, Arjun Mittra, Anna E Vilgelm
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTherapy-induced senescence (TIS) is a common outcome of diverse anticancer treatments, including chemotherapy, radiation, and small-molecule inhibitors. Senescence is characterized by stable growth arrest and the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), which includes various immune mediators. As the role of the immune system in controlling cancer becomes increasingly appreciated, understanding the impact of TIS on the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) is critically important. Here, we investigated how senescence can be leveraged to enhance antitumor immune responses.MethodsWe investigated the effects of an Aurora kinase A inhibitor (AURKAi), a potent inducer of senescence in melanoma models, using transcriptome and secretome profiling. The role of the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) pathway was investigated using imaging, inhibitors, and gene knockout. We also examined the effect of AURKAi on the surface expression of major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), as well as on signal transducer and activator of transcription 1 (STAT1) activation. In vivo, the effects of AURKAi treatment on the TIME were investigated using spectral cytometry and cell depletion studies. Finally, we assessed combining AURKAi with immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), adoptive cell therapy, and natural killer (NK) cell therapy in murine models.ResultsWe observed significant gene expression rewiring and the secretion of immune-related mediators and chemokines associated with AURKAi-induced senescence, which was favorably modulated by a senolytic BCL-2/xL inhibitor navitoclax. Mechanistically, AURKAi induced the formation of micronuclei linked with the activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, which, in turn, initiated pro-inflammatory transcriptional programs. Senescent melanoma cells exhibited increased surface expression of MHC-I and PD-L1, along with interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) and STAT1 activation, indicating enhanced immunogenicity. In vivo, AURKAi treatment significantly enriched the TIME with activated CD8+ T cells and NK cells, with depletion studies confirming their critical role in antitumor effects. While combining AURKAi with immune checkpoint blockade was not beneficial, AURKAi pretreatment significantly augmented T and NK cell therapies in murine models, resulting in enhanced tumor control and prolonged survival.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that senescence, specifically when induced by an AURKAi, can be harnessed to promote effector cell engagement in immune-cold tumors, thereby enhancing responsiveness to cell therapies.

Introduction

Melanoma, the most aggressive and lethal form of skin cancer, continues to pose a formidable clinical challenge due to its propensity for early metastasis and intrinsic resistance to conventional treatments such as radiation and chemotherapy. Historically, despite advances in early detection and surgical interventions, metastatic melanoma was among the malignancies considered notoriously difficult to treat, with poor long-term survival rates in advanced stages.1 2 The advent of immunotherapies has transformed the therapeutic landscape for melanoma by leveraging the body’s immune system to recognize and eradicate tumor cells. Among these, immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), particularly antiprogrammed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) (eg, pembrolizumab, nivolumab) and anticytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (eg, ipilimumab) therapies, have become the cornerstone of treatment, achieving durable responses in a subset of patients.3–6 Recent 10-year follow-up data show an impressive 71.9 months median overall survival in patients with melanoma who received nivolumab and ipilimumab.7 An additional benefit can be achieved by the sequential administration of immunotherapy and targeted therapy, such as BRAFV600E and MEK inhibitors.8 9 Furthermore, additional promising immunotherapy strategies are now emerging, including advanced cancer vaccines and novel biomarkers for patient stratification.10 These exciting clinical data demonstrate the outstanding potential of therapeutic strategies that harness antitumor immune responses by combining immune therapies with drugs targeting tumor cells. These and other new combination strategies would help address the needs of patients with melanoma who initially fail to respond to immunotherapy (primary resistance) or develop resistance over time (acquired resistance).11–13

Adoptive cell therapy (ACT), including tumor-infiltrating lymphocyte (TIL) therapy14–16 and engineered T-cell approaches (eg, chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T) and T-cell receptor engineered T cells (TCR-T)),17 18 has emerged as a promising alternative, particularly for patients refractory to checkpoint inhibitors.19–21 ACT exploits the cytotoxic potential of autologous or allogeneic T cells to directly target tumor antigens. While some patients achieve remarkable and sustained remissions, significant challenges persist, including T-cell exhaustion, limited persistence of infused T cells in vivo, immune evasion mechanisms employed by tumors (eg, loss of antigen presentation), and the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME).22–24

Several strategies have been suggested to address these limitations. These include combination therapies that modulate the TME, reinvigorate T-cell function, or target alternative pathways.25–27 Here, we explored therapy-induced senescence (TIS) and whether it can be harnessed to counteract immune evasion. TIS has recently gained attention as a paradoxical yet potentially exploitable phenomenon in modulating TME and antitumor immune response.28 29

TIS refers to a stable cell-cycle arrest induced by cancer therapies, including chemotherapy, radiation, or targeted agents (eg, CDK4/6 inhibitors, Aurora kinase A inhibitor (AURKAi)).30–32 Unlike apoptotic cells, senescent tumor cells remain metabolically active and secrete a complex array of pro-inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, and matrix-remodeling enzymes, collectively referred to as the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP).33 34 SASP is a double-edged sword. On one hand, SASP factors can promote tumor clearance by recruiting and activating immune cells and enhancing antigen presentation.35 36 Notably, recent studies have indicated that strategically inducing senescence can enhance tumor immunogenicity, improve major histocompatibility complex class I (MHC-I) expression, and sensitize melanoma cells to interferon (IFN)γ signaling, a crucial mediator of T-cell cytotoxicity.37 38 This could be a result of the activation of the pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) in response to the damage induced by cancer therapeutics. While typically studied in the context of viral infections, PRRs also respond to aberrant self RNA/DNA,39 40 such as retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I) and melanoma differentiation-associated protein 5 (MDA5), and mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS) that can be activated by cytosolic RNA,41 and cyclic GMP-AMP synthase-stimulator of interferon genes (cGAS-STING) activated by cytosolic DNA.42 These innate immune sensors, in turn, activate immune-related transcription factors such as nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) and interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3), which drive the expression of cytokines and chemokines that recruit and activate immune cells.43–45

On the other hand, chronic SASP may facilitate tumor growth by providing growth signals for non-senescent cells, recruiting immunosuppressive cells, or inducing tumor-promoting fibrosis.46 To mitigate the potential tumor-promoting effects of lingering TIS, a one-two punch therapeutic approach has been suggested. This strategy involves inducing senescence and administering a senolytic drug that selectively kills senescent cells while sparing non-senescent cells.30

To understand how the TIS program can rewire melanoma cells and impact the tumor immune microenvironment, we employed AURKAi to induce senescence in murine and human melanoma cell lines. AURKA plays a crucial role in cell division, regulating key processes like centrosome maturation, mitotic spindle assembly, and chromosome segregation.47 In this study, we used alisertib, a specific small-molecule AURKAi that demonstrated preclinical antitumor activity across a wide range of solid tumors and hematological malignancies.48–52 AURKAi disrupts chromosome segregation and mitotic progression, and induces growth arrest, senescence, and apoptosis in various cancer models.53–57 It was also reported to induce mitotic slippage resulting in polyploidy.55–57 Alisertib is being investigated in phase I–III clinical trials, both as a single agent and in combination with other therapies, for indications such as breast cancer, small cell lung cancer, and other advanced solid tumors (https://www.cancer.gov/research/participate/clinical-trials/intervention/alisertib). We have previously demonstrated prominent induction of melanoma senescence in vitro and in vivo by AURKAi treatment.32 58–61 We also identified the BCL-2/xL inhibitor navitoclax as a senolytic that works well for one-two punch therapy with alisertib.60

By analyzing the transcriptome and secretome of senescent melanoma cells, we uncovered how the senescence pathways induced by senogenic AURKAi modulate antitumor immunity and immunotherapy responses and how the addition of a senolytic drug, navitoclax, impacts these effects. Using preclinical models and immune cell assessments, we identified immune subsets that were enriched by senescence treatment. We demonstrated that combining senescence-inducing drugs with ACT can enhance treatment outcomes and survival in a murine melanoma model.

This work proposes a novel ‘senescence priming’ concept to sensitize resistant, immune-excluded melanomas to cell therapies.

Results

Transcriptional rewiring of melanoma cells following AURKAi-induced senescence

To investigate TIS in melanoma, we treated A375 human melanoma cells with the AURKAi alisertib. AURKAi treatment significantly reduced 5-ethynyl-2'-deoxyuridine (EdU) incorporation (EdU+ cells), indicating a decrease in DNA synthesis (figure 1A), and increased the proportion of polyploid cells with >4n DNA content (figure 1A,B). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction (~60%) in S-phase population and increased polyploidy after AURKAi (figure 1C). AURKAi also induced morphological and biochemical changes typical of senescent phenotype, such as increased cell size and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining (figure 1D) in multiple melanoma cell lines, including human (A375, Hs294T, WM115), patient-derived (PDC9164), and mouse (B16F0). Western blot analysis revealed an accumulation of the canonical cell cycle arrest and senescence regulators, p53 and p21. In contrast, the expression of cyclin A2, which is essential for S-phase cell cycle progression and G2/M transition, was significantly downregulated post-treatment (figure 1E). We also observed significantly fewer cells in cultures treated with AURKAi than in vehicle controls and increased cell size, characteristic of senescence, in all tested melanoma cell lines (figure 1F). Lastly, AURKAi induced the secretion of a common SASP cytokine, interleukin (IL)-8 (figure 1G). In conclusion, we observed cell cycle arrest, based on reduced cell numbers and DNA synthesis, increased p21 expression, decreased cyclin A2 expression, as well as senescence, and SASP in AURKAi-treated melanoma cells.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis of A375 cells treated with AURKAi identified 1025 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 362 upregulated and 662 downregulated genes (online supplemental table S1). The DEGs are summarized by scatter and volcano plots (figure 1H,I) and heatmaps (figure 1J–L). Genes associated with inflammation (eg, IL1B) and cell cycle progression (eg, CCNA2, CENPA) were among the most significantly altered (figure 1I). The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of AURKAi downregulated genes revealed significant enrichment of Gene Ontology (GO) terms associated with mitosis and cell cycle, such as ‘mitotic spindle organization,’ ‘chromosome segregation,’ and ‘cell division’ (figure 1K and M). AURKAi-upregulated genes showed an enrichment of GO terms related to inflammation and immunity, such as ‘innate immunity,’ ‘cytokine signaling in immune system,’ and ‘positive regulation of NF-κB signal transduction’ (figure 1L and N).

Secretome profiling of senescent melanoma cells reveals an increased secretion of proteins involved in immune cell recruitment and regulation

We next tested whether AURKAi-mediated activation of the immune transcription program translates into pro-inflammatory secretome using a multiplex ELISA assay of 200 cytokines, chemokines, and other immune modulators (figure 2A,B, online supplemental table S2). This assay detected 82 proteins that were differentially secreted by AURKAi-treated cells compared with vehicle-treated cells. The majority of these proteins (76%) were upregulated by AURKAi, indicating increased secretory activity (figure 2C). Notably, the addition of a senolytic agent, such as BCL-2/xL inhibitor navitoclax (BCLi), downmodulated 73% of the AURKAi-responsive proteins (figure 2A). This was further confirmed by principal component analysis (PCA) (figure 2B), which showed a clear separation between AURKAi-treated and control (vehicle) groups by PC1, while AURKAi-treated and BCLi-treated samples showed intermediate PC1 values.

OmicsNet protein-protein interaction network analysis of AURKAi-upregulated proteins revealed three distinct functional modules: (1) the top left ‘immune cell recruitment’ module, enriched with chemokines; (2) the bottom ‘stroma remodeling’ module, enriched for stromal cytokines and growth factors, such as transforming growth factor (TGF)-β1, vascular endothelial growth factor A (VEGFA), and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1); and (3) the top right ‘immune-modulation’ module, including epidermal growth factor receptor as a central hub and several immunoregulatory molecules, such as IL-1β, IL-9, IL-11, IL-17A, IL-28A (type III IFN), migration-inhibitory factor (MIF), tumor necrosis factor (TNF), and others (figure 2D). Quantitative profiling identified TGF-β superfamily proteins, such as TGF-β1, bone morphogenetic protein 5 (BMP-5), activin A, and a regulator of Wnt signaling, Dickkopf-1 (DKK-1), as the most abundant growth factors, while c-x-c motif chemokine ligand 16 (CXCL16), IL-8 (CXCL8), and regulated on activation, normal t cell expressed and secreted (RANTES) or c-c motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) were the predominant AURKAi-induced chemokines (figure 2E). Notably, the addition of a senolytic selectively refined this secretome. While immune-evasive proteins, such as IL-6, TGF-β, DKK-1, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, were suppressed, critical mediators of T and natural killer (NK) cell-mediated immunity were spared or only partially affected. These included the chemokines CXCL11, CCL5, and CXCL16, the adhesion molecule intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1), and the stress ligand mhc class i polypeptide-related sequence a (MICA) (figure 2F). In summary, AURKAi induced a complex secretory phenotype. The addition of senolytic skewed secretion to enrich immune cell recruitment factors over immune evasion.

AURKA inhibition promotes immune cell recruitment to drive antitumor immunity

To determine the in vivo significance of the AURKAi-induced pro-immunogenic secretome, we treated B16F0 melanoma-bearing mice with vehicle, BCLi, AURKAi, or a combination. Administration of AURKAi significantly attenuated tumor growth by day 13, and the co-treatment with BCLi (senolytic) further enhanced this effect (online supplemental figure S1A–D). Tumor immune infiltrate analysis using high-dimensional spectral cytometry (online supplemental table S3) coupled with uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) dimension reduction and FlowSOM clustering algorithms, identified 12 distinct clusters of CD45+ tumor-infiltrating immune cells (figure 3A–D and online supplemental figure S2A,B). Expression of the tested markers across UMAP is shown in figure 3C. AURKAi treatment induced a robust enrichment of total CD45+ leukocytes in the TME compared with vehicle treatment. This effect was most pronounced at an earlier time point (day 7). BCLi had minimal impact on the early time point and limited leukocyte infiltration at a later time point (day 13) (figure 3D).

Within the myeloid and antigen-presenting cell (APC) compartment, AURKAi increased the percentages of immature dendritic cells (DCs) and macrophages (MPh) (clusters 2 and 9, CD11c+ and F4/80+) and mature DCs (cluster 1, CD11c+MHC-II+) across both time points and this was not affected by BCLi. The late-stage enrichment of antigen-presenting MPh (F4/80+MHC-II+, cluster 6) and proliferating DCs (cluster 12, CD11c+MHC-II+Ki-67+) by AURKAi was downmodulated by BCLi. Monocytes (cluster 8, CD11b+, GR-1loCD11c−F4/80−) and other myeloid cells (cluster 10, CD11b+) were also induced by AURKAi. Within the lymphoid/effector compartment, AURKAi enriched NK/NKT cells (cluster 4, NK1.1+) by day 13, and this was downmodulated by BCLi co-treatment (figure 3C). While total CD8+ T-cell frequencies remained stable, AURKAi significantly increased the proportion of activated (CD69+) and proliferating (Ki67+) CD8+ T, activated (CD69+) CD4 T cells, regulatory T cells (FoxP3+CD4+), and NK cells, which were not affected by co-treatment with BCLi (figure 3E,F, online supplemental figure S2C,D).

We next investigated whether AURKAi-induced CD8+ T cells, NK cells, or CD4+ T cells were important for controlling tumor growth. Depletion of either CD8+ T or NK cells significantly decreased survival in AURKAi+BCLi-treated mice, while CD4+ T-cell depletion had minimal effect (figure 3G). These findings confirm that CD8+ T cells and NK cells are necessary for AURKAi-induced and BCLi-induced antitumor effects.

Therapy-induced senescence augments adoptive cell transfer therapy and prolongs survival in a murine melanoma model

Based on pro-immunogenic secretome and TME remodeling observed with AURKAi, we hypothesized that it may augment immunotherapy responses. We administered AURKAi in combination with αPD-1+α4-1BB antibodies to model ICB therapy (online supplemental figure S3A). However, we did not observe improved efficacy (figure 4A). High-dimensional spectral cytometry (online supplemental table S4) revealed that concurrent administration of AURKAi along with ICB limited the upregulation of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) and MHC-II on melanoma tumor cells (online supplemental figure S3B). Analyzing the immune compartment in treated tumors, we observed that the addition of AURKAi to ICB inhibited the infiltration of CD45+ cells (online supplemental figure S3C). Co-treatment with AURKAi and ICB predominantly impaired the expansion of myeloid cells (CD11b+) and, specifically, APCs, such as macrophages (F4/80+ cells), conventional DCs (CD11c+), and cross-presenting DCs (CD8+MHC-II+CD11c+) (online supplemental figure S3D,E). Moreover, the addition of AURKAi to ICB compromised the proliferative capacity of host T cells as shown by a reduction in Ki67+ CD4+ T cells and Ki67+ CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure S3E). This observation was confirmed with an in vitro experiment that showed that AURKAi treatment impairs the proliferation of stimulated primary T cells (online supplemental figure S3F). Thus, concurrent AURKAi exerted an antiproliferative effect on the host’s immune cells, limiting ICB benefit.

We next hypothesized that a sequential AURKAi pretreatment strategy, as well as the use of ex vivo-activated effector cells that do not require activation by host APCs, might overcome the host antiproliferative effects while leveraging the senescent tumor secretome. To test this, we established a model of ACT with ex vivo-expanded TIL by transferring melanoma-specific T cells from pmel-1 transgenic mice into B16 melanoma-bearing C57Bl/6 Thy1.2+. After 7 days of AURKAi or vehicle treatment, mice received a myeloablative treatment with cycloheximide and an infusion of in vitro primed Thy1.1+ pmel-1 (donor) T cells (figure 4B). While ACT alone only temporarily controlled tumor growth, AURKAi pretreatment resulted in a sustained antitumor response and significantly longer survival (figure 4C–E).

To investigate the impact of AURKAi pretreatment on early immunological responses, we repeated the experiment and harvested tumors 5 days after ACT administration (online supplemental figure S4A,B). About 83.3% of the AURKAi and ACT-treated mice exhibited tumor regression (50%) or stable disease (33.3%) (figure 4F). The AURKAi+ACT treatment group exhibited the greatest tumor volume reduction, although the difference did not reach statistical significance, possibly due to the short treatment duration (figure 4G). Spectral cytometry of the tumor immune infiltrate (online supplemental table S5) was used to study how AURKAi pretreatment affected host and donor immune responses. We observed an inverse correlation between tumor size and total infiltrating leukocytes (CD45+ cells), with smaller tumors (predominantly from the AURKAi+ACT group) exhibiting the highest proportion of CD45+ cells (online supplemental figure S4C). Similarly, tumor volume inversely correlated with markers of host T and NK cell activation (CD44+, CD69+, Ki67+) and infiltration of host Th1 CD4+ T cells (T-bet+CD4+) (online supplemental figure S4C,D). These data show that the therapeutic efficacy of donor T cells was associated with host immune activation. Of note, unlike the concurrent dosing with ICB, the sequential AURKAi followed by ACT treatment strategy did not inhibit tumor infiltration by host CD45+ leukocytes (online supplemental figure S5A,B) or any specific immune subset (online supplemental figure S5C,D), and did not limit host immune cell activation based on CD69 marker analyses (online supplemental figure S5D).

Critically, while effective donor Thy1.1+ T-cell infiltration was observed in both ACT groups (online supplemental figure S5C), AURKAi pretreatment qualitatively enhanced their function. Donor CD8+ T cells from the combination group exhibited higher CD44 expression (figure 4H,I), suggesting enhanced activation, and their activated subsets (CD44+, CD69+) showed a significant inverse correlation with tumor size (online supplemental figure S4C). Collectively, these data show that priming with a short-term AURKAi treatment improves tumor response to T-cell transfer.

Enhancing NK cell activity in melanoma through AURKA blockade

We next sought to determine whether AURKAi treatment could enhance other types of cell therapies, such as NK cell therapies. To evaluate NK cell-mediated cytotoxicity against melanoma, we established a co-culture system using NK-92 cells and melanoma patient-derived organoids (PDOs) that we generated previously.62–64 AURKAi pretreatment significantly increased the recruitment of NK-92 and enhanced PDO cell death in two tested models: PDO2132 (figure 5A–D) and PDO0431 (figure 5E,F). Melanoma patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) implanted into human IL15-expressing NSG-SGM mice were used to test the combined AURKAi and NK therapy in vivo. Adoptive transfer of donor NK cells alone did not impede tumor growth, while AURKAi monotherapy was sufficient to slow tumor proliferation (figure 5G,H). A group that received concurrent administration of AURKAi and donor NK cells exhibited the most pronounced inhibition of tumor growth (figure 5G). Furthermore, we observed that the combination stabilized tumor growth in a subset of mice, whereas this was not observed in the AURKAi single-agent or vehicle groups (see guidelines and arrow in figure 5H, indicating tumors below 400 mm3 as late as day 45 after treatment initiation).

AURKA inhibition induces micronuclei and activates cGAS-STING signaling to promote an immune transcriptional program

We next explored the mechanism explaining the immunogenic properties of AURKAi-induced senescence. We observed an increase in micronuclei formation in AURKAi-induced senescent B16F0 and A375 cells (figure 6A,B). Given the upregulation of the ‘Regulation of Defense Response to Virus by Host’ pathway by AURKAi in our RNA-seq data (figure 1), we investigated key viral-sensing PRR pathways, such as RIG-I-MDA5-MAVS and cGAS-STING. While AURKAi treatment activated NF-κB and IRF3-regulated transcription in reporter cell lines, knockout of RNA sensors RIG-I, MDA5, or MAVS did not abrogate this effect (figure 6C,D). We next focused on DNA sensors cGAS-STING. AURKAi treatment increased the expression of cytosolic cGAS, which frequently co-localized with micronuclei (figure 6E,F). Moreover, STING knockout abrogated AURKAi- and AURKAi and BCLi-induced IRF transcriptional activity (figure 6G). In addition, western blot analysis showed increased activating phosphorylation of IRF3 in human (figure 6H) and mouse melanoma cells (figure 6I). The IRF activation was retained after adding senolytic BCLi (figure 6H,I), showing that adding a senolytic does not compromise STING-mediated IRF activation in AURKAi-treated cells.

AURKAi also increased the proportion of PD-L1-expressing cells, and PD-L1 surface expression levels were upregulated to an extent comparable to that induced by IFNγ stimulation alone (figure 7D–F). Given that MHC-I and PD-L1 are canonical targets of the JAK-STAT pathway, we analyzed the phosphorylation status of immune-activating STAT1 and immunosuppressive STAT3. AURKAi-treated cells displayed a dramatic increase in phosphorylated and total STAT1, with a concurrent repression of phosphorylated STAT3 (figure 7G,H). These data show that AURKAi shifts the STAT1/STAT3 balance toward immune-stimulating STAT1 (figure 7I).

Finally, we confirmed the generality of our findings across multiple models, including melanoma patient-derived cells (PDC9164), several additional human melanoma cell lines (Hs294T, WM115), and mouse melanoma cells (B16F0). In all cases, AURKAi significantly increased the secretion of T-cell-recruiting chemokines CCL5, CXCL9, and CXCL10 (figure 7J). Together, these data demonstrate that AURKAi promotes tumor-cell intrinsic immune pathways across diverse melanoma models, as shown in the summary schematic (figure 7K).

Discussion

This study provides preclinical evidence that senescence, specifically that induced by AURKA inhibition, can be harnessed to promote the immune-active TME and enhance melanoma response to ACT.

We demonstrated that therapy-induced senescence (TIS) reprograms melanoma cell interactions with the immune system, leading to enhanced antitumor immune activity. In accordance with documented senescence-associated changes,65 AURKAi-induced senescence led to downregulation of cell cycle genes and upregulation of genes involved in inflammation, immunity, and secretory signaling, suggesting SASP induction. Consistent with this, we described the immunogenic secretome of melanoma senescence cells, rich in chemokines that attract monocytes and effector T and NK cells (eg, CCL4, CXCL10, CXCL16, CXCL20). In vivo, this feature translated into a robust recruitment of leukocytes, particularly monocytes, DCs, and activated and proliferating CD8+ T and NK cells, resulting in CD8+ T-cell-dependent and NK cell-dependent antitumor response in our preclinical model. In the context of the current clinical literature, expression of these and other secreted cytokines has been linked with good response to immunotherapy in various cancers.66–68

However, SASP is a double-edged sword and can include immunosuppressive molecules that may foster immune escape.34 The presence of IL-6 is particularly concerning, as elevated levels correlate with poor prognosis in cancers like skin cancer.69 It has been established that patients with elevated serum levels of this inflammatory cytokine have worse responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors.70 Furthermore, IL-6 is linked with immunotherapy side effects.71 Notably, our data reveal that the addition of the senolytic agent navitoclax selectively rebalances the SASP. Specifically, the senolytic suppressed the release of detrimental mediators (TGF-beta, DKK-1, IL-6) while critically retaining the secretion of beneficial, pro-immunogenic signals (CXCL11, CCL5, IL-28A). This ‘SASP re-balancing’ mechanism explains the enhanced in vivo efficacy observed in combination therapies. Clinically, this approach offers a critical safety and efficacy advantage: by actively mitigating the release of immunosuppressive and pro-tumorigenic cytokines, the combined senogenic/senolytic strategy enhances antitumor activity and potentially reduces the systemic inflammatory burden and associated toxicity. These findings support a ‘one-two-punch’ strategy for translating our research to the clinic, which aims to improve the efficacy and safety of senotherapies by introducing a senolytic agent that eliminates lingering senescent cells without compromising the initial antitumor immune response.

To harness these pro-immunogenic effects of TIS, we first combined a senogenic AURKAi with immune checkpint blockade (ICB) therapy. ICB is the established standard of care for advanced melanoma.12 However, resistance remains a hurdle, with close to half of patients failing to respond or acquiring resistance via various mechanisms that, most commonly, compromise tumor neoantigen presentation by tumor cells or DCs.72 These could include MHC-I loss or defects in IFN signaling.73–76 We used B16 tumors for our studies, which are considered immunologically ‘cold’ melanoma models. Therefore, they are often used as models to test new strategies to enhance the effectiveness of immunotherapy and to evaluate novel immunotherapeutic combinations.77 In our model, the addition of AURKAi to immunotherapy combining ICB (αPD-1) and T-cell-stimulating antibody α4-1BB did not enhance immunotherapy efficacy. Our mechanistic explanation based on tumor analysis is that AURKAi negatively affected the host’s leukocytes, particularly DCs and T-cell proliferation, potentially counteracting the pro-immunogenic effects of AURKAi on tumor cells. We should also note that we have only tested one immune checkpoint therapy strategy, anti-PD1 and anti-4-1BB, and the results with other immunotherapeutic antibodies could have been different. For instance, an anti-LAG3-anti-PD1 combination has emerged as a promising immunotherapy, and it would be of interest to explore this regimen in combination with senotherapy in subsequent studies.

The failure with ICB prompted us to explore a different immune-based approach: ACT with TIL, which shows promise for ICB-refractory cases, with good response and potential long-term remission.16 78 However, a pre-existing immunosuppressive TME can inhibit infused T-cell function and persistence.79 Additionally, some cancer cells may downregulate antigen presentation, rendering ACT less effective.80 81 Considering this, we decided to explore the impact of combining AURKAi with ACT to treat melanoma. To avoid inhibiting transferred T cells, we employed a sequential administration regimen, preconditioning tumors with a senescence-inducing drug for 7 days before administering ACT. We found that pretreatment with AURKAi significantly improved survival in a mouse melanoma model. This enhancement is likely due to the creation of a more favorable TME, which promoted activation of donor CD8+ T cells. Evidence included increased CD44 expression on donor CD8+ T cells, a marker of T-cell activation and memory formation. Additionally, there was a positive correlation between these activated CD8+ T cells and the presence of CD86-expressing DCs, which are essential for effective antigen presentation and subsequent T-cell priming. These observations suggest that AURKAi-induced senescence potentiates a more robust adaptive antitumor immune response by enhancing T-cell activation within the tumor.

The success of combining senescence-inducing drugs and ACT may also rely on the ability of the senescence-inducing drug to increase tumor MHC-I expression. In line with recent literature,37 38 our results showed that the senescent state may prime tumor cells to become more immunogenic. This is initiated by activation of AURKAi-dependent DNA damage, consequent micronuclei formation, and activation of the cGAS-STING-NF-κB/IRF pathway. This signaling cascade drives a reprogramming of STAT signaling toward STAT1 dominance, revealing a mechanism by which senescent cells may establish a tumor-suppressive immune microenvironment. Our findings are consistent with the established role of the cGAS-STING pathway in regulating senescence and SASP.82 83 Furthermore, our recent study with OSU13, a novel inhibitor of a mitotic kinase TTK, implicated cGAS-STING as a regulator of a favorable pro-immunogenic SASP induced by this drug.84 Lastly, our findings demonstrate that pharmacological senescence induction has wide-ranging applications, with enhanced efficacy when paired with adoptive NK cell transfer, an immune-based approach that is emerging as a promising treatment option for cancer. These findings provide a rationale for further exploring senogenic therapy in combination with emerging improved NK cell products.

The clinical significance of our preclinical study is that it establishes a ‘prepare-and-infuse’ clinical strategy, in which AURKAi and potentially other senescence-inducing drugs can be used as pharmacological preconditioning tools to sensitize tumors to cell-based therapies. This study is particularly timely as cell therapies, such as TIL therapy, gain regulatory approval for solid tumors like melanoma. Our findings offer a scalable strategy to enhance the efficacy and infiltration of these newly approved cellular products in resistant patients.

One caveat of this work is that it focuses primarily on the preclinical phase of cancer therapy development. Some of the therapies used in this study, such as AURKAi alisertib, BCLi navitoclax, and NK cell therapy, are not yet approved by the Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of patients with cancer. Additional clinical studies would be necessary to translate this work into clinical practice. Another important point to consider is systemic toxicity that may become an issue in trials combining multiple agents. For instance, BCLi navitoclax, used as a senolytic in this study, can cause thrombocytopenia,85 86 whereas senogenic AURKAi can cause neutropenia.87 TILs and ICB also have adverse effects; however, some combination effects could be prevented by administering senotherapy and immunotherapy sequentially, as we have done in our alisertib and ACT experiment. Another caveat concerns the use of models. For instance, while PDOs are reliable models of human tumor cells, they lack the full architectural complexity of the tumor stromal, vascular, and immune components. In contrast, murine syngeneic models address these aspects but may exhibit species-specific features distinct from those of human melanoma. By integrating human-derived organoids alongside syngeneic murine models, we aim to enhance the translational confidence of our findings.

In summary, our results indicate that TIS halts tumor proliferation and represents a promising approach to sensitize tumors to immune surveillance, thereby promoting immune-mediated tumor rejection. This strategy could be beneficial to those patients whose melanoma has progressed with other therapies and still have a high unmet medical need.

Materials and methods

Pmel-1 T cells and human donor NK cells expansion

Splenocytes from pmel-1 donor mice were collected by mashing tissue through a 70 µm cell strainer. Following erythrocyte depletion via hypotonic lysis in ACK buffer (Cat. #RGF-3015, KD Medical), 5×106 splenocytes were stimulated for 3 days with 1 μM hgp100 peptide (Cat. #RP20344, GenScript) and 100 IU/mL recombinant mouse IL-2 (Cat. #402-ML-100/CF, Biotechne) in complete T-cell medium (RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum, 1% antibiotic-antimycotic, 10 mM 4-(2-hydroxyethyl)-1-piperazineethanesulfonic acid (HEPES), 2 mM L-glutamine, 100 μM non-essential amino acid, and 50 µM β-mercaptoethanol). Activated Thy1.1 T cells were expanded in G-Rex6M Plates (Cat. #80 660M, Wilson Wolf), with mIL-2 (100 IU/mL) refreshed every 2 days. Human donor NK cells were kindly provided by Dean Lee Lab (Nationwide Children’s Hospital, Columbus, Ohio) and cultured as described previously.88

In vivo studies

All animal experiments were approved by the Ohio State University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC), protocols number #2019A00000119 and #2022A00000003. Pmel-1 mice (B6.Cg-Thy1a/Cy Tg(TcraTcrb)8Rest/J/J) were kindly provided by Dr Zihai Li (The Ohio State University, Columbus, Ohio). NSG-Tg (Hu-IL15) (NOD.Cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1WjlTg(IL15)1Sz/SzJ) and C57BL/6 mice were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory. To establish tumors, B16-F0 cells (50–75K) were injected subcutaneously into the right flank of female and male C57BL/6 mice aged 6–10 weeks. Treatment was initiated when tumors were palpable and included: (1) AURKAi alisertib (25 mg/kg via daily gavage) and BCLi navitoclax (100 mg/kg daily, gavage), (2) AURKAi alisertib (30 mg/kg) and ICB (αPD-1, clone 29F.1A12, 10 mg/kg+α4-1BB, clone LOB12.3, 5 mg/kg; BioXcell via intraperitoneal injection every 2 days), and (3) AURKAi alisertib (30 mg/kg) and Pmel-1 T cells (10⁷ cells via a single retro-orbital injection) with recombinant mouse IL-2 via intraperitoneal injection (10 000 IU for females, 15 000 IU for males). For NK cell therapy experiments, PDX fragments were implanted subcutaneously into NSG-Tg (Hu-IL15) mice as described previously.89 When tumors reached ~40 mm³, mice were randomized to receive vehicle, AURKAi (30 mg/kg/day), donor NK cells (10⁷ cells via retro-orbital injection once weekly), or the combination. For all in vivo experiments, tumor growth was measured thrice weekly with calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula: (width×width×length)/2. Mice were euthanized when tumors reached 16 mm in diameter or became ulcerated.

Bioinformatics analysis

For RNA-seq, differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 in GenePattern (https://cloud.genepattern.org/gp/pages/index.jsf) between vehicle and AURKAi, with two independent biological replicates per condition. DEGs were determined by <0.75 and >1.5 fold changes in gene expression. To minimize the risk of type II errors (false negatives) due to the small sample size, we applied a nominal p value cut-off of 0.1 to increase sensitivity and capture broad transcriptional shifts in a downstream pathway analysis. Heatmap visualization of DEGs was generated using the ClustVis web portal (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis/). For GO enrichment analysis of DEGs, we used the Functional Annotation Tool in the DAVID Bioinformatics Knowledgebase (V.2024q4) (https://davidbioinformatics.nih.gov/home.jsp). For secretome studies, PCA plot and heatmaps of secretome expression data were generated using the ClustVis web portal (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis/). Protein networks were created using OmicsNet, a network analytics platform for multi-omics integration (https://www.omicsnet.ca/). Briefly, the Uniprot protein ID list of cytokines that were significantly upregulated by at least 1.2-fold under AURKAi treatment (compared with vehicle) was uploaded to OmicsNet using the ‘Proteins’ option. InnateDB database of protein-protein interactions was selected for network creation, and Steiner Forest network viewer tool was applied to simplify the network by identifying a subnetwork enriched with input values. The network was visualized in a two-dimensional layout. The GO Molecular Function database was used for pathway enrichment analysis via the Function Explorer tool.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts, USA, www.graphpad.com) and GenePattern (for RNA-seq). For comparisons between two experimental groups, a two-tailed Student’s t-test was employed (EdU incorporation, SA-β-Gal, cell size, and MHC-I/PD-L1 surface expression), or a Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric data (micronuclei quantification and STAT1/3 ratios). To evaluate differences among three or more groups with a single variable (secretome profiling), one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s or Dunnett’s post hoc tests for multiple comparisons was used. For longitudinal tumor growth data, cytokine secretion time courses, and multivariable flow cytometry datasets, two-way ANOVA was performed, followed by Šídák’s, Tukey’s, or Dunnett’s multiple comparisons tests as appropriate. Animal survival was analyzed using the Kaplan-Meier method, with significance determined by the log-rank (Mantel-Cox) test. Unless otherwise specified, data are presented as mean±SD or mean±SEM for biological replicates, or as median for microscopic and flow cytometry cluster frequencies. All in vitro experiments with cell lines were performed with at least three biological replicates, except for RNA-seq, which was performed in duplicate. Exact numbers of replicates for each experiment are reported in the figure legends.
