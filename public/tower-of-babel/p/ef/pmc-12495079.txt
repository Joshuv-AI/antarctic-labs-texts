Incisor pulp chamber tomographic image dataset for dental age estimation
Authors: Davi Magalhães Pereira, Alexandre Vieira Pereira Pacelli, William José Lopes Junior, Saulo Moraes Villela, Heder Soares Bernardino, Marcelo Bernardes Vieira, Karina Lopes Devito
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Accurate tooth detection, segmentation, and age estimation are critical tasks in forensic odontology, helping to identify undocumented individuals in immigration cases, unidentified persons, and in the analysis of corpses and body fragments in accidents. This dataset was assembled to support such applications, comprising images of the upper central incisors from a diverse cohort of Brazilian individuals aged 18 to 60 from the Zona da Mata Mineira, obtained using standardized Cone Beam Computed Tomography (CBCT) scanning protocols. Each scan includes both coronal and sagittal views, providing a comprehensive coverage of the tooth structure. The dataset includes bounding box annotations for precise tooth localization. Additionally, the tooth crown and pulp chamber are essential for age estimation, as the crown’s enamel protects the dentin, and the dimensions of the pulp chamber decrease over time due to secondary dentin deposition. Therefore, segmentation annotations are provided for both the crown and pulp chamber to capture these critical structural details. Each image is also labeled with detailed metadata, including age, sex, and tooth number, to support a wide range of research approaches and objectives, maximizing the dataset’s utility and making sure it provides comprehensive information for various analytical needs. In addition, the patient code is present in the images, serving as an identifier. This dataset is a valuable resource for researchers aiming to develop and validate machine learning models for various dental applications. Its versatility supports a wide range of tasks, from automated dental diagnostics to advanced forensic investigations, making it a significant contribution to the field of dental image analysis.

Background

Age estimation plays an important role in fields like forensic odontology, aiding in the identification of undocumented individuals, unidentified persons, and the analysis of corpses at accident scenes [1]. The tooth crown is covered by enamel, protecting the underlying dentin, which makes up most of the tooth. At its core lies the pulp chamber, containing the tooth’s blood vessels and nerves. One widely accepted method for estimating age involves evaluating the crown-to-pulp size proportion of permanent teeth, as these change over time due to wear and secondary dentin deposition [2]. Notably, forensic studies have highlighted the value of dental analysis for age estimation [3], [4], offering non-invasive techniques applicable even with partial remains, making it ideal for complex forensic cases.

Therefore, the motivation for compiling this dataset is to overcome the limitations of traditional age estimation methods, which are often manual, time-consuming, and subjective. The dataset includes detailed annotations supporting machine learning tasks such as object detection, semantic segmentation and age prediction. While age estimation is the primary focus, the dataset’s versatility extends to other applications such as automated tooth identification and forensic dental record analysis, offering a valuable resource for both dental and forensic research.

Data Description

The IPCTI (Incisor Pulp Chamber Tomographic Images) dataset includes CBCT images of the dental pulp chambers of upper central incisors collected from 662 patients, comprising 452 females and 210 males.

To ensure patient privacy, the images were exported directly from the tomograph’s native software (iCat Vision), which automatically removes any information that could identify the patient (such as name, identification number, or date of birth). Only isolated images of the teeth to be evaluated were saved for methodological application, making it impossible to identify the individuals. Additionally, each exported image for every patient was assigned a random, non-sequential numerical code. This procedure ensures compliance with data protection and anonymization standards.

In the “dataset” folder, the data are organized into folders based on patient identifiers, where each folder contains all the images extracted from a given patient in the JPG format (Joint Photographic Experts Group), along with the corresponding detection (in the “bbox” subfolder) and segmentation annotations (in the “segmentations” subfolder) derived from these images. Visual aids, such as example images and summary statistics, are included in the “data_summary” folder.

Each patient in the dataset has two upper central incisors represented (teeth numbered 11 and 21), with images available in both coronal and sagittal views, resulting in a total of 2648 images. As each folder contains all images related to a particular patient, it is easier for researchers to analyze or extract information regarding each patient. This is crucial for medical or forensic tasks where patient-specific data is often analyzed as a whole. Thus, future work can estimate the patient’s age by leveraging the full set of available information. On the other hand, it also allows for focused analysis, enabling the extraction of specific image subsets, such as coronal views of tooth 11, from each patient’s folder for more targeted investigations.

To promote standardization and facilitate comparability across studies, we established a cross-validation protocol. This technique results in a much more accurate measure of model performance [5]. More specifically, we employed a stratified 5-5-fold cross-validation. In this approach, 5 different sets of 5-fold cross-validation samples are generated. This ensures that every image in the dataset is used for both training and testing, providing robust and unbiased performance estimates.

The data splits for each cross-validation set are located in the subfolders “cv_1” through “cv_5” within the “cross-validation” directory. Each of these folders contains five additional subfolders, named “split_n,” where n refers to the split number. Within each split, the data distribution is specified in two CSV files, “train.csv” and “test.csv,” which include the patient identifier, age, and sex information. Although the coronal view images may initially appear similar to each other, they are distinct as each image is captured with a focus on a specific tooth. In other words, while coronal images might include both teeth 11 and 21, the focal point of each image is directed towards one particular tooth. So a patient’s pair of coronal view images may appear similar at first glance, but the pulp chambers present there are distinct, as each image is captured with a focus on a single specific tooth.

As a result, even though these images often depict more than one tooth, only one annotation is provided, that is the localization of the tooth properly aligned for the pulp chamber assessment. In addition, these teeth may also be slightly different due to anatomical and morphological variations in the individual’s dental arch. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

The dataset consists of images (in JPEG format) extracted from tomography scans of upper central incisors. The scans were obtained from the Radiology Service image bank at the Faculty of Dentistry of the Federal University of Juiz de Fora (UFJF). Each record includes raw CBCT images and their corresponding annotations, meticulously prepared by a specialist, making the dataset ideal for machine learning applications. Beyond that, the dataset also holds significant value for research in forensic odontology, dental imaging, and anatomical studies, primarily those focused on age estimation based on radiographic features. However, the annotations also enable investigations into anatomical and morphological variations.

To provide researchers with maximum flexibility, no additional pre-processing was applied to the images beyond the sharpening filter implemented by the i-Cat Vision software (specifically, the “very sharp” setting). It is important to note that the application of this sharpening filter does not alter the original image resolution. This allows for users to apply any image processing technique, tailored to their needs.

Data acquisition

The CBCT scans were captured using a standardized protocol with the i-Cat Next Generation device. The DICOM files of the tomographic images for each patient are loaded into the i-Cat Vision software, which is specifically designed for the CBCT equipment used in the exams performed.

The examination is accessed through the Multiplanar Reconstruction functionality, where three viewing windows (sagittal, coronal, and axial) are displayed. First, the slice thickness is adjusted. The right maxillary central incisor (tooth 11) is then located and aligned in the sagittal plane using the orientation lines, ensuring that both the root apex and incisal edge touch the line simultaneously, with the dental pulp centered. In the coronal plane, the tooth is adjusted so that the guide line divides it into two equal parts, touching both the apex and the incisal edge. The same procedure is followed in the axial plane, ensuring that the guide line passes through the tooth, touching the mesial and distal edges of the incisal border.

It is important to emphasize that the accuracy of dental age estimation of this dataset relies on the crown-to-pulp size proportion, making the alignment process crucial for displaying the correct proportion. After aligning the tooth, the image’s brightness and contrast parameters are set to 3210 and 810, respectively. Finally, the orientation lines are removed from the region of interest, and the image is saved in JPEG format. This procedure, carefully carried out by an experienced dental surgeon specialized in CBCT imaging, is then repeated for the left maxillary central incisor (tooth 21). The acquisition parameters are defined as follows:•Voltage: 120 kV.•Current: 8 mA.•Rotation time: 26.9 seconds.•Slice thickness: 0.25 mm.•Field of View (FOV) height: 6 to 10 cm.

Inclusion criteria

The dataset includes images extracted from CBCT scans of adult Brazilian individuals from the Zona da Mata Mineira region (Minas Gerais, Brazil), aged between 18 and 60 years. Both male and female subjects are included. The inclusion criteria are:•High-quality CBCT scans suitable for evaluation.•Presence of two intact upper central incisor teeth with fully formed roots (closed apex).

Annotations

The dataset includes comprehensive annotations created using specialized software to enhance its utility for machine learning tasks. In addition, the annotations were made by a dental specialist. The annotations are divided into two main types:•Segmentation Annotations: These were created using LabelMe (version 5.3.1),1 a tool that allows for precise outlining of tooth structures within the images. The output format for LabelMe is a JSON file, which contains detailed information of the annotated shapes as polygons. Two shapes were annotated, as can be seen in Fig. 6, the dental crown region and the dental pulp region, which is located within the crown region. Evaluation criteria

For age estimation, as a regression task, standard evaluation metrics include MAE (Mean Absolute Error), MSE (Mean Squared Error), R2 (Coefficient of Determination) and Cumulative Score (CS). The CS metric measures the percentage of predictions that fall within an acceptable error level, providing an intuitive assessment of model performance. It is defined as:(1)CS(θ)=1N∑i=1NI(|y^i−yi|<θ),where y^i represents the predicted age, yi is the ground truth age, N is the total number of samples, and I is an indicator function that returns 1 when the absolute error is within the threshold θ, and 0 otherwise.

A commonly adopted threshold is θ=5 years [6], indicating that CS(5) represents the proportion of predictions with an absolute error smaller than 5 years. Alternatively, an interval-based approach can be applied, wherein the CS values are calculated across a specified range of tolerance levels. This approach provides a more comprehensive analysis of model performance by evaluating its behavior over varying error levels. Typically, an error level interval from 0 to 10 years is employed for such assessments [7], [8].

In terms of detection and segmentation tasks, commonly used metrics include mean Average Precision (mAP) at different Intersection over Union (IoU) thresholds, such as mAP50 and mAP50:95, as well as precision and recall. Precision is defined as the proportion of correctly predicted positive instances (True Positives, or TP) among all predicted positives, including both TP and False Positives (FP). Recall, on the other hand, measures the proportion of correctly predicted positives out of all actual positive instances, which includes TP and False Negatives (FN).

In both tasks, a prediction is considered a True Positive when the IoU between the predicted region (bounding box or segmentation mask) and the ground truth exceeds a predefined threshold. Predictions with IoU below the threshold, or those that do not correspond to any ground truth instance, are counted as False Positives. Conversely, ground truth instances that are not matched by any prediction contribute to the count of False Negatives. The mAP is computed by averaging the Average Precisions (AP) of each class. In general, for each class i, AP is obtained as the area under the Precision-Recall curve. It is defined as:(2)mAP=1N∑i=1NAPi,where N is the total number of classes. Specifically, mAP50 corresponds to the average of the AP values when a single IoU threshold of 0.5 is used to determine a correct detection. Meanwhile, mAP50:95 is calculated by averaging the mAP values obtained across multiple IoU thresholds ranging from 0.5 to 0.95 in increments of 0.05.

This standardized evaluation approach allows for comparisons across cross-validation splits and different methodological approaches. By following this protocol, researchers can ensure fair benchmarking and comparability with future studies while maximizing the dataset’s potential for diverse forensic and medical applications.

Beyond its use in regression-based age estimation, the dataset can also be adapted for a multi-class classification approach using fixed age intervals. Several works in the literature employ different age ranges, such as 10-year age intervals [9], 5-year intervals [10], [11], or even 1-year intervals [3], [4], [12]. In our case, we propose an 8-class setting with the following groups: (1) 18-22, (2) 23-27, (3) 28-32, (4) 33-37, (5) 38-42, (6) 43-47, (7) 48-52, and (8) 53-60. It can be seen that the last group spans an 8-year range (53–60), since maintaining strictly 5-year intervals from 18 years of age onward was not possible.

Fig. 13 illustrates the distribution of patients in these eight categories. When using these intervals, typical classification metrics can be adopted for performance evaluation, including accuracy, precision, recall, F-scores, and the ROC curve. Limitations

The primary limitation of this dataset is its focus on a single type of tooth (upper central incisors). Additionally, the dataset is limited to individuals from a specific geographic region (Zona da Mata, Minas Gerais, Brazil), which may introduce biases.

Another limitation is the imbalance regarding the sample distribution across age groups. As illustrated in Fig. 2, individuals aged 21 to 25 are overrepresented in the dataset, while older individuals, especially those over 53, are underrepresented. This imbalance is due to the difficulty in obtaining exams from older individuals, since it is more challenging to find older individuals with intact or even retained teeth. However, this imbalance reflects real-world data distributions and presents both opportunities and challenges for machine learning research, requiring models to be adaptable and robust to these variations.

Finally, the total number of images available in the dataset is limited when compared to other studies from the literature, which use images collected from thousands of patients for tooth age estimation tasks [3], [4], [13]. Nevertheless, it is important to highlight that these datasets are not public.
