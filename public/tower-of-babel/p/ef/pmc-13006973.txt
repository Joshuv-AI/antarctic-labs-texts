Feasibility of an Online Grocery Intervention Pilot to Improve Fruit and Vegetable Purchase and Food Security Among Adults With Children Eligible for SNAP
Authors: Angela CB Trude, Zoya Naaz Rehman, Stefani Wiloejo, Kellie McLean, Laura Catalina Velasco Daza, Pasquale Rummo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjective:To determine the feasibility of an online grocery pilot aimed at supporting healthy food purchases for caregivers of individuals with low income.Methods:A pretest-posttest pilot study was conducted among 59 primary household food shopper caregivers living ≤ 130% of the poverty line. The 8-week randomized pilot had 4 groups: (1) free delivery-only, (2) trust-targeting SMS, (3) matching credit for online healthy purchases, and (4) grocery list recommendations. The groups received the program concomitantly from October to December, 2022. Feasibility was assessed through the setup of an online grocery account and receipt of the intervention materials via text. Acceptability was assessed via postintervention interviews and participants’ ratings of the intervention.Results:Feasibility was medium-high: 47% created an online grocery account, 61% watched the program tutorial. Acceptability was high: 90% found the tutorial helpful, all received text messages, 82% deemed them useful.Conclusions and Implications:The promising feasibility and acceptability suggest a potential for a fully powered trial behavioral intervention to support online healthy food shopping.Keywords: SNAP, food assistance programs, online grocery, consumer behavior, policy

INTRODUCTION

Disparities in food insecurity persist, with Black (29%) and Hispanic (32%) households having the highest rates in the country compared with their White counterparts and the national average.1 Previous studies have identified factors such as low physical access to food retailers, relatively higher prices of healthy foods, and limited time for shopping as possible causal mechanisms explaining disparities in food and nutrition security across ethnic and socioeconomic groups.2 Moreover, supermarkets often offer a greater variety of foods at lower prices compared with other community food sources,3 and individuals who live near them tend to have higher diet quality, which studies have shown reduces health risks.4 However, predominantly White neighborhoods with high income tend to have more supermarkets than communities with diverse ethnic backgrounds and low income, which forces the latter to rely on higher-priced, less healthful small food stores.5,6

Online grocery services may be a promising strategy to increase access to healthy foods by addressing transportation barriers. This strategy could be especially beneficial for families living in areas with limited access to supermarkets or healthful food outlets and who are at higher risk for poor dietary habits. To increase online grocery access for individuals of low income, the 2014 Farm Bill authorized households participating in the Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) to use their monthly government benefits at authorized online retailers and called for the expansion of the US Department of Agriculture Online Purchasing Pilot.7 Data from previous studies have shown that the ability to use government benefits online is a major motivator for online grocery purchases among households with low income.8–10

Nevertheless, many gaps remain in ensuring equity in online healthy food access. First, many families with low income have yet to avail themselves of the service because of a lack of awareness about SNAP online transactions, and because delivery fees are not covered by SNAP benefits.11 Second, those who shop for groceries online are likely to purchase less fresh produce8 and more high-sugar foods12 than they would in-store, not because of personal food choice, but rather because of barriers such as mistrust of online shoppers’ ability to select produce, fear of financial losses from unsatisfactory purchases, and decision fatigue which can lead to impulse purchases of unhealthy foods.11,13 Third, although existing programs such as financial incentives for fruit and vegetable purchases and grocery list recommendations aim to improve food selection in physical supermarkets,14,15 few studies have tested how feasible these strategies are in the online grocery space.

Considering the existing literature, this study pilot-tested a 4-group randomized trial aimed at selecting feasible and acceptable intervention components that directly target these gaps, before implementing a fully powered factorial trial called the Optimizing Selection of Fruits and Vegetables (OPT-FRESH) trial. The Multiphase Optimization (MOST) framework of intervention development and testing16 will inform OPT-FRESH, with the overarching goal of addressing barriers to online fruit, vegetable, and legume (FVL) selection among families with low income. This approach addresses weaknesses in prior studies that use the traditional 2-arm randomized controlled design, which is unable to assess the contribution of individual components of a multicomponent intervention because they are often packaged together. This paper presents findings from the first phase (preparation), which will inform the 2 subsequent phases: optimization and evaluation of the OPT-FRESH design and testing.

METHODS

Study Design

This pilot was a 4-group randomized trial implemented over 8 weeks. Research staff followed the Consolidated Standards for Reporting Trials guidelines for conducting and reporting on this pilot. The study included families with children aged ≤ 10 years and with an income ≤ 130% of the federal poverty level (FPL) or who were enrolled in SNAP. Researchers conducted the study in diverse neighborhoods with low income in an urban borough of New York City (NYC), and selected these neighborhoods because they have the highest poverty levels, obesity prevalence, and ethnic diversity compared with overall city estimates according to the NYC Poverty, Health, and Food Access database.17 These areas also have lower-than-average supermarket density relative to other boroughs, with an average ratio of 20 bodegas to 1 supermarket.18

Participants and Recruitment

From August to October 2022 (10 weeks), research staff recruited adult caregivers in 5 Head Start programs and 1 after-school program in a Settlement House in person and by putting up flyers in the recruitment centers. Caregivers were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) primary shoppers of a household with a young children aged ≤ 10 years; (2) anyone in the household participated in SNAP in the past year, or were income eligible (annual household income ≤ 130% of the FPL); (3) had access to a smartphone or computer to place online grocery orders; and (4) spoke English or Spanish. To adequately ascertain the feasibility of the study and acceptability of intervention components within the constraints of available funds and resources, researchers prespecified a sample size of 60 caregivers.19 A total of 30 participants or fewer is acceptable in feasibility studies focused on recruitment, adherence, and acceptability.20 A conventional power calculation is not necessary because evaluating intervention effectiveness is not the aim of a pilot feasibility trial. The proposed sample size is sufficient to assess feasibility outcomes (e.g., recruitment, fidelity, retention) and to estimate key parameters (e.g., SD of behavioral outcomes).21

Data Collection

Interested participants could either scan a QR code or send a text to receive a unique link to a QualtricsXM (Qualtrics) survey eligibility screener, available in both English and Spanish. Participants could either use their cellphones or a study tablet to complete the survey when recruited in person. This first stage of the survey included questions related to household composition, food assistance participation, and household FPL. Although these were not part of the eligibility screener, researchers included them to further characterize households that were potentially not eligible. Researchers programmed the survey to end on submission of responses indicating ineligibility. Eligible respondents continued to the second stage of the online survey that included demographic questions (i.e., caregiver self-reported race and ethnicity from a list including non-Hispanic White, non-Hispanic Black or African American, Hispanic or Latin American, Asian American, Asian Indian, Asian Chinese, Asian Filipino, Asian Korean, or Asian Vietnamese, Other Asian, American Indian or Alaska Native, Middle Eastern or North African, Native Hawaiian or other Pacific Islander, or other [specify]), and other baseline measures (i.e., household food security, online grocery shopping behaviors, and household dietary and food preferences). This online survey used request-response validation, which prompted participants to answer questions they initially left blank, but allowed them to proceed without responding if they chose to skip. However, questions used to assess participant eligibility applied force-response validation and could not be left unanswered. Researchers assessed all survey responses for variability and patterns in answers (e.g., evidence of straight-lining, skipping patterns). The postintervention survey included similar questions to the baseline as well as Likert-scale questions to assess participant satisfaction with the intervention materials. Caregivers received $20 and $30 electronic gift cards for completing the assessments and submitting itemized grocery receipts at baseline and postintervention, respectively. In addition, caregivers received a $20 electronic gift card for completing in-depth interviews that explored the acceptability of the pilot.

Research staff collected at least 2 itemized grocery receipts of an online and/or in-store grocery trip at baseline and immediately after the 8-week intervention pilot. Research has shown that 2 weeks’ worth of grocery receipts is adequate to reliably characterize household food purchasing behavior.22 Researchers contacted participants submitting incomplete (i. e., missing total dollar amount purchased or items) or ineligible (i.e., exclusively of nonfood items [e.g., household cleaning products]) receipts via text message with additional instructions or for clarification.

Intervention

The Theory of Planned Behavior and the Prospective Theory of Behavioral Economics theoretically informed the design intervention strategies and materials of OPT-FRESH, to enable researchers to understand the processes by which cognitive (i.e., attitudes, norms, perceived behavior control) and behavioral factors (i.e., loss aversion and framing effects) affect household purchase of FVL and food insecurity (Supplementary Figure 1). The intervention was targeted to caregivers of children, as it recognizes that they shape the household food environment and serve as role models to young children, which in turn can influence their nutrition and health. Research staff randomized participants to 1 of the 4 components testing each of the OPT-FRESH components after the baseline assessment and run-in period (i.e., submission of 2 completed itemized grocery receipts): delivery-only (delivery fee waived), texting to improve trust (weekly text messages to improve trust in online grocery services and delivery fee waived), financial incentive (weekly matching of up to $10 as a financial incentive for purchasing FVL online and delivery fee waived), or grocery list (weekly text messages of selected meal suggestions using SNAP recipes to reduce impulse purchasing and delivery fee waived). The University’s Institutional Review Board (IRB-FY2022–6623) conducted a limited expedited review and approved the study protocol. All adult caregiver participants provided written consent virtually. Because children were not directly involved in the intervention, child assent or caregiver consent was not required.

Delivery fee waiver.

Researchers sent all participants an Instacart Express membership e-gift card to waive the online delivery fee on all orders of $35 or more placed online for the duration of the pilot. All participants received a text message notifying them of emails with their Instacart Express memberships. Text messages also included a link to a tutorial video on setting up an Instacart account, adding SNAP benefits as a payment method, selecting items online, and identifying SNAP-eligible products, as well as a list of stores that accept SNAP benefits as an online payment method. The video was available in both English and Spanish.

Texting to improve trust.

Participants randomized to this component received weekly text messages via the EZ Texting platform (https://www.eztexting.com/). The weekly text messages were designed to target key constructs of the Theory of Planned Behavior,23 specifically aiming to improve participants’ perception of behavioral control and trust in online grocery services.

Financial incentive.

Participants randomized to this component received $1 loaded onto an electronic gift card for each dollar spent on fresh, frozen, or canned FVL on submission of online grocery purchase receipts that validated purchases. Researchers set a maximum amount of matched incentive at $10 per week for online FVL (fresh, frozen, canned) purchases to mimic recent FVL incentive policies in some brick-and-mortar grocery stores (e.g., Get the Good Stuff in NYC).

Grocery list component with SNAP-Ed recipes.

The research team texted participants randomized to this condition seasonally and culturally appropriate SNAP-Ed-based weekly meal plans with grocery list recommendations. These meal plans took into account household size, product seasonality, dietary preferences (e.g., vegetarian, kosher), convenience (i. e., total preparation time of ≤ 90 minutes and ingredient repurposing), and food allergies. Research staff generated final grocery lists from the recipe meal plans and organized them by store department and by recipes. Researchers shared weekly meal plans via text as a photograph and a link to a PDF document with embedded links to related recipes on the SNAP-Ed website. Supplementary Figure 2 shows an example of a weekly menu plan and related grocery list recommendations.

Feasibility Outcomes

The prespecified primary outcomes for this pilot were the feasibility of conducting an online grocery trial among families with low income and participant acceptability of OPT-FRESH intervention components. While acknowledging that the present pilot study is not fully powered to detect changes in behavioral outcomes, this manuscript secondarily assessed behavior change in terms of household food security and household food purchasing to demonstrate that these measures can be assessed appropriately in the future optimization trial (optimization phase) and randomized controlled trial (evaluation phase) informed by the MOST framework.

The research team set a benchmark (i.e., minimum acceptable values) a priori to indicate whether the pilot trial was feasible and acceptable. Researchers assessed feasibility as the ability of the research team to conduct a subsequent fully powered trial in terms of recruitment, randomization, intervention fidelity, and retention rates (Table 1). Researchers assessed acceptability as participant satisfaction with the intervention materials via postintervention online survey among participants randomized to receive the intervention, and via in-depth interviews among a subsample using convenience sampling. After completing the postintervention assessment, researchers invited all participants to participate in in-depth interviews conducted via the phone. Interested participants answered questions about their overall impressions of the program and the practical aspects of participation, including any recommendations they had for improvement (Supplementary Table 1). The first author trained interviewers in qualitative research at the graduate level. Interviewers included bilingual researchers (English and Spanish), who had prior knowledge of the intervention to inform their probes, and had no relationship with the participants.

Behavioral Outcomes

Researchers assessed household food security within the past 30 days via the 6-item validated US Department of Agriculture screening tool.24 Researchers coded household food security using recommended cutoff points for household-referenced questions ranging from 0 to 6: very low, 5 or 6 affirmatives; low, 2–4; and marginal or high, ≤ 1. Researchers assessed household FVL purchases using data from itemized grocery receipts (from both online and in-store purchases) to generate the average household purchase of FVL. Researchers categorized itemized grocery items into fruits, vegetables, or legumes. Items categorized as fruits included fresh, canned, dried, or frozen fruit, including applesauce, and did not include 100% fruit juices, avocados, olives, or tomatoes. Vegetables included fresh, canned, or frozen vegetables, including fresh herbs, tomatoes, roots, avocados, fresh sauce, tomato sauce, and salad kits of a majority of vegetables. These did not include condiments. Legumes included nuts or beans in any form: nut butters, nut milks, nut snacks, hummus, canned/dry beans, and seeds/seed products, and soy products. Researchers extracted the total dollar spent per item, then summed by category and divided by the total grocery bill.

Data Analysis

Analytical researchers conducted descriptive analyses, including frequency, mean, and SD values using Stata software (version 16.1, StataCorp, 2019) to tabulate sociodemographic characteristics, rates of intervention and questionnaire completion, and acceptability ratings.

Researchers employed thematic analysis to analyze the acceptability interviews.25 Trained research assistants coded textual data from in-depth interviews using a codebook focused on Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) constructs26 to inform the analysis of factors that influence effective implementation (i.e., individual adherence to intervention). The CFIR organizes constructs into domains that include (1) intervention characteristics (perceived benefits of the intervention), (2) outer setting (barriers and facilitators), and (3) individual characteristics (participants’ attitude, self-efficacy, and stage of change). Bilingual research assistants transcribed Spanish interviews in the original language and then translated them into English. Research staff double-coded all transcripts and conducted deductive analysis of the qualitative data using CFIR to inform theme development using MAXQDA software (VERBI Software, 2022).

To evaluate the preliminary efficacy of behavior change, researchers examined univariate distributions to identify extreme values (outliers) and assess the asymmetry of the distributions. The distributions of all continuous outcomes were symmetrical; thus, researchers used mean values in the analyses. Researchers performed a comparison between the baseline and postintervention outcomes using a paired t test analysis. For this pilot study, analytical researchers did not use inferential statistics because the study was underpowered to detect clinically significant differences; thus, this paper does not report P values.

RESULTS

Feasibility of Study Recruitment

From August to October 2022, research staff approached a total of 160 individuals during school drop-off and pick-up times, and 34 (20%) expressed disinterest. As research staff put up flyers in Head Start centers and distributed them to center staff during the recruitment period, additional individuals interested in the program may have scanned the QR code outside of the in-person recruitment hours, making accurate tracking by the research staff challenging. A total of 181 individuals attempted the baseline of the survey (Figure), with a weekly average of 17.3 and a weekly enrollment rate of 5.9 participants (Table 1). The research team successfully enrolled participants in the program within 2–3 weeks of their completing the online baseline survey.

Feasibility of Randomization and Study Participants

Among those who completed the 2-part baseline survey (n = 96), 59 shared at least 2 itemized grocery receipts and were randomized into 1 of the 4 groups (enrollment rate: 61.5%) (Figure). Most participants of the pilot identified as female (93%), were aged 30–39 years (51%), identified as Hispanic (78%), and reported receiving SNAP benefits in the 12 months before the baseline assessment (78%) (Table 2). Two-thirds of participants reported never having shopped online (40 [68%]). Caregivers who enrolled in the pilot (n = 59) had similar demographic characteristics to those who were eligible but did not enroll in the intervention by submitting the itemized grocery receipts (n = 37).

Feasibility of Retention and Fidelity

Participants in each experimental condition were retained in the program at an average rate of 79.6%, which is slightly below the high standard for retention. We implemented the program with moderate fidelity based on the benchmarks established a priori. Those who answered the postintervention assessment (n = 47), about half reported setting up an Instacart membership to access the free delivery incentive (25 [47%]). Participants who did not set up their membership stated they either did not know how to do so (n = 13) or did not have a credit card (n = 7). All participants reported receiving text messages throughout the intervention.

Acceptability

Overall, participants rated the intervention as highly acceptable. On the basis of the postintervention survey data, most participants reported reading the text messages and finding them somewhat or very helpful (39 [82%]). Of those who reported receiving the tutorial video on setting up an online grocery account and placing an order (n = 19), 82% found it somewhat or very helpful. Participants completed the online assessment in an average time of about 20 minutes, ranging from 18 minutes at baseline and 23 minutes at postintervention.

On the basis of thematic analysis of the data from qualitative interviews with participants who completed the pilot trial (7 in English, 8 in Spanish), in relation to intervention characteristics, many commented on how the text messages were “a helpful reminder to grocery shop online, were clear and interactive” (R1061, English). Although some shared that setting up the Instacart membership was straightforward and that it was “easy to meet the minimum $35 purchase requirement to qualify for the free delivery because everything is expensive right now” (R1003, Spanish), others who did not set up the Instacart membership shared that “someone in [their] household had one” (R1092, English), that they thought it would take a lot of time to learn about “a new way to shop” (R1024, English), or that the process of setup was difficult: “So, I don’t know how to redeem it. I don’t even remember receiving the email to redeem the membership” (R1014, English).

In terms of individual characteristics related to the implementation of the intervention, most participants shared positive attitudes toward online grocery shopping, noting that it saved time, offered convenience, provided more product variety, and addressed the issue of transportation or “not having to go carry them” (R1025, English). Participants’ self-efficacy related to shopping for groceries online increased over time, with some mentioning their first time was “a little complicated but once you know it, it’s easy” (R1049, Spanish). Negative attitudes included reservations about others selecting certain items, such as produce and meat. In addition, participants shared that it was challenging to visualize how much produce or meat to order online:

I don’t know how much that [1 pound of fruit or meat] would be. You go to the supermarket and just pick them up, I take how many and that’s it. But I’ve never checked how many pounds it has. [R1062, Spanish]

High delivery and service fees had previously deterred participants from using online grocery shopping, so they appreciated the fee waiver during the intervention:

I don’t want to pay extra fees to order food online. If I can go buy it, then get another item for what I’m spending on delivery charge or taxes, and not like this was like a free program (R1092, English)

Another outer setting factor that seemed to influence individuals’ experiences when ordering groceries online was their ability to communicate with online hired shoppers, which gave them a greater sense of control over their purchases:

I had one experience with avocados. ‘Do you want them green green? Or do you want them tender? or softer?’ They gave me a little option there (R1024, English)

Preliminary Efficacy of Behavioral Outcomes

Participants shared an average of 2.1 grocery receipts (range 2–4) that totaled $87.30 and $90.00 at baseline and postintervention, respectively (Table 3). Across the full sample, households spent about one-quarter of their grocery bills on FVL at baseline (mean 20.2% of the total bill) and 21.6% postintervention, an average increase of 1.45 ± 4.04 percentage points, independent of group assignment. The analysis of behavioral outcomes also revealed a reduction in household food insecurity over time, with a prepost difference in mean −0.60 ± 2.1, regardless of group assignment.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates how an online grocery intervention can be successfully implemented in a real-world setting, and specifically informs future behavioral interventions for households with young children and of low income and/or participating in the SNAP program. Pilot testing these strategies allowed for the refinement of procedures and protocols to effectively conduct an intervention with 4 experimental conditions, with medium-to-high feasibility and high acceptability.

The pilot had an enrollment rate of 61.5% and included a diverse sample, with Hispanic and Black participants comprising the majority of the sample (97%). This enrollment rate was similar to that of another community-based eHealth intervention focusing on a population with low income (63.5%),27 although it was lower than the 78% rate reported in a primary care-based intervention with Hispanic patients.28 This study’s requirement of 2-itemized grocery receipts may have contributed to the relatively lower enrollment rate. The incentive for participation was distributed after both baseline and the 2 receipts were submitted, which may not have been convenient or timely for participants.29 Therefore, to improve the enrollment rate in the fully powered optimized trial, we plan to use a tiered approach to incentives, in which all participants are immediately incentivized on completion of the baseline online survey and then further compensated for each grocery receipt submission.

Overall, the adherence rate of the intervention was 79.6%, which almost met the a priori benchmark of 80%. Retention potentially benefited from the contact between study staff and participants via weekly text messages. Previous studies have discussed the importance of intensive and consistent contact between research staff and participants to positively influence retention rates.27,30 Among the retention challenges was the unpredictable prepaid phone coverage previously reported among populations with low income.30,31 The retention rate, however, is comparable to other community-based studies among populations of low income, ranging from 69% to 75%.28,31

Although the video tutorial on how to order groceries online was highly acceptable, many participants reported either not receiving the video link or experiencing difficulties setting up the account, as indicated in qualitative interviews and postsurvey findings. In the subsequent OPT-FRESH trial, the research team will contact participants at the beginning of the trial to provide technical assistance on how to receive free delivery and order groceries online, including step-by-step instructions on how to redeem the free delivery membership. All intervention materials, including text messages, grocery lists, and recipes, were highly acceptable.

Consistent with guidance for pilot behavioral intervention trials, which recruit small samples by design, a key study limitation is the lack of inferential conclusions that can be drawn from the preliminary efficacy analysis comparing prepost differences. Although this study reported the analytic approach for each outcome, the wide variations expected from the small sample of this pilot make these inferential estimates merely suggestive and hypothesis-generating. Researchers will address this limitation through the recruitment and enrollment of a larger sample size in a fully powered trial to yield reliable estimates and inferential conclusions. Second, it is possible that many families enrolled in the pilot were also participating in the Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children. However, research staff did not collect this information because this trial focused on online grocery shopping, for which only SNAP was available in the setting. Third, researchers did not collect sensitive data on immigration status. Instead, research staff used information on the preferred language spoken as a proxy of immigration and acculturation for the qualitative analysis. In the subsequent fully powered large trial, researchers will examine the modifying effect of language spoken and ethnicity on the intervention. Fourth, the study sample was biased toward those who had access to the internet and a smartphone/technology, and were motivated to share grocery receipts. Although the nature of the intervention required internet access to place online grocery orders, the study tried to minimize barriers to technology access by providing participants with a study tablet to complete the online baseline survey via Qualtrics. In the subsequent trial, the research team will implement an in-person postintervention data collection to increase participant retention and address technology-related barriers.

IMPLICATIONS FOR RESEARCH, PRACTICE AND POLICY

This pilot study demonstrated that the 4 strategies, which are individual candidate components of OPT-FRESH—an online grocery intervention to support FVL purchases for households with young children of low income—are both feasible and acceptable. The literature demonstrates a need to test interventions aimed at addressing the social determinants of health that negatively influence families with low income and ethnically diverse people who experience higher rates of cardiovascular diseases than other groups. Shifting behavioral interventions to online grocery environments is a significant and innovative approach, as consumers currently navigate both physical and online grocery modes for food acquisition. The subsequent fully powered trial will employ a factorial design informed by the MOST framework, allowing for the testing of individual intervention components to be included in an optimized intervention package.

On the basis of these findings of the pilot, temporary delivery fee waivers are relevant for real-world behavior change programs, as some online grocery services recently started offering 50% discounts for SNAP shoppers on membership supporting free delivery, and another third-party service has just announced $0 delivery fees for 2 months for SNAP customers. This pilot intervention suggests that temporary delivery fee waivers meaningfully support families of low income in adopting online grocery shopping.

Moreover, data from this study support programmatic efforts led by SNAP-Ed educators, alongside other health educators, in focusing on developing and testing culturally relevant recipes for ethnically diverse communities. These recipes can then be swiftly integrated into online grocery lists that are instantly shoppable, promoting healthier habits.

Furthermore, text messaging, a low-cost and scalable approach, also presents an opportunity to raise awareness about SNAP online, improve digital literacy, and address consumer mistrust of online grocery services. The business literature underscores the importance of fostering interactions between consumers and services to build trust, a relationship mediated by access to supportive information.32 Visual-based media, such as infographics and images, in combination with text-only messaging, have been shown to significantly increase consumer trust,33,34 which research staff will test in the subsequent study.
