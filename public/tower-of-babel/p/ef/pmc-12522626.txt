Nursing students’ perception of integrating flipped learning in a nursing theory course: a qualitative study
Authors: Waad Hasan Ali
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFlipped learning (FL) is an innovative student-centered approach that fosters active engagement and critical thinking. It is increasingly being adopted in higher education globally, including nursing education. In Saudi Arabia, aligning educational strategies with Saudi Vision 2030 requires producing independent, collaborative, and technologically competent healthcare professionals. This study explores nursing students’ perceptions of flipped learning in a theoretical nursing course, contributing insights into how flipped learning supports workforce development goals.MethodsA qualitative descriptive design was employed. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with eight third-year nursing students enrolled at a public university in Saudi Arabia. Qualitative content analysis was used to interpret students’ perceptions and experiences with the FL approach.ResultsStudents’ responses revealed five main themes: enhanced understanding of course content, improved exam preparation and retention, strengthened communication and collaboration skills, increased learning independence, and challenges in implementation. Overall, flipped learning was perceived as supportive of deeper learning and skill development.ConclusionFlipped learning promotes independent learning and collaborative engagement, aligning well with the goals of Saudi Vision 2030 to prepare a competent healthcare workforce. The findings support integrating flipped learning into nursing curricula to foster essential professional competencies and lifelong learning habits among future nurses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-025-07836-x.Keywords: Flipped learning, Nursing Education, Nursing, Undergraduate Education, Learning Approaches

Background

The Saudi Arabian healthcare sector faces increasing demands due to population growth and evolving expectations of care. One of the key concerns is the persistent shortage of qualified nurses. Projections suggest that the Kingdom will require over 100,000 additional nurses by 2030 to meet its national healthcare goals [1]. This challenge is addressed in part by Saudi Vision 2030, which aims to not only expand the healthcare workforce but also reform nursing education to produce skilled, independent, and technologically competent professionals [2]. Traditional lecture-based teaching methods, however, are increasingly viewed as inadequate in preparing students to meet the complexities of modern healthcare practice [3].

To align with these reforms, nursing programs must adopt innovative pedagogical strategies that foster critical thinking, collaboration, and lifelong learning. Flipped learning (FL) has emerged globally as an effective student-centered approach that addresses these needs. In the flipped classroom model, students engage with instructional materials before class—such as lectures, readings, or videos—and spend in-class time applying knowledge through discussions, problem-solving, and peer collaboration [4]. Recent studies have shown that FL improves academic performance, enhances student motivation, and promotes deeper engagement in health sciences education [5, 6].

The pedagogical foundation of FL lies in several well-established learning theories. First, constructivist learning theory suggests that students learn best when actively involved in the construction of knowledge through experience and reflection. This theory underpins flipped learning’s emphasis on pre-class preparation and in-class participation. Second, the model aligns with Bloom’s revised taxonomy, which encourages shifting lower-order cognitive activities (e.g., remembering, understanding) to pre-class tasks, while using class time for higher-order skills like applying, analyzing, and evaluating. Finally, FL supports Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, which involves concrete experience, reflective observation, abstract conceptualization, and active experimentation—all of which are embedded in the flipped classroom format [7].

Despite its growing application in nursing education internationally, the FL model has not been widely implemented or evaluated in Saudi nursing programs. Most existing research is quantitative and focused on student achievement or satisfaction. Qualitative insights, particularly in non-Western or Arab cultural contexts, remain limited [8, 9]. Moreover, challenges such as resistance to change, unfamiliarity with digital platforms, and cultural expectations about teacher-led instruction may shape students’ perceptions in unique ways that cannot be captured through surveys alone. González-Sanz et al. [10] emphasize the need for in-depth qualitative research to understand how students experience FL in content-heavy, theory-based nursing courses.

In Saudi Arabia, implementing FL may support the broader goal of cultivating independent, collaborative, and lifelong learners, in line with Vision 2030’s human capital development objectives. FL models foster accountability and time management while encouraging students to explore additional resources and become active participants in their own learning [11]. Understanding how Saudi nursing students perceive FL is crucial for informing faculty development, guiding curriculum design, and ensuring culturally responsive implementation.

This study addresses these gaps by exploring third-year undergraduate nursing students’ perceptions of FL in a theoretical course at a Saudi university. Using a qualitative descriptive approach, the study aims to capture students’ experiences, challenges, and perceived benefits, offering insights that can guide the effective integration of FL in nursing education.

Literature review

Flipped learning (FL) has become an increasingly popular instructional strategy in nursing education due to its emphasis on active learning, critical thinking, and student-centered engagement. In recent years, a growing body of literature has explored the benefits and challenges of FL across diverse educational contexts. However, much of the literature presents findings descriptively, often as isolated case studies. This section organizes the literature thematically to enhance coherence and critique, focusing on three key areas: perceived efficacy, student resistance and implementation challenges, and methodological limitations in existing research.

Perceived efficacy of flipped learning

Numerous studies have demonstrated that FL positively influences students'academic achievement and learning engagement. This model integrates pre-class activities, such as video lectures and readings, with in-class collaborative tasks that enhance critical thinking, communication, and applied knowledge [10, 12]. The integration of such active learning strategies enables students to play an active role in their education and shift from passive recipients to engaged participants [13, 14]

In the context of nursing education, FL has been shown to foster deep learning by allowing students to identify their knowledge gaps prior to class and address them through discussion and collaboration. According to Shih and Tsai [15], in-class activities supported by pre-class preparation significantly improve students’ ability to analyze content and retain information. Similarly, Betihavas et al. [16] concluded from their systematic review that FL models contribute to improved academic performance, clinical reasoning, and student satisfaction in nursing.

More recently, Wilson and Hobbs [5] demonstrated that implementing a flipped classroom in nursing skills education led to increased student confidence, motivation, and engagement, especially when instructors provided timely feedback and structured support. Another study by Nacaroğlu and Bektaş [7] found that the flipped classroom significantly enhanced self-regulation skills and academic performance among gifted learners in a health education context. These recent contributions affirm the continued relevance and adaptability of FL to modern nursing curricula.

Furthermore, Ahmed Uzir et al. [17] noted that flipped classrooms encourage better time management, as students are required to consistently engage with content rather than cram before assessments. The layered nature of learning in FL—pre-class, in-class, and post-class—offers iterative opportunities for reinforcement, which many students found helpful in improving performance on final examinations [18].

Student resistance and implementation challenges

While much of the literature supports the benefits of flipped learning, several studies identify resistance and adaptation challenges among students and faculty. One common concern among students is the additional time and effort required to prepare for classes. According to Martínez-Jiménez and Ruiz-Jiménez [19], students reported that the pre-class workload was overwhelming, especially in the absence of clear guidance or technical support.

Similarly, Shih and Tsai [15] observed that students sometimes failed to complete pre-class assignments, which limited their ability to participate meaningfully in class activities. These findings are echoed by E. Nweiran [11], who found that although students appreciated the flexibility and autonomy of FL, many struggled with managing deadlines and felt pressured by the volume of assigned tasks.

Cultural context also plays a significant role in how FL is received. Wilson [9] highlights that in hierarchical educational systems—like those in many Arab countries—students may be unfamiliar with the expectations of self-directed learning and may hesitate to take initiative without explicit instruction. In such contexts, FL may require additional scaffolding and orientation to be effective.

Technological challenges also hinder implementation. Ahmed Uzir et al. [17] and El Sadik & Al Abdulmonem [18] emphasized that limited access to reliable internet, lack of digital literacy, and unfamiliarity with learning management systems can significantly impair the effectiveness of flipped classrooms. These barriers are particularly pronounced in resource-constrained environments, where students rely heavily on institutional infrastructure.

Research gaps and methodological limitations

Despite its growing popularity, FL research in nursing education still faces several limitations. A predominant concern is the overreliance on quantitative methodologies—typically surveys and exam scores—which provide limited insight into students'lived experiences [14, 16]. Seitz and Orsini [8] were among the first to call for qualitative, phenomenological investigations that explore how students experience flipped classrooms emotionally, cognitively, and socially.

In particular, theoretical nursing courses—unlike clinical or laboratory-based ones—require students to engage with abstract concepts and complex reasoning. However, most studies evaluating FL in nursing focus on practical or clinical courses. This leaves a significant gap in understanding how FL supports or hinders theoretical knowledge acquisition [10].

Additionally, few studies address FL in the context of non-Western or Arabic-speaking regions. The majority of empirical data comes from North America, Europe, or East Asia, with limited research from the Middle East. Joseph et al. [6] attempted to bridge this gap through their study of flipped classrooms in Oman, concluding that although students showed improved satisfaction and performance, localized adaptations were necessary to account for cultural learning styles.

The need for context-sensitive, qualitative research is therefore evident. Recent studies stress that FL cannot be “copied and pasted” from Western models into other contexts without modification. As Wilson and Hobbs [5] emphasize, the success of FL is deeply contingent on instructional design, institutional support, digital readiness, and cultural alignment.

Moreover, recent studies point toward a growing interest in hybrid models and the importance of faculty training in making flipped classrooms sustainable. For example, Abdel-Wahab et al. [20] investigated the long-term effect of flipped classrooms on Saudi nursing students'engagement and found that success depended largely on instructor presence, clarity of expectations, and adaptability of materials.

The literature confirms that FL holds promise for enhancing engagement, comprehension, and academic performance in nursing education. However, challenges related to student resistance, digital access, and instructional design persist. Methodologically, the dominance of quantitative research leaves a gap in our understanding of how students perceive and adapt to flipped learning, especially in theoretical courses and in non-Western settings. This study aims to address this gap by using a qualitative approach to explore Saudi nursing students’ perceptions of FL in a theoretical nursing course, thereby contributing contextually relevant insights to the ongoing debate.

Methods

Design of the study

A qualitative descriptive research design was employed to explore nursing students’ perceptions of the FL approach. This design was appropriate for gaining rich, detailed insights into participants’ experiences. The FL strategy was implemented in a theoretical nursing course as part of the instructional intervention.

Setting of the study

The study was conducted at the College of Nursing within a public university in Saudi Arabia. The institution offers undergraduate and graduate nursing programs and is committed to integrating innovative teaching methodologies in its curriculum.

Samplling and participants

Purposive sampling was employed to recruit third-year undergraduate nursing students who had completed a course taught using the FL model. A total of eight students participated in the study. Data collection continued until thematic saturation was reached—that is, when no new themes or codes emerged during successive interviews. The sample size was therefore deemed sufficient to capture the diversity of experiences and perspectives relevant to the research question.

Intervention

The FL approach was integrated into a theoretical nursing course (illustrated in Fig. 1). The course included 10 topics. Materials were available for students to help them prepare for class. For example, the required course book, course lectures, articles, and videos. Each topic had one assignment before class, a learning activity during class, and one assignment after class. The pre-class assignment aimed to encourage students to prepare for class. Students were required to study the lecture material, view a video, or read an article to complete the pre-class homework. The lecturer used case studies and other exercises to get the students involved in group projects and class discussions on the material being covered. Students were expected to complete the pre-class work to be prepared for the in-class activities and discussions. Students were then asked to complete the post-class assignment to assess their understanding of the material they had learned. The instructor's roles were to evaluate, facilitate, direct, and guide the learning process. To decide what topics needed to be covered in the class exercises and discussions, the instructor went over the replies that the students had submitted for the pre-class assignment. Throughout class exercises, the teacher helped and led the students in addition to responding to their inquiries. For post-class assignments, the instructor evaluated if the students comprehended the learning content. The assignments were graded, and students were required to participate in these assignments as part of their evaluation. However, participation in the interviews to express their perceptions of FL was voluntary.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was obtained from the relevant institutional review board. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection. Confidentiality and anonymity were assured, and participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any stage without consequence.

Recruitment

Recruitment started after the IRB approval. The primary investigator used an active recruitment plan [21]. The principal investigator asked students to take part in the research study during class. The primary investigator provided a brief presentation that included background about the research study, explained the purpose of the study, and how the students’ insight would contribute to the body of knowledge about FL. The students who expressed interest in taking part were then given dates for interviews. Interviews were conducted after completing all the course’s requirements to assure students that their perceptions would not affect their academic performance.

Data collection

Data were collected through semi-structured interviews, allowing participants to freely express their experiences while ensuring consistency across interviews. The interview guide was developed based on a comprehensive review of the literature on FL in nursing and higher education. It included open-ended questions focused on students’ engagement, learning experiences, perceived challenges, and perceived benefits of the FL model.

To ensure content validity and clarity, the interview guide underwent peer review by two subject matter experts in nursing education. Their feedback was used to refine the phrasing and focus of the questions to ensure they aligned with the study’s aims and theoretical foundations. All interviews were conducted in person, audio recorded with participants'consent, and subsequently transcribed verbatim for analysis. Key interview questions were;

Data management

The interviews were anonymous. No personal information was collected from students. Each interview had a special code to be identified. Students'interviews were recorded using a recording device. Only the primary investigator had access to the students’ interviews.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using inductive content analysis, allowing themes and categories to emerge organically from the data. Transcripts were read multiple times to ensure immersion in the content. Initial coding was conducted line by line, identifying meaning units and assigning labels to capture key concepts. These codes were then grouped into subcategories and subsequently into broader categories representing patterns across participants’ narratives.

The inductive approach was chosen to avoid imposing preconceived categories and instead prioritize the voices and interpretations of the participants. Coding and analysis were conducted manually to enable close engagement with the data. To enhance the rigor and credibility of the analysis, reflexive memo writing and constant comparison techniques were used throughout the coding process.

Results

The analysis of students’ responses revealed five major categories that reflect their perceptions of FL in a theoretical nursing course. These categories include: (1) Deepened Understanding of Course Content, (2) Exam Preparation and Retention, (3) Development of Communication and Collaboration Skills, (4) Increased Learning Independence, and (5) Implementation Challenges. Each category includes subthemes based on pre-class, in-class, and post-class learning activities, and incorporates student reflections to illustrate the findings.

Category 1: deepened understanding of course content

Pre-class assignment

Pre-class assignments allowed students to come to class prepared. Students reported that because of the pre-class assignment, they had to read the lecture. By doing the pre-class assignment, students come to class with an idea about the learning topic. One student mentioned, “I like that I had the opportunity to read the lecture before class time; this gave me the opportunity to have an idea about the topic that we are going to cover. Answering the question before the class made me read the lecture and allowed me to identify the focus of the lecture”. Students also mentioned that preparing for class by reading the lecture, watching videos, reading articles, and doing assignments helped them to improve their focus and understanding of the learning content. One student mentioned, “I understood the lectures better because I prepared for the class”. Students appreciated the pre-class assignment as it helped them to identify their learning challenges before the class. One student said, “Preparing for class helped me to identify the points that I did not understand, so I prepared my questions”. Other students said, “The assignments allowed me to understand the lecture before the class time …. I studied the lectures …. and when I came to class, I had the questions beforehand to ask the instructors to explain”.

In-class activities and discussions

FL provided an active and engaging learning environment. Students reported that they understood the learning content through in-class activities and discussions. Coming to class prepared allowed students to take an active role in class by participating in discussions and activities. In flipped learning, students lead the learning, and the instructor provides guidance and instructions. One student said, “Class activities and discussions helped my understanding of the lecture because I gave the answer and discussed the learning content with my classmate …. Your guidance during the discussion also helped to guide my thinking of the learning content”. Students reported that they prepared for class because they wanted to participate actively in class. Also, students understood the importance of their participation in class in the learning process. One student mentioned, “Knowing that there will be discussions in class forced me to prepare for the class to focus and engage in the class because I know that my participation would add value to the discussion”.

Post-class assignment

FL helped students to evaluate their understanding of the learning content. Students reported that post-class assignments allowed them to identify which of the learning content they did not understand. Students considered post-class assignments as a strategy to review the lecture and identify the challenging areas before the exam night. Having to do the assignment might require students to go back to the lecture, which helped in absorbing the information in students’ heads. One student mentioned, “post-class assignment was like a revision …. it allowed me to measure my understanding of the lecture. I tried my best to solve the assignment from my understanding and memory”. Another student said, “I know the area that I had challenges and the area that I need to work on or focus on to understand it …. FL helped for the learning to be saturated and concentrated”.

Category 2: exam preparation and retention

Pre-class preparation as a study aid

FL helps students in their preparation for the exam as well as their performance in the exam. Students reported that preparing for class helped them when they studied for the exam. Students believed that because of flipped learning, they had a better understanding of the learning content, thus facilitating their studying for the exam. One student reported, “I found that using FL when studying for the exam was beneficial. I am aware of what I am learning while I study.”. The same student emphasized, “I do not study just to pass the exam; I know the learning content”.

In-class discussions supporting retention

Furthermore, the FL approach helps students to keep their knowledge fresh in their minds, which helps in preparing for and performing in exams. Students appreciated in-class activities and discussions as they helped them when they studied and in the exam. Students reported that these approaches facilitated their studying for the exam because they remembered the points that were discussed in class. Students also reported that discussions helped them to recall knowledge when they were doing the exam. One student said. “FL approach helps to keep my knowledge fresh when I study for the exam …. when we had the exam even though some of the lectures, we took in the beginning I still remember the content from the class activities and class discussions”. Another student said, “Because of the FL approach, studying for exam became easy! The discussions helped when I studied for the exam and in the exam, too! In the exam, if I am stuck with a question, I say Oh! I remember this point from the class discussion”.

Category 3: development of communication and collaboration skills

Enhanced peer interaction and confidence-building

FL helps in developing students’ collaboration and communication skills. Students reported that working in groups to complete in-class activities encouraged them to interact more openly and confidently with their peers. This was particularly meaningful for students who described themselves as shy or reserved. One student shared, “I am a shy person; being in a team helped me to socialize and be more confident communicating with my classmates.” The group setting provided a safe environment for students to voice their thoughts, exchange ideas, and engage in meaningful discussions—an experience that many found empowering. Students indicated that this repeated interaction gradually improved their communication confidence and participation in classroom dialogue.

Collaborative learning and mutual understanding

Students also reported that the collaborative aspect of flipped learning helped them grasp course content more effectively. By working in teams, they were able to break down complex information and support one another in the learning process. One student said, “We helped each other to understand the learning content. Sometimes, I missed the point or did not understand it, and my friend helped me to understand.” Another student emphasized, “Sometimes my classmates explained it to me in a simple way that made it easy for me to comprehend. Also, sometimes I misunderstand the learning content, and when I share it, the group corrects my misunderstanding.” This peer-supported environment promoted not only academic growth but also the development of listening, clarification, and negotiation skills—key communication competencies for future nursing professionals.

Category 4: increased learning independence

Motivation for self-directed learning

FL encouraged students to become independent learners by requiring them to take responsibility for pre-class preparation. Students understood that class participation depended on their readiness, which motivated them to engage more actively with the material in advance. This led them to not only review provided lectures and readings but also seek out supplemental resources to enhance their understanding. One student said, “To prepare for the class, I look for resources to help me come to class prepared… I search about the topic, look for videos and articles to support my understanding.” This behavior reflects a shift from passive learning to active, inquiry-driven learning habits.

Development of time management and accountability

Students also highlighted that FL helped them develop time management skills. The structured flow of assignments—before, during, and after class—encouraged consistent study habits rather than last-minute cramming. Preparing for class and meeting deadlines made them more disciplined and accountable. One student said, “FL helped me to become an independent learner because I search for resources to help me understand the learning content.” Students acknowledged that managing their own learning process, even when challenging at first, helped build academic responsibility and confidence in their ability to direct their own education—an essential skill for nursing professionals in lifelong learning environments.

Category 5: implementation challenges

Technical difficulties affecting preparation

Students stated that they had difficulties, even though the FLtechnique was well-received. One of the challenges was technical issues with accessing, responding to, and solving the assignment online. Students reported that this issue made preparing for class challenging. Students expressed their frustration when they had technical problems, as their understanding of the learning content and their engagement in class depended on their preparation. One student said, “Technical difficulties hindered me from preparing for class and participating in the class activities and discussions. I might have to ask another student about the questions because I want to come prepared”.

Time demands of assignments

Another challenge students identified was the time required to complete pre-class and post-class assignments. These tasks often involved reviewing lectures, reading supplementary materials, and reflecting on concepts. Despite the workload, students remained committed, recognizing the value these assignments added to their understanding and class engagement. One student mentioned, “Assignments take time, although I was determined to do it because I know that the assignment would help me to understand the content and that I wanted to engage in the class activities and discussion”.

Adapting to deadlines and time management

Another challenge that students faced over time was submitting assignments on the due date. Students explained that submitting assignments on time was challenging. Students reported that at the beginning, it was challenging not to forget to do the assignment. Some students also reported that having a specific due date made them nervous, but at the same time, made them responsible. Students explained that having to submit assignments allowed them to manage their time and study. One student mentioned, “Even though there was a specific due date that helped me to be on track and be responsible about my assignment …. I have consistency in studying not only before the exam, which helped me to be confident and comfortable about the exam and decreased my anxiety”. Another student mentioned, “At the beginning, it was challenging to prepare for class and manage my time …. because I was forgetting to do the assignment …. but then this helped me to manage my time and become more responsible about the study”. Discussion

This study explored nursing students’ perceptions of flipped learning (FL) within a theoretical course and revealed five overarching themes: deepened understanding of content, enhanced exam preparation, improved communication and collaboration skills, development of learning independence, and implementation challenges. These findings align with existing literature, yet also reveal important context-specific insights, especially regarding Saudi Arabia’s educational and cultural environment.

The students in this study perceived FL as effective for understanding complex theoretical content, particularly when pre-class assignments, in-class discussions, and post-class reviews were well-integrated. These findings are consistent with González-Sanz et al. [10], who emphasized the benefits of FL in promoting continuous learning across different phases of instruction. Students appreciated being able to arrive prepared for class and use face-to-face time to apply and clarify knowledge, a pattern also reported by El Sadik and Al Abdulmonem [18] in anatomy courses. Moreover, this perceived benefit extended to exam preparation, where students recalled class discussions during assessments, reinforcing the argument that active learning facilitates long-term knowledge retention [5].

Importantly, students in this study emphasized the value of group interaction and peer-supported learning. They found collaborative discussions not only helpful for clarification but also for building communication confidence, particularly for more reserved students. This supports findings by Joseph et al. [6], who noted that Omani students in a flipped classroom showed improved collaboration and participation, and also experienced reduced classroom anxiety. Similarly, Jawabreh et al. [22] reported that high-fidelity simulation significantly improved nursing students’ self-confidence and satisfaction in mental health nursing education, reinforcing the value of active, student-centered learning environments. However, as in other Gulf-region studies, students initially struggled with group work expectations and time management, suggesting the importance of gradual pedagogical transitions and clear guidance [23, 24].

A key contribution of this study is the connection students made between FL and learning independence. Many participants described how the FL model helped them take ownership of their learning by seeking additional resources and developing time management habits. These patterns reflect constructivist and experiential learning theories, where learners actively construct knowledge through self-directed engagement and reflection [25]. Several students moved beyond assigned materials to seek out videos, articles, and peer input, suggesting that FL not only changed their academic habits but also fostered a shift in learning identity.

This transition to self-regulated learning is particularly significant in the Saudi context, where traditional education has historically emphasized teacher-centered instruction, rote memorization, and passive absorption of knowledge [3]. In such settings, students may initially feel unprepared or even resistant to learning models that demand autonomy and initiative. The findings of this study, however, suggest that with scaffolding and consistent expectations, Saudi nursing students can adapt to and benefit from learner-driven approaches.

Technological readiness also plays a vital role in shaping the FL experience. While students appreciated the flexibility of accessing content before class, several reported difficulties with unstable internet connections, unclear digital platforms, and submission deadlines. These obstacles echo concerns raised by Amed Uzir et al. [17], who emphasized that the success of FL depends heavily on technological infrastructure and institutional support. Moreover, as Wilson [9] notes, students in hierarchical educational systems may experience friction when shifting from passive to participatory roles, especially when digital tools are unfamiliar or poorly introduced.

From a broader perspective, the findings of this study align with national efforts under Saudi Vision 2030, which emphasizes the development of a health workforce that is not only numerically sufficient but also technologically competent, collaborative, and self-reliant [2]. By encouraging responsibility for pre-class preparation, active in-class participation, and post-class reflection, FL supports the cultivation of self-directed learners—a foundational skill for nurses in complex, fast-evolving clinical settings. The students’ increasing engagement with autonomous learning strategies signals progress toward these educational goals.

Despite its contributions, this study also highlights implementation challenges. Students reported stress related to managing assignments and unfamiliar digital tools. Such barriers underscore the need for well-structured faculty development, integration of user-friendly platforms, and policies that allow flexibility while promoting consistency. These concerns mirror international findings, such as those by Shih and Tsai [15], who found that pre-class workload and unclear expectations can reduce the effectiveness of FL.

In summary, this study supports the growing body of evidence that FL enhances nursing students’ understanding, engagement, and independence. However, successful implementation requires more than providing pre-class materials—it demands careful consideration of cultural context, technological capacity, and students'readiness for self-directed learning. When integrated thoughtfully, FL can serve as a transformative pedagogy aligned with both educational theory and Saudi Arabia’s strategic vision for higher education and healthcare.

Implications

The nursing profession demands that nurses stay current on new evidence in practice in order to deliver safe and high-quality treatment. Thus, nursing educational programs need to prepare students with the competency to search for current evidence to improve healthcare services. Integrating FLin nursing education helps in preparing students to become lifelong learners. The FL approach requires students to come to class prepared, and part of their preparation includes searching for resources to help them understand the learning content.

Moreover, healthcare practice is interdisciplinary, which requires healthcare providers, including nurses, to work collaboratively and communicate effectively to provide safe and quality healthcare services to patients. FL provides an active and engaging learning environment, allowing students to communicate and collaborate to understand the learning content. As a result, students will develop and practice communication and collaboration skills, which are essential competencies to work in an interdisciplinary environment successfully.

Strengths

This paper provides a detailed qualitative inquiry into the experiences of the perception of FL by undergraduate nursing students in a theoretical course, which is not widely represented in current nursing educational sources. Purposive sampling approach was used to ensure that the participants were directly involved in the intervention, hence the validity of the findings. The use of semi-structured interviews enabled participants to voice their clear, real opinions, which were assessed through a strict content analysis technique. Verbatim quotes enhance the thematic meaning and add transparency to the analytic work. The interviews performed after completing the course minimized the probability of response bias due to academic pressure, instructor presence or evaluation issues. In addition, the study combined key components of flipped learning: pre-class preparation, active in-class engagement, and reflection after class, which made it possible to assess the model comprehensively. These methodological strengths contribute to the trustworthiness of the study and form the basis of future qualitative as well as mixed-method studies within these contexts.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, this study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a single institution and limited to one theoretical nursing course, which restricts the generalizability of the findings to other nursing disciplines or institutions. Second, although not explicitly stated in the original demographic section, the study was conducted in an all-women’s university, and all participants were female. This gender homogeneity may limit the applicability of results to more diverse nursing student populations, especially in coeducational settings. Third, the dual role of the researcher as both instructor and interviewer introduces potential bias, despite efforts to minimize its impact through post-course data collection and confidentiality protocols. Additionally, while students discussed technical challenges related to digital access and platform use, these variables were not systematically measured or assessed. Variability in digital literacy, access to devices, and internet connectivity could have significantly shaped students'engagement with FL and their perceptions of it. Finally, the absence of longitudinal follow-up limits insight into whether the positive perceptions and skill development reported by students had sustained effects on their academic performance or clinical practice preparedness.

Recommendations

Integrating FL into a nursing theoretical course was effective in improving students'understanding of the learning content as well as developing skills, such as becoming lifelong learners. This researcher recommends integrating this learning approach into theoretical and clinical nursing courses and studying its effect on the student’s learning outcomes and acquiring competencies. The developed competencies from integrating FL are essential for nurses to provide safe and effective healthcare services.

In Saudi Arabia, most nursing classes are large to overcome the nursing shortage. The government is supporting nursing education to increase the nursing flow into the practice setting. The role of nursing education is to graduate nurses not only to meet the demand of healthcare in terms of numbers but also to the quality and safety of healthcare. Integrating FL in nursing courses might be challenging for both the students and the educator. Thus, educators need to understand these challenges to make integrating FL effective.

Conclusion

FL provides a meaningful learning experience for learners. In flipped learning, students actively engage in the learning process and environment. Students prepare for class before class time to participate in class. Research studies reported that even though FL improves students’ academic achievement, they face challenges. The purpose of this research project was to ascertain how students felt about flipped learning. FL presents several difficulties for students, one of which is that it consumes a lot of time. Despite that, student appreciated the competencies that developed from integrating FL into their education.

The study's findings recommended that FL enhanced students'comprehension of the material, which in turn helped them prepare better for the test. Also, according to students, because FL required them to participate, they had to come prepared, which helped them to become independent learners. Students appreciated the active and engaging learning environment as it allowed them to develop their communication and collaboration skills. Integrating FL into the nursing curriculum helps prepare students with essential competencies for healthcare practice, such as working in interdisciplinary teams.
