Late Diagnosis of Prader-Willi Syndrome in an Adolescent With Significant Complications of Type 2 Diabetes
Authors: Alyssa Kahane, Emma Metivier, Allison Bahm, Jill Hamilton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractYouth-onset type 2 diabetes (YO-T2D) is on the rise and disproportionately affects those from low socioeconomic status and specific ethnic and racial minority groups. YO-T2D has a more aggressive phenotype than adult-onset T2D. We present a case of a 14-year-old patient who presented after a fall and was found to be acutely unwell with significant peripheral edema. Initial investigations indicated hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state, acute kidney injury, and nephrotic range proteinuria, leading to a new diagnosis of T2D. Subsequent evaluation revealed mild retinopathy and diabetic nephropathy. Sixteen months prior, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) was > 16% (reference range, ≤ 6.0%) with no treatment initiated at that time. The patient was initially treated with multiple daily insulin injections prior to starting empagliflozin-metformin. The presence of developmental delays, short stature, and hypogonadism prompted genetic testing, which confirmed a diagnosis of Prader-Willi syndrome, a neurodevelopmental disorder associated with hyperphagia and early-onset obesity. This case underscored the severity of YO-T2D, risks of delayed healthcare access, and considerations for optimal diabetes management.Keywords: type 2 diabetes, hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state, diabetic nephropathy, nephrotic range proteinuria, microvascular complications, Prader-Willi syndrome

Introduction

Youth-onset type 2 diabetes (YO-T2D), defined as a diagnosis of T2D before the age of 25 years, is increasing in prevalence and disproportionately affects those from low socioeconomic status and specific ethnic and racial minority groups [1, 2]. A Canadian national surveillance study (2017-2019) reported a 60% increase in T2D incidence compared to 10 years prior, particularly among children of Asian and Indigenous backgrounds [2]. The pathogenesis, phenotype, comorbidities, and complications of YO-T2D are more severe than later-onset T2D [1]. One rare and acute complication is hyperglycemic hyperosmolar state (HHS), which is characterized by significant hyperglycemia, hyperosmolarity, lack of ketosis, and profound dehydration [3].

We discuss the evaluation and management of an adolescent with T2D, manifesting initially with HHS and significant microvascular complications, followed by a diagnosis of Prader-Willi syndrome (PWS).

Case Presentation

The patient is a 14-year-old female patient with a history of intellectual disability, short stature (height percentile <0.01, Z-score −4.06) and obesity (BMI 99.6 percentile, Z-score +2.61) who presented to the emergency department after a fall. This was preceded by a 1-month history of dizziness, abdominal pain, headaches, edema, polydipsia, polyuria, increased appetite, and weight gain. In the days prior to presentation, there was decreased fluid intake and lower urine output. Additionally, she had symptoms of oral and vulvovaginal candidiasis. Notably, 16 months prior, bloodwork ordered through a walk-in clinic demonstrated a glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) level of > 16% (reference range ≤ 6.0%) and a random glucose of 414 mg/dL (SI: 23.1 mmol/L) (reference range, 80–200 mg/dL, [SI: 4.0–11.0 mmol/L]), with no treatment initiated at that time.

The patient was born at 35 weeks followed by a 1-week neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) admission due to hypotonia and feeding difficulties, which resolved. There was no established primary care, and the family sought medical attention through walk-in clinics. Her past medical history included myopia and bilateral cataracts.

Parents are of Jamaican descent and are non-consanguineous, with no known family history of diabetes. Two siblings have language delay.

Diagnostic Assessment

On initial assessment, the patient was alert, mildly tachycardic (heart rate 104 bpm), afebrile, and hypertensive (stage 2; BP 135/111 mmHg). Dysmorphic features included prominent orbits, midface hypoplasia, short flat philtrum, short forehead, low anterior hairline, sparse eyebrows, widely spaced teeth, short chin, and brachyclinodactyly. She had a normal respiratory rate without respiratory distress. There were decreased breath sounds and low flow oxygen was required to maintain normal oxygen saturation. The patient had ascites and pitting edema of the lower extremities. Acanthosis nigricans to the neck and axilla was present.

Initial labs demonstrated an elevated glucose of 44 mmol/L (800 mg/dL), serum osmolality 330 mOsm/kg (SI: 330 mmol/kg) (reference range, 282–300 mOsm/kg [SI: 282–300 mmol/kg]), sodium 129 mEq/L (SI: 129 mmol/L) (reference range, 135–145 mEq/L mmol/L [SI: 135–145 mmol/L]). There was no significant acidosis with a pH 7.31 (reference range, 7.32–7.43) and bicarbonate 21.8 mEq/L (SI: 21.8 mmol/L) mol/L (reference range, 22–28 mEq/L [SI: 22–28 mmol/L]); urine ketones were negative. HbA1c was greater than 15%.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) was present as indicated by creatinine 1.76 mg/dL (SI: 156 umol/L) (reference range, 0.5–0.9 mg/dL [SI: 37–67 umol/L]). Nephrotic range proteinuria (NRP) was indicated by urinalysis 3+ protein, microalbumin/creatinine ratio 4366 mg/g (SI: 493.9 mg/mmol) (reference range, < 31 mg/g [SI: <3.5 mg/mmol]), and protein/creatinine ratio 14 335 mg/g (SI: 1622 mg/mmol) (reference range, <177 mg/g [SI: <20 mg/mmol]). Hypoalbuminemia was present with serum albumin of 2.3 g/dL (SI: 23 g/L) (reference range, 37–50 g/L [SI: 3.7-5 g/dL]).

Treatment

The patient was transferred to the pediatric intensive care unit for further management of HHS and a new diagnosis of T2D. Fluid resuscitation was initiated at a significantly reduced rate than typical for HHS due to hypoalbuminemia and third spacing, with concomitant albumin infusion and furosemide. She received intravenous normal saline of 50 mL/hour (1 mL/kg/h) and an insulin infusion at the lower end of typical range at 0.02 units/kg/h. She then was transitioned to multiple daily injections (MDI) insulin regimen. Metformin was held due to the AKI.

Due to her clinical presentation of nephrotic syndrome, initial investigation included complements, anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies (ANCA), antinuclear antibodies (ANA), anti-double-stranded DNA antibodies (anti-dsDNA), and hepatitis serology, which was unremarkable.

Following resolution of HHS, the patient was transferred to the ward for ongoing management. Diabetic nephropathy (DN) was confirmed on biopsy, with diffuse and nodular diabetic glomerulosclerosis. Once the creatinine normalized, she started a combined tablet of empagliflozin and metformin (5 mg/1000 mg). Despite hypertension management with fosinopril (15 mg) and amlodipine (5 mg), there was ongoing NRP. Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor treatment was chosen as an adjunct agent, based on the effects of reducing proteinuria and blood pressure [4]. An oral agent was preferred as the family expressed challenges with injections. Initial ophthalmology exam demonstrated bilateral posterior subcapsular cataracts and scattered dot-blot macular hemorrhages indicating mild diabetic retinopathy.

During admission, ongoing overnight desaturations prompted a polysomnogram, revealing severe obstructive sleep apnea with elevated central apnea-hypopnea index. She subsequently underwent a tonsillectomy and adenoidectomy.

During the admission, she was noted to have significant hyperphagia with food seeking and hoarding behaviors. The patient's evolving presentation—characterized by intellectual disability, short stature, obesity, hyperphagia, and sleep apnea—raised concern for a genetic form of obesity.

Pituitary assessment revealed normal thyroid function; hypogonadotropic hypogonadism indicated by low follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) 0.1 IU/L (reference range, 0.3–7.8 IU/L), low luteinizing hormone (LH) <0.1 IU/L (reference range, 0.4–6.5 IU/L) and estradiol 5.7 pg/mL (SI: 21 pmol/L); robust morning cortisol 12.1 µg/dL (SI: 335 nmol/L) (reference range, 5.0–23.4 µg/dL [SI: 138–645 nmol/L]) and normal insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1) 147 µG/L (SI: 19.2 nmol/L) (reference range, 115–601 µG/L [SI: 15.0–78.4 nmol/L). Prolactin was minimally elevated 23.5 µg/L (SI: 497 mIU/L) (reference range, 3.2–18.5 µg/L [SI: 68–390 mIU/L]). Bone age was concordant with chronological age.

Microarray revealed a pathogenic deletion in 15q11.2q13. Methylation specific multiplex ligation-dependent probe amplification (MS- MLPA) testing confirmed a diagnosis of PWS.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Over the 53-day admission, there was significant improvement in glycemic control, with a decrease in HbA1c from >16% to 10%. She was weaned off prandial insulin prior to discharge. Upon discharge, there was ongoing NRP. At outpatient follow-up, HbA1c was 7.6%. There had been weight loss of 4 kg compared to 6 weeks prior, with body mass index of 28.1 kg/m2 (just above 97th percentile). Empagliflozin-metformin (5 mg/1000 mg) was increased to twice daily with a goal of weaning basal insulin.

Discussion

We highlight a rare case of adolescent T2D which manifested with HHS, severe nephropathy, and mild retinopathy at onset, with subsequent diagnosis of PWS. The prolonged duration of hyperglycemia contributed to the severity of her presentation.

PWS is a neurodevelopmental disorder caused by deletion or imprinting changes affecting expression of the paternal chromosome 15q11-q13 with clinical features varying by age [5]. Findings in our patient consistent with PWS included developmental disability, hyperphagia, and hypogonadism. Earlier diagnosis facilitates early understanding for patients and families as well as enables proactive management of endocrinopathies, hyperphagia, and metabolic risks [5]. Patients with PWS are at higher risk for T2D, with 10% to 25% of patients diagnosed at a mean age of 15 to 20 years [6-8]. Most patients with PWS are asymptomatic at the time of T2D diagnosis and identified through routine screening [6-8].

HHS is present at diagnosis in 2% of YO-T2D, with risk factors including male sex and Black race [9-13]. There is literature to suggest overrepresentation of children with developmental disabilities in HHS cohorts [12, 13]. The mortality rate in HHS is reported to be 5 to 10 times higher than in diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) [9-11, 13, 14]. There is limited literature on youth with PWS presenting with hyperglycemic crises, including one patient who presented with mixed diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)/HHS and one patient who developed DKA while on growth hormone treatment [8, 15].

DN is the leading cause of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in adults [4]. The initial marker is microalbuminuria, and early recognition is essential to prevent or delay progression to ESRD [4]. Data from the TODAY study indicated that 8% of YO-T2D had renal disease at diagnosis, with the incidence reaching 54.8% at 15 years [16]. NRP, as observed in this patient, is exceedingly rare in pediatric T2D, with the typical presentation in the sixth decade of life [17]. NRP is associated with risk of rapid progression of renal disease [17].

There is limited literature on microvascular complications of T2D in youth with PWS, although some studies suggest that diabetes-related complications may be less frequent [18]. In one retrospective study of PWS patients younger than 21 years old, 9 patients were found to have diabetes mellitus and none had microvascular complications or dyslipidemia [8]. In a cohort of patients with PWS aged 10 years or older, 7 of 29 patients developed microvascular complications [19]. One of the 7 patients was younger than 18 years when diagnosed with diabetic retinopathy 1.2 years after T2D diagnosis [19]. There is one other case reported in the literature of a youth with PWS who progressed to DN (stage III) in childhood [20].

Management of DN involves targeting glycemic control, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system blockade, and hypertension treatment [4, 17]. Additionally, empagliflozin has proven to be nephroprotective, including delaying progression of decline in renal function and reducing albuminuria [4, 21]. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) have also demonstrated nephroprotective effects, including reducing the risk of albuminuria and composite kidney outcomes [22].

There were multiple considerations for the choice of long-term therapy for this patient, including her developmental delay, the need for a regimen that was easy for the family to adhere to and preference for a nephroprotective agent. Metformin was started first and the addition of empagliflozin was deferred as the initial significant hyperglycemia posed risks including euglycemic DKA, fluid depletion leading to exacerbation of the AKI, and potential decreased efficacy in the setting of hyperglycemia [23, 24]. Empagliflozin-metformin was initiated following normalization of her creatinine and resolution of vulvovaginal candidiasis, as SGLT2 inhibitors can exacerbate this [25].

This case underscored critical issues surrounding the ability to access consistent healthcare, which contributed to significant delays in diagnosis and management of T2D and PWS in our patient. The patient's mother was informed of the diagnosis of diabetes 16 months prior to presentation; however, there was a likely misunderstanding possibly stemming from health literacy issues and lack of a primary care provider. This is not an uncommon problem, as statistics from 2023 indicate that 17.2% of Canadians do not have a consistent primary care provider [26]. Increased access to longitudinal healthcare is essential for improved health outcomes.

In conclusion, we have presented a case of a late diagnosis of PWS in an adolescent with severe manifestations of YO-T2D, characterized by HHS, nephropathy with NRP, and retinopathy.
