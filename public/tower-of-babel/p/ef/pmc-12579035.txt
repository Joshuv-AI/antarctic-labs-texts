The causal association between psychiatric disorders and gynecological cancer: a bidirectional Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yaru Wang, Lin Qi, Yitong Li, Qi Fu, Huanran Ma, Li Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPrevious studies have suggested a positive causal relationship between schizophrenia and ovarian, breast, and lung cancers. Additionally, research has indicated a positive correlation between bipolar disorder and thyroid cancer. However, some studies have also shown that mental disorders do not increase the risk of cancer, however, the causal relationship has yet to be thoroughly explored. In this study, we employed Mendelian randomization (MR) to examine the genetic connections between mental disorders and the risk of gynecological cancers.MethodsWe analyzed data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) on five mental disorders [schizophrenia, bipolar disorder (BD), major depressive disorder (MDD), attention-deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), and autism spectrum disorder (ASD)] and three gynecological cancers [cervical cancer (CC), ovarian cancer (OC), and endometrial cancer (EC)]. Instrumental variables were identified using significant single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs). We applied inverse variance weighting (IVW), weighted median (WM), and MR-Egger regression methods to assess the results, reporting effect sizes as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsGenetically predicted MDD was found to be causally associated with an increased risk of LGSOC (OR = 1.50; 95% CI: 1.07–2.09; P = 0.01), MOC (OR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.0–1.94; P = 0.008), BD with a decreased risk of EEC (OR = 0.76, 95% CI: 0.60–0.97, P = 0 .0249), and ADHD with an increased risk of LGSOC (OR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.04–2.1, P = 0.0293).ConclusionsOur study provides evidence of a causal relationship between genetically predicted MDD and OC risk, as well as between ADHD and OC risk. Additionally, we found that BD is linked to a reduced risk of EC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-025-03737-z.

Background

Cancer represents a major global health burden, with gynecological cancers primarily including OC, CC, and EC. OC include high-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (HGSOC), low-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (LGSOC), invasive mucinous ovarian carcinoma (MOC), clear cell ovarian carcinoma (CCOC), and endometrioid ovarian carcinoma (EOC). EC comprises two distinct histological subtypes: endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (EEC) and non-endometrioid endometrial carcinoma (NEEC). The incidence and mortality rates of these cancers have steadily increased [1]. Cancer is a leading cause of premature death among women worldwide [2]. The primary risk factor for CC is Human Papillomavirus (HPV) infection. Although vaccination rates are increasing [3], CC remains a leading cause of incidence, particularly among young women, according to 2018 global cancer statistics [4]. OC is among the most aggressive malignancies of the female reproductive system, with high incidence and mortality rates [5]. Early symptoms of OC are often subtle and can be mistaken for digestive disorders, leading to delayed diagnosis. As a result, most patients are diagnosed at an advanced stage [6, 7]. EC is the sixth most common cancer among women worldwide, with an increasing incidence, especially in developing countries [8]. Obesity is the strongest risk factor for EC [9, 10]. While many risk factors for gynecological cancers have been identified, others, such as mental health disorders, remain unexplored. Investigating these unexplored risk factors could offer new strategies for the prevention and treatment of gynecological cancers. Mental health disorders, including schizophrenia, BD, MDD, ADHD, andASD, contribute significantly to the global disease burden [11]. The impact of mental health disorders is increasingly recognized as a major public health issue [12]. Cancer not only causes physical harm but also profoundly affects psychological well-being, frequently exacerbating the onset of psychiatric disorders [13]. Schizophrenia is a complex psychiatric disorder characterized by cognitive impairments, emotional disturbances, and behavioral abnormalities [14]. The global annual incidence is approximately 15–20 cases per 100,000 people, with lower rates in high-income countries compared to low- and middle-income nations [15]. Patients with schizophrenia exhibit substantially elevated mortality rates, predominantly attributable to poor treatment adherence, inadequate medical care, adverse drug reactions, and suicide-related behaviors [16]. As early as 1995, studies reported that the incidence of gynecological cancers in schizophrenia patients was either slightly lower or comparable to the general population [17]. However, recent studies have demonstrated an elevated risk of gynecological cancers among patients with schizophrenia [18]. A meta-analysis revealed that antipsychotic use was associated with an increased risk of gynecological cancers, particularly endometrial carcinoma [19]. The prevalence of MDD has steadily increased. Approximately 300 million people worldwide are affected by MDD, which is now a leading cause of disability [20]. Research has shown that female cancers, such as OC and breast cancer, are associated with an increased risk of developing MDD [21]. A meta-analysis found a significant correlation between depression and higher rates of breast cancer recurrence, all-cause mortality, and cancer-specific mortality [22]. BD is a chronic mood disorder characterized by alternating episodes of elevated mood (mania or hypomania) and depression [23]. Early studies have established an association between bipolar disorder (BD) and increased cancer risk, particularly breast cancer. However, evidence regarding the BD-gynecological cancer relationship remains limited [24]. ADHD, a neurodevelopmental disorder, is commonly diagnosed in childhood, with hallmark symptoms of hyperactivity, inattention, and impulsivity. Some symptoms persist into adulthood [25]. The global prevalence of ADHD is estimated at 5% in children and 3. 4–4. 4% in adults, contributing to attention deficits, hyperactivity, and impulsive behavior [26]. The relationship between ADHD and cancer has received increasing attention in recent years. Although ADHD is primarily viewed as a neurodevelopmental disorder, emerging research explores its potential links to various health risks, including cancer [27]. Studies suggest that certain genetic and environmental factors may overlap between ADHD and specific cancers. Additionally, ADHD patients may have an elevated cancer risk due to impulsive behaviors, substance use, lifestyle choices, and delayed medical intervention [6]. ASD is another neurodevelopmental condition that has increased in global prevalence, with approximately 1–2 children per 100 diagnosed with autism [28]. The relationship between ASD and cancer has become an area of active research. Some studies suggest that individuals with autism may carry genetic mutations linked to cancer, such as PTEN gene mutations, which are associated with an increased risk of various cancers and may also contribute to the development of autism [29]. Several studies have suggested a potential link between certain psychiatric disorders and gynecological cancers. However, inherent biases in these studies may undermine the validity of the inferred causal relationships. Mendelian randomization (MR) is a robust tool in epidemiological research that uses genetic variation as an instrument to assess the causal relationship between risk factors and specific diseases [30]. In MR studies, alleles are randomly assigned to offspring, similar to a randomized controlled trial. This approach effectively mitigates confounding factors and reverse causality, common challenges in observational research [31]. In this study, Mendelian randomization is used to explore the potential causal relationship between psychiatric disorders and gynecological cancers, aiming to offer new strategies for their prevention and treatment.

Methods

Study design

The study design uses datasets from the GWAS public catalog (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/gwas/) and the Psychiatric Genomics Consortium (PGC) (https://pgc.unc.edu/) to conduct a two-sample Mendelian Randomization (MR) analysis. As this study relies solely on pre-existing data and does not involve new data collection from human populations, ethical approval was not required. In this study, mental disorders were treated as exposure variables, and gynecological cancers as outcome variables. We assessed the impact of SNPs, strongly associated with the exposure variables, on the outcomes. The MR analysis was based on three key assumptions [32]: (1) Correlation assumption: The selected instrumental variables (IVs) must be strongly associated with the exposure variables (schizophrenia, BD, MDD, ASD, and ADHD). (2) Independence assumption: The IVs should be associated only with the exposure variables, and not with potential confounding factors. (3) Exclusion assumption: The IVs should affect the outcome only through their influence on the exposure variables, not directly. The study design is shown in Fig. 1.

GWAS data

As shown in Table 1, the genetic data for mental disorders were obtained from the PGC database. Schizophrenia data were derived from a recent GWAS meta-analysis involving 320,404 individuals of European ancestry (76,755 cases and 243,649 controls) [33]. BD data were from a GWAS meta-analysis of 413,466 European participants (41,917 cases and 371,549 controls) [34]. MDD data were obtained from the most recent GWAS meta-analysis of depression. involving 3,887,532 European participants (525,197 cases and 3,362,335 controls) [35]. ADHD data were from a GWAS meta-analysis involving 225,534 European participants (38,691 cases and 186,843 controls) [36], and ASD data from a meta-analysis of 14 cohort studies involving 15,954 European participants (7387 cases and 8567 controls) [37]. CC data were from a 2021 GWAS meta-analysis (563 cases and 198,523 controls) [38]. OC data were mainly derived from summary data of a GWAS conducted by the Ovarian Cancer Association Consortium (OCAC) [39], include HGSOC (13,037 cases, 40,941 controls), LGSOC (1012 cases, 40,941 controls), MOC (1417 cases, 40,941 controls), CCOC (1366 cases, 40,941 controls), and EOC (2810 cases, 40,941 controls). EC data were primarily obtained from the GWAS summary data of the Endometrial Cancer Association Consortium (ECAC) [40], include EEC (8758 cases, 46,126 controls) and NEEC (1230 cases, 35,447 controls). Diagnosis of mental health disorders and gynecological cancers

The diagnosis of mental health disorders relies primarily on clinical interviews and standardized assessments, such as the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD), the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI), and the Self-Rating Anxiety Scale (SAS). Symptoms are typically assessed based on the diagnostic criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5) or the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10). These criteria define specific conditions to ensure diagnostic consistency.

The diagnosis of gynecological cancers involves laboratory tests, imaging diagnostics, pathological examinations, and genetic testing.

Selection of IVs

The selection of instrumental variables (IVs) must meet the following criteria for genome-wide significance: (1) All IVs must have genome-wide significance (P < 5 × 10^−8). For ASD, the threshold was relaxed to P < 5 × 10^−6 due to a lack of suitable IVs under the stricter threshold. This inevitably compromises the reliability of the findings. (2) The linkage disequilibrium (LD) parameter R² must be below 0.001, with the region restricted to within 10,000 kb. (3) SNPs strongly associated with the exposure variables in this study were excluded.

Statistical analysis

In this study, mental health disorders were considered exposure factors, and the three major gynecological cancers were the outcome variables. We assessed the relationship between exposure and outcomes using previously selected Ivs and the TwoSampleMR and MRPRESSO packages in R (version 4.4.1). The primary method used was the random-effects IVW method [41], supplemented by MR-Egger, weighted median (WM), simple mode, and weighted mode methods. The IVW method combines Wald estimates for each SNP using a meta-analysis approach to provide an overall estimate of the exposure’s effect on the outcome, enabling reliable causal inference [42]. Incorporating multiple variants in MR analysis enhances statistical power, but may also introduce ineffective pleiotropic genetic variants. To address this issue, we conducted supplementary analyses using the weighted median and MR-Egger regression methods [43].We performed sensitivity analyses to evaluate the robustness of the MR results. Cochran’s Q statistic (MR-IVW) was used to assess heterogeneity, with P >0.05 indicating no significant heterogeneity [38]. Pleiotropy was evaluated using the MR-Egger intercept method, with P >0.05 indicating the absence of significant pleiotropy [44]. Horizontal pleiotropy was assessed using the overall test in MR-PRESSO, with P >0.05 indicating no horizontal pleiotropy. MR-PRESSO was also used to identify and exclude outliers, enhancing result accuracy. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.1.

Results

The causal link between psychiatric disorders and CC risk

The results of the MR analysis are presented in Figure S7. The IVW analysis found no causal relationship between schizophrenia (OR = 1.00, 95% CI = 1.00–1.00, P = 0.39), BD (OR = 1.00, 95% CI = 1.00–1.00,P = 0.58), MDD (OR = 1.00, 95%CL = 0.99-1.00 P = 0.94), ADHD (OR = 1.00, 95% CI = 1.00–1.00, P = 0.88), ASD (OR = 1.00, 95% CI = 1.00–1.00, P = 0.78), and CC risk. The results from the WM and MR-Egger regression methods were consistent with those of the IVW method. Table 2 presents the P-values after false discovery rate (FDR) adjustment.Following FDR correction, the results remained consistent. The scatter plots (Figures S1-S5) show the causal estimates from each instrumental variable (IV). No pleiotropy was detected using the MR-Egger intercept (P > 0.05), and no significant heterogeneity was found in the genetic instruments (Cochran’s Q P-value > 0.05) (Table S1-S5). No significant outliers were identified, so none required removal using the MR-PRESSO method.The funnel plots for each MR analysis were roughly symmetric, with no significant deviations (Figures S9-S13). The leave-one-out analysis showed that the MR estimates remained stable even after excluding any single SNP.

The causal link between psychiatric disorders and OC risk

The results of the MR analysis are summarized in Fig. 3 and Figure S6. According to the IVW results, ADHD was significantly associated with an increased risk of LGSOC (OR = 1.48; 95% CI: 0.04–2.1; P = 0.03). Similarly, MDD showed significant associations with elevated risks of both LGSOC (OR = 1.50; 95% CI: 1.07–2.09; p = 0.01) and MOC (OR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.10–1.94; P = 0.008). No genetic association was found between ADHD and HGSOC (OR = 1.02; 95% CI 0.91–1.14; P = 0.75), MOC (OR = 1.19; 95% CI 0.87–1.63; P = 0.28), CCOC (OR = 0.79; 95% CI 0.59–1.07; P = 0.13), or EOC (OR = 1.10; 95% CI 0.84–1.44; P = 0.49). Similarly, no strong evidence of a causal relationship was found between schizophrenia and any gynecological cancers, including HGSOC (OR = 1.00; 95% CI 0.77–1.29; P = 1.00), LGSOC (OR = 1.05; 95% CI 0.767–1.65; P = 0.81), MOC (OR = 0.96; 95% CI 0.68–1.34; P = 0.81), CCOC (OR = 1.01; 95% CI 0.71–1.43; P = 0.95), and EOC (OR = 1.10; 95% CI 0.85–1.44; P = 0.46). Similarly, no significant genetic association was found between BD and these cancers, including HGSOC (OR = 1.01; 95% CI 0.93–1.10; P = 0.75), LGSOC (OR = 1.06; 95% CI 0.81–1.37; P = 0.67), MOC (OR = 1.00; 95% CI 0.79–1.28; P = 0.97), CCOC (OR = 1.14; 95% CI 0.92–1.42; P = 0.24), and EndoOC (OR = 1.21; 95% CI 0.98–1.48; P = 0.07). For MDD, no causal associations were found with HGSOC (OR = 1.02; 95% CI 0.91–1.14; P = 0.77), CCOC (OR = 0.98; 95% CI 0.74–1.30; P = 0.88), or EOC (OR = 1.09; 95% CI 0.85–1.38; P = 0.5). Similarly, no causal relationship was found between ASD and HGSOC (OR = 1.07; 95% CI 0.99–1.15; P = 0.11), LGSOC (OR = 0.91; 95% CI 0.72–1.15; P = 0.43), MOC (OR = 1.12; 95% CI 0.88–1.42; P = 0.43), CCOC (OR = 1.01; 95% CI 0.82–1.23; P = 0.96), or E0C (OR = 0.99; 95% CI 0.85–1.15; P = 0.85). The causal link between psychiatric disorders and EC risk

The MR analysis results are summarized in Fig. 3 and Figure S8. The IVW method revealed a causal association between BD and the risk of EEC (OR = 0.76; 95% CI 0.60–0.97; P = 0.02), the MR-Egger method indicated a causal relationship between BD and the risk of NEEC (OR = 1.91; 95% CI 1.04–3.51; P = 0.04), However, we prioritized the IVW results as the primary findings. No evidence was found for a causal relationship between schizophrenia and either EEC (OR = 1.15; 95% CI 0.94–1.40; P = 0.19) or NEEC (OR = 1.06; 95% CI 0.92–1.22; P = 0.44). Similarly, no causal associations were found between ASD and EEC (OR = 1.00; 95% CI 1.00–1.00; P = 0.78), NEEC (OR = 0.98; 95% CI 0.90–1.07; P = 0.71), or between ADHD and either EEC (OR = 0.87; 95% CI 0.66–1.14; P = 0.32) or NEEC (OR = 1.02; 95% CI 0.89–1.18; P = 0.78). Similarly, no causal relationship was observed between major depressive disorder (MDD) and risk of either endometrioid EC (OR = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.70–1.11; P = 0.31) or non-endometrioid EC (OR = 1.05; 95% CI: 0.92–1.20; P = 0.39). After FDR correction, whereas no evidence was found for causal links between schizophrenia, ASD, ADHD, MDD, BD and EC risk. Table 2 shows the P-values after FDR adjustment. We then performed heterogeneity and pleiotropy analyses. Cochran’s Q test results indicated that the Q-P value for MDD and ECC, NEEC was less than 0.05, suggesting the presence of significant heterogeneity. To mitigate the risk associated with this heterogeneity, we employed a random-effects model and conducted sensitivity analysis. The results showed that the intercept term had a P-value less than 0.05, indicating that the model was sensitive to potential biases, such as pleiotropy or sample selection bias. After excluding significant outliers using the MR-PRESSO method, the Cochran’s Q test P-values for the remaining analyses were greater than 0.05, indicating no significant heterogeneity (Table S1-S5). MR-Egger regression showed that the intercept term for each result was close to zero, with all P-values greater than 0.05, suggesting no horizontal pleiotropy in the MR analysis. The scatter plots illustrating causal estimates from each IV are shown in (Figures S1-S5). The funnel plot results were approximately symmetric, with no evident skew (Figures S9-S13). Leave-one-out analysis, excluding individual SNPs, confirmed that the MR results remained stable.

Discussion

Our findings provide evidence for causal relationships between genetically predicted MDD and OC, ADHD and OC, as well as BD and EEC risk. However, we observed an inverse causal association between BD and EC risk. After FDR correction, only MDD and MOC retained a significant causal link. The results suggest that psychiatric disorders are largely not genetic risk factors for gynecological cancers. Some of our findings differ from previous studies.

The risk of comorbid conditions in patients with psychiatric disorders is often overlooked. The relationship between schizophrenia and cancer has been widely discussed in the scientific literature. Early studies suggested that patients with schizophrenia exhibit immune resistance to cancer, leading to a lower cancer incidence. However, subsequent research indicates that schizophrenia may affect immune function, influencing cancer development and progression [45]. Some studies suggest that the risk of breast and prostate cancer in schizophrenia patients may be lower than in the general population [46, 47], with potential protective genes mitigating cancer risk. Conversely, other research suggests that schizophrenia patients may have an increased cancer risk. Our study provides genetic evidence of a causal relationship between psychiatric disorders and gynecological cancers. The reduced cancer risk in schizophrenia patients may be associated with differences in pathogenesis. Cancer generally involves abnormal cell proliferation, whereas schizophrenia is associated with neurotransmitter imbalances.

While cancer patients often experience significant psychological stress, this stress typically manifests as anxiety or depression. This study also provides new insights into the conflicting results regarding schizophrenia’s impact on cancer incidence. From a genetic perspective, we demonstrate that schizophrenia has no causal relationship with OC, EC, or CC risk. As for whether MDD is a risk factor for cancer, previous studies have suggested that MDD increases cancer risk. MDD is thought to be associated with Type C personality traits, which are characterized by abnormal emotional expression and regulation, potentially impairing immune function and increasing cancer risk [48]. A meta-analysis of 16 observational studies found a significant association between depression/anxiety and cancer mortality [49]. However, some studies suggest that depression may inhibit the activity of natural killer cells and DNA repair enzymes, both of which play crucial roles in cancer defense. In this study, MDD was found to increase the risk of OC, particularly elevating the risk of invasive mucinous ovarian carcinoma, potentially due to its influence on the female endocrine system and hormonal imbalances [48]. This is likely related to certain cancer types, such as breast cancer [50], and may also be associated with delayed medical visits or cancer screening due to a lack of social support or psychological factors [51].

Regarding BD, some studies suggest that BD patients may have an increased cancer risk, with cohort studies indicating a higher cancer incidence in BD patients, particularly for breast and lung cancer. This may be linked to lifestyle, medication use, or changes in the immune system [52]. In contrast, other studies suggest that certain cancer risks in BD patients may be lower, such as the reduced incidence of colorectal cancer compared to that in the general population. These differences may be explained by factors such as medical monitoring frequency, medication effects, or other unknown factors, which are consistent with our study’s findings [53]. In our study, BD was associated with EC but not with non-endometrial EC, possibly reflecting tumor susceptibility. Tumor cells may be more susceptible to EC. Previous research has suggested that BD treatment, especially with certain drugs like lithium or antidepressants, may indirectly affect hormone levels related to endometrial cancer development.

Research on ADHD and cancer risk is still in its early stages, and no conclusive findings have emerged. In this study, however, ADHD was found to increase the risk of OC, opening a new avenue for future research. Regarding ASD and cancer, the literature is controversial. Some studies argue there is no causal relationship between ASD and cancer [54], aligning with our findings. However, other scholars suggest a potential link between ASD and cancer [55]. Recent research has identified rare genetic mutations in ASD patients that may affect cell proliferation and differentiation processes, similar to those in cancer development mechanisms. Since MR studies are based on genetic exposure influencing outcomes without the interference of confounding factors, we believe our results are more reliable. Our study shows causal relationships between ADHD/MDD and ovarian cancer, and between BD and endometrial cancer, interpreted through the lens of genetic factors. Inconsistent findings may stem from factors such as medication use, lifestyle, or early cancer screening, while consistent results offer new genetic evidence linking psychiatric disorders with gynecological cancers and providing insights for further exploration of their pathogenesis and clinical prevention.

In summary, this study has several strengths, such as using MR’s random distribution of genetic variants, similar to randomization in RCTs, which helps minimize confounding variables and strengthen causal inference. By leveraging genetic characteristics, this approach avoids the biases inherent in traditional observational studies. Data for MR analysis are more accessible, facilitating better causal relationship analysis between diseases, which can lead to novel insights for disease prevention. However, this study has some limitations. While data were sourced from authoritative GWAS databases, the sample size remains relatively small, and the data are limited to European populations, lacking external validation. Moreover, there is a lack of clinical data to substantiate the findings.

Despite employing rigorous Mendelian randomization analyses, this study has several limitations: (1) Although our data were sourced from authoritative GWAS databases, the sample sizes remained relatively limited. (2) The study population exclusively comprised European ancestry, lacking external validation cohorts and clinical data to substantiate our analytical findings. (3) While we strictly adhered to the three core MR assumptions, the limited number of extracted SNPs for both ASD and cancer groups necessitated a moderate relaxation of the P-value threshold, potentially violating the relevance assumption. (4) Reverse MR analysis was not performed due to insufficient numbers of valid instrumental variables (SNPs) meeting both relevance and independence assumptions for the exposure variables (cancer groups).

Conclusion

This study is the first to use MR analysis to investigate the causal relationship between psychiatric disorders and the risk of gynecological cancers. Our findings provide genetic evidence supporting causal inference, suggesting that certain psychiatric disorders may influence the development of specific gynecological cancers. This discovery addresses a critical gap in research on mental health and cancer and emphasizes the need for enhanced early cancer screening and monitoring in individuals with psychiatric disorders, aiming to improve health outcomes in high-risk populations.
