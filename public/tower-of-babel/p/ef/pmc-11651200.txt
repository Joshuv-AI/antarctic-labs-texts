Stroke‐like episodes in patients with adult‐onset neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease and patients with late‐onset MELAS: A comparative study
Authors: Yuzhi Shi, Gehong Dong, Hua Pan, Hongfei Tai, Yi Zhou, An Wang, Songtao Niu, Bin Chen, Xingao Wang, Zaiqiang Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo delineate the characteristics of stroke‐like episodes (SLEs) in patients with adult‐onset neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID) and to compare these characteristics with those of patients with MELAS.MethodsTwenty‐three adult‐onset NIID patients who presented with acute or subacute brain disorders and 13 late‐onset MELAS patients were enrolled in the study. Patients with NIID were categorized into the SLEs group and the encephalopathy‐like episodes (ELEs) group according to the associated stroke‐like lesions (SLLs) findings. Clinical characteristics were compared between the SLEs group and the ELEs group among NIID patients and between NIID patients with SLEs and MELAS patients.ResultsEleven (47.8%) NIID patients who manifested acute or subacute brain disorders had detectable associated SLLs and were categorized into SLEs group. SLEs patients were more likely to report fever, headache, and seizures instead of sleep disorders than ELEs patients. Four (36.4%) NIID patients with SLEs absence of diagnostic or suggestive NIID imaging features. The clinical manifestations, laboratory test results, and neuroimaging and muscle biopsy histological features of NIID patients with SLEs majorly overlapped with those of late‐onset MELAS patients. Older age at the first SLE (OR [95% CI], 1.203 [1.045–1.384]), symptoms of movement disorders on admission (OR [95% CI], 9.625 [1.378–67.246]), and white matter hyperintensity in corpus callosum (OR [95% CI], 16.00 [1.542–166.46]) associated with the NIID diagnosis in patients with SLEs.InterpretationNIID patients with SLEs exhibit evident features of mitochondrial disorders. Interventions aimed at mitochondrial dysfunction might be a promising therapeutic approach for treating this disease.

Introduction

Stroke‐like episodes (SLEs) were first described in mitochondrial encephalopathy, myopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke‐like episodes syndrome (MELAS) in 1984. 1 SLEs are characterized by manifestations similar to those in ischemic stroke but additionally manifest with confusion, seizures, cognitive disturbances, or lactic acidosis. 2 Stroke‐like lesions (SLLs) associated with the SLEs can be found on cerebral imaging. SLE is a hallmark of mitochondrial diseases and other hereditary diseases, such as urea cycle disorders 3 and congenital disorders of glycosylation Ia. 4

Neuronal intranuclear inclusion disease (NIID) is a progressive neurodegenerative disease characterized by pathologic eosinophilic hyaline intranuclear inclusions in the central and peripheral nervous systems and multiple visceral organs. 5 In recent years, acute and subacute brain disorders with or without SLLs on cerebral imaging have been reported in patients with NIID in case reports or case series, which were described as MELAS‐mimicking episodes, 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 encephalitis‐like episodes 12 or acute encephalopathy. 13 Mitochondrial abnormalities, such as mitochondrial swelling and decreased mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, were also recently found in patients with NIID. 14

Given the diversity in definitions of acute‐ or subacute‐onset brain disorders, the specific characteristics of the episodes in patients with NIID remain unclear. Additionally, due to the overlap of clinical characteristics in both NIID patients and MELAS patients, it is necessary to thoroughly understand the features of SLEs in the two diseases. Distinguishing the two diseases during the acute phase is crucial for guiding decisions related to genetic sequencing strategy and treatment options.

To address this knowledge gap and improve the care of these patients, the aims of this study were (i) to delineate the characteristics of SLEs in patients with adult‐onset NIID and (ii) to compare the clinical manifestations, laboratory test results, neuroimaging features, and muscle biopsy pathology findings in adult‐onset NIID patients who presented with SLEs on admission and patients with late‐onset MELAS.

Materials and Methods

Participants

Patients with NIID

Patients with NIID were diagnosed based on their clinical manifestations, neuroimaging findings, and the GGC repeat expansion in the 5'‐UTR of NOTCH2NLC according to the diagnostic criteria for NIID. 5 Expansion with more than 60 repeats was considered pathogenic. 12 , 15 Ninety‐eight patients were diagnosed with NIID in the Department of Neurology at Beijing Tiantan Hospital between January 2019 and December 2023.

Among these patients with NIID, patients who presented with acute or subacute brain disorders manifesting as epilepsy, altered consciousness, cognitive impairment, dysarthria, mental or mood disorders, or other focal neurological deficiencies within 2 weeks before their admission were enrolled in the study (n = 23) and were categorized into two groups according to whether anatomically relevant acute or subacute cortical and subcortical SLLs were detected on brain magnetic resonance (MR) imaging 16 : the group of SLEs (n = 11) and the group of encephalopathy‐like episodes (ELEs) (n = 12) (Fig. 1). The categories of SLEs and ELEs were systematically validated by two experienced neurologists and radiologists.

Flowchart of the participants' enrollment.

Patients with MELAS

Patients with MELAS were diagnosed based on clinical manifestations, genetic sequencing results, and muscle biopsy findings according to the diagnostic criteria for MELAS. 16 Forty‐seven adult‐onset MELAS patients were diagnosed in the Department of Neurology at Beijing Tiantan Hospital between January 2019 and December 2023. Patients with late‐onset MELAS for whom the first SLE occurred older than 40 years old 17 and the last episode occurred within 2 weeks before their admission were enrolled in the study (n = 13) (Fig. 1).

The participant with previous brain lesions or epileptogenic comorbidities that were considered unrelated to their primary mitochondrial disease or NIID were excluded from the study.

Clinical data

Clinical data (sex, age at onset, number of episodes, presentations, etc.) were collected primarily from structured medical records. Disease severity of the SLEs was assessed by the Newcastle Mitochondrial Disease Adult Scale (NMDAS), which is a validated method for both measuring and monitoring disease manifestations and clinical features in patients with mitochondrial disease and is established out of three sections: current function, system‐specific involvement, and current clinical assessment. 18 The functional status of the patients was rated by Karnofsky performance status scale. 19

Neuroimaging

MR imaging was performed on a 3.0T scanner (Siemens or GE Healthcare). The protocol included T1‐weighted imaging, T2 and fluid‐attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR)‐weighted imaging, diffusion‐weighted imaging (DWI), and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) imaging. The severity of white matter hyperintensity (WMH) in the periventricular regions and deep white matter was assessed using the Fazekas score. 20 Single‐voxel 1H‐MR spectroscopy (MRS), post‐contrast axial T1‐weighted imaging, and arterial spin‐labeling (ASL) or computed tomography perfusion (CTP) were conducted for NIID patients who presented with SLEs and patients with MELAS.

Laboratory tests and electroencephalogram

Plasma lactate levels were tested in NIID patients who presented with SLEs and patients with MELAS. The cell counts, lactate, and protein levels in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) were documented in seven NIID patients with SLEs of NIID and four MELAS patients for diagnostic purposes. An electroencephalogram (EEG) was conducted in all patients. The encephalopathy‐like changes and epileptic discharge on EEG were recorded.

Genetic data

All results of the genetic analyses were reviewed by two neurologists with expertise in hereditary neurological diseases in consultation with a genetic counselor to confirm the pathogenicity of the variants.

A repeat‐primed polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was conducted to test the repeats of GGC expansion in the 5'UTR of NOTCH2NLC in all patients enrolled in the study, and nanopore sequencing with Cas9‐mediated enrichment was performed when the GGC expansion was too large to be detected by fragment analysis. 15 Whole‐exome sequencing of nuclear DNA and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) sequencing were performed in blood samples from patients who presented with SLEs. Long‐range PCR was used to detect deletion in mtDNA extracted from urine.

The abnormal repeat number of GGC expansion in NOTCH2NLC in patients with NIID and causative mutations to MELAS are listed in Table S1.

Skeletal muscle specimens

Skeletal muscle biopsy specimens from six NIID patients [three patients in the ELEs group (P5, P10, and P11) and three patients in the SLEs group (P2, P6, and P9), and four patients with MELAS (P7, P8, P12, and P13) were analyzed. All biopsies were taken from the biceps brachii muscle. All skeletal muscle specimens were cryopreserved at −80°C before analysis.

Histologic and immunohistochemical analysis

All samples of biceps brachii muscle were analyzed in the Department of Pathology at Beijing Tiantan Hospital following standardized procedures. Routine histological staining, immunohistochemical staining, and double‐immunofluorescent staining were conducted following standard protocols. The 7‐μm thick cryosections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (HE), modified Gomori trichrome (MGT), NADH‐tetrazolium reductase (NADH‐TR), succinate reductase (SDH), cytochrome C oxidase (COX), COX/SDH, oil red O (ORO), adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase PH 4.3 and 9.4), P62, etc., as described in a consensus statement. 21

The digital whole‐slide image (WSI) for specimens from the 10 patients mentioned above was created by digitally scanning the original H&E‐, NADH‐, Gomori‐, SDH‐, SDH/COX‐, and P62‐ stained glass slides at ×20 magnification with the Slide Scanning system SQS 40P (TEKSQRAY, China). An image viewer (TEKSQRAY, China) was used to view the slides and to quantify the muscle fibers with pathology features of mitochondrial impairment.

The sections were evaluated for evidence of mitochondrial abnormalities. Ten high‐power fields (HPFs) (×400 overall magnification) were used for muscle sample quantitative analysis. 22 Typically, 4–5 HPFs have a combined area of approximately 1 mm2. 23 Ragged red fibers (RRFs), ragged blue fibers (RBFs), and COX‐deficiency fibers on HE‐, SDH‐, and COX/SDH‐ staining WSI were counted in two randomized squares or rectangles covering a 1‐mm2 region respectively 21 (Fig. S1).

Statistical analysis

The data were analyzed by SPSS 20.0 software. Categorical variables are presented as numbers and /or percentages and were compared using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test. Quantitative variables are presented as the mean (±SD) or median (Q1 and Q3) and were compared using a t‐test or nonparametric test. Variables with statistical significance in the comparison of the characteristics of SLEs in patients with NIID and MELAS were entered into the single‐factor logistical regression models respectively to identify significant factors associated with the final diagnosis. Then, the variables with significance were entered into the discriminant analysis model to predict the final diagnosis of the patients with SLEs. A p value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Approval

This study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Beijing Tiantan Hospital, Capital Medical University, and was conducted following the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki and adhered to the Guidelines for Good Clinical Practice. Written informed consent was obtained from all individuals or their authorized surrogates at enrollment.

Clinical characteristics of patients with adult‐onset NIID who presented with acute or subacute brain disorders

Among adult‐onset NIID patients who presented with acute or subacute brain disorders, the mean age at disease onset was 53.60 ± 12.96 years. Eleven of the patients (47.8%) were women, 52.2% had a family history of dementia, parkinsonism‐like or psychotic symptoms, and the rest were sporadic cases. Acute or subacute brain disorders were reported as the initial manifestations in four (17.4%) patients. The mean age of the first acute or subacute brain disorders occurred at 56.91 ± 13.18 years. The most common manifestations on admission were cognitive decline (82.6%), movement disorders (73.9%), and bladder dysfunction (73.9%) (Table 1).

Comparations of clinical manifestations, neuroimaging and EEG features, and genetic sequencing findings between the SLEs group and the ELEs group among patients with adult‐onset NIID

Patients in the SLEs group (n = 11) were more likely to present with symptoms of fever (54.5% vs. 8.3%, p = 0.027), headache (72.7% vs. 16.7%, p = 0.012), and seizures (54.5% vs. 0%, p = 0.005) on admission than those in the ELEs group (n = 12). Sleep disorders were reported in half of the patients in the ELEs group and none of the patients in the SLEs group (p = 0.014) (Table 1).

Confluent WMH lesions between the subcortical and periventricular regions (Fig. S2A–C) and DWI hyperintense signals along the corticomedullary junction (CMJ) (Fig. S2F–I) were found in 78.3% and 73.9% of patients with NIID, respectively. Referring to the diagnostic or suggestive imaging features of NIID, WMH in the middle cerebellar peduncles (MCPs) (Fig. S2D) were less frequently found in the SLEs group than in the ELEs group (9.1% vs. 58.3%, p = 0.027), and a similar percentage of WMH in the paravermis (Fig. S2E) (45.5% vs. 66.7%, p = 0.414) and DWI hyperintense signals along the CMJ (54.5% vs. 91.7%, p = 0.069) were detected in the two NIID groups. Four (36.4%) NIID patients presented with SLEs showed none of the diagnostic or suggestive neuroimaging features mentioned above. Hypoperfusion in the subcortical regions of the brain (Fig. S2J) was found in patients with NIID by ASL or CTP imaging.

Encephalopathic abnormalities and epileptic discharge on EEG were detected in 73.9% and 43.4% of patients with NIID, respectively. The percentage of epileptic discharges detected was slightly higher in the SLEs group than in the ELEs group, but the difference was not significant (63.6% vs. 25.0%, p = 0.062) (Table 1).

The number of NOTCH2NLC GGC repeats was compared between the two NIID groups, and no significant difference was found [88.0 (113.0–126.0) vs. 102.5 (89.2–119.5), p = 0.498].

Clinical feature, laboratory, and neuroimaging findings of SLEs in patients with adult‐onset NIID and late‐onset MELAS

SLEs on admission

The mean age of the first occurrence of SLE (60.09 ± 8.72 vs. 47.53 ± 5.73, p < 0.001) was much older in patients with NIID than in patients with MELAS. Six (46.1%) patients with late‐onset MELAS harboring causative mutations in TL1 m. 3243 A>G (Table S1). Compared to SLEs in patients with NIID, SLEs in patients with MELAS less frequently presented as movement disorders (15.4% vs. 63.6%, p = 0.033), and none of the patients had bladder or defecation dysfunction or peripheral neuropathy (Table 1). The severity of SLEs and functional impairment were evaluated by the NAMDS and Karnofsky performance status scale, respectively, and no significant difference was found between the two SLEs groups (Table 2).

Similar blood lactate levels were observed on admission in NIID patients presented with SLEs and MELAS patients (3.24 ± 1.19 mmol/L vs. 2.80 ± 1.20 mmol/L, p = 0.504). CSF pressure was normal (normal reference value: 80–180 mmH2O) in all of them. Three patients with NIID had abnormally high CSF white blood cell (WBC) counts, with a range of 6–10/μL, and all patients with MELAS had normal WBC counts. The mean of WBC count, and protein, glucose, and lactate levels were compared between the two SLEs groups, and no significant difference was found (Table 2).

The neuroimaging features of SLEs on admission are listed in Table 2, and the representative neuroimaging findings of lesions associated with SLEs are shown in Figure 2A1–B3,D1–E2. In patients with NIID, brain MRI in the acute stage of the SLEs showed focal restricted diffusion on DWI and hyperintensity with edematous changes on T1‐weighted and FLAIR sequences in the cortical regions. Ten out of 11 patients with NIID had SLLs detected in the parietal, temporal and/or occipital lobes. Frontal involvement was found in 4 patients, and 1 patient had a bilateral frontal lobe involvement only (Fig. 2C). The location of the SLLs on admission was not significantly different between the NIID patients with SLEs and MELAS patients (Table 2). Bilateral cortical lesions were found in 1 patient (Fig. 2C) with NIID and 5 patients with MELAS (Fig. 2F–J) (9.1% vs. 38.5%, p = 0.085).

Gadolinium‐enhanced lesions in the cortical region on T1‐weighted images were found in the patients with SLEs both in the NIID group and in the MELAS group and typically presented as a meningeal‐like enhancement (72.7% vs. 53.8%, p = 0.703) (Table 2, Fig. 2B3,E2). The percentage of lesions with high perfusion was similar between the groups (45.5% vs. 69.2%, p = 0.114) (Table 2, Fig. 2A4,D3). Lactate peak appearance on MRS focusing on the lesions was less frequent in the NIID group than in the MELAS group (36.4% vs. 84.6%, p = 0.031) (Table 2, Fig. 2A5,D4).

Other neuroimaging characteristics of mitochondrial diseases were compared in patients with NIID and patients with MELAS, and no significant difference in the percentage of patients with cerebellum atrophy and calcification in the basal ganglia between the two groups was found (Table 2).

Clinical manifestations before the first SLE

The manifestations before the first SLE in patients with NIID and patients with MELAS were retrospectively recorded. Patients with NIID were more likely to suffer cognitive impairment (45.5% vs. 0%, p = 0.002), movement disorders (45.5% vs. 0%, p = 0.002), and bladder dysfunction (27.3% vs. 0%, p = 0.023) before the first SLE than those with MELAS (Fig. 3A).

Presentation and neuroimaging features of all recorded SLEs in the MELAS and NIID groups

Five patients with NIID and 8 patients with MELAS had experienced more than one SLEs. There were 23 SLEs in patients with NIID and 30 SLEs in patients with MELAS that were documented and retrospectively analyzed. The manifestations of the SLE events were recorded and are shown in Figure 3B. The symptoms of fever (52.5% vs. 10.0%, p = 0.002), movement disorders (43.5% vs. 6.7%, p = 0.002), cognitive decline (65.2% vs. 33.3%, p = 0.028), the disorder of consciousness (30.4% vs. 6.7%, p = 0.031), bladder dysfunction (26.1% vs. 0%, p = 0.004), constipation or diarrhea (21.7% vs. 0%, p = 0.012), and peripheral neuropathy (39.1% vs. 0%, p < 0.001) were more frequently reported in patients with NIID than in patients with MELAS.

SLLs associated with the 53 SLEs mentioned above were recorded and analyzed. The lesions were mainly located in the parietal, temporal and occipital lobes and there was no significant difference in the lesion location between the NIID group and the MELAS group. The basal ganglia (1/30, 33.3%) and cerebellum (1/30, 33.3%) were solely involved in patients with MELAS (Fig. 3C).

There were two patterns of lesion progression among different episodes based on location: lesions involving from one hemisphere to another (including bilateral hemisphere involved in an episode) (four patients with NIID and five patients with MELAS) and lesions expanding in the same hemisphere centered in the original lesions (one patient with NIID and three patients with MELAS) (Fig. 4). There was no significant difference in the two patterns of lesion progression mentioned above between patients with NIID and patients with MELAS (Fig. 3D).

Pathological characteristics of patients with SLEs in the MELAS and NIID groups

Compared to the pathological features of the three NIID patients who presented with ELEs (Fig. 5A–C), three NIID patients who presented with SLEs and four MELAS patients displayed obvious mitochondrial abnormalities indicated by the presence of RRFs, RBFs, and/or COX‐deficient fibers (Fig. 5D–I). There were more fibers with mitochondrial abnormalities per 2 mm2 in the MELAS group than those in patients with NIID presented with SLEs (Table 2). P62‐positive intranuclear inclusion bodies were found in the skeletal muscle cell nuclei of all six patients with NIID.

Factors associated with the discrimination of NIID and MELAS in patients with SLEs

Variables with statistical significance in the comparison of the characteristics of SLEs in patients with NIID and patients with MELAS, which are those listed in Tables 1 and 2 and Figure 3A with p < 0.05, were entered the single‐factor logistical regression model, respectively. Age at the first SLE onset (OR [95% CI], 1.203 [1.045–1.384], p = 0.010), presence of movement disorders on admission (OR [95% CI], 9.625 [1.378–67.246], p = 0.022) and WMH in corpus callosum (OR [95% CI], 16.00 [1.542–166.46], p = 0.020) were associated with the final diagnosis. The subsequent discriminant analysis showed that 83.3% of the patients who presented with SLEs were correctly classified by these three variables.

Discussion

To delineate the characteristics SLEs in patients with adult‐onset NIID and to compare these characteristics with those of patients with MELAS, we studied the clinical features of 23 patients with adult‐onset NIID who presented with acute or subacute brain disorders and compared the characteristics of SLEs between patients with NIID and patients with MELAS. In patients with NIID, patients with SLEs were more likely to present with fever, headache, and seizures and were less likely to have WMH in the MCPs or sleep disorders than patients with ELEs. The patients with MELAS were much younger at the age of the first SLE onset and less likely to report movement disorders, bladder dysfunction, constipation or diarrhea, and peripheral neuropathy on admission. The neuroimaging features of the SLLs in NIID patients were quite similar to those in patients with MELAS, except NIID patients showed less frequent detection of a lactic acid peak on MRS. Moreover, SLLs in the basal ganglia and cerebellum were exclusively found in patients with MELAS. Besides those known distinguishing neuroimaging features of NIID, NIID patients with SLEs showed more severe WMH rated by the Fazekas score and a higher likelihood of detection of WMH in the corpus callosum compared to the MELAS patients. Finally, age at the first SLE onset, presentation of movement disorders on admission and WMH in the corpus callosum were found to be associated with the final diagnosis.

In our study, 23 patients out of 98 patients (23.4%) with NIID who presented with acute or subacute brain disorders were enrolled. This percentage is in line with the previous reports that approximately one‐fifth of patients with adult‐onset NIIDs experienced a subacute encephalitic episode. 5 Additionally, NIID patients with moderate or severe WMH were presumed to be more susceptible to developing acute encephalitis‐like episodes with or without cortical lesions, which occurred in 32.4% of patients as the initial presentations and 55.9% as overall manifestations according to the report of a previous study on adult‐onset non‐vascular leukoencephalopathy. 12 These rates are substantially higher than those in the current study.

We found that approximately half of the patients with acute or subacute brain disorders had associated cortical lesions on MRI, which is consistent with the findings of Liu et al. 12 We further compared the clinical manifestations and neuroimaging features between patients with or without associated cortical lesions and found that patients with acute cortical lesions were more likely to present supratentorial cortical symptoms, such as fever, headache, and seizure during the indexed SLEs on admission, and were less likely to show the WMH in the MCPs, which is one of the suggestive imaging features of NIID. All of these cortical involvement symptoms mentioned above are common manifestations of SLEs in patients with MELAS as well. 16 , 24

In the present study, we compared the current function, specific system involvement, current clinical assessment and functional impairment, and blood and CSF laboratory test results in the acute stage of SLEs on admission, and no significant difference were found between the NIID patients and the MELAS patients. Cortical hyperintense lesions with edematous changes on FLAIR with or without contrast enhancement in the parietal‐temporal‐occipital lobes, hyper‐perfusion of the lesions, and a lactate peak on MRS can be observed in the acute stage of SLEs in patients with NIID. All these neuroimaging findings of SLEs mentioned above are extensively known as the neuroimaging features of MELAS, which resulted in the SLEs in patients with NIID being described as MELAS‐mimicking events. 7 , 8 , 10 Laminar necrotic changes, neuronal loss, and the proliferation of vessels with hypertrophic walls and petechiae were found in the swollen and enhanced cortical lesions of patients with NIID, which is also consistent with the histological findings of brain biopsy from patients with MELAS. 13 , 25 , 26 Mitochondrial abnormalities have been identified in muscle samples from patients with NIID and transgenic Drosophila models via electron microscopy. 14 In the present study, histological examination of muscle samples under light microscope indicated mitochondrial metabolism disorders in NIID patients who presented with SLEs. Mitochondrial metabolic disorders are confirmed to be one of the pathophysiological factors for patients with NIID based on the pathological findings. Furthermore, NIID patients with SLEs exhibit more prominent mitochondrial metabolic disorders compared to those with ELEs for the more overlapping clinical manifestations, neuroimaging features, laboratory test results, and pathological findings observed in NIID patients presented SLEs and patients with MELAS.

Notably, only about one‐third of SLEs in patients with NIID exhibited an elevated lactic acid peak, in contrast to more than four‐fifths of SLEs in patients with MELAS. The varied mitochondrial metabolic abnormalities in muscle biopsies were also observed in the patients with late‐onset MELAS had more muscle fibers with mitochondrial metabolic abnormalities than NIID patients presenting with SLEs. However, the statistical significance failed to show, possibly due to the limited sample size and the confounding effects of age. The age at the first SLEs in patients with NIID was over 10 years older compared to those in patients with late‐onset MELAS. The influence of age on the increase in mtDNA deletions 27 and histological changes in muscle fibers 28 should be taken into consideration in these conditions. It is noteworthy that one NIID patient in the SLEs group with no diagnostic or suggestive neuroimaging features of NIID, had an apparent mitochondrial metabolic disorder on muscle biopsy.

We also compared chronic neuroimaging features of NIID and MELAS in the patients who presented with SLEs, including WMH, DWI hyperintense lesions along the CMJ and in the corpus callosum, cerebellum atrophy, and calcifications in the basal ganglia. 12 The severity of periventricular and/or deep WMH in NIID patients with SLEs was marginally sever than in those with MELAS. A DWI hyperintense lesion along the CMJ is the best diagnostic neuroimaging feature for NIID, and WMH in the paravermis and MCPs are also suggestive neuroimaging features for NIID diagnosis. 12 Symmetric WMH in the MCPs is also considered to be one of the neuroimaging features indicative of mitochondrial leukodystrophies, especially Complex II deficiency, KSS and NDUFS1 variants. 29 In the present study, among the NIID patients who presented with SLEs, half of them were absent of hyperintense lesions along CMJ and one‐third of them displayed none of the diagnostic or suggestive neuroimaging features mentioned above. Patients lacking these neuroimaging features of NIID could be misdiagnosed with MELAS, autoimmune encephalitis, or encephalitis before reaching the final diagnosis. 7 , 30 In addition, these patients are likely to have an obvious mitochondrial metabolic disorder on muscle biopsy, making the diagnosis even more difficult. The older age at the first SLE onset, presentation of movement disorders and WMH in the corpus collapse can help in differentiating SLEs in NIID from those in MELAS, which would be much more helpful in those patients without diagnostic or suggestive neuroimaging features.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first comparative study on the acute‐ and subacute‐onset brain disorders in patients with adult‐onset NIID and systemically evaluate SLEs in patients with NIID and late‐onset MELAS. However, there are several limitations to our study. First, the sample sizes of the adult‐onset NIID and late‐onset MELAS groups were relatively small, and all patients were from a single center. Second, a portion of our data was gathered retrospectively to comprehensively determine the overall features of SLEs in patients with NIID and patients with MELAS. Third, we did not perform an external validation of the predictive value of the identified variables due to the limited study samples; therefore, this validation should be performed in future studies on another cohort.

Conclusion

Approximately one‐quarter of patients with NIID presented with acute or subacute brain disorders, and half of them had detectable associated SLLs on MR imaging. SLEs in patients with adult‐onset NIID displayed clinical manifestations, laboratory test results, neuroimaging findings, and pathological features that resemble those of late‐onset MELAS, and one‐third of them were without any diagnostic or suggestive neuroimaging feature of NIID. The age at the first SLE, presentation of movement disorders, and WMH in the corpus collapse would help in differentiating SLEs in NIID from those in MELAS. NIID patients with SLEs exhibit evident mitochondrial metabolic disorders. Interventions aimed at mitochondrial dysfunction might be a promising therapeutic approach for treating this condition.
