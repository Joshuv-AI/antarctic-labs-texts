All-two-dimensional, ion-gating synaptic transistors for high-temperature and ultralow-energy-consumption neuromorphic applications
Authors: Yonghuang Wu, Xiangpeng Liang, Ting Pan, Chenghui Wu, Wenxin Wang, Jiayuan Chen, Ruixuan Peng, Yuanmin Zhu, Cheng Zhen, Run Shi, Bochen Zhao, Zonglin Li, Yiqun Liu, Zhou Zhu, Jing Ma, Meng Danny Gu, Jianshi Tang, Kai Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-temperature intelligent devices operating above 125°C are crucial for applications where cooling is insufficient or impossible. Synaptic transistors, as an intelligent hardware in neuromorphic computing, allow multifunctionality and precise control of channel conductance, but they face challenges in achieving low energy consumption and optimal performance at high temperatures. This study introduces an all-two-dimensional (2D) high-temperature ion-gating synaptic transistor (A2D-HTIGST) using tungsten disulfide (WS2) as the channel, copper indium thiophosphate (CuInP2S6) as the ion-gating layer, hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN) as the electron barrier, and graphene as the gate. The A2D-HTIGST demonstrates efficient ion gating, low leakage current, high on/off ratio, pronounced hysteresis, and linear synaptic plasticity at elevated temperatures. Notably, it achieves an energy consumption of under 3 femtojoules per pulse at 200°C, surpassing other high-temperature and nearing room-temperature synaptic devices. The A2D-HTIGST further enables self-powered neuromorphic applications coupled with thermoelectric generators that use waste heat and advances high-temperature reservoir computing for temporal signal processing.

INTRODUCTION

Modern electronics is continuously advancing, becoming more intelligent and sophisticated (1, 2). As the key hardware in artificial intelligence, intelligent devices are trending toward high integration and multifunctionality, and in many cases, they must also adapt to complex environments that are inhospitable to humans, such as high temperatures (3, 4). High-temperature intelligent devices, capable of operating above 125°C, are highly needed for advanced applications in high-integration circuits, as well as in high-speed automobiles, deep-well drilling, and aerospace, where effective cooling—whether active or passive—is either insufficient or impossible. For example, electronic components around deep-well drillers and high-speed automotive engines must reliably operate at temperatures ranging from 150° to 250°C, whereas devices on spacecraft need to function at even higher temperatures (3, 4). As a type of intelligent device, artificial synaptic devices are used to process unstructured data and perform complex tasks in neuromorphic computing (1, 2, 5–7). Nevertheless, traditional electronic materials used in high-temperature devices, such as silicon-on-insulator and silicon carbides (8, 9), fall short in meeting the requirements of emerging neuromorphic applications. Consequently, previously unexplored electronic materials are very desired for the development of high-temperature neuromorphic devices.

In recent years, two-dimensional (2D) materials have received growing attention due to their atomic-level thickness and dangling bond–free van der Waals (vdW) interfaces, which minimize interface carrier scattering and potentially mitigate performance degradation at elevated temperatures (10). 2D materials exhibiting relatively high thermal stability, such as MoS2, WSe2, graphene, and BN (11), have been identified as promising materials for high-temperature devices (10, 12–16). It is shown that MoS2 field-effect transistors (FETs) using graphene-buffered electrodes can function reliably even after an annealing process at 350°C (17). By incorporating graphene electrodes and h-BN encapsulation, MoS2 FETs demonstrate the ability to operate as logic gates at 550°C, and WSe2 photodetectors exhibit a high photoresponsivity at 400°C (18, 19). Furthermore, a two-terminal graphene/MoS2−xOx/graphene memristor is developed, realizing remarkable switching performance at an elevated operating temperature of up to 340°C (13). For accurate neuromorphic computing, however, three-terminal synaptic transistors have been a focus of attention because they can provide a more efficient and precise control of channel conductance than memristors (20–24). To build a high-temperature synaptic transistor, Na+-diffused SiO2 is used as the ionic gate layer in a MoS2 transistor, which well emulates multiple synaptic functions including short-term plasticity at 350°C as well as long-term potentiation/depression (LTP/LTD) and spike timing–dependent plasticity (STDP) at 150°C (14). In addition to the ion-gating mechanism, a MoS2 transistor equipped with a charge tapping layer GaPS4 also enables memory and photoelectric synaptic functions at 400°C (16).

Despite these progresses on high-temperature functionality, there are still problems of decayed synaptic performance and elevated energy consumption for the 2D synaptic devices working at high temperatures. In particular, minimizing additional heat (self-heating effect) except the environmental heat is crucial to ensure the optimal performance of high-temperature devices (25, 26). Therefore, high-temperature devices must not only withstand environment heat but also exhibit strictly low energy consumption to avoid potential degradation or failure due to heat accumulation (Fig. 1A). However, current 2D high-temperature synaptic devices are still very preliminary in term of energy consumption, reaching as high as 103 to 106 fJ per event at ≥100°C (14–16, 20), which is far more than the biological level (several femtojoules) (6) and room-temperature devices (24, 27). This shortcoming mainly results from the decayed interface structure and increased leakage current at elevated temperatures, especially at the interface between the 2D channel and the gate dielectric. It remains a great challenge to develop high-temperature synaptic transistors with ultralow energy consumption and optimal synaptic performance.

In this work, we report an all-2D, high-temperature ion-gating synaptic transistor (A2D-HTIGST) with ultralow energy consumption and optimized synaptic performance. The A2D-HTIGST uses WS2 as the channel, layered CuInP2S6 (CIPS) as an ion-gating layer, h-BN as an electron barrier layer, and graphene as a gate electrode. The multilayer graphene/h-BN/CuInP2S6/WS2 heterostructure enables the device with exceptional ion gating efficiency, minimal carrier scattering at the vdW heterointerfaces, and effective reduction of leakage current. Consequently, the A2D-HTIGST exhibits a high on/off ratio, substantial hysteresis window, and remarkably low gate leakage current (<10 pA) at 200°C. Furthermore, outstanding linearity and symmetrical LTP/LTD plasticity are achieved in the A2D-HTIGST at high temperatures due to the efficient modulation of the channel conductance by mobile Cu+ ions in CIPS. The energy consumption of a single pulse is less than 3 fJ at 200°C, orders of magnitude lower than those of previously reported high-temperature synaptic devices, even reaching levels equivalent to the values of room-temperature devices. Encapsulated by h-BN, the A2D-HTIGST exhibits consistent performance during 20,000 pulses of weight update at 180°C in ambient air, with minimal cycle-to-cycle variation of less than 0.9%. Coupled with thermoelectric generators (TEGs) that harness waste heat, the A2D-HTIGST realizes self-powered operation for neuromorphic application. Moreover, on the basis of the ion dynamics at high temperatures, our device has pioneered the development of a high-temperature reservoir computing (RC) system for forecasting sunspot activity. Our work offers a promising way to build 2D synaptic transistors for neuromorphic computing at high temperatures, featuring multiple neuromorphic functionalities and ultrahigh energy efficiency (table S1).

RESULTS

Device design of A2D-HTIGST

An ideal high-temperature device necessitates both high-performance robustness and low energy consumption under high-temperature environments (Fig. 1A). Figure 1B shows the schematic of an A2D-HTIGST. The core component of this device is a vertical vdW heterostructure, including a graphene gate electrode, an h-BN electron barrier layer, a CIPS ion-gating layer, and a WS2 channel layer. All these 2D layers were mechanically exfoliated and then sequentially transferred on a SiO2/Si substrate, followed by a lithography and deposition of source/drain electrodes, to fabricate the A2D-HTIGST (fig. S1A). Typical thicknesses of the h-BN, CIPS, and WS2 flakes are 13.1, 57.2, and 4.8 nm, respectively, as measured by atomic force microscopy (AFM) (fig. S1, B and C). Magnified high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image demonstrates a clean and high-quality WS2/CIPS interface (Fig. 1C). HAADF-STEM image and corresponding elemental mapping images across the entire multilayer heterostructure by energy-dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) also clearly show the stacked layers of graphene, h-BN, CIPS, and WS2. The Raman spectrum of the WS2/CIPS heterostructure exhibits WS2 Raman modes of E2g ~ 353 cm−1 and A1g ~ 420 cm−1, as well as the anion (P2S64−) vibration mode at 100 cm−1 and cation (Cu+) vibrational mode at 315 cm−1 (fig. S2) (28–30), all of which are present without apparent peak shifts compared with pure WS2 or CIPS, suggesting little charge transfer between WS2 and CIPS.

As a layered material, CIPS consists of both ionic and covalent bonds (31, 32) and has a wide bandgap of ~3.1 eV (fig. S3). Within each layer, Cu and In cations as well as P-P dimers are arranged in a triangular pattern and confined within an octahedral framework formed by S atoms. Under a large electric field, Cu+ ions can escape from the lattice and migrate within/outside the plane, making CIPS an ion conductor (33, 34). There are three reasons for selecting CIPS as the ionic layer in our device. First, the mobility of Cu+ ions in CIPS enhances at high temperatures, which contributes to a high ion-gating capacitance. As shown in Fig. 1D, the capacitance of CIPS appears independent of frequency at room temperature but notably increases with the rise of temperature in the low frequency region (<105 Hz). This result suggests that, at high temperatures, there exist double-layer capacitances at CIPS-electrode interfaces due to the migration of Cu+ ions, having CIPS an ionic conductor, whereas at room temperature, CIPS functions more like a normal dielectric. Second, CIPS has a moderate ionic conductivity, not too high or too low, for ion-gating synaptic devices working at high temperatures (e.g., 125°~300°C, applicable for many high-temperature scenarios). Although some liquid and solid electrolytes like LiClO4/PEO (35), LiPON (36), and LaF3 (37) can operate within a suitable range of ion conductivity (10−5~10−3 S cm−1, green region in Fig. 1E) as ion-gating dielectrics at room temperature, the markedly increased ion conductivity at elevated temperatures (>10−3 S cm−1) will shorten the retention time in synaptic devices and lead to high leakage current. Other solid electrolytes like silica (38) and yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ) (39) have very low ion conductivities even at high temperatures, also not suitable for high-temperature synaptic transistors. In contrast, CIPS has an ionic conductivity of ~10−4 S cm−1 in the temperature range from 125° to 200°C, which guarantees both high ion-gating efficiency and long retention time. Third, CIPS also exhibits a good thermal stability. It is found that there are no apparent changes of a CIPS flake in the surface morphology and in the characteristic Raman peaks before and after an annealing process in air at 200°C for 30 min, indicating no apparent oxidation (fig. S4). All of the advantages make CIPS suitable for high-temperature ion-gating transistors.

Besides the ion-gating layer, a channel material with wide bandgap and high thermal stability is also preferred in the A2D-HTIGST to adapt to high-temperature environments. This selection ensures that the channel maintains a lower carrier concentration at elevated temperatures, thereby helping to preserve the device performance and stability. Among transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), WS2 stands out for its relatively wide bandgap (~2 eV) and good thermal stability [~250°C in air (40) and ~350°C in vacuum (41)], making it an ideal choice for the channel. Moreover, we also use h-BN, an insulating 2D material having a bandgap of ~6 eV that is much larger than CIPS, as a charge barrier layer to reduce the gate leakage current between WS2 and CIPS at high temperatures. Figure 1F clearly shows that, at the temperatures more than 150°C, the leakage current for the device with the h-BN layer is always lower than that without the h-BN layer and reaches about three orders of magnitude lower than the latter at 200°C. Our all-2D ionic gate transistor, which integrates the design of high-interface-quality 2D materials, the selection of materials with matched electrical conductivity at high temperatures, and the barrier layer to reduce leakage current, lays a solid foundation for the device to simulate synaptic plasticity under high-temperature conditions and to achieve novel neuromorphic computing.

Working mechanism of A2D-HTIGST

Figure 2 (A and B) illustrates the mechanism of the A2D-HTIGST. At high temperatures, mobile Cu+ ions in CIPS migrate toward and accumulate at the WS2-CIPS interface under a positive gate voltage. The double-layer capacitance due to the accumulation of Cu+ ions at the interface induces an electron-doping of the n-type WS2 channel, thereby enhancing the channel conductivity (Fig. 2A). When a negative gate voltage is applied, Cu+ ions move away from the WS2-CIPS interface, depleting the WS2 channel (Fig. 2B). Figure 2C shows that the A2D-HTIGST exhibits a large counterclockwise hysteresis and a high on/off ratio approximating 103 in the transfer curves measured at high temperatures ranging from 180° to 220°C. When the temperature increases from 180° to 200°C, Cu+ are more sufficiently activated, depleting the WS2 channel more effectively under a negative Vgs bias. This enhancement of the ion-gating effect outweighs the inherent increase in current due to the higher temperature, resulting in an overall decrease in Ids. However, as the temperature further rises to 220°C, the increase in carrier concentration becomes the dominant factor, leading to a rise in the off-state current and a reduction in the hysteresis. The notable hysteresis existing at high temperatures has the device potentially function as high-temperature synaptic transistors to implement artificial neural synaptic functions. With the increase in the sweeping rate, the hysteresis narrows and the switching ratio increases (fig. S5), aligning with the behavior of previous devices based on the ion-modulating mechanism (27). Notably, the ferroelectric properties of CIPS should not prominently exhibit at the high temperatures ranging from 180° to 200°C that have already exceeded the Curie temperature of CIPS (only 42°C) (42), which suggests that ion gating is the predominant mechanism for the transfer curve of A2D-HTIGST. Moreover, although the gate leakage current (Igs) of the device increases with temperature, it is still less than 15 pA at Vgs = 2 V at 220°C due to the charge barrier h-BN layer (fig. S6), providing the basis for low energy consumption of the high-temperature device.

To further elucidate the migration dynamics of Cu+ ions along the out-of-plane direction in CIPS, time-of-flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (TOF-SIMS) and STEM-EDS are used to analyze the Cu+ spatial distribution in a vertical graphene/CuInP2S6/graphene device. Under a constant voltage of 1 V for 5 min at 200°C, the device shows a notable decrease in the Cu signal close to the positive graphene electrode and an increase in the opposite electrode in the TOF-SIMS depth profiles of Cu+ ions (Fig. 2D), suggesting that Cu+ ions can be driven by an electric field and migrate across CIPS interlayers at high temperatures. Furthermore, cross-sectional STEM-EDS mapping images of the device reveals that Cu and In elements accumulate near the negative electrode (Fig. 2E and fig. S7), showing inhomogeneous distributions, whereas P element remains uniformly distributed. This contrast is attributed to electric field–driven migration of Cu+ and In3+ ions, contrasting with the immobile neutral P atoms.

We also used Raman mapping spectroscopy to characterize the migration of Cu+ ions in CIPS (Fig. 2, F and G to I, and fig. S8). Figure 2 (G to I) shows the mapping image in the depth profile of CIPS for the characteristic Raman peak of Cu+ vibrational mode at 315 cm−1 under varied voltages. Compared with the pristine CIPS before applying a voltage (Fig. 2G), the distribution of the Raman peak intensity apparently shifts downward after the applying of a 2-V voltage to the top electrode for 300 s (Fig. 2H), suggesting that the Cu+ ions migrate out of the CIPS lattice plane and toward the bottom electrode. When a voltage of −2 V is applied to the top electrode for 300 s, Cu+ ions migrate toward an opposite direction (Fig. 2I). The apparent change of the Cu+ vibrational mode intensity in the depth profiles further indicates the migration of Cu+ across CIPS interlayers along the electric field (Fig. 2J). These TOF-SIMS, STEM-EDS, and Raman spectroscopic results, combined with the capacitance measurement, suggest that the electric field–induced Cu+ migration can induce high double-layer capacitance at high temperatures, which results in the gate control of A2D-HTIGST.

Multiple synaptic plasticity and low energy consumption

In biological neuron systems, a neural impulse can be transmitted from one neuron (presynapse) to the next (postsynapse) through the influence of neurotransmitters, thereby triggering a postsynaptic current (PSC) (Fig. 3A). In the artificial synaptic transistor, the gate serves as the presynaptic terminal and the channel as the postsynaptic terminal. The conductance of the channel is considered as the synaptic weight, which mimic the degree of connectivity between two neurons. The synaptic weight can be modulated by the gate voltage, and such modulation is referred as synapse plasticity.

In our A2D-HTIGST, when a gate voltage pulse (2 V, 100 ms) is applied, the retention time of the PSC increases with temperatures due to the Cu+ migration at high temperatures (fig. S9). Figure 3B shows the time-dependent PSCs induced by the stimuli of the gate voltage (±2 V) with different pulse durations ranging from 0.1 to 1 s at 200°C. The PSC increases immediately after a single Vgs pulse of 2 V and then gradually decreases but finally stabilizes at a nearly constant plateau level and does not return to the baseline value after 300 s, indicating a long-term change in synaptic weight. The applied positive Vgs pulse drift Cu+ ions in CIPS toward the WS2 channel and accumulate at the WS2/CIPS interface, thereby n-doping the channel and increasing the PSC. The gradual decay of PSC after the Vgs pulse suggests that a portion of Cu+ may undergo back-diffusion, whereas the plateau above the baseline indicates that the remaining Cu+ trapped at the WS2/CIPS interface retain the long-term conductance increase. When the pulse duration increases from 0.1 to 1 s, the PSC increases and remains from 184 to 222 nA (obtained at t = 300 s) after the stimuli. This is because a longer Vgs stimulation causes more Cu+ ions to drift, accumulate, and partly be trapped at WS2/CIPS interface, thereby increasing the stable value of the PSC. When a negative single gate pulse is applied, similar long-term synaptic plasticity is observed although the change of PSC is negative. A quantitative analysis of the retention behavior is provided in fig. S10 and note S1.

Figure 3C shows the LTP and LTD in our A2D-HTIGST through applying a series of current pulses at high temperatures. At 180°C, the channel conductance increases gradually from 0.49 to 3.84 μS under 64 pulses (2 pA, 50 ms) and then returns to 0.49 μS under another 64 pulses (−2 pA, 50 ms). As the temperature increases, the conductance of the devices decreases slightly, but the normalized conductance of LTP/LTD remains almost unchanged (Fig. 3D). The LTP/LTD exhibits high symmetry and good cyclic repeatability due to the stable regulation of Cu+ ions migration in CIPS at high temperatures, facilitating high-temperature neuromorphic computing. Linear update of LTP and LTD conductance is very important for constructing artificial neural networks (ANNs). The nonlinearity of the A2D-HTIGST is as low as −0.8, −1.0, and −0.8 for LTP and 1.9, 2.1, and 2.2 for LTD, at 180°, 200°, and 220°C (fig. S11), respectively, close to the ideal value of 0, meaning a highly linear conductance update. Figure S12 shows measured Vgs values under the applied Igs pulses at different temperatures. The excellent cycle-to-cycle reproducibility confirms that our results are reliable and not limited by instrumental resolution or background noise.

STDP is an important form of long-term plasticity, which refers to the adjustment of synaptic weights based on the relative timing difference (Δt) between presynaptic spikes (Vpre) and postsynaptic spikes (Vpost). The changes in the synaptic weight (ΔW) are defined as ΔW = (I − I0)/I0 × 100%, where I0 and I are the channel currents before and after applying the equivalent voltage pulse (Vpre − Vpost), respectively. ΔW is positive when the presynaptic spike arrives before the postsynaptic spike (Δt > 0), and it is negative when Δt < 0 (Fig. 3E). The absolute value of ΔW exponentially decreases with increasing the time interval between the two spikes for both Δt > 0 and Δt < 0, conforming with the asymmetric Hebbian rule (43). Benefiting from the long-term plasticity at high temperatures, our A2D-HTIGST can also simulate a simplified Pavlovian conditioning experiment. As shown in Fig. 3F, gate voltage pulses (3 V, 100 ms) and drain voltage pulses (0.1 V, 100 ms) mimick the “feeding food” neutral inputs and “ringing bell” unconditional inputs, respectively, whereas the output current over a threshold (14 nA) at a drain voltage of 0.01 V represents “salivation.” Before training, “feeding food” triggers the “salivation” output, whereas “ringing bell” cannot reach the threshold current. After the simultaneous training with “ringing bell” and “feeding food,” the “ringing bell” alone can trigger the “salivation” output, revealing the establishment of an association between a neutral stimulus and an unconditional stimulus.

As mentioned in the Introduction, minimizing the energy consumption is very essential to avoid the heat accumulation and potential destroy for the devices working at high temperatures. Figure 3G compares our A2D-HTIGST with other ion-gating synaptic transistors in terms of minimum single-pulse energy consumption and operating temperatures. It can be found that the energy consumption generally increases with the increase in temperature due to the larger leakage current at elevated temperatures. Notably, owing to the ultralow gate leakage current, our A2D-HTIGST has the minimum single-pulse energy consumption of only 1.1, 1.8, and 2.7 fJ at 180°, 200°, and 220°C, respectively. The switching energy is calculated as the integral of the product of voltage and current over the pulse duration and is comparable to that in voltage-driven synaptic devices (more discussions in note S2). The femtojoule-level switching energy at elevated temperatures is attributed to the combination of the high capacitance of CIPS, for which the needed gate voltage can be greatly reduced, and the low gate leakage current due to the existence of the h-BN barrier layer. Such low energy consumption is six orders of magnitude lower than that of the reported synaptic transistor operating at 150°C (14) and even reaching to the lowest level of room-temperature devices, potentially enabling more efficient neuromorphic computing at high temperatures. In addition, our A2D-HTIGST is promising for energy-efficient large-scale integration at high temperatures. This is because femtojoule-level per-pulse energy consumption minimizes the switching energy of synaptic transistor arrays, and the low operation voltage (below 2 V) along with low gate leakage current (at the pA level) enables the lower-power peripheral circuits (more discussions in note S3).

Self-powered A2D-HTIGST for ANN computing

Because of its low-energy consumption, the A2D-HTIGST can be efficiently driven by the waste heat in high-temperature environments. As a demonstration of such a function, two TEGs attached to a heat source (mimicked with a hot plate at 180°C) is used to capture the ambient thermal energy to power the A2D-HTIGST (Fig. 4A). In this circuit, dc voltages (~0.8 V) are generated by the TEGs due to the temperature gradient between the heat source and ambient environment, then converted into a pulsed voltage by electric relays, and lastly applied to the gate of the device, enabling the modulation of electrical conductivity of the device for neuromorphic computing (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 4B, in response to the stimuli of a series of voltage pulses (0.8 V, 0.2 s) realized by the TEGs, the PSC of the device increases from 3.9 to 9.2 nA, revealing a learning process. After removing the stimuli, the PSC gradually decreases, simulating the forgetting process. This forgetting process can be described by an exponential decay function, I=I0+A exp(−tτ), where I is the PSC, t is the time, τ is the decay constant, I0 is the stabilized current, and A is a fitting coefficient. After the first cycle of learning and forgetting processes, the second learning process reaching to the same PSC value becomes shorter, and the decay time constant for the following second forgetting process changes from 26 to 37 s, well emulating the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve that characterizes memory retention over time in the human brain (44).

Furthermore, the A2D-HTIGST has the potential to perform excellent neuromorphic computing self-powered by the waste heat. As shown in Fig. 4C, continuous LTP and LTD are tested by a series of stimulations with 20 positive/negative voltage pulses (±0.8 V, 0.2 s) generated by the TEGs, and the conductance of the self-powered A2D-HTIGSTs can be updated step by step at 180°C. To demonstrate the potential of our device in high-temperature neuromorphic computing, we build an ANN based on the LTP/LTD conductance levels to perform supervised learning (Fig. 4D). First, cumulative distribution function (CDF) of the conductance change (ΔG) at different conductance states is calculated (Fig. 4E). Then, a three-layer network including one hidden layer is built for backpropagation supervised learning by the CrossSim software (44). The network, with architectures of 784 × 300 × 10, is trained and evaluated using handwritten digits of 28 × 28 pixels from the Modified National Institute of Standards and Technology (MNIST) dataset. After training, the recognition accuracy of the network based on the experimental ΔG can reach 97.8% at 180°C with only a 0.4% deviation from the ideal training, revealing the low noise of the A2D-HTIGST (Fig. 4F).

To further enhance the stability and durability of the high-temperature device in ambient environments, we used graphene as source/drain electrodes and packaged the device with a 20-nm-thick h-BN layer (fig. S14A). The device is then subjected to long-term cycling tests of LTP and LTD at 180°C in ambient air. As shown in fig. S14B, the device conductance can be modulated between 3.3 and 158 nS under the stimulation of consecutive gate current pulses (±20 pA, 70 ms). Notably, the device maintains very stable and controllable LTP and LTD during more than 20,000 current pulses and demonstrates a small cycle-to-cycle variation of 5.8% in conductance, which indicates an SD normalized to the entire weight range of around 0.9% for each weight-update cycle. The device maintains highly stable and controllable LTP/LTD characteristics over 255 cycles with consistent dynamic range and nonlinearity (fig. S14, D to F). Raman spectra of the device after the cycling test reveals that the characteristic peaks of both WS2 and CIPS remain unchanged in both position and intensity (fig. S14C), indicating that the A2D-HTIGST exhibits very good stability at high-temperature operating conditions. The long-term stability of the Cu+ migration is further confirmed by the cycling test of transfer curves at 200°C, showing consistent current and hysteresis window over 800 cycles (fig. S15).

RC at high temperatures

Because the high-temperature kinetic behavior of Cu+ ions under gate voltage pulses leads to a gradual change in the channel conductivity over time, the A2D-HTIGSTs exhibits superior nonlinear characteristics to serve as neurons for extending low-power RC to high-temperature scenarios. RC is a bioinspired computing paradigm that leverages the dynamic properties of materials for high-efficiency computation, enabling broad application prospects for the processing and prediction of time-series data in physical domain (45–48). For example, as a fundamental indicator of solar activity, sunspots are the dark spots that frequently appear on the sun and vary over time. The RC system can be used on artificial satellites to process and predict the number of observed sunspots (Fig. 5A). A schematic diagram of the parallel RC architecture based on the A2D-HTIGSTs is shown in Fig. 5B. The network is composed of an output layer, a reservoir layer, and an input layer. Multiple A2D-HTIGTs that act as processing cores receive the masked signal from a common input signal. Different mask matrices are used for different transistors to increase the state richness (49). We measured the random voltage pulse responses of the synaptic transistor between −1.5 and 1.5 V by applying pulsed voltage at 200°C. Next, a long short-term memory (LSTM) model was fitted to the above data to obtain a model of the volatile properties of the device, as shown in Fig. 5C and fig. S16 (50).

The RC system is testified by performing a waveform classification task to classify randomly distributed sine and square waves with the same amplitude and frequency. The input values were mapped to the range of [−1 V, 1 V] and then were masked by a random but fixed mask with a length of 5 (49). Because 10 devices with different masks are used in parallel, 50 states will be read from the virtual nodes at each time step, which was used in training the output weights by Ridge regression. The target output is a binary sequence of 0 and 1 representing the waveform types of sin and square wave, respectively. As shown in the bottom of Fig. 5D, the RC system based on the A2D-HTIGSTs can correctly classify the sine and square waves after training. Figure 5D also shows the reservoir states corresponding to the input signal of waveform sequences. Furthermore, the dependence of classification performance on device nonlinearity and noise is explored as described in figs. S17 and S18 and note S4. It can be concluded that the network with the moderate nonlinearity performs much better than that with the linear or strong nonlinear region. Meanwhile, although the current noise slightly declines its performance as temperature increases, the A2D-HTIGST can still perform effectively at around 200°C.

To further demonstrate the potential of the RC based on all-2D synaptic transistors for applications in high-temperature environments, it was used to predict the number of sunspots. Twenty devices were used in parallel in RC and the states of all virtual nodes at different time steps (fig. S19). Figure 5E shows the actual and single-step predicted values of the sunspots number. The number of sunspots exhibits a certain periodicity to be captured by the A2D-HTIGST–based RC system. The normalized root mean square error (NRMSE) between the predicted and actual values is 0.13, indicating that the A2D-HTIGST–based RC system can accurately predict the number of sunspots. The impact of the number of prediction steps and the number of parallel reservoirs on the accuracy of sunspot number prediction was evaluated, as shown in Fig. 5F. The prediction accuracy gradually decreases with an increase in the number of prediction steps, with the NRMSE rising from 0.16 to 0.38. Although increasing the number of parallel reservoirs improves the prediction accuracy initially, no further apparent improvement is observed beyond five parallel reservoirs. This may be due to the difficulty of obtaining more linearly independent states through time-multiplexing operations when the mask length is limited. These findings highlight the A2D-HTIGST–based RC system’s effectiveness in processing nonlinear signals in high-temperature environments.

DISCUSSION

In conclusion, we demonstrate high-temperature resistant and low-energy consumption A2D-HTIGSTs using vdW graphene/h-BN/CIPS/WS2 multilayer heterostructures. Owing to the exceptional ion-gating efficiency of CIPS and the effective reduction of leakage current by h-BN, the device exhibits a high on/off ratio of 103, a substantial hysteresis window, and a low gate leakage current (<10 pA) at high temperatures. Benefiting from the efficient modulation of the channel conductance by the mobile Cu+ ions in CIPS, high linearity and symmetrical LTP/LTD plasticity are achieved in the temperature range from 180° to 220°C. The device exhibits an ultralow energy consumption of less than 3 fJ per single pulse, marking the lowest among high-temperature synaptic devices reported to date and even reaching to the lowest level of room-temperature devices. Integrating with TEGs to harness ambient thermal energy, the device realizes self-powered operation, under which the device can achieve neuromorphic computing with ANNs that have a remarkable 97.8% accuracy and replicate learning and forgetting curves. Moreover, on the basis of the ion dynamic behavior of the device at high temperatures, an RC system used for high-temperature scenario is constructed to predict sunspot numbers, with an NRMSE of less than 0.13. Although the inherent trade-off in long-term retention or back-diffusion of Cu+ exists in our device, the high-temperature dynamics of Cu+ are exploited for diverse neuromorphic computing (more discussions in note S5). Our work offers an effective strategy that combines all-2D and ion-gating designs for synaptic devices toward high-efficiency neuromorphic computing at high temperatures.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials and device fabrication

Graphene/h-BN/CIPS/WS2 multilayer heterostructures were fabricated by a general dry transfer method using polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS). Few-layer graphene, h-BN, WS2, and CuInP2S6 flakes were mechanically exfoliated from bulk crystals (purchased from 2D Materials Corp.) by Scotch tape onto PDMS films. Then, the 2D materials were sequentially transferred onto a target Si substrate with 300-nm-thick SiO2 through a homemade deterministic transfer stage under an optical microscope. After the transfer process, the obtained heterostructures were annealed in vacuum (<1 × 10−3 Pa) at 200°C for 60 min to remove residuals. Last, the source/drain electrodes were fabricated through a standard electron-beam lithography followed by an electron-beam deposition (4 × 10−4 Pa) of 5-nm-thick Cr at a rate of 0.5 Å s−1 and 50-nm-thick Au at a rate of 1 Å s−1.

Structural characterizations

Surface morphology of the all-2D heterostructure was characterized by an optical microscope (Olympus BX51M) and an atomic force microscope (Bruker Multimode 8). A smart Raman confocal micro-Raman module (Institute of Semiconductors, Chinese Academy of Sciences) was used for Raman measurement with a ×100 objective lens [numerical aperture (NA) = 0.90] under the backscattering geometry, which is coupled with a Horiba iHR550 spectrometer and a charge-coupled device (CCD) detector. A Wi Tec alpha300R instrument with an excite wavelength of 532 nm was used for Raman mapping. Cross-sectional STEM samples were prepared using focused ion beam (FIB) milling under FEI Helios Nanolab 600i. Before the sample preparation, a protective layer of poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) was spin coated on the surface of the sample to avoid the damage during the FIB milling. Cross-sectional HAADF-STEM and EDS mapping images were observed under a double Cs-corrected Themis 60-300 microscope.

TOF-SIMS analysis was performed using an ION-TOF GmbH TOF SIMS 5 instrument. During the experimental process, depth profiling of the sample was carried out with O2+ ions, assisting in the identification of the CIPS layer through the detection of C+ signals originating from the graphene layers. A comparative evaluation of the Cu intensity profiles between the device subjected to a 1-V constant voltage and the device without it showed a notable decrease in the Cu signal near the top electrode and a simultaneous increase near the bottom electrode. These findings strongly suggested the directed migration of Cu+ ions under the influence of an electric field.

Electrical measurements

Electrical measurements of devices were conducted using a Keithley 4200SCS semiconductor parameter analyzer equipped with high-resolution Source Measurement Unit (SMU) including a current amplifier and Pulse Measurement Unit (PMU). All measurements were conducted inside a shielded vacuum probe station (FC-D-02, Lan Hai Science Instrument Ltd.) using low-noise coaxial cables with the system grounding to minimize external electromagnetic interference. For the testing atmosphere, long-term cycling tests of LTP and LTD were conducted in air. All other electrical measurements were carried out in vacuum.

To obtain the ionic conductivity of CIPS at different temperatures, a bulk CIPS with a thickness of about 32 μm was sandwiched by two electrodes. An ac voltage of 1 mV was applied across the sample over a frequency range spanning from 1 Hz to 1 MHz, and the corresponding current response was measured to analyze the variation of impedance with frequency. Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) of CIPS were measured at different temperatures, and the equivalent circuits of the device as well as the Nyquist plots at 25° and 150°C were shown in fig. S20. According to the fitting results, the resistance (R) of CIPS along the vertical direction was obtained, and the ionic conductivity (σ) of CIPS could be calculated using the following formulaσ=1R·LSwhere L is the distance between the electrodes, i.e., the thickness of CIPS, and S is the effective area between the electrodes. The temperature dependence of ionic conductivity of CIPS follows the Arrhenius equation (37)σ=A·exp(−EakBT)where A is the constant associated with the material, T is the absolute temperature, kB is the Boltzmann constant, and Ea is the activation energy. The fitted activation energy of CIPS is found to be 0.64 eV.

The switching energy per pulse is given byE=Igs×Vgs×twhere Igs is the amplitude of the gate current pulse, Vgs is the corresponding gate voltage measured during the pulse application, and t is the pulse width. For the LTP and LTD processes, Igs was set to +2 and −2 pA, respectively, with a fixed t of 50 ms.

The nonlinearity of LTP and LTD was evaluated by (51)GLTP=B(1−e−PνP)+GminGLTD=−B(1−e(P−1)νD)+GmaxB=Gmax−Gmin1−e−νwhere GLTP and GLTD are the conductance of potentiation and depression, respectively, P is the normalized number of pulses, and νP and νD are the nonlinearity for conductance update of potentiation and depression, respectively.

The cycle-to-cycle variation is calculated by averaging the coefficient of variation of each conductance state, which is defined as the SD divided by the mean over the 255 cycles.

Benchmark for RC

The RC network was composed of an output layer, a reservoir layer, and an input layer. The input signal, after undergoing time-division multiplexing (TDM) with a mask length of 5, was loaded onto the synaptic transistor device model, thereby generating multiple virtual nodes in the time domain and obtaining a high-dimensional mapping vector of length 5. The richness of the state vector was increased by adding more devices and configuring different masks to receive the same input signal in parallel. During the training phase, the state vectors within the reservoir were computed using a linear regression algorithm to obtain the output weights (Wout). In the inference phase of the network, the state vectors were multiplied by the trained output weights to produce the output results.

As for waveform classification task, the input signals consisted of randomly distributed sine and square waves composed of 16 points that had the same amplitude and frequency. We generated a total of 4000 input signals, half of which were used to train the output weights Wout and the other half were used to test the classification results. As for sunspots number prediction, we used sunspot dataset from the World Data Center, Sunspot Index and Long-term Solar Observations (WDC-SILSO, Royal Observatory of Belgium, Brussels) that provides the monthly sunspot data from 1749 to 2023 encomprising 3296 months (52). Here, we used the sunspot data of first 2000 months (1749 to 1915) for training and the subsequent 1296 months until September 2023 for testing.

The definition of the NRMSE isNRMSE=1m∑n=1my′(n)−y(n)2σ2y(n)where m is the data length, y′(n) and y(n) are the predicted and target values, respectively, and σ2 is the output variance.
