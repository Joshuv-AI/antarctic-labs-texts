Severe hypotension requiring resuscitation in tumour-related carotid sinus syndrome: a case report
Authors: Martijn G Klingenberg, Steef J Sinkeler, Niels Koopmans, Robin E Plaat, Bogdan Enache, Sebastian Ekkehard Beyer, Dmitry Duplyakov, Niklas Schenker

Introduction

Syncope is defined as ‘transient loss of consciousness due to cerebral hypoperfusion, characterized by rapid onset, short duration, and spontaneous complete recovery’.1 According to the European Society of Cardiology guidelines of 2018, syncope is divided into reflex syncope, orthostatic hypotension syncope, and cardiac syncope.1 An uncommon cause of reflex syncope is carotid sinus syndrome (CSS) (1% of all syncope cases). CSS is usually caused by pressure on the carotid sinus by movement of the neck and almost exclusively affects males above 65 years of age. Both hypotension and bradycardia caused by CSS may lead to syncope. In rare cases, compression of the carotid sinus by a progressive or invasive tumour may lead to CSS.2 Here, we report a case of a metastasized oropharyngeal tumour in a 57-year-old female with encasement of the left carotid sinus causing severe recurrent syncope, leading to an in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA).

Case report

A 57-year-old caucasian female, from the Northern Netherlands, without previous medical history, was presented at the emergency department with recurrent syncope. Syncope usually occurred in the morning and was preceded by visual impairment. She was admitted for telemetric monitoring. During a 24-h observation period, no significant rhythm or conduction abnormalities were observed; hence, discharge followed. The day after, the patient presented with a recurrence of syncope.

Contrary to previous admission, symptomatic hypotension and bradycardia were observed (Figure 1). At this time, the patient mentioned a painful swelling in her neck that she first noticed a few weeks prior.

ECG during syncope. Sinus bradycardia 25 bpm, normal axis and conduction (PQ 160 ms; QRS 60 ms; QTc 284 ms). No significant ST/T-abnormalities.

Upon physical examination, a mass was found on the left side of her neck located near the carotid artery. No other masses were palpable in the head or neck area. Further inspection by an ear, nose, and throat (ENT) specialist showed an abnormality at the base of the tongue, suggestive of malignancy. The laboratory results and electrocardiogram (ECG) after regaining consciousness did not show any significant abnormalities. Ultrasound of the neck revealed a mass caudal to the submandibular gland of 35 × 19 × 18 mm.

Biopsy of the tongue revealed a squamous cell carcinoma, additional positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) scan showed a primary mass at the base of the left side of the tongue with bilateral lymph node metastases. A large necrotizing mass was found on the left side of the neck, with encasement of the external carotid artery near the carotid sinus extending to the parotid gland (Figure 2). There were multiple small fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG)-active lymph nodes caudal to this mass. Furthermore, abnormal FDG activity of parapharyngeal lymph nodes in Level 1B/II on the right side of the neck was noted.

FDG PET-CT. PET-CT showed an FDG-positive mass at the base of the tongue of 21 mm crossing the midline. Furthermore, an FDG-positive necrotizing mass is visible on the left with encasement of the left external carotid artery extending to the parotid gland.

Hence, the diagnosis was CSS, secondary to compression or invasion of the carotid sinus by squamous cell carcinoma. The patient was admitted to the cardiology department for telemetric observation. During admission, frequent self-limiting episodes of bradycardia as low as 25 bpm (Figure 1) and hypotension of 75/40 mmHg were noted, without transient loss of consciousness (TLOC).

In the morning, an IHCA occurred due to severe hypotension with persistent loss of consciousness. Remarkably, telemetric observation revealed a persistent sinus rhythm of 55–60 bpm (Figure 3). Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) was started for a total of two full minutes before the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) with a maximal Glasgow coma score.

Heart rhythm during resuscitation: sinus rhythm, 55–60 bpm.

The ENT specialist, radiotherapist, and oncologist were consulted. Acute surgical intervention was deemed unfeasible due to the risk of tumour spill. Additionally, the location and size of the tumour would result in having to sacrifice the left carotid artery, phrenic nerve, and vagal nerve. Radiotherapy was not expected to have any immediate effects, while also risking a possible blowout of the tumour.

Hence, dexamethasone was started in an attempt to reduce oedema surrounding the tumour and reduce compression of the carotid sinus. Additionally, the patient was started on midodrine. Midodrine is an α1-adrenergic receptor agonist that increases vasoconstrictor tone. Midodrine has shown positive effects in patients with CSS,3 but its exact mechanism of action on CSS is unknown. After starting dexamethasone and midodrine, the recurrence of syncope decreased. After multidisciplinary consultation, the patient was started on neoadjuvant chemotherapy (docetaxel/cisplatin/5-fluorouracil) for volume reduction and would start afterwards with chemoradiotherapy.

Unfortunately, the patient subsequently suffered multiple complications from chemotherapy. Hypovolemic shock, caused by massive enteral bleeding secondary to mucositis from chemotherapy, prompted the initiation of palliative care. The patient died from the consequences of multi-organ failure and hypovolemic shock.

Discussion

Here, we describe a 57-year-old female with severe CSS, requiring CPR. CSS was caused by compression of the carotid sinus by a malignancy and successfully treated with midodrine. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first time a case has shown CSS leading to severe hypotension, requiring CPR.

Three subtypes of CSS exist: primary vasopressor-mediated CSS defined by hypotension, cardio-inhibitory CSS defined by severe bradycardia or asystole, or a mixed variant.2–4 The therapeutic strategy depends on the subtype. The vasopressor subgroup should be managed with sufficient hydration and salt intake as long as there are no contraindications (e.g. heart failure). In refractory cases, fludrocortisone or midodrine may be considered.4 Cardiac pacing may be considered for primary cardio-inhibitory CSS, resulting in symptomatic bradycardia or asystole, which may reduce the recurrence of syncope.1,4 In the mixed variant, cardiac pacing may still be considered, but it is unlikely to prevent recurrent episodes of syncope.1

In our patient, we observed episodes of both vasopressor-mediated and mixed variant CSS. The episode requiring CPR was caused by a vasopressor-mediated variant of CSS, due to its persistent nature with no bradycardia observed on telemetric monitoring at the time. Afterwards midodrine, shown previously to be effective in CSS,3 and dexamethasone, for a reduction of oedema, were started after which the frequency of recurrence decreased.

The cause of CSS in this case was a necrotizing metastasis of a squamous cell carcinoma of the tongue. No other precipitating factors were identified. Neurectomy or surgical debulking of the tumour encasing the carotid sinus was considered,5 but deemed unfeasible in this case. Chemotherapy may be beneficial for long-term treatment of the underlying cause of CSS2; hence, the patient was started on neoadjuvant chemotherapy. Radiotherapy was considered, which could relieve symptoms of CSS in tumour-related encasement of the carotid artery in a palliative setting,6 but the risks of tumour blowout were deemed too large.

This patient was started on docetaxel/cisplatin/5-fluorouracil and developed mucositis as a consequence of this therapy. Mucositis has been previously described as a complication of this chemotherapy.7

Ultimately, CSS requiring CPR is rare. One case report by Lilitsis et al.8 described the need for CPR during thyroidectomy because of a multinodular goitre. That patient presented with bradycardia, followed by asystole after positioning of the head. After repositioning of the head, heart rhythm was restored. In contrast to the case by Lilitsis et al., which described mainly the cardio-inhibitory type, our case described a primarily vasodepressor-mediated type of CSS. No other literature on CSS leading to CPR could be found. Therefore, this case report illustrates a severe and rare complication of tumour-related CSS.
