The role of frailty in older adults with Non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome: a Chinese population-based cohort study
Authors: Jingwen Shi, Wen Tang, Shan Wang, Xiaopei Hou, Yunli Xing, Junpeng Kan, Bing Liu, Hongwei Li, Ying Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo investigate the risk factors for major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) and rehospitalization in older adults with non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS) within one year after discharge.MethodsThis single-center prospective cohort study enrolled 528 patients, who were categorized into robust, pre-frail, and frail groups based on the FRAIL scale. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was used to identify factors influencing frailty or pre-frailty in these patients. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to assess the factors associated with MACCE and rehospitalization in these patients and examine the prognostic differences among subgroups.ResultsThe prevalence of frailty was 11.4%, and pre-frailty was 51.9%. Frailty was found to be an independent risk factor for MACCE (HR = 2.193, 95%CI 1.111–4.326, P = 0.024) and rehospitalization (HR = 1.906, 95%CI 1.031–3.522, P = 0.040) in older adults with NSTE-ACS. Subgroup analysis revealed that the old-old frailty group had a higher risk of MACCE compared to the old-old non-frailty group (HR = 2.389, 95%CI 1.072–5.322, P = 0.033), while no significant difference in MACCE risk was observed between the young-old frailty group and the old-old frailty group. The comorbid frailty group had a higher risk of rehospitalization than the comorbid non-frailty group (HR = 1.884, 95%CI 1.113–3.190, P = 0.018), but no significant difference in rehospitalization risk was found between the non-comorbid frailty group and the comorbid frailty group.ConclusionsFrailty is an independent risk factor for MACCE and rehospitalization in older adults with NSTE-ACS. Subgroup analysis further suggests that frailty maybe have greater impact on MACCE and rehospitalization in this population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40520-025-03207-0.Keywords: Older adults, Non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome, Frailty, Prognosis

Introduction

According to the statistics and forecasts from the United Nations Population Division, the global elderly population is expected to reach approximately 1.6 billion by 2050. Cardiovascular disease is common among older adults and is a leading cause of death and hospitalization in this population [1, 2], accounting for 40% of mortality in China [3]. Studies indicate that non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS) is a significant contributor to in-hospital mortality among older individuals, with a mortality rate of 2–8% following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) [4].

In recent decades, there has been growing concern about the impact of frailty on comorbidities. Reports suggest that the prevalence of frailty in older adults with NSTE-ACS ranges from 4.7% to 27% [5–8]. These studies show that frail older adults with NSTE-ACS also experience higher mortality and hospitalization rates.

According to various reports, frailty is generally considered an independent risk factor for out-of-hospital mortality in older adults with NSTE-ACS [9–11]. However, some studies have not confirmed frailty’s predictive ability for poor prognosis in older adults with NSTE-ACS. There are limited studies examining the relationship between frailty and the occurrence of MACCE and rehospitalization in older adults with NSTE-ACS, and there are no unified guidelines on the impact of frailty on invasive treatment for these patients [7, 8, 12]. Frailty is often associated with mechanisms such as oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and metabolic imbalances, which overlap with the pathogenesis of cardiovascular disease [13]. Whether early detection of frailty and effective intervention can delay the progression of cardiovascular disease remains inconclusive.

This study enrolled 528 older adults with NSTE-ACS in Beijing. Our aim was to explore the relationship between frailty and major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) and rehospitalization within one year of discharge in older patients with NSTE-ACS. We also sought to assess the value of frailty in predicting prognosis in this patient population.

Materials and methods

The datasets generated during and/or analyzed during the current study are not publicly available, but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Subjects

A total of 528 patients with non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome (NSTE-ACS) were enrolled from the Cerebrovascular Center and Geriatrics Department of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University, between January 2020 and November 2021. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age ≥ 65 years; (2) meeting the 2016 Guidelines for the Diagnosis and Treatment of NSTE-ACS, which include typical angina symptoms, normal or abnormal electrocardiogram (ST-segment depression, transient ST-segment elevation, T-wave changes), and normal or abnormal markers of myocardial injury (enzymes). The exclusion criteria were: (1) refusal to undergo frailty assessment; (2) patients with end-stage renal disease on dialysis, or those with end-stage renal disease and malignant tumors, severe liver insufficiency, acute infection, severe dementia, or complete disability. All patients provided written informed consent, and the legally authorized representative signed the informed consent if the patient was deemed to have cognitive impairment. All data were de-identified prior to analysis, and this study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of Beijing Friendship Hospital, Capital Medical University (Ethics Approval Number: 2020-P2-097).

Groups

Group methodology: In clinical research, common scales for assessing frailty include the FRAIL scale, the Fried scale, and the Clinical Frailty Scale. Among hospitalized cardiovascular patients, the FRAIL scale has proven feasible for assessing frailty in stable individuals. Studies have shown that this scale is highly effective for screening and assessing frailty, predicting mortality, and limitations in daily activities, particularly in older adults [14]. The FRAIL scale is a questionnaire-based screening tool including five items: fatigue, resistance, ambulation, illness, and weight loss, with each item scoring 1 point. Patients were grouped according to their FRAIL score: frail group (score of 3 or more), pre-frail group (score of 1 or 2), and non-frail group (score of 0).

There is no consensus in current research on the classification of the very elderly. Some studies suggest that individuals aged 80 and above are considered very elderly, while others define this group as 75 and older [15–18]. For this study, very elderly individuals were defined as those aged 75 and older. Further classification was based on both age and frailty status. Patients aged 75 or older were classified as the old-old group, and those aged 65–74 years as the young-old group. A FRAIL score of 3–5 points was used to define the frailty group, while a score of 0–2 points defined the non-frailty group. The participants were divided into four subgroups: young-old non-frailty group, young-old frailty group, old-old non-frailty group, and old-old frailty group.

Currently, comorbidities in older adults with cardiovascular disease are commonly assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) [19, 20]. Patients were further grouped based on comorbidities and frailty. A FRAIL score of 3–5 indicates frailty, while a score of 0–2 indicates non-frailty. A CCI score < 1 indicates no comorbidities, and a score ≥ 1 indicates the presence of comorbidities. Based on these criteria, the enrolled patients were divided into four subgroups: non-comorbidity with non-frailty, comorbidity with frailty, comorbidity with non-frailty, and comorbidity with frailty.

Research methods

General data collection

Clinical data at admission were collected, including age, gender, body mass index (BMI), abdominal circumference, comorbid chronic diseases, cardiac function class, and GRACE score.

Laboratory data collection

Laboratory indicators at admission were collected, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin (ALB), Creatinine (Cr), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), white blood cell count (WBC), hemoglobin (HB), platelet count (PLT), troponin I (cTnI), N-terminal pro-brain natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and fasting blood glucose.

Comprehensive Geriatric Assessment: (1) Functional ability: Assessed using the Barthel Index. A higher score indicates better performance in activities of daily living. A score of 100 represents no dependence, 61–99 indicates mild dependence, and 0–60 indicates moderate to severe dependence. (2) Comorbidity: Assessed using the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI). A higher score correlates with a higher risk of mortality due to comorbidities. (3) Gait speed: Patients were asked to walk 4 m at a steady pace without obstacles, and the walking speed was measured. (4) Physical activity: Evaluated using the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) and categorized into three levels of physical activity: low, moderate, and high. (5) Nutritional status: Assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment short-form (MNA-SF), which classifies patients as having normal nutritional status, at risk of malnutrition, or malnourished. (6) Polypharmacy: Defined as the use of five or more medications (including health supplements), based on medications taken the day before admission. All geriatric comprehensive assessments were conducted by two professionally trained nurses.

Follow-up data

Follow-ups were conducted at the 6th and 12th months after discharge, using a combination of telephone interviews and review of inpatient and outpatient medical records. The follow-up time was up to 12 ± 2 months after discharge. The primary endpoint was major adverse cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events (MACCE), while the secondary endpoint was rehospitalization. MACCE included recurrent angina, recurrent myocardial infarction, repeat revascularization, recurrent heart failure, and cardiac death. Rehospitalization events were defined as hospital stays or emergency department visits lasting 24 h or more for any reason. The analysis of the results of this study was based on the follow-up data at 12 months after discharge.

Statistical methods

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 27.0.1. For normally distributed quantitative data from multiple independent samples, results are presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̅ ± s), and one-way ANOVA was used for analysis, followed by post-hoc group comparisons using the LSD method. For non-normally distributed quantitative data, the median and interquartile range (M(Q1, Q3)) were used, and independent samples non-parametric tests were conducted. For categorical data from multiple independent samples, percentages (%) are presented, and chi-square tests were used, with post-hoc comparisons performed using Z-tests. Bonferroni correction was applied after multiple pairwise comparisons for indicators showing statistically significant differences. Baseline variables with missing data exceeding 10% were excluded from analysis. For variables with ≤ 10% missing values, multiple imputation was employed to handle missing values. Confounding factors with P < 0.05 in univariate regression are included in multivariate regression. Before multiple logistic regression analysis was conducted to identify factors influencing frailty or pre-frailty in elderly patients with NSTE-ACS, it was tested for multicollinearity. Multiple Cox regression analysis was used to examine the correlation between frailty and adverse events occurring outside the hospital. Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using GraphPad Prism to represent the occurrence of adverse events in patients with different frailty statuses. Differences in the prognostic impact of various subgroups on older NSTE-ACS patients were explored using the Cox proportional hazards regression model. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 817 older adults with suspected diagnosed acute coronary syndrome initially were included in the study. According to the exclusion criteria, 289 (35.4%) patients were excluded because of the following reasons: (1)ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (n = 89); (2) other angina pectoris or other unrelated diagnoses (n = 34); (3) no frailty assessment (n = 159); (4) age < 65 years (n = 7). Finally, 528 patients were enrolled in the analyses (Fig. 1).

Clinical characteristics of elderly NSTE-ACS patients with frailty

In these patients, the prevalence of frailty was 11.4%, and pre-frailty was 51.9%. The median age was 72 (68, 76) years. Among the patients, 307 were male (58.3%) and 221 were female (41.7%). Analysis of baseline clinical characteristics showed that frail patients were older, had a higher proportion of females, worse cardiac function, higher GRACE scores, and were more likely to have a history of chronic diseases such as diabetes and cerebrovascular disease. Frail patients were also more likely to experience a decline in daily living activities and physical activity, along with poor nutritional status. Laboratory analysis revealed that frail patients had lower levels of albumin and hemoglobin, and higher levels of inflammatory markers, troponin, and NT-proBNP (Table 1).

Influencing factors of elderly NSTE-ACS patients with frailty or pre-frailty

This study further investigated the factors influencing frailty in older adults with NSTE-ACS. No evidence of multicollinearity was demonstrated after including confounding factors in the collinearity diagnostics (VIF < 5) (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Table 2). Multivariate logistic regression analysis identified poor nutritional status (OR 3.468, 95%CI 1.136–10.587, P = 0.029), low physical activity (OR 5.253, 95%CI 2.114–13.054, P < 0.001), high comorbidity status (OR 1.552, 95%CI 1.072–2.247, P = 0.020), and slow walking speed (OR 1.505, 95%CI 1.254–1.806, P < 0.001) as independent risk factors for frailty in older adults with NSTE-ACS. Similarly, poor nutritional status (OR 2.386, 95%CI 1.342–4.244, P = 0.003), low physical activity (OR 1.877, 95%CI 1.235–2.884, P = 0.003), high comorbidity status (OR 1.303, 95%CI 1.105–1.536, P = 0.002), slow walking speed (OR 1.131, 95%CI 1.020–1.253, P = 0.020), and polypharmacy (OR 1.638, 95%CI 1.075–2.496, P = 0.022) were identified as independent risk factors for pre-frailty in older adults with NSTE-ACS. These findings indicate that poor nutritional status, low physical activity, slow walking speed, and high comorbidity status are independent risk factors for both frailty and pre-frailty in older adults with NSTE-ACS (Supplementary Tables 1 and Supplementary Table 2).

Relationship between frailty and prognosis in elderly paitents with NSTE-ACS

In this study, 528 older adults with NSTE-ACS were enrolled, of whom 476 completed a one-year follow-up, while 52 were lost to follow-up. Reasons for loss to follow-up included unreachable phone numbers, refusal to participate, incorrect contact information, and prolonged phone shutdowns. Comparison of baseline clinical characteristics between patients who completed follow-up and was lost to follow-up showed no significant differences except for age (P > 0.05) (Supplementary Table 3). The main adverse events were MACCE and rehospitalization. During 6 months after discharge, frail older adults with NSTE-ACS demonstrated an elevated risk of adverse events (Supplementary Table 4). At the end of follow-up (Median 12.6(12.3,13.0) months), statistical analysis showed that frail older adults with NSTE-ACS had a higher risk of MACCE and rehospitalization (Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 1, and Supplementary Fig. 2). Multivariate Cox regression analysis identified frailty (HR = 2.193, 95% CI 1.111–4.326, P = 0.024) and age (HR = 1.074, 95% CI 1.027–1.123, P = 0.002) as independent risk factors for MACCE within one year post-discharge in older adults with NSTE-ACS. Additionally, frailty (HR = 1.906, 95% CI 1.031–3.522, P = 0.040) and comorbidity status (HR = 1.173, 95% CI 1.020–1.349, P = 0.025) were found to be independent risk factors for rehospitalization during 12 months follow-up after discharge in this patient population (Table 3and Table 4).

Further grouping of follow-up patients by age and frailty showed that, compared to the young non-frailty group, the old-old frailty group had a significantly higher risk of developing in-hospital MACCE (HR = 4.338, 95% CI 1.889–9.967, P < 0.001). The young-old frailty group also had a higher risk of in-hospital MACCE (HR = 2.321, 95% CI 1.043–5.163, P = 0.039), while the old-old non-frailty group had a moderately increased risk (HR = 1.861, 95% CI 1.032–3.198, P = 0.030). Compared to the old-old non-frailty group, the old-old frailty group had a higher risk of MACCE outside the hospital (HR = 2.389, 95% CI 1.072–5.322, P = 0.033). However, there was no significant difference in the risk of out-of-hospital MACCE between the old-old frailty and old-old non-frailty groups (Supplementary Table 5).

Further grouping of follow-up patients by comorbidities and frailty showed that, compared to the non-comorbid non-frailty group, both the comorbid frailty group (HR = 4.111, 95% CI 1.619–10.441, P = 0.003) and the non-comorbid frailty group (HR = 4.315, 95% CI 1.070-17.399, P = 0.040) had an increased risk of readmission events outside the hospital. Additionally, compared to the non-frailty comorbid group, the frailty comorbid group showed a higher risk of out-of-hospital readmission (HR = 1.884, 95% CI 1.113–3.190, P = 0.018). There was no significant difference in readmission events between the non-comorbid non-frailty group and the comorbid non-frailty group, nor between the non-comorbid frailty group and the comorbid frailty group (Supplementary Table 6).

Conclusions

This study identified a high prevalence of frailty among older adults with NSTE-ACS and found that frailty is an independent risk factor for both out-of-hospital MACCE and rehospitalization during 12 months follow-up after discharge. Further subgroup analysis, stratified by age, frailty, and comorbidities, showed that frailty maybe have a more important impact on the occurrence of adverse events in these patients after discharge.

Discussions

Cardiovascular diseases are particularly prevalent among older adults, who often have conditions such as polypharmacy, comorbidities, sarcopenia, and frailty [21]. These factors complicate the management of cardiovascular diseases in older adults, contributing to higher mortality, increased risk of recurrent myocardial infarction, and a greater likelihood of rehospitalization. Therefore, cardiovascular diseases in the elderly are increasingly recognized as a critical health priority.

This study found that the prevalence of frailty in older adults with NSTE-ACS is 11.4%, while the prevalence of pre-frailty is 51.9%. International studies support these findings. For example, a retrospective cohort study using the Fried scale for frailty assessment included 4,996 patients aged ≥ 65 years from the TRILOGY ACS database and reported a frailty prevalence of 4.7% and a pre-frailty prevalence of 23% in older adults with NSTE-ACS [5]. Another multicenter prospective cohort study conducted across 44 medical centers in Spain, including 535 NSTE-ACS patients aged 80 years or older, used the FRAIL scale and found a frailty prevalence of 27% and a pre-frailty prevalence of 38.8% [6]. A multicenter prospective cohort study from the United Kingdom, involving 217 older adults with NSTE-ACS aged ≥ 65 years (mean age 80.9 ± 4.0 years), also used the Fried scale and reported a frailty prevalence of 23.9% and a pre-frailty prevalence of 55.8% [7]. The prevalence of frailty and pre-frailty varies between studies. The TRILOGY ACS study enrolled a global cohort, whereas the other two investigations recruited European populations. In contrast, our study population was exclusively from Beijing. Variations in genetic ancestry, socioeconomic status, and healthcare resources may account for disparities in frailty and pre-frailty prevalence. Furthermore, significant heterogeneity in age ranges across studies and diverse frailty assessment scales could contribute to observed differences in frailty rates. Currently, research on frailty prevalence among older adults with NSTE-ACS remains limited in China. This study provides clinically relevant documentation of frailty rates in Beijing’s elderly NSTE-ACS population. Future studies should delineate frailty prevalence across distinct age strata and compare outcomes using validated assessment scales, thereby enabling comprehensive investigation into multidimensional determinants of frailty in elderly NSTE-ACS patients.

This study found that older adults with NSTE-ACS who are frail tend to be older, have a higher proportion of females, and are more likely to have comorbidities such as diabetes and cerebrovascular diseases. They also exhibit poorer functional capacity and are at greater risk of malnutrition. Similar findings have been reported in international studies, indicating that frail older adults with NSTE-ACS are generally older and predominantly female. Research has shown that among older adults with acute coronary syndrome, the prevalence of frailty in females is nearly twice that of males. Female gender is recognized as an independent risk factor for frailty in older adults with acute coronary syndrome [22]. The drastic decline in estrogen levels and significant reduction in growth hormone secretion in postmenopausal women contribute to markedly greater rates of osteoporosis and muscle wasting compared to men, which may account for the higher prevalence of frailty in elderly women [23, 24]. Several studies, both domestic and international, have demonstrated that frailty is associated with various chronic diseases [25–27], including diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and cerebrovascular diseases. Relevant research suggests that older adults with cardiovascular diseases typically have at least one geriatric syndrome, with the incidence of these syndromes ranging from 10% to 60% [28]. Many of these syndromes, such as frailty, polypharmacy, and comorbidities, interact and exacerbate each other, leading to increased risks of falls, caregiving dependency, and mortality. Chronic diseases share common mechanisms with frailty, comorbidities, malnutrition, and sarcopenia, including chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and mitochondrial dysfunction [29]. Chronic diseases and geriatric syndromes interact synergistically, reciprocally accelerating progression and contributing to adverse outcomes.

This study also identified frailty as an independent risk factor for MACCE (HR = 2.193, 95% CI 1.111–4.326, P = 0.024) and rehospitalization (HR = 1.906, 95% CI 1.031–3.522, P = 0.040) in older adults with NSTE-ACS within the first year after discharge. A prospective cohort study of 2,192 patients with NSTE-ACS [30] assessed frailty using the GFI and found that patients with frailty had a higher risk of developing MACE within one year (HR 1.57, 95%CI 1.01–2.43, P = 0.04). A multicenter prospective cohort study conducted in Vietnam involving 324 patients with ACS aged ≥ 60 years [10] (mean age 73.5 ± 8.3 years) found that frailty was associated with increased in-hospital mortality (OR 3.02, 95%CI 1.35–6.75), 30-day mortality (OR 3.28, 95%CI 1.59–6.76), and 30-day rehospitalization (OR 2.53, 95%CI 1.38–4.63). Additionally, a meta-analysis of nine cohort studies [25], including 2,475 older adults with ACS, revealed that after adjusting for confounding factors including age, sex, and chronic diseases, frailty was associated with increased in-hospital mortality (HR 5.49, 95%CI 2.19–13.77), short-term all-cause mortality (HR 3.56, 95%CI 1.96–6.48), and long-term all-cause mortality (HR 2.44, 95%CI 1.92–3.12). Multiple studies have consistently demonstrated that frailty correlates with both short- and long-term adverse outcomes in elderly NSTE-ACS patients. However, significant heterogeneity exists across studies regarding the association between frailty and adverse outcomes in elderly NSTE-ACS patients, pertaining to both the risk for adverse events and the specific time points at which these events occur. Variations in regional healthcare resources, patient adherence levels, and baseline disease severity appear to account for the observed prognostic heterogeneity. Frailty’s association with adverse outcomes in older adults with NSTE-ACS is mediated through interconnected biological mechanisms, clinical complexities, and sociodemographic determinants. Firstly, chronic inflammation accelerates the instability of atherosclerotic plaques [31]. At the same time, mitochondrial dysfunction reduces the repair capacity of cardiomyocytes [32]. This leads to more severe myocardial injury after reperfusion, increasing the risk of adverse outcomes. Secondly, frailty reduces the body’s physiological reserves, which limits the intensity of blood vessel reconstruction treatment [13, 33]. Also, the existence of multimorbidity at the same time can cause drug interactions. This reduces the rate at which antiplatelet drug treatment reaches its desired effect [34]. Lastly, low socioeconomic status and a lack of understanding of diseases can reduce patients’ adherence to secondary prevention medications. Poor transportation can prevent patients from following up on time and getting their treatment plans adjusted. All of these factors can increase the risk of adverse outcomes. However, frailty is a gradual process and can be reversed. For older adults with NSTE-ACS, we should regularly assess their frailty to identify frail patients early. Then, we should improve the chronic inflammation status of these patients. We should also carry out comprehensive management of multimorbidity. Moderate exercise can improve physiological reserves [32, 35]. And increasing social care is also important. By managing these patients in a scientific way from the aspects of “physiology-clinical-community”, we can reduce the risk of adverse outcomes.

Currently, there is no consensus in national or international guidelines on the scientific management of frailty in elderly patients with NSTE-ACS. This study aims to explore the correlation between frailty and NSTE-ACS in older adults to provide a foundation for clinical management.

Limitations

This was a single-center study with a moderate sample size, and all participants were hospitalized patients. Additionally, the follow-up period was of moderate length. The confounding factors in this study were selected based on key factors reported in previous literature and other factors that might be relevant in clinical practice. However, there might be other potential confounding factors, which is one of the limitations of this study. In addition, the subgroup analysis involved groups with significantly different sample sizes, which may compromise the reliability of the results. Also, although this study found that frailty was linked to adverse prognosis in elderly NSTE - ACS patients, it did not explore the underlying mechanisms or possible interventions. Further in - depth studies will be conducted in the future.
