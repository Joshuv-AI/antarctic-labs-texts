Glucagon‐Like Peptide‐1 Receptor Agonists and Risk of Mental Health Disorders in Type 2 Diabetes: Active Comparator, New User Cohort Study
Authors: Chungsoo Kim, Seonji Kim, Dong Yun Lee, Eugene Han, Yong‐ho Lee, Seng Chan You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsWe evaluated the risk of mental health disorders among glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonist (GLP1RA) users compared with users of dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 (DPP‐4) inhibitors and sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitors.Materials and MethodsWe conducted a nationwide, active‐comparator new‐user cohort study using the Korean claims database from 2012 to 2022. Patients aged ≥ 18 years who initiated GLP1RAs, SGLT2 inhibitors, or DPP4 inhibitors for type 2 diabetes were included. We compared the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for depression, anxiety, sleep disorders, and suicide using the Cox proportional hazards models after propensity score matching.ResultsThe 18 825 GLP1RA users were matched with 623 750 SGLT2 inhibitor users, and 5762 GLP1RA users with 874 902 inhibitor users. Compared with DPP4 inhibitors, GLP1RAs were not associated with increased risks of depression (HR 1.00 [95% CI, 0.83–1.20]), anxiety (0.92 [0.77–1.08]), or sleep disorder (1.14 [0.97–1.33]). In contrast, compared with SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP1RA use was associated with higher risks of depression (1.20 [1.09–1.31]) and anxiety (1.09 [1.00–1.19]). Suicidality events were rare and not observed among GLP1RA users.ConclusionsInitiation of GLP1RAs was not associated with increased risk of mental health disorders compared with DPP4 inhibitors. Differences in the risk of depression and anxiety were observed compared with SGLT2 inhibitors. These findings suggest that psychiatric safety signals may vary by active comparator and provide real‐world evidence on the psychiatric safety of GLP‐1–based therapies. Further studies incorporating detailed clinical data and high‐risk subgroups are needed to clarify underlying mechanisms.

Introduction

Diabetes and its related complications represent one of the leading contributors to illness and death and place a significant financial burden on the health care system [1]. Globally, it is estimated that 529 million individuals, corresponding to approximately 6.1% of the age‐standardized population, are affected by diabetes, and its prevalence has nearly doubled over the past two decades [2]. Diabetes is known to be a chronic metabolic condition associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular complications, kidney disease, and premature mortality [1]. Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP1RAs) have been increasingly recommended for patients with type 2 diabetes, particularly those with cardiovascular or chronic kidney disease, due to their demonstrated benefits in glycemic control, weight reduction, and mitigation of diabetes‐related complications [3, 4, 5, 6].

However, recent safety concerns have emerged regarding the potential psychiatric effects of GLP1RAs [4]. In July 2023, the Icelandic Medicines Agency reported signals suggesting an association between GLP1RA therapy and suicidal ideation, prompting regulatory investigations by the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) [4, 5]. Nevertheless, the psychiatric safety profile of GLP1RAs remains controversial due to inconsistent findings across previous studies [7]. While some pharmacovigilance studies found suicidality signals with GLP1RAs, especially liraglutide [5, 8], large‐scale observational studies and analyses of global pharmacovigilance databases have generally failed to support a causal relationship [4, 9, 10, 11]. Moreover, a recent meta‐analysis found no association between GLP1RA use and psychiatric outcomes; however, most included trials relied on placebo comparators rather than active treatments, limiting the interpretability of these findings in routine clinical practice [12]. These inconsistencies and methodological limitations highlight the need for additional real‐world evidence to clarify the psychiatric safety profile of GLP1RAs.

Several biological mechanisms have been proposed to explain the potential link between GLP1RAs and neuropsychiatric outcomes, including dysregulation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis, rapid weight reduction leading to psychological stress, and alterations in neurotrophic signalling [4, 13]. Conversely, experimental evidence suggests that GLP‐1 signalling may exert neuroprotective and mood‐stabilizing effects, adding the complexity of interpreting clinical observations [7, 13].

To contextualize psychiatric safety signals in this setting, active comparators should be selected based not only on shared clinical indications but also on mechanistic proximity. Dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 (DPP‐4) inhibitors enhance endogenous incretin activity and therefore represent a mechanistically proximate comparator to GLP‐1RAs, whereas sodium‐glucose cotransporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitors lower glucose through renal pathways and may differ in their downstream metabolic and neurobiological effects [14, 15]. Accordingly, comparing GLP‐1RAs with both a mechanistically similar class and a distinct class enables discrimination between class‐specific safety signals and comparative effects driven by the choice of comparator. Given the expanding use of GLP1RAs for both diabetes and obesity management, and the high burden of psychiatric comorbidity in these populations, clarifying these safety concerns is of critical importance. Therefore, we conducted an active‐comparator, new‐user cohort study to evaluate the risk of incident mental health disorders among GLP1RA users compared with users of DPP4 inhibitors and SGLT2 inhibitors in routine clinical practice.

Methods

Study Design and Database

This is a retrospective observational cohort study, which used a nationwide administrative claims database from Health Insurance Review and Assessment services (HIRA) in Republic of Korea from 2012 to 2022 [16]. The HIRA database provides comprehensive health information for the entire Korean population, encompassing anonymized personal identifiers, demographic characteristics, diagnoses, as well as details on medical procedures and medications included in the national reimbursement lists. Furthermore, the mortality data from the national death registry of Statistics Korea was linked to the HIRA database. The HIRA database was standardized to Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership common data model (OMOP–CDM) version 5.3. Analyses of de‐identified data were performed in accordance with local laws and regulation and with approval from respective scientific and ethics committees (Severance Hospital IRB number: 4‐2023‐0856).

Study Population and Exposure

Adults (≥ 18 years) who were prescribed their first antihyperglycemic agent for type 2 diabetes, SGLT2 inhibitors, or DPP4 inhibitors were identified. The GLP1RA group included dulaglutide, exenatide, lixisenatide, and liraglutide. Semaglutide and tirzepatide were not included because they are not reimbursed under the national insurance system in the Republic of Korea. The SGLT2 inhibitor group comprised dapagliflozin, empagliflozin, ertugliflozin, and ipragliflozin. The DPP4 inhibitor group included alogliptin, anagliptin, evogliptin, gemigliptin, linagliptin, saxagliptin, sitagliptin, teneligliptin, and vildagliptin.

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) enrollment in the database for at least 1 year prior to the index date, (2) a documented diagnosis of type 2 diabetes before the index date, and (3) initiation of therapy with a GLP1RA, SGLT2 inhibitors, or DPP4 inhibitors based on prescription records. The index date was defined as the date of the first prescription for the drug of interest. Patients were excluded if they had received insulin therapy (including insulin‐combination products) before the index date or if they had a diagnosis of type 1 diabetes. Patients with a history of heart failure were excluded due to potential confounding by indication. As of 2022, SGLT2 inhibitors were approved in Korea for heart failure, but GLP1RAs (included in our study) were not approved. Pairwise comparative analyses were performed between GLP1RAs and SGLT2 inhibitors, as well as between GLP1RAs and DPP4 inhibitors. Within each group, patients with prior exposure to other study drugs and a previous history of study outcomes were additionally excluded (e.g., prior DPP4 inhibitor use in the GLP1RA group). The detailed code lists are provided in Table S1.

Outcome and Follow‐Up

We assessed the study outcomes in the follow‐up period, which were defined based on the structured codes to identify them (Table S2). The date of occurrence was determined as the first occurrence of any code corresponding to the definition. The study outcomes included depression, anxiety, and sleep disorder. The definition of depression included major depressive episodes; however, it excluded bipolar disorder. Sleep disorders included sleep apnea, insomnia, and hypersomnia. All study outcomes were restricted to new‐onset events in the database.

Patients were followed up to the last date of assigned treatment, the data of last observation in the database, the date of occurrence of the endpoint, and the date of censoring. Each treatment was considered continued if the patient received their new prescription within 30 days of the last date of the previous prescription. Treatment discontinuation was defined as the last prescription at which no more prescription was given within 30 days, and the discontinued date was defined the date of last administration. Censoring events were defined as events wherein patients were exposed to another therapy (e.g., patients in the GLP1RA group were considered censored when exposed to a prescription of DPP4 inhibitors).

Statistical Analysis

All variables were denoted as frequency and percentage. Propensity score (PS) was calculated for adjusting the effects of bias from confounders between the two study groups. When the empirical equipoise between two groups was greater than 25%, the two groups were defined as comparable through PS adjustment (reference). We used L1 regularized logistic regression to estimate the PS including all the available characteristics in the database. All variables were dichotomized, and all missing variables were considered not present. Study groups were matched in 1: n pair based on the PS, and an absolute standardized mean difference (aSMD) was assessed to describe the balance of covariate distribution. For all outcomes, the incidence rates (IRs) were estimated. The Cox proportional hazard model was used to develop an outcome model for calculating hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Treatment group assignment was included as the only covariate in the Cox model. The Kaplan–Meier curve was used to derive cumulative incidence.

We conducted sensitivity analysis in the following analytic settings: PS adjustment method, follow‐up strategy. We varied PS adjustment methods from 1: n PS matching to 1:1 matching or stratification with 5 strata. We also varied our follow‐up strategy to intention‐to‐treat (ITT) for estimating the effect of being assigned to a given treatment regardless of non‐adherence. All analyses were performed using R, version 4.4.2 and its open‐source statistical packages, including HADES from the Observational Health Data Sciences and Informatics.

Results

Cohort Characteristics

Tables 1 and 2 present the baseline characteristics and corresponding aSMD for the two comparisons before and after the PS matching. In comparison between the GLP1RA and SGLT2 inhibitor groups, 18 825 patients in the GLP1RA group and 623 750 patients in the SGLT2 inhibitor group were matched. A total of 5331 covariates used for the PS model were balanced, with all aSMDs below 0.1 (max aSMD = 0.06), indicating there were no significant differences between groups. The median (IQR) follow‐up duration was 195 (67–795) days in the GLP1RA group and 264 (87–1050) days in the SGLT2 inhibitor group. The most frequent age category was 50–54 years (15.3% vs. 15.1% in the GLP1RA and SGLT2 groups, respectively), and females accounted for 46.1% of both groups. Among comorbidities, hyperlipidemia was the most prevalent (87.9% vs. 87.8%), whereas acid‐related drug use was the most common concomitant medication in both groups (78.8% vs. 78.5%).

Sensitivity Analyses

In the sensitivity analysis comparing GLP1RAs and SGLT2 inhibitors, the results varied according to the PS adjustment methods (Figure 2, upper panel). In the 1:1 matching analysis, no significant difference in depression risk was observed between the two groups. However, in the PS stratification analysis, the risk of depression was significantly higher in the GLP1RA group (HR 1.18 [95% CI, 1.07–1.30]). Similarly, in the ITT analysis with an alternative follow‐up strategy, GLP1RA was associated with a significantly elevated risk of depression (HR 1.16 [95% CI, 1.10–1.22]). For anxiety disorder, no significant differences were identified when alternative PS adjustment methods were applied. Nevertheless, in the ITT analysis, the incidence of anxiety disorders was significantly higher in the GLP1RA group (HR 1.09 [95% CI, 1.03–1.14]). Regarding sleep disorders, the ITT analysis likewise revealed a significantly increased risk in the GLP1RA group (HR 1.10 [95% CI, 1.05–1.16]).

Sensitivity analysis results according to matching methods and follow‐up strategy.

Across the comparison of GLP1RA and DPP4 inhibitor (Figure 2, lower panel), the risks of depression and anxiety disorders did not differ significantly under any analytic condition. By contrast, the incidence of sleep disorders was consistently higher in the GLP1RA group, showing significance both in the 1:1 matching (HR 1.30 [95% CI, 1.04–1.63]) and in the ITT analysis (HR 1.19 [95% CI, 1.08–1.30]).

Discussion

In this nationwide cohort study using an active‐comparator, new‐user design, we evaluated risk of incident mental health outcomes across major glucose‐lowering drug classes. The initiation of GLP1RAs showed no evidence of increased risks of depression (HR 1.00 [95% CI, 0.83–1.20]), anxiety (HR 0.92 [95% CI, 0.77–1.08]), or sleep disorder (HR 1.14 [95% CI, 0.97–1.33]) when compared with DPP4 inhibitors. In contrast, when GLP1RAs were compared with SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP1RA use was associated with a higher risk of depression (HR 1.20 [95% CI, 1.09–1.31]) and anxiety (HR 1.09 [95% CI, 1.00–1.19]).

In our study, initiation of GLP1RAs was not associated with a substantially higher risk of depression, anxiety, or sleep disorder compared with DPP4 inhibitors. This absence of increased risk may, in part, reflect shared biological features of the two drug classes, as both modulate the incretin pathway through different pharmacologic mechanisms, including direct GLP‐1 receptor agonism and inhibition of endogenous incretin degradation [14, 15]. Because both therapies act within the same therapeutic pathway, their net effects on downstream neuropsychiatric outcomes may be similar in routine clinical practice. Consistent with this mechanistic overlap, prior studies evaluating the psychiatric effects of GLP1RAs have shown no uniform direction of association [7]. Early observational and pharmacovigilance analyses reported heterogeneous findings, ranging from potential benefits to null or adverse signals, whereas more recent large‐scale observational studies with rigorous confounding control have generally suggested no meaningful psychotropic effect of GLP1RAs [17, 18, 19, 20]. An EHR‐based retrospective study comparing semaglutide with sitagliptin found non‐significant differences for bipolar disorder (HR 0.79 [95% CI, 0.59–1.06]) and anxiety disorder (HR 0.92 [95% CI, 0.84–1.01]), along with a significantly lower risk for depression (HR 0.84 [95% CI, 0.75–0.93]) [21]. Similarly, a large target trial emulation study demonstrated a lower risk of depression with GLP1RAs compared with DPP4 inhibitors (HR 0.90 [95% CI, 0.82–0.98]) [22]. Furthermore, randomized controlled trial evidence synthesized in a recent meta‐analysis demonstrated no significant differences in overall psychiatric adverse events (log[RR] −0.03 [95% CI, −0.21 to 0.16]), or serious psychiatric adverse events (log[RR] −0.02 [95% CI, −0.20 to 0.17]) between GLP1RA treatment and placebo [12]. Our analysis builds on this literature by using a mechanistically proximate comparator class and confirming long‐term outcomes.

Conversely, compared with SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP1RAs were associated with a higher risk of depression and anxiety in our analysis; however, this relative excess risk does not preclude the possibility that both drug classes may exert beneficial effects on mood. Prior studies indicate that SGLT2 inhibition reduces neuroinflammation, oxidative stress, and apoptotic signalling and modulates stress‐related neural circuits, including the lateral habenula and central serotonergic pathways, leading to antidepressant‐like behavioural phenotypes [11, 23, 24, 25]. Several clinical studies have reported improvements in depressive and anxiety symptoms, sleep quality, and health‐related quality of life after initiation of SGLT2 inhibitors, and a randomized trial in individuals with major depressive disorder suggested that adjunctive empagliflozin on selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor therapy may further reduce depressive symptom scores compared with selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor alone [26, 27, 28]. Moreover, both GLP1RAs and SGLT2 inhibitors improve glycemic status, promote weight loss, and provide cardiovascular and renal advantages, factors that may contribute to better overall health and, indirectly, to fewer depressive symptoms [29]. These overlapping metabolic and systemic benefits raise the possibility that the two drug classes share downstream pathways relevant to mood, even if their comparative risks differ in specific clinical contexts.

This finding contrasts with two prior observational studies that did not report a statistically significant increase in psychiatric risk for GLP‐1RAs relative to SGLT2 inhibitors; however, several methodological differences may explain these discrepancies [21, 22]. One cohort study focused on short‐term outcomes within 1 year and compared single agents within each class (semaglutide vs. empagliflozin), limiting generalizability [21]. In a target trial emulation study, although no significant association was observed, the intention‐to‐treat analytic approach may have yielded more conservative estimates, and point estimates were consistently above unity (HR 1.07 [95% CI, 0.98–1.18]; 0.5‐year risk ratio 1.14 [95% CI, 0.98–1.33]; 2‐year risk ratio 1.07 [95% CI, 0.98–1.16]), suggesting limited statistical power [22]. In light of the complex evidence, it remains unclear whether GLP1RAs have a protective role in depression in diverse situations, reinforcing the need for further pharmacovigilance as well as well‐designed longitudinal studies in various at‐risk subgroups and active comparators to elucidate their potential psychiatric pathways [30].

A notable advantage of our investigation lies in the use of nationwide, longitudinal health records, which enabled inclusion of an entire population. This approach reduces the likelihood of selection‐related distortions and enhances the relevance of our findings to routine clinical practice. Nevertheless, several limitations inherent to our study should be considered. First, our study adopted broad inclusion criteria that encompassed patients regardless of their glycemic status. Although this approach increases the relevance of our findings to routine clinical care, it may also introduce certain biases compared with the more narrowly defined populations typically enrolled in randomized trials. To mitigate potential confounding and strengthen the reliability of our comparative analyses, we employed an active‐comparator design using SGLT2 inhibitors and DPP4 inhibitors. Second, although extensive propensity score matching was applied to minimize confounding, the presence of unmeasured factors cannot be fully ruled out. Important clinical indicators including haemoglobin A1c, body mass index, and diabetes severity were not available in the claims data, and these omissions may influence both treatment allocation and vulnerability to depressive outcomes [31, 32]. As an indirect approach incorporating thousands of covariates to reducing this limitation, we restricted our cohort to individuals not using insulin before the index date, thereby reducing variability related to advanced disease severity and applied large‐scale PS matching to achieve balance between treatment groups across demographic factors, clinical profiles, and baseline comorbidities and medication use [33]. Nevertheless, channelling bias may persist. To mitigate this, obesity was included as a covariate, and temporal prescribing patterns and treatment selection were further considered by incorporating the index year and evaluating preference scores. However, these approaches may not fully eliminate residual bias, and the results should be interpreted with caution [34, 35]. A previous study identified that implementing a new‐user, active comparator design with PS matching to proxy indicators of diabetes severity using claims data yielded an improved balance in unmeasured confounders [36]. Third, our analysis could not encompass the full spectrum of currently available GLP‐1RAs. In Korea, semaglutide and tirzepatide have not been reimbursed and therefore cannot be identified within claims data, and liraglutide was generally reimbursed only as a fixed‐dose combination. As a result, the magnitude of class effects in our study may differ from estimates reported in prior studies. Further studies including these newer medications are needed to improve the generalizability of findings to current clinical practice. Patients with a history of heart failure were excluded due to potential confounding by indication. As of 2022, SGLT2 inhibitors were approved in Korea for heart failure, but GLP1RAs (included in our study) were not approved.

Oses may lead to underestimation of the true burden of these conditions and differential detection bias. While prescription records can be additionally utilized in the outcome definition, due to the characteristics of the Korean health insurance system, most prescriptions are tied to diagnostic codes to justify reimbursement; consequently, there may not be much additional information obtained, and off‐label cases can lower the specificity of the outcome definition. Future research applying the added definitions using lab records, psychological assessment results, and so forth, may be necessary. Fifth, in sensitivity analyses using various statistical approaches, the direction of the estimates was generally consistent; however, the reduced number of patients or events in certain analyses resulted in wider confidence intervals. Lastly, the follow‐up duration was short. Although this is a common characteristic in previous observational studies regarding the evaluation of antihyperglycemic medications, our study is also limited to the evaluation of acute and early cases from initiators, considering that psychiatric disorders can manifest in a chronic and multifactorial manner. Evidence for studies with longer follow‐ups is further needed.

In conclusion, initiation of GLP1RAs was not associated with an increased risk of depression, anxiety, or sleep disorders compared with DPP4 inhibitors. These findings provide real‐world evidence on the psychiatric safety of GLP‐1–based therapies. Whereas higher risks of depression and anxiety were observed relative to SGLT2 inhibitors. Differences in the risk of depression and anxiety were observed compared with SGLT2 inhibitors, highlighting that comparative estimates may depend on the choice of active comparator. Further studies incorporating richer clinical measures and diverse at‐risk subgroups are warranted to clarify the underlying mechanisms and inform clinical decision‐making.
