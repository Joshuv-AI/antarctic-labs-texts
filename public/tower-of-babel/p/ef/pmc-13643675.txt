CRB1 ‐Associated Inherited Retinal Dystrophies: Prospective Natural History Study With 4 Years of Follow‐Up
Authors: Jessica S. Karuntu, Xuan‐Thanh‐An Nguyen, Mays Talib, Mary J. van Schooneveld, Jan Wijnholds, Maria M. van Genderen, Jos Twisk, Nicoline E. Schalij‐Delfos, Caroline C. W. Klaver, Ralph J. Florijn, Jacoline B. ten Brink, Magda A. Smoor, L. Ingeborgh van den Born, Carel B. Hoyng, Alberta A. H. J. Thiadens, Camiel J. F. Boon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundThe lack of validated and sensitive clinical endpoints remains a major challenge in the design of gene therapy trials for inherited retinal dystrophies (IRDs). This prospective longitudinal cohort study describes the natural disease progression of IRDs caused by pathogenic mutations in the Crumbs homologue 1 (CRB1) gene, and identifies sensitive clinical endpoints.MethodsIn this single‐centre study, patients with genetically confirmed CRB1‐associated IRD were assessed at baseline, 2‐year and 4‐year follow‐up. The phenotyping protocol included best‐corrected visual acuity (BCVA), Goldmann perimetry, mesopic microperimetry, full‐field stimulus testing (FST), ophthalmoscopy and assessment of retinal structural integrity on spectral‐domain optical coherence tomography and fundus autofluorescence.ResultsTwenty of the 22 enrolled patients completed the 4‐year follow‐up, including patients with retinitis pigmentosa (n = 17), cone‐rod dystrophy (n = 2) or macular dystrophy (n = 1). Significant decreases were observed for BCVA (−6.2 ETDRS letters, 95% confidence interval [CI] −10.1 to −2.3; p = 0.003), V4e isopter seeing retinal area (−43.9 mm2, 95% CI, −72.3 to −15.5; p = 0.003) and mean sensitivity on microperimetry (−2.5 dB, 95% CI, −3.3 to −1.6; p < 0.001). FST thresholds for white, blue and red stimuli did not significantly change over 4 years (p = 0.24; p = 0.78; p = 0.60, respectively). The continuity of the ellipsoid zone (EZ) and the external limiting membrane (ELM) on SD‐OCT were increasingly disrupted in six patients over the course of this study.ConclusionExcept for FST responses, a significant decrease was observed over 4 years in the visual function parameters of BCVA, Goldmann perimetry and microperimetry and these may thus be used as sensitive clinical endpoints.

Introduction

Pathogenic variants in the Crumbs homologue 1 (CRB1) gene are associated with a broad spectrum of inherited retinal dystrophies (IRDs), including Leber congenital amaurosis (LCA), retinitis pigmentosa (RP) and isolated macular dystrophies [1]. LCA is considered one of the most severe retinal dystrophies with an onset at birth or within the 1st year of life and presents with severe bilateral visual impairment. Pathogenic CRB1 variants account for up to 17% of LCA cases [2, 3]. In addition, pathogenic variants in CRB1 are the underlying cause in up to 9% of non‐syndromic RP cases, and patients typically present with an early onset of symptoms during childhood or adolescence, including nyctalopia and visual field restriction [4]. Half of the patients with CRB1‐associated RP are expected to reach low vision around the age of 18 years, and blindness around the age of 44 years, with a mean rate of decline of 0.03–0.07 logMAR in visual acuity per year [5, 6]. Like in other forms of RP, there is a high phenotypic variability in CRB1‐associated IRDs with regard to age at onset and natural history between and within families [7]. Typical features of CRB1‐associated IRDs include hyperopia, early‐onset maculopathy, optic nerve drusen, preservation of para‐arteriolar retinal pigment epithelium and Coats‐like exudative vasculopathy [7, 8]. Interestingly, most patients with a CRB1‐associated IRD display a certain degree of progressive disturbance of retinal layering and inner retinal thickening [5, 6, 9]. These findings suggest a potential role of the CRB1 protein in retinal development and maturation, although this protein has also been associated with cellular processes such as cell polarity, adhesion, division and morphogenesis [10, 11].

Adeno‐associated virus‐mediated gene augmentation strategies for CRB1 have shown promising results in vitro and in rodent models, as well as in human‐induced pluripotent stem cell‐derived retinal organoids [12, 13, 14]. As these preclinical experiments will be translated to the clinic, researchers need to compose clinically relevant and sensitive endpoints for use in clinical trials. Thus far, our knowledge was limited to cross‐sectional case series and retrospective studies [4, 5, 6, 15]. Large retrospective cohort studies previously performed by our and other research groups, already suggested an optimal treatment window for CRB1‐associated LCA before the first decade of life, and for CRB1‐associated RP before the third or fourth decade of life [6, 9]. Earlier, we reported mesopic macular sensitivity on microperimetry (also known as fundus‐controlled perimetry) as the only parameter that was significantly decreased at the 2‐year follow‐up of the prospective natural history study of CRB1‐associated IRD [9]. Although a trend of decreasing best‐corrected visual acuity (BCVA) and visual field was observed, both parameters were deemed less suitable as outcome measures over a follow‐up period of 2 years. Here, we present the results of the 4‐year prospective natural history study in patients with a CRB1‐associated IRD.

Methods

Patients

The study protocol of this natural history study in patients with CRB1‐associated retinal dystrophies, baseline characteristics and genotype–phenotype analysis have been reported earlier and will only be briefly described here [7, 9]. Patients were recruited for this nationwide collaborative study from two Dutch registries: the Delleman Archive for hereditary eye disease at the Amsterdam University Medical Center, and from the RD5000 database of the Dutch national consortium for patients with IRDs [16]. Inclusion criteria for the natural history study were (1) biallelic pathogenic variants in the CRB1 gene (pathogenicity classes 4 or 5 [17]), (2) a Snellen BCVA in decimal of ≥ 0.05 in the better‐seeing eye at the last available examination and (3) the last available examination should not have taken place ≥ 20 years prior to study enrolment. Following these criteria, 22 patients were initially included and phenotyped at baseline, and during the 2 and 4 year follow‐up at Leiden University Medical Center (Table S1). The study adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and ethics approval for the phenotyping protocol was obtained from the Medical Ethics Committee of Erasmus Medical Center and Leiden University Medical Center Review Board (MECC‐2010‐359 P11.100). Patients and/or their parents have provided and signed informed consent forms before each follow‐up.

Ophthalmological Assessment

Extensive descriptions of the phenotyping protocols have been reported in earlier publications of this natural history study [7, 9]. BCVA and refraction were determined using the Early Treatment Diabetic Retinopathy (ETDRS) letter charts and later converted to logMAR values for statistical analyses. Presence and progression of cataract was evaluated as a binary variable. Microperimetry was performed to assess macular sensitivity using the macular integrity assessment (MAIA; ICare Finland Oy, Vantaa, Finland) and the 37‐stimuli grid in mesopic conditions using the built‐in follow‐up software. This grid covered 20° of the central macula with stimuli located at 0°,1°, 5° and 10° from fixation, and used a 4–2 staircase strategy. In addition to the macular sensitivity, fixation stability was quantified using the 95% bivariate contour ellipse area (BCEA95). Patients performed a practise session before each formal examination to minimise learning effects. Two formal examinations were completed: the first measurement is used for the four‐year follow‐up analysis, the second measurement was used to investigate intrasession variability with the BCVA assessment in between.

Goldmann kinetic perimetry (III4e and V4e isopters) was performed, and the visual fields were converted to digital seeing retinal areas (in mm2) according to the method described by Dagnelie [18]. A total change of ≥ 20% compared to previous Goldmann examinations was considered significant [19]. To investigate photoreceptor function and to distinguish between cone and rod function, patients underwent a minimum of 30 min dark‐adaptation before the full‐field stimulus test (FST) with a 0 dB reference level of 0.1 cd·s/m2. Thresholds were measured for white (6500 K), red (627 nm) and blue stimuli (448 nm) in triplicate with each stimulus lasting 4 ms. Unreliable results, based on excessive false‐positives or negatives, were repeated where possible or excluded from the analysis. Photoreceptor mediation was determined based on the differences between blue and red stimuli: rod‐mediated (blue–red difference of > 22 dB), cone‐mediated (blue–red difference of < 3 dB), mixed rod‐ and cone‐mediation (blue–red difference between 3 and 22 dB) [7].

Multimodal imaging included fundus photography (Topcon TRC‐50DX, Topcon Medical Systems Inc), spectral‐domain optical coherence tomography (SD‐OCT; Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) and 488 nm wavelength fundus autofluorescence (FAF) imaging of the central 30° of the retina (Spectralis, Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany). Inner retinal lamination was evaluated as well as the integrity of the external limiting membrane (ELM) and ellipsoid zone (EZ) at the parafovea (within 2.5 mm of the foveal centre) and perifovea (outside of 2.5 mm of the foveal centre) according to our previous descriptions [7, 9].

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed using IBM SPSS 25.0.0.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, New York, USA). Data normality was visually assessed and analysed with the Shapiro–Wilk test. Normal distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and range, and nonnormal distributed data are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). Changes in parameters over time were analysed using a linear mixed‐effect model, the effects of cataract, age and being part of the genetic isolate were tentatively explored. Agreements between microperimetry measurements, and correlations between fixation stability and visual acuity were analysed with Spearman's rank correlation coefficients. Macular sensitivity on microperimetry was transformed to volume sensitivity using a hill of vision approach previously published by Josan et al. using the collaborative open‐source statistical programming software R version 4.3.1 with the packages ggplot, rgl, fields [20, 21]. Generally, significance level was set at p‐value < 0.05.

Results

Patient Characteristics

Of the original 22 patients who were assessed at baseline and the 2‐year follow‐up, 20 patients were assessed for the final 4‐year follow up. Their characteristics are summarised in Table 1, and the case‐by‐case baseline characteristics and genotypes can be found in Table S1. Two patients (P1 and P20) dropped out for the 4‐year follow‐up due to the global COVID‐19 pandemic, either due to the national restrictions or due to the long recovery of COVID‐19 in an elderly patient. Nine of the patients (45%), all homozygous for variant c.3122T>C (p.Met1041Thr) in the CRB1 gene, originated from a previously‐described Dutch genetic consanguineous isolate [22]. This variant is associated with an early‐onset RP phenotype [9]. One other patient carried this variant in a compound heterozygous manner. Other disease‐associated CRB1 variants were 11 missense variants, 1 splice site variant, 1 in‐frame deletion and 1 nonsense variant [9]. Based on earlier clinical data and electroretinography (ERG) findings, 17 patients had an RP phenotype (85%) and 3 patients presented with a central phenotype, either a cone‐rod dystrophy (2 patients, 10%) or a macular dystrophy (1 patient, 5%). Despite originating from the same genetic isolate, patient P10 presented with a late‐onset cone‐rod dystrophy whereas the others presented with an early‐onset RP phenotype. Mean follow‐up was 4.1 ± 0.1 years (range 4.0–4.7 years) from baseline and 2.1 ± 0.2 years (range 1.9–2.7 years) from the 2‐year follow‐up.

Visual Acuity and Refraction

Across the entire cohort, mean BCVA decreased significantly over 4 years with a mean of −6.2 ETDRS letters (95% confidence interval [CI] −10.1 to −2.3; p = 0.003).

At baseline, the mean BCVA of both eyes of patients with an RP phenotype was 39.1 ± 4.0 ETDRS letters (range: 18.0–75.5 letters; mean BCVA is equivalent to 0.92 logMAR or 20/166 Snellen), which did not significantly decrease with −2.6 ETDRS letters (95% CI, −7.0 to 1.8; p = 0.24) at the 2‐year follow‐up (Figure 1A). At the 4‐year follow‐up, BCVA had significantly decreased with −7.0 ETDRS letters (95% CI, −11.6 to −2.4; p = 0.004) (Figure 1A). The mean annual decline in visual acuity was −1.8 ETDRS letters in patients with an RP phenotype. Over the 4‐year follow‐up period, five patients with an RP phenotype progressed to a more severe category of visual impairment according to the World Health Organisation criteria: from mild to moderate visual impairment (n = 1, patient P6, Table S1), from moderate to severe visual impairment (n = 2, patients P12 and P15, Table S1), from moderate to blindness (n = 1, patient P17, Table S1) and from severe visual impairment to blindness (n = 1, patient P19, Table S1).

In patients with a central phenotype, either cone‐rod dystrophy or a macular dystrophy, mean BCVA ranged from 30.5 to 78.0 ETDRS letters at baseline, which decreased with a mean of −1.8 ETDRS letters over 2 years and −2.2 ETDRS letters over 4 years. This is consistent with a mean annual of −0.6 ETDRS letters in patients with a central phenotype. Due to the low sample size, no statistical analyses were performed for this subgroup.

In seven eyes of five patients (25%), a loss of ≥ 15 ETDRS letters (i.e., three ETDRS lines) was observed in patients aged between 27 and 57 years. In three of these seven eyes posterior subcapsular cataract was noted. In two of these patients, the cataract had remained stable since baseline. A loss of 23 ETDRS letters was observed in one patient over 4 years, whose cataract also notably increased over 4 years. One patient with subcapsular posterior cataract underwent uncomplicated cataract surgery between the second and last visit, but BCVA did not improve between the 2‐year follow‐up and 4‐year follow‐up. No new instances of cataract developed during this study. An exploratory mixed model analysis revealed no significant effects of the presence of cataract (p = 0.26), age (p = 0.31), or being part of the genetic isolate (p = 0.59) on the clinical course of BCVA (Table S2). Hyperopia was a consistent finding in all patients with RP or cone‐rod dystrophy, and mean spherical refraction equivalent at the baseline measurement was 2.6 ± 3.0 dioptre which did not change significantly over 4 years (p = 0.31) in phakic eyes. The patient with a macular dystrophy was myopic with a mean spherical refraction equivalent of −6.2 dioptre.

Kinetic Perimetry and Microperimetry

The median size of V4e isopter seeing retinal area on Goldmann perimetry was 135.4 mm2 (IQR, 242.7; range: 12.3–760.3 mm2) averaged over both eyes of each patient at 4‐year follow up. The mean V4e isopter seeing retinal area decreased from the baseline visit with a non‐statistically significant loss of −3.6 mm2 (95% CI, −31.0 to 23.8; p = 0.79) after 2 years. However, after 4 years a statistically significant loss was found of −43.9 mm2 (95% CI, −72.3 to −15.5; p = 0.003) (Figure 1B). In 18 eyes of 10 patients a decrease of ≥ 20% in V4e seeing retinal area was found compared to baseline measurement. Four eyes of two paediatric patients (10%) showed an improvement of ≥ 20% in V4e seeing retinal area, although these patients showed minimal improvement on ETDRS BCVA. Generally, loss of visual field sensitivity was mainly observed in the posterior pole, with a small peripheral island of vision remaining. Exploratory analysis showed a significant effect of cataract (p = 0.01) on the decline of V4e seeing retinal area. Patients with cataract at baseline showed a greater decline compared to patients without cataract, with a difference of 90 mm2 (Table S3). Age (p = 0.82) and being part of the genetic isolate (p = 0.96) did not affect the decline in V4e retinal seeing area (Table S3).

The seeing retinal area using I4e isopter was still measurable in 22 eyes of 13 patients (65%) at baseline, and significantly decreased over 4 years (−12.6 mm2, 95% CI, −22.5 to −2.7; p = 0.05) (Figure 1C). In 18 eyes of 10 patients, I4e isopter seeing retinal area was not measurable at 4‐year follow‐up, although 8 of these eyes still had some I4e seeing retinal area at baseline.

Microperimetry was successfully performed by 18 patients (85%) in 35 eyes. In three patients (15%), microperimetry measurement was not completed due to (a combination of) young age (patients P7 and P16) or severe visual impairment accompanied by unstable fixation (patient P19) (Figure 2). Fixation stability was determined based on BCEA95 with increasing values denoting higher fixation instability. Fixation stability did not significantly change over 4 years (−8.6°2, 95% CI, −33.5 to 16.18; p = 0.74). However, higher BCEA95 values, corresponding to poorer fixation stability, were correlated significantly to worse BCVA (Spearman's ρ = 0.58; p = 0.01).

Mean macular sensitivity decreased significantly from 8.0 to 5.5 dB (−2.5 dB, 95% CI, −3.3 to −1.6 dB; p < 0.001) over 4 years in all patients (Figures 1D, 2 and 3). At the 2‐year follow‐up, a significant decrease was already observed, although it was less pronounced then (−1.6 dB, 95% CI, −2.4 to −0.8 dB; p < 0.001). Similar results were found after exclusion of patients with a mean macular sensitivity of ≤ 1 dB at baseline (−2.8 dB, 95% CI, −3.8 to −1.8; p < 0.001). The analysis using volume sensitivity as a more reliable method of presenting macular sensitivity also showed a statistically significant decrease over 4 years (−181.8 dB × deg2, 95% CI 104.8–258.9; p < 0.001). Here, 11 eyes of seven patients with a mean macular sensitivity of zero still had a measurable volume sensitivity, and could thus be included in the analysis. The baseline volume sensitivity was not significantly associated with a faster or slower rate of decline over 4 years.

Age at inclusion (p = 0.92), presence of cataract (p = 0.64), and being part of the genetic isolate (p = 0.52) did not significantly affect the natural decline on microperimetry (Table S4).

In previous visits, we noticed a variability between microperimetry measurements, and accordingly we investigated intra‐visit variability of both eyes on microperimetry. We found a mean difference between the two measurements of 0.04 and −0.36 dB, and a high Spearman's ρ of 0.99 and 0.99 for the right and left eye, respectively, suggesting no significant intra‐visit measurement variability.

In 23 eyes of 14 patients (70%) a decrease of > 7 dB was found at more than five different loci after 4 years of follow‐up. However, in six other eyes of four patients mean macular sensitivity was already too low at baseline to measure a > 7 dB decrease (Figure 2B).

Full‐Field Stimulus Test (FST)

FST measurements were available for 16 patients (80%) at the 4‐year follow‐up, of which 10 patients (62.5%) had completed the same FST at baseline measurement (Figure 4). Longitudinal FST data was not available for two patients of the original cohort at 4‐year follow‐up, due to temporary technical malfunction of the FST device (patients P5 and P12).

(B) Using the blue stimulus (448 nm), the rod‐mediated response is investigated. (C) The red stimulus (627 nm) reflects cone function. Only patients with at least two measurements are included in this graph.

The mean thresholds on FST for the white, blue and red stimuli at 4‐year follow‐up were −37.9 ± 14.1 dB (range: −56.15 to −7.9 dB), −42.3 ± 14.2 dB (range: −62.4 to −10.2 dB) and −24.7 ± 9.6 dB (range: −39.3 to −9.0 dB), respectively. Compared to baseline measurements, there were no significant changes in thresholds for white (−0.3 dB, 95% CI, −2.2 to 1.7 dB; p = 0.24), blue (+0.3 dB, 95% CI, −2.8 to 3.3 dB; p = 0.78), or red (−0.8 dB, 95% CI, −2.7 to 1.1 dB; p = 0.60) stimuli averaged over both eyes (Figure 4A–C).

Based on the difference of the blue and red threshold values on FST, the photoreceptor mediation type was determined in 32 eyes (Figure 4D). Mixed model analysis showed no significant change in photoreceptor mediation over 4 years (−1.1 dB, 95% CI, −3.3 to 1.1 dB, p = 0.53). At the 4‐year follow‐up, rod mediation was found in 9 eyes (28.1%), mixed rod‐cone mediation in 20 eyes (62.5%) and cone‐mediation in 3 eyes (9.4%). Rod‐mediated responses were associated with better sensitivity to white stimuli (i.e., lower FST thresholds), whereas patients with mixed and cone mediation had a lower sensitivity to white stimuli (i.e., higher FST thresholds) (Table S5). Longitudinally, over 4 years, one patient (P9) transitioned from rod mediation to mixed mediation. Two patients (P17 and P18) transitioned from mixed mediation to rod mediation, although the blue–red differences of both patients at baseline were already borderline rod‐associated: 21.87 and 21.96 dB, respectively. Two patients (P10 and P11) had no baseline measurement, but mediation deteriorated from rod mediation to mixed mediation over the final 2 years. Patient P4 with early‐onset RP, severe visual impairment and severe visual field constriction, was the only patient who maintained cone mediation from baseline until the end of the study.

Multimodal Retinal Imaging

SD‐OCT data were obtained from 19 patients of 20 patients (95%), with one patient (P15) being excluded from retinal imaging due to the development of acute angle‐closure glaucoma.

According to previous descriptions, retinal integrity was evaluated and graded in the current study as (1) normal organisation without coarse lamination, (2) normal organisation with coarse lamination and (3) relative disorganisation with coarse lamination [7, 9]. At baseline, five patients presented with Grade 1, eight patients with Grade 2 and eight patients with Grade 3. Over the course of 4 years, two patients retained grade 1 (10.5%), whilst five patients (26.3%) revealed slow progressive changes from coarse lamination towards mild disorganisation of the retinal layers (Figure 2B). The majority of patients (63.2%) revealed no change in retinal integrity (Table 2).

Discussion

This prospective study investigates the natural history of functional and structural parameters in patients with CRB1‐associated retinal dystrophies over 4 years without intervention, thereby providing better prognostic information and a foundation for choosing relevant clinical endpoints for upcoming treatment trials. Macular sensitivity on mesopic microperimetry remained a robust clinical endpoint to measure functional decline. While our previous 2‐year analysis did not capture statistically significant disease progression, the current 4‐year data demonstrate a significant decline in BCVA and visual field. Furthermore, although FST has proven valuable in showing visual improvement following an intervention, the trend to decline at the 2‐year analysis was not supported by the data of the current 4‐year analysis. Thus, FST does not appear to qualify as a reliable measure for tracking disease progression over time.

In our previous report on the 2‐year follow‐up in this CRB1 patient cohort, we did not find a statistically significant decrease in BCVA. As a measure of predominantly cone function, BCVA may remain relatively preserved in earlier stages of rod‐cone dystrophies, requiring longer follow‐up to detect changes. Importantly, in the current study, we found a significant decrease in BCVA, which remained robust even when excluding patients with a cone‐rod or macular dystrophy. Based on the current BCVA data, the optimal treatment window is likely before the third decade of life, treatment should ideally be considered as early as safely possible to rescue as much visual function as possible. The mean annual decline in BCVA appears lower compared to other studies on CRB1‐associated retinopathy [4, 6]. Patients with an RP phenotype showed a mean decline of 1.8 letters per year, compared to 3.5 letters per year reported in the RP cohort of Daich Varela et al. [6]. This difference may be partly explained by the differences in study design, as previous studies were retrospective and may have captured visual acuity measurements when patients reported a subjective visual decline. In contrast, BCVA measurements in our patient cohort were performed regardless of perceived changes in BCVA.

Regulatory agencies such as the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) have shown preference for a ≥ 15 ETDRS letter‐change (equivalent to +0.3 logMAR) after intervention [24]. This change was observed in only 7 eyes of 5 patients with an RP phenotype, suggesting that this criterion is of less value in patients with CRB1‐associated IRDs. Based on our previous studies, we have suggested an optimal window of treatment before the third decade of life in CRB1‐associated IRD, as moderate and severe visual impairment are typically reached at 18 and 32 years, respectively [5].

Although cataract progression could have been more accurately evaluated using a standardised grading system such as the Lens Opacities Classification System [25], our analysis revealed a limited impact of cataract on BCVA loss. This indicates that BCVA loss is attributed mainly to progressive retinal degeneration. Despite the limited effect of cataract on BCVA loss, our data suggest that progressive cataract may be a confounding factor of visual field loss alongside retinal degeneration. Thus, cataract surgery may be considered prior to therapeutic intervention. However, ophthalmologists should remain mindful of the increased risk of intra‐ and postoperative complications in patients with RP [26].

As a potential key clinical endpoint in IRD clinical trials, the patients also underwent microperimetry [27, 28]. Mean macular sensitivity on microperimetry, which was already significantly decreased after 2 years, showed a further reduction after 4 years, confirming its reliability as a robust clinical endpoint. The FDA has suggested using a locus‐based endpoint for IRD trials, which has been adopted by some clinical IRD trials, where a change of ≥ 7 dB at 5 or more test loci is clinically significant [29, 30, 31]. In our study, 70% of the patient satisfied this criterion over 4 years, but patients with a mean sensitivity of less than 7 dB at baseline, effectively reaching a floor effect, were unable to meet this requirement. Therefore, this pointwise sensitivity measure may only be applicable to patients with sufficient baseline sensitivity. Josan et al. have proposed using volume sensitivity as a measure of macular sensitivity on microperimetry, which is less prone to averaging and floor effects [21]. Our study group has recently demonstrated the superiority of volume sensitivity over mean and pointwise sensitivity in patients with RP, which allows for broader inclusion criteria [32]. Specifically, our findings demonstrate that even patients with a mean macular sensitivity of zero retained measurable volume sensitivity, suggesting that volume sensitivity can serve as a more inclusive endpoint, particularly for those with advanced disease. Though baseline volume sensitivity was not significantly associated with a faster or slower decline over time, we noticed a pattern where patients with higher baseline sensitivities had a greater decline over time, suggesting a greater dynamic range of possible deterioration compared to patients with an already lower baseline volume sensitivity. Alternatively, it implies that patients with more advanced disease reach a plateau with minimal residual functional vision.

For patients with low visual function and unstable fixation, microperimetry was challenging. For these patients, FST was particularly useful, as it is fixation‐independent and gives a global measure of retinal function. However, the current study did not show a statistically significant change in FST sensitivity over 4 years, possibly caused by a relatively high test–retest variability seen in RP patients [33]. However, that does not negate its success in demonstrating treatment effect in the first FDA‐approved gene augmentation therapy of voretigene neparvovec in RPE65‐associated IRD [34, 35]. In addition, FST can be used to assess residual photoreceptor viability, which may be helpful during the inclusion process. Therefore, FST should still be considered as a secondary clinical endpoint, especially in patients with low visual function.

In addition to psychophysical measurements, we monitored structural integrity of the retina on SD‐OCT and FAF imaging. Overall, we noticed thickened and disorganised inner retinal layers, as described previously [4]. Additionally, progressive thinning of the EZ and ELM layers was noted, both reflecting photoreceptor viability. Even though these structural markers are valuable for participant selection, they may be less appropriate for evaluating disease progression and treatment efficacy due to the early extensive disruption and challenging quantitative assessment of individual layers [7]. Nonetheless, retinal imaging remains an essential part of the workup, especially to monitor potential therapy‐associated chorioretinal atrophy as demonstrated in the voretigene neparvovec trials [36, 37].

Besides the functional and structural outcome measures, inclusion of patient‐reported outcome measures is becoming increasingly important, as these add an important dimension to the evaluation of efficacy. At the start of the current natural history study, we used the Visual Function Questionnaire to evaluate the general quality of life in patients with a CRB1‐associated IRD [38]. However, during the study period, the Michigan Retinal Degeneration Questionnaire was developed which provides a more disease‐specific evaluation of symptoms and the impact on daily life of patients with an IRD [39]. We have shown high correlations between visual function measures and the self‐reported visual function on this questionnaire, supporting its inclusion in future clinical trials [40, 41, 42].

An important drawback in designing clinical trials for IRD patients is the relatively unknown reliability of most of the clinical endpoints [43]. Particularly little is known on the test–retest variability, even though understanding this can aid in distinguishing natural variability from true clinical change [44]. Therefore, we assessed intrasession test–retest variability of microperimetry, which was found to be neither statistically nor clinically relevant. The higher intrasession variability seen in the left eye is likely the result of fatigue, as patients were required to complete a training test first and the right eye was tested first. Recently, we also investigated intersession variability of mesopic microperimetry and FST in patients with RP, which better mimics real‐life clinical trial situations and defined evidence‐based cut‐off values that distinguish clinically significant changes from natural variability [32, 33].

The current study is limited by the low prevalence of CRB1‐associated retinal dystrophies, which affected the study sample size. Although CRB1 pathogenic mutations are amongst the more prevalent genes associated with IRDs, [45] the disease is rare and finding a sufficiently large patient cohort is challenging. To overcome this, study collaborations between different centres should be formed to observe more patients and provide more generalisable data. As a result of the limited study size, robust subgroup analyses could not be reliably performed, although exploratory analyses provided some context on the effects of age, cataract and belonging to the genetic isolate. Despite the relatively small size of the study cohort, statistically significant changes were still observed. Additional missing FST measurements may have further reduced statistical power, thereby limiting the ability to detect small changes.

In conclusion, this natural history study underscores the need for a multimodal approach that includes psychophysical measurements and structural measures. Here, we underline the sensitivity of microperimetry as a clinical endpoint for CRB1‐associated IRDs, and propose the use of BCVA and visual field as long‐term clinical endpoints. Our findings can guide clinicians and regulators in designing future clinical trials for CRB1‐associated IRDs.
