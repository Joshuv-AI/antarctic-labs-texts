Crack propagation mechanism and life prediction of liner under thermal fatigue loads
Authors: Xinlin Wang, Wu Li, Mingxin Zheng, Qinghua Zeng, Yinhuai Li, Huiping Yang, Chengji Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the crack propagation issue in thin-walled multi-inclined-hole liners of aero-engine combustion chambers during service, a high-fidelity thermo-fluid-structure interaction (TFSI) numerical methodology was systematically developed in conjunction with experimental validation to elucidate the crack propagation mechanisms and the influence of initial crack parameters on remaining life characteristics. Research findings demonstrate that increases in both initial crack opening angle α₀ and azimuth angle β₀ significantly prolong the remaining life N, with their positive correlation to N strengthening as these parameters increase concurrently. Conversely, augmenting initial crack length L₀ leads to a substantial reduction in N, although the decay rate diminishes with increasing L₀. The angular intervals α₀∈[45°,60°] and β₀∈[15°,30°] are defined as the remaining life enhancement region for crack propagation. Furthermore, an echo state network (ESN)-based surrogate model with superior training efficiency and small-sample handling capability was developed, achieving an average relative error below 5% in predicting liner crack propagation life. This work provides robust theoretical support for condition monitoring and maintenance decision-making in aero-engine liners.Keywords: Aircraft combustor, Crack propagation, Life prediction, Surrogate modelSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

As the core component of an aircraft’s propulsion system, the combustor facilitates the conversion of chemical energy into thermal energy through organized fuel combustion, which is ultimately transformed into mechanical energy to propel the aircraft. To meet the evolving demands for high performance, reliability, durability, and fuel efficiency in future aircraft, increasing the combustor exit temperature has emerged as a critical strategy for enhancing the thrust-to-weight ratio1. Currently, the combustor exit temperature of next-generation propulsion systems is estimated to reach 2400 K, with the internal temperature rise maintained within a high-temperature range of 1600 K. Such extreme conditions subject the combustor liner to significant thermal stress2. Under the cyclic effects of thermal stress, the combustor liner is highly susceptible to fatigue cracking. Historical failure statistics indicate that 63% of engine combustor failures are concentrated in the liner, followed by 5% in the casing and 4% in the fuel nozzles. Due to this disproportionately high failure rate, the combustor liner has become the critical factor determining engine service life and is a primary focus for reliability optimization3. Therefore, an in-depth investigation into the crack growth mechanisms and life prediction methodologies for combustor liners under thermal fatigue loading is of critical significance for optimizing engine durability design and establishing scientific maintenance strategies.

Current research is primarily advanced through two categories of methods: experimental investigations and numerical simulations. Regarding experimental methods, Chen et al.4 conducted thermal fatigue tests on GH536 nickel-based alloy plates, confirming that an increase in peak temperature leads to a shorter crack initiation time and a significant acceleration in crack propagation rates. Chen et al.5 employed V-notched specimens of GH230 superalloy to conduct thermal cycling tests between 700 and 1000℃, confirming that elevated temperatures lead to a substantial increase in the crack propagation rate. Research by Yujie Zhang et al.6 on V-notched plates of GH3230 alloy further demonstrated that an increase in peak temperature simultaneously shortens the initiation life and accelerates crack propagation. Numerical simulation techniques have achieved breakthrough progress in recent years, effectively overcoming the limitations of high economic and time costs associated with experimental methods. Based on the ABAQUS platform, Guo et al.7 integrated temperature field and load spectrum simulations to estimate the thermal fatigue crack growth rate of a combustor liner using the Pearson empirical formula. They further analyzed the variation of the critical stress intensity factor for cracks at the film cooling lip and found that exhibits a decreasing trend as the crack propagates. Junhong Zhang et al.8 performed a failure analysis of a combustor casing using MSC/NASTRAN. They discovered that the concentration of plastic strain in the vicinity of the dilution holes (reaching a maximum of 0.4476%) was the primary driver for crack propagation. Geng et al.9 integrated ABAQUS with the Coffin-Manson and Paris equations to elucidate the mechanism of crack initiation at the edges of the primary holes in a combustor liner. Their study demonstrated that cracks typically initiate after approximately 1,500 thermal cycles.

The introduction of machine learning techniques has further enhanced prediction efficiency. Li et al.10 implemented rapid crack growth prediction based on Bayesian Regularized Neural Networks (BRNN). Bassel Hafiz et al.11 integrated Convolutional Autoencoders (CAE) with Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) networks for remaining useful life (RUL) prediction. Junying Hu et al.12 proposed a framework integrating compressed sensing, adaptive hybrid sampling, and Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (BiLSTM) networks to address the issue of data imbalance. Shuhan Sun et al.13 designed a hybrid 1D CNN-GRU model to achieve high-precision remaining useful life prediction under small-sample conditions.

In recent studies, Chen et al.14 introduced a random forest approach with high physical interpretability for Stress Intensity Factor (SIF) prediction. To improve model generalizability in complex structural applications, Zhang et al.15 developed a hybrid machine learning framework for crack detection and life assessment, specifically for the fatigue life prediction of welds in bridge decks. However, for thin-walled structures with complex geometries, such as aero-engine liners with multi-inclined holes, achieving a balance between computational efficiency and structural complexity under small-sample conditions remains a significant challenge. Building upon these insights, this paper innovatively introduces the Echo State Network (ESN) machine learning model. By integrating ESN with linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM), a crack propagation and life prediction methodology is established. This approach effectively reduces prediction costs and provides efficient theoretical support for the condition monitoring and maintenance decision-making of aircraft propulsion systems.

Model and boundary conditions

In this study, the combustion chamber liner was taken as the research object. First, a full-field thermal numerical simulation was conducted to obtain the temperature distribution of the liner. Subsequently, a one-way sequential thermo-structural coupling approach was employed, where the calculated temperature field was mapped onto the static structural model as a body load to extract the thermal stress distribution of the liner. On this basis, the critical region of the primary combustion hole containing a crack was selected for investigation. The extracted thermal stresses were applied as boundary inputs to construct a parametric crack propagation dynamic model based on linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM). Given that the crack size is relatively small, its influence on the global flow and thermal fields is negligible; therefore, the thermal stress load in the model was transferred via a one-way path without the need for real-time iterative updates. The model revealed the nonlinear influence mechanisms of initial crack length, opening angle, and azimuth angle on the remaining life, thereby identifying and defining a “life enhancement region” to improve service reliability. Finally, the Reservoir Computing (RC) framework of the Echo State Network (ESN) model was employed to capture the high-dimensional mapping relationships inherent in small-sample datasets, thereby constructing a high-fidelity surrogate model for life prediction.

Geometric model

The combustor features an annular configuration consisting of 20 liners with multi-inclined hole effusion cooling. To simplify the computation, a 1/20 sector of the combustor (Fig. 1(a)) was selected for the full-field thermal numerical simulation. Specifically focusing on the cracks around the primary holes of the liner, a critical region measuring 50 mm × 50 mm (Fig. 1(b)) was extracted as the geometric model for crack propagation simulation. As illustrated in Fig. 1(c), the crack length L is defined as the distance of the line segment OP connecting the midpoint O of the primary hole arc AB to the crack tip P. The crack opening angle α is defined as the angle between AP and BP, while the crack azimuthal angle β refers to the angle between OP and the X-axis.

Mesh generation and boundary conditions

The combustor liner was discretized using polyhedral meshing within the ANSYS Workbench environment. Three different element sizes—0.6 mm, 0.8 mm, and 1.0 mm—were prescribed, resulting in mesh counts of 0.67 million, 0.43 million, and 0.30 million cells, respectively. The analysis indicates that the sensitivity of numerical results to mesh density decreases significantly when the cell count exceeds 0.43 million. In this case, the relative error is maintained within 5%. Consequently, it is determined that the discretization error of the 0.43 million-cell mesh satisfies the requirements for grid independence. Therefore, this mesh density was selected for the final combustor liner simulations, as illustrated in Fig. 2.

Fixed supports were applied to both sides of the combustor head plate to restrict displacement. Concurrently, the temperature field calculated via ANSYS Fluent was mapped onto the model as surface thermal boundary conditions. The mesh of the crack zone is illustrated in Fig. 3. To further reduce discretization errors, local mesh refinement was performed at the crack front using tetrahedral elements with a size of 0.25 mm, resulting in a total of 0.23 million elements.

Crack zone diagram.

In the crack-zone simulation, frictionless supports were applied to the X-Z and X-Y planes to constrain normal displacements in the Y and Z directions, respectively. Simultaneously, a unidirectional pressure load was imposed on the X-Z plane along the Y-axis, with a stress ratio of R = 0. The maximum crack growth increment per step was set to 0.1 mm. The simulation was terminated when the crack propagation length reached 3 mm, This criterion is defined based on the geometric constraints of the multi-inclined-hole liner. Under these conditions, a crack with a length of 3 mm typically approaches the adjacent cooling holes, leading to the loss of structural integrity and a significant degradation in cooling performance. Furthermore, this value aligns with the standard maintenance limits for thin-walled components in aero-engines17. The corresponding number of cycles at this point was defined as the remaining life N of the combustor liner. By fixing the connection weights between hidden units and the weights from the input layer to the hidden units, the ESN model effectively addresses the issues of vanishing forward-propagating information and exploding gradients typically encountered in Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs) during training. Since the output function adopts a standard linear form, the optimal solution for the output weight matrix can be solved directly using the least squares method. This design significantly reduces the number of trainable parameters, thereby decreasing the dependency on sample size. Consequently, the model is more conducive to the small-sample scenarios explored in this study. Meanwhile, the high-dimensional nonlinear mapping capability of the reservoir computing framework enhances the richness of feature representation under limited sample conditions and, to some extent, alleviates the risk of overfitting.

The sample dataset for the ESN model developed in this study is entirely derived from high-fidelity TFSI numerical simulations and linear elastic fracture mechanics (LEFM) analysis based on Paris’ Law. This ‘physics-informed’ data acquisition approach ensures that when initial crack parameters (, , ) are input, the model’s output (N) inherently adheres to the physical principles governing the evolution of stress intensity factors. For small datasets, the reservoir size of the ESN model should be limited to prevent overfitting. The leaking rate can be appropriately increased to optimize the fitting performance for non-temporal data, while a larger regularization coefficient is required to effectively suppress overfitting. Furthermore, a smaller spectral radius should be prescribed to capture the short-term memory relationships inherent in limited non-temporal samples. Experimental validation of the numerical method

The calculation results for thermal stress are illustrated in Fig. 4(a). The equivalent stress distribution is consistent with the direction of airflow and exhibits distinct gradient characteristics along the flow path.

Numerical Simulation and Experimental Validation.

The peak equivalent stress occurs at the first cooling hole adjacent to the main combustion hole along the airflow gradient direction. This location is subjected to significant alternating cold and hot thermal cycles, which is highly susceptible to thermal stress concentration. The numerical simulation indicates that the thermal stress at this position reaches a maximum value of 2243 MPa. Figure 4(b) illustrates the crack initiation site identified after engine disassembly. Its distribution characteristics are in high agreement with the numerical simulation results. This validation demonstrates the model’s high fidelity in capturing crack initiation locations driven by thermal stress concentrations. It should be noted that the experimental data primarily serves to verify the spatial accuracy of the stress field and the identified critical regions, rather than the real-time crack growth rate. Note that the peak stress of 2243 MPa is a theoretical elastic value at the crack tip. In reality, local plasticity would occur, but the LEFM framework is utilized here to focus on the comparative effects of crack geometry on the stress intensity factor.

The maximum equivalent thermal stress at the edge of the main fuel hole reaches 837 MPa, which is taken as the applied cyclic load in the subsequent crack growth analysis. As shown in Fig. 5, The thermal fatigue load is applied periodically, with the stress amplitude alternating between 0 MPa (corresponding to the ground idle state) and 837 MPa (corresponding to the maximum takeoff thrust state), forming a stress range of = 837 MPa for the Paris law-based crack propagation simulation.

Conclusion

To address the issue of wall cracking in thin-walled multi-inclined hole combustor liners during service, this study develops a high-fidelity thermal-fluid-solid coupling numerical method based on experimental data. The expansion mechanism of cracks and the influence of initial crack parameters on service life are systematically revealed. Furthermore, a surrogate life prediction model based on Echo State Network (ESN) is developed, achieving a Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) of less than 5%. This work provides an efficient and reliable life assessment solution for aero-engine combustor liners. The main conclusions are as follows:

Increases in the initial crack opening angle () and azimuthal angle () significantly extend the remaining life (N), with their positive correlations with N intensifying as each other increases. Conversely, an increase in the initial crack length () leads to a significant decrease in N, although the rate of decline diminishes as increases.

Conversely, an increase in the initial crack length () leads to a significant decrease in N, although the rate of decline diminishes as increases.

The “Remaining life Growth Intervals” are defined based on the sensitivity of N to initial crack parameters: a significant enhancement in remaining life remaining useful life N is observed when the initial crack opening angle is between 45° and 60°, and a marked increasing trend in N occurs when the initial azimuthal angle is between 15° and 30°. Accordingly, in [45°, 60°] and in [15°, 30°] are defined as the life growth intervals for the opening angle and azimuthal angle, respectively.

Owing to its significant advantages in training efficiency and handling small sample sizes, the ESN is identified as the optimal solution for constructing the life prediction surrogate model.
