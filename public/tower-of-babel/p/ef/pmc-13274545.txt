Efficacy and safety of tocilizumab for neoplastic fever in patients with advanced solid tumors: a multicenter retrospective study
Authors: Yu Yan, Xiaoyan Liu, Yang Du, Qiqi Dou, Hanping Wang, Xiaoyan Si, Xiaotong Zhang, Xuefei Wu, Yunzhi Zhou, Mei Li, Wei Zhong, Mengzhao Wang, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeoplastic fever (NF) severely impairs quality of life and often limits antitumor therapy, yet effective treatment remains lacking. Given the central role of interleukin-6 (IL-6), we evaluated tocilizumab for NF management.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed NF cases at 3 Chinese hospitals (2019-2023). Patients who received tocilizumab and had evaluable follow-up data were included in analyses of fever resolution, afebrile duration, Karnofsky Performance Status, inflammatory markers, antitumor outcomes, and adverse events (AEs). In patients with sequential exposure to non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) and tocilizumab, changes in inflammatory markers after each treatment were compared.ResultsForty-five patients were identified (median age 65). All had elevated CRP and IL-6. Among 22 treated with tocilizumab, all achieved fever resolution within 24 hours, sustained for ≥2 weeks, with significant reductions in CRP (mean, 95.7-19.0 mg/L) and other inflammatory markers. Median afebrile duration was 3 weeks, extended to 4 weeks with re-treatment. Systemic therapy was subsequently administered in patients with poor baseline performance, who achieved an objective response rate of 50%, a median progression-free survival of 11.6 months, and an overall survival of 21.3 months. AEs occurred in 81.8% (grade ≥3, 31.8%) but were manageable. Compared with NSAIDs, tocilizumab uniquely improved anemia and inflammatory control.ConclusionTocilizumab provided rapid and durable fever resolution with systemic anti-inflammatory effects, accompanied by functional and hematologic improvement. These findings suggest IL-6 blockade as a promising therapeutic option for NF.

In this retrospective multicenter analysis, tocilizumab was associated with rapid and sustained control of neoplastic fever, improvement in performance status, and attenuation of tumor-related inflammation. Its use in selected patients may help create a therapeutic window for subsequent antitumor therapy in the setting of poor general condition.

Introduction

Neoplastic fever (NF), a common paraneoplastic phenomenon in advanced cancer,1 is primarily mediated by cytokines released from tumor and immune cells within the tumor microenvironment.2 It accounts for 7%-31% of fevers of unknown origin3 and approximately 41% of non-infectious pyrexia in this population.4 Despite its prevalence, NF remains underrecognized and constitutes a significant diagnostic challenge—particularly in the era of immune checkpoint inhibitors, where fever may mimic immune-related adverse events (irAE) or infection.5 This diagnostic uncertainty often leads to extensive workups and empiric treatments. Conventional management involves non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or corticosteroids,6–8 which provide transient symptom relief but carry substantial limitations. NSAIDs require continuous administration and risk gastrointestinal, renal, and cardiovascular toxicity, while corticosteroids induce broad immunosuppression that may compromise anticancer immunity.9 For persistent or refractory cases, no standard targeted therapy exists, highlighting an unmet need for approaches that control NF without undermining host defenses or oncologic outcomes.

Among the key mediators of cancer-related inflammation, interleukin-6 (IL-6) plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of NF.10 Secreted by both tumor and stromal immune cells,11 IL-6 triggers multiple downstream effects characteristic of the paraneoplastic inflammatory state: it drives hepatic production of acute-phase reactants such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and fibrinogen, promotes neutrophilia and thrombocytosis, and contributes to hypoalbuminemia and anemia of chronic disease.12 Blay et al.'s seminal 1997 study13 established a causal relationship between IL-6 and NF: cancer patients with paraneoplastic fever exhibited significantly higher IL-6, CRP, and neutrophil counts, alongside lower hemoglobin levels compared to afebrile controls—abnormalities that were reversed by experimental IL-6 blockade. Furthermore, IL-6 plays a critical role in cancer-associated cachexia and systemic inflammation,14 linking it to impaired nutritional status and declining physical performance. Beyond systemic symptoms, IL-6 also drives tumor immune evasion by expanding myeloid-derived suppressor cells, promoting regulatory T cells, and inhibiting cytotoxic T-cell function.15–18 High circulating IL-6 is associated with worse prognosis and resistance to therapies such as immune checkpoint inhibitors.19–22 Thus, IL-6 blockade represents a promising strategy to simultaneously control NF, resolve cancer-associated inflammation, and potentiate antitumor efficacy.

Tocilizumab—a humanized monoclonal antibody against the IL-6 receptor—has been successfully used to treat IL-6–driven conditions, including rheumatoid arthritis, cytokine release syndrome (CRS) in CAR-T therapy, cancer-associated cachexia,23,24 and paraneoplastic inflammatory syndromes (PIS).25 Given the paucity of data specific to NF and motivated by these clinical successes, we conducted a retrospective analysis to examine the use of tocilizumab in patients with advanced solid tumors and refractory NF. We sought to determine whether IL-6 receptor blockade was associated with fever control, reduced inflammatory burden, and improved functional status that could facilitate anticancer therapy. Here, we report real-world evidence on tocilizumab’s therapeutic potential for NF management.

Methods

Patient selection

The patients included in this analysis had histologically confirmed solid tumors and a clinical diagnosis of neoplastic fever, defined as persistent fever >37.8 °C occurring at least once daily for more than 2 weeks, in the absence of identifiable causes such as infection, drug reaction, or autoimmune disease. As part of their management, all patients had undergone a comprehensive evaluation—including physical examination, microbiological testing (blood, sputum, urine, stool, cerebrospinal fluid, and bone marrow), and imaging studies—to exclude infectious or immune-related etiologies. All had fever refractory to at least 7 days of empirical antibiotics, with a transient response to NSAIDs. We excluded from our analysis patients with hematologic malignancies, cytokine release syndrome, or immune checkpoint inhibitor-related fever, including cases where fever was an early manifestation or concurrent symptom of an irAE such as pneumonitis or hepatitis. Patients whose history satisfied these attributes were included in the overall NF cohort.

All patients initially received NSAIDs for fever control. A subset later met the definition of NSAID-refractory fever, defined as incomplete or unsustained fever control despite adequate NSAID dosing. Specifically, this included (1) recurrence of fever within the expected dosing interval after escalation to the maximum recommended dose of NSAIDs or (2) a pattern of chronic or intermittent fever persisting for more than one month despite NSAID use. Those who subsequently received tocilizumab for NSAID-refractory fever and concurrently or subsequently underwent anticancer therapy with at least one post-treatment radiologic evaluation were included in the tocilizumab-treated cohort (primary cohort) for further analysis.

Between December 2019 and June 2023, 55 patients were identified across 3 Chinese centers. After excluding 2 cases with incomplete records and 8 with suspected infectious or immune-related fever, 45 patients were included in the overall NF cohort. Of these, 24 received tocilizumab; 2 were excluded due to lack of subsequent antitumor therapy. Thus, 22 patients treated with tocilizumab comprised the primary analysis cohort.

Study design

This retrospective, multicenter observational analysis aimed to evaluate the clinical characteristics of NF and the efficacy and safety of tocilizumab in managing this condition. Tocilizumab was administered based on routine clinical decision-making and was not required for a patient to be included in this analysis. Tocilizumab was administered intravenously at a dose of 4-8 mg/kg based on clinical assessment. Patients continued their existing antitumor therapies throughout the period that we analyzed. Clinical data, including body temperature, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), laboratory tests (CRP, cytokines, hemoglobin, leukocyte subpopulations, platelet count, and others), and fever recurrence intervals, were extracted from medical records at baseline and weeks 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12 after the initial tocilizumab infusion. For the overall NF cohort, clinical features were comprehensively described. For the primary cohort, both the clinical characteristics and treatment outcomes (including efficacy and safety) were systematically analyzed. Data were collected from all patients until death, disease progression, or August 1, 2024, whichever occurred first.

Outcomes and assessments

For the primary cohort, the primary outcome assessed from chart review was the proportion of patients with a complete fever response to tocilizumab, defined as resolution of fever within 24 hours and sustained defervescence for at least one week, accompanied by CRP normalization (≤10 mg/L) or a ≥ 70% reduction from baseline. Secondary outcomes included duration of afebrile status, change in KPS score and inflammatory markers, anti-tumor outcomes, including response and survival, and treatment safety profiles. Among patients receiving concurrent antitumor therapy, tumor response was retrospectively assessed by examining their imaging studies and, where possible, assessing the extent of benefit according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) v1.1. Objective tumor response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete or partial response (CR/PR). Stable disease (SD) and progressive disease (PD) were also assessed. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the interval from the first dose of tocilizumab to disease progression, often assessed clinically, or death; and overall survival (OS) was defined as the interval to death from any cause. Adverse events extracted from the medical records were recorded and graded according to the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0.

For the exploratory analysis comparing the effects of NSAIDs and tocilizumab on inflammatory markers, a subgroup of 11 patients with available pre- and post-treatment data for both therapies was included. Changes in inflammatory markers before and after each treatment were evaluated to assess the differential impacts of NSAIDs versus tocilizumab on inflammation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 24.0. Data visualizations were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 8.0, R version 4.1, and Adobe Illustrator 2020. Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation, while non-normally distributed data were summarized as median with IQR. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Changes in biomarkers at different time points were assessed using repeated measures analysis. Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed to estimate PFS and OS. Cox regression models were used to evaluate the duration of afebrile status and OS. Statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P-value of <.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics of overall patients with neoplastic fever

The baseline clinical characteristics of the study cohort are summarized in Table 1. A total of 45 patients were included, with a median age of 65 years, and 36 (80%) were male. The majority of patients (80%) were diagnosed with non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), followed by gastric cancer (6.7%) and small-cell lung cancer (4.4%). All patients presented with advanced-stage disease; 38 (84.4%) had stage IV disease, and 7 (15.6%) had stage III disease. Bone (40.0%), adrenal glands (26.7%), and liver (22.2%) were the most frequently involved metastatic sites. Cachexia was present in 23 patients (51.1%), defined as >5% weight loss within the previous 6 months.26 The peak body temperature associated with tumor-related fever was 38.7 ± 0.6 °C. Inflammatory markers were markedly elevated across the cohort. The median serum CRP level was 93.0 mg/L (IQR, 70.3-127.2), and the median IL-6 level was 54.1 pg/mL (IQR, 30.9-85.0). White blood cells (WBC), neutrophils (NEUT), and platelets (PLT) had median values of 12.8, 10.8, and 328, respectively, suggesting a trend of elevation in the cohort. Anemia was commonly observed, with a mean hemoglobin concentration of 101.7 g/L.

Characteristics of the tocilizumab-treated cohort

Among the total cohort, 22 patients who satisfied our inclusion criteria received tocilizumab for what was clinically considered NSAID-refractory neoplastic fever (Table 1). The median age of this subgroup was 68 years, and 21 patients (95.5%) were male. Lung cancer remained the most common primary malignancy, including lung adenocarcinoma and squamous cell carcinoma. Nearly all patients (95.5%) had stage IV disease at the time of tocilizumab initiation. Baseline inflammatory burden in the group treated with tocilizumab was substantial, with a median CRP of 90.7 mg/L and a median IL-6 of 52.4 pg/mL. Anemia was common but relatively less severe than in patients who did not receive tocilizumab. Most patients were receiving first-line systemic anti-tumor therapy (77.3%), while the remainder were treated in later lines. Like the patients who did not receive tocilizumab, more than half had an ECOG PS ≥2.

In most patients, tocilizumab was administered prior to the initiation of anti-tumor therapy, with a median interval of 6 days (IQR, −6.25 to 8), using tocilizumab initiation as day 0. The administered dose was 4 mg/kg for 3 patients and 6-8 mg/kg for the remaining patients. The choice of a 4 mg/kg dose in 3 patients was based on concerns regarding potential myelosuppression induced by concurrent chemotherapy. Repeated tocilizumab administration was frequently observed. Four patients received only one dose of tocilizumab, one patient received 2 doses, 3 patients received 3 doses, and 14 patients received 4 or more doses. The median follow-up duration was 28.5 months (range, 13-56).

Primary and secondary outcomes

Primary outcome

After tocilizumab administration, all 22 patients met the prespecified definition of complete fever response on retrospective review, with fever resolution documented within 24 hours and sustained afebrile status (≤37.5 °C) for at least one week. CRP levels decreased to ≤10 mg/L or were reduced by ≥70% from baseline within 1 week (Figure 1).

CRP levels in patients with neoplastic fever before and after one week of tocilizumab treatment. The mean CRP level decreased from 95.7 mg/L at baseline (W0) to 19.0 mg/L at week 1 (W1) (P < .001). Dashed lines indicate mean values.

Duration of afebrile status

All patients experienced tumor fever recurrence during follow-up. The interval from the first tocilizumab administration to the first recurrence, recorded as T1, had a median duration of 3 weeks (range, 2-6). Among them, 18 patients received a second dose upon recurrence, all with subsequent normalization of body temperature documented in the medical records. The interval from the second tocilizumab dose to the next fever recurrence (T2) had a median duration of 4 weeks (range, 1-9). Overall, the median durations of T1 and T2 were 3 and 4 weeks, respectively, suggesting a trend toward prolonged afebrile intervals, although the difference was not statistically significant (Figure S1A). Factors influencing duration of afebrile status

To identify factors associated with the recurrence time of tumor fever (T1 and T2), univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed. Univariate analyses revealed no significant factors associated with T1 (Table S1). Conversely, in terms of T2 (Table S2), 4 factors demonstrated significant associations in univariate analysis: baseline KPS score, KPS score at 1-week post-treatment, CRP levels at 3 weeks post-tocilizumab, and treatment response (P < .05 for all). Among them, elevated CRP levels at 3 weeks and lack of anti-tumor response (SD/PD) were risk factors for tumor fever recurrence, while higher baseline KPS scores and higher KPS scores at 1 week were protective factors. Multivariate Cox regression analysis further confirmed that non-responders had a higher risk of fever recurrence than responders (HR 3.21, 95% CI 1.08-9.49, P < .05), and higher CRP at 3 weeks was also independently associated with an increased risk of fever recurrence (HR 1.07, 95% CI, 1.01-1.13, P < .05; Figure 3).

(A) Univariate Cox analysis identified treatment response, KPS scores at week 0 and week 1, and CRP levels at week 3 as significant factors influencing the duration of afebrile interval T2. (B) Multivariate Cox analysis confirmed treatment non-response and higher CRP levels at week 3 as independent risk factors for earlier fever relapse. To further identify factors associated with a longer T2 than T1, subgroup analysis revealed that patients who received immunotherapy, had baseline KPS scores ≥50, achieved treatment response, had lower baseline CRP level, achieved CRP normalization (<10 mg/L) at week 3, or had lower baseline neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) experienced significantly longer T2 durations (P < .05) (Figure S1B-G). No significant differences in T2 compared to T1 were observed for subgroups stratified by age, histological type, baseline IL-6 levels, or baseline platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR) levels (Figure S1H-K).

Change in KPS score and inflammatory markers over time following tocilizumab treatment

As shown in Figure 4, KPS scores improved over time after tocilizumab use, with notable increases observed from week 3 onward (P < .001). Median KPS scores were 50, 50, 70, 70, 80, and 80 at baseline and at weeks 1, 3, 6, 9, and 12, respectively. CRP levels decreased significantly over time (P < .001), with mean levels of 95.70 ± 5.96 mg/L at baseline, followed by 18.96 ± 2.27, 14.51 ± 3.40, 12.57 ± 5.57, 14.55 ± 7.26, and 15.54 ± 8.68 at the corresponding follow-up time points, respectively. Anemia also demonstrated a trend toward improvement, with hemoglobin levels significantly elevated at 3 weeks (mean difference 9.50 g/L, 95% CI, 1.36-17.64, P < .05), although no significant changes were noted at other time points. Additionally, WBC, NEUT, PLT, NLR, and PLR levels significantly decreased after treatment (P < .01 for all), while lymphocyte counts remained stable without notable differences (P > .05).

Tumor efficacy evaluation

Among the 22 patients included in the primary cohort, 17 were receiving first-line anti-tumor treatment, with NSCLC comprising the majority (14 patients). Retrospective tumor response assessment showed 11 patients achieved PR, 7 had SD, and 4 experienced PD, resulting in an ORR of 50%. The median PFS was 11.6 months, and the median OS was 21.3 months (Figure 5).

Bar colors indicate tumor response: PR (green), SD (dark green), PD (purple). Rows below show corresponding tocilizumab doses, treatment cycles, anti-tumor therapy lines, and mode of tocilizumab administration (concurrent vs rescue). Concurrent TCZ was defined as use before or within 1 week of anti-tumor therapy initiation; rescue TCZ as continued use ≥1 week after anti-tumor therapy initiation due to persistent fever. (B) Kaplan–Meier curve for overall survival (OS); median OS was 21.3 months. (C) Kaplan–Meier curve for progression-free survival (PFS); median PFS was 11.6 months.

Further analysis identified response to treatment, KPS score at week 1 post-treatment, and the duration of afebrile status (T2) as significant factors associated with OS (Figure S2). Patients who did not respond to anti-tumor therapy had significantly shorter OS compared to responders (15.8 vs 34.5 months, HR 3.11, 95% CI, 1.03-9.44, P < .05). Similarly, patients with lower KPS scores at 1-week post-treatment had shorter OS (10.6 vs 22.7 months, HR 12.19, 95% CI, 2.50-59.46, P < .01). A T2 interval of less than 4 weeks was associated with poorer survival compared to those with longer afebrile periods (13.2 vs 34.5 months, HR 13.19, 95% CI, 2.98-58.34, P < .001). Multivariate Cox regression analysis further confirmed that T2 duration of less than 4 weeks was an independent prognostic factor for OS (HR 20.70, 95% CI, 2.50-171.58, P < .01).

Exploratory analysis: NSAIDs vs tocilizumab in controlling inflammation

We performed an exploratory analysis in 11 patients with available pre- and post-treatment data for both tocilizumab and NSAIDs (Figure 6). Tocilizumab treatment was associated with significant improvements in inflammatory burden, with marked increases in hemoglobin levels (107.0 g/L vs 120.0 g/L, P < .001) and substantial reductions in CRP (94.0 mg/L vs 27.4 mg/L, P < .001), NLR (8.8 vs 4.5, P < .01), and PLR (278.9 vs 129.4, P < .01). In contrast, NSAID treatment was not associated with significant changes in hemoglobin (100.4 g/L vs 98.9 g/L, P > .05), CRP (90.1 mg/L vs 94.4 mg/L, P > .05), NLR (6.4 vs 7.6, P > .05), or PLR (206.6 vs 206.0, P > .05), suggesting limited effects on anemia and inflammatory markers.

Safety profile

One patient who received 4 mg/kg of tocilizumab developed an acute allergic reaction during infusion that was considered directly attributable to the tocilizumab; the reaction resolved after slowing the infusion rate and administering epinephrine, and did not recur. No other adverse events (AEs) were considered directly attributable to tocilizumab.

Because concomitant antitumor therapy was administered during the period analyzed, attribution of the remaining AEs specifically to tocilizumab could not be determined with certainty. Adverse events of any grade were observed in 81.8% of patients, while grade 3 or higher AEs were observed in 31.8% of cases. The most common AEs included neutropenia (31.8%), liver function abnormalities (31.8%), nausea (18.2%), thrombocytopenia (18.2%), rash (13.6%), lymphocytopenia (13.6%), and dyspnea (9.1%). Grade 3 or higher AEs consisted of neutropenia (18.2%), dyspnea (9.1%), nausea (9.1%), and thrombocytopenia (4.5%).

Discussion

In this multicenter retrospective analysis of tocilizumab use for NF, we observed rapid and sustained clinical benefits after IL-6 blockade, including documented fever resolution within 24 hours and maintenance of normothermia for more than 2 weeks in patients with fever refractory to NSAIDs management. In our cohort, tocilizumab treatment was also associated with better systemic inflammation control, improvement in anemia, and improved performance status relative to the biomarker changes observed after NSAIDs exposure, potentially enabling subsequent antitumor therapy.

Building upon the foundational work of Blay, who established IL-6 blockade for PIS,13 our analysis specifically addresses NF as a distinct entity. Patients demonstrated profound systemic inflammation, evidenced by elevated CRP, IL-6, leukocyte counts, NLR, and PLR, along with anemia; these features were consistent with prior reports in cancer cachexia and PIS.13,23,25,27,28 Our results support the potential effectiveness of tocilizumab. These findings mirror and extend the work of Blay et al.,25 which focused on PIS rather than NF and did not explicitly examine the duration of fever control with tocilizumab or its influencing factors. We found that recurrent fevers typically reemerged approximately 2-6 weeks after the first recorded tocilizumab dose, accompanied by rising inflammatory markers. In addition, 2 independent NF cases outside this cohort also experienced fever recurrence with marked CRP elevation (Figure S3), suggesting that repeated or maintenance dosing might be necessary for sustained clinical benefit. Further analysis revealed that higher post-treatment CRP levels and lack of tumor control were significantly associated with shorter afebrile duration. These findings suggest that effective inflammation control and tumor control may be important for sustained NF management. Clinically, tocilizumab may serve as an adjunct or bridging therapy to improve patients’ conditions and facilitate antitumor treatments. Repeated tocilizumab dosing also appeared feasible in our cohort, without an obvious signal of cumulative toxicity, although vigilant infection monitoring remains essential.

Prior studies on NF have largely described symptom relief following NSAID use, without examining associated changes in inflammatory biomarkers.29–31 In contrast, our analysis captured dynamic biomarker changes after IL-6 blockade, although the retrospective design limited temporal precision. Notably, CRP levels decreased by approximately 80% within one week after treatment, alongside improvements in leukocytosis and thrombocytosis. The observed decrease in neutrophils and increase in lymphocytes following tocilizumab may be related to the inhibition of IL-6-driven Th17 polarization and myeloid inflammation, and may partly underlie the potential compatibility of IL-6 blockade with anticancer therapies.32–34 Anemia also appeared to improve, with hemoglobin increases observed in 14 of 16 patients (87.5%) by week 3 (mean increase, 12.4 g/L). This may relate to reduced inflammation and IL-6-dependent hepcidin regulation reported in prior studies.35,36

It is worth comparing IL-6 blockade to the conventional management of NF using NSAIDs. Naproxen and other NSAIDs effectively abolish tumor-related fevers temporarily, historically serving diagnostic and symptomatic roles.6 Many patients in our cohort partially responded or required daily NSAIDs before tocilizumab use. However, NSAIDs do not reduce IL-6 production, acting instead downstream by inhibiting prostaglandin E2.37 Thus, the fever tends to recur quickly (often within 8-12 hours) once the NSAID wears off. In contrast, a single tocilizumab dose provided sustained relief—typically several weeks of normal temperatures—through upstream IL-6 receptor blockade. Additionally, NSAIDs generally fail to significantly improve associated laboratory abnormalities such as CRP, anemia, or NLR, whereas tocilizumab notably reduced systemic inflammation beyond fever suppression alone. Long-term NSAID use can lead to gastrointestinal, renal, or cardiovascular complications, particularly in frail cancer patients.38 Taken together, these observational data support further study of tocilizumab as a promising approach for sustained NF control, especially in patients ineligible for immediate tumor-directed therapy.

Although our study lacked a control arm, we evaluated functional and oncologic outcomes, which have been under-characterized in prior NF studies. Given the heterogeneity in cancer types and treatment regimens, these analyses were exploratory. Our results showed that lower post-treatment KPS scores and shorter afebrile durations were associated with poorer survival, suggesting that effective control of systemic inflammation and symptom burden may reflect a more favorable clinical trajectory. Importantly, these findings do not support a direct survival benefit attributable to tocilizumab, but rather suggest that IL-6 blockade did not appear to compromise survival and may facilitate the delivery and tolerability of anti-tumor therapy in selected patients. This aligns with accumulating data implicating elevated IL-6 as a marker of poor prognosis, and with preclinical evidence that IL-6 promotes tumor growth, metastasis, and immune evasion.39–41

From a safety standpoint, no unexpected adverse events associated with tocilizumab were identified in this analysis, consistent with its established safety profile in other diseases.42–44 Nonetheless, when combined with cytotoxic chemotherapy, overlapping myelosuppressive effects warrant close hematologic monitoring. In our cohort, grade ≥3 neutropenia was transient and reversible with supportive care, and did not lead to dose reductions or delays in subsequent anti-tumor treatment. Additionally, one patient developed an allergic reaction to tocilizumab at the 4 mg/kg dose. Notably, previous trials have reported several-fold higher rates of anaphylaxis with the 4 mg/kg dose compared to 8 mg/kg, highlighting the need for clinical vigilance even at lower doses.42

This study has several limitations. Its retrospective design, small sample size, and single-arm nature limit definitive outcome conclusions, and potential selection bias cannot be excluded. Repeated dosing was not standardized and was often guided by symptom recurrence, CRP elevation, or antitumor treatment cycles, preventing consistent evaluation beyond the second dose. As such, the optimal timing and strategy for repeated dosing remain unclear. These limitations underscore the need for prospective trials to determine optimal dosing intervals and assess the impact of IL-6 blockade on patient-reported outcomes and quality of life within comprehensive cancer treatment protocols.

The cost-effectiveness of this approach also warrants careful consideration. Tocilizumab is substantially more costly than conventional antipyretic therapies. In our cohort, it was used as a step-up strategy after failure of standard treatments, addressing an unmet clinical need rather than replacing routine care. Treatment selection in this real-world setting was influenced by multiple factors, including symptom severity, physician judgment, and patient accessibility, which may introduce selection bias. Although tocilizumab appeared effective in this selected population, our findings should not be extrapolated to support routine or first-line use. Consequently, the cost-effectiveness of tocilizumab and the identification of optimal patient populations—such as those defined by inflammatory burden, symptom severity, or performance status—cannot be determined from this retrospective analysis. Future prospective studies are warranted to better define patient selection strategies and to evaluate the feasibility and economic implications of IL-6–targeted therapy in routine oncology practice.

In conclusion, this retrospective multicenter analysis suggests that tocilizumab may be an effective option for managing NF in patients with solid tumors. IL-6 blockade was associated with rapid alleviation of fever and systemic inflammation. Managing cytokine-driven symptoms such as NF may improve supportive cancer care by helping selected patients better tolerate necessary treatments. These findings support further prospective evaluation of this targeted supportive care strategy.
