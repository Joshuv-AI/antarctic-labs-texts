Balancing Solvation Ability of Polymer and Solvent in Gel Polymer Electrolytes for Efficient Lithium Metal Batteries
Authors: Mingnan Li, Dmitrii A Rakov, Yameng Fan, Caoyu Wang, Cheng Wang, Jodie A Yuwono, Shuixin Xia, Jianfeng Mao, Zaiping Guo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGel polymer electrolytes (GPEs) show practical potential in lithium metal batteries (LMBs), but their development is hindered by insufficient understanding of Li+ solvation chemistry and its impact on ion transport and solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation. By regulating the solvation abilities of polymer and solvent, this work explores the relationships between GPE composition, Li+ coordination structures, Li+ transference number, and interphase chemistry. The GPE combining weakly coordinated solvents with strongly coordinating polymer results in strong Li+‐polymer attachment, leading to sluggish ion transport. Employing strongly chelating solvents causes complete Li+‐polymer detachment, forming micelle structures that obstruct ion transport. The GPE with both weakly coordinated solvent fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC) and polymer 1H,1H,2H,2H‐tridecafluoro‐n‐octyl acrylate (TFOA) enables optimal interactions between Li+ and polymer/solvent, facilitating partial Li+‐polymer detachment and aggregates (AGGs) generation, avoiding micelle formation. This promotes efficient Li+ transport and anion‐derived SEI generation. The resulting GPE achieves 99.2% Coulombic efficiency (CE) in Cu||Li cells and enables 4.5 V LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2||Li to demonstrate 81% capacity retention after 140 cycles. These findings provide valuable insights for further advanced GPE design.Keywords: Gel polymer electrolyte, Lithium metal batteries, Polymer, Solvated ability, Solvent

Introduction

Current lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs), with an energy density ranging from 150 to 220 Wh kg−1, have dominated the market for over 30 years.[ 1 ] However, with the rise of electric vehicles, drones, and AI data centers, there is an increasing demand for batteries with even higher energy density.[ 2 , 3 ] Lithium metal batteries (LMBs) with higher energy density are a potential candidate;[ 4 ] however, these systems suffer from non‐uniform metal deposition, leading to safety concerns and unstable cycling performance.[ 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 ] Using solid‐state electrolytes is a viable strategy to enhance both the electrochemical performance and safety of LMBs.[ 9 , 10 ] Compared to all‐solid‐state electrolytes based on polymer or inorganic chemistries, gel polymer electrolytes (GPEs) are a promising alternative due to their good flexibility, good interfacial contact, non‐flammability, and high room‐temperature ionic conductivity.[ 11 ]

Typical GPE consists of a polymer matrix and a liquid electrolyte. A variety of polymer molecules and solvents have been investigated previously;[ 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 ] however, their advancement is limited by a lack of understanding regarding the rational design of these polyelectrolytes. A deeper insight is needed for the competitive Li+ solvation in polymer‐gel mixtures and its impact on ion transport and solid electrolyte interphase (SEI) formation. Like a liquid electrolyte, the SEI plays a crucial role in passivating Li metal and regulating Li0 deposition in GPEs. For example, an undesirable organic SEI causes continuous Li consumption, severe side reactions, and a short cycling lifespan. In contrast, a desirable inorganic‐rich SEI minimizes cell polarization, insulates electrons while allowing ion movement, and prevents non‐uniform Li0 deposition, including dendrites and dead‐Li formations.[ 19 ] Therefore, designing GPEs with a high Li+ transference number (t Li+) and robust SEI formation is essential for the development of high‐performance LMBs. To ensure rational polyelectrolyte design, the question “what is the role of polymer and solvent chemistry on the ionic transport and reduction kinetics?” needs to be answered.

Conventional solid polymer electrolytes, such as polyethylene glycol (PEO)‐based electrolytes, exhibit sluggish Li+ transport at room temperature and unstable cycling of LMBs. This is primarily caused by high crystallites of PEO electrolytes when lithium bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide (LiTFSI) is below 37 wt% or exceeds 50 wt%.[ 20 , 21 ] However, in the case of poly(ionic liquid)s, Kondou et al. reported that an ultrahigh Li‐salt concentration of ∼90 mol% results in stable Li anode cycling due to the detachment of Li+ from a polymer chain.[ 22 ] Chen et al. also suggested that minimizing interaction between metal cations and poly(ionic liquid) polymer by replacing Li+ with ions like Na+ and K+, improves ionic transport and cycling performance of metal anode.[ 23 ] Although poly(ionic liquid)s offer some advantages over PEO electrolytes, they also suffer from low ionic conductivity at room temperature (10−9–10−7 S cm−1) and have halide‐purification issues affecting their cost and aluminum corrosion during cycling at high voltages.[ 24 , 25 ] Recently, it has been shown that a mixture of functionalized non‐ionic polymers with a high ratio of Li‐salts, called “polymer‐in‐salt,” can enhance Li+‐anion interactions to enable stable Li0 deposition at room temperature.[ 26 , 27 , 28 ] These previous studies suggest that enabling weak Li+‐polymer interaction is a desirable strategy for efficient ion transport and improved electrochemical performance.

Unlike the mentioned solid polymer electrolytes, GPEs with high ionic conductivity of 10−5–10−3 S cm−1, which are based on a mixture of Li‐salt, polymer, and organic solvent, can promote weaker Li+‐polymer interaction by altering the solvation ability of both polymer and solvent. However, the impact of such changes on ion dynamics and interphase formation remains an unanswered question.

In this work, to explore the relationship between the solvation ability of polymer and solvent on Li+ transport and SEI formation in GPEs, various solvents with different solvation powers were investigated. 1H,1H,2H,2H‐Tridecafluoro‐n‐octyl acrylate (TFOA) and n‐octyl acrylate (OA) were selected as polymer components. Although they show similar structure, the Li+‐polymer binding energy of TFOA is lower than that of OA, as fluorination reduces the electron density, thereby decreasing solvation ability.[ 29 ] The donor number (DN) value of solvents is closely related to the Li+‐solvent binding energy;[ 30 ] therefore, solvents such as 1,2‐dimethoxyethane (DME, DN: 20), sulfolane (SL, DN: 14.8), and fluoroethylene carbonate (FEC, DN: 9.1) with different solvation abilities were used to prepare GPEs. Our findings reveal that the solvated strength of both polymer and solvent determines Li+ transport and interphase formation. In GPEs comprising weakly solvated solvent and strongly coordinating polymer, strong Li+‐polymer attachment hinders ion mobility (Figure 1a). Complete Li+ detachment from the polymer can be achieved by incorporating strongly solvated solvents. However, unlike in poly(ionic liquids) systems, complete Li+ detachment in non‐ionic polymer‐based GPEs leads to the formation of micelle structures that obstruct long‐range ion transport (Figure 1c). An optimal interaction is achieved by pairing a weakly solvated solvent with a weakly coordinating polymer, which facilitates partial Li+ detachment from the polymer and the formation of aggregates (AGGs) while avoiding micelle structure formation. This configuration ultimately enables fast ion hopping and stable interphase formation (Figure 1b). These findings provide molecular‐level design insights for further GPE development.

Schematic illustration of the influence of solvent and polymer solvation ability on ion transport in GPEs. a) Strong Li+‐polymer chain attachment in GPE containing weakly solvated solvent and strongly coordinating polymer, b) Partial Li+‐polymer detachment in GPE combining weakly solvating solvent and weakly coordinating polymer, c) Complete Li+‐polymer detachment driven by strongly solvated solvents, leading to micelle structures.

Results and Discussion

The Role of Solvation Power of Polymer on Ion Transport and Interphase

Firstly, FEC TFOA and FEC OA GPEs were prepared to explore the effects of polymer solvation ability on ion transport and SEI stability in GPEs. As an example, the FEC TFOA electrolyte was prepared by dissolving LiTFSI into a mixed solution of TFOA monomer, FEC, and crosslinker. The mixture was then heated at 70 °C to obtain the GPE. Solid state 1H NMR confirms the successful copolymerization,[ 31 ] Figure 2a. Figure S3 shows the Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of the mixed precursor solution before and after heating. The peak at 1640 cm−1, corresponding to the stretching vibration of the C═C bond, disappears after heating, further confirming the successful polymerization of GPE.[ 32 ] The ionic conductivity of the GPEs is influenced by the ratio of monomer, salt, and solvent. The molar ratio of LiTFSI to TFOA was set at 0.725:1, while the molar ratio of FEC to TFOA varied from 1.5:1 to 4.5:1. At a 4.5:1 ratio, the high FEC content increased the ionic conductivity to 3 mS cm−1 at room temperature but resulted in unsuccessful gelation, Figure S5. In contrast, at a high TFOA ratio, the gel with a 1.5:1 FEC to TFOA ratio showed a lower ionic conductivity of 0.0418 mS cm−1 at room temperature. Therefore, the ratio of 3:1 was identified as optimal, achieving successful polymerization and a comparable ionic conductivity of 0.29 mS cm−1 at room temperature. For comparison, the FEC OA gel was prepared using the same ratio, and it showed an ionic conductivity of 0.32 mS cm−1 at room temperature, Figure 2b. In terms of t Li+, the FEC TFOA gel exhibited a higher t Li+ of 0.55, compared to 0.44 for FEC OA gel. The higher t Li+ and lower ionic conductivity were also observed on the solvent‐free fluorinated polymer‐LiTFSI mixture.[ 33 ] The high t Li+ of FEC TFOA results in a higher Li+ conductivity of 0.1595 mS cm−1, compared to 0.1408 mS cm−1 for the FEC OA. The activation energy (E a) of bulk ionic conductivity was calculated using the Arrhenius equation,[ 34 ] Figure 2c. The FEC TFOA gel displays a lower E a of 19.7 KJ mol−1, compared to FEC OA, which has an E a of 21.1 KJ mol−1, Figures S8 and S9. The solvent content has a significant influence on the safety of GPE. As shown in Figure S10, when a high solvent ratio is used in FEC TFOA (4.5:1, FEC/TFOA, molar ratio), the precursor solution fails to obtain GPE, resulting in a flammable liquid. With reduced solvent content (3:1 and 1.5:1, FEC/TFOA, molar ratio), successful gelation occurs, and the resulting GPEs are non‐flammable. Even using different organic solvents, such as DME, the successful polymerization also contributes to nonflammable GPEs (Figure S10).

Chemical structure and experimental ion transport information for FEC‐based gel electrolytes. a) Preparation and solid‐state 1H NMR of FEC TFOA gel electrolyte, b) Li+ transference number (t Li+), and ionic conductivity of FEC TFOA and FEC OA gel electrolytes at room temperature, and c) activation energy (E a) of bulk ionic conductivity.

Although the prepared GPE can achieve free‐standing, the glass fiber was used as the substrate to prevent short circuits. Therefore, the in situ polymerization GPE on the glass fiber was employed for the electrochemical testing. The cycling stability of Li anode at FEC OA and FEC TFOA GPEs was evaluated by Li||Li symmetric cells at 0.5 mA cm−2 and 0.5 mAh cm−2 at room temperature, Figure 3a. The FEC OA gel shows a cycling stability of 400 h, while the FEC TFOA gel demonstrates a better cycling lifespan of 900 h and smaller overpotential. In Cu||Li half cells, Figure 3b,c, the cell with FEC OA reaches a low Coulombic efficiency (CE) of 80% at 0.25 mA cm−2, 0.5 mAh cm−2 at room temperature, and it decays rapidly. In contrast, the cell with FEC TFOA achieves a CE of 99.2% after the activation process. In critical current density (CCD) testing, short‐circuit occurs at 2 mA cm−2 in FEC OA gel, Figure S12. However, the cell with FEC TFOA shows no short‐circuiting up to a high current density of 3.5 mA cm−2, further confirming the superior stability of Li anode in FEC TFOA gel. In terms of morphology of cycled Li anode, the deposited Li0 is relatively dense in FEC OA gel with some non‐uniform nodule‐like structures, Figure 3d. In contrast, the cycled Li in the FEC TFOA gel shows denser, Figure 3e, more uniform, and chunky morphology, maintaining the integrity of the electrode surface for stable electrochemical reactions.

X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was applied to analyze SEI chemistry of cycled Li surface after 50 h cycling, Table S1 and Figures S13–S16. The atomic ratio shows a higher F content on the Li anode surface when using FEC TFOA gels, with value of 21.16% for FEC TFOA and 15.92% for FEC OA, Table S1. The SEI formed with FEC TFOA gels also exhibits higher N and S content, as well as lower C content, suggesting that more anions are reduced to generate SEI in FEC TFOA than in FEC OA. From the F 1s spectra, Figure S15, LiF (685 eV) and C─F (688.4 eV) are the main fluorinated species in the SEI formed with FEC OA,[ 35 ] while the SEI generated in FEC TFOA only contains LiF, Figure S13. Additionally, the SEI formed in FEC OA does not show Li2O (529 eV in O 1s) in the outer SEI; in contrast, a strong Li2O peak is observed in the SEI formed in FEC TFOA. The SEI from FEC OA has more Li─O (531 eV in O 1s), C─O (532 eV in O 1s), and SOx (534 eV in O 1s) species.[ 36 ] High contents of LiF and Li2O are also observed in the inner SEI from FEC TFOA during the etching, with the atomic ratio of F and O remaining high at 12.6% and 9.67%, after 90 s of sputtering (Figures S13 and S14). In contrast, the SEI of FEC OA gel shows a higher atomic ratio of C during etching compared to that of FEC TFOA gel. Furthermore, the atomic ratios of F and O for SEI from FEC OA gel are also lower during the sputtering process (7.84% of F and 3.79% of O after 90 s etching), with a Li2O signal detected only after 90 s etching (Figures S15 and S16). Time‐of‐flight secondary ion mass spectrometry (ToF‐SIMS) three‐dimensional ion maps also reveal a high abundance of F− (LiF), O2 − (Li2O), and a lower CF− (C─F) in the SEI from FEC TFOA throughout the depth profiling (Figures 3d,e and S17). In contrast, the SEI formed in FEC OA GPE exhibits a higher content of C2HO− and CF− (C─F), indicating a higher presence of organic species. The XPS and ToF‐SIMS results consistently confirm the inorganic‐rich SEI (LiF and Li2O) formation in FEC TFOA GPE, which effectively suppresses dendrite growth and achieves stable Li0 deposition with high CE.

Ion Transport Mechanism and Li+ Solvation Environment with Different Polymers

Raman spectroscopy was used to examine Li+‐anion interaction in different GPEs. For FEC TFOA gel, a higher proportion of AGG (21%) is observed, compared to 13% in the FEC OA system (Figure 4a).[ 37 ] After the fluorination, the solvation ability of TFOA polymer decreases, leading to weaker Li+‐polymer interactions and enhanced Li+‐anion interactions. The solid‐state 7Li NMR has confirmed the weaker Li+‐TFOA interaction. The 7Li NMR spectra of FEC OA and FEC TFOA are shown in Figure S18. Two peaks are observed, corresponding to different lithium environments: the peak at −0.55 ppm is attributed to LiTFSI‐polymer interaction, while the peak at −0.89 ppm represents LiTFSI‐FEC interaction.[ 32 , 38 , 39 , 40 ] Compared to FEC OA, the FEC TFOA system exhibits a much weaker peak at −0.55 ppm, indicating a lower ratio (11%) of Li+ coordinated by polymer, versus 62% in the FEC OA system. This result further confirms the weaker interaction between the Li+ and the TFOA polymers. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the Li+ coordination structure and ion transport mechanism in GPEs, classical molecular dynamics (MD) simulations and density functional theory (DFT) calculations were applied. The energy analysis of the MD trajectory confirms that the fluorination of the polymer reduces the interactions between the polymer and Li+, while enhancing the Li+‐solvent and Li+‐anion interactions in the TFOA system, Figure S20. DFT analysis indicated that the presence of fluorine in TFOA withdraw electrons from Li+—coordinating oxygen, which decreases Li+ desolvation energy in the TFOA system (−162.73 KJ mol−1) compared to OA (−215.91 KJ mol−1) (Figure S21). MD simulations reveal a higher proportion of AGGs in the FEC TFOA gel compared to the FEC OA gel. In contrast, a higher ratio of CIP is observed in FEC OA gels (Figure S22). The coordination of Li+ was quantitatively evaluated by radial distribution function (RDF) and coordination number (CN) analysis (Figures 4b–d and S23). The largest difference is observed in the CN for the Li+‐polymer coordination, especially the backbone, with the CNbackbone being 1.3 in the FEC OA gel, which is greater compared to 0.9 of CNbackbone in the FEC TFOA gel. The RDF result indicates there are weaker Li+‐polymer interactions in the TFOA system. The self‐diffusion coefficients of Li+ and TFSI− in FEC‐based GPEs, as well as the simulated t Li+, were calculated by MD. The individual self‐diffusion coefficients are lower in the FEC TFOA systems compared to that of the FEC OA gel electrolyte, Figure S24. However, the t Li+ calculated through the Nernst–Einstein (NE t Li+) approximation, assuming ionicity (H R −1 = 1), is higher when using the TFOA polymer. This trend is also consistent with the t Li+ obtained experimentally by the potenstiostatic polarization method (Figure 2b).

RDF and CN analysis for Li+ to oxygens of b) Li+─Obackbone, c) Li+─OFEC, and d) Li+─OTFSI−. e) Percentage of Li+ to oxygens of different electrolyte species by classical MD. f) The contact time of various interactions between Li+ and the oxygen of electrolyte species.

To further understand the Li+ transport mechanism within different Li+‐polymer interactions, the contact time of Li+ around the oxygen of the anion, polymer, and solvent, as well as the percentage of different Li+‐oxygen contacts for both FEC‐based GPEs, was calculated based on MD trajectory data (Figure 4e). The FEC OA gel shows a higher percentage of Li+─Omacromolecules contacts (27.6%) compared to 22.8% of the FEC TFOA gel. The FEC TFOA gel shows a shorter Li+─Omacromolecules contact time, with a total of 82.34 ns compared to 138.1 ns for FEC OA gel, Figure 4f. Notably, in the FEC TFOA gel, the weak solvation ability weakens the interaction of Li+ with the polymer, promoting the formation of AGGs. This is because the fluorinated functional group of TFOA has an electron‐withdrawing effect on the Li+ coordinating oxygen of the polymer (Figure S21). It forces Li+ to seek alternative coordinating centers—the oxygens of FEC and TFSI− anions. As a result, more AGGs are formed in FEC TFOA. These AGGs, which show lower binding energies of −151.126 KJ mol−1 (−183.932 KJ mol−1 for CIP (type 1) and −168.776 KJ mol−1 for CIP (type 2), Figure S25), make it easier for Li+ to hop within the AGGs complex, facilitating ion transport. Therefore, FEC TFOA gel, which contains more AGGs, also shows a shorter Li+─OTFSI contact time. As a result, FEC TFOA gel shows a shorter total contact time of Li+ with coordinating species, illustrating a high Li+ hopping frequency between neighboring coordination sites. This enhanced Li+ hopping further explains the higher t Li+ and lower E a in FEC TFOA gel. Thus, the fluorinated polymer with decreased solvating ability weakens Li+‐polymer interactions, promoting Li+ detachment from polymer and enabling faster Li+ transport. This reduced solvation ability of the polymer also enhances Li+‐anion interactions for the formation of stable and efficient SEI.

The Role of Solvation Power of Solvent on Ion Transport and Interphase

Based on the above discussion, weak Li+‐polymer interactions are beneficial for Li+ transport. However, in FEC TFOA gel, some Li+‐polymer interaction remains. What would happen if the Li+‐polymer interaction were further reduced? To investigate it, other solvents such as DME and SL, with higher DN value and stronger Li+ solvation abilities, were used to modify Li+ solvation in GPEs by further weakening Li+‐polymer interactions.

For DME‐based GPEs, the Li0/+ galvanostatic plating/stripping is unstable, with a short‐circuit occurring after 100 h (Figure 5a). Interestingly, the LiTFSI DME liquid electrolyte with a comparable salt concentration shows high stability against the Li anode[ 41 , 42 ] (Figure S26); however, when DME is used as GPEs with OA or TFOA, it exhibits poor cycling stability. For SL‐based gels, the cycling is also unstable, lasting only 30 h for SL OA gel and 80 h for SL TFOA gel in Li||Li cells. This also contrasts with LiTFSI SL liquid electrolyte, showing more stable cycling (Figure S26). The morphology of the cycled Li anode also supports these findings. The Li0 deposition in DME‐based gel is non‐uniform, with large cracks, corresponding to its unstable cycling stability, Figure S27. In SL‐based gel, the Li0 deposition has a nodule‐like structure and small particles. This enhances the contact area between the electrolyte and the Li surface, leading to severe side reactions. The uneven and loose Li deposition also suggests insufficient cycling stability ofthe Li anode with DME‐ and SL‐based GPEs.

In contrast to FEC‐based gels, the XPS results for the DME‐ and SL‐based gels show a lower F content on the Li anode surface. Specifically, the relative F contents are 11.33% for SL TFOA, 4.19% for SL OA, 7.06% for DME TFOA, and 2.48% for DME OA (Table S3). The F 1s spectra shows a weaker LiF peak, and the O 1s spectra exhibits no Li2O peak in the SEI formed with DME‐based gels (Figure 5b,c), suggesting a lower content of inorganic compounds in the SEI formed with these GPEs. This also correlates with the increased content of C─C (284.8 eV in C 1s), C═O (287.8 eV in C 1s), and C─F (291.18 eV in C 1s) in the SEI of DME‐based gels, further confirming the high ratio of organic species[ 43 ] (Figure S28). Although the use of TFOA polymer provides additional F resource for the SEI of DME TFOA, the stable ─CF2 and ─CF3 functional groups in TFOA are difficult to completely reduce.[ 44 ] This leads to a higher ratio of organic C─F species in the SEI of DME TFOA gel. In O 1s spectra, the organic Li─O and C─O are primary species in the SEI of the DME‐based gel. Considering results for F 1s, O 1s, and C 1s spectra, it is fair to say that an organic‐rich SEI is generated during the Li anode cycling with DME‐based GPEs.[ 45 ] In SL‐based gels, the presence of SOx, possibly resulting from the reduction of SL solvent, suggests the presence of solvent‐derived SEI.[ 46 ] The lower presence of inorganic species (LiF and Li2O) in the SEI of SL‐based gels explains the fast occurring short‐circuit in symmetric cells (Figure 5a).

Ion Transport Mechanisms and Li+ Solvation Environment with Different Solvents and Polymers

The DME OA gel exhibits the highest ionic conductivity of 0.35 mS cm−1 compared to SL‐ and FEC‐based gels (Figure 5d). The ionic conductivity of DME TFOA, SL OA, and SL TFOA are 0.29, 0.2, and 0.1 mS cm−1, respectively. The t Li+ decreases when using a solvent with a higher DN value, for example, 0.365 in DME OA, and 0.41 in DME TFOA, 0.425 in SL OA, 0.445 in SL TFOA. This trend is also confirmed by MD simulation results, showing that higher DN of solvents in GPEs leads to a lower NE t Li+ (Figure S24). For the SL‐based gels (Figure 5e), the E a values are 22.97 KJ mol−1 for SL TFOA and 23.7 KJ mol−1 for SL OA gels, respectively. The DME‐based gels exhibit higher E a values, with 27.18 KJ mol−1 for DME TFOA gel and 27.9 KJ mol−1 for DME OA gel. Compared to FEC‐based gels, the stronger solvation ability of DME and SL solvents used in GPEs deteriorates Li+ transport.

Among all gels, the DME‐based gels show the lowest Raman shift at 737 cm−1. This is attributed to the stronger solvation ability of DME (Figure 5f), which results in complete dissociation of Li‐salts, higher ionic conductivity, and a higher solvent separated ion pairs (SIPs) ratio. For SL‐based gels, the main Raman peak is observed within the range of CIPs and SIPs.[ 37 ] According to MD simulations, DME‐based gels also have a higher ratio of SIPs (Figure S22). The RDF analysis reveals weaker interactions between Li+ and the polymer backbone when using a solvent with a high DN value, with CNbackbone values of ∼0.1 for DME TFOA and ∼0.2 for DME OA GPE (Figure S32). The CNbackbone values are 0.25 and 0.4 observed in SL TFOA and SL OA gel, respectively (Figure S31).

The contact time of anion, polymer, and solvent around Li+, as well as the percentage of different Li+‐oxygen contacts for DME‐ and SL‐based GPEs, were also analyzed in Figure 5g,h. Due to the strong solvation ability of DME, the Li+ in both DME OA and DME TFOA gels has minimal contact with the macromolecules (0% contact between Li+ and polymer in DME OA, 1.9% contact between Li+ and polymer in DME TFOA). This results in short Li+─Omacromolecules contact time. The SL TFOA gel exhibits a longer Li+─Omacromolecules contact time of 139.48 ns compared to the FEC TFOA gel of 82.34 ns, despite having fewer contacts between Li+ and macromolecules (7.8% contact between Li+ and macromolecules in SL TFOA, 22.8% contact of FEC TFOA gel).

Regarding the interactions with solvent, the Li+─Osolvent contact time increases in the following order: FEC<SL<DME. The long total Li+─Osolvent contact time and short Li+─OTFSI− contact time suggest a vehicular Li+ transport in DME‐ and SL‐based gel electrolytes. Screenshots from MD simulations show that, in gels containing FEC, Li+ is coordinated by both polymer and solvent, with all coordinated species evenly distributed (Figures S33 and S34). The OA polymer and FEC solvent place Li+ in the polymer cages, leading to the formation of CIPs. In the case of TFOA polymer, increased AGGs improves t Li+. The gel with the strongly coordinating solvent, for example, DME TFOA, displays the almost complete Li+‐polymer detachment, forming micelles with coagulated TFOA polymer inside and a low molecular weight phase of Li(DME)(TFSI) outside (Figure 5i). This micelle structure increases the ion transfer energy barrier, slowing the ion transport to the electrode surface and thereby reducing mass transport efficiency. Therefore, in the strongly coordinated solvent‐based gels (such as DME‐ and SL‐based), the slow Li+ transport toward the anode resulting from the micelle formation and the sluggish Li+ desolvation during the charge transfer are contributing to the sluggish diffusion kinetics of these gels. On the other hand, to explore the effect of solvents with lower DN values than FEC, we introduced solvents, such as nitrobenzene (NB, DN: 8.1) and 1,1,2,2‐tetrafluoroethyl 2,2,3,3‐tetrafluoropropylether (TTE, DN: 1.9). As shown in Figure S35, GPEs can also be obtained using the NB and TTE. However, their ionic conductivity at room temperature is significantly lower than FEC system (0.29 mS cm−1 for FEC TFOA, 0.009 mS cm−1 for TTE TFOA, and 0.01 mS cm−1 for NB TFOA). This is attributed to the non‐solvating nature of NB and TTE,[ 47 ] which are unable to compete for Li+ solvation, causing Li+ to remain strongly coordinated to the polymer with limited detachment. As a result, the ion transport relies on polymer chain motion, thereby leading to low ionic conductivity. Combined ethyl difluoroacetate (EDFA), which has a similar DN to FEC, in GPEs results in stable Li symmetric coin cell cycling, with the EDFA TFOA system showing a lower overpotential (Figure S36). These results further confirm that, for the gel electrolytes, balanced interactions between Li‐salt, polymer, and solvent are needed to achieve partial Li+ detachment from the polymer while avoiding the formation of micelles, thereby enabling efficient Li+ transport.

Performance of LFP and NMC811||Li Full Cells with Balanced Solvation Ability of Polymer and Solvent

To further evaluate the electrochemical performance of GPEs with balanced solvation ability of polymer and solvent, full cells with FEC OA and FEC TFOA electrolytes were assembled and tested. The LiFePO4 (LFP)||Li with FEC TFOA electrolyte delivers an initial capacity of 143.5 mAh g−1 and maintains 141.7 mAh g−1 at 0.5 C, with a high average CE of 99.44% after 700 cycles, Figure 6a. In contrast, the LFP||Li full cell using FEC OA gel experiences rapid capacity decay (116.8 mAh g−1 after 300 cycles). This degradation is attributed to lower CE with FEC OA electrolyte, exacerbating side reactions during cycling, further increasing interface resistance, and accelerating battery failure (Figure S38). The anodic stability for FEC TFOA gel is above 5 V (versus Li/Li+), Figure S39, suggesting this GPE is suitable for a high‐voltage cathode (LiNi0.8Mn0.1Co0.1O2, NMC811). Figure 6b shows the galvanostatic charge/discharge curves of the NMC811||Li full cell using FEC TFOA gel with a high cut‐off voltage of 4.5 V (versus Li/Li+). The FEC TFOA enables the NMC811||Li cell to achieve high‐capacity retention of 81% (145.9 mAh g−1 after 140 cycles) and a high average CE of 99.42%, Figure 6c. Although the FEC OA delivers a high initial discharge capacity of 184.4 mAh g−1 at 0.2 C, it suffers from rapid capacity fading, retaining 94.2 mAh g−1 after 130 cycles. The improved Li+ transport in the FEC TFOA gel electrolyte contributes to higher capacities at a higher rate in Figure 6d. The NMC811||Li using FEC TFOA delivers 134.2 and 84.5 mAh g−1 at 1 and 2 C, respectively, compared to only 78.3 and 40.3 mAh g−1 with the FEC OA gel. Upon returning to 0.2 C, cells with FEC TFOA maintain a high capacity of 180.8 mAh g−1, whereas cells using FEC OA gel show a low capacity of 65.7 mAh g−1, highlighting the superior cycling reversibility of FEC TFOA electrolyte. To verify the scalability, an NMC811||Li‐pouch cell was assembled with a high area capacity of 3 mAh cm−2 and N/P ratio (the capacity ratio of the negative electrode and positive electrode) of 3.5 using FEC TFOA. This pouch cell shows a total capacity of 95 mAh and a high initial specific capacity of 191.2 mAh g−1, maintaining a higher specific capacity of 160.7 mAh g−1 and a high average CE of 99.8% after 50 cycles, demonstrating a great application potential of FEC TFOA gel in LMBs, Figure 6e.

To explore the origin of improvement in the high‐voltage cathode, a float test was used to assess the side reaction between NMC811 cathode and electrolytes. After maintaining at 4.5 V (versus, Li/Li+) for 20 h, the leakage current of the NMC811||Li cell with the FEC TFOA electrolyte is 0.001 mA, while a higher leakage current of 0.011 mA is observed in cell using FEC OA electrolyte in Figure 6f, demonstrating that fewer side reactions occur on the cathode surface when using FEC TFOA electrolyte.[ 48 ] XPS analysis of the cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) formed in the FEC OA and FEC TFOA reveals the presence of C─C, C─O, and C═O species[ 49 ](Figure 6g). In F 1s, the CF2CH2 (688.9 eV) peak corresponds to the polyvinylidene fluoride binder in the cathodes.[ 50 ] However, there is higher LiF content in the CEI formed in the FEC TFOA electrolyte, indicating the formation of a LiF‐rich CEI in the FEC TFOA electrolyte. These results prove that the FEC TFOA electrolyte can generate a robust LiF‐rich CEI, which prevents contact between the cathode and electrolyte, thereby suppressing side reactions on the cathode side and improving performance at high voltage. Current collector corrosion compromises the integrity of electrodes, influencing electron transfer and cycling stability.[ 51 ] A floating test was also performed to assess the Al corrosion in FEC‐based gel electrolytes, Figure S40. Compared to liquid electrolyte, the FEC TFOA gel electrolyte exhibits a much lower leakage current, suggesting its effectiveness in preventing Al corrosion.

Conclusion

Here, we examined the impact of Li+ solvation ability of both solvent and polymer in GPEs on the Li+ coordination structure, ion transport, and lithium anode cycling performance. We found that in GPE containing a weakly coordinated solvent, stronger Li+‐polymer interactions deteriorate Li+ transport, whereas weaker Li+‐polymer interactions facilitate Li+ detachment from the polymer, forming AGGs that facilitate ion hopping and efficient SEI formation. Although using high DN solvent can achieve complete Li+ detachment, this is not ideal, as it results in the formation of micelles, which obstruct Li+ transport and prevent sufficient Li+ supply to the interface. Therefore, achieving a balanced interaction between Li+, solvent, and non‐ionic polymer is important for efficient Li+ transport and stable SEI formation in GPEs. As a result, the FEC TFOA gel, with reduced solvation ability of both solvent and polymer, facilitates partial Li+‐polymer detachment and AGG formation, preventing the formation of micelle structure, and enabling efficient Li+ transport. This leads to the highest t Li+ of 0.55 among examined GPEs and promotes the formation of an inorganic‐rich SEI (LiF and Li2O). The FEC TFOA gel electrolyte demonstrates stable performance over 900 cycles at 0.5 mA cm−2, 0.5 mAh cm−2 in Li||Li coin cells, and achieves stable Li deposition with 99.2% CE in Cu||Li half cell at room temperature. Solid‐state LFP||Li using this electrolyte achieves high‐capacity retention of 93.3% after 700 cycles at room temperature. The FEC TFOA gel electrolyte also forms a LiF‐rich CEI, allowing NMC811||Li batteries to cycle well with 99.8% CE at a cut‐off voltage of 4.5 V. This work provides a design strategy for GPEs via the regulation of Li+ coordination environment.
