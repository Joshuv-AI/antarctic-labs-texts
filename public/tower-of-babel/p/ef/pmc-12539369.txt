Virtual Versus In‐Person Interviews: The Impact of Perceived Success on the Applicant Experience
Authors: Sacha Moufarrej, Andrew Yousef, Deborah Watson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo comparatively assess otolaryngology residency applicant perspectives on virtual versus in‐person interviews and identify associations between perceived application success and interview preferences during the 2023–2024 application cycle using a cross‐sectional survey‐based study from nationwide otolaryngology residency applicants to a single institution.MethodsApplicants to an otolaryngology residency program completed a 36‐item survey comparing their experience participating in virtual and in‐person interviews. Demographics, medical school performance, and application cycle progress data were collected and analyzed for associations between application success and interview preferences. Participants' overall opinion of virtual interviews was calculated by summing their Likert‐scale responses, with higher scores (maximum 30) indicating a greater preference for the virtual interview.ResultsA total of 81 applicants, 30% of those invited, completed the survey. An average of 41.1% (SD 18.6%) of respondents' total interviews were in‐person. Opinions on the virtual interview were mixed (mean total score of 15.2 (SD 5.2)). Despite only 25.6% preferring virtual interviews, 62.9% supported their continued availability. Those with a higher interview yield were more likely to have a more positive opinion of virtual interviews (OR 1.05, p = 0.0002).ConclusionWhile applicants had mixed sentiments regarding the virtual interview, most indicated that the option for virtual interviews should continue to be available for otolaryngology programs. Participants with a higher interview yield preferred the virtual interview, suggesting applicants' preferences are partly informed by their overall feelings of success during the application cycle.Level of Evidence3.

Introduction

The COVID‐19 pandemic brought about significant changes in the residency application process in general, and in applicant interviews in particular. While residency interviews traditionally required applicants to travel to the program, attend social receptions, and formally interview with faculty and resident evaluators, the COVID‐19 pandemic necessitated an abrupt transition to virtual interviewing [1]. Now, with the removal of lockdowns and social distancing requirements, residency programs have been mixed regarding a decision to return to in‐person interviews or continue with virtual interviewing. Cited benefits of virtual interviewing include flexibility in scheduling interviews, decreased carbon footprint related to travel [2], and lower costs for all stakeholders [1, 3]. Given the reduced cost of virtual interviews, it was hoped that this option would lead to a more equitable application process, alleviating financial disparities that favor students with higher socioeconomic status [4]. However, concerns about the efficacy of virtual interviewing have arisen, with some studies indicating concern for an increase rather than a decrease in bias against underrepresented groups [4], and a decreased ability for both programs and applicants to adequately assess interpersonal fit [5].

Residency programs nationwide conducted studies to assess program and applicant perspectives on the virtual interview during the COVID‐19 pandemic [6, 7]. Within otolaryngology residency programs specifically, there have been mixed reviews of virtual interviewing when analyzing this interview approach alone. While some applicants considered otolaryngology programs to be among the most unsuitable for virtual interviews [7], others considered virtual interviews to be more inclusive and equitable than in‐person interviews given the higher‐than‐average cost of interviewing in otolaryngology compared to other specialties [8], and many were satisfied with the virtual interview process [9].

The 2023–2024 application cycle provided a unique landscape for comparing virtual interviewing to in‐person interviewing, as it was the first year with a relatively even split between the two interview approaches across otolaryngology programs, as well as the first year that otolaryngology was a “large” signaling specialty, allowing applicants to signal to 25 programs. As otolaryngology programs continue to evaluate whether they will proceed with virtual interviews or return to in‐person interviews, studies of applicant perspectives from the 2023–2024 cycle, in which applicants experienced both interview formats concurrently, are critical to identifying the comparative benefits and disadvantages of each approach. While studies from earlier applicant cohorts have described applicants' opinions on virtual interviews [9], or have assessed the association between demographic factors and opinions on virtual versus in‐person interviews [8], further analysis is needed to provide a comparative assessment between the two interview formats. In addition, an unbroached factor that may influence applicants' perspectives on the interview process is their objective and perceived success, which can be quantified by the number of interview invitations received, or total interview yield when determined based on the number of programs applied to [10]. The aforementioned studies have not accounted for this potential association of interview yield with applicant perspectives on current interview practices. Thus, the primary aim of this study is to provide a direct comparative analysis of the applicant experience with virtual versus in‐person interviewing using a cross‐sectional survey, with its targeted focus on application success. A second aim of this study is to provide a holistic assessment of other applicant characteristics and how this might impact their interview format preference.

Methods

We conducted a cross‐sectional anonymous survey‐based study of otolaryngology residency applicants during the 2023–2024 application cycle to understand applicant opinions regarding the virtual interview. This study received approval for exemption status from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at our university.

Survey Design

The original survey was a 36‐item questionnaire that was adapted from similar studies conducted across other specialties [11, 12]. The survey was split into three sections (Appendix A). The first part of this survey assessed applicant information, which included demographics and medical school performance measures. The second section assessed factors in signaling decision‐making. The results from this section have been previously published [13]. The last section evaluated applicant opinions regarding virtual interviews on a 5‐point Likert Scale.

Data Collection

All applicants to our institution who had received interview offers to at least one otolaryngology residency program (not exclusive to our program) were invited to participate in this study. The total number of invited applicants was 274, which represented 53.4% of the total otolaryngology applicant pool for the 2023–2024 cycle (n = 513). Applicants were required to have completed at least one in‐person and one virtual interview to be eligible for participation. Participants submitted their informed consent and surveys through our institution's REDCap online data capture tool [14]. The survey was sent out after all interviews had been completed, but before rank lists were due. This timing was selected to mitigate recall bias—as participants would have recently completed their interviews—while minimizing nonresponse due to match outcome. Email reminders were sent out to all applicants at one‐week intervals for a total of four reminders as needed. As described in our prior study, all participant data was anonymous and confidential; there was no incentive related to the application process for participating [13].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics such as means, medians, and frequencies were computed as appropriate to assess the distribution of demographic characteristics, medical school performance measures, and opinions on virtual interviews.

Participants were asked six Likert‐scaled questions to gauge their opinion on virtual interviews. Their responses to the six questions were summed to provide a total virtual interview opinion score, with a maximum score of 30—a higher score indicating greater preference for virtual interviews. Associations between applicant characteristics, medical school performance, and overall opinion of virtual interviews were analyzed using univariate ordered logistic regressions with statistical significance set at p < 0.05. A multivariable logistic regression was used to assess the association between total interview yield and applicant opinions regarding virtual interviews. Variables were included in the multivariable model if they were significantly associated (p < 0.05) with overall virtual interview opinions on univariate analysis. All analyses were conducted using Stata v18.

Results

Participant Demographics

A total of 81 out of 274 individuals (29.6%) invited to partake in this study completed the survey, and 77 were included in the final analysis, as 4 did not provide enough responses to be included. The average age of participants was 28 (SD 4.6), and 50% were female (Table 1). The majority of participants identified as White (n = 44, 56.4%), followed by Asian (n = 28, 35.9%), and Black/African American (n = 8, 10.3%) (Table 1). The largest proportion of participants indicated West as their home region (n = 38, 49.4%), followed by South (n = 13, 16.9%), Northeast (n = 12, 15.6%), and Midwest (n = 11, 14.3%). There was a more even geographic distribution of participants' medical schools: 22 (28.6%) attended schools in the Midwest, 20 (26.0%) in the West, 16 (20.8%) in the Northeast, and 14 (18.2%) in the South. Only two participants were re‐applicants.

Applications and Interview Yield

Respondents applied to an average of 63.6 programs (SD 25.8), with a mean of 14.1 interview invitations (SD 6.4), yielding an average interview yield of 25.9% (SD 15.2%), as calculated for each individual participant (Table 1). Those who completed away rotations had an interview yield at their away rotation institutions of 81.5% (SD 29.1%). An average of 41.1% (SD 18.6%) of respondents' interviews were in‐person.

Participant Perspectives on Virtual Interviews

The mean total score of virtual interview opinions was 15.2 (SD 5.2) out of 30, indicating mixed opinions from many participants. The results of the individual questions indicated that only 29.5% of participants either strongly or slightly agreed that they could adequately assess program fit through the virtual interview, and only 15.4% of participants strongly or slightly agreed that they were more likely to apply to a program if it offered a virtual interview (Figure 1). A majority of participants (73.1%) agreed that in‐person interviews were more likely to positively change their ranking of a program. Additionally, only 28.2% of participants indicated that they were more likely to drop an interview if it was in‐person. Overall opinions on the continuation of virtual interviewing were mixed: while only 25.6% preferred virtual interviews over in‐person interviews, 62.9% agreed that virtual interviews should continue being offered in the future.

Applicant Characteristics and Perspectives on the Virtual Interview

On univariate analysis of application‐related factors and perspectives on the virtual interview, a higher virtual interview opinion score was significantly associated with higher total interview yield (OR 1.05, p = 0.0002) (Table 2). On the other hand, a higher virtual interview opinion score was significantly associated—but with minimal effect size—with a lower number of programs applied to (OR 0.98, p = 0.0024), as well as a lower proportion of in‐person interviews (OR 0.96, p = 0.0003). On multivariable analysis conducted to account for the number of programs applied to and the proportion of in‐person interviews, total interview yield remained significantly associated with a higher virtual interview opinion score (OR 1.06, p = 0.009) (Table 3). The E‐value for this odds ratio point estimate is 1.31 [15], and the E‐value for the lower limit of the confidence interval is 1.11.

Discussion

This study seeks to investigate the applicant perspective regarding the option for virtual interviewing and to provide a direct comparative analysis of the applicant experience with virtual versus in‐person interviews. It addresses several gaps in the existing literature stemming from older studies with limited statistical evaluation and a paucity of work investigating the impact of participants' application success, namely their interview yield, on their perception of the virtual versus in‐person interview. This study is also the first of its kind in providing a timely, direct assessment of opinions regarding the virtual interview in the first cohort of otolaryngology applicants who completed a nearly even mix of virtual and in‐person interviews and experienced the initiation of the large‐signal approach to preference signaling, both of which were introduced into the 2023–2024 application cycle. Given that over half of all otolaryngology applicants were invited to participate in this study, and that our sample's application characteristics showed no significant difference from this cycle's aggregate NRMP data, our findings appear to be representative of the total applicant pool.

In this analysis, participant responses indicated a largely mixed opinion of virtual interviews. Notably, only 1/4 preferred virtual interviews over in‐person interviews. Despite this, 2/3 agreed that virtual interviews should continue being offered in the future, indicating that while virtual interviews were not preferred, most applicants continued to want the option available. Lastly, this study found that higher interview yield was associated with a higher preference for virtual interviews, a finding not previously described.

The overall mixed sentiment towards virtual interviews seen in our study is consistent with similar quantitative findings within other specialties [16, 17, 18]. The heterogeneous survey results corroborate the anecdotal perspective that virtual interviews have a mix of advantages, especially related to decreased costs [18], and also disadvantages, such as decreased interpersonal connection [6]. Our study expands on this assessment of virtual interviews by allowing for comparative assessment of perspectives on virtual versus in‐person interviews. In previous studies, participants were asked about their opinions on virtual interviews when that was the only approach offered due to COVID‐19 social distancing requirements. Our study cohort had experience with both virtual and in‐person interviews, providing a more reliable evaluation of the comparative advantages and disadvantages of the two prominent residency interview approaches.

Our main finding that interview yield is associated with a higher opinion of virtual interviews is novel and not previously described in the literature. While we are unable to delineate the exact reason for this association, we hypothesize that it may be rooted in the virtual interview allowing for more cost savings and improved flexibility, as applicants do not have to decide between interview invitations based on available dates, duration of flights, and costs of travel as much as was required when only traditional in‐person interviews were offered. However, the advent of the virtual interview option precipitated concerns that the virtual interview would lead to a smaller pool of applicants completing the majority of interviews [9]. Our study potentially corroborates these prior concerns, as participants with a higher yield of interview invites had a significantly stronger preference for the flexibility of the virtual interview. This finding is important because it suggests that applicant preferences for virtual interviews are not merely based on modality, but are shaped by perceived success and opportunity within the process. While potential solutions have been proposed to limit the number of interview invites per applicant, such as the implementation of signaling, increasing the cost of applications [19] and placing a cap on interview invites [9], further research is needed to determine the effect of these interventions.

Though studies have shown that signaling, for example, demonstrates the potential to increase the likelihood of interview invitations for most applicants to otolaryngology and to mitigate over‐application [20], there continues to be a concern for skewed interview invitation distribution within many competitive specialties such as otolaryngology, in which highly qualified applicants receive and accept more interview spots than are required to match. This phenomenon may be exacerbated by the accessibility of virtual interviewing [21]. To our knowledge, no data exist on whether increasing preference signals from 5 to 25 improved interview distribution, since early AAMC findings from the initial institution of signaling showed 10% of programs received 20% of signals [22]. Further evaluation is needed to determine whether there is potential for a synergistic exacerbation of interview invitation disparities due to virtual interviewing and the expansion of signaling.

Our post hoc analysis revealed a higher preference for virtual interviews from non‐White applicants, consistent with the literature [8]. We similarly hypothesize that the cost of in‐person interviewing is a key contributing factor to this preference. Disparities in the accessibility of opportunities for non‐White applicants continue to persist, especially in regards to research experiences and away rotations during medical school, and in the high cost of in‐person interviews during the residency match process [23]. This has impacted the diversity of otolaryngology residencies, with African American students continuously underrepresented among residents though equally represented in otolaryngology applicants, and Asian American and Hispanic physicians underrepresented in otolaryngology faculty [24]. The virtual interview may improve non‐White applicants' ability to apply to more programs and receive more invitations for interviews, thereby increasing the chance of closing the diversity gap within the field of otolaryngology. However, the aforementioned potential for continued disparities in interview invitation distribution due to the synergistic effects of virtual interviewing and signaling coincides with preliminary findings from our previously published study on this same cohort, which showed that 37% (n = 29) of surveyed applicants did not feel that signaling was equitable [13]. Of the 14 applicants who strongly or slightly disagreed that signaling was equitable, 12 (85.7%) were non‐White, implying the potential persistence of racial and ethnic disparities in the application process. This is a concern that warrants further evaluation, as the combination of signaling and interview changes may be having unforeseen limitations on the improvement of application equity for URiM applicants, though their modest support for virtual interviews and signaling has been documented in our study. Potential solutions, such as increased outreach, mentorship, and application guidance from otolaryngology residency programs directed towards applicants who may face a lower interview yield, should be developed and assessed.

Our study limitations are inherent to the retrospective nature of our study design. First, there is a potential risk of recall bias, as participants had completed the majority of their interviews at the time of survey completion. However, the recency of participants' interviews may mitigate this recall bias, and their unique perspective as the first otolaryngology cohort undergoing a true mix of both in‐person and virtual interviews continues to provide valuable insights into the complexity of opinions on residency interviews. Moreover, by administering our survey between completion of all interviews and the rank list submission deadline, we sought to minimize both recall bias and selection bias based on applicants' willingness to participate. Secondly, the participants of this study were all applicants to a single institution's otolaryngology residency program, which may also introduce selection bias and strategic bias, as some applicants may have been concerned about participating or indicating strong preferences despite the anonymous nature of the survey. However, our sample size, the wide distribution of participants' demographic characteristics—including gender, home and medical school geographic location, and race—and their consistency with the NRMP data for all otolaryngology applicants mitigate the risk of these biases. Additionally, since our survey targeted all applicants to the program regardless of single‐institution interview offer status, our sample appears to be representative of a broad range of interview experiences across multiple programs. As with all observational studies, residual or unmeasured confounding remains possible. To evaluate robustness, we calculated an E‐value of 1.31, meaning an unmeasured confounder would need to be associated with both interview yield and virtual interview preference by a risk ratio of at least this magnitude to explain away the observed associations between interview yield and preference for the virtual interview. While such confounding cannot be excluded, we adjusted for key known covariates, making the presence of a single unmeasured factor of this strength less likely.

Conclusion

As programs decide whether to make the complete switch back to in‐person interviews, this study provides a unique perspective by presenting a direct comparison of the two interview approaches in applicants who completed a mix of in‐person and virtual interviews at the time when signaling options increased. While applicants have a largely mixed opinion regarding the virtual interview, the majority of applicants felt that virtual interviews should continue to be an option. Participants with a higher interview yield had a strong preference for the virtual interview, which may indicate that the overall feelings of success during the application process appear to have a role in applicants' preferences.
