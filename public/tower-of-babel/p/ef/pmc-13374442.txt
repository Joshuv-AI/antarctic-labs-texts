Effects of whole-body vibration therapy on motor function, muscle tone, balance, walking endurance and quality of life in children with cerebral palsy: study protocol for a randomised controlled trial

Introduction

Cerebral palsy (CP) is a neurodevelopmental disorder that results in permanent motor disabilities, primarily occurring in childhood.1 2 Individuals with CP commonly present with abnormal muscle tone, impaired coordination, postural abnormalities and limitations in motor function, often accompanied by sensory deficits and secondary musculoskeletal complications.3 These impairments may substantially restrict daily activities and participation, thereby reducing quality of life (QoL).4

Rehabilitation interventions for children with CP, including physical therapy, occupational therapy and speech therapy, play an important role in improving motor function and QoL.5 6 However, conventional rehabilitation methods may have limitations in certain cases, necessitating exploration of new rehabilitation approaches to enhance efficacy.7 Whole-body vibration therapy (WBVT), initially developed for neurological disorders, has gained increasing attention in paediatric rehabilitation. Previous studies have reported improvements in muscle tone, gross motor function, balance, walking ability, lower limb muscle strength and joint range of motion (ROM) in children with CP.8,10 Nevertheless, the current evidence remains limited by several methodological concerns. First, many previous studies included relatively small sample sizes, typically ranging from 10 to 30 participants, which may have limited statistical power and reduced the generalisability of the findings.11 12 Second, substantial heterogeneity exists across WBVT protocols. Variations in vibration frequency, amplitude, body position, session duration, intervention intensity, treatment period and device settings were identified across studies, making direct comparisons difficult and highlighting the lack of standardised WBVT protocols for children with CP.13 Third, participant characteristics differed considerably among studies, particularly regarding CP subtype, age range and Gross Motor Function Classification System (GMFCS), which may have contributed to inconsistent findings. In addition, outcome measures were not standardised across studies, and some studies lacked sufficient reporting of intervention parameters and participant functional classification.13 Therefore, further well-designed randomised controlled trials with larger sample sizes and standardised intervention protocols are still needed to further clarify the efficacy of WBVT in children with CP.

Rehabilitation for children with CP typically relies on multidisciplinary approaches, including physical therapy, occupational therapy, orthotic support and task-oriented functional training.7 However, the integration of WBVT within multidisciplinary rehabilitation frameworks remains underexplored. In particular, the effects of WBVT on lower limb function, optimal vibration parameters and long-term outcomes among different CP subtypes (eg, spastic bilateral, hemiplegic and ataxic CP) are not yet clearly defined. Furthermore, current clinical rehabilitation guidelines for CP do not include WBVT.14

Therefore, this study aims to evaluate the effects of WBVT with standardised intervention parameters, including vibration frequency, body position, training frequency and a 12-week intervention duration, on motor function, muscle tone, balance, walking endurance and QoL in children with CP. The findings are expected to provide clinical evidence and practical guidance for optimising rehabilitation strategies for children with CP.

Methods and analysis

Research design

This study is designed as a single-centre, two-arm randomised controlled clinical trial. The study follows the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for clinical trials and meets the recommended criteria outlined in the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Intervention Trials (SPIRIT) checklist. A total of 40 participants will be consecutively enrolled and randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the experimental group (WBVT+conventional therapy) or the control group (conventional therapy only). All participants will receive therapy three times per week for 12 weeks. Each session includes 30 min of occupational therapy and 30 min of physical therapy. In addition, the experimental group will receive 18 min of WBVT after the conventional treatment.

Prior to enrolment, participants will be informed of the potential benefits and risks of the study interventions. Thereafter, they will be asked to sign the informed consent form and may withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.

To maximise adherence to the intervention protocol, therapists will record attendance at each session. Parents and caregivers will be encouraged to support the child’s participation, and reminders will be provided before each session. Any missed sessions will be documented, and efforts will be made to reschedule them whenever feasible. During the trial, concomitant care unrelated to the study interventions will be permitted as usual, while any additional experimental therapies will be prohibited to avoid confounding effects.

Participants: recruitment and eligibility criteria

The target participants of this study were children with CP who met the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria and were receiving rehabilitation at the Lanzhou Disabled Care, Employment and Rehabilitation Center. Participant recruitment will commence on 1 December 2025, at the outpatient clinic of the Lanzhou Disabled Care, Employment and Rehabilitation Center, and will continue until a total of 40 eligible participants have been enrolled.

Each participant will undergo 12 weeks of intervention, as well as assessments conducted at baseline, week 6 and at the end of the 12-week intervention.

The trial will be conducted as planned, without any influence from the study funder.

Inclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria are as follows: (1) children aged 6–17 years with a confirmed diagnosis of CP, including spastic hemiplegia, spastic diplegia or ataxic type; (2) classified as levels I–III on the GMFCS; and (3) sufficient cognitive ability, as confirmed by a licensed physician, to understand simple instructions.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria are as follows: (1) recent surgery or unhealed fractures (including surgery within the previous year or fractures that had not fully healed); (2) uncontrolled epilepsy or other neurological conditions that may affect participation or safety during the intervention; (3) severe intellectual disability or psychiatric conditions that could interfere with cooperation during assessment or intervention; (4) severe fixed musculoskeletal deformities limiting safe participation in WBVT or conventional therapy; (5) severe visual, auditory or sensory impairments affecting communication, intervention participation or outcome assessment; (6) severe cardiovascular, vascular or other medical conditions contraindicating WBVT, including acute thrombosis or deep vein thrombosis; (7) severe osteoporosis or spinal disorders such as spinal instability or intervertebral disc herniation; (8) implanted electronic devices (eg, pacemakers) or other medical implants considered contraindications to WBVT by the treating physician; and (9) use of botulinum toxin injections, anabolic steroids, glucocorticoids (excluding inhaled forms) or growth hormone within 3 months prior to enrolment.

Sample size calculation

The sample size was estimated using G*Power software V.3.1 for a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with a within–between interaction design. The primary outcome measure of this study is Gross Motor Function Measure 88 (GMFM-88).

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials demonstrated significant improvements in gross motor function following WBVT in children with CP, with a pooled standardised mean difference (SMD) of 0.69 for the GMFM-88 scores.9 However, the pooled SMD was derived from between-group comparisons and may not accurately reflect the group-by-time interaction effect targeted in the present repeated-measures design. Therefore, a medium effect size (f=0.25), as recommended by Cohen, was adopted for sample size estimation when a more precise ANOVA effect size was unavailable.15

Using a significance level (α) of 0.05, a statistical power of 85%, two groups and three measurement time points (baseline, 6 weeks and 12 weeks), the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 32 participants using G*Power V.3.1. A statistical power of 85% was selected to reduce the risk of type II error. To account for an anticipated attrition rate of 20%, the final target sample size was increased to 40 participants. Participants will be randomly allocated in a 1:1 ratio, with 20 participants assigned to the experimental group and 20 participants assigned to the control group.

Research team

The study will be conducted at the Lanzhou Foster Care, Employment and Rehabilitation Centre for Persons with Disabilities. Multiple clinical and administrative staff will be involved, including therapists A, B and C (responsible for interventions), an independent assessor (Therapist D), an independent research assistant (E) and a clinical physician. Participant screening will be initiated by the clinical physician using the admission database, and eligibility will be confirmed according to predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Eligible and willing children will undergo baseline assessments conducted by the independent assessor (Therapist D). Subsequently, the independent research assistant (E) will perform random allocation using the online tool Study Randomizer; E will not participate in assessments or intervention to ensure allocation concealment.

WBVT will be delivered by Therapist A, while conventional rehabilitation will be provided by the therapy team, with Therapist B responsible for physical therapy and Therapist C responsible for occupational therapy.

All therapists are licensed professionals with at least 3 years of clinical experience in paediatric neurorehabilitation, and they will receive standardised training on the study protocol to ensure consistency and fidelity of the interventions.

Final outcome assessments will be conducted by an independent assessor (Therapist D), who will remain blinded to group allocation throughout the study to ensure the integrity and objectivity of the evaluations (single-blind design).

Randomisation

To ensure balanced distribution of functional ability between groups, participants will be randomised using a stratified method based on GMFCS level. The random allocation sequence will be generated by an independent research assistant (E) who is not involved in participant enrolment, baseline assessment or intervention, using the online tool Study Randomizer (https://www.studyrandomizer.com/). Group assignments will be coded as ‘A’ (intervention group: WBVT+conventional therapy) or ‘B’ (control group: conventional therapy). The allocation sequence will be stored in a password-protected file accessible only to the independent research assistant (E). Group allocation will remain concealed from the clinical physician, therapists and assessors until participants are formally assigned to a group. The research assistant (E) will record the group assignment only at the time of allocation and will not be involved in enrolment, intervention delivery or outcome assessment, ensuring full allocation concealment.

Blinding

This study will use a single-blind design (assessor-blinded). All outcome assessments will be conducted by an independent therapist (Therapist D), who will remain blinded to group allocation and will not participate in any intervention, ensuring objectivity and accuracy of the outcome assessments.

Unblinding will only be permissible if adverse medical events occur requiring clinical decision-making related to treatment safety. In such cases, unblinding will be performed by the clinical physician, who will contact the independent research assistant (E) to obtain the participant’s allocation. The assessor will not be present during unblinding and will continue outcome assessment for other participants under blinded conditions.

Informed consent, registration, baseline assessment and group allocation

After confirming that eligible participants meet the inclusion criteria, the research team will provide both the participants and their parents with a detailed explanation of the study procedures. Each participant and their parent will receive an information sheet describing the study background, objectives, procedures, potential risks and benefits, and data confidentiality (online supplemental file 2). Written informed consent will be obtained from both the child and their parent prior to enrolment (online supplemental file 3).

Following consent, baseline assessments and demographic data collection will be jointly conducted by a therapist and a clinical physician who are blinded to group allocation. Participants will then be randomised into either the intervention group or the control group using a stratified randomisation method based on GMFCS level to ensure balanced distribution of functional severity.

Intervention for the experimental and control groups

Whole-body vibration therapy

The experimental group will receive WBVT and conventional therapy. WBVT will be conducted three times per week, with each session consisting of 3 min of vibration followed by a 3-minute rest, repeated three times, for a total duration of 18 min. If participants experience any physical discomfort during the training (eg, dizziness, nausea, pain), the session will be immediately stopped, and the therapist will record the adverse event (AE) and take appropriate measures. Conventional therapy

Both the control and experimental groups will receive conventional therapy, consisting of physical and occupational therapy aimed at improving functional abilities in children with CP. The conventional therapy programme will include a variety of interventions targeting functional outcomes, such as muscle strengthening, range of motion (ROM) exercises, gait training, balance training and activities of daily living (ADL) training.

The physical therapy programme will include muscle tone regulation, ROM training, balance training, core stability exercises and gait training. For children with spastic CP and increased muscle tone, passive stretching and functional positioning will be applied to prevent contractures and deformities. For children with hypotonia, resistance training and postural control exercises will be used to promote muscle strength development. Balance training will involve the use of foam pads, balance boards and uneven surfaces to simulate diverse environmental challenges. Core stability exercises will include bridging, bird-dog and plank exercises. Gait training will use assistive devices such as walkers or treadmill systems to correct gait rhythm, step length and weight shifting. Occupational therapy will focus on ADL training, including basic self-care tasks (eg, dressing, tooth brushing, feeding and toileting), as well as school adaptation, handwriting and participation in group activities. All interventions will be individualised and tailored to the specific needs and functional levels of each participant while remaining within a standardised treatment framework.

Both groups will receive conventional rehabilitation three times per week for 12 weeks. Each treatment session will consist of 30 min of physical therapy and 30 min of occupational therapy, resulting in a total of 60 min of conventional rehabilitation per session. The frequency, duration and overall dosage of conventional rehabilitation will be identical in both groups throughout the study period.

Although treatment activities may be adapted to individual functional abilities, all participants will receive the predefined core rehabilitation components according to the standardised study protocol.

To ensure treatment consistency, all therapists will receive standardised training before study initiation. A treatment protocol manual and session checklist will be used throughout the intervention period to guide therapy implementation. Each treatment session will be documented using a standardised treatment checklist, including intervention content, treatment duration, participant attendance and any protocol deviations. Adherence to the protocol will be reviewed weekly by the principal investigator to ensure consistency across therapists and study groups.

Outcome measures

Outcome measures will be assessed at three intervals: (1) baseline, (2) at 6 weeks and (3) at 12 weeks. All data, including consent forms, sociodemographic information and outcome measures, will be collected using hardcopy clinical research forms (CRFs). Data from the CRFs will be transferred into an Excel spreadsheet for storage and further analysis. All hardcopy documents will be kept in a secure location, and electronic files will be protected on a password-secured computer with restricted access. These research records, in both physical and electronic formats, will be retained for a minimum of 5 years following the study’s publication.

The primary outcome

The GMFM-88 is a tool specifically designed to assess gross motor function in children, widely used in the evaluation of children with CP.16 The GMFM-88 consists of 88 items that cover five domains of gross motor ability: crawling and kneeling, standing, walking, running and jumping. These domains assess the completion of different motor tasks, helping clinicians evaluate a child’s motor function level and rehabilitation progress. Each item is scored on a scale from 0 to 3 (0=unable to complete, 1=partially completed, 2=can complete, 3=fully completed),17 with the total score used to quantify the child’s motor abilities.

The GMFM-88 has demonstrated excellent reliability and responsiveness in children with CP, making it a suitable outcome measure for detecting changes in gross motor function following rehabilitation interventions.18

Secondary outcomes

The secondary outcomes include six measures.

Muscle tone

Muscle tone will be assessed using the Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), which is widely applied in clinical practice to evaluate spasticity.19 This test assesses the resistance of muscles to passive stretching through manual manipulation.20 Assessments will be performed with participants in the supine position, focusing on the hip adductors, hamstrings and triceps surae (gastrocnemius and soleus), which are among the most commonly affected lower limb muscle groups in children with CP.21

The MAS consists of six grades: Grade 0: No increase in muscle tone; Grade 1: Slight increase in muscle tone, manifested by a catch and release or by minimal resistance at the end ROM when the affected part is moved in flexion or extension; Grade 1+: Slight increase in muscle tone, manifested by a catch followed by minimal resistance throughout the remainder (less than half) of the ROM; Grade 2: More marked increase in muscle tone through most of the ROM, but affected parts are easily moved; Grade 3: Considerable increase in muscle tone, making passive movement difficult; Grade 4: Affected part(s) rigid in flexion or extension.22

With respect to reliability, a recent study by Ansari23 demonstrated good to excellent intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC) across different muscle groups in children with CP, with consistency ICC values ranging from 0.60 to 0.83. Moreover, both inter-rater reliability (ICC=0.82) and intrarater reliability (ICC=0.85) were reported to be good, supporting the stability and reproducibility of repeated MAS measurements in clinical practice. Considering these findings, this study will implement strict assessor training, consistency testing and blinded assessment procedures to maximise the reliability and reproducibility of MAS scoring.21

Balance

Balance will be assessed using the Pediatric Balance Scale (PBS), which is a child-adapted version of the Berg Balance Scale specifically designed for children aged 3–18 years. It has been widely applied in both clinical and research contexts to evaluate balance in children with motor impairments, particularly those with CP.24 The PBS consists of 14 items that measure a child’s ability to perform balance-related tasks such as sitting, standing, reaching, turning and transitioning between positions.25 Each item is scored on a 5-point ordinal scale ranging from 0 to 4, with higher scores indicating better balance performance. The total score ranges from 0 to 56, with higher scores reflecting greater postural control and functional balance.26

The PBS has demonstrated strong measurement properties. Franjoine25 originally reported excellent inter-rater and test–retest reliability (ICC values >0.95) in children with mild to moderate motor impairments. Subsequent studies have further supported its validity and clinical utility in children with CP, supporting its use as a reliable outcome measure in both clinical practice and research.27

Importantly, evidence has shown that it is responsive to change following rehabilitation. Chen et al reported moderate responsiveness in children with CP, indicating its ability to detect clinically meaningful changes.28

Functional mobility

Functional mobility will be assessed using the Timed Up and Go (TUG) test. The TUG is a standardised and widely applied clinical measure originally developed for older adults29 and later adapted for individuals with motor impairments, including children with CP.30 The TUG provides a comprehensive evaluation of multiple aspects of motor performance, such as balance, gait, mobility and fall risk, and serves as a practical indicator of functional independence in daily activities.31

During the test, participants are instructed to rise from a standard chair, walk three metres forward, turn around, return to the chair and sit down again. The total time required to complete the task (measured in seconds) is recorded. Longer completion times indicate poorer functional mobility and a higher risk of falls, whereas shorter times reflect greater independence and efficiency in movement.31

TUG test is a widely used measure of functional mobility that assesses the time required for an individual to stand up from a chair, walk 3 m, turn around, return and sit down. The TUG has demonstrated good feasibility, reliability and validity in children with CP and other paediatric populations.32 33

In addition, evidence suggests that the TUG is responsive to changes in functional mobility following rehabilitation interventions. Carey et al reported that the TUG is sensitive to improvements in mobility performance in children with CP, supporting its use as an outcome measure in clinical trials and rehabilitation research.32

Walking endurance

The 6-minute walk test (6MWT) is a standardised measure of functional exercise capacity and walking endurance that assesses the distance an individual can walk over a period of 6 min. The 6MWT is widely used in children with CP to assess walking endurance and community ambulation ability. Participants will be instructed to walk back and forth along a flat, straight walkway for 6 min at a self-selected pace while attempting to cover as much distance as possible.34 Assistive devices may be used if required, but their use will remain consistent across all assessments. The total distance walked, recorded in metres, will be used for analysis.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the 6MWT has good test–retest reliability in ambulatory children with CP, with reported ICC values exceeding 0.80.35 36 It has also been shown to be a valid measure of walking endurance and functional exercise capacity in this population.37 Furthermore, previous studies have reported that the 6MWT can detect clinically meaningful changes in walking performance following rehabilitation interventions, supporting its use as an outcome measure for evaluating treatment-related improvements over time.38 39

The 10-metre walk test

The 10-metre walk test (10MWT) is a standardised measure of short-distance walking speed, expressed in metres per second.40 The testing protocol typically involves a 14-metre straight walkway, with a central 10-metre segment used to measure walking speed. An additional 2 m at each end allows for acceleration and deceleration, thereby ensuring a more accurate assessment of steady-state gait performance.41 Depending on the test objective, participants are instructed to walk either at a comfortable pace or at their fastest safe speed. Assistive devices may be used, if necessary, but their use must remain consistent across all trials. Three trials are usually performed, and the average walking speed is calculated for analysis.36

Chrysagis et al demonstrated that the 10MWT is a valid measure of walking speed and functional mobility and can discriminate between different functional levels in children with CP.42 In ambulatory school-aged children with CP classified as GMFCS levels I–III, the test has demonstrated good test–retest reliability under fast-walking conditions, with an ICC of 0.81.36 Therefore, the 10MWT is considered an appropriate outcome measure for evaluating walking speed and functional mobility in children with CP.

Quality of life

The Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) CP Module is a disease-specific instrument designed to assess health-related QoL in children and adolescents with CP. The questionnaire includes both child self-report and parent proxy-report versions and evaluates multiple domains related to daily functioning, participation and well-being. In this study, the Chinese version of the PedsQL CP Module will be used, as all participants are native Chinese speakers.

Previous studies have demonstrated satisfactory reliability and validity of the PedsQL CP Module in children with CP. Varni et al reported good psychometric properties of the original instrument, including acceptable internal consistency, construct validity and feasibility in children with CP.43 In addition, Chen et al validated the Chinese version of the PedsQL Module and reported satisfactory reliability and validity in Chinese children with CP.44 Therefore, the PedsQL CP Module is considered an appropriate outcome measure for assessing QoL in children with CP.

Data analysis

Data will be analysed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.29. Descriptive statistics will be presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables (eg, sex, GMFCS level, age group and CP subtype), and as means and SD when normally distributed. For non-normally distributed continuous data, medians and IQRs will be reported.45 The Shapiro-Wilk test will be used to assess normality.

Inferential analyses will be conducted using repeated-measures ANOVA to compare within-group and between-group changes at baseline, 6 weeks and 12 weeks. The independent factor will be treatment group (WBVT plus conventional therapy vs conventional therapy alone), with GMFM-88 as the primary outcome and MAS, PBS, TUG, 6MWT, 10MWT and PedsQL as secondary outcomes. Main effects of time and group, as well as time×group interactions, will be examined. Post hoc pairwise comparisons will be performed using Bonferroni correction when significant main effects or interaction effects are identified.

Assumptions of ANOVA will be evaluated. Mauchly’s test will be used to assess sphericity; if violated (p<0.05), Greenhouse-Geisser or Huynh-Feldt corrections will be applied. Levene’s test will be used to assess homogeneity of variance. If the assumptions for repeated-measures ANOVA are substantially violated and cannot be corrected through data transformation, linear mixed-effects models will be considered as an alternative analytical approach.

Results will be reported as mean differences with corresponding two-sided 95% CIs. Effect sizes (partial eta-squared) will also be reported where appropriate. Statistical significance will be set at p<0.05, and exact p values will be reported to three decimal places; values <0.001 will be reported as ‘< 0.001’.

All analyses will follow the intention-to-treat principle, whereby all randomised participants will be included in the analysis according to their originally assigned groups, regardless of adherence, protocol deviations or withdrawal.46

Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation. The imputation model will include treatment group, assessment time points and relevant baseline outcome variables. Estimates from the imputed datasets will be pooled using Rubin’s rules.47

Safety and data monitoring

Safety monitoring

All participants receiving WBVT will undergo a comprehensive physical assessment to identify any changes in health status and potential adverse reactions, such as dizziness, headache, nausea or muscle soreness. During the intervention, participants will be closely monitored, and the treatment intensity or content will be adjusted as needed based on individual responses to ensure safety and minimise potential harm.

Participant privacy and confidentiality will be strictly maintained. All data will be stored in password-protected databases, with personal identifiers kept separately and linked only through a unique study identification code. For analysis and reporting, only study identification codes will be used. Disclosure of participant information will occur only with explicit consent or if required by law.

Any AEs or harm related to trial participation will be managed appropriately, and participants will receive necessary medical care at no cost. Post-trial care, if needed, will be provided according to standard clinical practice at the study site. Compensation for any trial-related injury will be handled in accordance with local regulations and institutional policies.

Data Monitoring Committee

This study does not have an independent Data Monitoring Committee due to the relatively small sample size and the non-invasive nature of the intervention (WBVT combined with conventional rehabilitation). Instead, participant safety will be continuously monitored by the research team, including therapists and the clinical physician. Any AEs or serious AEs (SAEs) will be reviewed immediately by the clinical physician and reported to the local ethics committee. The research team members involved in safety monitoring have no conflicts of interest related to the study sponsor or funding.

Interim analysis and stopping guidelines

An interim analysis will be conducted after each cohort completes 6 weeks of intervention to monitor participant safety and evaluate preliminary trends. Only the research team, including therapists and the clinical physician, will have access to these interim results. The trial will not be terminated early based on efficacy; the study will only be terminated if SAEs are judged by the clinical physician and ethics committee to pose unacceptable risk.

Emergency response plan

Emergency stations will be established at the study site, equipped with trained medical personnel and necessary emergency equipment to address acute incidents such as falls, dizziness or nausea during WBVT. A standardised emergency protocol will be implemented to ensure that medical staff can respond promptly and appropriately, thereby safeguarding participants’ safety.

Ancillary and post-trial care

After completion of the 12-week trial, participants in the control group will be offered an optional post-trial intervention consisting of 3 months of free WBVT, delivered three times per week. This post-trial care aims to provide equitable access to the intervention and ensure participants in the control group can benefit from the therapy. Any AEs occurring during the post-trial care period will be monitored and managed by the same clinical team.

Data management

All participant information will be anonymised and stored in password-protected computer systems. A structured database will be established to track each participant’s progress throughout the trial, including baseline, midterm and final assessment results. All data will be securely stored in encrypted computers or servers, with access restricted to authorised research team members only.

Adverse event reporting

All AEs occurring during the study period will be systematically recorded for each participant. An AE is defined as any unfavourable or unintended sign, symptom or medical condition that occurs during the study and may be related to the intervention.48 Therapists B and C will monitor AEs during each intervention session and document them using standardised reporting forms. All SAEs will be promptly reported to the local ethics committee to ensure compliance with ethical and regulatory requirements.

The frequency and type of AEs will be summarised descriptively by study group, using counts and percentages. Formal statistical hypothesis testing will not be conducted, but any notable differences or unusual patterns between groups will be reported.

Oversight and trial coordination

The trial will be conducted at the Lanzhou Foster Care, Employment and Rehabilitation Center for Persons with Disabilities, which serves as the coordinating site. The principal investigator (PI) will act as the trial coordinator and is responsible for overall supervision of the study, ensuring adherence to the protocol, monitoring intervention fidelity and safeguarding participant safety. The data management team, consisting of the independent research assistant and the clinical physician, will oversee data entry, data security and quality control throughout the trial.

All AEs and SAEs will be reviewed by the PI and reported to the institutional Ethics Committee, which supervises trial conduct and ensures compliance with ethical standards.

No external steering committee or endpoint adjudication committee has been established due to the small scale and self-funded nature of this trial.
