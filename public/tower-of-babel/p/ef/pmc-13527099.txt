Personality development from early to middle adulthood in the general population and those with mental health difficulties
Authors: Jessica A Kerr, S Ghazaleh Dashti, Hanafi Mohamad Husin, Elizabeth A Spry, Susan M Sawyer, Paul Moran, Jonathan Monk-Cunliffe, George C Patton, Craig A Olsson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe describe personality trait maturation from early to middle adulthood in the population and in populations with common mental health or substance use problems to inform personality-sensitive public health and health services care. Data were drawn from the population-based Victorian Adolescent Health Cohort Study. Traits were measured using the NEO Five Factor Inventory at 24 and 42 years. We estimated trait rank-order stability, mean-level change, and standardised mean difference (SMD) scores in subgroups defined by patterns of substance use and common mental disorder from adolescence (15–17 years) to adulthood (21–35 years). Multiple imputation was used to address missing data for the full sample of 1,943 participants. Between early and middle adulthood, population-levels of agreeableness and conscientiousness increased; extraversion, neuroticism, and openness decreased. These patterns were also largely present in those within subgroups, the most pronounced drop being neuroticism in those with persistent common mental disorder (SMD − 0.31, 95% CI -0.41 to -0.18). Compared with other subgroups and the overall population averages, for this group with persistent mental disorder, neuroticism was higher on entry to adulthood, dropped more substantially over time, but still remained high in middle adulthood. A similar decline was not observed for those with no or transient mental disorder, who entered adulthood with lower neuroticism. Subgroup results for neuroticism imply some variation in the timing of maturation by the presence and duration of common mental disorder. Personality trait maturation from early to middle adulthood plays a seminal role in structuring multiple domains of human development (e.g., employment, divorce, mortality), with aetiological significance likened to socioeconomic status and cognitive ability1–3. Despite this, there is little research on personality trait maturation in those living with common mental health difficulties (including substance use problems) within the general population. In this study we draw on data from the population-derived Victorian Adolescent Health Cohort Study (VAHCS, 1992) to progress understanding of personality trait maturation across early adulthood (twenties4), to middle adulthood (forties;5), both in the general population as well as in populations experiencing common mental health problems who may require additional support for personality-related difficulties.

One of the most influential models of personality is the Five-Factor Theory which includes the Big Five trait structure, Agreeableness, Conscientiousness, Neuroticism (emotional stability), Extraversion, and Openness to experience6. The social investment and maturity principles of Neo-Socioanaltyic Theory7 posit that age-related trait changes are triggered by the transition to responsible roles and the need to display socially acceptable and psychologically mature characteristics by early adulthood8–11.

The most robust population-level trait changes replicated in the literature include increasing mean levels of agreeableness, conscientiousness, and emotional stability after adolescence, in early adulthood, and throughout the transition to adulthood10,12,13. Complementary research on rank-order stability typically shows that traits increase in stability across childhood and adolescence, with stability peaking in early adulthood prior to age 35, before decreasing stability (i.e., slight change) in later adult life9.

It is a limitation that most of this longitudinal research focuses on personality development across adolescence and early adulthood (twenties), with an average five years between trait assessments9. Although increasing in volume9, comparatively less research has tracked personality over long periods (e.g., two decades) such as spanning the years between early and middle adulthood, especially in population-derived cohorts. This current pool of literature may obscure what we know about the timing of personality development, favouring maturity and social investment principles across the early adulthood years. However, with more cohort studies from decades prior beginning to mature into middle adulthood there is an opportunity to extend our understanding of trait maturation.

Moreover, although the personality characteristics of most people appear to stabilise in broadly predictable ways, a proportion of people deviate from population-level trends14–16. Given the importance of personality trait maturation for the successful transition to adult roles and functioning2, it is surprising that scarce literature has characterised whether the maturity principle of Neo-Socioanalytic Theory is relevant for vulnerable social groups such as those experiencing highly prevalent mental health difficulties.

Describing trait maturation in the context of mental health difficulties could inform relevant public health policy, services, and clinical guidelines by helping to identify populations who may benefit from additional targeted support. We acknowledge that the field already understands that mental health and personality are intimately linked17–20 and we recognise that there are causal models in this space21,22, with an accompanying large literature examining predictive and causal research questions23–25. Yet, recent calls have been made to prioritise prospective investigations examining personality and mental health26. Within this context, longitudinal research that applies a descriptive epidemiological framework, as distinct from predictive or causal approaches remains relatively rare. Such descriptive work is important because it enables the characterisation of personality change within populations and for whom these changes differ, which can help inform targeted support services where relevant27.

In the current study we use mature cohort data from the Victorian Adolescent Health Cohort Study to extend current understanding of personality trait maturation. The overriding purpose of this descriptive paper is to inform service provision and delivery of specific personality-related supports to those presenting with a history of substance use or mental health difficulties. Between early and middle adulthood (24 to 42 years), we describe: (1) basic patterns of trait stability and change at the population mean-level; and (2) mean-level change in those who have and have not experienced a common mental disorder or substance use problems during adolescence and/or adulthood.

Method

Study design and participants

Between 1992 and 2021, we conducted an eleven-wave, longitudinal, population-based cohort study focused on the health of young people in the state of Victoria, Australia known as the Victorian Adolescent Health Cohort Study (VAHCS). VAHCS is a cohort recruited from the general population; hence participants were not selected on the bases of any mental health or substance use criteria. Sampling and recruitment have been previously described28, briefly we recruited a representative sample of 2032 adolescents aged between 14 and 15 using a two-stage cluster sampling procedure. The inclusion criterion was adolescents studying Year 9 in a secondary school within the state of Victoria, Australia; there were no exclusion criteria. Supplementary Fig. 1 shows that data collection occurred at 6-monthly intervals between ages 14 and 17 years, followed by five follow-up waves during adulthood between ages 20 and 42 years. Of those recruited, 1943 students (96%) participated in VAHCS at least once in waves 1–6. Thereafter, 82% of participants were retained through to wave 7 in adulthood, and 73% through to wave 11.

Informed written consent was obtained from participants’ parents at study inception, with adolescent consent thereafter implied by their voluntary participation in the self-administered questionnaires. From wave 7 (age 20 years), participants gave informed consent prior to participating in each subsequent wave. The Human Research Ethics Committee of the Royal Melbourne Children’s Hospital approved all data collection protocols (RCH HREC #33168).

Results

Table 2 shows the sample characteristics, including proportions of missing data. Sex was evenly distributed (male: 48.5%), most participants completed secondary school (89.6%) and participated in post-school education by the age of 24 (63.2%), with 33.7% of the sample living in neighbourhoods experiencing relatively low levels of disadvantage (quintile 5).

At both personality assessments (wave 8 age 24; wave 11 age 42), trait level scores were highest for conscientiousness and agreeableness, and lowest for neuroticism. Of those with available data on common mental disorder during adolescence and adulthood, one-third (35.0%) experienced a common mental disorder during both adolescence and adulthood, with another third having a common mental disorder limited to the adolescent (16.8%) or to the adulthood period (16.4%). The remaining third did not meet the criteria for a common mental disorder across either adolescence or adulthood.

Proportions were similar for substance use problems, though compared with common mental disorder, the experience of persistent substance use problems across both adolescence and adulthood was slightly more common (42.7%).

Aim 1: Population mean-level trait development

Supplementary Fig. 2 shows the variability in scores for each trait at ages 24 and 42. For a random sample of 25 participants, the repeated measures at the two timepoints are connected to illustrate the longitudinal nature of the data. Despite the relative stability over time in mean traits at the population level (illustrated by the red line in Supplementary Fig. 2), it is evident that changes at the individual level were variable in both the extent and direction of change for all traits.

Longitudinal rank-order stability coefficients were moderate (around 0.50) for most traits (Table 3). In comparison to the other four traits, openness to experience had the higher stability (coefficient 0.66, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.70), indicating high continuity. Absolute trait-level (SMD) increases were seen in agreeableness and conscientiousness, with absolute decreases seen in neuroticism, openness to experience, and extraversion (Table 3). Most trait changes were small during this period (SMDs ≤ 0.25), although extraversion showed a relatively large change at the population level, with a standardised mean difference of -0.38 (95% CI -0.44 to -0.32).

Aim 2: Mean-level trait development by substance use problems or common mental disorder

For each trait, the mean trait score at ages 24 and 42 are shown in Supplementary Table 2. For both substance use problems and common mental disorder, for agreeableness, conscientiousness, and extraversion the direction and strength of standardised mean differences were largely similar between all subgroups, and similar to the population average (Figs. 1 and 2; Supplementary Table 3). Conversely, participants in the different subgroups did not necessarily mirror population-level reductions in neuroticism and openness to experience. Most subgroups of participants reduced in openness and neuroticism by insignificant amounts (Figs. 1 and 2). The exceptions are described below.

Substance use problems

Although the overall population demonstrated a small decrease in openness over time (Table 3), when considering subgroups of the population with substance use problems, a decrease in openness was only distinct among the subgroup with adolescent-limited substance use problems (-0.19, 95% CI -0.33 to -0.03). However, the 95% CIs corresponding to this change overlapped with other subgroups (Fig. 1).

For neuroticism, the overall population decreased over time (Table 3), yet when considering subgroups, this decrease was only distinctive among those with persistent substance use problems or with adulthood-only substance use problems (Fig. 1), although the corresponding 95% CIs overlapped with other subgroups. Of note, both of these subgroups had higher neuroticism scores at age 24 compared with both population average at age 24 and with participants in the other subgroups of substance use (Supplementary Table 2).

Common mental disorder

As observed with the subgroups of substance use disorder, a decrease in openness was most distinct among the subgroup with adolescent-limited common mental disorder (-0.15, 95% CI -0.29 to 0.02). However, the 95% CIs corresponding to this change overlapped with other subgroups and crossed zero (Fig. 2).

The magnitude of change was consistent across subgroups (i.e., considerable overlap in confidence intervals) for most other traits, with two exceptions. For the subgroup with persistent disorder, larger changes were observed for agreeableness (0.40, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.48) and for neuroticism (-0.31, 95% CI -0.41 to -0.18). For the change in agreeableness, the 95% CIs overlapped with other subgroups. For the change in neuroticism, there was no overlap between the 95% CIs between subgroups (Fig. 2). Of note, individuals who experienced persistent disorder had lower agreeableness scores and higher neuroticism scores at age 24 compared with the population average and the averages in other subgroups of common mental disorder (Supplementary Table 2). The large increase in agreeableness scores observed in the subgroup with persistent disorder resulted in close-to-average agreeableness by midlife (age 42). Conversely, despite the large decrease in neuroticism scores (i.e., maturation in emotional stability) in the subgroup who experienced persistent disorder, midlife average neuroticism scores remained highest compared with other subgroups and the overall population averages (Supplementary Table 2). It is also noteworthy that, over time, the average neuroticism score remained almost the same in the other subgroups of common mental disorder.

Discussion

Over an 18-year period spanning early to middle adulthood, for conscientiousness and extraversion, those reporting mental disorder or substance use problems mirrored cohort population-level trends in maturation. The cohort population and those reporting mental disorder or substance use problems also matured similarly in agreeableness, with those reporting persistent common mental disorder showing the largest increase. Evidence was more nuanced for neuroticism and openness. Decreases in openness were generally small in the population and across subgroups, except in those with adolescent-only substance use problems or common mental disorder, in whom we observed larger trait reductions. However, the most distinctive observation was that although the well-documented8 maturation of emotional stability (decreasing neuroticism) was observed in the population across adulthood, this maturation was mainly evident in those with persistent common mental disorder. Of note, these individuals entered early adulthood with high levels of neuroticism, and reached midlife with lower, but still elevated, levels of neuroticism.

Comparison with previous research

At the cohort level, participants’ midlife trait scores were directly comparable to other population-based cohorts46. Also, in contrast to the original Five-Factor Theory that personality changes insignificantly after age 306, we observed small population-level changes and moderate rank-order stability coefficients between early and middle adulthood that were mostly consistent with recent research and indicated maturation across adulthood9.

Specific to rank-order stability, a recent meta-analysis demonstrated that stability peaks by the fourth decade of life, with 96% of the total lifespan increase in relative-level stability occurring prior to age 359. Overall, such analysis of 189 longitudinal studies obtained an average lifespan stability of 0.61 (95% CI 0.58 to 0.64). Comparable to other longitudinal studies, our analysis of rank-order stability found moderate-sized correlations over an 18-year period of adulthood: 0.42 to 0.66. However, distinct from recent Big Five investigations, we found openness, rather than extraversion9,47, to be the most stable of the five traits over time.

Specific to absolute population-level mean changes, consistent with the maturity principle of Neo-Socioanalytic Theory and with past literature9, the population-level mean changes found in our cohort were in the direction of greater maturity across adulthood. Average levels of agreeableness, conscientiousness, and emotional stability increased by small amounts from early to middle adult life. Past evidence on population-level mean change in extraversion and openness is more mixed9. Between the five traits, we observed the smallest change (-0.05, 95% CI -0.13 to -0.02) for openness, which is consistent with meta-analytic trends between ages 20 and 409. However, the larger change we observed for extraversion (-0.38, 95% CI -0.44 to -0.32) was inconsistent with Bleidorn et al. meta-analysis, which observed the highest rates of mean-level change for neuroticism, agreeableness, and conscientiousness9. While we cannot comment on trait development beyond age 42, Bleidorn et al.’s work demonstrates that the Big Five traits can continue to develop throughout middle adulthood and beyond.

Employing a descriptive epidemiology framework29, we also described trait changes among those with different experiences of mental health difficulties across adolescence and adulthood. Such observations highlight the possibility that the natural history of neuroticism across adulthood could be distinct in those who experience long-term common mental disorder. Earlier in the life course, findings from the Minnesota Twin Family Study show that participants with recurrent depressive disorder (age 17 and 29) failed to show the same decrease in negative emotionality between late adolescence (age 17) and early adulthood (ages 24–29) as did participants without depressive disorder20. Although these findings are not derived from a population cohort and deviate from a descriptive epidemiology framework, this past study is broadly consistent with our current findings which imply that individuals without persistent mental disorder mature in neuroticism earlier than do those with persistent mental disorder.

There is scarce other descriptive and community-based research with which to compare our subgroup results. For instance, a large body of research has examined the major life events that can cause or trigger personality change (e.g., marriage, parenthood, trauma)48,49. This research examines why personality changes, but little is known about the sub-groups within the population for whom personality develops distinctively (i.e., in contrast to average personality development). Some researchers have examined sub-groups by age, gender, or sex16, and there is a large literature reporting predictive and causal associations between anxiety, depression, substance use, and personality21,23,24,50. However, we did not design our analysis to investigate causal concepts such as the predisposition model51, vulnerability model18,19, or the complication/scar model21,22. We appreciate that this literature has allowed theorists to understand why personality changes, but this was not our focus.

Implications

From early to middle adulthood, success in navigating major changes in relationships, education, training, employment and other important life transitions is facilitated by stable and mature trait levels52. Consistent with the social investment and maturity principles7, past evidence shows that the majority of trait change, including change in neuroticism, occurs during and after early adulthood rather than in adolescence8, with the transition to adult roles10,11,13.

In contrast to this timeline, we observed that the three subgroups that did not experience persistent common mental disorder entered early adulthood with already mature levels of emotional stability and showed less meaningful change during their adult years. Although recent evidence suggests trait maturation occurs as early as adolescence for agreeableness and conscientiousness53, our findings imply that emotional stability may also mature during adolescence (i.e., before age 24) among individuals without persistent common mental disorder20. This specific finding reinforces calls to consider refining the maturity principle of personality development54 which posits that the majority of trait maturity occurs after adolescence during the transition to adult roles7,10,11,13,55.

In contrast, despite entering adulthood (age 24) with higher-than-average neuroticism, the subgroup with persistent common mental disorder demonstrated trait maturation between early adulthood and midlife. This timing corresponds to population-level trends9,13 and is consistent with the timeline posited within the neo-socioanalytic principles of social investment and maturity7,10,11,55. Yet, it raises the possibility that this group of individuals (≈ 30%) could be distorting or obscuring such previously identified population-level trends for both the timing of, and degree of, maturation in emotional stability. Moreover, although we observed trait maturation in this subgroup, it is noteworthy that these individuals still had elevated neuroticism at midlife.

Nevertheless, although this distinct decrease in neuroticism may imply that emotional stability is maturing during adulthood in those with persistent common mental disorder, we note the importance of comparing mean differences in trait scores across subgroups with caution. Due to the well-known phenomenon of regression to the mean56, subgroups who have higher trait scores at age 24 are expected to have lower scores at their second measurement at age 42, and it is difficult to separate this from real changes in trait scores between the two time points.

Yet, it remains possible that some of those with persistent common mental disorder represent a group with distinct personality profiles (e.g., exaggerated personality traits) in midlife who may continue to deviate from theorised change (e.g., delayed or absence of decreasing neuroticism with age). For this reason, monitoring personality changes, or lack of expected development in those presenting clinically with persistent anxiety or depression could be helpful in identifying individuals with delayed trait maturation. This information could be used to provide more holistic support to those with common mental disorder who may also experience personality difficulties, which can predict a range of health and social outcomes.

Strengths and weaknesses

Our large population-based cohort with personality measured 18 years apart during early adulthood and midlife is a key strength. Moreover, the random sampling and narrow age range of the cohort (i.e., of similar age and time) increases the likelihood that participants were exposed to similar period and cohort effects.

We also acknowledge some limitations and recommend areas for further research. First, while VAHCS is an 11-wave longitudinal cohort study with a long assessment timeframe, allowing examination of 20-year changes in personality, personality assessment was only conducted at two time points spaced about two decades apart. Future research using multiple waves of personality data is needed to capture granular change in the pace and timing of trait development57, although we acknowledge the participant burden associated with frequently completing extensive self-report items. Furthermore, we could not explore the possible non-linear population-level and individual-level personality development over time16, as we only had data at two time points. Ideally, the current study should be replicated with more frequent assessments of personality to pinpoint if distinctive trait development is apparent within subgroups defined based on having persistent mental disorder across multiple or specific developmental periods.

Second, because personality was self-reported any observed trait stability may reflect stability in one’s self-perception rather than personality stability. While one’s self-perception of personality is important, there will be value in incorporating multi-informant designs in future studies54. In addition, we acknowledge that there are other important traits and constructs that are not positioned in the Big Five structure58. Third, although missing data were low on most variables, they were higher for symptoms of common mental disorder and substance use problems, but are nonetheless comparable with those of other major cohorts of a similar timescale59. We further acknowledge that participant attrition may not have been at random, yet we employed multiple imputation to reduce the impact of potential bias due to missing data on our findings. Finally, we recognise the potential overlap between the measurement of some traits and mental/substance disorder (e.g., neuroticism with anxiety and depressive disorders). We note that personality traits and disorder were measured at different assessment waves and that our aim was to use the measures of common mental disorder and substance use problems to describe which subgroups deviate from population-level trait development. If our aim had been causal, overlap between constructs would have been more noteworthy. Related, because our aims were descriptive aims to characterise patterns within the VAHCS dataset, caution should be applied when generalising these results to other populations and we acknowledge that we have not specifically validated these findings which is more suited to predictive research questions. We encourage future research using independent datasets and alternative study designs to assess the extent to which these patterns replicate across settings and populations. Future research could also extend our findings to clarify whether emotional stability continues to mature beyond middle adulthood among individuals who experience persistent mental disorder and may disentangle patterns by anxiety disorder and major depressive disorder.

Conclusion

In this study we provide a comprehensive picture of personality by describing the natural history of personality trait maturation from early to middle adulthood at both the population and subgroup level. Our findings broadly support the maturity principle of Neo-Socioanalytic Theory. We observed that maturation appears to be a consistent phenomenon across population subgroups, although the timing of trait maturation may differ by the presence and/or persistence of common mental disorder. Despite some vulnerable groups transitioning to adult life with relatively immature personality trait levels, our results are encouraging, demonstrating that trait maturation is also evident in vulnerable subgroups who may begin their adult life with low agreeableness or emotional stability. Despite this finding, we still observed elevated levels of neuroticism in those with persistent common mental disorder as they reached middle adult life. Given that mature personality traits predict consequential life outcomes, this finding is important. Our results may suggest that lack of expected trait maturation by midlife could be something to monitor and support in groups who present clinically for persistent anxiety or depression.
