Beta-blockers after myocardial infarction in the reperfusion era—Time for reappraisal?
Authors: Satyavir Yadav, Bharath Raj Kidambi, Vibhav Sharma, Nitish Naik
Published: 2026

Beta-blockers have long been a cornerstone of secondary prevention after myocardial infarction (MI). Early landmark randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in the pre-reperfusion era when large infarcts, pump failure, and arrhythmic deaths were common demonstrated striking mortality reductions of 25–40% with agents such as propranolol, metoprolol, atenolol, and timolol. These studies, including BHAT, Göteborg, ISIS-1, and the Norwegian Timolol trial (Table 1), formed the foundation for the enduring Class I recommendation by the ACC/AHA and Class IIa endorsement by the ESC for post–MI beta-blocker therapy. 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6.

However, the landscape of acute coronary care has transformed. The widespread adoption of primary percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), dual antiplatelet therapy, high-intensity statins, and renin–angiotensin system inhibitors has dramatically reduced infarct size and arrhythmic mortality. In this modern context, the incremental value of chronic beta-blockade has been increasingly questioned—particularly in patients with preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF).

Evidence from the reperfusion era — a changing paradigm

The paradigm of post-infarction management has evolved dramatically with the advent of early reperfusion and comprehensive medical therapy. In contrast to the pre-reperfusion era, contemporary trials have examined beta-blockers in patients with smaller infarcts, preserved left ventricular function, and markedly reduced arrhythmic mortality.

Recent large RCTs—including CAPITAL-RCT, REDUCE-AMI, REBOOT, and BETAMI/DANBLOCK—evaluated beta-blockers in PCI-treated patients receiving optimal modern therapy 7, 8, 9, 10. Across these studies, no consistent reduction in all-cause mortality or recurrent MI was observed, though CAPITAL-RCT and certain subgroup analyses suggested a possible benefit among patients with mildly reduced EF (40–50%) or larger infarcts (Table 2).

These findings highlight a crucial shift: while beta-blockers remain beneficial in left ventricular dysfunction, their routine use in all post–MI patients may no longer be warranted. The heterogeneity of outcomes reinforces the need to define which patient subsets continue to derive meaningful benefit.

Meta-analyses and subgroup insights — defining the nuance

Despite overall neutral results in the reperfusion era, recent analyses suggest that not all post–MI patients respond uniformly to beta-blockade. In particular, those with mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF 40–50%) may still derive selective benefit.

A meta-analysis by Sripal Bangalore et al. including nine randomized trials and 54,771 patients compared outcomes of beta-blocker therapy after myocardial infarction across treatment eras. While beta-blockers were associated with significant reductions in mortality in the pre-reperfusion era, these benefits were not observed in the reperfusion era, where no significant reductions were seen in all-cause mortality, cardiovascular mortality, stroke, or heart failure. The modest reduction in recurrent myocardial infarction was largely driven by the COMMIT trial, which primarily enrolled patients treated with thrombolysis rather than contemporary primary PCI.11

More recently, an individual patient data meta-analysis by Xavier Rossellò et al. pooled 14,418 patients from four contemporary randomized trials (REBOOT, BETAMI, DANBLOCK, and CAPITAL-RCT) directly comparing beta-blocker therapy with no beta-blocker therapy after myocardial infarction in the reperfusion era. Overall, beta-blocker therapy was not associated with a statistically significant reduction in the composite endpoint of death, myocardial infarction, or heart failure. However, a signal toward benefit was observed among patients with mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (40–50%), suggesting that patients with incomplete ventricular recovery may continue to derive benefit.12

Taken together, these analyses indicate that beta-blockers may retain a role in selected subgroups—particularly those with mildly reduced EF or larger infarcts—while offering little incremental value in fully revascularized patients with preserved ventricular function.

Duration of therapy — an unsettled question

The optimal duration of beta-blocker therapy after myocardial infarction remains uncertain, particularly in patients who are fully revascularized and have preserved ventricular function. While both the 2023 ESC and 2025 ACC/AHA guidelines refrain from firm recommendations, emerging evidence suggests that indefinite therapy may not be necessary for all.

The ABYSS trial recently demonstrated that withdrawing beta-blockers after one year in stable, reperfused post–MI patients with preserved EF was not associated with increased risk of death or reinfarction. Similarly, large observational studies in contemporary PCI cohorts (e.g., OBTAIN registry, Korean National Database) have shown comparable outcomes between patients who discontinued versus continued therapy beyond 12 months.13

In contrast, patients with reduced EF (<40%), prior heart failure, or high arrhythmic risk continue to derive clear survival benefit from long-term beta-blockade. For others, a more individualized approach—guided by ventricular recovery, symptoms, and risk profile—appears both rational and evidence-based.

The traditional “one-size-fits-all” paradigm for post–MI beta-blockade is therefore increasingly questionable in the era of precision cardiology.

Clinical implications

From a practical standpoint, the implications of these findings are significant. In patients with reduced ejection fraction (<40%) or clinical heart failure, the survival benefit of beta-blockers remains unequivocal and guideline-mandated a cornerstone of therapy.

In contrast, for patients with preserved left ventricular function who have undergone successful revascularization and are receiving optimal secondary prevention therapy, the rationale for indefinite beta-blockade is increasingly weak. In such individuals, chronic use may confer more adverse effects—fatigue, bradycardia, depression, or impaired exercise tolerance—than measurable survival advantage.

Patients with mildly reduced EF (40–50%) represent a gray zone of ongoing debate. Emerging data from DANBLOCK and BETAMI, reinforced by the Lancet individual-patient meta-analysis, hint at possible benefit, but the evidence remains inconclusive. Until further outcome trials clarify this subgroup, a personalized, physiology-guided approach should replace the historical blanket prescription model.

Conclusion — toward evidence-based individualization

The cumulative evidence now challenges one of cardiology's longest-standing dogmas. While beta-blockers remain life-saving in patients with left ventricular dysfunction, clinical heart failure, or arrhythmia risk, their routine continuation in all post–MI patients no longer reflects the realities of modern reperfusion medicine.The goal moving forward should not be universal prescription, but targeted precision—identifying who continues to benefit, for how long, and at what cost. Large-scale outcome trials are urgently needed to refine both the selection criteria and the optimal duration of therapy. Until then, clinicians must balance evidence with judgment, aligning treatment decisions with the principles of personalized, value-based cardiovascular care.
