Metabolic engineering of Escherichia coli for de novo production of lauryl glucoside
Authors: Kasimaporn Promubon, Chaiwat Arjin, Chayakorn Pumas, Aussara Panya, Patrik R. Jones, Pachara Sattayawat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Lauryl glucoside, a non-ionic surfactant used in various cosmetic products, is valued for its biodegradability and gentleness on the skin. However, its current production through chemical synthesis is considered unsustainable, necessitating the search for alternative methods. In this work, we engineered Escherichia coli BL21(DE3) with a novel lauryl glucoside biosynthetic pathway. Optimisation of 1-dodecanol, a lauryl glucoside precursor, was first implemented. Under optimised conditions, the strain produced 1-dodecanol at a titre of 185.39 ​± ​3.62 ​mg/L and a yield of 11.60 ​± ​0.29 ​mg/g glucose. These conditions were subsequently used to identify UDP-glycosyltransferases capable of converting 1-dodecanol to lauryl glucoside. Among six UDP-glycosyltransferases, MtH2 from Medicago truncatula showed the highest activity, with a titre and a yield of 0.72 ​± ​0.07 ​mg/L and 0.06 ​± ​0.004 ​mg/g glucose, respectively. The lauryl glucoside biosynthesis by MtH2 was confirmed using HPLC and targeted LC-MS. Moreover, the limited availability of 1-dodecanol was primarily identified as the bottleneck in this pathway. Supplementing the cells with twice the amount of 1-dodecanol led to an increase in lauryl glucoside production, achieving a titre of 13.44 ​± ​0.21 ​mg/L and a yield of 1.35 ​± ​0.04 ​mg/g glucose. Fermentation products of all strains were also monitored and suggested the redirection of carbon flux from acetate to the desired products. These findings demonstrate the successful characterisation of a newly designed lauryl glucoside biosynthetic pathway in engineered E. coli and highlight substrate limitation as a bottleneck in the pathway offering a sustainable alternative to traditional production methods.

Introduction

The extensive use of high-value chemicals derived from petroleum industries has increased environmental concerns due to their negative impacts and the finite nature of petroleum reserves [1,2]. As a result, there is an urgent need for sustainable alternatives to the production of these chemicals. Microbial cell factories offer several advantages, including the use of microorganisms with rapid growth, which leads to a high production rate, and all reactions are performed inside microbial cells, ensuring enzyme stability without the need for expensive requirements to facilitate reactions. Despite these advantages, microbial cell factories face certain limitations, including the inability to naturally synthesise all desired chemicals, and moreover, some native pathways exhibit low metabolic flux resulting in low productivity [1,3]. To overcome these limitations, the production of chemicals through newly designed biosynthetic pathways has emerged as a promising strategy [4,5].

Lauryl glucoside or dodecyl β-d-glucopyranoside, a glycolipid surfactant, is a high-value chemical due to its use as a non-ionic surfactant in many cosmetic products, coupled with its biodegradability and low toxicity, which make it mild on the skin. These properties have driven market demand to USD 0.3 billion in 2024, with expectations to reach USD 0.5 billion by 2033 [6]. Currently, lauryl glucoside is produced through a condensation of 1-dodecanol, a C12 straight-chain alcohol, with glucose that involves high temperatures and acidic conditions [7,8]. This process is considered unsustainable because of the required substrates, which are obtained from either petroleum-based or plant-based sources [7]. Additionally, lauryl glucoside has not been reported to be naturally synthesised in any microorganisms. This could be primarily because microorganisms lack both the precursor, 1-dodecanol, and the specific enzyme converting 1-dodecanol into lauryl glucoside [9,10].

Here, a biosynthetic pathway for lauryl glucoside was designed and implemented in E. coli. This pathway involves the synthesis of 1-dodecanol, a fatty alcohol that serves as a substrate for lauryl glucoside biosynthesis, via the extended native fatty acid biosynthetic pathway. The 1-dodecanol biosynthetic pathway used in this work harbours three heterologous enzymes — Tes12 (a thioesterase), CAR (a carboxylic acid reductase), and Sfp (a phosphopantetheinyl transferase) — while the native pathway includes the fatty acid synthesis (FAS) pathway and the aldehyde reductase (AHR) enzyme. Subsequently, the final O-glycosylation reaction of 1-dodecanol is catalysed by a UDP-glycosyltransferase, which uses UDP-glucose as a glucose donor, resulting in the formation of lauryl glucoside. Therefore, this study aims to characterise the lauryl glucoside biosynthetic pathway in E. coli BL21(DE3). Firstly, we focused on optimising the production of 1-dodecanol as a precursor following by synthesising lauryl glucoside using six UDP-glycosyltransferase genes from different organisms. These genes were selected based on their previously reported activity towards medium-chain fatty alcohols and their broad substrate specificity, including RO3G from Rhizopus delemar RA 99–880 [10], AdGT4 from Actinidia deliciosa (hereafter GT4) [11,12], AtGT1 from Arabidopsis thaliana (hereafter At) [13], MtG1 from Medicago truncatula [14], MtH2 from Medicago truncatula [15], and VvGT1 from Vitis vinifera (hereafter Vv) [11,16]. Lauryl glucoside biosynthesis was quantified using HPLC and validated using targeted LC-MS and fermentation profiles of all strains were also observed. Taken together, this work provides a new approach for sustainable microbial production of lauryl glucoside and contributes to a better understanding pathway optimisation in E. coli-based platforms.

Materials and methods

Strains and plasmids

E. coli DH5α was used as the propagation host for all plasmids in this study, while E. coli BL21(DE3) served as the production host for both 1-dodecanol and lauryl glucoside. The genes encoding a thioesterase obtained from Umbellularia californica (UcafatB1-2 or Tes12), a phosphopantetheinyl transferase (Sfp) obtained from Bacillus subtilis, and a carboxylic acid reductase (CAR) obtained from Mycobacterium marinum were constructed in a pET-plasmid for 1-dodecanol production under the control of the pA1lacO1 promoter [17]. UDP-glycosyltransferase genes (RO3G, GT4, At, Vv, MtH2, MtG1) for lauryl glucoside production were constructed in a pCDF-plasmid under the control of pA1lacO1 promoter. In this work, plasmids carrying GFP were used as negative controls to confirm that the cells could express heterologous proteins from the corresponding plasmid backbones and to ensure that the expression process did not induce excessive burden on the cells. All plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3) for the expression and production of the desired products (Supplementary Table S1).

Determination of the toxicity of 1-dodecanol on E. coli

E. coli BL21(DE3) was cultivated in 10 ​mL of Lurial-Bertani (LB) broth (1 ​L; 10 ​g tryptone, 10 ​g NaCl and 5 ​g yeast extract) and incubated at 37 ​°C, 180 ​rpm overnight in a shaking incubator. The overnight culture was washed twice with a fresh M9 minimal medium containing 2 ​% (w/v) glucose (1 ​L; 200 ​mL 5X M9 salts, 100 ​mL 20 ​% (w/v) glucose, 2 ​mL 1 ​M MgSO4, and 100 ​μL 1 ​M CaCl2) and then resuspended in the same medium before transferring to 25 ​mL of fresh M9 minimal medium in a 100-mL Erlenmeyer flask by adjusting the optical density at 600 ​nm to 0.1. Then, various concentrations of 1-dodecanol (Sigma-Aldrich), ranging from 0.0 to 2.0 ​mM, were added to the culture. The E. coli BL21(DE3) cultures were incubated at 37 ​°C, 180 ​rpm in a shaking incubator for 24 ​h. When the solvent overlay was used, 10 ​% (v/v) dodecane (Sigma-Aldrich) was added to the culture after the addition of 1-dodecanol and incubated in the same condition. The growth was determined for 24 ​h using a microplate reader (SpectraMax iD3, Molecular Devices) by measuring optical density at 600 ​nm every 3 ​h.

Optimisation of 1-dodecanol production in engineered E. coli

Optimisation of 1-dodecanol production using solvent overlay

The overnight cultures of E. coli BL21(DE3) carrying pET-Tes12-Sfp-CAR were grown in 10 ​mL LB broth containing 50 ​μg/μL kanamycin and washed twice with fresh M9 minimal medium. Then, the cultures were resuspended in the same medium before transferring to fresh 25 ​mL of M9 minimal medium in a 100-mL Erlenmeyer flask by adjusting the optical density at 600 ​nm to 0.1. M9 minimal medium was used for production experiments to allow calculation of 1-dodecanol and lauryl glucoside yields from glucose. The cultures were incubated at 37 ​°C, 180 ​rpm for 4 ​h before they were induced by adding IPTG (Vivantis) to make a final concentration of 0.5 ​mM IPTG. When a solvent overlay was used, 10 ​% (v/v) dodecane was added to the cultures at the same time with IPTG induction. The cultivation was then continued at 30 ​°C and 150 ​rpm for a total duration of 48 ​h. One mL of cultures was sampled every 24 ​h to measure bacterial growth and glucose consumption. After 48 ​h, 1 ​mL of cultures was collected for fermentation product analysis, and 1-dodecanol production was analysed by injecting the sample into Gas Chromatography-Flame Ionization Detector (GC-FID). In the absence of solvent overlay, dodecane extraction was performed. Dodecane (10 ​% (v/v)) was added to the cultures and then vortexed vigorously for 30 ​s, while in the presence of solvent overlay, the overlay was injected directly to GC-FID. Moreover, glucose consumption and fermentation products (acetate, formate and lactate) in the supernatant were analysed using High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), while ethanol was analysed using GC-FID.

Optimisation of 1-dodecanol production by varying IPTG concentrations

To optimise the IPTG concentration, the culture was prepared as previously described, but with variations in the IPTG concentrations (0.0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75 and 1.0 ​mM). A 10 ​% (v/v) dodecane overlay was also used to capture the produced 1-dodecanol. After 48 ​h, the solvent overlay was injected into GC-FID to quantify the amount of 1-dodecanol, while the supernatant was used to measure cell growth and to analyse glucose consumption and fermentation products.

Production of lauryl glucoside in engineered E. coli

Production of lauryl glucoside

For the production experiments, the E. coli strain engineered for 1-dodecanol production contained a single plasmid carrying the necessary biosynthetic genes, whereas the lauryl glucoside-producing strains carried two plasmids: one for 1-dodecanol synthesis and another for UDP-glycosyltransferase expression. Corresponding negative charontrol strains carried the GFP plasmid(s) only. The overnight cultures of E. coli BL21(DE3) carrying a pET-Tes12-Sfp-CAR and a plasmid containing different UDP-glycosyltransferases in LB broth containing 50 ​μg/μL kanamycin and 50 ​μg/μL spectinomycin were washed twice with a fresh M9 minimal medium, and then resuspended in the same medium before transferring to 250 ​mL of a fresh M9 minimal medium in a 500-mL Erlenmeyer flask. The optical density at 600 ​nm of all cultures was adjusted to 0.1 ​at the beginning of the experiment (0 ​h). The cultures were incubated as previously described and induced with 1.0 ​mM IPTG induction. The solvent overlay (10 ​% (v/v) dodecane) was also added at the time of induction. The sampling involved collecting 1 ​mL of cultures at 0, 24, and 48 ​h to measure bacterial growth and glucose consumption. A 1 ​mL sample of cultures at 0 ​h was collected to measure the 1-dodecanol concentration by GC-FID using dodecane extraction as described above, while the solvent overlay was injected directly for 48-h samples. One mL of 48-h cultures was collected for fermentation product analysis, and the rest of the culture was used for lauryl glucoside extraction. The lauryl glucoside was extracted from both extracellular (supernatant), and intracellular (cell pellet) fractions with chloroform:methanol (2:1). For the extracellular fraction, the supernatant was collected and 10 ​% (v/v) of chloroform:methanol (2:1) was added. The mixture was then vortexed vigorously for 10 ​s before the chloroform phase (lowest phase) was collected after the centrifugation at 4000×g for 3 ​min. For the intracellular fraction, cell pellets from 250 ​mL culture were resuspended with 10 ​mL ultrapure water, and 20 ​mL of chloroform:methanol (2:1) was added. The mixture was then vortexed vigorously for 10 ​s before the chloroform phase (lowest phase) was collected after centrifugation. All of extracts were lyophilised to eliminate the chloroform residue, after that lauryl glucoside appeared as white pellets was collected.

Feeding experiment

The feeding experiment was used to determine metabolic bottlenecks of the pathway. Overnight cultures of E. coli BL21(DE3) expressing only UDP-glycosyltransferases in 150 ​mL LB broth containing 50 ​μg/μL spectinomycin were used as precultures. The cultures were washed twice with a fresh M9 minimal medium containing 2 ​% (w/v) glucose and resuspended in 20 ​mL of the same medium before transferring to 250 ​mL of M9 minimal medium containing 2 ​% (w/v) glucose in a 500-mL Erlenmeyer flask. The optical density at 600 ​nm was adjusted to 0.1. IPTG (0.5 ​mM) was added to culture before incubating at 30 ​°C, 150 ​rpm for 2 ​h. After 2 ​h, 2.0 ​mM (an approximately doubled amount of 1-dodecanol as compared to the production from the cell) 1-dodecanol was added, then the cultures were continuously cultivated in the same condition for the total duration of 48 ​h. The sampling and lauryl glucoside extraction were similar to the production of lauryl glucoside, but the 1-dodecanol was extracted from culture using 10 ​% (v/v) dodecane extraction method.

Quantification and analysis of 1-dodecanol, lauryl glucoside, and other metabolites

1-Dodecanol and ethanol

GC-FID was used to quantify 1-dodecanol and ethanol compared with the chemical standards; 1-dodecanol (Sigma-Aldrich) and ethanol (RCI labscan). One-μL samples were directly injected to an Agilent 7890B GC, 7693 autosampler and G4513A injector system equipped with HP-5 column (Agilent 1909J1-419, 3m ​× ​320 ​μm ​× ​0.25 ​μm, 7 inch). The split ratio of the injected sample was at 100:1. The samples were then flamed at 250 ​°C and passed through the column in the oven with an initial temperature set at 40 ​°C. This temperature was held for 3 ​min before it was increased to the final temperature of 250 ​°C at the rate of 35 ​°C/min. Helium was used as a carrier gas at a rate of 5.6 ​mL/min. The detector was set at a temperature of 250 ​°C. The lignite system used air (400 ​mL/min), hydrogen gas (30 ​mL/min), and nitrogen gas (25 ​mL/min), all at a temperature of 250 ​°C [18].

Lauryl glucoside and other metabolites

An Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity II HPLC (Agilent Tech., Santa Clara, CA, USA) was used to quantify lauryl glucoside, glucose, acetate, formate, and lactate compared with chemical standards, lauryl glucoside (Biosynth), D-glucose (VWR), acetate (RCI labscan), formate (Fisher Chemical), and lactate (Lobachemie). For lauryl glucoside, the pellets were resuspended in 500 ​μL of mobile phase, and 50 ​μL of the samples were injected. The HPLC was equipped with EC-C18 column (Agilent InfinityLab Poroshell 120, 4.6 ​× ​150 ​mm 4-μm) and refractive index (RI) detector (Agilent G1362A). The mobile phase was 75:25 acetronitirle:water flowing at 0.8 ​mL/min with the column temperature set of 45 ​°C [19]. For glucose, 10-μL samples were injected to an HPLC equipped with NH2P-50 4E column (Shodex Asahipak 4.6 ​× ​250 ​mm 5-μm) and RI detector (Agilent G1362A). After injection, the samples were passed through and eluted from the column with 75:25 acetronitirle:water flowing at 0.8 ​mL/min with the column temperature set of 30 ​°C. For fermentation products, 50 ​μL of the samples were injected into an HPLC equipped with Inertsil ODS-3 C18 column (GL Science, 4.6 ​× ​150 ​mm 5-μm) and UV detector (Agilent G7115A). After injection, the samples were passed through the column with 20 ​mM KH2PO4 elution buffer with a pH of 2.4. The flow rate was adjusted to 0.8 ​mL/min, and the column temperature was maintained at 25 ​°C [20].

An Agilent 6130 Single Quadrupole LC-MS system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), coupled with a RESTEK Ultra C-18 column (4.6 ​× ​150 ​mm, 5 ​μm), was employed to verify the production of lauryl glucoside [21]. Samples (10 ​μL) were injected and separated using a mobile phase consisting of acetonitrile and water (60:40, v/v) at a flow rate of 0.6 ​mL/min. The column was maintained at 30 ​°C throughout the analysis. Mass spectrometry was carried out using an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in negative mode, with single ion monitoring (SIM) at m/z 348.48, corresponding to lauryl glucoside. The ion source temperature was maintained at 300 ​°C. The capillary voltage was set to 3.5 ​kV, and the fragmentor voltage was 135 ​V. The nebulizer gas pressure was adjusted to 45 psi.

Statistical analysis

Averages ​± ​SD from three biological replicates were used to represent the results in this work. A two-sided Student's t-test was used to determine significant differences when comparing 2 treatments, and one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD post hoc test was used when comparing more than 2 treatments. All statistical tests were performed on R Studio. The significant differences are indicated by letters and asterisks (∗p-value ≤0.05, ∗∗ p-value ≤0.01, ∗∗∗p-value ≤0.005)

As a solvent overlay would be used in the following experiment, the toxicity was investigated both in the absence and presence of 10 ​% (v/v) dodecane as a solvent overlay. Production of lauryl glucoside in engineered E. coli

After successfully determining the optimal conditions for 1-dodecanol production, they were further used for lauryl glucoside production. In this study, all six UDP-glycosyltransferases were successfully demonstrated to produce lauryl glucoside in E. coli BL21(DE3) carrying two plasmids: one carrying the genes for 1-dodecanol production and the other encoding each UDP-glycosyltransferase (Fig. 5A and B). Among six UDP-glycosyltransferases, the MtH2 showed the highest lauryl glucoside conversion efficiency compared to the other UDP-glycosyltransferases, with a titre and a yield of 0.72 ​± ​0.07 ​mg/L and 0.06 ​± ​0.004 ​mg/g glucose, respectively, secreted in the supernatant, and 0.12 ​± ​0.01 ​mg/L and 0.011 ​± ​0.002 ​mg/g glucose, respectively, in the cell pellet (Fig. 5). The lauryl glucoside conversion was confirmed using targeted LC-MS, showing a peak at approximately a retention time of 13 ​min with a molecular mass of [M ​− ​H]- m/z ​= ​348.5, matching the lauryl glucoside standard (Supplementary Fig. S1). Discussion

Microbial cell factories stand out as one of the alternatives for high-value chemical production from renewable substrates. However, the reliance on native metabolic pathways limits the production of desired non-native chemicals with no existing biosynthetic pathways. These limitations can be overcome by the design of novel metabolic pathways to extend the range of desirable chemicals [1,4,5]. Lauryl glucoside is an important non-ionic surfactant used in many cosmetic products. The production of lauryl glucoside relies on chemical reactions of petroleum- and plant-based substrates as it cannot be naturally synthesised by any microorganisms [[7], [8], [9]]. Therefore, in this work, a newly designed lauryl glucoside biosynthetic pathway was implemented and characterised in engineered E. coli. Prior to its implementation, the toxicity of 1-dodecanol was investigated using a concentration range shown to be toxic to E. coli cell membrane [9]. The toxicity of this chemical is due to its hydroxyl group that can attach to the cell membrane, consequently increasing membrane fluidity or causing leakage [3,9]. Interestingly, the results in this work showed that 1-dodecanol did not pose toxicity. As the concentrations of 1-dodecanol increased, E. coli appeared to grow well. This might be due to the secondary structure formation of 1-dodecanol resulting from its amphiphilic properties, with a C-12 chain that is hydrophobic and a hydroxyl group that is hydrophilic. As the concentration of 1-dodecanol increases beyond its solubility limit, it starts forming secondary structures or micelles. These micelles consist of hydrophobic C-12 chains clustering together, with hydrophilic hydroxyl groups facing outward towards the solution [9,24]. This aligns with a previous study discussing the toxicity of 1-dodecanol to E. coli cells. At low concentrations, 1-dodecanol is in a free form that can attach to the cell membrane, increasing membrane fluidity. However, at concentrations above 0.7 ​g/L, small droplets of 1-dodecanol may cluster together, reducing the attachment of hydroxyl groups to E. coli cell membranes, thereby reducing toxicity [9]. The use of solvent overlay was expected to reduce 1-dodecanol toxicity and also to prevent the of volatile products during the cultivation because 1-dodecanol was captured in the solvent layer above the culture during production, reducing its presence in the culture medium and thereby decreasing toxicity [3,25].

The 1-dodecanol biosynthetic pathway, involving three heterologous enzymes namely Tes12, Sfp, and CAR, successfully yielded 1-dodecanol in engineered E. coli BL21(DE3). However, the produced 1-dodecanol titre was considered low. In a previous study, Tes12 was used in a pathway for fatty alcohol production and it was found that even though dodecanoate was produced from Tes12, the fatty acid with 14 carbon atoms were also present [26] suggesting its broad specificity. Moreover, native fatty acid synthesis in E. coli typically synthesises fatty acids with 16 or 18 carbon atoms rather than with 12 or 14 atoms suggesting the presence of a native thioesterase specific for longer chain length substrates [27]. These fatty acids can also be converted by CAR, the other enzyme in the pathway responsible for converting fatty acids to aldehydes, which is less specific to substrates [28], which led to the unspecific production of different chain-length fatty alcohols that may result in the low production of 1-dodecanol. Interestingly, the GC-FID chromatograms of E. coli BL21(DE3) carrying pET-Tes12-Sfp-CAR also showed minor peaks in addition to the 1-dodecanol peak, indicating the presence of other fatty alcohols, although their amounts appeared to be much lower.

After the 1-dodecanol biosynthetic pathway was successfully implemented and the toxicity of 1-dodecanol was investigated, the next step was to optimise the production to increase the chances of successful lauryl glucoside conversion. The production of 1-dodecanol was optimised using two different methods – the use of solvent overlay to capture the product and optimisation of IPTG concentrations to tune the gene expression.

In this study, dodecane was used as a solvent overlay to prevent the loss of 1-dodecanol that is considered as a volatile compound [3,25]. A previous study showed that a solvent overlay can retain approximately 80 ​% of total 1-octanol, a volatile fatty alcohol, in the solvent phase [3]. Here, the use of a solvent overlay resulted in a 42 ​% higher titre of 1-dodecanol compared to the treatment without the solvent overlay and was therefore used in subsequent optimisations. The optimisation of IPTG concentrations is one of the commonly used methods for improving plasmid expression, by which IPTG can maintain the expression at an appropriate level [22,23]. IPTG concentrations ranging from 0 to 1.0 ​mM were used, corresponding to the concentrations typically used to minimise metabolic burden and to avoid its toxicity to E. coli cells [29]. This study showed that the titre and yield of 1-dodecanol increased when IPTG concentration increased, with 1.0 ​mM being the most optimal concentration, giving the highest titre and yield of 1-dodecanol. This aligns with a previous study that optimisation of IPTG concentrations (ranging from 0.1 to 1.0 ​mM) resulted in increased 1-octanol production upon the increase of IPTG concentrations with 0.75 ​mM being the optimal concentration. Moreover, it should be noted that other factors could also influence protein expression, such as the timing of inducer addition and the protein expression temperature. In this study, the cultivation was consistently performed under the standard conditions of induction at 4 ​h after inoculation and at a protein expression temperature of 30 ​°C, following previously reported protocols [3]. Given that our previous work on 1-octanol biosynthesis showed that changing the enzyme source improved production [3], it is likely that the insufficient activity of Tes12, Sfp, or CAR contributes to the limited 1-dodecanol yield in this study, although differences in expression levels among these enzymes may also play a role.

The lauryl glucoside biosynthetic pathway, modified from octyl glucoside pathway [3], encounters a significant challenge as it requires a UDP-glycosyltransferase to catalyse O-glycosylation in the final reaction step. This reaction transfers a glucoside group from UDP-glucose, readily available in microbial cells, to the hydroxyl group of 1-dodecanol [3,10,30]. Our previous study implemented the use of computational tools to identify UDP-glycosyltransferases that were active towards 1-dodecanol and the candidate was validated in an in vivo feeding experiment. RO3G from Rhizopus delemar RA 99–880 was found to catalyse this reaction successfully, resulting in lauryl glucoside synthesis [10]. Moreover, in another study, octyl glucoside was aimed to be synthesised using various UDP-glycosyltransferase enzymes reported to catalyse medium-chain fatty alcohols. These enzymes included the GT4 from Actinidia deliciosa, the At from Arabidopsis thaliana, the MtG1 and MtH2 from Medicago truncatula, and the Vv from Vitis vinifera. However, only the MtH2 could convert 1-octanol to octyl glucoside [3]. The result demonstrated that the engineered strain yielded 377.37 ​mg/L of 1-octanol and the introduction of the MtH2 UDP-glycosyltransferase into this strain resulted in the conversion of 1-octanol to octyl glucoside, achieving a maximum titre of 214 ​mg/L [3]. Interestingly, this work employed the same MtH2 enzyme, suggesting that MtH2 may exhibit higher specificity towards shorter-chain fatty alcohols such as 1-octanol. Here, all these potential UDP-glycosyltransferases were characterised for their C12-active activity, and all were able to catalyse the lauryl glucoside pathway with variation in their activity. The MtH2 provided the highest lauryl glucoside titre, even though the concentration of produced lauryl glucoside was considered low. As the concentration of the substrates is associated with the flux of the metabolic pathway [3,31], the bottleneck causing the low efficiency of lauryl glucoside conversion was anticipated to be attributed to the low concentration of pathway substrate precursor such as 1-dodecanol. In general, an excess of upstream metabolites in a pathway enables a flux push towards the downstream reactions and final product synthesis [32]. In this study, the 1-dodecanol feeding experiment showed that doubling the concentration of 1-dodecanol from its intracellular production resulted in up to an 18-fold increase in lauryl glucoside titre from MtH2-expressing strain. These results confirm that the increased supply of 1-dodecanol (2.0 ​mM) could create a metabolic flux push towards lauryl glucoside production. One key observation is that although the MtH2-expressing strain produced the highest lauryl glucoside titre, a relatively high level of 1-dodecanol remained. This suggests that most of the substrate was not converted to product but was likely lost due to volatility. Interestingly, the results showed that the control strain, without the expression of heterologous UDP-glycosyltransferase enzymes, showed lauryl glucoside synthesis. This could be a result of native glycosyltransferases in E. coli BL21(DE3) that are often broad specific. They are mainly responsible for essential cellular processes, such as O-antigen biosynthesis in lipopolysaccharides [33], capsular polysaccharide formation [34], and peptidoglycan assembly [35]. However, due to their low substrate specificity, these enzymes can also catalyse the conversion of 1-dodecanol to lauryl glucoside, resulting in low concentration of lauryl glucoside [10].

The fermentation profiles of all strains were also monitored to understand the direction of carbon flux with and without the introduced pathways. High levels of fermentation products typically indicate carbon flux diversion away from the desirable products. In this study, acetate accumulation was significantly reduced, suggesting that carbon flux was redirected primarily towards lauryl glucoside production. However, a considerable amount of acetate was still detected and the correlation between reduced acetate levels and increased product formation was not explicit, likely due to the low titres of lauryl glucoside achieved under the present conditions. Future optimisation could involve knocking out the genes responsible for acetate formation to further enhance lauryl glucoside production. This strategy has been implemented previously in other biosynthetic pathways and cell factories. For example, a knockout of ackA gene involved in acetate synthesis showed to produce 1.51-fold higher glycolate compared to the corresponding strain without gene deletion [36]. Applying a similar strategy in our lauryl glucoside-producing strains could help redirect more carbon flux towards the target compound, potentially increasing its yield. Moreover, a recent work has demonstrated that the production of recombinant proteins increased for fivefold compared to the wild-type strain when the ackA gene was deleted from the E. coli BL21(DE3) strain [37].

Finally, in this study, we have optimised the production of 1-dodecanol from engineered E. coli and characterised the lauryl glucoside biosynthetic pathway using UDP-glycosyltransferases from six different sources. The implementation of this newly designed pathway allowed the synthesis of lauryl glucoside with documented fermentation profiles. These findings not only offer a promising alternative to the traditional chemical synthesis of lauryl glucoside through microbial production but also provide a foundation for enhancing production yields and improving our understanding of metabolic flux in engineered strains.

Conclusions

In this study, a novel lauryl glucoside biosynthetic pathway was implemented and characterised in E. coli BL21(DE) to offer a more sustainable alternative to traditional production methods. The experiment was divided into two parts. The initial focus was on optimising the production of the lauryl glucoside precursor, 1-dodecanol, to enhance the likelihood of its conversion to lauryl glucoside. It was found that the use of dodecane as a solvent overlay and 1.0 ​mM IPTG induction were the optimal condition, achieving the highest yield of 1-dodecanol. While the focus shifted to lauryl glucoside production using UDP-glycosyltransferases from various organisms. Among the potential candidates, MtH2 showed the highest lauryl glucoside conversion activity. Altogether, this study demonstrates an alternative method for producing lauryl glucoside, an important non-ionic surfactant in the cosmetic industry, using engineered E. coli with the novel biosynthetic pathway.
