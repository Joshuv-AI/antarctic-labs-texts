Luminal LPS is transported across murine small intestine by a caveolin-1 dependent endocytic mechanism induced by chronic feeding of a Western-style diet
Authors: Yasutada Akiba, Nikita Pokala, Jonathan D. Kaunitz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetabolic endotoxemia is an important risk factor for the metabolic syndrome (MetS). We report that lipopolysaccharides (LPS) are transcellularly transported from the jejunal lumen during long-chain fatty acids (LCFA) absorption via caveolin (Cav)-1-mediated endocytosis. Here, we examined the effects of diets of varying fatty acid (FA) composition on the induction of MetS and LPS transport. We fed wild-type (WT) and Cav-1 knockout (KO) mice with a standard diet (SD), or one of three high-fat/high-sucrose diets enriched with saturated LCFA, medium-chain FA, or polyunsaturated FA, measuring FITC-LPS or FITC-dextran (FD4) mucosa-to-serosa transport in the jejunum and colon. MetS rapidly developed in the male WT LCFA diet group with increased portal endotoxemia, less so in WT other dietary groups or in KO mice. Interestingly, in male WT LCFA, increased basal FITC-LPS and FD4 transport was observed without electrical resistance change. Lipid rafts, CD36, or Src kinase inhibitors abolished LCFA diet-induced increase of LPS/FD4 transport, with intracellular vesicular uptake of dextran and LPS in male WT LCFA diet group observed. Chronic feeding of a Western-style diet tonically activates endocytic LPS transport in the small intestine via a Cav-1-dependent mechanism, leading to MetS. This novel mechanism reveals several novel targets for inhibiting LPS uptake.

Introduction

The incidence of metabolic syndrome (MetS), including obesity, insulin resistance, visceral obesity, hyperlipidaemia, and fatty liver, is increasing not only in Western countries but also in much of the developing world, with a consequent increase in medical expenditures and mortality.1,2 This increase has been attributed primarily to environmental factors, particularly the consumption of a diet enriched in saturated fats and simple sugars.3 To date, the mechanistic link between diet and MetS has remained controversial. Here, we propose the novel hypothesis that luminal long-chain fatty acids LCFA activate the transcellular uptake of luminal lipopolysaccharides LPS via a pathway dependent on the lipid sensor cluster-of-differentiation 36 (CD36), the tyrosine kinase Src, and the caveolae coat protein caveolin-1 (Cav-1). All these proteins are essential for transendothelial particulate transport.4 Furthermore, chronic feeding with a high-fat high-sucrose (HFHS) diet enriched in saturated LCFA tonically activates the system, transporting luminal LPS independently of luminal lipids. Moreover, this system delivers LPS to the portal vein (PV) in addition to chylomicrons,5 in part bypassing endogenous endotoxin-neutralizing defensive systems. We propose that this lipid-activated transcytotic LPS transport system provides a novel explanation for the strong association of the MetS with consumption of a Western-style diet.

One important aspect of the pathogenesis of MetS is metabolic endotoxemia, also known as post-prandial endotoxemia, in which low levels of circulating LPS induce chronic low-grade inflammation in adipose tissues and the liver, leading to MetS.6,7 Since the major reservoir of LPS is the gut lumen, it is most likely that LPS enters the circulation through the intestinal mucosa from the intestinal lumen. Since LPS is a macromolecule (15–20 kDa), LPS is generally believed to enter the circulation through damaged gut mucosa via paracellular gaps or mucosal defects such as ulcerations.8,9 Many studies have focused on increased paracellular intestinal permeability, termed the ‘leaky gut’,10,11 where macromolecules, including LPS or fluorescence-labeled dextran may traverse the intestinal epithelial layers through widened paracellular spaces. High-fat diet feeding increases circulating LPS levels with disrupted epithelial tight junction proteins, including zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) and occludin,6 which is mimicked by chronic systemic infusion of low-dose LPS.12 These results suggest that the consumption of a high-fat diet enhances the passage of LPS via increased gut paracellular permeability. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms of LPS transport through the intact intestinal epithelium remain undefined, primarily owing to a dearth of direct measurements of LPS transport in intact tissue.

Since more than 99% of the gut microbiota are present in the colon,13 LPS is primarily found in the colonic lumen. Although it is tempting to speculate that the bulk of LPS entry occurs in the colon due to the enormous concentration gradient between the lumen and blood, few studies have addressed the segmental uptake of LPS. Furthermore, foods may contain LPS in quantities sufficient to stimulate host immune cells;14 moreover, acute consumption of a high-fat meal increases venous LPS levels within ~30 min in humans,15 suggesting that luminal LPS levels in the upper small intestine in humans are sufficient to induce metabolic endotoxemia. In contrast, since rodents are coprophagic, the LPS content in the upper small intestine of rodents is similar to that in the cecum and colon.5 Nonetheless, LPS transport in response to dietary interventions has not been studied in the intact small intestinal and colonic mucosae.

We have reported that LPS mucosa-to-serosa (m-to-s) movement across the intact jejunal mucosa of rats mounted in Ussing chambers is mediated by lipid rafts and CD36-dependent pathways during luminal LCFA exposure, without observable mucosal injury or changes of transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER).5 These results suggest that LPS is transported via transcellular mechanisms during LCFA absorption. Furthermore, LPS transport during LCFA exposure is regulated by caveolae-mediated endocytosis, as demonstrated by Cav-1 knockout (KO) mice,16 suggesting that LPS can be intracellularly transported during lipid absorption. Cav-1 KO mice have compromised fatty acid absorption17 and are resistant to high-fat diet-induced obesity,18 although these findings remain controversial.19 Nevertheless, a Cav-1 genetic polymorphism is associated with a greater risk of MetS with greater saturated fatty acid intake,20 suggesting the involvement of Cav-1 in the development of MetS.

In contrast, we have found that medium-chain fatty acids MCFA facilitate LPS transport via Cav-1-independent, clathrin-mediated endocytosis,16 suggesting that the FA structure may differentially affect the mode of LPS transport. Since the FA structure affects the development of obesity21 and acute LPS permeability,22 we hypothesized that the FA structure may influence the development of MetS, particularly with respect to LPS transport activity in the small intestine.

We thus studied the effects of the chronic feeding of diets of varying FA structures, including diets enriched in saturated LCFA, MCFA, or polyunsaturated FA (PUFA) on the development of MetS and on LPS transport in the jejunum of wild-type (WT) and Cav-1 KO mice. Our results support the hypothesis that the consumption of LCFA specifically enhances LPS transport, inducing MetS.

Materials and methods

Animals

Male and female C57BL/6J WT and global Cav-1 KO mice (Cav-1tm1Mls/J and B6.Cg-Cav−1tm1Mls/J) were obtained from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) or Inotiv (Lafayette, IN, USA) and bred in our animal facility. WT (or littermates) and KO mice were fed a standard pellet diet and water ad libitum before the diet study started. All studies were performed with the approval of the Veterans Affairs Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee.

Feeding of specialized diets

Mice at 6–8 wks of age were fed a standard diet (SD, 3.7%w sucrose, 5%w fat, Laboratory Rodent Diet 5001, LabDiet, St. Luis, MO), or one of 3 HFHS diets (34%w sucrose, 21%w fat) enriched in saturated LCFA (LCD, TD.88137, Envigo Teklad Diet, Madison, WI), MCFA (MCD, TD.140297) and PUFA (PUD, TD.200495) for up to 6 wks. We used HFHS diets mimicking Western diets to facilitate the development of MetS.23 The compositions of the major dietary nutritional components are listed in Table 1. Daily food consumption was monitored at 3 and 7 d after feeding commenced by measuring the differences in diet weight (g)/day and dividing by the number of animals to calculate average food consumption/animal/day. The mice were fasted for 2 hr before the experiments to empty the stomach on the day of the experiments.

Tissue and blood sampling

Under isoflurane anesthesia, the abdomen was opened, and portal venous (PV) and arterial (A) blood from the abdominal aorta was collected, followed by euthanasia by thoracotomy. Blood glucose levels were immediately measured with glucose test strips by a glucose meter (Accu-Chek meters, Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA). The remaining blood was mixed with 1-µl of EDTA (5M), followed by centrifugation at 5,000xg for 5 min, after which the plasma was stored at −80°C until use. The jejunum (~10 cm from the ligament of Treitz) and proximal colon (PC; ~3 cm from the ileocecal junction) were removed for Ussing chamber experiments as described below. Whole liver and bilateral perigonadal fat tissues were removed and weighed. Small blocks of jejunum, liver and fat tissues were fixed with 10% formalin solution and Zamboni's fixative for histology, stored in RNAlater solution (Qiagen, Germantown, MD, USA) for RNA extraction, or stored at −80°C for protein analysis.

Reagents and chemicals

Sulfosuccinimidyl oleate (SSO) and PP2 were obtained from Cayman Chemicals (Ann Arbor, MI, USA). Alexa-488-dextran 3 kDa (488-D3) and Alexa-568-LPS (568-LPS) were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). Oleic acid (OA), taurocholic acid (TCA), and fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-conjugated LPS (FITC-LPS; from E. coli O55:B5), FITC-dextran 4 kDa (FD4), methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD), indomethacin (IND), 4',6-diamidino−2-phenylindole (DAPI) and other chemicals were purchased from Sigma Chemical (St. Louis, MO, USA). IND was dissolved in ethanol. OA was added to TCA-containing phosphate buffer saline (PBS, pH 7.4), followed by vigorous vortexing just prior to performing the experiments. SSO and PP2 were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to make a stock solution. All other chemicals were dissolved in distilled water. The concentrations of OA and TCA used in this study was based on postprandial luminal concentrations of free fatty acids 4.05–75 mM24 and of bile acids 3.7–12.2 mM25 in humans. The concentrations of FITC-LPS (10 µg/ml) used in this study was based on our previous observation, in which LPS levels measured by the limulus amoebocyte lysate (LAL) test were ~8 µg/ml in the jejunal luminal wash in rats.5

Transport measurements

Ussing chamber measurements

Ussing chambers were prepared as previously described.5,16 In brief, mucosa–submucosa preparations of the collected jejunum and PC described above were prepared by muscle stripping using fine forceps under a stereomicroscope. Three to six preparations for the jejunum and two preparations for the PC were prepared from one animal. The tissue was then mounted in an Ussing chamber system (Physiologic Instruments, San Diego, CA, USA). Macroscopically recognized Peyer's patches were eliminated from the chambered segments. For the jejunum, Krebs-Ringer solution bubbled with 95% O2–5% CO2 to maintain the pH at 7.4 was used for the serosal bathing solution, whereas the luminal bathing solution (Krebs buffer solution) contained NaCl, 136; KCl, 2.6; CaCl2, 1.8; HEPES, 10 (pH 7.0); mannitol, 11 (mM); and bubbled with 100% O2. For the PC, Krebs–Ringer solution was used for both baths. The tissues were short-circuited by a voltage clamp (VCC MC6, Physiologic Instruments), and the short-circuit current (Isc) was continuously measured. Tissue conductance and TEER (ohm·cm2) were determined every 20 s to assess tissue integrity during LPS, FD4, and fatty acid exposure. The current was recorded by the DataQ system (Physiologic Instruments). IND (10 μM) was added to the serosal bath to eliminate the effects of endogenous prostaglandin production. The tissues were stabilized for ~30 min for the jejunum and ~60 min for the PC before the addition of the test chemicals. TEER was reported as the average of 2 channels per animal.

Transport of LPS and FD4

FITC-LPS and FD4 movements m-to-s were assessed by measuring the FITC fluorescence intensity in the serosal solution using a multi-mode microplate reader (Synergy-2, BioTek Instruments, Inc., Winooski, VT, USA) as previously described.5,16 After tissue stabilization, time was set as t = 0 min. Fifty microliters of the serosal solution was collected at t = 0 min as a blank sample, followed by collection at t = 15, 30 and 45 min. At t = 0 min, FITC-LPS (10 µg/ml) or FD4 (0.1 mM) was added into the mucosal solution. Vehicle (PBS alone) or OA (10 mM) with TCA (0.1 mM) in PBS was added into the mucosal solution at t = 15 min. The tissues were pretreated with drugs or vehicle (DMSO, 0.1%) 10 min before the addition of FITC-LPS or FD4 (i.e., at t = −10 min). The collected serosal solutions were stored at −20°C until use. The value of background at t = 0 min was subtracted, and the serosal LPS or FD4 appearance was expressed as Δ[FITC-LPS] (ng/ml) or Δ[FD4] (nM). We have compared LPS measurements among the LAL test, FITC measurements, and TLR-4 reporter cell assay,5 confirming that intact, active LPS appeared in the serosal solution. After the last sampling at 45 min, glucose (Glu, 10 mM) was added into the mucosal solution (only for the jejunum), followed by the addition of bethanechol (1 mM) into the serosal solution. These controls confirmed the integrity of epithelial glucose absorption (electrogenic sodium-dependent glucose transporter 1 SGLT-1 activity) and muscarinic receptor-mediated epithelial chloride secretion, respectively.

Drug treatment in Ussing chambers

The following drugs were added to the mucosal bath 10 min before FITC-LPS or FD4: the lipid raft inhibitor MβCD (1 mM), the CD36 inhibitor SSO (0.1 mM), as previously described,16 and the Src kinase inhibitor PP2 (1 µM).26

Other measurements

Real-time PCR measurements of transport-related proteins

The jejunal mucosa was separated from muscle layers using sharp dissection under a stereomicroscope. RT-PCR was performed as previously described27 with primers for mouse zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1; Tjp1) forward (F) 5′-CACTGCCAGCTTTAAGCCTC-3, reverse (R) 5′-CCACAGCATCAGTTTCGGGT-3′; occludin (Ocln) F 5′-GGCAAGCGATCATACCCAGA-3′, R 5′-AGGTGGATATTCCCTGACCC-3′; tricellulin (Marveld2) F 5′-TGCCTGACTACGTGGCAAAA−3′, R 5′-TGAGGTAATCGCAACGCTCC-3′; claudin-2 (Cldn2) F 5′-GAAAGGACGGCTCCGTTTTCTA-3′, R 5′-GTCGCACACTCCATCCAGAG-3′; Cldn7 F 5′-CATTGTGGCAGGTCTTGCT-3′, R 5′-CCCCAGCTCACACGTATTCC-3′; Cldn15 F 5′-CACATACTTGCTGGAGCCTG-3′, R 5′-GTCACTCTCATCCGAGGTGG-3′; CD36 F 5′- GATCGGAACTGTGGGCTCAT-3′, R 5′-CTGCTGTTCTTTGCCACGTC-3′; Src F 5′-CGCCTCACTACCGTATGTCC-3′, R 5′-CCAGTTTCTCGTGCCTCAGT-3′; Cav-1 F 5′-CGTAGACTCCGAGGGACATC-3′, R 5′-CCCAGATGTGCAGGAAGGAG-3′, and for β-actin F 5′-GTGGGCCGCCCTAGGCAC CA-3′, R 5′-TGGCCTTAGGGTTCAGGGGG-3′, as internal control. The expression level was presented as the fold induction per 103 copies of β-actin by ΔCt method.

PV LPS levels

LPS levels in the PV plasma were measured using a LAL test (Pierce Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit, Pierce Biotechnology, Rockford, IL) according to the manufacturer's protocol, as previously reported.5

Lipid droplet staining

Intracellular lipid droplets were stained with LipidSpot™488 (Biotium, Fremont, CA, USA) on cryostat sections of fixed liver and jejunum according to the manufacturer’s protocol and counterstained with DAPI. Images were taken with x20 objective with a laser confocal microscope (LM710, Zeiss, Germany), and the positive area (µm2) was quantified using Photoshop (Adobe, San Jose, CA, USA).

In vivo laser confocal microscopy of LPS and dextran movement

In vivo imaging of fluorescent probes exposed to the jejunal or colonic mucosa was performed using an upright laser confocal microscope (LM710, Zeiss) as previously described.16 Briefly, under isoflurane anesthesia, the proximal jejunum (~3 cm; ~1 cm caudal from the Treitz ligament to the distal ligament) or the PC (~3 cm from the ileocecal junction) was removed. Then, an intestinal loop was created by ligation with nylon ligatures at both ends of the segment and filled with 0.5 ml of prewarmed phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4). The anterior wall of the jejunum or PC was cut open with an electrocautery. The mucosa was placed on a custom-fabricated small metal support table covered with watertight silicone adherent (Silly Putty™, Crayola, Easton, PA, USA). After rinsing the mucosal surface with warm PBS, the silicone adherent was placed on the outline of the mucosa to make a ‘dam’ over the mucosa, followed by filling the dam with 50 µl of test solution in PBS. The dam was covered with a cover glass (Matsunami, Osaka, Japan). The fluorescent probes 488-D3 (0.1 mM) and 568-LPS (10 µg/ml) in PBS were applied to the mucosa. The fluorescence probes were excited with a 488 or 565 nm laser under a ×10 or ×63 oil immersion objective lens. The laser power was set to 10%, and the digital gain was set at ~900. The images were digitally captured at ~5-min intervals for up to ~45 min. For higher magnification, the digital zoom was set to 3.0x. At the end of the experiments, the animals were euthanized by terminal exsanguination.

Statistics

The values are expressed as the means ± SEM The number of animals in each experimental group was n = 6 otherwise noticed. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad® Prism 10 (La Jolla, CA, USA) using one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons. Differences were considered significant when the P-values were < 0.05.

Results

Overall experimental strategy

Although we originally hypothesized that chronic feeding with LCD induces MetS by increasing LPS uptake in the small intestine, we did not anticipate that the model paracellular permeability marker dextran is also transported by the transcytotic pathway. Furthermore, we did not predict that mice that developed MetS absorbed LPS in the absence of perfused luminal LCFA Moreover, we did not predict that there were sex differences in the development of MetS. We predicted that, in contrast to the presence of a dedicated lipid uptake mechanism in the small intestine, LPS uptake was unlikely to occur in the colon. Furthermore, since we predicted that enhanced LPS transport was caused rather than a consequence of MetS, we measured the time course of LPS entry into the PV. We thus describe the following experiments in this order: (1) effect of the four diets on the development of MetS in WT and Cav-1 KO male and female mice; (2) effect of the four diets on epithelial barrier function (LPS transport, TEER, tight junction protein expression, and time course of PV LPS levels); (3) comparison of the expression of tight junction and endocytosis proteins between male and female mice to help explain the observed sex differences; (4) Contribution of lipid rafts, CD36, and Src kinase to LPS transport in the jejunum; and (5) in vivo confocal fluorescent microscopy of the uptake of LPS and dextran in the jejunum and colon to corroborate the indirect findings listed above.

Metabolic parameters in WT and Cav-1 KO mice fed different diets

Body weight

To determine if the mice developed MetS, we first compared the effects of diets of different fat compositions (Table 1) on metabolic parameters in WT and Cav-1 KO male and female mice after 6 wks. Since body weight is an important parameter, we measured this parameter in all the groups. In male WT mice, body weight increased faster than in the LCD group, compared with the SD, MCD, and PUD groups (Figure 1A). Consumption of MCD also increased body weight faster than in the SD and PUD groups after 4 wks of feeding. In female WT mice, consumption of LCD increased the body weight after 4 wks of feeding compared with all other groups (Figure 1B). In male Cav−1 KO mice, consumption of LCD accelerated body weight increase after 3 wks of feeding compared with all other diets (Figure 1C), whereas among female Cav-1 KO, all groups presented similar weight curves (Figure 1D). Comparing male and female WT LCD groups revealed that body weight changes were more marked in males than in females (Figure 1E), suggesting that males are more susceptible to diet-induced metabolic changes. Comparing WT and Cav-1 KO groups, body weight changes were greater in the WT LCD group than in the Cav-1 KO LCD group both in males and females (Figure 1E), suggesting that Cav-1 KO mice are relatively resistant to LCD-induced body weight increases, which is consistent with the findings of a previous study.18 Food consumption in male and female WT mice was greater in the LCD, MCD and PUD groups compared with the SD groups, with no difference among the HFHS diet groups (Figure 1F), possibly due to the increased palatability of high-sucrose diets. These results also suggest that MCD and PUD have fewer effects on body weight increase than LCD.

Blood glucose, perigonadal fat, and liver weight

Since other criteria of MetS include fasting glucose levels, visceral fat, and liver fatty infiltration, we measured these parameters in all groups. Arterial glucose levels were higher in the LCD groups of male and female WT mice than in the SD group, with MCD and PUD levels being the same as those in the SD group (Figure 2A, B). In contrast, there was no difference in glucose levels among the groups of male and female Cav-1 KO mice (Figure 2C, D). Perigonadal fat weights, a measure of visceral fat in rodents,28 were greater in the LCD and MCD groups but not in the PUD groups of male and female WT mice, compared with the SD group (Figure 2E, F), whereas there was no difference in perigonadal fat weights between all groups of male and female Cav-1 KO mice (Figure 2G, H). Liver weights were greater in the male WT LCD group, compared with the SD group (Figure 2I). In female WT mice, liver weights were greater in the LCD and PUD groups (Figure 2J). In male Cav-1 KO mice, liver weights were greater in the LCD and MCD groups, with no differences in female Cav-1 KO mice (Figure 2K, L). These results suggest that metabolic changes predominantly occur in the male WT LCD groups compared with the male WT MCD and PUD groups, all the female WT groups, and all the male and female Cav-1 KO groups.

Liver histology

To confirm fatty infiltration of the liver, we assessed liver histology and the presence of lipid droplets in the liver of the male mice with HE stained sections (Figure 3A). In male WT mice, the consumption of LCD increased the number of vacuoles, representing lipid droplets, in hepatocytes. In contrast, lipid droplets appeared less frequently in the WT MCD group, and were almost absent in the WT PUD group. In male Cav-1 KO mice, lipid droplets were observed in the LCD and MCD groups but not in the PUD groups, with a much lower aggregate area than that in the male WT LCD groups. Lipid droplet staining quantitatively confirmed these changes (Figure 3B, C). These results also suggest that Cav-1 KO mice are resistant to metabolic changes during LCD consumption, and that the consumption of MCD and PUD less effectively induces fatty liver than in LCD.

HE sections of the jejunum of male mice demonstrated that there were no apparent differences among the LCD and PUD groups compared with the SD group in WT and KO mice (Figure S1A). In contrast, the jejunum of the MCD group of WT and KO mice presented numerous small vacuoles in the villous enterocytes (Figure S1A and inserts, WT MCD, KO MCD). Lipid SpotTM staining confirmed that the intracellular vacuoles observed in the WT and KO MCD groups were indeed lipid droplets (Figure S1B).

Measurement of epithelial barrier functions

Ussing chamber studies – LPS and FD4 transport and TEER in the jejunum

To support our hypothesis that a Western-style diet induces an LPS transport mechanism that functions in the absence of luminal lipids, we examined the m-to-s transport of FITC-LPS with or without luminal OA/TCA in Ussing-chambered murine jejunum in all groups. Since luminal LPS alone is not transported in the intact jejunum,5,16 LPS transport was measured with and without perfused luminal lipids. Furthermore, we measured FD4 movement and TEER, both accepted measures of epithelial paracellular permeability,10 to determine whether the paracellular pathway was contributory.

LPS movement – SD and LCD groups

In male WT mice, although FITC-LPS alone was not transported, exposure to OA/TCA (10/0.1 mM) increased the amount of FITC-LPS in the serosal solution in the SD group (Figure 4A), which is indicative of transepithelial transport, as previously reported.16 Interestingly, in the male WT LCD group, FITC-LPS alone profoundly increased FITC-LPS m-to-s movement without OA/TCA at t = 15 min and thereafter, the subsequent luminal addition of OA/TCA had no further effect on FITC-LPS transport (Figure 4A). In female WT mice, FITC-LPS alone in the LCD group tended to increase FITC-LPS compared with the SD group although the differences were not significant (Figure 4B). In female mice, OA/TCA increased FITC-LPS transport in the SD and LCD groups in similar amounts. In male and female Cav-1 KO mice, FITC-LPS transport with or without OA/TCA exposure was not observed in the SD and LCD groups (Figure 4C, D), which is consistent with our previous report.16

LPS movement – MCD and PUD groups

In the male WT MCD and PUD groups, FITC-LPS alone did not affect basal FITC-LPS movement, whereas luminal OA/TCA increased FITC-LPS transport, as observed in the SD group (Figure 4E). In contrast, in the female WT MCD and PUD groups, FITC-LPS alone or with OA/TCA was not transported (Figure 4F). In male and female Cav-1 KO mice, FITC-LPS transport with or without OA/TCA exposure did not occur in the SD, MCD and PUD groups (Figure 4G, H).

These results suggest that chronic feeding of a diet enriched in saturated LCFA uniquely activates a Cav-1-dependent-LPS transport mechanism that is independent of luminal FA in WT male mice.

FD4 movement

FD4 movement was increased in the male WT LCD group, whereas there was no change in the MCD and PUD groups compared with the SD group (Figure 4I). FD4 movement was also greater in the female WT LCD and MCD groups, compared with the SD group (Figure 4J), whereas there was no difference among the dietary groups in the male and female KO groups (Figure 4M–P).

TEER

In all the WT and KO groups, TEER remained unchanged in the LCD group (Figure 4M–P). In contrast, MCD and PUD increased TEER compared with the SD group (Figure 4M–P). These results demonstrate that the enhanced FITC-LPS and FD4 transport in the male WT LCD group was not associated with TEER changes. These results suggest that enhanced macromolecular transport in the male WT LCD group is probably not due to increased paracellular permeability. Furthermore, MCD and PUD increased the TEER in all the groups, suggesting that MCFA and PUFA may affect the structure or composition of the plasma membrane, transporter activity or tight junction function.

Measurement of mRNA expression of tight junction proteins

As an additional measure of gut barrier function and paracellular permeability, we measured the expression of major junctional proteins.29 TEER was unchanged in the LCD group, whereas MCD and PUD increased TEER (Figure 4M–P), and a high-fat diet generally decreased TEER with reduced expression of tight junction proteins.6,30 Therefore, we examined the effects of diet on the mRNA expression of tight junction proteins in the jejunal mucosa of male WT mice. Real-time PCR showed that the relative expression of Tjp1 (coding ZO-1), Ocln and Marveld2 (coding tricellulin) were unchanged in the LCD and PUD groups but increased in the MCD group (Figure 5A–C). The expression levels of Cldn7 and Cldn15 were increased in the MCD and PUD groups but not in the LCD group, with no change in Cldn2 expression in any group (Figure 5D–F). These results suggest that the unchanged mRNA expression of tight junction proteins in the LCD group is consistent with unchanged TEER, and that the increased TEER in the MCD and PUD groups is associated with increased expression of Tjp1, Ocln, Marveld2, Cldn7 and Cldn15.

Measurement of mRNA expression in males and females

Since sex differences exist in the induction of MetS, we also measured the mRNA expression of tight junction and endocytosis proteins in the jejunal mucosa of male and female WT mice. There was no difference in the mRNA expression of Tjp1, Ocln, Marveld2, Cldn2, Cldn7 or Cldn15 between males and females in the WT SD and LCD groups (data not shown). Nevertheless, the expression levels of CD36, Src, and Cav-1 were increased in the male WT LCD group compared with those in the SD group and the female WT LCD group (Figure 5G–I). These results suggest that increased expression of CD36, Src, and Cav-1 in the male WT LCD group is consistent with increased LPS transport and the induction of MetS in males and that the unchanged expression of CD36, Src, and Cav-1 in the female WT LCD group is associated with unchanged LPS transport and less prominent induction of MetS in females.

Measurement of PV LPS levels

As an additional indicator of gut barrier function, we measured PV LPS levels as a direct measure of transepithelial LPS transport in intact animals. Since male mice developed MetS more noticeably than female mice, we focused on male mice in the following measurements. We measured LPS levels in the PV plasma using the LAL test in male WT and Cav-1 KO mice fed a HFHS diet. The LPS level in the WT LCD group was greater than that in the WT SD group, whereas there was no difference between the WT MCD and PUD groups (Figure 6A). Furthermore, the LPS level was unchanged in the KO LCD group compared with the WT SD or KO SD groups. This result suggests that LPS is transported from the gut lumen to the PV by a Cav-1-dependent system and that the MetS observed in LCD-fed mice is due to increased PV LPS levels in male WT mice.

Time course of the development of MetS in the LCD group

We next examined the time course of PV LPS levels, liver weight, hepatic lipid droplet accumulation, and FITC-LPS and FD4 transport over the course of the 6-wk exposure period to the LCD. This enabled us to determine whether enhanced LPS uptake was the cause or effect of the MetS. PV LPS levels were increased after consumption of LCD for 3 and 6 wks, compared with the SD group (Figure 6B). Liver weights (Figure 6C) and the lipid-positive area of the liver (Figure 6D) were increased only after consumption of LCD for 6 wks when compared with the SD group, suggesting that the increase in PV LPS levels precedes the induction of fatty liver. Enhanced basal FITC-LPS transport was observed partially after consumption of LCD for 1 wk and fully at 3 and 6 wks, compared with the SD group (Figure 6E), corresponding to the increase in PV LPS levels (Figure 6B). FD4 transport at t = 45 min in the LCD group compared with that in the SD group also increased after 3 and 6 wks, though not at 1 wk (Figure 6F). These results suggest that consumption of LCD increased FITC-LPS and FD4 transport in parallel with the elevation of PV LPS levels, followed by the development of MetS.

Contribution of lipid rafts, CD36, and Src kinase to jejunal LPS transport

Next, we explored the mechanisms underlying the enhanced LPS and FD4 transport in the jejunum of the male WT LCD group by inhibiting key hypothesized components of LPS transport: Cav-1/lipid rafts, CD36, and Src kinase. The increase in basal FITC-LPS transport in the LCD group was abolished by luminal application of the lipid raft inhibitor MβCD (1 mM) and the CD36 inhibitor SSO (0.1 mM) (Figure 7A), suggesting that lipid raft- and CD36-mediated endocytosis is involved in these mechanisms. Furthermore, the enhanced FD4 transport in the LCD group was also inhibited by MβCD and SSO (Figure 7B), further suggesting that the enhanced FD4 m-to-s passage is mediated by a pathway similar to that of FITC-LPS transport.

Since Cav-1 is the first-described substrate for Src family kinases,31,32 we further hypothesized that Src kinase activation may increase LPS transport during LCD feeding. This hypothesis is supported by the observation that LCD-induced enhanced LPS transport was not observed in the LCD jejunum of Cav-1 KO mice (Figure 4C). Luminal application of the selective Src kinase inhibitor PP2 26 (1 µM) reduced the FITC-LPS transport (Figure 7A) and FD4 transport (Figure 7B) in the WT LCD group, suggesting that epithelial Src kinases are involved in the LCD-induced increase in LPS and FD4 transport.

In vivo imaging of LPS and dextran transport

Jejunum

The Ussing chamber studies described above did not clarify whether the basal increase in LPS and dextran transport in the LCD group was mediated by endocytosis or paracellular passage. Thus, the cellular uptake of fluorescently labeled dextran 3 kDa (488-D3) and LPS (568-LPS) in the jejunal epithelium was visualized using in vivo laser confocal microscopy as previously described.16 In male WT SD jejunal mucosa, no 488-D3-positive or 568-LPS-positive signal was observed in the villi, with a bright signal present in the luminal solution (Figure 8A). In contrast, in WT LCD jejunal mucosa, 488-D3- and 568-LPS-positive signals colocalized in intracellular vesicle-like structures in the villous epithelial cells, with no apparent paracellular staining (Figure 8B). These data are consistent with the endocytic uptake of dextran and LPS into villous cells. In Cav-1 KO LCD jejunal mucosa, no vesicular-like signal for 488-D3 or 568-LPS was observed in the villous cells (Figure 8C), suggesting that the 488-D3- and 568-LPS-positive vesicles are derived from Cav-1-mediated endocytosis.

Proximal colon (PC)

To clarify whether the colonic mucosa is involved in LPS and FD4 transport during LCFA exposure or LCD consumption, we examined the effects of acute LCFA exposure or chronic LCD feeding on LPS and FD4 movement in the PC. In Ussing chamber experiments, we used OA at 10 mM, which increases LPS transport in the jejunum16 and greatly exceeds the physiological concentration of LCFA observed in the colonic lumen in humans and rats,33,34 although it is possibly observed in rats fed a high-fat diet (~8 mM).34 Luminal application of OA/TCA (10/0.1 mM) had no effect on FITC-LPS movement across the PC mucosa of male WT mice (Figure 9A). OA/TCA also had no effect on FD4 movement (Figure 9B) and TEER (Figure 9C) compared with the vehicle group. Furthermore, there was no difference in FITC-LPS and FD4 movement and TEER between the male WT SD and LCD groups (Figure 9D–F). Notably, the TEER of the PC was approximately double the TEER of the jejunum in the male WT SD group (59.8 ± 3.4 vs. 28.0 ± 0.6 ohm·cm2, respectively). N = 5, p < 0.05 by t-test). We also examined whether PC epithelial cells were stained with 488-D3 or 568-LPS using in vivo confocal microscopy. Compared with the luminal fluorescence signals of 488-D3 and 568-LPS, no fluorescence was observed in the surface epithelial cells or in the paracellular space of the PC epithelium of male WT SD (Figure 10G) and LCD (Figure 10H) groups. These data suggest that there are no changes in LPS or dextran transport or paracellular permeability in the PC of male WT mice during acute LCFA exposure or the consumption of LCD.

Discussion

Summary

We demonstrated that male WT mice chronically fed a diet enriched in saturated LCFA and sucrose (LCD) preferentially developed metabolic endotoxemia and the MetS with elevated PV LPS levels and increased jejunal LPS and FD4 transport. These changes were not observed in female WT or Cav-1KO mice. The enhanced LPS and FD4 transport in male WT LCD jejunum was inhibited without changes in TEER; furthermore, dextran and LPS were colocalized in intracellular vesicles in male WT LCD jejunal enterocytes. In the proximal colon, LPS and FITC transport were minimal at baseline and not affected by the presence of luminal lipids or LCD feeding. Moreover, increased LPS uptake and elevated PV LPS levels preceded the increases in liver weight and hepatic lipid droplet accumulation. These data support our hypothesis that LPS is transcellularly transported acutely via Cav-1-dependent endocytosis during LCFA absorption in the jejunum, a process that becomes luminal LCFA-independent and Src kinase-dependent with chronic LCD feeding. Elevated PV LPS levels precede the development of MetS, suggesting that increased LPS transport induces MetS, as opposed to MetS, which enhances LPS transport. The proximal colon does not transport a substantial amount of LPS. Chronic feeding with dietary MCFA and PUFA does not induce the Cav-1/Src-dependent LPS transport process and is much less effective with enhancing LPS transport and inducing MetS.

LCFA dependence of LPS transport

The most important finding in the present study is the enhanced LPS and FD4 transport induced by LCD; the transport of both substances was inhibited by MβCD, SSO, and PP2 and was abolished in Cav-1 KO, and both were colocalized in the intracellular vesicles in the villous cells. These results suggest that: (1) LPS entry from the small intestine is accelerated by chronic LCD consumption and maintained by constitutive Cav-1-dependent endocytosis, (2) upregulation of CD36 and Src kinase may be involved in the constitutive Cav-1-dependent endocytosis observed in LCD mice, and (3) m-to-s FD4 transport involves transcellular uptake in parallel with LPS transport rather than generally accepted paracellular transport.

Mechanism of LCFA-dependent LPS transport

The CD36/Cav-1/Src pathway

CD36 is a membrane protein that senses extracellular LCFA and other substances that are intrinsically involved in lipid metabolism.35 Since CD36 is present on lipid rafts36 and lipid A of LPS may bind to CD36, CD36 internalization via caveolae-mediated endocytosis may incorporate LPS into caveolae. Transcytotic lipid transport via a CD36-Src-Cav-1-mediated pathway is present in endothelial cells, where a CD36-Src-Cav-1 complex mediates FA transport from the bloodstream across endothelial cells to the basal membranes, with exocytosis of Cav-1- and FA-containing vesicles to the interstitium.4 Therefore, we speculate that chronic overconsumption of a Western-style diet persistently activates CD36 with subsequent phosphorylation of Y14 of Cav-1 by Src, with constitutive activation of LPS transcytosis by jejunal enterocytes. This hypothesis is supported by our observation that LCD-associated enhanced LPS transport was inhibited by CD36 and selective Src kinase inhibitors and was also inhibited in Cav-1 KO. Since dextran is water soluble and has no known corresponding receptor or transporter, transcellular passage of dextran with LPS through Cav-1-dependent endocytosis suggests that dextran may be included in the caveolae during LPS transcytosis by the CD36-Src-Cav-1 complex.

Dextran absorption by the same pathways as LPS

Since FD4 is generally believed to be a non-absorbable paracellular permeability marker, increased serum FD4 concentrations following FD4 gavage are conventionally thought to be due to increased paracellular FD4 movement.11 Most reports of intestinal paracellular permeability also refer to the reduced TEER of the intestinal mucosa accompanied by reduced expression of tight junction proteins, usually assessed by mRNA expression, protein amount, and immunohistochemistry. Nevertheless, our data suggest that increased FD4 movement is due to transcellular absorption with no change in TEER or in the mRNA expression of tight junction proteins in WT LCD, at least in our diet-induced MetS model. The possible difference between our study and prior studies is the duration of the dietary interventions; only 6 wks of a HFHS diet facilitated the development of MetS,23 whereas conventional high-fat diets in most studies take 12−16 wks to develop MetS, suggesting that aging effects may be involved. Therefore, over a shorter duration, confounders such as systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and hyperlipidaemia may have been less prominent and of insufficient duration to increase intestinal paracellular permeability, with consequent changes in TEER and tight junction proteins. Prolonged feeding with LCD or induction of inflammation is needed to further test this hypothesis.

Sex differences

We observed sex differences in the development of MetS and LPS transport in WT mice. Males are more susceptible to diet-induced MetS with increased LPS transport than females. Generally, males increase in body weight faster than females in mice.37 Possible mechanisms include the effects of sex hormones (i.e., androgen vs. estrogen) on intestinal function, lipid/sucrose absorption, energy expenditure, and the expression of components of the CD36/Src/Cav-1 pathway. We found that LCD feeding increased the mRNA expression of CD36, Src and Cav-1 in males, but not in females, whereas the expression of tight junction proteins was unchanged in both males and females. Future studies are warranted to explore why females are protected from diet-induced MetS in our model system.

Effects of chronic MCD feeding

The consumption of MCD also induced MetS in both male WT and Cav-1 KO individuals, although to a lesser degree than LCD. Since MCFA acutely increases LPS transport in WT and Cav-1 KO jejuna by Cav-1-independent, clathrin-mediated endocytosis,16 we predicted that MCD also enhances LPS transport with consequent development of MetS, similar to the LCD group. MCFA acutely inhibit SGLT−1-mediated glucose transport in the jejunum,16,38 and blood glucose levels in the MCD group were lower than in the LCD group. Therefore, one possible explanation is that the reduced glucose absorption during high-sucrose diet feeding might mitigate LPS transport, metabolic endotoxemia and the development of MetS. The effects of a high-sucrose diet alone on LPS transport and metabolic parameters will be examined in the future to explore whether high sucrose has additive or synergistic effects on LPS transport. Nevertheless, we observed no effect of acute glucose exposure on LPS transport in the Ussing chambered jejunal mucosa (unpublished data).

Another finding in the MCD group is an increase in TEER. Although acute exposure of MCFA transiently decreased the TEER followed by rapid recovery,16 the increased TEER in the MCD group suggested that exposure of jejunal villous enterocytes to MCFA may alter the expression of tight junction proteins or transporters or the membrane lipid composition. Interestingly, consumption of MCD increased the mRNA expression of the tight junction proteins Tjp1, Ocln, Marveld2, Cldn7 and Cldn15, suggesting that the upregulation of tight junction proteins is involved in the increased TEER in the MCD group. One unexpected finding in the MCD group is the accumulation of lipid droplets in the jejunal villous and crypt cells in both the WT and KO mice. Further studies using clathrin inhibitors or KO mice will clarify the effects of MCD consumption on LPS transport and MetS induction.

Effect of PUFA

Compared with LCD and MCD, consumption of PUD did not induce MetS in either male or female WT or Cav-1 KO mice, except for liver weight in female WT. PUD consumption did not affect LPS or FD4 transport, whereas TEER was increased in all groups. As with the MCD group, the upregulation of Cldn7 and Cldn15 in the PUD group may be associated with increased TEER. The beneficial effects of fish oil or other n-3 PUFA such as eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), on MetS are well accepted;39,40 fish oil supplements improve diet-induced obesity with increased expression of ZO-1 in the ileum and Ocln in the colon.41 EPA and DHA exposure prevents the reduction of TEER and increased FD3 permeability induced by proinflammatory cytokines in T84 monolayers by the alteration of lipid composition of lipid rafts.42 These findings suggest that PUD prevents MetS induction by affecting lipid rafts involved in LPS transport, although OA/TCA-induced LPS transport was not affected in the PUD group. Another possibility is that PUFA especially n-3 PUFA activate peroxisome proliferator-activated receptors (PPARs) that increases FA β-oxidation, improving MetS.43 Further study will clarify the effects of n-3 and n-6 PUFA on LPS transport, since the dietary n−3/n−6 ratio is associated with the induction of MetS,44 or the involvement of PPARs activation.

LPS transport in the colon

This is also the first study demonstrating that the proximal colonic mucosa does not transport LPS or dextran during OA/TCA exposure or LCD consumption. It is generally believed that LPS enters the circulation from the colonic lumen, where >99% of all gut LPS is present,13 in mice with colitis,9 and in mice with diet-induced obesity.45 Despite this belief, the CD36- and Cav-1-dependent mechanisms involved in LCFA absorption are not present in the colonic mucosa.46-48 FA exposure may change the mucosal integrity or damage colonic epithelial cells. Nevertheless, our data demonstrated that OA (10 mM), an FA concentration in the proximal colonic lumen that is far higher than the reported concentration normally found in the human colon (50−100 µM)33, did not affect LPS or FD4 transport or TEER in WT PC Since the FA concentration may increase to ~8 mM in the rat cecum and colon after the ingestion of a high-fat diet,34 we also examined the effects of LCD feeding on LPS and FD4 transport in the PC. We found that the consumption of LCD had no effect on these parameters, as confirmed by in vivo imaging of PC epithelial cells. These data suggest that colonic luminal LPS does not increase PV LPS levels in the male WT LCD group, despite the enormous concentration gradient (>107:1) of LPS between the lumen and plasma. This may be a consequence of physical barriers such as thick bacteria-resistant mucus layers49 and high-resistance tight junctions50 compared with those in the small intestinal mucosa, as also shown in this study.

The sequence of MetS development

The currently postulated overall sequence of MetS development includes: (1) high-fat diet-induced dysbiosis, (2) increased intestinal paracellular permeability, (3) increased LPS entry and induction of metabolic endotoxemia, (4) low-grade systemic inflammation, and (5) the development of MetS.3 In contrast, proinflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor α increase intestinal paracellular permeability,51 which may facilitate LPS entry and metabolic endotoxemia. Though it is still unclear which comes first, increased intestinal paracellular permeability or LPS entry,52 our results support an alternative sequence: (1) overfeeding a diet enriched with saturated LCFA activates CD36, (2) subsequent phosphorylation and constitutive activation of the Cav-1-dependent endocytic pathway, (3) transcellular uptake of LPS in CD36-containing endosomes,4 (4) subsequent entry of LPS into the PV, (5) metabolic endotoxemia that activates proinflammatory pathways, and (6) the well-described chronic low-grade inflammation that characterizes MetS.53 Chronic inflammation may also constitutively activate the enterocyte Cav-1 pathway with LPS uptake independent of luminal LCFA perpetuating MetS.

Other effects of high-fat diets

High-fat diets affect both the composition of the microbiome and the microbial metabolome.54 The endotoxic activity of lipid A of bacterial LPS is dependent on its FA composition.55 Therefore, high-fat diets may affect the composition of the acyl chains of lipid A of bacterial LPS, although data are lacking to support this hypothesis. Interestingly, high-fat diets stimulate a commensal species, Fusimonas intestini, to produce the trans-fatty acid elaidic acid, which facilitates diet-induced obesity with increased intestinal paracellular permeability, as assessed by FD4 absorption and tight junction protein expression in the colon.56 These findings suggest that changes in microbial metabolites modulate LPS entry during consumption of a high-fat diet. Furthermore, LPS is absorbed by the fish M. ambycephala by an OA/CD36-dependent mechanism,57 also supporting our hypothesis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the FA composition of chronically fed HFHS diets differentially affects LPS entry from the small intestinal lumen, inducing MetS predominantly in male mice. LCD consumption likely activates the CD36-Src-Cav-1 transcytotic pathway, which constitutively activates Cav-1-dependent endocytic LPS transport in the jejunum (Figure 10). Identification of the signaling mechanisms underlying intestinal LPS transport may provide novel therapeutic targets for the prevention and treatment of LPS-associated MetS.

A proposed mechanism of endocytic LPS transport during acute LCFA absorption and chronic feeding of a Western-style diet in the jejunum. In general, macromolecules such as LPS do not cross the small intestinal epithelium. Changes in tight junction structure and function increase ion permeability (blue circles), which is accompanied by the reduction of TEER known as ‘pore’ pathway, followed by macromolecule entry through the widened paracellular space, known as the ‘leak’ pathway.58 In contrast, LPS are transcellularly transported during acute LCFA absorption via the lipid raft/CD36/Cav-1 pathway (green dots).16 Furthermore, chronic feeding of a Western-style diet enhances LPS and FD4 transport via an upregulated CD36/Src/Cav-1 pathway, as shown in this study. The absorbed LPS exits the basolateral membrane and enters the PV.
