Honeycomb construction on designed foundations reveals geometry-dependent developmental pathways
Authors: Takayuki Narumi, Ryo Ogawa, Koichi Osaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Honeycomb construction by honeybees is a classical example of collective biological structure formation, yet how ordered cell patterns develop under imposed geometric constraints remains insufficiently understood. Here, we experimentally investigated comb development by introducing 3D-printed designed foundations into an active honeybee colony and comparing conditions that differed in lattice symmetry and scale. The foundations consisted of three hexagonal lattices with nominal across-flats opening sizes of 5.0, 6.7, and 20 mm, and a square lattice with a nominal opening side length of 5.4 mm. On the small and medium hexagonal foundations, construction proceeded through local wax attachment, formation of dome-like protrusions, lateral expansion, and incorporation into a more continuous planar comb. In contrast, on the large hexagonal foundation, wax attachment was localized mainly along the raised lattice walls, and subsequent growth proceeded mainly outward from the foundation surface. On the square foundation, early wax attachment followed the imposed orthogonal boundaries, but this arrangement was not maintained during later development. Initially dominant four-sided cells progressively decreased, whereas five- and six-sided cells increased, indicating a transition from an orthogonal arrangement toward hexagonal-like mixed polygonal configurations. These results suggest that imposed geometry influences developmental pathways and coverage dynamics, while comb organization can progressively evolve toward hexagonal-like structures through local attachment, growth, and subsequent morphological reorganization.

Introduction

Honeycomb construction by honeybees (Apis mellifera) is a canonical example of collective biological structure formation, in which global geometric order emerges from distributed local material manipulations by many individuals1–4. Although mature honeycombs are often described as exhibiting regular hexagonal patterns, natural combs frequently contain irregularities and transitional structures that reflect ongoing construction, fusion, and local reorganization processes5,6. Understanding honeycomb geometry therefore requires not only describing final cell arrangements, but also elucidating how ordered structures emerge as a developmental process under geometric constraints.

The question of how honeycomb geometry emerges is part of a broader problem of how polygonal patterns and space-partitioning structures arise in natural and engineered systems. Hexagonal tilings have long been studied as efficient planar partitions7. Related polygonal tessellations also emerge in molecular self-assembly8, in the statistical geometry of convex mosaics9, and in biological tissues shaped by growth and local mechanical interactions10. These examples suggest that hexagonal or near-hexagonal order can reflect general constraints of local interaction, packing, and growth, in addition to system-specific mechanisms. Honeycomb construction therefore provides a useful biological system for examining how such constraints operate during development.

For honeycomb construction specifically, two influential explanatory frameworks have been proposed for the formation of hexagonal comb structures. One attributes hexagonal geometry to physical processes, such as relaxation of softened wax toward configurations analogous to liquid equilibrium11,12. However, subsequent studies have raised questions about whether the temperature and material properties of wax during construction are consistent with this interpretation13,14. An alternative framework emphasizes self-organization, in which repeated local actions of bees under shared constraints generate ordered structures without requiring explicit geometric planning15–17. From this perspective, comb geometry emerges through iterative local attachment, growth, and interactions among neighboring structures.

Recent studies using artificial foundations and high-resolution observations have shown that comb construction involves not only wax addition but also subsequent morphological change during development18–21. Experimental evidence further suggests that local geometric cues, including sub-cell-scale concave features and boundary cues, can influence where newly constructed cells and walls are placed during early comb building22. Honeybees can also respond flexibly to non-natural boundary conditions imposed by artificial foundations. In particular, a recent study using square-ridge wax foundations reported size-dependent responses, in which bees either disregarded small square ridges or incorporated larger ones, leading to distinct final arrangements depending on the imposed geometry23. From a broader structural perspective, Lehner et al. reviewed honeycomb as a meso-structured system and emphasized that features beyond the canonical hexagonal unit cell, such as coping, attachment, and growth-front geometry, may play essential roles in honeycomb design and function24. However, previous studies have primarily examined specific artificial conditions or particular reconstruction processes, and have not systematically compared how developmental pathways diverge across multiple imposed geometric constraints. In particular, it remains unclear how early attachment patterns, subsequent structural reorganization, and final comb geometry depend jointly on foundation scale and symmetry. It is also not well understood whether initially imposed geometric configurations persist or are progressively transformed during development.

In this study, we address these questions using 3D-printed designed foundations in which the imposed lattice geometry was specified at the design stage. Specifically, we prepared hexagonal foundations with nominal across-flats opening sizes of 5.0 mm, 6.7 mm, and 20 mm, representing scales slightly smaller than, moderately larger than, and far larger than typical commercial worker-cell foundations, respectively. We also prepared a square lattice foundation with a nominal opening side length of 5.4 mm as a non-hexagonal boundary condition at a scale comparable to worker-cell foundations. These foundations were introduced into an active colony, and comb development was observed over time within a comparative framework spanning both symmetry and scale.

Our objectives were threefold: first, to compare developmental trajectories across foundations with different scales and symmetries; second, to examine whether initially imposed geometric cues were maintained or reorganized during growth; and third, to clarify how local attachment and subsequent morphological reorganization together contribute to geometry-dependent comb formation. Through this comparative approach, we investigate how local interactions, body-scale constraints, and boundary geometry jointly shape developmental pathways toward ordered comb structures. In addition, quantitative analyses of planar coverage and polygonal composition were performed to characterize developmental differences across conditions.

Study colony and experimental setting

Experiments were conducted from April to June 2024 in Yamaguchi City, Japan, using a managed queenright colony of the western honeybee (Apis mellifera). All treatments were tested within the same colony. Accordingly, the present study should be interpreted as a within-colony comparative experiment under different geometric constraints rather than as a replicated comparison across multiple colonies.

Commercial wax foundations were also present in the hive as part of normal colony management, but the present analyses focus exclusively on comb development on the designed artificial foundations described below.

Fabrication of designed foundations

Artificial foundations with controlled geometry were fabricated using a Guider 2s fused-filament 3D printer (FLASHFORGE) using polylactic acid (PLA) filament. PLA was selected because preliminary trials indicated no clear avoidance behavior and confirmed that bees readily initiated wax attachment on PLA surfaces.

All geometries were prepared using FlashPrint version 5 (Fig. 1a). Printing was performed with the foundation panel placed flat on the build plate. Four foundation geometries were examined (Fig. 1b–e). Across-flats size refers to the distance between two opposite parallel sides of a hexagonal opening. The three hexagonal conditions were selected to span biologically relevant and strongly perturbed spatial scales. Hex-small was designed to be slightly smaller than typical commercial worker-cell foundations (approximately 5.2–5.4 mm25), while remaining close to the natural worker-cell scale. Hex-medium provided a moderately enlarged condition beyond the typical worker-cell range while remaining well below body size. Hex-large represented an extreme supra-natural scale substantially exceeding the typical body length of worker bees (approximately 10–15 mm)26. A fourth design, hereafter referred to as Square, consisted of a square lattice with a nominal opening side length of 5.4 mm. This condition was introduced as a non-hexagonal boundary geometry with a scale comparable to natural worker-cell foundations. Because of finite wall thickness and possible printing-related deviations, effective opening sizes could differ from the nominal design values. For this reason, foundation conditions are described primarily by their nominal design sizes throughout the manuscript.

Installation procedure and observation schedule

Printed foundations were mounted onto standard wooden hive frames (Fig. 1f) and introduced into the colony. The installation dates were as follows: Hex-medium on 24 April 2024; Hex-small on 9 May 2024; Hex-large on 15 May 2024; and Square on 31 May 2024. Each foundation remained in a fixed hive position during its observation period. Because treatments were introduced sequentially rather than simultaneously, seasonal or colony-state effects cannot be completely excluded.

Image acquisition

Comb development was recorded photographically at intervals of several days using a digital camera. Whenever feasible, images were obtained after temporarily clearing bees from the foundation surface to improve visibility of the constructed wax structures. Observations focused on macroscopic morphological changes, including the spatial distribution of wax deposition, development of protruding dome-like structures or structures extending away from the foundation plane, formation of boundaries between neighboring patches, merging of adjacent structures, and signs of functional use such as pollen storage or brood presence.

Polygonal composition in the Square condition

To quantify structural reorganization in the Square treatment, visible cell openings were classified by polygon type (4-sided, 5-sided, 6-sided, etc.) within a fixed observation region near the upper central part of the panel. This region was selected from the earliest usable image because it initially exhibited limited wax thickening, allowing subsequent changes in visible cell geometry to be tracked more clearly through time. The same approximate region was used for later dates whenever visibility allowed. The selected region avoided heavily occluded zones. Thus, this analysis was intended to track local geometric changes consistently over time, rather than to represent the entire structure. Cells whose polygonal type could not be reliably determined because of occlusion or incomplete visibility were classified as unknown and excluded from proportional summaries of identified polygons.

Comparative scope and limitations

Because all treatments were conducted in a single colony, the present comparisons are descriptive within-colony contrasts rather than population-level statistical inference. In addition, treatments were introduced sequentially, and image acquisition intervals were not uniform across all conditions. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as evidence of geometry-dependent developmental tendencies under controlled artificial constraints, rather than as definitive estimates of colony-wide behavioral parameters. Reliability of the descriptive comparisons was assessed by examining whether the same morphological tendencies persisted across successive observation dates within each condition; individual images were not treated as independent replicates.

Ethical considerations

Experiments involving honeybee colonies in this study did not require specific institutional approval under applicable regulations. All procedures were conducted in accordance with relevant institutional and local guidelines for the handling of managed honeybee colonies.

Results

Scale-dependent development on hexagonal foundations

The three hexagonal foundations showed distinct scale-dependent developmental trajectories. In all three hexagonal conditions, comb construction began with local wax attachment to the printed foundation. However, subsequent development differed between the smaller-scale foundations and the large-scale foundation. On Hex-small and Hex-medium, wax structures expanded laterally over the foundation surface through dome-like intermediate protrusions, whereas on Hex-large, construction remained localized and developed outward from the foundation surface.

On Hex-small, local wax attachment was first observed at multiple sites on the foundation surface (Fig. 2a). By day 14, these initial attachments had developed into shallow dome-like protrusions extending above the foundation surface (Fig. 2b). As development proceeded, the protrusions expanded laterally and became incorporated into a more continuous planar comb (Fig. 2c).

A similar developmental sequence was observed on Hex-medium (Fig. 2d–f). At early observation times, wax deposition formed dome-like protrusions on part of the foundation surface (Fig. 2d). These structures subsequently expanded laterally, and by later observations the constructed region formed a broadly continuous planar comb (Fig. 2e,f). Thus, despite their difference in nominal opening size, Hex-small and Hex-medium shared a common developmental pattern: local attachment was followed by dome-like protrusion formation, lateral expansion, and incorporation into a planar comb. The coverage ratio also increased over time in both conditions, although the observed temporal patterns differed between them (Fig. 3).

Comb development on Hex-large differed qualitatively from that observed on Hex-small and Hex-medium (Fig. 4). By day 8, wax attachment was localized primarily along the raised lattice walls, while large regions of the foundation surface remained uncovered (Fig. 4a). Unlike the smaller-scale hexagonal conditions, dome-like protrusions spreading over the foundation surface were not observed.

By day 15, the constructed structures had developed further but remained spatially localized (Fig. 4b–d). Growth proceeded outward from the foundation surface rather than as lateral surface coverage, and neighboring structures showed limited lateral merging directly along the foundation surface. The opposite-side view also showed incomplete surface utilization (Fig. 4d). These observations indicate that Hex-large followed a distinct developmental trajectory characterized by localized attachment along the raised lattice walls, outward growth, and incomplete planar coverage.

The temporal change in polygonal composition further supported this structural reorganization (Fig. 6). At day 3, four-sided cells dominated among the identified cells. By day 9, five- and six-sided cells had appeared, and their proportions increased over time. At later stages, six-sided cells became dominant among the identified cells, while the fraction of four-sided cells decreased. Taken together, these observations show that the square lattice influenced the initial placement of wax, but the visible cell geometry was not determined solely by this imposed structure. Instead, the initially formed orthogonal arrangement was not maintained during subsequent development, and the constructed region exhibited mixed polygonal configurations with an increasing dominance of six-sided cells at later stages.

Discussion

The present study shows that honeybee comb construction on designed foundations exhibits geometry-dependent developmental tendencies that vary with both scale and symmetry. Across all conditions, construction was initiated by local wax attachment, but subsequent development diverged: on Hex-small and Hex-medium, structures expanded laterally through dome-like intermediates and became incorporated into planar combs, whereas on Hex-large, attachment remained localized and growth proceeded outward from the foundation surface. On the square foundation, initial construction followed the imposed orthogonal geometry, but this arrangement was not maintained and progressively transitioned toward hexagonal-like mixed polygonal configurations.

These observations indicate that honeybees flexibly respond to locally imposed geometric constraints at early stages of construction. In particular, the square foundation shows that bees can initiate wax attachment along non-hexagonal boundaries, forming structures consistent with orthogonal geometry. However, such configurations were not stable over the course of development. As construction proceeded and interactions between neighboring structures increased, the initially imposed orthogonal pattern became less apparent in the visible cell geometry, and mixed polygonal forms emerged. This suggests that initial adherence to local geometric cues does not uniquely determine the final structure, but instead represents an early stage in a developmental process that can progressively evolve toward hexagonal-like organization through subsequent interactions and reorganization.

Similar early-stage responses to artificial non-hexagonal constraints have been reported in recent experimental studies16,18–21,23. In particular, Shima et al.23 showed that honeybees respond differently to square-ridge wax foundations depending on square size and arrangement, either disregarding smaller square ridges or incorporating larger square ridges into subsequent construction. At a finer scale, Gallo et al.22 provided experimental evidence that sub-cell-scale concave features and boundary cues can bias the placement of new structures. Together, these findings support the view that local geometric cues can guide early construction, while the subsequent developmental outcome depends on continued interaction, growth, and reorganization.

A similar distinction between initial attachment and subsequent development is also evident in the comparison between hexagonal conditions at different scales. While Hex-small and Hex-medium exhibited lateral expansion and incorporation of dome-like structures into planar combs, Hex-large showed a qualitatively different pattern characterized by localized attachment and outward growth. This scale-dependent divergence suggests that the mode of construction is not fixed, but depends on the geometric context in which local attachment occurs. In particular, sufficiently large openings were not associated with planar comb formation through lateral expansion in the present observations. Instead, wax attachment and extension occurred mainly along the raised lattice walls. Recent experimental studies have similarly reported that cell size and foundation geometry influence growth direction and structural organization19,20.

The transition observed in the square condition can be interpreted in terms of local geometric constraints and junction structure. Hexagonal arrangements are typically associated with three-fold junctions, whereas square arrangements involve four-fold junctions, which may introduce competing directions of extension. While bees initially follow the imposed square boundaries, such configurations may be less readily maintained under continued growth, leading to local rearrangements and the emergence of mixed polygonal structures. In this sense, the observed reorganization from orthogonal to hexagonal-like organization can be understood as a consequence of local geometric interactions during development, rather than as the direct realization of a predefined global pattern. This transition is consistent with a tendency for the developing structure to evolve toward hexagonal-like organization, even when the initial geometric constraints are non-hexagonal.

From a broader perspective, these results are consistent with a self-organized view of honeycomb construction, in which global geometric order emerges from repeated local interactions rather than from a centrally specified template15,27,28. The dependence of developmental pathways on both scale and symmetry, together with the observed transition from an imposed orthogonal arrangement toward hexagonal-like organization, is consistent with the interpretation that comb geometry arises through the interaction of local attachment processes and subsequent structural changes under geometric constraints. This interpretation is also consistent with modeling studies suggesting that hexagonal-like organization can arise from simple local rules combining attachment and excavation17. At the same time, the present analysis is based on morphological observations and does not directly identify the underlying behavioral mechanisms, and therefore should be interpreted as evidence consistent with self-organization, rather than as a direct demonstration of specific generative mechanisms.

From a geometric perspective, hexagonal patterns are often discussed as efficient solutions to space-partitioning problems, as formalized in studies such as the honeycomb conjecture7. However, such results characterize optimal final configurations and do not address how these configurations are reached through a developmental process. The present results suggest that, even under conditions where hexagonal arrangements are geometrically feasible, construction does not necessarily proceed directly toward such configurations. Instead, intermediate structures, local interactions, and geometry-dependent pathways appear to play important roles in shaping the final outcome. In this sense, honeycomb construction can be interpreted not only as a problem of geometric optimality, but also as a process of constrained development in which local rules and boundary conditions influence the trajectory of structure formation. This perspective is consistent with observations from other systems, including molecular assemblies and biological tissues, where polygonal organization has been reported to emerge through growth and local interactions rather than direct optimization8–10.

An important limitation of the present interpretation concerns the mechanism of structural change during development. Here, we use “morphological reorganization” to refer to observed changes in comb morphology, including boundary deformation and changes in polygonal cell configurations. However, these changes do not necessarily require processes other than continued wax deposition. For example, the merging of neighboring structures or the gradual alteration of boundary geometry could in principle arise through successive attachment of wax, without invoking distinct excavation or remodeling behaviors. Because individual-level actions were not directly observed, the present results cannot distinguish between these alternative mechanisms. Accordingly, the results do not exclude the possibility that the observed patterns could be generated by attachment processes alone. At the same time, previous studies have suggested that attachment and excavation can occur in parallel during comb construction18–21.

Several additional limitations should be acknowledged. First, all experiments were conducted within a single colony, and treatments were introduced sequentially rather than simultaneously, so colony-specific or temporal effects cannot be excluded. Second, the analysis is based on images acquired at discrete time intervals, and does not fully capture continuous developmental dynamics. Third, although the present design enables comparison across different geometric conditions, scale and symmetry are not fully independent factors; in particular, the comparison between small and large hexagonal foundations reflects both size and geometric context. Accordingly, the results should be interpreted as evidence of geometry-dependent developmental tendencies, rather than as isolating the effects of individual parameters. In addition, while some images suggest possible functional use of the constructed structures, such as storage-related activity, these aspects were not systematically analyzed in the present study and remain for future investigation.

Despite these limitations, the present findings provide a comparative experimental perspective on how imposed geometric constraints influence the development of honeycomb structures. Rather than being determined solely by initial templates, comb geometry appears to arise through a developmental process in which imposed geometry influences early construction and coverage dynamics, while local attachment, geometric constraints, and subsequent reorganization can progressively evolve toward hexagonal-like organization over time. In particular, the observed dependence on both scale and symmetry highlights the importance of geometric context in shaping developmental trajectories. This perspective is consistent with recent work emphasizing the role of meso-scale structure and growth processes in honeycomb organization24, and suggests that geometry-dependent developmental pathways may represent an important component in understanding how ordered structures arise in biological construction systems.
