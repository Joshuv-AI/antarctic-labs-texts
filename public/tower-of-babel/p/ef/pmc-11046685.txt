ANCA-negative ANCA-associated vasculitis: pitfalls of the ‘vasculitis screen’
Authors: Hannah Cooke, Matthew Wells, Keith Miller, Andrew R L Medford, Sam Patel
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDespite its recognition as an ‘ANCA-associated vasculitis’ (AAV), eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) is ANCA negative in up to 60% of cases. Herein, we report the case of a young man with a clinical syndrome highly suggestive of EGPA but with repeated negative ANCA serology, ultimately presenting with cardiac arrest before recognition of the primary systemic vasculitis, whereupon he received successful induction therapy with high dose glucocorticoids and cyclophosphamide. The case illustrates the importance of awareness of ANCA negative AAV among general physicians in order to minimise morbidity and mortality.KEYWORDS: vasculitis, ANCA, ANCA-associated vasculitis, eosinophilic granulomatous polyangiitis

Case presentation

A 38-year-old man with adult-onset asthma was referred to respiratory outpatients with difficult-to-control symptoms of intermittent wheeze and breathlessness. He had been managed as sub-optimally controlled asthma in primary care, requiring steroids on several occasions. Blood tests demonstrated peripheral eosinophilia of 1.10 109/L with negative IgE aero-allergen profiling, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA) screening and negative ANCA.

Despite commencing treatment with montelukast and inhaled salbutamol and fluticasone/vilanterol, peak flow continued to deteriorate. He required multiple acute hospital admissions for nebulised bronchodilators and prednisolone courses, at doses of up to 60 mg/day. Systemic enquiry revealed recent worsening nasal congestion, but he was otherwise systemically well. Peripheral eosinophilia persisted. High-resolution CT thorax demonstrated patchy ground-glass opacification with localised tree-in-bud changes in keeping with pulmonary infection, for which he was referred for bronchoscopy.

Following cessation of oral steroids, he was re-admitted 2 weeks later acutely unwell with respiratory compromise. He went into cardiopulmonary arrest while in the emergency department, with post-arrest CT pulmonary angiogram (CTPA) demonstrating a large pericardial effusion and multiple foci of consolidation throughout both lungs (Fig 1a) and no pulmonary embolism. Blood tests revealed ongoing eosinophilia alongside acute kidney injury, with elevated urine albumin creatinine ratio of 25.3 mg/mmol.

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit for respiratory and renal support with a working diagnosis of eosinophilic pneumonia. Bronchoalveolar lavage was performed post CTPA with MC&S negative for infection, and differential cell count demonstrating 30% eosinophils (Fig 1b). Repeat ANCA testing was again negative. Following antibiotic therapy with piperacillin-tazobactam and azithromycin alongside IV methylprednisolone, there was clinical improvement and the patient was discharged from hospital to a brain injury rehabilitation unit, with MRI findings compatible with hypoxic brain injury.

Several weeks later he re-presented with worsening breathlessness and purpuric rash over his upper limbs (Fig 1c). Blood tests again demonstrated eosinophilia with elevated C-reative protein but negative ANCA again. He was referred to intensive care with escalating oxygen requirements, with CTPA again demonstrating tree-in-bud opacification. Following rheumatology review a unifying diagnosis of ANCA-negative eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) was made. Urgent nerve conduction studies were consistent with mononeuropathy multiplex and skin biopsy demonstrated leukocytoclastic vasculitis with high eosinophil infiltrate (Fig 1d). He improved following treatment with IV methylprednisolone and meropenem.

Following six pulses of cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil was commenced as maintenance therapy. He is now in steroid-free remission on mycophenolate mofetil and lives independently with minimal residual symptoms of vasculitis.

Clinical features of EGPA

EGPA (formerly Churg–Strauss syndrome) is a rare and life-threatening condition characterised by an eosinophil-rich granulomatous inflammation of small- to medium-sized vessels.1 By virtue of the pattern of vasculitis, the effects of EGPA are multi-systemic.

The lower and upper respiratory tract are the most commonly involved systems,2 demonstrated in the present case by prominent airways disease, pulmonary infiltrates and nasal polyposis. However, a broader constellation of features are also recognised, frequently including cutaneous and neurological disease. Less commonly there can be cardiovascular, gastrointestinal, renal and eye involvement, in descending order of prevalence.2

In 2022 a multinational group published updated classification criteria for ANCA-associated vasculitis (AAV) including EGPA,3 replacing the previous 1990 American College of Rheumatology (ACR) classification criteria.4 While not intended for use as a diagnostic tool, these criteria can facilitate recognition of the clinical features of AAV and guide further investigations. Notably, these modern criteria do not offer positive weighting for ANCA positivity when classifying EGPA,3 but acknowledge the presence of obstructive airway disease, nasal polyposis, mononeuritis multiplex and peripheral eosinophilia as key clinical features.

Serology in EGPA

Historically EGPA is considered one of the ANCA-associated vasculitides, but positive ANCA is only observed in around 40% of cases.5 This is a much lower proportion of ANCA positivity than is seen in granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA) and microscopic polyangiitis (MPA), where positive ANCA is seen in >90% of cases,6 leading to diagnostic pitfalls.

EGPA serology influences disease phenotype, with ANCA-negative patients less likely to manifest typical recognisable features of small vessel vasculitis such as a purpuric rash, neuropathy or renal involvement.2 Asthma is typically more severe in seronegative patients, and cardiac complications are more frequently observed,5 ranging from cardiomyopathy, pericarditis and myocardial infarction with subsequent high morbidity and mortality.2 It is hypothesised that tissue eosinophil infiltration is the predominant pathology in seronegative patients as opposed to a more vasculitic process in the seropositive cohorts, perhaps accounting for these differences.7

Conclusion

Diagnosis of vasculitis is challenging and requires early consideration in the context of multi-system disease. The physician must consider vasculitis mimics, underlying secondary causes (commonly infections, malignancy and drugs), assess the extent of vasculitis and ultimately arrange confirmatory testing.8 There can be a tendency to be over-reliant on serology in the diagnosis of AAV as a confirmatory or exclusionary test. If the broader pretest probability of AAV is high, negative ANCA testing must not delay further testing in the form of tissue biopsy (rather than broncho-alveolar lavage alone when there is lung parenchymal involvement) in the case of EGPA consisting of eosinophilic tissue infiltrate with or without granuloma formation.

Thorough clinical history taking, including systems review, should determine a broad pretest probability and be considered a vital component of the ‘vasculitis screen’ rather than a battery of blood tests. ANCA test results should then be interpreted in the context of this. This case highlights the devastating impact of overreliance on serological testing, which in the case of AAV can be very unreliable.
