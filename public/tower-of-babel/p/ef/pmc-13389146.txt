Effectiveness and Safety of the MiniMed 780G Advanced Hybrid Closed-Loop System in Older Adults With Type 1 Diabetes: A Comparative Cohort Study
Authors: María Alejandra Robledo Gomez, Oscar Muñoz, Diana Cristina Henao, Darío Alfredo Parra, Yeimer Ortiz, Margarita García, Valentina Vásquez, Sofia Galvis, Ana Maria Gomez-Medina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveEvidence on advanced hybrid closed-loop (AHCL) systems in older adults with type 1 diabetes (T1D) remains limited. This study compared the effectiveness and safety of the MiniMed 780G system between adults aged ≥60 years and younger adults.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study in patients with T1D using the MiniMed 780G system. Eligible participants were aged ≥40 years and had used the device for at least four weeks before study entry. Clinical variables, body composition, grip strength, and glycemic outcomes were compared according to age group (<60 vs. ≥60 years).ResultsA total of 118 patients were included, 45% of whom were aged ≥60 years. In this group, 79% were independent for basic activities of daily living and 60% used SmartGuard at optimal settings. Glycemic management was similar in both age groups, including time in range (76.58±8.59 vs. 76.73±9.58; p=0.535), time in tight range (52.0±10.02 vs. 52.75±10.73; p=0.694), and time below range <70 mg/dL (1.39±1.33 vs. 1.81±1.39; p=0.100). In adjusted ANCOVA models, age group was not significantly associated with either TIR or TITR. Optimal SmartGuard settings, grip strength, fat mass, and phase angle were more strongly associated with glycemic profile than age.ConclusionsMiniMed 780G use was safe and effective in older adults with T1D, achieving glycemic outcomes comparable to younger adults. Optimal SmartGuard settings are associated with a better metabolic profile and should be considered regardless of age. Additionally, grip strength and body composition should be included in the clinical assessment of patients with T1D using AHCL systems.

Introduction

It is estimated that 9.5 million people worldwide have type 1 diabetes (T1D), with 513,000 new cases expected by 2025. Of those affected, 11% are aged over 60. 1 The therapy of choice for patients with T1D currently are the Advanced Hybrid Closed Loop systems. 1 A recent systematic review and meta-analysis of 28 controlled clinical studies showed that AHCL increases time in range (TIR) between 70 and 180 mg/dL and decreases time below range (TBR) compared to multiple-dose insulin therapy in people with T1D. 2 Technological advances have increased life expectancy, resulting in a growing population of older adults with T1D who use Advanced Hybrid Closed-loop (AHCL) systems.

Glycemic management is particularly complex in older adults. The coexistence of diabetes and hypertension significantly increases the risk of disability and long-term complications, including chronic kidney disease and stroke. 3 Compared to older adults without diabetes, these patients have a higher prevalence of polypharmacy and frailty. Additionally, overall mortality is nearly twice as high.3,4 For this reason, the American Diabetes Association (ADA) recommends different glycemic management objectives and goals according to each patient’s functional status and comorbidities. 5 Generally, older patients have more flexible goals, and preventing hypoglycemia is prioritized as it poses a greater risk to this group.1,5

Severe hypoglycaemia (SH) is the most significant safety issue for this population. The reported rate of SH is 40.1 events per 100 patient-years in people over 60, which is almost double the rate in people under 60 (24.3 per 100 patient-years). 6 One study showed that patients with sarcopenia (low grip strength with reduced muscle mass) were associated with a higher probability to have TBR <70 mg/dl greater than 1% with increased glycemic variability, demonstrating that sarcopenia in older adults is a risk factor for hypoglycemia. 7 Other risk factors include chronic kidney disease, polypharmacy, liver disease, insulin treatment, cardiovascular disease, retinopathy, neuropathy, and dementia, which tend to be more prevalent in people over 60 years of age. 1

Small descriptive studies have shown that technology can help patients achieve glycemic management goals similar to those of young adults, without increasing the risk of hypoglycemia. In a cohort of older Colombian patients with diabetes who were treated with automated insulin delivery systems, AHCL systems were shown to achieve a higher TIR with less exposure to hypoglycemia than other devices. 7 An Italian cohort study of 18 older adults using the MiniMed 780G system demonstrated improved TIR and decreased TBR from the first months of therapy until the one-year follow-up. 6 Another randomized controlled trial (RCT) of patients over 60 years of age using AHCL systems demonstrated that these therapies improve TIR with a decrease in TAR, TBR, and HbA1c. 8 Due to the limited evidence regarding the use of AHCL systems among older adults with T1D, this study aimed to compare the effectiveness and safety of this technology in this population with that in young adults.

Methodology

A cross-sectional study was conducted on people with T1D who were treated with the AHCL system (MiniMed 780G, Medtronic, Northridge, California) from May 2024 to November 2025 in the endocrinology unit of Hospital Universitario San Ignacio in Bogotá, Colombia. Patients aged 40 years or older, diagnosed with T1D, and treated with the MiniMed 780G for at least four weeks prior to entering the study were included. Those who were pregnant or breastfeeding; had active neoplasms, Child-Pugh C liver cirrhosis, or liver failure; had alcoholism or active drug dependence; or had physical or mental disabilities that prevented them from completing the comprehensive geriatric assessment were excluded. Patients were included without matching the cohorts. The institutional ethics committee of the Hospital San Ignacio approved this study (FM-CIE-1170-23, date November 15th 2023), and written consent was obtained from all patients.

Clinical information and laboratory results were systematically obtained during routine medical consultations after informed consent was signed. Trained personnel entered the variables into a standardized database. Each device was initially programmed and configured according to the treating physician’s criteria and in accordance with international guidelines. 9 Continuous glucose monitoring reports were downloaded from the CareLink platform (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN) obtaining data from the four weeks prior to the consultation. TIR, TBR, and TAR targets were defined according to the ADA international consensus. 9

To measure weight and body composition for all patients a Seca mBCA 514 medical-grade body composition analyzer (Hamburg, Germany) was used. Patients over 60 years of age underwent a comprehensive geriatric assessment by trained personnel using the following scales: Barthel, Lawton, MiniCog, Fried frailty phenotype, and grip strength (using a CAMRY dynamometer).

Polypharmacy was defined as taking five or more medications per day. For grip strength, two cut-off points were used, one for women (16 Kg) and one for men (27 Kg). Time in tight range (TITR) was defined as the time between blood glucose values of 70-140 mg/dL, time in range as the time between 70-180 mg/dL, and optimal SG configuration as when the device is programmed with the following target glucose conditions: 100 mg/dL with 2 hours of active insulin time.

The sample size was determined a priori for the primary outcome, TIR, assuming a minimum clinically relevant between-group difference of 5 percentage points, an SD of 9, a two-sided alpha level of 0.05, and 80% statistical power. Under these assumptions, the required sample size was 51 participants per group, for a total of 102 participants. An additional margin was considered to account for potential missing data.

Categorical variables are presented as absolute numbers and percentages. Continuous variables as mean and standard deviation or median and interquartile range according to their distribution, assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test and graphical review. To compare the two age groups (<60 vs. ≥60 years), Student's t-test was used when normality and homogeneity of variances were plausible, and the Mann–Whitney U test when these assumptions were not met. Categorical variables were compared with Pearson’s χ2 and, when there were low expected frequencies, with Fisher’s exact test. The glycemic management targets were compared using the Z-test or χ2/Fisher’s exact test, depending on the type of variable.

TIR and TITR were compared between groups using analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with age group (<60 vs. ≥60 years) as the fixed factor, and glomerular filtration rate, polypharmacy, SmartGuard (SG) mode configuration, and body composition variables (phase angle, grip strength, appendicular index, and fat mass index) as confounding covariates. This approach was chosen to account for baseline imbalances to obtain an adjusted estimate of the between-group difference. Model assumptions (linearity, homoscedasticity, normality of residuals, and homogeneity of regression slopes) were checked and were not materially violated.

Finally, an exploratory subgroup and sensitivity analyses were performed by repeating the main comparisons after targeted exclusions, including the elimination of cases with polypharmacy, stratification by the presence of chronic complications, and analysis by weight status. All tests were two-tailed, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered significant. Statistical analysis was performed using Stata version 16.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, USA).

Results

A total of 118 patients were included, 55% of whom were under 60 years of age. The median age in each group was 53 (IQR 48-57 years) vs. 66 (interquartile range [IQR] 63-69 years), p <0.001, with no differences in the duration of diabetes or complication rate. The glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was lower in patients over 60 years of age (92.5 [IQR 80-104 ml/min] vs. 77 [64-89 ml/min]; p <0.001). Polypharmacy was higher in the older group (23±35.38% vs. 31±63.27%, p 0.003). With regard to body composition, differences were observed in the appendicular index, which was higher in those under 60 years of age (4.22±0.88 kg/m2vs. 3.74±0.75 kg/m2, p 0.017); as well as the phase angle (5.0 [IQR 4.50–5.80 degrees] vs. 4.60 [IQR 4.40–5.20 degrees], p 0.008). Discussion

The use of the MiniMed 780G system in people with T1D over 60 years of age is safe and effective. This group achieved metabolic control targets similar to those achieved by young adults, with an average time in range of over 70%, achieving TITR targets with minimal exposure to hypoglycemia. Additionally, this study showed that programming at optimal parameters was safe in this population, achieving therapeutic goals in both groups.

Our findings are similar to those reported in previous studies. In a study of older Colombian adult patients using AHCL systems, the TIR and TBR goals were achieved. 7 Similar findings were reported in the RCT of 38 patients with different types of DM, where TIR targets were also achieved with shorter TBR times. 8 This evidence reinforces the usefulness of the technology in achieving metabolic control targets similar to those of young adults with a reduced risk of hypoglycemia, which is the most relevant factor in the management of older adults with T1D.

When evaluating the evidence regarding the use of the MiniMed 780G system across different age groups, including children, adults, adolescents, and older adults, the available data appear to be consistent. Previous studies have demonstrated that users across these populations are able to achieve recommended glycemic targets, with improvements in time in range and reductions in time below range, ultimately leading to better overall glycemic management.10,11 Interestingly, studies conducted in adolescent populations have also shown that greater time spent in automatic mode and fewer exits from SG mode are associated with improved time in range, 10 findings that are consistent with those observed in our cohort.

TIR was lower in patients over 60 years of age without complications. However, a higher proportion of young adults had their SG set with optimal settings (Glucose target 100 mg/dl, AIT 2 hours) compared to patients over 60 years of age (66% vs. 33%, p=0.032). Corroborating the impact of system configuration, a real-world retrospective study of 35,366 older adults (>56 years old) using the MiniMed 780G system found that the use of optimal settings was associated with higher TIR and TITR compared to users without these settings. Furthermore, TBR <70 mg/dL was slightly lower in older users than in younger ones. Notably, this age group demonstrated greater adherence to both the sensor and the SG system. Finally, metabolic control was sustained at the one-year follow-up. Consequently, optimal SG settings appear to be the primary determinant of glycemic success, independent of age.Therefore, the use of optimal SG parameters should be considered in this population. 12

Other parameters can affect glycemic management in the elderly population, including grip strength, fat mass, and phase angle. Among women under 60 years of age, diminished grip strength was linked to suboptimal glycemic metrics. A cohort of Korean patients showed that lower grip strength is associated with a higher risk of hyperglycemia and diabetes, with this effect being greater in younger patients and even greater in women, with an OR for diabetes of 3.38, 95% CI 2.70–4.24. 13 This suggests that grip strength in young women could be a predictor of low TIR associated with an increased risk of hyperglycemia. Another study showed an inverse association between grip strength and risk of hypoglycemia, 7 which is consistent with the findings of this study. With regard to the fat mass index in men over 60 years of age, it was found that a fat mass index of less than 4 is a predictor of suboptimal glycemic metrics with a statistically significant difference in TIR, being lower in elderly patients, with a clinically relevant difference in TBR. The evidence regarding adipose tissue is divergent; it has been reported that a higher amount of fat is associated with lower TIR and higher TAR. 14 With regard to TBR, it is consistent, as it has been seen that low fat mass increases the risk of hypoglycemia.7,14 These findings highlight the importance of comprehensive evaluation of patients with T1D, including body composition measurements in clinical practice.

This study describes the first cohort comparing older adults with T1D who use AHCL systems with young adults who use the same technology. Additionally, multiple confounding variables of metabolic control were controlled for, and clinical, functional, SG settings and body composition parameters were integrated. The main limitation of this study is its cross-sectional design, which precludes longitudinal follow-up and limits the ability to determine whether the observed glycemic outcomes are sustained over time. Therefore, further longitudinal studies with extended follow-up periods are needed to evaluate the long-term effectiveness of the therapy. In addition, the cohorts were not matched at baseline, which may increase the risk of selection bias and residual confounding. To minimize this limitation, multiple potential confounding variables were assessed and included in the analysis. Additionally, the older adult population included in this study was composed predominantly of relatively healthy, fit, and functionally independent individuals with a low burden of comorbidities. Therefore, these findings may not be fully generalizable to frail older adults, particularly those with higher levels of dependency, cognitive impairment, or multiple comorbidities. This limitation should be considered when extrapolating our results to the broader older adult population with T1D. Further studies including larger and more heterogeneous cohorts are needed to better characterize potential age-related differences in outcomes.

Conclusion

The use of MiniMed 780G is safe and effective in patients over 60 years of age with T1D, achieving strict targets for TBR, TIR, and even TITR. The use of the optimal SG settings is associated with a better metabolic profile in older adult patients, so the use of this configuration should be considered regardless of the patient’s age. Additionally, this study showed other factors that can affect glycemic metrics in patients using this technology, including body composition parameters and grip strength.
