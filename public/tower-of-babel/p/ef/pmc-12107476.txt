Contribution of T cell subsets to different food allergic diseases
Authors: Lisa Hung, Brianna Zientara, M. Cecilia Berin
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
SummaryFood allergies occur due to a lack of tolerance to the proteins found in foods. While IgE- and non-IgE-mediated food allergies have different clinical manifestations, epidemiology, pathophysiology, and management, they share dysregulated T cell responses. Recent studies have shed light on the contributions of different T cell subsets to the development and persistence of different food allergic diseases. This review discusses the role of T cells in both IgE- and non-IgE-mediated food allergies and considers the potential future investigations in this context.

INTRODUCTION

Food allergies are reproducible, immune-mediated reactions to a particular food.1 The most common form of food allergy is mediated by allergen-specific IgE antibodies (IgE-FA). However, the term “food allergy” also encompasses non-IgE or cell-mediated disorders such as eosinophilic gastrointestinal disorders (EGIDs) and food protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES). While the symptoms differ between these diseases, they often develop in early life, appear to be increasing in prevalence,2–4 and are frequently present with other atopic comorbidities.5–7 Immunological memory is a key component of these disorders, allowing for reproducible reactions to the same stimulus indicating the presence of an atypical adaptive immune system.

While the degranulation of innate immune cells such as mast cells and basophils are essential for the generation of acute systemic allergic reactions in IgE-FA, the adaptive immune system comprised of T and B lymphocytes plays a key role in the development and maintenance of all forms of food allergy. These cells mount antigen-specific responses and continue to persist as memory cells. B cells are required for the generation of pathogenic allergen-specific IgE antibodies and serve as reservoirs for IgE memory.8,9 B cells are also necessary to produce allergen-specific IgG4 antibodies, a marker of food exposure that has protective functions in IgE-FA10 and has also been described in the context of eosinophilic esophagitis (EoE).11–14 Regulation of antibody responses is achieved through a subset of CD4+ T helper cells called T follicular helper (Tfh) cells. The non-IgE-mediated food allergies EoE and FPIES are thought to derive their specificity from CD4+ T helper cells that secrete cytokines that drive pathophysiology in response to stimulation with food antigens.

Indeed, a central factor linking all forms of food allergies is the evidence of an aberrant T cell response. This review provides a brief overview of the major T cell subsets involved in the pathogenesis of both IgE- and non-IgE-mediated food allergies.

OVERVIEW OF IgE- AND NON-IgE-MEDIATED FOOD ALLERGIES

The different forms of food allergy can be distinguished by their clinical manifestations (Figure 1). IgE-FA are characterized by a rapid response to the eliciting food within minutes and up to several hours after exposure. Type I hypersensitivity reactions drive symptoms in the skin (hives, angioedema), lungs (wheezing, throat tightness), and gastrointestinal tract (nausea, vomiting, diarrhea). Reaction severity varies broadly, but IgE-FA can be fatal and are the leading cause of life-threatening anaphylaxis in children.15 The prevalence is estimated at 8%–10% of children16 and approximately 10% of adults17 in the United States, although precise numbers can be difficult to obtain due to inflation via self-reports. Food-challenge confirmation of food allergy is often too laborious for large studies. In IgE-FA, food-specific IgE antibodies are continuously produced. These antibodies readily bind the high-affinity IgE receptor (FcεRI) expressed primarily by tissue-resident mast cells and circulating basophils.18 Following ingestion, the IgE antibodies recognize and bind the food allergen, resulting in receptor crosslinking. Subsequent signal transduction prompts degranulation, releasing histamine and inflammatory mediators and the rapid production of arachidonic acid metabolites (e.g., prostaglandins, leukotrienes), cytokines, and chemokines that mediate systemic allergic reactions.19 While the most common method of management for IgE-mediated food allergies is avoidance of the trigger-food and emergency intervention with epinephrine, there is increasing availability of alternative treatment options, as exemplified by the recent FDA approval of Xolair20 (omalizumab, anti-IgE) to reduce risk of severe allergic reactions and Palforzia21 (peanut powder for oral immunotherapy [OIT]) to treat peanut allergy.

Non-IgE mediated food allergies present with delayed or chronic symptoms generally affecting the gastrointestinal tract.22 These are considered T cell-mediated hypersensitivities, or type IV hypersensitivity reactions. EoE is a chronic allergic inflammatory disorder of the esophagus that affects both children and adults.2 The estimates of EoE prevalence differ based on definition, age, and geographical location but range between 26 and 60 cases per 100,000 individuals in North America.2,23 EoE is caused by an infiltration of eosinophils, mast cells, and CD4+ T cells into the leaky esophageal epithelium, and subsequent esophageal remodeling and changes in motility.24 Symptoms can be gradual and evolve with age. In early life, symptoms involve feeding difficulties and failure to thrive. This can progress to reflux symptoms and dysphagia, with food impaction observed in older adolescents and adults. In any stage, esophageal dysfunction can greatly affect quality of life.24 EoE is diagnosed based on clinical symptoms and the presence of 15 or more eosinophils per high power field (hpf) in esophageal biopsies.25 Treatments for EoE include food elimination diets, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), topical steroids, esophageal dilation and more recently, dupilumab (anti-IL-4Rα antibody).25,26 Food allergen-specific T cells are thought to drive EoE pathology consistent with a type IV (cell-mediated) hypersensitivity response.

FPIES is another non-IgE-FA that was previously considered rare. Current estimates on the prevalence of FPIES are 0.5% in children and 0.2% in adults in the United States.27–29 FPIES is characterized by reproducible, prolonged, and repetitive vomiting 2–4 h after allergen ingestion. There is no skin or respiratory involvement in FPIES symptoms. Left untreated and in the absence of avoidance of the food trigger, individuals may progress to chronic FPIES, causing intermittent diarrhea, vomiting, and failure to thrive.28 While usually present in infancy and outgrown with age, FPIES can persist into adolescence and adulthood. Common trigger foods for FPIES are those introduced early in life such as cow’s milk, oats, and rice; and include foods not commonly associated with IgE-mediated allergy.30 However, increasing incidence of peanut-triggered FPIES has been observed, potentially resulting from early dietary introduction of peanut, implemented after guideline changes.31–33 Unlike IgE-mediated allergies, injected epinephrine is not effective in treating acute reactions. Treatments for symptomatic FPIES are currently limited to IV fluid resuscitation and antiemetics such as ondansetron.28,34 A subset of FPIES patients exhibit allergen-specific IgE sensitization to their trigger food, termed “atypical FPIES,” but their symptoms are not consistent with IgE-mediated reactions. Individuals with atypical FPIES were previously thought to have slower FPIES resolution,35 but recent studies have challenged this association.36,37 As with EoE, FPIES is thought to be a T cell-driven disease and therefore would fall into the category of type IV hypersensitivity.

T CELLS IN FOOD ALLERGY

Regulatory T cells (Tregs)

In healthy, non-allergic individuals, primary exposure to common food allergens results in oral tolerance; the default state of immunological non-responsiveness.38 It is possible that the lack of tolerance observed in IgE-FA is due to lack of opportunity. Delayed dietary introduction is a risk factor for the development of IgE-FA,39 and conversely early dietary exposure to peanut effectively prevents the development of peanut allergy40 indicating that normal oral tolerance pathways exist in individuals with IgE-FA. Additionally, food sensitization has been proposed to occur through non-oral routes (inflamed skin or respiratory tract), endorsing a lack of opportunity to form oral tolerance. Conversely, early introduction is not protective against adult onset IgE-FA, FPIES or EoE, which is clearly a failure of oral tolerance to ingested foods.

The induction of oral tolerance is dependent on the presence and functionality of a robust, immunosuppressive T regulatory cell (Treg) response to ingested foods. Most food proteins are digested and degraded into non-immunogenic peptides and amino acids. The small fraction of dietary antigens that cross the gut epithelium are captured, processed, and presented by migratory CD103+ conventional dendritic cells (cDCs). Recently, a population of RORγt+ antigen presenting cells, possibly distinct from cDCs, have also been identified as central to the induction of Tregs through the gastrointestinal tract.41–43 Antigen presentation to naïve T cells induces differentiation into Tregs which go on to suppress immune activation, resulting in a pro-tolerogenic response.44 Mice with mutations in the FOXP3 gene, the master transcription factor controlling the development of Tregs, exhibit a severe allergic inflammatory response featuring eosinophilia and dysregulated Th2 cytokine production.45 Humans with mutations in the FOXP3 gene have also been identified with manifestations that include severe IgE-FA.46 This demonstrates the importance of Tregs in the prevention of IgE-FA.

Tregs have been described as reduced in number or function in IgE-FA. Food allergic individuals have been observed to have a lower frequency of circulating Tregs compared to non-allergic controls.47–49 Allergen-specific Tregs in a mouse model of IgE-FA (driven by a gain-of-function mutation in IL-4Rα) were observed to undergo reprogramming into IL-4-secreting Th2-like cells that maintained expression of FOXP3 but contributed to disease pathogenesis.50 These reprogrammed Tregs promoted allergic dysregulation via suppression of TFGB1 transcription.51 Overexpression of a TGFB1 transgene in these Tregs suppressed mast cell degranulation, protected mice from anaphylaxis, and suppressed total and allergen-specific IgE antibody levels.51 Notably, these Th2-reprogrammed Tregs have also been detected in milk allergic children.50 Lewis et al. also identified an expansion of FOXP3+ T cells within the milk-responsive CD4+ T cell population of milk allergic individuals, however these cells had a significant change in their transcriptional profile including a type I interferon production signature and altered chemokine receptor expression.52 The milk-responsive Tregs were also transcriptionally distinct from the antigen non-specific Treg pool. Therefore, food allergies may be attributable to a failure to induce immune tolerance either due to a lack of Tregs or an inability to mount a normal, effective Treg response.

There is some question about whether food allergen-specific Tregs are responsible for tolerance to foods in healthy humans. Using proliferation-based assays, Tregs proliferating in response to food allergens have been identified in allergic individuals,53,54 but there is limited information from healthy individuals. Activation-based assays that use upregulation of activation markers such as CD154 (CD40L) after a short period of stimulation with allergen, or MHC-II-allergen tetramers, have identified a much lower frequency of food allergen-specific T cells in peripheral blood from healthy individuals, with a Th1 phenotype. We previously found that Tregs upregulated CD154 in response to peanut allergen stimulation, but only in allergic individuals and with delayed kinetics compared to effector CD4+ T cells.55 These were determined to be IL-2-dependent, and therefore activated via a bystander mechanism. Bacher et al. used a dual activation marker approach that used CD154 to identify effector cells versus CD137 to identify allergen-specific Tregs.56 While they were able to detect Tregs responsive to aeroallergens, they did not find food-responsive Tregs in circulation. We examined Tregs more carefully in the context of peanut allergy or tolerance using an array of activation markers including CD154, CD69, CD137, OX40, and CD25. We found that Tregs preferentially upregulated CD137 or CD40 together with CD25 in response to peanut stimulation, but only in allergic individuals and as we had previously observed with CD154, the kinetics were delayed as compared to effector cells.57 These activated Tregs were capable of suppressing Th2 cell activation. While this was mediated by IL-2, it was not merely an in vitro artifact, as the same delayed Treg activation was observed after allergen challenge in vivo. We speculate that antigen specificity is provided by the effector cells, while the regulatory activity is provided by IL-2-activated Tregs. This is not to say that Tregs with food allergen peptide-specific T cell receptors (TCRs) do not exist, but that they are exceedingly rare in peripheral blood, are likely resident in the gastrointestinal mucosa, and that in the periphery there is a mechanism for a regulatory response secondary to any effector cell activation.

Although there is a lack of information on the contribution of allergen-specific Tregs in oral tolerance in humans, there has been more focus on the role of Tregs in the resolution of IgE-FA, naturally or through immunotherapy. In 10%–20% of peanut or tree nut allergic children, and 50%–80% of milk or egg allergic children, there is spontaneous resolution or natural tolerance development.58–60 Investigations into those who have experienced natural resolution of their allergies have shown a decrease in allergen-specific IgE antibodies and an increase in allergen-specific IgG4 antibodies.61 Natural resolution is also associated with an increase of antigen-specific Tregs that can suppress effector T cell responses.62,63 Allergen immunotherapy (AIT) can be implemented via epicutaneous, sublingual, or oral routes and involves controlled exposure to the relevant allergen to induce a state of immune nonreactivity or desensitization. Continued exposure to the allergen provides some level of protection, usually to accidental exposure or cross contamination. However, the ideal state of immune modulation in response to AIT is sustained unresponsiveness to the allergen, or remission, where protection remains even after intentional, continued allergen exposure is ceased.

Oral immunotherapy (OIT) trials in patients have produced mixed results on the induction of Tregs. A small cohort study found allergen-induced Tregs (proliferating CD4+CD25hiFOXP3+) were increased in patients that achieved sustained unresponsiveness after peanut OIT. These Tregs also had enhanced suppressive function and greater levels of FOXP3 hypomethylation.54 However, larger studies utilizing TCR sequencing, gene expression analyses, and activation marker-based approaches have found no changes in allergen-specific Treg frequencies during or following OIT.64–66 Conversely, total Tregs have been observed to expand following OIT,67 and most significantly in patients that achieved sustained unresponsiveness.57 We speculate that the expansion of total Tregs in the periphery may be due to IL-2 released during chronic antigen stimulation.57

In non-IgE-mediated food allergies, where there has been previous oral exposure prior to onset of symptoms, it is unclear why oral tolerance has not prevented disease. In individuals with EoE, there is an enrichment in Tregs in the inflamed esophagus and in the periphery, despite (or because of) persistent inflammation.68–71 Treg frequency correlated with infiltration of eosinophils and mast cells in tissues.68,71,72 Single-cell RNA sequencing and flow cytometry using cells isolated from esophageal biopsies from EoE patients also identified a population of FOXP3+ T cells co-expressing GATA3.71 The presence of these cells indicates a potential role for Th2-reprogrammed Tregs in allergic inflammation in EoE, or an impairment in suppressive functionality. It is not clear if the Tregs in the esophagus are contributing to disease or are recruited into the tissue and activated through an IL-2-dependent mechanism.

In FPIES, where reactivity develops shortly after introduction of the first foods into the diet in early life, it seems obvious that there must be an impairment in normal regulatory mechanisms in the gastrointestinal tract. Access to gastrointestinal tissue is extremely limited in FPIES since there is no clinical need for endoscopy or biopsy. A single study reported a decrease in the TGF-β receptor 1 on epithelial cells in children with FPIES (diagnosed by food challenge) compared to controls.73 However, no role for Tregs in FPIES has yet been identified.

Th2 Cells

The presence and activity of T helper 2 (Th2) cells have long been associated with IgE-FA. Th2 cells are defined by their production of type 2 cytokines (IL-4 as the defining cytokine but commonly co-expressing IL-13 and other type 2 associated cytokines such as IL-5 and IL-9), and expression of the transcription factor GATA3.74 Type 2 cytokines have numerous functions which can be protective in the context of parasitic infections, toxins, or venoms, but can also have detrimental effects in atopic individuals in the form of acute or chronic allergic diseases.75 As such, allergic individuals have been observed to have constitutively higher plasma levels of type 2 cytokines.76

Food allergen reactivity or specificity of Th2 cells was initially shown by proliferation assays, where proliferated cells in response to peanut stimulation (having low expression of the membrane dye CFSE) co-expressed IL-4, IL-5, and IL-13.77 Subsequent studies used methods with greater specificity, including activation-induced marker (AIM) studies or MHC-Class II tetramers to show co-expression of type 2 cytokines.78,79 While some studies indicated that controls were Th1-skewed, other studies showed that the ratio of IFN-γ+ cells among antigen-specific cells was increased in healthy controls, but the frequency of antigen-specific CD4+ Th1 cells was not different between allergic and control.79

Allergen-specific Th2 cells are associated with different clinical phenotypes within IgE-FA. Allergen-specific Th2 cell frequency is negatively correlated with the threshold of reactivity.80 As such, a greater number of allergen-reactive T cells indicates increased sensitivity to the allergen.55,65,80 Allergen-reactive Th2 polarized cells also correlate with levels of allergen-specific IgE.55,80 Individuals with allergy to cows’ milk or egg are often tolerant to baked or extensively heated forms of the food due to the disruption of conformational epitopes by high heat in those foods. Baked-egg-tolerant individuals have been observed to have significantly fewer Th2 cells among egg-reactive T cells.81

EoE is also an antigen-specific type 2 driven disorder.82 Like Tregs, effector Th2 cells are enriched in peripheral blood and esophageal tissue derived from individuals with active EoE.71,83 In the periphery, Th2 cells have been shown to be milk-reactive and produce substantial levels of IL-4, -13, and -5 compared to milk-reactive cells derived from patients in remission.83 Of the Th2 cytokines, IL-13 is the most robustly produced in tissues, and likely contributes to further esophageal barrier dysfunction.71 IL-5 also plays a key role in eosinophilic disease as it is a known recruitment, activation, and survival signal for eosinophils.84 Increased IL5 gene expression correlates with increased symptom severity, though this relationship is not directly proportional due to disease heterogeneity.82

Both IgE-FA and EGIDs are associated with a type 2 immune response to foods, yet the manifestations of disease are very different. Prussin et al. examined the phenotype of peripheral blood peanut-specific CD4+ T cells by CD154 assay in three groups of individuals: eosinophilic gastroenteritis, IgE-mediated peanut allergy, and healthy controls.79 They identified peanut-specific CD4+ T cells making IL-4 at elevated levels in both IgE-mediated peanut allergy and EGID compared to healthy controls. However, the frequency of peanut-specific CD4+ T cells co-expressing IL-5 was significantly higher in EGID compared to peanut allergy, and both were elevated compared to controls. IL-5 is a growth factor for eosinophils, which could underlie the eosinophilia in EGID. This was one of the first papers to identify heterogeneity within the Th2 compartment that could explain differences in clinical manifestations of food-triggered diseases.

Pathogenic effector Th2 (peTh2) cells/Th2A cells

IL-4 is not inherently pathogenic. It is important for B cell help and the generation of protective antibody responses, including IgG4. IL-4 and IL-13 are also important for wound repair.85 Some stages of pregnancy are associated with Th2 predominance which promotes growth of the fetus.86 A Th2 bias is also normally found in infancy. The regulatory cytokine IL-10 was also initially described as a Th2 cytokine. So what distinguishes benign from pathogenic Th2 immunity? In the context of EGID and atopic dermatitis, Mitson-Salazar et al in 2016 identified a subset of CD4+ T cells that they termed pathogenic effector Th2 cells, or peTh2.87 These cells were defined as expressing CRTH2 (for Chemoattractant Receptor-homologous molecule expressed on T-Helper type 2 cells, also known as the Prostaglandin D2 receptor 2), hematopoietic prostaglandin D synthase (hPGDS), and high levels of CD161. Conventional Th2 cells were defined as expressing only CRTH2. peTh2 cells made significantly more IL-5 and IL-13 than conventional Th2 cells, correlated with eosinophilia, and were highly elevated in both atopic dermatitis and EGID. They also expressed receptors for and were more responsive than conventional Th2 cells to the allergic alarmins TSLP, IL-33, and IL-25. In 2017, Wambre et al. described a population of terminally differentiated Th2 cells that expressed CRTH2, CD161, and CD49d that were termed Th2A cells.88 These cells comprised most allergen-specific Th2 cells and were exclusively present in allergic individuals.88 They showed that these cells were activated during pollen season, and in the context of IgE-mediated peanut allergy, showed that they were reduced in frequency after OIT. They subsequently showed that activation of these cells can be amplified by the presence of IL-33 signaling through ST2.89 These two subsets appear to be very similar, so we will refer to these as peTh2 cells as they were reported prior to Th2A cells. peTh2 cells arise after repeated rounds of TCR stimulation of Th2 cells in the presence of differentiation factors,90 and we have shown that peanut-specific Th2 cells gain expression of IL-5 and IL-9 as they lose expression of CD27,55 consistent with terminal differentiation. Cells consistent with peTh2 cells have also been described via single cell RNAseq methods by Shreffler and colleagues in the context of peanut allergy66 and EoE.91 In peanut allergy, these cells were identified as antigen-specific based on their expression of CD154 after peanut stimulation,66 while in EoE these cells were milk-responsive in peripheral blood,91 and had shared expanded clonotypes between tissue and peripheral blood suggesting milk-responsive peTh2 cells in the esophagus.

Peanut allergic individuals with a greater frequency of peanut-reactive peTH2 cells have higher levels of circulating peanut-specific IgE and IgG4.65 The frequency of circulating allergen-specific Th2/peTH2 cells reproducibly decreases following OIT for IgE-FA.64–66,80 Successful clinical outcome was also associated with a stronger suppression of Th2 gene signatures during OIT,65,66 but these cells have the potential to rebound after treatment discontinuation.65,80 Treatment failure was associated with higher baseline Th2 responses and peTh2 cell frequency.65,80 Therefore, peTh2 cells appear to be highly correlated with disease activity in peanut allergy. However, it is unclear how peTh2 cells could induce such distinct clinical manifestations as anaphylaxis in IgE-FA versus localized tissue inflammation in EoE. Direct comparisons of peTh2 cells from patients with EGID and IgE-FA have shown distinct cytokine expression profiles following stimulation with PMA/ionomycin.92 peTh2 cells from EGID patients exhibited elevated IL-5, and IL-13 expression whereas peTh2 cells from IgE-FA patients upregulated IL-9, IL-10, IL-17A, and IFN-γ. However, elevated IL-5 and IL-13 expression by peTh2 cells or Th2 cells have been identified in peanut allergy by several groups.55,66,88 Morgan et al. identified expression of GPR15, a receptor involved in homing to the esophagus, on peTh2 cells in EoE.91 This together with the esophageal epithelial barrier dysfunction identified in EoE could allow for the tissue-specific inflammation observed. It is not clear from the study of Th2 or peTh2 cells why individuals with IgE-FA have pathogenic levels of IgE and can undergo anaphylaxis in response to allergen exposure while other atopic diseases that also have peTh2 cells do not.

The role of Th2/peTh2 cells and type 2 cytokines in the pathogenesis of EoE is clearly demonstrated by the efficacy of dupilumab, a human monoclonal antibody blocking the shared IL-4 and IL-13 receptor (IL-4Rα). Dupilumab treatment improved global symptoms and histological outcomes in patients with active EoE.26,93–95 Significant reductions in eosinophil counts resulting in histological remission reproducibly occurred and were maintained following continued dupilumab use, even in patients with refractory fibrostenotic EoE.94 There is a lack of rigorous published information on the impact of dupilumab on IgE-FA. A case report of a patient on dupilumab for AD described tolerance development to 2 previously allergy-triggering foods after 3 months of treatment.96 Further, 3 years of dupilumab use in AD patients with food allergies resulted in a significant and sustained decrease (at least 80%) in allergen-specific IgE levels and patient-reported decrease in symptom severity in accidental food-induced allergic reactions.97 Two trials of dupilumab have been completed, one as a monotherapy (NCT03793608) and the other in combination with peanut OIT (NCT03682770). Results have not been published for either trial, but the primary outcome reported on clinicaltrials.gov indicates that as a monotherapy, 2 of 24 participants treated with dupilumab increased their cumulative tolerated dose from <143 mg to at least 443 mg of peanut protein after 24 weeks. This was not placebo controlled for statistical comparison, but the benefit of treatment as a monotherapy for 24 weeks appears to be relatively minor. When dupilumab was given together with peanut OIT, the percent of individuals who passed a 2044 mg peanut challenge was 56% compared to 36% in those treated with OIT alone for 28–40 weeks. Dupilumab appears to have some additional effect when provided together with OIT, indicating that reactivation of the Th2 cells needs to be achieved for dupilumab to have an effect in IgE-FA. Given the previous findings that individuals with high Th2/peTh2 frequency at baseline were more likely to fail OIT, it would be interesting to stratify results based on this baseline biomarker.

T follicular helper cells

Frequency of Th2 or peTh2 cells did not discriminate between allergic diseases with a role for IgE (IgE-FA) versus those with IgE-independent localized type 2 inflammation (EoE). Although frequency of Th2 and peTh2 cells were described to correlate with levels of food allergen-specific IgE, these cells are not the main cells that would be expected to provide B cell help for IgE class-switch. T cell-dependent antibody production, including IgE, relies on a subset of CD4+ T cells called Tfh cells.44,98 These cells operate in B cell follicles, driving germinal center reactions and class switch recombination in B cells through the secretion of specific cytokines.44

It had been known for a number of years that Tfh cells could be further subdivided (similar to Th1/Th2/Th17) into different subsets based on the cytokines that they produced. IgE was shown to be regulated by IL-4 derived from Tfh cells.98 In their search to understand why only some IgE results in pathology, Gowthaman et al. discovered a Tfh subset that co-expressed IL-4, IL-13, and IL-5, distinct from Tfh2 cells that just produced IL-4.99 These cells were necessary for the generation of high-affinity IgE antibodies essential for anaphylaxis.99 Their function was dependent specifically on the production of IL-13, and therefore these cells were called Tfh13 cells. Tfh13 cells are identified by their expression of transcription factors GATA3/BCL6, and high levels of IL-4, -5, and -13 and low levels of IL-21 production.99,100 These cells have also been identified in humans sensitized to peanut or aeroallergens. It is not yet clear why IL-13 is a better driver of high-affinity IgE class switch than IL-4. Of note, allergen-specific Tfh13-like cells have both been observed to decrease101 or remain unaffected by OIT,66 but neither observation was specific to treatment success. It has not yet been shown if Tfh13 cells distinguish IgE-FA from EoE, but this is a plausible explanation for the difference in IgE dependence of the two diseases.

Other Tfh subsets may also contribute to IgE-FA. There are mixed reports about the role of T follicular regulatory (Tfr) cells in regulation of IgE. Tfr cells are CXCR5+Foxp3+ cells that have been reported to suppress102 or augment103 the production of IgE. A distinct population of IL-10+ Foxp3− Tfh cells has also been described to suppress IgE in human cells.104 IgG4 plays an important role in both oral tolerance and the response to OIT, but the phenotype of Tfh cells regulating this antibody production is not yet clear. Food-specific IgG4 antibodies have also been observed in the esophageal tissue and plasma of patients with active EoE.12,105 These antibodies correlate with tissue eosinophilia and endoscopic reference score.105 Following food elimination diets, food-specific IgG4 levels in tissues were suppressed in those that responded to the treatment.12 It is not clear if the same Tfh cell is responsible for IgG4 in oral tolerance, response to OIT, and in EoE.

FPIES does not feature elevated levels of food-specific antibodies of any isotype,106,107 although a subset has food-specific IgE but a lack of symptoms consistent with type I hypersensitivity reactions.108 Interestingly, patients with milk-induced FPIES produce less milk-specific IgA, IgG, and IgG4 compared to controls.106,109,110 Therefore, in FPIES there may be a deficiency in Tfh responses that normally promote oral tolerance.

Th17 cells

While the classical Th1/Th2 axis in IgE-FA is well-appreciated, emerging data is revealing a potential role for Th17 cells in this context. Th17 cells are characterized by their production of inflammatory cytokines including IL-17A/F and IL-22 and have been implicated in the pathogenesis of several autoimmune diseases.111 The function and pattern of Th17 derived IL-17 in IgE-FA remains unresolved.112 In adult patients with IgE-FA, serum levels of IL-17A were observed to be higher than controls.113 In contrast, IL-17 production by CD4+ T cells in response to antigen stimulation was significantly reduced in food allergic children,114 and a trend towards negative association of IL-17A and allergic sensitization was also observed.115 Further, a relatively recent analysis of Th17-related cytokines in the serum of allergic patients did not detect a significant difference from controls.116

However, a population of allergen-reactive Th17-like CCR6+ cells has been repeatedly observed in food-allergic patients.65,66,80,117 These T cells expressing Th17-related genes were found to be inversely related to the proportion of peTh2 cells in allergic individuals and were observed to increase significantly during peanut immunotherapy.64,65,80,118 An increased proportion of these allergen-reactive Th17-like cells at baseline characterized a group of allergic individuals with less peanut-specific IgE, and better clinical outcomes following OIT (desensitization/remission).65 Interestingly these Th17-like cells express mRNA but not protein for IL-17 and IL-22. Although the function of these cells has not yet been identified, their negative association with IgE and peTh2 cells and their expansion in response to OIT indicate a potential protective role for Th17-like cells in IgE-FA.

Of note, patients with active EoE have recently been observed to have a significant depletion of Th17 cells in esophageal biopsies compared to healthy controls.119 This characteristic is retained even in patients in remission. These Th17 cells also showed a reduced capacity to generate cytokines.119 It was suggested that this reduction in Th17 cells may be due to the suppressive activity of eosinophils.120 Interestingly, Th17 cells isolated from esophageal biopsies of EoE patients exhibited higher levels of activation via gene expression than T cells isolated from the duodenum,91 further emphasizing the perplexing role of Th17 cells in food allergic diseases.

Although FPIES is considered a cell-mediated disease, it has been difficult to identify a food allergen-specific T cell subset that is associated with disease manifestations. CD154-based activation assays do not reveal an increased frequency of food allergen-responsive T cells in peripheral blood of FPIES.121 T cell restimulation assays and proliferation assays also failed to detect a signal of food allergen reactivity in FPIES.107 However, during FPIES reactions after an oral food challenge, there is a significant increase in plasma levels of IL-2, IL-17, and IL-22, consistent with a Th17 signature.122 This suggests that the cellular source of these cytokines is not found in peripheral blood and may be localized to the gastrointestinal tract. Within the gastrointestinal tract, there are several sources beyond conventional Th17 cells that could produce these cytokines. Intraepithelial lymphocytes with a γδTCR,123 mucosal associated invariant cells,124 and innate lymphoid cells type 3 (ILC3)125 have all been described as sources of IL-17 associated cytokines. Access to the gastrointestinal tissues may be necessary to identify pathogenic food allergen-responsive T cells in FPIES, but based on the cytokine profile of FPIES reactions, we would categorize this disease as an IL-17-associated type IV hypersensitivity reaction. Despite the lack of association with type 2 immunity or IgE, this is categorized as a food allergy based on the definition of “an adverse health effect arising from a specific immune response that occurs reproducibly on exposure to a given food.”1

FUTURE DIRECTIONS

Basic immune mechanisms of food allergy

There are many unexplored avenues for research on the role of T cells in food allergic diseases that are essential to understanding the pathophysiology of these disorders and to find new therapeutic targets. In IgE-FA, a better understanding of the relative contribution of peTh2 and Tfh13 cells to the maintenance of disease is essential. The role of peTh2 cells has not been well explored in mouse models, and we are lacking information about the immune milieu in the gastrointestinal tract of IgE-FA. The source of IL-4 or IL-13 required for the sequential switching of IgG1 memory B cells to IgE-producing plasma cells to maintain allergen-specific IgE is currently undefined. The relative contribution of peTh2-associated cytokines beyond IL-4 and IL-13 would also be of interest. peTh2 cells come back after OIT is stopped, understanding the reservoir of these cells and regulation of peTh2 development, as begun by Brennan and colleagues126 would be important for more sustained therapeutics.

In EoE there is a need to better understand the downstream effects of T cells and relative contribution of IL-4 and IL-13 to disease. There is a need for tests (ideally non-invasive) that can accurately identify food triggers. Because peTh2 cells express receptors for Th2 alarmins, it would be of interest to understand the relative contribution of activation through TCR versus cytokine receptors in disease. Dupilumab is not 100% effective, suggesting pathways other than those signaling through the IL-4Rα may be involved. An interferon signal was identified by Ruffner and colleagues in EoE,127 and in preprint form it was reported that interferon-gamma, the source of which is CD8+ T cells, disrupts the esophageal epithelial barrier.128 A better understanding of the role of these non-Th2 pathways and CD8+ T cells in EoE is essential.

In FPIES, there is a need to identify the cell type responding to foods, and to define the mechanism of recognition (classical MHC II-peptide recognition of conventional CD4+ T cells or alternative mechanisms). There is also a need to better understand the link between immune activation and symptoms which appear to be primarily systemic and mediated by the central nervous system. There is also an urgent need for a mouse model of FPIES, which is lacking.

Therapeutics

The central role of T cells in food allergies suggests that more specific targeting of T cells may be effective treatment options. Janus Kinase (JAK) inhibitors prevent signaling downstream of many cytokine receptors. There are four JAK family kinases (Jak1, Jak2, Jak3, and Tyk2). Jak1 inhibitors block signaling downstream of many cytokines derived from peTh2 and Tfh13 cells, including IL-4, IL-13, IL-9, and IL-21 as well as the upstream cytokine TSLP. The JAK1 inhibitor abrocitinib is currently under investigation for use in IgE-FA (NCT05069831), and pre-clinical work showed that abrocitinib suppressed activation of effector T cells while preserving Treg activation in vitro.129 Treatment also decreased peanut-specific basophil activation and suppressed peanut-induced cytokine release (IL-5, -9, -10, -13) from PBMCs.129 An antibody targeting IL-13 (cendakimab) is currently in a Phase III trial for the treatment of EoE (NCT04753697). This would target IL-13 binding to either of the two IL-13 receptor subtypes, and therefore may have more inhibitory activity than dupilumab in EoE. Tezepelumab (an anti-TSLP monoclonal antibody that would suppress one of the three upstream Th2-inducing alarm-ins) is being tested for EoE (NCT05583227). The anti-IL-33 antibody etokimab was shown to have some efficacy in peanut allergy when given as a single administration in the absence of OIT,130 there is a need for further work in this area.

Abatacept is a CTLA4 agonist that binds to CD80 and CD86 and modulates co-stimulation of T cells. It is currently being investigated for use as an adjuvant in peanut OIT (NCT04872218) and the study is expected to be completed in 2024. An approach that targets trafficking of T cells is etrasimod, which is a sphingosine 1-phosphate receptor modulator that sequesters lymphocytes in the lymph nodes and prevents their entry into tissues. It has been tested in EoE (NCT04682639), with a positive primary outcome posted to clinicaltrials.gov in 2023 but no peer-reviewed publication yet released. There is a complete lack of therapeutic studies for FPIES. IL-17-targeting biologics may not be an acceptable approach for young infants with a high likelihood of resolution. There is a need to develop safe approaches to promote oral tolerance to foods in FPIES.

To advance the basic science and therapeutics of food allergies, it is important to have basic, translational, and clinical researchers working side-by-side. The NIH-funded Consortium for Food Allergy Research (CoFAR) and Consortium of Eosinophilic Gastrointestinal Disease Researchers (CEGIR) have brought together clinical and translational researchers and changed the landscape of research in IgE-FA and EoE. A similar collaborative approach is needed for FPIES. It would also be beneficial to have closer relationships between basic scientists working on type 2 immunity and mucosal immunology and the translational/clinical consortia.

CONCLUSIONS

CD4+ T cells play an important role in the pathophysiology of all forms of food allergy. They mediate disease through the production of cytokines and interactions with other innate and adaptive immune cell subsets. However, they may also play a crucial role in the shift from allergy to tolerance development through their multi-functionality and plasticity. Investigations into the basic biology of different subsets of T cells will aid in understanding the fundamental aspects of food allergy pathogenesis and define novel therapeutic targets for the treatment of these disorders.
