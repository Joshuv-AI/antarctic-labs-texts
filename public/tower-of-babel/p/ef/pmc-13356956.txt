Pheochromocytoma unmasked by rapid deterioration of previously stable type 2 diabetes
Authors: Nozomi Sasatsuki, Shotaro Nakamura, Naoki Sekine, Takehiro Katahira, Satoshi Kadowaki, Hiroaki Satoh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 46-year-old man with a 15-year history of type 2 diabetes had maintained hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) around 6.5% (SI: 48 mmol/mol) (reference range, 4.7%-6.2% [28-44 mmol/mol]) for approximately 12 years on saxagliptin monotherapy, but glycemic control worsened over 1 year despite additional ipragliflozin and metformin. Abdominal computed tomography (CT), performed after health-screening urine dipstick protein positivity, detected a left adrenal mass. He had tachycardia and weight loss but no palpitations, headache, pallor, or diaphoresis. Biochemical tests showed marked catecholamine excess and elevated 24-hour urinary metanephrines, and iodine-123 metaiodobenzylguanidine (123I-MIBG) scintigraphy showed concordant uptake. Before surgery, fasting C-peptide was 0.64 ng/mL (SI: 0.21 nmol/L) (reference range, 0.80-2.50 ng/mL [SI: 0.26-0.83 nmol/L]), C-peptide index was 0.51, and homeostasis model assessment of beta-cell function (HOMA-beta) was 11.0. After alpha blockade and volume expansion, laparoscopic left adrenalectomy confirmed pheochromocytoma. Antihypertensive and glucose-lowering medications were discontinued postoperatively, and glycemic control remained stable without antidiabetic medication at 6 months. This case highlights pheochromocytoma as a reversible endocrine cause of rapid worsening of previously stable type 2 diabetes.Keywords: pheochromocytoma, type 2 diabetes, adrenalectomy, catecholamines, insulin secretion

Introduction

Pheochromocytomas and paragangliomas are catecholamine-producing tumors that can alter glucose homeostasis by suppressing insulin secretion, increasing hepatic glucose production, and peripheral insulin resistance [1-4]. Glucose intolerance and diabetes are common in these tumors, and glycemic control frequently improves after resection [1, 2, 5-9]. However, pheochromocytoma-associated dysglycemia may be overlooked in patients with established type 2 diabetes because worsening hyperglycemia is often attributed to normal progression of diabetes.

We present a patient with long-term type 2 diabetes whose glycemic control had been stable for many years but then deteriorated rapidly. Subsequent evaluation revealed pheochromocytoma, and adrenalectomy was followed by sustained medication-free improvement in glycemic control. The central clinical message of this case is that an abrupt change in glycemic trajectory can indicate a reversible endocrine cause, even without classic paroxysmal symptoms.

Case presentation

A 46-year-old man was referred for evaluation of a left adrenal/retroperitoneal mass detected by abdominal computed tomography (CT) to assess renal morphology after a health-screening urine dipstick test showed proteinuria (1+). At our hospital, urinalysis was negative for protein, glucose, ketones, and blood. He had a 15-year history of type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and dyslipidemia. There was no known family history of diabetes, pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma, or endocrine neoplasia. His glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) had remained around 6.5% (SI: 48 mmol/mol) (reference range, 4.7%-6.2% [SI: 28-44 mmol/mol]) for approximately 12 years with saxagliptin 5 mg/day monotherapy, but increased over the previous year to 8.7% (SI: 72 mmol/mol) despite sequential addition of ipragliflozin 50 mg/day and metformin 2000 mg/day (Table 1). At first visit, postprandial plasma glucose was 149 mg/dL (SI: 8.3 mmol/L) (reference range for fasting plasma glucose, 70-109 mg/dL [SI: 3.9-6.1 mmol/L]) and HbA1c was 8.4% (SI: 68 mmol/mol). Because reduced endogenous insulin secretion was suspected and saxagliptin was not available in our formula, the treatment was changed to teneligliptin 20 mg/day, imeglimin 2000 mg/day, and ipragliflozin 50 mg/day. The anti-glutamic acid decarboxylase antibody was negative. He had never used insulin before this episode, except for transient perioperative management.

At admission for endocrine evaluation, height was 172 cm, weight was 53.1 kg, and body mass index was 17.9 kg/m2. He reported an unintentional weight loss of approximately 3 kg in the previous year. While receiving cilnidipine 10 mg/day, blood pressure was 126/91 mmHg with a pulse rate of 113 beats/min. He had no cushingoid features and no palpitations, headache, pallor, or diaphoresis.

Diagnostic assessment

Routine laboratory tests showed creatinine 0.91 mg/dL (SI: 80.4 μmol/L) (reference range, 0.61-1.04 mg/dL [SI: 53.9-91.9 μmol/L]), estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) 72 mL/min/1.73 m2 (reference range, ≥60 mL/min/1.73 m2), thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) 1.16 μIU/mL (SI: 1.16 mIU/L) (reference range, 0.560-4.30 μIU/mL [SI: 0.560-4.30 mIU/L]), free thyroxine (fT4) 1.14 ng/dL (SI: 14.7 pmol/L) (reference range, 1.00-1.70 ng/dL [SI: 12.9-21.9 pmol/L]), adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) 22.7 pg/mL (SI: 5.0 pmol/L) (reference range, 7-63 pg/mL [SI: 1.5-13.9 pmol/L]), and cortisol 10.3 μg/dL (SI: 284 nmol/L) (reference range, 7.07-19.60 μg/dL [SI: 195-541 nmol/L]) (Table 2). At preoperative admission, fasting plasma glucose was 125 mg/dL (SI: 6.9 mmol/L), fasting insulin was 1.9 μU/mL (SI: 13.2 pmol/L), fasting C-peptide was 0.64 ng/mL (SI: 0.21 nmol/L), C-peptide index was 0.51, homeostasis model assessment of beta-cell function (HOMA-beta) was 11.0, and homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) was 0.59, suggesting markedly impaired endogenous insulin secretion without clear fasting insulin resistance (Table 3).

Unenhanced abdominal CT demonstrated a 67-mm, well-circumscribed, heterogeneous left adrenal mass cranial to the left kidney (Fig. 1A). The mean unenhanced CT attenuation was 48.91 Hounsfield units, not compatible with a lipid-rich adrenal adenoma. Because the CT was unenhanced, contrast enhancement and vascularity could not be evaluated. Plasma adrenaline was 1282 pg/mL (SI: 7.0 nmol/L) (reference range, 0-100 pg/mL [SI: 0-0.55 nmol/L]) and noradrenaline was 1448 pg/mL (SI: 8.6 nmol/L) (reference range, 100-450 pg/mL [SI: 0.59-2.66 nmol/L]). Twenty-four-hour urinary metanephrines was 6.80 to 7.90 mg/day (SI: 34.5-40.1 μmol/day) (reference range, 0.04-0.19 mg/day [SI: 0.20-0.96 μmol/day]), and normetanephrine was 3.06 to 3.33 mg/day (SI: 16.7-18.2 μmol/day) (reference range, 0.09-0.33 mg/day [SI: 0.49-1.80 μmol/day]) (Table 3). Iodine-123 metaiodobenzylguanidine (123I-MIBG) scintigraphy demonstrated tracer uptake corresponding to the mass (Fig. 1B).

Catecholamine excess, elevated urinary metanephrines, and concordant uptake on 123I-MIBG scintigraphy established the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma (Table 3 and Fig. 1B).

Treatment

Preoperative management consisted of gradual titration of doxazosin up to 16 mg/day, discontinuation of cilnidipine, and expansion of volume with normal intravenous saline. Because glycemic control remained suboptimal perioperatively, basal insulin glargine 4 units/day with corrective insulin was used transiently (Fig. 2A and 2B). The patient underwent laparoscopic left adrenalectomy. Histopathology showed an encapsulated solid brown tumor with hemorrhage measuring 8.0 × 5.9 × 6.0 cm. Tumor cells were arranged in nested and trabecular patterns within a rich vascular network. Immunohistochemistry was positive for chromogranin A and S100 and negative for AE1/3. There was no capsular invasion, vascular invasion, necrosis, or atypical mitotic activity, and the Ki-67 labeling index was less than 1%. These findings were consistent with pheochromocytoma with low-risk pathological features.

Outcome and follow-up

After adrenalectomy, the excess catecholamine resolved, and antihypertensive therapy and all glucose-lowering medications were discontinued. On postoperative day 7, fasting plasma glucose was 81 mg/dL (SI: 4.5 mmol/L), fasting insulin was 3.8 μU/mL (SI: 26.4 pmol/L), fasting C-peptide was 1.34 ng/mL (SI: 0.44 nmol/L), C-peptide index was 1.65, and HOMA-beta was 76.0. A 75-g oral glucose tolerance test on postoperative day 7 showed 2-hour plasma glucose of 143 mg/dL (SI: 7.9 mmol/L). Plasma catecholamines and urinary metanephrines decreased markedly (Table 3). Without glucose-lowering medication, fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c remained stable: 87 mg/dL (SI: 4.8 mmol/L) and 6.7% (SI: 50 mmol/mol) at postoperative month 1, 107 mg/dL (SI: 5.9 mmol/L) and 6.0% (SI: 42 mmol/mol) at month 3, and 99 mg/dL (SI: 5.5 mmol/L) and 6.1% (SI: 43 mmol/mol) at month 6 (Table 1 and Fig. 2C). No recurrence-related symptoms or adverse events were observed during 6 months of follow-up.

Discussion

This case has two main clinical messages. First, pheochromocytoma should be considered when previously stable type 2 diabetes worsens unexpectedly over a relatively short period. Second, adrenalectomy can allow medication-free glycemic improvement even in a patient with long-term diabetes and apparently impaired endogenous insulin secretion.

Previous studies have shown that diabetes or glucose intolerance is relatively common in pheochromocytoma and that glycemic control often improves after tumor resection [1, 2, 5-9]. In a surgical series of 153 patients with pheochromocytoma, 23.4% had diabetes or glucose intolerance before surgery; among evaluable patients, 78.6% had complete resolution of diabetes, and 93.0% had either resolution or improvement after resection [9]. Several case reports have also described remission of severe or insulin-dependent diabetes after adrenalectomy [10-12].

Mechanistic studies have also shown that both impaired insulin secretion and insulin resistance contribute to pheochromocytoma-associated dysglycemia. Abe et al evaluated 12 patients with pheochromocytoma and demonstrated significant postoperative improvements in fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c, HOMA-beta, and HOMA-IR after tumor extirpation [13]. In that study, improvement in HOMA-beta was positively associated with reduction in urinary metanephrine, whereas improvement in HOMA-IR was positively associated with reduction in urinary normetanephrine. These findings suggest that epinephrine excess may contribute predominantly to impaired insulin secretion, while norepinephrine excess may contribute more prominently to insulin resistance.

Several case reports have described remission of severe or insulin-dependent diabetes after adrenalectomy for pheochromocytoma. Leng et al reported remission of longstanding insulin-treated diabetes after adrenalectomy in bilateral pheochromocytoma [10]. Sato et al described remission of insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus in a patient with multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A after adrenalectomy [11]. Cao et al also reported a case of pheochromocytoma presenting as atypical or labile diabetes, with subsequent remission after tumor resection [12]. These reports are clinically important because they show that even severe or insulin-treated diabetes may improve substantially when catecholamine excess is removed.

Compared with these previous reports, the distinctive feature of the present case is the clinical trajectory. Our patient had a 15-year history of type 2 diabetes and had maintained stable HbA1c of approximately 6.5% for about 12 years on saxagliptin monotherapy. He then developed a relatively rapid 1-year deterioration in glycemic control despite intensification of oral therapy. He also lacked classic paroxysmal symptoms such as palpitations, headache, pallor, or diaphoresis. After adrenalectomy, catecholamine excess resolved, all glucose-lowering medications were discontinued, and fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c remained stable without antidiabetic medication through postoperative month 6. Thus, the novelty of the present case is not merely that glycemic control improved after adrenalectomy, but that a change in glycemic trajectory in long-standing, previously stable type 2 diabetes served as the clue to a reversible endocrine cause.

The most important feature of this case is that the tumor was recognized in the setting of an altered diabetes trajectory. This patient had maintained HbA1c around 6.5% (SI: 48 mmol/mol) for approximately 12 years, after which glycemic control worsened progressively despite intensification of oral therapy. Together with a low body mass index, recent weight loss, tachycardia, and later identification of an adrenal mass, this pattern suggested that deterioration was not fully explained by the ordinary progression of type 2 diabetes alone.

Pheochromocytoma-associated dysglycemia reflects multiple mechanisms, including inhibition of insulin secretion, increased hepatic glucose production, lipolysis, and peripheral insulin resistance [1-4, 8, 9, 13]. Catecholamines, particularly epinephrine, can suppress insulin secretion through alpha-2 adrenergic receptors on pancreatic beta cells, while beta-adrenergic signaling can enhance hepatic glucose output and lipolysis [3, 4, 8, 13]. Norepinephrine-predominant tumors may contribute more prominently to insulin resistance, whereas epinephrine excess may strongly suppress insulin release.

In the present case, low fasting insulin, low fasting C-peptide, low C-peptide index, and low HOMA-beta before surgery suggest that impaired insulin secretion played a major role in dysglycemia. The increase in fasting C-peptide index after adrenalectomy is consistent with relief of catecholamine-mediated suppression of insulin secretion. However, early postoperative metabolic indices must be interpreted cautiously because surgical stress, transient insulin exposure, changes in nutritional intake, and acute changes in glucose concentration can influence insulin and C-peptide responses.

Longer-term follow-up is, therefore, especially important. The patient remained off all antidiabetic medications, and fasting plasma glucose and HbA1c remained stable through the postoperative month 6. This supports a clinically meaningful benefit beyond immediate postoperative fluctuation and strengthens the inference that pheochromocytoma contributed substantially to the previous worsening of diabetes control.

This report has limitations. It describes a single patient, repeated dynamic postoperative tests beyond day 7 after the operation were not available, genetic tests for pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma were not performed, and follow-up was limited to 6 months. However, the temporal sequence of stable long-term diabetes, rapid glycemic deterioration, biochemical confirmation of pheochromocytoma, tumor resection, and sustained postoperative stability without medications provides an educationally valuable clinical narrative.

In conclusion, pheochromocytoma can present as a rapid worsening of previously stable long-standing type 2 diabetes. Recognition of this altered glycemic trajectory can lead to the diagnosis of a reversible endocrine cause and to a clinically meaningful improvement after adrenalectomy.
