Integrated experimental, machine learning, and life-cycle assessment of fly ash–silica fume based self-compacting geopolymer concrete
Authors: Siva Shanmukha Anjaneya Babu Padavala, Siva Avudaiappan, Sri Ram Ravi Teja Prathipati, Yeswanth Paluri, Vanakuri Sainath, Adamu Mulatu Kumara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the performance of self-compacting geopolymer concrete (SCGC) incorporating fly ash (FA) and silica fume (SF) as aluminosilicate precursors, with emphasis on mechanical behaviour, durability, microstructural characteristics, environmental impact, and machine-learning-based performance prediction. Four geopolymer SCC mixes were prepared using SF to substitute FA at 0, 5, 10, and 15% by weight and an OPC-based SCC of the same class of strength was prepared to serve as a benchmark. Fresh properties were assessed as per EFNARC standards after which compressive, split tensile and flexure strength were done at intervals of 28, 90, and 180 days. Sorptivity, rapid chloride permeability (RCPT) and ultrasonic pulse velocity (UPV) were used to determine durability, whereas scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was used to measure the microstructural evolution. Findings reveal that SF is a considerable improvement to fresh and hardened geopolymer SCC, the best performance being found at a 10% replacement. The G10 mix recorded the best compressive strengths of 65.3 MPa at 180 days, which was 21% higher than the FA only geopolymer mix, and tensile and flexural strengths were 14–18% higher compared to FA only geopolymer mix. The performance of durability increased significantly, as sorptivity was reduced by about 21% and RCPT was lower than 1000 Coulombs (extremely low permeability) and the largest values of UPV were the highest, which shows the presence of a dense and uniform internal structure. Refined pore structure and well-developed the formation of aluminosilicate gels in the G10 mix had been confirmed by SEM observations. Parameters of mix, fresh properties, and curing age were used as inputs to develop machine learning models (KNN, SVM, Decision Tree, and Random Forest). Among them the predictive accuracy of the Random Forest model (R2 = 0.94) with the least error showed excellent performance forecasting and mix optimization. It was found by Life Cycle Assessment (LCA) that geopolymer SCC mixtures had less environmental impact through global warming potential (30–45% less than OPC-SCC) and energy (around 20–25% less than OPC-SCC) and the G10 mix had the lowest environmental impact. Overall, the study demonstrates that FA–SF geopolymer SCC with 10% SF replacement provides a good combination of workability, strength, durability, and sustainability that is proven in experimental, microstructural, data-driven modelling, and environmental analysis.

Introduction

The construction industry is the focal point of the world development as well as a leading contributor to. Climatic degradation by energy consumption, material mining, and greenhouse gas (GHG). emissions. The cement and concrete, the foundation of the modern infrastructure is still one of the most popular artificial substances. Nevertheless, the manufacturing of cement is associated with a heavy consumption of carbon both the burning of fuels to attain clinkerization temperatures as well as the emissions of the process of limestone calcination. Recent synthesis work and world evaluations all point towards cement and concrete as one of the sectors where the decarbonization should be prioritized since the scale of emissions is enormous is huge and the demand is likely to keep on changing to higher levels due to urbanization and infrastructure. Growth increase, especially in the developing economies1–7. The future cement- projection related emissions point to the fact that regional demand change can counter gains without action due to efficiency and gradual advancements, which emphasizes the necessity of low-carbon binder. technologies with the potential to scale at a high rate6,7.

Ordinary Portland cement (OPC) remains dominant in the construction industry due to its standard performance, strong sourcing chains and compatibility with various admixtures and aggregates. However, the environmental shortcomings of OPC, high embodied energy, and energy intensity, and reliance on non-renewable raw materials directly oppose climate mitigation goals and the objectives of the circular economy1,8–12. Simultaneously, OPC concrete is not durable in aggressive service conditions. Marine and de-icing exposure, sulfate exposure in soil and groundwater conditions, and thermal decay in high-temperature conditions can also shorten the service life and raise the maintenance expenses, and these effects are multiplied by repair and rehabilitation, which all can be reduced by chloride ingress and reinforcement corrosion13–17. All these environmental and durability limitations drive the creation of alternative binders that may lead to the minimization of emissions and the provision of durable and high-performance concretes that are applicable to the real-life infrastructures.

One of the most promising low-carbon alternative binder systems is geopolymer concrete (GPC) commonly classified as an alkali-activated material (AAMs)18–23. Geopolymers do not need the production of high-temperature clinkers as OPC does. Rather, aluminosilicate precursors, which are usually industrial by-products, including FA, slag, or calcined clays are reacted with alkaline activators to produce hardened binder gels. According to several reviews and comparative studies, geopolymer and alkali-activated concretes are capable of providing considerable CO2 savings compared to OPC concrete, but the extent of this depends on the availability of precursors, the chemistry of activators, the energy source used, and the transport distances22–27. In addition to sustainability, GPC has also been reported to have increased chemical resistance, enhanced thermal stability, and a lower permeability than traditional OPC concretes, which makes it desirable in the case of durable infrastructure purpose14–17,20,21.

Geopolymerization mechanisms that involve dissolution, reorganization, polycondensation, and hardening have control over the performance of geopolymer binders. In the alkali atmosphere, the precursor material reacts to give out the reactive Si and Al species, which dissolves on the precursor surface, reacts to form oligomers and subsequently polycondense to form a three dimensional aluminosilicate network, the structure of which together with degree of polymerization determine mechanical performance and longevity28–32. The resulting gels are usually characterized as N–A–S–H (sodium aluminosilicate hydrate) in low-calcium systems, C–(N)–A–S–H / C–A–S–H in calcium-containing systems, and the gel assemblage is highly affected by precursor chemistry and activator composition30–33. Dissolution kinetics, silicate species distribution, rheology, and final gel structure are influenced by activator concentration, silicate modulus (SiO2/Na2O), and Na2SiO3/NaOH ratio, and therefore, affect the early-age strength development and long-term strength development34–36. These mechanistic interdependences play a role in the familiar inconsistency of the geopolymer performance between studies and the significance of systematic mix design and sound experimental protocols.

FA has been widely utilized as an available precursor, particularly low-calcium Class F FA, because of its richness in aluminosilicate, the morphology of particles, namely, spherical, and its wide distribution in various regions37–40. With proper activation, FA-based geopolymers can attain long-term strength and high durability, and can help enable circularity by upcycling industrial residues, which would otherwise be deposited in landfills38–41. Although these benefits exist, FA-based systems are known to be slow-reacting at ambient-curing temperatures, leading to poor early-age strength and curing sensitivity. This difficulty is often cited as one of the major obstacles to field deployment, especially in the case of cast-in-place structural usage where the rapid attainment of strength is sought after41–44. According to prior research and reviews, such problems may be reduced through higher curing temperature, optimized use of the activator dosage, blended precursors, and reactive micro-fillers, but most of them add some practical constraints of curing infrastructure, cost, and quality control43–46.

SF is a highly reactive micro-silica by-product characterized by ultrafine particle size, high amorphous SiO₂ content, and large specific surface area. These characteristics make SF an effective modifier for binder systems by improving packing density, supplying additional reactive silica, and refining pore structure47–51. In OPC concretes, SF has long been recognized for its benefits to strength and durability through micro-filling and pozzolanic reaction, which reduces calcium hydroxide and densifies the interfacial transition zone (ITZ)52–54. In alkali-activated and geopolymer systems, SF can enhance geopolymerization by increasing the availability of soluble silica, adjusting the Si/Al ratio, and promoting gel formation and network connectivity, effects that can translate to higher compressive, tensile, and flexural strengths as well as improved durability indices49–51,55,56. However, SF can also increase yield stress and plastic viscosity due to its ultrafine surface area, potentially reducing workability if superplasticizer dosage and activator composition are not carefully optimized57–59.

Self-compacting concrete (SCC) technology offers significant practical benefits, including improved constructability, reduced labor and vibration requirements, and better filling and passing ability in congested reinforcement. Extending SCC principles to geopolymer matrices, self-compacting geopolymer concrete (SCGC) is attractive but technically complex because geopolymer pastes exhibit distinct rheological behavior compared with OPC systems60–62. SCC performance depends on a delicate balance between flowability and stability, often assessed through standardized tests such as slump flow, T₅₀₀, V-funnel, and L-box passing ratio60,61. In SCGC, activator viscosity, ionic strength, and silicate speciation can alter dispersion and thixotropy, while the inclusion of SF can further increase viscosity and modify segregation resistance57,59,63. Prior experimental work has demonstrated that SF replacement levels around 5–15% can improve hardened properties but may reduce slump flow unless counteracted by optimized superplasticizer dosage and aggregate skeleton design58,61,64. Recent reviews emphasize that robust SCGC design requires simultaneous control of binder fineness, activator-to-binder ratio, activator chemistry, and chemical admixture compatibility, but systematic studies on FA–SF SCGC remain limited compared with OPC-SCC literature62,65.

Mechanical performance of FA–SF geopolymer systems is typically evaluated using compressive strength as the primary indicator, yet structural applications demand a broader characterization including splitting tensile and flexural strength as proxies for cracking resistance and post-peak behaviour. While many studies report improvements in compressive strength with SF addition, fewer provide a consistent long-term dataset across 28–180 days, and comprehensive reporting of tensile and flexural strength evolution remains comparatively scarce55,66–68. Long-term strength development is particularly relevant for FA-based geopolymers because continued reaction and microstructural refinement can extend well beyond 28 days under ambient curing66,67. Recent investigations focusing on extended curing periods report continued increases in strength and durability indices as gel formation progresses and pore structure refines; however, variability remains high due to differences in FA source, activator dosage, curing regime, and SF characteristics66–69. This motivates controlled experimental programs that isolate SF replacement effects under SCC constraints while tracking performance through 90 and 180 days.

Durability performance in geopolymer concrete is governed by pore connectivity, gel continuity, microcracking, and transport mechanisms for water and ions. Sorptivity reflects capillary suction and pore refinement, RCPT provides an indication of ionic penetrability, and UPV is a non-destructive indicator of internal quality and homogeneity70–72. Numerous studies have linked improved durability in alkali-activated systems to microstructural densification and reduced connectivity of capillary pores, which in turn depend on reaction extent and gel formation69–72. SF is frequently associated with reduced sorptivity and chloride penetrability through packing enhancement and additional gel formation, although over-dosage can impair workability and potentially create dispersion-related heterogeneity49,56,59. Establishing robust microstructure–property relationships is therefore critical, and SEM combined with complementary tools (XRD/FTIR/EDS) is widely used to confirm gel morphology, unreacted particle content, and pore refinement trends that correspond to measured strength and durability indices30–33,73–80.

Beyond experimental characterization, predictive modeling is increasingly demanded by high-impact journals to support performance-based design and to reduce trial-and-error in mixture optimization. Traditional empirical regression models are often insufficient for geopolymer systems because the relationships among precursor chemistry, activator parameters, rheology, curing, and performance are nonlinear and interactive. Machine learning (ML) approaches such as KNN, SVM, decision trees, and random forests have demonstrated strong capability for mapping multivariate inputs to outputs in cementitious materials, often outperforming classical regression in strength prediction and sensitivity exploration. However, current ML literature in geopolymer concrete still exhibits limitations, including dependence on small datasets, inconsistent feature definitions, limited inclusion of durability outputs, and insufficient attention to SCGC-specific inputs and rheological parameters. This points to a need for ML models trained on experimentally consistent FA–SF SCGC datasets that include both mechanical and durability targets and that can be linked to microstructural interpretation.

Finally, sustainability claims for geopolymer concrete require careful quantification. Although geopolymers can reduce emissions by avoiding clinker production and utilizing industrial by-products, environmental benefits depend strongly on activator production burdens particularly for NaOH and Na₂SiO₃, as well as transportation and electricity mix. Cradle-to-gate LCA is therefore essential for validating whether a proposed geopolymer SCC mixture provides net environmental advantages compared with a functionally equivalent OPC-SCC benchmark. Prior LCA studies show that optimized precursor selection and reduced activator demand can substantially lower global warming potential, but trade-offs may appear in categories such as energy use and acidification depending on supply chain assumptions. Importantly, integrated studies combining fresh SCC performance, long-term mechanical and durability performance, microstructural evidence, ML prediction, and LCA-based sustainability evaluation are still limited for FA–SF geopolymer SCC, despite increasing calls for such unified frameworks in recent reviews.

Despite significant research on fly ash–based geopolymer concrete and isolated studies investigating silica fume modification, durability enhancement, machine learning prediction, or environmental assessment, an integrated framework combining all these aspects within a single systematic investigation of self-compacting geopolymer concrete (SCGC) remains limited. Most existing studies focus on either fresh and mechanical performance at 28 days, or durability characterization without extended long-term monitoring, while predictive modeling and environmental quantification are typically treated independently. The present study addresses this research gap by providing a comprehensive evaluation of FA–SF based SCGC incorporating: (i) fresh-state rheological performance under SCC constraints, (ii) long-term mechanical strength development up to 180 days under ambient curing, (iii) durability assessment through sorptivity, RCPT, and UPV, (iv) microstructural validation using SEM analysis, (v) machine learning-based multivariate prediction of strength and durability parameters, and (vi) cradle-to-gate LCA benchmarking against OPC-SCC. By integrating experimental investigation, microstructural interpretation, data-driven modeling, and environmental sustainability assessment within a unified framework, this study differentiates itself from existing FA–SF geopolymer SCC literature and establishes a performance, durability, sustainability optimization pathway for practical structural applications.

Materials and methods

Fly Ash

The geopolymer binder system used Class F FA as the main aluminosilicate precursor obtained from Thermal Power Plant Station, Vijayawada, Andhra Pradesh. The FA had high levels of silica (SiO2) and alumina (Al2O3), and the level of calcium oxide (CaO) was relatively low, which is in line with the ASTM C618 requirements of Class F. This chemical composition is beneficial to geopolymerization because it increases the development of N–A–S–H gel structures. The FA had a very smooth and spherical morphology that enhances workability and minimizes water requirements. The median size of the particles was 15–25 µm and the specific gravity was 2.1. The Blaine fineness of material was 315 m2/kg. Silica Fume

The SF was undensified and used as an additional reactive silica source and micro-filler. The SF utilized in the present research was procured from Dundi Vinayaka Traders, Vijayawada, India which contained more than 90% of SiO2 and the ultrafine particle size was between 0.1 and 0.2 µm, which resulted in a much higher specific surface area than FA. Having a specific gravity of 2.2 and a surface area of 30,000 m2/kg, SF helps to enhance the particle packing, decrease the interconnectivity of pores, and increase the formation of geopolymer gels due to its highly amorphous silica structure. It was anticipated that the incorporation of SF would polish the microstructure of the geopolymer matrix and aid in enhanced mechanical and durability performance. Alkaline activators

A binary mixture of sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and sodium silicate (Na2SiO3) was used to alkaline activate the FA-SF binder. A 10 M NaOH solution was prepared by dissolving sodium hydroxide pellets in distilled water, and this was done at least 24 h before mixing to allow the solution to be fully dissolved and the temperature to stabilize. The sodium silicate solution had a ratio of SiO2/Na2O of about 2.5 and a density of about 1.55 g/cm3 which is also a major contributor to the viscosity of the activator. The mass ratio of Na2SiO3 to NaOH was kept at 2.5 in all mixes and the activator to binder (A/B) ratio was kept at 0.45.

The selection of 10 M NaOH concentration and a Na₂SiO₃/NaOH mass ratio of 2.5 was based on prior studies reporting optimal mechanical performance and reaction kinetics for ambient-cured fly ash-based geopolymer systems. Previous investigations have shown that NaOH molarity in the range of 8–12 M provides sufficient alkalinity to promote dissolution of Si and Al species without excessively increasing solution viscosity or inducing rapid setting. A 10 M concentration was therefore adopted as a balanced and widely reported value for Class F fly ash geopolymers under ambient curing conditions. Similarly, a Na₂SiO₃/NaOH ratio between 2.0 and 2.5 has been shown to enhance silicate availability, promote N–A–S–H gel formation, and improve strength development while maintaining workable rheology. Ratios significantly higher than this range may increase mixture viscosity and hinder self-compacting behaviour. By fixing these activator parameters based on established literature, the present study isolates the influence of silica fume replacement on fresh, mechanical, durability, and environmental performance without introducing additional variability from activator chemistry28,31,36,37,42.

Aggregates

The fine aggregate was natural river sand that met the requirements of Zone II as per the IS 383. The sand was with a specific gravity of 2.65 and a fineness modulus of between 2.8, which was appropriate in generating self-compacting mixtures. The crushed coarse aggregate of nominal size 10–12.5 mm was used, which has a specific gravity of about 2.70 and water absorption of 0.5%. Aggregate proportions were optimized to meet EFNARC (2005) requirements, to provide balanced filling capability, passing capacity and segregation resistance of SCC. The overall coarse aggregate content was also restricted to obtain a mortar rich mix that could be self-compacted.

Chemical admixture

The flow characteristics of the geopolymer mixes were improved with the help of a polycarboxylate ether (PCE)-based high-range water-reducing admixture. The solid content of the superplasticizer was 40%, the density was 1.10 g/cm3 and the dosage was 1–1.5% of the binder mass. The fine-tuning of the exact dosage was done according to the initial tests to keep the slump flow values between 650 and 750 mm, which is sufficient to fill and pass through the mixture typical of SCC mixtures. The addition of the PCE admixture served to overcome the viscosity caused by the alkaline solution and SF, allowing the enhancement of the workability without affecting the mix stability.

Mix proportions

Five concrete mixtures were prepared in this study, four geopolymer SCC mixes incorporating SF at varying replacement levels (0–15%) and one OPC-based self-compacting concrete used as a control. The geopolymer mixtures were designed with a constant total binder content of 450 kg/m3, fixed activator-to-binder ratio of 0.45, and a Na₂SiO₃/NaOH mass ratio of 2.5. Aggregate proportions, activator content, and superplasticizer dosage were kept constant across all geopolymer mixtures to isolate the influence of SF. The OPC control mix was proportioned to achieve an equivalent strength class and SCC consistency for benchmarking mechanical, durability, and LCA comparisons. The total binder content of 450 kg/m3 was selected based on commonly reported ranges for both conventional SCC and geopolymer SCC systems. In conventional SCC, binder contents typically vary between 400 and 500 kg/m3 to ensure adequate paste volume for self-compactability, stability, and resistance to segregation. Similarly, previous studies on fly ash-based self-compacting geopolymer concrete have reported binder contents within the range of 420–480 kg/m3 to compensate for the absence of vibration and to maintain sufficient flowability under ambient curing conditions41,44,60. A binder content of 450 kg/m3 was therefore adopted as a balanced and representative value that ensures adequate paste volume for self-compaction while avoiding excessive heat generation or shrinkage risks. Additionally, maintaining a constant binder content across geopolymer and OPC-SCC mixes allows meaningful comparison of mechanical, durability, and environmental performance.

Preparation and curing of specimens

All concrete mixtures were prepared in a pan-type laboratory mixer to ensure uniform blending and controlled mixing conditions. The dry constituents FA, SF, fine aggregate, and coarse aggregate, were first introduced into the mixer and blended for approximately 2 min to achieve a uniform distribution of the binder and aggregates. This initial dry mixing step was particularly important for FA–SF blends to ensure proper dispersion of the ultrafine SF particles and avoid agglomeration.

After dry mixing, the pre-prepared alkaline activator solution, which was a mixture of sodium hydroxide and sodium silicate, was added slowly within 1 min to avoid localized concentration variations and to allow the powders to be wetened gradually. The activator solution was added together with the superplasticizer to regulate the mix viscosity and allow self-compacting behaviour. The mixture was stirred further 3–4 min until a cohesive and homogenous geopolymer paste was formed. The fresh mix was evaluated in terms of workability as per EFNARC protocols and any modification of the dosage of superplasticizer was done within the range that was set during the initial tests.

The fresh concrete was then poured into moulds without any external compaction, and the concrete was left to self-compact to reach complete consolidation. The moulds were covered with a thin sheet of polyethylene to reduce the loss of moisture in the first set. Demoulding of the specimens was done after 24 h and the specimens were transferred to an environmentally controlled laboratory environment at a temperature of 27 ± 2 °C and relative humidity of 60–70%. the present study intentionally adopted ambient curing (27 ± 2 °C) to enhance practical applicability for cast-in-place construction. Heat curing, while effective for early strength development, is often impractical for large-scale structural applications and in-situ infrastructure projects. Under ambient conditions, the geopolymerization process proceeds more gradually, with dissolution and polycondensation reactions extending over longer curing durations. The extended monitoring up to 180 days in this study was designed to capture this progressive gel maturation and matrix densification. Furthermore, the incorporation of silica fume provides additional reactive amorphous silica and increases nucleation sites, thereby enhancing early-stage geopolymerization kinetics and partially mitigating the reduced reactivity typically associated with low-calcium fly ash systems under ambient curing. All geopolymer and OPC control specimens were cured in the same ambient conditions to make the results comparable. The mechanical, durability, and microstructural tests were conducted after 28, 90, and 180 days, which allowed assessing the early-age and long-term performance properties of the FA-SF geopolymer system.

Test methods

Fresh properties

The fresh-state performance of all geopolymer and OPC control mixtures was assessed immediately after mixing to ascertain compliance with the demands of self-compacting concrete (SCC). The tests were performed in line with the guidelines of EFNARC (2005) that provide standardized procedures to evaluate filling ability, passing ability, and resistance to segregation in SCC systems. Such assessments are necessary since the rheological properties of geopolymer binders vary considerably with those of Portland cement, especially when alkaline activators and ultrafine SF particles affect the viscosity, yield stress, and flow dynamics.

Slump flow and T500

The ability to quantify the free-flowing and deformability of the mixtures was done using the slump flow test. Based on this test, the slump flow diameter and the T500 flow time were obtained. The slump flow diameter is used to determine the ability of the mixture to spread under its own weight and T500 is used to give information on viscosity and flow kinetics. All mixes were kept at a specific range of 650–750 mm to guarantee similar self-compacting behaviour.

V-funnel

V-funnel test was conducted to determine the viscosity and flow time of the mixtures under constrained conditions. The discharge time measured is a sensitive measure of internal friction, paste cohesion and possible blocking behaviour, which are of particular interest to geopolymer systems with high-solids activator solutions.

L-box

L-box test was used to determine the passing ability and the ability of the mixture to pass through congested reinforcement without segregation. The h2/h1 ratio was measured to determine the blocking resistance, and the values near 1.0 were the evidence of excellent passing performance.

Mechanical properties

Mechanical performance of the geopolymer and OPC control mixtures was evaluated using compressive, split tensile and flexural strength tests. These tests were performed at 28, 90 and 180 days to obtain early-age behaviour as well as long-term strength development which is especially important in geopolymer systems where polymerization rate and microstructural refinement proceeds over longer curing times. All mechanical tests were conducted according to the applicable Indian and international standards to achieve consistency and reliability of the results. All strength values reported represent the mean of three specimens. The standard deviation (SD) and coefficient of variation (COV) of compressive strength were calculated to assess experimental variability and reproducibility.

Compressive strength

The compressive strength was measured on 150 × 150 × 150 mm cube specimens as per IS 516:2018. Each mix was tested on three specimens at each curing age and the average value was reported. The loading was done at a constant rate of 0.25 MPa/s until failure.

Split tensile strength

Split tensile strength was determined on 150 × 300 mm cylindrical specimens according to IS 5816:1999. The specimens were placed in the horizontal position and compressive load was applied on the longitudinal axis of the specimen at a constant rate.

Flexural strength

Flexural strength was calculated on 100 × 100 × 500 mm prismatic beams under third-point loading as per ASTM C78/C78M. Each mix was tested on three specimens at each age and the average flexural strength was calculated.

Durability properties

Durability testing is an important part of determining the long-term performance and service life of geopolymer concretes, especially since their pore structure, ion transport properties, and microstructural development vary greatly compared to traditional OPC systems. The durability performance of all mixtures in this study was investigated using sorptivity, RCPT and UPV tests. These tests were performed at 28, 90 and 180 days to record the progressive densification and gel formation related to geopolymerization and the effect of SF on the refinement of pore networks.

Sorptivity

Sorptivity was determined based on ASTM C1585, which determines the rate of water capillary suction into partially saturated concrete. The specimens (150 × 150 × 150 mm) were dried in the oven to a constant mass at 50 ± 2 0C, left to cool to ambient temperature, and partially immersed in water. Mass gain was measured at predetermined intervals and sorptivity coefficients were obtained.

Rapid chloride permeability test

The RCPT was used to assess chloride ion penetration resistance as per ASTM C1202. Before testing, disc specimens (100 mm diameter × 50 mm thick) were vacuum-saturated. The samples were put in an electrochemical cell where 3% NaCl solution was on one side and 0.3 N NaOH on the other and a constant voltage of 60 V was applied over 6 h. The amount of charge transferred, in Coulombs, was taken as a measure of the movement of chloride across the matrix.

Ultrasonic pulse velocity

The UPV test was used to determine the internal quality and homogeneity of the mixtures, and it was conducted in compliance with IS 13,311 (Part 1). At each curing age, UPV measurements were made on 150 mm cube specimens. Ultrasonic pulses were sent and received through the specimen using a pair of transducers and the pulse travel time was measured.

The reference geopolymer mix (G0) had a slump flow of 655 mm and a V-funnel time of 11.2 s, which means that it is moderately deformable and viscous. The improvement observed at 10% SF reflects an optimal balance between enhanced particle packing and manageable rheological modification. At moderate replacement levels, the ultrafine SF particles fill interstitial voids between FA grains and aggregates, increasing packing density and reducing internal friction. This results in improved flowability and reduced flow time despite the high specific surface area of SF. However, as SF content increases beyond the optimal level (e.g., 15%), the very high surface area of silica fume significantly increases the paste’s water and activator demand. The additional surface adsorption of alkaline solution and superplasticizer molecules leads to increased plastic viscosity and yield stress. Consequently, the beneficial packing effect becomes counterbalanced by rheological stiffening, slightly reducing slump flow and increasing V-funnel time. This behaviour reflects the well-known trade-off in fine particle systems between packing optimization and surface-area-driven viscosity increase.

Nevertheless, when the SF concentration was 15% (G15), there was a minor decrease in performance. The slump flow decreased to 705 mm and the V-funnel time slightly increased, which means that the mix viscosity increased. This is due to the fact that the specific surface area of SF is significantly greater at high doses, and this raises the requirement of activator solution and superplasticizer. This causes low workability and low flow characteristics when not properly compensated. The OPC-SCC control mix was relatively less in overall performance compared to the G10 geopolymer mix, especially in passing ability and viscosity. This underscores the natural benefit of geopolymer binders, which, when optimized with SF, can attain better fresh-state behaviour that can be used in SCC applications.

Split tensile strength

Figure 6 shows the split tensile strength results of all FA-SF geopolymer SCC mixtures at 28, 90, and 180 days. The data indicate a steady increase in tensile performance with the addition of SF, in a similar trend to that of compressive strength. The reference mix G0 had tensile strength of 3.42 MPa at 28 days, and the tensile strength of the mixes with 5% and 10% SF were 3.68 MPa and 3.89 MPa, respectively. G10 mixture had the greatest tensile strength at all curing ages with the highest tensile strength of 4.52 Mpa at 180 days. This is equivalent to an improvement of about 14% compared to the control mix at 28 days, which means that SF has a positive effect on the tensile response of the geopolymer matrix. The improvement in split tensile strength can be attributed to enhanced crack resistance mechanisms resulting from silica fume incorporation. The ultrafine SF particles refine the interfacial transition zone (ITZ) between the geopolymer matrix and aggregates by reducing microvoids and improving bond strength. A denser ITZ delays crack initiation under tensile stress and improves stress transfer across the matrix–aggregate interface. Additionally, the increased formation of N–A–S–H gel and reduced pore connectivity contribute to improved matrix cohesion. The refined microstructure restricts microcrack propagation and enhances crack-bridging capacity at the microscale, thereby increasing tensile load-bearing ability. These mechanisms collectively explain the consistent improvement in tensile strength observed for the G10 mixture.Fig. 6Split tensile strength of all mixes.

Split tensile strength of all mixes.

Though the G15 mix also exhibited greater tensile strength than the control, a slight decrease relative to G10 indicates that too much SF raises mix viscosity and decreases effective workability, which may result in localized agglomeration and incomplete reaction. These can be detrimental to the formation of an optimally bonded ITZ and restrict tensile strength gains. Although the OPC-SCC mixture demonstrated satisfactory tensile performance, the values were lower than those of the geopolymer mixes with SF, especially at later ages. This once again points to the high long-term gel development and densification of microstructure with FA–SF geopolymer binders. In general, the findings support the fact that 10% SF offers the most desirable improvement of split tensile strength in FA-based geopolymer SCC.

Flexural strength

The flexural strength outcomes of all geopolymer and OPC-SCC mixtures at 28, 90 and 180 days are summarized in Fig. 7. There is a definite trend of increase with the SF content up to 10%, which is in line with the trends observed in compressive and tensile strength. The flexural strengths of G0 reference mix were 5.25 MPa at 28 days and 5.92 MPa at 180 days, and the addition of 5% SF raised the flexural strengths to 5.61 MPa and 6.38 MPa, respectively. The greatest improvement was at the 10% replacement level (G10), which had 5.94 MPa at 28 days and a maximum of 6.78 MPa at 180 days. This is the best flexural performance of all geopolymer mixes, which shows a great enhancement in the capability of the material to withstand bending stresses and crack propagation. The enhanced flexural performance is closely linked to improved resistance to crack initiation and propagation under bending stresses. Flexural loading induces tensile stresses at the extreme fibers of the beam, where microcracks typically originate. The incorporation of silica fume refines the pore structure and strengthens the ITZ, resulting in improved bond characteristics and stress redistribution capacity. The denser geopolymer gel matrix enhances internal cohesion and delays crack coalescence, while the refined microstructure improves energy dissipation during crack growth. This microstructural densification and improved matrix–aggregate bonding collectively increase flexural strength and fracture resistance. The optimal performance at 10% SF reflects a balanced enhancement of gel formation and ITZ refinement without excessive viscosity-related heterogeneity.Fig. 7Flexural strength of all mixes.

Flexural strength of all mixes.

The fact that flexural strength of the G15 mixture is slightly lower than that of G10 suggests that too much SF can raise the viscosity of the mix and decrease the overall homogeneity, which can lead to local particle agglomeration and incomplete FA dissolution. These effects can reduce microstructural uniformity, thus reducing flexural performance. Conversely, the OPC-SCC control mixture had lower flexural strength values at all ages with the highest being 5.75 Mpa at 180 days. This supports the fact that geopolymer binders, particularly when optimized with SF, have better microstructural development and fracture resistance with time. Taken together, the findings affirm that a 10% SF replacement produces the most desirable flexural strength improvement in FA-based geopolymer SCC.

Sorptivity

Figure 8 shows the sorptivity performance of the FA-SF geopolymer mixtures and the OPC control. It was found that there was a distinct decrease in initial and secondary sorptivity with the addition of SF up to 10%, indicating the strong effect of SF on the properties of moisture transport. The reference mix (G0) had an initial sorptivity of 0.162 mm/ sq min and incorporation of 5% SF decreased the sorptivity to 0.145 mm/ sq min. The greatest enhancement was in the G10 mixture, which recorded an initial sorptivity of 0.128 mm/ sq min, a decrease of about 21% relative to G0. The same trend was observed with secondary sorptivity, which validated the increased resistance to long-term ingress of capillary water. The increase in the performance of sorptivity can be mainly explained by the microstructural refinement caused by SF. The high amorphous silica content and ultrafine particle size of SF enhance the development of a more continuous and denser aluminosilicate gel network, which leads to less pore connectivity and smaller capillary channels. This densification reduces the rate of water absorption into the matrix, thus enhancing durability. Also, SF micro-filling effect leads to a smaller matrix, which further decreases the permeability routes.Fig. 8Sorptivity of all mixes.

Sorptivity of all mixes.

Sorptivity values were lower than the control but slightly increased with 15% SF replacement (G15) as compared to G10 mixture. This implies that too much SF may cause an increase in mix viscosity and poor dispersion, which decreases the efficiency of pore refinement. However, all the geopolymer mixtures that included SF showed better sorptivity performance than the OPC-SCC mix that recorded initial and secondary sorptivity values of 0.158 mm/ sqmin and 0.090 mm/ sqmin, respectively. In general, the findings highlight the efficiency of 10% SF in significantly enhancing the resistance to capillary absorption in FA-based geopolymer SCC, which leads to the increased long-term durability.

Rapid chloride permeability test

The penetrability of the FA-SF geopolymer SCC mixtures to chloride ions was tested with the RCPT and the findings at 28, 90 and 180 days are summarized in Fig. 9. The addition of SF resulted in a significant decrease in charge passed, which is a sign of increased resistance to chloride ingress. The reference mix (G0) was 1820 Coulombs at 28 days, which declined gradually to 1210 Coulombs at 180 days. The permeability was further decreased by the addition of 5% SF, and the values were 1605 Coulombs at 28 days and 1085 Coulombs at 180 days. The greatest increase was recorded in the G10 mixture, which recorded the lowest RCPT values at all ages, with the highest value of 1420 Coulombs at 28 days and the lowest value of 980 Coulombs at 180 days. Based on the ASTM C1202 classifications, this value would put the G10 mixture in the very low category of permeability. This remarkable decrease in the chloride ion transport is explained by the enhanced microstructural densification due to the optimum SF content. The existence of ultrafine silica particles improves the geopolymerization reaction, gel continuity, and pore connectivity, which limits ionic movement through the matrix.Fig. 9Rapid Chloride Permeability of all mixes.

Rapid Chloride Permeability of all mixes.

Even though the G15 mix also showed significant improvement compared to the reference mixture, the RCPT values were a bit higher than G10. This implies that too much SF can cause higher viscosity of the mixture and agglomeration of fine particles, which can inhibit the formation of uniform gels and cause localized pores. However, even the G15 mixture showed a much lower chloride permeability than the OPC-SCC control, which recorded high RCPT values, 2320 Coulombs at 28 days and 1920 Coulombs at 180 days, which falls within the moderate permeability range. These findings are a clear indication of the high chloride resistance of FA-SF geopolymer SCC in comparison to the conventional OPC-based SCC. We acknowledge that in alkali-activated and geopolymer systems, RCPT results may be partially influenced by the electrical conductivity of the pore solution rather than solely by chloride ion permeability. The presence of residual alkali ions (Na⁺, OH⁻) in the pore solution can increase ionic conductivity, potentially leading to higher measured charge passed values compared to OPC systems with similar pore structure. Therefore, RCPT in geopolymer concretes should be interpreted cautiously and preferably in conjunction with complementary durability indicators. The results also support the fact that 10% SF offers the best combination of pore refinement, gel development, and microstructural integrity, which leads to the least chloride ion penetrability and the greatest long-term durability.

Ultrasonic pulse velocity

Figure 10 shows the UPV results of all mixtures at 28, 90, and 180 days. UPV is a dependable non-destructive measure of the internal density, homogeneity, and general quality of concrete. The findings indicate a definite tendency of rising pulse velocity as SF is added, which is indicative of gradual enhancement of the microstructural integrity of the geopolymer matrix. The reference mix (G0) gave the values of UPV 4.28 km/s at 28 days and 4.52 km/s at 180 days, which showed that the densification continued with time as the geopolymerization continued. The values of the UPV increased significantly with the addition of SF, especially at the 10% replacement level. The G10 mixture had the highest pulse velocities at all the curing ages with the highest pulse velocity of 4.74 km/s at 180 days. This significant improvement shows that a thick and uniform aluminosilicate gel structure with a reduced number of internal voids or microcracks is formed. This densification is enhanced by the synergistic effects of SF including enhanced particle packing, micro-filling, and faster gel formation.Fig. 10Ultrasonic pulse velocity of all mixes.

Ultrasonic pulse velocity of all mixes.

The improvement in UPV is in line with other durability and mechanical performance indicators. Higher UPV mixes also showed lower chloride permeability (RCPT), lower sorptivity and greater compressive and tensile strength, showing uniform improvements in all performance measures. These correlations affirm that SF improves the structural compactness of the geopolymer binder, which leads to the enhancement of the quality of the material. The G15 mixture also showed higher values of UPV than the control, but the difference between the two is not as significant as that between G10 and G15, which indicates that too much SF can cause a slight rise in internal heterogeneity because of increased viscosity and possible agglomeration of particles. However, the geopolymer mixtures performed better than the OPC-SCC control, which had much lower values of UPV at all ages, indicating the better microstructural development and long-term densification of geopolymer systems. In general, the UPV findings support the conclusion that 10% SF offers the most desirable enhancement in the quality of internal materials, which is in line with the trends in the strength and durability tests.

The reduced presence of unreacted fly ash particles and visible voids in the G10 mixture corroborates the experimentally measured reduction in permeability and improvement in strength. Although quantitative image analysis or higher-magnification pore characterization could provide additional resolution of pore-size distribution and gel morphology, the current SEM observations adequately support the mechanistic interpretation within the scope of this study.

Curing age emerged as the most influential predictor across mechanical and durability outputs, which is consistent with the progressive geopolymerization and gel maturation observed experimentally up to 180 days. The continuous increase in compressive, tensile, flexural strength, and UPV values with age reflects ongoing densification and structural refinement of the aluminosilicate network, validating the model’s emphasis on curing duration. SF content was identified as the second most dominant feature, directly corroborating the experimental findings where 10% SF replacement yielded optimal improvements in strength, permeability reduction, and microstructural densification. The high feature importance of SF content aligns with SEM observations indicating refined pore structure and enhanced ITZ bonding, as well as with the reduced sorptivity and RCPT values. The prominence of rheological parameters such as slump flow and V-funnel time further indicates that fresh-state characteristics influence hardened-state performance indirectly through their effect on packing density and homogeneity. In general, the ML analysis proves that the most reliable and comprehensive predictive model of the dataset used in this study is the Random Forest. Its high predictive power in compressive strength, durability indices and UPV indicates its applicability in future mix optimization and in the creation of performance-based design strategies in geopolymer SCC. The combination of machine learning with the study enhances the knowledge of the interaction of complex variables and offers an effective means of predicting the performance trends with a much lower workload in the experiment. We acknowledge that the dataset used for model development was derived from experimentally generated mixtures within a controlled laboratory program and is therefore limited in size compared to large multi-source databases. Although fivefold cross-validation and train–test splitting were employed to minimize overfitting and enhance model robustness, smaller datasets may restrict the generalizability of the developed models beyond the investigated parameter space. The predictive models presented in this study should therefore be interpreted as data-driven tools optimized for the specific FA–SF SCGC system investigated, rather than universally applicable predictors for all geopolymer compositions. Future work incorporating larger, multi-source datasets and external validation across different precursor chemistries and curing regimes would further enhance model transferability and scalability.

Life cycle assessment results

A cradle-to-gate LCA was conducted to supplement the mechanical, durability, and microstructural analysis to measure the environmental effects of the FA-SF geopolymer SCC mixtures and compare them to a traditional OPC-based SCC. Since the previous sections have not discussed LCA methodology, this section starts with the description of the goal, scope, boundaries, and inventory considerations and then provides the calculated environmental impacts. The methodological framework is based on the ISO 14,040/44 standards, and the emission factors were derived using the internationally recognized databases, including Ecoinvent v3.8, IPCC Guidelines (2019), and the World Business Council for Sustainable Development (WBCSD) GNR dataset.

Goal and scope of the assessment

The main aim of the LCA was to assess the claim that FA-SF geopolymer SCC has quantifiable environmental advantages over traditional OPC-SCC. The functional unit was determined as 1 m3 of self-compacting concrete that was to be used to attain similar structural performance. This makes environmental comparisons significant and representative of the real material substitution potential. The analysis was limited to effects related to the production of raw materials, transportation, and the production of concrete.

System boundaries

The selected system boundary includes all the processes involved in the extraction of raw materials until the concrete mixing. The processes within the boundary were FA production, SF production, alkaline activators (NaOH and Na2 SiO3) production, aggregates production, and chemical admixtures production, transportation, and batching plant operations. The processing and transport emissions were attributed to FA and SF, which were treated as industrial by-products, which is in line with the Ecoinvent and WBCSD allocation rules. Concrete mixing plant energy consumption, such as electricity to batch and mix, was also included. The system boundary was limited to construction, use phase, maintenance, and end-of-life scenarios to ensure a cradle-to-gate focus, which could be directly compared with the existing concrete LCA studies reported in literature.

Life cycle inventory (LCI) assumptions

The analysis of inventory was based on the known emission factors. The emission factor of OPC was set at 0.85–0.95 kg CO2/kg, which is in line with the global averages of the WBCSD GNR and IEA data, with calcination alone contributing about 60% of the total emissions. FA, which is a by-product of power plants, was only credited with post-processing burdens of 0.03–0.05 kg CO2/kg, according to Ecoinvent datasets. SF, which is generated in the process of ferrosilicon alloy production, was attributed 0.30–0.45 kg CO2/kg, which is the densification, grinding, and transport emissions. The footprint of sodium hydroxide is relatively high (1.215 kg CO2/kg), which is in line with the models of electrolysis-based production found in industrial LCA literature. The amount of sodium silicate was allocated 0.851.00 kg CO2/kg based on the silicate production route of Ecoinvent. The aggregate production emissions were 0.005–0.10 kg CO2/kg and for transportation modeling, a uniform average transport distance of 75 km was assumed for all raw materials from production site to batching plant, representing typical regional sourcing conditions. Road transportation by diesel-powered heavy-duty trucks was considered based on standard Ecoinvent transport datasets. This assumption reflects moderate procurement distances commonly encountered in regional construction supply chains. The electricity consumption associated with batching and activator preparation was calculated using the Indian regional grid emission factor of 0.82 kg CO₂/kWh, consistent with IPCC and national energy statistics. While transportation and electricity mix assumptions may vary across geographic regions, these values were consistently applied across all mixtures to ensure comparative integrity. It is noted that, within cradle-to-gate concrete LCA studies, binder production remains the dominant contributor to environmental impact, whereas transport and batching electricity typically represent a smaller proportion of total emissions. To make a fair comparison of all mixtures, these values were scaled to the functional unit of 1 m3 of concrete.

Impact assessment categories

Three environmental indicators were evaluated, Global Warming Potential (GWP, kg CO₂-eq) based on 100-year time horizon characterization factors from IPCC (2019), Embodied Energy (MJ) incorporating both direct and upstream energy use associated with material production and Acidification Potential (AP, kg SO₂-eq) using midpoint characterization factors from internationally recognized LCIA methods (e.g., CML, TRACI). These categories were selected because they represent the most critical environmental burdens associated with binder-intensive construction materials and are widely reported in comparative concrete LCA studies. GWP captures climate change implications, embodied energy reflects resource and energy demand intensity, and AP addresses emissions contributing to acid deposition. While additional impact categories such as water consumption, eutrophication potential, or human toxicity could provide broader environmental insight, these were beyond the scope of the present cradle-to-gate assessment. Moreover, for cementitious materials, environmental dominance is typically governed by clinker production and alkaline activator manufacturing, which are primarily reflected in GWP and energy-related indicators.

OPC-SCC exhibited the highest environmental burden due to the energy-intensive clinker manufacturing process. In contrast, the geopolymer mixes demonstrated substantially lower impacts across all categories. Discussion of environmental impact trends

The OPC-SCC mixture had the greatest emissions (368 kg CO2-eq/m3), which is consistent with the established carbon intensity of cement production documented by IPCC (2019) and the WBCSD GNR dataset. Geopolymer mixtures, in contrast, decreased CO2 emissions by 30–45%, in part because OPC was substituted with FA and SF, the upstream environmental impact of which is significantly less. G10 showed the lowest impact values of geopolymer mixes, which means that moderate substitution with SF offers the best balance between increased reactivity and reduced embodied emissions. Further addition of SF (G15) to more than 10% resulted in a minor rise in impact values, primarily because of the increased energy and emissions of SF processing, but still much lower than those of OPC-SCC.

The acidification potential trends were consistent with GWP and energy consumption, which once again confirmed that the lower the dependence on high-temperature cement production and the use of fossil fuels, the lower the SO2-eq emissions. In general, the LCA findings are highly in favor of the environmental excellence of FA-SF geopolymer SCC. This is due to the lower embodied carbon, lower energy intensity, and better mechanical and durability performance that makes geopolymer SCC especially the G10 formulation a viable and more sustainable alternative to OPC-based SCC in structural and infrastructure applications.

Practical implications

The findings of this study demonstrate that FA–SF based self-compacting geopolymer concrete, particularly with 10% silica fume replacement (G10), can be feasibly implemented in practical construction scenarios. The mixture achieved EFNARC-compliant flowability without external vibration, making it suitable for heavily reinforced structural elements and complex formworks.

The demonstrated long-term strength development under ambient curing conditions eliminates the need for heat curing, enhancing field applicability for cast-in-place construction. The reduced sorptivity and chloride permeability suggest strong suitability for aggressive environments such as marine structures, bridges, and industrial infrastructure.

From a sustainability perspective, the 30–45% reduction in global warming potential compared to OPC-SCC highlights its potential contribution to low-carbon construction initiatives. Additionally, the machine learning framework developed in this study offers a foundation for performance-based mix optimization, enabling faster material design and reduced experimental iterations in future projects. Overall, the proposed FA–SF geopolymer SCC provides a technically viable and environmentally advantageous alternative for structural applications requiring high workability, durability, and sustainability.
