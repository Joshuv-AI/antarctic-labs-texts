Unique features of peer relationships in specific developmental periods predict borderline personality disorder and major depressive disorder in late adolescence
Authors: Suniya Naeem, Kirsten Gilbert, Alecia C. Vogel, Charlie Huntington, Rebecca Tillman, Deanna M. Barch, Joan Luby, Diana J. Whalen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
It is well established that peer relationships and psychopathology reciprocally influence each other across development, but it is less clear how trajectories of specific peer functioning domains are associated with borderline personality disorder (BPD) and major depressive disorder (MDD). We hypothesized that trajectories reflecting greater relational aggression would show stronger associations with later BPD symptoms, consistent with developmental models linking interpersonal conflict strategies to borderline features, and that lower peer acceptance, particularly from middle childhood through adolescence, would show stronger associations with later MDD symptoms. Using data from a 17-year prospective longitudinal study that oversampled preschoolers (ages 3–5) with elevated depressive and disruptive symptoms from the St. Louis region (N = 189; 50.8% female; 54.0% White, 34.4% Black), we examined how parent-reported bullying victimization, peer acceptance, and relational aggression— assessed up to 8 times via the MacArthur Health and Behavior Questionnaire (HBQ-P) across early childhood (ages 3–8) and middle childhood through adolescence (ages 9+) — predicted self-reported BPD symptoms (Borderline Personality Features Scale for Children) and interviewer-assessed MDD symptoms (Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia) in late adolescence (ages 13–21). Multilevel models estimated individual intercepts and slopes for each peer domain within each developmental period, and these trajectory parameters were entered as predictors of adolescent outcomes in general linear models, covarying for age, sex, and income-to-needs ratio. False discovery rate correction was applied to account for multiple comparisons. Elevated relational aggression in early childhood (intercept at age 6) uniquely predicted greater adolescent BPD symptoms after FDR correction. Increases in peer acceptance from middle childhood through adolescence were associated with fewer adolescent MDD symptoms, though this finding did not survive FDR correction. Bullying victimization trajectories were not significantly associated with either outcome. These findings suggest that specific peer functioning domains during distinct developmental windows may differentially relate to BPD and MDD risk, highlighting the potential value of developmentally informed screening and monitoring of peer relationship difficulties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08372-8.

Introduction

Development of the capacity to establish and maintain meaningful interpersonal relationships is a core task of childhood and adolescence; failure to do so is consistently associated with increased risk for psychopathology [1, 2]. However, psychopathology and negative experiences with peers can transact to worsen both mental health and peer functioning over time [3]. For example, social problems amplify negative affect and beliefs, which then erode future peer success, creating self-reinforcing cycles. While such interactions may occur across a variety of psychiatric disorders, the specific profile of maladaptive peer interactions likely differs across psychiatric disorders even from a young age [3], such that the specific nature of dysfunctional peer relationships a child experiences may both reflect and contribute to the unique psychiatric symptoms and disorder(s) they are experiencing. For example, prior research indicates that peer relationships - encompassing peer acceptance or rejection, bullying, and relational aggression - could play a crucial role in the development and persistence of both borderline personality disorder (BPD) and major depressive disorder (MDD) in childhood and adolescence [4–6]. We therefore focus on BPD and MDD, two conditions in which childhood-adolescent peer functioning is closely tied to both risk and course, yet there is a notable gap in research examining the longitudinal developmental pathways linking specific aspects of peer relationships to both disorders. Understanding these trajectories is important, as both commonly emerge during adolescence, impair affected individuals, are costly to society, and are strongly influenced by peers and other social relationships [7, 8]. By utilizing a unique longitudinal dataset, the present study assessed whether specific aspects of peer relationships experienced during two developmental periods (early childhood vs. middle childhood adolescence) are associated with later BPD and MDD.

Associations of peer acceptance with BPD and MDD

Peer acceptance is defined as the degree to which a child is socially accepted or rejected by their peer group [9]. Peer acceptance and social integration are essential developmental tasks from childhood through adolescence, with large cohort and longitudinal studies confirming that low peer acceptance and related problems (e.g., social withdrawal, aggression) are consistently linked to increased risk for internalizing and externalizing disorders, including anxiety and depression [10–12]. Meta-analyses and genetically informed studies have shown that peer problems in youth predict anxiety and depression in emerging adulthood, independent of familial and genetic factors [12–14]. Not only is peer acceptance likely to differentially impact symptoms, but a developmental psychopathology perspective suggests that there may be period-specific effects. In early childhood, the influence of peer acceptance may be less pronounced, as peer groups are more fluid and adult guided but remain foundational for future social and emotional development [2, 15, 16]. By later childhood and into adolescence, social reorientation and pubertal onset heighten both neural and motivational sensitivity to peer evaluations and status, making changes in peer acceptance/rejection especially consequential for psychopathology symptoms [17, 18].

Consistent with this, across adolescence, higher perceived social acceptance predicts better concurrent and long-term psychosocial adjustment, whereas lower acceptance is linked to greater depressive symptoms and risk for MDD [19–21]. Peer acceptance is robustly associated with depressive symptoms across adolescence, and longitudinal studies suggest that lower acceptance often precedes increases in depressive symptoms over time (e.g., Beeson et al. [22], Zhao [23]. Although reciprocal peer–symptom processes are plausible and supported in some prior work (e.g., Yu et al. [24], the present study focuses solely on prospective associations between peer trajectories and later symptoms rather than formally testing bidirectional pathways.

Peer acceptance is also salient to BPD because early peer acceptance may decrease rejection sensitivity over time [25], a core feature of BPD [26]. The findings suggest that these patterns start early, in accordance with Linehan’s [27] biosocial theory of BPD: children and adolescents who experience less peer acceptance show more borderline personality features [4, 28], whereas adolescents with BPD appear more sensitive to experimental manipulations of peer acceptance [29, 30]. A recent review revealed pervasive peer-relationship difficulties, including problems maintaining peer acceptance within the broader peer context, as characteristic of youth with BPD features [6]. Given prior evidence, the current study considers peer acceptance a potential predictor of both BPD and MDD. We tentatively expected peer acceptance to show associations with MDD symptoms during middle childhood through adolescence, given that depressive risk is closely tied to perceived social status, belongingness, and evaluation sensitivity during this period. Separately, we expected relational aggression to show associations with BPD symptoms, given that BPD-related risk may be closely coupled with patterns of interpersonal instability and conflict-related behaviors (including relationally aggressive strategies).

Associations of bullying victimization with BPD and MDD

Bullying consists of repeated, harmful actions between peers [31] and has been associated with psychopathology from childhood into adulthood [32–35]. Longitudinal work implicates bullying victimization in the emergence of both BPD and MDD. In the Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) cohort of 11,844 children (mean baseline age ≈ 9.9 years), approximately 20% reported victimization, and greater exposure predicted increases in MDD symptoms across two years [11]. In addition, a 2025 longitudinal study of over 26,000 adolescents by Cheng et al. [36] demonstrated that bullying victimization consistently led to increased internalizing symptoms (including depression) for both genders and reduced friendship and social support for girls. A 2024 meta-analysis of adolescent studies (27 samples) revealed that peer victimization was associated with 2.8x greater odds of depression [37]. While peer victimization can be consequential in both early and middle childhood, it appears that effects often stabilize and compound during the later window and into adolescence when exposure becomes more chronic and sensitivity to peer evaluation increases.

Similarly, prospective cohorts have shown that childhood victimization forecasts early adolescent BPD symptoms and self-injury, particularly under adverse family circumstances [38, 39]. Additional research highlights the relationship between childhood dysregulated behaviors, including bullying victimization, and the presence of BPD symptoms by early adolescence [40–43]. A recent multilevel meta-analysis synthesizing 106 studies (N = 86,871) confirmed that problematic peer relationships—such as bullying and relational aggression —represent moderate and temporally stable risk factors for BPD features, comparable in magnitude to established predictors such as emotion dysregulation and early psychopathology, thereby reinforcing a transactional developmental model for BPD [44]. Given prior evidence, the current study considers peer victimization as a potential predictor of both BPD and MDD.

Associations of relational aggression with BPD and MDD

A third variable of interest when investigating peer problems and psychopathology is relational aggression. Unlike physical aggression, which relies on causing physical harm or the threat of it, relational aggression involves perpetrating behaviors that damage or threaten relationships, such as social exclusion, rumor spreading or withdrawal of friendship [45]. relational aggression is linked to broad adjustment difficulties, including peer rejection, poor-quality friendships, and increases in both internalizing and externalizing problems [46–48]. Longitudinal studies consistently affirm a strong positive correlation between relational aggression and internalizing problems across various developmental stages [49–51], which can be linked to the development of MDD later in life. For example, Crick and colleagues [52] demonstrated that perpetrating relational aggression foretells a gradual amplification in internalizing symptoms among school-age children, and other studies have reported that this pattern holds across demographic variables and accounts for rates of physical aggression [46, 53]. More recent longitudinal work further supports a transactional model: Ji et al. [54] identified bidirectional associations between aggression—including relational aggression —and depressive symptoms from late childhood through mid-adolescence, with academic difficulties mediating the pathway from depression to subsequent aggression.

A substantial body of literature also connects relational aggression to borderline features—childhood relational aggression predicts adolescent borderline symptoms over and above physical aggression—with evidence from both community and clinical samples [4, 28, 55–57]. Several possible mechanisms may explain why relational aggression is particularly linked to BPD risk. The core features of BPD— identity disturbance, affective instability, and unstable interpersonal relationships—closely mirror the consequences and dynamics of relational aggression. Children who engage in relational aggression — that is, those who use relational strategies to harm others — often exhibit preoccupation with relational concerns and form intense, enmeshed relationships [58]. They also display heightened sensitivity to relational events, including hypervigilance and distress, in response to interpersonal stressors [59, 60]. These relational vulnerabilities may foster the use of sensitive information acquired during close interactions as a tool in conflicts, further perpetuating aggressive behaviors [61].

The meaning and impact of relational aggression likely changes across development. In early childhood, relational aggression is less normative and may occur as isolated incidents that provide scaffolding for its later use as a persistent interpersonal style; higher levels at this stage are consistently associated with increased risk for BPD features. By later childhood and adolescence, relational aggression becomes more integrated into ongoing peer interactions, as sensitivity to peer evaluations and social status increase. Given prior evidence, the current study considers relational aggression a potential predictor of both BPD and MDD.

Associations between sex, peer dynamics and the risk of BPD and MDD

Sex can shape how peers experience BPD and MDD, but effects are mixed across constructs and samples. For peer victimization and MDD, multiple longitudinal studies and meta-analyses have demonstrated that the association and transactional coupling are generally stronger for girls than boys [62, 63]. Likewise, large cohorts show that victimization predicts internalization (including depression) in both sexes, with reduced friendship and social support, specifically for girls [36].

The patterns of peer victimization and BPD features vary. For example, Vaillancourt et al. [64] demonstrated that childhood relational aggression and depression predict BPD features in boys, whereas physical and relational aggression, depression, and ADHD predict BPD features in girls. For girls, physical aggression was the strongest predictor of BPD features, whereas for boys, all risk indicators had similar predictive impacts. Antila et al. [65] reported that female victims of bullying had an almost fourfold increased risk of developing a personality disorder (mostly BPD) in early adulthood, whereas no such association was found for males.

With respect to peer acceptance/rejection and relational aggression, sex often moderates associations for both BPD and MDD. Girls tend to be more affected by relational victimization and peer rejection, which are linked to higher internalizing symptoms and BPD features, yet boys also exhibit meaningful risk [36, 62–65]. More broadly, girls and boys can differ in the salience of peer relationship requirements, exposure to interpersonal stressors, and relationship processes such as co-rumination. A comprehensive review and peer-socialization model proposed by Rose and Rudolph [66] highlights how sex-linked peer relationship processes may confer both costs and benefits for emotional and behavioral adjustment. These findings highlight the importance of considering sex-specific pathways in understanding the risk for BPD and MDD, though formal tests of sex moderation require larger samples than were available in the current study.

Both MDD and BPD are characterized by interpersonal impairment, yet they have distinct core clinical features that may be shaped by partially distinct social-developmental processes. MDD symptoms are closely tied to perceived belongingness [67, 68], social evaluation [69], and withdrawal [70], suggesting that low peer acceptance and social disconnection may be especially relevant. BPD symptoms include interpersonal instability, heightened reactivity, and conflictual relationship patterns, suggesting that peer processes involving hostility, coercion, or relational aggression strategies may be particularly informative. Examining whether specific peer domains are associated with MDD and BPD independently advances developmental models beyond the general conclusion that peer problems are associated with psychopathology, by identifying which components of peer functioning are associated with each symptom profile. Such specificity may ultimately help refine prevention strategies by informing whether interventions should prioritize improving peer belongingness and support, reducing conflictual peer relationships, or promoting interpersonal skills.

The current study

Although prior research has established associations between peer relationships and BPD and MDD, no longitudinal study to date has simultaneously examined these disorders using repeated assessments of peer functioning across early development to inform the developmental specificity and course of this risk trajectory. The current study leverages data from a 17-year prospective longitudinal cohort that began in the preschool period (ages 3–5) and oversampled children with elevated depressive and disruptive symptoms. Peer functioning was assessed repeatedly across childhood and adolescence (up to 8 times per child), providing a rare opportunity to examine developmental trajectories of three core domains of peer relations—peer acceptance, bullying victimization, and relational aggression —and their unique contributions to later psychopathology. Specifically, the present analyses test whether distinct trajectories of these peer experiences during unique developmental periods predict BPD and MDD symptom severity in late adolescence. We focused on trajectories of peer functioning because repeated assessments across childhood allow us to separate stable individual differences from developmental change. Multilevel models provide an intercept, reflecting the estimated level of peer functioning at the reference age/developmental window, and a slope, reflecting the rate of change across assessments. These components may have different implications for risk (e.g., chronic low peer acceptance versus declining acceptance), and trajectory approaches can reduce measurement bias by leveraging multiple observations per participant. We hypothesized that trajectories reflecting greater engagement in relational aggression would show associations with later BPD symptoms, consistent with developmental models linking interpersonal conflict strategies and relational instability to borderline features. We also hypothesized that lower peer acceptance, particularly from middle childhood through adolescence when peer evaluation becomes more salient, would show associations with later MDD symptoms. Finally, given prior evidence linking victimization to both depressive and borderline outcomes, we examined bullying victimization trajectories as an additional predictor of both outcomes.

Peer functioning is multi-dimensional and includes additional domains not captured here (e.g., friendship quality, dyadic reciprocity, teacher- or peer-reported sociometrics, and online/technology-mediated peer stress). The domains we assess are also interrelated: for example, low peer acceptance can increase vulnerability to victimization, and relationally aggressive behavior can decrease acceptance and increase conflictual peer contexts. Importantly, the parent study’s measurement strategy also creates an asymmetry in roles assessed: we examine bullying victimization (but not perpetration) and relational aggression perpetration (but not relational victimization). We therefore interpret each domain as one observable piece of a broader peer-ecology rather than an exhaustive characterization of peer functioning.

Methods

Participants

The participants were 306 (148 females) children enrolled in the longitudinal Preschool Depression Study at the Washington University School of Medicine in the St. Louis Early Emotional Development Program, which prospectively investigated associations between childhood factors and later adolescent outcomes ([71]; Fig. 1). Participants were initially recruited during the preschool period (ages 3.0–5.11) from daycare and primary care centers in the St. Louis region, and the final sample of 306 children was selected after oversampling for higher depressive and disruptive symptoms on the basis of scores from the Preschool Feelings Checklist but also included children with no psychopathology [72]. Children and their caregivers completed the baseline (T1) assessment and up to 9 follow-up assessments (T2–T10). Waves T1–T3 occurred at 1-year intervals; then, after an approximately 2.5-year period, waves T4–T8 also occurred approximately one year apart. Finally, T9 occurred approximately 3 years after T8, with T10 occurring approximately 2 years after T9. The participants were between the ages of 13 and 21 at waves T9–T10, with a mean time between assessments of 2.23 (SD = 0.43) years for those completing both waves. In addition to the 306 participants who began the study at T1, 42 subjects (20 F) were included in the study at T6 to increase the size of the healthy comparison group. This subset of 42 subjects did not differ from the original sample of 306 on sex (47.6% vs. 48.4% female, χ2 = 0.01, p = 0.928), race (47.6% vs. 53.6% white, χ2 = 1.59, p = 0.452), or income-to-needs ratio (1.98 ± 0.76 vs. 2.03 ± 1.19, t=-0.40, p = 0.688). However, they were significantly younger at T6 (10.26 ± 1.40 vs. 11.16 ± 0.88, t=-4.04, p < 0.001) and continued to be younger at all waves through the end of the study.

Preschool-to-school-age peer relationships

The MacArthur Health and Behavior Questionnaire–Parent (HBQ-P) [73] is a parent-report questionnaire that includes scales of children’s social functioning. It is a validated, psychometrically robust tool for assessing internalizing, externalizing, and attention problems in children, demonstrating strong reliability, cross-informant agreement, and discriminant validity, and it performs well against structured diagnostic interviews—especially for detecting underrecognized internalizing disorders [74, 75]. The HBQ-P for middle childhood (version 1.0) was administered when the subjects were aged 3–8 years, and the HBQ-P for late childhood and adolescence (version 2.1) was administered when the participants were aged 9 years and older through the T8 assessment. Parents completed the HBQ-P up to 8 times from early childhood into adolescence. As detailed in Fig. 1, n = 308 participants completed HBQ-P version 1.0 at at least one wave (mean [SD] = 3.00 [1.04] waves) during middle childhood, and n = 306 participants completed HBQ-P version 2.1 at at least one wave (mean [SD] = 2.81 [1.14] waves) during late childhood/adolescence.

Table S1 details the items from the two HBQ-P versions that were included in the Bullied by Peers (3 items in 1.0, 5 items in 2.1), Peer Acceptance (8 items), and relational aggression (6 items in 1.0, 7 items in 2.1) subscales. The values for the Bullied by Peers and Peer Acceptance items were 1 = not at all, 2 = very little, 3 = somewhat, and 4 = very much, whereas the values for the relational aggression items were 0 = never or not true, 1 = sometimes or somewhat true, and 2 = often or very true. Subscale scores are the means of the items, and higher scores indicate more bullying by peers, greater peer acceptance, and greater relational aggression. The Cronbach’s alphas of the HBQ-P for middle childhood and the HBQ-P for late childhood and adolescence were 0.73–0.81 and 0.82–0.91, respectively, for bullied by peers; 0.81–0.89 and 0.85–0.91, respectively, for peer acceptance; and 0.81–0.87 and 0.73–0.83, respectively, for relational aggression.

Adolescent outcomes

BPD symptoms

Adolescents (aged 13–21 years) self-reported BPD symptoms via the Borderline Personality Features Scale for Children (BPFS-C) [4], which has demonstrated high criterion validity [76, 77]. The BPFS-C has 24 items rated on a scale ranging from 1 = not at all true to 5 = always true. The BPFS-C total score is the sum of the items, with values ranging from 24 to 120 and higher scores indicating greater BPD severity. For participants completing the BPFS-C at both adolescent assessments, the maximum BPFS-C total score was used for analysis to capture peak symptom severity across the two waves, consistent with methods used in a previous publication from this cohort [78]. Cronbach’s alphas for BPFS-C total score were 0.90 at T9 and 0.91 at T10.

MDD symptoms

Adolescents (aged 13–21) and caregivers were interviewed separately via the Kiddie Schedule for Affective Disorders and Schizophrenia (KSADS; [79], a semi-structured diagnostic interview with well-established reliability and validity for mood disorders in youth. For subjects aged 18 or older at T10, only the subject was interviewed. An MDD core score was defined as the number of 9 core MDD symptoms endorsed at a threshold level by either the parent or the child, so that higher scores indicate greater MDD severity. This dimensional symptom count approach has been validated in prior Preschool Depression Study publications: depression severity scores differentiated depressed preschoolers from those with non-affective disorders and healthy controls [80], demonstrated temporal stability across assessment waves [71], and predicted full DSM MDD at school age and early adolescence [81]. When participants completed the KSADS at both adolescent assessments, the maximum of their two scores was used for analysis. Cronbach’s alphas for MDD core score were 0.89 at T9 and 0.87 at T10.

Covariates

Age at the last assessment and parent-reported sex at preschool age were included as covariates in all analyses of BPD/MDD outcomes.

Income-to-needs ratio

Caregivers reported their family income at the baseline assessment. The income-to-needs ratio was computed as the total family income divided by the federal poverty level, based on family size, at the time of data collection [82]. This variable was included as a covariate because socioeconomic disadvantage is an established risk factor for both MDD and BPD in youth [83], and prior work from this cohort has demonstrated that lower preschool-age income-to-needs ratio predicts greater depression severity and altered neurodevelopment at school age [84, 85].

Analytic plan

Multilevel models of the HBQ-P Bullied by Peers, Peer Acceptance, and relational aggression subscales across waves T1-T8 were conducted in SAS v9.4. Separate models were conducted for the early childhood (ages 3–8) and late childhood/adolescence (ages 9 and older) versions of the HBQ-P. These models used restricted maximum likelihood, included random intercept and slope components, did not include covariates, and assumed an unstructured covariance structure. The time variable was age at assessment, centered at the median age of 6 years for HBQ-P version 1.0 and 11 years for HBQ-P version 2.1. Results of the multilevel models are shown in Table S2. Individual subject intercepts and slopes were extracted and examined as potential predictors of BPD and MDD severity at waves T9/T10. Separate general linear models were used to assess the relationships between the 6 intercept and slope variables for each developmental period (independent variables) and BPD and MDD severity (dependent variables), covarying for age at the last assessment, baseline income-to-needs ratio, and sex.

To account for multiple comparisons, the false discovery rate (FDR) correction [86] was applied separately to each set of 6 models for each developmental period in the primary analyses of BPD and MDD severity outcomes. As a sensitivity analysis to partially address concerns about directionality, all MDD models were re-run with the addition of MDD diagnosis at any point during waves T1–T8 as a covariate, to assess whether prospective associations between peer trajectories and adolescent MDD symptoms were robust to adjustment for pre-existing depression.

Results

Of the 348 study participants, 343 completed the HBQ-P during at least one of the first eight assessment waves (T1-T8); 308 completed at least one HBQ-P during middle childhood (version 1.0) and 306 completed at least one HBQ-P during late childhood/adolescence (version 2.1). Of these 343 participants, a subsample of 189 had BPD/MDD data from one or both final adolescent waves (T9-T10) and were therefore included in the present study (Fig. 1).

The sample was 50.8% female, 54.0% white, 34.4% black, and 11.6% more than one race and had a baseline income-to-needs ratio of 2.04 (SD = 1.12). MDD symptoms

Both the peer acceptance intercept and slope in late childhood/adolescence were negatively associated with the MDD core score, indicating that greater peer acceptance at age 11 and a sharper increase in peer acceptance across late childhood/adolescence were associated with fewer MDD symptoms in adolescence, but these results did not survive FDR correction (Table 3). In sensitivity analyses additionally covarying for MDD diagnosis during waves T1–T8 (Table 4), the pattern of results was consistent with the primary analyses. The peer acceptance slope in late childhood/adolescence remained the strongest predictor of adolescent MDD core score (β = −0.157, p = 0.0252), though this association continued to not survive FDR correction (FDR p = 0.1512). No other peer functioning predictors reached significance in either developmental period. The inclusion of prior MDD diagnosis did not substantively alter the magnitude or significance of the peer functioning predictors.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to investigate how childhood peer interactions, such as acceptance, bullying/peer victimization, and relational aggression, predict the emergence of BPD or MDD in adolescence. Taken together, results partially supported our hypotheses. Consistent with predictions, higher early-childhood relational aggression was associated with greater adolescent BPD severity and increases in peer acceptance across late childhood/adolescence were associated with lower adolescent MDD severity. In contrast, bullying/victimization trajectories were not significantly associated with either outcome. These patterns suggest that relational aggression and peer acceptance may be more informative predictors than bullying/victimization; however, null findings should be interpreted in light of power constraints.

The absence of associations for bullying victimization is notable given prior literature linking peer adversity to internalizing outcomes and, in some studies, borderline features. Several factors may contribute to these null findings in the present study. First, our victimization measure reflects parent-reported “bullied by peers,” which may be less sensitive in adolescence when many peer experiences occur outside of caregivers’ knowledge. Second, restricted variability (e.g., low base rates for some behaviors) and heterogeneity in timing and chronicity of peer adversity may reduce power to detect effects in trajectory-based models. Finally, the developmental window captured by the HBQ-P may not align with the most etiologically sensitive period for victimization-related risk in this sample.

These findings are broadly consistent with developmental cascade models, which posit that early social challenges can initiate a sequence of problematic interactions, ultimately leading to adverse psychosocial outcomes [87]. However, the current analyses tested direct prospective associations between peer trajectory components and later symptoms rather than the sequential mediational pathways that cascade models imply. Notably, the intercept and slope did not independently predict the same outcome; instead, different peer domains and trajectory components emerged as significant predictors for BPD and MDD, respectively. While this pattern is consistent with the notion that failure to complete salient developmental tasks in peer relationships may contribute to risk for psychopathology [88], formal tests of cascading effects — in which early peer difficulties sequentially influence later developmental milestones, which in turn predict symptoms — were beyond the scope of the current study. The social information processing model further clarifies these pathways, suggesting that children who experience peer rejection or engage in relational aggression may develop biased interpretations of social cues, maladaptive attributions regarding peers’ behaviors, and patterns of reinforcement or punishment that perpetuate these behaviors [89]. Over time, these processes can entrench relational aggression and peer rejection, amplifying risk for both BPD and MDD patients.

The finding that increases in peer acceptance from middle childhood through adolescence were associated with fewer MDD symptoms is supported by prior research. Peer rejection entails negative judgements about the target of the rejection, and these statements may be internalized over time, gradually taking the form of depression [90, 91]. Relatedly, social withdrawal and isolation may be particularly consequential in middle childhood and adolescence, when withdrawal is often perceived as less normative and can limit opportunities for belonging and support [92]. Over time, reduced social connection may contribute to loneliness, which is a known correlate and risk factor for depressive symptoms [93]. By considering several aspects of peer functioning simultaneously, these analyses reinforce a previous finding that peer acceptance may be especially predictive of depressive symptoms relative to other peer-related constructs [19]. Notably, these peer acceptance-MDD associations did not survive FDR correction and should therefore be interpreted cautiously as suggestive rather than confirmatory.

Our finding that higher early-childhood relational aggression levels were associated with later BPD symptoms is consistent with prior work linking relational aggression to borderline features across development (e.g., [4, 57]). The present results extend this literature by leveraging repeated assessments across an earlier developmental window. However, because BPD features were not assessed in early childhood in this dataset, we cannot determine temporal ordering between emerging borderline features and relational aggression. Accordingly, we interpret these results as evidence of a prospective association rather than a directional pathway. Future studies with repeated assessments of both peer functioning and borderline features are needed to evaluate reciprocal or transactional processes.

Strengths and limitations

It is important to acknowledge several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, BPD symptoms were assessed via adolescent self-report. Self-report provides critical access to internal states central to borderline features, but it can also be influenced by response style and recall. Multi-informant and interview-based assessment can strengthen measurement by integrating perspectives and reducing method-specific bias (e.g., [94]). Relatedly, peer functioning was assessed via parent report on the HBQ-P, which may be less sensitive during adolescence when independence increases and caregivers may be unaware of many peer dynamics occurring at school or online. Future work would benefit from incorporating peer- or teacher-reports and/or youth-report measures of peer functioning, particularly in later developmental periods.

The absence of BPD feature measurement in preschool-aged and school-aged children introduces a limitation in inferring the directionality of our findings, as it is not possible to conduct bidirectional models. Without early childhood data on BPD features, we cannot definitively determine whether relational aggression precedes or follows the emergence of BPD features in adolescence. Although sensitivity analyses adjusting for prior MDD diagnosis were conducted for MDD models (see Table 4), the absence of BPD feature measurement during childhood precluded analogous analyses for BPD outcomes. Therefore, caution should be exercised when interpreting the implied directionality of our findings. Third, our study was based on secondary data analysis, as the original data collection was designed primarily to validate measures of preschool depression. As a result, there were limitations in the types and scopes of assessments that could be conducted. Fourth, the HBQ-P was used to measure peer relationships; as a parent-report questionnaire, it is potentially subject to reporter bias, although prior research by Luby and colleagues [75] has demonstrated encouraging levels of specificity and sensitivity of parental reports in predicting psychopathology in young children. Fifth, the mean levels of peer acceptance in the current sample were high relative to the scale maximum of 4 (middle childhood intercept M = 3.51, SD = 0.36; late childhood/adolescence intercept M = 3.49, SD = 0.44), and mean levels of relational aggression were low relative to the scale maximum of 2 (middle childhood intercept M = 0.35, SD = 0.27; late childhood/adolescence intercept M = 0.14, SD = 0.09; see Table 1). These distributional patterns are consistent with those observed in community (non-psychiatric) samples using the HBQ-P - in the foundational validation study, Essex et al. [73] reported similarly favorable parent-reported social functioning among community-recruited children, though descriptive statistics were provided at the composite level (Social Competence Composite) rather than for individual subscales. High parent-reported peer acceptance and low relational aggression are well-documented features of parent-report measures in this age range, likely reflecting parents’ tendency to rate peer acceptance favorably and the lower frequency and observability of relational aggression in early childhood [74]. Despite potential ceiling and floor effects, the subscales demonstrated adequate variability to detect significant associations with adolescent outcomes, as evidenced by the standard deviations and ranges reported in Table 1. Additionally, the sample size (N = 189) limited statistical power, particularly for detecting moderation effects; accordingly, exploratory moderation analyses included in the original submission were removed from the current version, and future studies with larger samples are needed to examine potential sex-specific pathways. Furthermore, although we reference developmental cascade models as a theoretical framework, the current analyses tested only direct prospective associations between peer trajectories and adolescent outcomes. Formal tests of sequential mediational pathways — in which early peer difficulties cascade through later developmental milestones to predict psychopathology — would require more complex modeling approaches (and a substantially larger sample than was available in the current study). Future research with adequately powered samples should directly test these cascading mechanisms.

A strength of the study is its longitudinal and prospective design, tracking trajectories of peer functioning across 17 years. This extends prior research by simultaneously examining trajectories of multiple peer functioning domains across two developmental periods, using repeated assessments beginning in the preschool years, to predict both BPD and MDD symptoms in adolescence. Unlike previous studies limited to school-age or adolescent assessments, our research underscores the importance of examining risk patterns in early childhood, with significant associations observed for relational aggression levels at age 6, highlighting the potential for early screening and intervention during this period. Furthermore, we employed a well-validated questionnaire for measuring BPD, the BPFS-C, which has been shown to be uniquely associated with theoretically identified indicators of borderline personality in childhood and demonstrates discriminant validity with respect to depressive symptoms in prior validation work. This is significant given the high degree of comorbidity between borderline pathology and depression observed in previous studies [95].

Clinical implications

Peer relationships have the potential to be both beneficial and harmful [96]. Our findings highlight the importance of early identification and routine screening for problematic peer dynamics, including low peer acceptance and relational aggression, as opportunities for targeted, disorder-specific interventions that enhance positive social experiences. relational aggression around age 6 was associated with greater adolescent BPD severity, suggesting that early interpersonal dysfunction may be a useful clinical signal for later risk, and trajectories of peer acceptance were associated with MDD symptoms. Clinicians should incorporate regular screening protocols with direct questions about peer relationships and social exclusion, as these social indicators often precede the onset of psychopathology. Future work should evaluate whether prevention and early intervention approaches that target interpersonal functioning can be feasibly adapted for younger children, and whether such approaches alter later risk. Although the present study cannot test mechanisms, these findings are broadly consistent with intervention models for BPD that target social-cognitive and interpersonal processes (e.g., Mentalization-Based Treatment, which focuses on strengthening mentalizing in interpersonal contexts), and they raise the possibility that developmentally adapted, caregiver-supported mentalizing and emotion-coaching strategies could be explored earlier in childhood to shift trajectories.
