Unraveling the link: Beta-thalassemia intermedia and Moyamoya syndrome—a hidden connection
Authors: Teck Cheng Yap, Muhammad Ihfaz Ismail
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Beta-thalassemia intermedia (BTI) is a chronic hematologic disorder that may predispose to systemic complications, including rare cerebrovascular events. Moyamoya angiopathy (MMA) is a progressive steno-occlusive cerebrovascular disease, often presenting with recurrent strokes and transient ischemic attacks (TIAs). The association between BTI and MMA remains exceedingly rare. Case Presentation: We report a 25-year-old woman with BTI who developed recurrent ischemic strokes and TIAs. Imaging studies revealed bilateral chronic infarcts and stenosis of distal internal carotid and middle cerebral arteries, consistent with MMA. Laboratory investigations showed chronic anemia with hemoglobin of 8.1 g/dl. She underwent a successful right superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass without worsening of her neurological deficits. Conclusion: This case highlights the potential link between chronic anemia and progressive cerebrovascular pathology in BTI patients. Early diagnosis and surgical intervention can improve neurological outcomes in such complex cases.Keywords: Beta-thalassemia intermedia, Moyamoya angiopathy, ischemic stroke, cerebrovascular bypass, transient ischemic attacks

Introduction

Beta-thalassemia intermedia (BTI) is a chronic hemoglobinopathy caused by partial deficiency in beta-globin chain synthesis. It results in varying degrees of anemia, ineffective erythropoiesis, iron overload, and systemic complications, although it is generally milder compared to beta-thalassemia major. Clinical manifestations can include growth retardation, bone deformities, extramedullary hematopoiesis, and thromboembolic events. Cerebrovascular complications, although rare, are increasingly recognized due to improved survival rates in BTI patients [1, 2].

Moyamoya angiopathy (MMA) is a progressive cerebrovascular condition characterized by stenosis or occlusion of the distal internal carotid arteries and their branches, with compensatory formation of fragile collateral vessels [3]. Clinically, MMA manifests as recurrent ischemic strokes, transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), and hemorrhagic strokes. It may present as a primary idiopathic condition, termed Moyamoya disease, or as a secondary phenomenon associated with various systemic conditions such as neurofibromatosis, sickle cell anemia, Down syndrome, and hemoglobinopathies, termed Moyamoya syndrome [4].

Several risk factors for MMA have been identified, including younger age of onset (peak incidence in childhood), female predominance (approximately 2:1), higher prevalence among East Asian populations, and associations with genetic mutations such as RNF213. Geographic distribution suggests a higher incidence in countries like Japan, Korea, and China. Environmental and inflammatory triggers have also been implicated in the disease pathogenesis.

The association between BTI and MMA is exceedingly rare but poses significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges [5–8]. Chronic anemia in BTI may exacerbate cerebral hypoxia, increase oxidative stress, and contribute to the progression of cerebrovascular pathology in patients with MMA. This case report describes the unique clinical course of a young woman with BTI and MMA, emphasizing the importance of recognizing this association and implementing timely interventions to optimize patient outcomes.

Case presentation

A 25-year-old Malay woman with a known diagnosis of beta-thalassemia intermedia presented with a history of recurrent ischemic strokes and transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) since 2018. She was diagnosed with beta-thalassemia intermedia during childhood, confirmed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), which showed elevated levels of hemoglobin F (HbF) and hemoglobin A2 (HbA2). Her glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) status was normal.

She had no past surgical history, and notably, no splenectomy had been performed.

Her family history revealed that her parents and siblings were asymptomatic thalassemia carriers, with no history of cerebrovascular diseases. No parental consanguinity was reported.

She had not been on regular blood transfusions throughout her life, except for transfusion during pregnancy to optimize hemoglobin levels. She was on long-term folic acid supplementation but had not been prescribed antiplatelet therapy prior to this admission.

Her neurological history included two prior ischemic strokes in 2018. The first stroke presented with left-sided upper and lower limb weakness, which gradually improved over 3–4 months. Later the same year, she developed a second stroke characterized by right-sided weakness and dysarthria. Recovery was partial, and she was left with persistent left-sided spastic hemiparesis. Since 2018, she continued to experience intermittent TIAs, typically triggered by febrile illnesses, manifesting as left-sided weakness that resolved spontaneously after fever subsided.

On examination during the current admission, she was alert, oriented, and hemodynamically stable. Neurological evaluation revealed dysarthria and left-sided spastic hemiparesis with motor strength graded 4/5 on the Medical Research Council (MRC) scale.

Laboratory investigations demonstrated chronic anemia, with a hemoglobin level of 8.1 g/dL. Other parameters, including white blood cell count, platelet count, and coagulation profile, were predominantly within normal or slightly elevated ranges (summarized in Table 1).

Serial computed tomography (CT) scans demonstrated multiple well-defined hypodensities at the bilateral centrum semiovale, corona radiata, and left frontal region, indicative of chronic infarcts. Despite the underlying pathology, the patient remained clinically stable with no further neurological deterioration since her last ischemic event. She subsequently underwent a right-sided superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery (STA–MCA) direct bypass, with an uneventful postoperative course and no new neurological deficits.

Discussion

The coexistence of beta-thalassemia intermedia (BTI) and Moyamoya angiopathy (MMA) is an exceedingly rare but clinically important phenomenon. This case underscores the potential interplay between chronic hematologic disorders and progressive cerebrovascular diseases, highlighting the need for heightened clinical vigilance when BTI patients present with neurological symptoms.

In BTI, chronic anemia, ineffective erythropoiesis, and iron overload contribute to systemic oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, and a prothrombotic state [9]. Another potential explanation involves synergistic genetic triggers. Like sickle cell anemia, nitric oxide deficiency might contribute to oxidative stress, as well as a predisposition to pro-inflammatory and prothrombotic conditions [10]. These factors may exacerbate the vascular pathology of MMA, which is characterized by progressive stenosis of intracranial arteries and the formation of fragile collateral networks. The recurrent ischemic strokes and transient ischemic attacks (TIAs) in this patient may reflect the compounded effects of reduced cerebral perfusion and chronic hypoxia from anemia.

Our patient’s history of recurrent strokes and TIAs, often triggered by febrile illnesses, illustrates the vulnerability of compromised cerebral circulation to additional metabolic stressors. Fever increases metabolic demand and exacerbates hypoxic injury in an already impaired vascular bed, emphasizing the importance of infection prevention and early intervention.

The successful management of this patient involved a multidisciplinary approach, including optimal anemia management and surgical revascularization via superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) bypass. The primary focus of management should be preventing anemia to avoid chronic hypoxia. Regular blood transfusions and hydroxyurea therapy in transfusion-dependent patients can help prevent MMD by mitigating hypoxia. For medical management, antiplatelet therapy, preferably with aspirin, is recommended [11]. Direct revascularization procedures, such as STA-MCA bypass, can improve cerebral perfusion and reduce the risk of future ischemic events, particularly in patients with established Moyamoya vasculopathy [12]. The absence of postoperative neurological worsening in this patient supports the role of timely surgical intervention in stabilizing symptoms and preventing further complications.

Further research is needed to elucidate the pathophysiological mechanisms linking chronic anemia to Moyamoya angiopathy, which may provide insights into targeted therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

This case highlights the rare coexistence of beta-thalassemia intermedia and Moyamoya angiopathy, offering valuable insights into the complex interplay between hematologic and cerebrovascular pathologies. Chronic anemia and associated endothelial dysfunction may contribute to cerebrovascular remodeling and progressive ischemia. Early diagnosis, comprehensive systemic evaluation, and timely multidisciplinary intervention, including surgical revascularization, can significantly improve neurological outcomes. Greater awareness among clinicians and further research into the underlying mechanisms are essential to enhance diagnostic accuracy and therapeutic strategies for these rare and challenging presentations.
