Von Hippel–Lindau (VHL) disease is an autosomal dominant inherited syndrome characterized by the development of benign and malignant tumors, including hemangioblastomas, renal cell carcinoma, pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors, and pheochromocytomas.1 Among its cardiovascular manifestations, stress-induced cardiomyopathy secondary to catecholamine excess is rarely reported but may represent the initial presentation that unmasks an underlying pheochromocytoma.2 Here, we describe a case in which a complex multisystemic VHL phenotype was revealed by Takotsubo syndrome (TTS), which led to the diagnosis of pheochromocytoma and subsequent cascade genetic testing, with significant clinical implications for family members.

Case Presentation

A 57-year-old woman was admitted following acute onset of severe headache, neck pain, profuse sweating, and palpitations occurred after a meal. Emergency medical services found tachycardia at 150 beats/min, blood pressure asymmetry, and severe hyperglycemia at 4.96 g/L. Initial presentation suggested acute coronary syndrome with ST-segment changes and elevated cardiac biomarkers. Serial electrocardiograms demonstrated the typical evolution seen in stress-induced cardiomyopathy, with initial ST-segment elevation followed by progressive deep T-wave inversion in precordial leads, and gradual resolution during follow-up (Supplemental Figure 1). Echocardiography revealed characteristic apical ballooning with hypokinesis of the entire apical collar compensated by hyperkinesis of basal segments, consistent with TTS (Video 1). However, coronary angiography unexpectedly revealed significant stenosis of the left anterior descending artery, which was treated with angioplasty and placement of a drug-eluting stent (Video 2, Video 3, Video 4). An abdominal computed tomography scan disclosed a 6 × 6 × 6 cm left adrenal mass highly suggestive of pheochromocytoma (Figures 1A to 1D). 18F-dihydroxyphenylalanine positron emission tomography–computed tomography (18F-DOPA PET-CT) confirmed intense uptake in the left adrenal mass, consistent with a catecholamine-secreting tumor, with no evidence of metastatic disease (Figures 1E and 1F).

Plasma-free catecholamines were markedly elevated, with norepinephrine at 225,146 pmol/L (reference: <2,364), dopamine at 1,655 pmol/L (ref: <324), and adrenaline at 2,655 pmol/L (ref: <480). Normetanephrine and metanephrine levels were also significantly increased at 112,064 pmol/L (ref: 120-1,180) and 24,524 pmol/L (ref: <510), respectively, with free metanephrine at 10,368 pmol/L (ref: 80-510). Urinary analysis confirmed catecholamine excess: normetanephrine was 5,202 nmol/24 hours (ref: <1,924), metanephrine 244.9 nmol/24 hours (ref: <169.2), and vanillylmandelic acid 75.6 μmol/24 hours (ref: 1.8-36.7). Chromogranin A was also markedly elevated at 4,270 ng/mL (ref: <100), supporting the diagnosis of a catecholamine-secreting pheochromocytoma. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging performed on day 28 showed complete recovery of left ventricular function, with a left ventricular ejection fraction of 66% compared with 53% at the acute phase. No regional wall motion abnormalities, myocardial edema on T2-STIR sequences, or late gadolinium enhancement were observed, ruling out myocardial infarction or myocarditis. These findings confirmed the reversible nature of the myocardial dysfunction, consistent with stress-induced cardiomyopathy (Video 5).

Preoperative medical preparation included sequential introduction of prazosin 5 mg extended-release (alpha-blocker), followed by bisoprolol 2.5 mg (beta-blocker), in accordance with recommended protocols for catecholamine-secreting tumors. Histological examination revealed a 7 × 5 × 5 cm, well-demarcated tumor with characteristic pheochromocytoma features: large granular cells arranged in Zellballen pattern, minimal mitotic activity (1/10 high-power fields [HPF]), no necrosis or angioinvasion. Immunohistochemistry confirmed chromogranin positivity with Ki-67 index of 1%. PASS (pheochromocytoma of the adrenal gland scaled score) was 1, indicating benign potential with complete resection. Postoperatively, comprehensive genetic testing identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in the VHL gene (c.340C>T), confirming VHL syndrome. This finding prompted cascade screening of the patient's family members. Her 2 sons underwent genetic testing: the elder son (age 26 years) tested positive for the same VHL mutation and subsequently underwent adrenalectomy for left adrenal pheochromocytoma, while the younger son (age 25 years) tested negative (Figure 2).

The patient's complex medical history further included invasive ductal carcinoma of the right breast diagnosed 6 months postadrenalectomy, which was treated with total mastectomy, axillary lymph node dissection, and adjuvant radiotherapy. She also developed a toxic thyroid adenoma requiring endocrinologic management and stage 3a chronic kidney disease. Notably, she experienced significant weight gain of 20 kg between 2021 and 2024, possibly related to metabolic changes after adrenalectomy.

Throughout follow-up, comprehensive VHL surveillance protocols were implemented, including regular biochemical monitoring for catecholamine excess and imaging surveillance for VHL-associated tumors. Annual follow-up visits confirmed no recurrence of catecholamine excess or development of other VHL-associated manifestations (Supplemental Figure 2).

Discussion

This case illustrates the diagnostic complexity of VHL disease and emphasizes the critical importance of recognizing cardiovascular presentations as potential markers of underlying hereditary tumor syndromes. TTS, while classically attributed to emotional stress, can be precipitated by catecholamine-secreting tumors and may represent the initial manifestation of occult pheochromocytoma.3 Pheochromocytoma-induced TTS is increasingly recognized as a distinct and severe form of catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy. Compared with classic TTS, pheochromocytoma-induced TTS more often presents with hemodynamic instability, cardiogenic shock, and a broader spectrum of wall motion abnormalities, including atypical patterns such as mid-ventricular or basal ballooning. As highlighted by Aw et al,4 pheochromocytoma-induced TTS frequently leads to emergency presentations, with atypical forms affecting younger patients and showing more severe clinical courses. The coexistence of coronary artery disease further complicated the clinical picture, requiring careful interpretation of cardiac findings and highlighting the multifactorial nature of cardiovascular risk in these patients. Our patient fulfilled the InterTAK diagnostic criteria for TTS, including transient left ventricular dysfunction with apical ballooning, new electrocardiographic abnormalities, and complete functional recovery documented by follow-up cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (Supplemental Figure 3).5,6

Pheochromocytomas occur in approximately 10% to 20% of VHL patients and may present with paroxysmal hypertension, cardiac arrhythmias, or stress cardiomyopathy.7 The pathophysiology involves excessive catecholamine release leading to coronary vasospasm, direct myocardial toxicity, and potentially TTS.8 In our patient, the cardiovascular presentation provided the diagnostic key that led to identification of the underlying genetic syndrome.

The genetic confirmation of VHL disease had profound implications extending beyond the index patient. Cascade testing identified a positive carrier in her son, enabling pre-emptive surgical intervention before the development of symptoms or complications. This case demonstrates the lifesaving potential of genetic screening in hereditary tumor syndromes and underscores the importance of involving genetic counselors in the management of these complex cases.9

Although breast cancer and thyroid adenomas are not classic VHL manifestations, their occurrence in our patient raises questions about potential phenotypic expansion or coincidental association. The presence of multiple neoplasms in a single patient warrants comprehensive genetic evaluation and multidisciplinary coordination to ensure appropriate surveillance and management.10

The patient's development of chronic kidney disease after adrenalectomy, while potentially related to perioperative complications, also requires consideration in the context of VHL syndrome, as renal manifestations including cysts and clear cell carcinoma are well-recognized features of the disease.11

Conclusions

This case emphasizes the multisystemic nature of VHL syndrome and demonstrates how cardiovascular presentations can serve as the initial diagnostic clue for hereditary tumor syndromes. The development of TTS in association with pheochromocytoma represents a rare but important clinical presentation that requires high clinical suspicion and systematic evaluation. Early identification through genetic screening enabled pre-emptive management in a family member, highlighting the critical importance of cascade testing in hereditary syndromes. This case reinforces the need for multidisciplinary collaboration involving cardiology, endocrinology, genetics, and surgery clinicians in the comprehensive management of VHL disease. The successful identification and treatment of this complex case demonstrate the importance of maintaining high clinical suspicion for hereditary tumor syndromes in patients presenting with unusual cardiovascular events, particularly when associated with concurrent endocrine abnormalities. Prompt genetic evaluation and family screening can have lifesaving implications and should be considered an integral component of comprehensive patient care in these challenging cases.
