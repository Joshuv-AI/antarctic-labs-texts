Organophosphate Flame Retardants Disrupt Autism-Relevant Gene Networks Across Development: A Cross-Species Multi-Omics Study
Authors: Yi-Hui Zhou, George Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrganophosphate flame retardants (OPFRs) are widely used environmental contaminants with suspected developmental neurotoxicity, yet their stage-specific molecular impacts and potential relevance to autism spectrum disorder (ASD) remain poorly defined. We integrated transcriptomic and lipidomic analyses from two rat models to investigate OPFR-induced disruption across early neurodevelopment. In dataset GSE148266, fetal forebrain and placenta were analyzed following in utero OPFR exposure; in dataset GSE211430, neonatal cortical RNA-seq and lipidomics were profiled after postnatal exposure to triphenyl phosphate and isopropylated triaryl phosphate (1000 μg/day; n = 10/sex/group). Differential expression (DESeq2; FDR <0.05), pathway enrichment (GSEA), and multi-omics integration (DIABLO; ∣r∣ > 0.9) were performed. Fetal exposure altered 191 genes (144 mapped to human orthologues), including ASD-relevant genes such as ADNP, BRAF, and MAPK3, with enrichment in spliceosome (NES = 2.39), cell-cycle regulation (NES = 2.26), and suppressed Toll-like/NOD-like immune signaling (NES = −2.06). Postnatal exposure disrupted 34 genes and 12 lipids, notably PC(15:0_16:0) and TG(18:1_20:1_20:1), which correlated with synaptic and immune-related genes. Eighteen neonatal DEGs overlapped with human ASD cortical transcriptomes, and integrated analysis revealed shared neurotransmission networks across developmental stages.These findings demonstrate that OPFRs disrupt conserved neurodevelopmental gene networks in a stage-specific manner. The convergence of transcriptomic and lipidomic signals with ASD-relevant features supports further investigation of OPFRs as candidate environmental risk factors and highlights molecular pathways for future biomarker and toxicity studies.Keywords: Organophosphate flame retardants, Neurodevelopment, Transcriptomics, Lipidomics, Gene expression, Autism, Environmental toxicology

Background

Organophosphate flame retardants (OPFRs) have become pervasive environmental contaminants. According to biomonitoring data from the U.S. National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), OPFR metabolites are detectable in over 90 % of the general population (Ospina et al., 2018). Originally developed as alternatives to phased-out brominated flame retardants, OPFRs are now widely used in furniture, electronics, textiles, and building materials (Zhu et al., 2023). As a result, human exposure occurs through multiple pathways, including ingestion of contaminated food, inhalation of indoor air and dust, and dermal absorption (Hou et al., 2016; Zhang et al., 2016).

Emerging evidence indicates that OPFRs can cross biological barriers, including the placenta and blood–brain barrier, and are detectable in placental tissue, cord blood, and breast milk (Varshavsky et al., 2021; Yan et al., 2023). Recent studies have linked prenatal OPFR exposure to adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes. For example, a prospective birth cohort study (Cheng et al., 2024) reported that higher levels of organophosphate flame retardant (OPFR) metabolites in maternal urine were associated with poorer social development scores in infants, suggesting a potential link to autism spectrum disorder (ASD)-related traits children. Other epidemiological studies have found associations with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) symptoms (Wang et al., 2024) and changes in cortical brain structure (Newell et al., 2023). Despite this growing concern, the molecular mechanisms underlying OPFR-associated neurodevelopmental effects remain poorly understood.

Prior experimental studies suggest that OPFRs may disrupt thyroid hormone signaling, oxidative stress pathways, and neurotransmitter systems (Dishaw et al., 2011; Kim et al., 2015), all of which are critical for brain development. Additionally, lipids play a central role in neurodevelopment by supporting synaptic membrane formation, neuronal signaling, and myelination (Tracey et al., 2018; Barnes-Vélez et al.,2023) . However, how OPFRs affect gene–lipid networks relevant to ASD has not been systematically investigated.

To address this gap, we conducted a cross-species multi-omics study to characterize the transcriptomic and lipidomic effects of OPFR exposure across key neurodevelopmental stages. We analyzed fetal and neonatal rat brain tissues to identify stage-specific gene expression and lipid alterations induced by OPFRs. We also integrated lipid–gene networks using multi-omics analysis and evaluated their overlap with high-confidence autism risk genes curated by the SFARI Gene database (Abrahams et al., 2013). We hypothesized that OPFR exposure would disrupt neurodevelopmental gene–lipid networks, and that these disruptions would intersect with molecular features previously implicated in ASD.

Methods

Study design and data sources

This study involved the secondary analysis of three publicly available datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) to investigate the molecular effects of organophosphate flame retardant (OPFR) exposure and their potential relevance to autism spectrum disorder (ASD). Datasets were selected based on exposure timing (prenatal and postnatal), inclusion of transcriptomic and/or lipidomic profiles, and relevance to neurodevelopment.

GSE211430 (Witchey et al., 2023) includes transcriptomic and untargeted lipidomic profiles from neonatal rat cortices exposed to a daily oral dose of 1000 μg of triphenyl phosphate (TPHP) and isopropylated triaryl phosphate esters (ITPs). This dataset was used for both re-analysis of lipidomic features and differential gene expression profiling, followed by multi-omics integration.

GSE211430 (Witchey et al., 2023) includes transcriptomic and untargeted lipidomic profiles from neonatal rat cortices exposed to a daily oral dose of 1000 μg of triphenyl phosphate (TPHP) and isopropylated triaryl phosphate esters (ITPs). This dataset was used for both re-analysis of lipidomic features and differential gene expression profiling, followed by multi-omics integration.

GSE148266 (Rock et al., 2020) provides RNA-seq data from rat brain samples exposed to OPFRs, including control and exposed groups. It served as an independent dataset for transcriptomic validation and cross-species comparative analysis.

Transcriptomic and pathway enrichment analyses

Transcriptomic data from rat neonatal cortex (GSE211430) and fetal forebrain/placenta (GSE148266) were analyzed using the DESeq2 R package (Love et al., 2014) (v1.40.1 for GSE211430; v1.42.0 for GSE148266). Raw gene count matrices underwent variance-stabilizing transformation, with genes deemed differentially expressed if they met false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and absolute log2 fold change >1 (a tiered biological filtering strategy). For GSE148266, litter was included as a blocking factor. Rat genes were mapped to human orthologs via NCBI HomoloGene (build 68) and validated using Ensembl BioMart (Kinsella et al., 2011).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using clusterProfiler (v4.6.0) on both datasets. Gene ranks were derived from signed log10-adjusted p-values multiplied by fold-change direction, tested against KEGG and Gene Ontology (GO) databases. Pathways with FDR-adjusted p < 0.05 were considered significant.

Lipidomic analysis

Untargeted lipidomic profiles from GSE211430 were re-analyzed using data provided in the original dataset. Spectra were acquired via ion mobility-mass spectrometry (IMMS) using an Agilent 6560 QTOF system (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA), operated in both positive and negative ionization modes. Lipid identification was performed using the LIPID MAPS database (v2.3), with internal standards from Avanti Polar Lipids.

Quality control steps included internal standard recovery >80 % and coefficient of variation (CV) < 15 % in pooled quality control samples. Lipid species present in more than 95 % of samples were retained for analysis. Differential abundance analysis was performed using the limma (Ritchie et al., 2015) R package (v3.54.0), applying empirical Bayes moderation and an FDR threshold of 0.05.

Cross-species analysis using SFARI autism-risk genes

To assess the translational relevance of rat OPFR exposure signatures, we conducted a cross-species comparison with human autism risk genes curated in the SFARI Gene database (categories 1–3, Q1 2025 release; 1136 genes). All rat genes were mapped to one-to-one human orthologues using HomoloGene (build 68) prior to SFARI overlap or enrichment analyses.

GSE211430–neonatal cortex multi-omics integration

Transcriptomic and lipidomic profiles from postnatal day 1 rat cortex were jointly analyzed using the DIABLO (Rohart et al., 2017) framework (mixOmics (Rohart et al., 2017) v6.24.0, R 4.3.2). Input features included genes with ∣log2FC∣ ≥ 1 and Benjamin-adjusted P < 0.05, and lipids with FDR <0.05. Model parameters (5 components; design matrix weight = 0.1) were optimized by leave-one-out cross-validation. Gene–lipid associations were extracted from the first two latent components, and pairs with ∣Pearson r∣ > 0.90 were retained. Rat genes in these high-confidence pairs were mapped to human orthologues and intersected with the SFARI gene list. The top 20 ASD-associated genes were prioritized by lipid connectivity, and networks were visualized in Cytoscape (Shannon et al., 2003) (v3.9.1).

GSE148266–fetal forebrain and placenta transcriptomics

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with ∣ log2FC∣ ≥ 1 and Benjamin-adjusted P < 0.05. DEGs were mapped to human orthologues and tested for enrichment within the SFARI gene set using a hypergeometric test (phyper, R base), with 15,342 one-to-one orthologues used as the background universe. Functional clustering of overlapping genes was performed in DAVID (Huang et al., 2009) (v6.8), using a Benjamin-adjusted P < 0.05 significance threshold.

Ethical considerations

All datasets analyzed in this study were de-identified and publicly available through GEO. The original studies were conducted in compliance with institutional guidelines for animal care or human subjects research, as reported in their respective dataset descriptions. This secondary analysis was exempt from additional ethical review.

Results

Cross-species gene expression analysis in OPFR-exposed rats (GSE148266)

Differential expression analysis using DESeq2 was conducted on 18,264 rat genes. Ortholog mapping via NCBI HomoloGene identified 12,431 rat genes with human orthologs (68.1 %). Among the 191 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) identified at FDR <0.05, 144 were mapped to human orthologs. For example, Adnp (log2FC = 0.19; adjusted p = 0.017) corresponds to human ADNP, a gene implicated in neurodevelopmental pathways.

Pathway-level transcriptional changes in GSE148266

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA (Subramanian et al., 2005)) of DEGs revealed enrichment in multiple KEGG pathways (Zhou, 2016). Upregulated pathways included spliceosome (hsa03040; NES = 2.39, FDR = 0.004) and cell cycle regulation (hsa04110; NES = 2.26, FDR = 0.007). Downregulated pathways included Toll-like receptor signaling (hsa04620; NES = −2.06, FDR = 0.012) and NOD-like receptor signaling (hsa04621; NES = −2.11, FDR = 0.009).

Gene Ontology analysis identified additional enrichment in mitotic regulation (GO:0010389), chromosome segregation (GO:0007091), and histone modification (GO:0031058) (FDR <0.05). Enrichment statistics are summarized in Supplementary Table S2, Table S3.

Discussion

Stage-specific mechanisms of OPFR neurotoxicity with convergent links to ASD pathobiology

Our integrated analyses reveal that OPFRs disrupt neurodevelopment through temporally distinct yet biologically convergent mechanisms, with fetal and neonatal exposures targeting complementary pathways that collectively increase ASD risk. The fetal phase is characterized by epigenetic-immune priming, where OPFR-induced downregulation of chromatin remodeler ARID1A (rat Arid1a) and neuroimmune mediator TNFSF13 (rat Tnfsf13) establishes lasting transcriptional and immunological vulnerabilities. These changes create a permissive environment for synaptic dysfunction by impairing microglial surveillance and exacerbating oxidative stress through GDA suppression. In the neonatal phase, OPFRs shift their focus to lipidcentric synaptic disruption, as evidenced by strong correlations between MAPK3 and membrane plasmalogens (PC(15:0_16:0)) - interactions that may destabilize synaptic architecture against the backdrop of pre-existing fetal insults. The functional convergence is striking: fetal SHANK3 (rat Shank3) alterations and neonatal MAPK3-lipid perturbations both target glutamatergic signaling, while TNFSF13 suppression (fetal) and its receptor TNFRSF13B (rat Tnfrsf13b) downregulation (neonatal) sustain immunodeficient states that could impair synaptic pruning. This two-phase model suggests OPFRs exploit developmental stage-specific vulnerabilities - epigenetic programming during neurogenesis and lipid metabolism during synaptogenesis - to produce functionally similar outcomes relevant to ASD pathophysiology. That distinct sets of genes are differentially expressed across developmental windows powerfully illustrates how exposure timing dictates distinct molecular outcomes—yet OPFRs repeatedly converge on synaptic, immune, and metabolic pathways, revealing their capacity to engage multiple ASD risk axes. Future studies should investigate whether fetal epigenetic-immune priming increases susceptibility to later lipid disruption, and whether interventions targeting plasmalogen metabolism can rescue synaptic deficits in OPFR-exposed models.

Confounding variables and impact on interpretation

While our study integrates multi-omics datasets across different developmental stages and exposure paradigms, several considerations merit discussion. The GSE148266 (gestational high-dose exposure in fetal forebrain/placenta) and GSE211430 (perinatal 1000 μg/day exposure in neonatal cortex with lipidomics) datasets differ in their experimental designs, including dose regimens, exposure windows, and tissue contexts. These differences preclude direct quantitative comparisons of effect sizes but importantly provide complementary biological perspectives on OPFR neurotoxicity. Our analytical approach specifically addressed these variations through rigorous within-study contrasts (DESeq2) followed by cross-study pathway integration rather than raw data pooling. Combined with our tiered filtering scheme, the multiomics framework (DIABLO ∣r∣>0.9) further strengthened our ability to identify conserved biological signals, including fetal-specific spliceosome alterations, postnatal synaptic-immune network disruptions, and shared ASD-relevant pathways that replicated across models. While housing conditions (EDC-minimized vs standard vivarium) and technical batch effects represent potential confounders, they cannot account for the directional consistency of core findings like immune suppression or the specific overlap with human ASD cortical transcriptomes. These experimental differences ultimately reinforce the robustness of our central conclusion: that OPFRs disrupt neurodevelopmental pathways in both stage-specific and conserved manners, with particular relevance to ASD-associated biological processes.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that OPFRs disrupt neurodevelopment via convergent effects on synaptic gene-lipid networks, neuroimmune suppression, and epigenetic dysregulation—mechanisms shared with idiopathic ASD. Multi-omics integration demonstrates that OPFR exposure induces evolutionarily conserved transcriptional and lipidomic alterations, positioning these chemicals as environmentally relevant neurotoxicants. This work underscores the utility of integrated omics approaches for environmental ASD research and highlights candidate gene-lipid axes (e.g., BRAF–triglycerides) for biomarker development. Given the robustness of these findings across fetal and neonatal models, urgent validation in human epidemiological cohorts and stem cell-derived systems is warranted to establish translational relevance and inform potential intervention strategies.
