A global land daily 10-km-resolution surface ozone dataset from 2013–2022
Authors: Rui Wang, Huanfeng Shen, Chao Zeng, Jiajia Chen, Yuan Wang, Yuyu Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal ozone pollution has increased recently. Key aspects of global ozone pollution and its health assessment are not yet fully understood owing to the limited temporal resolution of existing datasets. In this study, a global surface ozone dataset was developed, providing 0.1° daily maximum 8-hour average surface ozone for 2013–2022. An efficient Light Gradient Boosting Machine model was constructed in this study to combine surface ozone observations, satellite products, atmospheric chemistry model simulations, meteorological reanalysis data, emission inventories, and other geographically distributed data. Importantly, this study implemented rigorous quality control for surface ozone observations from China and Europe, while also considering temporal and spatial features of the model inputs. The dataset yielded a spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation coefficient of determination (R2) of 0.79–0.88 and root mean square error (RMSE) of 11.32–13.26 μg m−3, demonstrating an accuracy comparable to that of the current state-of-the-art dataset. Daily surface ozone datasets offer more granular information than monthly or annual datasets, thereby providing critical support for air quality management and research on ozone-related health issues.Subject terms: Environmental sciences, Environmental monitoring

Background & Summary

Ambient ozone (O3) is one of the most significant air pollutants after PM2.5 that threatens human health1. Ozone pollution has been worsening recently2. From 2010 to 2020, ozone concentrations rose in many regions worldwide, especially in India, Nigeria, Pakistan, and so on1. Vazquez Santiago et al.3 assessed changes in ozone concentrations across 45 global megacities, 35 of which experienced increases. Exposure to ozone pollution can damage human health2, elevating the risk of respiratory4,5 and cardiovascular diseases6, triggering chronic obstructive pulmonary disease7,8, contributing to premature death9, among others. Progressively severe ozone pollution exacerbates the threat to human health. Mapping surface ozone concentrations is crucial for better understanding the spatiotemporal patterns of ozone pollution and quantifying its health effects10, especially as it grows severe.

Obtaining surface ozone concentrations through ground stations is currently considered the most reliable method11 due to its high accuracy and time continuity. However, the ground stations are unevenly distributed. Remote sensing satellites can provide large-scale measurements, which are generally used to monitor air pollution. However, the capability of satellites to detect surface ozone is limited12,13. This is partly because the presence of the ozone layer in the stratosphere, with boundary layer ozone accounting for less than 10% of total atmospheric ozone14. Satellite remote sensing only provides total O3 column, tropospheric O3 column, and O3 profile products15.

Recent advances, numerous studies have mapped surface ozone concentrations using multi-source data such as ground station observations, remote sensing products, and so on16. Approaches for mapping surface ozone concentrations include statistical methods, atmospheric chemical model simulations, and machine learning techniques. Statistical methods estimate ozone by modeling the relationship between ozone and its influencing indicators17–21. Atmospheric chemical models simulate chemical reactions, atmospheric pollutant transportation, and other chemical processes to estimate surface ozone22–25. Machine learning techniques capture complex nonlinear relationships between ozone and influencing indicators, and enable efficient and highly accurate estimation of surface ozone, making them widely used in current research26–29.

There are very few global high-temporal resolution surface ozone datasets at present. While previous studies have produced surface ozone datasets, most are regional16,30, which limits their utility for analyzing global ozone pollution trends and comparing pollution across regions. Importantly, due to constrained computing resources previously, the handful of available global surface ozone datasets exhibited low temporal resolution. For example, Liu et al.31 released a monthly 0.5° global surface ozone dataset. Delang et al.2 generated an annual 0.1° global surface ozone dataset. Such monthly or annual coarse-resolution surface ozone datasets smooth out ozone pollution information, making it difficult to capture sudden or short-term ozone pollution events, discern detailed ozone pollution trends, and conduct health assessments based on short-term ozone exposure. Therefore, mapping global surface ozone concentrations with high temporal resolution is important and meaningful.

Against this backdrop, this study developed a global land-based dataset of daily maximum 8-hour average (MDA8) surface ozone concentrations spanning 2013–2022, with a spatial resolution of 0.1° × 0.1°. Surface ozone concentrations were estimated using the efficient LightGBM algorithm, which integrates data from multi-source data, including surface ozone observations, atmospheric chemistry model simulations, meteorological reanalysis data, satellite products, emission inventories, and so on. The performance of the dataset was evaluated by spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation, cross validation considering the uneven spatial distribution of stations (SDCV) method, and leave-one-out cross-validation method across different geographic scales. The dataset is highly consistent with the existing global coarse temporal resolution dataset and the high temporal resolution China dataset. Moreover, it can better reveal the detailed information about the extreme ozone pollution event. This dataset can provide high spatiotemporal resolution surface ozone concentration information, capture the characteristics of ozone pollution events, quantify ozone pollution’s health impacts, as well as analyze its interactions with other atmospheric pollutants and ecosystems.

Methods

Data and preprocessing

Surface ozone observations

To obtain more accurate surface ozone estimates, this study collected as many high-quality surface ozone observations as possible. Hourly surface ozone observations were obtained from the Tropospheric Ozone Assessment Report (TOAR, https://zam2061.zam.kfa-juelich.de/dashboard), the China National Environmental Monitoring Center (CNEMC, observations obtained from https://air.cnemc.cn:18007/ via https://quotsoft.net/air), the Taiwan Ministry of Environment Environmental Information Open Platform (https://airtw.moenv.gov.tw/CHT/Query/His_Data.aspx), the Hong Kong Environmental Protection Interactive Centre (https://cd.epic.epd.gov.hk/EPICDI/air/station/), the Chile National Air Quality Information System (https://sinca.mma.gob.cl/mapainteractivo/), and the America Environmental Protection Agency (https://www.epa.gov/outdoor-air-quality-data/download-daily-data).

The TOAR database provides dense surface ozone observations across North America, Europe, Japan, and South Korea, but the coverage is sparse in other regions. Notably, hourly surface ozone observations from TOAR are measured in ppb, except for the surface ozone observations in France for 2013 and 2014, which are measured in μg m−3. In addition, TOAR contains almost no European data for 2021 and 2022. To address these gaps, the surface ozone observations from the European Environment Agency (https://discomap.eea.europa.eu/App/AQViewer/index.html?fqn=Airquality_Dissem.b2g.Measurements) were used to replace the 2013–2014 French data in TOAR, and to supplement the European surface ozone observations during 2021–2022.

To align with international standards for surface ozone observations of air pollutants, Chinese surface ozone observations were adjusted from the standard state (0 °C, 1 standard atmospheric pressure) to the reference state (25 °C, 1 standard atmospheric pressure) starting from August 31, 201832. The conversion factor for ozone concentration before and after this date is 0.9142916.

As shown in Fig. 1, a total of 6,193 ground stations were included from 2013 to 2022. Oceania lacked observations for the 2013–2015 period, whereas all other continents had annual observation records. North America, Europe, and eastern Asia are characterized by a higher density of stations. The total number of stations exhibited an overall upward trend from 2013 to 2020. During 2021–2022, due to the unavailability of TOAR European data, the number of stations recorded decreased slightly, which nonetheless remained above 4,000.

Atmospheric chemical model simulations

The four Chemical Climate Modeling Initiative (CCMI) Phase I models were used in this study, and all of which generated hourly ozone volume mixing ratios for the model surface layer. These models fall into three categories of atmospheric chemistry models. Specifically, they include the Earth System Models (the atmospheric chemistry coupled version of the MIROC Earth System Model CHASER MIROC-ESM model (CHASER)33,34, the Meteorological Research Institute’s Japan Earth System Model version 1 model (MRI)35,36), the Chemistry-Climate Model (the Goddard Space Flight Centre GEOSCCM model (GEOSCCM)37,38), and the Chemistry and Transport Model (CNRM-CERFACS CNRM-CM5-3 and MOCAGE model (MOCAGE)39,40). According to Table 2, the order by R² is MRI > CHASER > GEOSCCM > MOCAGE, and the order by RMSE is GEOSCCM < CHASER < MRI < MOCAGE. The MOCAGE and GEOSCCM models achieved lower accuracy. However, the MOCAGE model’s RACM chemical mechanism41 and the GEOSCCM model’s “Combo CTM” chemical package both incorporate HONO chemistry, whereas the other models do not explicitly account for it. This is significant because HONO plays a key role in the formation of the hydroxyl radical42, thereby significantly influencing the ozone concentrations.

However, due to the incomplete knowledge of complex HONO sources, realistic HONO mechanisms have not yet been implemented in global models43, including the GEOSCCM and MOCAGE models in this study. Currently, reliable global atmospheric chemical model simulation datasets that incorporate multiple HONO sources remain limited. Despite these limitations, this study employed these models based on their simulation accuracy, categorical characteristics, and chemical mechanisms. In future research, atmospheric chemical models integrating more comprehensive chemistry mechanisms should be accessed to enable a higher-precision estimate of surface ozone concentrations44.

Reanalysis data

Photochemical reactions for ozone production are closely related to meteorological conditions, with high temperatures, intense solar radiation, and low wind speeds contributing to its formation and accumulation45–47. This relationship makes meteorological variables a reliable basis for estimating surface ozone concentrations. In this study, meteorological variables were obtained from reanalysis data. Specifically, MERRA-2 and ERA5 reanalysis data were used.

The ERA5 reanalysis48,49 data utilized in this study included the following variables: ozone mass mixing ratio, ten-meter U wind component, ten-meter V wind component, relative humidity, two-meter dew point temperature, two-meter temperature, surface pressure, evaporation, total precipitation, boundary layer height, surface solar radiation downwards, mean surface downward UV radiation flux, and vertical integral of divergence of ozone flux.

The MERRA-2 reanalysis data selected for this study included the surface model layer ozone mass mixing ratio from M2I3NVASM, M2T3NVASM, and M2I3NVCHM. M2I3NVASM50 and M2I3NVCHM51 are instantaneous 3-dimensional 3-hourly data collections. Specifically, the M2I3NVASM dataset contains meteorological parameters, and the M2I3NVCHM dataset includes data on carbon monoxide (CO) and ozone. M2T3NVASM52 is a 3-hourly time-averaged data collection, which consists of assimilations of meteorological parameters. Additionally, CO from M2I3NVCHM was also used in this study because it is a precursor to ozone. The details of these datasets are shown in Table 1.

Remote sensing product

While satellites cannot directly measure surface ozone concentrations, other satellite-derived products are valuable for estimating surface ozone. Wang et al.53 estimated MDA8 ozone concentrations using total ozone column and tropospheric nitrogen dioxide (NO2) column from the TROPOMI sensor, along with ozone profile product from the OMI sensor, and demonstrated that satellite products improved the estimation of extreme values. Xue et al.30 fed the ozone profile measured by the OMI into a Random Forest model, which enhanced the accuracy of surface ozone estimation. Given the temporal scope of the dataset, the OMTO3e54 and OMNO2d55 datasets from the OMI sensor were utilized.

Missing data in OMI products compromises the surface ozone estimations56. To fill in the missing data strips, previous studies have employed interpolation57,58, statistical methods59,60, and artificial intelligence methods61,62. This study employed the temporal fitting followed by spatial residual correction method (TFFSRC)63 to preliminarily fill the gaps in remote sensing products, and subsequently used the LightGBM algorithm based on meteorological reanalysis data to further address the remaining gaps. The TFFSRC method is less sensitive to the width of missing strips compared to weighted linear regression and kriging. It operates under the assumption that the relationship between spatial locations is consistent over a short period. Specifically, remote sensing products from the day before and after the target day serve as auxiliary data. A time regression relationship is established between the target day’s grid and the auxiliary data grids in the vicinity of missing locations. Through this regression relationship, two predictions for the missing grid on the target day are derived from the same location in the auxiliary data, these two predictions are then weighted and fused to generate preliminary predictions. Following this step, kriging interpolation is applied to predict the residuals. The final predicted values are obtained by combining the preliminary predictions with the residuals.

Landcover data were also incorporated in this study, sourced from the Moderate Resolution Imaging Spectroradiometer (MODIS) landcover product, MCD12C164.

Auxiliary data

In addition to CO, this study also incorporated emission inventories65 from the Emissions Database for Global Atmospheric Research (EDGAR) for two other ozone precursors, nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) and non-methane volatile organic compounds (NMVOCs).

Elevation data were provided by General Bathymetric Chart of the Ocean67, which integrates datasets from many international and national data repositories and regional mapping programs.

Data preprocessing

This section describes the preprocessing of the aforementioned surface ozone observations and image datasets. The surface ozone observations were stripped of outliers, their unit was normalized to μg m−3, and their time was normalized to the UTC + 0 time zone. Hourly surface ozone observations were processed into MDA8 surface ozone concentrations, for which a valid value was defined as an 8-hour average derived from at least 6 hours of valid observations, with a minimum of 18 valid 8-hour averages required per day. The surface ozone observations were gridded at 0.1° resolution by averaging, and the processed grid values served as the labels for the machine learning model.

For image datasets, spatial resolution was uniformly resampled to 0.1° × 0.1° using bilinear interpolation. Images with temporal resolution finer than daily were uniformly resampled to daily. Ozone mass mixing ratio variables were aggregated to MDA8, while other variables were averaged to daily temporal resolution.

Accuracy validation

The accuracy validation metrics employed in this study were R2, RMSE, and mean absolute error (MAE), as shown in Eqs. (3) to (5) below. This study employs spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation for model validation. The truth values for validation are surface ozone observations that have been resampled to 0.1° × 0.1° grids. The spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation was employed to assess the spatial accuracy, and leave-one-out cross-validation was utilized to evaluate the performance in the absence of station locations. The spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation randomly partitions the data into 10 parts based on grid locations. Nine parts are used for training, and the remaining part for validation. This process is repeated ten times, with each part serving as the validation set once. The final accuracy is the average of the ten iterations. The leave-one-out cross-validation is conducted across multiple geographical scales, including continents, different resolution grids, and stations. In each iteration, data from one geographical unit are used for validation, and the remaining units are reserved for training. This process is repeated iteratively until every geographical unit has served as the validation set exactly once.

where n is the number of data, yi is the surface ozone concentrations gridded at 0.1° (referred to as true value), yˆi is the model prediction, and y−i is the mean true value.

The spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation generally evaluates the accuracy at the locations of the stations. To assess the accuracy at locations farther from stations, as well as to verify the relationship between accuracy and the density of the station distribution, the SDCV method68 was employed in this study. This approach builds on spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation with a key modification: during each training iteration, grids within a specified distance (d) of the validation grids are excluded from the training set. As a result, the distance between the grids in the training set and the validation set is always greater than d. The model performance changes gradually as d varies. This approach can also be used to assess how much the model’s accuracy depends on nearby stations.

Model and inputs

Model selection

Several studies have compared the performance of different machine learning algorithms in estimating surface ozone concentrations. Watson et al.69 compared the accuracy of 10 machine learning algorithms in predicting surface ozone concentrations during wildfires, concluding that the Gradient Boosting Algorithm was the most accurate. Wang et al.70 used the LightGBM, Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Random Forest, Deep Belief Network, and Back Propagation Neural Network to estimate O3, NO2, and CO concentrations. LightGBM demonstrated the highest accuracy and efficiency, followed by XGBoost, while other machine learning algorithms lagged significantly behind the Gradient Boosting Algorithm. Overall, the Gradient Boosting Algorithm has been shown to perform well in estimating surface ozone concentrations36.

The three most popular Gradient Boosting Algorithms are XGBoost, Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), and LightGBM71. XGBoost improves the model generalization by incorporating regularization into the loss function, and its column and row subsampling methods effectively mitigate overfitting72. CatBoost performs well with categorical data, and addresses gradient bias and prediction bias to reduce overfitting73. The Gradient-based One-Side Sampling and Exclusive Feature Bundling techniques of LightGBM excel at handling high-dimensional sparse data74. Additionally, its use of histograms for continuous features significantly improves computational efficiency71,75. In this study, the same data were input to these three machine learning models, with appropriate tuning of each model’s parameters.

The accuracy of spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation is shown in Table 3. LightGBM achieved an R2 of 0.80–0.88 and RMSE of 11.13–13.25 μg m−3. XGBoost showed comparable performance to LightGBM, with R2 of 0.80–0.88 and RMSE of 11.06–13.32 μg m−3. In contrast, CatBoost exhibited significantly lower accuracy with R2 of 0.73–0.80 and RMSE of 13.03–16.72 μg m−3. It is worth mentioning that LightGBM was very efficient, while XGBoost required almost 3 times as much training time. For these reasons, LightGBM was selected to estimate surface ozone in this study.

Model inputs

In most related studies estimating surface ozone, multi-year data are fed into the machine learning model without considering the time range of the input data. However, surface ozone exhibits high variability, and its behavior on the same date across different years may not be consistent, as it can be influenced by complex meteorological conditions that vary from year to year. This study discusses in detail whether data from the same date in different years can aid in surface ozone estimation.

The models were trained using data from one, two, three, four, and five years, referred to respectively as the One-year, Two-year, Three-year, Four-year, and Five-year models. Spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation was employed to verify the accuracy of these five models. Considering that the number and distribution of stations vary yearly, all stations were grouped into a 10-fold station catalog, and the training and validation sets were partitioned into the corresponding 10 folds based on this catalog. The parameters of the five models were kept consistent.

According to Fig. 2, the accuracy gradually decreased as more data were fed into the model. The One-year model outperformed the multi-year models. The rate of accuracy decline slowed as the volume of training data increased, with the R² difference between the one-year and two-year models consistently larger than that between the four-year and five-year models. Data from the same station across different years provided very limited auxiliary information, introducing noise that diluted the useful information from the current year’s data. This aligns with the high variability observed in surface ozone dynamics, which is why this study used only one year’s data for modeling.

Comparison of multi-year model accuracy.

Furthermore, many related studies have incorporated spatial features76,77, such as latitude, longitude, grid row and column into their machine learning model. These spatial features have been shown to significantly improve model accuracy. However, feeding spatial features into the decision tree-based machine learning models can result in stripe-like anomalies in surface ozone concentration maps, which are closely linked to the distribution of these spatial features. The impact of adding the spatial features is illustrated in the second row of Fig. 3a. Moreover, spatial features are theoretical metrics that help the model understand locations, distinct from features like temperature, humidity, and wind speed that reflect natural conditions. This study discusses whether to add spatial features or not by taking grid row and column indices as an example (simplified here as x and y). Leave-one-out cross-validation was applied to the six continental station networks (shown in Fig. 3b) to evaluate how spatial features influence locations without monitoring stations. The stations that were relatively sparsely distributed or close to more than one observation network were excluded to eliminate their impact. The results of the intercontinental station network leave-one-out cross-validation are presented in Fig. 3c,d.

Data quality control for surface ozone observations

Surface ozone observations are critical for accurately estimating surface ozone, therefore, they were meticulously filtered to ensure data quality. As shown in Table 4, this study found that the accuracy of spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation using only Chinese observations exhibited a notable gap before 2017, which aligns with the accuracy trend reported by Wei et al.16. Specifically, the spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation R2 increased from 0.73 in 2016 to 0.84 in 2017, marking a significant improvement of 0.1. The accuracy of the global surface ozone estimation was significantly lower when including Chinese observations than when excluding them from 2014 to 2016. However, the difference between the R2 in 2017 (global surface ozone estimation including Chinese observations) and in 2016 (global surface ozone estimation excluding Chinese observations) was minimal, approximately 0.02.

Framework for surface ozone dataset generation

According to Fig. 4, the generation of surface ozone datasets consists of three main processes: data processing, preliminary model building, and finally model & verification. In data processing, image datasets were processed into seamless images with consistent spatial and temporal resolutions, while surface ozone observations were subjected to quality control, temporal normalization, and unit harmonization, followed by gridding onto 0.1° grids. In the model building, three machine learning algorithms were evaluated, among which the LightGBM model was chosen due to its superior performance. Subsequently, two key decisions were made: first, to exclude spatial features from the model input; second, to determine the model input’s optimal time range. In the model and verification, the efficient LightGBM algorithm was applied to construct the model annually, and the best-performing model in spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation was selected. Key hyperparameters of LightGBM are as follows: ‘max_depth’: 18; ‘num_leaves’: 680; ‘learning_rate’: 0.08; ‘num_boost_round’: varies with the model. Subsequently, validation was conducted using the SDCV method and the ‘leave-one-out’ cross-validation method across different geographical scales. Finally, surface ozone concentrations were mapped annually based on the best-performing model corresponding to each year.

Data Records

The global land daily surface ozone dataset (2013–2022) in NetCDF format is freely available at Zenodo, accessible via 10.5281/zenodo.1668395279. Each NetCDF file contains one day’s surface ozone concentration data, and the NetCDF files are compressed into ZIP archives by year. Daily files are named ‘ozone-YYYYMMDD.nc’, and annual files are named ‘YYYY.zip’, where ‘YYYY’ denotes the year, ‘MM’ the month, and ‘DD’ the day. Each NetCDF file is approximately 25 megabytes, and each zip file is around 2 gigabytes. Surface ozone concentrations are given in units of μg m−3, with a spatial resolution of 0.1°. Values for non-land areas are denoted as NaN.

Technical Validation

Model accuracy

Spatial-based 10-fold cross-validation was used to assess the model’s spatial prediction capability. In this study, the R2 was 0.80–0.88, the RMSE was 11.13–13.25 μg m−3, and the MAE was 8.10–9.57 μg m−3. Monthly accuracy metrics were also calculated, which showed higher performance, with R2 ranging from 0.84 to 0.91, RMSE ranging from 7.66 to 9.04 μg m−3, and MAE ranging from 5.45 to 6.40 μg m−3. The accuracy gradually improved, with daily R2 increasing from 0.80 in 2013 to 0.88 in 2022, possibly related to the enhancements in the quality of the surface ozone observations. In comparison to the daily accuracy, the monthly R² increases by approximately 0.03 annually, whereas the RMSE and MAE decrease by around 3.9 μg m−3 and 2.9 μg m−3 per year, respectively.

At 2°, R2 remained above 0.6, indicating moderate spatial dependence among estimation grids. This resilience suggests the dataset maintains reliable accuracy in regions with sparse station coverage.

The result of the SDCV validation.

To gain a more thorough understanding of the accuracy at the locations without stations, leave-one-out cross-validation was conducted across different geographic scales: continent, 30° grid, and 10° grid. The intercontinental station network leave-one-out cross-validation was designed to simulate the accuracy for continents without stations, and the results are presented in Fig. 3(c–d) under the “exclude_xy” condition. Across all continents, the average R² reached 0.5 and the average RMSE reached 20.63 μg m−3. The African station network exhibited the lowest accuracy, with an average R² of 0.39 and RMSE of 25.38 μg m−3. This could be attributed to inadequate support from station networks across other continents, particularly limited station availability from the Southern Hemisphere station network, as well as the unstable influence of scarce stations within Africa. In contrast, the European station network achieved the highest accuracy, with an average R² of 0.58 and an RMSE of 17.66 μg m−3. The accuracy across continents follows the order: European > Asian > South American > North American > Oceanian > African station network.

To validate the accuracy under different scenarios of station absence, this study reduced the distance scale for leave-one-out cross-validation. The results of leave-one-out cross-validation across 30° and 10° grids are presented in Fig. 7. For the 30° grid leave-one-out cross-validation, the average R² is 0.50 and the average RMSE is 17.75 μg m−3. Regions including North America, Europe, and East Asia demonstrate higher accuracy, with grids in western Russia, Germany, Poland, and Sweden achieving the highest. This higher accuracy is likely attributable to the dense distribution of surrounding stations. The lowest accuracy is observed in grids near Greenland and southern Africa. For the grid near Greenland, this may stem from its location in the polar zone, whereas the surrounding grids lie in the cold temperate and warm temperate zones, where significant climatic differences weaken mutual supportive relationships. For the grids in southern Africa, lower accuracy could be due to limited station availability in adjacent grids, particularly when compared to Australia. For the 10° grid leave-one-out cross-validation, the average R² is 0.55 and the average RMSE is 16.39 μg m−3. In contrast to the 30° grid, the 10° grid reveals more detailed spatial patterns. Grids with fewer surrounding stations or weaker supportive relationships exhibit lower accuracy, as exemplified by those in Greenland, northern South America, southern Africa, and the Middle East.

Comparison with other datasets

This study’s dataset was compared with high-quality surface ozone mapping datasets from previous studies16,31,80,81. Yang et al.81 and Wei et al.16 provided China daily MDA8 surface ozone datasets ChinaHighO3 and CHAP, with spatial resolutions of 1 km and 10 km, respectively. For the period 2017–2020, the average R2 and RMSE between this study’s dataset and the ChinaHighO3 dataset were 0.71 and 12.54 μg m−3, and the average R2 and RMSE between this study’s dataset and the CHAP dataset were 0.70 and 12.81 μg m−3. For the other two global datasets, the average R2 and RMSE between this study’s dataset and Liu et al.’s dataset31 from 2013 to 2019 were 0.61 and 15.22 μg m−3, and the average R2 and RMSE between this study’s dataset and Chang et al.’s dataset80 from 2013 to 2017 were 0.54 and 13.04 μg m−3. The dataset of this study exhibited strong consistency and high reliability relative to existing products.

The leave-one-out cross-validation method was used to assess the performance of this study’s dataset, CHAP dataset, and ChinaHighO3 dataset. Five grids in China from the training set were selected, and in each iteration, the data from one grid was removed to validate the model’s performance build with each dataset. The ChinaHighO3 dataset and the CHAP dataset exhibited comparable validation accuracy. Given that CHAP shares the same spatial resolution as this study’s dataset, further details are provided here (the scatterplots are shown in Fig. 9). The fitting results showed that this study’s dataset had a slope of 0.90 and an intercept of 8.43 μg m−3, while the CHAP dataset had a slope of 0.90 and an intercept of 9.15 μg m−3. The CHAP data training set may contain surface ozone observations from these five grids. Nevertheless, the R2 for this study’s dataset was 0.90, and the R2 for CHAP was 0.88, confirming this study’s dataset maintains high applicability.

Performance of the dataset under extreme ozone pollution conditions

Ozone concentration maps provide improved visualization of ozone pollution in areas lacking monitoring stations, especially during extreme pollution events. This study further verified the applicability of this study’s dataset in extreme ozone pollution events. Temperature ranks among the most critical factors influencing the production of surface ozone. Influenced by high temperatures, surface ozone increased in Europe during the summer of 2022, exceeding the ozone pollution warning standard several times. According to the information provided in the Bulletin of the World Meteorological Organization (WMO)82, ozone pollution initially spreads from southwestern Europe to central Europe, and finally reaches northeastern Europe.

Other global datasets fail to capture short-term ozone pollution variability due to their low temporal resolution (monthly or yearly). Therefore, this study selected the MRI atmospheric chemistry model simulations, as they exhibited a higher correlation with surface ozone observations than other atmospheric chemistry model simulations. Figure 10 illustrates the change in ozone pollution in Europe from 10 July 2022 to 20 July 2022, as captured by this study’s dataset and MRI model simulations. The performance of this study’s dataset was consistent with the distribution of ozone concentration exceedance stations published in the WMO Bulletin. On 10 July 2022, ozone pollution was mainly in Spain and Portugal, and it then spread to central Europe, and by 20 July 2022, was mainly distributed in southern France, Italy, Germany, and eastern Europe. As for the MRI model simulations, the simulation images are centered on 180° longitude, leading to a certain discontinuity at the 0° longitude. The spatial distribution trend of ozone pollution simulated by the MRI model partially aligns with the ozone pollution distribution in the published WMO Bulletin, which is characterized by an eastward migration. However, the specific spatial distribution of the ozone pollution deviates significantly from the WMO Bulletin published, notably due to the overestimation of ozone concentrations between 10 and 14 July 2022, which led to simulated concentrations exceeding 100 μg m−3 across large parts of Europe. This study’s dataset performs better than atmospheric chemistry model simulations in extreme ozone pollution events, and it can provide detailed ozone distribution in station-deficient areas during extreme pollution events.

Usage Notes

This study provides a 0.1° daily maximum 8-hour average surface ozone concentration dataset from 2013 to 2022. The daily data are initially stored in individual NetCDF files. To improve download efficiency, the annual NetCDF files are compressed into a single ZIP file, enabling yearly single-file downloads. Users can view and operate the NetCDF files through Panoply, MATLAB, Python, among other tools.

The accuracy of this dataset performs well in validation. Notably, owing to the uneven global distribution of monitoring stations, this study’s dataset is less representative of regions with few or no stations compared to those regions with high station density, despite our efforts to validate these areas as comprehensively as possible. Furthermore, residual uncertainty remains due to the inherent errors of machine learning. This residual uncertainty compels researchers to meticulously implement appropriate correction and analysis methods based on specific objectives. This research dataset can provide high spatiotemporal resolution information on the variations and trends of surface ozone concentrations, facilitate the characterization of extreme ozone pollution events, and enable the quantification of ozone pollution’s impacts on human health and ecosystems.
