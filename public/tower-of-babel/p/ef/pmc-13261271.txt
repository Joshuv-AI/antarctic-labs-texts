Is there a role for measuring coronary artery calcium progression in 2026?
Authors: Omar Dzaye, Michael J Blaha, Alexander C Razavi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractImage, graphical abstract

The 2026 American College of Cardiology (ACC) / American Heart Association (AHA) / Multisociety Dyslipidemia Guideline provides a Class 1 recommendation for the use of CAC scoring among individuals with borderline to intermediate risk to improve risk classification and guide shared decision-making involving preventive therapies, with an increased emphasis on CAC to guide LDL-C goals and preventive treatment intensification [1]. Because many of such preventive therapies stabilize plaque by increasing plaque density and in turn increase CAC scores (density paradox), one of the key questions has been the degree of prognostic utility to measuring CAC progression [2]. While the role of CAC to improve precision in risk assessment and initial management of asymptomatic individuals without clinical ASCVD is well-established, the potential utility of longitudinal CAC measurement for further treatment personalization is less clear for individuals with known subclinical atherosclerosis.

In this issue of the American Journal of Preventive Cardiology, Glidden et al. assessed the prognostic value of serial assessment for incident CAC and CAC progression (average follow-up 5.9 years) among >4,100 individuals in the CLARIFY registry (average 58 years, 50% women, 90% White). Approximately one-half (52%) of individuals had CAC=0 at baseline and maintained persistent CAC=0, whereas about one-in-three had mild progression (10–50 Agatston Units [AU]) and one-in-five had moderate (51–150 AU) or severe progression (>150 AU). Older individuals, men, those with hypertension or diabetes, and those on statin medication at baseline were more likely to have severe CAC progression. Compared to individuals with persistent CAC=0, those with CAC progression had up to a 2-fold higher risk in 5-point major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), defined by myocardial infarction, stroke, all-cause mortality, coronary revascularization, and heart failure after adjusting for demographics, traditional risk factors, and baseline CAC score (HR: 1.96, 95% CI: 1.38–2.80). Contrastingly, individuals who developed incident CAC versus CAC=0 (HR: 0.99, 95% CI: 0.54–1.81) or had stable CAC scores (HR: 1.75, 95% CI: 0.24–12.7) did not have a significantly higher risk of MACE over the median 5–6 year follow-up.

This study builds on prior evidence regarding the assessment of CAC progression, which has largely showed there is modest incremental statistical improvement in event prediction yet uncertain clinical utility [3]. Strengths of the current analysis include a relatively large sample size, serial assessment of CAC via three separate methods of modeling CAC progression, and its linkage with both atherosclerotic and non-atherosclerotic CVD outcomes.

There are key considerations when interpreting the results of the analysis. First, the repeat CAC scanning was not offered to all CLARIFY participants and was performed according to clinician discretion, increasing the potential for selection bias towards likely higher risk individuals. Second, the exponential growth rate of CAC must be considered despite adjustment for baseline CAC score especially when evaluating CAC progression without natural log or square root transformation, as prior CAC scores increase by an estimated 20–25% per year [4] and individuals with higher baseline CAC will almost always progress faster than those with lower CAC scores [5]. Third, while CAC progression was statistically associated with a higher risk of MACE, the clinical setting for which to apply these findings is unclear and how often treatment decisions were changed in CLARIFY after knowledge of a second CAC score is unknown. Lastly, the non-significant risk associated with the development of incident CAC within 5–6 years does not necessarily reflect zero incremental lifetime risk but rather underlines the ample amount of time available for clinicians to initiate preventive therapies for individuals with mild subclinical atherosclerosis burden to reduce risk of future CVD. A similar concept may apply to the non-significant risk associated with stable CAC scores over the follow-up period due to either short interscan intervals or possible treatment intensification by clinicians, which were not able to be measured in the current analysis.

The 2026 AHA/ACC/Multisociety Dyslipidemia Guideline provides a Class 2a recommendation for repeat CAC scanning within 3–7 years among individuals with CAC=0 for ongoing risk assessment. These recommendations are based on prior findings in the Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis, which showed that the warranty period of a CAC=0 until CAC incidence ranges between 3 and 7 years assuming a number needed to scan of 4–5 individuals and depending on baseline risk (low risk patients: 5–7 years; borderline-intermediate risk patients: 3–5 years; high risk patient or those with diabetes: 3 years) [6].

Select specialty guidelines in the United States [7,8] as well as international guidelines [9] comment on the potential role of rescanning among individuals with prevalent CAC. The National Lipid Association [7] states that repeat CAC scoring may be considered for 1) individuals with CAC 1–99 in 3–5 years if it might change treatment decisions (Class 2B, Level of Evidence B-Non Randomized), and 2) individuals with CAC ≥100 and LDL-C ≥ 70 mg/dL in 3 years to assess potential progression beyond CAC ≥300 (Class 2B, Level of Evidence C-Limited Data), a key threshold that demarcates secondary prevention level risk [10].

The value of rescanning in the prevalent CAC versus CAC=0 populations should be reviewed. Among individuals with CAC=0, the benefit of rescanning in 3–7 years is to detect the earliest conversion to CAC >0 and initiate statin therapy among those with mild subclinical atherosclerosis. For those with prevalent CAC and on statin therapy, the potential value of a rescan would be to assess progression beyond what would be considered “normal” (e.g. ≥25% baseline value) and identify those individuals most likely to benefit from additional preventive therapies, including but not limited to non-statin lipid-lowering, aspirin, intensity of blood pressure lowering, incretin mimetics, and SGLT2i. However, further prospective data in this space is required to study the natural progression of individuals with prevalent CAC on statin therapy.

Overall, we propose a cautious approach for consideration of repeat CAC scanning among individuals with prevalent CAC. Individuals with CAC 1–99 are the most heterogenous risk group among all individuals with prevalent CAC, therefore may have the greatest theoretical benefit from a rescan to assess progression and potential treatment intensification beyond clinically meaningful thresholds of CAC ≥100 or ≥300. For example, a 55 year old male with CAC of 25 who is placed on a moderate intensity statin may benefit from statin intensification or future additional pharmacotherapy depending on 3–5-year CAC progression, including but not limited to aspirin [11], non-statin lipid lowering therapy [12], and GLP-1RA [13]. Whether benefit from such pharmacotherapy can be adequately modeled based on baseline prevalent CAC and risk factor profile alone [14] and how best to correct for CAC progression among statin versus non-statin users [15] are ongoing areas of investigation (Fig. 1).
