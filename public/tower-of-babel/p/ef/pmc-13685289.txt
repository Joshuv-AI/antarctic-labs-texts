Development of a nomogram for risk factors of wheezing in infants with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia
Authors: Chen Jianlong, Ye Yingying, Zhang Zhiling, Zhou Weifang, Ji Wei, Chu Chu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo construct a nomogram model for analyzing risk factors of wheezing in infants with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP), so as to provide evidence for clinical intervention.MethodsA retrospective analysis was performed on clinical data of 569 infants with MPP hospitalized in the Department of Respiratory Medicine and Department of Infectious Diseases, Children’s Hospital of Soochow University. They were divided into a wheezing group (204 cases) and a non-wheezing group (365 cases) according to symptoms. Demographic characteristics, clinical manifestations, laboratory examinations, bronchoscopy frequency and chest imaging findings were collected. Differences in clinical and laboratory indicators between the two groups were compared. Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) regression was used for preliminary screening, followed by Logistic regression to identify independent risk factors for wheezing in infants with MPP. Finally, a nomogram prediction model was established, and its performance was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and decision curve analysis (DCA).Results1. Univariate and multivariate analysis of predictors for wheezing in MPP: Univariate Logistic regression screening revealed 16 potential risk factors: fever, cough, history of wheezing, history of eczema, WBC, N#, L#, PLT, CRP, IgG, IgM, CKMB, D-D (ug/L), HRV, HRSV, HMPV (P < 0.05). Multivariate Logistic regression showed that history of wheezing (OR = 5.894, 95%CI: 3.541–9.812), history of eczema (OR = 1.951, 95%CI: 1.295–2.939), elevated IgG (OR = 1.166, 95%CI: 1.074–1.266), elevated CKMB (OR = 1.083, 95%CI: 1.007–1.164), and combined HRSV infection (OR = 3.430, 95%CI: 1.819–6.468) were risk factors, while fever (OR = 0.389, 95%CI: 0.252–0.599) was a protective factor against wheezing in infants with MPP.2. Establishment and validation of the nomogram model: Based on multivariate Logistic regression, six indicators were included to construct the nomogram for predicting wheezing in MPP: history of wheezing, history of eczema, elevated IgG, elevated CKMB, combined HRSV infection, and fever. Each predictor was scored independently, and the total score corresponded to the predicted probability of wheezing caused by MPP. Model evaluation: Training set: AUC = 0.7862, sensitivity = 72.4%, specificity = 78.1%; Internal validation set: AUC = 0.6863, sensitivity = 53.7%, specificity = 79.5%, indicating favorable discrimination. DCA suggested potential clinical applicability.To further evaluate the robustness of the nomogram and to correct for potential optimism, we performed 200 bootstrap resampling iterations within the development cohort. The optimism-corrected area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) was calculated by subtracting the average optimism (estimated from the difference between apparent performance in each bootstrap sample and the performance applied to the original dataset) from the training-set AUC. The corrected AUC was 0.705 (95% CI: 0.662–0.748), which is consistent with the internal validation result (AUC = 0.686) and indicates moderate discriminative ability. Although the corrected AUC remained above 0.70, the decline from the training-set AUC (0.786) suggests some degree of overfitting, likely attributable to the single-center retrospective design and limited sample size. These bootstrap-corrected estimates support the potential clinical utility of the nomogram.ConclusionAtopic constitution and mixed viral infection are risk factors for wheezing in infants with MPP. The nomogram model is clinically valuable for predicting wheezing in infants with MPP.Keywords: Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia, Wheezing, Infants, Risk factors, Nomogram

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is one of the major pathogens causing community-acquired pneumonia in children. Clinical manifestations of MPP in infants are often atypical. Some infants present with wheezing and tachypnea as predominant symptoms, which are also main features of severe MPP in young infants, differing from high fever and severe cough in older children.A meta-analysis of 15 cohort studies involving 128,065 children confirmed that recurrent wheezing in preschool children is strongly associated with the future development of asthma [1].Furthermore, infants hospitalized with wheezing during bronchiolitis episodes were more likely to develop preschool wheezing at 12 months and asthma at 4 years of age [2].A 6-year follow-up study of infants with first-episode wheezing showed that eczema (OR = 2.19), history of allergy (OR = 2.34), family history of asthma (OR = 5.07), and low birth weight (OR = 4.97) were independent risk factors for recurrent wheezing [3]. These findings underscore the importance of early identification and intervention in wheezing infants to mitigate the long-term risk of asthma.But, at present, studies on risk factors of wheezing in infants with MPP are limited.

Moreover, Several anatomical and immunological features unique to infants may account for their increased susceptibility to wheezing during MPP, compared with older children. Anatomically, the infant airway is characterized by smaller caliber, reduced elastic recoil, and relatively immature cartilaginous support, particularly in the peripheral airways [4]. These structural properties result in higher baseline peripheral airway resistance and greater propensity for dynamic airway collapse during expiration.Therefore, this study retrospectively analyzed clinical manifestations and laboratory examinations of infants with MPP hospitalized in our hospital, aiming to explore risk factors for wheezing in infants with MPP.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

A total of 569 infants aged ≥ 1 month and < 3 years old with MPP hospitalized in the Department of Respiratory Medicine and Department of Infectious Diseases from January 2016 to December 2024 were enrolled. According to the presence of wheezing, they were divided into two groups:

Wheezing group: 204 cases, 137 males and 67 females, age 1.17 (0.50, 2.00) years.

Non-wheezing group: 365 cases, 231 males and 134 females, age 1.17 (0.37, 2.00) years.

Feature selection

Variables with statistically significant differences (P < 0.05) between the wheezing and non-wheezing groups were selected. Univariate Logistic regression was used to screen variables with P < 0.05. LASSO regression was applied to identify key predictors.

Model construction and validation

Screened features were entered into multivariate Logistic regression analysis. A nomogram model was generated using R packages. All patients were split into a training set and an internal validation set at a ratio of 4:1. Model discrimination was assessed by ROC curve and AUC (AUC > 0.7 indicated good discrimination). Calibration curves were used for predictive accuracy, and decision curve analysis (DCA) for clinical utility.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 and R 4.4.2. Measurement data were tested for normality (Shapiro-Wilk test). Normally distributed data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (x̄±s), and compared using independent-samples t-test (homogeneous variance) or Welch t-test (heterogeneous variance). Non-normally distributed data were presented as median (interquartile range) [M(P25, P75)] and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Enumeration data were expressed as cases (percentage) [n(%)] and compared using χ² test or Fisher exact test. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the Children’s Hospital of Soochow University (IRB approval number:2026CS390). Because this is a retrospective study using only anonymized medical records and no additional invasive procedures, the requirement for written informed consent to paticipate was waived by the Ethics Committee of Children’s Hospital of Soochow University. All patient data were de‑identified and analyzed confidentially to protect privacy .

Internal validation

For internal validation, all patients were divided into a training set and an internal validation set in a 4:1 ratio. The ROC curve demonstrated an AUC of 0.7862, with a sensitivity of 72.4% and specificity of 78.1% in the training set (Fig. 3A), and an AUC of 0.6863, sensitivity of 53.7%, and specificity of 79.5% in the internal validation set (Fig. 3B), indicating robust discriminative capability. Additionally, DCA analysis was performed to evaluate the clinical utility of the nomogram, plotting the threshold probability on the x-axis and the net benefit rate on the y-axis. Data from Fig. 3C and D demonstrate that the nomogram provides greater net benefits than the “all” and “none” strategies in both the training set (threshold probabilities: 0.00–0.80; 0.95–0.98) and the internal validation set (threshold probabilities: 0.25–0.60; 0.64–0.75; 0.86–0.94), highlighting its potential for clinical application.

Conclusion

The line model enables real-time assessment of a child’s current condition based on severity grading; clinical symptom clustering identifies distinct clinical phenotypes; and prognostic predictions include hospitalization duration, ICU admission necessity, and complication occurrence. Different models collectively facilitate precise risk stratification and early intervention for Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP) across multiple dimensions. This study initially screened potential predictive factors using univariate logistic regression, followed by Lasso regression to further identify key variables associated with wheezing occurrence in children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia. Finally, a visual decision curve plot integrating six independent predictive factors was constructed via multivariate logistic regression. The performance of the decision curve plot was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) on both the training set and internal validation set. Internal validation demonstrated its potential for clinical application, enabling clinicians to early identify SMPP cases associated with wheezing in infants and young children.

Discussion

Mycoplasma pneumoniae is a major pathogen responsible for pediatric respiratory infections worldwide, with a notably increased infection risk in infants and young children post-COVID-19 pandemic [6]. Mycoplasma infections in this population frequently manifest as wheezing and dyspnea. This study found that most infants and young children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae-associated pneumonia and wheezing had a history of wheezing and eczema, often coexisting with viral infections. As a potent antigenic stimulator, Mycoplasma pneumoniae can induce and exacerbate airway hyperresponsiveness, particularly in children with allergic constitutions, who are more susceptible to wheezing symptoms [7]. Human respiratory syncytial virus (HRSV), rhinovirus, and human metapneumovirus infections are also significant pathogens causing wheezing in pediatric infections [8–10]. Mixed infections exert a synergistic effect, worsening airway inflammation and obstruction while elevating the risk of severe and critical outcomes [11]. Non-wheezing children exhibit higher fever incidence, likely attributable to Mycoplasma-induced immune system activation triggering systemic inflammatory responses; in contrast, wheezing children primarily exhibit localized respiratory inflammatory symptoms with relatively milder systemic inflammatory reactions [12–14].

Infants and young children with Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia and wheezing exhibit higher white blood cell counts, neutrophil counts, lymphocyte counts, and platelet counts compared to those without wheezing, while their CRP levels are lower. This phenomenon may be attributed to the coexistence of allergic inflammatory responses and post-infectious inflammatory responses in wheezing patient, leading to elevated counts of white blood cells, neutrophils, and lymphocytes. Following Mycoplasma infection, the body enters a persistent inflammatory state, which stimulates megakaryocyte proliferation and results in increased platelet counts. Additionally, wheezing patient experience varying degrees of airway spasms and hypoxia, stimulating the bone marrow to release more platelets [15]. A study found that Mycoplasma pneumoniae causes vascular endothelial injury in children. Additionally, Mycoplasma pneumoniae can induce excessive inflammatory and immune responses, releasing large amounts of inflammatory cytokines, which elevate the body’s coagulation state and consequently lead to increased D-D levels [16]. Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection exacerbates the Th1/Th2 imbalance in wheezing children.Following Mycoplasma pneumoniae infection, Th0 cells differentiate into Th2 cells, while the secretion of Th1 factors decreases. Because, macrophages secrete immunosuppressive substances known as sIL-2R.SIL-2R could inhibit Th1 cell activity, accelerate airway narrowing, and e the proliferation and differentiation of B cells, increasing the synthesis of IgG and IgM.Meanwhile, the reduction in Th1 lymphocytes and decreased secretory activity led to diminished secretion of cytokines such as IL-2 and IFN-γ, thereby suppressing the inflammatory cytokine response; thus, children with wheezing exhibited lower levels of CRP.Consequently, laboratory tests in wheezing patient typically show normal CRP levels [17–19]. In the wheezing group, the disease is typically more severe, leading to a more pronounced systemic inflammatory response and more severe myocardial hypoxia, which results in higher CK-MB levels. In infants and young children, the smooth muscle of the small airways is poorly developed, so smooth muscle spasm is uncommon; instead, airway narrowing is predominantly caused by submucosal edema of the airway wall, giving rise to wheezing [20].

A recent study on a dynamic online line model for predicting pulmonary consolidation in MPP patients [21] incorporated seven variables: age, duration of fever, lymphocyte count, C-reactive protein, ferritin, CD8+T lymphocyte percentage, and CD4+T lymphocyte percentage. The model demonstrated acceptable performance in both predictive value and clinical benefit. Another study identified age, atopic constitution, wheezing, hypoxemia, and pleural effusion as independent predictors of Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia with bronchiolitis in children, leading to the development of a line model. This model effectively predicts the risk of bronchiolitic MPP in children, and early intervention reduces the risk of subsequent obstructive bronchiolitis [22]. A study on severe Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (SMPP) revealed that D-dimer, neutrophil percentage, delayed hospital admission, pleural effusion, and necrotizing pneumonia were independent risk factors for pulmonary embolism in SMPP patients [23].

This study is a single-center retrospective study that underwent only internal validation, lacking external validation and prospective validation. In addition, several clinically relevant variables were not available in our retrospective database, including family history of atopy/allergy, environmental exposures (such as tobacco smoke and air pollution), and objective severity scores (e.g., respiratory rate, oxygen saturation). These factors may potentially confound or modify the observed associations between the included predictors and wheezing. Their absence should be acknowledged as a limitation, and future prospective studies are warranted to incorporate these variables to validate and refine our nomogram. The predictive performance of the model in other institutions, across different time periods, or among patients of diverse ethnicities and regions requires further refinement. Future efforts should involve utilizing multi-center, retrospective independent datasets to conduct external validation of the existing model, along with prospective validation and optimization to enhance its representativeness and robustness.
