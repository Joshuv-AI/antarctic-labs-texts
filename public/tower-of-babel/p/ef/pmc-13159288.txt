Experiences and perspectives of children and adolescents participating in pediatric clinical trials: a qualitative evidence synthesis
Authors: Yiming Ren, Si Tang, Guanghe Ji, Rui Li, Jianping Liu, Ruyu Xia, Yutong Fei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChildren are increasingly involved in clinical trials, yet their own perspectives on participation are often overlooked in favor of parental or professional views. Understanding children’s lived experiences is essential for improving trial design and ethical practice.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and qualitative evidence synthesis of studies exploring children’s experiences and perspectives regarding clinical trial participation. We searched PubMed, Embase, APA PsycInfo, APA PsycArticles, CINAHL, Web of Science, and three Chinese databases from inception to January 1, 2026. Two reviewers independently screened studies, extracted data, and assessed quality. Data were synthesized using the thematic synthesis method and confidence in the findings was assessed using the GRADE-CERQual approach.ResultsA total of 56 qualitative interview studies involving 2,101 children from 15 different countries were included.The synthesis identified three overarching analytic dimensions that frame the child’s participation journey: (1) Negotiating Agency within Information Asymmetry, where children strive for control despite limited understanding; (2) Relational Autonomy in the Child-Parent-Professional Triad, highlighting that children’s decision-making is deeply embedded in social relationships; and (3) The Dynamic Balance of Burden and Altruism, where children continuously weigh personal risks against altruistic benefits. These dimensions encompassed six descriptive themes: children’s understanding of trial components; requests for personal rights; influence of other people; potential benefits; potential concerns; and the trial participation experience.ConclusionsChildren’s participation in clinical trials is not a single decision but a dynamic, relational process. The findings support a shift from protecting children from research to protecting them through ethical engagement. Researchers should adopt a stance of “ethical symmetry,” treating children’s assent with the same ethical gravity as adult consent, and facilitate “relational autonomy” by supporting the child-parent-professional triad. Tailored, age-appropriate information and continuous emotional support are critical to optimizing children’s trial experiences.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-026-01904-4.

Background

Children are underrepresented in clinical trials, leading to a lack of evidence-based practice and pediatric therapeutic options [1]. Considering that pediatric prescribing is often based on extrapolation from trials in adults due to the lack of pediatric data [2], children are placed at risk of treatment failure [3], and adverse effects and toxicity that may affect their health and development [4–6]. Significant advances in child health have been made due to the conduct of various pediatric trials, but the number of pediatric trials remains low [7], which is disproportionate to the burden of disease among children [8].

Legal frameworks governing children’s participation in research vary significantly across jurisdictions [9]. While English law utilizes the concept of ‘Gillick Competence’ (defined as a child having sufficient understanding and intelligence to fully understand what is proposed) to validate a child’s independent consent [10], this is not a universal standard. In many other states, children are not afforded this legal right; instead, their participation hinges entirely on parental permission, with ‘assent’ practices varying widely based on age and maturity [9]. Consequently, consent in childhood is a complex, socially constructed issue, and the ethical implications of such ‘assumed assent’ remain controversial [11]. Furthermore, competence assessment may be difficult and contentious because it involves value judgements [12]. Theoretical perspectives from childhood studies suggest that competence is not solely a fixed developmental milestone but is socially constructed and contingent upon the support provided by adults during the research process [13].

The methods and meanings of assent remain uncertain; furthermore, children’s dissent is largely ignored [14]. Children often feel a power imbalance relative to adults and their preferences may be neglected [15]. To challenge this dynamic, scholars in the sociology of childhood argue that children should be viewed as ‘competent social actors’ rather than merely ‘becomings’ [16]. This perspective underpins the concept of ‘ethical symmetry’, which advocates that children’s rights and views should be accorded the same ethical respect as those of adults, rather than being overshadowed by a purely protectionist focus [17]. Recognizing their agency in this way is essential to addressing the power imbalance and respecting their rights in the research process. Studies have shown that how adults view minors can affect whether they live up to their potential, so when children feel viewed as less capable than adults, they may not be informed sufficiently and not be fully supported in taking a role in decision-making [18–20].

It has been shown that adolescents’ expectations for clinical improvements through participation are sometimes unrealistically high and they are prone to risk-taking, particularly in emotional situations [18, 21]. Understanding children and adolescents’ perspectives on trials and the factors that influence their decisions and satisfaction with research is essential [22]. Methodologically, moving from ‘research on children’ to ‘research with children’ is crucial [23]. In this synthesis, this does not imply children act as co-investigators; rather, following Christensen and James, it engages children as competent subjects capable of articulating their own realities [23]. Prioritizing children’s direct narratives over adult proxy reports allows qualitative methods to uncover unique lived experiences that adults cannot fully interpret. While quantitative instruments often rely on predefined categories, qualitative approaches offer the flexibility for unexpected themes to emerge, capturing participants’ broader lived experiences. Significant efforts have been made in this direction, most notably by the UK Medicines for Children Research Network (MCRN), which established a dedicated programme of research to investigate participation (e.g., the RECRUIT study [24]). However, despite these important initiatives, the synthesis of global evidence remains limited. Three Cochrane reviews have focused on adults [25–27], while other reviews have prioritized participation burden [28] or recruitment within pharmacological trials [29], largely overlooking children’s perspectives in non-pharmacological settings. Therefore, we aim to synthesize qualitative interview studies on the perspectives and experiences of children and adolescents participating in any type of pediatric clinical trial.

Methods

Eligibility criteria

We included qualitative interview studies that explored knowledge, attitude and experiences of children and adolescents regarding pediatric clinical trials. Studies describing their participants as children or adolescents were also included, but the mean or median age must not exceed 18 years old. We did not restrict our search to any particular setting, social status, ethnic background, or country. We excluded studies that solely investigated the feasibility or acceptability of a specific clinical intervention (e.g., usability of a device) without addressing the broader experience of trial participation, as the focus of such findings is limited to the specific therapeutic product rather than the research process itself. Mixed method research, where it was not possible to extract qualitative data separately from quantitative data, was also excluded. Finally, to ensure a comprehensive synthesis of available perspectives, no studies were excluded based on their methodological quality assessment results.

Information sources and search strategy

We searched PubMed, Embase, APA PsycInfo, APA PsycArticles, CINAHL, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), WanFang, and Chinese Scientific Journal Database (VIP) from their inception to 1 January 2026, without date or language restrictions. The details of search strategy can be found in eTable 1–8 in the Supplement. Additional studies were identified by searching reference lists of included studies and existing systematic reviews.

Selection of studies

After duplicate records were removed, four review authors worked in pairs to independently screen the title and abstract of each citation against the original inclusion and exclusion criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion. The same process was adopted for full-text screening. When the same study with the same sample and methods was presented in different reports, we merged the reports so that each study (rather than each report) was the unit of analysis.

Data extraction

After identifying eligible studies, review authors extracted the data from each of the included studies using standardized data extraction forms with detailed instructions. These forms were pilot-tested on the first ten included studies and refined to ensure consistency and comprehensiveness before being applied to the full dataset. Extraction of data was managed with Microsoft Office Excel using two separate data extraction tables. The first table recorded the methodological characteristics, including study ID, title, authors, publication year, research aims, participant characteristics, data collection methods, and data analysis methods. The second table focused on the qualitative results, extracting all content related to children’s understanding, attitudes, and experiences, including both the participants’ original quotes and the authors’ interpretations. Through the pilot testing, a initial classification framework was established to guide the extraction, allowing us to adjust and modify the forms as needed. Importantly, the development of this framework was a data-driven process, directly informed by the content emerging from the piloted studies. During this step, only the content associated with the review question was extracted and no coding was involved. We applied a specific strategy for studies involving participants over the age of 18. If qualitative findings for adolescents and young adults were inseparable, we extracted the combined data but subsequently downgraded the confidence using the GRADE-CERQual approach to account for indirectness (see Assessment of methodological limitations in primary studies for further details). Data explicitly identified as coming from young adults were excluded. Furthermore, given that the definition of adulthood varies by country, we included data if the original study referred to participants as ‘adolescents’ and they were considered minors in the specific study context.

Assessment of methodological limitations in primary studies

The quality of included studies was assessed by paired reviewers using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) Qualitative Checklist tool (http://www.casp-uk.net), which contained the following ten domains: aims, methodology, design, recruitment, data collection, reflexivity, ethical considerations, data analysis, findings, and research contribution. Once both had completed their assessment, comparison of the two appraisals was made. We resolved disagreements by discussion or, when required, by involving a third review author. The methodological assessment was considered when assessing the confidence of the review findings, rather than determining the inclusion of studies. Specifically, the CASP assessment results informed the ‘Methodological limitations’ component of the GRADE-CERQual approach. While all eligible studies contributed to the thematic synthesis, findings heavily reliant on studies with significant methodological limitations were graded with lower confidence (i.e., downgraded from High to Moderate, Low, or Very Low) (for detailed procedures, please refer to Sect. Assessment of confidence in the review findings).

Synthesis method

A thematic synthesis approach was used to analyze and synthesize the qualitative data, which was designed to enhance the transparency of synthesizing process and facilitated the construction of new analytical themes [30]. This qualitative evidence synthesis sought to inductively synthesize the perspectives and experiences of children participating in trials and provide suggestions to practitioners. Guided by the seven (RETREAT) criteria (Review question, Epistemology, Time, Resources, Expertise, Audience, and Type of data) [31], we selected the Thematic Synthesis method developed by Thomas and Harden [30]. We prioritized the Audience and Type of Data criteria in this decision: specifically, this method is well-suited for synthesizing a large volume of studies to produce practical recommendations for healthcare professionals, rather than purely conceptual constructs often associated with other interpretive methods [31]. Following Thomas and Harden [30], our synthesis was conducted in three overlapping stages: (1) the free line-by-line coding of the findings of primary studies; (2) the organization of these ‘free codes’ into related areas to construct ‘descriptive themes’ and subthemes; and (3) the development of ‘analytical themes’.

Stage 1: Line-by-line coding. Two reviewers independently coded each line of text, including authors’ interpretations and participants’ accounts. We used Microsoft Office Excel to arrange the codes and linked each code with the original texts and the Reference number. An inductive approach was used with the set of codes expanded as additional studies were added. There was at least one code assigned to statements related to understanding, perspectives or experiences, and some statements were assigned multiple codes.

Stage 2: Generation of descriptive themes and subthemes. Codes were systematically applied to the text units. Through an iterative process of comparison, we grouped relevant codes into a hierarchical tree structure to form descriptive themes. To capture the nuances within the data, we organized the codes into multiple layers. Consistent with Thomas and Harden’s description of creating a “tree structure with several layers” [30], we designated the broader, upper-level categories as ‘descriptive themes’ and the more specific, underlying layers as ‘subthemes’. This involved hand-sorting, where codes were organized to capture the meaning of the initial data. The review team constantly cross-checked the context of each code against the primary qualitative findings to ensure the descriptive themes and subthemes remained close to the original data.

Stage 3: Generation of analytical themes (Analytic Dimensions). This stage involved generating analytical themes from descriptive themes by adding interpretation. Review authors independently ‘went beyond’ the original studies to create new constructs and explanations that inferred barriers and facilitators to participation. All codes, subthemes, descriptive themes, analytical themes, and interpretations were discussed and refined by review team.

Assessment of confidence in the review findings

To facilitate the assessment of confidence in the evidence using the GRADE-CERQual (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation-Confidence in the Evidence from Reviews of Qualitative Research) approach [32], we distilled the synthesized analytical themes into specific ‘Review Findings’. A review finding was defined as an analytic output (summary statement) based on data from primary studies that describes a specific aspect of the phenomenon of interest [33]. Confidence in the evidence was defined as an assessment of the extent to which a review finding is a reasonable representation of the phenomenon of interest [33]. Three review authors independently assessed the confidence of each review finding by considering four components: (1) methodological limitations of the individual qualitative studies contributing to the finding, (2) coherence of the finding, (3) adequacy of data supporting the finding, and (4) relevance of the data to the review question [32]. The overall confidence was assessed at four levels: “high”, “moderate”, “low”, or “very low”. Following the CERQual approach, all review findings started as high confidence by default and were graded down if there were important concerns regarding any of the four components [33]. Final judgement about the overall confidence was reached through consensus among the review authors.

Results

This qualitative evidence synthesis is reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 and Enhancing Transparency in Reporting the synthesis of Qualitative research (ENTREQ) statement guidelines (eTable 9 in the Supplement) [34, 35].

Study characteristics and quality assessment

In total, 56 studies (62 articles) involving 2,101 children and adolescents were included after screening (Fig. 1). Over half of the studies (34/56) were published between 2016 and 2026. Sample size varied from 2 to 256 and the age range was 6–23 years, mostly from the United States (19 studies) and the United Kingdom (13 studies). Thirty-eight studies were based on real clinical trials, while eighteen studies were based on hypothetical trials. The most frequently involved diseases were communicable diseases (16 studies) and cancer (13 studies). The objectives of included studies were exploring the influencing factors (24 studies), perspectives (24 studies), experiences (20 studies) and understanding (12 studies) of pediatric clinical trials. Characteristics of included studies are presented in eTable 10 in the Supplement. No study was excluded on the basis of quality. According to the CASP tool, 42 studies had minor methodological limitations, 8 studies had minor to moderate limitations, and 6 had moderate limitations (eTable 11 in the Supplement).

Analytic dimension 1: negotiating agency within information asymmetry

This dimension synthesizes the tension between children’s developing capacity and their desire for control. The core logic here is that limited understanding (asymmetry) does not diminish the child’s right to agency; rather, it highlights the need for tailored communication to bridge the gap between “not knowing” and “deciding.”

Theme 1: Children’s understanding of trial components

Subtheme 1.1: providing information about trials

Review finding 1

Children prefer to have explanations in a straightforward manner, utilizing face-to-face interactions and visual methods. More detailed information and enough time can help them to consider options. Using visual methods and the second explanation improved children’s understanding. Inadequate explanations and a fear of asking questions hindered understanding.

Evidence: Children and adolescents wanted information delivered efficiently by using age-appropriate information [24, 36–39], personal face-to-face interactions [24, 38, 40], and an individualized approach tailored to their specific age and understanding [36], which would reduce anxiety [39]. Children and adolescents needed detailed information [36, 39, 41–45] and adequate time [15, 36, 42] to consider the options and be emotionally prepared for decision-making. There were some children younger than 9 years old who preferred their parents to provide information or explanations, while others wanted medical staff to do so [38]. It was found that adolescents preferred hospital-led trials over commercially funded trials [45], and they wanted a website to contact or hear from other child participants [43, 45]. Understanding was enhanced by visual demonstrations [41, 46], repeated explanations [47], and self-learning [48]. The lack of information/explanations and the overload of technical information limited children’s involvement and contributed to “bad memory” [15, 36, 39, 40, 46, 49].

Review finding 2

Participation in trials is an optional process requiring the child’s assent or consent. They must understand they are free to refuse or withdraw at any time. Medical research is different from regular medical care, which tests effectiveness and safety. Furthermore, they accept research involving emergency treatment without prior consent.

Evidence: Knowing they were free to leave was a relief for children and adolescents [49–52], which increased acceptance of the trial [24, 45, 53]. Adolescents understood the purpose of the trial from clear verbal explanations rather than written information [24, 52].

Subtheme 1.2: No memory or limited understandings of trial aim or intervention

Review finding 3

Children’s comprehension of trial aims manifested on a continuum. While some clearly distinguished research from care, others held misconceptions (e.g., therapeutic misconception)—a variation that reflects the complexity of the information and mirrors the diverse understanding often found in adult populations.

Evidence: It was shown that children younger than 10 years old might be less articulate about their understanding and expressed difficulties in making decision independently [22, 38]. Children and adolescents were aware that participating in medical research was different from seeing a regular doctor [24, 51, 54]. However, a few children and adolescents might misunderstand the research aim as individualized treatment [24, 51, 55], medical tests [56], surveys [52], forced social services [57], and did not realize that the primary purpose of clinical research is to produce generalizable knowledge [38, 41, 51]. Some didn’t remember anything and were unaware they had participated in research at all [58].

Subtheme 1.3: Acceptance or lack understanding of random assignment

Review finding 4

Conceptualizing randomization proved challenging. While the concept was accepted as “fair” by many, detailed understanding varied significantly, suggesting that grasping such abstract concepts is a common hurdle for participants regardless of age, rather than a specific deficit of childhood.

Evidence: Children, to some extent, understood the concept of randomization and placebo [51, 57, 59, 60] and its positive aspects [49, 59, 61]. Children’s perceptions of randomization and placebo ranged from adequate understanding to clear misunderstanding [51, 59, 60]. Some stuck with the literal meaning of randomization, such as ‘flip of a coin’ [60], and the misconception that they or professionals would choose the treatment based on their personal conditions or resources [41, 57, 59]. A few were unable to explain or misunderstood the use of placebo [41, 51, 60].

For instance, some adolescents believed that any study drug provided in a trial should contain active ingredients to ensure protection, finding the concept of a placebo difficult to accept [62].

When assigned to less favorable arm, children and adolescents often felt obligated to continue or gradually discovered the positive effects [53, 57, 63].

Subtheme 1.4: Personal factors that affect understanding

Review finding 5

The acquisition of new knowledge, accumulated experience and personal maturation contribute to improved comprehension.

Evidence: As children gained more experiences, their confidence and understanding of trials increased [15, 22, 47]. In low-income settings, specific life experiences—such as being a head of a household or being married at a young age—were considered more reliable indicators of a child’s maturity and capacity to understand research than age alone [64]. In rural Kenya, a ‘generational shift’ was observed where adolescents often perceived themselves as having better understanding of science and research than their parents due to higher education levels and exposure to technology [65].

Theme 2: Children’s requests for personal rights during and after trial participation

Subtheme 2.1: A demand for respect in the decision-making process

Review finding 6

In contrast to their variable understanding, children expressed a consistent and universal desire to be treated as responsible individuals. Regardless of their comprehension levels, they prioritized respectful communication and demanded active involvement in the decision-making process.

Evidence: Children and adolescents expressed their desire to be involved in the decision-making process [15, 38, 66], to be treated like responsible persons [15, 36, 41, 49, 67] and to react positively when they felt a sense of control [15, 38]. Many children and adolescents thought it was their responsibility to make such decisions [22, 24, 38, 49, 52, 61, 65], and they had the final say [22, 38, 49, 68, 69]. Even when acknowledging parental authority, adolescents actively employed strategies to exert agency, such as reminding parents to attend meetings or enlisting the support of other family members (e.g., aunts) to influence the decision [70].

Subtheme 2.2: Personal factors affecting involvement

Review finding 7

Children’s physical or mental condition may restrict their involvement. Neglecting their feelings may result in nonchalance, refusal, and avoidance.

Evidence: A few adolescents did not want to be involved because they disliked being reminded of having the condition, which set them apart from others [15]. The new or bad diagnosis overwhelmed children and adolescents [22, 24, 40, 46, 48, 68, 71]. Due to the long wait for the diagnosis and the need for quick decision-making, children and adolescents were highly anxious and stressed [46, 48, 68]. Some children and adolescents were not involved in Informed Consent Process, often due to physical pain or severe illness [22, 48, 68, 72]. In some instances, children obeyed parents’ decisions [68] and parents limited or subtly shaped their engagement or added more overt persuasion, which might result in children’s frustration and refusal [15, 24, 73].

Subtheme 2.3: Self-consent and privacy protection

Review finding 8

Some adolescents need self-consent and feel capable of doing so. Their sensitive information should be protected from parents.

Evidence: Adolescent participants in Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) prevention trials mentioned self-consent most often [37, 49, 61]. The presence of parents might inhibit children and adolescents from sharing sensitive information [15, 52]. Specifically, in families with complex dynamics (e.g., step-parents), children preferred to be approached separately to prevent sensitive health information (such as HIV status) from being spread as rumors [64]. It might be better to give them the choice whether or not to be separate from parents [24] or let them make the decision of participation before obtaining parents’ permission [41]. Children and adolescents would only inform their parents if they were supportive, even if not requiring parental permission [74]. Some adolescents asserted strong independence, stating they would participate in research they deemed important even if it meant hiding it from unsupportive parents [65]. Moreover, children and adolescents wanted privacy regarding their test results [37]. Medical staff should respect religious beliefs when obtaining parents’ consent, according to an adolescent’s suggestion [75].

Subtheme 2.4: Expectations after participation

Review finding 9

Children desire access to trial results or lay summaries, as well as gestures of appreciation such as thank-you notes. Some also want information about post-trial medication provision or referrals for continuous access.

Evidence: Five studies illustrated that learning about future impact of the trials [76, 77] and receiving thank you notes [76] might reflect their altruistic motivation. The result of the trials [38, 43, 76], information and referrals for post-trial medication for continuous access [49] were important to children and adolescents.

Analytic dimension 2: relational autonomy in the child-parent-professional triad

Moving beyond individual decision-making, this dimension situates the child within a social web, arguing that a child’s autonomy is inherently relational. Parents and professionals act as essential scaffolds who can either facilitate “supported decision-making” or, conversely, exert “overcontrol” that silences the child’s voice. This highlights that the quality of the child-parent-professional relationship is a critical determinant of ethical participation.

Theme 3: the influence of other people on trial participation

Subtheme 3.1: parental support or overcontrol

Review finding 10

Children rely on their parents for guidance in processing information and weighing risks and benefits. However, in some cases, parents may become overly controlling and dominate the decision-making process.

Evidence: Children and adolescents wanted to make the decision together with their parents [24, 38, 41, 42, 65, 69], and valued their parents’ support during Informed Consent Process [15, 24, 41, 42, 49, 69, 73, 78, 79] and in trials [37, 69, 74]. Children who were 7y or without research experience might be content with their parents’ decision [38, 79], while some would just accept the undesirable decision made by their parents [24, 38]. Children and adolescents appreciated parents’ help and support in the decision-making [24, 48, 56, 69, 73], as many of them made major decisions in collaboration with their parents [22, 48, 65, 66, 68, 80]. Parents usually served as messengers between researchers and children [69] and mediated information about clinical trials and the process of eliciting children’s opinions [24, 47, 73]. Some children in low-income settings preferred to be approached first so they could understand the study and explain it to their parents, fearing that parents might otherwise misunderstand or reject the study due to lack of education [64]. Conversely, others felt a joint approach prevented parents from discouraging participation based on misconceptions [64]. There were several children and adolescents who felt relieved to deliver much of their work to parents [24, 40, 68, 71, 73]. In some cases, they obeyed parents’ decisions [68] but parents limited or subtly shaped their engagement or added more overt persuasion, which might result in children’s frustration and refusal [15, 24, 73]. For married adolescents in certain cultural contexts, this authority extended to husbands and in-laws, whose permission was deemed essential for trial participation, overriding the adolescent’s own wishes [62]. Additionally, financial dependence was highlighted as a critical factor enforcing obedience; children felt they had to follow their parents’ refusal to avoid losing financial support, even if they wished to participate [64]. In some cultural contexts, adolescents accepted parental final authority not just out of obedience, but due to a sense of duty, fearing that going against parents’ wishes could lead to being cursed [70].

Subtheme 3.2: Support or help from peers

Review finding 11

Children value the opinions of others. They also seek to enhance their understanding by searching the Internet or listening to peer discussions.

Evidence: Children and adolescents discussed participation with peers, other child participants or other adults outside of the family for advice [40, 49, 61], while others simply kept their friends informed [48]. Furthermore, the visible participation of peers created a sense of “safety in numbers,” which served as a powerful motivator to overcome perceived risks and barriers to participation [62].

Subtheme 3.3: Support or expectation of professionals

Review finding 12

Children view professionals as trustworthy sources of expertise regarding their condition and appreciate their support. However, some children find it difficult to refuse participation due to a fear of disappointing professionals.

Evidence: There was a desire for influential and trustworthy doctors who provide expertise based on the family’s goals and values [15, 36, 48, 61, 66, 78, 79]. But some might not be interested in being involved in Informed Consent Process and just went along with the doctors [15]. Children and adolescents also appreciated that professionals treated them like “big persons” [24, 48], addressed their concerns, translated medical terms into lay language, made recommendations [24, 36, 46], helped them become involved and developed close relationships with each other [24, 48]. They reported positive experiences with research staff and research assistants [24, 42, 52, 53, 56, 59, 77, 81, 82]. A researcher helped a child to communicate with her parents to ensure that her wish to withdraw was respected [42].

Analytic dimension 3: the dynamic balance of burden and altruism

This dimension captures the decision-making process as a continuous weighing of competing factors. It links potential benefits-particularly altruism-against fears regarding medical risks, and connects these pre-trial expectations with the actual trial participation experience. This shows how the reality of physical discomfort or psychological satisfaction continually reshapes the child’s willingness to stay in the trial.

Theme 4: potential benefits for children participating in trials

Subtheme 4.1: Benefit to themselves

Review finding 13

It is believed that the intervention being tested has a better preventive or therapeutic effect than the comparison or placebo.

Review finding 14

Participation offers the opportunity to gain greater understanding and knowledge regarding health issues. Some children would like to participate to fulfill their curiosity, while others may have no interest or desire to participate in trials.

Review finding 15

Children may be motivated by the prospect of improved or expedited access to medical care, or by monetary incentives.

Evidence: Personal benefits included possible better treatment [22, 24, 38, 43, 51, 53, 54, 68, 71, 83], knowing their health status through testing [62], prevention from HIV [49, 61], attentive care [43, 45, 59, 84], extra resources [22, 56, 61, 74, 77], monetary compensation [22, 61, 69, 74, 81, 85], learning more about the illness/health knowledge [43, 49, 58, 61, 71, 74, 78, 80, 81], restoring self-esteem [43]. Some adolescents specifically viewed participation as an opportunity to understand the drivers of their vulnerability to diseases like HIV [62]. Some children and adolescents were hesitant to admit or quite objected to it [56, 59], while others honestly spoke of money as an important motivator [43, 56, 59, 77, 81].

Subtheme 4.2: For the benefit of others

Review finding 16

When considering participation, children usually mention helping other children, contributing to medical development, and improving health care services.

Evidence: The most common reason for participating in trials was helping other children and adolescents or future patients (altruistic motives) [22, 24, 36, 38, 43, 45, 46, 49, 51–56, 58, 59, 61, 63, 67, 68, 71, 74, 77, 78, 80, 83, 85–88]. They also wanted to make sure their efforts could contribute to medical development [22, 24, 38, 41, 43, 44, 46, 47, 50, 54, 55, 59, 61, 74, 77, 78, 83–87, 89, 90], or share experiences with others suffering from the same condition [80]. There were some children who mentioned personal benefits and less burden along with altruism [24, 41, 43, 45, 46, 49, 52, 56, 63, 77, 86, 88], while others felt a sense of moral duty to give back [43, 78], even without any compensation or benefits for themselves [41, 56, 61, 77, 80, 86]. Children and adolescents thought their contribution in the prevention trials could benefit the society in terms of increasing the public awareness, reducing stigma, and eventually reducing the prevalence of HIV/STI (Sexually Transmitted Infection) [61, 74, 86] and early pregnancy [91]. In contrast, some adolescents declined participation due to not feeling ill or affected by their disease [80]. Some children simply did not feel like participating [55].

Theme 5: Potential concerns of children as trial participants

Subtheme 5.1: Medical risks, discomfort, or inconvenience

Review Finding 17

Treatments that are ineffective or have unknown side effects. Assignment to an unfavorable or placebo group can lead to feelings of being treated like ‘guinea pigs’. Furthermore, some believe monetary incentives may obscure potential risks.

Review finding 18

Children are often concerned about painful or uncomfortable procedures, especially needle procedures. The strict time schedule is also cited as an inconvenience.

Evidence: Concerns about research might lead to children and adolescents refusing participation or withdrawing from trials [49, 65, 91]. Some children found it difficult to refuse participation because they worried about disappointing others [24, 49], not respecting professionals [79] or facing penalties for dropping out [91]. They were worried about [40, 49, 54, 57, 59, 72] or passively accepted [57, 72] the fact they were assigned to a less favored group, making them feel like “guinea pigs” [42, 49, 54, 75]. Some children preferred having the choice or trying all options rather than randomization [59]. Unknown/unrevealed side effects or adverse effects were the common concern of risk [24, 41, 44, 45, 49, 55, 61, 66, 74, 78, 85, 86, 88], including worries about whether they would receive medical care if the study products caused harm later on [62], which also served as a barrier to participation [55, 66, 74, 88, 89]. Children and adolescents were concerned about how common the side effects were and whether they could tolerate them [49, 51, 90]. They initially evaluated the magnitude of the procedure risks and only then attended to probability [90]. Increasing medications, involving “new medications” [24], no more effective than standard treatment [66, 68], intervention being ineffective [49, 83, 85], being assigned to placebo group [74], painful needle sticks [39, 45, 51, 56, 61, 69, 78], strict time schedules and more time commitments [24, 49, 51, 61, 68, 69, 80] were also concerned. The balance or choices made by children and adolescents have to do with the types of diseases and treatment, such as “nothing compared to being infected by HIV” [49], “less pain and side effects outweighed the risk of leukemia treatment failure” [66]. Children who declined participation were concerned about potential risks associated with trials, or thought the experimental intervention was no better than conventional treatment [55, 66], or exaggerated fears that a harmful vaccine could ‘wipe out’ a generation [65]. The burdens, such as extra time investment, inconvenience of sitting still and missing school were also the reasons for children not to participate in trials [55, 58, 71].

Subtheme 5.2: Psychological or relationship damage

Review finding 19

Psychological stress may negatively affect children’s relationship with others when they think about positive HIV test results. Participants expressed concern regarding the disclosure of private information, fearing embarrassment or stigma within their families or communities.

Evidence: In case of infectious diseases or diseases associated with misbehaviors, such as HIV/STI or alcoholic abuse etc., confidentiality risks might also raise social and personal relationship concerns, including fears of stigma, embarrassment, familial punishment, or misunderstandings caused by positive test results [44, 49, 52, 61, 89]. Adolescents and their families also had to navigate disturbing community rumors, such as fears that vaccines were linked to infertility, terrorism, or “Freemasonry,” which required them to place significant trust in healthcare professionals to overcome [70].

In trials involving emerging technologies (e.g., AI), concerns extended beyond physical side effects to data security, with participants fearing that their digital health information could be hacked, leaked, or sold for profit [67].

Theme 6: trial participation experience

Subtheme 6.1: psychological satisfaction or benefit

Review finding 20

Through participation, children gained knowledge, integrate into peer groups, develop new relationships, and find enjoyment in the process.

Review finding 21

Through participation, children gained extra attention from professionals, vouchers, or payments, or the enjoyment of temporary absence from school.

Evidence: Children and adolescents usually felt that participation increased their knowledge, awareness of healthcare issues, sense of personal enrichment and self-management skills [43, 48, 52, 56, 63, 81, 82]. Some children and adolescents found the whole experience [52, 56, 82] or medical tests [39, 43, 55, 77] fun and interesting. Children and adolescents with long-term conditions highly wanted to maintain as normal lives as possible [15, 80], so participation was an opportunity for them to meet other child patients, which made them feel more normal [15, 59, 63, 80], to connect with fellow community members, to meet new people and to develop new relationships [53, 56]. Through participation, children could provide comfort for future patients by sharing similar experiences [46].

Subtheme 6.2: Emotional or physical discomfort

Review finding 22

For some children, trial-related social activities may generate negative emotions, such as social anxiety. Many children felt embarrassed by some physical examinations or requirements and frustrated by the inconveniences of trial requirements, such as transportation difficulties and strict schedules of study visits.

Review finding 23

Invasive procedures and painful examinations made children anxious. While some may adapt to these, others may refuse or quit participation because of the suffering.

Evidence: Sometimes, participation brought children and adolescents challenges in terms of social activities, since they might need to meet research staff [24, 39, 59] or fellows [80]. They valued the continuity of relationships with research staff and felt uncomfortable with seeing different people each time [24, 51, 59]. It was awkward for adolescents with chronic illness to talk about their condition in front of others [78, 80]. Children and adolescents often felt embarrassed when undergoing physical examinations [39, 86], abstinence [86] and non-adherence [49]. It was painful to go through some tests [39] and research procedures [39, 79]. Nevertheless, some of them got used to needles and overcame the fear of needles/injections [56, 90]. There were some requirements that were boring [39, 53, 58, 59] as well as a lack of distraction during long research procedures and waiting periods [39]. A few discomforts can be attributed to study requirements, including the inconvenience of sitting still [55] during MRI scans [58], the need to fast before certain procedures [39], and additional burden created by the intervention [40]. Children described the routine burdens, such as taking study medication every day, as “much harder than we thought,” highlighting a gap between anticipated and actual participation fatigue [71].

Conceptual model of pediatric trial participation

A conceptual model (Fig. 2) was developed based on qualitative findings to illustrate the entire clinical trial participation process for children and adolescents. This model integrates the identified themes to visualize the journey from recruitment to trial closure, highlighting the interplay between a child’s understanding, their need for agency, and the support systems required. To uncover the underlying phenomena, the participation process is conceptualized as three progressive phases structured around three key analytic dimensions:ⅰ) Foundation: Negotiating Agency within Information Asymmetry (Theme 1 & 2). The model places "Children’s Requests for Personal Rights" as the constant foundation underpinning the entire trajectory. It resolves the tension between children's variable understanding (Theme 1) and their universal demand for respect (Theme 2). This reflects 'Ethical Symmetry', asserting that a child's right to respect, privacy, and involvement is constant and does not depend on their level of comprehension. The foundation emphasizes that agency relies on meeting expectations for Involvement, Respect, Privacy, and Autonomy, regardless of the child’s age.ⅱ) Context: Relational Autonomy in the Child-Parent-Professional Triad (Theme 3). The decision-making process is embedded in the "Child-Parent-Professional Triad". The model highlights that support from parents and professionals serves as a facilitator when it enables supported decision-making, but becomes a barrier when it turns into overcontrol. This context is critical during Phase 1 (Understanding) and continues to influence the child's entry into the research environment. ⅲ) Process: Dynamic Balance Journey (The Dynamic Balance of Burden and Altruism) (Theme 1, 4, 5, & 6). Participation is depicted not as a one-time event but as a continuous weighing of Altruism/Benefits against Burdens/Risks. Phase 1 (Understanding) initiates the process, where comprehension is recognized as a spectrum shaped by specific "Barriers" (e.g., information overload) and "Facilitators" (e.g., age-appropriate visual tools). The journey then moves to Phase 2, where children weigh specific "Benefits" (Theme 4, e.g., altruism, medical benefit) against "Risks" and concerns (Theme 5). It extends into Phase 3 (Participation Experiences), where the outcome—categorized into "Positive" (e.g., psychological satisfaction) and "Negative" (e.g., emotional or physical discomfort) experiences—continuously reshapes the dynamic balance.

Discussion

Summary of main findings

Based on the included 56 qualitative studies, this synthesis highlights that pediatric trials were generally acceptable to children and adolescents. To move beyond descriptive themes, our analysis identified three overarching analytic dimensions characterizing the participation process: ⅰ) Negotiating Agency within Information Asymmetry: Similar to adult populations, children’s comprehension of trial concepts varied significantly; however, this variability did not diminish their desire for agency. Children consistently expressed a strong desire for their privacy to be protected and to be respected as responsible agents (Themes 1 & 2). ⅱ) Relational Autonomy in the Child-Parent-Professional Triad: Children’s participation was deeply embedded in family dynamics, revealing a distinct need for age-appropriate support from family and professionals, while also highlighting the tension between parental support and control (Theme 3). ⅲ) The Dynamic Balance of Burden and Altruism: Altruism was a predominant motivation, yet the actual trial experiences were a mix of positive aspects and negative burdens (physical discomfort, psychological stress). Decision-making was thus a continuous weighing of these benefits against prevalent concerns regarding medical risks and invasive procedures (Themes 4, 5, & 6).

Interpretation and significance of the conceptual model

The proposed Conceptual Model of Pediatric Trial Participation (Fig. 2) serves as a holistic framework to interpret the complex interplay of factors identified in our review. Significantly, this model shifts the analytical lens from a static focus on “recruitment rates” to a dynamic view of participation as a continuous “journey”. This perspective aligns with the paradigm shift advocated in childhood sociology—moving from research on children to research with children [92], wherein children are valued as active participants who actively construct meaning from their trial experiences, rather than being viewed merely as passive subjects. Regarding the first dimension (Negotiating Agency), drawing on Christensen and Prout’s concept of ‘ethical symmetry’ [93], we suggest that the variability in children’s understanding identified in our review should not be viewed as a lack of competence, but rather as a reflection of the diverse social reality found in any group of research participants, including adults. Just as adults may struggle with complex concepts like randomization yet retain their right to consent, our model highlights that children’s rights to respect and information should be symmetrical to those of adults, regardless of their developmental stage. Consequently, by recognizing children as competent social actors rather than passive subjects, our model interprets the child’s decision-making not as a one-time event (signing a form) but as an ongoing process of “weighing risks and benefits” that evolves with their changing understanding and experiences.

Significantly, the desire for agency identified in our review echoes the broader discourse on children’s participation in other public spheres beyond healthcare, such as education and legal systems [94, 95]. This aligns with Article 12 of the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child (UNCRC), which mandates that children have the right to form and express views in all matters affecting them. Scholars in childhood sociology argue that limiting children’s participation in schools or communities often stems from adult-centric underestimations of their competence—a pattern we clearly observed in the clinical trial context [96, 97]. By situating our findings within this wider sociological framework, it becomes clear that the “relational autonomy” we advocate for is not a novel medical concept but a necessary extension of children’s evolving citizenship and social rights [98].

By placing “Children’s Requests for Personal Rights” as the foundational layer, the model has critical applications for research ethics: it suggests that successful and ethical engagement requires supporting a child’s agency and privacy throughout the entire trial life-cycle, rather than just seeking assent at the beginning. This framework can guide practitioners to design stage-specific interventions—such as educational tools for the “Understanding” phase or peer-support platforms for the “Participation” phase—to mitigate drop-outs and enhance the child’s experience.

Regarding the second dimension (Relational Autonomy), our findings challenge perceptions of children as merely dependent. Instead, the “Child-Parent-Professional Triad” in our model suggests that researchers should develop practices to assess the quality of this relationship. Ethical practice requires moving away from the assumptions of absolute adult authority. For parents, this means shifting from asserting “rights over the child’s body” and towards a framework where parents accept “responsibilities for a child’s care” by facilitating supported decision-making. For professionals, it means leveraging trust to empower the child rather than unintentionally coercing them through medical authority. As illustrated in our model, adult involvement acts as a facilitator when it provides support and trust, but becomes a critical barrier when it manifests as parental overcontrol or professional exclusion that silences the child.

Regarding the third dimension (Dynamic Balance), our findings regarding the complexity of decision-making align with and extend prior research. Consistent with Hunfeld et al. (2012) [28] and Tromp et al. (2016) [29], we identified altruism and burden as key motivating and discouraging factors. However, our analysis reveals a critical divergence: while previous reviews largely framed these as static factors influencing recruitment, our qualitative synthesis highlights that these motivations are fluid and deeply intertwined with the child’s experience of agency. For instance, “burden” is not just about physical pain (as noted in Hunfeld et al. [28]) but also about the lack of respect and privacy during the process. Furthermore, unlike previous Cochrane reviews which focused on recruitment strategies and decision aids [25–27], our results emphasize the experiential gap in current protocols. We found that children’s capacity to consent is often treated as a legal hurdle rather than a dynamic engagement process. This variability in understanding suggests that standard consent procedures are often insufficient, supporting a shift towards the interactive and supportive decision-making models illustrated in our conceptual framework.

Implications for clinical practice

Dimension 1: facilitating agency via ethical symmetry

Our synthesis highlighted that although children’s understanding of trial components varies—a trait they share with many adult participants—this vagueness does not justify paternalistic exclusion (Theme 1). Instead, clinicians should adopt a stance of ‘ethical symmetry’ [93]. While current legal frameworks differentiate between adult ‘consent’ and child ‘assent’—often treating the latter merely as a liability-limiting device [99]—ethical practice must actively bridge this gap. A child’s assent, or dissent, should carry the same ethical gravity as adult consent, regardless of their strict legal capacity [17]. Consequently, obtaining agreement must move beyond a binary test of competence or a bureaucratic checkbox [100] to become an ongoing educational process. Practitioners must respect the child’s constant right to straightforward information by utilizing age-appropriate tools, such as visual booklets [101] or interactive nonfiction narratives [102], prioritizing a genuine moral commitment to the child’s autonomy over mere legal compliance.

Dimension 2: supporting relational autonomy practitioners must navigate the delicate balance within the child-parent-professional triad

To foster ‘supported decision-making’ rather than ‘exclusion’ (Fig. 2), professionals must act as mediators who bridge the gap between parental protection and child agency. Even when not legally required, respecting a child’s assent is crucial, as we found that a lack of understanding or respect directly diminishes their willingness to comply. For adolescents, our identification of “parental overcontrol” as a barrier (Theme 3) suggests researchers should establish private platforms where young people can express opinions independent of parental influence, ensuring participation is truly voluntary. Additionally, our findings also revealed that adolescents in sensitive trials (e.g., HIV/STDs) face unique family pressures. In these contexts, offering family psychological support could be beneficial to address the strained relationships. Adolescents should be encouraged to communicate with parents to seek support, but professionals must ensure that their privacy and autonomy remain paramount. Recent research on pediatric patient-reported experience measures (PREMs) also confirms that children in general hospital settings prioritize shared decision-making and positive staff relationships [103, 104], suggesting these are universal needs rather than specific to clinical trials.

Dimension 3: managing the dynamic balance of burdens and benefits

To optimize the participation experience, trial procedures should be designed to minimize these specific physical (e.g., invasive procedures) and mental discomforts (e.g., embarrassment during exams), as these negative experiences heavily weigh against altruistic motivations. Furthermore, the “journey” does not end with the last dose. Our synthesis revealed that children often feel their altruistic contributions go unrecognized when a trial ends abruptly. To address this gap, researchers should provide feedback on the child’s contribution or the study outcomes. Consistent with the positive experiences noted in Theme 6, small, unexpected gifts or financial rewards at the end of the trial can foster a sense of appreciation [39, 43, 77], and reimbursing families for expenses is essential to mitigate the “frustrating burdens” identified by parents and children alike. However, the issue of incentives warrants a nuanced ethical approach. We acknowledge that direct payment to children is legally restricted or prohibited in many jurisdictions to prevent coercion [105]. Introducing financial transactions opens a complex debate regarding ‘children and work’. There is a risk that children may struggle to distinguish between participation as ‘compensation for time’ versus a transactional ‘job’ where compliance is purchased. Therefore, researchers must ensure that any token of appreciation is culturally appropriate and clearly framed as recognition of contribution rather than a wage for submission, thereby avoiding the commodification of the child’s participation [106].

Strengths and limitations

To our knowledge, this is the first qualitative evidence synthesis specifically focusing on experiences and perspectives of children and adolescents participating in pediatric clinical trials. The inclusion of studies from diverse geographical and economic contexts supports a comprehensive synthesis. This includes high-income countries (e.g., US, UK, the Netherlands, Canada, Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Germany) and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) such as China, South Africa, Brazil, and Zambia. The use of the rigorous CERQual approach to assess the confidence of review findings further adds credibility and transparency.

However, several limitations should be noted. Despite a broad search strategy, the underrepresentation of children from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) limits the global generalizability of our findings. Regarding linguistic variance, although we did not exclude non-English studies, all included papers were published in English. This introduces a potential bias regarding the “loss of nuance” or misinterpretation when children’s verbatim accounts in their native languages were translated into English for publication. Furthermore, inherent power dynamics in researching children may have influenced the findings. As highlighted by Gardner and Randall [107], the views expressed by children can be significantly shaped by the interview setting (e.g., home vs. hospital) and the presence of adults. In our review, although we sought to extract data on parental presence, many primary studies did not explicitly report whether parents were present during interviews or how their presence might have shaped the children’s responses. Consequently, it was difficult to fully disentangle the children’s independent voices from “negotiated conversations” influenced by parental authority. Additionally, confidence in certain findings was downgraded where data from adolescents and young adults were inseparable in the primary studies [36, 43, 45, 48, 53, 68]. Finally, the lack of public and patient involvement precluded the inclusion of their direct insights; we aim to integrate public and patient involvement in future reviews, particularly those examining parental perspectives.

Implications for future qualitative research

There is a need for more high-quality qualitative studies that investigate the deeper reasons why children and adolescents refuse participation or drop out of trials. Furthermore, given the underrepresentation of children and adolescents in Asia, Africa, and South America, future research should prioritize these regions. We recommend the use of semi-structured in-depth interviews and robust qualitative methods, such as thematic analysis (including framework analysis), to better understand the barriers and concerns specific to these populations.

Conclusions

This synthesis, based on generally high or moderate quality qualitative research evidence, provides a comprehensive overview of children and adolescents’ experiences and perspectives through trial participation. We conclude that variable comprehension—a trait shared with adult participants—should not preclude involvement. Instead, the key to respecting autonomy lies in prioritizing children’s agency, ensuring they are adequately involved in the decision-making process regardless of trial complexity. Rather than treating children’s agreement merely as legal ‘assent’ to mitigate liability, researchers must ensure children are afforded a genuine, ethically symmetrical role in the consent process. Listening to children’s opinions, addressing their concerns and discomforts can bring more positive experiences and increase their willingness for further research. Qualitative studies are needed to investigate trial participation experiences and reasons for nonparticipation and nonadherence. Additionally, qualitative studies are needed from Asia, Africa, and South America.
