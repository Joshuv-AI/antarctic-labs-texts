End-to-end CNN-based deep learning enhances breast lesion characterization using quantitative ultrasound (QUS) spectral parametric images
Authors: Laurentius Oscar Osapoetra, Amir Moslemi, Daniel Moore-Palhares, Schontal Halstead, David Alberico, Alexander Hwang, Lakshmanan Sannachi, Belinda Curpen, Gregory J. Czarnota
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
QUS spectral parametric imaging offers a fast and accurate method for breast lesion characterization. This study explored using deep CNNs to classify breast lesions from QUS spectral parametric images, aiming to enhance radiomics and conventional machine learning. Predictive models were developed using transfer learning with pre-trained CNNs to distinguish malignant from benign lesions. The dataset included 276 participants: 184 malignant (median age, 51 years [IQR: 27–81 years]) and 92 benign cases (median age, 46 years [IQR: 18–75 years]). QUS spectral parametric imaging was applied to the US RF data and resulted in 1764 images of QUS spectral (MBF, SS, and SI), along with QUS scattering parameters (ASD and AAC). The data were randomly split into 60% training, 20% validation, and 20% test sets, stratified by lesion subtype, and repeated five times. The number of convolutional blocks was optimized, and the final convolutional layer was fine-tuned. Models tested included ResNet, Inception-v3, Xception, and EfficientNet. Xception-41 achieved a recall of 86 ± 3%, specificity of 87 ± 5%, balanced accuracy of 87 ± 3%, and an AUC of 0.93 ± 0.02 on test sets. EfficientNetV2-M showed similar performance with a recall of 91 ± 1%, specificity of 81 ± 7%, balanced accuracy of 86 ± 3%, and an AUC of 0.92 ± 0.02. CNN models outperformed radiomics and conventional machine learning (p-values < 0.05). This study demonstrated the capability of end-to-end CNN-based models for the accurate characterization of breast masses from QUS spectral parametric images.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-15772-5.

Introduction

Breast cancer is the most frequently diagnosed cancer in women and ranks as the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths among them1. In 2020, the United States reported 239,612 new cases of female breast cancer, resulting in 42,273 fatalities2. The importance of early detection cannot be emphasized enough, as it significantly improves overall prognosis and enables more effective treatment options, particularly when the disease is caught at an early stage. Achieving this objective requires the use of accurate and reliable diagnostic methods.

Breast cancer screening generally begins with mammography and may include standard ultrasound imaging (B-mode imaging), dynamic contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (DCE-MRI) when necessary, and core-needle biopsy as needed. Mammography has limitations in dense breast tissue, potentially causing false negatives3,4. Biopsy, while definitive, is invasive and carries some risk. Additionally, the relatively low specificity of ultrasound B-mode3 and MRI can lead to unnecessary biopsies4. Although DCE-MRI improves specificity of breast cancer detection5, it is less accessible for rapid diagnosis. Consequently, there is a critical need for fast and accurate imaging techniques that could enable an “imaging-based” biopsy, enhancing early detection and patient triage.

Past studies have employed sonographic features of B-mode US images to characterize breast lesions6–8. However, B-mode US images, while valuable for visualizing anatomical structures, have limitations when used for diagnostic and prognostic purposes. Firstly, they do not provide quantitative information about tissues microstructures, potentially introducing inter-observer variability in diagnosis and prognosis based on qualitative information. Secondly, these images are system- and operator-dependent, leading to further variation in qualitative analysis.

Quantitative US (QUS) techniques improve on traditional B-mode imaging by determining various characteristics of tissue microstructures with diagnostic and prognostic significance. QUS Spectral parametric images depict the differing scattering properties in malignant versus benign breast lesions, offering a quantitative diagnostic tool. QUS Spectroscopy is used for characterizing tumour9–13, monitoring treatment14–17, and detecting tumour deposits in lymph nodes18. Additionally, QUS envelope statistics examines the spatial arrangement of scatterers through statistical analysis of US RF envelopes19,20. Moreover, US shear wave elasticity imaging (SWE) provides macro-elasticity parameters like shear modulus for characterization purposes, also based on RF-data13.

Previously, distinguishing malignant breast lesions from benign breast lesions relied on feature engineering of QUS features and conventional machine learning approaches. Sadeghi-Naini et al., and Osapoetra et al. developed multivariate models based on textural features from QUS spectral parametric images, drawing on the hypothesis that quantification of tumour heterogeneity resulted in robust diagnostic features9–11. Similarly, Destrempes et al.13 reported a multivariate model using QUS spectral, QUS echo envelope statistics, and QUS elasticity parameters, achieving an AUC of 0.74. Recent results corroborate these findings, demonstrating that a multivariate radiomics model using QUS spectral parametric images achieved an average test AUC of 0.84 ± 0.06, by leveraging first-order statistical and textural features. Whereas these feature sets from comprehensive textural analyses of QUS spectral parametric images are promising, the generalization performance of models based on conventional machine learning still requires improvement.

In this study, the transfer learning of pre-trained CNNs to enhance the differentiation between malignant and benign breast lesions using QUS spectral parametric images was investigated. The hypothesis for the work here was that CNNs could effectively learn valuable representations to improve diagnosis. To achieve that objective, convolutional sub-networks from pre-trained CNNs were generated and appended with a classification layer. Feature maps from these sub-networks were analyzed to identify those yielding optimal classification performances. The transfer learning approach fine-tuned a single last convolutional layer, along with its batch normalization (BN) layer, from the last block in the sub-networks. Experiments were conducted to compare various CNNs, including ResNet21, Inception22, Xception23, and EfficientNetV224 models, using five patient-level train-validation-test splits, reporting both average and peak generalization performance. The generalization performance of CNN-based models was compared with conventional machine learning and the baseline standard of the Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS) scores25. The BI-RADS scores were thresholded to facilitate binary classification into malignant and benign groups.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort

The institutional research ethics board (SUN-2094) approved this prospective study conducted in a single institution and registered with clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04050423). The study was conducted following good clinical practice according to the Declaration of Helsinki. We obtained written informed consent from all eligible candidates enrolled in this study. Eligible candidates had sonographically identified breast lesions that had previously been detected through clinical and mammographic examinations. Exclusion criteria included a prior diagnosis of breast cancer and lesions that were sonographically unidentified. Further quality control measures for RF data collection excluded participants with US image artifacts such as acoustic coupling issues and non-standard acquisition settings. Study accrual was carried out between September 2014 and October 2021. The study cohort comprised 276 participants with breast lesions, consisting of 92 benign and 184 malignant cases. Previous investigations relied on radiomics and conventional machine learning9–11, whereas this study significantly enhanced prior results with CNNs.

Benign and malignant classification was determined using clinical reports, including radiologist assessments of US B-mode and MR images, and histopathology for biopsied lesions. Benign lesions under surveillance, with no biopsy required, were confirmed benign through patient history over an average of ten years.

US radiofrequency data were collected using a clinical US system capable of acquiring RF signals. Specifically, a Sonix Touch US imaging system (Ultrasonix Medical Corp., Richmond, BC, Canada) equipped with a linear-array transducer (L14-5/60W) operating at a center frequency of 6.5 MHz and a bandwidth of 3–8 MHz was used. Supplementary Table 1 details the specific characteristics of the US imaging system used.

A radiologist experienced in breast US imaging conducted the scans and manually delineated the tumors along with their 5-mm tumor rims. Tumor margin extended from the tumor core up to a 5-mm maximum distance to the surrounding area (peri-tumoural region)10. Multiple US RF frames were collected across the three-dimensional tumor volumes, representing slices from the tumors.

QUS spectral parametric imaging

In addition to obtaining standard B-mode images of tumours, QUS spectral parametric images for both tumours and their 5-mm margins were calculated. These QUS spectral parameters were derived from the spectral analysis of windowed US RF data. The approach involved employing a 2-mm by 2-mm sliding window with a 94% window overlap in both the range and lateral directions. The window size was selected to encompass an adequate number of acoustic wavelengths for reliable spectrum estimation while preserving the resolution necessary for distinguishing areas of distinct microstructures26. This window was systematically moved across all points within the ROI to capture each pixel in the resulting parametric images.

To estimate the average spectrum from a block of US RF data, a Hanning gating function was applied along the range direction, followed by a fast Fourier transform to obtain the frequency domain representation of the RF signal. As a next step, the average power spectra across the lateral directions (columns in the RF block) was computed, incorporating attenuation correction to account for attenuation caused by the propagation through intervening tissue layers and the tumour. A predetermined attenuation coefficient was used for the skin attenuation, while the tumour attenuation coefficient was estimated using a spectral difference method27.

Spectral parameters were derived from the linear parametrization of the averaged power spectra, producing mid-band fit (MBF), spectral slope (SS), and 0-MHz spectral intercept (SI). MBF and SI indicate acoustic backscattering, whereas SS is associated with the effective scatterers size26. An acoustic scattering model using a spherical Gaussian form-factor was then applied to fit the backscatter coefficient (BSC) data, estimating scatterer properties such as average acoustic concentration (AAC) and average scatterers diameter (ASD)26.

The analysis covered all points within the ROI, resulting in parametric images that depicted the spatial distributions of QUS spectral parameters. Several parametric maps corresponding to slices across the three-dimensional tumor volume were generated from each patient.

Data preprocessing

The work began by cropping the parametric images, centering them on the tumor and its 5-mm margin. These cropped images were then resized to a uniform dimension of 160 × 160 (height x width).

Subsequently, the dataset was partitioned into training (60%), validation (20%), and test (20%) sets at the patient level, ensuring stratification by sub-characteristics. Benign sub-groups comprised of fibroadenoma (FA), simple cyst, complicated cyst, and other benign lesions, while malignant sub-groups included invasive ductal carcinoma (IDC), invasive lobular carcinoma (ILC), and other malignant lesions. This approach ensured balanced representation of these sub-characteristics across sets. Partitioning was repeated five times for statistical analysis.

Pixel statistics from the training set were used to normalize QUS spectral parameters to a 0–255 range. The scaling factor, derived from the training data, was uniformly applied to training, validation, and test sets. We acquired 1764 parametric images from 276 patients with breast lesions, structured as 1,764 × 160 × 160 × 5 (B × H × W × C), where B represents batch, H represents height, W represents width, and C represents channel.

Data augmentation was applied to reduce overfitting, using random horizontal and vertical translations (± 5% image size), horizontal flips, and zooms (up to 15%). The augmented training dataset was five times larger than the original, with transformations applied consistently across all five parametric maps per instance.

Transfer learning

Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are a type of neural network architectures that employ convolution operations in at least one of their layers, well-suited for learning from images28. CNNs are founded on several key principles to enhance machine learning systems: hierarchical feature learning, the locality principle, translation equivariance and parameter sharing, and invariance28–30.

Transfer learning utilized pre-trained CNNs as feature extractors, applying a global average pooling layer to the final CNN block’s output before feeding the feature vector to a classification head. The work then fine-tuned a single last convolutional layer from the last block and its associated batch normalization (BN) layer. Models evaluated included Inception-v322, Xception23, ResNets21, and EfficientNetV224, pre-trained on the ImageNet-1k dataset 31. Except for EfficientNetV2, these models have been previously applied to breast lesion characterization on B-mode US images32–34.

Pre-trained CNNs processed multi-dimensional QUS parametric images, were adapted to three channels via a one-pixel convolutional matching layer. The output was scaled by the number of parametric images (5) and passed through a ‘tanh’ activation to ensure intensities ranged from − 1 to 1, consistent with pre-trained CNN requirements. The matching layer nonlinearly combined the five parametric maps into three optimized channels, with parameters optimized during network training. Subsequently, the images were standardized using ImageNet’s RGB means (0.485, 0.456, and 0.406) and standard deviations (0.229, 0.224, and 0.225), with background pixels reset to zero post-normalization.

Recent investigations on transfer learning for medical image classification highlighted key insights: ImageNet performance does not predict downstream task performance, and shallower CNN architectures with less convolutional layers can outperform deeper CNNs with more convolutional layers35. Based on these, we investigated pre-trained CNN models of varying complexity by extracting sub-networks from full-depth models. Each model consisted of four major blocks, where each block contained a number of convolutional layers. The sub-networks outputted feature maps from blocks 1, 2, 3, or 4, respectively. These feature maps were processed using global average pooling to obtain features vectors, which were classified by a downstream classification head.

A single convolutional layer from the last block of the sub-networks was fine-tuned, along with its corresponding batch normalization (BN) layer. Depending on the network architecture, if the network consisted of multiple branches of convolutional layers at the same level, we fine-tuned the parameters of these convolutional layers as well. The classification performance of models based on the aforementioned CNN blocks was compared.

Model building and evaluation

The model here was trained on the training data and tested against the validation data to assess out-of-sample performance, determining the optimal point for early stopping. Subsequently, the model’s generalization performance was evaluated on an independent test set. Utilizing the five patient-level train-validation-test random partitions, five separate models were trained, and their generalization performance were evaluated on the respective hold-out test set. Classification performance was reported for both the validation and test sets.

We utilized classification metrics that include recall, specificity, accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision/positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), F1-Score, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). We performed statistical analysis on the classification metrics to demonstrate the strength of the proposed deep learning framework. A repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare test classification performance across CNN, conventional machine learning, and the clinical standard of BI-RADS. Subsequently, we performed post-hoc test using paired t-tests for between-group comparisons.

Network’ parameters were optimized using batched Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) with a momentum of 0.99 and enabled Nesterov momentum. The batch size was chosen to be 32. In the course of training, the learning rate was systematically reduced every ten epochs by multiplying the previous rate by 0.9, with a minimum learning rate fixed at 1e−6. Weights for trainable neurons were initialized using GlorotUniform36 for ‘tanh’ activations and HeUniform37 for ‘ReLU’ activations. To reduce generalization error, a weight decay regularization technique was applied.

Class imbalance was addressed by assigning different weights for the two classes based on their proportions in the training dataset, giving the minority class a larger weight. These weights were utilized in the calculation of the training loss function.

Deep learning experiments were conducted using the PyTorch API, within a customized Anaconda environment on a Linux system. We utilized a single NVIDIA RTX 3090 graphics card to run the experiment. A global mixed precision policy was implemented during the forward pass in the training and validation loop, utilizing half precision for significant computational speedup.

Training and inference were conducted using a custom training loop Python script. A binary cross-entropy loss function was used and the loss metric was closely tracked. Mini-batch stochastic gradient descent (SGD) was used as the optimization technique, with training samples randomly shuffled each epoch. Models were check-pointed when validation loss consistently decreased. Training was set for 1000 epochs, with early stopping applied if validation loss plateaued for 100 epochs.

The network was trained at the parametric map level and the final classification performance was reported at the patient level. Each multi-channel parametric image has an associated class label that corresponds to the respective patient label.

A systematic comparison of sub-networks consisted of blocks from the pre-trained CNNs was carried out. All models, except Inception-V3, utilized the output feature maps from the third block. In contrast, Inception-V3 utilized the output feature maps from the second block. The classification performance of the sub-networks on the five randomly stratified train-validation-test partitions was compared and the sub-networks that achieved the highest averaged balanced accuracy on the validation sets were reported. Supplementary Tables 4–13 detail the classification performance from the different models.

All base CNNs achieved averaged test balanced accuracy scores greater than 80%, highlighting good generalization performance. Xception-41 achieved the highest average test performance with a recall of 86 ± 3%, specificity of 87 ± 5%, balanced accuracy of 87 ± 3%, and an AUC of 0.93 ± 0.02 on the hidden test sets. Similarly, EfficientNetV2-M also presented strong performance with a recall of 91 ± 1%, specificity of 81 ± 7%, balanced accuracy of 86 ± 3%, and an AUC of 0.92 ± 0.02. Furthermore, EfficientNetV2-S demonstrated strong performance with a recall of 88 ± 1%, specificity of 83 ± 7%, balanced accuracy of 85 ± 4%, and an AUC of 0.92 ± 0.03 on the test sets. These results demonstrated the robustness of the proposed CNN models across different data partitions.

Based on the maximum performance, Xception-41 achieved a peak test performance with 87% recall, 95% specificity, 91% balanced accuracy, and 0.93 AUC on an independent test set. EfficientNetV2-S also attained a peak test performance of 89% recall, 90% specificity, 90% balanced accuracy, and 0.94 AUC on an independent test set. The maximum test performance from EfficientNetV2-M model was 92% recall, 90% specificity, 90% balanced accuracy, and 0.95 AUC on an independent test set.

Among the ResNet architectures investigated, simpler models such as ResNet-18, ResNet-34, and ResNet-50 performed better that their deeper counterpart, ResNet-101. For instance, ResNet-50 achieved an averaged test performance of 91 ± 1% recall, 78 ± 4% specificity, 84 ± 3% balanced accuracy, and 0.93 ± 0.02 AUC. In contrast, ResNet-101 obtained an averaged test performance of 90 ± 2% recall, 71 ± 6% specificity, 81 ± 3% balanced accuracy, and 0.92 ± 0.02 AUC. Additionally, ResNet-34 achieved peak test classification performance with a recall of 95%, specificity of 80%, balanced accuracy of 87%, and an AUC of 0.92 on an independent test set. On the other hand, ResNet-101 achieved peak test classification performance with a recall of 92%, specificity of 80%, balanced accuracy of 86%, and an AUC of 0.95 on an independent test set.

Comparison with the BI-RADS and the radiomics-based conventional machine learning approaches

The generalization performance of CNN-based deep learning, conventional machine learning, and BI-RADS methods was compared. The average classification metrics across five randomly stratified train-validation-test partitions of the entire dataset are reported (described below). The Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS)25, which measures the likelihood of malignancy, was used as the baseline standard. Breast lesions with a BI-RADS score greater than or equal to 4 were classified as malignant, while those with BI-RADS score less than 4 were classified as benign.

The conventional machine learning approach involved the determination of a comprehensive set of radiomics features using PyRadiomics39, including first-order statistical and textural features from the tumour core and its 5-mm margin in QUS images. Data preprocessing steps included skew correction, standardization, outlier detection, MRMR-based feature selection40, data balancing using the synthetic minority oversampling technique (SMOTE)41, and a wrapper-based feature selection of thirteen optimal features. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) and linear support vector machines (SVM) were utilized. For the SVM classifier, we performed a hyper-parameter search for SVM’s penalty parameter across {0.01, 0.1, 1.0, 10.0}.

Subsequently, we performed a post-hoc test using a paired t-test, with Bonferroni correction, at an adjusted significance level of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} = 0.017. Deep learning significantly outperformed conventional machine learning with p-values < 0.017 for recall, accuracy, balanced accuracy, precision, NPV, F1-Score, and AUC. For the specificity metric, CNN-based outperformed conventional machine learning at an adjusted significance level of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} = 0.022. These results suggest that CNN outperforms conventional machine learning in this specific context.

Discussion

The high incidence and morbidity of breast cancer underscore the critical importance of early detection, as timely interventions can significantly improve prognosis. Although screening mammography is widely used, the potential variability in mammogram interpretation highlights the need for more robust diagnostic frameworks. The approach used in the study here leverages sub-networks from pre-trained CNNs to identify relevant features directly from QUS spectral parametric images for distinguishing malignant from benign breast lesions, achieving excellent generalization. The Xception-41 model achieved an average recall of 86 ± 3%, average specificity of 87 ± 5%, average balanced accuracy of 87 ± 3%, and an average AUC of 0.93 ± 0.02 on hidden test sets. Similarly, EfficientNetV2-S model achieved an average recall of 88 ± 1%, average specificity of 83 ± 7%, average balanced accuracy of 87 ± 3%, and an average AUC of 0.93 ± 0.02 on hidden test sets. On an independent test set, Xception-41 achieved 87% recall, 95% specificity, 91% balanced accuracy, and 0.93 AUC. EfficientNetV2-S also attained peak performance of 89% recall, 90% specificity, 90% balanced accuracy, and 0.94 AUC. Our CNN-based deep learning approach significantly outperformed conventional machine learning methods. Furthermore, the method was more robust than the conventional machine learning with respect to data partitioning. The end-to-end deep learning approach on QUS spectral parametric images developed here provided a robust, objective, non-ionizing, and portable means for breast cancer diagnosis, with a potential application for screening.

QUS techniques have shown promise for objectively characterize breast tissue microstructures to distinguish malignant from benign tumors9–11,13. However, recent studies have noted performance limitations in such approaches13. For example, Destrempes et al.13 reported a maximum 0.74 classification AUC using a combination of QUS spectral and shearwave elastography (SWE) features. Similarly, Osapoetra et al. developed a multi-variate model based on comprehensive radiomics analysis of QUS spectral parametric images, achieving an average recall of 82 ± 8%, specificity of 79 ± 9%, balanced accuracy of 80 ± 5%, and an AUC of 0.87 ± 0.05. These findings have motivated further research to improve QUS model performance. Inspired by CNN advancements in computer vision, the work here demonstrated that partially fine-tuned sub-networks, developed from pre-trained CNNs, outperformed conventional machine learning in differentiating breast lesions.

The study here conducted an extensive comparison of several prominent CNN architectures commonly applied to breast lesions characterization in B-mode US images, including ResNet21, Inception22, an Xception23, with the exception of EfficientNet24. From these networks, we systematically selected sub-networks by evaluating their performance on validation sets. Our approach was motivated by recent works that investigated transfer learning for medical imaging, particularly addressing the issue of how transferable features are from general object recognition to the specific downstream task of medical image classification35,42. Generally, all sub-networks derived from pretrained CNN models achieved peak validation performance when utilizing feature maps from the third block. Consistent with Raghu et al.35 findings, simpler CNN models within the same architecture family, such as ResNet and Xception demonstrated superior generalization capabilities.

To facilitate this comparison, we leveraged the flexibility of the timm (Torch image model) library43 to extract feature maps from key CNN blocks. For ResNet, we evaluated ResNet-18, ResNet-34, ResNet-50, and ResNet-101 (see Supplementary Tables 4–7 for detailed performance on the validation and test sets). For Xception, we compared Xception-41 and Xception-71 (Supplementary Tables 8–9), and for EfficientNetV2, we compared EfficientNetV2-S, EfficientNetV2-M, and EfficientNetV2-L (Supplementary Tables 11–13).

Our results indicated that mid-complexity architectures outperformed their more complex counterparts. Specifically, the ResNet-34 sub-network with three blocks achieved the highest test performance, with an average recall of 89 ± 4%, average specificity of 78 ± 5%, average balanced accuracy of 84 ± 3%, and average AUC of 0.93 ± 0.03 across five train-validation-test partitions. Similarly, the Xception-41 model achieved an average recall of 86 ± 3%, average specificity of 87 ± 5%, average balanced accuracy of 87 ± 3%, and an average AUC of 0.93 ± 0.02 on hidden test sets, surpassing the Xception-71 model, which showed lower metrics (recall of 89 ± 2%, specificity of 78 ± 4%, balanced accuracy of 84 ± 2%, and an AUC of 0.92 ± 0.03). A similar trend was observed in the EfficientNetV2 family, where the EfficientNetV2-M model, utilizing three sub-blocks, achieved an average recall of 91 ± 1%, average specificity of 81 ± 7%, average balanced accuracy of 86 ± 3%, and average AUC of 0.92 ± 0.02 on hidden test sets.

Pre-trained CNN models have previously been applied to the characterization of breast lesions using B-mode US images. Han et al.32 used a GoogLeNet model, pre-trained on ImageNet, to classify 7408 B-mode US images (3154 malignant and 4254 benign) from 5151 patients. They reported a peak performance of 86% recall, 96% specificity, 90% accuracy, and 0.90 on the test set. Byra et al.33 further developed a model to differentiate malignant from benign breast lesions using B-mode US images. Through transfer learning techniques, they achieved 85% sensitivity, 90% specificity, 89% accuracy, and 0.94 AUC on an independent test set33. Similarly, Wang et al.34 applied a modified Inception-v3 architecture for breast lesion classification, reporting 89% recall, 88% specificity, 88% accuracy, and 0.95 AUC. More recently, Xu et al.44 focused on classifying BI-RADS category 4 breast tumors by fusing B-mode US images with K-means clustering and entropy images using CNNs. They achieved a top performance of 80.22 ± 1.45% accuracy, 77.97 ± 2.89% precision, and 0.82 ± 0.01 AUC with B-mode US images. Our results are comparable to these studies. Notably, the Xception-41 model achieved peak performance with 87% recall, 95% specificity, 91% balanced accuracy, and 0.93 AUC on an independent test set, demonstrating competitive effectiveness in breast lesion characterization.

The best CNN-based models statistical significantly outperformed the conventional machine learning models. For example, Xception-41 model with the average performance of 88 ± 1% recall, 91 ± 4% specificity, 89 ± 1% balanced accuracy, and 0.94 ± 0.02 AUC statistical significantly outperform the LDA model with 76 ± 18% recall, 75 ± 8% specificity, 76 ± 9% balanced accuracy, and 0.81 ± 0.12 AUC. Sub-networks from the EfficientNetV2 families in EfficientNetV2-S and EfficientNetV2-M models also demonstrated a similar trend.

Transformers, originally developed for natural language processing (NLP) tasks to efficiently manage long-range dependencies in sequences and improve the scalability of NLP models45, have recently emerged as a competitive alternative to CNNs for computer vision46. Although ViTs lack some of the image-specific inductive biases inherent to CNNs, they have achieved state-of-the-art performance on vision tasks47,48. A recent study showed that a sufficient number of parallel heads in the multi-head self-attention (MSA) layers allows ViTs to perform the equivalent of convolutions49. It is believed that ViTs can further enhance the characterization of breast lesions and in the future will be applied to the same partitions of our dataset to explore their effectiveness in this domain.

There are a few limitations in the work here. As it is single-institutional, further validation on data from multiple institutions is needed, to a degree. Additionally, manual segmentation was used to identifying ROIs, which could introduce inter-observer variability. However, this effect was minimized by meticulously following clinical patient records and radiologist guidance. Future work will explore using deep learning for more automated and objective contouring.

For future work, options include to consider the knowledge distillation approach to train models more efficiently. Both teacher-student and self-distillation techniques can be explored in order to enhance the model’s ability to discriminate between benign and malignant tumors. The rationale behind this approach is to train a shallow model using a pre-trained deep model, instead of fine-tuning the deep model.

Conclusion

The results here demonstrated that the proposed CNN-based approach on QUS spectral parametric images significantly outperformed conventional machine learning methods in discriminating malignant from benign breast lesions, highlighting the robustness of the framework. The framework may be utilized as a working tool for rapid breast cancer screening in clinics, alongside the baseline standard of Breast Imaging Reporting and Data System (BI-RADS)25, which could help prevent unnecessary biopsies.
