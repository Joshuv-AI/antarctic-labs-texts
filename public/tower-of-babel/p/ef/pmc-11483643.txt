Mechanistic concepts involved in biofilm associated processes of Campylobacter jejuni: persistence and inhibition in poultry environments
Authors: Iulia A Bundurus, Igori Balta, Ioan Pet, Lavinia Stef, Cosmin Alin Popescu, David McCleery, Joanne Lemon, Todd Callaway, Alastair Douglas, Nicolae Corcionivoschi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractCampylobacter species, predominantly Campylobacter jejuni, remains a significant zoonotic pathogen worldwide, with the poultry sector being the primary vector for human transmission. In recent years. there has been a notable rise in the incidence of human campylobacteriosis, necessitating a deeper understanding of the pathogen’s survival mechanisms and transmission dynamics. Biofilm presence significantly contributes to C. jejuni persistence in poultry and subsequent food product contamination, and this review describes the intricate processes involved in biofilm formation. The ability of Campylobacter to form biofilms on various surfaces, including stainless steel, plastic, and glass, is a critical survival strategy. Campylobacter biofilms, with their remarkable resilience, protect the pathogen from environmental stresses such as desiccation, pH extremes, biocides and sanitizing agents. This review explores the molecular and genetic mechanisms of C. jejuni biofilm formation, highlighting regulatory genes involved in motility, chemotaxis, and stress responses. Flagellar proteins, particularly flaA, flaB, flaG, and adhesins like cadF and flpA, are identified as the main molecular components in biofilm development. The role of mixed-species biofilms, where C. jejuni integrates into existing biofilms of other bacteria to enhance pathogen resilience, is also discussed. This review also considers alternative interventions to control C. jejuni in poultry production, in the context of increasing antibiotic resistance. It explores the effectiveness of prebiotics, probiotics, synbiotics, bacteriocins, bacteriophages, vaccines, and organic acids, with a focus on their mechanisms of action in reducing bacterial colonization and biofilm formation. Studies show that mixtures of organic acids and compounds like Carvacrol and Eugenol significantly downregulate genes linked with motility and adhesion, thereby disrupting biofilm integrity. It discusses the impact of environmental factors, such as temperature and oxygen levels on biofilm formation, providing insights into how industrial conditions can be manipulated to reduce contamination. This paper stresses the need for a multifaceted approach to control Campylobacter in poultry, integrating molecular and genetic insights with practical interventions. By advancing our understanding of biofilm dynamics and gene regulation, we aim to inform the development of more effective strategies to enhance food safety and protect public health.Key words: Campylobacter biofilm, poultry environment, mechanistic concept, biofilm inhibition, Campylobacter persistence

INTRODUCTION

Campylobacter spp., a globally prevalent zoonotic Gram-negative bacterium is a major concern for public health and Campylobacter jejuni is at the forefront of human related disease (Kim et al., 2020). Broiler chickens, known carriers of high levels of this pathogen, serve as the primary vector for its transmission to humans and despite challenges in modelling the transmission dynamics among populations, it is essential to comprehend these dynamics and the potential impact of interventions to prevent infection (Scallan et al., 2015; Kim et al., 2020). The primary pathways for Campylobacter transmission to poultry are environmental and horizontal transmission among flock mates, with entire flocks likely to become infected within a week after a single positive diagnosis in a broiler chicken Abd El-Hack et al. (2021).

Despite being classified as a fastidious and slow-growing bacteria, Campylobacter species (spp.) has demonstrated remarkable survival capabilities. It exhibits sensitivity to various stresses such as desiccation, extreme pH, freezing, UV light, and disinfectants, yet it can persist outside its host for extended periods (Whiley et al., 2013). Its resilience is primarily attributed to its ability to adhere to surfaces and form or join biofilms which significantly contributes to its environmental persistence and widespread distribution and the establishment and growth of biofilm is a pivotal factor in the survival and spread of Campylobacter and highlights the importance of our research in understanding and inhibiting biofilm formation, (Giaouris, 2023). The initial step in biofilm formation is bacterial adhesion to various industrial surface materials. This attachment occurs via electrostatic and van der Waals forces, after which bacteria begin to communicate and establish a biofilm community (Klančnik et al., 2021). The interplay between bacterial adhesion to meat surfaces and biofilm production is a complex puzzle that continues to be the subject of extensive research. The intricate dynamics and their implications, particularly for meat surfaces encountered in poultry processing, present a challenging yet engaging field of study, underscoring the need for further investigation.

The biofilm serves to protect the bacteria from various environmental stresses and grant resistance against the host immune system response, and formation occurs when bacterial cells aggregate, become enveloped by a self-produced matrix layer and attach to the host surface (Bronnec et al., 2016; Shabbir et al., 2018). Comprehension of C. jejuni biofilm gene regulation is incomplete. The genes described in this review are known to manage movement and chemotaxis (Tram et al., 2020; Püning et al., 2021; Whelan et al., 2021), lipooligosaccharide formation (Lim and Kim, 2017; Burnham and Hendrixson, 2018; Tram et al., 2020; Whelan et al., 2021), N-linked glycosylation, capsule polysaccharides (Guerry et al., 2012; Burnham and Hendrixson, 2018; Tram et al., 2020), and stress responses (Elgamoudi and Korolik, 2021).

Following its discovery in the late nineteenth century, research on the flagellum include morphological studies, detailed biochemical and biophysical investigations, and led to an intensive comprehension of its regulation for various physiological purposes (Nedeljković et al., 2021). In addition to facilitating movement, the bacterial flagellum also has many other versatile functions, including adherence to substrates, aiding biofilm formation, and functioning as a protein secretion machine for diverse biological processes (Nedeljković et al., 2021). Whereas the exact regulatory processes of biofilm formation remain unclear, several virulence genes coupled with cellular movement, such as flaA, flaB, flaC, flaG, fliA, fliS, and flhA, are believed to play a role in the complex developmental process (García-Sánchez et al., 2019). Biofilms shield microorganisms from sanitisers, increasing the chances of persistence and eventual food contamination, whereas their resilience, due to a sturdy and enduring matrix, makes them difficult to eliminate once formed on surfaces within food processing facilities and equipment (Giaouris and Simões, 2018).

Conventional cleaning and disinfection strategies in food manufacturing, including physical and chemical methods, inadequately address biofilms and may amplify resistance transmission, promoting the imperative need to examine biofilm resilience in industrial environments. In the meat sector, contamination by foodborne pathogens is an acute public health issue, often leading to product withdrawal and substantial financial repercussions (Giaouris and Simões, 2018). Interestingly, Whelan et al. (2019) documented that fluoroquinolone-resistant C. jejuni displays increased virulence and forms biofilms in oxygen-rich environments, contributing to rising resistance (Whelan et al., 2019).

A recent publication by Sierra-Arguello et al. (2021) reinforces the need to adopt further specific legislation to prevent the dissemination of potentially pathogenic strains via poultry product consumption. They illustrate that eight potentially pathogenic genes of C. jejuni were prevalent in slaughter lines and other poultry sources in Southern Brazil (Sierra-Arguello et al., 2021). Understanding the strong link between adhesion, motility, and biofilm formation is imperative, as this combination is key to Campylobacter colonization of surfaces, particularly on poultry meat (Akahoshi and Bevins, 2022). This review provides data to accentuate the close relationship between these processes and emphasizes the importance of considering this connection when implementing control measures in poultry production concerning Campylobacter contamination. Moreover, these measures must be implemented due to the high-risk nature and global prevalence of campylobacteriosis. Understanding gene expression or the molecular strategies employed by Campylobacter spp. during attachment to poultry meat, and the role of flagellar apparatus or adhesins in biofilm production, underpins the knowledge base needed to address the issues of contamination and public safety. Although it is un-likely that Campylobacter spp., will form biofilm in food processing environments it has been suggested that on these surfaces in can survive as part of other species biofilms (Teh et al., 2014).

Factors Affecting Campylobacter spp. Persistence in Biofilms at Slaughterhouse

The formation of biofilms is a complex process that begins with the initial adherence of planktonic (free-swimming) bacteria to a surface, followed by proliferation, secretion of extracellular polymeric substances (EPS), establishment of microcolonies, maturation (culminating in the development of biofilm architecture), and eventual detachment (Giaouris and Simões, 2018). The process occurs in stages: attachment (Melaugh et al., 2016), maturation, matrix development, and lastly, dispersal (Fleming and Rumbaugh, 2017). This matrix, or “matrixome,” is composed of diverse components like DNA and proteins and is indispensable for biofilm integrity (Kalia et al., 2023). Biofilm genetics are regulated through quorum sensing (QS) (Subhadra et al., 2018), cyclic dinucleotides (López-Baena et al., 2019), and small non-coding RNAs (Taylor et al., 2017; Kalia et al., 2023). Biofilm development is a dynamic biological process. It begins with the adherence of planktonic cells to a substrate, forming small communities, followed by proliferation of bacterial cells which continually secrete EPS to evolve the biofilm from a thin monolayer into a robust multi-layer structure. Finally, the biofilm structure disintegrates, releasing sessile bacterial cells back into the environment to find and colonize new contact surfaces. In a biofilm system, these sessile cells are encased within an EPS matrix, constituting up to 90% of the biofilm's mass (Ma et al., 2022). Given that biofilm formation relies on various biological and environmental factors and that different biological and inorganic compounds can inactivate Campylobacter biofilm there is a need for research to investigate the feasibility of specified chemicals to quench quorum sensing of this microbe which would reduce biofilm formation indirectly (Ma et al., 2022). It is important to note that the extracellular matrix makes up between 65% and 95% of biofilm volume, so methodologies in biofilm research should focus on identifying both bacteria and the various constituents of the matrix (Carrascosa et al., 2021).

Notably, Šimunović et al. (2020) demonstrated that slaughterhouse C. jejuni isolates display greater adhesion to abiotic surfaces, particularly under conditions that mimicked the chicken- host environment, such as that of a temperature of 42°C in the presence of chicken juice. This suggests that these isolates have adapted to thrive in the host-like conditions of the slaughterhouse, leading to better survival and increased potential for food chain transmission (Šimunović et al., 2020c). This adaptation to the host environment for enhanced adhesion was also observed in other studies, indicating a pattern of environmental selection within food processing environments that favour bacteria with higher adhesion capabilities (Melo et al., 2017; Šimunović et al., 2020c). Similarly, the presence of chicken juice noticeably increased biofilm formation on inert surfaces, reinforcing the role of environmental factors in biofilm development and the survival of C. jejuni outside the host (Li et al., 2017; Melo et al., 2017; Šimunović et al., 2020c). Moreover, Karki et al. (2019) demonstrated that chicken liver juice significantly enhanced the survivability of C. jejuni and C. coli at low temperatures, contributing to the high prevalence of Campylobacter spp. in retailed liver products. In addition, chicken liver juice enhanced biofilm formation in most C. coli strains and supported their growth under un-favourable conditions (Karki et al., 2019). Araujo et al. (2022) assessed biofilm formation in various Campylobacter strains under conditions mimicking the slaughterhouse environment, finding that biofilm formation is strain-dependent and influenced by temperature and oxygen levels, particularly effective at 42°C. The research emphasized that numerous C. jejuni strains consistently form biofilms under conditions resembling those in the poultry prcoessing factories, shedding light on the necessity for enhanced hygiene practices to address issues associated with biofilm in slaughterhouses (Araújo et al., 2022). Corcionivoschi et al. (2023) have shown that, under aerobic conditions, C. jejuni augments carbon metabolism and surface polysaccharide production, forming a protective barrier against oxygen. This thickening of surface capsules not only aids in sustaining viability but also promotes biofilm formation. Their findings deepen our understanding of how oxygen-sensitive bacteria cope with oxygen-rich environments, offering insights into the complex mechanisms underlying aerotolerance in microaerophiles (Corcionivoschi et al., 2023).

Factors with impact on Campylobacter spp., adhesion to poultry meat

The poultry industry, a key component of the agricultural sector, has experienced remarkable expansion over the past 50 years, hence the need for enhanced monitoring of this pathogen is necessary (Oh et al., 2018; Thames and Theradiyil Sukumaran, 2020). Upon ingestion, Campylobacters swiftly colonize the gastrointestinal tract, typically inhabiting areas such as the ceca, large intestine, jejunum, and cloaca and rapid dissemination across the flock. Despite numerous interventions, human infections with Campylobacter continue to occur annually due to contaminated poultry meat (Thames and Theradiyil Sukumaran, 2020). Like other bacteria, C. jejuni can create biofilms in laboratory settings and on surfaces used in poultry processing, such as plastic, stainless steel, or glass (García-Sánchez et al., 2018). However, the structure and consistency of the biofilm is highly dependent on the number of genes involved and on the direct environmental impact on their expression. Genes involved in this process include those governing cell motility, such as flaA, flaB, flaC, flaG, fliA, fliS, and flhA (García-Sánchez et al., 2018). Interestingly, regulatory genes involved in community growth and quorum sensing impact motility and biofilm formation, advising shared regulatory mechanisms. Many genes conferring motility are also critical for the infectivity of C. jejuni, indicating potential complex overlap between pathogenicity and biofilm-forming states (El Abbar et al., 2019; Šimunović et al., 2020a; Teren et al., 2019; Tram et al., 2020).

The role of flagellar proteins in attachment to meat

Several studies [22,36] have reported that Campylobacter flagella play a role not only in motility but also in the formation of biofilms. In their research, Kim et al. (2015) (Kim et al., 2015) observed a decrease in biofilm production in a mutant strain lacking the flgA gene compared to a wild-type strain, indicating the involvement of flagella in Campylobacter biofilm formation through the production of extracellular fibre-like material. Similarly, Reuter et al. (2010) (Reuter et al., 2010) found that under microaerobic conditions, a motile Campylobacter strain produced more biofilm than a non-motile strain, highlighting the importance of flagella in biofilm formation. Moreover, it has been suggested by some authors that the flagellar system may secrete non-flagellar extracellular proteins such as FLAC, CIAB, and FSPA, which contribute to the biofilm development (Konkel et al., 2004; Lamas et al., 2018; Y. Song et al., 2004). Bacteria often utilize flagellar motility to navigate through mucus and access the epithelium. This movement is a probable approach for bacteria to reach epithelial surfaces for the purposes of adhesion, invasion, and translocation (Akahoshi and Bevins, 2022; Furter et al., 2019).

Genes displaying functions in flagella and motility also undergo phase variation - a process where the expression of certain genes is switched on and off, influencing pathogenesis. Polar flagella in C. jejuni serve multiple functions in pathogenesis, including motility, adhesion, and immune system evasion (Kreling et al., 2020). They allow efficient penetration of the intestinal mucin layer, further supporting colonization and pathogenicity. C. jejuni flagellin is heavily glycosylated, operating in both biofilm development and motility (Kreling et al., 2020). In a study investigating biofilm production under aerobic and anaerobic conditions, Reuter et al. (2010) compared the motile reference strain C. jejuni NCTC 11168 with a non-motile variant of the same strain and found that aerobic conditions appeared to foster increased biofilm formation in C. jejuni NCTC 11168, potentially as a protective mechanism. Moreover, the study implicated flagella in biofilm formation, as the strain with non-motile flagella exhibited diminished capacity for biofilm formation compared to others (Lamas et al., 2018). Moreover, a deletion mutation carried out by Feng et al. (2018) demonstrated that flagella plays a role in biofilm formation, primarily aiding in attachment rather than inducing lysis (Feng et al., 2018).

In C. jejuni, autoinducer-2 (AI-2) and luxS are involved in various necessary processes, including biofilm formation. Teren et al. (2019) found that disrupting the luxS gene in C. jejuni affected cell-to-cell communication, indicating a role for QS. Additionally, they found cjA, a new compound altering virulence factor expression, possibly replacing AI-2 in C. jejuni. Host-correlated conditions may inhibit AI-2 production (Teren et al., 2019). The results of Sałamaszyńska-Guz et al. (2018) display how rRNA methylation by CJ0588 governs the physiology and pathogenicity of C. jejuni. Reduced motility and impaired biofilm formation are direct consequences of altered ribosomal function and protein synthesis due to the absence of rRNA methylation at nucleotide C1920 (Sałamaszyńska-Guz et al., 2018). Notably, the findings of Handley et al. (2015) suggest that FLIW mutations lead to C. jejuni cells with considerably shortened flagella, decreased motility, diminished biofilm-forming capacity, and a lowered capability to colonize chicks (Handley et al., 2015), but a FLHF mutant strain has shown significantly reduced biofilm-formation and autoagglutination abilities (Li et al., 2018).

The inactivation of CCML (TLP3 – multiligand chemoreceptor) in C. jejuni 11168 altered avian host colonization, resulting in biofilm-like micro-colonies in chicken caeca and increased in vitro biofilm production. A double mutant lacking TLP3 and TLP4 exhibited reduced colonization ability in mice (Korolik, 2019). Notably, C. jejuni strain 81-176, which lacks naturally TLP3, displayed similar colonization patterns to the TLP3 mutant of strain 11168, signalling a relationship between TLP3 and biofilm formation (Korolik, 2019). Shabbir et al. (2018) observed suppressed expression of flagella biosynthesis and assembly genes, such as CJ0440C and CJ0547 (flaG), reducing flagellar motility. This aligns with previous research indicating CJ0440C's involvement in flagella biosynthesis (Hao et al., 2017). Their findings suggest that cas9 regulates membrane integrity, host cell adherence and flagella assembly genes which could contribute to the decreased virulence observed in the Δcas9 mutant strain compared to the wild type (Shabbir et al., 2018). In addition, it was discovered that elevated cbrR expression decreases biofilm formation. Consequently, cbrR suppresses flaA expression and motility in C. jejuni pathogenesis (Cox et al., 2021).

Recent research has indicated the importance of the C. jejuni FLIW protein for generating intact flagella. The absence of FLIW leads to disruptions in both bacterial motility and biofilm formation, as well as a decreased capability to establish itself in the intestinal tracts of poultry (Akahoshi and Bevins, 2022). Furthermore, regulation of biofilm development is tied to CSRA, especially under oxygen-rich conditions, and disrupting CSRA function impairs biofilm formation (Kim et al., 2021). Laconi et al. (2023) evaluated the biofilm-forming capabilities of non-intestinal Campylobacter spp. from poultry and analyzed the associated genetic traits. This led to the identification of genes such as flaA and maf7 as potential contributors to biofilm development. However, the study did not include transcriptional analysis and involved a limited number of Campylobacter strains, which could weaken the statistical significance of the results (Laconi et al., 2023). Linking Biofilm Formation and Adhesion Proteins

The protective properties of intestinal mucus, rich in sulfated and sialylated mucins, may diminish the adhesion and penetration abilities of C. jejuni, by shielding the mucins from bacterial glycosidase degradation. This protection also depends on mucin subtypes, such as MUC2 and MUC5AC, which interact through protein domains influencing mucus layer permeability (Duangnumsawang et al., 2021). Intestinal mucus not only acts as a barrier but also supports bacterial colonization by offering ligands for adhesion and nutrients for bacteria which can degrade mucins or utilize the resulting saccharides. Bacterial colonization hinges on attachment, mucin degradation by bacterial enzymes, and the utilization of mucin-derived carbohydrates. The beginning of colonization is marked by bacterial adhesion to mucins and may involve mechanisms such as Van der Waals and hydrophobic, including electrostatic interactions (Duangnumsawang et al., 2021). Bacteria often employ strategies like cell-surface interraction proteins, pili, fimbriae, and flagella to adhere to mucins. For instance, commensal lactic acid bacteria utilize mucus-binding proteins and pili to attach to mucin oligosaccharides, like mannose, whereas pathogenic bacteria, including C. jejuni, use carbohydrate-lectin, flagella subunit proteins, and major outer membrane proteins for adhesion (Sicard et al., 2017; Duangnumsawang et al., 2021).

Adhesion through adhesins is instrumental in colonizing and internalizing C. jejuni into host cells (Kemper and Hensel, 2023). Using anti-adhesive agents to inhibit bacterial adhesion may provide an opportunity for early intervention in infection development (Ajene et al., 2013; Gottesmann, Goycoolea, et al., 2020; Gottesmann, Paraskevopoulou, et al., 2020). Therefore, a major focus for preventing Campylobacter infections should be adhesion (Kemper and Hensel, 2023). The bacterium predominantly utilizes outer membrane proteins known as adhesins for binding to cellular structures of the host through interactions that are either protein-protein or protein-carbohydrate in nature. Such interactions are vital for the bacterium's ability to colonize and persist within the host and complicate C.jenuni eradication strategies.due to its intricate interactions with the intestinal lining of the host. Early prevention of infections may be achievable by targeting these adhesins (Kemper and Hensel, 2023).

C. jejuni employs proteases to disrupt cell-cell junctions and then transmigrates through these gaps. Fibronectin on the basolateral side of polarized epithelial cells act as binding sites for the adhesins CADF and FLPA, initiating intracellular signaling to induce membrane ruffling and diminished host cell migration through focal adhesion. Other than proteins, that bind to fibronectin, additional adhesins targeting different structures, lectins, and their corresponding sugar structures promote the host-pathogen interaction (Kemper and Hensel, 2023). The cadF and flpA genes were shown to be conserved across C. jejuni isolates (Konkel et al., 2020). Notably, Talukdar et al. (2020) demonstrated that neither cadF nor flpA alone can completely compensate for defects in host cell fibronectin-binding. Furthermore, their data suggests that CADF and FLPA attach to distinct sites on the glucan-binding (GBD) fragment of fibronectin, accentuating their cooperative interaction in binding C. jejuni to host cells (Talukdar et al., 2020).

Interventions and Mechanisms Involved in Reducing Campylobacter spp., Presence in Biofilms at Poultry Production

C. jejuni, a symbiotic bacterium, inhabits the intestinal tracts of various wild and agriculture-associated animals (McKenna et al., 2020). Consequently, handling and consuming poultry meats contaminated with the bacterium are prominent risk factors for sporadic cases of diarrheal illness attributed to C. jejuni in humans (Burnham and Hendrixson, 2018). The study of biofilms is of great importance because understanding the phenomenon enables the discovery of control methods. Infection by Campylobacter, a leading cause of human gastroenteritis, is difficult to control in poultry farms and processing plants. With complex operations and a ban on antibiotics as growth promoters, approved antimicrobials have limited efficacy. Innovative solutions, such as bioactive packaging using bacteriophage or nanoparticles and optimized antimicrobial combinations, are vital for reducing C. jejuni in raw chicken, enhancing product safety (Hakeem and Lu, 2021).

New biochemical methods have been explored in recent years to prevent biofilm formation. The most effective approach is to disrupt bacterial adherence, the initial step in biofilm development. This can be achieved by directly blocking surface receptors or through a general strategy that utilizes compounds known for their anti-adherence effects (Carrascosa et al., 2021). Previously, control measures for Campylobacter in poultry involved antibiotics, phage therapy, competitive exclusion, and vaccination (Lin, 2009; Meunier et al., 2017; Umaraw et al., 2017). However, owing to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant pathogens that can be transmitted to humans during food consumption, leading to more severe illnesses, the use of antibiotics in poultry has become increasingly restricted (Deng et al., 2020). Consequently, alternative strategies and feed additives to control Campylobacter colonization in poultry gastrointestinal tracts have gained interest (Deng et al., 2020). Interestingly, it was found that an alternative method to diminish biofilm development during C. jejuni infection involved the use of D-amino acids and L-amino acids (Elgamoudi et al., 2020). Distinctly, the latest findings suggest suppressing cadF and flpA adhesins, biofilm compounds, and TLP or CHE proteins inhibit C. jejuni colonization (Tikhomirova et al., 2024).

Pathogenesis of C. jejuni in poultry involved four primary stages: 1) ingestion, 2) tolerance to acid and resistance to bile, 3) proliferation within mucus, and 4) invasion of epithelial cells. Notably, the concentration of C. jejuni in the host illustrated an inverse correlation with the presence of sulphated O-glycans. Moreover, enhanced sulfation and sialyation amplify the anionic charge within chicken mucin, eliciting a repulsive effect against C. jejuni (Hakeem and Lu, 2021). Campylobacter can spread rapidly throughout a flock in as little as a few days (Awad et al., 2018), primarily through environmental exposure and horizontal transmission between flock mates (Sibanda et al., 2018; Deng et al., 2020). Recent surveys in the U.S. broiler production sector showed a lack of routine diagnostic testing for Campylobacter spp. by farm managers, though some veterinarians and most processing plants do include these tests (Hwang and Singer, 2020). Only a third of farms implemented validated measures to limit contamination, and despite biosecurity being seen as the most effective strategy on farms, it is often deemed insufficient by farmers and veterinarians (Deng et al., 2020). Understanding the various sources and transmission routes of C. jejuni in poultry is central to implementing practical control measures to reduce the possible risk of contamination. The farm environment is identified as a primary infection source, and controlling the pathogen through interventions such as vaccines, feed and water additives, and stringent biosecurity measures should be applied at this level. Intervention at this stage is key in preventing the spread of Campylobacter within the food chain (Lu et al., 2021). The findings of Wysok et al. (2022) indicate that species of wildlife waterfowl may also act as a reservoir for Campylobacter, potentially spreading these bacteria to other hosts or directly influencing human disease (Wysok et al., 2022). Nonetheless, comparing the effectiveness of these interventions on a global scale is challenging due to variations in experimental designs and sampling methods across different studies. The costs of implementing these measures are significant and often fall on producers who may require incentives to mitigate potential losses. Beyond farm management, critical points in the food processing and handling chain can play a significant for microbial transfer, emphasizing the need for comprehensive food safety measures from farm to fork to manage this widespread pathogen effectively (Lu et al., 2021). These factors can affect Campylobacter properties, including binding capacity and biofilm formation, which are basic aspects of food safety and quality control (Carrascosa et al., 2021). For example, Carrascosa et al. (2021) reminded us of the importance of surface charge, hydrophobicity, temperature and the presence of substrates required for bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation (Carrascosa et al., 2021).

In poultry production facilities, lipid- and protein-rich exudate from slaughtered chickens enhances C. jejuni biofilm formation significantly more than Brucella broth (Araújo et al., 2022). Similarly, a range of proteins affiliated with membranes, virulence, and immunogenicity were consistently found in lower abundance during growth in chicken exudate. This group included CADF, CJAA, CJAC/HISJ, PEB1A, PEB1C, PEB2, PEB3, PAL/CJAD, and PEB4A. The presence of chicken exudate led to a reduction in proteins involved in adherence and immune response. Notably, some of these proteins, like CADF and the PEB antigens, are linked to bacterial colonization of chickens (Konkel et al., 2020; Kemper and Hensel, 2023), suggesting that the molecular signals triggering their expression might be absent in chicken exudate (Man et al., 2024).

Recent methods to manage Campylobacter infection in poultry primarily aim to prevent and reduce broiler chicken colonization by Campylobacter. By decreasing the colonization of Campylobacter in the guts of broilers, the levels on the carcass are also reduced, thereby lessening the occurrence of contaminated meat and the subsequent risk of human campylobacteriosis. Three main strategies are recognized for controlling Campylobacter in poultry flocks: (1) biosecurity and hygiene practices primarily designed to block the entry of Campylobacter into poultry farms, (2) dietary interventions to boost disease resistance and immune system responses, and (3) vaccination strategies to improve antibody responses and vaccination coverage (Abd El-Hack et al., 2021).

Asymptomatic colonization in broilers results in large reservoirs of the pathogen (Smith et al., 2020). Consequently, hygiene measures to minimize Campylobacter contamination in chicken farming and the food supply chain directly result in a reduction of human disease associated with Campylobacter infection. Humans can develop enteritis from ingesting even small amounts of the pathogen, with just a few hundred live bacteria being enough (Heimesaat et al., 2023). An in vitro study also observed that the polyphenol curcumin reduced C. jejuni-induced epithelial barrier dysfunction (Lobo de Sá et al., 2019). Additional reports show decreased motility and adhesion of C. jejuni due to clove oil, thyme, and campynexins and modulation of stress response and virulence by peppermint essential oil (Kovács et al., 2019; Kreling et al., 2020). Carvacrol's impact on C. jejuni is marked by its reduction in the expression of key motility and adhesion genes. Specifically, Wagle et al. (2020) have shown that it downregulates flaA, flaB, and flgA, which are important for the bacterium's mobility, and cadF, essential for its adhesion. These effects, observed under simulated poultry gut conditions at 42°C, highlight the potential of Carvacrol to interfere with the pathogen's colonization abilities (Wagle et al., 2020). Supporting these findings, studies by Wagle et al. (2017) have similarly shown that Carvacrol and other phytochemicals can decrease the motility and adherence of C. jejuni by affecting these gene expressions (Šimunović et al., 2020a; Wagle et al., 2017). These studies collectively underline the efficacy of carvacrol in modulating virulence factors of C. jejuni, thereby inhibiting its pathogenic mechanisms (Wagle et al., 2020). In a similar way, Eugenol noticeably downregulated motility genes (motA, motB) in C. jejuni, reducing its mobility and biofilm formation potential, whereas chitosan upregulated the jlpA gene, enhancing bacterial adhesion to surfaces and potential for biofilm formation under stress (Wagle et al., 2019). Similarly, Shrestha et al. (2019) discovered that exposure to sublethal concentrations of Carvacrol, chitosan or their combination modulated the expression of select genes (MOTA - motility, cadF - binding, and jlpA - attachment) in C. jejuni (Shrestha et al., 2019). Wagle et al. (2019) suggested that Trans-cinnamaldehyde, Eugenol and Carvacrol can disrupt C. jejuni biofilms by altering key genetic and protein expressions necessary for biofilm integrity and survival. These substances effectively downregulated motility genes (flaA, flaB, flgA) and proteomic analysis revealed that Trans-cinnamaldehyde and Carvacrol reduced the expression of proteins involved in oxidative stress response (napA) and biofilm formation (DnaK, bacterioferritin) (Wagle et al., 2019). These reductions could result in lower product contamination at the processing plant. However, research has not yet established a correlation between decreased C. jejuni biofilm counts and corresponding reductions in pathogen load on carcasses, which is a topic that could be explored in future studies (Wagle et al., 2019).

Klančnik et al. (2018) described how extracts from Juniperus communis (Juniper fruits) inhibit C. jejuni adhesion to abiotic surfaces in vitro (Klančnik et al., 2018). Moreover, Takó et al. (2020) demonstrated that phenolic compounds like flavonoids, tannins, and lignans, along with phenolic plant extracts, exhibit antimicrobial properties that can suppress biofilm and toxin formation through their anti-quorum sensing activity (Kreling et al., 2020; Takó et al., 2020). Other recent findings explored how natural antimicrobial mixtures at subinhibitory concentrations inhibit the genes involved in attachment and biofilm formation in C. jejuni (i.e luxS, flaA, flaB), targeting biofilm mechanisms like EPS production. An organic acid-based mixture effectively reduced the attachment of C. jejuni 11168 to surfaces such as chicken skins, straws, and eggshells.(Corcionivoschi et al., 2023).

Interventions against Campylobacter can be applied throughout the poultry production chain (Sahin et al., 2015; Umaraw et al., 2017; Upadhyay et al., 2019) using a variety of feed additives like organic acids, probiotics, and plant-derived compounds (Guyard-Nicodème et al., 2016; Meunier et al., 2016; Dittoe et al., 2018; Kim et al., 2019; Micciche et al., 2019). Probiotics often include strains of Lactobacillus, Streptococcus, Bacillus, Escherichia coli, Bifidobacterium, and Saccharomyces (Deng et al., 2020). Probiotics can modify the expression of genes in pathogens within a biofilm and reduce the colonisation of bacteria on surfaces thus offering a novel approach to disrupting or hindering biofilm formation. (Hussaini et al., 2024). Specifically, incorporating Lactobacillus spp. into livestock feed additives is seen as a novel strategy to prevent Campylobacter adherence to epithelial cells, thereby reducing transmission and the incidence of human campylobacteriosis (Šikić Pogačar et al., 2020). Šikić Pogačar et al. (2020) showed that Lactobacillus spp. significantly reduced the adherence of C. jejuni to epithelial cell lines from chickens (B1OXI). This reduction also negatively impacted C. jejuni's ability to invade the cells (Šikić Pogačar et al., 2020). Moreover, research by Sakaridis et al. (2018) suggests that manipulating Lactobacillus and Enterobacteriaceae populations might help reduce Campylobacter colonization (Sakaridis et al., 2018). Additionally, Connerton et al. (2018) found that chickens colonized by Campylobacter spp. (or jenuni) showed a different cecal microbiota composition early post-inoculation, which could be influenced by the microbiota's age-dependent changes (Connerton et al., 2018). Campylobacter colonization leads to a reduction in Lactobacillaceae and Clostridium cluster XIVa (Deng et al., 2020). Taha-Abdelaziz et al. (2019) reported that six Lactobacilli spp. inhibited C. jejuni growth in vitro, reducing its motility, invasion genes, and AI-2 production (Taha-Abdelaziz et al., 2019). Mortada et al. (2020) demonstrated that certain probiotic strains could reduce C. coli in vitro and influence colonization in vivo, although supplementation did not significantly mitigate colonization in Cobb-500 broilers at 42 d (Deng et al., 2020; Mortada et al., 2020). Whereas feed probiotics have been shown to effectively lessen Campylobacter colonization, they cannot eliminate it. Combining probiotics with other interventions, like prebiotics and postbiotics, may offer a more comprehensive solution for reducing or eliminating Campylobacter in poultry (Deng et al., 2020).

Hou et al. (2020) noted that indigestible dietary prebiotics like lignin alter the gut microbial composition. Whereas these natural products demonstrate specific anti-Campylobacter effects, detailed research is required to ascertain if they impact the entire host microbiome, whether they can reach and remain active in the distal jejunum and colon of the host or if they are metabolized during early intestinal transit. Ensuring the integrity of these compounds in food production is paramount and prebiotic use warrants further investigation to advance our knowledge in methods of safer food production, to minimize antibiotic use in animal farming and to enhance sustainability (Hou et al., 2020; Kreling et al., 2020).

To control bacterial transmission to humans via the poultry food chain, Singh et al. (2019) assessed the effectiveness of recombinant hcp immunization in preventing C. jejuni colonization in chickens. Their study indicates that chitosan-based intra-gastric delivery of RHCP offers several benefits over injectable vaccines and could represent a promising approach to effectively controlling C. jejuni colonization in chickens (Singh et al., 2019). Taniguchi et al. (2021) discovered that the number of C. jejuni bacterium adhering to chicken skin significantly decreases following treatment with sodium hydroxide and subsequent washing with PBS. It was determined that this reduction in adhesion could be attributed to proteins located in the alkaline extracts of the chicken skin, including chicken serum albumin, flagellar hook protein E (FLGE), and MOMP of C. jejuni. As the binding of C. jejuni to chicken skin involves proteins from both the bacterium and the host, it further elucidates infection mechanisms and host-microbe interactions, promoting the development of new methods for Campylobacter decontamination of chicken carcasses in food production systems. (Taniguchi et al., 2021).

Immunizing chickens can reduce C. jejuni colonization and its transmission to humans. Pumtang-on et al. assessed the efficacy of various C. jejuni vaccines in reducing the colonization of poultry. Although several trials demonstrated post-vaccination reductions in C. jejuni loads in broilers, results varied considerably across studies. The results emphasized the need for a standardized evaluation and reporting model for future vaccine studies to ensure robust trial comparisons. The potential of certain vaccines, particularly those using OMP antigens and subunit vaccines with recombinant FLAA-LT-B, was identified as promising, but further testing is recommended using a standardized approach to evaluate their efficacy better (Zhang et al., 2020). Notably, antibodies against the flagellar capping protein FLID have been found in tested chicken sera, indicating its potential as a vaccine candidate (Zhang et al., 2020).

The impact of organic acids on the expression of key genes (luxS, fimC, csgD in E. coli; luxS, flaA, flaB in C. jejuni) that regulate attachment and biofilm formation was also examined, and significant changes in gene expression and bacterial surface structures were noticed. Furthermore, expression of the genes responsible for biofilm formation was reduced depending on the dosage. The findings support the effectiveness of organic acids as natural antimicrobials in reducing bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation, with potential applications in poultry production and healthcare sectors (Corcionivoschi et al., 2023).

CONCLUSIONS

Inadequate systems within broiler farms to control C. jejuni infection is at the core of global issues concerning campylobacteriosis. This review aimed to establish a link between the molecular mechanisms regulated by key genes in attachment to poultry meat, and discuss the effects on biofilm formation. This review evidenced the central role of C. jejuni as a zoonotic pathogen, primarily transmitted through poultry. The persistence of C. jejuni, facilitated by biofilm formation on various surfaces, poses significant challenges for food safety. Understanding the genetic and molecular mechanisms of biofilm development, particularly the roles of flagellar motility and adhesion proteins, is required. Whole genome sequencing and proteomics allow researchers to gather an abundance of data at an unprecedented speed. This will allow the scientific community to disseminate information and collaborate on studies into the causes, effects and solutions available to address the public health burden of campylobacteriosis.
