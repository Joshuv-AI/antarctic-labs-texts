Optimal nitrate supply improves plant thermotolerance through NLP7-mediated transcriptomic changes in Arabidopsis
Authors: Shiyun Zhao, Zilong Xu, Zhenhua Zhang, Ziqiang Zhu, Hong Li
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

High temperatures can negatively affect crop yields across diverse regions and plant species (Cox et al., 2020). Plants have evolved sophisticated regulatory mechanisms that induce the expression of HEAT SHOCK PROTEINS (HSPs), which are controlled by HEAT SHOCK FACTORS (HSFs) (Wu et al., 2023). The experiences of Chinese farmers suggest that nitrogen fertilization in rice paddies during the summer can alleviate yield losses caused by high temperatures (Liu et al., 2019), but the detailed regulatory mechanisms remain unclear. Nitrate (NO3−), a primary nitrogen source, plays a major role in plant responses to abiotic stress. To investigate the effect of nitrate on plant thermotolerance, we first examined the expression of heat-responsive reporter gene under various NO3− concentrations (He et al., 2022). HSFA2:luciferase (LUC) luminescence was rapidly induced within 20 min of heat treatment, peaking at 50–60 min (Supplemental Figure 1A). Interestingly, heat-induced HSFA2:LUC activity was weaker at 1 mM NO3− than at higher nitrate concentrations (Supplemental Figure 1A). A plausible explanation is that NO3− was depleted under low-nitrate conditions, although it is difficult to quantify residual NO3– in the medium. Regardless of whether NO3− was fully depleted at a 1 mM concentration, exogenous NO3− application enhanced heat-induced HSFA2:LUC expression (Supplemental Figure 1A). HSFA2:LUC luminescence signals remained low and unchanged when the HSFA2:LUC plants were kept at 22°C (Supplemental Figure 1A). The LUC signal itself (in the 35S:LUC transgenic plants) did not change significantly at these concentrations (Supplemental Figure 1B), indicating that the observed HSFA2:LUC dynamics were not caused by changes in luciferase enzyme activity in response to temperature or nitrate treatment. To further clarify the role of NO3− in enhancing heat-induced HSFA2:LUC expression, we examined HSFA2:LUC reporter activity under heat stress in seedlings supplied with different nitrogen sources (NO3− or glutamine [Gln]). Under equimolar nitrogen concentrations, HSFA2:LUC induction by heat stress was significantly higher in NO3−-cultured seedlings than in those grown with Gln (Supplemental Figure 1C). These results demonstrate that nitrate upregulates the expression of heat-responsive genes and likely contributes to plant thermotolerance.

To investigate how nitrate regulates thermotolerance, we examined the thermotolerance phenotypes of mutants for two known nitrate sensors in Arabidopsis, NITRATE TRANSPORTER1/PEPTIDE TRANSPORTER FAMILY6.3 (NPF6.3/CHL1) and NIN-LIKE PROTEIN 7 (NLP7) (Ho et al., 2009; Liu et al., 2022). Two independent nlp7 mutant alleles were hypersensitive to heat, whereas NLP7-overexpressing plants exhibited enhanced thermotolerance (Figure 1A and 1B and Supplemental Figure 2). Moreover, genetic complementation lines (NLP7-GFP/nlp7-1) fully rescued the reduced thermotolerance of the nlp7-1 mutant (Figure 1A and 1B and Supplemental Figure 2). However, the thermotolerance phenotypes of chl1 null mutants were comparable to those of wild-type plants (Supplemental Figure 3). These results demonstrate that NLP7-mediated, but not CHL1-mediated, NO3− signaling makes a positive contribution to plant thermotolerance. Because plant thermotolerance is enhanced by NO3− and positively regulated by NLP7, we hypothesized that NLP7-mediated NO3− signaling regulates plant thermotolerance. We analyzed the thermotolerance phenotypes of nlp7-1 and wild-type plants grown with or without NO3−. Improved recovery of wild-type plants grown under different NO3− concentrations was observed only when plants were supplied with optimal NO3− levels, indicating that exogenous application of NO3− at optimal concentrations enhances plant thermotolerance (Supplemental Figure 4). By contrast, the nlp7-1 mutants exhibited much greater sensitivity than the wild type only when grown with more than 1 mM of NO3− (Supplemental Figure 4). When ammonium (NH4+) was provided as the sole nitrogen source, nlp7 and Col-0 showed comparable thermotolerance (Supplemental Figure 4). These results indicate that NLP7 plays a positive role in NO3−-upregulated thermotolerance.

To determine how high temperature regulates NLP7 activity, we measured NLP7 transcript and protein levels under heat stress. However, high temperatures did not significantly affect NLP7 transcript levels or protein accumulation (Supplemental Figure 5A and 5B). We then examined the subcellular localization of NLP7-GFP in response to high-temperature treatment in NLP7-GFP/nlp7-1 transgenic plants grown on medium with NO3− or ammonium as the nitrogen source. When seedlings were continuously grown on medium with ammonium at 22°C, the NLP7-GFP fluorescence signals were mainly localized in the cytoplasm (Figure 1C). However, upon exposure to 37°C for 15 min, NLP7-GFP fluorescence was detected in both the cytoplasm and nuclei, and the nuclear fluorescence of NLP7-GFP was more pronounced after high-temperature treatment for 30 min (Figure 1C). When seedlings were grown continuously on medium with KNO3, strong NLP7-GFP fluorescence signals were observed primarily in the nuclei after 15 min of 37°C treatment (Figure 1C). We also assessed the subcellular localization of NLP7-GFP in seedlings subjected to nitrogen starvation followed by treatment with both heat and NO3− and found that heat stress induced nuclear accumulation of NLP7-GFP independently of NO3− availability (Supplemental Figure 5C). These results indicate that heat stress promotes the translocation of NLP7 from the cytoplasm to the nuclei independently of NO3− availability. To confirm these results, we extracted nuclear and cytoplasmic proteins from NLP7-GFP/nlp7-1 plants grown on medium containing either NO3− or ammonium under heat-treatment conditions. We observed increased NLP7-GFP protein accumulation in nuclei isolated from 37°C-treated plants in both the presence and absence of NO3− (Figure 1D), suggesting that heat promotes the nuclear translocation of NLP7 proteins.

To characterize the role of NLP7 in heat-induced global transcriptomic changes, we performed RNA sequencing of nlp7-1 mutants and wild-type seedlings under heat treatment. A total of 3003 genes were modulated by high temperature in wild-type plants (fold change ≥ 2 and false discovery rate < 0.01) (Figure 1E and Supplemental Table 1), and we defined these as heat-stress-responsive (HSR) genes. We identified 1078 differentially expressed genes between nlp7-1 and the wild type under heat stress (Figure 1E and Supplemental Table 1), 306 of which were identified as NLP7-regulated HSR genes (Figure 1E and Supplemental Table 1). Gene Ontology analysis suggested that the NLP7-regulated genes were not only involved in heat stress but also participated in other processes, including light signaling and responses to cold stress, salt stress, and phytohormones (Figure 1F). Hierarchical clustering indicated that some of these genes were involved in heat stress, whereas others encoded molecular chaperones involved in protein folding (Figure 1G). A number of these genes have previously been identified as master transcription factors in the heat-stress response, including HEAT SHOCK TRANSCRIPTION FACTOR A3 (HSFA3), and others encode heat shock proteins, such as HEAT STRESS ASSOCIATED 32 (HSA32) and BOBBER1 (BOB1) (Schramm et al., 2008; Wu et al., 2013; Friedrich et al., 2021). Thus, NLP7 is a pivotal transcription factor that coordinates the transcriptome to regulate plant resilience in response to heat stress.

To further pinpoint the genome-wide targets of NLP7, we performed chromatin immunoprecipitation followed by sequencing using NLP7-GFP/nlp7-1 transgenic seedlings subjected to heat stress. We identified 4893 genes bound by NLP7, with more than 80% of the peaks located within promoter regions (Figure 1H and Supplemental Table 2). Among these NLP7-bound genes, 121 were classified as NLP7-regulated HSR genes (Figure 1I and Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). By comparing the NLP7-bound and NLP7-regulated HSR genes, we found that HSFA3 and BOB1 were directly bound and regulated by NLP7 (Figure 1J and Supplemental Tables 1 and 2). The transcript levels of HSFA3, its downstream target HSA32, and BOB1 in the nlp7-1 mutant were about 30% of those in the wild type (Figure 1K–1M). Previous studies have shown that hsfa3 mutants show defects in both basal and acquired thermotolerance (Schramm et al., 2008; Friedrich et al., 2021). As a key transcription factor in the heat response, HSFA3 is activated by DREB2A, which itself is regulated by HSFA1A, DREB2B, and DREB2C (Schramm et al., 2008; Friedrich et al., 2021). By analyzing the enriched motifs bound by NLP7 within genomic regions, we identified the element 5′-xxAA(G/A, A/G)GxC(A/G)-3′, which is highly similar to reported NLP7-bound motifs in response to cold stress (Figure 1N). However, the HSR genes and cold-responsive genes associated with NLP7 are largely distinct (Supplemental Table 1) (Ding et al., 2022). Only eight genes (6.6% of NLP7-regulated HSR genes) are common targets of NLP7 in response to both temperature stresses. It would be interesting to characterize the mechanisms by which NLP7 distinguishes cold and heat signals and regulates the expression of specific downstream target genes.

In conclusion, we identified a previously unrecognized function of NLP7, which undergoes nuclear translocation in response to heat stress, positively regulates the expression of HSFA3 and BOB1, and enhances Arabidopsis thermotolerance in a partially nitrate-dependent manner. Moreover, NLP7 underwent nucleocytoplasmic shuttling under heat stress even in the absence of NO3−, indicating that NLP7 regulates thermotolerance through additional mechanisms. Gln also regulates heat-induced HSFA2:LUC expression, suggesting its involvement in thermotolerance, although the underlying mechanisms require further investigation. Our work thus confirms the experiences of Chinese farmers in coping with high temperatures and provides insight into the underlying mechanisms. We believe that further investigation of traditional information passed down through generations by farmers will be an effective supplement to modern biological approaches for combating climate change.
