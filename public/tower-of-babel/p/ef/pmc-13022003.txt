Regulating complexity in AI-enabled omics and multi-omics technologies for precision medicine
Authors: Cindy Welzel, Gökhan Ertaylan, Irina S Babina, Stephen Gilbert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe convergence of (multi-)omics and artificial intelligence is transforming precision medicine by enabling data-driven diagnostics, treatment prediction, and patient stratification. Yet, regulatory frameworks lag behind this innovation. This commentary discusses emerging issues around data integrity, algorithm transparency, validation and real-world evidence integration, highlighting gaps between device and pharmaceutical regulations. Drawing on examples of regulatory-approved AI-enabled (multi-)omics tools in the EU and US, we explore evolving regulatory pathways shaping the clinical adoption of next-generation tools for precision medicine.Subject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Medical research

Introduction

The convergence of multi-omics and multi-parameter omics (many parameters measured within a single omics layer) technologies with artificial intelligence (AI) is widely regarded as a cornerstone of next-generation precision medicine1. By integrating genomic, transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, microbiome, and digital biomarker data, AI-enabled (multi-)omics platforms promise to move clinical decision-making beyond isolated biomarkers toward systems-level insights1–3. Such approaches hold particular potential for early diagnosis4, treatment response prediction5, and patient stratification6, as they can capture nonlinear biological interactions that are difficult to resolve using traditional statistical methods1.

Despite rapid technological progress, however, the translation of AI-enabled (multi-)omics into routine clinical practice remains uneven1,7. At the core of this tension lies a structural shift: regulatory systems were originally built for static, single-analyte diagnostic tests with clearly defined inputs and outputs, whereas today’s AI-enabled (multi-)omics platforms increasingly function as evolving, data-intensive systems that integrate multiple biological layers and generate model-derived predictions7,8. Oversight frameworks designed for discrete assays are now confronted with adaptive, platform-like technologies that blur boundaries between diagnostics, prognostics, and treatment guidance.

This mismatch helps explain why regulatory pathways often struggle to accommodate AI-enabled (multi-)omics tools. While algorithmic capabilities and data availability continue to expand, existing regulatory frameworks do not readily capture the complexity, modularity, and lifecycle evolution of these systems7,8. This tension is already visible in current market examples. The Dante Citizen Test represents a CE-marked in vitro diagnostic (IVD) whole genome sequencing assay integrated into clinical reporting in Europe, reflecting how individual omics technologies can be accommodated within existing device frameworks9. By contrast, more integrative AI-enabled multi-omics platforms such as REVEAL or FarrSight®-Twin combine genomics with additional molecular and clinical data to generate systems-level predictions, yet remain designated for research use only (RUO)10,11. As a result, a growing gap has emerged between methodological innovation and regulatory readiness (Fig. 1). In the following sections, we unpack the sources of this gap and explore emerging regulatory strategies aimed at aligning oversight with the realities of next-generation precision medicine.

This commentary primarily draws on regulatory developments in the European Union and the United States, where most formal guidance on AI-enabled medical technologies is currently emerging. The examples discussed focus largely on oncology and chronic disease applications, reflecting areas in which (multi-)omics integration and AI-driven clinical decision support are most advanced. While similar challenges are likely to arise in other regions and disease domains, the regulatory signals highlighted here are grounded in these contexts.

Regulatory challenges: when frameworks designed for single-analyte tests meet multi-modal AI

The regulatory challenges associated with AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies arise from a structural mismatch between existing regulatory assumptions and the technical reality of these systems (Table 1). Current medical device and in vitro diagnostic frameworks were largely designed for tests with clearly defined, static inputs and outputs, where analytical and clinical performance can be assessed at the level of individual analytes12,13. In contrast, (multi-)omics AI systems integrate multiple data points and multiple heterogeneous data layers into composite, model-derived outputs, fundamentally challenging these assumptions.

Key regulatory challenges for AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies

A primary challenge relates to data integrity and provenance. AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies depend on diverse data types generated across different platforms, laboratories, and time points, each associated with distinct sources of technical and biological variability. Inadequate control or documentation of pre-analytical factors, batch effects, and metadata consistency can propagate uncertainty throughout the analytical pipeline, complicating downstream performance assessment and traceability1. Closely linked to this is the challenge of validation. Traditional validation concepts assume that analytical accuracy and clinical performance can be independently established for each analyte or test component. For AI-enabled (multi-)omics systems, however, predictive performance emerges from interactions between features across molecular layers rather than from individual measurements. A related challenge also arises for AI-enabled single-omics applications, where models may be trained on thousands of molecular features derived from a single omics modality, such as genome-wide sequencing, transcriptome-wide expression profiles, or high-plex proteomics. In these cases, clinical performance similarly reflects complex, multivariate feature representations rather than the behavior of individual parameters. As a result, defining appropriate reference standards, ground truth, and validation endpoints becomes increasingly difficult, particularly when outputs are probabilistic or intended for risk stratification rather than binary classification14. Intended use drives evidence, hence without clarifying intended use (i.e., screening vs triage vs therapy), it is not possible to map risk to evidence.

Algorithm transparency and interpretability represent an additional regulatory hurdle. Many (multi-)omics AI models rely on complex, nonlinear architectures that are not readily interpretable, limiting insight into how individual biological signals contribute to a given prediction. This opacity complicates regulatory assessment, especially in clinical contexts where understanding model behavior and failure modes is essential for patient safety15. Another challenge concerns robustness over time. (Multi-)omics data distributions may shift as laboratory protocols evolve, patient populations change, or new clinical practices are adopted. Static regulatory approvals offer limited mechanisms to account for such temporal variability, creating uncertainty around long-term performance and reliability12. Finally, regulatory boundary ambiguities pose a persistent challenge. AI-enabled (multi-)omics tools increasingly inform treatment selection, dosing decisions, or expected therapeutic response, positioning them at the interface between diagnostics, prognostics, and therapeutics. However, device and pharmaceutical regulatory pathways remain largely separated, resulting in fragmented evidence requirements and uncertainty for developers navigating overlapping domains16. A practical example of this boundary blurring can be seen in AI-enabled genomic and multi-parameter tests that function as companion or predictive diagnostics in oncology. Assays such as comprehensive genomic profiling tests used to guide targeted therapies or immunotherapies do not merely provide diagnostic information, but directly inform treatment eligibility, drug selection, or expected response. In such cases, the evidentiary expectations for a diagnostic device intersect with those traditionally applied to therapeutics, yet regulatory pathways for devices and drugs remain largely separate, creating uncertainty around appropriate validation standards and co-development requirements17.

Emerging regulatory strategies supporting AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies

One approach is separating platform assurance/certification from test or pipeline validation, which aims to reduce regulatory complexity without lowering safety. In practice, this means distinguishing between the technical infrastructure and the clinical application built on top of it. A practical way to operationalize oversight for AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies could be to treat the overall system as two coupled but distinguishable layers. The first is the computational platform layer (data ingestion, storage, compute environment, software lifecycle controls, Machine Learning Operations (MLOps), cybersecurity, audit trails, and change management). This layer ensures that the system runs reliably and securely. The second is the analytical pipeline/clinical application layer (sample-to-result workflow, feature generation and harmonization across omics modalities, the trained model, reporting logic, and the intended use and clinical claims). This layer determines the clinical meaning and risk of the output. This separation matters because many failure modes, such as reproducibility gaps, version drift, inadequate logging, or insecure deployment, originate in the platform even when the underlying biomarker model is sound. Conversely, the clinical risks associated with false positives or negatives, subgroup underperformance, and unclear clinical utility primarily arise from how the application or pipeline is defined and validated. A layered approach, therefore, enables regulators and developers to build confidence in each layer independently, while still validating the complete system for its intended use.

One central strategy gaining momentum is the expanded use of real-world evidence (RWE). For complex AI systems operating on heterogeneous biological data, evidence generated during routine clinical use can reveal performance variability, subgroup effects, and safety across diverse populations and settings. These are failure modes that are often invisible in controlled pre-market studies. Therefore, RWE is increasingly viewed as a complementary component to pre-market evaluation rather than being confined to post-market surveillance, requiring structured data capture, longitudinal outcome linkage, and predefined performance monitoring metrics18. Another emerging approach is platform-level evaluation, in which regulatory assessment focuses on the underlying analytical or AI framework rather than on individual biomarkers or outputs. This strategy acknowledges that many (multi-)omics systems are modular and extensible, with shared computational infrastructure supporting multiple clinical applications. Evidence requirements, therefore, emphasize core platform characteristics, such as data handling, model architecture, and validation principles, rather than repeated, output-specific re-certification. To address the evolution of AI models after deployment, Predetermined Change Control Plans (PCCPs; pre-agreed rules describing how an approved model may be updated and how safety and performance will be maintained) have been proposed as a mechanism to define, in advance, which types of model modifications are permissible and how they will be assessed. PCCPs operationalize evidence generation around controlled updates, including retraining criteria, acceptance thresholds, and post-update verification. This enables limited adaptation while maintaining regulatory oversight and without requiring full re-certification19. More broadly, dynamic algorithm regulation has emerged as a lifecycle-oriented framework for overseeing AI systems whose behavior may change over time. Rather than relying on one-time certification, this approach requires ongoing performance monitoring, drift detection, and risk-based intervention thresholds. Therefore, oversight becomes continuous rather than episodic, aligning regulatory supervision with the adaptive nature of modern AI technologies.

A significant regulatory signal for AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies arrived in January 2026 with the FDA’s draft guidance, ‘Use of Bayesian Methodology in Clinical Trials’20. For the first time, the regulator explicitly endorsed the use of informative priors (structured assumptions derived from existing biological knowledge or previous data) and complex simulations to support primary evidence of effectiveness, particularly where patient populations are limited. This is especially relevant for rare molecular subgroups where large trials are often not feasible. Importantly, this shift signals regulatory openness to simulation- and prior-based evidence. It creates a unique opening for mechanistic AI models, which use established biological ‘priors’ to simulate patient outcomes rather than learning from scratch like traditional deep learning. In this context, regulators may evaluate not only predictive performance, but also the transparency and plausibility of the underlying assumptions. By validating the methodology of the simulation (checking the priors) rather than just the final output, regulators are moving toward a framework where in silico digital twins can formally de-risk or augment clinical trials. Furthermore, this approach directly addresses the validation bottleneck by shifting the focus from ‘black box’ accuracy to the transparency and interpretability of the underlying biological assumptions. Additional strategies focus on transparency and reproducibility. Plausible mechanism frameworks encourage alignment between AI outputs and established biological knowledge, supporting interpretability in high-dimensional (multi-)omics models21. Federated learning and privacy-preserving analytics enable model development and validation across distributed datasets without centralizing sensitive data, while digital evidence ecosystems provide standardized infrastructures for benchmarking, documentation, and performance tracking22. Altogether, these also contribute to measuring of a liability risk: if an AI-enabled (multi-)omics tool provides an interpretable rationale (e.g., linking a specific transcriptomic signature to a known drug mechanism), liability for the final clinical decision should unambiguously rest with the clinician. This encourages developers to build interpretable ‘glass box’ systems rather than opaque ‘black box’ oracles, aligning technical safety with legal certainty.

In parallel, policy-level instruments are emerging to facilitate early interaction between innovators and regulators for complex AI-enabled biomedical technologies. Regulatory sandboxes, as promoted under the EU AI Act and increasingly discussed for digital health and biotechnology applications, provide controlled environments in which novel AI-enabled (multi-)omics systems can be tested under regulatory supervision before full market authorization23. Such mechanisms are particularly relevant for (multi-)omics platforms, where evidence generation, validation strategies, and clinical integration often evolve iteratively. Complementing these efforts, the proposed EU Biotech Act aims to strengthen Europe’s biotechnology ecosystem by streamlining regulatory pathways, supporting innovation-friendly frameworks, and improving coordination across sectors24. In this context, collaborative initiatives such as the EU-funded BRIDGE project - an Innovative Health Initiative public-private partnership developing a flexible, evidence‑based framework and related operating modalities for regulatory sandboxes to support the safe and timely integration of emerging health technologies and breakthrough methodologies - illustrate how coordinated, cross-sector efforts can enhance regulatory readiness alongside ongoing technical and policy innovation25.

Together, these initiatives reflect a shift toward adaptive regulatory frameworks that support the clinical translation of AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies while maintaining patient safety and trust.

Examples of emerging AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies

Despite regulatory hurdles, a limited number of AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies have achieved certification or regulatory clearance, demonstrating that elements of this innovation are beginning to reach clinical practice. At the same time, commercially available AI-driven analytics platforms such as Paradigm4’s REVEAL10 illustrate the growing maturity of (multi-)omics data integration in translational and discovery research, enabling population-scale analyses, hypothesis generation, and exploratory patient stratification. However, despite their use in clinically oriented research and pharmaceutical development, these platforms are currently designated for RUO and are not approved as regulated medical devices or in vitro diagnostics. The Dante Citizen Test represents one of the first examples of a clinically approved whole genome sequencing (WGS) assay with full CE-IVD certification in Europe, marking a transition from RUO genomics toward regulated in vitro diagnostic use at national healthcare system level9. Adjacent developments such as multi-cancer early detection (MCED) tests based on cell-free DNA and machine-learning classifiers further exemplify the translational ambition of AI-enabled molecular diagnostics; some of these tests, including GRAIL’s Galleri26, are clinically available by prescription as laboratory-developed tests (LDTs), yet remain outside formal regulatory approval pathways for population-level screening. Hence, today, most approvals attach to narrowly scoped intended uses; generalizable (multi-)omics platforms remain mainly RUO.

Future perspectives: toward regulatory readiness for (multi-)omics AI

Looking ahead, the responsible clinical adoption of AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies will require coordinated progress across scientific, regulatory, and institutional domains. Greater harmonization between device and pharmaceutical regulations will be essential to address tools that function simultaneously as diagnostics, predictors, and treatment-guiding systems.

From an evidence perspective, regulators will need to further operationalize lifecycle-based evaluation models, integrating RWE, adaptive validation, and continuous performance monitoring into routine oversight. Advances in digital infrastructure, including interoperable data standards and digital evidence ecosystems, could play a pivotal role in enabling such approaches at scale.

Finally, trust in AI-enabled (multi-)omics systems will depend not only on technical performance but also on transparency, interpretability, and clinical integration. Embedding human oversight, ensuring usability within clinical workflows, and maintaining clear accountability structures will be critical for sustainable adoption.

Conclusion

AI-enabled (multi-)omics technologies challenge not only existing diagnostic paradigms but also the regulatory frameworks that govern their clinical translation. While early examples demonstrate that regulatory approval is feasible, most current platforms remain confined to research or operate in regulatory gray zones. Bridging this gap will require flexible, lifecycle-oriented oversight mechanisms that balance innovation with patient safety. If successfully implemented, such approaches can enable the responsible deployment of next-generation precision medicine tools and unlock the full clinical potential of (multi-)omics and AI.
