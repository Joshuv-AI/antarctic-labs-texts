Photoactivatable, Biomimetic Ligand photoMultiTASQ Traps DNA/RNA G‑Quadruplexes and Their Protein Binding Partners
Authors: Sandy Raevens, Yehuda M. Danino, Cécile Desingle, Francesco Rota Sperti, Marc Pirrotta, Lucie Mazzucotelli, Léa Letissier, Eran Hornstein, Sarah Cianferani, Oscar Hernandez-Alba, Ibai E. Valverde, David Monchaud
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The biological role of DNA and RNA G-quadruplexes (G4s) is relayed to the cellular circuitry by a plethora of proteins known as G4-binding proteins (G4PBs). It is thus critical to decipher the formation of the G4/G4BP complexes to accurately understand their involvement in G4-mediated regulatory mechanisms. While hundreds of G4-interacting compounds (G4 ligands) have been used to uncover G4 biology in a rather indirect manner, only a handful of multivalent ligands allowing for identifying associated proteins have been designed, mostly relying on the covalent capture of G4BPs upon photoactivation. We report herein on such a multivalent ligand named photoMultiTASQ, which belongs to a family of biomimetic and smart G4 ligands known as template-assembled synthetic G-quartets (TASQs). We show how photoMultiTASQ interacts with and photo-cross-links both DNA/RNA G4s and related G4BPs, and develop a new mass spectrometry (MS)-based technique to characterize the covalent adducts. Collectively, these results make photoMultiTASQ a new molecular tool in the arsenal of chemical cross-linking and isolation by pull-down (Chem-CLIP) technologies that uniquely identify targets and validate target engagement in cells.

Introduction

DNA and RNA G-quadruplexes (G4s) are noncanonical nucleic acid structures involved in many key cellular processes (such as gene expression and chromatin organization), which place them as ideal drug targets for therapeutic interventions in many genetic diseases including neurodegeneration and cancers. An efficient strategy to probe the cellular biology of G4s (Figure ) − is a chemical biology approach , relying on the use of cell-permeable small molecules targeting G4s specifically, or G4 ligands. Simple (i.e., nonfunctionalized) G4 ligands were used to both assess their therapeutic potential, mostly as anticancer agents, , and gain insights into their cellular activity. − More recently, multivalent (i.e., functionalized) ligands were developed for both interacting with cellular G4s and being manipulated once in their binding sites (i.e., in situ). This development allowed for rapid progress in measuring the extent to which G4s contribute to cellular biology at multiple levels.

These advances have been made possible mostly thanks to the opportunities opened up by the recent development of bioorthogonal chemistry, notably biocompatible click chemistry covering both copper-catalyzed or strain-promoted alkyne–azide cycloaddition (CuAAC and SPAAC, respectively). − Clickable G4 ligands were used, for instance, to visualize G4s in human cells when conjugated with fluorophores − and to isolate them by affinity precipitation when conjugated to a biotin handle. More recently, a second-generation handle was used, comprising both an alkyne moiety for click chemistry and a diazirine moiety for capturing G4-interacting partners upon photoactivation. Given the central role of G4-binding proteins (G4BPs) in the biological activity of G4s, as they relay the information on G4 folding to the whole cell circuitry, , it is critical to develop such multivalent molecular tools. To date, only two prototypes of such multivalent ligands have been designed and used for G4 proteomics, photoPDS used in the cobinding-mediated protein profiling (CMPP) protocol, and PDB-DA-A used in the G4 ligand-mediated cross-linking and pull-down (G4-LIMCAP) protocol. These two ligands are derivatives of pyridostatin (PDS), by virtue of its well-established high G4 affinity, derivatized with a handle containing both a diazirine and a terminal alkyne. However, we believe that beyond affinity, the selectivity for G4s is the most critical parameter. This explains why we report herein on multivalent biomimetic G4 ligands from the template-assembled synthetic G-quartet (TASQ) family, which are actively selective for G4s thanks to a G4-mediated conformational switch that governs both G4 affinity and selectivity.

Briefly, TASQs are made of four guanine (G) units held together by a central template (Figure ), in our hands mostly the water-soluble cyclen. Several features make TASQs quite efficient ligands: (i) their G4-binding properties are driven by a highly favorable interaction between two G-quartets (one from the TASQ, one from the G4), making them biomimetic ligands; (ii) they are structurally labile and their G4-binding conformation (in which the intermolecular G-quartet is formed) is promoted by their interaction with G4s only, making them smart G4 ligands; and (iii) this conformational selection concomitantly ensures a very high level of G4 selectivity, making them uniquely actively selective for their targets.

We thus describe herein the synthesis and in vitro study of photoMultiTASQ, a new photoactivatable and clickable G4 ligand. This molecule aims at expanding the scope of application of TASQ probes, two of which being commercially available, the biotinylated BioCyTASQ, an analogue of BioTASQ that was used for fishing G4s out of cell lysates, , and the clickable MultiTASQ, used for both in situ G4-click imaging and click chemo-precipitation of G4s, or G4-click-CP. To this end, the central cyclen template of the first TASQ prototypes was changed for an aminomethyl cyclen (AMC), which allowed for the introduction of various handles (e.g., a biotin for BioTASQ and BioCyTASQ, an alkyne side arm for MultiTASQ). G4-click-CP globally belongs to the family of chemical cross-linking and isolation by pull-down (Chem-CLIP) techniques, − now widely used to profile ligand/RNA interactions in human cells. However, in our previous studies, the G4 target was not covalently bound by TASQ prior to isolation. To further abide by the definition of Chem-CLIP, and in turn improve the overall efficiency of G4-click-CP, TASQs thus need to be appended to a cross-linking module. The design of photoMultiTASQ, which harbors a diazirine handle, follows this strategy: it relies on the introduction of a 3-(3-(but-3-yn-1-yl)-3H-diazirin-3-yl)­propanoic acid handle, which makes it both a photoactivatable and clickable ligand. We show here how it interacts with both DNA/RNA G4s and G4BPs, and what is the added value of its diazirine appendage. This new TASQ is thus fully applicable for Chem-CLIP investigations and, in doing so, reinforces the arsenal of TASQs usable as molecular tools for G4 chemical biology investigations, from which to select the best-suited tool (all TASQs interact with G4s in a similar manner; they only vary by the nature of their reactive handle) for the intended application.

Synthesis and Characterization of PhotoMultiTASQ

The synthesis of photoMultiTASQ from AMC is directly inspired by that of previous multivalent TASQs by swapping the biotin (BioCyTASQ), 5-hexynoic acid (MultiTASQ) or 6-azido-hexanoic acid handle (azMultiTASQ) for a 3-(3-(but-3-yn-1-yl)-3H-diazirin-3-yl)­propanoic acid side arm. As seen in Scheme A, the first step is performed using N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyl-O-(N-succinimidyl)­uronium tetrafluoroborate (TSTU) as a coupling agent and N,N-diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) as a base to lead to compound 1 (39% chemical yield). The four arms were introduced using an excess of tert-butyl (5-bromopentyl)­carbamate (2, 8 molar equiv) and K2CO3 as a base to lead to compound 3 (52% chemical yield). The four primary amines were then deprotected by trifluoroacetic acid (TFA) (compound 4, quantitative) and subsequently coupled with Boc-PNAG-OH using N,N,N′,N′-tetramethyl-O-(1H-benzotriazol-1-yl)­uronium hexafluorophosphate (HBTU) as a coupling agent and triethylamine (TEA) as a base to lead to protected photoMultiTASQ (25% chemical yield). A final deprotection of the four Boc protecting groups by TFA (quantitative) led to photoMultiTASQ with a rather low overall chemical yield (5% over 5 steps, on a ca. 50-mg scale), which mostly originates in the loss of material inherent to systematic high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) purifications. As a precautionary measure, all of these steps were performed with protection from light.

The reactivity of the alkyne appendage was next verified by engaging photoMultiTASQ in a coupling reaction with a commercially available azide-containing fluorophore (AF594-azide), and the corresponding conjugate was conveniently used in subsequent experiments. CuAAC was carried out using CuSO4 as a source of Cu­(II) in combination with both sodium ascorbate as a reductant and tris­(3-hydroxypropyltriazolylmethyl)­amine (THPTA) as a Cu­(I) ligand (Scheme B). The resulting conjugate 6 was isolated with 72% chemical yield after HPLC purification (unoptimized); of note, a similar reaction was performed with MultiTASQ to provide 7 with 78% chemical yield. We point out that both 6 and 7 were obtained as stable Cu­(II) complexes and, even if a demetalation is possible (and has already been implemented, , by Na2S treatment), we decided to use the metal complexes in further experiments, since we already showed that metalated and nonmetalated TASQ complexes behave alike. This is mainly motivated by the fact that sulfide-mediated demetalation, which relies on the formation of insoluble CuS and a series of precipitation and washing steps, could lead to a loss of TASQ conjugates.

G4-Interacting Properties of PhotoMultiTASQ

The G4-binding properties of photoMultiTASQ were next assessed implementing both fluorescence energy resonance transfer (FRET)-melting assays and fluorescence quenching assays (FQA). These experiments were performed with well-studied fluorescently labeled G4-forming oligonucleotides (Table ) including the telomeric DNA and RNA , sequences (F21T and F-TERRA-T for FRET-melting; Cy5-22AG and Cy5-TERRA for FQA) and the sequences found in the promoter region of Myc gene (F-Myc-T and Cy5-Myc) and in the untranslated region (5′-UTR) of NRAS mRNA (F-NRAS-T and Cy5-NRAS).

Using the FRET-melting experiments, photoMultiTASQ imparts a higher thermal stabilization to both DNA and RNA G4s than MultiTASQ, used as a control, with ΔT 1/2 values of 17.9 ± 2.1 (F21T), 19.9 ± 0.6 (F-TERRA-T), 15.4 ± 0.1 (F-Myc-T) and 12.8 ± 1.5 °C (F-NRAS-T) versus 11.8 ± 0.7 (F21T), 17.7 ± 0.8 (F-TERRA-T), 9.7 ± 0.2 (F-Myc-T) and 10.3 ± 1.2 °C (F-NRAS-T) for MultiTASQ (Figure A and Table ). The G4 selectivity of photoMultiTASQ was also confirmed as no F-duplex-T stabilization is observed (ΔT 1/2 < 1 °C). This behavior was further illustrated by competitive experiments (Table ) performed with F21T against an excess of the duplex ds26, during which excellent FRET S values (with FRET S = ΔT 1/2(+ds26)/ΔT 1/2(−ds26)) were obtained (i.e., 0.91 and 0.83 in the presence of 15 and 50 molar equiv ds26, respectively). We also partly confirmed these results by FQA experiments showing app K D in the micromolar and submicromolar range with values of 2.09 ± 0.37 (Cy5-22AG), 1.15 ± 0.29 (Cy5-TERRA), 1.21 ± 0.10 (Cy5-Myc) and 0.61 ± 0.03 μM (Cy5-NRAS) versus 3.71 ± 0.60 (Cy5-22AG), 2.73 ± 1.29 (Cy5-TERRA), 0.66 ± 0.11 (Cy5-Myc) and 0.48 ± 0.05 μM (Cy5-NRAS) for MultiTASQ (Figure B and Table ). Collectively, the G4 affinity of the two TASQs is good and fully in line with previously reported TASQs, the discrepancy between FRET-melting and FQA results likely originates in the difference in the experimental setups (variable-temperature versus isothermal assays).

Photoactivation of PhotoMultiTASQ

The reactivity of the diazirine moiety was next verified by irradiating photoMultiTASQ at 365 nm for 20 min in a PhotoRedOx Box (EvoluChem) equipped with a water chiller (15 °C). We followed the disappearance of the starting material by reverse phase HPLC (RP-HPLC), and we observed that photoirradiation led to the formation of a species with a mass of 1827 Da, as determined by high-resolution mass spectrometry (MS) (see the Supporting Information). This is consistent with the formation of an alkene moiety, which indicates that the transiently formed carbene might have undergone a 1,2-hydride shift to yield an olefin (or a mixture of olefins), and confirms the reactivity of the diazirine. ,

With both the G4 interaction and photoactivation being established, we thus engaged photoMultiTASQ in a series of photocapture experiments, using 22AG as a model of G4 and BG4 , as a model of G4BP. Indeed, in the CMPP protocol, the photoactivation of photoPDS was assessed by a gel shift assay using Myc as a G4 (2.2 μM) and BG4 as a G4BP (0.5 μM) upon irradiation at 365 nm for 10 min on ice in the presence of photoPDS (10 μM), which was then conjugated with AF594-azide by CuAAC for in-gel fluorescence scanning. It has to be noted that these experiments focused on the photocapture of BG4 (ca. 31 kDa), and that no such validation was reported for characterizing the photoactivation of PDB-DA-A in the G4-LIMCAP protocol.

We thus decided to validate the use of photoMultiTASQ in a more straightforward manner using fluorescent photoMultiTASQ derivative 6 (Scheme B). We first checked its ability to interact with G4 by FRET-melting experiments and found that it was poorly active against both DNA and RNA G4s under classical conditions (i.e., 5 molar equiv), with ΔT 1/2 values <4.1 °C (Table ), presumably because of the modification of the overall charge and steric hindrance of the conjugate. Consequently, we used higher concentrations (i.e., 25 and 50 molar equiv) of 6 to reach ΔT 1/2 values up to 11.8 ± 0.9 (F21T), 23.0 ± 1.9 (F-TERRA-T), 13.7 ± 1.2 (F-Myc-T), and 11.1 ± 3.2 °C (F-NRAS-T) at the highest concentration (Table ). Of note, 6 was found to be G4-selective as well, with no interaction with F-duplex-T (ΔT 1/2 < 1 °C) and good FRET S values calculated during competitive experiments performed with F21T against an excess of ds26 (FRET S = 0.83 and 0.75 in the presence of 15 and 50 molar equiv ds26, respectively; Table ).

Next, we performed gel shift experiments using 22AG as a G4 (0.5 μM) and BG4 as a G4BP (0.5 μM) upon irradiation in the presence of different concentrations of 6 (Figure ; 10, 25, and 50 mol equiv). The reagents, including prefolded G4s, were first mixed and steered for 1 h at 25 °C (in the dark) before being irradiated at 365 nm for 20 min at 15 °C. The samples were then heated (8 min at 90 °C), to disassemble non-covalent complexes, and directly loaded in the gels. Using protein-compatible denaturing gels (4–20% Mini-PROTEAN TGX gels), we observed that the G4 was more efficiently photo-cross-linked with 6 than the G4BP (Figure A): the band corresponding to the covalently labeled G4 (<10 kDa, lower-mobility band with respect to unlabeled G4 (gray arrow) in mixture with the excess of 6) was of higher intensity that the band corresponding to the labeled BG4 (ca. 37 kDa, gray arrow). Of course, the cross-linking required irradiation (“+hν”), as these bands were not observed in nonirradiated mixtures (“–hν”).

The preferential photolabeling of G4s versus G4BPs observed here is interesting, as such a comparative evaluation has not yet been done in previous studies. , This likely originates in the close contacts established by the ligand with the G4 (its primary target) versus the G4BP (whose G4 is the primary target). However, this does not preclude the use of photoactivatable ligands for cell-based experiments, as the purification steps implemented involve proteome isolationnotably through enzymatic digestion of nucleic acids using benzonase, for instancebefore MS-based analyses.

The reliability of the weakly shifted upper band seen in Figure A was verified via experiments performed with other G4s (22AG along with TERRA, Myc, and NRAS G4s): a similar band was systematically observed upon irradiation (Figure B), indicating that the photocapture was not G4-dependent. To increase the difference in migration, we then performed this experiment without BG4 using nucleic acid-compatible denaturing gels (TBE-Urea Gels, 15%): a fluorescent, low-mobility band (ca. 25 nucleotides) was clearly distinct from the unlabeled G4/6 mixture (Figure C), and once again observed only upon irradiation. The fluorescence intensity (FI) of the upper band (corresponding to the G4/6 covalent adducts) testifies to an efficiency of the photocapture step rather G4-independent, with 28, 45, 38, and 48% of FI in the upper band (normalized against free 6) in the presence of 50 molar equiv of 6 versus TERRA, NRAS, Myc and 22AG, respectively. As seen in the Supporting Information, this FI is however dependent on the G4/ligand ratio, with a 11, 17, and 44% of FI (normalized against free 6) in the upper band in the presence of 10, 25, and 50 molar equiv of 6 versus 22AG, respectively. As a control, we also showed that the cross-linking required the presence of a diazirine handle, as the characteristic bands were not observed using the nonphotoactivatable 7 conjugate (Scheme and Figure D). Finally, to go a step further toward the use of this TASQ in a complex biological environment, we assessed the ability of 6 to capture G4s and/or G4BPs in a competitive context: gel shift experiments were performed using 22AG (0.5 μM) and BG4 (0.5 μM) upon irradiation in the presence of an excess of DNA fragments (calf thymus DNA, CT-DNA, 25, 50, and 75 μM); as seen in Figure E, the efficiency of the photoproximity labeling is yet impacted but in a limited manner by an excess of DNA competitors (−37% of FI at most in the presence of 150 molar equiv of CT-DNA), making photoMultiTASQ an ideal molecular tool for G4 proteomics investigations.

A recent study of a G4 ligand (6OTD) equipped with another activatable moiety (a benzophenone, Bp) reported on the characterization of the 6OTD-Bp/G4 photoadducts by gel electrophoresis, followed by a MS, here a matrix assisted laser desorption ionization time-of-flight (MALDI-TOF) analysis of the extracted band of interest. This provided a novel detection method of the adduct along with a quantitative analysis of the DNA/ligand complex that results from the capture step, which was not performed during both CMPP and G4-LIMCAP investigations. We developed a methodology to strongly improve the characterization of the final reaction products via the hyphenation of an ion pairing reversed-phase liquid chromatography (IP-RPLC) with a high-resolution MS. This methodology provides a streamlined analysis of the reaction products without prior fractionation, thus increasing the throughput of the analysis with online qualitative and quantitative (relative) information about the resulting products in less than 15 min.

A reaction mixture comprising 22AG and photoMultiTASQ (from 1:1 to 1:10 ratio) was photoirradiated (365 nm, 20 min, 15 °C) and the crude material subsequently analyzed by IP-RPLC-MS (Figure ): two peaks were found, one at 6.9 min that corresponds to unreacted 22AG (M = 6966.1 ± 0.1 Da) and one at 8.7 min that corresponds to the 22AG/photoMultiTASQ covalent adduct (M = 8793.2 Da). According to the experimental mass of the adduct, the reaction between 22AG and photoMultiTASQ with different molar excess systematically leads to the formation of a 1:1 covalent adduct with the corresponding activation (photocleavage) of the diazirine moiety (−28.0 Da). Beyond the demonstration of the actual nature of this covalent complex, this analytical strategy also allows to perform a relative quantification of the cross-linking reaction efficiency by integrating the underneath areas of the UV signals of each individual compound (22AG and 22AG/photoMultiTASQ) obtained within the chromatographic separation. According to this, the cross-linking reaction efficiency varies between 3.3 ± 0.1% (1:1 photoMultiTASQ/22AG ratio) to 11.9 ± 0.6% (1:5 ratio) and 13.0 ± 0.2% (1:10 ratio), thus being in line with the classical photocapture efficiency using diazirine-based chemical probes.

Conclusions

Chemical biology hinges on the use of ever more efficient, accurate, and user-friendly molecular tools to address increasingly complex biological questions in a fast and reliable manner. The last years have witnessed a rapid increase of the pool of available chemicals, notably in the G4 field: these tools have been used for manipulating G4s in their cellular localizations, and biorthogonal chemistry has created new opportunities for visualizing, isolating, and identifying them in a straightforward manner. This has thus helped portray the DNA and RNA G4 biology accurately, which had major implications for better understanding and harnessing G4-associated genetic diseases (chiefly cancers and age-related diseases). For the sake of reliability, the way these molecular tools exert their cellular activity should be precisely established. However, given that they are intended to meet highly technical challenges, the technicality of the requested validations increases concomitantly, so much so that they are often not complete. Herein, we aim at characterizing the way a photoactivatable G4 ligand named photoMultiTASQ behaves when faced with its G4 targets and related proteinic partners (G4BPs). The design of photoMultiTASQ is directly inspired by that of two compounds previously developed to photocapture G4BPs in cells, photoPDS and PDB-DA-A. Both molecules are equipped with the same diazirine-containing handle, obtained after a coupling reaction with 3-(3-(but-3-yn-1-yl)-3H-diazirin-3-yl)­propanoic acid. This choice was dictated by the advantages linked to the use of aliphatic diazirines, chiefly a low steric hindrance and a very high photoactivity, bearing in mind their intrinsic limitations, mostly their rather low photocapture efficiency. This originates in a combination of several factors, including intramolecular olefin formation and fast hydrolysis, being usually ca. 10% (at physiological pH), exceptionally reaching up to 30% efficiency in the case of very strong ligand/biomolecule interactions. The G4-photocapture results that we obtained herein (12–13%) are thus in line with literature precedents. Of note, this low efficiency does not compromise the selectivity of the photocapture, as it is dictated by the ligand/target recognition itself combined with the fast kinetics of the photochemical step (in the nanosecond range), which limits diffusion and thus, random interactions. , It simply results in less abundant but not less selective biomolecule recovery. While the interaction of diazirines with proteins is governed by the preferential carbene’s insertion within polar amino acids (such as tyrosine, glutamic acid, and cysteine), , their interaction with G4s is less studied, as the examples are sparse in the literature. Information has been gained with different photoactive handles and ligands: for instance, 6OTD-Bp (vide supra), exhibiting a benzophenone group, was shown to interact with Gs belonging to the G-quartet core of the telomeric G4; similarly, the azide-containing PDC-N3 reacts exclusively with G-quartets’ Gs, while its Bp counterpart, PDC-Bp, exclusively reacts with Ts in the loops of the telomeric G4; likewise, Pt-ttpy-N3, exhibiting an azide group, was found to covalently interact with Gs localized in the external G-quartet of both the telomeric and Myc G4s. These examples confirm that the different nucleotides of a G4 are possible targets for photocapture but also that the pattern of labeling is strongly G4- and ligand-dependent. Such investigations remain to be performed with photoMultiTASQ. After evaluating its ability to interact with both DNA and RNA G4s through established in vitro assays (both comparative and competitive FRET-melting and FQA), we investigated the way it reacts to light irradiation to covalently interact with its surrounding partners, being either G4 or G4BP. Our effort to precisely determine the chemical nature of these adducts (by both comparative and competitive gel shift assays and IP-RPLC-MS) is essential to provide confidence about the properties of photoMultiTASQ, delineating both its strengths (e.g., a pan-G4 recognition) and its limitations (e.g., a modest yield of photocapture). Our next steps will thus be to better characterize the anchoring sites within G4 structures on one side, and to investigate the cellular properties of photoMutiTASQ, to validate its use for Chem-CLIP investigations (covering chemogenetics, chemotranscriptomics, and chemoproteomics) aimed at both uncovering the interaction networks between G4s and their cellular partners, and the modulation of these networks as a function of the cell status (healthy versus diseased state) and in response to pharmacological treatments.

Compound 1

To a solution of (3-(3-but-3-yn-1-yl)-3H-diazirin-3yl) propanoic acid in dry dimethylformamide (DMF) (149.5 mg, 0.68 M, 0.9 mmol, 0.9 molar equiv), DIPEA (382.0 μL, 3.2 mmol, 3.0 mol equiv) and TSTU (414.7 mg, 1.3 mmol, 1.3 mol equiv) were added. The solution was stirred for 1 h at 25 °C to afford the corresponding activated ester (as monitored by HPLC). The activated ester was added dropwise (1 mL over 3 h) to a solution of (1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecan-2-yl)­methanamine (aminomethyl cyclen, AMC) in dry DMF (213.0 mg, 0.04 M, 1.1 mmol, 1.0 molar equiv) at 25 °C. The reaction conversion was carefully monitored by HPLC-MS and concentrated under reduced pressure upon reaction completion. The residue was purified through flash reversed-phase chromatography (H2O + 0.1% TFA/MeCN 99:1–85:15). The purified product was diluted in a 8 M NaOH solution (30 mL) and extracted 3 times with CH2Cl2 (50 mL), dried, filtered and concentrated under reduced pressure to afford 1 as a brown oil (122 mg, 39%). 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3): δ (ppm) 5.88 (br s, 2H), 5.68–5.41 (m, 16H), 4.85 (br d, J = 2.6 Hz, 1H), 4.60 (br t, J = 7.3 Hz, 2H), 4.51 (br t, J = 7.2 Hz, 2H), 4.32 (dt, J = 2.3, 7.3 Hz, 2H), 4.14 (dt, J = 2.5, 7.2 Hz, 2H). 13C NMR (126 MHz, CDCl3): δ (ppm) 178.4, 86.9, 72.4, 54.3, 48.8, 48.7, 46.9, 46.8, 46.7, 45.1, 44.5, 41.6, 33.5, 32.3, 31.3, 30.1, 14.9. trLCMS: 4.78 min. MS (ESI+): [M + H]+ m/z = 350.3 [M + H]+ (calcd for [C17H32N7O]+: 350.26629). ESI-HRMS: [M + H]+ m/z = 350.26519.

Compound 2 (tert-Butyl (5-Bromopentyl)­carbamate)

Step 1: To a solution of 5-aminopentanol (5.5 g, 53.3 mmol, 1 molar equiv) in CH2Cl2 (250 mL) was added triethylamine (7.9 mL, 58.6 mmol, 1.1 molar equiv), and the solution was cooled down to 0 °C with an ice bath. Di-tert-butyl dicarbonate (12.8 g, 58.6 mmol, 1 molar equiv) was added portionwise, and the solution was warmed up to 25 °C and stirred for 18 h. The reaction mixture was monitored by TLC (CH2Cl2/MeOH 9:1) and concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by silica gel chromatography (CH2Cl2/MeOH 100:0–95:5) to afford 5-((tert-butoxycarbonyl)­amino)­pentanol as a clear oil (9.75 g, 91%). 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3): δ (ppm) 4.56 (s, 1H), 3.63 (dt, J = 6.4, 5.1 Hz, 2H), 3.12 (dt, J = 7.0, 6.3 Hz, 2H), 1.63–1.54 (m, 2H), 1.52–1.47 (m, 2H), 1.43 (s, 9H), 1.42–1.35 (m, 2H). 13C NMR (125 MHz, CDCl3): δ 156.1, 79.1, 62.7, 40.4, 32.2, 29.8, 28.4, 22.9.

Step 2: 5-((tert-Butoxycarbonyl)­amino)­pentanol (1.0 g, 4.92 mmol, 1.0 mol equiv) and triphenylphosphine (1.68 g, 6.40 mmol, 1.3 mol equiv) were dissolved in anhydrous dichloromethane (40 mL). The reaction was cooled down to 0 °C in an ice bath under vigorous stirring. N-Bromosuccinimide (1.14 g, 6.40 mmol, 1.3 mol equiv) was carefully added over 2 h. The reaction mixture was warmed up to 25 °C and kept under vigorous stirring for 18 h. The solution was concentrated under reduced pressure, and the residue was purified by silica gel chromatography (heptane/EtOAc 95:5–85:15) to afford tert-butyl (5-bromopentyl)­carbamate as a pale-yellow oil (6.10 g, 52%). 1H NMR (500 MHz, CDCl3): δ (ppm) 4.52 (s, 1H), 3.40 (t, J = 6.7 Hz, 2H), 3.13 (dt, J = 6.8, 6.0 Hz, 2H), 1.90–1.85 (m, 2H), 1.57–1.46 (m, 4H), 1.44 (s, 9H). 13C NMR (125 MHz, CDCl3): δ 156.1, 79.3, 40.5, 33.8, 32.5, 29.4, 28.6, 25.5. The spectroscopic signals were found to be identical to those found in the literature.

PhotoMultiTASQ

Step 1: To a solution of Boc-PNAG-OH in 350 μL of dry DMF (57.0 mg, 0.4 M, 140.3 μmol, 4.4 molar equiv), triethylamine (25.9 μL, 191.3 μmol, 6.0 mol equiv), and HBTU (53.1 mg, 140.3 μmol, 4.4 mol equiv) were added to afford the corresponding activated ester. The activated ester, triethylamine (25.9 L, 191.3 μmol, 6.0 molar equiv), was then added to a solution of 4 in 318.0 μL dry DMF (22.0 mg, 0.1 M, 32.0 μmol, 1.0 molar equiv). The solution was stirred for 12 h at 25 °C (monitored by HPLC-MS). After the reaction had reached completion, the solution was concentrated under reduced pressure. The residue was purified by HPLC semipreparative (H2O + 0.1% TFA/MeCN 72:28–55:45) to afford the protected photoMultiTASQ­(Boc)4 as a white powder (18.0 mg, 25%). trLCMS: 3.20 min. MS (ESI+): [M + H]2+ m/z = 1128.8 (calcd for [C101H161N39O21]2+: 1128.13591). ESI-HRMS: [M + H]2+ m/z = 1128.13458. Step 2: The previously prepared protected photoMultiTASQ (18.0 mg, 8.0 mmol, 1.0 mol equiv) was dissolved in an excess of TFA and stirred for 30 min (monitored by HPLC-MS). After completion, the mixture was diluted with water and lyophilized twice to afford photoMultiTASQ as a brown oil (14.0 mg, 100%). trLCMS: 0.52 min. MS (ESI+): [M + H]2+ m/z = 1128.9 (calcd for [C81H130N39O13]3+: 619.02313). ESI-HRMS: [M + H]3+ m/z = 619.02258.

General Procedure for CuAAC of Alkyne-Derivatized TASQs with AF594

A 60 μL solution containing CuSO4 (1.9 μL of 300 mM solution in water, 9.1 mM final concentration), tris­(3-hydroxypropyltriazolylmethyl)­amine (THPTA, 12.4 μL of a 300 mM solution in water, 59.9 mM final concentration, 6.6 molar equiv), sodium ascorbate (6.2 μL of 300 mM solution in water, 30 mM final concentration, 3.3 molar equiv), and water (41.5 μL) was briefly stirred to afford the Cu­(I) source. A 120 μL-solution containing the alkynylated TASQ (60 μL of a 1 mM solution in water, 0.5 mM final concentration) and AF594-azide (60 μL of a 1 mM solution in water, 0.5 mM final concentration, 1 mol equiv) was briefly stirred at room temperature. 60 μL of the Cu­(I) solution was added to the 120 μL of the second solution and the final solution was stirred for 1 h at 25 °C. The residue was purified by semipreparative HPLC (H2O + 0.1% TFA/MeCN 88:12–25:75) and lyophilized to afford the TASQ-AF594 conjugates as a blue solid. Compound 6 was synthesized using the general procedure for CuAAC of alkyne-derivatized TASQs with AF594 using photoMultiTASQ as the alkynylated TASQ to afford 6 in 72% yield (0.12 mg). trLCMS: 2.62 min. MS (ESI+): [M + H]3+ m/z = 922.1 (calcd for C122CuH172N45O23S2 3+: 921.8). Compound 7 was synthesized using the general procedure for CuAAC of alkyne-derivatized TASQs with AF594 using MultiTASQ as the alkynylated TASQ to afford 7 in 83% yield (0.14 mg). trLCMS: 2.62 min. MS (ESI+): [M + H]3+ m/z = 904.1 (calcd for C120CuH171N43O23S2 3+: 903.4).

FRET-Melting

A 320 μL-solution containing labeled oligonucleotide (2.56 μL of 25 μM solution, 0.2 μM final concentration), TASQ (3.2 μL of a 100 μM solution in water, 1 μM final concentration, 5 molar equiv or 16 μL of a 100 μM solution in water, 5 μM final concentration, 25 mol equiv or 32 μL of a 100 μM solution in water, 10 μM final concentration, 50 mol equiv) in CacoK10 buffer (10 mM lithium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2),10 mM KCl/90 mM LiCl to F-duplex-T and F21T) or CacoK1 buffer (10 mM lithium cacodylate buffer (pH 7.2), 1 mM KCl/99 mM LiCl to F-NRAS-T, F-Myc-T F-Terra-T) was prepared. Each sample was placed in a 96-well plate (3 × 100 μL). FRET-melting experiments were performed with a Mx3005P qPCR machine (Agilent) with FAM filters (λex = 492 nm; λem = 516 nm). After an initial equilibration step (25 °C, 30 s), a stepwise increase of 1 °C every 30 s for 65 cycles to reach 90 °C was performed, and measurements were made after each cycle. The emission of FAM was normalized (0–1), and T 1/2 was defined as the temperature for which the normalized emission is 0.5.

Fluorescence Quenching Assay

A diluted solution of 200 nM 5′Cy5-ON (ON: 22AG, Myc, TERRA or NRAS) was prepared by diluting the 50 μM stock solution with Tris HCl buffer (50 mM Tris, 150 mM KCl, 0.5% (v/v) Triton, pH 7.2). Diluted solutions of G4 ligands (MultiTASQ and photoMultiTASQ) at various concentrations (see Table S7) were prepared by diluting them with the same previous Tris HCl buffer. Experiments were performed in 96-well plates (chimney well, flat bottom, black plate; Greiner Bio-One cat. no. 655090) at 25 °C using CLARIOstar Plus plate-reader device (BMG LABTECH) equipped with Cy5 filters (λex = 610 nm; λem = 675 nm) in a 100 μL final volume. To 10 μL of the 200 nM 5′Cy5-ON (final concentration: 20 nM) were added 90 μL of the Tris HCl buffer for experiments without G4 ligand (control) or 90 μL of various concentrations of diluted small molecule solutions for experiments with small molecules in technical triplicates. After the plate was mixed at 25 °C for 1 h, protected from light, the Cy5 fluorescence emission was recorded. Final data were analyzed and the app K D was calculated using a sigmoidal fit (dose response) with GraphPad Prism version 9.5.1 for Mac OS (GraphPad software). Duplicates of triplicates were used.

IP-RPLC-MS Analysis of 22AG with PhotoMultiTASQ

A 65 μL solution containing 22AG (3.25 μL of a 100 μM solution, 5 μM final concentration, 1 molar equiv), photoMultiTASQ (1.3 μL of a 250 μM solution in water, 5 μM, 1 molar equiv or 6.5 μL of a 250 μM solution in water, 25 μM, 5 mol equiv or 13 μL of a 250 μM solution in water, 50 μM, 10 molar equiv), ammonium acetate (6.5 μL of a 1 M solution in water, 100 mM final concentration) and H2O was incubated at 25 °C and 350 rpm for 1 h (protected from light). The samples were irradiated under 365 nm light at 15 °C for 20 min and were directly analyzed by IP-RPLC-MS (loaded material, 0.5 μg 22AG). The analyses were carried out using a BioAccord LC-MS system (Waters, Manchester, U.K.). This platform is integrated with a UPLC i-Class system, including a binary solvent manager, a sample manager at 4 °C, a column oven, and a UV detector operating at 260 and 280 nm. The chromatographic system was equipped with an RP LC column (Protein BEH C4 300Å 1.7μm, 2.1×50mm, Waters, Wilmslow). Two mobile phases (MP) were used to separate the cross-linked reaction products. MPA was composed of 8.6 mM triethylamine (TEA) and 100 mM hexafluoropropan-2-ol (HFIP) in H2O, while MPB was composed of 4.3 mM TEA and 50 mM HFIP in 50:50 H2O/MeOH. The column was heated at 60 °C and compounds were eluted under the influence of a continuous flow rate of 200 μL/min, with a gradient starting at 10% of MPB from 0 to 1 min, increasing to 40% from 1 to 11 min, reaching 60% from 11 to 11.5 min, and staying constant at 60% from 11.5 to 12.5. The UPLC system was interfaced with an ACQUITY RDa Detector (mass spectrometer) to provide the experimental molecular masses of the compounds. The ACQUITY RDa Detector was calibrated using the ACQUITY RDa detector calibrant and Wash Kit (186009013), which is a mixture of sodium iodide and rubidium iodide in an 80:20 isopropanol/water solvent. The RDa detector operated with a capillary voltage of −0.8 kV and a pressure of 2 mbar at the source interface. The cone voltage was set to 40 V, and the gas desolvation temperature was set to 400 °C. Acquisitions were performed on the high m/z range 400–5000 with a scan rate frequency of 2 Hz. Data processing was performed using UNIFI v3.13.0.682 (Waters, Manchester, U.K.).
