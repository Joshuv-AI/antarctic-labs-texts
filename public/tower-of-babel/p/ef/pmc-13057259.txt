Seismic performance of weak-axis steel beam-to-column connections with a T-adapter
Authors: Orkun Yılmaz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents a bolted end-plate connection detail for weak-axis beam-to-column joints, incorporating a T-shaped adapter to improve constructability and facilitate field installation. A total of six finite element (FE) models were developed in Ansys to systematically evaluate the seismic performance of the proposed connection by varying the thicknesses of the T-shaped adapter flange, end-plate, and continuity plates. The numerical modeling approach was validated against available experimental results from the literature for both strong-axis and weak-axis moment connections to ensure reliability under cyclic loading conditions. Following the validation process, a parametric investigation was conducted to determine the component dimensions required for the proposed connection to satisfy the seismic provisions of ANSI/AISC 341 − 16 for Special Moment Frames (SMFs). The results were assessed in terms of hysteretic behavior, ductility, joint stiffness classification, plastic deformation, and bolt force demand. The findings indicate that the proposed configuration, especially in stiffer models (M1 and M2), exhibited stable cyclic response and significant plastic rotation capacity, successfully meeting the 4% story drift angle and 0.80Mp moment capacity requirements. Ductility coefficients were found to range between 2.11 and 2.85, validating the energy dissipation potential of the connection detail. The stiffer models achieved a rigid joint classification and effectively confined plastic deformations primarily to the beam flanges, validating the potential applicability of the T-adapter detail in seismic design scenarios. The results also suggest that existing design provisions for strong-axis end-plate connections may provide a reasonable benchmark for proportioning this connection type. The validity of this observation was further verified through additional numerical analyses involving beams with substantially different depths and flange proportions.Keywords: Weak-axis connections, Bolted end-plate connection, Finite element analysis (FEA), Seismic performance, T-shaped adapterSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Steel moment-resisting frames are commonly used in high-seismic regions due to their robust ductility and capacity to dissipate energy. Nonetheless, the 1994 Northridge and 1995 Kobe earthquakes revealed unexpected brittle failures in many beam-column connections, prompting extensive analytical and experimental investigations worldwide1. Today, numerous refined and, in some cases, patented connection details are incorporated into the AISC Prequalified connections for special and intermediate moment frames (ANSI/AISC 358 − 22)2. However, the connections documented in ANSI/AISC 358 − 22 focus on beam-to-strong-axis attachments. As illustrated in Fig. 1, a strong-axis connection engages the column flanges, where the major axis of the column resists the bending moments. Conversely, a weak-axis connection attaches the beam to the column web, utilizing the minor axis of the column. In typical practice, the beam is oriented along the strong-axis of the column to form a moment-resisting system, whereas any beam framing into the weak-axis is often regarded as pinned or semi-rigid.

A representative configuration for (a) strong-axis connection and (b) weak-axis connection.

Nevertheless, beam-to-weak-axis connections can be designed as fully rigid if detailed appropriately. To validate these designs, both numerical and experimental evidence must confirm their sufficient strength and ductility under seismic demands. Although prequalified connections in ANSI/AISC 358 − 22 do not explicitly address weak-axis attachments, they may serve as a preliminary benchmark for understanding how beam-to-weak-axis joints behave.

Weak-axis beam-to-column connections have not been investigated as extensively as strong-axis connections. Most studies have focused on strategies to improve seismic performance, including the use of Reduced Beam Section (RBS) details and tapered configurations. Gilton and Uang3 reported that RBS connections can achieve 3% radian plastic rotation without brittle fracture or significant stress concentrations. Oh et al.4 and Lu et al.5 also found that weak-axis RBS connections provide ductile behavior up to a story drift of 5% without brittle failure. Vatansever and Kutsal6 showed that a built-up I-shaped adapter plate with a tapered extension reduces plastic strain and rupture index, while meeting the requirements for special moment frames.

Column-tree connections, widely used in Korea and Japan, represent another significant research direction. Lee et al.7 and Oh et al.4,8 verified that these systems provide stable ductile behavior and satisfactory seismic performance up to 5% drift. In tests with composite beam action, Oh et al.9 observed that brittle fracture at the bottom flange was prevented, although severe crushing of the concrete slab was noted. Lu et al.10 reported that composite weak-axis joints can reach a story drift of 4% before a 20% reduction in strength occurs.

Several studies have investigated alternative energy dissipation mechanisms. Koetaka et al.11 achieved stable hysteresis behavior across a wide deformation range by using hysteretic dampers. Shim et al.12 developed scallop-free web connections, which provided improved structural performance compared to standard details. Peker et al.13 found that controlled panel-zone yielding can be used as an effective energy dissipation mechanism.

Studies on load-transfer mechanisms to the column web have compared direct bolted connections7,14,15 with indirect methods using skin plates or U-shaped connectors5,10,15–18. Lee et al.7 and Kim et al.14 reported that bolted configurations are simple to construct and provide sufficient flexural behavior. Box-strengthened solutions, as examined by Lu et al.5,10,16, Yılmaz and Bekiroğlu15, and Nie et al.18, were found to improve shear resistance and limit plastic deformation in the column web. These results indicate that the performance of weak-axis joints is strongly influenced by the ability to transfer inelastic demand away from the column web.

Although many previous studies have focused on welded or modified bracket-type weak-axis connections, bolted alternatives remain relatively under-investigated. The connection proposed in this study features a bolted end-plate configuration, where an I-section beam is connected to the column web via a T-shaped adapter. The primary motivation for this configuration is to resolve the constructability challenges inherent in standard weak-axis connections, such as difficult welding access and site fit-up issues. Accordingly, the proposed detail improves accessibility and facilitates assembly, offering greater flexibility for practical field applications.

To evaluate the seismic performance of this connection, two experimentally tested specimens from the literature were simulated and validated using nonlinear finite element analysis. Subsequently, a comprehensive parametric investigation was conducted on six models with varying thicknesses of the T-flange, end-plate, and continuity plates. The investigation aimed to determine the required component dimensions for the proposed connection to satisfy the seismic provisions of ANSI/AISC 341 − 1619 for Special Moment Frames (SMFs). In particular, the models were designed to promote a stable energy dissipation mechanism through plastic hinge formation in the beam and controlled yielding of the end-plate, while avoiding brittle failure modes such as bolt rupture. The results were assessed in terms of hysteretic response, ductility, joint stiffness classification, plastic deformation, and bolt-force demand to validate the applicability of this new detail for seismic design. In addition, the robustness and general applicability of the proposed connection were further examined through additional numerical analyses considering beam-column configurations with different beam geometries.

Finite element models

To investigate the nonlinear cyclic response of the proposed connection, three-dimensional finite element models were developed in Ansys20. SOLID186 higher-order 20-node elements21 were utilized to accurately capture material, geometric, and contact nonlinearities.

The connection design, illustrated in Fig. 2, consists of a T-shaped adapter, end-plate, continuity plates, and high-strength bolts. In this design, an end-plate is welded to the face of the beam and bolted to the flange of the T-shaped adapter (hereafter referred to as the T-flange) using high-strength bolts. The web of the T-shaped adapter (hereafter referred to as the T-web) is directly welded to the column web, and continuity plates are installed within the column at the level of the beam flanges. This arrangement ensures uniform force transfer and enhances stiffness, as well as providing adequate access for bolt assembly. This detail is particularly effective in accommodating bending around the weak-axis of the column.

The joint configuration and dimensions of the weak-axis beam-column connection.

Six finite element models were established by varying the thicknesses of the T-flange, end-plate, and continuity plates, as summarized in Table 1. The reference model, designated as M1, was proportioned according to the design philosophy and plate dimensioning procedures specified in ANSI/AISC 358 − 22 for bolted unstiffened extended end-plate moment connections. Although these provisions are formally defined for strong-axis connections, they were applied in this study to provide a consistent and code-based reference configuration for the proposed weak-axis T-adapter detail. In the reference configuration, the T-adapter was designed so that its cross-sectional properties matched those of the column. The T-flange thickness was set to 17 mm to correspond to the column flange, and the T-web thickness was set to 10 mm to correspond to the column web. The remaining models, M2 through M6, were developed by systematically modifying the plate thicknesses to investigate the effects of different stiffness conditions. The continuity plate thicknesses were selected based on the IPE270 beam flange thickness (tbf = 10.2 mm), with 12 mm representing a stiffer plate and 8 mm representing a more flexible condition. For the T-flange and end-plate, the thicknesses were reduced by 5 mm from the reference M1 model, resulting in thicknesses of 12 mm for the T-flange and 20 mm for the end-plate, in order to evaluate the connection behavior under reduced component stiffness.

Material nonlinearity was incorporated in the finite element models using the Von Mises yield criterion and a multilinear kinematic hardening rule. The true stress–strain curves, as adopted from Gerami et al.22, are illustrated in Fig. 3. The end-plate was modeled with ASTM A36, the bolts with ASTM A490, and all other components with ASTM A572 Grade 50. Poisson’s ratio was taken as 0.3 for all materials. The profiles were modeled as built-up sections using the web and flange dimensions corresponding to IPE270 and HE240B, which are European profiles used for beams and columns, respectively. Both profiles are seismically compact for highly ductile members, as specified in AISC 341 − 16. To simplify the complex geometry, bolt threads, washers, and weld metals were not explicitly modeled. As a result, the root fillets typical of hot-rolled sections were omitted, and the flange and web components were represented with sharp rectangular transitions. This approach to modeling steel members as built-up sections without root fillets is consistent with previous studies on steel connections23–26. For consistency between the numerical simulations and the analytical design, the theoretical plastic moment capacity (Mp) was calculated based on the built-up geometry. The plastic section modulus (Zx) used for normalization is 460.54 cm3. Fig. 4 illustrates the numerical setup, including its boundary conditions, dimensions, and loading conditions. All translational degrees of freedom were restrained at the top and bottom faces of the column. In addition, lateral supports were applied at various points along the beam span to prevent out-of-plane deformations. The loading protocol specified in AISC 341 − 16 was applied at the free end of the beam (Fig. 4) in the vertical direction. The loading procedure was conducted in two distinct stages. First, a pretension force (Tb) of 172 kN was applied to the M20 bolts, which corresponds to the minimum required pretension for M20 bolts made of the specified high-strength bolt material27. Following this, cyclic vertical displacements (as shown in Fig. 5a) were imposed at the loading point. The story drift angle (θ) was calculated by taking the ratio of the total vertical displacement at the loading point (ΔCL) to the distance from that point to the column centerline (LCL), as illustrated in Fig. 5b. The loading protocol continued until the story drift angle reached a maximum of 6% radians. The mesh structure of the numerical model is illustrated in Fig. 6. In regions of high stress concentration, such as around bolt holes and at contact surfaces, the mesh was locally refined to improve the accuracy of stress distribution results. Node-to-node contact elements (CONTA178) were utilized between all connecting components. Bonded (linear) contacts were used to simulate welded connections, while frictional (nonlinear) contacts, which allow for separation and sliding, were assigned to represent bolted interfaces. At all bolt-plate and plate-plate interfaces (Fig. 7), frictional contacts were defined with a friction coefficient (µ) of 0.40. This coefficient was selected as a representative average value between the 0.30 (Class A) and 0.50 (Class B) coefficients specified in AISC 360 − 22.27

Validation of the finite element modeling approach

Test of Sumner and Murray

To verify the reliability of the developed finite element (FE) modeling approach, the experimental test conducted by Sumner and Murray28,29 was selected for the validation of the bolted end-plate connection model. The specific benchmark specimen chosen for simulation is the 8ES-1.25-1.75-30 configuration. This configuration involves a W-section beam welded to an extended end-plate, bolted to a column flange to form a strong-axis connection, and subjected to cyclic displacement at the beam tip.

The FE model was developed in Ansys using exact geometric dimensions and connection detailing as in the original test. Material behavior was defined using a nonlinear kinematic hardening rule (Chaboche model), with parameters obtained from a numerical study by Morrison et al. [30]. This study utilized the same 8ES configuration for validation purposes, as Sumner’s work lacked explicit material data. Further validation of the model was performed by examining the failure modes. As illustrated in Fig. 13, at a story drift angle of 4% radian, equivalent plastic strains are concentrated within the RBS region, indicating that plasticity is effectively confined away from the beam–column interface. This observation is consistent with the experimental results and confirms that the model accurately reproduces the intended plastic hinge relocation mechanism.

Quantitative verification of the model was conducted based on the flexural capacity at the center of the RBS. The theoretical plastic moment capacity, calculated using the reported material properties, was Mp, RBS = 2362 kNm. The maximum moment obtained from the finite element analysis at the same location (2684 mm from the beam end) was 2424 kNm. The close agreement between the theoretical and numerical results (ratio ≈ 1.03) indicates that the model provides a reliable representation of the section capacity.

The deviation in maximum strength is attributed to the use of a multilinear kinematic hardening model, which accounts for the Bauschinger effect but conservatively neglects isotropic hardening. Similar conservative predictions for specimen CW-1 were reported by Xu and Hao [31], who also observed lower numerical envelopes despite accurate failure mode predictions. Therefore, the proposed model is considered to provide a safe and reliable basis for further parametric studies.

Summary of validation

The finite element simulation results for hysteretic behavior and damage distribution exhibit a high degree of agreement with the experimental observations for both strong-axis and weak-axis configurations. The developed models accurately capture essential performance parameters, including stiffness degradation, flexural capacity, and failure mechanisms, thereby confirming the reliability and effectiveness of the modeling methodology adopted in the present study.

Result and discussion

Hysteretic behavior and energy dissipation

Figure 14 compares the hysteresis responses of all six models under the prescribed loading protocol, expressed in terms of the ratio of the applied moment to the plastic moment capacity (M/Mp) versus total plastic rotation. The applied moment (M) was computed by multiplying the reaction force from the displacement load by the distance from the loading point to the column face. The total plastic rotation (θp) was obtained by subtracting the elastic rotations from the story drift angle.

M/Mp – total plastic rotation curves of the models.

In accordance with ANSI/AISC 341 − 16, beam-to-column connections in seismic load-resisting systems (e.g., SMFs) must accommodate a story drift angle of 4% radian or a plastic rotation of 3% radian. Additionally, the flexural moment at the column face must reach at least 0.80Mp at the story drift angle of 4% radian or plastic rotation of 3% radian. As illustrated in Fig. 14, the connections examined in this study provide a flexural moment exceeding 0.80Mp at a 3% radian plastic rotation, thereby meeting the ANSI/AISC 341 − 16 requirements for SMFs.

Table 3 displays the maximum normalized moment (M/Mp)max and the maximum plastic rotation (θp)max for each model. It is important to note that these maximum values may not occur during the same cycle. All six models exceed (M/Mp) = 1.0 at some point, indicating that each connection can temporarily surpass the nominal plastic moment of the beam. However, variations in the thicknesses of the T-flange, end-plate, and continuity plate influence both the maximum moment capacity and the maximum plastic rotation. For all models, (M/Mp)max ranged from 1.011 to 1.076, demonstrating a stable strength increase. This outcome arises from the Cpr factor, which accounts for peak connection strength contributions, including strain hardening, local restraint, additional reinforcement, and other connection-specific conditions.

Model M1 achieves the largest moment ratio and sustains the maximum plastic rotation. This behavior is likely due to the relatively thicker end-plate, T-flange, and continuity plates, which facilitate stable load transfer. Model M2, which adopts the same T-flange and end-plate but thinner continuity plates, reaches a similar maximum moment ratio but with a slightly reduced rotation capacity, suggesting that thinner continuity plates may limit inelastic deformation. Model M3 features a thinner T-flange, leading to slightly earlier local yielding and a lower rotation limit; as a result, its hysteresis curve exhibits minimal pinching. By contrast, Model M4 employs a thinner end-plate combined with a thicker T-flange, resulting in a well-balanced response that retains ductility despite the reduced end-plate thickness. Model M5 similarly achieves a high moment ratio but exhibits a smaller rotation capacity compared to Model M4. This behavior indicates that the reduced continuity plate thickness limits the ability of the connection to sustain extensive inelastic deformations. Model M6 employs the thinnest T-flange and end-plate among all configurations. This results in noticeable pinching in its hysteresis loops, as well as the lowest peak moment ratio and rotation capacity before significant strength degradation. Overall, while all six models can reach and occasionally exceed the plastic moment capacity of the beam, the thickness of each plate critically influences the balance between ultimate strength and ductility in these weak-axis beam-to-column connections.

The envelope curves of moment versus story drift angle, obtained by connecting the peak points of the hysteresis loops for all models, are presented in Fig. 15. As observed, the curves demonstrate an initial linear elastic response, followed by strain hardening and subsequent softening, the extent of which varies according to the connection geometry. It should be noted that Models M1 and M4 exhibited earlier strength degradation after attaining their maximum moment capacities, which can be attributed to increased localized stress accumulation in these configurations.

Figure 16 compares the initial rotational stiffness (Rin) values for all six models, which range from approximately 7286 to 7719 kNm/rad. In particular, Model M4 displays the highest Rin at 7719 kNm/rad, followed closely by Model M1 (7649 kNm/rad). By contrast, Model M6 emerges with the lowest value (7286 kNm/rad), reflecting its thinner plates and overall reduced rigidity. Models with thicker T-flange and continuity plates (e.g., M1 and M4) demonstrate marginally higher initial stiffness, indicating greater resistance against small rotational deformations. Conversely, models with thinner components exhibit a lower Rin, as evidenced by M3, M5, and, notably, M6. Despite these minor variations in stiffness, the results indicate that the geometric changes have a limited impact on the elastic rigidity of the proposed weak-axis connections.

Figure 17 illustrates the total energy dissipation among the beam, column, end-plate, T-flange, continuity plates, and bolts for Models M1 through M6. These results clarify how variations in the thickness of the T-flange, continuity plates, and end-plate influence the distribution of inelastic demands. In Model M1, the beam dissipates most of the energy at drift levels of 3% to 6%, accounting for approximately 96% of the total dissipated energy at a 6% story drift ratio. This indicates that plastic deformations are primarily confined to the beam while the connection remains essentially elastic. Model M2 also shows that the beam is the main contributor to energy dissipation (approximately 56%) but reveals a significant contribution from the continuity plates, which dissipate about 38% of the total energy at 6% drift. This change suggests broader inelastic engagement when the continuity plates are thinner. In contrast, Model M3 diverges from Model M2, as the T-flange dissipates the largest share of energy, accounting for approximately 53% of the total energy dissipation at 6% drift, while the beam contribution decreases to about 21%. This behavior is attributed to the reduced thickness of the T-flange, which leads to earlier yielding and a redistribution of inelastic demand away from the beam. Model M4 features a thinner end-plate compared to Model M1 but still shows strong participation from the beam, which dissipates approximately 85% of the total energy at 6% drift, while the T-flange contribution remains limited (about 1–1.5%). However, the contribution from the end-plate is higher than in Model M1, indicating increased plastic deformation in the connection region. Due to the reduced thickness of the continuity plates in Model M5, the energy dissipation contributions of the continuity plates and the beam are comparable at higher drift levels, with the continuity plates dissipating approximately 41% and the beam about 40% of the total energy at 6% drift. Finally, Model M6, characterized by minimal thickness in the T-flange and end-plate, exhibits the highest energy dissipation in the T-flange, which alone accounts for approximately 55% of the total energy at 6% drift, leaving the beam and other elements relatively underutilized. Overall, these findings demonstrate that plate thickness plays a critical role in determining where inelastic behavior occurs. Even minor changes can shift plastic demands among the beam and connection components, which ultimately affects the ductility and total energy dissipation capacity of weak-axis beam-to-column connections.

The total energy dissipation values among the beam, column, end-plate, T-flange, continuity plates, and bolts at story drift levels of 3% to 6%.

Figure. 18 presents the hysteretic damping ratios of Models M1 through M6 across increasing story drift angles. All models display a consistent upward trend, reflecting the growing contribution of inelastic deformation to energy dissipation under larger drift demands. Model M1 exhibits the highest damping performance throughout, reaching 42.35% at 6% drift. Model M2 closely follows, slightly exceeding Model M1 at the maximum drift level (43.08%), though it remains lower than Model M1 at the intermediate stages. Model M4 also performs strongly, with damping ratios above 30% from 5% drift onward. Model M5 demonstrates moderate yet stable growth, indicating a balanced plastic response. By contrast, Models M3 and M6 maintain lower damping ratios throughout, not surpassing 26% even at maximum drift, which suggests more limited or localized inelastic action. These outcomes emphasize the sensitivity of hysteretic damping to variations in plate thickness and confirm that even modest geometric changes can substantially influence energy dissipation efficiency in weak-axis connections.

Ductility and overstrength evaluation

To evaluate the seismic performance of the proposed connections, the ductility coefficient (µd) and the overstrength factor (Ω) were also evaluated. The ductility coefficient is defined as the ratio of the ultimate rotation capacity (θu) to the effective yield rotation (θy). The overstrength factor is determined as the ratio of the maximum moment (Mmax) to the nominal plastic moment capacity of the beam (Mp), as previously specified in Table 3. The yield point of the connections was identified using the energy equivalent method, as described in FEMA 356 [32] and widely adopted by other researchers [33–35] for the standardized seismic evaluation of structural joints. As shown in Fig. 19, the initial tangent line, representing the initial rotational stiffness (Rin), is drawn from the origin (0) on the moment–story drift angle envelope curve. This tangent intersects the line passing through the maximum moment (Mmax, Point C) at Point A.

Definition of yield point.

According to the energy balance principle, the coordinates of the yield point (θy, My) are determined so that the shaded area A1 below the idealized bilinear curve is equal to the shaded area A2 above the curve, where both areas represent the regions between the idealized bilinear curve and the numerical envelope curve. It should be noted that, although the AISC 341 − 16 provision defines the ultimate limit state as a 20% reduction from the plastic moment capacity (0.80Mp), in this methodology, the ultimate rotation (θu) is taken at Point D, which corresponds to a 15% reduction from the maximum moment (0.85Mmax). This energy-based method provides a more comprehensive assessment of the inelastic behavior and overstrength characteristics of the connections. As shown in Table 4, all models achieved ductility ratios greater than 2.0, indicating stable inelastic response and adequate energy dissipation capacity for seismic applications. Deformations

Figures 20, 21, 22 and 23 present the plastic strain distributions at a story drift of 6% radian for the connection region, end-plate, T-flange, and continuity plates in Models M1 through M6. In Models M1 and M2, pronounced plastic strains concentrate directly in the beam flanges, while the end-plate, T-flange, and continuity plates exhibit only limited yielding. This behavior indicates that the inelastic demand is primarily resisted by the beam itself. Model M4, which incorporates a thinner end-plate than Model M1, exhibits slightly increased plastic strains within both the end-plate and the T-flange. However, pronounced yielding remains predominantly concentrated in the beam flanges, indicating no major shift in the primary inelastic mechanism. In Model M5, where continuity plate thickness is reduced, plastic strains in the end-plate and T-flange remain comparable to Model M4. However, a marked increase occurs in the continuity plates, making them a dominant region for energy dissipation. For Models M3 and M6, the reduction in T-flange thickness to 12 mm results in a notable redistribution of plasticity within the joint. In these models, the T-flange experiences extensive plastic strains, and the end-plate undergoes significant yielding concentrated near the beam flange levels. The beam, however, remains predominantly elastic throughout the loading process. In particular, Model M6, which incorporates a thinner end-plate in addition to the reduced T-flange thickness, exhibits the most pronounced plastic deformation in the connection components. These observations indicate that decreasing the T-flange thickness increases the flexibility of the joint and shifts the inelastic demand from the beam to the connection region.

Stiffness classifications

Figure 24 presents the moment-rotation of connection hysteresis curves for Models M1 to M6, compared with the boundary rotational stiffness lines defined as 20EI/L (fully rigid) and 2EI/L (pinned). Connections exhibiting stiffness above 20EI/L are considered rigid, whereas those below 2EI/L are pinned, as specified in the Commentary to Chapter B of ANSI/AISC 360 − 1627. Connections with intermediate stiffness values between these boundaries are classified as semi-rigid. The hysteresis loops of Models M1 and M2 are narrow and steep, consistently remaining above the 20EI/L reference line throughout the loading cycles, thus clearly behaving as rigid connections. Conversely, the hysteresis loops of Models M3 and M6 exhibit wider shapes with considerable pinching and evident stiffness degradation, indicating predominantly semi-rigid behavior that occasionally approaches the 2EI/L boundary. This reduction in connection rigidity can be primarily attributed to the thinner T-flange. Models M4 and M5 demonstrate intermediate responses, characterized by moderate hysteretic loop widths and partial stiffness degradation, positioned between the 20EI/L and 2EI/L boundaries but notably closer to the 20EI/L boundary, unlike the more flexible responses observed in Models M3 and M6. This behavior suggests their classification as semi-rigid, where adequate moment transmission capability is preserved despite observable relative rotations. Overall, the observed differences across the models validate that connection rigidity is significantly influenced by plate thickness, with decreasing plate dimensions systematically reducing joint stiffness and shifting the connection behavior from rigid to semi-rigid and, in extreme cases, toward a pinned response.

Bolt forces

Figure 25 shows the axial force histories of the bolts as a function of story drift angle for Models M1–M6, where Bolt‑1 is located in the extended portion of the end-plate and Bolt‑2 is located between the beam flanges. Positive drift corresponds to downward beam loading, placing both bolt rows in tension. For the stiff configurations (e.g., Models M1 and M2), the force traces are narrow with limited hysteresis, reflecting small joint rotations and modest variations in bolt demand. In contrast, Models M3–M6 develop progressively wider loops, indicating larger cyclic rotations and pronounced force redistribution within the connection.

The axial force histories of the bolts.

It is noteworthy that, despite the comparatively shorter primary moment arm of the Bolt-2, it attains higher peak tension levels in some cycles, particularly in Models M4 and M5. This result can be attributed to two interacting mechanisms: (i) neutral‑axis migration toward the compression flange, thereby enlarging the tensile zone acting on Bolt-2, and (ii) prying action that develops when the end‑plate locally separates from the column face, a phenomenon amplified at the adjacent to Bolt‑2. These mechanisms are consistent with earlier observations by Krishnamurthy [36]. It was emphasized that in end-plate connections where the beam web is welded to the end-plate, the longitudinal stresses due to the beam moment are partially transmitted through the beam web to the end-plate. A significant portion of these stresses is directed into the stiffer region between the beam flanges, where the web acts as a rib, leading to increased force demand on the inner bolt rows.

This value is in agreement with the peak bolt forces observed in Fig. 25, thereby confirming that the observed force fluctuations and amplifications are consistent with theoretical expectations for prying action under cyclic loading conditions.

Sensitivity analysis

A sensitivity analysis was carried out to assess the effects of critical contact parameters, namely bolt pretension (Tb) and friction coefficient (µ), on the cyclic behavior of the proposed connection. To improve computational efficiency, a reduced loading protocol was adopted, applying single cycles at 1%, 1.5%, 2%, 3%, and 4% story drift ratios. The analysis was conducted on two representative models: Model M1, which represents the stiffest configuration, and Model M6, which features the thinnest end-plate and T-flange. For each model, four different cases were considered:

1- Baseline case: µ = 0.40 and Tb = 172 kN (the primary values utilized throughout this study).

2- Friction variation (low): µ = 0.30 and Tb = 172 kN.

3- Friction variation (high): µ = 0.50 and Tb = 172 kN.

4- Pretension variation: µ = 0.40 and Tb = 120 kN (representing approximately 70% of the minimum required pretension).

The results of the sensitivity analysis are presented in Fig. 27. It is observed that the hysteretic curves for all four cases are almost identical, with substantial overlap between the two models. The minimal influence of these parameters demonstrates that, within the scope of the numerical simulations, the structural response of the proposed T-adapter weak-axis connection is predominantly controlled by component yielding. The connection moment is transferred through the tension-compression couple of the bolt groups; therefore, variations in clamping force and surface friction have only a minor effect on the simulated inelastic capacity and hysteretic stability.

The results of the sensitivity analysis.

It should be noted that the stiffness classification of the proposed connection is mainly determined by the geometry of the components, rather than by contact-related parameters. The results of the sensitivity analysis indicate that changes in the friction coefficient within the range of 0.30 to 0.50 and variations in bolt pretension level have a negligible effect on the initial rotational stiffness and the overall cyclic response for both the stiff (M1) and flexible (M6) configurations. While a lower friction coefficient may cause a slight increase in local slip at the bolted interfaces, this does not significantly affect the global moment-rotation behavior or the classification of the connection as rigid or semi-rigid. In addition, moderate changes in material yield strength are expected to influence the strength demand, but not the stiffness-based classification, as the initial rotational stiffness is primarily controlled by the plate geometry and the main load-transfer mechanisms.

Verification on additional beam-column configurations

In this section, the applicability of the proposed weak-axis end-plate connection is investigated for beam-to-column assemblies with significantly different cross-sectional properties. While the initial finite element models (M1-M6) were established using an IPE270 beam and an HE240B column, it should be noted that practical applications frequently require consideration of beams with various depths and flange proportions.

Accordingly, two additional beam-column configurations were developed in accordance with the strong-axis end-plate design provisions specified in AISC 358 − 22, while maintaining the same connection concept, detailing philosophy, loading protocol, and boundary conditions. In both configurations, the beam-column strength ratio was selected to be consistent with that of the initial models (M1-M6), thereby ensuring a consistent strength hierarchy between the beam and the column. The evaluation was conducted based on the moment - total plastic rotation response, energy dissipation capacity, and stiffness classification, in order to directly assess the robustness and scalability of the proposed connection system.

Description of additional configurations

In the first configuration, an HE240A beam was connected to an HE300B column. This configuration was selected to represent a relatively shallow beam with wide flanges, enabling the assessment of connection behavior under conditions of reduced beam depth and increased flange width. In the second configuration, an IPE500 beam was used to represent a deep-beam scenario. Due to the absence of a standard rolled column section with comparable weak-axis flexural resistance, a built-up column section with overall dimensions of d = 330 mm, b = 340 mm, tf = 40 mm, and tw = 20 mm was used. Consistent with the reference model (Model M1), the T-adapter components in both additional configurations were dimensioned to match the flange and web thicknesses of their respective columns. The geometric details of the end-plate and connection layout for these additional configurations are shown in Fig. 28.

Geometric details of the end-plate for the additional beam–column configurations.

High-strength preloaded bolts were utilized in both configurations, with pretension applied at the minimum specified level. For the HE240A beam configuration, M24 bolts with a pretension force of 247 kN were used, while M27 bolts with a pretension force of 321 kN were adopted for the IPE500 beam configuration.

Global seismic response

Figure 29 presents the normalized moment-plastic rotation responses of the additional beam-column configurations. Both additional configurations satisfy the seismic performance requirements prescribed in ANSI/AISC 341 − 16 for SMFs. Specifically, the HE240A configuration reaches a maximum normalized moment ratio of 1.083 and sustains a plastic rotation capacity of 4.07% rad, while the IPE500 configuration attains a higher ratio of 1.12 with a plastic rotation capacity of 5.09% rad. In both cases, the connections develop flexural moments exceeding 0.80Mp at a plastic rotation of 3% rad, thereby meeting the SMF acceptance criteria. These results indicate that the proposed connection maintains adequate strength and ductility when applied to beams with substantially different geometric characteristics.

The energy dissipation results shown in Fig. 30 further confirm a stable and desirable inelastic response. For both configurations, the majority of the dissipated energy is concentrated in the beam, indicating that plastic deformations are predominantly confined to the beam rather than the connection components. At a story drift ratio of 6%, the beam accounts for approximately 94% of the total dissipated energy in the HE240A configuration and about 97% in the IPE500 configuration. This behavior is consistent with the intended seismic design philosophy, in which the beam acts as the primary energy-dissipating element while the connection remains largely elastic.

Despite significant differences in beam depth and flange geometry, the proposed weak-axis end-plate connection consistently satisfies the SMF performance requirements in terms of strength, plastic rotation capacity, stiffness classification, and energy dissipation characteristics.

Limitations and future work

Although this study provides important insights into the seismic performance of T-adapter-based weak-axis connections, certain limitations should be acknowledged. The parametric investigation was conducted using six representative finite element models. Model M1, designed in accordance with ANSI/AISC 358 − 22 for strong-axis extended end-plate moment connections, was adopted as the reference configuration. The plate thicknesses in Model M1 already satisfied the seismic performance requirements of ANSI/AISC 341 − 16 for Special Moment Frames. Therefore, no additional plate thickening was considered. Instead, Models M2-M6 were generated by reducing the thicknesses of the T-flange, end-plate, and continuity plates to enable a comparative evaluation of the connection response. Only a limited number of plate thickness combinations were considered in this study, as the primary objective was not a detailed thickness optimization but a comparative investigation of the proposed connection behavior; intermediate values (e.g., 10 mm continuity plates) were not explicitly examined. This approach allowed the influence of plate thickness variation on connection stiffness, plastic deformation patterns, and rigidity classification to be assessed without altering the fundamental load-transfer mechanism or violating SMF performance limits.

The results presented in this study are based on detailed nonlinear finite element simulations, which were validated using established experimental data. It is recommended that further experimental investigations be conducted to assess the seismic performance of these connections under practical conditions, including the effects of surface imperfections, bolt-tightening procedures, and cumulative damage. The findings are primarily applicable to the member sizes and geometric proportions considered in this study. While the observed performance trends offer a reference for similar bolted connection details, the absolute strength and deformation capacities may differ for other profile combinations. To improve computational efficiency, certain modeling simplifications were implemented, including neglecting bolt threads and root fillets. These simplifications are consistent with standard finite element modeling practices. Future studies incorporating more detailed geometric features and potential weld fracture mechanisms are suggested to enhance the comprehensiveness of the analysis.

Conclusions

This study proposed and numerically assessed a novel T-adapter-based bolted extended end-plate connection for weak-axis beam-to-column joints. Following the validation of the three-dimensional finite element modeling approach against experimental data, a comprehensive parametric investigation was conducted across six distinct plate-thickness configurations, and additional verification analyses were performed on two assemblies with different beam sizes to assess the generality of the findings. The main results and engineering implications are summarized below.

Within the investigated parameter range, the proposed connection satisfied the Special Moment Frame (SMF) requirements as specified in ANSI/AISC 341 − 16. Under the prescribed loading protocol, each model achieved the required 4% story drift and satisfied 3% plastic rotation, while maintaining a flexural moment at the column face greater than 0.80Mp. The peak normalized moment capacities (M/Mp) for the main parametric models (M1–M6) ranged from 1.011 to 1.076, indicating stable global strength without premature loss of moment capacity.
