Unlocking Battery-Grade MnSO4 via Synergistic Chelator Regulation: Molecular Insights into Inhibiting Ca/Mg Cocrystallization
Authors: Wenrui Zhang, Yurong Kang, Jukai Tan, Xianwei Wu, Longjiang Li, Chengbing Fu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The selective removal of divalent cations like Ca2+ and Mg2+ from a matrix containing a predominant divalent ion (Mn2+) is the fundamental challenge in inorganic synthesis, critically impeding the production of battery-grade MnSO4. Current purification methods are plagued by high energy consumption due to the similar physicochemical properties of these ions. Herein, we report a breakthrough strategy that addresses this selectivity issue via the synergistic use of multidentate chelators. Using MnSO4 feedstock containing 756.87 mg·kg–1 Ca2+ and 2126.67 mg·kg–1 Mg2+, we integrated diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA) and citric acid (CA) into a conventional crystallization process. This approach selectively sequesters Ca2+ and Mg2+, effectively inhibiting their cocrystallization without complicating manganese recovery. As a result, the removal efficiencies for Ca2+ and Mg2+ are markedly enhanced, reaching 94.6% and 95.1%, respectively. This molecular-level design reduces the required number of crystallization cycles to just two, significantly lowering the energy footprint. The product unambiguously qualifies as battery-grade. Beyond the practical achievement, we provide a profound mechanistic understanding through DFT calculations, which quantify the relative binding affinities and coordination mechanism of the chelator cocktail, rationalizing the observed synergy. This study represents a fundamental advance in the application of coordination chemistry to control crystallization processes, offering a powerful and general platform for the purification of advanced inorganic materials.

Introduction

The escalating global energy crisis has intensified the demand for efficient and sustainable energy storage systems. Among promising candidates, Mn-based lithium-ion batteries have garnered significant interest due to their advantageous combination of efficiency and stability. Especially in the fields of battery power and high-end consumer electronics, it is highly anticipated. , The performance of these batteries is critically dependent on the purity of their precursor materials, particularly manganese sulfate monohydrate (MnSO4·H2O), which is typically derived from mineral sources through sulfuric acid leaching. This process, however, inevitably codissolves geologically associated alkaline earth metal ions, notably Ca2+ and Mg2+. , The persistent incorporation of these impurities, owing to their large ionic radii, disrupts Li+ diffusion pathways within the cathode lattice, leading to irreversible capacity degradation and poor cycling stability. − Consequently, the pursuit of next-generation Mn-based batteries necessitates the development of advanced purification paradigms capable of delivering ultrapure MnSO4.

Conventional hydrometallurgical techniques for removing Ca2+ and Mg2+, such as fluoride precipitation and solvent extraction, are hampered by intrinsic limitations, including equipment corrosion, operational complexity, and the generation of problematic waste. − While multistage evaporation–crystallization offers an environmentally benign alternative by leveraging differential solubility, its industrial implementation is energy-intensive, requiring numerous cycles to achieve battery-grade purity. This high energy cost stems from a fundamental scientific challenge: the similar hydration and crystallization behaviors of these divalent cations undercut the efficacy of purely physical separation.

Herein, we postulate a molecular-level solution to this macroscopic problem. We introduce a chelation-assisted crystallization strategy that disrupts the crystallization pathways of Ca2+ and Mg2+ impurities within the MnSO4 matrix. By employing a suite of organic chelators, including citric acid (CA), − ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), − ethylene glycol-bis­(2-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid (EGTA), − and diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DTPA), − we aim to selectively sequester the impurity ions in solution. This molecular intervention is designed to suppress their cocrystallization with MnSO4, thereby drastically reducing the number of required purification cycles.

This study took Ca2+ and Mg2+ as samples to investigate the microscopic mechanism of the combination of organic acids with Ca2+ and Mg2+ and to compare the binding capabilities of different types of organic acids to Ca2+ and Mg2+. By combining DFT calculations with experiments, the study clarified the mechanism of the binding of organic acids to Ca2+ and Mg2+ by calculating and analyzing Mulliken atomic charge, electrostatic potential, binding energy, and frontier molecular orbitals.

Experimental Section

Chemical Reagents and Raw Materials

The industrial-grade manganese sulfate was taken from Material Technology Co., Ltd. for this experiment. The pharmaceutical reagents, such as citric acid (CA), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), glycol-bis­(2-aminoethyl ether)-N,N,N′,N′-tetraacetic acid (EGTA), etc., were all analytically pure and purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. Diethylenetriaminepentaacetic acid (DPTA) is analytically pure and purchased from TCI (Shanghai) Chemical Industry Development Co., Ltd.

Characterization

The chemical composition of the valuable components (the mass fractions of Ca and Mg) in the dried raw materials was analyzed. The phase structure of the samples was analyzed by an X-ray diffractometer (Ultima IV D8-Advance, Japan) with Cu radiation, operated at 40 kV, with a scanning range of 5–90°, to determine the forms of Ca and Mg in MnSO4. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy mapping analysis was performed using an Oxford Instruments AZtecOne spectrometer attached to a scanning electron microscope. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (ESCALAB 250XiX, ThermoFisher) using a ThermoFisher model K-α Plus instrument was employed to perform narrow scans of Mn, Ca, and Mg in the sample. The Avantage analysis software was used to interpret the spectra. The valence information on the main elements in MnSO4 was analyzed.

Removal of Ca 2+ and Mg 2+

Raw material treatment and solution preparation: Commercially available industrial-grade MnSO4 was dried in an oven at 120 °C for 24 h and subsequently placed in a dryer for future use. Next, 530.00 g (±0.02 g) of industrial-grade MnSO4 was placed in a 1000 mL beaker, and 800 mL of ultrapure water was added. The resulting mixture was uniformly stirred for 8 h, made to stand for 8 h, and filtered using a vacuum suction filter. Thereafter, the filtrate was diluted to a volume of 1000 mL to prepare the original solution of MnSO4.

Reaction condition experiment: Initially, 100 mL of the original solution of MnSO4 is poured into a three-necked flask, placed in a constant-temperature oil bath, and stirred at a constant speed of 700 r·min–1 at the heating temperatures of 110 °C, 120 °C, 130 °C, 140, and 150 °C for evaporation and crystallization under the condition that the evaporation volume is 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mL, respectively.

Chelating agent experiment: Initially, 100 mL of the original solution of MnSO4 is poured into a three-necked flask. Four single chelating agents (described in Tables S1 and S2) and a complex chelating agent (0.2 g, 0.4 g, 0.6 g, 0.8 g, 1.0 g) were added. Thereafter, the primary and secondary evaporation–crystallization experiments were conducted at the optimal heating temperature and evaporation amount determined using the experimental steps outlined in the reaction conditions.

DFT Calculations

All of the DFT calculations were carried out using the DMol3 module in the Materials Studio software. Four small-molecule organic acids, including CA, EDTA, EGTA, and DPTA, and their Ca and Mg complexes models were constructed by using the DMol3 module. To ensure structural fidelity, each model underwent sequential geometry optimization. Geometry convergence criteria were set to an energy change of 1.0 × 10–5 Ha, maximum force of 0.002 Ha/Å, and maximum displacement of 0.005 Å. The generalized gradient approximation with the GGA-PBE-D functional was adopted. Core electrons were treated using DFT semicore pseudopotentials, and a double numerical basis set with polarization functions was used. A global orbital cutoff of 4.7 Å was used. van der Waals interactions were treated via the DFT-D correction inherent to the PBE-D functional. − Electrostatic interactions were handled via direct summation with a cutoff distance of 1.2 nm. A self-consistent field tolerance of 1.0 × 10–6 was applied. Based on the geometrically optimized simulation, the Mulliken atomic charges, electrostatic potential, binding energy, and frontier molecular orbitals of the models were analyzed.

Results and Discussion

Influence of Heating Temperature and Evaporation

The association between the heating temperature and removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ is illustrated in Figure a. Heating temperature considerably influenced the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+. At temperatures of 110 °C, 120 °C, 130 °C, 140, and 150 °C, the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ initially increased and subsequently decreased with increasing heating temperatures. At heating temperatures of 110 °C–130 °C, the solubility characteristics of MnSO4 were different from those of CaSO4 and MgSO4. The crystallization rate of MnSO4 was higher than the corresponding rates of CaSO4 and MgSO4. Consequently, the removal rate of Ca2+ increased from 35.48% to 48.26%, while that of Mg2+ increased from 16.86% to 49.97%. Conversely, at heating temperatures of 130 °C–150 °C, rapid crystal growth occurred, forming pores. This resulted in the adhesion of the mother liquor. Consequently, during the later stage, the removal rate of Ca2+ decreased from 48.26% to 43.68%, whereas that of Mg2+ declined from 49.97% to 44.07%. Therefore, the optimal heating temperature for evaporation–crystallization was 130 °C.

Effects of reaction conditions on the removal of Ca2+ and Mg2+: (a) heating temperature and (b) evaporation volume.

The relation between the evaporation volume of different solutions and the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ is depicted in Figure b. As the evaporation volume increased within the solution, the removal rates of both ions initially increased and subsequently decreased. Specifically, when the evaporation volume was between 10 and 30 mL, growth in the early stage of crystallization was dominant, and the formation of mixed crystals was difficult. The impurity removal effect was noticeable, resulting in the effective elimination of impurities. The removal rate of Ca2+ increased from 60.63% to 67.59%, whereas that of Mg2+ increased from 46.56% to 47.44%. However, when the evaporation volume was increased from 30 to 50 mL, the mother liquor adhered to the crystalline surface, precipitating impurities along with crystallization, resulting in a notable decrease in the removal of ions. Specifically, the removal efficiency of Ca2+ decreased from 67.59% to 43.85%, whereas that of Mg2+ sharply diminished from 47.44% to 13.24%, demonstrating an overall downward trend. Therefore, the optimum evaporation volume for evaporation–crystallization was 30 mL.

When the heating temperature is 130 °C and the evaporation volume is 30 mL, the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ reach the optimal values of 67.59% and 47.44%, respectively. Consequently, the Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations decrease from 298 and 1,136.50 mg·kg–1 to 245.33 and 1,117.69 mg·kg–1, respectively. A further increase in heating temperature or evaporation volume decreases the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+. Similar trends are observed for Ca2+ and Mg2+ because the solubility of CaSO4 and MgSO4 decreases at heating temperatures of 110 °C–150 °C, leading to the continuous precipitation of Ca2+ and Mg2+ as sulfates during evaporation–crystallization. Therefore, the effective inhibition of Ca2+ and Mg2+ with SO4 2– is crucial for achieving efficient ion removal.

Effects of the Addition of a Single Chelator

At the optimum heating temperature (130 °C) and evaporation volume (30 mL), organic acids, namely, CA, EDTA, EGTA, and DPTA, were added to the MnSO4 solution. The removal effects of these organic acids on Ca2+ and Mg2+ are depicted in Figures and after primary and secondary evaporation–crystallization experiments, respectively. From Figure , after one evaporation and crystallization cycle using the four chelators, the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ initially decrease, subsequently increase, and finally decrease. The addition of 0.5 g of CA results in the maximum removal effect on Ca2+ and Mg2+, achieving the removal rates of 76.22% and 60.29%, respectively. Similarly, the addition of 0.4 g of EDTA led to maximum Ca2+ and Mg2+ removal effect, achieving the removal rates of 74.20% and 63.62%, respectively. When 0.3 g of EGTA was added, the removal efficiencies of Ca2+ and Mg2+ were 78.87% and 63.34%, whereas the addition of 0.5 g of DPTA resulted in the Ca2+ and Mg2+ removal efficiencies of 81.09% and 58.41%, respectively.

Removal effects of a single chelator on Ca2+ and Mg2+ in primary evaporation–crystallization: (a) CA, (b) EDTA, (c) EGTA, and (d) DPTA.

Removal of Ca2+ and Mg2+ via the addition of a single chelating agent during secondary evaporation–crystallization: (a) CA, (b) EDTA, (c) EGTA, and (d) DPTA.

Based on the analysis of the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ achieved using different addition amounts of the four chelating agents in a single evaporation–crystallization process, the removal of Ca2+ in primary evaporation–crystallization is better than that of Mg2+; this is because although Ca2+ and Mg2+ belong to the same main group and exist as divalent ions, the radius of Ca2+ is larger than that of Mg2+, resulting in more favorable selection of Ca2+ by the effective functional groups. As shown in Figure , when the concentration of the chelating agent increases to a certain level, the removal rates of Ca and Mg will decrease. This is because the increase in free H+ concentration in the solution will compete with Ca2+ and Mg2+ for the coordinating atoms on the chelating agent. In primary evaporation–crystallization, the optimum amounts of the CA, EDTA, EGTA, and DPTA chelators, when used individually, are 0.5, 0.4, 0.3, and 0.5 g, respectively.

After the addition of the four chelators to the MnSO4 solution in secondary evaporation–crystallization, the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ first increase and subsequently decrease (Figure ). For CA, the optimal removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ of 87.22% and 85.08%, respectively, were achieved at an addition amount of 0.2 g, and their concentrations were 96.73 and 257.62 mg·kg–1, respectively. When 0.4 g of EDTA was added, the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ were 85.13% and 92.91%, and the concentrations were 112.58 and 122.38 mg·kg–1, respectively. The addition of 0.3 g of EGTA led to the Ca2+ and Mg2+ removal rates of 88.85% and 91.60%, resulting in the Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations of 84.37 and 145.10 mg·kg–1, respectively. When 0.3 g of DPTA was added, the optimum removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ of 93.02% and 83.05% were achieved, and their concentrations were 52.80 and 292.71 mg·kg–1, respectively.

A comparison of the Ca2+ and Mg2+ removal rates between primary and secondary evaporation–crystallization experiments is provided in Figure S1. In the secondary evaporation–crystallization cycle, the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ obtained by adding the four organic acid chelators were higher than those observed for the primary evaporation–crystallization experiment, and the removal rate of Mg2+ was clearly higher than that of Ca2+. Various factors contributed to the different removal effects of the four chelators on Ca2+ and Mg2+ in the secondary evaporation–crystallization. The change in Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations in the secondary evaporation–crystallization was one of the contributing factors. During the secondary evaporation–crystallization experiment, the concentration of residual Ca2+ in the solution was substantially low, and the concentration of residual Mg2+ was considerably higher than that of Ca2+. Therefore, the experimental data indicated that the removal effect on Mg2+ was more effective than that on Ca2+. Based on the experimental data, the optimum amounts of the CA, EDTA, EGTA, and DPTA chelators in the secondary evaporation–crystallization experiment were 0.2, 0.4, 0.3, and 0.3 g, respectively.

After the addition of the four chelators and two evaporation cycles, the Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations in MnSO4 still did not meet the battery-grade MnSO4 specifications. To further improve the removal effects of Ca2+ and Mg2+ in MnSO4, compound chelators were employed.

Effects of the Chelating Agent on the Physical Properties of Manganese Sulfate

Based on the aforementioned experimental results, the addition of chelators, such as CA, EDTA, EGTA and DPTA, in either individual or compound forms, can effectively eliminate Ca2+ and Mg2+ from industrial-grade manganese sulfate. Therefore, considering the samples obtained under the optimal conditions in each experiment as representative examples, this section presents the results of the systematic analysis of the crystal composition, morphology, and valence states of MnSO4 conducted via XRD, SEM, and XPS in Figures , and , respectively. The XRD analysis results in Figure (a–f) reveal that the main phases of the samples and raw materials in each experiment include MnSO4·H2O, CaSO4·2H2O, and MgSO4, and the changes in the reaction conditions and the addition of chelating agents do not alter the phase composition of Mn, Ca, and Mg. The SEM analysis results in Figure (a–f) reveal that both the raw materials and the samples present a layered structure. Further analysis of the samples at higher magnifications reveals that the distribution of Ca and Mg remains uniform, indicating that changes in physical conditions and the addition of chelating agents do not alter the structural form of MnSO4 or the distribution form of Ca and Mg within it. The raw materials and samples 1–3 show radioactive growth characteristics, while samples 4 and 5 exhibit planar growth, suggesting that the addition of chelating agents will have a certain impact on the growth characteristics of MnSO4. The XPS spectra of the samples and raw materials in Figure (a–f) reveal three characteristic peaks of Mn at 653.08, 646.98, and 641.43 eV. Furthermore, two characteristic peaks of Ca are observed at 347.50 and 351.40 eV, , whereas a characteristic peak of Mg is noted at 1,303.63 eV. − Therefore, the reaction conditions and chelators do not alter the valence states of Mn, Ca, and Mg, which exist in the divalent state. These characterization results indicate that the changes in the reaction conditions and the addition of chelating agents (single and compound) do not alter the existing forms, distribution, and valence states of Ca, Mg, and Mn in MnSO4.

Interaction of Mulliken Atomic Charges before and after the Combination of Ca, Mg, and Organic Acids

In an organic system with multiple receptor sites, the best donor will preferentially bind to the best receptor. Molecular electrostatic potential is a simple model for analyzing and understanding a variety of structural features and noncovalent interactions in a diversity of systems. The positive and negative values of interaction energy, respectively, represent positive potential and negative potential; locally, most positive charges correspond to regions lacking electrons and may become donor sites, while most negative values reflect regions with significant electron density, which are subsequently associated with acceptor sites.

Figure shows the distribution of the molecular electrostatic potential of chelating agents and their ligands. For the four ligands (CA, EDTA, EGTA, and DTPA), as the number of active sites increases, the positive charge regions also increase. This is mainly due to the increase in the number of carboxylic acid groups in the four chelating agents. The negative charges of four chelating agents are concentrated near the O (mainly distributed in the CO of the carboxylic acid group) and the N, whereas the positive charges are mainly distributed near Ca2+ and Mg2+. The binding of chelating agents to Ca2+ and Mg2+ will change the negative charges and distribution of the chelating agents and appear in regions away from the Ca2+ and Mg2+. However, for the four ligands (CA, EDTA, EGTA, and DPTA), as the number of active sites increases, the negative charges still exist, indicating that the selected chelating agents cannot completely neutralize the positive charge influence of Ca2+ and Mg2+ on the ligands. After comparing these four chelating agents with the ligands of Ca2+ and Mg2+, it was found that the four ligands are superior to binding to Ca2+ and then to Mg2+. This is consistent with the experimental results.

The calculation of binding energies facilitates the quantification of the stability of chelates and reveals the strength of the intermolecular interactions. The binding energy directly reflects the stability of chelates. A negative adsorption energy indicates that the reaction can occur spontaneously, and the larger the adsorption energy, the easier the adsorption process and the higher the stability. Similarly, the order of the binding energies of the chelates formed by the chelators and Mg2+ coordination is as follows: EGTA < CA < EDTA < DPTA. Theoretically, the binding ability of DPTA to single Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions is stronger than that of the other three chelators.

Conclusion

We have developed a highly efficient chelation-assisted crystallization process that achieves battery-grade purity of MnSO4 in significantly fewer cycles. A synergistic DTPA/CA (3:1) chelator system proved optimal, enabling removal rates of 94.6% for Ca2+ and 95.1% for Mg2+ in a single secondary crystallization step. This reduced impurity concentrations to 40.7 and 84.0 mg·kg–1, respectively, well below the required threshold. Theoretical simulations confirm the high binding affinity of the chelators for impurity ions, rationalizing the experimental selectivity. Our work provides a molecular-guided, energy-efficient strategy for the sustainable production of high-purity energy materials.

This study combined conventional evaporation–crystallization technology with a green chelating agent method to reduce the Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations in industrial MnSO4 to lower than 100 mg·kg–1. The introduction of chelating agents decreased the number of evaporations–crystallization cycles, considerably decreasing the energy consumption. The addition of CA, EDTA, EGTA, and DPTA facilitates the efficient removal of Ca2+ and Mg2+. Among these chelating agents, DPTA exhibits the most pronounced removal effect. After secondary evaporation–crystallization, the optimal removal rates of Ca and Mg reach 93.02% and 83.05%, respectively. The selection type, compound ratio, and addition amount of the complex chelating agent affect the removal of Ca2+ and Mg2+ in MnSO4. When the DPTA:CA ratio was 3:1 and the dosage was 0.4 g, the removal rates of Ca2+ and Mg2+ were the highest after secondary evaporation–crystallization, reaching 94.63% and 95.14%, and the Ca2+ and Mg2+ concentrations in MnSO4 decreased from 756.87 and 2,126.67 mg·kg–1 to 40.68 and 84.00 mg·kg–1, respectively, fulfilling the control requirements for Ca2+ and Mg2+ for battery-grade MnSO4. Furthermore, the four chelating agents was calculated via DFT calculations. Therefore, these chelating agents can form stable chelates with Ca2+ and Mg2+, confirming their efficacy in the efficient removal of Ca and Mg from MnSO4. Future research will focus on the regeneration and cyclic stability of chelating agents, among other related studies.
