Alkylsilane-extended hydrogen migration enhanced photothermal Sabatier reaction
Authors: Zhe Lu, Wenxuan Liu, Zeshu Zhang, Zhongyi Chen, Junchuan Sun, Wanguo Gao, Xiaolu Zhuo, Wenguang Tu, Guoping Hu, Yingfang Yao, Dongxu Ji, Junhua Zhao, Zhigang Zou, Lu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hydrogen migration is a process of key importance in hydrogenation reactions. In past decades, surface oxygen has been considered the major pathway for hydrogen migration, whereas the reduction reaction may occur to yield water and inhibit the migration. Thus, extending hydrogen migration via precise surface decoration could potentially be beneficial to the activity of hydrogenation reactions, yet it remains challenging. Herein, the Sabatier reaction is chosen as the archetype reaction, in which the catalytic performance is highly dependent on the activity of hydrogen migration. By decorating a minor amount of non-reducible alkylsilane over the benchmark Ni/CeO2 catalyst, the hydrogen activation, migration, and overall hydrogenation reaction have been enhanced significantly. As a result, with the help of the exothermic nature of the Sabatier reaction, the solely solar-driven reaction can be conducted with a CO2 single pass conversion of 99.9%, a near-unity CH4 selectivity, and an energy conversion efficiency of ~25.0% for outdoor practice.

Introduction

Hydrogen migration over the catalyst surface is a well-known and significant phenomenon that sways the catalytic activity and product distribution of hydrogenation and dehydrogenation reactions1–4. In principle, the protons could be formed via the activation of hydrogen molecules over the active sites and thus migrate through the negatively charged surface oxygen atoms5,6. Therefore, a common strategy for extending the hydrogen migration is to employ a reducible or highly defective non-reducible oxide as the support, which usually results in interface reduction with low stability or limited performance7–10. Thus, it would be of high scientific interest and technological importance to have an alternative strategy to extend hydrogen migration via a stable “network” over the catalyst that could facilitate the catalytic performance.

Alkylsilane, a combination of silane and a hydrocarbon chain, is a stable compound widely utilized for surface hydrophobic treatment11,12. Hydrophobic alkylsilanes, with strong repelling of polar molecules like water, have been considered an efficient strategy for various water-involved reactions, such as the Sabatier reaction13,14, Fischer-Tropsch synthesis15,16, and epoxidation reaction17,18. More recently, combined with the C–H activation site, the inert alkyl chains could undergo a dynamic deuteration process19,20, implying a possible network for hydrogen migration. To further examine such an idea, Ni, as the known active site for both hydrogenation and dehydrogenation reactions21–25, was used as a terrific candidate to examine the possibility of extending hydrogen migration by decorating alkylsilane onto the surface.

Herein, the 5.0 wt% Ni/CeO2 offering good metal dispersion, balanced metal–support interactions, and moderate cost26–28, was chosen as the catalyst for the photothermal Sabatier reaction. The Sabatier reaction involving the hydrogenation of CO2 to CH4 under mild conditions has been extensively studied as a cornerstone process in power-to-gas technologies and carbon-neutral energy cycles29,30. This reaction proceeds with a stoichiometric H2/CO2 ratio of 4:1, strongly dependent on the performance of hydrogen migration, and has been chosen as the probe reaction31,32. The alkylsilane with an octyl carbon chain was employed and decorated onto the catalyst to act as a network for hydrogen migration. During the Sabatier reaction, extending hydrogen migration over surface alkylsilane could promote the hydrogenation of absorbed CO2-related species, thereby enhancing the overall methane production. As a result, the CO2 single-pass conversion of 99.9% with a near-unity CH4 selectivity could be achieved with natural sunlight.

Results

Structural characterizations

The Ni/CeO2 catalyst, with approximately 5.0 wt% nickel loading labeled as S0, was synthesized using the impregnation technique. The decorated samples of S1 to S4 with the Si loading of 0.009, 0.014, 0.022, and 0.041 wt%, respectively, underwent silanization under reflux conditions with toluene. All samples exhibited the Ni loadings of 5.03 ± 0.21 wt% (Supplementary Table 1). The attenuated total reflectance-Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) shown in Supplementary Fig. 1 revealed CH3 and CH2 signals at 2980 and 2900 cm−1, respectively, which shifted to higher frequencies compared to typical peak positions (2960 and 2854 cm−1)16,33. This shift suggested that alkanes from the introduced alkylsilanes were likely lying on the surface18. The progressive enhancement of Si-OH stretching bands at 3740 cm−1 from samples S1 to S4 suggested an increased incorporation of modified silanes16,34. The introduced alkylsilanes caused a gradual increase in the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) surface area from 44.6 m2 g−1 of S0 to 53.0 m2 g−1 of S4 in Supplementary Fig. 2. This trend could be caused by the formation of new pores on the CeO2 surface. The optical absorbance spectra of all samples showed similar absorption characteristics, indicating that alkylsilanes did not alter their light-harvesting capabilities (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) results in Fig. 1a confirmed that all samples possessed a cubic fluorite structure characteristic of CeO2 (#PDF 34-0394) without any detectable SiO2-related signals35,36. Thermogravimetric (TG) tests demonstrated that the alkylsilane moieties attached to the samples could maintain stability up to 400 °C (Supplementary Fig. 4). The diffraction peaks for Ni were presented only in the S0 sample in Supplementary Fig. 5, indicating large Ni particles on the surface. The graphs from the high-resolution transmission electron microscope (HRTEM) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray analyzer (EDX) revealed that the Ni nanoparticles were embedded on the surface of CeO2 (Supplementary Fig. 6). Statistical analysis in Fig. 1b indicated that optimal alkylsilane modification on the CeO2 surface reduces the average Ni particle size from ~13 nm in S0 to ~7 nm in S2, improving Ni particle dispersion due to the confinement effect of surface ligands37,38. Aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) was further employed to elucidate the structural characteristics of the added alkylsilanes. As shown in Fig. 1c, Si atoms bonded to the surface of CeO2 were located in close proximity to Ni nanoparticles. The combined evidence from the confinement-induced particle size change and the aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM images supported the spatial proximity between Ni nanoparticles and the alkylsilane-modified sites on CeO2. The estimated Si atomic coverage of S2 was nearly 3.8 × 10−13 mol cm−2. A nanoparticle of Ni with a size of 7 nm is estimated to be accompanied by ~145 alkylsilane species (Supplementary Note 1). It can be inferred that the Ni nanoparticles were close to a portion of the lying alkylsilanes on the CeO2 surface, as shown in Fig. 1d.

Catalytic performance

The catalytic performance of the photothermal Sabatier reaction was evaluated for all samples, yielding CH4 with CO as a byproduct. As shown in Fig. 2a, the optimal alkylsilane coating in S2 significantly enhanced photothermal catalytic activity. Compared to the unmodified Ni/CeO2 catalyst (S0), which exhibited CO2 conversions of 53.0% in the dark and 54.7% under light conditions, S2 achieved markedly improved performance with 68.4% and 74.9% conversion under corresponding conditions. The S3 and S4 samples with higher concentrations of surface alkyl chains demonstrated diminished activity with CO2 conversion of 57.0% and 39.8% under light, respectively. This decline suggested that the increased particle size of Ni (Fig. 1b) and excessive coverage of alkyl chains could reduce catalytic performance. These trends can be also performed under the high weight hourly space velocity in the kinetic-control region (Supplementary Fig. 7). In addition, the surface alkylsilanes enhanced the stability, leading to a deactivation rate of ~7.9 × 10−5 h−1 over 35 hours at 300 °C under light, an order of magnitude lower than S0 (Supplementary Fig. 8). S0 was set as the reference material to evaluate the role of light during the reaction. As shown in Fig. 2b, light could increase CO2 conversions and methane selectivities over both S0 and S2 among various temperatures. The highest light-induced increase in CO2 conversion from 26.5% to 34.2% was observed for S2 at 260 °C, significantly greater than the 4.3% increase on S0. In Fig. 2c, S2 exhibited the highest solar-to-chemical efficiency (ηSTC) of ~43.4% at 250 °C under the light intensity of 1.5 W cm−2, nearly five times that of S0 under identical conditions. The results indicated a photothermal coupling effect at ~250 °C39. The linear slope between light intensity and the increased CH4 rate after light illumination over S2 was about four at 200 °C, while decreasing to ~1 at both 250 °C and 300 °C, suggesting the light domination became weaker (Supplementary Fig. 10). These values are lower than the corresponding slopes of ~3 on S0 at 250 °C. The super-linear relationship with a slope more significant than 2 demonstrated the prominent role of light-induced hot electrons40,41, which can be annihilated at high temperatures42. Therefore, the lower slope at 250 °C indicated that the effective coupling mode on S2 may be dominated by heat, while light serves as a promoter, much different from the mechanism on S0.

To verify the feasibility of the photothermal Sabatier reaction powered by natural sunlight on S2, a self-designed photon-recycling reactor and a plastic Fresnel lens with nearly concentrated 2.2 W cm−2 were used (Fig. 2d). The catalyst temperature was achieved at 360 ~ 370 °C as detected by thermocouple and infrared camera (Supplementary Fig. 11a, c). As shown in Fig. 2e, the highest CH4 yield of approximately 100% was achieved from 9:00 to 16:00. Concurrently, a ηSTC of ~25.0% was achieved under concentrated sunlight with the light intensity of ~2.2 W cm-2. These results demonstrated the superior methanation performance of S2 under sunlight. A Xenon lamp with a similar light intensity and heating temperature (Supplementary Fig. 11) was used to simulate natural sunlight to evaluate the stability of S2. Under the irradiation with a light intensity of ~1.9 W cm−2, without external heating, the CO2 conversion over S2 achieved 99.8% with a ηSTC of 28.9%, higher than that at 375 °C with the conversion rate of 90.5% under dark (Supplementary Fig. 11f). Figure 2f shows that the S2 sample demonstrated superior stability in converting CO2 to CH4 with a ~ 100% yield for over 100 h. Compared to recent photothermal methanation Ni-based catalysts, the S2 sample exhibited outstanding performance in converting solar energy to the chemical energy of methane (Supplementary Table 2). These results demonstrated the capability of near-unity methane yield powered by sunlight, catalyzed by S2.

Furthermore, a techno-economic analysis using a self-designed large language model (LLM) agent for the solar-driven Sabatier reaction (T2) was conducted (Supplementary Note 2, Supplementary Fig. 12). Under the operation with natural light and LED light concentrated by Fresnel lens, the average cost of T2 in 2025–2030 was calculated to be ~USD 554 per ton of CH4 at a given average price of green hydrogen at USD 1021 per ton of H2 and the average carbon tax of USD 155 per ton of CO2. This cost is lower than the ~USD 875 per ton of CH4 achieved by the thermocatalytic coal to methane process (T3)43. Notably, T2 became profitable at green hydrogen prices below USD 500 per ton of H2 (Fig. 2g)44, a potentially viable threshold assuming a 70% share of green hydrogen in the overall demand composition by 2050 (Supplementary Note 3, Supplementary Fig. 13). The superior methane yield, reduced costs of capital and fuel, and low CO2 emissions demonstrated great economic potential of T2 as a sustainable alternative to the conventional coal-to-methane process (Fig. 2h).

Catalytic mechanism

Generally, the H2 molecule was cleaved on Ni particles, generating H atoms, which can migrate to the surface of CeO2 and participate in reactions45. The kinetic behaviors were analyzed to investigate the role of introduced alkylsilanes in S2 for heat-dominated photothermal Sabatier reactions. As shown in Fig. 3a, the S2 sample exhibited a significantly lower reaction order for H2 at 0.21 in the dark for methane formation compared to ~0.59 for S0. Upon light introduction, the reaction order decreased to ~0.08 for S2. The near-zero H2 reaction order on S2 suggested the low hydrogen migration energy barriers. This was corroborated by the reduction in activation energy and CO2 order for CH4 formation from S0 to S2 (Supplementary Figs. 14, 15). The carbon chain length was adjusted further to explore the specific role of alkylsilanes in methane production. Samples using specified alkylsilanes were designated as “C”, followed by the number of carbon atoms, and the C8 with eight carbon atoms corresponded to S2. According to the H2-TPR results shown in Fig. 3b, longer carbon chains resulted in reduced peak intensities at approximately 100 °C, likely due to H2 consumption on the larger NiO particles weakly interacting with CeO226,46,47. This finding aligned with previous results indicating that the appropriate addition of alkylsilanes can enhance the dispersion of Ni particles (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 6)48–51. The peak observed between 150 and 250 °C, attributed to H2 consumption at the Ni-CeO2 interface52, increased with the length of the hydrocarbon chain, with a slight decrease noted for C12. After calcination of C8 in the air at 500 °C to remove the surface hydrocarbon network, the resulting sample, labeled as C8-A, showed reduced interface consumption compared to C8. The appropriate addition of alkylsilanes regulated H2 consumption at the interface, gradually increasing from S0 to S2 and decreasing from S2 to S4 (Supplementary Fig. 16). The latter phenomenon would be likely due to inhibited H2 activation reduced number of active sites caused by blockage of the CeO2 surface. Consequently, a volcano-type relationship in CH4 production performance could be observed with various hydrocarbon chains (Fig. 3c). Therefore, one can deduce that the hydrocarbon chains of alkylsilanes could enhance hydrogen activation, and thus facilitate the methanation. The decreased methane rate in C8-A is further evidenced by intermediate H2 reaction orders under dark and light conditions, compared with S2 and S0, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 17. This implies the promoted H migration with higher H consumption.

In terms of the color shift of the chromogenic experiment via WO3, distinct color changes could be observed from the physically mixed yellow WO3 with S0, C8-A, or S2, resulting in green, cyan, and blue, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 18). The results demonstrated a progressive increase in the level of oxide reduction from samples S0, C8-A, and S2. This phenomenon further confirmed the role of surface hydrocarbon chains in enhanced hydrogen migration and activation efficiency.

The enhanced hydrogen migration was further demonstrated by in situ diffuse reflection infrared spectroscopy (DRIFTS) results with three stages (I: H2, II: D2, and III: H2). As shown in Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 19, for S2, the reduction in CH2 (2848 cm−1) and the blue shift of ~3 cm−1 in CH3 (2964 cm−1) were attributed to C–H stretching of CH2D under D253,54. It can be suggested that the surface alkyl chains on S2 serve as mediators for extending hydrogen migration from Ni nanoparticles. Under continuous H2 (Supplementary Fig. 20), both CH2 and CH3 groups gradually increased as the dehydrogenated alkyl chains under He were regenerated by H2. Variable-temperature DRIFTS under dilute H2 further indicated a facile migration process, consistent with low apparent energy barriers (Supplementary Fig. 21). Similar trends could be found on the S0-C8 sample post-grafting alkylsilanes on S0, indicating that the promoted hydrogen migration by alkylsilanes could be performed on different Ni sizes (Supplementary Figs. 22–24). Under the atmosphere, the appearance of H signals could be observed by in situ DRIFTS and mass spectroscopy (MS) in Supplementary Figs. 25, 26. Control experiments using a perfluorooctyl-modified catalyst (FC8) in Supplementary Fig. 27 showed nearly no C–D stretching signals (2150 and 2022 cm−1) under D254, confirming that fluorinated chains suppress H-D exchange and that hydrogen transfer indeed occurs via the alkyl chains. Taken together, these results indicated that activated hydrogen can migrate along the alkyl chains, with the terminal alkyl groups in contact with Ni serving as mediators for extending hydrogen migration from Ni nanoparticles. Therefore, the extended hydrogen migration over S2 resulted in more active H species (Fig. 3e).

The extended hydrogen migration over S2 could be further demonstrated by the rapid hydrogenation of surface-absorbed CO2-related species. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 28, the catalyst surface with absorbed CO2 exhibited formate (HCOO*, 1587 and 1387 cm−1), bidentate carbonate (b-CO3*, 1287 cm−1), and monodentate carbonate (m-CO3*, 1525 and 1405 cm−1) from 50 to 300 °C26,55,56. At 50 °C, minor signals of methoxy species (*CH3O, 1090 cm−1) were observed, which disappeared as surface H2O decreased with rising temperatures47.

H2 was introduced to the reactor after saturated CO2 absorption at 300 °C, as shown in Fig. 4a–c. In the first 20 min, gaseous CO2 near 2300 cm−1 was swept and disappeared. Over S0, signals of HCOO*, b-CO3*, and m-CO3* were detected, along with minor CH3O species. Peaks located at 2830, 2115, and 1740 cm−1 appeared, corresponding to *CH2, *CO(g), and *CHO, respectively56–58, suggesting a general formate route where surface carbonates were hydrogenated to formates and then methane55. In contrast, S2 showed the conversion of HCOO* and b-CO3*, rapidly decreasing after one hour (Fig. 4b). The higher peak intensity of HCOO* on S2 indicated that more carbonates were hydrogenated. Remarkably, the HCOO* species on S2 completely vanished after 1 h in Fig. 4b. Conversely, no significant changes were observed on the formate species over S0 between 20 and 60 min (Fig. 4a). These findings suggested that the extended hydrogen migration facilitated by alkylsilanes on S2 enables remote hydrogenation of formates (Fig. 4d-II and III). The enhanced *HCOO was evident after introducing light illumination, as shown in Fig. 4c, demonstrating that light can also promote hydrogen migration to facilitate carbonate hydrogenation to formate. The light-promoted hydrogen migration was further corroborated by the navy-blue color over S2 mixed with WO3 after the reduction under light (Supplementary Fig. 14). Light illumination can further promote hydrogen migration, driving the photothermal coupling effect toward a light-assisted thermal catalytic regime, as demonstrated in Fig. 2c. Consequently, S2 exhibited an outstanding methanation performance, achieving nearly 100% methane yield under sunlight.

Discussion

In summary, we have demonstrated that decorating Ni/CeO2 with a minor amount of alkylsilane is an effective strategy to extend hydrogen migration and enhance the overall Sabatier reaction. Comprehensive characterizations revealed that the alkylsilane decoration reduced Ni particle size from ~13 to ~7 nm and introduced surface hydrocarbon chains positioned near Ni. Kinetic and mechanistic studies, including H2-TPR, in situ DRIFTS with H2/D2 switching, and reaction order analysis, consistently confirmed that alkylsilane decoration enriches active hydrogen species and facilitates their migration across the catalyst surface. This extended hydrogen migration enabled the rapid hydrogenation of surface formates and carbonates, resulting in a ~ 15% increase in CO2 conversion from S0 to S2.

Under photothermal conditions, the optimized catalyst S2 achieved a solar-to-chemical efficiency of ~43% at 250 °C in the laboratory, nearly five times higher than the pristine catalyst, and maintained a stable near-unity methane yield for over 100 h. Outdoor experiments under natural sunlight further validated the practical feasibility, with CO2 conversion approaching 100% and an energy conversion efficiency of ~25%. Moreover, techno-economic analysis suggested that the solar-driven Sabatier process could reach production costs competitive with coal-to-methane under foreseeable green hydrogen prices, highlighting the sustainability potential of this approach. Therefore, the integration of alkylsilane decoration into Ni/CeO2 not only provides mechanistic insights into hydrogen migration beyond oxide-mediated pathways but also establishes a robust and economically viable strategy for efficient solar-driven CO2 conversion.

Methods

Preparation of catalysts

Ni(NO3)2 ∙ xH2O (99.99%), Ce(NO3)2 ∙ xH2O (99.99%), NH4OH (GR, 25 ~ 28%), and toluene (AR) were purchased from Aladdin Chemistry Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Triethoxyoctylsilane (98%), Propyltriethoxysilane (98%), and dodecyltriethoxysilane (97%) were purchased from Innochem Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All chemicals were used without further purification. CeO2 was synthesized via a hydrothermal method with the feedstock of 2.5 g of Ce(NO3)2 ∙ xH2O, 62 mL H2O, and 10 mL of NH4OH at 90 °C for 6 h. After water-washing, the sediments were dried overnight and then air-calcined at 500 °C for 4 h. The preparation of alkylsilane-modified supports was achieved by silanization reactions. 600 mg of prepared CeO2 was mixed with 6 mL of toluene. Subsequently, 2 μl of triethoxyoctylsilane (TEOOS) was added. The mixture was then refluxed at 50 °C for 3 h under stirring. After that, the products were centrifugally separated, washed, and then dried in a vacuum oven at 120 °C and dried for 24 h. The decoration of Ni was then conducted via an impregnation method using the ethanol solution of Ni(NO3)2. Then, the mixture was dried overnight and reduced under 10% H2/Ar at 500 °C for 1 h with the total decomposition of nickel nitrates (Supplementary Fig. 31). The obtained catalysts were named S2. Similarly, the catalyst with the supports synthesized from 2 μl TEOOS and 1200 mg CeO2 was named S1. Samples synthesized from 600 mg CeO2 and 10 μl and 20 μl of TEOOS were named S3 and S4, respectively. C3 and C12 with the same theoretical Si content as S2 were prepared using a similar process, with the feedstock of propyltriethoxysilane and dodecyltriethoxysilane, respectively.

Structural characterizations

The Si content was determined through an ICP using Agilent 5110. The attenuated total reflectance infrared spectroscopy (ATR-IR) tests were performed on a Thermo Nicolet iS50 Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectrometer with an integrated ATR module. The phase compositions of the samples were characterized by a Rigaku Smartlab diffractometer equipped with a Cu Kα radiation source (λ = 1.54056 Å). The X-ray source was operated at 40 kV and 200 mA. The phase identification was made by comparison to the Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards (JCPDS). The N2 physical adsorption-desorption test can be used to analyze the specific surface area and pore structure. The test was confirmed on a Micromeritics ASAP 2010 instrument. The specific surface area was analyzed by the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) method. The pore structure was investigated by the Barrett-Joyner-Halenda (BJH) method. The morphology of the samples was investigated through transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The TEM images, high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images, and EDX mapping were obtained using a JEOL JEM-2100F microscope at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. The weight loss and heat changes of samples at different temperatures were investigated through TG with differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) and MS. The samples were tested in a Netzsch STA449F3-QMS403D thermogravimetric analyzer under N2.

Mechanistic study

H2-TPR was conducted on a chemisorption analyzer (Micromeritics Autochem II 2920). 100 mg of the as-prepared sample was pretreated under flowing argon for 0.5 h at room temperature and then heated to 300 °C for 0.5 h and further 400 °C for 1 h. Then the sample was cooled down to 50 °C under flowing argon. After stabilization, a mixture of gases of 10% H2 in Ar was introduced and heated (10 °C min−1) to 800 °C gradually. The effluent gas was analyzed using a PFEIFFER OmniSTAR. Moreover, we conducted in-situ DRIFTS to characterize the adsorbed species on the sample surface and further monitor the catalytic reaction process in real time. The tests were performed on a Thermo Nicolet iS50 FTIR spectrometer with a high-precision Mercury-Cadmium-Telluride (MCT) detector cooled by liquid nitrogen. Beyond an infrared spectrometer, the experimental system consists of a diffuse reflectance accessory (Praying Mantis, Harrick), a high-temperature cell (HVC, Harrick, equipped with BaF2 windows), a gas system, and a heating and temperature control device. Before the in-situ tests, the catalyst was purged under a flowing Ar atmosphere at 573 K for 1 h to remove possible impurity molecules. Then, the background spectrum under the Ar atmosphere at different temperatures was collected to subtract the interference of H2O and CO2 from the sample spectrum. The scanning resolution was 16 cm−1, and the number of scans was 64. For the MS analysis, the residual gas was detected via a Hiden HPR-20 EGA mass spectrometer.

Economic analysis

The energy and process economy were simulated using Aspen Plus v11, based on tested conversion rates and product yields. A techno-economic model assessing capital and operational costs for CH4 production via thermal catalytic Sabatier reaction (T1) and solar-driven Sabatier reaction (T2) was developed, assuming a 20-year lifetime, 10% discount rate, an 8% annual interest rate, 20% tax rate, 10-year depreciation, and 8000 h of operation annually. To consider diverse cost scenarios, an innovative LLM agent-based approach was then used (Supplementary Note 2).
