Integrated heart–blood RNA editome profiling reveals circulating A-to-I editing candidates linked to dilated cardiomyopathy myocardial signatures and heart failure☆
Authors: Korina Karagianni, Mantiana Topouzi, Ilektra Mitsi, Theodoros Sklaviadis, Antigone Lazou, Dimitra Dafou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is a leading cause of heart failure (HF), yet molecular signatures that are detectable in blood and mechanistically anchored in myocardial pathology remain limited. Adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) RNA editing is an important post-transcriptional regulator in the heart, but its biomarker potential in human DCM and its reflection in the circulation are not well defined.MethodsWe performed comprehensive RNA editome profiling of human myocardium from DCM patients and non-failing donors, and integrated these results with peripheral blood RNA editing profiles from HF patients and controls, using a SPRINT-based pipeline with stringent in-house post-postprocessing and quality filtering. Global editing distributions and site-level differential editing were assessed, and shared heart–blood candidates were evaluated for directionality and functional enrichment.ResultsCardiac RNA editing was dominated by ADAR-mediated A-to-I events and was significantly increased in DCM, with prominent enrichment in Alu elements and a higher number of unique editing sites compared with controls. Site-level analysis identified a predominantly hyper-edited DCM signature, mapping to genes and pathways linked to cardiomyopathy and cellular stress, including hypoxia and apoptosis. Cross-tissue comparison revealed 107 shared differentially edited sites between DCM heart and HF blood, with subsets showing concordant directionality. Shared targets were enriched for innate immune and type I interferon signaling, RIG-I pathway regulation, hypoxia-related responses, and apoptosis.ConclusionsDCM is associated with global myocardial A-to-I hyper-editing and a robust, multi-locus editing program. Importantly, a subset of these disease-associated editing events is detectable in peripheral blood from heart failure patients, supporting the feasibility of HF-associated circulating RNA editing signatures that reflect myocardial pathology.Keywords: Dilated cardiomyopathy, Heart failure, RNA editing, Biomarkers, A-to-I editing, ADAR

Introduction

Cardiomyopathies constitute a major myocardial substrate for heart failure (HF) and encompass a heterogeneous group of disorders characterized by structural and functional abnormalities of the myocardium ([1]). Among these, dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) is the most prevalent form worldwide and is characterized by left ventricular dilation and systolic dysfunction ([1], [2]). DCM arises from diverse genetic and acquired insults, including pathogenic variants, ischemic injury, inflammation, toxins, and metabolic stress, which converge on shared pathological mechanisms such as cardiomyocyte injury, energetic impairment, inflammation, and progressive extracellular matrix remodeling ([3], [4]). These myocardial processes are increasingly reflected by molecular changes in the circulation, supporting the concept that cardiac remodeling leaves a systemic molecular footprint amenable to biomarker discovery. Circulating biomarkers of collagen synthesis and turnover, including procollagen type I C-terminal propeptide (PICP), as well as matrix metalloproteinases such as MMP-1, have been associated with adverse remodeling and poor prognosis in DCM, independent of conventional clinical indices ([5], [6], [7]). More recently, large-scale proteomic profiling of DCM plasma revealed widespread dysregulation of proteins involved in inflammation, complement activation, wound healing, and lipid and energy metabolism, with accumulation of metabolic enzymes such as fructose-1,6-bisphosphate aldolase in patients who fail to undergo reverse remodeling ([8]). In parallel, metabolomic studies have identified elevated circulating tricarboxylic acid cycle intermediates, including succinate, α-ketoglutarate, and isocitrate, which correspond to altered myocardial expression of key metabolic enzymes, including succinate dehydrogenase and aconitase-1, indicating systemic reflection of disrupted cardiac energy metabolism ([9]). Together, these findings underscore the tight coupling between myocardial remodeling and circulating molecular signatures in DCM.

In addition to changes in gene expression levels, post-transcriptional regulatory mechanisms have emerged as a critical and relatively underexplored layer of cardiac pathobiology. Notably, RNA editing, a mechanism that introduces alterations in RNA sequences, has emerged as an important regulator of cardiomyocyte homeostasis, immune signaling, and cellular stress responses ([10], [11]). In mammals, the predominant forms of RNA editing are adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) editing catalyzed by adenosine deaminases acting on RNA (ADARs) and cytidine-to-uridine (C-to-U) editing mediated by APOBEC family enzymes, which can influence mRNA splicing, stability, localization, coding potential, and microRNA interactions ([12], [13], [14]). Genome-wide studies of human myocardium have shown that A-to-I editing represents the predominant type of RNA editing in cardiac tissue and that editing patterns are substantially altered in the failing heart ([15], [16]). Mechanistic studies provide strong causal evidence linking dysregulated RNA editing to cardiomyopathy and HF. Cardiomyocyte specific loss of ADAR1 disrupts normal RNA surveillance and immune regulation, leading to activation of interferon signaling pathways, autoinflammatory myocarditis, dilated cardiomyopathy, and progressive cardiac dysfunction in murine models ([17]). Furthermore, ADAR1 deficiency enhances cardiomyocyte apoptosis and maladaptive stress responses, while ADAR2 loss downregulates antifibrotic miRNAs (miR-29b, miR-405, miR-19), promoting myocardial fibrosis and cardiomyopathy ([18], [19], [20], [21]). Additionally, ADAR1-mediated RNA editing of Alu elements within the 3’UTR stabilizes CTSS mRNA, regulating vascular inflammation ([22], [23]). Furthermore, deregulated editing patterns in NEAT1 lncRNA ([24]) and circRNAs ([15]) strongly correlate with HF related transcriptomic profiles, highlighting dysregulated RNA editing as both a contributor to HF progression and a promising therapeutic target.

Despite these advances, the RNA editome of human DCM has not been systematically evaluated for biomarker potential, and the extent to which myocardial editing signatures are mirrored in the circulation remains unclear. In this study, we performed a comprehensive analysis of A-to-I RNA editing in human DCM myocardium versus non-failing donor hearts to define both global and site-specific editing alterations. We then integrated these myocardial signatures with peripheral blood RNA editing profiles from patients with heart failure (HF) and control individuals to identify a subset of differentially edited sites detectable in the circulation that overlap with DCM-associated myocardial events. By prioritizing overlapping candidates enriched in disease-relevant pathways, our aim was to establish a blood-accessible RNA editing signature anchored in myocardial pathology, providing a foundation for minimally invasive biomarker development and mechanistically informed follow-up studies.

Methods

Data

We aimed to establish RNA editing profiles and investigate the differential editome patterns between dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) patients and corresponding control donors in the heart. In parallel, we analyzed blood samples from patients with heart failure (no detailed underlying aetiology in the source metadata, only dataset GSE196656 specifies detailed inclusion criteria for patients with HF) and corresponding controls, with the final goal to cross-compare heart and blood tissue for the identification of editome signature biomarkers and potential therapeutic targets. Our data was retrieved from publicly available GSE141910, GSE71613, GSE135055, PRJNA574863, GSE101585, GSE196656, and PRJNA532278 datasets (Supplementary File 1). The samples selected for the initial analysis correspond to heart samples from DCM patients (n = 23) and corresponding control donors (n = 12), as well as blood samples from patients with heart failure (n = 14) and control (n = 9). Furthermore, to address potential between-study heterogeneity arising from integrating public cohorts, including datasets containing only one phenotype, we performed within-study sensitivity analyses restricted to datasets that included both phenotypes (heart: GSE141910 and GSE71613; blood: PRJNA574863 and GSE196656) (Supplementary File 2).

RNA editing analysis

Raw RNA-seq data were subjected to quality control analyses using the FastQC software (v0.11.7) (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc). Adapter sequence contamination, low quality read regions, and long homopolymeric stretches were removed using the TrimGalore software (https://github.com/FelixKrueger/TrimGalore). Data were processed following an in-house developed pipeline previously described ([25]), based on the SPRINT toolkit ([26]). All potential RNA editing sites were identified using the “sprint main” program within SPRINT with default parameters. A-to-G and T-to-C mismatches were considered as potential ADAR mediated RNA-editing sites, while C-to-T and G-to-A mismatches were considered as potential Apolipoprotein B mRNA editing enzyme catalytic polypeptide (APOBEC) mediated RNA-editing sites.

We conducted high quality control analyses, including the assessment of the whole RNA:DNA Difference (RDD) spectrum representation, the estimation of editing event enrichment in Alu and other repetitive regions, and the overlap of identified RNA editing sites with RNA editing events deposited in REDIportal v2, which is considered one of the most annotated and comprehensive RNA Editing databases [[27]]. The Open-CRAVAT toolkit (https://www.opencravat.org/) was utilized to obtain additional information about the sites where editing events were identified.

Strict filtering criteria were applied to ensure true RNA editing events and exclude false positive results. Samples with low percentage of uniquely mapped reads and high percentage of duplicated reads were excluded from the analysis. Only sites with a minimum base coverage of 10, minimum editing frequency of 5% and minimum quality per base of 25 were considered as adequate for our analysis. Furthermore, to reduce false positives, sites located in pseudogenes or sites that were assigned a dbSNP identification (ID) and were not reported in the REDIPortal database were also excluded.

Enrichment analysis, correlation analysis and heatmap generation

Pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the Enrichr web-based tool. Gene sets used as input for enrichment analyses were derived from differentially edited sites, regardless of the genomic region in which the editing event occurred. Specifically, genes harboring at least one differentially edited site with nominal p ≤ 0.05 and |Δ-editing frequency| ≥ 0.20 were used for heart (DCM) enrichment analyses, while for cross-tissue heart–blood comparisons genes were selected based on p ≤ 0.05 and |Δ-editing frequency| ≥ 0.10 in both datasets, with overlap at identical genomic coordinates and concordant directionality. Enrichment was performed against curated databases including ClinVar disease associations, Reactome and Gene Ontology (GO) pathways, and enriched terms were ranked based on adjusted p-values provided by Enrichr. Only statistically significant enriched terms (adjusted p ≤ 0.05) were reported.

Correlation analyses were used to assess the concordance of disease-associated RNA-editing changes between tissues. For editing sites shared between heart and blood datasets, the change in editing frequency (Δ-editing frequency = disease − control) was calculated independently for each tissue. Pearson correlation analysis was then performed to evaluate the relationship between Δ-editing frequencies in heart and blood.

Heatmaps were generated to visualize cross-tissue concordance for shared editing candidates using the raw Δ-editing frequencies computed for each tissue (heart and blood). Values were displayed as a paired heatmap (heart Δ-editing frequency alongside blood Δ-editing frequency) to enable direct comparison of direction and magnitude across compartments.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism (v8.0.2) and R (v4.3.2). Wilcoxon's signed-rank test was used to compare the distribution of global RNA editing events levels (% mean editing frequency per editing site) between groups (DCM vs CNTR, HF vs CNTR).

For site-level differential editing, editing frequencies were compared between groups at each editing site using a paired, two-tailed Student's t-test, generating nominal p-values. Given the discovery-oriented scope of the study, candidate selection for downstream analyses was based on nominal significance combined with effect-size thresholds. Specifically, for the heart (DCM) enrichment analyses, sites were considered significant candidates if p ≤ 0.05 and |Δ-editing frequency| ≥ 0.20. For cross-tissue identification of common heart–blood editome targets, the inclusion criteria were p ≤ 0.05 and |Δ-editing frequency| ≥ 0.10 in each dataset, followed by overlap at identical genomic coordinates and concordant directionality where applicable.

To improve transparency, p-values adjusted for multiple testing (Benjamini–Hochberg FDR) are provided in the full supplementary site-level results tables. However, adjusted p-values were not used as inclusion criteria for the primary discovery and prioritization analyses.

Results

Global RNA editing levels are increased in dilated cardiomyopathy patients in comparison to non-failing donors

High confidence RNA editing events were determined for dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM) patients and non-failing donors (CNTR) from heart tissue, and RNA editome profiles were established for both A-I and C-U RNA mediated editing. ADAR-mediated RNA editing almost exclusively contributes to the editing process in both groups (99.1% for controls and 99.9% for DCM patients) (Fig. 1A). To better understand the differences in RNA editing, Alu distribution was computed as a global measure of site-selective RNA editing activity for each group. Alu editing distribution was the most edited region in comparison to other repetitive and non-repetitive elements for both groups. DCM patients also exhibited higher editing levels in Alu elements in comparison to controls (Fig. 1B), with AluSz, AluSx1, Alusx and AluJb being the most prevalent Alu subfamilies being edited (Fig. 1C). Overall, non-repetitive sites were predominantly located in introns and 3′UTRs, with only a small fraction mapping to coding exons. In the coding non-repetitive set, we detected a limited number of events in RPEL1 (1 missense), PDHA2 (4 synonymous), MYH6 (2 synonymous), and ZNF814 (1 synonymous). However, none of these coding events showed a ≥ 10% differential editing change between DCM and controls (maximum Δ-freq = 8.3%), indicating that putative protein-recoding edits are not a prominent driver of the DCM-associated editing signal in our dataset (Supplementary File 3, Table 1–2). Notably, DCM group revealed significant upregulation in the global RNA editome levels (Fig. 1D, p-value <0.0001) and an overall increase in the absolute number of unique RNA editing events (Fig. 1E) in comparison to controls, revealing a potential disease-related editing pattern. Most editing events were detected in 3’ UTR regions with 52% editing distribution for both groups, followed by the intronic regions with 34% and 36% for CNTR and DCM groups respectively (Fig. 1).

RNA editing alterations identify a predominantly hyper-edited DCM signature and converge on cardiomyopathy-relevant pathways

To better characterize RNA editing changes associated with DCM, we performed a site-level differential editing analysis between DCM hearts and non-failing controls. This revealed a broad expansion of significant differentially edited (DEdit) sites in DCM (1802 sites) compared to CNTR (251 sites), with 230 sites detected in both groups. Overall, 1572 sites were specific to DCM and 21 were specific to CNTR (Fig. 2A). At the gene level, 339 genes were identified as differentially edited in DCM and 119 in CNTR group. From these DEdit genes, 109 were common between DCM and CNTR, leaving 230 DCM-specific and 10 CNTR-specific (Fig. 2A). Analysis of the gene-level distribution of DCM-specific editing events revealed a non-uniform editing burden across genes. While many of the 339 DCM-associated genes harbor only one or a few DCM-specific editing sites, a smaller subset of genes accumulates multiple independent editing events (Supplementary File 5). Notably, the most heavily edited genes included ZNF83 (30 sites), ABHD18 (26 sites), PRSS23 (26 sites), ZNF814 (23 sites), and CSAD (22 sites), with the majority of edits in these genes mapping to intronic regions. Overall, intronic and 3′UTR compartments represented the most frequently edited regions across DCM-associated genes, indicating preferential accumulation of disease-associated RNA editing within non-coding transcript regions (Supplementary File 5). Consistent with a global shift toward increased editing in disease, the volcano plot showed a strong directionality shift, with 1544 editing sites significantly increased in DCM and only 18 significantly decreased (p-value <0.05) (Fig. 2B).

To interpret the potential functional impact of the strongest editing changes, we performed pathway enrichment on differentially edited transcripts with large magnitude differences (Δ editing rate > 20%) using Enrichr. ClinVar enrichment highlighted multiple cardiomyopathy-related disease terms, including cardiomyopathy, primary dilated cardiomyopathy, and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (Fig. 2C–D). These terms were driven by DEdit genes with established links to cardiac structure and function, including Des, Nebl, Myl3, Nexn, Tmpo, and Ppp1r13l (Fig. 2D). Reactome enrichment analysis further pointed to stress and survival-related pathways, including oxygen sensing/hypoxia responses (Psmd12, Hif3a, Vhl), peroxisomal protein import (Lonp2, Pex1, Pex13), and programmed cell death/apoptosis (Psmd12, Dffa, Tfdp2, Dapk2, Dapk3, Cycs) (Fig. 2E–F). Additional enriched terms included glycosphingolipid catabolism/arylsulfatase activation (Arsa, Smpd4, Arsd) and aquaporin-mediated transport (Aqp7, Gnb4, Adcy6) (Fig. 2F).

To explicitly link site-level differential editing with gene-level enrichment, we further compiled all differentially edited sites contributing to the ClinVar- and Reactome-enriched pathways (Supplementary File 3: Tables 3–4). These analyses revealed that enrichment signals are driven by multiple concordantly hyper-edited sites per gene, predominantly located within intronic and 3′UTR regions. Several genes, such as TMPO, ARSA, PEX13, and SMPD4, contributed multiple independent editing sites to both disease and pathway enrichments, supporting convergence on shared stress-response and cardiomyopathy-related biological processes.

Cross-tissue common RNA editing targets highlight a blood-accessible DCM-related signature

To investigate whether the disease-associated RNA editing signature detected in DCM hearts is reflected in circulation, we compared differentially edited (DEdit) sites with Δ-editing frequency above 10% from heart tissue (DCM vs CNTR) against DEdit sites with Δ-editing frequency above 10% from peripheral blood (HF vs CNTR). In total, 107 DEdit sites overlapped between the two tissues, indicating a distinct shared editing component across compartments (Fig. 3A, Supplementary File 4). Because the blood cohorts comprise heart failure (HF) samples without matched aetiology, the cross-tissue overlap is interpreted as a circulating HF-associated signal that reflects myocardial pathology. Next, we assessed whether these shared targets exhibit concordant directionality between tissues by correlating the disease-associated changes in editing frequency (Δ-editing frequency = disease − control) in heart versus blood cohorts. The scatter plot revealed subsets of shared editing sites with positive correlation in both tissues, most of them hyper-edited (red circle) and a smaller subset of them as hypo-edited (blue circle) in the disease condition (Fig. 3B). The magnitude and direction of Δ-editing frequency for these shared candidates is summarized in the paired heatmap, highlighting heterogeneity across targets and enabling prioritization of sites that track disease consistently between heart and blood (Fig. 3C).

Pathway enrichment analysis of these shared differentially edited targets demonstrate a strong over-representation of innate immune and type I interferon-related processes, including regulation of type I interferon production, interferon-mediated signaling, and RIG-I pathway regulation, together with hypoxia-related responses and apoptotic processes (Fig. 3D). Finally, mapping these heart-blood shared editing events across genome showed that these candidate sites span multiple chromosomes and gene loci, supporting a distributed multi-gene editing signature that may be detectable in peripheral blood (Fig. 3E).

Discussion

Adenosine-to-inosine (A-to-I) RNA editing mediated by ADAR enzymes has emerged as a key regulatory mechanism in the heart, with both global editing activity and site-specific editing events implicated in cardiac disease ([11], [15], [16], [17], [23], [24], [28], [29], [30]). RNA editing can influence RNA localization, structure, stability, and transcript processing of both coding and noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs). Such modifications broaden the repertoire of expressed isoforms and tune key cellular functions, positioning RNA editing as a dynamic source of transcriptomic and proteomic diversity ([14], [31], [32]).

Our study defines a disease-associated remodeling of the global cardiac editome in dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM), characterized by a significant enrichment of high-confidence A-to-I events, predominantly within Alu elements, and a strong shift toward increased editing frequency and number of unique editing sites in DCM relative to non-failing controls (Fig. 1). Non-repetitive sites were mainly located within introns and 3′UTRs, with only a small fraction mapping to coding exons, indicating that putative protein-recoding edits are not a prominent driver of the DCM-associated editing signal. This global hyper-edited state highlighted in our results aligns with evidence reporting increased coding A-to-I editing in dilated and ischemic cardiomyopathies, including elevated editing within repetitive Alu elements in diseased hearts ([16]). Reports of reduced RNA editing in broader heart failure cohorts [15] contrast with our findings and likely reflect differences in disease context and editome definitions. Computational pipelines, particularly for intronic Alu editing, may further contribute to these discrepancies, highlighting the need for future harmonized analyses in etiologically matched cohorts. Pathway enrichment analysis of genes harboring significant differential editing highlighted cardiomyopathy-relevant terms and stress-response modules central to DCM pathophysiology (Fig. 2). ClinVar enrichment revealed multiple cardiomyopathy terms driven by genes with well-established roles in myocardial structure and inherited DCM susceptibility, including DES, NEBL, MYL3, NEXN and TMPO. In parallel, Reactome enrichment highlighted cell stress responses, hypoxia signaling, and apoptotic programs, pathways relevant to dilated cardiomyopathy pathophysiology.

Next, we focused on the cross-tissue comparison of significant editing targets across heart and blood, with the aim to identify editing targets with biomarker potential. Results revealed that a subset of the disease-associated editing signal observed in myocardium is also detectable in peripheral blood, highlighting a circulating RNA editing signature for cardiomyopathy-related conditions. Functional enrichment analysis on these shared RNA editing targets underline pathways such as type I interferon production and interferon-mediated signaling, as well as regulators of RNA sensing pathways (e.g., RIG-I–related processes) (Fig. 3). This is consistent with the established role of ADAR1 in innate immune activation, thereby linking RNA editing to prevention of maladaptive type I interferon signaling in dilated cardiomyopathy and heart failure ([17]). Additionally, a recent work has demonstrated that coronary artery disease (CAD) is heavily influenced by editing and that aberrant editing on Alu elements is associated with heightened interferon activity and disease prevalence ([33]), while a number of large-scale clinical trials of anti-inflammatory treatments in atherosclerosis further support the editing-mediated inflammatory hypothesis in cardiovascular diseases ([34], [35]). In parallel, mitochondrial antiviral-signaling protein (MAVS) signaling has been implicated in myocardial injury mechanisms that couple innate sensing to c-Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK) activation and apoptosis in ischemia-reperfusion models ([36]), providing a plausible biological bridge between the interferon/RIG-I enrichment observed in our shared signature and the apoptosis/hypoxia modules enriched in the heart dataset. Comparison of heart tissue Δ-editing frequencies showed that cardiomyopathy-associated genes exhibit the strongest editing changes in myocardium, whereas immune and inflammatory genes identified as circulating biomarkers display more moderate but consistent increases (Supplementary File 3, Fig. 2). This pattern indicates that circulating RNA-editing biomarkers capture disease-associated systemic immune responses rather than simply reflecting the most highly edited cardiac targets. In this context, our results suggest that the circulating editome profile overlap is particularly associated with immune mechanisms, potentially capturing systemic inflammatory states that accompany advanced cardiomyopathy and heart failure. Of note, these shared editing sites are distributed across multiple loci rather than being driven by a single gene, supporting the concept of a multi-site editing signature rather than a one-marker model. Collectively, the identification of a blood-detectable enriched overlap of differentially edited sites provides a rational starting point for developing editome-based biomarkers, an increasingly interesting direction in cardiovascular precision medicine ([37]).

Although our analyses provide new insight into RNA-editing alterations in dilated cardiomyopathy and nominate candidate circulating biomarkers and mechanistically relevant targets, several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Our work relies on publicly available datasets for which donor-level clinical and technical metadata are sometimes incomplete, potentially introducing residual heterogeneity within diagnostic groups. In particular, available metadata did not allow harmonized assessment of disease stage across cohorts, and differences in disease progression between DCM myocardial biopsies and HF blood samples may influence cross-tissue RNA-editing profiles. Multi-cohort integration introduces further study-level variability that can contribute to sample-level variance. We therefore interpret pooled cross-study findings as discovery-oriented and prioritize candidates supported by within-study sensitivity analyses (Supplementary File 4). In addition, to maximize the reliability of editing quantification and minimize false-positive calls, we restricted analyses to high-depth RNA-seq samples that passed stringent, standardized quality and filtering criteria. This conservative approach reduced the final sample size within each group. Given the high dimensionality of site-level testing, we treat the differential editing results as hypothesis-generating and support prioritization by reporting FDR-adjusted values alongside nominal statistics in the supplementary tables. Furthermore, while pathway enrichment supports biological coherence, establishing causality will require functional validation at prioritized sites and mechanistic dissection of how altered editing affects transcript fate (splicing, stability, translation) in relevant cardiac models. To address these limitations, future work will focus on validating the identified editing signatures in an independent, large-scale in-house cohort processed under standardized conditions. In parallel, we aim to implement targeted mechanistic experiments on top-priority candidates to define the functional consequences of differential editing and clarify its contribution to cardiomyopathy pathophysiology.

In conclusion, this work delineates a robust DCM-associated remodeling of A-to-I RNA editing in human myocardium, characterized by increased global editing activity and differential editing concentrated within gene networks relevant to cardiomyopathy and cellular stress responses. Importantly, cross-tissue analyses identify a shared subset of differentially edited sites detectable in peripheral blood from HF cohorts, enriched for interferon and RNA-sensing–related pathways, supporting the existence of a circulating, multi-locus RNA-editing signature associated with advanced cardiac disease and systemic inflammatory activation. Together, these findings provide a mechanistically anchored framework to prioritize RNA-editing sites for targeted validation as minimally invasive biomarkers and downstream functional studies to define candidate therapeutic vulnerabilities in cardiomyopathy-related inflammatory and stress-response signaling networks. Future validation in etiologically matched blood cohorts will be essential to determine DCM specificity of circulating signatures.
