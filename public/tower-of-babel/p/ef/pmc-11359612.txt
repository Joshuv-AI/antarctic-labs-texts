Alkali-Resistant Domestication of Bacillus pasteurii and Its Growth Kinetics in Sodium Carboxymethyl Cellulose Solid-Free Drilling Fluids
Authors: Shishu Zhang, Fenglin Li, Zuping Chang, Zehua Du, Xin Fang, Changjin Yuan, Zhijun Li
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMicrobially induced calcium carbonate precipitation (MICP) provides a novel approach for use in addressing the instabilities of borehole walls comprising broken formations, but the highly alkaline environments of drilling fluids are unfavorable for microbial growth. Therefore, this study investigated the alkali-resistant domestication of Bacillus pasteurii commonly used in MICP. Using gradient domestication, B. pasteurii was domesticated under different pH conditions (pH 8.0, 9.0, 10.0, and 11.0) in sodium carboxymethyl cellulose solid-free drilling fluids. Its growth patterns and variations in urease activity were analyzed to assess the effectiveness of domestication. The Gompertz and logistic models were used to fit the growth patterns of B. pasteurii under different pH conditions, and growth kinetic models were constructed based on the mean square error, Akaike information criterion, and coefficient of determination. The bacterial concentration and urease activity of B. pasteurii were enhanced after alkali-resistant gradient domestication. The Gompertz model accurately described the growth patterns of B. pasteurii after gradient domestication at pH 8.0, 10.0, and 11.0, whereas the logistic model accurately described the growth pattern after gradient domestication at pH 9.0. This study provides scientific evidence and a theoretical basis for the use of B. pasteurii in maintaining the stabilities of borehole walls comprising broken formations.

Introduction

Drilling is an indispensable method of obtaining valuable information from formations to uncover the mysteries of the earth, and the formations encountered become more complex as the drilling depth increases.1 Complex formations include faults and poorly cemented, interbedded soft and hard, karst, water-sensitive, and broken formations.2 When broken formations are encountered during drilling, various mechanical disturbances, e.g., collisions generated during drill string rotation, suction effects produced during tripping operations, and vibrations caused by striking the drill string during tripping or handling incidents, can easily lead to sticking or jamming incidents.3 These mechanical disturbances significantly affect construction, leading to increased construction costs and decreased drilling efficiencies and rates of core recovery.4 Research and practice have demonstrated that increasing the cementation between broken formation blocks could effectively improve the stabilities of broken formations.

Microbially induced calcium carbonate precipitation (MICP) provides valuable insights into the stabilization of borehole walls comprising broken formations. Recently, a high-urease-producing Bacillus pasteurii strain with a strong environmental adaptability was identified as the most suitable microorganism for use in MICP.5 With urea as an energy source, a large amount of highly active urease was produced via its metabolic activity. The urease subsequently hydrolyzed the urea to NH4+ and CO32–, which reacted with externally sourced Ca2+ to form calcium carbonate crystals around the cells as nuclei, leading to the precipitation of these crystals.

Currently, MICP is primarily used in soil improvement, and it can significantly enhance the strength, stiffness, permeability, and liquefaction resistance of soil.6−9 Compared to traditional soil improvement techniques, this technology is green and sustainable and thus consistent with the principles of sustainable development. Therefore, utilizing this technology to enhance the cementation of borehole walls comprising broken formations and improve the mechanical properties of surrounding rocks exhibits theoretical and practical significance.

Li et al. conducted a preliminary exploration of wall protection based on microbes using MICP, and B. pasteurii could effectively enhance the wall protection effects in sodium carboxymethyl cellulose (CMC) drilling fluid systems.10 As the microbial concentration and calcium ion content increased, the wall protection effect was enhanced and the stabilities of the broken formations increased. Additionally, the growth of B. pasteurii is improved in CMC drilling fluids compared to that in xanthan gum drilling fluids.11 However, during drilling operations, the pH levels of drilling fluid systems are generally high.12 Under such conditions, the growth and activities of microorganisms may be inhibited, or they may even lead to microbial death, and thus, the stabilities of the borehole walls are not maintained.

Microorganism domestication can improve environmental adaptability.13−15 Zhang et al. subjected Acidithiobacillus ferrooxidans and Burkholderia sp. to gradient acid-tolerance domestication, with pH levels of 7.0, 6.0, 5.0, 4.0, and 3.0 for successive generations.16 The biomass and denitrification capacity of bacteria obtained from a uranium mine in a groundwater environment with a pH of 3.0 were significantly increased. Microbial domestication has been applied in the field of MICP, e.g., Xiao et al. subjected microorganisms to gradient domestication in a seawater environment, which enabled them to adapt to marine environments and induce calcium carbonate deposition.17 This study aims to utilize alkali-resistance domestication to maintain certain growth activities of B. pasteurii in high pH environments, while retaining its capacity to induce calcium carbonate deposition, to cement the surrounding rocks of borehole walls and enhance borehole stability. Furthermore, clarifying the growth patterns and characteristics of domesticated B. pasteurii under different pH conditions is critical in its effective utilization in improving the stabilities of borehole walls comprising broken formations.

The growth patterns of microorganisms are generally described using growth kinetics, which are widely applied in numerous fields, such as food production, wastewater treatment, and oil and gas. In the food sector, Zhu et al. studied the growth kinetics of flavor yeast A10–2 selected from soy sauce fermentation lees using logistic and Luedeking-Piret models and analyzed the consumption of the substrate (total sugar) during growth.18 The growth of yeast A10–2 followed an S-shaped curve described by the logistic equation, whereas the substrate (total sugar) consumption adhered to the Luedeking-Piret equation. The established models effectively described yeast growth and sugar consumption during high-density cultivation, thus offering predictive guidance. Liu et al. fitted the dynamic optical density (OD) data of Bacillus subtilis as functions of different environmental factors using a modified Gompertz model, obtaining a growth kinetic model that supported the shelf life of processed white shrimp.19 Nilanjana et al. established a growth kinetic model for high-altitude red algae based on the logistic equation and investigated various factors influencing its growth to determine the optimal conditions for astaxanthin production and facilitate the further development of the strain.20 In the field of wastewater treatment, Li et al. studied the growth characteristics of Mychonastes under different temperatures and light intensities using the Lehman and Steele models, establishing a growth kinetic model that provided a basis for Mychonastes bloom warning.21 Additionally, Ma et al. compared the growth trends of microalgae in domestic sewage using the logistic, Gompertz, and Richards models, and the simulations of the growth trends using the logistic model were superior.22 In the petroleum sector, Rajashree et al. utilized the Monod model to establish the growth kinetic models of the alkali-producing bacterium RB10, Pseudomonas aeruginosa RB12, and Actinomadura rubteroloni RB29. These models were constructed by calculating the maximum specific growth rates and half-saturation constants of the microorganisms, which contribute to the remediation of petroleum waste.23 Bhattacharya et al. evaluated the growth kinetics parameters of mixed cultures of P. aeruginosa and Bacillus cereus using the Monod and Haldane models.24 Whereas the Monod model failed to represent the growth parameters within the specified concentration ranges, the Haldane model fitted the experimental data perfectly, aiding in the remediation of highly petroleum-contaminated sites.

In summary, no research has been conducted regarding the alkaline tolerance and subsequent growth kinetics of B. pasteurii in drilling fluid environments. Therefore, this study employed gradient domestication to cultivate B. pasteurii with enhanced alkali resistances in CMC drilling fluid environments under a pH gradient of ≤11.0. By comparing the growth statuses of B. pasteurii before and after domestication and analyzing the growth patterns, growth kinetic models based on the logistic and Gompertz models were established. Thus, a theoretical foundation for the successful application of B. pasteurii in maintaining the stabilities of borehole walls comprising broken formations under highly alkaline conditions was established.

Methods

Activation and Cultivation of Bacteria

Soy peptone (5 g), 15 g of casein peptone, 5 g of NaCl, and 20 g of urea were added to 1 L of deionized water in a beaker, and then the mixture was stirred until homogeneous. The pH was then adjusted to 7.3 using a 1 mol/L NaOH solution to prepare the liquid culture medium for B. pasteurii. The mixture was sterilized at 1.05 MPa and 110 °C for 10 min using a high-temperature and high-pressure autoclave (YXQ-SG46–280S, Shanghai Boxun Medical Biological Instrument, Shanghai, China), followed by cooling and storage in a laminar flow hood (SW-CJ1FD, Suzhou Antai Airtech, Suzhou, China) for subsequent use.

A sterile pipet was used to withdraw a small amount of sterilized culture medium, drip it into an ampule, and gently shake it until the milky white powder was suspended. The entire bacterial suspension was transferred using a sterile pipet into a conical flask containing 150 mL of liquid medium. The flask was sealed with medical gauze and placed in a shaking incubator (ZHTY-50s, Shanghai Zhichu Instrument, Shanghai, China), followed by incubation at 30 °C and 120 rpm for 24 h. Following incubation, the liquid medium became visibly turbid, indicating the formation of a bacterial culture.

CMC Solid-Free Drilling Fluid Configurations

An appropriate dosage of CMC not only satisfies the requirements of drilling engineering but also promotes microbial growth, and CMC doses of 0.4%, 0.6%, 0.8%, and 1.0% were utilized in this study. The rheological performance parameters of the solid-free drilling fluids and drilling fluids after 24 h of microbial growth were compared to determine the CMC dosage for use in subsequent experiments.

CMC (4 g/L), 5 g/L soy peptone, 15 g/L casein peptone, 5 g/L NaCl, and 20 g/L urea were added to deionized water and mixed well, and the pH was adjusted to 7.3. The mixture was then sterilized in an autoclave under 1.05 MPa at 110 °C for 10 min, placed on the ultraclean bench, and cooled to 25 °C to yield the 0.4% CMC drilling fluid. To ensure that the initial concentrations of the strains are the same, the activated bacterial fluid was diluted with deionized water to an OD600 of 0.500 and inoculated into the drilling fluid according to Vbacterial-fluid/Vdrilling-fluid = 10%. The mixture was then incubated for 24 h at 30 °C and 120 rpm to yield the microbial-CMC drilling fluid with a CMC addition rate of 0.4%, and the concentration of bacteria at 24 h was determined. The microbial-CMC drilling fluids with CMC addition levels of 0.6%, 0.8%, and 1.0% were configured using the same method, and their rheological properties and microbial concentrations at 24 h are shown in Table 2.

Determination of the Bacterial Concentration

The bacterial concentration was measured using a UV-2000 spectrophotometer (Shanghai UniCo Instrument, Shanghai, China). Samples of each culture group were collected at intervals of 2 h for 24 h, and the OD600 values, which represented the concentrations of B. pasteurii, were measured using the spectrophotometer.

Determination of Urease Activity

A urea solution (45 mL, 1.6 mol/L) was transferred into a beaker, and then 5 mL of the bacterial solution was aspirated using a sterile pipet and mixed with the urea solution. The change in conductivity over 5 min at room temperature was measured using a conductivity meter (DDS-11A, Shanghai Leici Instrument, Shanghai, China). According to the empirical values obtained by Whiffin,25 a conductivity change of 1 mS/min is equivalent to the hydrolysis of 11 mM urea/min. The average conductivity change per minute (mS/min) was converted to the amount of urea hydrolyzed per minute using the urease activity and multiplied by the dilution factor to obtain the amount of urea hydrolyzed per minute (mM urea hydrolyzed/min) by the test bacteria.

Alkali-Resistant Domestication of the Bacteria

Alkali-resistant domestication of the bacteria was conducted using a gradient method with pH values of 8.0, 9.0, 10.0, and 11.0. The 0.8% CMC drilling fluid was prepared, and the pH was adjusted to 8.0. The activated bacterial solution was diluted with deionized water to an OD600 of 0.500 and inoculated at a ratio of Vbacterial-solution/Vdrilling-fluid = 10%. The culture was incubated for 24 h at 30 °C and 120 rpm. Subsequently, the culture was serially passaged using the same method until the OD600 at 24 h exhibited a minimal change, resulting in the alkali-tolerant strain X8.0. The OD600 and conductivity change of X8.0 were measured at intervals of 2 h. Domestication was thus completed under different pH conditions. Using the same method, strain X8.0 was inoculated into CMC solid-free drilling fluid adjusted to pH 9.0, resulting in X9.0, and this procedure was repeated to obtain X10.0 and X11.0. Three experimental replicates were performed under each pH condition and the average values were calculated.

Results and Discussion

Growth of B. pasteurii in the Liquid Medium

Figure 1 shows the growth of B. pasteurii in the liquid culture medium. The first 2 h represent the lag phase of B. pasteurii growth, wherein a minimal change in the bacterial concentration is observed. Subsequently, B. pasteurii enters the exponential growth phase that is characterized by the rapid proliferation of bacterial cells, which leads to a significant increase in population size. This phase continues until 18 h, when B. pasteurii enters the stationary phase and its growth rate slows noticeably as the bacterial concentration reaches its peak and approaches stability. Additionally, the urease activity of the microorganism correlates positively with its concentration, with minimal variation once the concentration stabilizes. A comparison of the OD600 value of B. pasteurii at 24 h in Table 2 reveals that CMC affects the growth of B. pasteurii, with the effect being more obvious with increasing CMC dosage.

Figure 2a–d shows the bacterial concentration curves at pH 8.0, 9.0, 10.0, and 11.0 before and after gradient domestication. Clearly, the bacterial concentrations after gradient domestication for 24 h at all four pH values significantly exceed those before domestication, with noticeable decreases in their lag phases. This effect becomes more pronounced with increasing pH, with a reduction in the lag phase of ≥18 h observed at pH 11.0. At pH 8.0, B. pasteurii exhibits a higher maximum growth rate, resulting in a higher bacterial concentration during the stationary phase. At pH 9.0 and 10.0, the respective logarithmic growth phases are prolonged to 20 and 16 h, with the respective bacterial concentrations in the stationary phases reaching 4.562 and 4.768. Additionally, the maximum growth rate at pH 10.0 increases substantially after domestication. At pH 11.0, untamed B. pasteurii displays no sign of entering the logarithmic growth phase within 24 h. Conversely, after gradient domestication, it enters the logarithmic growth phase after 6 h, lasting for 16 h, with a bacterial concentration of 4.242 observed in the stationary phase. The results reported by Meng et al. similarly reveal a significantly increased number of B. pasteurii after high-alkali-resistant domestication.31 The results reported by Zhu (2023) and Zhao (2023) also indicate that the logarithmic growth phase of B. pasteurii is significantly advanced,32,33 which is also consistent with the results of this study. Meanwhile, a comparison of the growth of B. pasteurii in Table 2 reveals that the maximum OD600 value of domesticated B. pasteurii exceeded its OD600 value at 24 h in 0.8% dosage of the CMC drilling fluid under all four pH conditions. These results demonstrate the significant effectiveness of gradient domestication in enhancing the adaptation of B. pasteurii to high-pH environments.

Figure 3 shows the urease activities of the undomesticated and alkali-resistant gradient-domesticated strains at pH 8.0, 9.0, 10.0, and 11.0. In an environment with the 0.8% CMC solid-free drilling fluid, B. pasteurii can produce urease, which exhibits activity. Furthermore, the urease activities of B. pasteurii under all four pH conditions increase after gradient domestication, and the urease activity is enhanced with an increase in the bacterial concentration. The results reported by Zhu likewise reveal that the urease activities of B. pasteurii in specific environments increase significantly after high-alkali-resistant domestication.32 Meanwhile, similar to the growth conditions, the urease activity also displays certain trends that correlate with the growth pattern and changes in environmental conditions.

Stocks-Fisher et al. reported that urease extracted from bacteria exhibits the optimal activity when the pH ranges from 7.5 to 9.0, and a noticeable decrease in urease activity is observed beyond this range.34 Whiffin reached a similar conclusion, reporting that the optimal urease activities in bacterial cultures occur in the pH range 6.0–8.5, with minimal influences due to pH variations.25 At pH 8.0, the concentration of undomesticated B. pasteurii does not reach a stable phase, yet the urease activity stabilizes. Following domestication, the population and urease activity of B. pasteurii enter the logarithmic growth and stability phases simultaneously, but the urease activity remains stable even when the concentration enters a declining phase. At pH 9.0, the trends of the concentrations and urease activities of the undomesticated and gradient-domesticated B. pasteurii are consistent, indicating a significant increase in concentration due to gradient domestication.

When the pH is >9.0, the urease activity is significantly influenced by the pH. At pH 10.0, although the concentrations of the undomesticated and gradient-domesticated B. pasteurii remain stable, the urease activities barely stabilize before rapidly entering declining phases. Van Paassen reported that urease can occur within the cells in the bacterial culture, shielding it from external pH variations and providing a protective effect.35 Urease consumption continues when the bacterial concentration is constant, leading to a decline in urease activity. At pH 11.0, the gradient-domesticated B. pasteurii exhibits the same phenomenon. Therefore, when pH > 9.0, the logarithmic growth phase must be extended and the bacterial concentration increased to ensure a sufficient urease activity in the drilling fluid environment, thus ensuring continuous MICP.

Conclusions

In this study, the growth conditions of B. pasteurii in CMC solid-free drilling fluids after alkali-resistant gradient domestication were investigated and compared to the equivalent undomesticated conditions. Additionally, the growth patterns of B. pasteurii after domestication in CMC solid-free drilling fluids were analyzed. Theoretical analysis was used to obtain accurate growth kinetic models describing the growth of B. pasteurii in the CMC solid-free drilling fluids, providing a reference for use in maintaining the stabilities of borehole walls comprising broken formations under high-pH conditions. The main conclusions of this study are as follows:

(1)B. pasteurii was sequentially subjected to alkali-resistant gradient domestication in the 0.8% CMC solid-free drilling fluids with pH values of 8.0, 9.0, 10.0, and 11.0. After alkali-resistant gradient domestication, the concentration of B. pasteurii increased and the lag phase of growth was shortened. Therefore, alkali-resistant gradient domestication aided in shortening the lag phases of B. pasteurii in CMC solid-free drilling fluids with high pH values, promoting its robust growth. Moreover, the maximum specific growth rates were enhanced at pH 8.0 and 10.0.

Therefore, alkali-resistant gradient domestication aided in shortening the lag phases of B. pasteurii in CMC solid-free drilling fluids with high pH values, promoting its robust growth. Moreover, the maximum specific growth rates were enhanced at pH 8.0 and 10.0.

(2)B. pasteurii could produce urease, which exhibited a certain activity in the 0.8% CMC solid-free drilling fluid. The optimal urease activity was observed at pH 8.0 and 9.0, and thus, increasing the bacterial quantity to enhance the urease activity under these conditions is critical. At pH 10.0 and 11.0, the urease activity was significantly influenced by the pH, highlighting the importance of extending the logarithmic growth phase and increasing the bacterial quantity to increase the urease activity.
