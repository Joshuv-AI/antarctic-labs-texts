Identifying actionable druggable targets for breast cancer: Mendelian randomization and population-based analyses
Authors: Naiqi Zhang, Yanni Li, Jan Sundquist, Kristina Sundquist, Jianguang Ji
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundDrug repurposing provides a cost-effective approach to address the need for breast cancer prevention and therapeutics. We aimed to identify actionable druggable targets using Mendelian randomization (MR) and then validate the candidate drugs using population-based analyses.MethodsWe identified genetic instruments for 1406 actionable targets of approved non-oncological drugs based on gene expression, DNA methylation, and protein expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL, mQTL, and pQTL, respectively). Research in context

Evidence before this study

Previous studies have shown drug repurposing might be an attractive strategy to offer more effective cancer prevention as well as treatment options at a lower cost. However, these studies did not investigate the druggable targets of breast cancer. We searched PubMed for studies in any language using the search terms “drug repositioning OR druggable target OR drug target” AND “Mendelian randomization OR Mendelian randomisation” AND “breast cancer OR breast neoplasm”. Of the yielded six studies, one study's outcome was COVID-19, four studies focused on specific classes of drugs, and the other study focused on blood metabolome. However, there is no study systematically exploring all actionable druggable targets for breast cancer.

Added value of this study

This data-driven study applied two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR), using multi-omic genetic instruments to proxy 1406 actionable targets of approved non-oncological drugs, and GWAS summary statistics for breast cancer, to identify causal druggable targets for breast cancer. As a result, we identified six causal genes on gene expression level (TUBB, MDM2, CSK, ULK3, MC1R, and KCNN4) and two significant associations on DNA methylation level (RPS23 and MAPT). We further conducted a nested case–control study using data retrieved from Swedish nationwide registers to validate the candidate drugs that were identified from MR analyses. We found that the use of raloxifene was associated with a 35% reduction in breast cancer risk, confirming raloxifene as an effective chemoprevention against breast cancer.

Implications of all the available evidence

Our data-driven analyses support the increasing values in the application of publicly accessible datasets to inform public health. We provided a full list of druggable targets both for the prevention and treatment of breast cancer, and a full list of potential drugs which can be further validated in future experimental and population-based studies.

Introduction

In women, breast cancer has now surpassed lung cancer as the leading type of cancer overall (2.3 million cases, 11.7%), and it ranks 5th as the cause of cancer mortality, with 685,000 deaths worldwide in 2020.1 In high-income countries, the increase of breast cancer incidence has slowed or plateaued since the mid-2000s, and breast cancer mortality continues to decline, largely owing to access to high-quality prevention, improvements in awareness, earlier detection, and improved treatments.2 In contrast, the mortality rate is still rising in low-to middle-income countries, implying that less developed countries lack the resources for screening and adequate treatment resources.3 Since prevention potentially offers the most cost-effective strategy for cancer control and would reduce the many negative impacts of breast cancer, there is a need to identify drugs that can minimize the burden of breast cancer. Drug repurposing—i.e. the application of known drugs and compounds with a non-oncology primary purpose–might be an attractive strategy to offer more effective cancer prevention as well as treatment options at a lower cost.4

The growing wealth of human genetic data is now widely used to inform drug development programs. Drug–target disease pairs supported by human genetics could multiply the success rate in drug discovery pipelines.5 Using single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs, the most common type of genetic variants) as a powerful tool to deduce the on-target effects observed by pharmacological modification can imitate the design of an RCT without requiring a drug intervention. This is based on the concept that genotype is determined by a random allocation (Mendel's second law). Such Mendelian randomization (MR) analyses have been widely applied to putative repurposing opportunities for licensed drugs on account of the wide-ranging potential to improve the efficiency of drug development and reduce the risk of expensive late-stage failure.6

To identify potential druggable targets for breast cancer, we conducted large-scale MR analyses using gene expression, methylation, and translation data for genes that encode protein targets for approved drugs. Here, we focused on actionable druggable genes, which are defined as genes that encode the protein targets of drugs that are licensed. We subsequently conducted a nested case–control study using population-based data retrieved from Swedish national registers to validate whether the drugs targeting the candidate protein have a protective effect against breast cancer.

Methods

Identification of actionable druggable targets

Information about drugs and their therapeutic biological activity was extracted from the ChEMBL database, which is a large, open-source drug discovery database.7 To repurpose non-oncology drugs and subsequently validate using nationwide registers, only actionable proteins that are therapeutic targets or potential targets of FDA-approved non-oncology drugs (excluding oncology drugs with Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical code, ATC, “L01”) were considered. The therapeutic targets were determined from the ChEMBL drug mechanism of action data and linked to the component proteins. We then filtered out nonhuman proteins and those proteins that are known to be non-drug binding components of a protein complex. For approved drugs, further human target proteins were identified using ChEMBL's pharmacological assay data with affinity/efficacy measurements ≤100 nM (indicated by a pChEMBL value ≥ 7).8

We identified a total of 1406 unique human proteins as ‘actionable’ from data available in ChEMBL, including 660 therapeutic targets and 1074 potential targets that are bound by FDA-approved drugs. Although the biological relevance between targets in the latter group and the approved drug indications may not be established, the high affinity/efficacy measurements indicate that the capability of these drugs should modulate these proteins (Supplementary Table S2). We performed Pathway and Process Enrichment Analysis for the potential drug targets and found that there were 177 genes directly involved in the pathway of breast cancer (Supplementary Fig. and Table S2).

Selection of instruments for actionable druggable targets

We used eQTL summary statistics from eQTLGen Consortium (available at https://www.eqtlgen.org/cis-eqtls.html) to identify genetic variants associated with blood gene expression that are located within 1000 kb on either side of the coding sequence (in cis).9 These genetic variants were used to propose eQTL instruments for druggable targets. The eQTLGen Consortium contains information on 10,317 trait-associated single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) from 31,684 individuals. However, information on genes located on the X and Y chromosomes and mtDNA was absent. From cis-eQTL, a total of 438,534 SNPs associated with the expression of 852 druggable targets-related transcripts were extracted.

We then proposed eQTL instruments using data from GTEx v.8 in European ancestry individuals in 459 breast tissue samples.10 In total, 19,852 SNPs were selected corresponding to 406 druggable targets-related gene expression.

We further extracted instruments for genetic variants associated with the methylation level of druggable targets using summary statistics from a meta-analysis of two cohorts (n = 1980).11 In total, 1,092,040 SNPs were selected corresponding to 5592 druggable targets-related DNA methylation CpG sites.

Instruments for genetic variants associated with the expression of druggable proteins were obtained from a published paper that includes 4907 aptamers-measured plasma protein levels in 35,559 Icelanders.12 We obtained 2364 SNPs that were robustly associated with 146 druggable protein expressions.

We used the default genome-wide (GW) significance P-value threshold of 5 × 10−8 to select the top associated eQTL, mQTL and pQTL as instrument variants in our SMR analyses.

Estimates for breast cancer

GWAS summary statistics for breast cancer and subtypes were obtained for European ancestry from the Breast Cancer Association Consortium (BCAC) with 122,977 cases and 105,974 controls of European ancestry.13 The summary statistics data are available on the consortium's website (http://bcac.ccge.medschl.cam.ac.uk/). The details of all QTL and GWAS datasets for this study are presented in Supplementary Table S1.

Mendelian randomization and colocalization

We have used the power calculations from an online tool (http://sb452.shinyapps.io/power/) to estimate the power of the MR analysis based on the sample size of 228,951 participants which was 89.7%.

Summary-based MR (SMR) method, version 1.02, was used to conduct a 2-sample MR analysis with breast cancer as the outcome and druggable targets at gene expression, DNA methylation, and protein expression levels as the exposure, respectively, in the command prompt (the code is available on https://yanglab.westlake.edu.cn/software/smr/#Overview).14 Odds ratio (OR) estimates of druggable targets on the risk of breast cancer were calculated as the exponent of the β coefficient. The QTL effect size (β) of a variant reflects the direction of change in gene expression, DNA methylation, or protein expression. β > 0 means positive association, and β < 0 means negative association, while the ORs represent the ratio of odds for disease per 1-SD change in gene expression, DNA methylation, or protein expression. To control the genome-wide type I error, we performed Bonferroni correction by dividing the critical P value (α) by the number of multiple tests.

To estimate whether the associations were influenced by the linkage disequilibrium (LD), the HEIDI test was performed which was also employed as a colocalization method.15 Probes that show evidence of high LD (P for HEIDI < 0.05) were rejected.

In addition, we also conducted Bayesian testing to refine the results using the “coloc” R package (version 5.1.0; available at https://chr1swallace.github.io/coloc/) to estimate the posterior probability of common variants. For each significant MR result, all SNPs located within 100 kb up and downstream of each leading SNP were retrieved for colocalization analysis between QTLs and the GWAS. We applied a cut-off for posterior probability for hypothesis 4 (PP.H4) of 80% as evidence of colocalization of the QTLs and GWAS associations. We conducted a sensitivity analysis using ‘TwoSampleMR’ package to validate the significant associations identified from SMR analysis. Pleiotropy may introduce bias into MR findings, we therefore explored its potential existence using the MR-Egger method. This approach addresses pleiotropy by introducing a nuisance parameter that quantifies the average degree of directional pleiotropy. Additionally, we evaluate the heterogeneity in MR using Cochran's Q heterogeneity.

Validation of drug candidates in a population-based study

In order to validate whether the drug candidates identified from MR analyses play a role in the development of breast cancer, we conducted a nested case–control study using data retrieved from Swedish registers. The source population consisted of 4,537,661 Swedish-born females registered from 1st July 2005 through 31st March 2014 in the Swedish Total Population Register.16 Since the Prescription Register was established in July 2005, the cohort entry date was set as 1st July 2006. Individuals who had a diagnosis of any cancer before the entry date were excluded (n = 190,883).

We identified 41,917 breast cancer patients diagnosed between 1st January 2007 and 31st March 2014 by using the 10th International Classification of Diseases (ICD) code C50 from the Swedish Cancer Registry.17 The index date for cases was the date of a breast cancer diagnosis.

For each breast cancer case, five nested controls were randomly selected using incidence-density sampling among cohort members who had no cancer diagnosis before selection, matched by propensity score. The propensity score was computed based on the following variables: age, highest education (1–9, 10–11, ≥12 years), income (lowest, middle-low, middle–high, highest), residence area (big cities, south Sweden, north Sweden), parity (0, 1, 2, 3, ≥4), obesity (yes/no), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD, yes/no) as a proxy for smoking, and Charlson Comorbidity Index score (CCI, 0, 1, 2, ≥3). The index date for breast cancer cases was the date of diagnosis, and the index date for controls was the same as the corresponding cases.

Exposure to the drug candidates was identified from the Swedish Prescribed Drug Register if individuals received a prescription of the drug candidates between July 2005 and March 2014 according to the Anatomical Therapeutic Chemical Classification (ATC) codes.

We used a conditional logistic regression model to calculate ORs and 95% CIs of breast cancer associated with the use of candidate drugs for individually matched case–control study. We used Bonferroni correction to reduce the chances of obtaining false-positive results.

In order to further rule out the potential of confounding due to indication bias, we conducted sensitivity analyses using active-comparator design and new-user designs to validate the effects of drug candidates identified from the case–control study.

The population-based analyses were conducted using SAS, version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC) and Ethical permission was obtained from the Regional Ethical Review Board in Lund.

Results

MR analysis of cis-eQTLs and breast cancer outcomes

We performed Bonferroni corrections to control the genome-wide type I error, with significance thresholds of P < 5.87 × 10−5 (0.05/852 genes) for gene expression in blood and P < 1.23 × 10−4 (0.05/406 genes) for gene expression in breast tissue, followed by the HEIDI test (P HEIDI > 0.05). We thus identified seven association signals across seven unique genetic loci expressed in blood, and one genetic locus expressed in breast tissue. For the SMR results using instrument variants retrieved from blood samples, we found that per 1- standard deviation (SD) increase in blood TUBB expression was associated with 11% reduced risk of breast cancer (odds ratios, OR: 0.89; 95% confidence intervals, CI: 0.84–0.94; P SMR: 1.70 × 10−5). Similarly, per 1- SD increase in blood CSK and ULK3 expression was associated with 9% (OR: 0.91; 95% CI: 0.88–0.95; P SMR: 1.74 × 10−6) and 6% (OR: 0.94; 95% CI: 0.91–0.96; P SMR: 5.70 × 10−7) reduced risk of breast cancer, respectively. Per 1- SD increase in blood MDM2, MC1R and KCNN4 expression was associated with increased risk of breast cancer, with ORs (95% CIs) and P SMR of 1.07 (95% CI: 1.04–1.10; P SMR: 5.31 × 10−5), 1.05 (95% CI: 1.03–1.07, P SMR:4.57 × 10−5) and 1.10 (95% CI: 1.08–1.12, P SMR: 4.38 × 10−22).

Most importantly, we found that increased ULK3 expression in breast tissue was associated with a reduced breast cancer risk (OR per SD, 0.91; 95% CI: 0.87–0.95, P SMR: 9.35 × 10−6), which was consistent with the result in blood samples.

MR analysis of cis-mQTL and breast cancer outcomes

Bonferroni correction (PSMR < 8.94 × 10−6) and HEIDI test were performed to refrain the SMR results between the DNA methylation of druggable targets and breast cancer. We identified a total of 33 association signals across 28 unique genetic loci for breast cancer (Fig. 3 and Supplementary Table S4). We found a positive association between the methylation level of RPS23 and breast cancer, with an OR and 95% CI of 1.08 (1.06–1.13), and P SMR of 5.03 × 10−10. Sensitivity analysis using ‘TwosampleMR’ package

Results from sensitivity analysis using ‘TwosampleMR’ package further confirmed the significant associations between gene expression of TUBB, MDM2, CSK, ULK3, MC1R, KCNN4, RPS23 and MAPT and breast cancer risk (Supplementary Table S10).

In total, 319 candidate drugs were obtained from the MR analyses with significant colocalization results (Supplementary Table S7). Non-oral drugs and drugs with infrequent usage (less than 0.1% in the study population) were further excluded, thus 36 drugs were selected for further analysis in the nested case–control study. Fig. 4 shows the frequencies and ORs for use of candidate drugs in cases and controls. The Bonferroni correction was conducted to reduce false positive (P < 0.0014, 0.05/36). We found that the use of raloxifene was associated with a 35% reduction in breast cancer risk (95% CI, 0.51–0.83; P = 0.0004). In contrast, the usage of estradiol, tolterodine, and nitrofurantoin was associated with an increased breast cancer risk, with adjusted ORs of 1.10 (95% CI, 1.07–1.13; P < 0.0001), 1.16 (95% CI, 1.09–1.24; P < 0.0001) and 1.09 (95% CI, 1.05–1.13; P < 0.0001), respectively. Discussion

To identify drug-repurposing opportunities against breast cancer, we conducted a large-scale MR analysis based on robust evidence for underlying mechanisms linking the genetic loci, gene expression, and methylation. We identified important putative causal druggable target genes for breast cancer as follows: TUBB, MDM2, CSK, ULK3, MC1R, KCNN4, RPS23 and MAPT. We further validated the candidate drugs targeting those druggable genes which were identified from MR analysis and found that the use of raloxifene was associated with a 35% reduction in breast cancer risk, confirming raloxifene as an effective chemoprevention against breast cancer, which is consistent with the evidence from STAR trial18 and the recommendation from USPSTF.19 However, the effect of raloxifene was marginally insignificant in further validation analyses using active-comparator and new-user design due to insufficient sample size. Conversely, the use of estradiol and nitrofurantoin might increase the risk of developing breast cancer. The effect of nitrofurantoin lost significance in sensitivity analyses using active-comparator design and new-user design.

TUBB has been reported overexpressed in various cancers including breast cancer.20, 21, 22 Recently, studies demonstrated that the resistance to chemotherapy in breast cancer patients was associated with increased expression of TUBB.20,23 MDM2 is a proto-oncogene located at 12q15. MDM2 is a transcriptional target of p53, and thus plays a crucial role in p53 protein down-regulation. The MDM2 inhibitor targeting p53 is a promising therapeutic strategy for treating p53-wild-type cancers.24 Besides suppressing P53, the overexpression of MDM2 is involved in multiple cellular signaling pathways and contributes to the growth and progression of tumors including cell cycle, apoptosis, DNA repair, cell migration, invasion, angiogenesis, and resistance to chemotherapy.25 CSK encodes protein kinases involved in multiple pathways, including the downregulation of Src family kinases.26 Src interacts with EGFR (ErbB1) and ErbB2, which are frequently overexpressed in breast cancer and are currently targets of many US FDA-approved oncology drugs.27 ULK3 encodes protein that can induce autophagy in human fibroblasts.28 ULK3 expression and gene signatures involved in keratinocyte proliferation and oncogenesis. Extensive clinical data suggest that the expression levels of ULK3 are heightened in squamous cell carcinomas of the lung, head and neck, and cervix.29 MC1R encodes the receptor protein for melanocyte-stimulating hormone and thus plays a pivotal role in pigmentation. The activation of MC1R could upregulate cAMP signaling, boost the production of melanin, and improve DNA repair following exposure to ultraviolet radiation.30 KCNN4 intermediates conductance Ca2+-activated potassium channel. The overexpression of KCNN4 has been observed in breast cancer, leading to multiple chemoresistances in breast cancer.31 RPS23 is a direct target of miR-542-3p and plays a significant role in regulating p53. Downregulation of a subset of ribosomal proteins, including RPS23, by miR-542-3p leads to the upregulation of RPL11 and subsequent stabilization of p53.32

All four drugs validated in our population were targeting the methylation of MAPT. MAPT is responsible for encoding the tau protein. Tau proteins are mainly expressed in the neurons and also expressed in tissues including skeletal muscle, breast, etc.33 It has been reported that Tau expression is dysregulated and mismatched in some neurodegenerative diseases.34, 35, 36 Emerging data suggest that tau protein plays a role in cancer development. Tau is found almost exclusively bound to microtubules, which play an essential role in mitosis in the cancer process.37 MAPT expression is closely associated with estrogen receptor expression in breast cancer most likely due to it containing an imperfect estrogen-response element upstream of its promoter.38 Nuclear tau may also act on cancer by modulating P53, which is a crucial tumor suppressor. It has been found that P53 is a component of a complex that also includes nuclear tau, Pin1, and the poly(A)-specific ribonuclease, potentially resulting in changes in the expression of transcripts deregulated in cancer.39 Tau might also affect breast cancer through BRCA1 and BRCA2, which function as tumor suppressors to maintain genome integrity by facilitating efficient repair of double-strand DNA breaks.40, 41, 42

Several factors contribute to breast cancer risk, including sex, aging, estrogen, family history, gene mutations, and an unhealthy lifestyle. Increasing estrogen levels both endogenously and exogenously is associated with an increased risk of breast cancer.43 Consistent with our results, many studies have shown that hormone replacement therapy involving the administration of exogenous estrogen together with progesterone can increase the risk of breast cancer.44

Raloxifene, a second-generation selective estrogen-receptor modulator (SERM), can interact with intracellular ERs in target organs as estrogen agonists and antagonists, thus it can be indicated for the prevention or treatment of diseases caused by estrogen deficiency, including osteoporosis in postmenopausal-women. In addition, pre-clinical and clinical evidence both support its preventive effect against invasive breast cancer.45 A systematic review pooling placebo-controlled clinical trials from the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force showed that the use of raloxifene reduced 56% risk of invasive breast cancer.46 However, despite the benefits of raloxifene, it is also associated with some side effects, albeit rare, such as thromboembolic events (RR 1.56; 95% CI, 1.11 to 2.60 for raloxifene).

Tolterodine is the most prescribed medication to treat overactive bladder in Sweden.47 Previous studies have observed a high incidence of bladder and prostate cancer after the initiation of overactive bladder drugs. However, the result might be biased by protopathic bias, which is, for example, that the symptoms that prompted the patients to use the medication were early symptoms of cancer, thus leading to detection shortly after treatment initiation.48,49 Another study from Sweden observed an inverse association between exposure to overactive bladder medications and a diagnosis of colon cancer or lung cancer.50 So far, there has not been a study conducted that has investigated the relationship between tolterodine use and breast cancer and both preclinical and clinical studies are highly needed in the future.

Nitrofurantoin is an antibiotic used to treat and prevent urinary tract infections. Until now, evidence for the association between nitrofurantoin and cancer has been lacking.51 Only two large population-based studies from Nordic countries suggested that prenatal exposure to nitrofurantoin was associated with a higher risk of childhood leukemia.52,53 Results are needed to be validated in other populations and other cancer types.

The main strength of the present study is that we performed a comprehensive MR analysis to identify actionable druggable targets from various data sources, and further validated using a population-based study. Based on data from the population at a national level, we were able to efficiently minimize selection bias and recall bias. Secondly, with exceptionally large sample sizes both for MR analysis and the population-based study, our study possesses excellent statistical power, providing significant results that may help to elucidate causal relationships. Thirdly, besides SMR as the primary analysis, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using five additional MR approaches to investigate the association between MAPT expression and breast cancer. Fourth, we used the HEIDI test and colocalization methods to minimize the chances of false positive results due to confounding by LD and horizontal pleiotropy. Furthermore, to minimize the biases caused by various genetic backgrounds, we restricted our sample selection to individuals of exclusively European ancestry. Finally, by utilizing comprehensive datasets on gene expression, DNA methylation, and plasma protein levels, we employed a rigorous instrument selection process to assess around 1400 actionable drug targets and generated an updated catalog of all causal-inference targets and drugs that can be studied beyond breast cancer outcomes.

This study also has several limitations that warrant consideration. Firstly, although we utilized instrumental variants from various data sources, they were not sufficient to cover the entire druggable genome that could be targeted by drugs. So far, the available eQTL and mQTL datasets did not cover genetic variants which were associated with gene expression or methylation levels in the X and Y chromosome. Secondly, we restricted our eQTL and mQTL instruments to variants acting in cis to minimize the potential bias by horizontal pleiotropy. However, we might not be able to detect associations that are isoform-specific at the same time. Thirdly, although we identified eight causal druggable targets from MR analyses and mapping to 319 candidate drugs, only 36 of them were eventually validated in our population-based study to ensure enough power. Nevertheless, we provided a full list of potential drugs which can be further validated in future experimental and population-based studies. Fourthly, for the population-based study, information on BMI, smoking, alcohol drinking, physical activity, and dietary factors was not available in our nationwide registers. However, our regression models include COPD as a proxy for smoking. Although it is a crude proxy for smoking, it might partly exclude the confounding effect of smoking. Furthermore, we adjusted for education level and income, factors closely linked to lifestyle choices, aiming to partially offset their confounding influences. Lastly, it's important to note that we were unable to assess non-adherence to drug utilization using data from the Swedish Drug Register. It's worth acknowledging that our results might be underestimated, as the bias in information regarding non-adherence could potentially be limited to the study group.

In conclusion, this large-scale MR analysis combined with population-based validation found that the use of raloxifene is associated with a reduced risk of breast cancer, which is consistent with STAR clinical trial and provides further evidence that MR study might indeed find the causal targeting genes for breast cancer. In contrast, the use of estradiol, tolterodine, and nitrofurantoin might increase the risk of developing breast cancer, which calls for clinical attention and further validation.
