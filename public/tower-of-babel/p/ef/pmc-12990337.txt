Motivational interviewing for health behavior change among young adults in Canada: Findings from the MOTIVATE pilot randomized controlled trial
Authors: Taylor Incze, Sahar Khademioore, Elizabeth Alvarez, Matthew Kwan, Jean-Eric Tarride, Lawrence Mbuagbaw, Laura N. Anderson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: Young adults face rising rates of preventable chronic disease, including obesity and cancer; yet prevention interventions remain limited. Motivational interviewing may support behavior change but few studies have examined its feasibility in preventive settings. This study assessed the feasibility of a behavioral and educational intervention promoting healthy behaviors among young adults and explored secondary clinical outcomes.Methods: We conducted a six-month pilot randomized controlled trial at a Canadian university. Data collection occurred between February 2023 and January 2024. Participants aged 18–29 were randomized to an education-only control or a motivational interviewing intervention (up to seven sessions). All participants received a wearable fitness device. Primary feasibility outcomes included recruitment, retention, data completion, and satisfaction. Secondary outcomes included body mass index, physical activity, sedentary behavior, fruit and vegetable intake, quality of life, and mental health.Results: 101 students were randomized (52 intervention, 49 control), and 80.2% completed follow-up. Feasibility targets were met for recruitment (86.3%), retention (80.2%), data completion (93%), and satisfaction (82.7%). Clinical changes were modest. Most participants recalled early-life health factors, supporting the feasibility of future risk stratification.Conclusions: This trial met all prespecified feasibility thresholds and demonstrated high satisfaction; findings will inform progression to a full-scale trial.

Introduction

Young adulthood is a formative period when changes in nutrition, physical activity, and mental health begin to influence chronic disease risk (Rod et al., 2025). Excess weight gain during this period increases the likelihood of cardiovascular disease, cancer, and premature mortality (Adams et al., 2014; Allman-Farinelli, 2015). Establishing healthy behaviors during this time may reduce these risks (Lawrence et al., 2017; Mutschler et al., 2018; Rod et al., 2025; Stroud et al., 2015). Despite calls for action, young adults remain underrepresented in preventive health behavior research, limiting evidence for effective interventions (Allen & Wilkins, 2023; Fusar-Poli et al., 2021; Stroud et al., 2015; Watson et al., 2022).

A persistent challenge in prevention is bridging the gap between intention and action (Kelly & Barker, 2016). Motivational interviewing (MI) offers a solution by using guided, client-centered conversations to strengthen an individual's motivation and commitment to change (Miller & Rollnick, 2012). Grounded in collaboration and respect for autonomy, MI employs strategies such as reflective listening and eliciting change talk to help individuals explore and resolve ambivalence about health behaviors (Rollnick & Miller, 1995). Although MI has been widely applied across a variety of health contexts, including smoking cessation, substance use disorders, and chronic disease management, its use has been largely limited to secondary and tertiary care contexts (Christie & Channon, 2014; Ekong & Kavookjian, 2016; Jensen et al., 2011; Lee et al., 2016; Smedslund et al., 2011). To our knowledge, no studies have evaluated MI as a prevention strategy focused on healthy behavior change in young adults.

To address this gap, we conducted a six-month parallel-group, pilot randomized controlled trial (RCT). The primary objective was to evaluate the feasibility of a behavioral and educational intervention to promote healthy behaviors in young adults, assessed through recruitment, retention, data completion, and participant satisfaction. Secondary objectives were to explore preliminary effects on body mass index (BMI), physical activity, sedentary behavior, fruit and vegetable intake, quality of life (QOL) and mental health, and to assess the feasibility of early-life risk stratification.

Methods

Study design and population

This two-arm, parallel-group, pilot RCT was prospectively registered (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT05264740) and described in a published protocol (Anderson et al., 2023). The study was conducted at McMaster University in Hamilton, Ontario, Canada, with ethics approval from the Hamilton Integrated Research Ethics Board (Project: 14675, July 25, 2022). Data collection occurred between February 2023 and January 2024. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional and national guidelines. All participants provided written informed consent.

Eligible participants were McMaster University students aged 18 to 29 years with a BMI ≥18.5 kg/m2 who were fluent in English and had home internet access. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy or health conditions contraindicating weight loss (e.g., eating disorder). We recruited participants using convenience sampling through university-wide emails and posters.

Three trained facilitators delivered the intervention after completing accredited Level 2 MI training through the Association for Psychological Therapies (APT Canada; 18 h continuing professional development). To support consistent MI delivery, facilitators participated in guided practice sessions with a physician experienced in MI (co-author E.A.), who provided feedback on technique and supported skill development. This physician was also available for consultation and check-ins throughout the study period.

All participants received printed educational materials based on Canada's Food Guide and the Canadian 24-Hour Movement Guidelines, as well as an Inspire 2 Fitbit wearable activity tracker (The Canadian Society for Exercise Physiology (CSEP), 2025). The intervention group received seven individual MI sessions: one at baseline and one monthly thereafter over the six-month intervention period. Each session was 30–60 min in duration and was offered virtually or in person, based on participant preference. Facilitators employed MI principles, adapting each session to the participant's goals and challenges by using core techniques such as open-ended questions, affirmations, reflections, and summaries.

During the first session, facilitators focused on building rapport, exploring participants' motivations for change, and setting personalized goals. Participants were invited to select priority areas for goal setting from a predefined list (physical activity, sleep, screen time, eating behaviors, weight, substance use, and stress), and were also asked whether there were any additional areas they would like to discuss. Participants then selected one to two specific goals to work on. In follow-up sessions, facilitators reviewed participants' progress, explored barriers and facilitators, and adjusted goals as needed. They also helped participants develop specific plans for when, where, and how to act on their goals, and supported them in identifying strategies to overcome obstacles.

We used Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), a secure, web-based software platform hosted at St. Joseph's Healthcare Hamilton, to collect and manage study data (Harris et al., 2009). Facilitators used structured REDCap forms to document participant goals and session content during each MI session and referred to these notes in follow-up visits to support continuity across sessions.

Participants in both groups had access to usual health care without restrictions. To support adherence, intervention participants received email reminders before each session, and missed sessions were rescheduled when possible. The research team did not anticipate serious harms but identified possible adverse outcomes including disordered eating, frustration related to weight change, and emotional distress. We informed participants of these risks prior to enrollment, monitored for these concerns during the study and had protocols in place to safely address any signs of self-harm or harm to others.

PEPI-for-Windows (WINPEPI) was used to calculate the sample size based on feasibility objectives (Abramson, 2004). The estimate of 110 participants (55 per group) allowed a 7.5% margin of error at a 95% confidence level, based on an expected 80% completion rate.

The Research Methodology Centre at St. Joseph's Healthcare Hamilton generated the computer-based randomization sequence to allocate participants in a 1:1 ratio. Randomization used a stratified, permuted block design with randomly varying block sizes to promote group balance by sex (male, female) and BMI category (<25 vs. ≥25). The allocation sequence was concealed from both participants and the research team in REDCap until eligibility was confirmed and informed consent obtained. In this open-label trial, we did not blind participants or care providers (i.e., MI facilitators) to group allocation.

Measures

The primary outcomes assessed feasibility through four indicators. The recruitment rate reflected the proportion of eligible participants who enrolled after contacting the research team. Retention measured the proportion of enrolled participants who completed the six-month follow-up. Data completion captured the percentage of secondary clinical outcome data with no missing responses. Participant satisfaction was measured in the final survey with the question “How satisfied are you with this research study?”, with responses recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from highly dissatisfied to highly satisfied. The study defined feasibility thresholds (i.e., progression criteria) as ≥50% for recruitment and ≥ 80% for data completion at baseline, ≥80% retention over the six-month study period, and ≥ 60% of participants reporting satisfaction at six months.

Secondary outcomes included changes from baseline to six months in BMI, fruit and vegetable intake, physical activity, sedentary behavior, QOL, and mental health. The research team measured height and weight during the intake appointment (consent visit) to calculate baseline BMI. Participants reported their weight monthly for six months using either a Bluetooth-connected e-scale provided by the study, available in a discrete location outside the study office (which automatically synced to the Fitbit app), or a home scale, with weight manually entered into the app. All weight data were compiled via Fitabase, a research platform that aggregates and organizes Fitbit-linked data (Fitabase, 2025). These six follow-up BMI data points were used to assess change over time.

Online questionnaires collected the other secondary outcomes at baseline and every two months. Participants reported fruit and vegetable intake using the National Cancer Institute's Dietary Screener Questionnaire (DSQ). They completed the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) to report physical activity, from which we calculated moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) minutes per week from the IPAQ and categorized participants as meeting the physical activity guideline if they reported both ≥150 min/week of MVPA and ≥ 2 days/week of strength training. We measured sedentary behavior using the International Sedentary Assessment Tool (ISAT), and QOL through the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L). To evaluate mental health, participants completed the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale 10-item version (CES-D-10) to assess depressive symptoms and the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item scale (GAD-7) to assess anxiety symptoms, with scores ≥10 on each indicating elevated symptoms.

Data was collected using Fitbit devices worn on the wrist, with raw data extracted via Fitabase. Wrist placement (dominant vs non-dominant) was not standardized, but was specified by participants within the Fitbit app. We defined adherence as wearing the Fitbit device for ≥10 h per day. Weight data collected via the Fitbit app were also used to assess adherence to self-monitoring. Weigh-in adherence was defined as weighing in at least once per month over the six-month study period (100% adherence) or at least once in three or more months (50% adherence). Weekly minutes of MVPA were calculated by summing moderate and very active minutes and analyzed for all participants with available Fitbit MVPA data.

Feasibility of collecting self-reported early-life obesity risk factors was explored. These included gestational age (preterm vs. full-term), birth weight, breastfeeding history, parental obesity history (maternal and paternal), childhood and teenage physical activity, childhood financial concern, and age at menarche (among female participants). These self-reported baseline data were analyzed descriptively to assess the proportion of participants who completed each item and to examine variability in responses across the sample.

Statistical analysis

Analysis followed an intention-to-treat approach. Feasibility outcomes were summarized descriptively and compared to prespecified thresholds. Baseline characteristics were described as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges for continuous variables, and as frequencies with percentages for categorical variables. We reported between-group differences in secondary outcomes descriptively without hypothesis testing as the pilot trial was not designed to detect statistical significance. We did not apply imputation methods (e.g., multiple imputation) and assumed missing data were missing at random due to loss to follow-up or nonresponse. For secondary outcomes, when six-month data were missing, we used the most recent available measurement from the 4- or 2-month time point. Since this was an exploratory feasibility trial, we did not conduct sensitivity analyses. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.4.2.

Results

Participant characteristics

Between February and June 2023, the team assessed 120 individuals for eligibility. Of these, three were excluded and 101 were randomized (52 intervention, 49 control). One intervention participant did not receive the intervention due to an administrative error but was analyzed in their assigned group. One control participant withdrew immediately post-randomization without providing data. At six-month follow-up, 81 participants (80.2%) completed assessments (43 intervention, 38 control). Follow-up was completed by January 2024, and the trial completed as planned without early termination. See Fig. 1 for the full Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) diagram. All participants chose virtual MI sessions. Attendance declined from 51 participants at baseline to 24 at month six. Eighty-eight participants recorded at least one valid wear day (≥10 h). Over the six-month study period (180 expected days), median valid wear days were 73 (IQR: 34, 152; n = 40) for the control group and 80.5 (IQR: 36.5, 153; n = 48) for the intervention group. Among these, 10 participants (11.4%) met the 100% adherence threshold (weighing in at least once per month), and 41 (46.6%) met the 50% adherence threshold (weighing in at least three months). Median MVPA per week was 106 min (IQR: 16, 276; n = 46) in the control group and 119 min (IQR: 25, 315; n = 50) in the intervention group.

Most participants reported early-life factors (Table S.1). Specifically, 90% reported gestational age (preterm or full-term), 89% reported breastfeeding history, and 92% and 91% reported maternal and paternal obesity histories, respectively. Childhood physical activity was reported by 96% of participants, and teenage physical activity by 99%. Data on childhood financial concern were available for 97% of the sample. Age at menarche was reported by 53 of 54 female participants (98%). In contrast, only 26% of participants knew their birth weight.

Discussion

This pilot trial demonstrated that MI for healthy behavior change in young adults is feasible, with high recruitment, retention, and satisfaction. Secondary outcomes were collected with minimal missing data, though changes over six months were modest. A larger study would be needed to evaluate clinical significance. Early-life risk stratification data were successfully gathered, suggesting potential for tailoring future prevention interventions.

Feasibility findings align with results from other MI studies across diverse populations (Lamoureux et al., 2024; Scott et al., 2019; Simpson et al., 2015; Wintle et al., 2025). One similar feasibility trial targeting weight loss in adults with a current or prior BMI ≥30 kg/m2 found 84% retention and over 80% adherence across both arms over 12 months (Simpson et al., 2015). All participants chose virtual delivery of MI sessions, which may have enhanced accessibility and convenience. Although some studies suggest that young adults prefer in-person care, growing evidence indicates that many young adults see advantages in telehealth (e.g., alleviation of stigma and increased accessibility) (Sweeney et al., 2019). This aligns with findings from a recent scoping review of technology-delivered MI, which found that such formats are feasible, well-received, and especially suited to digitally literate populations such as young adults (Pedamallu et al., 2022).

Changes in secondary clinical outcomes were modest, with varying trends between groups. BMI and weight decreased slightly in both arms. A meta-analysis of 24 RCTs in adults found that MI interventions, most of which were delivered over six months or longer, led to significant post-treatment weight loss in 37.5% of studies, with 54.2% achieving ≥5% loss of initial body weight (Barnes & Ivezaj, 2015). However, these findings were based on older adult populations, with average ages in the 40s to 60s, and most studies recruited individuals with existing cardiometabolic risk factors or obesity, limiting comparability to our sample.

In our study, MVPA increased in the intervention group and decreased in the control group, while sedentary time increased in both. A 2023 meta-analysis of 97 trials found that MI-based interventions led to modest increases in physical activity and reductions in sedentary time, but effects were small, short-lived, and based on low to very low certainty evidence (Zhu et al., 2024). Most included studies enrolled adults with chronic health conditions. Our findings suggest that MI may help support MVPA among young adults, though changes in sedentary behavior may be more difficult to achieve. Fitbit-derived MVPA trends aligned with self-reported improvements and with group differences in MVPA guideline adherence, supporting the feasibility of wearables for capturing behavior change in this population. In contrast, engagement with active self-monitoring behaviors, such as regular weight logging, was inconsistent. While most participants recorded at least one weigh-in, few did so regularly, indicating a need for additional prompts or support.

Fruit and vegetable intake remained low across both groups, consistent with national data showing that in 2023, only 17.6% of adults aged 18–34 reported consuming fruits or vegetables five or more times per day (Statistics Canada, 2025). Health-related QOL, measured by EQ-5D-5L utility scores, remained high and stable in both arms, slightly above the Canadian population average, suggesting that participants entered the study in generally good health, which may have limited room for improvement.

Rates of clinically significant anxiety and depression were high and increased over the study period, with nearly 40% of participants screening positive for moderate to severe anxiety and over half meeting criteria for moderate to severe depressive symptoms. These findings are consistent with U.S. national data from the 2019 National College Health Assessment, where 69% of students reported overwhelming anxiety, 51% reported depression severe enough to impair functioning, and 16% had seriously considered suicide in the past year (American College Health Association, 2019). These results underscore the mental health burden among postsecondary students and highlight the importance of embedding mental health supports within prevention-focused interventions targeting this population.

Our study also explored the feasibility of early-life risk stratification using self-reported data on childhood and family history, with the goal of supporting more tailored approaches to chronic disease prevention. A substantial proportion of participants were able to recall key indicators from their early life, such as parental weight status, birth weight, and early growth patterns, suggesting this data could be pragmatically collected in future prevention trials. These factors influence long-term health through genetic predisposition and shared environmental exposures (Whitaker et al., 1997). While most current risk stratification approaches rely on clinical or biomarker data, systematically collected self-reported information such as family history or early-life exposures can also support risk-based intervention strategies (Clark et al., 2001; Morales et al., 2021). Our findings highlight the potential of engaging young adults in reflective, preventive health assessments that incorporate their early-life history, laying the groundwork for future trials designed around individual risk profiles.

Key strengths of this study include a pragmatic design, fully virtual delivery and comprehensive data collection across behavioral, psychosocial, and early-life domains. The study population was also diverse and consistent with the overall student population at McMaster University. Limitations include recruitment from a single university, a six-month follow-up that may not capture seasonal or academic influences, and variable engagement with active self-monitoring. While standardized training and oversight were used for MI delivery, MI fidelity was not assessed in this pilot study.

These findings offer useful considerations for designing future prevention trials in young adults. Future studies may benefit from more active integration of wearable technology, such as routine Fitbit check-ins or automated reminders to support consistent engagement. Future work may explore optimal session frequency, and the integration of mental health and health promotion supports. Additionally, future research should consider the influence of seasonal and academic factors on mental health symptoms. Lastly, future definitive trials should incorporate formal MI fidelity monitoring as an important methodological component.

Conclusion

This pilot study demonstrated the feasibility of a MI intervention targeting health behavior change in young adults. Although clinical outcomes remained relatively stable, the trial successfully engaged participants, collected high-quality data, and identified areas for methodological refinement. These findings support the rationale for a fully powered randomized trial with modifications and highlight the potential of virtually delivered MI as a scalable strategy for supporting healthy behavior change in young adults.
