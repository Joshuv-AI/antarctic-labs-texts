Redirecting AAV vectors to extrahepatic tissues
Authors: Aravind Asokan, Shen Shen
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecombinant adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors are the current benchmark for systemic delivery of gene therapies to multiple organs in vivo. Despite clinical successes, safe and effective gene delivery to extrahepatic tissues has proven challenging due to dose limiting toxicity arising from high liver uptake of AAV vectors. Deeper understanding of AAV structure, receptor biology, and pharmacology has enabled the design and engineering of liver-de-targeted capsids ushering in several new vector candidates. This next generation of AAVs offers significant promise for extrahepatic gene delivery to cardiovascular, musculoskeletal, and neurological tissues with improved safety profiles.Keywords: adeno-associated virus, capsid, toxicity, adverse events, safety, liver-de-targeting, vector engineering, extrahepatic, biodistribution, tropism, gene delivery, gene transfer

Introduction

Adeno-associated viral (AAV) vectors have emerged as the leading platform for delivering gene therapies in the past decade. Currently, there are five AAV-based products approved by the FDA: voretigene neparvovec-rzyl (Luxturna) for treating RPE65-associated Leber’s congenital amaurosis type 2 (LCA2), onasemnogene abeparvovec (Zolgensma) for a subtype of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA), etranacogene dezaparvovec (Hemgenix) for treating hemophilia B, delandistrogene moxeparvovec (Elevidys) for treating Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) in children ages 4–5, and valoctocogene roxaparvovec-rvox (Roctavian) for treating hemophilia A.1,2,3,4,5 In addition, there are 206 registered gene therapy clinical trials based on recombinant AAV vectors registered at ClinicalTrials.gov as of September 2023.

As clinical trials progress, safety and efficacy data in humans continue to provide insight into risk-benefit profiles for different gene therapy modalities. Importantly, these readouts have helped guide adjustments in dosage and immunomodulatory regimens while highlighting unmet needs for improved vector design. As reviewed in these meta-analysis studies,2,6 a few safety concerns have emerged from systemic AAV gene therapies across different therapeutic areas. First, transient elevation in liver enzymes has been documented following intravenous dosing in a wide range of subjects. Secondly, innate immune responses to high viral load leading to complement activation, thrombotic microangiopathy, and in some cases, death have been reported. Although several immunomodulatory regimens and interventions have been utilized to address these clinical sequalae,7 significant concerns remain with regard to the high dose needed for systemic AAV gene therapies. There is a critical need to minimize the risks posed by high AAV doses to hepatic tissues and expand the relatively narrow therapeutic window of development candidates for cardiac, musculoskeletal, neuromuscular, and neurological disorders. Here, we highlight the rationale, structure-function correlates, and engineering principles behind liver-de-targeted AAV capsids and the considerations surrounding their clinical translation for systemic gene therapies that do not involve the liver as a target organ. Many research groups in the field are utilizing different approaches to capsid evolution, capsid mining from different species, as well as rational targeting strategies to enhance the safety profile of systemic AAV gene therapies. While these aspects have been reviewed elsewhere, the focus of this report is to detail the rationale and design principles behind redirecting AAV to extrahepatic tissues to improve clinical safety.

High dose AAV and the human liver: The dose makes the poison

The liver is among the largest solid organs in the human body, composed of five major cell types: hepatocytes, liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, Kupffer cells (resident macrophages), stellate cells, and pit cells (natural killer cells). As part of the reticuloendothelial system (RES) responsible for phagocytosis of intravenously circulating exogenous substances, the liver plays a vital role in clearing and metabolizing a range of molecular, macromolecular, and cellular entities. Consequently, the liver is heavily exposed to recombinant AAV vectors that enter the blood circulation, particularly after systemic injection. Many naturally occurring AAV serotypes are sequestered by the liver after intravenous (i.v.) dosing. This “hepatotropic” phenotype can be attributed to the recirculation of AAV vectors after i.v. infusion, substantial exposure of the liver in particular, and high expression of AAV receptor (AAVR) in hepatocytes. The high volume of blood flow, combined with the microvascular structure of liver sinusoids, provides a large surface area for plasma contact and consequently results in abundant uptake by different cell types. While AAV serotypes can display vastly different transduction profiles in the liver of different species, preclinical biodistribution studies unequivocally show that AAV1-9 and AAVrh.10 capsids are highly sequestered by the liver in a species-independent manner.8,9,10,11 The risk of genotoxicity posed by random integration of AAV vector genomes at high vector doses continues to be the subject of investigation.12

In postmortem analysis of two human subjects treated with Zolgensma, vector genome copy numbers were 300–1000 times higher in liver than in the CNS tissues after intravenous administration.13 Correspondingly, SMN transgene expression was also detected in liver samples using RT-ddPCR and immunohistochemistry (IHC). In a study of 325 SMA patients who received Zolgensma via intravenous infusion, 90 out of 100 patients analyzed had elevated liver transaminases (alanine aminotransferase [ALT] and/or aspartate aminotransferase [AST] and/or bilirubin concentrations). In addition, 34% patients experienced liver-associated adverse events in clinical trials.14 More than 40% of patients received concomitant medications upon onset of hepatotoxicity, with all related events in these patients being resolved with prednisolone treatment, albeit at varied duration. However, in August 2022, nearly 6 weeks after receiving Zolgensma, two patients died of acute liver failure despite receiving a tapering dose of corticosteroids.15 Similarly, in the ASPIRO phase 1/2 clinical trial (NCT03199469) treating X-linked myotubular myopathy (XLMTM), a recombinant AAV8 vector encoding human MTM1 gene under the Des promoter was delivered to XLMTM patients under 5 years of age. Three patients in the high dose group (3 × 1014 vg/kg) died in 2020.16 A fourth patient in this trial died in September 2021 after lowering the dose (1.3 × 1014 vg/kg).17 All four patients had abnormal liver function tests in the weeks following vector administration but also had evidence of pre-existing hepatobiliary disease. In case of recently approved Elevidys for treating DMD in 4- to 5-year-old patients, FDA highlighted acute liver injury as one of the most commonly reported side effects and recommended monitoring of liver function prior to Elevidys treatment and weekly after treatment for the first 3 months.18 Taken together, these serious adverse events and regulatory guidelines clearly highlight a need to develop strategies to mitigate the impact of viral load on the liver posed by systemic gene therapies.

Although not the subject of this review, vector quality (or purity) is another critical factor that may contribute to dose-dependent toxicity observed with AAV vectors. In this regard, empty or partial virions composed of AAV capsids lacking genomes or packaging DNA fragments, respectively, can significantly contribute to viral load. While generally presumed pharmacologically inactive from a transgene expression perspective, such viral particles have been shown to decrease transduction levels and have also been associated with elevated liver enzyme levels upon i.v. dosing.19 Moreover, the role of empty AAV virions in triggering immune responses has been established separately.20 Overall, empty virions, contaminating DNA packaging virions, and/or partial vector genome packaging virions can act as defective interfering particles and, as a consequence, decrease the potency of AAV vector preparations and contribute to increased risk of adverse effects (reviewed elsewhere in detail).21

Decreasing AAV vector sequestration by the liver

Approaches to mitigate AAV-mediated hepatoxicity could involve reducing vector dose, manipulating the vector genome, the capsid, or both. While reducing viral load can mitigate liver toxicity, lowering systemic vector dose decreases gene delivery efficiency in extrahepatic tissues such as muscle and brain. Approaches to incremental dosing of AAV vectors by transient immunosuppression have been studied in preclinical models and humans, offering a possible solution for clinical applications that may warrant high doses.22,23 Another strategy to decrease transgene expression in hepatocytes has involved the development of cell/tissue-specific promoters that limit expression to extrahepatic tissues such as skeletal muscle, heart, or neurological tissues.2 Alternatively, incorporation of microRNA binding sites in the 3′ untranslated region of AAV vector genomes (specifically, targeting miR-122) has been shown to reduce hepatic transgene expression.24,25 While these latter approaches decrease the likelihood of a host immune response to transgene product expressed in the liver, the risk of acute liver toxicity arising from viral load is not alleviated.

The most direct strategy to decrease liver exposure to viral load involves redirection of AAV capsids away from the liver. The working hypothesis is that reducing AAV capsid interactions with their cognate cell surface attachment factors and receptors can reduce liver sequestration. One of the first examples of such was demonstrated by mutating heparan sulfate binding (R585, R588) residues on the AAV2 capsid surface.26 This approach was further embellished by the finding that domain swapping of the AAV2 variable region (VR) VIII with corresponding residues from other AAV serotypes such as AAV8 resulted in improved cardiac and musculoskeletal gene expression accompanied by decreased liver uptake in mice and monkeys.27,28 This engineered variant, AAV2i8, is the first example of a liver-de-targeted vector currently being evaluated in a phase I gene therapy clinical trial for congestive heart failure.29

This hypothesis was further corroborated with AAV9, which utilizes terminal N-linked galactose as a cell surface attachment factor.30,31 Random mutagenesis of AAV9 VP residues revealed a cluster of residues (N498, W503, Q590, and P504) that when modified, markedly reduce liver sequestration in mice.32 With the exception of Q590, three of these residues are directly implicated or lie adjacent to the galactose binding pocket in the AAV9 capsid. In subsequent studies using barcode-seq, this list of residues was substantially expanded and upon mutation exhibited a liver-de-targeted phenotype. Further mechanistic studies proposing a pharmacokinetic model confirmed that increased galactose binding avidity is correlated with high liver uptake and inversely correlated with circulation half-life of AAV9 capsids.33

Similar studies with AAV1 have implicated that mutating W503 as well as residues 263–270 in the VR-I domain can influence sialic acid binding avidity and, consequently, liver de-targeting. Interestingly, cryo-EM studies of AAV1 capsids complexed with the cognate AAVR reveal that N269, W503, T504, and D590 mutations all consistently reduce receptor binding and decrease transduction efficiency. Exactly how capsid engagement of glycans vs. AAVR may impact liver de-targeting in an overlapping (or perhaps even synergistic) manner is currently unclear. Nevertheless, it is important to note these residues have also been implicated in capsid-AAVR interactions for several serotypes.34,35,36 Consistently, studies utilizing an in silico designed ancestral AAV library highlighted a critical role for amino acid residues 266/267 as a “liver toggle,” which can influence the biodistribution and transduction of capsid variants in murine and non-human primate livers.37 These residues were transferable to the VR-I of AAV9 to achieve liver de-targeting. Overall, these studies with AAV1, 2, and 9 clearly implicate VR-I and VR-VIII residues on the capsid surface and their interactions with glycan or AAVR molecules as holding the key to liver de-targeting.

Systemic fate of liver-de-targeted AAV: Can we redirect to extrahepatic tissues?

Another key question regarding AAV capsid liver de-targeting is whether vector biodistribution to extrahepatic tissues is impacted. From a pharmacokinetic perspective, reduced liver sequestration should increase the amount of vector available to redistribute to other tissues. However, this is also contingent on the ability of capsids to cross the endothelial barrier in the case of muscle, brain, heart, kidney, and other vascularized organs. Alternatively, vector elimination via the RES via organs such as the spleen is likely (modeled in Figure 1). Indeed, pharmacokinetic/biodistribution analysis of multiple liver-de-targeted AAV mutants corroborates (1) increased circulation half-life, (2) increased splenic uptake, and (3) no change in vector uptake for extrahepatic tissues.27,28,33,37,38,39 Taken together, liver de-targeting of AAV capsids can largely be regarded as a loss of function, albeit posing potential advantages from the perspective of reducing viral load that can help improve clinical safety.

One approach to embellish this loss-of-function attribute of liver-de-targeted AAVs is to provide additional functionality by way of engineering noncanonical receptor engagement. In this regard, peptide display on the surface of AAV capsids has been particularly successful.40,41 Improved uptake of peptide display in extrahepatic tissues, particularly the brain42,43,44,45 and skeletal muscle,41,46,47 has been demonstrated by multiple groups. It is interesting to note that liver de-targeting of these engineered capsids (e.g., PHP.eB, MyoAAV, AAVMYO, CAP-B10, etc.) across animal species is a common theme in these reports. Thus, insertion of peptide epitopes into the VR-VIII region could perform a dual role: loss of function by way of disrupting cognate receptor interactions (i.e., glycan or AAVR) and a gain of function involving engagement of a noncanonical host receptor (e.g., LY6A, integrin αVβ6, etc.). The latter hypothesis is further corroborated by structural studies with AAV-PHP.eB capsid, wherein the peptide insert was shown to partially destabilize interactions with AAVR while simultaneously mediating interactions with the LY6A receptor.48 As our understanding of AAV capsid biology in humans continues to evolve, one can envision the generation of “blank canvas” capsid templates that can be engineered with targeting and de-targeting functions for improved clinical safety and efficacy.

Pre-existing immunity and immunogenicity of liver-de-targeted capsids

Minimal data exist with regard to the impact of pre-existing humoral immunity to liver-de-targeted AAV vectors in preclinical animal models or from clinical studies. Arguably, pre-existing humoral immunity to naturally existing AAV serotypes can potentially be extrapolated to liver-de-targeted AAV capsid mutants or their peptide-displaying counterparts. While it is plausible that disruption of antibody recognition epitopes in VR-VIII or VR-I (which are predominant locations for liver de-targeting mutations) may help evade some specific neutralizing antibodies, it is evident that iterative and multiple capsid alterations will be required for clinically meaningful antibody evasion.49,50,51,52 An additional consideration for peptides displayed on AAV capsids for improved gene transfer to extrahepatic tissues is the possibility that such epitopes may share homology with epitopes recognized by pre-existing antibodies (NAbs) against other antigens. Importantly, such antibodies may neutralize engineered capsids or activate complements as seen with conventional AAV vectors. Thus, in-depth analysis of the impact of pre-existing immunity on liver-de-targeted AAV capsids in appropriate models is warranted.

Our current understanding of the potential immunogenicity of liver-de-targeted capsids is also minimal. A generally accepted paradigm is that liver gene transfer with conventional AAV vectors induces tolerance by mechanisms including programmed cell death of CD4+ T helper cells and the induction of FoxP3+ regulatory T cells.53,54,55 Notably, these findings are in the context of AAV-mediated transgene expression, wherein antigen-specific tolerance to the translated protein has been demonstrated in multiple animal models. Whether presentation of AAV capsid proteins as antigens to draining lymph nodes or resident antigen-presenting cells is a detriment (as seen at high vector doses) or poses some benefits from an immune tolerance perspective is unclear from a mechanistic perspective. More importantly, while the decreased hepatic sequestration of liver-de-targeted AAV may reduce the likelihood of dose-dependent immunotoxicity, potential interplay with immune tolerance mechanisms, especially with gene supplementation strategies in patients with genetic truncations/deletions needs further evaluation.

Synopsis

After a decade-long effort, liver-de-targeted AAVs have entered the conversation as viable candidates for systemic gene transfer in a clinical setting. Discovered serendipitously as loss-of-function mutations, a better understanding of the biology underlying liver sequestration (and conversely, de-targeting thereof) of AAV capsids has fueled an increased interest in leveraging these vector platforms to mitigate the risk of liver immunotoxicity and genotoxicity at high doses. The potential for “add-on” functions such as increased uptake in extrahepatic tissue (e.g., in CNS or muscle) through peptide display has now been demonstrated in large animal models. The success of liver-de-targeted AAVs hinges on developing a deeper understanding of clearance mechanisms, extrahepatic tissue residence time, and immunogenicity in the clinical setting, ultimately gathering clear evidence of expanding the therapeutic window for systemic gene therapies in patients.
